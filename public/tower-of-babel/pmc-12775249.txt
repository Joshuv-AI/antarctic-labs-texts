Patterns of multiple caregiving and the influence on depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults in China
Authors: Xinyi Zhao, Quan Zhang, Vivian W. Q. Lou, Siqing Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With extended life expectancy, middle-aged and older adults have become more involved in multiple types of caregiving. This study aimed to investigate the patterns of multiple caregiving on the basis of the level of participation in parent care, spouse care, and grandchild care and to determine whether these patterns predict follow-up depressive symptoms. Data were obtained from the 2011–2018 waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. A total of 11,775 participants aged 50 years and older in 2011 were included in the latent class analysis to identify the patterns of multiple caregiving. A regression model was used to analyse the associations between baseline caregiving patterns and subsequent depressive symptoms. The results revealed four caregiving patterns: “part-time care for all,” “full-time parent care and grandchild care,” “part-time spouse care,” and “full-time spouse care.” Compared with the “part-time care for all” group, the “full-time spouse care” group and the “part-time spouse care” group reported significantly greater depressive symptoms seven years later, whereas the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group did not significantly differ. The use of categorising multiple caregiving could vividly describe middle-aged and older adults’ engagement in caregiving activities and provide a comprehensive prediction of mental health outcomes. Social services should be tailored to sandwiched caregivers in certain caregiving patterns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10433-025-00904-7.

Introduction

Middle-aged and older adults play a crucial role in family caregiving. They often assume caregiving responsibilities when their older parents or spouses experience illness or functional impairments (Gruijters 2017; Zhao et al. 2023). Moreover, middle-aged and older adults are highly involved in grandparenting. For example, more than half of the grandparents in China provide childcare (Zhang et al. 2020), which is reinforced by the relatively young retirement age (i.e. 50 or 55 for females and 60 for males) in this country (Falkingham et al. 2020). Owing to extended life expectancy, increased labour participation in females, and delayed marriage and childbearing, the period in which middle-aged and older adults need to care for their parents, spouses, and/or grandchildren may overlap, increasing the likelihood that they will take on multiple caregiving roles. From 1990 to 2020, the average life expectancy in China increased from 68.55 years to 77.93 years, whereas the average age of women giving births for the first time increased from 24.03 years to 29.13 years (Office of the Leading Group of the State Council for the Seventh National Population Census 2022). Although there have been studies on the effects of family caregiving on mental health among middle-aged and older adults, research specifically addressing multiple caregiving roles is still lacking. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the patterns of multiple caregiving activities and their impact on mental health.

Family caregiving and mental health among middle-aged and older adults

Previous studies on the impact of family caregiving on mental health have focused predominantly on a specific care task, such as caring for parents, spouses, or grandchildren. In terms of caring for older parents, some studies have demonstrated its adverse effects on caregivers’ mental health (Brenna and Di Novi 2016; Heger 2017), as adult children are confronted with conflicts between the caregiving role and other responsibilities (such as paid employment) (Coe and Van Houtven 2009). However, some studies in East Asia have shown that gratitude from older parents and recognition from family networks and social spheres could increase adult child caregivers’ perceived rewards and mitigate their care burden (H. Choi 1993). In terms of spouse care, empirical studies have well documented that spousal caregivers report greater depressive symptoms than noncaregivers do (Dunkle et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2021a, b) and even show higher levels of burden or depression than other informal caregivers do (Penning and Wu 2016; Pinquart and Sörensen 2011). Spouses often serve as the primary attachment objects for older adults (Hazan and Shaver 1987), so caring for a loved one can be quite stressful (Pinquart and Sörensen 2011). With respect to grandparent care, numerous studies examining its impact on grandparents’ mental health have indicated that providing childcare is linked to better mental wellness (Choi and Zhang 2021; Di Gessa et al. 2016), whereas other studies have associated grandparent care with health decline (Szinovacz et al. 1999). The inconsistency may be due to differences in region or intensity of care (Yang 2022). To the best of our knowledge, few studies have incorporated all three caregiving activities—spousal care, grandchild care, and parent care—to investigate their impact on caregivers’ mental health.

Dual caregiving of the sandwich generation

The term “sandwich generation” was introduced in the 1980s to describe middle-aged individuals who are in the position of being caregivers for their children and ageing parents (Miller 1981). Early research focused on the caring experience of middle-aged adults caught between two generations (Chassin et al. 2010; Dautzenberg et al. 1998; Roots 1998). With extended life expectancy, the number of extended households with four generations has been growing. The concept of the sandwich generation has been extended to middle-aged and older adults who simultaneously have at least one grandchild and at least one older parent (Alburez-Gutierrez et al. 2021; Falkingham et al. 2020). Empirical studies on dual caregiving among middle-aged and older adults have emerged in recent years (Falkingham et al. 2020; Liu and Chen 2022; Luna et al. 2021; Xu 2019). However, they yielded different findings. For example, Xu (2019) reported that Chinese middle-aged and older people who cared for both older parents and grandchildren had greater life satisfaction and fewer depressive symptoms than noncaregivers did. A study in Spain showed that older women who cared for both old relatives and grandchildren reported low to moderate levels of stress (Luna et al. 2021). These inconsistent findings may be attributed to the fact that the intensity of intergenerational caregiving has been ignored. In addition, prior studies have focused only on dual caregiving for great-grandparents and grandchildren, neglecting other types of caregiving activities, such as spousal caregiving.

Theoretical perspectives on multiple caregiving and mental health

First, according to the theory of family adaptive strategies (Moen and Wethington 1992), families are recognised as dynamic societal agents that actively negotiate structural constraints while strategically capitalising on available opportunities. From this perspective, the provision of multiple care can be understood as the adaptive response of middle-aged and older adults to external challenges for the benefit of the family as a whole, for example, when adult children are working outside, when spouses or older parents are in poor health, and when it is difficult to access formal care. With extended life expectancy, increased female labour force participation, and insufficient early childhood or old-age social care, middle-aged and older adults in China are likely to assume various types of care responsibilities simultaneously, and the intensity of their involvement may vary. The strategies employed by middle-aged and older adults in China to meet intergenerational and intragenerational care demands under their unique sociocultural system remain underexplored. Therefore, we hypothesised that middle-aged and older adults in China may simultaneously undertake caregiving responsibilities for spouses, elderly parents, and/or grandchildren, and they could be classified into distinct multiple caregiving patterns.

Second, multiple caregiving is linked to mental health through several possible pathways, some of which are beneficial and others of which are detrimental. These links can be explained by two opposing theoretical perspectives: role enhancement theory (Moen et al. 1995) and role strain theory (Goode 1960). According to role enhancement theory (Moen et al. 1995), people may assume additional roles when they provide care for multiple generations simultaneously. Consequently, caregivers can derive advantages from greater personal interactions and emotional rewards that come with multiple caregiving roles, leading to improved psychological well-being. According to role strain theory (Goode 1960), providing care for more than one care recipient may result in role overload or role conflict and limit the time available for caregivers to engage in social activities and self-care, which can have a detrimental effect on caregivers’ mental health (Kim et al. 2019). For Chinese people, caring for older parents is in line with the traditional norms of filial piety (Chow 2006), caring for grandchildren is a strategic adaptation that reduces childcare pressure on adult children (Yang and Jin 2023), and spousal care has been recognised as a chronic, stressful process (Zhao et al. 2021a, b). It is worth exploring whether different caregiving activities conflict with one other, thereby exacerbating caregivers’ psychological strain, or whether caregivers can gain additional emotional rewards from undertaking more than one caregiving task. On the basis of existing theories and empirical findings, we hypothesised that concurrently providing parent care and grandchild care may not lead to negative mental health outcomes over time, whereas multiple care responsibilities that include spouse care may be associated with deterioration in psychological well-being.

Methods

Data and sample

We utilised data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey designed to collect data from individuals aged 45 years or above and their spouses living in households in China. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011. The probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method was used to randomly select 150 counties from 28 provinces. Then, villages in rural areas and neighbourhoods in urban areas were used as primary sampling units (PSUs). In each county, three PSUs were selected via PPS sampling, for a total of 450 PSUs. Within each PSU, CHARLS constructed a sampling frame using Google Earth base maps, subsequently extracting sample households through a computer-assisted personal interview programme. Responses were obtained from a total of 17,708 individuals, yielding an overall response rate of 80.5% (Zhao et al. 2014). The multi-stage PPS sampling technique ensured that the baseline sample closely matched the 2010 national census in demographics. The survey gathered comprehensive information, including sociodemographic characteristics, family information, caregiving activities, and a variety of physical and mental health status measures (Zhao et al. 2014). For this study, we used the 2011 and 2018 waves of the CHARLS. We first restricted our sample to participants aged 50 years and above in 2011 (n = 13,637) and then excluded 1,862 respondents with incomplete information on caregiving activities, resulting in 11,775 observations for the analysis of multiple caregiving patterns in 2011. To investigate the mental health outcomes of the patterns of multiple caregiving, we further focused on 7,083 participants who continued to participate in the 2018 survey and had nonmissing values for depressive symptoms.

Measures

Caregiving activities

The key independent variable in this study focuses on caregiving activity patterns. We included three types of family caregiving activities, namely, parent care, grandchild care and spousal care. We categorised the caregiving status of each respondent with respect to their parents into three categories: nonparent care, part-time parent care, and full-time parent care. Noncaregivers included respondents who did not provide care to any parent in the past year. Full-time parent caregivers were those who dedicated 40 or more hours per week to caregiving for their parents. Part-time parent care involved providing parent care for less than 40 h per week. With respect to caregiving for grandchildren, full-time grandchild care entailed caregiving for 40 or more hours per week, whereas part-time grandchild care included providing care for more than zero and less than 40 h per week. In terms of spouse care, the CHARLS asked respondents about their primary helpers when they reported difficulties with activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living. When a participant reported his or her spouse as the primary helper, we referred to this spouse as a spousal caregiver. We defined full-time spousal care as caregiving for 40 or more hours per week and part-time care as caregiving for less than 40 h per week. The choice of 40 h per week as the threshold for full-time care is based on the provisions of China’s Labor Law and existing research, which define a standard full-time work time of 40 h per week (eight hours per day on a five-day work schedule) (Zhao et al. 2021a, b).

Taking these measures into account, our analysis of caregiving patterns included six dichotomous caregiving activities measured in 2011: full-time parent care, part-time parent care, full-time grandchild care, part-time grandchild care, full-time spouse care, and part-time spouse care. We employed latent class analysis (LCA) using the six dichotomous caregiving activities to identify the patterns of multiple caregiving activities.

Depressive symptoms

We included depressive symptoms in 2018 as a key mental health outcome. Depressive symptoms were assessed by the 10-term Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CESD-10) scale (Radloff 1977). This scale assesses the frequency of mood and behavioural symptoms experienced by participants within the previous week. To ensure that higher scores indicate a greater level of depressive symptoms, each item, with negative meanings (e.g. lonely), was rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 3 (most or all of the time). For two items with positive meanings (hopeful and happy), the scoring was reversed from 0 (most of the time) to 3 (rarely). By summing the scores of the 10 items, the total score ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating higher levels of depressive symptoms (Lei et al. 2014). For the CESD scores in 2011, we imputed the missing value as the sample mean and included a dummy variable indicating imputation in the regression.

Control variables

To address the potential confounding effect of respondents' baseline mental health, we controlled for baseline CESD-10 scores in the analysis of subsequent depressive symptoms. We imputed missing values of the CESD-10 scores in 2011 via the mean score and introduced a dummy variable to indicate whether the score was imputed or not. Additionally, several other variables were considered potential confounders and were included as covariates in the study (Falkingham et al. 2020; Xu 2019; Zhao et al. 2021a, b). The sociodemographic characteristics included age group (50–54, 55–59, 60–64, and 65 years and above), sex (male or female), marital status (unmarried or married), residence (rural or urban), education (below primary or primary and above), household per capita expenditure (PCE) quintiles, working status (not working or working), and the presence of siblings (no or yes). Lifestyle indicators included smoking (never, past, or current) and drinking (never, past, or current). The health measures included self-rated health (poor, fair, or good) and any mobility restrictions (no or yes). All discrete covariates were organised into categorical variables. Family members’ socioeconomic characteristics included adult children’s education (measured by the highest education level attained among the children), older parents’ education (measured by the highest education level attained among the participants’ parents), and the education level of the spouse. Finally, a missing category was included for each covariate to account for any respondents with missing data, thereby ensuring that the maximum number of respondents was retained in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

First, we employed LCA on the 11,775 participants in the baseline survey, classifying them into distinct patterns according to their level of participation in three caregiving responsibilities: parent care, spouse care, and grandchild care. LCA applies an individual-oriented approach and assigns individuals to distinct caregiving classes on the basis of their model-based posterior membership probabilities (Masyn 2013). We examined two to five classes, and the best-fitting solution was selected based on our evaluation of the model fit statistics.

We subsequently examined the CESD-10 scores in both 2011 and 2018 across different caregiving patterns. Furthermore, we used the ordinary least squares (OLS) model to analyse the longitudinal association between baseline multiple caregiving patterns and subsequent depressive symptoms seven years later while controlling for baseline levels of depressive symptoms. Additionally, each model was further adjusted for baseline sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health indicators. We conducted three subgroup analyses by sex, age group, and urban/rural residence to investigate the associations between baseline multiple caregiving patterns and subsequent depressive symptoms. We also respecified logit analysis by using the CESD with a clinical threshold (a score higher than 10 was defined as depressed, otherwise nondepressed). All analyses were conducted using Stata 17.

Results

LCA of caregiving activities

Out of the two- through five-class models, the four-class model emerged as the best fit and presented the most apparent theoretical distinctions for interpretability. We named the four classes on the basis of their item response probabilities compared with the population average, as shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1 (i.e. the likelihood that individuals belonging to a certain class scored on each observed caregiving activity). Overall, the entire sample was grouped into four classes (latent membership probabilities shown in parentheses): “part-time care for all” (18.6%), “full-time parent care and grandchild care” (19.3%), “part-time spouse care” (60.0%), and “full-time spouse care” (2.1%). The “part-time care for all” class consisted of individuals who demonstrated a high likelihood of providing part-time care to their parents, spouses, and grandchildren. The “full-time parent care and grandchild care” class included participants with a higher probability of providing full-time care to parents and grandchildren. The “part-time spouse care” class was characterised by a high likelihood of engaging in spouse care with a low level of time commitment. Sensitivity analysis

The analyses described above were repeated by using a sample without a diagnosis of emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems at baseline. The regression model revealed that, compared with participants in the “part-time care for all” group, those in the “part-time spouse care” group and “full-time spouse care” group had 0.47 (95% CI 0.11–0.84, P < 0.05) and 0.99 (95% CI 0.01–1.97, P < 0.05) higher CESD-10 scores in 2018, respectively, whereas those in the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group did not show significant differences in the CESD-10 scores. These results were similar to those of the main analysis (see Supplementary Table 3). We also conducted logit analysis by using the CESD-10 with a clinical threshold (scores higher than 10 were considered depressed). As shown in Supplementary Table 4, compared with participants in the “part-time care for all” group, those in the “part-time spouse care” group had a greater risk of depression (OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.03–1.36, P < 0.05) in 2018, whereas those in the “full-time spouse care” group were more likely to be depressed at the 10% level.

Discussion

This study examined the longitudinal associations between multiple caregiving patterns at baseline and depressive symptoms seven years later. Using national representative data, we identified four multiple caregiving classes: “part-time care for all,” “full-time parent care and grandchild care,” “part-time spouse care,” and “full-time spouse care.” Compared with participants in the “part-time care for all” group, those in the “full-time spouse care” group and “part-time spouse care” group reported significantly greater levels of depressive symptoms seven years later, whereas those in the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group did not show significant differences in depressive symptom scores.

Although there have been studies about spousal caregiving (Dunkle et al. 2014; Polenick and DePasquale 2019; Zhao et al. 2021a, b), grandchild caregiving (Cong and Silverstein 2012; Di Gessa et al. 2016; Yang 2022), and dual caregiving for parents and grandchildren (Falkingham et al. 2020; Xu 2019; Xu et al. 2022) by middle-aged and older adults, this study extends the literature by focusing on three types of caregiving activities simultaneously and considering caregiving intensity. The current study established four patterns of multiple caregiving. The combination of multiple caregiving roles by middle-aged and older adults observed in the present study supported the family adaptive strategy theory (Moen and Wethington 1992). Specifically, the majority (60.0%) of the participants belonged to the “part-time spouse care” group, suggesting that middle-aged and older adults in China had a high probability of providing low- or moderate-intensity assistance to spouses with ADL or IADL disabilities. Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that a growing number of middle-aged and older adults in China take on caregiving roles once their spouses develop functional disabilities (Gu et al. 2017), as shrinking family size and increasing female employment rates have weakened adult children’s ability to provide parental care. This study revealed that 19.3% of the participants belonged to the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group. These participants may be at an age where both their older parents and grandchildren have care needs, where three or four generations live together or in close proximity to each other, and therefore take on dual caring responsibilities as a strategical adaptation to optimise the welfare of the family as a whole (Yang and Jin 2023). The traditional norms of filial piety, which hold that individuals are obliged to take care of their older parents, have remained largely unchanged in contemporary Chinese society (Chow 2006). Moreover, grandparents serving as primary caregivers have become increasingly prevalent in China (Zhang et al. 2020) due to limited access to early childhood care services, high labour participation rates among women, and large-scale rural-to-urban labour migration. Approximately 18.6% of the participants belonged to the “part-time care for all” group. This result revealed that Chinese middle-aged and older people may confront the needs of three generations at the same time and therefore provide part-time multiple care in an extended family, along with siblings, adult children, and/or babysitters. This is a strategy for extended families to allocate family resources to cope with multiple care needs. Approximately 2.1% of the participants were in the “full-time spouse care” pattern. As the samples in this pattern were older than those in the other groups were, the care needs of their spouses might be greater. When the demand for spousal care is high, it is possible that other types of care may be ceding grounds for spousal care.

The current study demonstrated that participants in the “full-time spouse care” group and the “part-time spouse care” group reported higher levels of depressive symptoms than did those in the “part-time care for all” group. Similarly, previous research reported that spousal caregivers experienced more stress than other informal caregivers did (such as child caregivers) (Oldenkamp et al. 2016; Pinquart and Sörensen 2011). Our results further support the conclusions of the problematic nature of spouse care (Dunkle et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2021a, b). Because spouses are often the most important attachment objects for older individuals (Hazan and Shaver 1987), caring for a loved one is usually accompanied by worries about health declines, which can be quite stressful (Pinquart and Sörensen 2011). Moreover, the “full-time spouse care” group reported the highest levels of depression among all four groups. This may be because full-time care implies that the caregivers should be on call or accessible to their spouses with disabilities. Caring for a spouse for more than 40 h per week obliges them to restructure their life by reducing working hours or even quitting work entirely and by reducing their involvement in sports or leisure activities (Kokorelias et al. 2020). They experience a loss of social identity and spousal identity (Hayes et al. 2009; Kokorelias et al. 2020), which has been shown to be detrimental to their mental health (Westrelin et al. 2024).

The participants in the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group and the “part-time for all” group did not show significant differences in their CESD scores seven years later. A previous study in China revealed that individuals who provided parent care and grandchild care simultaneously reported better mental health than noncaregivers did (Xu 2019). In the present study, for those in the “part-time care for all” group, caregiving tasks were likely to be occasional, with limited responsibilities. One possible explanation for the nonsignificant differences in the CESD scores between the two groups may be that people in the “full-time parent care and grandchild care” group were relatively young among all four groups. Selection bias might exist, whereby only those in good physical and psychological condition are willing to undertake the responsibilities of caring for both the previous and next generations, supporting the healthy caregiver hypothesis (Fredman et al. 2009). Second, the participants in our study were born before or during 1961, a period when the total fertility rate was approximately six children per woman (Zheng 2021). Therefore, they may have siblings to share the burden of parental care once they are committed to intensive grandchild care, which can relieve their psychological distress. Moreover, caring for older parents conforms to China’s cultural norms of filial piety and can bring family and social recognition to caregivers (Choi 1993). Grandchild care can not only enhance grandparents’ self-efficacy but also reinforce intergenerational bonds and reciprocal support through sustained multi-generational engagement (Mahne and Huxhold 2015). Therefore, individuals who take on parent care and grandchild care simultaneously may enjoy addictive emotional rewards and thus be less vulnerable to adverse impacts, supporting role enhancement theory (Moen et al. 1995).

Subgroup analyses revealed that men in the “part-time spouse care” group and “full-time spouse care” group experienced a substantial increase in depressive symptoms seven years later than their counterparts in the “part-time care for all” group; however, there was no significant difference for women. A study in Germany also revealed that spouse care had a detrimental effect on the mental health of husband caregivers, whereas no significant influence was observed among wife caregivers (Hajek and König 2016). The possible reasons might be as follows: First, according to traditional gender role norms, men are typically socialised to work outside the home (Graham 1983), thereby rendering providing ADL/IADL care for spouses unfamiliar to men. They would experience role conflict arising from the internalised “masculine” role and the “feminine” caregiving responsibilities (Zhao et al. 2023), making them reluctant to seek external assistance (Wiesch et al., 1995). Consequently, spouse care may exert long-term adverse effects on male caregivers. Second, women tend to be concerned with the holistic well-being of care recipients (Calasanti 2010) and are more likely to be emotionally involved in caregiving activities (Pöysti et al. 2012). Therefore, there were no significant differences in the longitudinal effects of different care patterns on depression. Subgroup analyses by residence revealed that among participants in rural areas, those in the “part-time spouse care” group and the “full-time spouse care” group reported higher CESD scores at follow-up than those in the “part-time care for all” group. In contrast, for participants in urban areas, we did not observe a significant association. Our findings indicate the detrimental influence of spouse care on caregivers in rural areas. Rural-to-urban migration in China has led to many older people being left behind in rural areas (Yuan 2019). Once older adults have ADL or IADL difficulties, their spouses are more likely to assume the caregiving role without assistance from their adult children (Zhao et al. 2023). The fact that elder care services are insufficient in rural areas further intensifies the negative effect of spouse care on mental health over time. Subgroup analyses by age group revealed that for people aged 50–64 years, the “part-time spouse care” pattern and “full-time spouse care” pattern were associated with increased levels of depression seven years later. In contrast, for those aged 65 + years, we did not observe a significant association. This might be because the younger group appears to be more concerned about the health decline of their loved one, coupled with the dual loss of social identity and spouse identity inherent in caregiving responsibilities, which can have a detrimental effect on mental health. In contrast, for the 65 + group, their caregiving experience in midlife may facilitate role adaptation through established coping mechanisms.

Our study had several limitations. First, we considered only three types of caregiving activities, while others (such as caring for disabled siblings) were not included. Second, there might be recall bias, as caregiving activities and hours were self-reported. Future studies could use more objective tools to collect data on time use in caregiving activities. Third, we included only caregiving activities at baseline for LCA, and changes in caregiving patterns at follow-up were not considered; these changes could be further examined in future research. Fourth, we employed a lagged dependent variable approach to estimate the longitudinal association between multiple care patterns and depression. However, potential endogeneity issues may exist, and our findings could not establish a causal relationship.

Despite these limitations, this study depicted a vivid picture of Chinese middle-aged and older adults participating in multiple caregiving and predicted the impact of multiple caregiving patterns on depressive symptoms. Our findings have important implications for policies and social services. First, given that spousal-dominated care patterns may constitute a unique source of psychological stress, establishing a support system for spouse caregivers is essential. Notably, China’s first generation of one-child parents will enter the 75–84 age bracket between 2023 and 2032, a critical period of age-related functional decline (Feng 2023). Spousal caregivers will then face greater challenges. Therefore, there is a need to develop respite services, mental health screening tools and evidence-based interventions for spousal caregivers. These support programmes should be customised to address heterogeneous subgroups with different genders, age groups, and urban/rural residences. Special attention should be given to male, middle-aged, and rural caregivers. Second, middle-aged and older people play a significant role in caring for their parents and grandchildren, and emotional rewards gained from the dual caring role buffer the stress. Policy innovations such as multi-generational housing incentives could be implemented to promote multiple caregiving. Moreover, we should note that since January 2025, the Chinese government has begun to implement a gradual delayed retirement policy. Delayed retirement will squeeze out the engagement in caring for grandchildren and/or older parents by middle-aged and older adults (Huang and Sun 2025). Hence, it is necessary to develop social services for older adults and children (especially infants and children aged 0–3 years). Our findings can also provide some implications for the development of family policies in developing countries (such as India and Brazil) that are experiencing dramatic changes in family structure.
