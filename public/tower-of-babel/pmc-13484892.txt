A Narrative Review of Recent Developments in Aspirin Therapy After Desensitization in NSAIDs-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease
Authors: Preena Ketan Patel, Aagat Sharma Khatiwada, Peter J Andrews, Glenis K Scadding, Alfonso Luca Pendolino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease (N-ERD) is characterized by asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), and intolerance to NSAIDs (including aspirin). In N-ERD, CRSwNP often remains refractory to standard medical and surgical treatment, necessitating escalation. Aspirin therapy after desensitization (ATAD) is an established option for CRSwNP in N-ERD, but its use is limited by adverse effects, non-response, and safety issues associated with aspirin desensitization. This narrative review focuses on newer developments in ATAD for N-ERD, including shortened desensitization schedules, lower aspirin maintenance doses, and use of intranasal preparations of aspirin such as intranasal lysine-aspirin. The developments in use of biomarkers for distinguishing the different underlying endotypes and phenotypes of N-ERD, and subsequent potential for individualization of treatment are also discussed. The availability of biologics for the treatment of CRSwNP may provide further treatment escalation options in N-ERD patients who do not tolerate or respond to ATAD; however, there is an absence of direct comparative evidence for ATAD versus biologics. Further, the conjunctive use of ATAD and biologics provides an interesting avenue of research. Finally, following a discussion of the recent advancements in ATAD and biologics in N-ERD, clinical recommendations for treatment options and pathways are made.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is an inflammatory disorder of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses characterized by nasal blockage, nasal discharge, facial pain and olfactory disturbance.1 Non-steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease (N-ERD) is a clinical triad of CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), asthma, and intolerance to aspirin or NSAIDs. Prevalence is estimated as 15% in patients with severe asthma, 9–10% overall in patients with asthma, 10% of CRSwNP patients and 9% of patients with CRS.2–5

A history of adult-onset asthma, bilateral nasal polyposis and one or more episodes of acute onset of respiratory symptoms following ingestion of aspirin or other NSAIDs suggest the diagnosis of N-ERD.4 Patients are typically diagnosed in their third or fourth decade, and it is more prevalent in women in most studies.6–8 The natural history of N-ERD appears to be onset of persistent rhinitis, then asthma, followed by NSAIDs sensitivity and nasal polyps.9–11 The form of CRS found in N-ERD is primary diffuse type-2 CRS, characterized by generalized type-2 mediated inflammation via eosinophils, immunoglobulin (Ig) E, and interleukin(IL)-4, IL- 5 and IL-13.1,2,8 CRSwNP is by far the most common phenotype associated with primary diffuse type-2 CRS, and the two are intrinsically linked.1

N-ERD is more resistant to both medical therapies (eg intranasal or oral corticosteroids) and surgery (endoscopic sinus surgery, ESS), with patients requiring more frequent courses of oral steroids, and more frequent surgery due to refractory CRSwNP.12 As such, many N-ERD patients experience chronic, uncontrolled symptoms and refractory disease contributing to an overall poorer quality of life (QoL). Additional treatment options and treatment escalation pathways are, therefore, pivotal for these patients.

One such treatment option which exists for N-ERD patients is aspirin treatment after desensitization (ATAD), whereby patients are desensitized to aspirin, followed by its lifelong treatment. Recent advances in ATAD have focused on making the therapy more tolerable for patients, including improved dosing schedules, and potential co-treatment with biologics. Biologics are newer options which have emerged for the treatment of eosinophilic asthma, and refractory CRSwNP, and may have significant utility in N-ERD patients, including those that do not respond or are intolerant to ATAD. This narrative review aims to synthesize advances in ATAD, the emergence of biologic therapy in inflammatory diseases as alternative or adjunctive treatment options and a body of new long-term outcome data published since the last major reviews. A literature search of PubMed and Embase for publications from 2000 onwards, using search terms related to “aspirin desensitization” and “AERD/N-ERD” was conducted. The structure and sections of this review was determined from expert author opinions and studies were subsequently selected based on relevance to topics and clinical outcomes. Studies were prioritized primarily by study design, strength of evidence and methodology (randomized controlled trials and larger cohort studies were weighted more heavily than small case series), though no formal systematic appraisal or risk-of-bias assessment was conducted, consistent with the narrative format of this review. A significant proportion of studies were identified based on a review of references lists for relevant studies.

This review summarizes the current evidence and recent advances in ATAD in patients with N-ERD, with a particular focus on the management of CRS associated with the disease. It aims to provide clinicians with a clearer framework for integrating aspirin-based therapies into contemporary management pathways for patients with difficult-to-treat N-ERD. Further, management options beyond ATAD, including the clinical scope of biologics are synthesized and presented in the review.

Pathological Mechanisms

Our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of N-ERD have continued to improved.12–15

The underlying pathophysiology involves alterations in the cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) metabolism pathways of arachidonic acid. Prostaglandins (PG) such as PGE2 and PGD2 are metabolites of the COX pathway.13–15 In N-ERD, a relative deficiency of PGE2, and reduction of its receptor EP2 are accompanied by an abundance of PGD2.16,17 PGE2 acting through the EP2 receptor is anti-inflammatory, bronchoprotective, and normally reduces the recruitment of eosinophils and degranulation of mast cells.17,18 Of specific significance in N-ERD, PGE2 inhibits 5-LO of the LOX pathway, which metabolises arachidonic acid to produce CysLTs.17 PGD2, on the other hand, is a key pro-inflammatory precursor, which activates both type-2 inflammation (via eosinophils and T-helper 2 cells), as well as innate immune responses through innate lymphoid cells (ICL2s), heralded by rises in IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP).19,20 ILC2s and T-helper 2 cells produce type 2 cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13.21 These cytokines signal through the common receptor (IL-4Rα) to result in several effects such as tissue fibrosis and remodeling, mast cell activation, goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus production.21

Arachnoid acid metabolism via the LOX pathway by 5-LO results in the production of CysLTs, which, acting via CysLT receptors (CysLTR) mediate bronchoconstriction and mucous secretion, activate inflammatory cells (eosinophils, mast cells, and granulocytes), and stimulate the release of pro-inflammatory mediators including cytokines and histamines.15 Activation of the group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2) by CysLTs also mediates type 2 inflammation.16,21 A hallmark of N-ERD is an over-production of CysLTs, which is now widely regarded as an important driver of the pathogenesis of N-ERD.14,20

NSAID-induced inhibition of the COX-1 pathway shifts the balance of arachidonic acid metabolism towards the overproduction of CysLTs and PGD2, while concurrently decreasing PGE2 levels.14,16 Additionally, alarmins, including TSLP, IL-25, and IL-33, are released in response to a broad range of environmental and inflammatory stimuli, such as allergens, cigarette smoke, air pollutants, and microbial agents.22 Thus, this cascade of events contributes to profound type 2 inflammation seen as airway inflammation and remodeling in the nasal mucosa of N-ERD patients.23

Endotypes and Phenotypes

N-ERD is an inherently heterogeneous condition with various endotypes and phenotypes. Studies have characterized inflammatory mediator profiles in the upper and lower airways of patients with N-ERD.24–26 Three separate clusters of patients with differing cytokine profiles have been identified: endotypes with high type 2 inflammatory markers (IL-4/IL-5/IL-13); low levels of type-2 markers (with elevated type-1 (interferon-γ) and type-3 (IL-17) markers); and low overall levels of markers of inflammation.25 There was a trend for patients with a high type-2 endotype to have the most severe sinonasal symptoms, while those patients with a low type-2 cytokine profile were more likely to experience nasal polyp regrowth within 14 months of surgery than patients with a high type-2 profile or those with low overall levels of markers of inflammation.25 This suggests that the inflammatory heterogeneity (ie endotypes) observed in N-ERD may correlate with the clinical presentation (ie phenotype). Different disease phenotypes may be characterized by a range of factors including asthma control and severity, intensity of upper airway symptoms, severity of airway obstruction, healthcare use, atopic status, and presence of CRS and chronic urticaria.27,28 From a therapeutic perspective, there may be differences in response to ATAD and biological therapy based on different disease endotype. Little is available in the literature at present to distinguish this.

Biomarkers

Biomarkers may have utility in the diagnosis of N-ERD, as well as to assess response to aspirin desensitization. Further, a role for biomarkers may exist in predicting certain clinical outcomes in N-ERD, such as polyp regrowth after ESS.29

Leukotriene E4 (LTE4) is an end-metabolite of CysLTs and is released in the urine.29 Urinary LTE4 reflects the activity of CysLTs in the respiratory mucosa.29 It is increased in N-ERD patients at baseline, increases further in response to aspirin challenge, and may be used to distinguish N-ERD from aspirin tolerance.29–36 Further, basal urinary LTE4 levels were higher in patients with N-ERD who failed aspirin desensitization compared to those who were desensitized successfully, and may therefore have utility to assess patients’ response to aspirin challenge.30 In this regard, urinary prostaglandin D2 may be an even more powerful predictive tool to assess response to aspirin challenge.30

Eosinophil activation plays a key role in the inflammatory response of upper and lower airways of N-ERD patients, and elevated blood eosinophil levels also increase the likelihood of underlying N-ERD.37 In conjunction with urine LTE4, raised blood eosinophil levels make a diagnosis of N-ERD six times more likely.37 Eosinophil levels in peripheral blood and tissue have also been suggested as disease severity, control, and polyp recurrence biomarkers.1 In this regard, in patients with N-ERD, the effect of ATAD is known to increase peripheral blood eosinophil counts with studies documenting a 3x increase from baseline levels.30

A study looking at biomarkers to predict which patients will respond to ATAD have shown that the best prediction model includes a multivariate model of baseline asthma control, SNOT-22 score, blood eosinophil count, and serum total IgE levels.38 Various other clinical and biochemical biomarkers, such as eosinophil cationic protein (a product of eosinophil granules), IL-5, pre-ESS Lund-Mackay score, and asthma may help predict the risk of polyp recurrence following ESS, but require more research.39,40

Endoscopic Sinus Surgery in N-ERD

Endoscopic sinus surgery is a vital part of the management of the CRSwNP in N-ERD.1 It serves two main functions – debulking of nasal polyps for symptom relief and facilitation of medical treatment in the form of nasal steroids.1 However, CRSwNP in N-ERD is highly recalcitrant, with high rates of polyp recurrence and need for revision surgery.20 As such, ESS alone is insufficient in the management of CRSwNP in N-ERD, and must be supplemented with adjunctive medical therapies.20

For the surgical approach itself, more extensive ESS is recommended over standard ESS to reduce the risk of disease recurrence and revision surgery, as well as to optimize QoL.41 Large-cavity ESS, including complete uncinectomy, wide middle meatal antrostomy, complete ethmoidectomy, wide sphenoidotomy, and Draf IIb/III, seems effective at achieving disease control in N-ERD.41

Interestingly, hyperplastic polypoid tissue may be a source for the surge in CysLTs that occurs with the aspirin desensitization procedure.42 Surgery to debulk polyps, therefore, may allow for a more agreeable desensitization.42 Jerschow et al studied a group of patients, whereby the diagnosis of N-ERD was confirmed by eliciting hypersensitivity reactions to aspirin prior to ESS.43 After ESS, aspirin reactions were less severe in all patients, with 43% having no detectable reaction.43

ESS, therefore, remains an essential adjunct in the medical management of N-ERD, including with ATAD.

Provocative Aspirin Challenges and Desensitization

NSAIDs ingestion in N-ERD can trigger both upper and/or lower respiratory symptoms, including nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion, cough, wheezing and breathlessness.44 Onset of symptoms can be rapid with a risk of fatal bronchospasm.44 Following sensitization, N-ERD symptoms will progress despite strict avoidance of NSAIDs in the majority of subjects.9 All cyclooxygenase (COX)-1 inhibitory drugs can cause symptom exacerbation while COX-2 inhibitors are usually well-tolerated by the majority of N-ERD subjects.45 For patients where the clinical history or diagnosis is uncertain, a provocation (challenge) test should be undertaken.46

Provocative aspirin challenge primarily serves to confirm the N-ERD diagnosis and, secondarily, to allow beginning of aspirin desensitization. Desensitization involves gradually increasing the dose of aspirin until either a pre-defined maximum dose is reached, or there is development of symptoms.47 A variety of protocols are documented which vary in terms of the route of administration, starting dose (usually 20 mg or 40 mg), dosing interval (60 minutes, 90 minutes, 180 minutes), and total duration of the procedure.47,48 The most commonly utilized protocols involve oral aspirin, intranasal ketorolac, or a combination of both. In oral aspirin challenges, reactions with lower respiratory tract symptoms result in temporary discontinuation of the procedure and treatment of the reaction. Once treated, the provoking aspirin dose is repeated, and the procedure may be continued to a defined maximum dose. Through the process, reactions become increasingly mild until they disappear.

The time, resources and expenses required for successful completion of aspirin desensitization therapy have been some of the barriers for the use of ATAD. Traditionally, desensitization took between 2–3 full days, and in many cases monitoring in a high dependency environment.51,52 Recently, there has been significant interest in shortening the desensitization duration. Research shows that using shorter intervals between dose escalation (eg 60–90 minutes compared to 3 hours) can be safely achieved and can help successfully shorten the desensitization procedure to 1 day.54 rOther strategies have included the use of intranasal preparations in conjunction with oral aspirin, which results in safer and faster desensitisation.46,56,59,61

It is important to recognize that desensitization does not produce tolerance. Patients who are desensitized need to remain on aspirin indefinitely to retain their desensitized state and if stopped for longer than 48 hours, then the patient should not resume taking aspirin at the same final dose as there is a risk of reaction.16

Aspirin Treatment After Desensitization

After desensitization, ATAD involves patients remaining on a maintenance regimen of lifelong daily aspirin. The main indications of ATAD are rapidly recurring nasal polyps following sinus surgery; uncontrolled rhinosinusitis despite use of standard medical therapies; and need for frequent bursts of systemic corticosteroids to control respiratory or sinus symptoms.48 Furthermore, aspirin desensitization and ATAD remain the preferred therapeutic option for those patients who rely on NSAIDs or aspirin for various other rheumatological or cardiovascular indications (eg stents, cardiovascular protection).

Available evidence for oral ATAD consistently demonstrates its benefit.20 Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled studies show that oral ATAD results in a decrease in sinonasal symptoms (Grade 1A evidence), decrease in intranasal corticosteroid use (Grade 2B), reduction in recurrence of nasal polyps (Grade 2B) and a decrease in need for revision surgery (Grade 2B).62–66 However, it is important to note that blinded randomised-controlled trials assessing the efficacy of ATAD are based on small sample size which is a significant limitation of the current evidence base. Long term (>10 years) use of aspirin for ATAD is demonstrated to be safe and effective in improving sense of smell and rhinitis scores.12,20

Various maintenance dosing regimens ranging from 100mg to 1300mg daily have been trialed, with studies showing reduction in sinonasal symptoms, polyp burden and need for endoscopic surgeries. Studies investigating the dose dependent efficacy of oral aspirin on CRSwNP outcomes in N-ERD show that most patients begin to have a return of nasal congestion when their daily aspirin dosage falls below 325 mg twice daily.20,62 Dosages of 325 mg twice daily and 650 mg twice daily are both associated with significant improvements in clinical markers of disease to a similar degree.51 In a randomized double blinded placebo-controlled study, a lower dose of aspirin (100mg) was associated with significantly improved overall symptoms, improved smell, and better quality of life related to nasal problems in the treatment group.65 Nasal polyp recurrence reduced in the treatment group (62 vs 28%) but these differences were not statistically significant.65 While the power of this study was limited by a very high dropout rate, it shows potential for effective treatment in N-ERD with low dose aspirin after desensitization, and highlights a need for studies assessing dose-response in ATAD for doses below 650mg daily.

Current practice recommends starting with oral aspirin 650mg twice daily for 1 to 6 months, with subsequent down-titration as tolerated.20 The total duration of therapy depends on the patient’s response to the intervention and any adverse effects. After an initial 6-month trial of ATAD, patients who show improved clinical outcomes can be maintained on aspirin indefinitely with life-long monitoring. Current evidence does not suggest that the effects of ATAD diminish over time.80

Intranasal LAS may also be a useful option in ATAD. Studies have demonstrated that long-term intranasal LAS (75 mg) can lead to lower rates of polyp recurrence.49,56,57,61,81,82 Improved nasal airflow scores, smell scores and a reduced need for rescue surgery with long-term intranasal LAS use have also been demonstrated.50 On the other hand, a randomized-controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the effects of intranasal LAS at a lower dose of 16 mg every 48 hours failed to demonstrate clinical benefit.56 A comprehensive summary of studies assessing intranasal LAS is shown in Table 4. Along with its benefits, the potential adverse effects of ATAD must also be considered. ATAD may be unsuitable in a significant proportion of patients for a variety of reasons including adverse effects, non-response, or difficulty with compliance. The most common adverse effects include gastritis, GI bleeding, asthma exacerbation, and rash.85,86 Adverse effects have been reported in up to 25% of all patients undergoing ATAD with oral aspirin but are lower with intranasal LAS.61,62 Furthermore, ATAD should be maintained as a long-term therapy. While some patients may not experience immediate adverse effects following occasional missed doses, interruption of treatment is not recommended. Due to these reasons, discontinuation rates are as high as 63%.87 For this group of patients in whom standard ATAD is unsuitable, focus on personalized medication regimen including lower maintenance doses and shorter therapy duration may be important.

Intuitively, a compelling reason for lower maintenance regimens relates to fewer adverse effects in a dose-related pattern. However, various trials have shown incidence of adverse symptoms ranging between 0–34% at a wide range of aspirin doses, suggesting that adverse effects may not arise in a dose-dependent manner. Similarly, the relationship between dyspepsia (the commonest reason for discontinuing aspirin therapy) and aspirin dosage is unclear, even at low doses, aspirin increases the risk of upper GI events 2–4 fold as demonstrated by cardiovascular literature.88 However, the use of GI prophylaxis mitigates against development of GI symptoms during ATAD. A study, for instance, had no cases of ATAD discontinuation due to GI symptoms despite a high maintenance dose of aspirin (1300mg/day), owing to the use of GI prophylaxis with either a proton pump inhibitor or histamine H2 receptor antagonist.71 Systematic reviews looking at the risk of GI hemorrhage with aspirin taken for non-ATAD indications demonstrate no relation of the adverse event with dosing.89 Limitations of studies looking at the relationship between dosing of aspirin and its adverse effects include differences in their methodology, and a lack of reporting on concomitant treatment with GI prophylaxis.90 Compared to oral aspirin, intranasal LAS seems to be a safer option with a reduced side effect profile, although head-to-head studies are lacking.

The underlying mechanisms of ATAD efficacy improvement remains unclear. Overproduction of CysLTs is a hallmark of N-ERD and levels rise in response to aspirin and other NSAIDs ingestion.48 This finding supports the hypothesis that modulation of CysLTs concentrations and preventing the of release CysLTs in response to exposure underpins the therapeutic benefit of ATAD. However, studies have consistently demonstrated otherwise - ATAD does not result in a decrease in CysLTs levels.30,91–93 Cahill et al92 found levels of urinary LTE4 in patients with N-ERD undergoing ATAD paradoxically increased. Instead, desensitization is suggested to decrease expression of the CysLT1 receptor leading to reduced sensitivity to LTE4.13,94,95 Whilst this may be the final common pathway underpinning desensitization, precisely how ATAD alters CysLTs receptor expression remains unclear. Importantly, as our understanding of these mechanisms advances, it is unlikely that any single pathway will fully account for the broad therapeutic benefits of ATAD and is likely a result of multiple potential overlapping mechanisms. A proportion of patients, up to 30%, may not respond to ATAD.73 The SNOT-22 score may be used to distinguish responders from non-responders, with a total score of 20 defined as the threshold.98 The SNOT-22 score obtained at 6 months post-desensitisation may have the greatest predictive value for long-term outcomes at 24 months post-desensitisation.99 Another marker for response to therapy is improvement in the SNOT-22 score from baseline, with a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) suggested to be 8.9 points.58 For this group of patients who do not respond to ATAD, newer alternatives may be available. It is worth noting that SNOT-22, being a patient reported outcome measure, is subjective. More objective measures, such as Lund-Mackay score, or objective smell tests, may be considered but require validation.

Recent Developments - Biologics

Following the success of biologics in the treatment of asthma, their scope has expanded to include other type-2 inflammatory disorders, including CRSwNP. More recently, RCTs have shown biologics to be very effective for the treatment of severe CRSwNP, although not specific to N-ERD patients (Figure 3). Biologics including dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab, tezepelumab, itepekimab, and lebrikizumab have been studied for their efficacy in CRSwNP. While there are no RCTs specifically for patients with N-ERD, sub-group analyses from trials evaluating biologics in CRSwNP provide important data.100,101 To date, dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab, and tezepelumab have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) to treat recurrent CRSwNP.100

Dupilumab is a fully human monoclonal IgG antibody that inhibits the IL-4Rα subunit shared by the type-2 inflammatory cytokines including IL- 4 and IL-13,100 and it is approved for inadequately controlled CRSwNP.101 Dupilumab improves upper tract symptoms in N-ERD patients, including reduction in nasal polyp size and improved sense of smell, within 16 weeks of treatment.102 Other observational studies have shown clinical improvement as early as 1 month.103 In the Phase 3 trials of dupilumab for the treatment of CRSwNP (SINUS 24 and SINUS52), sub-group analysis of the patients with N-ERD showed a dupilumab-induced improvement from baseline to week 24, in SNOT-22 and Lund-Mackay scores, as well as sense of smell.104 Additionally, a sub-group analysis of pooled SINUS 24 and SINUS 52 data showed that the dupilumab-induced improvements in nasal congestion and SNOT-22 scores were significantly greater in N-ERD patients than aspirin-tolerant CRSwNP patients.105

Omalizumab is a monoclonal antibody binding to circulating IgE which acts to downregulate and stabilize mast cells and basophils.106 In trials for the treatment of CRSwNP, 24 weeks of omalizumab led to slightly greater improvement in nasal polyp score in the subgroup of patients with N-ERD compared to NSAID-tolerant CRSwNP patients, although this difference was not statistically significant.107 Of note, N-ERD is not considered to be specifically IgE mediated. The effects of omalizumab are thought to be mediated through the suppression of CysLT production, decrease in eosinophils, IL-33 and IL-25.106 In another study that used omalizumab to treat CRSwNP, significant improvements in symptoms and nasal polyp size were observed.108 While patients were not specifically analyzed for the presence of N-ERD, it was likely present in a subgroup of the subjects.

Mepolizumab is a humanized anti-IL-5 antibody that inhibits IL-5Rα. IL-5Rα levels within plasma cells has been demonstrated in significantly high concentrations within nasal polyp tissue from N-ERD patients compared to aspirin-tolerant CRSwNP patients.109 In N-ERD, mepolizumab has been shown to reduce polyp tissue and blood eosinophils and decreases associated CysLT production.110 The SYNAPSE trial demonstrated its benefit on nasal obstruction and nasal polyp size and as such, it has been recently approved for treatment of CRSwNP.111 Although the primary treatment group in this trial as before was CRSwNP, approximately 22% of the mepolizumab-treated group had comorbid N-ERD.111 Subsequent subgroup analysis has confirmed efficacy of mepolizumab for nasal symptoms in N-ERD.112 Conversely, some observational studies indicate that a subset of N-ERD patients may not respond to mepolizumab.113,114 Patients with a high blood eosinophil count or high IgE levels have been hypothesized to be non-responders.113,114 Wangberg et al undertook a retrospective study where 50% of N-ERD patients were considered non-responders. Non-responders were defined as patients with no improvement or worsening of symptoms while on anti-IL5 therapy.115 Similarly, Bavaro et al undertook a retrospective review to assess the use of Dupilumab in a subset of N-ERD patients with an inadequate response to anti-IL-5.113 This subset of patients demonstrated a good clinical response to anti-IL4Rα therapy110 suggesting that failure to respond to one biologic does not necessarily mean that all biologics will fail.113

Tezepelumab blocks thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) which is an activator of the type-2 inflammatory pathway.100 In a recent large RCT, tezepelumab has been shown to improve nasal polyp size and nasal congestion in patients with severe CRSwNP refractory to oral corticosteroids and/or ESS.116 A proportion of the patients enrolled in the trial were N-ERD patients, and tezepelumab was effective in this subgroup. Tezepelumab seems to be especially effective at controlling asthma-related symptoms in patients with N-ERD compared with other subgroups of patients with severe asthma.117 In this context, it would be interesting to evaluate the effects of tezepelumab in N-ERD patients with dedicated trials.

Other biologics including itepekimab (human monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-33 (IL-33) and lebrikizumab (anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibody) are currently under investigation for their efficacy in refractory CRSwNP.100,101 Itepekimab seems to be effective at controlling asthma symptoms, whereas lebrikizumab has shown less consistent results, although it may have a role in patients specifically with high type-2 biomarkers such as periostin and blood eosinophils.118,119

To summarize, real-world clinical studies on biologics in CRSwNP and N-ERD patients are now emerging demonstrating their efficacy. At present, dupilumab is the only biologic showing a difference between aspirin-tolerant and N-ERD patients.120 While the findings across studies are broadly consistent, data for N-ERD patients is generated by post-hoc analysis from trials. Additionally, short follow-up periods (16–24 weeks) leave long term efficacy and adherence data lacking. These limitations should be addressed before being able to make concrete recommendations regarding the use of biologics in patients with N-ERD.

Further, there are currently no head-to-head studies comparing outcomes in biologics and ATAD. A large network meta-analysis did, however, find that dupilumab provides the greatest overall clinical benefit across all evaluated outcomes, compared to other biologics and ATAD.121 This study suggests that biologics in general seem to be more effective and have better safety profile compared to ATAD.

Biologics and ATAD

Co-treatment with biologics and ATAD is an interesting avenue of research. Various biologics, including omalizumab, dupilumab, mepolizumab, and benralizumab have been studied for their ability to induce aspirin tolerance in N-ERD patients. Omalizumab treatment, in particular, seems to increase NSAIDs tolerance in patients with N-ERD, measured as either lack of reactions to aspirin, or tolerance to higher doses of aspirin without reactions.122–124 Subsequent to this, omalizumab, or other biologics, may also have a role in improving aspirin tolerability during the initial desensitisation.123 Similar evidence showing increased aspirin tolerability in N-ERD patients also exists for dupilumab.125,126

A randomized trial comparing the aspirin-tolerance inducing effects of various biologics found significant effects from omalizumab and dupilumab but not from mepolizumab and benralizumab. Omalizumab and dupilumab were able to induce aspirin tolerance in 60% and 40% of patients respectively.127

In addition to the aspirin tolerance-inducing ability of biologics, a few studies have also investigated synergistic efficacy of ATAD and biologics in N-ERD. In a group of N-ERD patients with CRS refractory to at least 6 months of ATAD, the addition of dupilumab yielded clinical benefits including improved sinonasal symptoms.128 Post-hoc comparison showed significant reduction in nasal ECP levels in those receiving ATAD prior to the introduction of dupilumab with those not receiving ATAD pre-biologic. Patients not receiving ATAD pre-biologic also showed increased blood eosinophil levels as a percentage of their total CD45+ cells whereas those receiving ATAD did not. However, these biochemical differences did not translate to significant clinical differences between the two groups. Beyond this, evidence addressing the combined use of ATAD and biologics is scarce.

Safety of Biologics

Long term safety data for the biologics are lacking. In the short term, the use of biologics seems to be relatively safe, with a drop-out rate of less than 5% for most of the published phase 3 studies.129 Meta-analysis data from 24 RCTs shows that the occurrence of adverse events with dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab and reslizumab differs little from placebo.121 Highlighted issues associated with dupilumab treatment include risk of increased blood eosinophilia in roughly 10% of patients at approximately 16 to 20 weeks after starting the treatment.129 As a result, a proportion of patients (<5%) needed rescue treatment for their asthma or CRS129 in order to continue dupilumab treatment, and in some cases required permanent discontinuation.130 Of note, peripheral eosinophilia is also associated with high dose aspirin therapy in N-ERD.48

Cost of Biologics

Despite their efficacy in controlling CRSwNP and improving health-related QoL measures, the cost of biologics must be considered.

The onetime cost of a desensitization procedure ranges from $1700 to $3000.131 The cost of ATAD is less than $100 per year.132 Intranasal LAS is slightly more costly than oral aspirin (approx. $240 per year) owing to availability difficulties.58 However, desensitization using intranasal LAS is much faster (<1 day) and can be done in an outpatient setting, which saves on cost.61

In comparison, biologic therapy is estimated to cost $30,000 to $40,000 per patient per year.132 Given the relatively early age of onset of N-ERD, and the fact that it does not remit spontaneously, biologic therapy is assumed to be required life-long, costing multimillion dollars per patient over the course of their lives.21,131 Additionally, there are currently no biosimilar products on the market to stimulate market competitiveness and to reduce the costs for the health care system.

In conclusion, the strongest evidence for ATAD comes from several decades of clinical data. It is proven to be efficacious in N-ERD through large cohort studies, although this study design is prone to significant bias. RCTs have also shown benefit, especially in regard to patient-reported outcome measures, but suffer from some methodological flaws such as small sample size and short follow-up periods. These limitations have been acknowledged in systematic reviews assessing the use of ATAD in N-ERD.85,133 On the other hand, ATAD has a predictable side effect profile, including long-term risks, and simple mitigating measures are available to offset these. The side effects associated with ATAD, however, can be considerable and this is important to consider on an individual basis. Indeed, RCTs studying the effects of aspirin in N-ERD have reported adverse events such as GI bleeding.69,79 Advantages of ATAD include relatively low costs and better accessibility. Further, clinical data for ATAD comes from studies looking exclusively at N-ERD patients, whereas evidence for biologics currently have focused on CRSwNP with subset analyses for N-ERD. Therefore, until further evidence on the long-term safety, efficacy specific to N-ERD, and the cost-effectiveness of biologic therapy is available, ATAD should be trialled first in N-ERD patients when no clear contraindications exist. Biologics may be a suitable salvage therapy in N-ERD patients where ATAD is unsuitable.130 Additionally, biologics may have a role in improving the tolerability of aspirin and ATAD, although evidence on the concurrent use of ATAD and biologics is currently lacking.79

Recommended Clinical Approaches

For clinicians, treatment choices are determined by evidence on the efficacy and safety, and the cost effectiveness of available therapies within a healthcare system.

For a patient newly diagnosed with CRSwNP associated with N-ERD, ESS follows a period of trial of maximal medical therapy (intra-nasal topical corticosteroids, nasal douches with normal saline, a short course of oral steroids, a trial with Montelukast, and a course of long-term oral antibiotics).48 Failing that, ATAD should be the next step based on the reasons detailed above. ATAD in conjunction with ESS reduces the need for revision polypectomy from an average of once every 3 years to 10 years. While ATAD does not regress established polyposis, it is primarily effective in slowing the recurrence of nasal polyps and remains the only therapeutic option with long term data able to demonstrate this. Therefore, patients with early polyp recurrence following ESS are ideal candidates for ATAD. Current clinical consensus suggests that the optimal timing of aspirin desensitization and commencement of ATAD should be between 2 to 6 weeks following ESS.134 Similar to ATAD, Dupilumab is now an option available to act as an adjunct to ESS to decrease polyp recurrence in patients with N-ERD. As discussed above, however, ATAD should be trialled prior to considering biologics in patients who are able to have aspirin. Conclusions

ATAD has evolved into a well-established therapy for N-ERD. Long-term evidence showing sustained improvements in sinonasal symptoms, QoL, and reduced polyp recurrence albeit these studies are non-RCTs with small cohorts and short follow ups. Additionally, many patients either do not respond, cannot tolerate it, or are unsuitable, leading to ongoing refinements in protocols, dosing, and patient selection, while dose–response relationships and predictors of intolerance remain unclear.

Biologics such as dupilumab offer alternatives for those who fail or cannot use ATAD, but high costs, limited long-term safety data, and lack of head-to-head trials. Therefore, although biologics have not replaced ATAD as first-line therapy, they may diminish the role of ATAD in N-ERD. ESS remains important but is not curative, and its role continues to be refined.

Advances in understanding inflammatory endotypes, biomarkers, and the interaction between biologics and ATAD support a move toward precision medicine in N-ERD, integrating phenotype, biochemical profiling, patient preference, and resource considerations. ATAD therefore remains the cost-effective, key component of management despite variable tolerability, while research continues to optimize its use alongside newer therapies.
