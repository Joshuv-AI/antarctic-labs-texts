Physicians and hospital pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards polypharmacy in older patients with chronic diseases
Authors: Xiaolei Hu, Rui Liu, Ling Tang, Mei Mei, Yingsha Li, Gang Tang, Jie Feng, Weiqiong Chen, Guangcan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This web-based cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of physicians and hospital pharmacists towards polypharmacy in older adult patients with chronic diseases in China. This study enrolled 374 physicians and pharmacists (270 females, 92 physicians) in 20 Chinese provinces between December 2022 and March 2023. The knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 12.65 ± 2.05 (possible range, 0–18), 29.07 ± 2.68 (possible range, 7–35), and 26.16 ± 5.56 (possible range, 7–35), respectively. Working as a hospital pharmacist (vs. physician) was independently associated with adequate knowledge (OR = 2.190; 95% CI = 1.291–2.713; P = 0.004). Working in a tertiary hospital (OR = 4.296; 95% CI = 1.390-13.272; P = 0.011) was independently associated with a positive attitude. Knowledge score (OR = 1.176; 95%CI = 1.038–1.333; P = 0.011), hospital pharmacist (OR = 0.276; 95% CI = 0.137–0.557; P < 0.001), master’s degree or higher (OR = 1.754; 95% CI = 1.011–3.045; P = 0.046) and senior professional title (OR = 2.020; 95% CI = 1.032–3.952; P = 0.040) were independently associated with proactive practice toward polypharmacy in older adults. Physicians and hospital pharmacists had favorable knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice toward polypharmacy. In conclusion, enhancing knowledge through continuous education, promoting interprofessional collaboration, educating patients, and conducting regular evaluations for quality improvement are necessary to improve the KAP of healthcare professionals toward polypharmacy in older adults.

Introduction

It has been estimated that China will have 400 million citizens aged more than 65 years old and 150 million people aged more than 80 years old by 20501. Aging is associated with an increased incidence of numerous chronic diseases such as hypertension2, diabetes mellitus3, cardiovascular disease4, mental health disorders such as dementia and depression5, osteoporosis/hip fracture6 and geriatric syndromes7, which makes the healthcare needs of older people particularly challenging.

Many older adults have multiple comorbidities, which are managed by the long-term administration of several concurrent medications8. A recent study indicated that the prevalence of polypharmacy among older adults is 32%, with a mean number of 3.8 medications per patient9. Furthermore, the use of multiple medications is even more widespread among older adult home-care patients10. Polypharmacy has become more common in recent times due to a lack of guidelines that focus on the management of comorbid diseases, an increase in the number of people with multiple comorbidities due to population aging, and a lack of deprescribing methods8. In addition to increasing healthcare costs, polypharmacy can have negative outcomes due to inappropriate use of medications, adverse events, drug-drug interactions, and poor adherence to medication10,11. Indeed, studies have reported that polypharmacy is associated with a higher risk of falls and increased mortality12–14.

A variety of barriers to deprescribing and reducing polypharmacy have been studied, including a culture of diagnosing and prescribing, concerns about the risks of discontinuing medications, lack of time to consider polypharmacy issues, insufficient collaboration between physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and specialists, and a lack of evidence-based guidelines and decision-making tools in other countries15–18. However, few studies have evaluated the perceptions of healthcare providers in China regarding polypharmacy. Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) surveys can help guide the development and implementation of education and training programs to overcome barriers to improving healthcare19. A small number of previous questionnaire-based studies have indicated that physicians and nurses may have inadequate knowledge of and suboptimal practices towards polypharmacy20–22. However, published data for hospital pharmacists are limited23. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the KAP of physicians and hospital pharmacists with regard to polypharmacy in China22.

Methods

Study design and participants

This web-based cross-sectional study enrolled physicians and hospital pharmacists in 20 provinces in Mainland China between December 2022 and March 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) physician or hospital pharmacist; (2) had worked in clinical practice for at least 3 years; and (3) voluntarily agreed to participate in this study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the author’s hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the questionnaire.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire was designed with reference to the Expert Consensus on the Safety Management of Polypharmacy in Older Adult Comorbid Patients in China24 and modified following input from three senior geriatricians with more than 20 years of work experience. A pre-test was performed among 42 physicians and hospital pharmacists, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire was determined to be 0.846, which suggested good internal consistency (i.e., good reliability).

The final version of the questionnaire contained four dimensions: demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitude, and practice. The demographic characteristics dimension contained 13 items: age, gender, education, occupation, years of work experience, professional title, whether fully accredited (qualified and certified) to work as a clinical physician or hospital pharmacist, field of expertise, hospital grade, whether the hospital had a geratology department with geriatricians, whether the hospital had geriatric pharmacists, the proportion of all hospital outpatients who were older adult, and the number of older adult patients with chronic diseases at the hospital. The knowledge dimension consisted of 18 questions, each of which was scored 1 point for a correct answer and 0 points for an incorrect or unclear answer (total score range of 0–18). Item 1 was for the definition of polypharmacy. Items 2 ~ 18 were for common polypharmacy medication precautions and complications. The attitude dimension comprised 7 items, which were scored using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 5 points (“strongly agree”) to 1 point (“strongly disagree”) (total score range of 7–35). Items 1 ~ 7 were for: “Attention should be paid on polypharmacy in older adult patients”, “Attitude for risk of adverse reactions”, “Attention on potentially inappropriate use of medications”, “Financial burden”, “Medication adherence”, “Attitude for medications need to be optimized”, “Overall attitude on polypharmacy use”, respectively. The practice dimension consisted of 7 questions, which were scored using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 5 points (“always”) to 1 point (“never”) (total score range of 7–35). Items 1 was for “Inquiry patients’ medication”, Items 2 ~ 4 were for “Adjust for patient’s unreasonable medication”, Items 5 was for “Consideration for patients’ history of adverse drug reactions”, Item 6 was for “Patient education”, Item 7 was for “Successful adjustment experience for patients’ medication”. Adequate knowledge, positive attitude, and proactive practice were defined as scores of 70% or more in each of those dimensions19,25,26.

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

An online questionnaire was constructed using an online survey tool (Sojump; https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx), with care taken to ensure that the questionnaire had been correctly imported and a valid link had been generated. The questionnaire was distributed to the study subjects via WeChat. At the beginning of the questionnaire, the participants had to click the option “I agree to participate in this study” before they could answer the questions. All items in the questionnaire had to be completed before submission was permitted to ensure that no items were missing. All data were collected anonymously. In order to avoid repetition, IP restriction was applied, thus making sure that the survey could only be completed once from a single IP address. The research team, comprising three doctors trained as research assistants responsible for questionnaire promotion and distribution, meticulously reviewed all submissions for completeness, internal consistency, and logical coherence. Incomplete questionnaires and those completed using an obvious pattern (e.g., selecting all first choices) were considered invalid and were excluded.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the analysis. Quantitative variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared between groups using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables are described as numbers (percentage) [n (%)]. Correlations between the knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were analyzed using Pearson correlations. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify variables associated with adequate knowledge, positive attitude, or proactive practice. Variables with P < 0.1 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) were calculated. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The demographic characteristics of the 282 hospital pharmacists (75.40%) and 92 physicians (24.60%) are shown in Table 1. More than half of the respondents were aged > 35 years old (54.28%) and working in a tertiary hospital (78.07%). Nearly two-thirds of the participants (61.23%) had at least 10 years of work experience. Most of the participants’ hospitals had a geratology department with geriatricians (71.93%).

Knowledge, attitude, and practice

The mean knowledge score was 12.65 ± 2.05 points (total score range of 0–18), the mean attitude score was 29.07 ± 2.68 points (total score range of 7–35), and the mean practice score was 26.16 ± 5.56 points (total score range of 7–35), indicating favorable knowledge, positive attitudes and reasonably good practices with regard to polypharmacy. The responses to each of the items in the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions are shown in Tables 2, 3 and 4. The proportion of correct responses to item 1 (definition of polypharmacy) and item 18 (aminoglycoside interactions with other drugs) was significantly higher for hospital pharmacists than for physicians (P < 0.001 and P = 0.008, respectively), although the differences were small (Table 2). The responses to the other 16 items in the knowledge dimension were similar for hospital pharmacists and physicians. The majority of participants (82.35–98.13%) strongly agreed or agreed with items 1–6 in the attitude dimension, although only 55.61% of the respondents believed that polypharmacy did more harm than good (Table 3). However, only 41.44–79.14% of the participants reported always or often performing practices to help reduce polypharmacy (Table 4).

The multivariable analysis indicated that working as a hospital pharmacist was the only factor associated with adequate knowledge (OR = 2.190; 95%CI = 1.291–2.713; P = 0.004; Table 6). Discussion

The present study found that physicians and hospital pharmacists had favorable knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice on polypharmacy. Adequate knowledge was associated with working as a hospital pharmacist (vs. physician), a positive attitude was associated with working in a tertiary hospital, and good practice was associated with a higher knowledge score, having a master’s degree or higher, having a senior title, and working as a physician. This study provides new insights that could guide the design and implementation of targeted education and training interventions to increase awareness about polypharmacy among physicians and hospital pharmacists in China.

The physicians and hospital pharmacists in this study had favorable knowledge about polypharmacy, and pharmacists had a higher average knowledge score than physicians. Furthermore, despite generally positive attitudes, the practices of the study participants were suboptimal. Although few studies have directly evaluated the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of healthcare workers towards polypharmacy, the findings of this study broadly agree with those reported previously. A recent survey of physicians in China found that the mean score of questions evaluating medications that should not be prescribed to older patients was only 2.91 points out of a maximum of 7 points, indicating a poor-to-moderate level of knowledge20. Furthermore, only one-third of the physicians felt confident about prescribing for older patients, and more than half of them were unaware of screening tools for potentially inappropriate medication20. Furthermore, a study in the United Arab Emirates found that 61% of nurses considered polypharmacy to enhance the therapeutic effect in patients with comorbidities despite concerns being raised about drug interactions, adverse effects, and costs21. A survey of prescribers at a psychiatric teaching hospital in the USA found that around 40% of respondents did not attempt conversion from antipsychotic polytherapy to monotherapy, and only mild concerns were raised about polytherapy, such as adverse effects, poor adherence, mortality risk, and cost22. Another study evaluating psychiatrists’ views of antipsychotic polypharmacy also identified chronic adverse effects as a concern, although there was general satisfaction with the use of polypharmacy27. Although they do not have prescribing privileges, dental hygienists in the USA have to evaluate the patient’s conditions and were also found to have low knowledge of polypharmacy, with 25% of the respondents answering all the knowledge questions incorrectly28. Thus, awareness of and practices towards polypharmacy seem to be suboptimal among a wide range of healthcare professionals.

The finding that the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimension scores were positively correlated with each other implies that interventions to enhance the knowledge of polypharmacy among physicians and hospital pharmacists would lead to improvements in their attitudes and practices. Indeed, the multivariable analysis identified higher knowledge scores as being independently associated with a higher practice score. Numerous studies of physicians have highlighted inadequate knowledge and lack of formal training as barriers to appropriate prescribing for older adult patients29–31. Therefore, this study suggests that the development and implementation of education and training programs for physicians and hospital pharmacists would lead to a reduction in potentially inappropriate prescribing practices. It was notable that the knowledge, attitude, and/or practice scores were higher for respondents who were older, who were working in a tertiary hospital, or who had a higher level of education, an accredited qualification, greater work experience, or a senior professional title. The results are broadly supported by previous studies. Furthermore, polypharmacy tended to be less common for psychiatrists who worked in a university general hospital, published more research papers each year, or attended more psychiatric conferences27. These findings highlight the potential importance of targeting education and training for healthcare workers who are less experienced and less well-qualified. Indeed, a study assessing the knowledge of student pharmacists in the USA revealed that the mean scores were 61.0% for polypharmacy and 64.5% for deprescribing, indicating that there were deficits in awareness, and over 40% of the students stated that deprescribing was not taught as part of the curriculum32. Similarly, a study of medical trainees in Saudi Arabia revealed low awareness of polypharmacy as well as inappropriate prescription practices in older adults33. Although polypharmacy has been included in the physician and pharmacist training curricula for a long time, emphasis should be put on deprescription, methods of medication optimization, and polypharmacy management. Education would be the best approach to improve the management of polypharmacy in the study setting. Surveillance programs could also be implemented, but the cost-benefit ratio of such programs would have to be carefully evaluated. In addition, surveillance programs are expensive in terms of human resources. Continuing education activities are relatively inexpensive, can reach many people, and can be diffused using different media, such as conferences, podcasts, video capsules, etc.

An interesting observation of this study was that working as a hospital pharmacist rather than as a physician was independently associated with adequate knowledge and a positive attitude, whereas working as a physician rather than as a hospital pharmacist was independently associated with good practice. This apparent discrepancy may be related to pharmacists being unwilling to alter the medications prescribed by doctors. Barriers to deprescribing reported by previous studies include fear of conflict between physicians and clinical pharmacists, a reluctance among pharmacists to challenge physicians’ prescribing decisions, and inadequate communication30,34. Efforts to improve communication between physicians and pharmacists, as well as encourage pharmacists to be more proactive in deprescribing, may help to reduce polypharmacy in older patients. It is important to note that Chinese pharmacists’ practices and expertise may vary from those of their colleagues from other countries because of different training, healthcare policies, availability of over-the-counter medicine, community engagement, and other factors35,36.

However, it should be emphasized that improving knowledge alone will likely be insufficient to reduce potentially inappropriate prescribing. In addition to knowledge gaps and lack of formal training among healthcare workers, other barriers to appropriate prescribing/deprescribing include a lack of suitable guidelines or systems to facilitate appropriate prescribing/deprescribing, time constraints, feeling compelled to follow disease-specific guidelines, unwillingness of the physician to deprescribe disease-specific medications (especially without the advice of a specialist) due to fear of negative outcomes, deficient multidisciplinary co-operation, patient request to receive a particular medication, and patient resistance due to unrealistic expectations37–39. These issues will also need to be addressed to ensure that older adult patients are not prescribed potentially inappropriate medications.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was small, so the analysis may have had insufficient power to detect some real differences between groups. The number of hospital pharmacists was higher than the number of physicians, probably because of more availability to complete the questionnaire. Second, although it was a multi-center study, the generalizability of the results cannot be guaranteed. Third, although the questionnaire was developed based on published literature, it may have had limitations in terms of its ability to assess perceptions of polypharmacy. Fourth, this study did not investigate whether training programs would enhance the questionnaire scores.

Conclusions

In conclusion, physicians and hospital pharmacists had favorable knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practice. Enhancing knowledge through continuous education, promoting interprofessional collaboration, educating patients, and conducting regular evaluations for quality improvement are necessary.
