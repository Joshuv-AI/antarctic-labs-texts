Robust Tail Index Estimation under Random Censoring via Minimum
Density Power Divergence

Abstract




Abstract

We propose a new robust estimator for the tail index of a
Pareto-type distribution under random right-censoring, developed within the
framework of the minimum density power divergence. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first application of this divergence to randomly
censored extreme value models, providing a novel direction for robust
inference in this setting. Under mild regularity conditions, the proposed
estimator is shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal. Its
finite-sample performance is thoroughly examined through an extensive
simulation study, demonstrating enhanced robustness and efficiency compared to
existing methods. The practical usefulness of the approach is further
illustrated through an application to a real AIDS survival dataset.

Keywords:
Asymptotic normality; Heavy-tails; Robust
estimation; Random right-censoring; Tail index.

Introduction

Right-censored data present a common challenge in statistics, as
values are only known up to a certain threshold, while those beyond it remain
unobserved. This issue frequently arises in fields such as survival analysis,
reliability engineering, and insurance. Pareto-type distributions are commonly
used to model data exhibiting extreme values. These distributions are
characterized by a relatively high probability of producing observations far
from the mean, in contrast to the normal distribution. They are particularly
relevant in applications such as insurance, finance, and environmental
studies, where rare events can have a substantial impact. Let
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}
be a sample of size
n\geq 1
from a random variables (rv)

X,
and let
C_{1},C_{2},...,C_{n}
be another sample from a rv
C,
both
defined on a probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P}\right),
with continuous cumulative distribution functions (cdf’s)
F
and
G,

respectively. Assume that
X
and
C
are independent. Also, we suppose that

X
is right censored by
C,
that is each for stage
1\leq j\leq n,
we can
only observe the variable
Z_{j}:=\min\left\{X_{j},C_{j}\right\}
and the
indicator variable
\delta_{j}:=\mathbb{I}_{\left\{X_{j}\leq C_{j}\right\}},
which determines whether or not
X
has been observed. We assume that the
tails functions
\overline{F}:=1-F
and
\overline{G}:=1-G
are regularly
varying at infinity (or Pareto-type) of positive tail indices
\gamma_{1}>0

and
\gamma_{2}>0
respectively. On the other terms for any
x>0,
we have

\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\overline{F}(ux)}{\overline{F}(u)}=x^{-1/\gamma_{1}}\text{ and }\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\overline{G}(ux)}{\overline{G}(u)}=x^{-1/\gamma_{2}}.

(1.1)

We denote the cdf of
Z
by
H.
Then, by the independence between
X
and

C,
we have
\overline{H}=\overline{F}\times\overline{G},
which yields that

H
is also regularly varying at infinity, with tail index
\gamma:=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}/\left(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\right).
In the presence of
extreme values and right-censored data, several methods have been proposed for
estimating the tail index. Numerous techniques have been developed to address
the challenges arising from the nature of such data. Many studies have focused
on modifying traditional tail index estimation methods, such as Hill’s
estimator
(
Hill 1975
)
to make them more suitable for censored data.

Einmahl et al. 2008
adapted this estimator to handle the characteristics of
right-censored data, and it is given by

\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(EFG\right)}:=\frac{\widehat{\gamma}^{\left(H\right)}}{\widehat{p}},

where
\widehat{\gamma}^{\left(H\right)}:=k^{-1}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}\log\left(Z_{n-i+1:n}/Z_{n-k:n}\right),
denotes the famous Hill estimator
corresponding to the tail index
\gamma,
and
\widehat{p}:=k^{-1}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}}\delta_{\left[n-i+1:n\right]},
stands for an estimator of the proportion
of upper non-censored observations
p:=\gamma_{2}/\left(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\right).
The integer sequence
k=k_{n}
represents the number of
top order statistics used in the estimation of the tail index so that

k\rightarrow\infty
and
k/n\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
The
sequence of rv’s
Z_{1:n}\leq...\leq Z_{n:n}
represents the order statistics
pertaining to the sample
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
and
\delta_{\left[1:n\right]},...,\delta_{\left[n:n\right]}
denotes the corresponding concomitant
values, satisfying
\delta_{\left[j:n\right]}=\delta_{i}
for
i
such
that
Z_{j:n}=Z_{i}.
On the basis of a Kaplan-Meier integration,

Worms and Worms 2014
proposed a consistent estimator to the tail index
\gamma_{1}

defined by

\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(W\right)}:={\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}\frac{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(KM\right)}\left(Z_{n-i:n}\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(KM\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}\log\frac{Z_{n-i+1:n}}{Z_{n-i:n}},

where

F_{n}^{\left(KM\right)}\left(x\right):=\underset{Z_{i:n}\leq x}{1-{\displaystyle\prod}}\left(\dfrac{n-i}{n-i+1}\right)^{\delta_{\left[i:n\right]}},

denotes the popular Kaplan-Meier estimator of cdf
F

(
Kaplan and Meier 1958
)
. The
asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(W\right)}
is
established in
Beirlant et al. 2019
by considering Hall’s model
(
Hall 1982
)
.
The bias reduction in tail index estimation for censored data is addressed by

Beirlant et al. 2016
,
Beirlant et al. 2018
and
Beirlant et al. 2019
. By using a Nelson-Aalen
integration, recently
Meraghni et al. 2025
also derived a new estimator for

\gamma_{1}
given by

\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}:={\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}\frac{\delta_{\left[n-i+1:n\right]}}{i}\frac{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-i+1:n}\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}\log\frac{Z_{n-i+1:n}}{Z_{n-k:n}},

where

F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(z\right)=1-\prod_{Z_{i:n}<z}\exp\left\{-\frac{\delta_{\left[i:n\right]}}{n-i+1}\right\},

(1.2)

denotes the well-known Nelson-Aalen estimator of cdf
F

(
Nelson 1972
)
. Note that

\frac{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-i+1:n}\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}=\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}\exp\left\{-\frac{\delta_{\left[n-j+1:n\right]}}{j}\right\},

(1.3)

thus the above formula of
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}
may be
rewritten into

\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}:={\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}a_{ik}\log\frac{Z_{n-i+1:n}}{Z_{n-k:n}},

where

a_{ik}:=\frac{\delta_{\left[n-i+1:n\right]}}{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}\exp\left\{-\frac{\delta_{\left[n-j+1:n\right]}}{j}\right\}.

(1.4)

The authors showed that the two tail index estimators
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(W\right)}
and
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}

exhibit similar performances in terms both of bias and mean squared error
(MSE). Actually, the studies made on the comparison between the Kaplan–Meier
and Nelson–Aalen estimators reached that the latter exhibit an almost similar
statistical behavior; see, for instance,
Colosimo et al. 2002
. On the other
hand, reaching to establish the asymptotic properties of the extreme
Kaplan–Meier integrals poses somewhat difficulties. To overcome this issue,

Meraghni et al. 2025
introduced a Nelson–Aalen tail-product limit process and
established its Gaussian approximation in terms of standard Wiener processes.
Thus, they established the consistency and asymptotic normality of the
proposed estimator
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}
by
considering the first and second order conditions of regular variation
functions, namely the assumptions
\left(\ref{RV}\right)
and
\left(\ref{second-order}\right)
respectively. As expected, they also showed that
both the asymptotic bias and variance of
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(W\right)}
and
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}
are
respectively equal.

The most common method for estimating the parameters of an extreme
value distribution in extreme value analysis comes from the maximum likelihood
estimation method. Although these estimators possess desirable asymptotic
properties, they may be sensitive to outlying observations from the assumed
extreme value models; see, for instance,
Brazauskas and Serfling 2000
. Then, robust
statistics are a better approach to avoid outliers and therefore deviations
from the underlying parametric models. It has been shown that employing robust
statistical ideas in extreme value theory improves the quality and precision
of estimates
(
Dell’Aquila and Embrechts 2006
)
.
Juárèz and Schucany 2004
seem to be the first authors to
employ the the minimum density power divergence (MDPD) of
Basu et al. 1998
for
the robust estimation of the parameters of an extreme value distribution.
Since then, this divergence measure has become the most sought-after
divergence measure for robust estimation of parameters of extreme value
distributions.
Kim and Lee 2008
,
Dierchx et al. 2013
,
Goegebeur et al. 2014
,
Dierckx et al. 2021

have made use of the MDPD in estimating the tail index and quantiles from
Pareto-type distributions. Recently,
Ghosh 2017
proposed a robust MDPD
estimator for a real-valued tail index. This estimator is a robust
generalization of the estimator proposed by
Mattys and Beirlant 2003
and addresses the
non-identical distributions of the exponential regression model using the
approach in
Ghosh and Basu 2013
. Also,
Dierchx et al. 2013
employs the MDPD concept on an
extended Pareto distribution for relative excesses over a high threshold.

Minkah el al. 2023A
and
Minkah el al. 2023B
proposed a robust estimator for the tail
index of a Pareto-type using the MDPD approach on an exponential regression
model.

While robust estimation techniques have been extensively developed
for complete data settings, their extension to the context of randomly
right-censored observations remains considerably less explored. Such censoring
mechanisms are prevalent in survival analysis, reliability theory, and
actuarial applications, where incomplete information poses additional
statistical challenges. Classical estimators of the extreme value index under
censoring, such as Hill-type approaches or those based on Kaplan-Meier and
Nelson-Aalen integrals, often exhibit sensitivity to outliers and deviations
from the assumed model, thereby limiting their practical robustness. To the
best of our knowledge, a comprehensive robust framework for tail index
estimation in the presence of censoring and heavy-tailed behavior has not yet
been established. In this work, we address this gap by adapting the MDPD
methodology to the censored data setting. The resulting estimator preserves
the robustness properties inherent to the MDPD approach, while accounting for
the incomplete nature of the data and maintaining desirable asymptotic
characteristics.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section

2
provides a brief overview on the MDPD estimation method, originally
introduced by
Basu et al. 1998
. Section
3
discusses the asymptotic
properties (consistency and asymptotic normality) of the proposed estimator,
with detailed proofs deferred to Section
6
. The finite-sample
performance of the estimator is examined in Section
4
, through a
simulation study, including comparisons with existing estimators. Section

5
is devoted to a case study of AIDS survival data. Finally, Appendix
A compiles several technical lemmas and propositions that are instrumental to
our theoretical development, while Appendix B contains the figures related to
the simulation results.

2.
Minimum density power divergence

Basu et al. 1998
introduced a new measure of divergence between two
probability densities
\ell
and
f,
known as the density power divergence,
by

d_{\alpha}\left(f,\ell\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\ell^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\ell^{\alpha}\left(x\right)f\left(x\right)+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}f^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)\right]dx,&\alpha>0\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(x\right)\log\dfrac{f\left(x\right)}{\ell\left(x\right)}dx,&\alpha=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

where
\alpha\geq 0
is a tuning parameter that controls the trade-off between
efficiency and robustness. The case
\alpha=0
is the limit of the general
expression
\left(\alpha>0\right)
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
yielding the
classical Kullback-Leibler divergence

d_{0}\left(f,\ell\right).

Let us consider a parametric family of densities
\left\{\ell_{\theta}:\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\right\}
and suppose that the goal is to
estimate the parameter
\theta.
Let
F
denote the cdf corresponding to the
density
f.
The MDPD is a functional
T_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
defined
as
d_{\alpha}\left(f,\ell_{T_{\alpha}\left(F\right)}\right)=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}d_{\alpha}\left(f,\ell_{\theta}\right).
The term

\int f^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx
in
\left(\ref{d}\right)
does not
depend on
\theta,
and thus does not affect the minimization of
d_{\alpha}\left(f,\ell_{\theta}\right)
over
\theta\in\Theta.
Therefore, the
minimization involved in computing the MDPD functional
T_{\alpha}\left(F\right)
reduces to minimizing the objective function

\delta_{\alpha}\left(f;\theta\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{\theta}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{\theta}^{\alpha}\left(x\right)dF\left(x\right),&\alpha>0,\\
-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\ell_{\theta}\left(x\right)dF\left(x\right),&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

Given a random sample
X_{1},...,X_{n}
from the cdf
F,
we estimate

\delta_{\alpha}\left(f;\theta\right)
by replacing
F
with its empirical
counterpart
F_{n}\left(x\right):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}_{\left\{X_{i}\leq x\right\}}.
For a fixed tuning parameter
\alpha\geq 0,
the MDPD
estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n,\alpha}
of
\theta
is then defined as the
minimizer (over
\theta\in\Theta)
the quantity

L_{n.\alpha}\left(\theta\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{\theta}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{\theta}^{\alpha}\left(x\right)dF_{n}\left(x\right),&\alpha>0,\\
-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\ell_{\theta}\left(x\right)dF_{n}\left(x\right),&\alpha=0,\end{array}\right.,

which can be written in terms of the observed data as

L_{n.\alpha}\left(\theta\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{\theta}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\dfrac{1}{n}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}}\ell_{\theta}^{\alpha}\left(X_{i}\right),&\alpha>0,\\
-\dfrac{1}{n}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}}\log\ell_{\theta}\left(X_{i}\right),&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right..

In other words, we have

\widehat{\theta}_{n,\alpha}:=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\arg\min}L_{n.\alpha}\left(\theta\right).

That is,
\widehat{\theta}_{n,\alpha}
is solution of the estimating equation

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\ell_{\theta}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\dfrac{1}{n}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\ell_{\theta}^{\alpha}\left(X_{i}\right)=0,&\alpha>0,\\
\dfrac{1}{n}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\log\ell_{\theta}\left(X_{i}\right)=0,&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

The tuning parameter
\alpha
plays a key role by balancing the trade-off
between efficiency and robustness in the MDPD estimator. Specifically, when

\alpha
is close to zero, the estimator tends to be highly efficient but less
robust to outliers. Whilst,

as

\alpha
increases,
robustness improves at the expense of efficiency. Studies have shown that
small values of
\alpha
provide strong robustness properties with only a
minor loss in asymptotic efficiency compared to the maximum likelihood
estimator under correct model specification.

2.1.
MDPD estimator of
\gamma_{1}
for right-censored data

For a fixed threshold
u>0,
consider the relative excess rv

Y:=X/u
conditional on
X>u.
Its cdf is given by

F_{u}\left(x\right):=1-\frac{\overline{F}\left(ux\right)}{\overline{F}\left(u\right)},

with corresponding probability density function
f_{u}.
Assuming that

\overline{F}\in\mathcal{RV}_{\left(-1/\gamma_{1}\right)},
it follows
that
F_{u}\left(x\right)\sim 1-x^{-1/\gamma_{1}},
as
u\rightarrow\infty.
Therefore, the rv
Y
may be approximated by the Pareto distribution
with density

\ell_{\gamma_{1}}\left(x\right):=\frac{d}{dx}\left(1-x^{-1/\gamma_{1}}\right)=\gamma_{1}^{-1}x^{-1-1/\gamma_{1}},

for
x\geq 1
and
0
otherwise.

The density power divergence
objective function corresponding to this model is defined by:

\mathcal{L}_{u,\alpha}^{\ast}\left(\gamma_{1}\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{1}^{\infty}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\int_{u}^{\infty}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{\alpha}\left(x/u\right)d\dfrac{F\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}\left(u\right)},&\alpha>0,\\
\int_{u}^{\infty}\log\ell_{\gamma_{1}}\left(x/u\right)d\dfrac{F\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}\left(u\right)},&\alpha=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

Letting
u=Z_{n-k:n}
and replacing
F
with Nelson-Aalen estimator

F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}
yield the empirical objective function

\mathcal{L}_{k,\alpha}^{\ast}\left(\gamma_{1}\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\int_{1}^{\infty}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx-\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\int_{1}^{\infty}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{\alpha}\left(x/Z_{n-k:n}\right)d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)},&\alpha>0,\\
\int_{1}^{\infty}\log\ell_{\gamma_{1}}\left(x/Z_{n-k:n}\right)d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)},&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

Thereby, the MDPD estimator of
\gamma_{1}
is then obtained by minimizing

\mathcal{L}_{k,\alpha}^{\ast}\left(\gamma_{1}\right).
That is, by
solving the following equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}\begin{array}[c]{l}\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{1+\alpha}\left(\dfrac{x}{Z_{n-k:n}}\right)dx\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ -\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{x}{Z_{n-k:n}}\right)d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}=0,\end{array}&\alpha>0,\\
\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\log\ell_{\gamma_{1}}\left(\dfrac{x}{Z_{n-k:n}}\right)d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}=0,&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

By elementary calculations, we find:

\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{\alpha}\left(x\right)=\frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{1}^{2+\alpha}}\frac{\log x-\gamma_{1}}{x^{\alpha\left(1+1/\gamma_{1}\right)}},

\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\log\ell_{\gamma_{1}}\left(x\right)=\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\log\left(\gamma_{1}^{-1}x^{-1-1/\gamma_{1}}\right)=-\frac{\gamma_{1}-\log x}{\gamma_{1}^{2}}

and

\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d}{d\gamma_{1}}\ell_{\gamma_{1}}^{1+\alpha}\left(x\right)dx=-\frac{\alpha\left(\alpha+1\right)\left(\gamma_{1}+1\right)}{\gamma_{1}^{\alpha+1}\left(\alpha\left(1+\gamma_{1}\right)+1\right)^{2}}.

Consequently, we obtain:

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}{\displaystyle\int_{Z_{n-k:n}}^{\infty}}\dfrac{\gamma_{1}-\log\left(x/Z_{n-k:n}\right)}{\left(x/Z_{n-k:n}\right)^{\alpha\left(1+1/\gamma_{1}\right)}}d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}=\dfrac{\alpha\gamma_{1}\left(\gamma_{1}+1\right)}{\left(1+\alpha+\alpha\gamma_{1}\right)^{2}},&\alpha>0,\\
{\displaystyle\int_{Z_{n-k:n}}^{\infty}}\left(\gamma_{1}-\log\dfrac{x}{Z_{n-k:n}}\right)d\dfrac{F_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(Z_{n-k:n}\right)}=0,&\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.8)

Meraghni et al. 2025
stated that

dF_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)=\frac{\overline{F}_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)dH_{n}^{\left(1\right)}\left(x\right)}{\overline{H}_{n}\left(x^{-}\right)},

where
H_{n}
and
H_{n}^{\left(1\right)}
are the empirical counterparts
of the cdf
H
and the sub-distribution
H^{\left(1\right)},
respectively
defined by:

H_{n}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}_{\left\{Z_{i:n}\leq x\right\}}\text{ and }H_{n}^{\left(1\right)}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\left[i:n\right]}\mathbb{I}_{\left\{Z_{i:n}\leq x\right\}},

(see, for example,
Shorack and Wellner 1986
page 294). Here,
f\left(x^{-}\right)

denotes the left limit, at
x,
of the function
f.
By substituting

dF_{n}^{\left(NA\right)}\left(x\right)
with its expression in
equation
\left(\ref{int-formula}\right),
we get

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{lc}{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}a_{ik}\dfrac{\gamma_{1}-\log\left(Z_{n-i+1:n}/Z_{n-k:n}\right)}{\left(Z_{n-i+1:n}/Z_{n-k:n}\right)^{\alpha\left(1+1/\gamma_{1}\right)}}=\dfrac{\alpha\gamma_{1}\left(\gamma_{1}+1\right)}{\left(1+\alpha+\alpha\gamma_{1}\right)^{2}},&\alpha>0,\\
{\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}}a_{ik}\left\{\gamma_{1}-\log\dfrac{Z_{n-i+1:n}}{Z_{n-k:n}}\right\}=0,&\alpha=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.9)

where
a_{ik}
is the random weight sequence defined in
\left(\ref{aik}\right).
The MDPD tail index estimator under random censoring,
that we denote by
\widehat{\gamma}_{1,\alpha},
is defined as the solution to
this equation, which has not yet been addressed in the literature. It is worth
mentioning that, for
\alpha=0,
the solution of
\left(\ref{estim-equa}\right)
coincides with the estimator
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(MNS\right)}
introduced earlier.

3.
Consistency and asymptotic normality

From now on, we denote the true value of
\gamma_{1}
by
\gamma_{1}^{\ast}.

Theorem 3.1
.

(consistency) Assume that the cdf’s
F
and
G
satisfy
the first-order condition
\left(\ref{RV}\right)
and that
p>1/2.
Let

k=k_{n}
be a sequence of integers such that
k\rightarrow\infty
and

k/n\rightarrow 0,
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Then, for any
\alpha>0,
with
probability tending to
1,
there exists a solution
\widehat{\gamma}_{1,\alpha}
to the estimating equation
\left(\ref{estim-equa}\right)

such that
\widehat{\gamma}_{1,\alpha}\overset{\mathbf{P}}{\rightarrow}\gamma_{1}^{\ast},
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Since weak approximations of extreme value theory-based statistics
are typically achieved within the second-order framework

(
de Haan and Stadtmüller 1996
, see, i.e.,)
, then it is natural to assume that the cdf
F

satisfies the well-known second-order condition of regular variation.
Specifically, we assume that for any
x>0,
we have

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{U_{F}\left(tx\right)/U_{F}\left(t\right)-x^{\gamma_{1}}}{A_{1}^{\ast}\left(t\right)}=x^{\gamma_{1}}\dfrac{x^{\tau_{1}}-1}{\tau_{1}}.

(3.10)

Here
\tau_{1}\leq 0
is the second-order parameter and
A_{1}^{\ast}
is a
function tending to
0,
not changing sign near infinity and whose absolute
value is regularly varying at infinity with index
\tau_{1}.
When
\tau_{1}=0,
the expression
\left(x^{\tau_{1}}-1\right)/\tau_{1}
is
interpreted as
\log x.
In what follows, the functions
\mathcal{D}^{\leftarrow}\left(s\right):=\inf\left\{x:\mathcal{D}\left(x\right)\geq s\right\},

0<s<1
and
U_{\mathcal{D}}\left(t\right):=\mathcal{D}^{\leftarrow}\left(1-1/t\right),

Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the problem of robust estimation of the
extreme value index under random right censoring, a setting frequently
encountered in survival analysis, reliability engineering, and insurance.
While various estimators have been proposed in the literature, including
Hill-type methods and those based on Kaplan-Meier or Nelson-Aalen estimators,
many of these approaches are sensitive to outliers or rely on strong
parametric assumptions.

To overcome these limitations, we introduced a new estimator based
on the MDPD, a criterion well known for its robustness in the uncensored
context. We carefully extended this methodology to handle randomly
right-censored data, accounting for the inherent incompleteness in such
settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the
MDPD framework to tail index estimation under censoring, thereby addressing a
notable gap in robust extreme value literature.

We established the consistency and asymptotic normality of the
proposed estimator under standard regular variation assumptions. A
comprehensive simulation study demonstrated its strong performance in finite
samples, showing an advantageous balance between robustness and efficiency,
particularly under model misspecification or contamination.

This contribution opens new perspectives for developing robust
methods in censored extreme value models. It also provides a useful foundation
for future research, such as adaptive threshold selection, multivariate
extensions, models with dependent censoring and robust estimation under
covariate-dependent censoring.

J_{2n}\left(x\right):=\dfrac{x^{-1/\gamma_{1}}}{\gamma}\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{k}}{\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}}u^{1/\gamma-1}\left\{p\mathbf{W}_{n,2}\left(\dfrac{k}{n}u^{-1/\gamma}\right)-q\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\dfrac{k}{n}u^{-1/\gamma}\right)\right\}du.

Proposition 7.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\min\left(x^{-1/\gamma},y^{-1/\gamma}\right)K\left(x,y\right)dxdy

(7.24)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ -2\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-1/\gamma}\psi_{1}\left(x\right)dx+p\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-1/\gamma_{1}}\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}}\left(x\right)dx\right)^{2},

where
K\left(x,y\right):=p\psi_{1}\left(x\right)\psi_{1}\left(y\right)+\left(q/\gamma_{1}^{2}\right)\psi_{2}\left(x\right)\psi_{2}\left(y\right),

\psi_{1}\left(x\right):=x^{1/\gamma_{2}}\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)-qx^{1/\gamma}\phi^{\ast}\left(x\right),\text{ }\psi_{2}\left(x\right)=x^{1/\gamma}\phi^{\ast}\left(x\right),

\phi^{\ast}\left(x\right):=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{-1/\gamma_{1}}\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}}\left(t\right)dt
and
\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}}
is as
in
\left(\ref{phi}\right).

Proof.

Note that
I_{n}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}J_{n}\left(x\right)\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)dz=I_{n1}+I_{n2},
where

I_{n1}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}J_{n1}\left(x\right)\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)dx\text{ and }I_{n2}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}J_{n2}\left(x\right)\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)dx,

with
I_{n1}
and
I_{n2}
as defined in Proposition
\ref{Prop3}.
Let us
write

I_{n1}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\frac{k}{n}x^{-1/\gamma}\right)x^{1/\gamma_{2}}\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)dx-a\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right),

where
a:=\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-1/\gamma_{1}}\phi_{\alpha,\gamma_{1}^{\ast}}\left(x\right)dx.
By integrating
I_{n2}
by parts, we obtain

I_{n2}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{k}}\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{1/\gamma}\left\{p\mathbf{W}_{n,2}\left(\dfrac{k}{n}x^{-1/\gamma}\right)-q\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\dfrac{k}{n}x^{-1/\gamma}\right)\right\}\phi^{\ast}\left(x\right)dx.

Now, we may rewrite
I_{n}
as the sum of

\mathbf{I}_{n1}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\frac{k}{n}x^{-1/\gamma}\right)\psi_{1}\left(x\right)dx-a\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\mathbf{W}_{n,1}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right),

and

\mathbf{I}_{n2}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{k}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathbf{W}_{n,2}\left(\dfrac{k}{n}x^{-1/\gamma}\right)\psi_{2}\left(x\right)dx.

Since
\mathbf{W}_{n,1}
and
\mathbf{W}_{n,2}
are centred and independent
rv’s, then the rv’s
\mathbf{I}_{n1},

\mathbf{I}_{n2}
and their product

\mathbf{I}_{n1}\times\mathbf{I}_{n2}
are also centered. It is
straightforward to verify, by taking expectations, that

\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{I}_{n2}^{2}\right]=\frac{q}{\gamma_{1}^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\min\left(x^{-1/\gamma},y^{-1/\gamma}\right)\psi_{2}\left(x\right)\psi_{2}\left(y\right)dxdy,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{I}_{n1}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=p\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\min\left(x^{-1/\gamma},y^{-1/\gamma}\right)\psi_{1}\left(x\right)\psi_{1}\left(y\right)dxdy

\displaystyle+pa^{2}-2\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-1/\gamma}\psi_{1}\left(x\right)dx.

The sum of these two expressions corresponds to the variance formula

\sigma^{2}
given in
\left(\ref{sigma}\right),
as desired.
∎
