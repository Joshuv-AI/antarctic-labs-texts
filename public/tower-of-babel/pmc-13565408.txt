A Dynamic Model of Microsleep and Vigilance Tradeoffs in Nesting Chinstrap Penguins
Authors: Kerk L Phillips, Mark H Showalter, Eric R Eide, James H Cardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep in wild animals reflects both physiological regulation and ecological demands. Nesting chinstrap penguins exhibit extreme sleep fragmentation, obtaining substantial sleep through thousands of brief microsleep episodes while maintaining vigilance.ObjectiveTo examine whether a dynamic model integrating sleep regulation with ecological trade-offs can account for the fragmented sleep patterns observed in nesting chinstrap penguins.MethodsWe adapted a dynamic model of sleep choice in which an animal switches between sleep and wakefulness on the basis of homeostatic sleep pressure, circadian alignment, switching costs, and stochastic waking opportunities. The model was calibrated to published sleep data from 14 nesting chinstrap penguins using five simulated moments: average hours of sleep per day, the standard deviation of hours of sleep per day, the number of sleep events, the maximum sleep duration, and the percentage of sleep durations less than 10 seconds.ResultsThe model reproduced several key qualitative features of penguin sleep. Simulated sleep episodes were very short, with an average duration of 2.774 seconds compared with 3.91 seconds in the observed data. The model also generated frequent transitions between sleep and wake, closely matching the observed number of daily sleep events and reproducing the maximum observed sleep duration. However, it underestimated total sleep time and produced a larger share of very short episodes than observed in the data. Estimated parameters implied minimal switching costs, rapid homeostatic adjustment over seconds-long bouts, and a strong relative weight on sleep utility within the fitted model.ConclusionA dynamic optimization framework can capture important qualitative features of fragmented sleep in nesting chinstrap penguins. The findings suggest that microsleeps may reflect adaptive trade-offs between restorative sleep and wakeful activity under ecological constraint. Because the model treats sleep as a binary behavioral state, it does not distinguish unihemispheric from bihemispheric slow-wave sleep. We therefore interpret the model primarily as a model of microsleep timing and fragmentation rather than as a complete model of penguin sleep architecture or fragmented sleep across wild animals.

Introduction

Sleep is a complex biological phenomenon that exhibits remarkable variation across species. Sleep manifests in diverse forms, ranging from consolidated spells to highly fragmented patterns composed of numerous brief episodes. Various studies demonstrate that many birds and marine mammals are capable of unihemispheric slow-wave sleep (USWS), in which one brain hemisphere sleeps while the other remains awake.1,2 This adaptation allows individuals to maintain vigilance for predators or environmental hazards while still obtaining restorative sleep.3 Such findings underscore that sleep is not a uniform state but rather a flexible behavioral strategy shaped by ecological constraints. Concepts such as sleep fragmentation, microsleeps, and unihemispheric sleep are therefore central to understanding how animals balance the physiological need for sleep with competing demands such as foraging, thermoregulation, and predator avoidance.

The adaptive value of these sleep strategies is supported by both neurophysiological and ecological evidence. From a neurobiological perspective, slow-wave sleep is associated with neuronal recovery processes, including synchronized “off-periods” that allow for cellular maintenance following extended wakefulness.4 Sleep homeostasis further implies that sleep pressure accumulates during waking activity and is dissipated during sleep, yet this process can be expressed locally within specific brain regions depending on recent activity levels.5 These findings suggest that sleep need not occur exclusively as a global, uninterrupted state; instead, animals may satisfy sleep requirements through shorter, distributed episodes when environmental conditions demand sustained vigilance. Ecologically, the benefits and costs associated with sleep vary substantially across contexts. Animals exposed to predation risk or variable food availability often modify their sleep structure, increasing vigilance or fragmenting sleep to accommodate external pressures.6 Group living, environmental temperature, and metabolic state can all influence how sleep is organized, reinforcing the view that sleep is shaped by trade-offs between physiological restoration and survival-related behaviors.6 Hormonal pathways may provide an additional link between internal physiological state, environmental conditions, and sleep organization. For example, in birds, melatonin is closely associated with circadian timing.3,7–9

Recent empirical work has provided particularly striking evidence of extreme sleep fragmentation in wild animals. Documented sleep patterns in nesting chinstrap penguins show that individuals accrue large amounts of total sleep through thousands of microsleep episodes, each lasting only a few seconds.10 Despite the brevity of these episodes, penguins accumulate over eleven hours of slow-wave sleep per hemisphere per day. Both unihemispheric and bihemispheric sleep are observed, consistent with the possibility that penguins adjust sleep structure under vigilance demands during the breeding period. These findings demonstrate that sleep can be accrued incrementally through numerous brief bouts, challenging the traditional view that consolidated sleep is necessary to achieve restorative benefits. The penguin system therefore provides a compelling natural laboratory for examining how sleep behavior responds to ecological constraints and how physiological needs are balanced against environmental risks.

While biological models of sleep, such as the widely used two-process model,11 capture the interaction between circadian rhythms and sleep homeostasis, they are largely deterministic and do not explicitly account for the behavioral flexibility observed in many species. In particular, these models do not incorporate how the value of wakeful activities such as foraging, social interaction, or predator avoidance may influence sleep timing and structure. One way to extend this framework is to treat sleep behavior as reflecting trade-offs between the benefits of restorative processes and the benefits of wakeful activity.12,13 Incorporating such trade-offs into the traditional biological framework allows for predictions about how environmental variation and ecological pressures shape sleep behavior. Applications of this approach have shown that accounting for these factors can help reconcile differences in sleep patterns between wild and captive animals, highlighting the importance of context-dependent regulation in sleep behavior.12

The present study applies this integrated framework to the case of chinstrap penguins, a species whose sleep patterns differ markedly from those of mammals (elephants and sloths) previously studied. The penguin’s reliance on microsleeps, the presence of unihemispheric sleep, and the constant vigilance required in densely populated breeding colonies create a unique set of constraints and opportunities that are not well captured by traditional models of consolidated sleep. Our goal is not to model hemispheric switching directly, but to ask whether an optimization framework can explain the seconds-long sleep-wake cycling that gives penguin sleep its extreme fragmented structure.

By extending a framework that integrates biological processes with context-dependent trade-offs to a system characterized by extreme sleep fragmentation and behavioral flexibility, this study contributes to a broader understanding of how sleep behavior adapts under ecological constraints. Modeling sleep as a sequence of short, adaptive decisions rather than as a fixed biological schedule is useful because nesting penguins repeatedly face short-run trade-offs between restorative sleep and waking activities such as vigilance, nest defense, social interaction, and responses to colony disturbance. The insights gained from the penguin system therefore have implications not only for comparative sleep research but also for understanding how animals more generally balance physiological needs with the competing demands imposed by their environments.

Methods

A Model of Sleep Choice

Here we present an adaptation of a model used to analyze sleep behavior in elephants and sloths.12 We assume that animals allocate their time between sleep and productive waking activities, which we refer to collectively as consumption. The circadian cycle is denoted by yt. If the animal sleeps at time t, the value of the homeostatic process, or sleep pressure, Ht, declines, but no non-circadian utility (well-being) is generated. If the animal is awake, it engages in various activities to produce a composite consumption good, Ct, which in turn generates utility. In this model, consumption includes all waking activities that contribute to well-being, such as foraging, vigilance to avoid predation or accidents, caring for offspring, and other behaviors including play or rest.

The sleep state At is a binary variable equal to 1 when the animal is awake at time t and 0 when it is sleeping. This binary state should be interpreted as a simplified behavioral sleep state rather than a full neurophysiological classification of sleep architecture. The published penguin data distinguish unihemispheric and bihemispheric slow-wave sleep, but our calibration targets are aggregate moments of microsleep timing, including total sleep duration, event frequency, event duration, and variability. Modeling unihemispheric sleep explicitly would require additional state variables for hemispheric status and transition rules between wakefulness, unihemispheric sleep, and bihemispheric sleep. With the available summary data, those transition processes are not separately identifiable. We therefore use the binary formulation to study the timing and fragmentation of microsleep bouts, while treating hemispheric asymmetry as an important omitted mechanism.

Sleep in Nesting Chinstrap Penguins: Data and Parameter Estimation

Our data on penguin sleep come from research on nesting chinstrap penguins.10 In that study, fourteen penguins on King George Island in Antarctica were observed during the nesting season for an average of twelve days using EEG recordings from both hemispheres, electromyogram (EMG) sensors, accelerometers, and GPS tracking. The study focuses on slow-wave sleep (SWS), documenting both bihemispheric slow-wave sleep (BSWS) and unihemispheric slow-wave sleep (USWS), a phenomenon common in birds in which one hemisphere sleeps while the other remains awake.

The data in Table 1 show that penguins at the nesting site experienced, on average, 621.32 sleep events per hour, with an average duration of 3.91 seconds per event. Despite the brevity of individual episodes, penguins accumulated approximately 14.9 hours of sleep per day, or roughly 11–12 hours per hemisphere. Our model does not explicitly represent unihemispheric sleep, so we focus on the combined SWS measure in which consecutive BSWS and USWS episodes are treated as a single event. This abstraction preserves the temporal fragmentation that is central to the observed sleep patterns, but it omits hemispheric asymmetry and therefore should not be interpreted as a full model of penguin sleep architecture. The model is instead designed to study the timing and fragmentation of microsleep bouts under ecological trade-offs.

We estimate a subset of model parameters to match observed features of the penguin data using a calibration approach. This approach uses a limited set of empirical moments (in this case, summary statistics10) to fit a dynamic model by choosing parameter values that minimize discrepancies between simulated and observed outcomes.

Our moment matching procedure produces 21 days of synthetic observations. This corresponds to 1,814,400 one-second observations. We use the Simulated Method of Moments (SMM). Given a vector of parameters, SMM runs the simulation N times and averages the outcomes to compute each moment for the error criterion. The final values are those that minimize the error criterion. The random shocks are generated by Python’s NumPy package. This package uses OS-provided entropy values for the seeds. We use the same pseudo-random shocks for each iteration over the parameters. Convergence was achieved when changes in both the objective function and the parameters were less than a given tolerance level.

SMM normally uses values of N of 2000 or more. This is computationally burdensome, but we can reduce this burden by getting good starting values. We do this by running the same moment matching techniques as before (with 5 parameters and 5 moments) using a single simulation. We start with a simulation of 2 days. We use these estimates as starting values for an estimate based on 10 days of data. We then proceed similarly to 15 and then 20 days. These last estimates are used as starting guesses for the SMM estimation. We also varied the starting values to avoid finding local optima. We proceed similarly with SMM first using N=100 and then raising this to a final value of 2000 simulations.

For all our minimizations we use the Python package scipy.optimize.minimize. This allows for several different minimization algorithms. We used the Nelder-Mead algorithm as it does not require analytical derivatives and is a fairly robust method. For our final estimation with 2000 simulations the run time was 96 minutes. We ran this estimation using an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X processor running at 3.4 GHz. It used all 32 virtual cores at close to 100% and utilized around 50 GB of memory, most of this storing the 2000-by-1,814,400 array of random shocks. We simulate over 21 days but discard the first day of every simulation to avoid dependence on starting values for H and A.

Estimated Parameters

Table 3 gives results to show how well the model predicts observed behavior. Simulated average hours of sleep per day over all microsleeps is 11.6, compared with 14.9 in the data, so the model underpredicts by 22.15%. The standard deviation of sleep hours per day is 0.00 compared with 1.13 in the data. Our estimate of γ is close to zero, which implies that variation in non-sleep opportunities is unimportant. Therefore, the model is essentially deterministic and each penguin behaves the same each day. It is worth noting that we are modeling the sleep behavior of a single penguin while the target estimate for sleep variability in the data is computed over 14 penguins. To be specific, the model does not account for variability in sleep behavior across different penguins.

On the other hand, the model closely matches the average number of sleep events per day, with 15,014 simulated events compared with 14,315 in the data, a difference of 4.88%. The maximum sleep duration is also close to the observed value, at 34 seconds compared with 34.22 seconds in the data. The model does less well in predicting the distribution of event lengths: 95.48% of simulated sleep episodes last less than 10 seconds, compared with 71.94% in the data, which is a difference of 32.72%. Average sleep duration was not one of the moments used in the estimation, but the model predicts 2.774 seconds compared with 3.91 seconds in the data.

This pattern of fit is consistent with the model’s simplified treatment of sleep as a binary state. This is a limitation of the model. The model matches the overall frequency of sleep events reasonably well, but it underpredicts total sleep time and places too much mass on very short episodes. In the model, sleep requires a full transition away from wakeful activity, whereas in the biological system penguins can accrue slow-wave sleep in one hemisphere while retaining partial vigilance. A model that allowed partial sleep states could potentially lower the effective opportunity cost of some sleep and might therefore generate more accumulated sleep time and a less compressed distribution of episode lengths.

This combination of parameter values is able to generate patterns similar to observed microsleep behavior. Because switching costs are negligible, the model permits rapid alternation between sleep and wake states. Under these conditions, the estimated model predicts that it is optimal to enter and exit sleep repeatedly in short bursts. Consequently, most simulated sleep episodes are only a few seconds long, with nearly all lasting less than 20 seconds.

The parameter for the curvature of sleep utility, κ , is set to one, implying that sleep does not have diminishing marginal utility. As noted above, we set it to one because it was consistently close to that value over a variety of runs. If κ falls, then we would observe diminishing marginal utility. As κ rises, AvgHrs falls, NumEvents increases, the MaxDur falls, and Per10 falls.

The estimated value for the parameter γ is close to zero. As it rises, non-sleep utility becomes more responsive to the stock of sleep. As a result, AvgHrs and StdHrs both rise. NumEvents and Per10 also rise but MaxDur falls. λ is also close to zero and an increase means more resistance to changing sleep states. This leads to higher AvgHrs and StdHrs. Not surprisingly, NumEvents falls, and both MaxDur and Per10 rise. These patterns can be related to observed behavioral differences in penguins. For example, non-incubating penguins sleep more and exhibit shorter sleep durations.10 In the model, this behavior is consistent with lower values of μ or η which reduce the productivity of waking activities and therefore increase the relative attractiveness of sleep. One model-based interpretation is that non-incubating penguins may face lower opportunity costs of sleep, which would be consistent with more sleep in shorter bouts. Similar effects arise from a reduction in environmental volatility (lower σ ), although such changes would likely affect all individuals rather than only non-incubating penguins.

Spatial variation within penguin colonies also provides a useful comparison. If predation risk were the dominant factor, one might expect penguins at the edges of colonies to exhibit more fragmented sleep due to increased vigilance demands, as observed in other species such as mallards.2 However, penguins at the colony edge experience more total sleep, as well as deeper and less fragmented sleep.10 No single parameter change in the model fully reproduces this pattern. Instead, these findings suggest that penguins balance multiple competing risks, including predation (higher at the colony edge) and social disturbance (higher in the colony interior). This pattern is consistent with the possibility that colony position reflects multiple competing pressures rather than a single dominant environmental factor.

We estimate the remaining parameters by targeting four of the five moments we chose for our own model. Since this model is non-stochastic, there is no variation from day to day and the standard deviation of hours slept per day is zero. We target the remaining moments using the same weights as before. We run a single simulation of two days for each iteration in our minimization process. We discard the first day to alleviate any “burn in” problems. As before, we use the SciPy minimization routine with the Nelder-Mead simplex search algorithm.

The results of this estimation are given in Table 6 and Table 7. The half-life parameters are much smaller than in our model. In addition, the value for the sleeping half-life is much smaller than the value for the waking half-life, while they were very similar in our model. Both models produce zero variation in hours slept per day (StdHrs). The two-process model also underpredicts mean hours per day (AvgHrs) and struggles to capture penguin microsleep behavior, predicting only 97 sleep events per day (NumEvents). Similarly, it overestimates the maximum duration (MaxDur) and underpredicts the number of sleep events lasting under 10 seconds (Per10). Overall, our model provides a better representation of the extreme microsleep behavior observed in the penguin data. We note that the version of the two-process model we employed is simple and that a version adapted to penguin sleep would likely perform better than what we present here. Discussion

The present study extends a dynamic model of sleep choice to nesting chinstrap penguins, a species characterized by extreme sleep fragmentation, frequent microsleeps, and both bihemispheric and unihemispheric slow-wave sleep. Using published penguin sleep data, the model reproduced several important qualitative features of the observed sleep pattern, including very brief sleep episodes, frequent transitions between sleep and wake, and a maximum sleep duration close to the observed value. The model also matched the total number of sleep events reasonably well. However, it underestimated total daily sleep time and produced a larger share of extremely short episodes than observed in the data. These findings indicate that the framework captures essential features of penguin microsleep organization while also simplifying aspects of the biological system that likely contribute to the full structure of sleep in this species.15

The comparison with the adapted two-process model also helps clarify the interpretation of the present framework. A simple threshold model with varying circadian bounds did not reproduce the observed microsleep pattern, especially the very high number of daily sleep events and the predominance of seconds-long bouts. This comparison does not rule out all simpler mechanisms; for example, a stochastic threshold model might reproduce some of the same moments. Rather, it shows that the observed pattern is not automatically generated by a standard homeostatic-circadian switching rule. The value of the present model is therefore not that it confirms a specific physiological mechanism, but that it provides one parsimonious behavioral interpretation in which low switching costs and ecological opportunity costs can generate microsleep fragmentation.

An important contribution of the study is to show that highly fragmented sleep can be understood within a framework that integrates biological sleep regulation with ecological trade-offs. In the model, sleep timing reflects the joint influence of homeostatic pressure, circadian alignment, stochastic waking opportunities, and switching costs. Within the fitted model, the estimated parameters imply very low switching costs and a strong role for the modeled sleep-utility/circadian-alignment term in generating rapid sleep-wake cycling. We interpret these parameter values as features of the fitted optimization framework, not as direct physiological evidence that circadian regulation dominates homeostatic sleep regulation in chinstrap penguins. Indeed, the model remains explicitly homeostatic: sleep pressure evolves across wake and sleep states, and the estimated short half-lives allow the homeostatic process to respond over the seconds-long time scale of microsleep bouts. This combination favors repeated brief sleep bouts rather than consolidated sleep and offers a parsimonious account of how microsleeps may emerge as an adaptive response in a colony environment that requires ongoing vigilance. More broadly, the results support the view that sleep in wild animals may be organized as a sequence of short, state-dependent decisions shaped by ecological context rather than as a fixed consolidated state.

The discrepancies between the simulated and observed data are also informative. Although the model captured the high frequency of sleep events reasonably well, it underestimated total sleep time by 22.15% and generated too many episodes lasting less than 10 seconds. One likely explanation is that the model does not explicitly represent unihemispheric slow-wave sleep, despite its prominence in the penguin system. Instead, the model relies on a combined slow-wave sleep measure that preserves temporal fragmentation but does not capture hemispheric asymmetry. As a result, it may not fully represent the way penguins accumulate sleep while retaining some degree of vigilance.16 In addition, ecological pressures such as predation risk, social disturbance, colony position, and incubation status are not modeled directly. These omissions likely limit the quantitative fit of the model even where its qualitative predictions remain strong. Further limitations of the study are a small empirical sample of animals, a reliance on aggregate published statistics, lack of individual-level validation, an absence of direct ecological variables, and somewhat limited robustness testing beyond the comparator model and sensitivity analyses.

The sensitivity analyses nevertheless reinforce the ecological interpretation of the model. Changes in the productivity, persistence, and volatility of waking opportunities altered sleep duration, episode length, and event frequency in biologically plausible ways, and these predictions align naturally with observed differences across penguin contexts, including contrasts between incubating and non-incubating birds and between colony edge and interior locations. At the same time, no single parameter change reproduced all observed spatial patterns, suggesting that penguin sleep is shaped by multiple competing ecological pressures rather than by one dominant factor. Even so, the present study provides a useful step toward linking sleep regulation with ecological decision-making and offers a novel framework for understanding fragmented sleep in wild animals.

Conclusion

This study shows that a dynamic model of sleep choice can capture important qualitative features of the highly fragmented sleep observed in nesting chinstrap penguins. By integrating sleep homeostasis, circadian regulation, and ecological opportunity costs, the model offers one possible explanation for how microsleep-like fragmentation may arise under competing demands for restoration and vigilance. The model provides a framework for understanding microsleep fragmentation, not a complete account of hemispheric sleep architecture. Although the model does not yet explicitly incorporate unihemispheric slow-wave sleep and therefore does not fully reproduce the observed quantity and structure of penguin sleep, it provides a useful framework for linking biological sleep regulation with ecological decision-making. These findings contribute to comparative sleep research by suggesting that adaptive sleep-allocation models may be useful for studying microsleep fragmentation in ecologically constrained settings, while further tests in additional species and with individual-level data are needed before broader generalizations can be made. Future work could include individual-level EEG data, explicit modeling of unihemispheric sleep, colony position, incubation status, predation risk, more direct measures of ecological conditions, and social disturbance. These avenues of research suggest new areas for new data gathering.
