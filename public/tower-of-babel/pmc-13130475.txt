Characterization of the BPC genes in alfalfa and functional verification of MsBPC10 in salt tolerance
Authors: Li Zhao, Xianyang Li, Xinyue Ma, Yuqi Zhang, Hao Liu, Fei He, Mingna Li, Xue Wang, Ruicai Long, Junmei Kang, Qingchuan Yang, Changhong Guo, Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil salinization severely restricts the growth and forage yield of Medicago sativa (alfalfa), a globally critical leguminous forage crop, yet the regulatory mechanisms governing its salt tolerance remain incompletely understood-especially the role of the BASIC PENTACYSTEINE (BPC) transcription factor family, which has been implicated in plant stress responses but never systematically investigated in alfalfa’s salt adaptation processes.ResultsThis study presents the genome-wide identification and analysis of the BPC transcription factor family in tetraploid alfalfa (Medicago sativa), revealing 18 MsBPC genes randomly distributed across 14 chromosomes. The encoded proteins are predominantly alkaline and phylogenetically classified into three distinct subgroups. Promoter cis-acting element analysis indicated the presence of multiple elements associated with hormone response, growth and development, and stress adaptation. Tissue-specific expression profiling demonstrated that 7 MsBPC genes were ubiquitously expressed in all six examined tissues, 4 were expressed in only a single tissue, and the remaining genes showed expression in 3-5 tissues, suggesting their potential involvement in regulating various growth and developmental processes in alfalfa. Transcriptome analysis under salt, drought, and cold stress conditions revealed that 12, 11, and 12 MsBPC genes were responsive, respectively. RT-qPCR validation confirmed the differential expression of selected MsBPC genes under salt and drought treatments, reinforcing their roles in abiotic stress responses. Subcellular localization analysis showed that MsBPC5 and MsBPC10 are nuclear-localized, consistent with in silico predictions. Heterologous expression of MsBPC10 in yeast enhanced salt tolerance, supporting its functional role in stress adaptation.ConclusionsOverall, this comprehensive study provides foundational insights into the MsBPC gene family and identifies promising candidate genes for enhancing stress resilience in alfalfa breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08143-3.

Introduction

In natural environments, plant growth and development are consistently challenged by both biotic (e.g., pathogen infection) and abiotic stresses (e.g., salinity, drought, extreme temperatures) [1]. To cope with these challenges, plants have evolved sophisticated molecular regulatory networks centered on transcription factor-mediated gene expression. Transcription factors recognize specific DNA sequences in promoter regions to activate or repress downstream genes, thereby modulating growth, development, and stress adaptation [2–4]. They act as critical molecular switches essential for plant survival and environmental adaptation [5]. Among them, the Basic Penta Cysteine (BPC) transcription factors constitute a plant-specific family characterized by a conserved C-terminal domain with five cysteine residues that form a zinc finger structure, conferring sequence-specific DNA-binding activity [6]– [7]. This structural feature is evolutionarily conserved across plant species, underscoring the functional importance of BPC proteins. To date, the BPC gene family has been identified in several plant species, with considerable variation in gene copy number: 6 in Camellia japonica [8], 7 in Arabidopsis thaliana [9], 4 BPC members in Oryza sativa [10], 4 BPC members in Cucumis sativus [11], 12 and 6 BPC family members in Brassica oleracea and Cucurbita pepo, respectively [8, 12]. This interspecific divergence offers valuable insights into the functional diversification and evolutionary adaptation of the BPC family.

BPC transcription factors play pivotal roles in regulating plant growth and development. For instance, Arabidopsis mutants with multiple BPC genes disruptions exhibit a spectrum of developmental defects, including dwarfism, small and curled leaves, early flowering, abnormal ovule development, and impaired fertility [6–14]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, BPCs activate the expression of INO (Inner No Outer), a gene involved in ovule development, by binding to GA/TC-rich motifs in its promoter. With the exception of AtBPC5, all other AtBPC genes are expressed in both vegetative and reproductive tissues [15]. In cucumber, CsBPC2 regulates seed germination via the abscisic acid signaling component ABI3 (ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3) [16]. In rice, OsGBP1 negatively regulates grain length and seedling growth, while OsGBP3 positively influences plant height and grain length [17]. Additionally, OsGBP1 binds to GAGA repeats in the promoters of OsLFL1, Ghd8, and Hd3a, thereby delaying flowering [9, 18]. These findings collectively underscore the importance of BPC TFs in plant developmental processes.

BPC TFs are also integral to plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. In Arabidopsis, the BPC1 BPC2 double mutant shows reduced salt tolerance and altered β-1,4-galactan accumulation. BPC1/BPC2 bind directly to the promoter of GALACTAN SYNTHASE 1 (GALS1), which contains GAGA repeats, to regulate β-1,4-galactosyltransferase synthesis. Under salt stress, decreased *BPC1/BPC2* expression leads to enhanced β-1,4-galactan synthesis, suppressed cellulose production, and compromised salt tolerance [10–20]. In Brassica napus, BnBPC6 regulates cuticular wax deposition by modulating fatty acid elongation, thereby enhancing resistance to stresses [2, 21]. In cucumber, overexpression of CsBPC2 in tobacco inhibits seed germination under salt, PEG, and ABA treatments, identifying it as a negative regulator of germination under osmotic stress [22]. In chickpea, RNA-seq analysis of plants infected with Ascochyta rabiei revealed significant enrichment of BPC transcription factors among targets of differentially expressed genes, suggesting a role in biotic stress response, though functional validation remains pending [23, 24] These studies highlight the multifaceted involvement of BPC TFs in stress response pathways.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a perennial legume known as the “King of Forages,” is valued globally for its high nutritional quality, stress tolerance, and ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen [11]. Despite its agronomic importance, the BPC gene family in tetraploid alfalfa remains largely uncharacterized. The complex tetraploid genome, with its high degree of sequence redundancy and multiple gene copies, complicates accurate gene family annotation. Moreover, as a stress-tolerant species, alfalfa likely employs BPC genes in unique regulatory mechanisms underlying salt and drought resilience. Elucidating these mechanisms is crucial for molecular breeding aimed at enhancing alfalfa stress tolerance.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive genome-wide identification of the BPC gene family in tetraploid alfalfa. We analyzed their sequence characteristics, conserved domains, phylogenetic relationships, and expression patterns under abiotic stresses. Using RT-qPCR, we validated the expression of selected MsBPC genes under salt and drought conditions. Our findings provide a foundation for understanding the biological functions of MsBPC genes and offer candidate genes for molecular breeding of stress-resistant alfalfa varieties.

Materials and methods

Identification and chromosomal distribution of the MsBPC gene family

Protein sequences of Arabidopsis BPC genes were retrieved from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The genomic data of alfalfa cultivar ‘Xinjiang Da Ye’ were obtained from the Alfalfa Genome Project (https://fgshare.com/projects/whole_genome_sequencing_and_assembly_of_Medicago_sativa/66380). The Medicago truncatula genome data were sourced from Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org/). The conserved domain file (PF06217) for the BPC family was downloaded from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/) [25].

The Arabidopsis BPC protein sequences were used as queries for BLASTP search against the alfalfa protein database using TBtools, with an E-value cutoff of 1e-10. Simultaneously, HMMER (hmmsearch) was employed to identify genes containing the typical BPC domain (PF06217). Candidate protein sequences were further verified using NCBI-CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [26].to confirm the presence of conserved domains and remove redundant sequences.

Analysis of physicochemical properties

Genomic sequences, chromosomal locations, and protein lengths of MsBPC genes were extracted from the GFF file of the ‘Xinjiang Da Ye’ genome using TBtools. Theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (MW) were predicted using the ExPASy ProtParam tool. Subcellular localization was predicted with WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). Genes were systematically renamed according to their chromosomal positions.

Phylogenetic and motif analysis

Protein sequences of MsBPCs were aligned using MEGA11 software. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with the neighbor-joining method, Poisson model, and 1000 bootstrap replicates [27]. Conserved motifs were identified using the MEME suite (http://meme-suite.org/), with the number of motifs set to 10 and width limited to 6–50 amino acids.

Gene duplication and collinearity analysis

Intra-species and inter-species collinearity analyses were performed using MCScanX [28], and results were visualized with TBtools [29]. The non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates and their ratio (Ka/Ks) were calculated using the Ka/Ks Calculator in TBtools to infer selection pressure.

Analysis of Cis-acting elements in promoters

The 2000 bp upstream sequences from the start codon of each MsBPC gene were extracted as promoter regions. Cis-acting elements were predicted using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [30]. and visualized with GSDS 2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/).

Transcript abundance analysis using RNA-seq

To elucidate the expression characteristics of alfalfa MsBPC genes in different tissues and their responses to abiotic stresses, RNA-seq data were obtained from the NCBI database. The acquired datasets comprised two categories: one covering six alfalfa tissues (roots, elongated stems, pre-elongated stems, leaves, flowers, and nodules), and the other including transcriptomic data of alfalfa under salt, drought, and cold stresses (SRP055547). Among these, the stress-responsive RNA-seq data corresponding to SRR7091780-SRR7091794 and SRR7160313-SRR7160357 were selected for subsequent analyses [25, 31].

RT-qPCR analysis of the MsBPC gene in response to drought and salt stress

Uniform ‘Xinjiang Da Ye’ alfalfa For salt stress, seedlings were treated with 250 mM NaCl; for drought stress, 400 mM mannitol was applied. Root tips were sampled at 0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-treatment, with three biological replicates per time point (each containing 5 seedlings). The alfalfa cultivar ‘Xinjiang Da Ye’ was provided by the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. seeds were surface-sterilized with 5% NaClO for 10 min, rinsed thoroughly, and germinated on moist filter paper in a growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark, 28 days). Salt stress (250 mM NaCl) was applied, with root tip samples collected at 0 (control), 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Drought stress was simulated with 400 mM mannitol, and root tips were sampled at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. For cold stress, seedlings were exposed to 4 °C; untreated seedlings (0 h at 4 °C) served as controls, and leaves were sampled at 2, 6, 24, and 48 h. All treatments included three biological replicates (five seedlings each), and control plants were grown under non-stress conditions.

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using the EasyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. Primers were designed using Primer 5.0, with the sequences shown in Table 1. The RT-qPCR experiments were conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan) and a 7500 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each sample was set up with three replicates, with the Medicago sativa L. actin gene (Actin2) used as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [32]. Graph Pad Prism 8 software was used for graphing.

MsBPC5 and MsBPC10 subcellular localization

The MsBPC5 and MsBPC10 CDS were cloned into the Super 1300-GFP vector. The resulting constructs and empty vector control were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Laser confocal microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) was used to detect fluorescence signals.

Heterologous expression in yeast

The MsBPC10 CDS was amplified and cloned into the pYES2-NTB vector. The recombinant plasmid and empty vector were transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain INVSc1. Positive transformants were selected on SD/-Ura medium. For salt tolerance assays, yeast cells were serially diluted (100 to 10− 2) and spotted onto SG/-Ura plates containing 0, 0.5, 1.0, or 1.3 M NaCl. Growth was monitored after 7 days at 30 °C.

Result

Identification of the BPC Gene Family Medicago sativa

A total of 18 MsBPC genes with complete conserved domains were identified in Medicago sativa, distributed across 14 chromosomes. (Fig. 1). Based on the position of the genes on the chromosomes, the BPC genes were named MsBPC1 to MsBPC18. The important characteristics of gene and protein sequences are shown in Table 1 and Table S1. Further analysis indicates significant differences in the protein sequence lengths, molecular weights, and theoretical isoelectric points of the MsBPC genes. The protein sequence length ranges from 195 amino acids (MsBPC10) to 339 amino acids (MsBPC1 to 4), with molecular weights ranging from 22.26 kDa (MsBPC10) to 37.78 kDa (MsBPC4), and theoretical isoelectric points ranging from 6.71 (MsBPC10) to 9.68 (MsBPC15 to 17). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that seven MsBPC proteins were localized to the nucleus, four to mitochondria, two to chloroplasts, two to the cell membrane, MsBPC3 to the cytoplasm, MsBPC11 to the vacuole, and MsBPC1 to the extracellular space.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the MsBPC gene family

To explore the evolutionary relationship between the BPC gene of Medicago sativa and the BPC gene of Arabidopsis, this study utilized MEGA 11 software to construct a phylogenetic tree for Arabidopsis thaliana and Medicago sativa, which included 18 MsBPC sequences from Medicago sativa and 7 AtBPC sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 2). Based on gene structure and conserved motif analyses, the 25 BPC proteins were divided into three groups: Group I contains 9 members (6 MsBPC and 3 AtBPC), Group II contains 11 members (8 MsBPC and 3 AtBPC), and Group III contains 5 members (4 MsBPC and 1 AtBPC).

Analysis of the Gene Structure and Conserved Motifs of MsBPC gene family

To further understand the structure of the MsBPC gene, analyses were conducted using TBtools software and the MEME online platform. The gene structure analysis indicates that the MsBPC gene is relatively short, with most members containing two exons and one intron. The coding region features a GAGA repeat sequence specifically recognized by BPC transcription factors. Ten conserved motifs were predicted via the MEME website, ranging in length from 6 to 50 amino acids. The results were visualized using TBtools software (Fig. 3). Figure 4 presents detailed information about the ten conserved motifs. The findings show that the MsBPC family members in alfalfa encompass a varying number of conserved motifs, ranging from 5 to 9, with motif 4 present in all MsBPC members, while motif 2 was not identified in MsBPC10, suggesting that these two motifs are highly conserved within MsBPC proteins. Annotation of motifs 2 and 4 using NCBI’s CD search tool revealed that both cover the BPC gene-specific motif GAGA-bind. Variations in motifs among different subgroups highlight the diversity of functions in MsBPC proteins. Similarities in motif composition within the same subgroup indicate that these proteins have related functions.

Gene duplication events and chromosomal collinearity analysis of MsBPC gene family

To understand the expansion mechanism of the MsBPC family in alfalfa, the interspecies evolutionary relationship of alfalfa was analyzed by using MCScanX. The results showed that there were 22 segmental duplications events and 1 tandem repeat event between these members (Fig. 5 and Table S2). For example, MsBPC7 and MsBPC12 are located on chr4.1 and chr4.3, respectively. Segmental duplications events occur extensively in MsBPC genes, These results suggest that segmental duplication has played a major role in the expansion of the MsBPC gene family. In addition, The Ka/Ks of MsBPC8 and MsBPC11 were greater than 0.5 in the collinearity gene pairs, indicating that they underwent positive selection during evolution, while the Ka/Ks of the other gene pairs were less than 0.5 and underwent purification selection during evolution (Table 2).

To explore the evolutionary relationship of BPC gene family in different species, a collinearity plot of Medicago sativa with Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max were constructed (Fig. 6). The results showed that 4 MsBPC genes in alfalfa had a collinearity relationship with Arabidopsis thaliana, and a total of 8 homologous gene pairs were identified. 7 MsBPC genes were collinearity with Medicago truncatula, and 7 homologous gene pairs were identified. 15 MsBPC genes were collinearity with Glycine max, and 26 homologous gene pairs were identified. It is worth noting that the degree of collinearity between BPC genes between Glycine max, and Medicago sativa is significantly higher than that of Arabidopsis thaliana, which may be due to the fact that soybean and alfalfa belong to the same leguminous plant, are more closely related, and the evolutionary pattern of gene families is more similar.

Analysis of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of MsBPC genes family

To explore the potential function of BPC gene in alfalfa, the cis-acting element of MsBPC gene was predicted using the PlantCARE database (Fig. 7 and Table S3). The results showed that a total of 13 cis-acting elements related to hormone response, stress response, and growth and development were screened. These elements include ABRE elements related to abscisic acid response, P-box, GARE-motif and TATC-box elements related to gibberellin response, TCA-element related to salicylic acid response, TGACG-motif and CGTCA-motif elements related to methyl jasmonate response, LTR element related to low temperature response, and MBSI element related to the regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis. The cis-acting elements of each gene are unevenly distributed, which may be the reason why each gene has a different function.

Expression of MsBPC genes under abiotic stress response

To explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of the MsBPC genes under different stresses, the RNA-seq data of alfalfa plants under the abiotic stresses (salt, drought, cold) were analyzed the results showed that 12 MsBPCs could respond to salt stress. 7 MsBPCs could respond to drought stress. 12 MsBPC genes could respond to cold stress (Fig. 9 and Table S5). These results suggest that BPC transcription factors play an important role in alfalfa response to abiotic stresses.

Combined with transcriptome data and expression pattern analysis across different tissues, we identified Two tissue-specific MsBPC genes in Medicago sativa. 2 genes were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. As shown in Fig. 10, the expression of MsBPC10 decreased first, then increased, and finally decreased under drought stress. The expression of MsBPC5 decreased first and then increased, but was always lower than that of the control group. Under salt stress, the expression of MsBPC5 decreased first and then increased. The expression of MsBPC10 first increased, then decreased, and then increased, and was always higher than that of the control group. RT-qPCR results were consistent with RNA-seq data.

MsBPC10 improve salt tolerance in yeast

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis conducted after treatment with 200 mM NaCl demonstrated that the transcriptional level of MsBPC10 initially increased, subsequently decreased, and then increased again relative to the control group (CK). These results suggest that the protein encoded by MsBPC10 may play a pivotal role in the salt stress response of Medicago sativa.

To elucidate the effects of MsBPC10 on yeast growth and salt tolerance, the pYES2-BPC10-NTB construct was introduced into Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells. Under standard growth conditions, no significant differences in growth were observed between the transformed yeast strains and the corresponding empty vector control strains. However, when subjected to 1.3 M NaCl stress, the transformed yeast cells exhibited significant tolerance to high salinity. Notably, this enhanced salt tolerance was particularly evident at the 10⁻2 dilution level (Fig. 12).

Discussion

BPC transcription factors are an important gene family in plants. They’re known to regulate plant growth, development, and abiotic stress responses, as shown in many studies. This family has been identified in several plants, like Arabidopsis thaliana [9], Oryza sativa [10], Cucumis sativus [11], and Brassica oleracea [12], with 7, 4, 4, and 12 members found respectively. But in tetraploid Medicago sativa, this family hasn’t been reported yet. In this study, focusing on tetraploid Medicago sativa, we found 18 MsBPC genes. This number is much higher than the BPC members reported in Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Cucumis sativus, and Brassica oleracea This might be because Medicago sativa is a tetraploid with many homologous genes in its genome. multiple homologous genes can perform redundant functions. When certain genes are impaired due to mutations or environmental stresses, other family members can exert compensatory effects, thereby enhancing the tolerance of Medicago sativa to abiotic stresses (e.g., drought and salinity) and biotic stresses (e.g., pathogens and insect pests). This functional redundancy is of vital importance for the genetic improvement of stress resistance in this species.

We carried out a systematic analysis of the amplification and evolution of the MsBPC gene family in Medicago sativa. Compared with other species, the MsBPC family of alfalfa underwent significant gene family expansion, and fragment replication and tandem replication were one of the driving forces of gene family expansion [33]– [34]. In this study, we detected 22 segmental and 1 tandem duplication events in the MsBPC gene family suggesting a segmental duplication dominated evolutionary pattern. Tissue-specific expression analysis is an important method for predicting the biological function of genes in plant growth and development. Based on RNA-seq data, we systematically analyzed the expression patterns of 18 MsBPC genes in 6 different tissues. The results showed that 7 genes were expressed in all 6 tissues, 4 in just one tissue, and the remaining 7 in 3–5 tissues. Taken together, the diverse expression patterns of the MsBPC gene family indicate that its members may regulate distinct stages of Medicago sativa growth and development through a combination of conserved and tissue-specific functions. The functional redundancy of constitutively expressed genes can effectively buffer against genetic or environmental perturbations, thereby ensuring the stability of core biological processes. In contrast, tissue-specific and partially overlapping expression patterns enable the precise regulation of tissue-specific traits, a property that is crucial for the environmental adaptability and agronomic performance of Medicago sativa.

Cis-acting elements in promoter regions determine the spatiotemporal specificity of gene expression. They interact with corresponding transcription factors in different tissues and cells, enabling precise gene expression in specific tissues, developmental stages, and environmental conditions [35–37]. The cis-acting element distribution in alfalfa MsBPC promoters reveals functionally differentiated regulation, clarifying the family’s roles in alfalfa growth, development, and stress adaptation. Stress-responsive elements (ABRE, LTR, MBS, TC-rich repeats) dominate the promoters, with MsBPC10, MsBPC13, and MsBPC15 having high copy numbers-confirming the family as a core component of alfalfa’s abiotic stress response network. Differential element enrichment among paralogs (e.g., MsBPC10 has more TC-rich repeats than MsBPC1) indicates functional specialization in stress signaling. Diverse hormone-responsive elements (GARE-motif/P-box, AuxRR-core, TGACG/CGTCA-motifs, TCA-element/SARE) are scattered across promoters. For example, MsBPC5/MsBPC9 are enriched in GARE-motif (gibberellin response), and MsBPC12 harbors both ABRE and TGACG-motif, meaning MsBPC genes link hormonal signaling and stress responses to balance alfalfa growth and tolerance. Subsets of promoters contain MBSI (flavonoid biosynthesis) and O2-site (zein metabolism), with MsBPC3/MsBPC7 having moderate MBSI levels, implying roles in secondary metabolism and seed development. Ubiquitous AT-rich elements ensure basal MsBPC expression and dynamic modulation by stress/hormone signals.

In summary, the MsBPC cis-element profile forms a multifaceted regulatory system, with members mediating stress responses, hormonal crosstalk, and developmental/metabolic processes. Element distribution differences among paralogs reflect functional diversification, an adaptation to alfalfa’s tetraploid genome. Combining cis-element data with expression and functional validation will further elucidate MsBPC-mediated regulation of alfalfa phenotypic plasticity.

Studies have shown that BPC genes play an important role in regulating plant growth, development, and responses to abiotic stress. Their functions are both conserved and diverse across different species. In Oryza sativa, OsGBP1 negatively regulates grain length and seedling development by regulating the expression of grain shape - related genes. Overexpressing OsGBP1 inhibits seedling growth, causes leaf yellowing, reduces biomass, and delays flowering, while gene silencing or mutation promotes plant growth [10]. In Malus domestica, overexpression of MdBPC2 decreases auxin content and inhibits plant growth and root development, while exogenous application of auxin can restore normal growth [35]. Overexpression of CsBPC2 in tobacco significantly inhibited seed germination under salt, polyethylene glycol, and abscisic acid stress [11]. In Brassica oleracea, BcBPC9 enhances antioxidant enzyme gene expression, increasing cadmium stress tolerance in yeast and tobacco [36]. In addition, BPC transcription factors regulate Arabidopsis ovule development by controlling the STK gene. Mutations cause dwarfism, leaf curling, ovule development abnormalities, and reduced lateral roots [37]. These studies show that while BPC genes have different specific functions in different species, they mainly focus on regulating growth, development, and responses to abiotic stress.

Plant growth and development are orchestrated by complex regulatory networks that integrate external environmental cues with internal physiological signals, in which transcription factors serve as central mediators [38, 39]. The BPC family represents a plant-specific group of transcription factors that have been well documented to regulate various aspects of growth, development, and responses to abiotic stresses in Arabidopsis thaliana and multiple crop species.

Plant hormones play pivotal roles in modulating seed dormancy and germination [40, 41]. Among them, brassinosteroids (BRs) [42], cytokinins (CKs) [43], gibberellins (GAs) [44, 45], and ethylene [46]. act as positive regulators of germination, whereas abscisic acid (ABA) functions as a negative regulator [47].

Notably, BPC transcription factors modulate hormone signaling through distinct molecular mechanisms. In the Arabidopsis bpc1-1 bpc2 bpc3 triple mutant, expression levels of ISOPENTENYLTRANSFERASE 7 (IPT7) and ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR 7 (ARR7), two key genes involved in CK biosynthesis and signaling, are significantly upregulated [48, 49], resulting in elevated cytokinin levels within the inflorescence meristem [50]. Moreover, the bpc1-1 bpc2 bpc4 bpc6 quadruple mutant exhibits reduced sensitivity to ethylene, absence of apical hooks, and delayed senescence [36], underscoring the role of BPCs in ethylene-mediated developmental processes.

In addition to their involvement in development, BPCs play well-characterized roles in stress adaptation. For instance, CsBPC2 positively regulates tolerance to salt and osmotic stresses [11]. the NtBPC family has diversified into functionally specialized subfamilies: NtBPC1 and NtBPC2 specifically regulate responses to low temperature, whereas NtBPC3 and NtBPC4 mediate salt stress responses [51]. Collectively, BPC transcription factors function as essential integrators of developmental and environmental signals, maintaining the dynamic equilibrium between growth, development, and stress resilience. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms by which BPCs coordinate hormone signaling pathways and stress-responsive gene networks remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

This study systematically identified a total of 18 MsBPC genes, which are distributed across 14 chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis divided them into three evolutionary groups. Each group has similar gene structures and motif compositions. Segmental duplication was found to be the main driver of MsBPC gene family expansion. Expression profiling revealed that MsBPC genes exhibited different expression patterns across tissues: 7 were expressed in all 6 tissues, 4 were only expressed in a single tissue, and the remaining genes were expressed in 3–5 tissues, suggesting that MsBPC genes may be involved in the regulation of different growth and developmental stages of alfalfa. Stress response analysis revealed that 12, 11, and 12 MsBPC genes were significantly responsive to salt, drought, and cold stress, respectively. Promoter cis- element analysis revealed the potential regulatory mechanisms of MsBPC genes in hormone and stress responses. Collinearity analysis showed a higher collinearity between Medicago sativa and Glycine max BPC genes than Arabidopsis thaliana, BPC family evolution is relatively conserved in leguminous plants. indicating a more similar evolutionary pattern of the BPC gene family in leguminous plants. Subcellular localization assays showed MsBPC5 and MsBPC10 to be nuclear-localized, consistent with predictions. Yeast heterologous expression verified the salt tolerance function of MsBPC10.The findings of this study provide theoretical support for elucidating MsBPC gene functions and offer important candidate genes for stress-resistant breeding in Medicago sativa.
