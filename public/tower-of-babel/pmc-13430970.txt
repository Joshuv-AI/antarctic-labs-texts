Ending the HIV Epidemic in the United States Among Men Who Have Sex With Men: A Modeling Analysis
Authors: Justine A Scott, Hailey Spaeth, Kyu-Young Kevin Chi, Pamela Pei, Nattanicha Wattananimitgul, Anne M Neilan, David P Holland, Emily P Hyle, Kenneth A Freedberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Ending the HIV Epidemic in the US (EHE) initiative, launched by the White House in 2019, aims to reduce HIV transmissions by 75% by 2025 and 90% by 2030 as compared with total annual transmissions in 2017. We sought to determine levels of improvement in testing, care, and prevention needed to meet the EHE goals for men who have sex with men (MSM) in the United States.MethodsWe modeled HIV transmissions based on current care (Status quo) and strategies improving HIV testing, treatment, and/or preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Status quo included testing frequency (every 6.8 years), antiretroviral therapy linkage (77%), care engagement (78%), viral suppression (81%), and PrEP coverage (41% for MSM at increased HIV infection risk, 7% for lower risk). The primary outcome was percentage reduction in HIV transmissions. We examined data uncertainty in sensitivity analysis.Results Status quo would reduce HIV transmissions by 32% by 2035. The most influential individual intervention would be improved engagement in HIV care (67% reduction in transmissions with 98% engagement). Combined testing and care interventions at high levels, with expanded PrEP for MSM at increased risk, could achieve 89% reduction by 2035.ConclusionsOnly with major improvements in HIV testing, care, and prevention will EHE goals be met by 2036.Keywords: HIV policy, HIV prevention, HIV treatment, public health

HIV-1 (HIV) transmissions in the United States have declined substantially, from a peak of 130 400 in 1985 to 31 800 in 2022. However, approximately 5000 people still die from HIV-attributable causes annually [1–3]. The Ending the HIV Epidemic in the US (EHE) initiative set the goal of reducing HIV transmissions 75% by 2025 and 90% by 2030 from a 2017 baseline [4]. To support EHE, Congress approved $593 million in 2025 for the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and other agencies to support HIV prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and cluster and outbreak response for priority regions [5].

We have the tools to decrease HIV transmissions, but they are not reaching everyone who needs them. As of 2022, 87% of people with HIV (PWH) in the United States were diagnosed, 66% received care, and only 57% had viral suppression [3, 6]. While men who have sex with men (MSM) constitute 1.4% of the US population, they represented 71% of people diagnosed with HIV in 2022 [7]. The National HIV/AIDS Strategy accordingly identifies MSM as a priority population in reaching EHE goals [4].

Prior modeling focused on EHE has evaluated optimal allocation of prevention resources, intervention-specific strategies such as HIV testing and preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP), and local epidemics [8–11]. These studies provide insights into specific interventions and settings, but the national scale of improvement required to approach EHE targets has not been clearly characterized. Our objective was to quantify the magnitude and combination of improvements in testing, care, and prevention needed to meet EHE goals of reducing HIV transmissions by 90% for MSM in the United States.

METHODS

Analytic Overview

The CEPAC model (Cost-Effectiveness of Preventing AIDS Complications) is a widely published and validated microsimulation model of HIV disease, diagnosis, and treatment. We used the CEPAC model, as well as a novel extension of its approach to modeling transmissions, to project HIV transmissions among MSM ≥15 years in the United States from 2022 to 2045 for 11 strategies [12, 13]. The simulation begins in 2014, with calibration and validation of Status quo in the years 2016 to 2022 to CDC data (Supplementary Figure 2) [3]. All strategies matched Status quo projections from 2022 to 2026, after which interventions were implemented at a linear rate over 5 years, reaching full implementation by 2031 and continuing through 2045. For all strategies, we simulated subgroups defined by HIV care engagement: undiagnosed HIV, disengaged from care, and virally suppressed. Outcomes include annual HIV transmissions, percentage reduction in annual transmissions as compared with the 2017 baseline identified in the EHE targets, and the year in which EHE targets would be met.

Strategies

We modeled 11 strategies based on key pillars of EHE diagnose, treat, and prevent [4]. These strategies included Status quo, 8 individual strategies, and 2 combination strategies. For each strategy except Status quo, we modeled 2 levels of improvement: low and high. Each strategy's low and high values span a range of ambitious improvements to approach EHE targets. Strategies include the following:

Status quo: 2022 levels of testing, prevention, and treatment

HIV testing (MSM-HT): more frequent testing for MSM at higher risk of transmitting HIV (MSM-HT)

HIV testing (all): more frequent testing for all MSM

Linkage: improved linkage to care after routine testing

Engagement: improved engagement in care among PWH

Viral suppression: improved viral suppression at 12 months of antiretroviral therapy (ART)

PrEP coverage (MSM-HT): increased PrEP coverage among MSM-HT

PrEP coverage (all): increased PrEP coverage among all MSM

PrEP effectiveness: increasing PrEP effectiveness

Combined testing/care–Status quo PrEP: improvements across the HIV testing and HIV care continuum without improvements to PrEP coverage or effectiveness

Combined testing/care–PrEP (MSM-HT): improvements across the testing and HIV care continuum and improved PrEP coverage and effectiveness for MSM-HT

CEPAC Model

Overview

CEPAC is a state-transition microsimulation model that characterizes HIV disease progression as a sequence of monthly transitions between health states defined by HIV RNA, CD4 count, and opportunistic infections. ART can reduce HIV RNA and increase CD4 counts [14, 15]. Individuals face mortality risks from HIV and non-HIV causes each month. A random number generator and set of probabilities determine the clinical pathway that each simulated patient follows. Characteristics of simulated people (eg, age, CD4 count, adherence to ART) are drawn from distributions based on population-specific data. A full description of the model—including definitions of health states and structure, as well as details of inputs, outputs, and programming notes, is available on the CEPAC website [16]. The model code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/MGH-MPEC/CEPAC) [12, 17–19].

Transmissions

We employed a bidirectionally coupled modeling framework that integrates CEPAC, an individual-level microsimulation, with a population-level, dynamic susceptible-infected transmission model (Supplementary Figure 1). The 2 models are linked through a monthly exchange of information. Specifically, CEPAC informs the transmission model by providing time-varying estimates of the force of infection (ie, total number of transmissions each month), reflecting the evolving distribution of viral load and treatment status among PWH. In turn, the transmission model generates the number of incident HIV infections at each time step, and these individuals with newly acquired HIV are introduced into CEPAC, allowing the simulated PWH population to grow over time.

This approach creates a closed feedback loop in which transmission dynamics determine the inflow of individuals newly acquiring HIV, while the clinical and treatment trajectories of individuals within the microsimulation influence subsequent transmission risk. We calibrated projected transmissions to CDC HIV data for 2016 to 2021 and validated to the most recently reported CDC transmissions data from 2022 (Supplementary Methods) [3, 20].

Risk Stratification

In a simplification of the numerous factors that influence HIV transmission, we modeled 2 risk strata: MSM-HT and MSM-LT (ie, MSM at lower risk of transmitting HIV) [21]. Risk stratum sizes were estimated by survey data regarding whether MSM engage in condomless anal intercourse, as a proxy for higher risk of transmission [22–24]. With the transmission calibration and these population sizes, MSM-HT have a 4.8-fold increased risk of transmission as compared with MSM-LT (Table 1). The risk of acquiring HIV is reduced for MSM taking PrEP, and the risk of transmitting HIV to others is eliminated for MSM with HIV who are virally suppressed [37, 38].

HIV Diagnosis and Treatment

Modeled individuals are subject to monthly probabilities of HIV testing and linkage to care. People may also be diagnosed and linked to care following presentation to care with an opportunistic infection. Once linked to care, all PWH begin a second-generation integrase strand transferase inhibitor (INSTI)–based regimen representing guideline-concordant ART [39]. Individuals with high adherence have a higher likelihood of viral suppression and engagement in care [40]. Individuals in care face monthly probabilities of being disengaged from care, and PWH who leave care experience monthly probabilities of returning. While disengaged, individuals no longer attend clinic visits or receive ART.

Preexposure Prophylaxis

PrEP coverage is the proportion of MSM without HIV who are prescribed daily oral tenofovir-emtricitabine–based PrEP. PrEP effectiveness, capturing PrEP adherence and medication efficacy, informs reductions in transmissions during PrEP. MSM taking PrEP undergo HIV testing every 3 months [41].

Model Inputs

Cohort Characteristics

We defined cohort characteristics for the prevalent cohort in 2014 using CDC surveillance reports, including mean age and CD4 count (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1) [20, 29]. For the estimated 4 503 080 MSM in the United States, initial HIV prevalence is 13% (n = 601 930) [20, 25]. MSM with prevalent HIV at model start reflect the 2014 care continuum: 109 673 (18%) are undiagnosed or unlinked to care, 123 334 (20%) are disengaged from care, and 368 923 (61%) are virally suppressed [29, 42]. Among MSM with HIV, 71% (n = 427 370) are MSM-HT; among MSM without HIV, 14% (n = 531 534) are MSM-HT [22, 26, 43].

HIV Diagnosis and Treatment

HIV testing frequency for Status quo was calibrated to mean time until HIV diagnosis for MSM with newly acquired HIV (4.4 years), estimated from the reported median with a right-skewed distribution of time from infection to diagnosis [34]. For MSM taking PrEP, we assumed guideline-concordant testing frequency for people receiving oral PrEP (every 3 months) [41]. For testing, testing frequency ranges from 6 to 12 months. Linkage to care after diagnosis through routine testing is 77% for Status quo [42], increasing to 90% to 98% in the linkage strategy. For MSM taking PrEP, we assumed 100% linkage after HIV diagnosis. For Status quo, among MSM with >95% adherence to INSTI-based ART, viral suppression at 12 months is 96%, while average suppression across all levels of adherence is 81%. These inputs were derived from trial data and applied across adherence strata [15, 32, 33, 44]. For viral suppression, average suppression increases to 93% to 98% for all MSM on ART [32, 33]. For Status quo, monthly probabilities of disengagement from and return to care were calibrated to 78% in care at 24 months [45]. We calibrated probabilities of disengagement to reach 90% to 98% in care at 2 years with the engagement strategy.

Preexposure Prophylaxis

We estimated that PrEP coverage is 41% for MSM-HT and 7% for MSM-LT by year-end 2025 with Status quo (Table 1) [3, 23–25]. For PrEP coverage, we modeled 75%–90% coverage by 2031 for MSM-HT and 15%–20% for MSM-LT, after which PrEP coverage remains constant. PrEP effectiveness for Status quo is 76%, which is the risk reduction observed among people with adherence to 2 doses per week in pharmacokinetic analyses of the iPrEX trial [46]. For intervention strategies, PrEP effectiveness increases to 90% to 96% [46].

Transmissions

We assigned a per–sex act HIV transmission probability of 0.75% using CDC data regarding transmission between MSM [21, 30]. We then used this probability to calculate monthly transmission risk for MSM with HIV stratified by viral load, with a monthly average risk of 0.420% among MSM-HT and 0.088% among MSM-LT. Derivation details are in the Supplementary Methods.

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted 1-way sensitivity analyses for any strategy combination projected to attain EHE goals, assessing the impact of changing (1) the level of improvement for single interventions, (2) the distribution of interventions between MSM-HT and MSM-LT, (3) the Status quo PrEP effectiveness, and (4) the rollout time. For these analyses, we modified levels of improvement for each intervention one at a time, keeping all other interventions at the levels included in the “high” scenario. For Status quo projections, we examined the impact of variation in the proportion of MSM with HIV considered MSM-HT, a key parameter informing model calibration. In multivariate sensitivity analyses, we examined how increasing viral suppression among all MSM with HIV would decrease the impact and need for PrEP coverage for MSM-HT (Supplementary Methods).

RESULTS

Base Case

Status quo would result in a 32% reduction in annual HIV transmissions among MSM by 2035 as compared with 2017 (from n = 24 500 to n = 16 740) and a 41% reduction by 2045 (n = 14 560; Table 2, Figure 1). Results for single-intervention strategies would vary widely. Linkage and testing would have the lowest impact, with a 33% decrease in transmissions with improved linkage and 39% with improved testing by 2035 in the “high” scenario as compared with 2017 baseline. Engagement would be most influential: achieving 90% engagement in care (78% in Status quo) would result in a 57% reduction in transmissions by 2035 and a 68% reduction by 2045 as compared with 2017 baseline (Table 2, Supplementary Figure 3C). At 98% engagement, transmissions would be reduced by 67% by 2035 and 79% by 2045 as compared with baseline. PrEP coverage would also be impactful: while holding PrEP coverage for MSM-LT constant (7%), increasing PrEP coverage for MSM-HT to 90% (41% in Status quo) would result in a 54% decrease in transmissions by 2035 and 65% decrease by 2045 as compared with baseline (Table 2, Supplementary Figure 3E). Adding 20% coverage among MSM-LT would further decrease transmissions to 58% by 2035 and 69% by 2045.

Sensitivity Analysis

In 1-way sensitivity analyses, we varied inputs for the strategy of combined testing/care–PrEP (MSM-HT), the only strategy projected to meet EHE reduction targets within the modeled time frame. Engagement in care would be the most influential component of this combination strategy, as it would be in the individual strategies. Even with high levels of all other interventions, with 85% engagement (7% increase from the current Status quo), transmission reduction would be 74% by 2035 as compared with 2017 baseline (Figure 1). With a very high level of engagement (98%) in the combined testing/care–PrEP (MSM-HT) strategy, viral suppression would have a larger impact on the results. The level of PrEP coverage for MSM-HT would also be influential: with 50% coverage, the reduction in transmissions by 2035 with the combination strategy would be 84%. We also examined higher PrEP coverage for MSM-LT, as the base case strategy includes improvement only for MSM-HT. Adding 60% PrEP coverage for MSM-LT to the combination strategy would nearly eliminate transmissions by 2035 (94% reduction).

For Status quo projections, transmission reductions in 2035 would range from 11% to 37%, as the proportion of MSM with HIV classified as MSM-HT was varied from 30% to 90%. Across these strategies, the relative risk of transmission for MSM-HT vs MSM-LT ranges from 2.56 to 5.69 (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figure 4). The base case value of 71% yields a Status quo projection that aligns with the trend in transmissions reported by the CDC from 2016 to 2022. When PrEP effectiveness was varied from 44% to 96%, projected reductions in transmissions in 2035 for Status quo would range from 25% to 35%.

The time required to fully implement interventions would affect how soon transmission targets could be met. In the base case, interventions are rolled out over 5 years. In a sensitivity analysis with immediate implementation by 2026, the combination strategy would meet EHE targets by 2030 and would reach 91% reduction in transmissions by 2035 as compared with 2017 baseline (Figure 2). If interventions are rolled out over 10 years, transmissions would be reduced by 86% in 2035.

DISCUSSION

We projected HIV transmissions within the US MSM population from 2022 through 2045 to identify the magnitude of improvements in testing, HIV care, and prevention needed to reach EHE goals nationally. These strategies represent benchmarks for improvement required rather than the most likely epidemic trajectory. Reaching EHE goals would require a highly ambitious scale of improvement in HIV testing and care, combined with PrEP for MSM at highest risk of acquiring HIV.

Continuing the Status quo would result in a 32% reduction in HIV transmissions by 2035 and a 41% reduction by 2045. Even with substantial improvements in the care continuum (HIV testing every 6 months, 98% linkage, 98% engagement in care, 98% viral suppression), transmissions would fall just shy of the 90% EHE target (82% reduction by 2035).

Adding improvements in PrEP coverage (90%) and effectiveness (96%) for MSM-HT, in addition to improved testing (every 6 months) and engagement in care (98%) for all MSM with HIV, would achieve 89% reduction by 2035, meeting the 90% EHE target by 2036. Improving PrEP coverage among MSM-LT would further reduce transmissions, although cost and feasibility constraints may limit broader implementation [3].

The differential impact of individual interventions reflects the distribution of viremia across the HIV care continuum. In the United States, an estimated 62% of transmissions in 2016 and 54% in 2019 originated from individuals diagnosed but not virally suppressed [47, 48]. As of 2022, only 13% of MSM with HIV were undiagnosed, so testing and linkage improvements would affect a small group. In contrast, approximately 32% of diagnosed MSM with HIV remained unsuppressed, representing the larger group driving transmissions [6].

Sensitivity analyses of the combined testing/care–PrEP (MSM-HT) strategy, the only strategy projected to meet EHE goals within the next 20 years, again highlighted the importance of engagement in care combined with improved viral suppression through adherence and/or treatment modality. We found that viral suppression for MSM with HIV would be more influential in reducing transmissions than PrEP coverage for MSM at highest risk, although improving PrEP coverage for MSM at highest risk showed greater impact at lower estimates of viral suppression among MSM with HIV. The upper range of PrEP effectiveness (90%–96%) modeled also captures the potential impact of long-acting PrEP formulations, which may achieve higher effectiveness through improved adherence [49, 50]. Varying Status quo PrEP effectiveness across a plausible range (44%–96%) would result in 2035 transmission reductions of 25% to 35% as compared with 2017.

These results are consistent with studies highlighting the importance of engagement in HIV care for EHE goals and showing that reducing transmissions will require more effective interventions and increased funding. Sansom et al modeled the optimal allocation of HIV resources in the United States, finding that the most efficient allocations focus on early diagnosis and adherence to treatment [8]. Jacobson et al also examined optimal funding allocation, finding that major increases in resources for HIV prevention, testing, and treatment could lead to an 80% decrease in HIV transmissions over 10 years [10]. A modeling analysis set in 6 US cities demonstrated that high levels of viral suppression would reduce transmissions by up to 75% in some cities [51]. Our findings reinforce the crucial importance of interventions that increase engagement in care and extend prior work by estimating national benchmark requirements across HIV prevention, testing, and care needed to approach EHE targets over the next 2 decades, necessary for implementation planning. The delivery strategies and costs required to achieve improvements at this scale will be context dependent and need further investigation.

Our findings show that approaching EHE targets would require unprecedented improvements across the HIV prevention and care continuum. The most influential levers identified are programmatic, and prior evidence suggests that care coordination programs focused on engagement are highly cost-effective [52]. However, HIV funding in the United States is not secure. A federal spending bill proposed ending all EHE funding, and 2025 federal budget proposals included $880 billion in cuts to Medicaid, the largest HIV care payer in the United States [53–56]. Federal funding freezes also threatened Ryan White Care Act programs, which provide care, medications, and essential services for PWH [57]. These funding threats work against the large-scale programmatic improvements that are needed to achieve the ambitious EHE goals, which makes reaching them even less likely.

This analysis has several limitations. We modeled sexual transmission among MSM, the key population most affected by HIV in the United States. Reaching EHE goals across the United States will also require interventions for other key populations, including people who use drugs and people at increased risk of acquiring HIV via heterosexual contact. We also made simplifying assumptions about transmission risk heterogeneity, using mass-action principles with binary risk stratification, without detailed modeling of sexual networks. This approach is appropriate given that the interventions modeled target care continuum dynamics rather than network-specific dynamics such as partner reduction. Nevertheless, this may influence the impact of interventions if a small subset of individuals at higher risk of transmission who are less likely to be reached by interventions contributes disproportionately to new transmissions. The model represents interventions using population-average parameters: individuals who engage late with care are captured through delayed diagnoses, generally at the time of an opportunistic infection, and improvements in interventions may not proportionally reach this group. We estimated national benchmark requirements and did not explicitly model regional or racial differences in HIV transmissions or access to care. These results should not be interpreted as uniform across states or subpopulations, given heterogeneity in HIV transmissions, care continuum, and implementation capacity across the United States. Last, we modeled a second-generation INSTI-based regimen consistent with 2026 US guidelines. Long-acting injectable ART formulations are expanding in their use and may improve adherence and viral suppression; this potential impact is captured in viral suppression intervention strategies. As injectable ART becomes more widely used, achieving EHE targets may become more plausible.

This modeling analysis demonstrates that it would be extremely difficult to meet EHE targets to reduce HIV transmissions by 90% among MSM in the United States. This would be possible only with substantial and rapid improvements across all components of the HIV care continuum and PrEP. The most influential levers identified, engagement in care and viral suppression, are programmatic challenges that can be addressed through sustained investment, underscoring the urgency of that investment. Nevertheless, even improvements short of these benchmarks would yield meaningful reductions in HIV transmissions, morbidity, and mortality.
