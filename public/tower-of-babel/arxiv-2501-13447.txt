Intersection density and visibility for Boolean models in hyperbolic space
Tillmann Bühler, Daniel Hug, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
For Poisson particle processes in hyperbolic space we introduce and study concepts analogous to the intersection density and the mean visible volume, which were originally considered in the analysis of Boolean models in Euclidean space. In particular, we determine a necessary and sufficient condition for the finiteness of the mean visible volume of a Boolean model in terms of the intensity and the mean surface area of the typical grain. Keywords . Boolean model, hyperbolic geometry, hyperbolic space, intersection density, intrinsic volume, mean visible volume, Poisson point process, stochastic geometry. MSC . 52A22, 52A55, 60D05

Introduction

The Boolean model is a foundational concept in stochastic geometry that describes random spatial structures formed by the union of random shapes, called grains, which are placed at the points of a random point process. More precisely, the construction of a Boolean model usually begins with a point process, typically a Poisson point process, which defines the random locations in space where the grains are centered. Each grain, characterized by its shape, size, and orientation, is independently and randomly distributed according to a specified probability law on the space of compact subsets of the ambient space. By taking the union of all grains, the model generates a random set in space, capturing a rich variety of spatial patterns and structures. The Boolean model is particularly valued for its mathematical tractability and flexibility. Many key properties such as the probability of complete coverage or the mean volume fraction can be derived analytically. These features make the Boolean model a powerful tool for both theoretical studies and practical applications. We refer the reader to
[
6
,
14
,
24
]
, for example.

The present paper investigates the intersection density and the mean visible volume in a Boolean model. In this context, the intersection density refers to the number of intersection points of the boundaries of any
d
distinct particles with a given set, where
d
denotes the dimension of the space in which the Boolean model is constructed. On the other hand, the mean visible volume is defined as the expected volume of the region that remains uncovered by the random grains and is visible from a fixed observation point, such as the origin, conditioned on the event that this point is not covered. Both quantities have been extensively studied for Boolean models in a
d
-dimensional
Euclidean
space; see
[
24
, Section 4.6]
and
[
27
, Sections 4–6]
. In contrast, we focus on the intersection density and the mean visible volume of Boolean models in a
d
-dimensional
hyperbolic
space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
of constant curvature
-1
; see Figure
1
for a simulation. Also, here the Boolean model can be derived from a Poisson point process in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
with intensity
\gamma>0
, which is invariant under all isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, but the probabilistic construction required for placing the grains at the points of the Poisson process is more involved. For this reason, alternative approaches have been proposed in
[
13
, Sections 2.2 and 2.3]
and will be further developed here. Boolean models in hyperbolic space were studied in
[
1
,
13
,
18
,
25
,
26
]
and give rise to a number of new phenomena compared to their Euclidean counterparts. In addition, the analysis of models in hyperbolic stochastic geometry often requires the development of new geometric tools, which are of independent interest. We will see examples for both effects also in the present text.

Figure 1:
Simulation of a Boolean model in the hyperbolic plane whose grains are discs of random radius uniformly distributed in

. The saturation of a point represents the number of grains by which it is covered.

It is known from
[
18
]
that the visibility properties of hyperbolic Boolean models exhibit a remarkable threshold phenomenon, which cannot be observed in Euclidean space. To be precise, the paper
[
18
]
deals with Boolean models in the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space (and in fact more general spaces) whose grains are hyperbolic balls of fixed radius
R>0
. In this specific set-up, if we condition on the event that a fixed point
\mathsf{p}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
is not covered by the grains of the Boolean model, then there exists a critical intensity parameter
\gamma_{v}=\gamma_{v}(R,d)>0
such that:

•

for
\gamma<\gamma_{v}
the grains are sparse enough so that, with positive probability, one can find infinite geodesic rays emanating from
\mathsf{p}
that remain unobstructed,

•

for
\gamma>\gamma_{v}
the grains become dense enough to almost surely obstruct all geodesics starting at
\mathsf{p}
.

The value for
\gamma_{v}
is known and will be discussed in Remark
6.5
below. More refined properties of the length of the geodesic rays starting at
\mathsf{p}
are studied in
[
26
]
(in dimension
d=2
).

We build upon these findings by exploring the
mean visible volume
around a given point
\mathsf{p}
. Our primary result in this direction provides an explicit formula for the mean visible volume in terms of the intensity
\gamma
and the mean surface content
v_{d-1}
of the so-called typical grain, which is applicable to general space dimensions
d
and general random hyperbolically convex grain shapes. A significant aspect of our result is the identification of a precise condition under which the mean visible volume remains finite. Specifically, we prove that the mean visible volume is finite if and only if the product
\gamma v_{d-1}
exceeds the threshold value
(d-1)\frac{d\kappa_{d}}{\kappa_{d-1}}
, where
\kappa_{j}
denotes the volume of the
j
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball. This condition reveals the interplay between the intensity of the underlying Poisson point process and the geometric properties of the grains that must be satisfied for a finite mean visible volume. In particular, in the special case where all grains are balls of radius
R>0
, our results uncover a remarkable phenomenon: the mean visible volume around
\mathsf{p}
is finite if and only if the intensity
\gamma
surpasses the critical threshold
\gamma_{v}
, where
\gamma_{v}=\gamma_{v}(R,d)
coincides with the threshold mentioned earlier in the context of the existence of infinite geodesic rays starting from
\mathsf{p}
. In addition to this, we present an explicit formula for the intersection density of a
d
-dimensional hyperbolic Boolean model and establish a connection between this density and the mean visible volume. To further contextualize our findings, we draw a comparison with analogous results obtained for the zero cell of a stationary Poisson hyperplane tessellation in hyperbolic space.

Preliminaries

In this paper, we work in a
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
of dimension
d\geq 2
and with constant curvature
\kappa=-1
. We denote by
d_{h}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
the hyperbolic distance, by
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
the space of nonempty compact subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
and by
{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
the space of compact and (geodesically) convex subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
(including the empty set). The Lie group of isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is denoted by
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
. It carries an invariant Haar measure
\lambda
, which is unique up to normalization. We normalize
\lambda
so that

\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho x\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\lambda({\rm d}\varrho)=\mathcal{H}^{d}(\,\cdot\,),

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{H}^{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure constructed with respect to
d_{h}
. More generally, for
s\geq 0
we denote by
\mathcal{H}^{s}
the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure with respect to the hyperbolic distance
d_{h}
. Instead of
\mathcal{H}^{d}
, we often use
V_{d}
to denote the volume functional on compact convex sets in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. For
A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
and
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, we set
d_{h}(A,x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\{d_{h}(a,x):a\in A\}
. Moreover, we set
d_{h}(\varnothing,x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\infty
.

For
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
and
r\geq 0
, let
{\mathbb{B}}(A,r)=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}\colon d_{h}(A,x)\leq r\}

be the parallel set of
A
at distance
r
. For
j\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
and
r\geq 0
, let

l_{d,j}(r)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\binom{d-1}{j}\int_{0}^{r}\cosh^{j}(t)\sinh^{d-1-j}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

We note that the functions
l_{d,0},\ldots,l_{d,d-1}
are linearly independent in the real vector space of continuous functions on
[0,\infty)
.
The Steiner formula (
2.2
) involves the existence of (uniquely determined) continuous, isometry invariant, and additive functionals
V_{0},\ldots,V_{d-1}
on
{\mathcal{K}}^{d}

such that

\displaystyle V_{d}({\mathbb{B}}(A,r))=V_{d}(A)+\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}V_{j}(A)\cdot l_{d,j}(r),\quad r\geq 0;

(2.2)

a more general (local) version of (
2.2
) is provided in
[
16
, Theorem 2.7]
(note that the result in
[
16
]
is stated in
\mathbb{H}^{d+1}
and the coefficient functions are normalized differently). The normalization in (
2.2
) is chosen as in
[
13
, Equation (3.3)]
and consistent with the normalization in
[
23
, Equation (4.12)]
in the Euclidean framework. It is the natural choice of normalization, if
V_{j}(A)
is considered as a curvature integral involving a normalized elementary symmetric function (of order
d-1-j
) of principal curvatures of
\partial A
; see
[
22
, Equation (1.11)]
for the case of parallel sets of smooth hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space. Note that
V_{j}(\varnothing)=0
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
.

If
A
is convex and has nonempty interior, then

V_{d-1}(A)=\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial A).

(2.3)

We note that in the Euclidean case the corresponding relation involves an additional factor
1/2
on the right-hand side, due to the different normalization of
V_{d-1}
. However, if
A\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is compact, convex, and has dimension at most
d-1
, then
V_{d-1}(A)=2\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial A)
. If the dimension of
A
is at most
d-2
, then both sides of (
2.3
) vanish. Moreover,
V_{0}(A)
is the (total) Gauss curvature integral of
A
(which follows as a special case of
[
16
, Theorem 2.7 and (2.7)]
). In Euclidean space the normalization of
V_{0}
is chosen such that
V_{0}
is equal to the Euler characteristic
\chi
, whereas in hyperbolic space the relation between
V_{0}
and the Euler characteristic
\chi
is non-trivial and will be described below.

A subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{d}
is said to be
k
-rectifiable, for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
, if
k=0
and
A
is finite or if
k\geq 1
and
A
is the image of a bounded subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
under a Lipschitz map. We say that
A
is Hausdorff
k
-rectifiable if
\mathcal{H}^{k}(A)<\infty
and there exist
k
-rectifiable subsets
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots
of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{H}^{k}\left(A\setminus\bigcup_{i\geq 1}B_{i}\right)=0
. For these definitions, see also
[
7
, Section 3.2.14]
and
[
4
, Definitions 5.12 and 5.13]
. In particular, every bounded subset of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is
d
-rectifiable.

3
Hyperbolic intrinsic volumes

It is a well-known fact that the Euclidean intrinsic volumes are intrinsic in the sense that they do not depend on the dimension of the ambient space.
That is, if
1\leq k\leq d
,
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
is a compact convex set and
\iota\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is an isometric embedding of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(such as the one mapping
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
to
(x,0,\ldots,0)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
), then

V^{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}_{j}(\iota(A))=\begin{cases}V^{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}_{j}(A),&j=0,\ldots,k,\\
0,&j=k+1,\ldots,d,\end{cases}

where
V^{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}_{j}
and
V^{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}_{j}
denote the
j
-th intrinsic volume of a set measured in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, respectively.
For this reason, they are called
intrinsic
volumes, and the ambient space is usually omitted in the notation (for details, see
[
23
, Equations (4.1) and (4.22)]
or
[
15
, pp. 106-108]
).

With a different normalization, the same property holds for the hyperbolic intrinsic volumes as well.
Define for
j=0,\ldots,d-1
the functions
\ell_{d,j}
by

\ell_{d,j}(r)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\omega_{d-j}\int_{0}^{r}\cosh^{j}(t)\sinh^{d-1-j}(t)\,{\rm d}t,\qquad r\geq 0,

where
\omega_{i}=2\pi^{i/2}/\Gamma(i/2)=i\kappa_{i}
denotes the surface area of the
i
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
put
V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{d}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{H}^{d}(A)
and define the intrinsic volumes
V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{0},\ldots,V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{d-1}
as the coefficients in the Steiner formula

\mathcal{H}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}(A,r))=\mathcal{H}^{d}(A)+\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{j}(A)\cdot\ell_{d,j}(r),\qquad r\geq 0.

(3.1)

Note that (
3.1
) remains true for
d=1
with
V_{0}^{\mathbb{H}^{1}}(A)=1
if
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{1}
satisfies
A\neq\varnothing
.

In the following, a
k
-dimensional totally geodesic subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is called a
k
-plane for
1\leq k\leq d-1
.
We denote the space of all
k
-planes by
A_{h}(d,k)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
1\leq k\leq d-1
and identify
\mathbb{H}^{k}
with an arbitrary
L_{k}\in A_{h}(d,k)
via an isometry
\iota\colon\mathbb{H}^{k}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
such that
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{k})=L_{k}
.
If
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{k}
, then

V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{j}(\iota(A))=\begin{cases}V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{j}(A),&j=0,\ldots,k,\\
0,&j\geq k+1.\end{cases}

Remark 3.2
.

For
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
and
d\geq 1
, a comparison of (
2.2
) and (
3.1
) yields that

\omega_{d-j}V_{j}^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(A)=\binom{d-1}{j}V_{j}(A),\quad j=0,\ldots,d-1,

and
V_{d}^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(A)=V_{d}(A)
.
If
I\subset{\mathcal{K}}^{d}
is a geodesic segment, then Proposition
3.1
implies that

\displaystyle V_{0}^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(I)

\displaystyle=1,\quad

\displaystyle V_{1}^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}(I)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{1}(I),

and therefore

\displaystyle V_{0}(I)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d},\quad

\displaystyle V_{1}(I)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{d-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}(I).

In addition to the Steiner formula, the proof of Proposition
3.1
requires two integration formulas in hyperbolic space: an orthogonal disintegration formula that can be seen as a hyperbolic analogue of Fubini’s theorem and the hyperbolic polar integration formula.

Lemma 3.3
.

(a)

If
1\leq k\leq d-1
and
L_{k}\in A_{h}(d,k)
is a fixed
k
-plane, then

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}f(x)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\rm d}x)=\int_{L_{k}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}f(z)\,\cosh^{k}(d_{h}(z,L_{k}))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y),

for any measurable function
f\colon\mathbb{H}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
,
where
L_{k}^{\perp}(y)
is the unique
(d-k)
-plane orthogonal to
L_{k}
that intersects
L_{k}
in
y
and
d_{h}(z,L_{k})=d_{h}(z,y)
stands for the geodesic distance of
z
to
L_{k}
.

(b)

If
d\geq 1
and
f\colon\mathbb{H}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is measurable, then

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}f(x)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\rm d}x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}_{\mathsf{p}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\exp_{\mathsf{p}}(tu))\sinh^{d-1}(t)\,{\rm d}t\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{p}}^{d-1}({\rm d}u),

where
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}_{\mathsf{p}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{p}}^{d-1}
refer to the unit sphere and the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in the tangent space of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
at
\mathsf{p}
, respectively.

Proof.

To derive part (a) we use the method established in Section 5 of
[
3
]
and translate the problem into a problem in Euclidean space. For that purpose we work with the Beltrami–Klein (projective) model of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, which is given by the open
d
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball
\mathsf{B}^{d}
. Applying
[
3
, Lemma 5.1]
with
k=d
there gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}f(x)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\rm d}x)=\int_{\mathsf{B}^{d}}f(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{d}({\rm d}x),

where
\|\,\cdot\,\|
stands for the Euclidean norm and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{d}
for the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since we are working with the projective model of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, a
k
-dimensional totally geodesic subspace
L_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{d}
is the intersection
\widetilde{L}_{k}\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}
of a
k
-dimensional affine subspace
\widetilde{L}_{k}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\mathsf{B}^{d}
. Since each
k
-dimensional totally geodesic subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
can be mapped to any other by an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\widetilde{L}_{k}
passes through the center of
\mathsf{B}^{d}
. Then, using Fubini’s theorem and denoting by
\widetilde{L}_{k}^{\perp}
the
(d-k)
-dimensional Euclidean orthogonal complement of
\widetilde{L}_{k}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{B}^{d}}f(x)(1-\|x\|^{2})^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{d}({\rm d}x)=\int_{\widetilde{L}_{k}\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}}\int_{(\widetilde{L}_{k}^{\perp}+y)\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}}f(z)(1-\|z\|^{2})^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{k}({\rm d}y).

To the inner integral we now apply
[
3
, Lemma 5.1]
again to see that the last expression is the same as

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{L}_{k}\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}f(z)(1-\|z\|^{2})^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}(1-\|z\|^{2})^{\frac{d-k+1}{2}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{L}_{k}\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}f(z)(1-\|z\|^{2})^{-\frac{k}{2}}(1-\|y\|^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{k}({\rm d}y),

where we also used the fact that
\widetilde{L}_{k}+y
has Euclidean distance
\|y\|
from the center of
\mathsf{B}^{d}
; see also the argument in the second paragraph of the proof of
[
3
, Lemma 5.3]
concerning orthogonality of subspaces in the Beltrami–Klein model. Writing
\bullet
for the Euclidean scalar product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and using
[
19
, Theorem 6.1.1]
, we obtain

\cosh^{2}d_{h}(z,L_{k})=\cosh^{2}d_{h}(z,y)=\frac{(1-z\bullet y)^{2}}{(1-\|z\|^{2})(1-\|y\|^{2})}=\frac{1-\|y\|^{2}}{1-\|z\|^{2}}.

Plugging this into the last integral expression and applying once more
[
3
, Lemma 5.1]
to the outer integral, we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}f(x)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{L}_{k}\cap\mathsf{B}^{d}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}f(z)\,\cosh^{k}(d_{h}(z,L_{k}))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,(1-\|y\|^{2})^{-\frac{k+1}{2}}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{k}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}f(z)\,\cosh^{k}(d_{h}(z,L_{k}))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y),

which concludes the proof of part (a). Part (b) is a consequence of Equations (3.16) and (3.22) in
[
5
]
.

Remark 3.4
.

The special case
k=1
of Lemma
3.3
can be found as Lemma 2.1 in
[
20
]
. It was derived there using different methods such as the coarea formula.

We also need the following identity.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
0\leq j<k\leq d-1
, then

\int_{0}^{r}\ell_{d,k}\left(\arcosh\left(\frac{\cosh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\right)\right)\ell_{k,j}^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s=\ell_{d,j}(r),\qquad r\geq 0.

(3.2)

Proof.

We start by collecting some auxiliary facts.
The beta function, which is defined by
B(a,b):=\int_{0}^{1}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,{\rm d}t

for
a,b>0
, can be expressed in terms of the gamma function as
B(a,b)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)
, hence

\frac{1}{2}B\left(\frac{j}{2},\frac{k}{2}\right)=\frac{\omega_{j+k}}{\omega_{j}\omega_{k}},

(3.3)

for
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We will also make use of the integral identity

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}}{(1+ct)^{a+b}}\,{\rm d}t=(1+c)^{-a}B(a,b),

(3.4)

which holds for
a,b>0
and
c>-1
.
This can be seen by rewriting the integral as

\int_{0}^{1}(1+c)^{-a}\left(\frac{(1+c)t}{1+ct}\right)^{a-1}\left(\frac{1-t}{1+ct}\right)^{b-1}\frac{1+c}{(1+ct)^{2}}\,{\rm d}t,

and substituting
y=(1+c)t/(1+ct)
.

We now turn to the proof of the statement of the lemma. Let
r\geq 0
.
Both sides of (
3.2
) are zero for
r=0
, so it suffices to show that their
derivatives agree.
Differentiating the left-hand side (note that the integrand is continuous in
r
and
s

and becomes zero at
s=r
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\ell_{d,k}^{\prime}\left(\arcosh\left(\frac{\cosh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\right)\right)\partial_{r}\arcosh\left(\frac{\cosh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\right)\ell_{k,j}^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\omega_{d-k}\left(\frac{\cosh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\right)^{k}\left(\left(\frac{\cosh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\right)^{2}-1\right)^{\frac{d-1-k}{2}}\frac{\cosh(s)}{(\cosh^{2}(r)-\cosh^{2}(s))^{\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{\sinh(r)}{\cosh(s)}\ell_{k,j}^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-k}\omega_{k-j}\cosh^{k}(r)\sinh(r)\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\sinh^{k-1-j}(s)}{\cosh^{d-1-j}(s)}(\sinh^{2}(r)-\sinh^{2}(s))^{\frac{d-2-k}{2}}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-k}\omega_{k-j}\cosh^{k}(r)\sinh^{2}(r)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(x\sinh(r))^{k-1-j}}{(1+x^{2}\sinh^{2}(r))^{\frac{d-j}{2}}}(\sinh^{2}(r)-x^{2}\sinh^{2}(r))^{\frac{d-2-k}{2}}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-k}\omega_{k-j}\cosh^{k}(r)\sinh^{d-1-j}(r)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{k-1-j}}{(1+x^{2}\sinh^{2}(r))^{\frac{d-j}{2}}}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-2-k}{2}}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-k}\omega_{k-j}\cosh^{k}(r)\sinh^{d-1-j}(r)\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{\frac{k-j}{2}-1}(1-t)^{\frac{d-k}{2}-1}}{(1+t\sinh^{2}(r))^{\frac{d-j}{2}}}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-k}\omega_{k-j}\cosh^{k}(r)\sinh^{d-1-j}(r)\frac{1}{(1+\sinh^{2}(r))^{\frac{k-j}{2}}}\frac{1}{2}B\left(\frac{k-j}{2},\frac{d-k}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d-j}\cosh^{j}(r)\sinh^{d-1-j}(r),

where we used the substitution
\sinh(s)=x\sinh(r)
in the fourth line,
the substitution
t=x^{2}
in the sixth line, (
3.4
) in the seventh line,
and (
3.3
) in the last line.
We also used the identity
\cosh^{2}(u)-\sinh^{2}(u)=1
for
u\in\mathbb{R}
repeatedly. Differentiating the right-hand side gives

\ell_{d,j}^{\prime}(r)=\omega_{d-j}\cosh^{j}(r)\sinh^{d-1-j}(r),

as well. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now turn to the proof of the main statement of this section.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

Let
1\leq k\leq d-1
.
We fix some nonempty
A\in{\mathcal{K}}^{k}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, let
\pi_{L_{k}}(x)
be the orthogonal projection of
x
onto
L_{k}
.
By the Pythagorean theorem of hyperbolic geometry (compare, e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 3.5.3]
), it holds that

\cosh(d_{h}(x,\iota(A)))=\cosh(d(x,\pi_{L_{k}}(x)))\cdot\cosh(\pi_{L_{k}}(x),\iota(A)).

(3.5)

Since
\pi_{L_{k}}(z)=y
for all
z\in L_{k}^{\perp}(y)
, it follows using (
3.5
), Lemma
3.3
(a) and (with
L_{k}^{\perp}(y)\cong\mathbb{H}^{d-k}
) and Lemma
3.3
(b) that for
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}(\iota(A),r))

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\{d_{h}(x,\iota(A))\leq r\}\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{L_{k}}\int_{L_{k}^{\perp}(y)}\cosh^{k}(d_{h}(y,z))\mathbbm{1}\{\cosh(d_{h}(z,y))\cdot\cosh(d_{h}(y,\iota(A)))\leq\cosh(r)\}\,{\mathcal{H}}^{d-k}({\rm d}z)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{L_{k}}\omega_{d-k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-k-1}(t)\cosh^{k}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{\cosh(t)\cdot\cosh(d_{h}(y,\iota(A)))\leq\cosh(r)\}\,{\rm d}t\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{k}}\omega_{d-k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-k-1}(t)\cosh^{k}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{\cosh(t)\cdot\cosh(d_{h}(y,A))\leq\cosh(r)\}\,{\rm d}t\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y),

where we used in the last step the fact that
\iota\colon\mathbb{H}^{k}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
is an isometry with
\iota(\mathbb{H}^{k})=L_{k}
.
We write the integrand as
f(d_{h}(y,A))
, where

f(s)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\omega_{d-k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-k-1}(t)\cosh^{k}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{\cosh(t)\cdot\cosh(s)\leq\cosh(r)\}\,{\rm d}t,\qquad s\geq 0.

Note that
f(s)=\ell_{d,k}(\arcosh\left({\cosh(r)}/{\cosh(s)}\right))
for
s\leq r
and
f(s)=0
otherwise. Consequently,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}(\iota(A),r))

\displaystyle=\int_{A}f(0)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)+\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,r)\setminus A}f(d_{h}(y,A))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y).

The first integral is equal to

\int_{A}f(0)\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)={\mathcal{H}}^{k}(A)\ell_{d,k}(r)=V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{k}(A)\ell_{d,k}(r).

To evaluate the second integral, define
g(s)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\mathbb{B}}(A,s))
,
s\geq 0
, where
{\mathbb{B}}(A,s)
is the parallel set of
A
in
\mathbb{H}^{k}
.
By the Steiner formula,
g
is continuously differentiable with derivative given by

g^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\ell_{k,j}^{\prime}(s)V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{j}(A).

We claim that

\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,r)\setminus A}f(d_{h}(y,A))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)=\int_{0}^{r}f(s)g^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s.

To see this, take an arbitrary partition
s_{0}=0,\ldots,s_{n}=r
of
[0,r]
and pick
\xi_{i}\in(s_{i-1},s_{i})
, so that
g(s_{i})-g(s_{i-1})=g^{\prime}(\xi_{i})(s_{i}-s_{i-1})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
We can now write

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,r)\setminus A}f(d_{h}(y,A))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i})\setminus{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i-1})}f(d_{h}(y,A))\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\xi_{i})(g(s_{i})-g(s_{i-1}))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i})\setminus{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i-1})}f(d_{h}(y,A))-f(\xi_{i})\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\xi_{i})g^{\prime}(\xi_{i})(s_{i}-s_{i-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i})\setminus{\mathbb{B}}(A,s_{i-1})}f(d_{h}(y,A))-f(\xi_{i})\,{\mathcal{H}}^{k}({\rm d}y).

As the mesh
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|s_{i}-s_{i-1}|
of the partition goes to zero, the first sum converges to
\int_{0}^{r}f(s)g^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s
and the second sum converges to zero, since
f
is uniformly continuous on
[0,r]
.
Plugging the above representation for
g^{\prime}
into
\int_{0}^{r}f(s)g^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s
, we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{j}(A)\cdot\int_{0}^{r}f(s)\ell_{k,j}^{\prime}(s)\,{\rm d}s=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{j}(A)\cdot\ell_{d,j}(r),

where we used Lemma
3.5
.
In summary, we obtain

{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}(\iota(A),r))=\sum_{j=0}^{k}V^{\mathbb{H}^{k}}_{j}(A)\cdot\ell_{d,j}(r).

On the other hand, applying the Steiner formula directly yields

{\mathcal{H}}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}(\iota(A),r))={\mathcal{H}}^{d}(\iota(A))+\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}V^{\mathbb{H}^{d}}_{j}(\iota(A))\cdot\ell_{d,j}(r).

The claim now follows from the preceding two identities, since the functions
\ell_{d,0},\ldots,\ell_{d,d-1}
are linearly independent.
∎

4
Stationary Poisson particle processes in hyperbolic space

Let
\eta
be a stationary Poisson particle process on
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
. In our set-up stationarity refers to the invariance of the distribution of
\eta
under isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. We refer to
[
13
]
for an introduction to stationary Poisson particle processes in hyperbolic space and to stationary Boolean models derived from them. This means that the intensity measure
\Lambda:=\mathbf{E}\eta
of
\eta
is locally finite, diffuse by
[
13
, Lemma 2.4]
, and hence
\eta
is a simple particle process.

Let
c:{\mathcal{C}}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
be an isometry covariant center function, where the condition of isometry covariance means that
c(\varrho A)=\varrho c(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
and
\varrho\in{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
. By
[
13
, Lemma 2.4 and Theorem 2.3]
, the intensity

\gamma:=\mathbf{E}\sum_{K\in\eta}{\bf 1}\{c(K)\in B\},\qquad B\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}\text{ measurable with }{\mathcal{H}}^{d}(B)=1,

is independent of the center function and finite. In addition, we suppose that
\gamma
is also positive. Moreover, it is shown in
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
that there exists a unique probability measure

\mathbf{Q}
on
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
which is concentrated
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathsf{p}}^{d}=\{A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{d}:c(A)=\mathsf{p}\}
, invariant under isometries fixing
\mathsf{p}
, and satisfies

\Lambda(\,\cdot\,)=\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\lambda({\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K).

(4.1)

The probability measure
\mathbf{Q}
is called the distribution of the typical grain of
\eta
.

We complement the introduction of stationary Poisson particle processes and Boolean models in
[
13
, Section 2]
by explaining how the marking of a stationary Poisson point process in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
can be used for an alternative approach. Let
\xi
be a stationary Poisson point process in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
with intensity measure
\gamma\cdot\mathcal{H}^{d}
, where
\gamma>0
. We adopt the notation from
[
13
, Section 2]
and consider for each
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
the set
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},x):=\{\varrho\in{\mathcal{I}_{d}}:\varrho\mathsf{p}=x\}
and the probability kernel
\kappa
from
\mathbb{H}^{d}
to
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
introduced in
[
13
, Equation (2.6)]
; see also Equation (
4.3
) below.
For
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, the probability measure
\kappa(x,\,\cdot\,)
on
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
is concentrated on
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},x)
, and the kernel
\kappa
disintegrates the Haar measure
\lambda
on
{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
so that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(x,{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}({\rm d}x)=\lambda(\,\cdot\,).

(4.2)

We now define a kernel
\alpha
from
\mathbb{H}^{d}
to
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
(see
[
17
, p. 40]
) by

\alpha(x,\,\cdot\,):=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(x,{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K),\quad x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}.

Since
\mathbf{Q}
is concentrated on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathsf{p}}^{d}
and the center function is isometry covariant, the probability measure
\alpha(x,\cdot)
is concentrated on
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{d}=\{A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{d}:c(A)=x\}
.
Let
\Psi
be an
\alpha
-marking of
\xi
, as defined in
[
17
, Definition 5.3]
. Then
\Psi
is a (marked) Poisson process on
\mathbb{H}^{d}\times{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
with intensity measure

(\mathbf{E}\Psi)(\,\cdot\,)=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{(x,\varrho K)\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(x,{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}({\rm d}x);

see the marking theorem
[
17
, Theorem 5.6]
. The mapping lemma
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
for Poisson processes shows that
\widetilde{\eta}=\Psi(\mathbb{H}^{d}\times\,\cdot\,)
is a Poisson process on
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
with intensity measure

\displaystyle(\mathbf{E}\widetilde{\eta})(\,\cdot\,)

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(x,{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}({\rm d}x)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\lambda({\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)=\Lambda(\,\cdot\,),

where (
4.2
) was used in the last step. Thus,
\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{\eta}
can be obtained by (position dependent) marking with the kernel
\alpha
from a stationary Poisson point process in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.

There is yet another representation of a stationary Poisson particle process, which avoids the kernel
\kappa
. To present it, for each
x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
we select an isometry
\varrho_{x}\in{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},x)
in such a way that the mapping
x\mapsto\varrho_{x}
is measurable. Such an assignment exists as explained on page 121 of
[
19
]
. Then, the measure
\kappa(x,\,\cdot\,)
can be represented as

\kappa(x,\,\cdot\,)=\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{p})}{\bf 1}\{\varrho_{x}\varrho\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(\mathsf{p},{\rm d}\varrho).

(4.3)

Moreover, the kernel
\alpha
can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle\alpha(x,\,\cdot\,)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(x,{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{p})}{\bf 1}\{\varrho_{x}\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\kappa(\mathsf{p},{\rm d}\varrho)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{p})}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho_{x}\varrho K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)\,\kappa(\mathsf{p},{\rm d}\varrho)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho_{x}K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K),

where the last inequality follows since
\mathbf{Q}
is invariant under isometries fixing
\mathsf{p}
and
\kappa(\mathsf{p},\,\cdot\,)
is a probability measure.
Similarly, the representation of the intensity measure
\mathbf{E}\Psi
simplifies to

(\mathbf{E}\Psi)(\,\cdot\,)=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}{\bf 1}\{(x,\varrho_{x}K)\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}({\rm d}x)

and therefore

(\mathbf{E}\eta)(\,\cdot\,)=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}}{\bf 1}\{\varrho_{x}K\in\,\cdot\,\}\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}K)\,\mathcal{H}^{d}({\rm d}x).

5
Intersection density of a hyperbolic Poisson particle process

We adopt the set-up of the previous section and let
\eta
be a stationary Poisson particle process on
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
with positive intensity and intensity measure given by (
4.1
). We assume that the probability measure
\mathbf{Q}
, the distribution of the typical grain of
\eta
, is concentrated on the subset of
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}}
which only consists of sets whose boundaries are
(d-1)
-rectifiable. We recall that a subset
G\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is called
m
-rectifiable, for some
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, if
G
is the Lipschitz image of some bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In particular, each compact
m
-dimensional
C^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is
m
-rectifiable. That this class of sets is a measurable subset of
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
follows from the results of
[
28
, Section 2]
as explained in the remark on page 184 of
[
29
]
.

We write
s_{d}(\eta,B)
for the number of intersection points of the boundaries of any
d
distinct particles of
\eta
within a bounded measurable set
B\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
, that is,

s_{d}(\eta,B):=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})\in\eta^{d}_{\neq}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(B\cap\partial K_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\partial K_{d}),

where
\eta^{d}_{\neq}
denotes the
d
-th factorial measure of
\eta
(see
[
17
, Section 4.2]
). In Theorem
5.1
we show that
s_{d}(\eta,B)<\infty
holds almost surely.
Since
\eta
is stationary,
\mathbf{E}[s_{d}(\eta,\,\cdot\,)]
defines an isometry invariant measure on Borel sets in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. If this measure is locally finite, it is a multiple of the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. The following theorem provides more precise information and shows that the multiplicity can be expressed in terms of the mean
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the boundary of the typical grain and the intensity
\gamma
of
\eta
. Recall that
\kappa_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(1+d/2)
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, and
\omega_{d}=d\kappa_{d}
for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\eta
be a stationary Poisson particle process in
{\mathcal{C}}^{d}
whose particles almost surely have
(d-1)
-rectifiable boundaries, with positive intensity
\gamma
and distribution
\mathbf{Q}
of the typical grain. If
B\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is a bounded Borel set, then
s_{d}(\eta,B)<\infty
holds almost surely and

\mathbf{E}[s_{d}(\eta,B)]=\kappa_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{d\kappa_{d}}\cdot\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial G)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}G)\right)^{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(B).

The factor in front of
\mathcal{H}^{d}(B)
is called the
intersection density

\gamma_{d}(\eta)
of
\eta
, that is,

\gamma_{d}(\eta)=\kappa_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{\omega_{d}}\cdot\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial G)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}G)\right)^{d}\in[0,\infty].

If
\eta
has almost surely convex grains, then
\gamma_{d}(\eta)<\infty
, since
\Lambda
is locally finite. In fact, this follows from
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
and the Steiner formula.

Remark 5.2
.

Theorem
5.1
holds in all space forms (with proper definitions of the required concepts). The Euclidean case is analyzed in
[
24
, Section 4.6]
, especially on pages 153–154, also for anisotropic particle processes, but the analysis requires the restriction to convex grains.

Our proof of Theorem
5.1
is based on a general version of Poincaré’s formula in hyperbolic space, which is provided in
[
4
]
; see Theorem 5.15 in combination with Definition 2.13 and Remark 8.16. For smooth compact submanifolds, versions and extensions of this result are contained in
[
12
]
; see Theorem 3.8 and Corollary 3.9. We will need the following special case, which we state as a lemma. If
k\in\{0,d\}
or
l\in\{0,d\}
, then the assertion of the lemma follows from Fubini’s theorem and (
2.1
).

Lemma 5.3
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is a Hausdorff
k
-rectifiable Borel set and
C\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is an
l
-rectifiable Borel set with
k,l\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
and
k+l\geq d
, then

\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}\mathcal{H}^{k+l-d}(A\cap\varrho C)\,\lambda({\rm d}\varrho)=\gamma(d,k,l)\mathcal{H}^{k}(A)\mathcal{H}^{l}(C),

where

\gamma(d,k,l)=\frac{k!\kappa_{k}l!\kappa_{l}}{d!\kappa_{d}(k+l-d)!\kappa_{k+l-d}}.

Moreover, for
\lambda
-almost all
\varrho\in{\mathcal{I}_{d}}
the intersection
A\cap\varrho C
is a Hausdorff
(k+l-d)
-rectifiable Borel set.

Proof.

The second statement follows from
[
4
, Theorem 4.4 (4) (iii)]
(see also the statement about transversal intersections in
[
12
, Theorem 3.8]
) and the proof of Theorem 5.15 in
[
4
]
.
∎

We need the following iterated version.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. If
B\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is a bounded Borel set and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{i}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
are
(d-1)
-rectifiable Borel sets, then

\displaystyle\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i}}\mathcal{H}^{d-i}(B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i}C_{i})\,\lambda^{i}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i}))

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{d!\kappa_{d}}{(d-i)!\kappa_{d-i}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{d\kappa_{d}}\right)^{i}\mathcal{H}^{d}(B)\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(C_{1})\cdots\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(C_{i}).

(5.1)

Moreover, for
\lambda^{i}
-almost all
(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i})\in({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i}
the intersection
B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i}C_{i}
is a Hausdorff
(d-i)
-rectifiable Borel set.

Proof.

Note that
B
is
d
-rectifiable.
We proceed by induction over
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For
i=1
the assertion is the special case
(k,l)=(d,d-1)
with
\gamma(d,d,d-1)=1
of Lemma
5.3
. Assume that the assertion has been proved for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
. Then
B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i-1}C_{i-1}
is a Hausdorff
(d-i+1)
-rectifiable Borel set for
\lambda^{i-1}
-almost all
(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i-1})\in({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i-1}
. Since
C_{i}
is a
(d-1)
-rectifiable Borel set, the rectifiability statement for
B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i}C_{i}
follows from the rectifiability statement of Lemma
5.3
and Fubini’s theorem.

To verify the integral geometric formula, we apply for
\lambda^{i-1}
-almost all
(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i-1})\in({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i-1}
Lemma
5.3
with the Hausdorff
(d-i+1)
-rectifiable Borel set
B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i-1}C_{i-1}
and the
(d-1)
-rectifiable Borel set
C_{i}
. By means of Fubini’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i}}\mathcal{H}^{d-i}(B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i}C_{i})\,\lambda^{i}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i}))

\displaystyle=\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i-1}}\int_{{\mathcal{I}_{d}}}\mathcal{H}^{d-i}([B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\varrho_{i-1}C_{i-1}]\cap\varrho_{i}C_{i})\,\lambda({\rm d}\varrho_{i})\,\lambda^{i-1}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i-1}))

\displaystyle=\gamma(d,d-i+1,d-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{i-1}}\mathcal{H}^{d-i+1}(B\cap\varrho_{1}C_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{i-1}C_{i-1})\,\lambda^{i-1}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{i-1}))\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(C_{i})

\displaystyle=\gamma(d,d-i+1,d-1)\frac{d!\kappa_{d}}{(d-i+1)!\kappa_{d-i+1}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{d\kappa_{d}}\right)^{i-1}\,\mathcal{H}^{d}(B)\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(C_{1})\cdots\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(C_{i}),

where in the last step the induction hypothesis has been used. The assertion follows after simplification of the constants.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Relation (
5.4
) remains true for an arbitrary Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{d}
and for sets
C_{1},\ldots,C_{i}\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
such that
C_{i}\cap W
is a
(d-1)
-rectifiable Borel set whenever
W\subset\mathbb{H}^{d}
is a bounded Borel set. This follows from Lemma
5.4
via the monotone convergence theorem.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Starting from the definition of
s_{d}(\eta,B)
, we use the multivariate Mecke formula,
[
17
, Theorem 4.4]
and finally Lemma
5.4
. Using also (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[s_{d}(\eta,B)]=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})\in\eta^{d}_{\neq}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(B\cap\partial K_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\partial K_{d})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d!}\int_{({\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}})^{d}}\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(B\cap\varrho_{1}\partial G_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{d}\partial G_{d})\,\lambda^{d}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{d}))\,\mathbf{Q}^{d}({\rm d}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{d}}{d!}\int_{({\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}})^{d}}d!\kappa_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{d\kappa_{d}}\right)^{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(B)\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial G_{1})\cdots\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial G_{d})\,\mathbf{Q}^{d}({\rm d}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}))

\displaystyle=\kappa_{d}\left(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{d\kappa_{d}}\right)^{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(B)\left(\gamma\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\partial G)\,\mathbf{Q}({\rm d}G)\right)^{d},

by another application of Fubini’s theorem in the last step.

In order to verify that
s_{d}(\eta,B)<\infty
holds almost surely, we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})\in\eta^{d}_{\neq}}{\bf 1}\left\{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B\cap\partial K_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\partial K_{d})=\infty\right\}\right]

(5.2)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{d}\int_{({\mathcal{C}}^{d}_{\mathsf{p}})^{d}}\int_{({\mathcal{I}_{d}})^{d}}{\bf 1}\left\{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B\cap\varrho_{1}\partial G_{1}\cap\ldots\cap\varrho_{d}\partial G_{d})=\infty\right\}\,

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\lambda^{d}({\rm d}(\varrho_{1},\ldots,\varrho_{d}))\,\mathbf{Q}^{d}({\rm d}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})).

For given

-rectifiable sets

, Lemma

implies that

since the right-hand side of (

-a.e.

the inner integral on the right-hand side of (

i.e. almost surely

for

. Since

is locally finite, almost surely we also have

Theorem 6.2

The mean visible volume
\overline{\vol}_{s}(Z)
of a stationary Boolean model

Proof.

Using Lemma

6.1

\displaystyle\overline{\vol}_{s}(Z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}^{d}(S_{\mathsf{p}}(Z))\mid p\notin Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}_{\mathsf{p}}}\int_{0}^{s_{\mathsf{p},u}(Z)}\sinh^{d-1}(s)\,{\rm d}s\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}_{\mathsf{p}}({\rm d}u)\mid\mathsf{p}\notin Z\right]

To evaluate this integral, let

\displaystyle={1\over 2^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\left({1\over\sqrt{u}}-\sqrt{u}\right)^{d-1}(\sqrt{u})^{a-1}\,{{\rm d}u\over 2\sqrt{u}}

\displaystyle={1\over 2^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}u^{{a-d+1\over 2}-1}(1-u)^{d-1}\,{\rm d}u.

(6.9)

\gamma_{d}^{\ast}>d-1
, and in this case it is given by

\overline{\vol}_{s}(Z^{\ast})={d!\kappa_{d}\over 2^{d}}{\Gamma\left({\gamma_{d}^{\ast}-d+1\over 2}\right)\over\Gamma\left({\gamma_{d}^{\ast}+d+1\over 2}\right)}.

We note that this result is in line with
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
(and the references cited therein), which states that the random set
S_{\mathsf{p}}(Z^{\ast})
is bounded almost surely if
\gamma_{d}^{\ast}
satisfies (
7.2
), while
S_{\mathsf{p}}(Z^{\ast})
is unbounded almost surely if
\gamma_{d}^{\ast}<d-1
(note the difference by a factor
2
compared to
[
8
]
, which is due to a slightly different normalization of the invariant measure
\mu_{d-1}
). If
d=2
, then
[
8
, Theorem 3.4]
even ensures that in the critical case
\gamma_{2}^{\ast}=1
(or equivalently
\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}
) the random set
S_{\mathsf{p}}(Z^{\ast})
is bounded with probability one; in this situation, the expected value
\overline{\vol}_{s}(Z^{\ast})
is still infinite, as follows from Theorem
7.1
.
