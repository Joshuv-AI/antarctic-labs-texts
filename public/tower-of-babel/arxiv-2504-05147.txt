itizing Sensitive Prompts for LLMs
Amrita Roy ChowdhuryUniversity of Michigan, Ann Arbor, David GlukhovUniversity of Toronto and Vector Institute, Divyam AnshumaanUniversity of Wisconsin-MadisonPrasad ChalasaniLangroid IncorporatedNicolas PapernotUniversity of Toronto and Vector InstituteSomesh JhaUniversity of Wisconsin-MadisonMihir BellareUniversity of California, San Diego

Abstract
The rise of large language models (LLMs) has introduced new privacy challenges, particularly during inference where sensitive information in prompts may be exposed to proprietary LLM APIs. In this paper, we address the problem of formally protecting the sensitive information contained in a prompt while maintaining response quality. To this end, first, we introduce a cryptographically inspired notion of a prompt sanitizer which transforms an input prompt to protect its sensitive tokens. Second, we propose vel system that implements a prompt sanitizer. egorizes sensitive tokens into two types: (1) those where the LLM’s response depends solely on the format (such as SSNs, credit card numbers), for which we use format-preserving encryption ( FPE ); and (2) those where the response depends on specific values, (such as age, salary) for which we apply metric differential privacy (mDP). Our evaluation demonstrates that practical method to achieve meaningful privacy guarantees, while maintaining high utility compared to unsanitized prompts, and outperforming prior methods.

2.1
Language Model

Definition 1
.

A language model

V

\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\textsf{V}^{*}

A language model’s response to a prompt
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is a random variable

that is defined algorithmically as follows. We begin with an empty sequence of tokens
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\langle\rangle
. As long as the last token in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is not
\bot
(which we can be viewed as ”end of sequence” (EOS) token), we sample a token
\sigma
from the distribution

(using a decoding algorithm, such as standard multinomial sampling, or greedy sampling of the single most likely next token) and append it to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. The algorithm stops once a special token

in

\boldsymbol{\rho}

Prompt Sanitizer

Given an input prompt
\boldsymbol{\rho}

Definition 3

(Prompt Sanitizer)

A prompt sanitizer

•

•

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}\in(\textsf{V}^{\star}\times\textsf{T})^{*}
. It outputs a token sequence
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\textsf{V}^{|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}|}
, as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leftarrow\textsf{E}(\textsf{K},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau})
.

•

Desanitization
(\textsf{D})
. Desanitization takes a string (token sequence)
\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\in\textsf{V}^{*}
and processes it with the goal of reversing the effect of the sanitization algorithm, using the

Given a prompt
\boldsymbol{\rho}

(1)

(2)

,

(3)

,

(4)

\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=\textsf{D}(\textsf{K},\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}})

In the above workflow, the desanitization algorithm restores information about the original prompt
\boldsymbol{\rho}
in its output,
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}

\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
(which can be useful for instance if the

We require that the sanitization and desanitization algorithms are

\boldsymbol{\rho}=\langle\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n}\rangle

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}=\langle(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\cdots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\rangle\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\rho})

3.1
Privacy Guarantee

The privacy game, denoted as
\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{pp}}_{\textsf{PS},\mathcal{L}}
, is designed to capture an adversary’s ability to distinguish between the sanitized outputs of two different prompts. In the game if the adversary picks two prompts that have a very different structure (e.g. different type or number of tokens), then the adversary can trivially distinguish between the corresponding sanitized prompts. To avoid these pathological cases, in the game we constrain the adversary to pick two prompts that have “similar structure”, which is formalized as a leakage function.
Different instantiations of leakage function, which are discussed after the definition of the game, lead to different instantiations of the game.

Let

\displaystyle\alpha=\mathbb{E}_{f}\Big[Q\big(\boldsymbol{\rho},f(\boldsymbol{\rho})\big)\Big]

The utility of the prompt sanitizer

\boldsymbol{\rho}
=

Here, a high-quality

Second, the utility is defined as an expectation since in general both the

4.1.2

Differential privacy (DP) is a quantifiable measure of the stability of the output of a randomized mechanism to changes to its input. As a direct consequence of our threat model (Sec.
4.2

Design Goals

the following design goals.

•

Formal Guarantees.

•

High Utility.
We want ave high utility: i.e., the responses based on sanitized prompts should be “close” to the responses based on the original prompt.

•

Stateless.
Finally, the sanitization and de-sanitization process should be stateless: that is, uld not retain information (state) from any prior session. This design choice offers dual advantages. Firstly, storing sensitive information derived from users’ prompts/responses post-session termination would violate privacy and contravene legal frameworks, such as the EU’s GDPR
[
46
]
and California’s CCPA
[
18
]

Additionally, these regulations grant individuals the Right to Deletion or Right to be Forgotten, allowing data owners to retract authorization previously granted for the use of their personal data. A stateful solution, unfortunately, would struggle to support the Right to Deletion without sacrificing de-sanitization capabilities. Secondly, a stateless solution offers flexibility and storage efficiency. To illustrate, consider the following two sequences of user actions:

\displaystyle A_{1}=\langle\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1};\mbox{ Desanitize }\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{1};\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2};

\displaystyle\mbox{ Desanitize }\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{2};\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{3};\mbox{ Desanitize }\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle A_{2}=\langle\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1};\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2};\mbox{ Desanitize }\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{2},

4.6
Comparison with Strawman Solutions

We discuss few strawman solutions and why they don’t meet our goals.

Strawman Solution I: Redaction.
One intuitive solution is to simply redact all the sensitive tokens from the prompt. While this approach ensures perfect privacy, it severely impacts utility and can sometimes lead to a complete loss of functionality. For example, consider a factual information retrieval task in the context of RAG for financial documents, with the prompt: “Please return all bank accounts with balance greater than $2000.” If the sensitive tokens of the types [
Bank A/c
] or [
Bank Balance
] are redacted, the
LLM
is unable to provide any useful response. Furthermore, any tasks which rely on operations over sensitive information, such as numerical reasoning, would similarly provide little to no utility, even if redacted tokens are replaced by a ”best guess” of a masked language model.

Strawman Solution II: Substitution.

Another intuitive solution is to substitute the sensitive tokens with different tokens of the same type via a lookup table. However, there are a couple of issues with this approach. First, such a solution is not stateless as desanitization requires access to these lookup tables and hence, suffers from the drawbacks discussed in Sec.
4.3
. The issues are exacerbated here since the size of the lookup table grows linearly with the number of sensitive tokens. Additionally, a new lookup table is required for every user (otherwise the sanitized prompts will leak additional information: this is akin to using the same secret key for multiple users). he other hand, needs access to just a fixed size key per user irrespective of the number or length of prompts.

Strawman Solution III: Suppression.

Consider the following sanitization strategy for the tokens of the second category
(\tau_{\texttt{II}})
: a numerical token is sanitized by simply setting its
k
lowest order digits to 0. The rationale behind this approach is that the
LLM
’s response is most likely to depend on the higher-order digits, thereby preserving utility while only leaking information about the numerical value at a coarser granularity. However, the key issue with this method is the difficulty in formally quantifying its privacy guarantees. Prior work shows that such ad-hoc approaches are often vulnerable to attacks
[
36
,
31
,
75
]
. In contrast, the mLDP-based approach used in ers a principled way of balancing this privacy/utility trade-off.

Multi-Turn Financial Q/A.
LLMs are also frequently used in multi-turn conversational settings, and may be tasked with performing numerical reasoning over sensitive information. As an example, we assess financial multi-turn question answering benchmark ConvFinQA
[
28

.The dataset consists of financial reports written by experts
[
29
]
followed by a sequence of conversational, numerical-reasoning questions guiding the model through solving a multi-step problem. Each prompt consists of background text as well as a table consisting of yearly financial information over the span of several years. All numerical information, with exception of the years, is extracted using regular expressions and sanitized using mLDP. As the tables contain repeated values contained within parentheses, we employ the helper string
\Psi
in the regex updating of text to ensure that the sanitized text is updated to preserve this structure.

ConvFinQA performance is typically reported in terms of exact match accuracy of responses. As we are introducing noise to the numerical values, checking if the model responses exactly match the correct answer is no longer a reasonable evaluation criterion; instead, we measure utility after sanitization with the relative error of the prediction. Moreover, we observed that for this dataset, occasionally the answers returned by a model are correct up to the target answers sign or magnitude. Often, these mistakes occurred due to the questions being underspecified rather than as a fault of the model. Due to the sensitivity of relative error to incorrect magnitudes of predictions, we check if the relative error of the magnitude and sign adjusted response is less than
.1
of the correct answer. If the adjusted relative error is sufficiently small, we record the adjusted relative error.

Results:
To assess the impact itization of financial documents, we report 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of relative error of GPT-4o in it’s answers for sanitized prompts and clean prompts. We further report the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of “consistency”, measured as the relative difference between the models prediction on the sanitized query compared with the prediction on the clean query. We report the results in
Table 2

Surprisingly, we observe that the relative error of the sanitized prompts at the 75th percentile is lower than for unsanitized prompts, suggesting that addition of noise can regularize model behavior and prevent large outlier responses.

Lastly, we provide additional results for this task when considering a broad range of privacy budgets in Fig.

The Financial Q/A dataset primarily consists of numerical reasoning and retrieval, making its utility much more sensitive to the amount of noise present; as long as the model’s underlying reasoning is correct, the amount of noise would have a direct functional relationship with the relative error, explaining the consistent and smooth improvements.

Open Problems

Automated Discovery of Token Dependencies:
For maximal utility, the encryption space for sensitive tokens must be domain-constrained, keeping in mind the context and relationships between those tokens. For example, consider the sentence, “Paris is the capital of France”, (as part of some larger prompt) and suppose “Paris” and “France” are identified as sensitive tokens. Since the relationship between “Paris” and “France” is “capital city”, a suitable encryption would be a city and country that have the same relationship, such as “Rome is the capital of Italy”.
In the current version of s constraint would need to be provided by the user in the form of the helper string.
LLM
s have demonstrated great capacity for reasoning across many settings. This includes the ability to infer abstract relationships between different entities and then use it to answer questions, or draw some conclusion about them (
[
51
,
101
]
). However, inferring these relationships in an unsupervised setting can be difficult, especially if the relationship is subtle or implied. Furthermore, the dynamics of the relationships can change or be conflicting between different sets of sensitive tokens. We are actively looking in this direction for future iterations of
Encoding Token Dependencies:
Once we discover the relationship between different sets of sensitive tokens, we now have to meaningfully constrain the encryption space for those tokens. For example, consider the sentence, “of his regular income of
\$5000
, he always saved
\$500
”, (as part of a larger prompt) and suppose the two sensitive attributes are “
\$500
” and “
\$5000
”. Given the context, a user (through a helper string) or a LLM will infer the relationship to be “
10\%
of”. Once we have this information, we must use it to constrain the encryption space such that whatever “
\$5000
” gets encrypted as, “
\$500
” gets encrypted keeping the plaintext relationship in mind. It is not clear how this is practically accomplished, especially for more open-ended mappings or non-numerical relationships.

Critical Dependence for Utility:
There are a certain set of prompts that are critically dependent on the sensitive attribute for their utility. For example, it might be a product code that you are asking some questions about. Any sort of change to the number during sanitization would make the LLM agent think some other product is being referred to, and utility is lost. In this case, the user can provide a helper string indicating that a particular token is to be ignored during sanitization. Automating this is important, as the user can’t be expected to provide all such exceptions. We are actively looking into ways for discovering tokens which are important for utility, and identifying when sanitizing such prompts might lead to unacceptable loss in utility.

Very Small Integer Domains for
FPE
:
We found that the domains of many common sensitive attributes tend to be very small, with respect to
FPE
schemes. For example, if a country name was determined to be a sensitive attribute, it can only be replaced from a list of 195 internationally recognized countries. In such a case, FPE cannot be applied and other methods must be considered. This can be the case for other niche domains. We are actively looking into ways for using a
mLDP
like mechanism, with a unique distance metric defined for each of these domains.

Inferring Sensitive Information from Context:

uses solely on the privacy concerns stemming from the individual tokens: it does not address the privacy risks that arise when the entire context of the prompt is considered. For instance, the tokens “neighbor”, “abusive”, “marriage” aren’t sensitive on their own but the entire prompt “My neighbor is in an abusive marriage.” is sensitive. Hence, an important open question is to design high utility prompt sanitizers that protect against the sensitive information leakage from the entire prompt. However, this task is fraught with challenges. First, determining the precise privacy risk is nuanced and highly contextual. A privacy-preserving language model must ensure that private information, or “secrets”, is revealed only in appropriate contexts and to the right individuals. This involves a three-step process:
(1)
identifying the contexts in which sharing a secret does not violate privacy,
(2)
determining what information constitutes the secret, and
(3)
understanding which people are already aware of the secret
[
16
]
.
Another challenge lies in creating a general-purpose sanitization mechanism that consistently yields good utility, regardless of downstream task specification, as it’s not feasible to make a custom mechanism for each task.

[
42

8
Conclusion

LLM
s have brought forth new challenges for user privacy: we need to protect the sensitive information contained in a prompt during inference time. We have addressed this problem by introducing a new cryptographically inspired primitive called the prompt sanitizer, which transforms input prompts to protect the sensitive tokens they contain. We have then proposed stem that implements a prompt sanitizer with provable privacy guarantees. Our experiments demonstrate that ntains high utility for both structured prompts with clear ground truths as well as more open-ended prompts without a ground truth, highlighting its practicality. It also outperforms a contemporary method on relevant tasks.
