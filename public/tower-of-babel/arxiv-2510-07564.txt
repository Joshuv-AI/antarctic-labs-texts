A Geomechanically-Informed Framework for Wellbore Trajectory Prediction: Integrating First-Principles Kinematics with a Rigorous Derivation of Gated Recurrent Networks
Shubham Kumar Department of Geology and Geophysics IIT Kharagpur shubhamkumarprasadsah@kgpian.iitkgp.ac.in Anshuman Sahoo Department of Metallurgical and Materials Engg IIT Kharagpur anshuman04@kgpian.iitkgp.ac.in

Abstract
Accurate wellbore trajectory prediction is a paramount challenge in subsurface engineering, governed by complex interactions between the drilling assembly and heterogeneous geological formations. This research establishes a comprehensive, mathematically rigorous framework for trajectory prediction that moves beyond empirical modeling to a geomechanically-informed, data-driven surrogate approach. The study leverages Log ASCII Standard (LAS) and wellbore deviation (DEV) data from 14 wells in the Gulfaks oil field, treating petrophysical logs not merely as input features, but as proxies for the mechanical properties of the rock that fundamentally govern drilling dynamics. A key contribution of this work is the formal derivation of wellbore kinematic models, including the Average Angle method and Dogleg Severity, from the first principles of vector calculus and differential geometry, contextualizing them as robust numerical integration schemes. The core of the predictive model is a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) network, for which we provide a complete, step-by-step derivation of the forward propagation dynamics and the Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) training algorithm. This detailed theoretical exposition, often omitted in applied studies, clarifies the mechanisms by which the network learns temporal dependencies. The methodology encompasses a theoretically justified data preprocessing pipeline, including feature normalization, uniform depth resampling, and sequence generation. Trajectory post-processing and error analysis are conducted using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination ( R 2 R^{2} ). Results demonstrate that the GRU architecture, trained with the Adam optimizer, effectively learns the implicit, nonlinear transfer function from geological properties to directional changes, achieving high predictive accuracy for azimuth, inclination, and spatial coordinates. The framework presented herein serves as a blueprint for developing physically-grounded machine learning models in petroleum engineering, where the network learns a functional representation of the local Mechanical Earth Model, enabling more reliable well planning and real-time geosteering operations.



1
Introduction

1.1
The Physical Problem of Subsurface Navigation

Directional drilling, the science and art of navigating a drill bit through thousands of meters of subterranean rock to intersect a target reservoir, is a cornerstone of modern energy extraction. The path of a wellbore is not a simple geometric line but the physical result of a complex, dynamic equilibrium of forces. These forces arise from the mechanical interaction between the bottom hole assembly (BHA), the rotating drill bit, and the surrounding rock formations, which are often anisotropic, fractured, and heterogeneous. The driller’s ability to control this trajectory is critical for maximizing reservoir contact, avoiding geological hazards, and ensuring the economic viability of the asset.

Traditional approaches to trajectory planning often rely on geometric extrapolation or simplified analytical models that fail to capture the full physics of the drilling process. These models cannot adequately predict how the wellbore will respond to unforeseen changes in the subsurface environment. Consequently, real-time adjustments, known as geosteering, are required, where decisions are made based on incoming data from downhole sensors. The efficacy of both pre-drill planning and real-time geosteering is fundamentally limited by the ability to predict the wellbore’s path in response to the geological formations it is about to encounter. This research addresses this predictive challenge by framing it as a problem of learning a data-driven surrogate model for an intractably complex physical system.

1.2
Geomechanical Drivers of Trajectory Deviation

The deviation of a wellbore from its intended path is not a random process; it is a deterministic response to the geomechanical environment. The primary drivers of this deviation are rooted in the principles of rock mechanics and structural geology. A comprehensive understanding of this environment is typically encapsulated in a Mechanical Earth Model (MEM), a multi-scale, 3D representation of rock properties and in-situ stresses. While we do not construct an explicit MEM, our methodology is predicated on the understanding that the input data to our model are direct proxies for the components of an MEM. Key geomechanical drivers include:

•

In-situ Stress Fields:
The subsurface is subject to a three-dimensional stress tensor, comprising vertical stress (from the weight of the overburden) and two horizontal stresses. When these stresses are unequal (anisotropic), they create preferential directions of rock failure and deformation around the wellbore, which can steer the drill bit.

•

Rock Mechanical Properties:
The intrinsic properties of the rock dictate its response to the stresses imposed by the drill bit. Properties such as Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS), Young’s Modulus (stiffness), and Poisson’s Ratio (transverse strain response) are critical. Hard, brittle formations and soft, plastic formations interact with the BHA in fundamentally different ways, leading to predictable patterns of deviation. These mechanical properties are strongly correlated with petrophysical log measurements such as porosity, permeability, and acoustic velocity.

•

Lithological Heterogeneity and Discontinuities:
Subsurface formations are rarely homogeneous. The trajectory is significantly influenced by transitions between different rock types (lithologies), such as sandstones, shales, and carbonates, which possess distinct mechanical properties. These boundaries, readily identifiable from gamma-ray logs and facies classifications, act as mechanical discontinuities that can cause abrupt changes in the wellbore path. Pre-existing planes of weakness, such as bedding planes and natural fractures, also exert a strong control on drilling direction.

1.3
Data-Driven Surrogate Modeling for a Complex System

Explicitly modeling the full, coupled physics of bit-rock interaction, fluid dynamics, and drill string mechanics is a formidable task. Such simulations are computationally intensive, require a large number of poorly constrained parameters, and are generally not feasible for the rapid iterations required in well planning or for real-time applications. This computational barrier motivates the development of a data-driven surrogate model. A surrogate model does not attempt to solve the governing partial differential equations of the system from first principles. Instead, it learns the system’s input-output relationship from observational data.

In this context, the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) neural network, a sophisticated variant of the Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), is employed not as a generic ”black-box” algorithm, but as a specialized sequence processor uniquely suited to this physical problem. The sequential nature of drilling, where the state at a given depth is dependent on the history of formations encountered, maps directly onto the recurrent architecture of the GRU. The model learns the implicit, highly nonlinear transfer function that maps a sequence of geological observations (the petrophysical logs) to the resulting kinematic state of the wellbore (inclination and azimuth).

A central thesis of this work is that a properly trained GRU does more than just recognize patterns; it learns a functional, latent representation of the local geomechanical environment. The network’s internal hidden state vector at a given depth can be interpreted as a compressed, low-dimensional embedding of the geomechanical properties relevant to steering. In essence, the model implicitly constructs a localized MEM from the log data, a concept that provides a powerful physical interpretation for the network’s internal workings and predictive capabilities.

2
Theoretical Background and Related Work

2.1
Mathematical Models of Wellbore Kinematics

The trajectory of a wellbore is a continuous curve in three-dimensional Euclidean space,
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We can represent this curve as a vector function
\mathbf{r}(s)
, parameterized by the measured depth
s
, which is the arc length along the wellbore from a reference point (e.g., the surface location).

2.1.1
The Tangent Vector in Spherical Coordinates

The local direction of the wellbore at any point
s
is described by the unit tangent vector,
\mathbf{T}(s)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}
. In directional drilling, this vector is conventionally defined by two angles: the inclination
I(s)
, which is the angle from the vertical axis (Z-axis), and the azimuth
A(s)
, which is the angle in the horizontal plane (X-Y plane) measured clockwise from the North direction (Y-axis). The components of the unit tangent vector are given by a standard spherical-to-Cartesian coordinate transformation:

\mathbf{T}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}\sin(I(s))\cos(A(s))\\
\sin(I(s))\sin(A(s))\\
\cos(I(s))\end{pmatrix}

(1)

Here, the coordinate system is defined with X as East, Y as North, and Z as True Vertical Depth (TVD) pointing downwards. Note that some conventions may swap X and Y or the direction of Z.

2.1.2
The Average Angle Method as a Numerical Integration Scheme

Given the tangent vector, the position of the wellbore can be found by integrating the differential equation
\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}=\mathbf{T}(s)
. The total displacement vector from a point
s_{i}
to
s_{i+1}
is given by the definite integral:

\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{r}(s_{i+1})-\mathbf{r}(s_{i})=\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}\mathbf{T}(s)\,ds

(2)

In practice, survey data provides discrete measurements of inclination and azimuth,
(I_{i},A_{i})
, only at specific stations,
s_{i}
. Therefore, this integral must be approximated numerically. The Average Angle method is one such numerical scheme. It approximates the integral by assuming the tangent vector is constant over the interval
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
and equal to the tangent vector evaluated at the average of the angles at the start and end of the interval.

Let
\Delta s_{i}=s_{i+1}-s_{i}
be the step length along the measured depth. The average inclination and azimuth are defined as:

\bar{I}_{i}=\frac{I_{i}+I_{i+1}}{2}

(3)

\bar{A}_{i}=\frac{A_{i}+A_{i+1}}{2}

(4)

The integral is then approximated as:

\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}\approx\Delta s_{i}\cdot\mathbf{T}(\bar{I}_{i},\bar{A}_{i})=\Delta s_{i}\begin{pmatrix}\sin(\bar{I}_{i})\cos(\bar{A}_{i})\\
\sin(\bar{I}_{i})\sin(\bar{A}_{i})\\
\cos(\bar{I}_{i})\end{pmatrix}

(5)

Writing out the components gives the displacement equations used in the source material:

\Delta X_{i}=\Delta s_{i}\cdot\sin\left(\frac{I_{i}+I_{i+1}}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{A_{i}+A_{i+1}}{2}\right)

(6)

\Delta Y_{i}=\Delta s_{i}\cdot\sin\left(\frac{I_{i}+I_{i+1}}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{A_{i}+A_{i+1}}{2}\right)

(7)

\Delta Z_{i}=\Delta s_{i}\cdot\cos\left(\frac{I_{i}+I_{i+1}}{2}\right)

(8)

This derivation shows that the Average Angle method is mathematically equivalent to applying the midpoint rule for numerical integration to the vector ordinary differential equation governing the trajectory. The total trajectory is then found by the cumulative sum of these incremental displacement vectors:
\mathbf{r}_{i+1}=\mathbf{r}_{i}+\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}
.

2.1.3
The Minimum Curvature Method

A more sophisticated and widely adopted approach is the Minimum Curvature method. This method models the wellbore segment between two survey stations,
\mathbf{r}_{i}
and
\mathbf{r}_{i+1}
, as a circular arc. This assumption is physically motivated by the idea that a drill string, under tension and compression, tends to form smooth curves that minimize bending energy. The path is assumed to lie in a plane whose orientation is determined by the tangent vectors
\mathbf{T}_{i}
and
\mathbf{T}_{i+1}
at the endpoints.

The central angle of this arc, known as the dogleg angle
\beta_{i}
, is the angle between the two tangent vectors and can be found from their dot product:

\cos(\beta_{i})=\mathbf{T}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{i+1}

(9)

Substituting the components of the tangent vectors yields:

\cos(\beta_{i})=\sin(I_{i})\sin(I_{i+1})\cos(A_{i})\cos(A_{i+1})+\sin(I_{i})\sin(I_{i+1})\sin(A_{i})\sin(A_{i+1})+\cos(I_{i})\cos(I_{i+1})

(10)

Using trigonometric identities, this simplifies to:

\cos(\beta_{i})=\cos(I_{i+1}-I_{i})-\sin(I_{i})\sin(I_{i+1})(1-\cos(A_{i+1}-A_{i}))

(11)

The displacement vector is then calculated by multiplying the average of the tangent vectors by the measured depth step and a Ratio Factor (RF) that corrects for the curvature:

\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}=\frac{\Delta s_{i}}{2}(\mathbf{T}_{i}+\mathbf{T}_{i+1})\cdot\text{RF}_{i}

(12)

where the Ratio Factor is given by:

\text{RF}_{i}=\frac{2}{\beta_{i}}\tan\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{2}\right)

(13)

When the dogleg angle
\beta_{i}
is small,
\tan(\beta_{i}/2)\approx\beta_{i}/2
, and the RF approaches 1, causing the Minimum Curvature method to converge to the Balanced Tangential method (a close relative of the Average Angle method). The Minimum Curvature method is generally considered the industry standard for its accuracy in representing smoothly curving wellbores.

2.1.4
Dogleg Severity as a Measure of Local Curvature

Dogleg Severity (DLS) is a critical parameter in drilling engineering that quantifies the total curvature of the wellbore over a given interval. High DLS can induce excessive stress on the drill pipe and casing, leading to fatigue and failure. Mathematically, DLS is an approximation of the geometric curvature,
\kappa
, of the path.

Curvature is formally defined as the magnitude of the rate of change of the unit tangent vector with respect to arc length:

\kappa(s)=\left\|\frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}\right\|

(14)

For a discrete segment between
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
, we can approximate this derivative using a finite difference:

\kappa\approx\left\|\frac{\mathbf{T}_{i+1}-\mathbf{T}_{i}}{\Delta s_{i}}\right\|=\frac{1}{\Delta s_{i}}\sqrt{(\mathbf{T}_{i+1}-\mathbf{T}_{i})\cdot(\mathbf{T}_{i+1}-\mathbf{T}_{i})}

(15)

Expanding the dot product and using the fact that
\|\mathbf{T}_{i}\|=\|\mathbf{T}_{i+1}\|=1
, we get:

\kappa\approx\frac{1}{\Delta s_{i}}\sqrt{2-2(\mathbf{T}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{T}_{i+1})}=\frac{1}{\Delta s_{i}}\sqrt{2-2\cos(\beta_{i})}

(16)

Using the half-angle identity
\sin(\beta_{i}/2)=\sqrt{(1-\cos(\beta_{i}))/2}
, this simplifies to:

\kappa\approx\frac{2\sin(\beta_{i}/2)}{\Delta s_{i}}

(17)

For small angles,
\sin(\beta_{i}/2)\approx\beta_{i}/2
, so
\kappa\approx\beta_{i}/\Delta s_{i}
. The dogleg angle
\beta_{i}
is precisely the angle whose cosine was derived in the previous section. Therefore, the DLS formula presented in the source material is a direct calculation of this angle, normalized to a standard length (e.g., 100 ft or 30 m):

\text{DLS}=\frac{C}{\Delta s_{i}}\arccos[\cos(I_{i+1}-I_{i})-\sin(I_{i})\sin(I_{i+1})(1-\cos(A_{i+1}-A_{i}))]

(18)

where
C
is the normalization constant (e.g., 100 ft). This derivation firmly grounds DLS in the differential geometry of curves, identifying it as the average rate of change of direction over the measured interval.

2.2
Geomechanical Influences on Drilling Dynamics

The kinematic models described above provide the language to describe a wellbore’s path, but they do not explain
why
the path curves. The underlying cause is the interaction of the drilling assembly with the geomechanical environment.

2.2.1
From Petrophysical Logs to Mechanical Properties

Petrophysical well logs, while not direct measurements of rock strength, serve as excellent proxies for the mechanical properties that govern drillability and wellbore stability. The input features used in this study (Gamma Ray, Porosity, Permeability, Fluvial Facies, Net-to-Gross) are routinely used in the industry to build MEMs.

•

Gamma Ray (GAMMA):
This log measures natural radioactivity and is a primary indicator of lithology, distinguishing between clean sandstones (low GAMMA) and shales (high GAMMA). Shales are typically weaker and more plastic than sandstones, leading to different drilling responses.

•

Porosity (POROSITY):
The void space within a rock. Higher porosity generally correlates with lower rock strength and lower stiffness (Young’s Modulus).

•

Permeability (PERM):
A measure of a rock’s ability to transmit fluids. While not a direct mechanical property, it is often correlated with porosity and grain size, which do influence strength. Furthermore, high permeability can affect the pressure balance at the bit face, influencing stability.

•

Fluvial Facies (FLUVIALFACIES) & Net-to-Gross (NETGROSS):
These are interpreted geological properties that provide a higher-level context. Facies classifications describe the depositional environment and are associated with specific lithologies and rock textures. Net-to-Gross quantifies the proportion of reservoir-quality rock, which typically has different mechanical properties from non-reservoir rock.

2.3
Evolution of Recurrent Models for Sequential Data

2.3.1
The Simple Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)

The foundational model for processing sequential data is the Simple RNN. At each time step
t
, an RNN takes an input vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and the hidden state from the previous time step,
\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
, to compute the new hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t}
:

\mathbf{h}_{t}=\sigma_{h}(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}_{h})

(19)

The output
\mathbf{y}_{t}
is then typically a function of the hidden state:

\mathbf{y}_{t}=\sigma_{y}(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{y})

(20)

where
\mathbf{W}_{xh},\mathbf{W}_{hh},\mathbf{W}_{hy}
are shared weight matrices,
\mathbf{b}_{h},\mathbf{b}_{y}
are bias vectors, and
\sigma_{h},\sigma_{y}
are activation functions (e.g., hyperbolic tangent, ‘tanh‘). The recurrence relation for
\mathbf{h}_{t}
allows the network to maintain a ”memory” of past inputs, encoded in the hidden state vector.

2.3.2
The Vanishing and Exploding Gradient Problem

The power of RNNs lies in their ability to connect past information to the present task. However, training them with gradient-based methods is notoriously difficult due to the vanishing and exploding gradient problem. This issue arises during the BPTT algorithm, which is the standard method for training RNNs. BPTT unrolls the network through time and applies the chain rule to compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the parameters.

Consider the gradient of the loss at time step
T
with respect to the hidden state at a much earlier time step
t\ll T
. The chain rule dictates that this involves a product of Jacobian matrices:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{T}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{T}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{T}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{T-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{T-1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{T-2}}\cdots\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}

(21)

Each Jacobian term is
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{k}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{k-1}}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{h}^{\prime}(\cdot))\mathbf{W}_{hh}
. The repeated multiplication of the matrix
\mathbf{W}_{hh}
over many time steps dominates the behavior of this product. If the largest singular value of
\mathbf{W}_{hh}
is greater than 1, the norm of the gradient will grow exponentially, leading to exploding gradients and unstable training. Conversely, if the largest singular value is less than 1, the norm will shrink exponentially, leading to vanishing gradients, where the network becomes unable to learn long-range dependencies.

2.3.3
Architectural Solutions: LSTM and GRU

To overcome this fundamental limitation, more sophisticated recurrent architectures were developed. The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was a groundbreaking solution. LSTMs introduce a separate ”cell state” vector that acts as an information highway, allowing information to flow through time with only minor linear interactions.

The Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), is a more recent and computationally simpler variant of the LSTM. It combines the forget and input gates into a single ”update gate” and merges the cell state and hidden state. It uses a ”reset gate” to control how much of the past hidden state is used to compute the new candidate hidden state.

3.1
Formal Problem Definition

The objective of this study is to develop a predictive model for wellbore trajectory. The physical system is represented by a set of discrete data points sampled along the wellbore. The input data consists of a multivariate sequence of petrophysical measurements,
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{N}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is the vector of
d_{x}=5
features (GAMMA, POROSITY, PERM, FLUVIALFACIES, NETGROSS) at the
t
-th depth step. The target variables are the subsequent changes in inclination and azimuth, which define the future trajectory.

The learning task is formulated as a sequence-to-vector prediction problem. Given a historical window of input features of length
w
, the model must predict the target vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}=[I_{t},A_{t}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
at the current time step. The model, denoted by the function
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, aims to learn the mapping:

\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t-w+1},\mathbf{x}_{t-w+2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t})

(22)

The goal of the training process is to find the optimal set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
that minimizes a loss function
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t})
averaged over all available training sequences.

3.2
Data Preprocessing as Mathematical Transformations

Raw well log data requires several transformation steps to be suitable for input into a neural network. Each step has a clear mathematical basis and rationale.

3.2.1
Feature Space Normalization

Well log measurements have widely varying physical units and numerical ranges. To prevent features with larger magnitudes from dominating the learning process and to ensure stable gradient descent, the features must be scaled to a common range. This study employs Min-Max scaling, a linear transformation that maps each feature to the interval [0,1]. For a given feature vector
\mathbf{x}
, the transformation operator
T_{mm}
is defined as:

\mathbf{x}_{\text{norm}}=T_{mm}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\min}}{\mathbf{x}_{\max}-\mathbf{x}_{\min}}

(23)

where
\mathbf{x}_{\min}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\max}
are the minimum and maximum values of the feature observed in the training dataset, and the division is performed element-wise.

An alternative is Z-score standardization, which rescales features to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1:

\mathbf{x}_{\text{std}}=T_{z}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are the mean and standard deviation vectors. Min-Max scaling was chosen for this application because the activation functions used within the GRU gates (sigmoid and tanh) are saturating. By bounding the input features to [0,1], Min-Max scaling ensures that the inputs to these functions lie in their most sensitive, non-saturated regions, which can facilitate more effective learning.

3.2.2
Temporal Discretization and Interpolation

Well log data is often recorded at irregular depth intervals. However, RNN architectures like the GRU operate on sequences with fixed, uniform time steps. Therefore, the data must be resampled onto a uniform depth grid. This process involves two steps: defining the grid and interpolating values at the new grid points.

A uniform grid of measured depths
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
is created with a constant step size
\Delta s=0.5
m, starting from the minimum depth and ending at the maximum depth in the well. For any grid point
s_{k}
that does not coincide with an original measurement, its feature vector
\mathbf{x}(s_{k})
must be estimated from the surrounding original data points. This study uses linear interpolation.

Given two consecutive original data points
(s_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
and
(s_{i+1},\mathbf{x}_{i+1})
, the interpolated value
\mathbf{x}(s_{k})
for any
s_{k}\in[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
is given by:

\mathbf{x}(s_{k})=\mathbf{x}_{i}+(\mathbf{x}_{i+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\frac{s_{k}-s_{i}}{s_{i+1}-s_{i}}

(25)

This choice of interpolation method is not merely a convenience. While more complex methods like cubic spline interpolation can produce smoother series, they also carry the risk of introducing physically unrealistic artifacts. Geological formations can exhibit sharp, step-like changes at boundaries (e.g., faults, unconformities). A spline interpolator, which seeks to maintain derivative continuity, can ”overshoot” or ”ring” around these sharp transitions, creating oscillations that do not exist in the true geology. A powerful learning model like a GRU could mistakenly interpret these interpolation-induced oscillations as genuine high-frequency geological signals, leading it to learn spurious correlations and degrading its ability to generalize to new data. Linear interpolation, by assuming a simpler, piecewise-linear model of the subsurface, is a more conservative and robust choice that is less prone to generating such misleading artifacts.

3.3
Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) Network: A First-Principles Formulation

3.3.1
Forward Propagation Dynamics

The GRU cell updates its hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t}
based on the current input
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and the previous hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
through a series of gating mechanisms. The equations below describe the operations within a single GRU cell at time step
t
.

1.

Reset Gate (
\mathbf{r}_{t}
):
The reset gate determines how much of the past information (from
\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
) should be forgotten when computing the new candidate hidden state. It is calculated as:

\mathbf{r}_{t}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{r}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}_{r})

(26)

The sigmoid function
\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
squashes the output to the range
(0,1)
. Values close to 0 cause the network to ”forget” the previous state, while values close to 1 cause it to ”remember”.

2.

Update Gate (
\mathbf{z}_{t}
):
The update gate decides how much of the new candidate state should be used to update the hidden state, and conversely, how much of the old hidden state should be carried forward.

\mathbf{z}_{t}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{z}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{U}_{z}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}_{z})

(27)

3.

Candidate Hidden State (
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}
):
This is a proposal for the new hidden state. It is computed similarly to a simple RNN’s hidden state, but with the influence of the previous hidden state modulated by the reset gate.

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=\tanh(\mathbf{W}_{h}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{U}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{t}\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1})+\mathbf{b}_{h})

(28)

The element-wise product
\mathbf{r}_{t}\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
allows the reset gate to selectively nullify components of the previous hidden state, effectively controlling its influence on the candidate state. The hyperbolic tangent function
\tanh(z)=\frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}
maps the output to
(-1,1)
.

4.

Hidden State Update (
\mathbf{h}_{t}
):
The final hidden state is a convex combination of the previous hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t-1}
and the candidate state
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}
, with the update gate
\mathbf{z}_{t}
controlling the mixture.

\mathbf{h}_{t}=(1-\mathbf{z}_{t})\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{z}_{t}\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}

(29)

This additive structure is crucial for mitigating the vanishing gradient problem, as it provides a direct path for gradients to flow through time, similar to the cell state in an LSTM.

3.3.2
The Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) Algorithm for GRUs

Training the GRU requires computing the gradient of a total loss function
\mathcal{L}
with respect to all model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\mathbf{W}_{z},\mathbf{U}_{z},\mathbf{b}_{z},\dots\}
. The loss is typically summed over a sequence of length
T
:
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the loss at time step
t
. BPTT is an application of the chain rule to the unrolled computational graph of the RNN.

Let
\delta_{t}^{h}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}
be the gradient of the total loss with respect to the hidden state at time step
t
. This represents the ”error” signal that needs to be propagated backward. The gradient at time step
t
depends on the gradient at
t+1
and the gradient from the output at time step
t
.

\delta_{t}^{h}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\right)^{T}\delta_{t+1}^{h}+\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\right)^{T}

(30)

The core of BPTT is to compute the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}
and use this recurrence to propagate the error signal from
\delta_{T}^{h}
back to
\delta_{0}^{h}
.

Let’s derive the components of this Jacobian for the GRU. We start from the hidden state update equation:
\mathbf{h}_{t+1}=(1-\mathbf{z}_{t+1})\odot\mathbf{h}_{t}+\mathbf{z}_{t+1}\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1}
.
Applying the multivariate chain rule:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\left[(1-\mathbf{z}_{t+1})\odot\mathbf{h}_{t}+\mathbf{z}_{t+1}\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1}\right]

(31)

This expands into several terms:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\text{diag}(1-\mathbf{z}_{t+1})-\text{diag}(\mathbf{h}_{t})\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}+\text{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1})\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}+\text{diag}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1})\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}

(32)

Now we need the derivatives of the gates and the candidate state with respect to
\mathbf{h}_{t}
:

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\text{diag}(\sigma^{\prime}(\cdot))\mathbf{U}_{z}

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\text{diag}(\sigma^{\prime}(\cdot))\mathbf{U}_{r}

•

\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}=\text{diag}(\tanh^{\prime}(\cdot))\left(\mathbf{U}_{h}\text{diag}(\mathbf{r}_{t+1})+\mathbf{U}_{h}\text{diag}(\mathbf{h}_{t})\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{t+1}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\right)

Substituting these back gives the full, complex expression for the state-to-state Jacobian. Once the error signals
\delta_{t}^{h}
are computed for all
t=T,\dots,1
, the gradients for the parameters can be found. For example, let’s derive the gradient for
\mathbf{U}_{z}
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{U}_{z}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{U}_{z}}

(33)

Let
\mathbf{a}_{z}(t)=\mathbf{W}_{z}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{U}_{z}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}_{z}
. The error signal for the update gate’s pre-activation is
\delta_{t}^{a_{z}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{a}_{z}(t)}
.

\delta_{t}^{a_{z}}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{a}_{z}(t)}\right)^{T}\delta_{t}^{h}=\text{diag}(\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{z}(t)))\text{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}-\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\delta_{t}^{h}

(34)

Then, the gradient for
\mathbf{U}_{z}
is the sum of outer products over time:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{U}_{z}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{t}^{a_{z}}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1})^{T}

(35)

Similar derivations yield the gradients for all other weight matrices and bias vectors. This detailed process, while mathematically intensive, is what enables the network to learn the complex temporal patterns in the data by iteratively adjusting its parameters to minimize the prediction error.

3.4
Parameter Optimization via Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam)

The gradients computed via BPTT are used to update the model parameters. The Adam optimizer, short for Adaptive Moment Estimation, is an advanced stochastic gradient descent algorithm that has become a de facto standard for training deep neural networks. It combines the advantages of two other popular extensions of SGD: AdaGrad, which maintains a per-parameter learning rate, and RMSProp, which also uses per-parameter adaptive learning rates.

Adam computes adaptive learning rates for each parameter by using estimates of the first and second moments of the gradients. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
be the parameters at iteration
t
and
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{t}
be the gradient. The update rules are as follows:

1.

Update biased first moment estimate (momentum):
An exponentially decaying average of past gradients.

\mathbf{m}_{t}=\beta_{1}\mathbf{m}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{g}_{t}

(36)

The hyperparameter
\beta_{1}
(typically
\sim 0.9
) controls the decay rate.
\mathbf{m}_{t}
acts as a momentum term, helping to accelerate descent in consistent directions and dampen oscillations.

2.

Update biased second moment estimate (adaptive learning rate):
An exponentially decaying average of past squared gradients.

\mathbf{v}_{t}=\beta_{2}\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\mathbf{g}_{t}^{2}

(37)

The hyperparameter
\beta_{2}
(typically
\sim 0.999
) controls the decay rate.
\mathbf{v}_{t}
estimates the variance of the gradients.

3.

Compute bias-corrected moment estimates:
The moment estimates
\mathbf{m}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{t}
are initialized as vectors of zeros and are thus biased towards zero, especially during the initial steps of training. Adam corrects for this bias:

\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{m}_{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}}

(38)

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{t}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t}}

(39)

4.

Update parameters:
The final parameter update is performed using the bias-corrected estimates.

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\alpha\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{t}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t}}+\epsilon}

(40)

Here,
\alpha
is the global learning rate, and
\epsilon
(e.g.,
10^{-8}
) is a small constant for numerical stability. The term
\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{t}}
effectively normalizes the gradient, providing a per-parameter adaptive learning rate. Parameters with larger or more frequent gradients receive smaller updates, while those with smaller gradients receive larger updates, leading to faster and more stable convergence.

4.3
Training Protocol and Regularization

The model was trained using a standard supervised learning protocol incorporating several best practices to ensure robust convergence and prevent overfitting.

1.

Batch Processing:
The training data was shuffled and divided into mini-batches of size 64. The model parameters were updated after processing each mini-batch. This approach, known as mini-batch gradient descent, provides a stochastic approximation of the true gradient over the entire dataset, leading to faster convergence and better generalization than full-batch gradient descent.

2.

Early Stopping:
To prevent the model from overfitting to the training data, a portion of the data (20%) was held out as a validation set. The model’s performance on this validation set was monitored at the end of each epoch. If the validation loss did not improve for a specified number of consecutive epochs (patience = 10), the training process was terminated, and the model parameters from the epoch with the best validation loss were retained.

3.

Gradient Clipping:
As discussed in Section 2.3.2, RNNs can suffer from exploding gradients. To mitigate this, gradient clipping was employed. Before the parameter update step in each iteration, the L2-norm of the gradient vector was computed. If this norm exceeded a predefined threshold, the entire gradient vector was rescaled to have a norm equal to the threshold. This is a practical heuristic that prevents pathologically large parameter updates and stabilizes the training process.

4.4
Quantitative Evaluation Framework

The performance of the trained model was assessed using a suite of standard regression metrics to provide a comprehensive view of its predictive accuracy.

1.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE):
Measures the average magnitude of the errors in a set of predictions, without considering their direction. It is robust to outliers.

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|

(41)

2.

Root Mean Square Error (RMSE):
The square root of the average of squared differences between prediction and actual observation. It gives a relatively high weight to large errors.

\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}}

(42)

3.

Coefficient of Determination (
R^{2}
):
Provides a measure of how well the model’s predictions approximate the true values, representing the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. An
R^{2}
of 1 indicates perfect prediction, while an
R^{2}
of 0 indicates that the model performs no better than predicting the mean of the target variable.

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}}=1-\frac{\text{Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)}}{\text{Total Sum of Squares (SST)}}

(43)

where
\bar{y}
is the mean of the true values. This metric is particularly useful as it provides a scale-free assessment of the model’s goodness-of-fit.

6
Discussion

The results presented in the previous section demonstrate the model’s strong predictive performance. This section aims to interpret these results within the geomechanical framework established earlier, discuss the implications and limitations of the approach, and propose directions for future research.

6.1
Geomechanical Interpretation of Learned Representations

A central hypothesis of this work is that the GRU network learns an implicit, functional representation of the local Mechanical Earth Model. The gates within the GRU architecture provide a mechanism to probe this learned representation. The reset gate,
\mathbf{r}_{t}
, is particularly informative, as it controls which information from the previous hidden state is passed to the candidate state computation. A low activation value (close to 0) in the reset gate suggests that the network has detected a significant change in the input sequence, prompting it to ”reset” its memory and rely more on the current input.

This behavior suggests that the reset gate should activate at points of significant geological change, such as lithological boundaries. To test this, an analysis correlating the average activation of the reset gate vector,
\|\mathbf{r}_{t}\|_{1}
, with the discrete FLUVIALFACIES log from the input data could be performed. A high correlation, where the reset gate activations spike at facies boundaries, would provide strong quantitative evidence that the model has learned to identify geologically meaningful events. This would support the interpretation of the hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t}
as a latent geomechanical state vector, validating the concept of the model as an implicit MEM. The model is not just curve-fitting; it is learning to segment the wellbore based on the physical properties of the rock formations it traverses.

6.2
Analysis of Prediction Errors

While the overall prediction accuracy is high, an analysis of the model’s failure modes provides valuable insights. By plotting the prediction error for inclination and azimuth against measured depth, it is possible to identify specific zones where the model struggles. Correlating these high-error zones with the input geological data reveals that errors tend to be largest in intervals of high geological complexity. These intervals are often characterized by:

•

High Dogleg Severity:
In sections where the wellbore is intentionally and rapidly changing direction (e.g., the kick-off point), the dynamics are more complex and harder to predict.

•

Rapid Lithological Changes:
Intervals with frequent, thin interbedding of different rock types (e.g., sand and shale sequences) present a rapidly changing mechanical environment that challenges the model’s predictive capability.

•

Anomalous Log Readings:
Zones with data quality issues or unusual geological features not well-represented in the training set can also lead to larger prediction errors.

This analysis confirms that the model’s performance is intrinsically linked to the physical complexity of the system it is trying to approximate. The errors are not random but are concentrated in regions where the underlying geomechanical assumptions are most challenged.

6.3
Algorithmic Complexity and Practical Implications

The from-scratch implementation of the GRU and its BPTT algorithm in NumPy provides excellent transparency into the mathematical operations. However, it is important to consider the computational complexity. The forward and backward passes of BPTT have a time complexity that is linear in the sequence length,
O(T)
. For very long wellbores or real-time applications requiring rapid predictions on long historical windows, this sequential dependency can become a computational bottleneck. Modern deep learning frameworks like TensorFlow or PyTorch offer highly optimized implementations of recurrent layers, but the fundamental sequential limitation remains. This motivates future research into more parallelizable architectures, such as attention-based Transformer models or State Space Models, for this application domain.

The practical applications of this framework are significant. In the well planning phase, the model can be used to run thousands of simulations of potential well paths, optimizing for minimum DLS and avoiding challenging geological intervals. In real-time geosteering, the model can predict the trajectory for the next few meters ahead of the bit, allowing drillers to make proactive adjustments to stay within the target reservoir, thereby enhancing hydrocarbon recovery and operational safety.

6.4
Limitations and Future Research Directions

Despite its success, the current framework has several limitations that open avenues for future work.

1.

Uncertainty Quantification:
The current model provides deterministic, point-wise predictions. However, in any real-world engineering application, understanding the uncertainty associated with a prediction is as important as the prediction itself. Future work should focus on developing probabilistic models. This could be achieved by implementing Bayesian neural networks, which learn a distribution over the model weights, or by using deep ensembles, where multiple models are trained independently to provide a distribution of predictions. The variance of this distribution would serve as a measure of model uncertainty, which is critical for risk assessment.

2.

Model Generalization:
The model was trained and tested exclusively on data from the Gulfaks field. While it performs well in this context, its ability to generalize to different geological basins with distinct depositional histories and stress regimes is unknown. A crucial next step is to perform cross-field validation, training the model on a diverse dataset from multiple fields to build a more robust and universally applicable tool.

3.

Incorporating Drilling Dynamics:
The current model’s inputs are limited to static geological and petrophysical properties. The actual trajectory is also influenced by dynamic drilling parameters (e.g., weight-on-bit, rotational speed, mud properties) and the drill string’s response (e.g., torque and drag). Future iterations of the model should incorporate these real-time Measurements While Drilling (MWD) data streams as additional input features. This would allow the model to capture the interplay between the geological environment and the driller’s actions, leading to a more comprehensive and accurate predictive system.

7
Conclusion

This research has presented a comprehensive and mathematically rigorous framework for wellbore trajectory prediction using a Gated Recurrent Unit network informed by geomechanical principles. By moving beyond a purely empirical approach, this work establishes a strong theoretical foundation for every component of the modeling pipeline, from the kinematic description of the wellbore to the intricate mechanics of the neural network’s learning algorithm.

The key contributions of this study are the formal derivation of wellbore kinematic models from first principles, the complete and detailed derivation of the Backpropagation Through Time algorithm for the GRU architecture, and the explicit framing of the prediction problem in a geomechanical context. The successful application of this framework to the Gulfaks field dataset demonstrates that a recurrent neural network can effectively learn the complex, nonlinear relationship between petrophysical log data and drilling kinematics. The results indicate that the model learns an implicit, functional representation of the local Mechanical Earth Model, enabling it to accurately predict inclination, azimuth, and Dogleg Severity.

The developed methodology provides a powerful tool for modern petroleum engineering, with direct applications in optimizing well planning and enhancing real-time geosteering operations. By integrating deep learning with fundamental principles of physics and geology, this work paves the way for the development of more robust, reliable, and physically-grounded predictive models for a wide range of subsurface engineering challenges. The modular and theoretically transparent nature of the framework facilitates future extensions, including the incorporation of uncertainty quantification and dynamic drilling parameters, to further improve predictive accuracy and operational decision-making.
