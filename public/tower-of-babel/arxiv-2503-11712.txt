Comment on arXiv:2202.01553: The Distribution of a Gaussian Covariate Statistic
Joe Whittaker, Lancaster University

Abstract




Abstract

In regression Gaussian covariate p-values
(
Davies and Dümbgen,, 2022
)
are used
to control greedy forward subset selection by accounting for
choosing the best when fitting many variables.
Here we outline a simple proof of their Theorems 1 and 2, making slight
alterations to simplify the exposition
by including a new variable rather than
excluding an included variable and some slight changes in notation.

Background

The problem of variable selection in linear regression, especially in
high dimensions, is still the object of current interest.
Many sequential techniques depend on comparing sums of squares from
different fits.
Under the usual modelling assumption that each observation of the dependent
variable comes from a Normal distrbution with constant variance
the standard F-statistic for adding a single variable has an
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{F}}

distribution, with certain degrees of freedom, and a consequent p-value.
Adding a second variable also leads to an
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{F}}
distribution, with
different degrees of freedom, giving its p-value.
However, there is no easy theory for computing the p-value when the
first variable is chosen to be the better of the two, the one with the
smaller residual sum of squares.
The contribution of
Davies and Dümbgen, (2022)
is to show that within
the framework of independent Gaussian covariate regression such
p-values can be calculated.
Importantly these p-values are valid whatever the sampling
distribution of the dependent and explanatory variables.

Standard linear regression preliminaries

The vector
y
is a sample of size
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}
observations
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k}
covariates held in a matrix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
of dimension

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n\times{}k}
.
The least squares predictor of
y
from a linear combination of the
columns of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P=X(X^{T}X)X^{T}}
is the projection
matrix onto the space spanned by the observed
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
variables and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I-P}

is the residual space orthogonal to this.
The residuals are

r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I-P)\mbox{\boldmath$y$}

(1)

with residual sum of squares
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{T}(I-P)\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}
.
The standard analysis of variance decomposition of the total sum of
squares is

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{T}P\mbox{\boldmath$y$}+\mbox{\boldmath$y$}^{T}(I-P)\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}
,
and the F statistic for assessing the contribution of all covariates
is based on the ratio of these two terms.

Consider whether an additional variable
z
should be included
in the regression when the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
variables are already included.
The regression of
y
on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
and
z
can be acheived by first
fitting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
and then regressing the residuals of
y
from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
on
the residuals of
z
from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
.
Put

s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I-P)\mbox{\boldmath$z$}

(2)

then the projection onto the space spanned by the additional variable is

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})=\mbox{\boldmath$s$}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}^{T}/\mbox{\boldmath$s$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}

and the sum of squares decomposition of the
y
residuals is

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}(I-J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}))\mbox{\boldmath$r$}.

(3)

The ratio of the two residual sums of squares
to assess whether
z
contributes to
the regression is

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}(I-J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}))\mbox{\boldmath$r$}/\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}.

(4)

We outline the derivation of the distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B}
under the
standard model.
One (of several) formulations of the standard model is the
assumption that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}\sim\mathcal{N}(X\beta,\sigma^{2}I)}
where the
capitalisation makes it clear that
Y
is random and
y
is
a particular realisation.
Furthermore this distribution remains the same
under the supposition that
the additional variable
z
has no effect on the distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Y}
.
From (
1
)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}=(I-P)\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}

so that

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mbox{\boldmath 0},\sigma^{2}(I-P)).

(5)

Returning to the quadratic forms in the decomposition at
(
3
) note that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}J}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I-J}
are projections and are orthogonal, so by Cochran’s theorem, they
have independent
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\chi^{2}}
distributions with the appropriate
degrees of freedom, whatever
z
.
Consequently the ratio of the two residual sums of squares

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B(\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})\sim\mathcal{B}_{((n-k-1)/2,1/2)},

(6)

the Beta distribution,
under the assumption that
Y
does not depend on
z
.

The distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B}
in the Gaussian covariate framework

The alternative Gaussian covariate framework supposes that given
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}

and
y
, which may be fixed or random, the observations
z
are
sampled from the Normal distribution,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mbox{\boldmath 0},\sigma_{z}^{2}I)}
, and so are independent of both

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
and
y
,
In this distribution-free framework
Davies and Dümbgen, (2022)

showed that, in parallel to (
6
),

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$S$})\sim\mathcal{B}_{((n-k-1)/2,1/2)},

(7)

as well.
Here is a simple proof.

First note that from (
4
) the statistic
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B}
satisfies

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(\mbox{\boldmath$s$},\mbox{\boldmath$r$}),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad

(8)

\displaystyle B(a\mbox{\boldmath$r$},b\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(a\mbox{\boldmath$s$},b\mbox{\boldmath$r$})

(9)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a,b}
are positive scalars.
So
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B}
is symmetric in
r
and
s
, and is scale invariant.
Symmetry is because

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}(I-J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}))\mbox{\boldmath$r$}/\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=1-(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$s$})^{2}/(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}{\,}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$r$})}
,
which uses
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}J(\mbox{\boldmath$s$})\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$s$})^{2}/{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}

and is symmetric.
The right hand side is a function of the partial
correlation between
y
and
z
having adjusted for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X}
,
and is scale invariant.

The proof continues

\displaystyle B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$S$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(\mbox{\boldmath$S$},\mbox{\boldmath$r$}),\mbox{ symmetry,}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(\sigma_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$S$},\mbox{\boldmath$r$}),\mbox{ invariance,}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}

\displaystyle B(\sigma^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$r$}),\mbox{ distributional equivalence,}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$r$}),\mbox{ invariance.}

The distributional equivalence is because under the standard model

R
has the Normal distrbution (
5
) and from (
2
)
within the independent Gaussian covariate framework

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mbox{\boldmath 0},\sigma_{Z}^{2}(I-P))}
.

Corollary: application to p-values

The relationship between the Beta and the F distributions give
an alternative way to express (
6
): the F statistic

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})={(n-k-1)(1-B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$}))}/{B(\mbox{\boldmath$r$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})}}

is distributed under the standard model as

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F(\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1,n-k-1)}.

(10)

Let the subscript ‘obs’ denote a specific numerical value for the
subscripted variable.
Hence the standard p-value, for including
z
in the regression
when
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{obs}}
is observed, is

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Pr[F(\mbox{\boldmath$R$},\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{obs})>F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}]

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{F}{}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1,n-k-1)}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})

using (
10
).
Computing
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})}
requires values for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{obs}}
,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{obs}}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{obs}}
giving
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{obs}}
to get

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}}
and allowing the evaluation of (
11
).

In the Gaussian covariate framework of the previous
section we have shown that

\displaystyle p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})=Pr[F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},\mbox{\boldmath$S$})>F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}]=1-\mathcal{F}{}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1,n-k-1)}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})

as well, so that finally

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})=p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R}(F_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}),

(12)

the p-values are the same.

Now consider observing two additional variables,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$z$}_{2}}
,
and evaluating the p-value for selecting the better of the two.
The Gaussian covariate framework supposes that these
covariates are observations from Normal distributions, identical to
that associated with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z}
above, and also are mutually independently.
Put
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}=\max(F^{1}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},F^{2}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})}
where the superscript indexes the variate, then the p-value,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}(F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})}
, is

\displaystyle Pr[\max(F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{1}),F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{2}))>F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-Pr[F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{1})<F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}\mbox{ and }F(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs},\mbox{\boldmath$S$}_{2})<F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-\mathcal{F}{}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1,n-k-1)}(F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(1-p_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S}(F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}obs})))^{2}.

On the other hand the computation of

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Pr[F(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{1,obs})<F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}max}\mbox{ and }F(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{2,obs})<F^{12}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}max}]}

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{2}}
are the residuals of

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Y}
from the respective covariates
is very much more difficult.

The calculation generalises immediately to considering
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}
extra covariates
rather than 2.
