Modelling heavy tail data with bayesian nonparametric mixtures
Luis E. Nieto-Barajas

Abstract
In the study of heavy tail data, several models have been introduced. If the interest is in the tail of the distribution, block maxima or excess over thresholds are the typical approaches, wasting relevant information in the bulk of the data. To avoid this, two building block mixture models for the body (below the threshold) and the tail (above the threshold) are proposed. In this paper, we exploit the richness of nonparametric mixture models to model heavy tail data. We specifically consider mixtures of shifted gamma-gamma distributions with four parameters and a normalised stable processes as a mixing distribution. One of these parameters is associated with the tail. By studying the posterior distribution of the tail parameter, we are able to estimate the proportion of the data that supports a heavy tail component. We develop an efficient MCMC method with adapting Metropolis-Hastings steps to obtain posterior inference and illustrate with simulated and real datasets.



Keywords
: Bayesian nonparametrics, extremes, generalised pareto distribution, non conjugate analysis, adaptive MCMC.

1
Introduction

Heavy or fat-tailed data have been studied in different fields, such as finance
(
Mandelbrot and Hudson, 2008
)
, in the study of prices and returns, and insurance
(
Latchman et al., 2008
)
, in the study of claims from natural disasters such as earthquakes and floods.

According to
Cooke and Nieboer, 2011
, there are three main ways of defining a heavy tailed distribution. The first one is based on the kurtosis coefficient; leptokurtic distributions have fatter tails than normal distributions, so a distribution with kurtosis coefficient larger than zero is said to be heavy tail. The second definition is based on the theory of regular variation. A random variable
X
is said to have a heavy tail if its moment generating function
M_{X}(t)
diverges to infinity for
t>0
. In general, a survival function with a polinomial decay rate
-\alpha
, i.e.,
S(x)\approx x^{-\alpha}
, has infinite
k^{th}
moments for
k\geq\alpha
. The third definition is based on subexponentiality, that is, the tail of the sum of independent variables behaves like the tail of the maximum. In notation,
\mbox{P}(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}>x)\approx\mbox{P}(\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}>x)
. In general, the set of regular variation distributions is a strict subclass of the subexponential distributions.

Estimating the tail of a distribution using extreme value theory goes back to
Davison and Smith, 1990
who proposed methods for exceedances over thresholds and
McNeil, 1997
who used the generalised pareto distribution (GPD) in the context of insurance data. To avoid wasting valuable information in the body of the distribution (below the threshold), mixture distributions have been proposed to model the entire set of data. For example,
Behrens et al., 2004
and
Nascimento et al., 2012
considered gamma and mixtures of gammas, respectively, combined with a GPD and used a bayesian approach for parameter estimation including the threshold;
Carreau and Bengio, 2009
used a normal combined with a GPD and called their model hybrid pareto; and
Frigessi et al., 2002
proposed a dynamic mixture between a weibull and a GPD. In the discrete world,
Lee and Eastoe, 2020
considered a mixture of geometric and an integrer version of the GPD. On the other hand,
Bladt and Rojas-Nandayapa, 2008
suggested a scale mixture of phase-type distributions.

Recently, two proposals have been introduced in the context of bayesian nonparametric models.
Ayala et al., 2022
represented a phase-type distribution as an infinite mixture and connected the result to the dirichlet process mixture model to simplify bayesian inference. Finally,
Palacios et al., 2022
;
Palacios et al., 2024
characterised the tails of the Pitman-Yor and normalised generalised gamma mixture models and proposed scale and shape parameter mixtures of pareto type kernels.

In this article, we propose a particular bayesian nonparametric mixture model based on shifted gamma-gamma (SGG) densities with location, shape, tail, and scale parameters, and using a normalised stable as mixing distribution for the four parameters vector. Exploiting the discreteness (clustering) properties of the normalised stable process, the idea is to characterise the data with as few as possible mixture components, where some of them will be associated to model the bulk and others associated to the tail of the data, by means of the tail parameter of the SGG which controls light and heavy tails. At the same time, threshold parameters are not required to be fixed a-priori since these can be accounted for the location parameters of the SGG densities, which will also be estimated by the model. The main challenge of the proposal is to construct an efficient posterior sampler since the SGG does not admit conjugate priors for the four parameters vector.

The contents of the rest of the paper is as follows: Section
2
introduces the SGG density. In Section
3
we present the bayesian nonparametric SGG mixture. In Section
4
we characterise the posterior distribution and propose an adaptive MCMC sampler. In section
5
we illustrate the performance of our model with simulated and real datasets. We finally conclude in Section
6
.

2
Heavy tail densities

Let
X
be a continuous random variable with GPD density given by
(
Johnson et al., 1995
, e.g.)

f(x\mid\mu,\sigma,\xi)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left\{1+\frac{\xi(x-\mu)}{\sigma}\right\}^{-\left(1+1/\xi\right)}I_{[\mu,\infty)}(x),

(1)

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is the location parameter,
\sigma>0
is the scale parameter and
\xi\geq 0
is the shape/tail parameter that has been constrained to nonnegative values so that the density has no bounded upper support. When
\xi=0
the density (
1
) reduces to a shifted exponential
f(x\mid\mu,\sigma)=\exp\{-(x-\mu)/\sigma\}/\sigma
. For
\xi<0
the GPD density (
1
) remains valid, but the support is constrained to the interval
[\mu,\mu-\sigma/\xi]
.

The density (
1
) belongs to the regular variation class if
\xi\geq 1
, that is, in this case it is considered to have a heavy tail. For smaller values of
\xi
the tail becomes less heavy, in fact, for
\xi<1
the first moment exists taking the value
\mbox{E}(X)=\mu+\sigma/(1-\xi)
, for the second moment to exist we need a smaller value
\xi<1/2
, in which case
\mbox{Var}(X)=\sigma^{2}/\{(1-\xi)^{2}(1-2\xi)\}
. For the third and fourth moments to exist, we need
\xi<1/3
and
\xi<1/4
, respectively, and so on. In the limit, when
\xi\to 0
, the moment generating function exists, which means that all moments exist and the model has a light tail. In summary, the heaviness of the tail is controlled by the shape/tail parameter
\xi
.

The GPD density is closely related to a gamma-gamma (
GG
) density. This is defined
(
Nieto-Barajas, 2025
, e.g.)
as follows. Let
X\mid Y\sim\mbox{Ga}(\gamma,y)
and
Y\sim\mbox{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
, where the gamma density is parametrised in terms of shape and rate, that is,
\mbox{E}(Y)=\alpha/\beta
. Then marginally,
X\sim\mbox{GG}(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
with nonnegative parameters
\gamma,\alpha
and
\beta
. In practice
\gamma
is associated to a sample size and therefore usually takes positive integer values, but the model is well defined for nonnegative values. The GG density is given by

f(x\mid\gamma,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\gamma)}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\frac{x^{\gamma-1}}{(\beta+x)^{\alpha+\gamma}}I_{[0,\infty)}(x).

(2)

The GG density has no moments if
\alpha\leq 1
, so it is of regular variation and therefore it has a heavy tail. For
\alpha>1
the tail becomes lighter. The first moment
\mbox{E}(X)=\beta\gamma/(\alpha-1)
exists for
\alpha>1
, and the second moment
\mbox{Var}(X)=\beta^{2}\gamma(\gamma+\alpha-1)/\{(\alpha-1)^{2}(\alpha-2)\}
exists for
\alpha>2
. In general, the
k^{th}
moment exists for
\alpha>k
.

It is not difficult to prove that a
\mbox{GG}(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
with
\gamma=1
simplifies to a GPD with parameters
\mu=0
,
\sigma=\beta/\alpha
and
\xi=1/\alpha
. To make a complete connection between models (
1
) and (
2
) we require a location parameter
\mu
that shifts the GG density. For that we take
X-\mu\mid Y\sim\mbox{Ga}(\gamma,y)
and
Y\sim\mbox{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
. In this case, the marginal density for
X
is

f(x\mid\mu,\gamma,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\gamma)}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\frac{(x-\mu)^{\gamma-1}}{(\beta+x-\mu)^{\alpha+\gamma}}I_{[\mu,\infty)}(x).

(3)

We will refer to this density as shifted gamma-gamma and denote it as
\mbox{SGG}(\mu,\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
, where
\mu
is the location parameter,
\gamma
is the shape parameter,
\alpha
is the tail parameter and
\beta
the scale parameter. In this case, the SGG model (
3
) generalises both the GPD model (
1
) and the GG model (
2
). Specifically,
\mbox{SGG}(0,\gamma,\alpha,\beta)\equiv\mbox{GG}(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
and
\mbox{SGG}(\mu,1,\alpha,\beta)\equiv\mbox{GPD}(\mu,\beta/\alpha,1/\alpha)
.

The role played by the different parameters
(\mu,\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
, in the
SGG
model (
3
), is shown in Figure
1
. In the top left panel we take varying
\mu\in\{0,3,6\}
and fixed
\gamma=\alpha=\beta=1
; here
\mu
is a location parameter that shifts the whole density to the right for larger values. In the top right panel, we take varying
\gamma\in\{0.5,1,3\}
and fixed
\mu=0
,
\alpha=\beta=1
; here
\gamma
is a shape parameter, for
\gamma<1
the density has a vertical asymptote at zero, for
\gamma=1
the density takes the value of one at zero, and for
\gamma>1
the density pushes the mass away from zero inducing a positive mode. In the bottom left panel, we take varying
\alpha\in\{0.5,1,3\}
and fixed
\mu=0
,
\gamma=3
and
\beta=1
; here
\alpha
is a tail parameter that induces a lighter tail for
\alpha>1
and a heavy tail for
\alpha\leq 1
. Finally, in the bottom right panel, we take varying
\beta\in\{0.5,1,3\}
and fixed
\mu=0
,
\gamma=3
and
\alpha=1
; here
\beta
is a scale parameter where smaller/larger values produce a faster/slower decay.

3
Mixture model

There are different classes of bayesian noparametric priors, but most of them are almost surely (a.s.) discrete. This feature, combined in a mixture model, has recently been exploited for modelling purposes because it can be used to identify underlying groups in the data
(
Nieto-Barajas and Contreras-Cristán, 2014
, e.g.)
. On the other hand, recent studies made by
Palacios et al., 2022
show that the normalised stable process
(
Kingman, 1975
)
has heavy tails and can also be used as a mixing distribution for modelling heavy tail data.

The N-stable process admits two representations, it can be seen as normalised random measure
(
Barrios et al., 2013
, e.g.)
or as a stick breaking prior
(
Ishwaran and James, 2001
, e.g.)
. In general, an a.s. discrete random probability measure
G
can be written as
G(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}W_{j}\delta_{Z_{j}}(\cdot)
, where
\{W_{j}\}
is a sequence of nonnegative random variables such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}W_{j}=1
a.s.,
\{Z_{j}\}
is a sequence of random variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}
, and
\delta_{Z}(\cdot)
is the dirac measure.
G
is said to be a N-stable process with index
\nu\in(0,1)
and centering measure
G_{0}=\mbox{E}(G)
, denoted as
G\sim\mbox{NS}(\nu,G_{0})
, if for
j=1,2,\ldots
the random locations are
Z_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}G_{0}
, and the random weights are:
W_{j}=U_{j}^{-1/\nu}/\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}U_{k}^{-1/\nu}
, where
U_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}T_{i}
and
T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Ga}(1,1)
(normalised form); or equivalently,
W_{j}=V_{j}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(1-V_{k})
with
V_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Be}(1-\nu,j\nu)
(stick breaking form), where
\mbox{Be}(\alpha,\beta)
denotes a beta distribution with mean
\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)
.

If we have realisations from an NS process, say
\theta_{i}\mid G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}G
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
G\sim\mbox{NS}(\nu,G_{0})
, then marginally
\theta_{i}\sim G_{0}
, and the joint (predictive) distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\theta_{i}\}
can be represented as a generalised Pólya urn of the following form
(
Pitman, 1995
)

f(\theta_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i})=\frac{\nu m_{i}}{n-1}g_{0}(\theta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\frac{n_{j,i}^{*}-\nu}{n-1}\delta_{\theta_{j,i}^{*}}(\theta_{i}),

(4)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i}
denotes the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that excludes
\theta_{i}
,
(\theta_{1,i}^{*},\ldots,\theta_{m_{i},i}^{*})
denote the unique values in
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i}
, each occurring with frequency
n_{j,i}^{*}
for
j=1,\ldots,m_{i}
, which satisfy the condition
\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}n_{j,i}^{*}=n-1
, and
g_{0}
is the density associated with
G_{0}
. The number of unique values
m
in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is controlled by
\nu
, where larger
\nu
produces larger
m
.

We are now in a position to write our mixture model for the analysis of heavy tail data. Let
\theta_{i}=(\mu_{i},\gamma_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
be a four dimensional parameter vector, then we assume

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid\theta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{SGG}(\mu_{i},\gamma_{i},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\mid G

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{iid}}}}{{\sim}}G,\quad i=1,\ldots,n

(5)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\sim\mbox{NS}(\nu,G_{0}),

where

g_{0}(\theta)=\mbox{Ga}(\mu\mid a_{\mu},b_{\mu})\mbox{Ga}(\gamma\mid a_{\gamma},b_{\gamma})\mbox{Ga}(\alpha\mid a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha})\mbox{Ga}(\beta\mid a_{\beta},b_{\beta}).

(6)

Since the N-stable prior induces ties in the
\theta_{i}
’s, the number of mixture components
m
, in the mixture, depends on
\nu
and the hyper parameters
a_{\kappa},b_{\kappa}
for
\kappa\in\{\mu,\gamma,\alpha,\beta\}
. The idea is to choose these hyper parameters so that we have few mixture components where some of them are associated to model the bulk (small
\mu_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}>1
) and few others the tail (large
\mu_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}\leq 1
) of the data. In the presence of heavy tails, the location parameter
\mu_{j}
will determine the value of the support from where a data point is considered extreme. If the data do not admit heavy tails, all
\alpha_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
will be greater than one. The shape and scale parameters
\gamma_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
are of lesser importance but allow for greater flexibility in the model.

4
Posterior characterisation

There are different ways of characterising the posterior distribution of model (
5
). Via a conditional sampler that is based on obtaining the posterior law of the process
G
, as in
Barrios et al., 2013
, and via the marginal sampler which is based on marginalising the stochastic process
G
, as in
Favaro and Teh, 2013
. Here we propose the latter, since our interest is to characterise the clustering structure induced by the model and to potentially identify those groups associated to model either the bulk or the tail of the data.

The likelihood of the model is defined by the first equation in (
5
). However, as also noted in Section
2
, the SGG model can be obtained as the marginal distribution for
X_{i}
of the joint for
(X_{i},Y_{i})
given by
X_{i}-\mu_{i}\mid\mu_{i},\gamma_{i},Y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Ga}(\gamma_{i},y_{i})
and
Y_{i}\mid\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize{ind}}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Ga}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Therefore, the posterior becomes simpler if we consider the augmented likelihood for
({\bf X},{\bf Y})\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}
given by

f({\bf x},{\bf y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{Ga}(x_{i}-\mu_{i}\mid\gamma_{i},y_{i})\mbox{Ga}(y_{i}\mid\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}).

(7)

The posterior distribution is obtained proportionally by the product of (
7
) and (
4
). Therefore, the posterior conditional distributions for each
\theta_{i}
are given by

f(\theta_{i}\mid{\bf x},{\bf y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i})\propto\nu\,m_{i}\,g_{0}(\theta_{i})f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}(n_{j,i}^{*}-\nu)\delta_{\theta_{j,i}^{*}}(\theta_{i})f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{i}),

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Since
g_{0}
is clearly not conjugate with respect to the likelihood, to sample from these posterior conditional distributions, we resource to the generalisation of
Neal, 2000
’s Algorithm 8, also suggested by
Favaro and Teh, 2013
. Initialise the algorithm by sampling
\theta_{i}
values, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, from the marginal prior
g_{0}
, in (
6
). Then the algorithm proceeds as follows:

(i)

For each
X_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, sample a latent value
Y_{i}
from its conditional distribution

f(y_{i}\mid x_{i},\theta_{i})=\mbox{Ga}(y_{i}\mid\gamma_{i}+\alpha_{i},x_{i}-\mu_{i}+\beta_{i}).

(ii)

For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, sample
r
auxiliary values
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}=\{\theta_{m_{i}+1}^{\star},\ldots,\theta_{m_{i}+r}^{\star}\}
from
g_{0}
, given in (
6
). Values not selected in step (iii) can be reused.

(iii)

Draw
\theta_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, from

\hskip-312.9803ptf(\theta_{i}\mid{\bf x},{\bf y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star})=

\hskip 56.9055pt\frac{1}{c_{i}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}(n_{j,i}^{*}-\nu)f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{j,i}^{*})\delta_{\theta_{j,i}^{*}}(\theta_{i})+\sum_{j=m_{i}+1}^{m_{i}+r}\left(\frac{\nu m_{i}}{r}\right)f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{j}^{\star})\delta_{\theta_{j}^{\star}}(\theta_{i})\right],

where
c_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}(n_{j,i}^{*}-\nu)f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{j,i}^{*})+\sum_{j=m_{i}+1}^{m_{i}+r}(\nu m_{i}/r)f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{j}^{\star})
.

(iv)

Compute the unique values
(\theta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\theta_{m}^{*})
in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and re-sample each
\theta_{j}^{*}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
from

f(\theta_{j}^{*}\mid\mbox{data})\propto g_{0}(\theta_{j}^{*})\prod_{\{i:\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}^{*}\}}f(x_{i},y_{i}\mid\theta_{j}^{*}),

where
data
are the data points assigned to the same cluster.

The number of clusters in the nonparametric mixture
5
is determined by the normalised stable prior. In particular, small/large values of
\nu
produce small/large number of clusters. Instead of fixing it a-priori, we assign a hyper-prior of the form
\nu\sim\mbox{Be}(a_{\nu},b_{\nu})
. This prior is updated by the exchangeable partition probability function induced by the normalised stable process, which is given by

f(n_{1}^{*},\ldots,n_{m}^{*}\mid\nu)=\frac{(m-1)!}{\Gamma(n)}\nu^{m-1}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(n_{j}^{*}-\nu)}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}\right\}.

Therefore, we add a further step to the sampling algorithm as follows.

(v)

Sample a new value of
\nu
from

f(\nu\mid\mbox{data})\propto\nu^{a_{\nu}+m-2}(1-\nu)^{b_{\nu}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(n_{j}^{*}-\nu)}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}I_{(0,1)}(\nu).

Performing steps (i)–(iii) is straightforward, however, sampling from (iv) and (v) require Metropolis-Hastings steps
(
Robert and Casella, 2010
, e.g.)
, due to the nonconjugacy between the sampling distribution and the centering measure.
We suggest using a random walk proposal distribution for each element of
\theta_{j}
and for
\nu
. Let
\vartheta\in\{\mu_{j}^{*},\gamma_{j}^{*},\alpha_{j}^{*},\beta_{j}^{*},\nu\}
. At iteration
(t+1)
draw a proposal
\vartheta
from
\mbox{Un}(\max\{\vartheta^{(t)}-\delta_{\vartheta},0\},\min\{\vartheta^{*(t)}+\delta_{\vartheta},B_{\vartheta}\})
and accept it with probability
\min\{1,f(\vartheta\mid\mbox{data})/f(\vartheta^{(t)}\mid\mbox{data})\}
. Here,
B_{\vartheta}
is an upper bound that takes the values:
B_{\vartheta}=\min\{x_{i}:\theta_{i}=\theta_{j}^{*}\}
for
\vartheta_{j}=\mu_{j}^{*}
;
B_{\vartheta}=1
for
\vartheta_{j}=\nu
; and
B_{\vartheta}=\infty
for the other three parameters.

Parameters
\delta_{\vartheta}
are tuning parameters that control the acceptance probability. Instead of fixing them, we suggest adapting them every certain number of iterations to achieve a target acceptance rate. We consider as target interval
[0.3,0.4]
which, according to
Robert and Casella, 2010
, defines optimal acceptance rates. Specifically, we use batches of 50 iterations and for each batch
b
, we compute the acceptance rate
AR_{\vartheta}^{(b)}
and decrease
\delta_{\vartheta}^{(b+1)}=\delta_{\vartheta}^{(b)}(1.1)^{-\sqrt{b}}
if
AR_{\vartheta}^{(b)}<0.3
and increase
\delta_{\vartheta}^{(b+1)}=\delta_{\vartheta}^{(b)}(1.1)^{\sqrt{b}}
if
AR_{\vartheta}^{(b)}>0.4
. We use
\delta_{\vartheta}^{(1)}=1
as the starting value.

Note that the number of parameters
m
for
\mu_{j}^{*}
,
\gamma_{j}^{*}
,
\alpha_{j}^{*}
,
\beta_{j}^{*}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
, varies between iterations. For these, we use a single tuning parameter
\delta_{\vartheta}
and the acceptance rate
AR_{\vartheta}^{(b)}
is the average of acceptance rates in batch
b
for all
j=1,\ldots,m
.

This algorithm was implemented in Fortran and the code is available upon request.

5
Numerical analyses

5.1
Simulation Study

The objective of this study is two fold. First, test the posterior MCMC algorithm outlined in Section
4
and second, prove that under a control scenario, the model is able to determine the bulk and the tail of the data appropriately.

For that, let us consider a two component mixture of SGG densities of the form

f(x)=\pi\mbox{SGG}(x\mid\mu_{1},\gamma_{1},\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})+(1-\pi)\mbox{SGG}(x\mid\mu_{2},\gamma_{2},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}),

where
\pi=0.7
,
\mu_{1}=0
,
\gamma_{1}=3
,
\alpha_{1}=3
,
\beta_{1}=2
,
\mu_{2}=5
,
\gamma_{2}=1
,
\alpha_{1}=0.5
,
\beta_{1}=3
. The resulting density is presented as the dashed line in Figure
5
. It shows a bimodal behaviour with the first mode around one and the second mode at five. The first mixture component has a light tail
(\alpha_{1}=3)
, where the first two moments exist, and the second component has a heavy tail
(\alpha_{2}=0.5)
.

We took a sample of size
n=500
and fitted our model with the following prior specifications:
a_{\mu}=b_{\mu}=1/2
,
a_{\gamma}=b_{\gamma}=1/2
,
a_{\alpha}=b_{\alpha}=1/2
and
a_{\beta}=b_{\beta}=1/2
. For the parameter
\nu
, of the normalised stable process, we took several values to compare. Specifically,
\nu\in\{0.5,0.1,0.05,0.01\}
. Additionally, we also consider a beta hyper-prior with parameters
(a_{\nu},b_{\nu})=\{(1,1),(1/2,1/2)\}
to compare.

The MCMC algorithm was run for
15,000
iterations, a burn in of
1,000
and a thinning of
4
, i.e., keeping one of every fourth iteration to compute posterior summaries. Running times are reported in the last column of Table
1
and go from
2.67
to
3.50
minutes, which is quite fast.

We assess the performance of our adapting MH tuning parameters
\delta_{\vartheta}
by looking at the acceptance rates per batch, whose size was defined by
50
iterations. Figure
2
shows the acceptance rates for parameters
\mu_{j}^{*}
and
\alpha_{j}^{*}
. For the first parameter, the acceptance rates oscillate around the target interval
[0.3,0.4]
, shown as horizontal dotted lines. For the second parameter, we can see that for the first few batches, the acceptance rate is below the target, but after the 20th or so batches the acceptance rate oscillates around the target for the rest of the batches.

Comparison among the different fittings is made using the logarithm of the pseudo marginal likelihood (LPML) proposed by
Geisser and Eddy, 1979
. This is defined as
\mbox{LPML}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\mbox{CPO}_{i})
, where
\mbox{CPO}_{i}=f(x_{i}\mid{\bf x}_{-i})
. These CPO’s can be easily approximated via Monte Carlo as follows. Given a posterior sample
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(l)}
and a sequence of latent variables
y_{i}^{(l)}
for
l=1,\ldots,L
, then

\widehat{\mbox{CPO}}_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{1}{f(x_{i},y_{i}^{(l)}\mid\theta_{i}^{(l)})}\right)^{-1}.

(8)

The fitting measures for the different values of
\nu
and the two choices of hyper-prior distributions are presented in Table
1
. In the same table, we report the posterior mode of the number of groups in the BNP mixture as well as the running times. The best fitting is obtained when
\nu=0.5
but at a cost of using a larger number of groups than needed. The fitting worsens monotonically for
\nu=0.1
and

6
Concluding remarks

We have introduced a kernel based on the shifted gamma-gamma distribution. This is a very flexible model with four well identified parameters associated to location, shape, tail, and scale. In spite of the versatility of the kernel, we propose a normalised stable process as mixing measure over the four dimensional parameter vector to define a bayesian nonparametric mixture model for the analysis of heavy tail data.

Due to the form of the likelihood, there are no conjugate prior distributions for the model parameters. We managed to slightly simplify the likelihood by expressing it as a mixture of a conditional shifted gamma and a marginal gamma for the rate parameter. When combining the likelihood with the normalised stable process prior, we obtained a non conjugate marginal sampler which required a Metropolis-Hastings step. To optimise the proposal distribution, we introduced an efficient adapting mechanism that achieves a desire acceptance rate.

The code was implemented in Fortran in an Intel Xenon computer at 3.00 GHz and with 24GB of RAM. However, all calls to the executable file were made through the R Statistical Computing language.

Future work includes the inclusion of covariates in the analysis in a regression fashion and the study of multivariate heavy tail data.
