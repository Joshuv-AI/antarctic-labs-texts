Grilled nux vomica alleviates myasthenia gravis by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway: a study in a mice model
Authors: Chao Qiu, Liping Zhang, Jingya Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrilled Nux Vomica (GNV) is a promising traditional Chinese medicine to treat myasthenia gravis (MG), but its effects and mechanisms need further exploration.MethodsExperimental autoimmune MG (EAMG) model was established by muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) induction on C57BL/6 J mice. Mice were treated with GNV and/or ruxolitinib (JAK2 inhibitor) or AG490 (STAT3 inhibitor) for 30 days via gavage after modeling and randomized into 7 groups: control, model, low-dose GNV, middle-dose GNV, high-dose GNV, GNV + ruxolitinib, GNV + AG490. Body weight, muscle strength, clinical score, MuSK level, neuromuscular junction integrity (agrin and acetylcholine receptor [AChR] levels), inflammatory factors (IL-2 and IL-6), and the activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway were measured and compared between groups.ResultsGNV significantly improved body weight and muscle strength, as well as reduced clinical scores, MuSK levels, and inflammatory markers (IL-2 and IL-6) levels compared with untreated EAMG mice. GNV also protected the neuromuscular junction and increased agrin and AChR co-expression in a dose-dependent manner. In addition, GNV attenuated the levels of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3, which are aberrantly upregulated in EAMG mice. After co-treatment with ruxolitinib or AG490, the effect of GNV on body weight, muscle strength, clinical score, MuSK level, neuromuscular junction integrity, levels of inflammatory factors, and JAK2/STAT3 pathway was further amplified in EAMG mice.ConclusionsGNV improves MG by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, which might be an effective therapeutic strategy for MG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02100-2.Keywords: Myasthenia gravis, Grilled nux vomica, JAK2/STAT3 pathway, Neuromuscular disorder, Inflammation

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a disorder of neuromuscular transmission characterized by muscle weakness and fatigue [1]. The development of MG involves complex interactions between innate and adaptive immunity and is influenced by a combination of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors [2, 3]. The prevalence of MG in the general population is estimated to be between 70 and 320 cases per million, with a higher incidence in women than in men, particularly affecting women under 50 years of age [4]. MG is an autoimmune disease, driven by T-cell-dependent and B-cell-mediated responses. It is characterized by the presence of pathogenic serum autoantibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), or lipoprotein-related protein 4 (LRP4) in the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction [5]. MuSK-MG is a specific subtype of MG, accounting for approximately 10% of all MG cases [6]. MuSK antibodies predominantly belong to the IgG4 subclass, which can disrupt MuSK-LRP4 binding and inhibit protein function [7].

Currently, the main treatments for MG include acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory therapies [8, 9]. Due to the clinical heterogeneity of MG and varied treatment responses, there is an imperative need for personalized therapeutic approaches [10]. In clinical practice, corticosteroids, rituximab, and plasma exchange have effectively treated MuSK-MG [11]. However, many patients often require long-term immunosuppression, increasing the risk of opportunistic infections, osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, and permanent disability [11]. Therefore, there is a need to discover novel drugs with fewer side effects for treating MuSK-MG.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has significant potential as a natural medicine for treating MG, offering high efficacy with minimal adverse effects [12, 13]. Among these TCM remedies, Nux Vomica (NV), derived from the seed of Strychnos nux-vomica L. (Loganiaceae), has various biological functions, such as analgesia, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, inhibition of pathogenic microorganisms, and immune regulation [14]. In particular, a recent study has highlighted the potential of grilled NV (GNV) to attenuate inflammation in experimental autoimmune MG (EAMG) rats by inhibiting the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway [15]. GNV reduced AChR antibody levels in EAMG rats [16]. The prior network pharmacology study indicated that GNV could potentially treat MG by downregulating EGFR, TP53, and AKT1 and upregulating MYC levels [17]. These findings provide valuable evidence of the clinical value of GNV in MG treatment.

The Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is an intracellular signal transduction pathway involved in various physiological processes including cell differentiation and proliferation, inflammation, synaptic transmission, and tumorigenesis [18, 19]. JAK2 inhibitors mitigated the immune response and improved clinical symptoms in EAMG rats, highlighting the pivotal role of JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway dysregulation in autoimmune disorders [20]. Previous studies have highlighted the crucial role of STAT3 in the progression of MG. For example, glucocorticoids have been found to protect against MG by modulating the interleukin (IL)-21/STAT3/Blimp-1 signaling pathway in B cells [21]. Similarly, the use of caspase-1 inhibitors has been shown to suppress humoral immune responses in EAMG rats by targeting the IL-6-STAT3-Bcl-6 pathways [22]. However, the specific mechanism of action of GNV in MG, primarily its association with the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, remains unclear.

This study evaluates the therapeutic potential of GNV in a mouse model of MuSK-induced EAMG. Furthermore, the research conducted an in-depth analysis of the therapeutic mechanisms of GNV, with a specific focus on its potential involvement in the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. The findings of this investigation may offer valuable information on the use of GNV as a possible natural medicine for the treatment of MG.

Materials and methods

Establishment of EAMG model in mice

Male C57BL/6 J mice, 8 weeks old and weighing approximately 20 g, were obtained from HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China) and kept under standardized conditions (21 °C, humidity 45–65%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. The EAMG model was established in mice as previously described [23]. Mice were anesthetized with 50 mg/kg of pentobarbital sodium and then subcutaneously injected with 40 μg of MuSK (HY-P71568, MCE) emulsified in PBS and Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) (1:1, 200 μL/mouse) at the hind footpads. MuSK booster injections (40 μg) were administered after 30 days.

Determination of median effective dose (ED50) of GNV in EAMG mice

Thirty-five C57BL/6 J mice were randomly divided into seven groups for establishing EAMG models. Seven doses including 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 mg/kg GNV were given to the seven groups of mice after the modeling for 30 days. Clinical scores were measured for the calculation of ED50 value of GNV. The percentages of the number of mice with a clinical score less than 1 were recorded for the calculation of ED50.

Drug treatment

Another 56 C57BL/6 J mice were randomly divided into seven groups (n = 8): control, model, low-, middle-, high-dose GNV, high-dose GNV + ruxolitinib (JAK2 inhibitor), and high-dose GNV + AG490 (STAT3 inhibitor) groups. GNV (#170518) was prepared into capsules by Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine through the process of extraneous material removing, soaking, stir-frying, drying, powdering, and disinfecting, with each capsule containing 0.2 g of GNV. Low- (50 mg/kg), middle- (150 mg/kg), and high-dose (250 mg/kg) GNV was dissolved in 0.9% physiological saline for gavage administration [15]. The control and model groups were gavaged with an equal volume of 0.9% physiological saline. Furthermore, EAMG mice in the GNV + ruxolitinib group were administered 250 mg/kg GNV and 60 mg/kg ruxolitinib via gavage. EAMG mice in the GNV + AG490 group were administered 250 mg/kg GNV and injected intraperitoneally with 5 mg/kg of AG490. Mice in each group was treated once a day for a duration of 30 days. All animal experiments were approved by the ethical committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University and were conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (IACUC-20201109-02).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were taken from the mice through the tail vein every 10 days during the treatment. Following the manufacturer's instructions, MuSK serum titers were measured using an ELISA kit ((ml002038, Mlbio, Shanghai, China). Furthermore, on the 30th day after treatment, the serum levels of IL-2 (ml063136-J, Mlbio) and IL-6 (ml063159, Mlbio) were also measured using corresponding ELISA kits. The optical density at 450 nm was detected using a microplate reader (DR-3518G, Hiwell Diatek, Wuxi, China), and the results were calculated based on the established standard curves.

MG assessment

Following the establishment of the EAMG model, the mice's body weight was measured every week. Evaluation of symptoms, grip strength, and muscle strength occurred every 14 days. To assess the initial symptoms of EAMG, mice were placed on a flat platform for 3 min, and their mobility was observed while they were dragging their tails across a grid 20 times. The clinical score was classified on a scale of 0 to 4 as follows: 0 indicated normal posture, muscle strength, and mobility; 1 indicated normal posture before exercise and after exercise, hunched posture, restricted mobility, and difficulty raising the head (hang-time < 60 s); 2 suggested grade 1 symptoms without exercise (hang-time < 30 s); 3 indicated a dehydrated and moribund state with grade 2 symptoms (weight loss > 15%, suspension time = 0 s); and 4 signified death [24]. The grip strength of the mice was measured by pulling them off the grid after exercise using a dynamometer. Muscle strength was evaluated using an inverted screen test, in which mice were allowed to hang upside down from a wire mesh screen until they fell or until 300 s had elapsed. After evaluation, the mice were anesthetized and sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and their tendon tissues were removed for subsequent tests.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The pathological morphology of the neuromuscular junction was observed using TEM. The tissue samples were first fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 48 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for another 2 h at 4 °C. After fixation, the tissue samples were dehydrated with graded acetone, embedded in EPON812 medium, and sliced into sections measuring 50–70 nm using the Leica CM1900 Cryostat (Leica, Germany). Subsequently, the sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, sections were observed under a TEM microscope (JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan).

Immunofluorescence

Tendon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol and acetone, embedded in tissue-freezing medium, and cut into sections at a thickness of 20 μm. After 15 min of penetration with 0.2% Triton X-100, the sections were blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies [anti-Agrin (1:50; MA1-20378, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), anti-AChR (1:500; ab216485, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-p-JAK2 (1:1,000; ab32101, Abcam), and anti-p-STAT3 (1:500; ab76315, Abcam)] at 4 ℃ overnight. Subsequently, the sections were further incubated with secondary antibodies [goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; ab150083, Abcam) and goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200; ab150113, Abcam)] for 1 h at 37 ℃. Following counterstaining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindolefor 5 min in the dark, sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus, Japan).

Western blot

Total proteins in tendon tissues were extracted by lysing tissues in radio-immunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime) and quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, the protein samples were separated by 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at 25 °C. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies at the following dilutions: anti-JAK2 (1:5000; ab108596, Abcam), anti-STAT3 (1:2000; ab68153, Abcam), anti-p-JAK2 (1:10,000; ab32101, Abcam), anti-p-STAT3 (1:10,000; ab76315, Abcam), and anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:2500; ab9485, Abcam). The membranes were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000; ab6721, Abcam) for 1 h at 25 °C in darkness. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). The gray value was detected by a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA), and the protein level was quantified using GAPDH as an internal control.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. To compare the differences between different groups, one or two-way analysis of variance was conducted, followed by Tukey’s test for post hoc analysis. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GNV relieves MG symptoms in EAMG mice

To investigate the efficacy of GNV in MG, a MuSK-induced EAMG model was established in mice. This study first determined the ED50 of GNV by evaluating clinical scores of EAMG mice, to reveal appropriate dosages of GNV for the following interventions. The percentages of the number of mice with a clinical score less than 1 were recorded for the calculation of ED50. As shown in Figure S1, the ED50 of GNV calculated was equal to 102.5 mg/kg. Subsequently, low (50 mg/kg), middle (150 mg/kg), and high (250 mg/kg)-dose GNV were used to treat EAMG and evaluate therapeutic efficacy. The model group exhibited significantly lower body weight, grip strength, and inverted screen time than the control group, while EAMG mice showed significant recovery in each index in a dose-dependent manner after GNV treatment (P < 0.01, Figs. 1A–C). In contrast, the levels of clinical score, MuSK, and inflammatory factors (IL-2 and IL-6) were significantly higher in the model group than in the control group, and GNV treatment significantly reversed the abnormal upregulation of these indicators in EAMG mice (P < 0.01, Figs. 1D–F). Furthermore, EMG revealed a pathological decrease in muscle action in EAMG mice compared to control mice, but GNV treatment dose-dependently restored muscle action in EAMG mice (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G, H). These results suggested that GNV relieves MG symptoms with a dose-dependent manner in EAMG mice.

GNV relieves the pathological injury of the neuromuscular junction in EAMG mice

The pathological morphology of the neuromuscular junction was observed in EAMG mice using TEM. Compared to the control mice, the EAMG mice showed clear signs of pathological injury at the neuromuscular junction, with disrupted and irregularly arranged muscle fibers. However, the GNV intervention exhibited a dose-dependent alleviation of neuromuscular junction pathological injury in EAMG mice (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the co-expression of Agrin and AChR was assessed since Agrin-induced clustering of AChR is essential for forming the neuromuscular synapse. In Fig. 2B, the co-expression of Agrin and AChR was reduced in EAMG mice compared to control mice. However, with the administration of GNV at increasing concentrations, the co-expression of Agrin and AChR in EAMG mice gradually recovered.

Pathological injury of neuromuscular junction, and co-expression of Agrin and AChR in EAMG mice treated with different concentrations of GNV. A, pathological morphology was observed by TEM [scale bar = 2 µm (upper) and 1 µm (lower)]; B, co-expression of Agrin and AChR was detected by immunofluorescence (scale bar = 50 µm)

GNV inhibits the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in EAMG mice

Considering the significance of JAK2/STAT3 in the pathogenesis of MG, the effect of GNV on the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway was further explored. As shown in Fig. 3A, the protein expression of p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 was significantly raisedin the EAMG group compared to the control group (P < 0.01), indicating the activation of JAK2/STAT3 pathway. However, GNV treatment significantly decreased p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 protein expression in EAMG mice (P < 0.05). The intervention of ruxolitinib and AG490 further down-regulated p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 levels in GNV-treated EAMG mice, respectively (P < 0.01). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both ruxolitinib and AG490 enhanced the promoting effect of GNV on Agrin expression, as well as inhibiting effect of GNV on JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in EAMG mice (Fig. 3B, C). These findings were in line with the results obtained by Western blot analysis, which demonstrated the impact of GNV on the expression levels of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3.

A, protein expression of p-JAK2/JAK2 and p-STAT3/STAT3 was detected by western blotting; B, C, co-expression of Agrin and p-JAK2/p-STAT3 was detected by immunofluorescence (scale bar = 50 µm). **P < 0.01 vs. Control; #P < 0.05 and ##P < 0.01 vs. Model; ^^P < 0.01 vs. GNV

Inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway enhances the relieving effect of GNV on MG in EAMG mice

This study further investigated the therapeutic mechanism of GNV in MG, focusing on the involvement of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. In Fig. 4A, the body weight of GNV-treated EAMG mice was not significantly influenced by the intervention of ruxolitinib or AG490. However, both ruxolitinib and AG490 significantly augmented the beneficial effects of GNV on grip strength and inverted screen time in EAMG mice (P < 0.05, Figs. 4B and C). The inhibitory effects of GNV on clinical score, MuSK level, and IL-2 level were significantly potentiated by the intervention of ruxolitinib or AG490 (P < 0.05, Figs. 4D, F). Additionally, adding ruxolitinib or AG490 further enhanced the recovery effect of GNV on the muscle action of EAMG mice (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway induced by ruxolitinib or AG490 further increased the effects of GNV in relieving pathological injury to the neuromuscular junction (Fig. 5A) and promoting co-expression of Agrin and AChR in EAMG mice (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

MG is an archetypic neuromuscular junction disorder mediated by specific antibodies [25]. Among the various subtypes of MG, MuSK-MG is considered a severe form and often necessitates long-term immunosuppression for effective treatment. However, existing drugs used for treatment can cause strong side effects, highlighting the need for more suitable and safer alternatives for MuSK-MG patients [26]. This study revealed that the natural medicine GNV could relieve MG symptoms in MuSK-induced EAMG mice. Furthermore, the study shed light on the mechanism of action of GNV in MG, showing that it operates by blocking the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in the disease process.

Strychnos nux-vomica is a valuable TCM known for its rich and diverse composition, which includes alkaloids, iridoid glycosides, flavonoid glycosides, triterpenoids, steroids, and organic acids [14]. This combination of components bestows Strychnos nux-vomica with a wide range of pharmacological activities, rendering it beneficial for treating various health conditions including inflammation, microbial infections, bone health issues, irregular blood glucose, gastrointestinal disorders, nervous and cardiovascular system ailments, and cancer [27]. One of the most important medicinal components of Strychnos nux-vomica is NV, which is found in its seeds and plays a crucial role in providing analgesic effects [28–30]. In addition, NV promotes functional recovery of the sciatic nerve after mechanical injury by inhibiting oxidative stress [31], suggesting its potential in protection against neurological injuries.

In this study, the therapeutic potential of GNV against MG was investigated using a mouse model of MuSK-induced EAMG. The results demonstrated that GNV significantly improved the MG parameters, as well as alleviated the symptoms of neurological disorders associated with MG, particularly at a high dose of 250 mg/kg. Furthermore, this study examined pathological changes at the neuromuscular junction in EAMG mice and observed that GNV treatment mitigated such injuries, as evidenced by TEM analysis. Additionally, An experimental study based on the EAMG rabbit supported these findings and reported that GNV may have therapeutic effects on MG by improving transmission at neuromuscular junctions [32]. Additionally, this study also suggested that GNV restored decreased co-expression of Agrin and AChR in EAMG mice. As a crucial factor in neuromuscular transmission, the clustering of Agrin-induced AChR within the postsynaptic membrane is vital for proper neuromuscular function [33]. Recovery of this co-expression further indicates that GNV plays a role in restoring disturbed neuromuscular transmission in EAMG. These findings align with previous research reporting the therapeutic impact of GNV on EAMG by inhibiting AChR-ab and inflammatory mediators [15]. When compared to existing treatments such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, glucocorticoids, and intravenous immunoglobulin, GNV showcases superior tolerability and safety, attributed to its natural origin and fewer side effects [15, 30, 34].

The JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway is a well-conserved and fundamental intracellular cascade critical for regulating numerous genes responsible for cell survival, proliferation, differentiation, cell cycle control, angiogenesis, and inflammation [35]. Increasing evidence also suggests its crucial involvement in the nervous system, particularly in neural cell development, differentiation, and neuronal injury [18]. Through Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses, this study observed that GNV inhibited p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 protein expression in EAMG mice, suggesting that GNV exerts an inhibitory effect on the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Previous studies have shown that blocking the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway can reduce cerebral infarction volume and prevent neuron apoptosis after white matter injury [36]. Furthermore, this pathway has been implicated in synaptic plasticity in the brain, which can induce long-term depression dependent on N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors in the hippocampus [37]. Recent research has shown that glucocorticoids can reduce clinical severity and plasma cell frequency by regulating the IL-21/STAT3/Blimp-1 pathway [21]. Based on these findings, GNV may alleviate the symptoms of MG by blocking the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. To confirm this hypothesis, this study conducted feedback verification assays. As expected, when inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway using ruxolitinib (a JAK2 inhibitor) or AG490 (a STAT3 inhibitor), the relief effects of GNV on neurological disorder symptoms, inflammation (IL-2) levels, pathological injury to the neuromuscular junction, and disturbances in neuromuscular transmission (up-regulation of Agrin and AChR) were enhanced significantly in EAMG mice. These results provide additional evidence that inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway plays a crucial role in mediating the therapeutic capacity of GNV against MG.

However, this study presents some limitations. First, this study relies on a mouse model of EAMG, which may not fully represent the complexity of human myasthenia gravis. Second, the dose–response relationship and long-term safety profile of GNV remain further explored. Third, while focusing on the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, the study does not provide a comprehensive examination of all molecular mechanisms by which GNV exerts its effects. Follow-up studies are recommended to focus on the specific components of GNV and to explore their potentials and regulatory mechanisms in the treatment of MG.

Conclusions

GNV significantly relieves symptoms, pathological injury, and neuromuscular transmission in EAMG mice, indicating its promise as a potential medicinal drug for MG. The therapeutic benefits of GNV on MG depend on the blockade of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. More research is needed to identify the specific active components and dosage levels of GNV and explore the downstream genes associated with the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.
