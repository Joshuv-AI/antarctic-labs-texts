Unbiased cell clustering analysis of vaccine-induced T cell responses in the Imbokodo HIV-1 vaccine trial
Authors: Valentin Voillet, Janine van Duijn, Kanika Vanshylla, One B. Dintwe, Laura Pattacini, Faatima Laher Omar, Sharon Khuzwayo, Zelda Euler, Katharine Schwedhelm, Hugh R. MacMillan, Peter B. Gilbert, Andrew Fiore-Gartland, Evan W. Newell, Jenny Hendriks, M. Juliana McElrath, Daniel J. Stieh, Stephen C. De Rosa, Erica Andersen-Nissen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHIV-1 T cell responses are associated with viral control and may be protective in a prophylactic vaccination setting. Traditional methods for analysing these responses might be biased towards specific functionalities or epitopes. This study presents an unsupervised and unbiased clustering analysis workflow, using the Leiden algorithm followed by selection of antigen-specific clusters using MIMOSA positivity calls, for high-dimensional flow cytometry data to identify distinct T cell populations associated with protection in the HVTN 705/HPX2008/Imbokodo HIV-1 vaccine efficacy trial.MethodsParticipants were vaccinated with Ad26.Mos4.HIV (M0, M3) and Ad26.Mos4.HIV + Clade C gp140 (M6, M12), and a validated 28-colour intracellular cytokine staining assay was performed on PBMC isolated at M7, M13 and M24 in a pilot immunogenicity (n = 60) and case-control cohort (n = 283). 28-colour phenotyping assays were also performed on PBMC from the case-control cohort (n = 334).FindingsOur clustering analysis workflow allowed identification of vaccine-induced subpopulations of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing combinations of the 28 markers. Durable HIV-1 antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses were observed for up to 2 years, comprising mainly clusters of polyfunctional T cells expressing anti-viral cytokines and activation markers. Eight CD4+ and six CD8+ HIV-1 antigen-specific T cell clusters were induced by vaccination; only one CD4+ T cell cluster specific for Gag was mildly elevated in cases (acquiring HIV) compared to controls.InterpretationOur study introduces an innovative analysis approach to identify vaccine-induced T cell subpopulations in vaccine trials. Comparison of T cell clusters between cases and controls holds promise for improving the efficacy of future HIV-1 vaccination strategies.FundingThis work was funded by the 10.13039/100000060National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) and the Division of AIDS (DAIDS), both of the 10.13039/100000002US National Institutes of Health (NIH) [NIAID grants to the 10.13039/100029382HIV Vaccine Trials Network (HVTN) (10.13039/100005895Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center): UM1AI068618 (HVTN LC to M.J.M.) and UM1AI068635 (HVTN SDMC to P.B.G.)] and by Janssen Vaccines & Prevention B.V. Added value of this study

This work is a detailed study of T cell responses in the phase 2b Imbokodo efficacy trial (HVTN 705) that tested the heterologous vaccine regimen of Ad26.Mos4.HIV and aluminium-phosphate adjuvanted Clade C gp140 proteins vs. placebo to prevent HIV-1 acquisition in southern Africa among people assigned female sex at birth who had an increased likelihood of HIV acquisition. It provided the opportunity to study if subsets of T cells, other than the IFN-γ and/or IL-2 expressing cells that were assessed in the primary correlates analysis, were associated with the likelihood of HIV-1 acquisition. The unbiased clustering approach presented in this work ensured most markers that differentiated T cell subsets were considered for the analysis, rather than pre-specifying subsets of interest. To our knowledge, there is no such study employing clustering analysis combined with antigen-specific positivity calls to analyse flow cytometric ICS data. This is a study describing a method for analysing high-dimensional ICS data in a case-control study of an HIV-1 vaccine efficacy trial. The study found that several antigen-specific clusters of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were induced upon vaccination, and one previously unspecified cluster showed an association with HIV acquisition outcome in this study.

Implications of all the available evidence

An effective HIV-1 vaccine that can block transmission of the virus would be key to controlling the global epidemic. Despite the lack of protective efficacy in the Imbokodo trial, detailed evaluation of vaccine induced immune responses permits to test the hypothesis that protective immune responses were induced by the regimen. To understand the breadth and functionality of Ad26.Mos4.HIV plus gp140 vaccine induced T cell responses, we performed an unbiased UMAP clustering analysis on data derived from a 28-colour flow cytometry panel measuring T cell markers following HIV-1 Env, Gag and Pol stimulation.

The Leiden clustering method used here provides an unbiased cluster-based resolution across all functional and phenotyping markers, and in combination with statistical testing of each cluster compared to the negative control, focuses on those CD4+ and CD8+ T cell clusters that are antigen specific and likely vaccine induced. This method illustrates that several antigen-specific clusters expressing multiple inflammatory cytokines and activation molecules are induced upon vaccination and are responsive to Envelope peptide pools, and to a lesser extent also Pol and Gag peptide pools. Th2-like CD4+ and Tc2-like CD8+ T cell clusters were also observed. Longitudinal sampling shows durability of these clusters for up 2 years post vaccination. None of the clusters identified in this work were linked to protection from HIV-1 acquisition, although one cluster of CD4+ T cell expressing IL-2, TNF, CD154, CCR7 and PD-1 was enriched in case compared to control participants. Our approach to employing existing clustering methods to fully characterise and then focus on antigen-specific T cell profiles of interest can be implemented in other contexts to identify unique differences among vaccine candidates and to reveal T cell correlates of clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Preventing Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) acquisition through vaccination remains one of the main global health priorities.1,2 It is hypothesised that for an HIV-1 vaccine to be globally effective it should be able to induce a broad functional immune response against a wide spectrum of HIV-1 variants circulating worldwide.3,4 Effective T cell responses have previously been linked to lower viral load set points and viral control in natural HIV-1 acquisition.5, 6, 7 In prophylactic vaccination studies, several trials have demonstrated that cytokine-secreting, polyfunctional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells reduce viral loads in people living with HIV (PLWH), decrease the likelihood of HIV-1 acquisition, and are linked to vaccine-induced protection.8 Traditionally, T cell responses in HIV vaccine trials have been studied using ELISpot assays or flow cytometric analysis with pre-defined readouts of certain combinations of activation markers, which may introduce a bias in the analysis.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14

We tested a multivalent HIV-1 vaccine regimen comprised of a replication-incompetent adenovirus 26 (Ad26) vector encoding mosaic envelope (Env), group antigen (Gag) and polymerase (Pol) constructs in combination with an alum-adjuvanted glycoprotein 140 (gp140) protein. This regimen was tested in several clinical Phase 1 and 2a studies and demonstrated broad functional humoral and cellular immune responses.13,15 Unfortunately, the Imbokodo/HVTN 705 phase 2b efficacy study of this regimen failed to demonstrate any significant protection against HIV-1 acquisition in African people assigned as female sex at birth.16

The clinical development program investigated vaccine-induced T cell functionality mainly by traditional methods including IFN-γ ELISpot to Env, Gag and Pol Potential T cell Epitope (PTE) peptide pools, as well as IFN-γ and/or IL-2 expression as assessed by Intracellular Cytokine Staining (ICS) to both PTE and vaccine-matched pools through multi-colour flow cytometry9,13,14 Exploratory readouts included an analysis of the expression of CD154, TNF and IL-4/IL-13, as well as polyfunctionality assessed by combinatorial polyfunctionality analysis of antigen-specific T-cell subsets (COMPASS).14 A correlates of protection (CoP) analysis (associating immune variables with outcome of vaccination) in the Imbokodo efficacy trial did not provide evidence for linkage of the primary readout of IFN-y and/or IL-2 expressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to the likelihood of HIV acquisition.17 However, these cellular readouts were pre-specified and only explored cell subpopulations considered important a priori by the investigators. Therefore, other T cell subsets that could be of interest were not investigated. Of note, smaller subsets of highly polyfunctional Env-specific CD4+ T cells were associated with a reduced likelihood of HIV acquisition in the RV144 trial,18 indicating that lower frequency cell subsets still merit investigation. Given that the number of possible populations increases exponentially with the number of markers assessed, leading to too many combinations of markers to consider in a manual and prespecified manner, new methods are needed to identify new subpopulations in high dimensional data such as those generated by flow and mass cytometry.

As the 28-flow cytometry panel developed by the HVTN Laboratory Center allows for detection of many functional and activation markers on T cells, we sought to develop a high dimensional data analysis workflow to find and identify subsets of T cells induced by vaccination in an unbiased fashion to better understand Ad26.Mos4.HIV plus gp140 vaccine-induced T cell responses. Although several clustering algorithms have previously been developed and used for the analysis of high dimensional data such as PhenoGraph,19 FlowSOM20 and GigaSOM,21 we chose to combine a different clustering approach with antigen-specific positivity calls using the MIMOSA method22 to select and highlight HIV-specific clusters in an unbiased way. Our method involves an unsupervised clustering analysis, a statistical analysis that organises each measured cell into clusters based on similarity to other cells, using the Leiden algorithm,23 on the 28-marker flow cytometry staining panel measuring T cell responses following HIV-1 antigen stimulation. This allows us to ensure that all relevant markers that differentiate T cell subsets are considered, rather than pre-specifying subsets of interest as usually performed in traditional analyses. This work describes such a method for analysing high-dimensional ICS data in a case-control study of an HIV-1 vaccine efficacy trial and our method can be applied to other clinical trials going forward to elucidate new cellular subsets that could be critical for vaccine protection against HIV-1 or other pathogens.

Methods

Trial design and participants

The HVTN 705/HPX2008 (Imbokodo) trial (NCT03060629) was a phase 2b, multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study conducted in women in Malawi, Mozambique, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. Participants were randomised (1:1) to receive vaccine or placebo and followed for acquisition of HIV-1 for ≥2 years after enrolment. Participants in the treatment arm received Ad26.Mos4.HIV at Months 0, 3, 6, and 12, as well as aluminium-phosphate adjuvanted clade C gp140 (C97ZA) at Months 6 and 12. Placebo recipients received saline at Months 0, 3, 6, and 12. Participants were assessed for HIV-1/2 acquisition at Months 0, 3, 6, 7, 9, and every 3 months thereafter. Details on study design and oversight are described in the primary publication.16 The participant eligibility criteria for sampling into pilot and the case-control studies, including baseline characteristics of the cohorts, are described in the appendix of Kenny et al.17 and briefly detailed below.

Pilot selection

In total, 60 trial participants (n = 50 from active arm, n = 10 from placebo arm) were randomly selected from participants in the full immunisation set (receiving all 4 vaccinations within the respective visit windows) that reached the Month 27 visit, remained without HIV, and had samples available from Months 0, 7, 13, and 24 as well as data on BMI, country, and age.

Case-control selection

283 participants (n = 49 cases and n = 234 controls) were included in the per-protocol cohort. Cases were defined as participants who received vaccine or placebo and the primary endpoint of HIV-1 acquisition diagnosed after the Month 7 visit and before or at the Month 24 visit, with no evidence of HIV acquisition at the Month 7 visit or any earlier visit. Control participants were defined as having received vaccine or placebo and reached the Month 27 visit without acquiring HIV. Following the case and control definitions, all cases in the active arm and 6 randomly sampled cases from the placebo arm were included in the case-control study. Controls were randomly sampled in a 5:1 control:case ratio in the vaccine group and in a 1:1 control:case ratio in the placebo group from the pools of eligible controls. Controls were frequency-matched to cases on treatment (vaccine vs. placebo), region (Republic of South Africa [RSA] vs. non-RSA), and BMI (<25, ≥25 to <30, ≥30 kg/m2).

Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assays

ICS was performed on PBMC as previously described24 and analysed by lab staff blinded to treatment group. Briefly, previously cryopreserved PBMC were thawed, incubated overnight at 37 °C/5% CO2 in R10 media (RPMI 1640 [Gibco BRL Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA]) containing 10% FBS (Nucleus Biologics, San Diego, CA, USA), 2 mM l-glutamine (Gibco BRL Life Technologies), 100 U/ml penicillin G, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin sulphate (Gibco BRL Life Technologies)] and stimulated on Day 2 for 6 h at 37 °C with either the peptide pools listed in Supplementary Table S1(peptides of 15 amino acids overlapping in sequence by 11 amino acids), dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO, 0.5%; Sigma Aldrich; negative control) or staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB, 0.25 μg/ml; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; positive control) in the presence of costimulatory antibodies CD28 and CD49d (1 μg/ml, BD Biosciences) and brefeldin A (BFA, 10 μg/ml; Sigma Aldrich). Cells were incubated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, 2 mM; Life Technologies) overnight at 4 °C to allow adherent cells to detach,25 then stained with a 28-colour antibody staining panel (Supplementary Table S2A) and acquired on Day 3 on a BD FACSymphony A5 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analysed using FlowJo version 9.9.4 (FlowJo LLC).

Immune phenotyping assays

Phenotyping was performed using two phenotyping panels—one targeted at T cell phenotypes (Supplementary Table S2B) and another focused on myeloid cells that was built upon a previously published panel26 (Supplementary Table S2C). In brief, for both panels, PBMC were thawed and plated in a 96-well round bottom plate in preparation for staining. All staining steps and incubation steps were performed at room temperature in the dark and all wash steps were followed by centrifugation at 750×g for 3 min. PBMC were stained with viability dye for 20 min at room temperature, washed with FACS Wash (2% FBS in PBS), then stained with a cocktail of surface monoclonal antibodies prepared in FACS Wash for 20 min and washed twice with FACS Wash. The cells stained with the DC/Myeloid panel were then fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min followed by two wash steps with FACS Wash and a final resuspension in FACS Wash. Cells stained with the T cell panel were resuspended in 1× FOXP3 Fix/Perm buffer and incubated for 30 min prior to being washed twice with 1× FOXP3 Perm/Wash buffer. The cells were then stained with a cocktail of intracellular monoclonal antibodies prepared in 1× FOXP3 Perm/Wash buffer for 30 min followed by two washes with 1× FOXP3 Perm/Wash buffer before the final resuspension in FACS Wash.

Data processing (ICS)

Data filtering

During the assays and/or data analysis in FlowJo, specific markers may have been deemed unreliable for a whole sample or for a specific stimulation condition and were thus removed from the dataset. Data from a single stimulation were removed from the analysis if any marker was noted as unreliable. The total numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells must also have exceeded certain thresholds. If the number of CD8+ T cells was <5000 or CD4+ T cells was <10,000 for any of the HIV-1 peptide pools or for one of the negative control replicates for a particular sample, data for that stimulation was filtered.

FlowJo gating was performed for all markers included in the panel. For clustering analysis, the focus was placed on a single parent population such as CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. From those cells, we clustered only the cells that were positive by FlowJo gating for at least one of the following cytokines and/or functional activation markers (CD154, IL-2, IL-4/13, IFN-γ, TNF and MIP-1β), indicating a response to the stimulation. While expression of one marker may not be highly specific for an antigen-specific response, we preferred to be broadly inclusive of potentially responsive cells. MIP-1β was analysed in the case-control study panel only. CD45RA and CCR7 markers were used to define CD4+ and CD8+ T cell memory subsets with TNaïve CD45RA+CCR7+, TCM CD45RA−CCR7+, TEM CD45RA−CCR7−, and TEMRA CD45RA+CCR7−.

Data transformation

Compensated fluorescence intensity (FI) values from selected CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing at least one of the cytokines and/or functional activation markers were transformed in R. The arcsinh transformation was performed with the same co-factor (500) across batches for each marker (do.asinh (Spectre)).27 Then, an asymmetrical transformation was performed to stabilise variance in highly negative or positive FI in the arcsinh transformed distribution. The data were mean centred and scaled by standard deviation, prior to downstream analyses.

All markers, except the ones being used in the in the FlowJo gating hierarchy to identify the CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (/Time/Lv/K1/K2/K3/K4/K5/K6/K7/K8/14-/S/L/19-/3+/3+excl 16br/56-16-/4+ or 8+: UViD, CD14, CD19, CD3, CD56, CD16, CD4 and CD8), were used as input for clustering and UMAP visualisation.

Data analysis

Leiden clustering (ICS)

In both the pilot and case-control studies, all samples were used (two groups (P2 and T1)), three visit months (M7 in both studies, M13 and M24 in the pilot study only) and 14 stimulations (CMV, J Mos1 gp41, J Mos1 gp120, J Mos2S gp41, J Mos2S gp120, J Mos2 RNAseInt, J Mos2 Gag and NEGCTRL in both studies, as well as 97ZA012 gp41, 97ZA012 gp120, J Mos1 RT, J Mos1 RNAseInt, J Mos1 Gag and J Mos2 RT in the pilot study only), including the HIV-1 cases and non-cases in the case-control study, though the clustering was blinded to the case status indicator. Clustering was performed using all cells from all the samples combined.

Leiden clustering was performed in R, as described in the Rphenograph R package.19 The R package leiden was used for clustering (community detection) with n_iterations = 10 and by default parameters. It works in two steps1: creation of a network representing phenotypic similarities between cells by calculating the Jaccard coefficient between nearest-neighbour sets (k = 30)2; identification of communities using the Leiden algorithm.23

Leiden clustering (phenotyping)

A meta-clustering approach was developed to cluster cells from the monocyte, CD4+ and CD8+ T phenotyping datasets due to the high number of cells (more than 15 million). The following approach was taken1: for each batch, initial Leiden clustering was performed (as described above)2; sub-clustering of these initial clusters was run using the Leiden algorithm3; centroids of those sub-clusters from all batches were calculated (as being the median for each marker)4; and a final Leiden clustering using these centroids was performed. The results of this clustering were considered as the final Leiden clusters. Assignment of those final clusters at the single-cell level based on the sub-clusters used to calculate the centroids was then applied. The innovation to cluster then sub-cluster was taken to stabilise the over-clustering step, without altering the modularity formulation at the root of the Leiden algorithm's convergence on a specific clustering.28 The DC phenotyping dataset was clustered as described in the section above (Leiden clustering (ICS)).

Dimensionality reduction for visualisation

UMAP embeddings29 were calculated with the umap R function from the uwot R package with n_neighbors = 10 and min_dist = 0.1.

Cluster cell type annotation

Clusters were described using dot-plots colour-coded by MFI and proportional in size to the percentage of cells within each cluster expressing each of the markers. We used the marker gates determined by the lab (FlowJo gating) to tally cells that expressed or did not express each marker. Pearson's correlations were computed for cluster expression profiles as defined by FlowJo positivity (i.e., % positivity for each marker), and used for downstream hierarchical clustering and shown as dendrograms above each dot-plot. Pearson's correlations were thus used solely to define the order in which the clusters were displayed.

Statistics

HIV-specific positivity call

Positivity calls were made using the MIMOSA method22 by comparing the proportion of cells within the cluster in the HIV antigen stimulation compared to the negative control stimulation. MIMOSA was developed to test for differential biomarker expression using single-cell assays, and showed great sensitivity and specificity compared to more classical methods such as Fisher's exact test. For each sample and cluster this created a 2 × 2 table of counts (cluster member vs. non-member and antigen stimulation vs. negative control) that could be used as input for MIMOSA together with all samples in the analysis. MIMOSA estimated the posterior probability that the true abundance of cells in the cluster was greater in the stimulation vs. the negative control condition. Multiplicity adjustment was made within each antigen. For each individual, a cluster with FDR-q < 0.01 was considered as a positive, HIV-specific response to ensure that <1% of the clusters we labelled “vaccine-specific” were falsely labelled.

As an HIV-specific response criterion using data from vaccine recipients (cases, non-cases, and pilot), any cluster that had a response rate <20% was excluded; limiting the number of clusters helped preserve power for the analysis.

Vaccine-induced responses and visualisation

For each sample condition, we computed the proportion of cells within each cluster out of all cells in the parent population in that sample. Cells excluded from the clustering process (i.e., CD4+ T non-expressing cytokines and/or functional markers) were still included in the analysis, as they were included in the denominator when computing the fraction of cells in each cluster out of the whole parent population. When available, combined peptide pool responses and magnitudes (after background-subtraction) were calculated, including all pools regardless of positive or negative responder status. For the pilot study, in each combination of cluster, arm and stimulation, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests were run to compare the two visits (M13 and M24) against M7; and multiplicity adjustment (Benjamini and Hochberg procedure) was made over the number of selected clusters. p-values were two-sided.

For the case-control study, within each combination of stimulation and cluster, we compared the cases and controls in the vaccine group. Of the 54 participants in the PP vaccine arm of the trial, 49 were included in the analysis, representing 91% of the cases. This indicates that 91% of the cases had complete ICS data at Month 7. We acknowledge the potential for selection bias due to complete-case analysis, although the high data completeness (91%) and lack of predictive covariates for imputation suggest minimal impact on the results. The analyses mirrored the immune correlates analyses in HVTN 702/Uhambo, described in Moodie et al.30, which accounts for the stratified sampling design using the inverse probability sampling (IPS) weights from the per-protocol cohort.17 We used a Cox model of time to HIV diagnosis case status using the cluster frequencies as a predictor, adjusted for age (≤25, >25 years), BMI (<25, 25 ≤ BMI < 30, ≥ 30), region (South Africa (RSA), non-RSA) and a categorical baseline likelihood of HIV acquisition score for persons assigned female sex at birth to control for potential confounders (Supplementary Figure S1).31 The three-category score corresponded to the tertiles of the score using all participants from the protocol. The origin time and start time were at Month 7; whereas the end time was defined to be day 505 post the Month 7 visit.17 The median number of days of follow-up (interquartile range) from Month 7 to the first event of HIV diagnosis or right-censoring was 592 days (583–599, IQR). The probability of loss to follow-up by 24 months in the per-protocol vaccine cohort was 0.069 (95% CI 0.054 to 0.085). Neither treatment assignment nor demographics of vaccine recipients (indicator of enrolled in South Africa, numeric age, numeric BMI, numeric baseline risk score) predicted loss to follow-up, with all Cox model score test p-values above 0.10. While standard methods like fractional polynomials or regression splines32, 33, 34 could have been used to determine the optimal scale, we opted to categorise age due to the rationale that age ≤25 years vs. >25 years served as a strong discriminator of HIV-1 risk in the trial.17 An estimate of the association (coefficient from the regression model) and p-value were derived from the fitted model. In each antigen, multiplicity adjustment (Benjamini and Hochberg procedure) was applied across selected clusters. This model accounted for the primary time to HIV diagnosis outcome of the trial, and had the advantage that it could account for the fact that some participants were lost to follow-up, and the analysis used the information in differential follow-up times across participants.

Clustering analysis identifies highly polyfunctional CD4+ T cell clusters with a Th1-like phenotype that persist for up to 24 months

The Imbokodo trial immune correlates analysis included an immunogenicity pilot study17 to down-select the most relevant immunological readouts and timepoints to move forward in a subsequent case-control analysis. Of note, a validated 28-colour ICS assay was established for this trial (markers listed in Supplementary Table S2 and gating scheme in Supplementary Figure S2A), where linearity, accuracy and precision of the assay were determined to ensure reproducibility.17 Specific acceptance criteria were set to ensure only high-quality data were used for the T cell markers selected for CoP analysis.17 The original analysis of the pilot study data included expression only of IFN-γ and/or IL-2 in CD4+ or CD8+ T cells in a set of 50 vaccine and 10 placebo recipients to select peptide pools and timepoints for the case-control analysis.17 To gain deeper insight into the vaccine-induced T cell subsets and their functionality over time, we performed an unbiased cell clustering analysis for the pilot dataset on all flow cytometric measurements of the 28-colour panel, enabling a more in-depth study of the broad selection of cytokines and activation markers detected by the panel (Supplementary Figure S3). T cell markers were analysed at three time points post immunisation: M7 (4 weeks post 3rd vaccination), M13 (4 weeks post 4th vaccination), and M24 (12 months post 4th vaccination) for placebo and vaccine recipients in response to the peptide pools listed in Supplementary Table S1.

We chose to identify antigen-specific cells using cytokines and/or functional activation-induced markers in the panel: IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF, CD154 (CD40L) and IL-4/13 (the latter two detected in the same channel). We selected T cells expressing at least one of these markers for our analysis: an inclusive cut-off so as not to exclude any potentially reactive cells. Single-cell clustering was performed using the fluorescence intensity of each of the functional and phenotyping markers. For functional annotation of the clusters, we also made plots of the proportion of cells within each cluster positive for each of the markers based on the FlowJo gates from the primary data analysis (Supplementary Figure S2A); this was useful to characterise the expression profiles within each cluster numerically.

Antigen-specific T cell clustering shows a single elevated polyfunctional CD4+ T cell population in vaccinated cases compared to controls

Having established a clustering methodology and identified vaccine-induced T clusters, we next investigated whether any of the vaccine-induced clusters differed between cases (participants who acquired HIV after the 3rd vaccination up to M24 of the study, n = 49 (n = 44 vaccine, n = 5 placebo)) and controls (participants who did not acquire HIV up to M24, n = 234 (n = 229 vaccine, n = 5 placebo)).17 We selected the M7 timepoint for the case-control analysis, as vaccine efficacy was determined between M7 and M24 of the trial and thus sampling before the start of this window allowed for the inclusion of more breakthrough cases in the analysis, maximising the statistical power. Of note, 6 out of the 12 peptide pools were down-selected to be tested in the case-control cohort, based on having sufficient response rates/magnitudes, lower toxicity, and low response correlation with each other in the pilot study.17 To have optimal clusters for analysis of the case-control samples, we used the data generated from those samples to perform a new clustering, as opposed to using pre-specified clusters identified in the pilot study above. ICS data from all case-control samples were used for clustering, blinded to the case status indicator. Moreover, the ICS panel was expanded to include MIP-1β so this marker was included as input for UMAP visualisation and Leiden clustering.

Although our ICS assay enabled measurement of a variety of phenotypic markers that distinguished different T cell populations, we sought to further expand our evaluation using a 28-colour phenotyping panel to attempt to gain insight into additional differences between cases and controls. As baseline immune signatures have been linked to vaccine immunogenicity,41, 42, 43 we conducted T cell phenotyping at Month 0 as well as Month 7 to identify responses that may have differed between cases and controls. Previously cryopreserved PBMC were stained with the phenotyping panel listed in Supplementary Table S2B and shown in Supplementary Figure S2B. Due to the high number of cells detected by the phenotyping panels (no down-selection for antigen-specificity as in the ICS data), a meta-clustering approach based on the clustering approach described above was applied. This revealed 34 clusters in CD4+ T cells and 28 clusters in CD8+ T cells. Unfortunately, no meaningful significant differences were observed between cases and controls in any of these clusters at either baseline or post-vaccination (Supplementary Table S9).

A monocyte and a dendritic cell cluster differ between cases and controls at baseline

The baseline state of cells of the innate immune system can also influence vaccine responsiveness44 and therefore was of interest to investigate in this trial. Myeloid cell phenotyping was performed using the phenotyping panel listed in Supplementary Table S2C and Supplementary Figure S2C to identify phenotypically distinct clusters of dendritic cell (DC) and monocyte populations at Month 0. We identified 22 DC clusters and 18 monocyte cell clusters (Supplementary Figure S11). The comparison of cases vs. control samples showed very few significant differences in most DC and monocyte cell clusters, with two exceptions. DC cluster 5, which comprised classical DCs expressing CD32, CD86 (both >80%) and CD172a (>60%), was marginally higher in cases vs controls (p-value = 0.016 and FDR-q = 0.342) (Supplementary Table S10). In addition, monocyte cluster 5 that included cells expressing CD32 and CD86 (both >95%) was also marginally higher in cases vs controls (p-value = 0.006 and FDR-q = 0.099) (Supplementary Table S11). This may indicate that higher baseline levels of certain myeloid subpopulations were present in individuals who subsequently acquired HIV-1 during the trial period. Although we did not observe any strong significant differences between cases and controls, the clustering method developed for ICS also proved effective in identifying distinct populations in phenotyping panels and holds potential for application in future trials.

Discussion

Despite the lack of protective efficacy in the Imbokodo trial, detailed evaluation of vaccine-induced immune responses can provide insights into surrogate biomarkers for future vaccine trials. Sustained T cell immunity, in combination with neutralising antibody responses, is believed to be crucial for an effective HIV-1 vaccine.45 The unbiased clustering analysis of the 28-marker flow cytometry staining panels described in this study allowed the identification of several antigen-specific clusters of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells that were induced upon vaccination.

Several clustering methods have been developed by other investigators (e.g., FlowSOM20 and PhenoGraph19) and have demonstrated the advantages of unbiased clustering analysis for better interpretation of multidimensional single-cell cytometric data as opposed to pre-defined gating strategies. Although FlowSOM is a well-known tool and one of the top-performers for unsupervised clustering, it requires the number of clusters to be predetermined which is a caveat for exploratory analysis.28 We chose PhenoGraph for use in this study as it is both precise and coherent and can find meaningful refined subtypes,28 applying the Leiden method to the PhenoGraph algorithm for cluster detection. The Leiden algorithm is an extension of the Louvain algorithm that is used in PhenoGraph and shows excellent performance by uncovering better and well-connected communities through additional refining steps compared to Louvain.23 The Leiden algorithm used here provides an unbiased cluster-based resolution of antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations. Notably, increasing the number of staining colours beyond the 28 shown here could further improve clustering resolution of the data, while the use of a scRNA-seq based assay could offer even more detailed insights into cell clustering. However, one advantage of ICS over scRNA-seq is that there is an internal negative control for comparison to the antigen-stimulated cells. We thus combined this clustering approach with antigen-specific positivity calls using the MIMOSA method22 to select and highlight HIV-specific clusters in an unbiased way. In the context of an interventional clinical study such as a vaccine trial with a placebo group as a control, statistical analysis comparing placebo vs. vaccine recipients can further highlight those clusters that are vaccine-induced, especially if there is baseline positivity in the studied cohort.

The clustering methods described here are unbiased and only require the user-defined gates to identify the antigen-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to cluster. But, to better visualise and describe the characteristics of each cluster, the categorical positive/negative gating specification for each marker is subsequently applied to each cluster and thus provides a numerical measure of the proportion of cells within a cluster expressing that marker. Given the large number of markers in the ICS staining panel, our approach was to interpret the clusters first based on the cytokines and/or functional activation-induced markers upregulated in response to the antigen-specific stimulation, and this allowed us to classify the clusters based on polyfunctionality since this is commonly reported for ICS data. This was possible since for many of these markers, clusters were well distinguished as on/off for these functions. A second overall characterisation provided classification as naïve or one of three memory T cell subsets. Finally, the clusters as defined based on polyfunctionality and memory marker expression were then noted to be uniquely differentiated based on expression of the other cellular markers. Although the clustering resulted in a relatively large number of clusters, they were each unique when characterised in this way.

The phenotype of the CD4+ T cells in both pilot and case-control studies primarily consisted of central memory TCM cells with proliferative and robust recall response properties, or polyfunctional effector memory TEM cells. The presence of only a single Th2-like cluster indicates that the response was predominantly Th1-like with a high positivity rate of IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF expressing cells. Cytokine-expressing effector Th1 cells against Gag were previously shown to be maintained in HIV-1 controllers.46 Although we observed the largest number of antigen-specific clusters induced upon stimulation with envelope peptide pools, no unique clusters were observed for Gag and Pol peptide pool stimulations. Moreover, all peptide pool stimulations induced both Th1-like and Th2-like responses. Combinatorial expression of Th1 cytokines has been associated with better anti-viral responses47 and they are also upregulated in HIV-1 controllers.48,49 In the RV144 trial, polyfunctional T cell responses were linked to protection. We did not observe a strong increase in any clusters expressing high levels of CCR5 or the mucosal homing factor integrin β7, suggesting that the vaccine did not increase T cells that can potentially be infected more readily by HIV-1 at mucosal sites.50

Whereas we observed mainly TCM-like clusters in CD4+ T cells, within the CD8+ T cell compartment, mainly TEM and TTEMRA like clusters were observed. T cell populations in HIV controllers compared to non-controllers in a natural acquisition cohort demonstrated higher levels of CD4+ TCM cells and a higher rate of more differentiated TEM and TEMRA CD8+ T cells, indicating that these populations could be favourable in controlling HIV-1 infection.48,49,51,52 Effector memory CD8+ T cells expressing PD-1, IL2 and IFN-γ markers point towards high plasticity within the cells, and the IFN-γ, TNF and granzyme B are indicative of a more cytolytic effector phenotype. Cytotoxic polyfunctional CD8+ T cells are associated with better viral control as evidenced by higher proliferative and cytolytic capacity of these cells in persons who control HIV-1 compared with those whose illness progresses.53,54 Of note, there were several Pol-reactive CD8+ T cell clusters detected, but similar to CD4+ T cells, these overlapped with the Env-reactive clusters and there were no unique clusters identified as having positive responses to gag or pol (intracellular antigens) compared to env (extracellular antigen).

The study of the pilot cohort encompassing longitudinal sampling allowed for the characterisation of the kinetics of these clusters over time. We found that HIV-1 antigen-specific effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were induced in individuals immunised with the Ad26.Mos4.HIV plus gp140 vaccine regimen, several of which were also maintained up to 2 years after primary immunisation. For the HVTN 100 trial, immunisation with a subtype C pox-protein vaccine regimen showed more limited durability of some polyfunctional CD4+ T cells subsets in vaccinated individuals.55

When examining the case vs. control samples, the overall CD4+ and CD8+ T cell clustering patterns were similar between these samples and the pilot study. Cases and controls also exhibited a mainly early memory TCM or TEM CD4+ T cell response and a TEM or TEMRA CD8+ T cell response. Even though it is difficult to make clinical interpretations from the magnitude of model coefficients and this information should be treated carefully, in-depth analysis into cases vs. controls revealed one CD4+ T cell cluster, cluster 1 (IL-2+TNF+CD154+, both TEM and TCM, with some PD-1+ expression) as being marginally higher in cases vs. controls within the treatment arm for J Mos2 Gag. Based on their cytokine profile, cells in this cluster could represent highly plastic cells undergoing ongoing differentiation into effector memory cells. From the large body of research in people living with HIV, Gag-specific T cell responses have been linked to viral control.46,51,56, 57, 58 In contrast, we observed an increase in this polyfunctional T cell cluster in participants that went on to acquire HIV throughout the trial. Possibly, the moderate expression of PD-1+ (>40%) could indicate that a subset of the cells in this cluster are of an exhausted phenotype, which could explain their link to an increased likelihood of acquisition.35, 36, 37, 38, 39 Alternatively, the reduction in the periphery for controls compared to cases could indicate increased trafficking of these cells towards mucosal sites (via homing markers not measured in our study) where they could provide protection in control participants. Isolation of these cells from PBMC samples to study their functionality may be of interest to better understand their impact on HIV-1 acquisition risk. For CD8+ T cells, there were no significant differences seen between cases and controls in this analysis. Using the 28-colour phenotyping panel developed for further phenotypic profiling of T cells, no meaningful differences were observed between CD4+ or CD8+ T cell clusters in cases and controls.

To understand the effect of baseline DC and monocyte levels on protection outcomes, we performed immune phenotyping analysis of PBMC at Month 0 pre-vaccination. Even though CD4+ T cells are the primary HIV-1 targets, both DCs and monocytes are susceptible to HIV-1 and can transmit the virus to T cells.59 While most DC and monocyte clusters were comparable in cases and controls, we found slightly higher levels of classical DCs and monocytes in cases at baseline. Classical monocytes expressing CD32 (100%) and CD86 (>95%) have high replicative capacity and are implicated as a viral reservoir.59,60 Thus, higher DC and monocyte levels in the peripheral blood of treatment group cases at baseline could suggest a greater HIV-1 susceptible cell pool in these individuals; however, additional studies would be required to confirm and better understand these findings.

We note that this study has several limitations, one of which is that this HIV vaccine trial was conducted only in female participants. Previous studies have highlighted variability in vaccine efficacy and immune profiles based on sex. For instance, recent work by Lakshmikanth et al.61 highlighted the critical role of hormones in shaping the adaptive immune response. To confirm and further explore our findings, additional studies in the Mosaico vaccine trial (NCT 03964415—studying a similar vaccine design in a male and transgender population) or other vaccine trials involving both sexes will be necessary. Additional limitations include the fact that the antigens used in the immunogenicity assays were not an exact match to the HIV-1 strains circulating at the time of the trial; the relatively small number of breakthrough cases, which reduced precision and statistical power to identify immune correlates; and the CoP analyses, which rely on standard causal inference assumptions such as no unmeasured confounding, positivity, and consistency.17 Furthermore, it is crucial to recognise that our investigations are at an early exploratory stage, where the relationships between variables are not yet clearly understood or validated, making it hard determine how they could influence clinical decisions.

Despite these limitations, using unbiased clustering of T cells based on immune markers in response to HIV-1 antigen stimulation, we demonstrate a broad and polyfunctional T cell response that is maintained for up to 2 years after the primary vaccination. While CD4+ T cells were mainly of an early effector memory response phenotype, terminally differentiated CD8+ T cell clusters could be identified in vaccinees in response to HIV-1 antigens. Despite our inability to detect many significant cell clusters in cases vs. controls for this particular vaccine candidate that did not demonstrate clinical efficacy, our approach provides a template for high dimensional single-cell functional data investigation to better understand vaccine induced cellular immune correlates in future vaccine trials for HIV-1 or other pathogens.
