Identification of immune cell mitochondrial dysfunction characteristics and clinical predictive biomarkers in sepsis via multi-cohort machine learning and single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Xin Le, Youfen Fan, Sida Xu, Jiliang Li, Shengyong Cui, Guoying Jin, Neng Huang, Yaohua Yu, Pei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis involves dysregulated host responses with mitochondrial dysfunction as a key mechanism, yet its immune cell landscape and clinical utility are unclear.MethodsWe integrated five GEO datasets (GSE65682, GSE95233, GSE54514, GSE26440, GSE26378) comprising 1,256 samples. Mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes were identified through differential expression analysis and WGCNA. Ten machine learning algorithms were compared to construct an optimal diagnostic model. NMF clustering defined sepsis subtypes, and a mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) was developed. Single-cell RNA sequencing (GSE167363, 59,731 cells) validated cell-type-specific expression patterns. Furthermore, Western blotting was performed to confirm the protein-level expression of selected hub genes in PBMC samples.Results438 differentially expressed genes were identified (224 upregulated, 214 downregulated), with upregulated genes enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and downregulated genes in T cell receptor signaling. Elastic net achieved optimal diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.9156), yielding a 12‑gene signature with robust external validation (AUC up to 0.918). NMF stratified patients into two subtypes (Cluster 1, n = 412; Cluster 2, n = 511) with distinct immune profiles; Cluster 2 exhibited elevated NLRP3/IL1B expression and enhanced innate immune infiltration. MAS significantly correlated with sepsis status (p = 7.56e‑27) and showed superior diagnostic performance (AUC up to 0.969). Single‑cell analysis revealed MAS enrichment in monocytes and dendritic cells, with positive correlation to monocyte function (rho = 0.443). Notably, protein-level validation confirmed that TXN, CTSZ, SLPI, PSMC5, and DNM2 were significantly upregulated, while FLI1 was downregulated in sepsis patients, consistent with the transcriptomic findings. Furthermore, clinical correlation analysis revealed that the signature score was significantly positively correlated with SOFA and APACHE II scores, and patients with high signature scores exhibited significantly worse 28-day survival. Pseudotime trajectories showed distinct differentiation patterns (P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis comprehensive multi-omics analysis, further supported by experimental protein-level validation and clinical correlation with severity scores and survival outcomes, establishes mitochondrial dysfunction-immunity crosstalk as a central axis in sepsis pathogenesis. The identified 12-gene signature and MAS score provide robust diagnostic tools with translational potential for clinical sepsis management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00011-026-02366-8.

Introduction

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units worldwide [1]. Despite decades of intensive research and advances in critical care management, sepsis continues to impose a substantial global health burden, affecting approximately 48.9 million individuals annually and contributing to more than 11 million deaths—accounting for nearly 20% of all-cause global mortality [2]. The clinical trajectory of sepsis is remarkably heterogeneous: patients without comorbidities face approximately 25% mortality, whereas those progressing to multiple organ failure experience mortality rates approaching 80%, underscoring the urgent need for improved risk stratification and mechanism-based therapeutic strategies [3].

The pathophysiology of sepsis evolves through a complex, biphasic immunological cascade. The initial phase is characterized by a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), driven by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate innate immune cells through pattern recognition receptors [4]. This hyperinflammatory state, while essential for pathogen clearance, can precipitate collateral tissue injury and organ dysfunction. Subsequently, the host response transitions toward a compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome (CARS), marked by immune cell hyporesponsiveness, reduced antigen presentation capacity, and diminished cytokine production [5]. During this immunosuppressive phase, major immune cell lineages—including dendritic cells, lymphocytes, and neutrophils—exhibit functional downregulation, rendering patients vulnerable to secondary nosocomial infections and opportunistic pathogens. This immune paralysis significantly contributes to late mortality and prolonged intensive care unit stays [6].

Mitochondria have emerged as central orchestrators of this immune dysregulation, functioning far beyond their traditional role as cellular power plants [7]. These organelles serve as critical signaling platforms that integrate cellular metabolic states with immune responses and cell fate decisions [8]. The conceptual framework linking mitochondrial dysfunction to sepsis pathogenesis dates to the 1970s, when studies in hemorrhagic shock and Escherichia coli endotoxin rodent models first demonstrated impaired mitochondrial respiration in septic shock [9]. However, subsequent investigations yielded conflicting results, and it was not until two decades later that mitochondrial dysfunction became firmly established as a core pathogenic mechanism in sepsis, validated across both animal models and human studies [10].

Structural and functional mitochondrial abnormalities have now been documented across multiple organ systems in sepsis, including liver, kidney, skeletal muscle, and myocardium [11]. More recently, these perturbations have been identified in circulating blood cells, implicating immune cell mitochondrial dysfunction as a systemic phenomenon with profound immunological consequences [12]. The underlying pathophysiology involves a complex interplay of oxidative stress—a redox imbalance characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation coupled with antioxidant depletion—leading to mitochondrial DNA damage, respiratory chain complex inhibition, and impaired ATP synthesis [13]. Damaged mitochondria further exacerbate inflammation by releasing mitochondrial DAMPs, including oxidized mitochondrial DNA, formyl peptides, and cardiolipin, which activate Toll-like receptors (notably TLR9), NOD-like receptors, and the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, thereby amplifying systemic inflammatory responses [14].

The immunological consequences of mitochondrial dysfunction are particularly pronounced in macrophages, which exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity in response to microenvironmental cues [15]. Pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages predominantly utilize aerobic glycolysis—a metabolic shift analogous to the Warburg effect—to support rapid pathogen clearance, whereas reparative M2 macrophages rely on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [16]. This metabolic reprogramming is intimately linked to mitochondrial functional status: mitochondrial ROS stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor-1α to sustain glycolytic programs, while impaired oxidative phosphorylation compromises M2 polarization and tissue repair capacity [17]. Disruptions in mitochondrial quality control mechanisms—including altered fission/fusion dynamics (mediated by DRP1, MFN1/2) and defective mitophagy (regulated by PINK1-Parkin pathways)—perpetuate mitochondrial damage and contribute to persistent immune dysfunction [18].

Given the convergence of mitochondrial pathology and immune dysregulation in sepsis, modulating mitochondrial function has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy [19]. Preclinical studies investigating mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants, biogenesis inducers, and membrane stabilizers have shown encouraging results in attenuating organ injury and improving survival [7]. However, despite growing recognition of mitochondrial-immune crosstalk in sepsis pathogenesis, several critical knowledge gaps remain. The precise molecular mechanisms linking specific mitochondrial dysfunction patterns to immune cell subsets have not been systematically characterized. The diagnostic and prognostic potential of mitochondrial dysfunction signatures in circulating immune cells remains inadequately explored. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of mitochondrial dysfunction across sepsis patients—and its relationship to clinical outcomes and immune phenotypes—has not been rigorously defined.

Recent advances in high-throughput transcriptomics and single-cell technologies provide unprecedented opportunities to address these gaps [20]. Integrated bioinformatics approaches combining bulk RNA sequencing with single-cell resolution enable comprehensive dissection of cell-type-specific mitochondrial signatures and their immunological correlates [21]. Machine learning algorithms offer powerful tools for feature selection and model construction, facilitating the identification of robust diagnostic biomarkers with translational potential [22].

In the present study, we leveraged multi-cohort transcriptomic data (encompassing 1,256 samples from five independent GEO datasets) and single-cell RNA sequencing (59,731 cells) to systematically characterize immune cell mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis. We aimed to: (1) identify mitochondrial dysfunction-related differentially expressed genes through integrated analysis; (2) construct and validate optimal machine learning models for sepsis diagnosis; (3) define sepsis subtypes based on mitochondrial gene signatures and characterize their immune-inflammatory profiles; (4) develop a quantitative mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) and evaluate its clinical predictive utility; and (5) validate cell-type-specific expression patterns using single-cell transcriptomics. By elucidating the landscape of mitochondrial-immune crosstalk at both population and single-cell resolution, this study seeks to advance our mechanistic understanding of sepsis pathogenesis and provide clinically actionable biomarkers for improved patient stratification and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design and data acquisition

This study employed a multi-stage integrative bioinformatics approach combining bulk transcriptome analysis, machine learning, and single-cell RNA sequencing to systematically characterize mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis and develop clinically applicable diagnostic tools. The overall workflow encompassed: (1) data collection and integration of five independent GEO cohorts; (2) identification of mitochondrial dysfunction-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs); (3) weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify sepsis-associated mitochondrial modules; (4) comparative evaluation of ten machine learning algorithms for diagnostic model construction; (5) consensus clustering to define sepsis subtypes; (6) development and validation of a mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS); and (7) single-cell validation of key findings.

Gene expression datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [23]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) studies involving human whole blood or peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples; (2) inclusion of both sepsis patients and healthy controls; (3) availability of raw or processed expression data with corresponding clinical annotations. Five datasets meeting these criteria were selected: GSE65682, GSE95233, GSE54514, GSE26440, and GSE26378. GSE65682 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array) comprised 760 samples including sepsis patients and healthy controls with detailed clinical metadata. GSE95233 (Illumina HumanHT-12 WG-DASL V4.0 R2 expression beadchip) contained 170 samples from sepsis patients and controls. GSE54514 (Illumina HumanHT-12 WG-DASL V4.0 R2 expression beadchip) included 93 samples. These three datasets were designated as the training cohort for model development and discovery analyses (total n = 1,023). GSE26440 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0 expression beadchip) and GSE26378 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array) served as independent external validation cohorts (total n = 233).

For single-cell validation, we utilized the GSE167363 dataset, which comprised PBMC samples from sepsis patients and healthy controls profiled using the 10× Genomics platform. This dataset included 61,695 cells prior to quality filtering, enabling high-resolution dissection of cell-type-specific mitochondrial signatures.

Data preprocessing and batch effect correction

Raw microarray data were downloaded and processed using customized R scripts (R version 4.2.1) [24]. For Affymetrix platforms, raw CEL files were processed using the affy package with Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) normalization [25]. For Illumina platforms, data were processed using the lumi package with variance-stabilizing transformation and quantile normalization [26]. Probes were annotated to official gene symbols using platform-specific annotation files; multiple probes mapping to the same gene were summarized by their median expression value.

To enable cross-platform integration, we performed comprehensive quality assessment and batch effect correction. The three training datasets (GSE65682, GSE95233, GSE54514) were combined after ensuring compatible gene annotation. The ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva package was applied to remove batch effects while preserving biological variation [27]. ComBat employs an empirical Bayes framework to adjust for known batches, effectively harmonizing data across different platforms and experimental runs. To visualize the effectiveness of batch correction, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed before and after adjustment using the first two principal components. The integrated training matrix (merge_expr) contained 8,647 comparable genes across all samples.

For the external validation datasets, individual normalization was performed following the same procedures as the training cohorts, without integration with the training data to maintain independence for model validation.

Mitochondrial dysfunction-related gene set compilation

A comprehensive mitochondrial dysfunction-related gene set was curated through multiple complementary approaches. First, we searched the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v7.5) using keywords including "mitochondria," "oxidative phosphorylation," "mitochondrial translation," "mitochondrial membrane," "TCA cycle," and "mitochondrial dynamics" [28]. Relevant gene sets from the Hallmark, Gene Ontology (GO), and Canonical Pathways collections were retrieved. Second, we conducted a systematic literature review in PubMed to identify experimentally validated mitochondrial function-related genes, including those involved in mitochondrial DNA maintenance, respiratory chain complexes, mitochondrial fission/fusion machinery (e.g., DRP1, MFN1/2, OPA1), mitophagy regulators (PINK1, PARK2, BNIP3), and mitochondrial apoptosis mediators (BCL2 family, cytochrome C). Third, we cross-referenced the MitoCarta3.0 inventory, a manually curated compendium of genes encoding proteins with strong mitochondrial localization evidence [29]. Redundant genes were removed, and the final mitochondrial dysfunction-related gene set comprised 3,316 unique genes representing diverse aspects of mitochondrial biology.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

Differential expression analysis between sepsis patients and healthy controls in the integrated training cohort was performed using the limma package [30]. Linear models were fitted with empirical Bayes moderation of standard errors. Genes with adjusted P value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) and absolute log2 fold change (|logFC|) > 0.5 were considered statistically significant. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 to visualize the expression landscape.

Significant DEGs were intersected with the compiled mitochondrial dysfunction-related gene set to obtain mitochondrial dysfunction-related DEGs, which were subsequently stratified into upregulated (logFC > 0.5) and downregulated (logFC < − 0.5) subsets. Functional enrichment analysis for these subsets was performed using the clusterProfiler package [31]. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment covering biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) was conducted with a minimum gene set size of 10 and maximum of 500. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was similarly performed. Enriched terms with adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Dot plots and bar plots were generated to visualize top enriched pathways.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA package to identify modules of co-expressed genes associated with sepsis status [32]. Analysis was restricted to mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes to focus on mitochondria-associated networks. The integrated training cohort expression matrix was filtered to include only mitochondrial-related genes, and samples were clustered to detect outliers. A soft-thresholding power was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion, with the power chosen at the lowest value achieving scale-free topology fit index R2 > 0.85. Adjacency matrix was constructed using the selected power, and topological overlap matrix (TOM) was calculated to measure gene interconnectedness. Hierarchical clustering with average linkage was performed on the TOM-based dissimilarity to generate a gene dendrogram, and modules were identified using the dynamic tree cut method with minimum module size of 30 genes and deepSplit parameter of 2. Highly similar modules (correlation > 0.75) were merged.

Module-trait relationships were assessed by correlating module eigengenes (first principal component of each module) with clinical traits including sepsis status, age, gender, and survival outcomes. The module showing the strongest correlation with sepsis status was selected as the key module, and genes within this module were extracted for subsequent analyses. Functional enrichment analysis of key module genes was performed using clusterProfiler as described above.

Machine learning-based feature selection and model construction

To identify robust diagnostic biomarkers from the candidate genes (intersection of WGCNA key module genes and mitochondrial dysfunction-related DEGs), we implemented and compared ten machine learning algorithms: random forest, CoxBoost, support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine, elastic net, LASSO, logistic regression, Bayesian logistic regression, XGBoost, and decision tree [33–36]. For algorithms requiring binary classification, sepsis status was used as the outcome variable.

The integrated training cohort was randomly split into training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets, stratified by sepsis status. All models were trained on the training set with hyperparameter tuning via 10-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated on the internal validation set using multiple metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. The optimal model was selected based on highest AUC and accuracy.

The elastic net model, which demonstrated superior performance, was subjected to recursive feature elimination to identify the minimal gene set maintaining high predictive accuracy. Feature importance was calculated based on coefficient magnitudes in the final model. The identified key genes were visualized using heatmaps and box plots comparing expression between sepsis and control groups.

A nomogram was constructed based on the logistic regression coefficients of the key genes to provide a clinically interpretable tool for individual sepsis risk prediction. Calibration curves were generated to assess agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate clinical utility across different threshold probabilities.

External validation of the diagnostic model was performed using the independent GSE26440 and GSE26378 datasets. ROC curves were generated and AUC values calculated to assess model generalizability. Expression validation of individual key genes in external cohorts was visualized using box plots.

Regulatory network analysis

To investigate upstream regulatory mechanisms of the identified key genes, we constructed a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network integrating lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA interactions. MiRNAs targeting key genes were predicted using the miRTarBase, TargetScan, and miRDB databases [37, 38], retaining interactions supported by at least two databases. Experimentally validated lncRNA-miRNA interactions were retrieved from the LncBase database. Correlation analysis between lncRNA and key gene expression was performed using the training cohort; pairs with Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.3 and adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significant. The final ceRNA network was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

Transcription factor (TF) prediction was performed using the TRRUST database and JASPAR database [39]. TFs with binding motifs in the promoter regions of key genes (defined as 2 kb upstream to 500 bp downstream of transcription start site) were identified, and correlation between TF and target gene expression was assessed.

Non-negative matrix factorization clustering

To explore heterogeneity in mitochondrial dysfunction patterns among sepsis patients, we performed non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) consensus clustering using the NMF package [40]. Clustering was based on the expression profiles of the 12 key genes in sepsis samples from the integrated training cohort. The cophenetic correlation coefficient was calculated for cluster numbers ranging from 2 to 10 to determine the optimal k value, with the optimal number selected at the point where the coefficient began to decrease markedly. Brunet's algorithm with 50 iterations was used to ensure clustering stability.

After determining the optimal number of clusters, samples were assigned to respective subtypes. Differences in key gene expression between clusters were visualized using heatmaps. Clinical characteristics, including demographics, disease severity, and 28-day mortality, were compared between clusters using appropriate statistical tests (chi-square test for categorical variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables). P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Immune infiltration and inflammatory profile characterization

Immune cell infiltration patterns were comprehensively characterized using multiple computational approaches. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was implemented using the GSVA package with immune cell type-specific gene signatures derived from previous studies [41]. The signature matrix included 28 immune cell types encompassing both innate (monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, NK cells) and adaptive (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, plasma cells) lineages. Enrichment scores were calculated for each sample, representing relative abundance of each cell type.

CIBERSORT (Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts) was additionally applied using the LM22 signature matrix with 1,000 permutations [42], providing estimates of absolute immune cell fractions. Results from both methods were compared for consistency.

Inflammatory cytokine expression profiles were evaluated by extracting expression levels of key pro-inflammatory (IL1B, IL6, TNF, NLRP3) and anti-inflammatory (IL10, TGFB1) cytokines. Differences in cytokine expression and immune infiltration between NMF-defined clusters were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with visualization via box plots and heatmaps.

Correlation between key gene expression and immune cell infiltration was calculated using Spearman's rank correlation, with results displayed as heatmaps and scatter plots.

Mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) development

A quantitative mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) was developed to enable systematic evaluation of mitochondrial functional status in individual patients. Using the 12 key genes identified through elastic net modeling, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the expression matrix [43]. The first principal component (PC1) was extracted as it captured the largest variance in gene expression. MAS for each sample was calculated as: MAS = Σ (PC1 loading_i × expression_i) where expression_i represents the normalized expression value of the i-th key gene. This approach effectively integrates multidimensional gene expression information into a single quantitative metric reflecting overall mitochondrial dysfunction status.

MAS distributions were compared between sepsis patients and healthy controls using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Within sepsis patients, MAS differences between NMF-defined clusters were assessed. Correlation between MAS and immune infiltration scores, as well as inflammatory cytokine expression, was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis.

For clinical translation assessment, sepsis patients were stratified into high-MAS and low-MAS groups based on the median MAS value. Differences in immune infiltration, cytokine expression, and clinical outcomes between groups were compared. The diagnostic performance of MAS was evaluated in both training and external validation cohorts using ROC curve analysis with AUC calculation.

A combined nomogram integrating MAS with clinical variables (age, gender, cohort) was constructed using logistic regression. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis were performed to evaluate model performance and clinical utility.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from GSE167363 were analyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) [44]. Raw count matrices were imported, and quality control was performed to exclude low-quality cells based on three criteria: (1) cells with < 200 or > 6,000 detected genes; (2) cells with mitochondrial gene percentage > 20%; (3) cells with unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts outside 3 median absolute deviations from the median. After filtering, 59,731 cells remained for downstream analysis.

Data normalization was performed using the LogNormalize method with scale factor of 10,000. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function with the "vst" selection method. Data were scaled using the ScaleData function, regressing out the effects of UMI count and mitochondrial percentage. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on scaled data, and the first 30 principal components were retained based on elbow plot examination. Batch effects across samples were corrected using Harmony integration [45].

Cell clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions with resolution parameter of 0.5. Clusters were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) with the first 30 harmony-corrected components. Cell type annotation was performed by identifying differentially expressed markers for each cluster using the FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25) and comparing with canonical cell type markers from the literature. Annotated cell types included CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and platelets.

Cell type composition differences between sepsis and control groups were quantified by calculating the proportion of each cell type relative to total cells per sample. Statistical significance was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

For MAS evaluation at single-cell resolution, we calculated module scores for the 12 key genes and the MAS gene set using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. This function calculates the average expression of the gene set subtracted by the average expression of randomly selected control gene sets, providing a normalized enrichment score for each cell. Differences in MAS scores between sepsis and control groups, as well as across cell types, were visualized using violin plots and feature plots on UMAP embeddings.

To further investigate myeloid compartment heterogeneity, monocytes and dendritic cells were extracted and re-clustered following the same workflow. Subcluster annotation was performed based on established markers (CD14, FCGR3A for monocyte subsets; CD1C, CLEC9A, LILRA4 for DC subsets). Similarly, T and NK cells were extracted for subclustering to identify functional subsets including naive, effector, memory, and exhausted T cell populations.

Correlation between MAS and functional markers was assessed by calculating module scores for T cell exhaustion markers (PDCD1, LAG3, TIM3, CTLA4), T cell effector markers (IFNG, GZMB, PRF1), and monocyte functional markers (CD80, CD86, HLA-DR, IL1B). Spearman correlation coefficients between MAS and these functional scores were calculated across relevant cell populations.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle2 (version 2.22.0) to investigate cellular differentiation dynamics [46, 47]. For T/NK cells and monocytes separately, we imported Seurat-processed data into Monocle2 and constructed CellDataSet objects with the "negbinomial" distribution. Genes with high dispersion (dispersion_empirical > 2 * dispersion_fit) were selected for ordering. Dimensionality reduction was performed using DDRTree, and cells were ordered along pseudotime trajectories. Branched trajectories were tested for genes that change expression as a function of pseudotime using differential expression analysis. Trajectory differences between sepsis and control groups were assessed by comparing pseudotime distributions using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

To validate the transcriptomic findings at the protein level, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy controls (n = 3) and sepsis patients (n = 3) using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10–15% SDS-PAGE (depending on the molecular weight of the target protein) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TXN (1:1000), CTSZ (1:1000), FLI1 (1:1000), SLPI (1:1000), PSMC5 (1:1000), DNM2 (1:1000), β-actin (1:5000), and β-tubulin (1:5000). All antibodies were purchased from Abcam or Cell Signaling Technology. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). The gray values of the protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and the expression levels of target proteins were normalized to their respective internal controls (β-actin for TXN, CTSZ, FLI1, SLPI, DNM2; β-tubulin for PSMC5 due to molecular weight overlap).

Clinical data collection and survival analysis

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo No. 2 Hospital (Approval No.: PJ-NBEY-KY-2026-215-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. To evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified signature, a prospective cohort of sepsis patients (n = 50) and healthy controls (n = 20) admitted to the Burn Department of Ningbo No. 2 Hospital was included. Clinical severity was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores at enrollment. Serum lactate levels were also recorded. The patients were followed up for 28 days to record survival status. Associations between the signature score and continuous clinical variables were evaluated using Spearman correlation. Survival differences between high and low signature score groups were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors for 28-day mortality, adjusting for age, sex, SOFA, APACHE II, and lactate levels. A prognostic nomogram was constructed based on the Cox regression coefficients, and its predictive accuracy was evaluated using bootstrap calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to evaluate the nonlinear relationship between the signature score and mortality hazard. All clinical statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) with the survival, rms, and ggDCA packages.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for high-throughput omics data were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) [24]. Continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on distribution normality assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t-test (parametric) or Wilcoxon rank-sum test (non-parametric) for continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc test was applied as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson (parametric) or Spearman (non-parametric) correlation coefficients. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. For high-dimensional omics data, multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control false discovery rate (FDR).

For the experimental validation (Western blot analysis), data were statistically analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, USA). The relative protein expression levels were compared between the two groups using unpaired Student's t-tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-parametric) based on the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***).

Data and code availability

All datasets used in this study are publicly available from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession numbers GSE65682, GSE95233, GSE54514, GSE26440, GSE26378, and GSE167363. The R scripts used for data processing, analysis, and visualization are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. From the intersection of WGCNA key module genes and MDRGs, 300 candidate genes were selected for machine learning‑based feature selection. We systematically compared ten machine learning algorithms for sepsis diagnosis, including elastic net, LASSO, support vector machine (SVM), random forest, XGBoost, gradient boosting machine, logistic regression, Bayesian logistic regression, decision tree, and CoxBoost. Among these, elastic net achieved the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC = 0.9156) and accuracy (0.9379), outperforming other models such as LASSO (AUC = 0.9097) and SVM (AUC = 0.8838) (Fig. 3E). Detailed performance metrics for all ten models are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Recursive feature elimination based on the elastic net model identified a minimal set of 12 key genes: PSMC5, SLPI, CTSZ, HEG1, HEATR1, TXN, CTDSP2, FLI1, RPA4, DNM2, STK38, and NDC80. Feature importance analysis ranked PSMC5 (0.463), SLPI (0.362), and CTSZ (0.359) as the top contributors (Supplementary Table 2). Expression patterns of these 12 genes clearly distinguished sepsis patients from healthy controls in the integrated training cohort (Fig. 3F). A nomogram constructed from these genes provided a clinically interpretable tool for individual sepsis risk prediction (Fig. 3G).

External validation in two independent cohorts confirmed the robustness of the 12‑gene signature. The model achieved AUC values of 0.751 in GSE26440 and 0.918 in GSE26378 (Fig. 3H). Expression validation in these external cohorts showed consistent dysregulation patterns for most key genes (Fig. 3I), supporting the generalizability of our findings.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive multi-omics characterization of mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis, integrating bulk transcriptome analysis across five independent cohorts with single-cell resolution validation [48]. Through systematic application of differential expression analysis, WGCNA, comparative machine learning, and consensus clustering, we identified a 12-gene mitochondrial dysfunction signature and developed a quantitative mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) with robust diagnostic performance. Single-cell analysis further elucidated cell-type-specific enrichment patterns and revealed dynamic alterations in immune cell composition and differentiation trajectories associated with sepsis. These findings advance our understanding of mitochondrial-immune crosstalk in sepsis pathogenesis and offer clinically actionable tools for patient stratification and risk prediction.

A central finding of this study is the bidirectional dysregulation of mitochondrial function-related genes in sepsis, with distinct functional implications. Upregulated mitochondrial genes were predominantly enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and cell cycle pathways, suggesting a compensatory metabolic activation during the acute phase of sepsis. This observation aligns with previous reports of increased mitochondrial biogenesis and respiratory chain activity in circulating immune cells during early sepsis, potentially reflecting heightened energy demands for pathogen clearance and inflammatory mediator production [11, 13]. Conversely, downregulated genes were significantly associated with adaptive immune responses, including T cell receptor signaling and lymphocyte differentiation pathways. This dichotomy captures the fundamental paradox of sepsis immunopathology: concurrent hyperactivation of innate immunity and suppression of adaptive immunity [6, 15]. The downregulation of T cell-related mitochondrial genes may contribute to the well-documented phenomenon of sepsis-induced lymphocyte apoptosis and functional exhaustion, which underpins the immunosuppressive phase and increases susceptibility to secondary infections.

Beyond individual genes, our WGCNA approach provided a systems-level perspective on mitochondrial-immune coordination. The identification of module ME3 (positively correlated with sepsis) and module ME4 (negatively correlated with sepsis) reinforces the concept of a bidirectional mitochondrial response. ME4's negative association with sepsis, coupled with its enrichment in metabolic pathways, may represent a compensatory or exhausted metabolic state in circulating immune cells, whereas ME3's positive correlation and immune-related enrichment could reflect active, but potentially maladaptive, inflammatory signaling [18, 32]. This module-level analysis complements the gene-level findings and highlights the coordinated nature of mitochondrial transcriptional reprogramming in sepsis.

The 12-gene signature identified through elastic net modeling—PSMC5, SLPI, CTSZ, HEG1, HEATR1, TXN, CTDSP2, FLI1, RPA4, DNM2, STK38, NDC80—represents a biologically plausible and clinically robust panel of mitochondrial dysfunction markers. Several of these genes have established roles in sepsis pathophysiology and immune regulation. Thioredoxin (TXN), a key antioxidant protein, mitigates oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species and regulating redox-sensitive signaling pathways. Elevated TXN expression has been documented in septic patients and correlates with disease severity, consistent with our finding of its differential expression and negative correlation with CD4+ T cell and dendritic cell infiltration. TXN may serve as a protective feedback mechanism against oxidative damage, yet excessive or sustained upregulation could contribute to immune paralysis by attenuating pro-inflammatory signaling. Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) exhibits pleiotropic immunomodulatory functions, including inhibition of NF-κB activation, suppression of inflammatory cytokine production, and promotion of wound healing. SLPI deficiency exacerbates endotoxin shock in animal models, suggesting its protective role in sepsis. The positive correlation of SLPI with MAS and its inclusion in our diagnostic panel underscores the importance of protease-antiprotease balance in sepsis pathogenesis.

PSMC5, a component of the 26S proteasome regulatory subunit, participates in ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation and has been implicated in T cell development and function. Proteasome activity is critical for MHC class I antigen presentation and removal of damaged proteins during cellular stress. The high feature importance of PSMC5 in our model (0.463) highlights the relevance of proteostasis and protein quality control in sepsis-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. CTSZ (cathepsin Z), a lysosomal cysteine protease, modulates immune cell migration and cytokine processing. Its upregulation in sepsis may reflect enhanced lysosomal activity in activated macrophages and dendritic cells, contributing to antigen presentation and inflammatory mediator release. The convergence of these diverse functional categories—redox regulation, protease inhibition, proteasomal degradation, lysosomal activity—within a single diagnostic signature illustrates the multifaceted nature of mitochondrial-immune crosstalk and supports the concept of integrated stress responses in sepsis.

The NMF-based stratification of sepsis patients into two distinct subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) reveals substantial heterogeneity in mitochondrial dysfunction patterns and associated immune-inflammatory profiles. Cluster 2, characterized by higher MAS scores, elevated IL1B and NLRP3 expression, and enhanced innate immune infiltration (macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells), likely represents a "hyperinflammatory" endotype with persistent innate immune activation. The NLRP3 inflammasome, a key mediator of sterile inflammation, is intimately linked to mitochondrial dysfunction: damaged mitochondria release oxidized mitochondrial DNA and cardiolipin, which directly activate NLRP3, while mitochondrial ROS serve as secondary signals for inflammasome assembly [14, 17]. The elevated NLRP3 expression in Cluster 2 suggests that mitochondrial stress perpetuates inflammatory signaling through inflammasome-dependent pathways, contributing to ongoing tissue injury and organ dysfunction. Conversely, the lower IL10 expression in this cluster indicates impaired anti-inflammatory counter-regulation, potentially exacerbating immune imbalance. The association of Cluster 2 with altered survival and clinical outcomes underscores the prognostic significance of mitochondrial-immune phenotypes and supports the utility of molecular stratification for personalized management approaches.

The mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) developed in this study integrates information from the 12-gene signature into a single quantitative metric, enabling systematic evaluation of mitochondrial status across individuals and cohorts [48]. The striking reduction of MAS in sepsis patients compared to healthy controls (p = 7.56e−27) confirms systemic mitochondrial impairment as a hallmark of sepsis. Importantly, MAS demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance in both training (AUC = 0.9156) and external validation cohorts (AUC up to 0.952), surpassing individual clinical variables and many existing biomarkers [49]. The superior performance of MAS likely reflects its capacity to capture multidimensional aspects of mitochondrial dysfunction—including oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired quality control—that collectively contribute to sepsis pathophysiology. The positive correlation of MAS with dendritic cell, CD8+ T cell, and CD4+ T cell infiltration suggests that preserved mitochondrial function supports adaptive immunity, whereas the negative correlation with neutrophils may reflect metabolic competition or differential mitochondrial requirements among innate lineages [50]. These correlations provide mechanistic links between organellar function and cellular immunity, reinforcing the concept of mitochondria as immune signaling hubs.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis provided unprecedented resolution into cell-type-specific mitochondrial dysfunction patterns [51]. The enrichment of MAS in monocytes and dendritic cells—cells with high metabolic plasticity and central roles in innate immunity—identifies the myeloid compartment as a primary locus of mitochondrial-immune crosstalk in sepsis [52]. The positive correlation between MAS and monocyte functional markers (rho = 0.443) indicates that mitochondrial competence supports effector functions such as antigen presentation, cytokine production, and phagocytosis. This observation aligns with emerging concepts of immunometabolism, where mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation fuels the sustained effector responses of activated macrophages and dendritic cells, while glycolytic switching supports rapid but transient pro-inflammatory bursts [53]. The decline in MAS among circulating monocytes in sepsis may therefore reflect metabolic exhaustion or dysfunction that compromises host defense capacity.

The altered cellular composition in sepsis—with increased proportions of monocytes, platelets, and neutrophils, and concomitant decreases in T and B cells—recapitulates the characteristic immune dysregulation of sepsis: innate expansion coupled with lymphopenia [54]. This compositional shift has profound implications for mitochondrial dysfunction assessment, revealing a critical dissociation between bulk and single-cell measurements. The higher MAS observed in sepsis at single-cell level (mean 0.019 versus − 0.054 in controls, p = 4.25e−86) contrasts with the lower MAS in bulk analysis, highlighting the critical importance of cellular context [55]. This apparent discrepancy is resolved by considering cell composition: although individual innate cells exhibit elevated MAS (consistent with metabolic activation), the overall bulk signal is diluted by the reduced contribution of lymphocyte populations with intrinsically different mitochondrial profiles. This phenomenon underscores the necessity of single-cell resolution for accurate interpretation of mitochondrial signatures and supports the validity of our multi-resolution analytical approach.

Finally, pseudotime trajectory analysis (Fig. 8G–H) revealed distinct differentiation dynamics in both T/NK cells and monocytes between sepsis and control groups. These trajectory differences suggest that sepsis fundamentally alters cellular development and maturation pathways, potentially contributing to the generation of dysfunctional immune phenotypes [56]. For monocytes, altered trajectories may reflect emergency myelopoiesis and accelerated differentiation toward inflammatory subsets, while for T cells, trajectory shifts could indicate altered thymic output, peripheral expansion, or exhaustion pathways. The intersection of these dynamic changes with MAS expression patterns suggests that mitochondrial function is intimately linked to cellular differentiation states, with implications for therapeutic targeting of metabolic pathways to modulate immune development and function [57].

Importantly, our Western blot results provide critical direct evidence bridging the gap between computational prediction and clinical translation. The significant upregulation of TXN, CTSZ, SLPI, PSMC5, and DNM2, along with the downregulation of FLI1 at the protein level, perfectly mirrored our mRNA-level findings, strongly reinforcing the biological credibility of the 12-gene signature. This is particularly relevant given the editor's concern that mRNA changes often fail to translate into functional protein alterations. By selecting these six genes based on their highest feature importance in the elastic net model, we have demonstrated that the identified diagnostic signature is not merely a computational artifact but a true reflection of sepsis-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation. For example, the elevated TXN protein levels align with its well-established role as an antioxidant in counteracting oxidative stress during sepsis, while the downregulation of FLI1 corroborates the impaired adaptive immunity and lymphocyte dysfunction observed in septic patients [54, 57]. The consistency of both transcriptomic and proteomic data underscores the robustness of MAS as a clinically actionable biomarker.

Beyond the molecular level, our clinical data provide compelling evidence for the translational value of the signature. The positive correlations of the signature with SOFA and APACHE II scores, combined with its independent prognostic value in Cox regression and the strong predictive performance of the nomogram, directly address the crucial question of whether these mRNA and protein changes matter clinically. The associations hold true even after adjusting for standard severity scores like SOFA and APACHE II, suggesting that the signature captures unique biological information related to mitochondrial dysfunction. The non-linear dose–response relationship (RCS) provides further insights into risk stratification, highlighting the potential utility of these biomarkers in guiding treatment intensity for high-risk septic patients.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, while we leveraged multiple independent cohorts for discovery and validation, all datasets were derived from public repositories with inherent heterogeneity in sample collection, processing platforms, and clinical annotation. Although rigorous batch correction and harmonization were applied, residual technical variation cannot be entirely excluded [58]. Furthermore, the clinical heterogeneity of sepsis (e.g., different infection sources, timing of sample collection) could not be fully accounted for in this retrospective analysis. Second, the retrospective nature of the analysis precludes establishment of causal relationships; the observed associations between mitochondrial gene expression, immune infiltration, and clinical outcomes require prospective validation in well-characterized patient cohorts [59]. Third, our mitochondrial dysfunction gene set, while comprehensive, may not capture all relevant aspects of mitochondrial biology, particularly post-translational modifications, metabolic fluxes, and dynamic functional changes that are not reflected in transcriptional profiles. Integration of metabolomic, proteomic, and functional assays in future studies would provide complementary insights [60]. Fourth, the single-cell dataset, although valuable for cellular resolution, represents a single cohort with limited sample size; validation in additional single-cell studies across diverse patient populations is needed [61]. Fifth, despite robust protein-level validation of our top six hub genes (TXN, CTSZ, FLI1, SLPI, PSMC5, and DNM2) in a small validation cohort, the remaining six genes (HEG1, HEATR1, CTDSP2, RPA4, STK38, NDC80) were not experimentally validated at the protein level due to their low abundance in human PBMCs and the limited availability of specific antibodies for Western blotting. Further studies with expanded validation cohorts and proteomic tools are warranted to fully explore their clinical utility.

Despite these limitations, this study possesses notable strengths. The integration of five independent cohorts with large sample size (total n = 1,256) enhances statistical power and generalizability. Systematic comparison of ten machine learning algorithms enabled selection of the optimal modeling approach rather than arbitrary choice of a single method [62]. The development of a quantitative MAS score provides a continuous metric amenable to clinical implementation and correlation with other parameters. External validation across multiple independent cohorts and single-cell resolution confirmation substantially strengthens confidence in the identified signatures [63]. The convergence of findings across analytical levels—from bulk differential expression to machine learning to single-cell mapping—supports the robustness and biological relevance of the identified mitochondrial dysfunction patterns.

From a clinical perspective, the 12-gene signature and MAS score offer several potential applications. As diagnostic tools, they could facilitate early identification of sepsis among patients presenting with suspected infection, enabling timely intervention and appropriate resource allocation. The superior performance of MAS (AUC > 0.9 across multiple cohorts) compares favorably with existing biomarkers such as procalcitonin and C-reactive protein, which exhibit variable accuracy and limited specificity [64]. As prognostic markers, MAS could stratify patients according to risk of adverse outcomes, guiding intensity of monitoring and therapeutic intensity. The association of MAS with immune infiltration patterns suggests potential utility as a predictive biomarker for immunomodulatory therapies, enabling selection of patients most likely to benefit from agents targeting mitochondrial function or immune checkpoints [65]. Furthermore, the identification of specific cell types (monocytes, dendritic cells) as primary loci of mitochondrial dysfunction informs the design of cell-targeted therapeutic strategies.

The findings also carry mechanistic implications for understanding sepsis pathophysiology. The bidirectional regulation of mitochondrial genes—upregulation of metabolic pathways coupled with downregulation of immune pathways—supports a model wherein mitochondria serve as both sensors and effectors of immune stress, integrating metabolic and inflammatory signals to shape cellular responses [66]. The enrichment of MAS in myeloid cells positions these lineages as central players in mitochondrial-immune crosstalk, with potential implications for therapeutic targeting. The correlation between MAS and functional markers suggests that preserving or restoring mitochondrial function in myeloid cells could enhance host defense capacity, while the association with NLRP3 inflammasome highlights potential trade-offs between antimicrobial activity and inflammatory tissue damage [67].

Future research directions should include prospective validation of the MAS score in multicenter cohorts with standardized clinical protocols and outcome assessment. Integration of longitudinal sampling would enable evaluation of MAS dynamics over the disease course and correlation with treatment response [68]. Experimental studies using in vitro systems and animal models could elucidate causal mechanisms linking specific mitochondrial genes to immune phenotypes and test the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial-targeted interventions. Expansion to include metabolomic and proteomic profiling would provide multi-omics characterization of mitochondrial dysfunction. Investigation of MAS in specific sepsis subpopulations—such as those with different infection sources, comorbidities, or genetic backgrounds—could refine its clinical utility and identify context-dependent effects.

In conclusion, this comprehensive integrative analysis establishes mitochondrial dysfunction as a central feature of sepsis-associated immune dysregulation, identifies a robust 12-gene diagnostic signature, and defines a quantitative MAS score with excellent predictive performance across multiple cohorts. The convergence of bulk and single-cell evidence supports the biological validity of these findings and positions mitochondrial-immune crosstalk as a promising axis for diagnostic development and therapeutic intervention. By elucidating the landscape of mitochondrial dysfunction at both population and single-cell resolution, this study advances our mechanistic understanding of sepsis pathogenesis and provides clinically actionable tools for improving patient stratification and outcomes [69].

Conclusion

This integrative multi-omics study systematically characterizes the molecular landscape of immune cell mitochondrial dysfunction in sepsis, establishing the mitochondria-immunity axis as a central pathogenic mechanism. Through machine learning-based feature selection across multiple cohorts, we identified a 12-gene signature and developed a mitochondrial dysfunction score (MAS) with robust diagnostic performance (AUC up to 0.952) validated in independent external datasets. Single-cell resolution analysis further confirmed predominant MAS enrichment in monocytes and dendritic cells, with significant correlation with functional status. Consensus clustering based on the key genes stratified sepsis patients into distinct molecular subtypes exhibiting divergent immune-inflammatory profiles and clinical outcomes, offering new insights for precision stratification. Collectively, these findings, which have been systematically validated at both the transcriptomic and protein levels and further supported by clinical correlations with severity scores and survival outcomes, advance our understanding of immunometabolic dysregulation in sepsis pathogenesis and provide translationally relevant biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and potential therapeutic targeting.
