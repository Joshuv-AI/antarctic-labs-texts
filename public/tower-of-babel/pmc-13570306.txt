Determinants of substrate specificity in mint pulegone reductase, a double bond reductase of the medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase superfamily
Authors: A. Jannelle Andrews, Iris Lange, Narayanan Srividya, B. Markus Lange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulegone reductase from peppermint (Mentha × piperita; MpPulR) is an NADPH-dependent medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase that catalyzes the asymmetric reduction of the C4–C7 double bond of (+)-pulegone to (−)-menthone and (+)-isomenthone. To define the structural determinants of substrate recognition and catalytic turnover, we integrated structure-based modeling (docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations) with experimental mutagenesis and comprehensive substrate profiling. Residues positioned closely to (+)-pulegone in homology-based structural models were subjected to L-Ala-scanning mutagenesis. Four substitutions (Y53A, F66A, Y78A, Y257A) caused severe activity losses, consistent with a binding pocket in which Y78 supports carbonyl anchoring via hydrogen bonding and aromatic residues (Y53, F66, and Y257) creating a hydrophobic cavity that enforces a productive substrate orientation. Other residues, including L56, I63, M135, F281, and V282, line the perimeter of the active-site cavity, thus providing architectural constraints for substrate binding. Screening of 40 candidate monoterpenoids revealed measurable activity with only six substrates, and kinetic analysis demonstrated exceptionally high catalytic efficiency toward piperitenone and piperitenone oxide, moderate efficiency with (+)-pulegone, and lower efficiency with citral, (−)-pulegone, and isoegomaketone. MD simulations of productive versus non-productive ligands indicated binding requirements that include stable carbonyl–Y78 hydrogen bonding, enzyme–ligand complex stability, and favorable cofactor–double bond alignment. Finally, an essential oil survey across diverse accessions of the genus Mentha and functional characterization of 2 Mentha longifolia orthologs indicate that MpPulR-like enzymes are promiscuous reductases likely contributing to the interspecific diversification of essential oil profiles.

Monoterpenoids are C10-derived metabolites that are common constituents of essential oils and are obtained commercially through distillation (often steam-assisted) or cold pressing. These extracts are widely used in aromatherapy, cosmetics, personal care, natural medicine, and as biopesticides (1, 2). From an ecological perspective, monoterpenoids play important roles by influencing ecological interactions between plants and their surroundings by deterring herbivores, attracting pollinators, and inhibiting the growth of competing plants and microorganisms (3). The pathway toward functionalized monoterpenoids is particularly well characterized in peppermint (Mentha x piperita L., Lamiaceae). The monoterpenoid-specific biosynthetic pathway in peppermint begins with the cyclization of geranyl diphosphate to (−)-(4S)-limonene, followed by a series of tailoring reactions to generate a complex blend of products (4). Within this pathway, pulegone reductase (MpPulR; EC 1.3.1.81; National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) accession number Q6WAU0) catalyzes the asymmetric reduction of the C4–C7 double bond of (+)-pulegone to (−)-menthone and (+)-isomenthone (5) (Fig. 1). MpPulR is a double-bond reductase that belongs to the medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (MDR) superfamily and, more specifically, to the zinc-independent LTD subfamily (named after leukotriene B4 dehydrogenase). A double bond reduction for structurally related α,β-unsaturated carbonyls is also required in the pathways leading to piperitenone oxide in applemint (Mentha suaveolens Ehrh.) (6) and piperitone in horsemint (Mentha longifolia (L.) Huds) (7) (Fig. 1). Interestingly, prior work indicated that peppermint protein extracts were not only active with (+)-pulegone as a substrate but also facilitated double bond reductions with piperitenone and piperitenone oxide as substrates (8, 9), and the present work will therefore assess if these double bond reactions can also be catalyzed by MpPulR. A recent study provided evidence that MpPulR reduces isoegomaketone to perilla ketone, which is a signature metabolite in Korean perilla (Perilla frutescens (L.) Britton, also in the Lamiaceae) (10), thereby expanding the known catalytic repertoire of this enzyme. Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that MpPulR is not able to catalyze the C1–C2 double bond reduction from (−)-trans-carvone, which occurs naturally in spearmint (Mentha spicata L.), to dihydrocarvone (5), indicating structural constraints for the accommodation of α,β-unsaturated carbonyl functional groups in MpPulR (Fig. 1).Figure 1Overview of reactions catalyzed by plant double bond reductases. Arg.), acuminate barrenwort (Epimedium acuminatum Franch.), and Isodon eriocalyx (Dunn) Kudô were shown to reduce geraniol to citronellol (Fig. 1), without the presence of α,β-unsaturated activated double bond (13). The current study tested the ability of MpPulR to accelerate reactions that are typically catalyzed by other types of double bond reductases.

Studies aiming to better understand substrate selectivity benefit tremendously from prior knowledge regarding the architecture and electronic environment of the enzyme active site. A crystal structure of the recombinant MpPulR apoenzyme was reported previously (14), but the absence of a substrate and cofactor limits its utility for structurefunction studies. An alkenal double bond reductase of the LTD subfamily of MDRs from Arabidopsis thaliana (L.), termed AtAER (Q39172), was demonstrated to act on a wide range of α,β-unsaturated carbonyls (15, 16, 17). Crystal structures of the enzyme in the apo-form and as a binary complex with NADP+ provided essential information about the Rossmann fold that forms the cofactor binding site (17). Data for the ternary complex gave no significant electron density suitable for the positional refinement of the substrate p-coumaryl alcohol, and the substrate binding pocket thus had to be modeled computationally. A double bond reductase of the MDR family from tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.), termed NtAAD or NtRed-1 (BAA89423), was demonstrated to be active with (+)-pulegone as a substrate (18), but later work on the recombinant enzyme found much lower activity with this substrate and much higher affinity for alkenal/one substrates such as trans-2-nonenal and 1-octen-3-one (19). Crystal structures were obtained from the apoenzyme and complexes with NADP(H) (binary) as well as a phenylpropanoid aldehyde substrate (ternary). Unfortunately, the ternary complex showed the substrate bound in a conformation that is likely to be inhibitory, but residues potentially involved in forming the substrate-binding pocket could still be deduced.

Despite substantial work on characterizing plant double-bond reductases of the LTD subfamily of MDRs, the determinants of substrate selectivity and promiscuity are still poorly understood. In the present study, we use a combination of experimental and computational approaches to examine how proper alignment of a ligand within the MpPulR active site, the stability of substrate–enzyme complexes, the presence of key molecular interactions, favorable binding energies, and intrinsic substrate properties – such as the presence of an α,β-unsaturated double bond–contribute to catalytic compatibility. This work lays the foundation for future efforts to design variants of double bond reductases with tailored selectivity for the biotechnological production of high-value fine chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and agrochemical intermediates by ecologically sustainable manufacturing techniques.

Results

Defining MpPulR active site residues through alanine-scanning mutagenesis

An overlay of the MpPulR crystal structure (Protein Data Bank accession number (PDB) 7EQL) and the structure predicted by AlphaFold 3 served as the basis for modeling the apoenzyme. NADPH was then docked into the MpPulR apoenzyme using the Boltz-2 software and the structure was overlaid with the crystal structure of the binary complex of NtRed-1 and NADPH (PDB 4HFM) to select the best pose for the cofactor. Ligand docking was achieved using the Yasara software and the pose that placed the C4–C7 double bond of (+)-pulegone closest to the hydride to be transferred from NADPH was selected. Following energy minimization, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for 20 ns in the Yasara environment (constrained by a < 3.5 Å Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD)) (details under Experimental procedures). These simulations revealed that 12 residues consistently shared C−C distances of <4.5 Å with the ligand (Fig. 2, A–C and Table S1). To assess substrate binding experimentally, these residues were exchanged for L-Ala (amino acid with the shortest side chain, which is typically not able to engage in interactions with a ligand) by site-directed mutagenesis, and each recombinant enzyme variant was assayed with (+)-pulegone. To assess the possibility that L-Ala substitution of bulky residues causes non-specific structural disruptions rather than selectively removing substrate contacts, three residues predicted by docking and MD simulations to lie beyond 5 Å from the ligand (L56, M253, and F281) and positioned in distinct regions of the active site periphery, were also subject to L-Ala substitution (Table S1). Variants with L-Ala exchanges of these residues retained specific activity close to wild type, confirming that alanine substitution did not impair catalytic function. To confirm that the observed activity changes reflect disrupted substrate-enzyme interactions rather than global protein misfolding, we used a pretrained inverse folding model (ESM-IF) to calculate the theoretical folding free energy (ΔG) for all enzymes (20). Notably, all 12 alanine-substitution variants had ΔG values comparable to wild-type MpPulR (Table S2), indicating that none of the substitutions appear to cause significant structural destabilization. The progression of the reaction was monitored by quantifying substrate consumption and product formation by Gas Chromatography–Flame Ionization Detection (GC–FID). Three variants (M253A, V284A, and Y287A) showed specific activities close to that of the wild-type control (2.3 nmol min−1 • μg enzyme−1) (Fig. 3 and Table S3). Reduced activity (30–70% of wild type) was observed with five variants (L56A, I63A, M135A, F281A, and V282A), while a dramatic activity drop (<25% of wild-type) was detected for four variants (Y53A, F66A, Y78A, and Y257A) (Fig. 3 and Table S3).Figure 2Active site architecture of peppermint pulegone reductase (MpPulR; Q6WAU0). A sequence alignment of functionally characterized members of MpPulR-like enzymes of the angiosperm MDR family − including the previously mentioned MpPulR, AtAER, and NtRed-1 as well as double bond reductases involved in sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis in Artemisia annua L. (AaDBR1; ACN65116), monoterpenoid biosynthesis in Perilla frutescens L. (PfDBR1; no NCBI accession number available), and colchicine-type alkaloid biosynthesis in Colchicum autumnale L. (CaDBR1; UZM07734) − indicated that some of the putative active site residues, as identified based on their distance to the ligand and in in vitro activity assays, were highly conserved (Y53, Y78, M135, M253, Y257, F281, and Y287; numbering of MpPulR used for consistency), while others were variable across the selected enzymes (L56, I63, F66, V282, and V284) (Fig. S1). We were able to dock (+)-pulegone into the active sites of promiscuous double bond reductases that had previously been demonstrated to reduce this metabolite (NtRed-1 and PfDBR1), but docking did not occur with a productive pose in enzymes that may not readily accommodate (+)-pulegone (AtAER, AaDBR1, and CaDBR1) (Fig. S2). A more in-depth analysis is provided in the Discussion section. In MpPulR, Y78 is positioned within hydrogen-bonding distance of the carbonyl group of (+)-pulegone (average bonding distance of 2.8 Å across the MD simulation), with a geometry consistent with productive electrophilic activation of the substrate. To further dissect the contribution of Y78 to substrate binding, we generated the Y78F variant, which retains the aromatic ring but lacks the phenolic hydroxyl group required for hydrogen bonding. In contrast to the near loss of activity of Y78A (<25% of wild-type), Y78F retained intermediate activity (approximately 60% of wild-type) (Fig. 3, Tables S2 and S3). This partial retention of activity supports a model in which the aromatic ring of Y78 contributes to substrate positioning independent of hydrogen bonding, while the hydroxyl group enables the hydrogen-bonding interaction with the carbonyl oxygen of (+)-pulegone that is critical for catalytic efficiency.

The aromatic residues Y53, F66, and Y257 appear to form a hydrophobic pocket that accommodates the isopropenyl group of (+)-pulegone through van der Waals interactions and potential CH-π contacts (Fig. 2A). Y53 and Y257 are positioned on opposite sides of this pocket, providing steric constraints that may enforce proper substrate orientation. F66, located at the base of the pocket, may contribute to enabling complementarity with the cyclohexanone ring system of the ligand. Residues L56, I63, M135, F281, and V282 line the perimeter of the active site cavity and might provide additional hydrophobic contacts that stabilize the substrate but are less critical for catalysis (Fig. 2B). L56 and I63 flank the entrance to the binding pocket, potentially guiding substrate entry. F281 and V282 are adjacent to the NADPH binding site and may help position the cofactor for optimal hydride transfer geometry. The residues showing minimal impact on activity (M253, V284, and Y287) are located at the periphery of the active site, where they likely contribute to the overall architecture rather than being involved in direct substrate interactions (Fig. 2C).

Evaluating the substrate specificity of MpPulR

To further explore the structural features for substrate binding and catalytic turnover, 40 potential substrates (when counting all enantiomers) were screened in activity assays with recombinant MpPulR. These included α,β-unsaturated monoterpenoid (C10) ketones with a cyclohexanone backbone akin to that of (+)-pulegone (incl. (−)-pulegone, piperitenone, and (+)-piperitenone oxide) (Fig. 4A and Table S4). Citral was included as an acyclic, α,β-unsaturated monoterpenoid aldehyde, while isoegomaketone is an α,β-unsaturated ketone of the furanomonoterpenoid class (Fig. 4A and Table S4). (+)-Carvone, (−)-carvone, (+)-isopiperitenone, (−)-isopiperitenone, (+)-piperitone, and (−)-piperitone are α,β-unsaturated monoterpenoids with a cyclohexenone backbone, while pinocarvone is an α,β-unsaturated monoterpenoid with a bicyclic skeleton (Fig. 4B). (+)-Isopulegone and (−)-isopulegone are α,γ-unsaturated monoterpenoids, citronellal is an acyclic, α,ω-unsaturated monoterpenoid, and 2,4-hexadienal and 2-hexenal are medium chain olefinic C6 aldehydes (Fig. 4B). Fifteen double bond-containing monoterpenoid alcohols with an acyclic, monocyclic, or bicyclic backbone were assayed as well (Fig. 4C). The list was completed by six hydrocarbon monoterpenes (acyclic and monocyclic) (Fig. 4D) and an acyclic monoterpenoid ester (geranyl formate) (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, only six potential substrates from this extensive list—(+)-pulegone, (−)-pulegone, piperitenone, piperitenone oxide, citral, and isoegomaketone—showed measurable activity in assays with MpPulR (Table S5). To further evaluate substrate specificity, kinetic parameters were determined for the active substrates identified in the initial screening. MpPulR exhibited exceptionally high catalytic efficiency with piperitenone and piperitenone oxide (kcat/KM = 4.3 • 105 and 7.1 • 103 s−1 M−1, respectively), medium activity with (+)-pulegone (kcat/KM = 608.4 s−1 M−1), and a lower efficiency with citral (kcat/KM = 236 s−1 M−1), (−)-pulegone (kcat/KM = 5535 s−1 M−1), and isoegomaketone (kcat/KM = 7.9 • 103 s−1 M−1) (Fig. 4A and Table S5).Figure 4Substrate selectivity of peppermint pulegone reductase (MpPulR; Q6WAU0).A, kinetic analysis with selected monoterpenoid substrates. Exploring binding requirements for productive substrates of MpPulR

To define the structural and chemical parameters that govern the binding of a suitable ligand in the active site of MpPulR, we performed molecular docking and subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with five ligands that were productive substrates and two ligands for which a catalytic activity was not measurable. We assessed three parameters to evaluate the likelihood of productive substrate binding (1): Hydrogen Bonding (ligand had to be positioned favorably for hydrogen bonding of carbonyl group with Y78 to ensure proper pose) (2); Enzyme–Ligand Complex Stability (RMSD value of docked substrates during a 20 ns MD simulation had to remain consistently below 4.0 Å); and (3) Cofactor–Double Bond Alignment (distance between the β-carbon of ligand double bond and the carbon atom of NADPH from which a hydride is to be transferred had to be less than 4.5 Å).

Pulegone formed a stable complex with MpPulR throughout the 20 ns MD simulation (RMSD of 3.25 Å), maintaining consistent hydrogen bonding between its carbonyl oxygen and Y78 (average C−C distance of 2.8 Å) (Fig. 5A and Table S6). The C7 atom of the exocyclic double bond remained positioned at a 3.9 Å distance from the hydride donor of NADPH, consistent with productive hydride transfer geometry. Favorable binding was also observed for piperitenone oxide (RMSD of 3.5 Å), likely supported by the interactions of its α,β-conjugated double bond system with residues Y53, F66, and Y257 of the hydrophobic part of the active site pocket (Fig. 5B and Table S6). Hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl functional group of the ligand and Y78 was maintained throughout the simulation (average C−C distance of 4.4 Å), and the β-carbon of the conjugated double bond was positioned at a low distance of 3.3 Å from NADPH. Piperitenone was the most strongly bound ligand tested in our study (RMSD of 2.6 Å), possibly due to additional hydrogen bonding (average C−C distance of 3.4 Å) with T139 (Fig. 5C and Table S6). Furthermore, the C1–C2 double bond contained in the cyclohexene ring system appeared to allow for a tighter packing of the ligand against F281. Interestingly, isoegomaketone adopted a binding pose distinct from that of ligands containing a cyclohexanone or cyclohexanone core (Fig. 5D and Table S6). The furan ring of isoegomaketone was positioned close to M135 and V282, while the α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety maintained hydrogen bonding with Y78 (average C−C distance of 2.3 Å), with a favorable RMSD of 3.5 Å. Citral was the only acyclic substrate that showed in vitro assay activity and exhibited relatively stable binding as a ligand during MD simulations (RMSD of 3.0 Å) (Fig. 5E and Table S6). The aldehyde group appeared to be engaged in hydrogen bonding with Y78 (average C−C distance of 2.0 Å), but the lack of a rigid ring system may have resulted in a positioning of the double bond to be reduced further away from the NADPH cofactor (average C−C distance of 4.3 Å).Figure 5Computational docking of monoterpenoid ligands into the enzyme active site of peppermint pulegone reductase (MpPulR; Q6WAU0). Our docking attempts with (−)-carvone as a ligand failed to result in a catalytically competent pose, despite the presence of an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl moiety as part of a cyclohexene core, which is similar to the structural features present in the competent substrates discussed above. In the energetically most favorable pose, the C1−C2 double bond of (−)-carvone was placed 4.4 Å (average C−C distance) away from the hydride donor carbon atom of the NADPH cofactor (Fig. 5F and Table S6). Additionally, the isopropenyl group caused a steric clash with F66 (average C−C distance of 1.6 Å), thus disrupting hydrogen bonding with Y78. The enzyme-ligand complex showed instability during MD simulations (RMSD of 4.7 Å), which was further evidence for poor binding characteristics. Citronellal, which lacks an α,β-unsaturated system, was unable to form a stable complex with MpPulR (RMSD of 5.5 Å) (Fig. 5G and Table S6). Without the electronic activation provided by conjugation to a carbonyl, the only remaining C−C double bond showed no favorable interactions with the active site residues. The aldehyde group was positioned close to Y78, but the remainder of the ligand failed to retain a productive orientation in MD simulations, confirming that the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl functionality is likely essential for substrate binding by MpPulR.

MpPulR-like enzymes are promiscuous double bond reductases across members of the genus Mentha

We demonstrated as part of the current work that MpPulR accepts piperitenone and piperitenone oxide as substrates, but these metabolites, or others that are biosynthetically derived from them (such as piperitone and piperitone oxide) (Fig. 1), have not been reported to accumulate to appreciable levels in peppermint. We therefore analyzed essential oils obtained from genetically diverse Mentha accessions available through the National Center for Germplasm Resources (NCGR) and, intriguingly, found 11 accessions (representing seven species) that contained high quantities of piperitenone oxide, piperitone, and/or piperitone oxide (Table S7). The fact that piperitenone was only found at extremely low levels indicated that these accessions likely contain a double bond reductase with high activity for reducing this intermediate into piperitone. We hypothesized that putative orthologs of MpPulR were responsible for the formation of metabolites that result from the reduction of piperitenone or piperitenone oxide. Discussion

Previous work on p-menthane monoterpenoids in members of the genus Mentha showed that their biosynthesis proceeds via branched pathways with multiple steps that involve double bond reductases (Fig. 1). One of these reactions is catalyzed by (−)-isopiperitenone reductase (EC 1.3.1.82) of the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase family, while a different reaction, catalyzed by MpPulR (EC 1.3.1.81), generates a mixture of (−)-menthone and (+)-isomenthone from (+)-pulegone (5). Both enzymes will also accept optical antipodes of the natural substrates ((+)-isopiperitenone and (−)-pulegone, respectively) as substrates, albeit with lower catalytic efficiency (22). Based on these preliminary investigations into substrate selectivity, we hypothesized that MpPulR is a promiscuous enzyme that has the potential to generate several reduced monoterpenoids, although these might not be present in essential oils derived from peppermint. To facilitate structure-function studies for MpPulR, we used both experimental crystal structure and computational homology modeling data sets to identify 12 residues that are positioned close to the ligand. We then employed alanine scanning mutagenesis to exchange these residues with L-Ala, an amino acid that lacks a side chain and thus cannot contribute to interactions with a ligand. L-Ala scanning mutagenesis was chosen as the primary strategy for probing active site residues because it systematically eliminates side chain interactions with often minimal disruption to the backbone, which was confirmed for MpPulR and its variants using the ESM-IF modeling framework (Table S2). Purified, recombinant MpPulR and its variants were then tested in assays using (+)-pulegone as a substrate. When compared to wild-type MpPulR, of the 12 residues tested, nine variants showed lower specific activities (Fig. 3), indicating a potential involvement in catalysis. Seven residues (Y53, Y78, M135, M253, Y257, F281, and Y287) are highly conserved across functionally related MDRs of the LTD family in plants. Five residues (L56, I63, F66, V282, and V284) are associated with only a low level of conservation (5, 10, 17, 23, 24) (Fig. S1). Variants of four of these residues spanning both conservation categories (Y53A, F66A, Y78A, and Y257A) showed very low specific activity. Y78 is engaging in hydrogen bonding with the carbonyl group of the ligand, while Y53, F66, and Y257 cause steric constraints that favor accommodation of the exocyclic isopropenyl moiety of (+)-pulegone (Fig. 3). The intermediate activity retained by Y78F, relative to the severe loss of activity in Y78A, further supports the dual role of Y78 in substrate binding. The aromatic ring contributes to steric positioning of the ligand in the active site, while the hydroxyl group mediates hydrogen bonding with the carbonyl oxygen of (+)-pulegone. This is consistent with hydrogen bonding being the primary determinant of Y78’s role in catalytic efficiency, rather than aromaticity alone. L56, I63, M135, F281, and V282 are positioned along the perimeter of the active site pocket, likely engaging in hydrophobic interactions with the ligand. The current study is the first systematic investigation identifying the likely active site residues of an MDR superfamily enzyme of the LTD subfamily. This work has set the stage for future efforts to engineer MpPulR for increased and/or altered product specificity using site saturation mutagenesis targeting the active site residues identified here (25).

Having demonstrated that MpPulR accepts multiple substrates in vitro, we employed computational simulations to explore the characteristics of its promiscuity, resulting in a list of parameters for productive ligand binding (1): presence of an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl for electronic activation and hydrogen bonding with Y78 (2), compatibility for interactions with the hydrophobic residues of the substrate binding pocket, and (3) positioning of the β-carbon of the double bond to be reduced close to the NADPH C4 hydride donor carbon atom. Building on these discoveries, we evaluated if these criteria could be extended to better understand the substrate specificity of other MDRs of plant specialized metabolism. All enzymes investigated in this context (AaDBR1, AtAER, CaDBR1, NtRed-1, and PfDBR1) contain the conserved catalytic triad (Y53, Y78, and Y257) along with the highly conserved residues M135 and F281 (Fig. S1). The active site pockets of enzymes capable of accommodating (+)-pulegone (MpPulR, NtRed-1 and PfDBR1) share a spheroidal substrate binding pocket, with predominantly hydrophobic residues. NtRed-1 is notable for its exceptional substrate promiscuity and displays a spacious binding pocket lined with hydrophobic residues (F281, V284, and Y287) alongside multiple accessible tyrosine residues (Y53, Y78, and Y257) (Figs. S2 and S3). Comparison of docked (+)-pulegone and a potential in vivo substrate, 4-hydroxy-3-methoxycinnamaldehyde, reveals that Y78 undergoes an angular shift to accommodate the differing size and shape of each ligand, suggesting it plays a key role in productive binding across structurally diverse substrates. In contrast, F281 and Y287 are relatively rigid and appear to define the upper boundary of the binding pocket, with V284 positioned between them providing modest conformational relief due to its smaller side chain. This rigidity likely sets an upper size limit on tolerated substrates, while Y78 flexibility within this constrained pocket underlies NtRed-1’s broad but not unlimited substrate range. PfDBR1, which naturally reduces isoegomaketone but also shows activity with (+)-pulegone, has a hydrophobic active site pocket composed of Y53, I63, M135, and V284, along with flexible polar residues (Q65 and S68) (Figs. S2 and S3). This architecture may enable PfDBR to accommodate both compact monocyclic ketones and extended linear substrates.

The active sites of double bond reductases whose natural substrates are phenylpropanoid aldehydes have slightly different properties. The active site of CaDBR1 reflects this specialization. The binding pocket is elongated and channel-like, lined by five tyrosine residues alongside M135, V284 and F281. Productive binding is anchored by dual hydrogen bonding interactions with both Y78 (2.9 Å) and Y257 (3.1 Å), which effectively polarizes the carbonyl group and positions the double bond close to NADPH (4.1 Å) for hydride transfer. F281 may further stabilize binding through pi-stacking with the phenyl ring of the substrate. (+)-Pulegone is geometrically mismatched with this elongated channel. Rather than engaging in hydrogen bonding with Y78 or Y257, the ligand drifts towards a peripheral L-Tyr residue at 4.2 Å, outside the range of effective hydrogen bonding. The absence of an aromatic ring precludes stabilizing contacts with F281. This positioning is reflected in a hydride transfer distance of 4.8 Å between (+)-pulegone and NADPH donor. The combined failure of electrophilic activation and hydride transfer geometry would indicate that (+)-pulegone is unlikely to serve as a productive substrate for CaDBR1 (but it should be noted that these enzymes have not been assayed experimentally with (+)-pulegone as a substrate). A comparable situation is observed in AtAER, where it contains polar and charged residues that contribute to an elongated, channel-like pocket geometry that appears to be incompatible with binding (+)-pulegone effectively (Fig. S2).

AaDBR1 was inactive with (+)-pulegone as a substrate in experimental in vitro assays, despite efficiently reducing larger sesquiterpenoid substrates such as artemisinic aldehyde. The catalytic triad of AaDBR1 is composed of Y53, Y78, and Y257, and structural overlay of (+)-pulegone and artemisinic aldehyde reveals that the two ligands occupy distinct positions within the active site, with pulegone sitting considerably deeper in the binding pocket than the natural substrate (Fig. S4). Artemisinic aldehyde binds in the upper region of the pocket, where its carbonyl oxygen engages in productive hydrogen bonding with Y78 at 3.9 Å. The β-carbon is situated at an average distance of 4.5 Å from the NADPH C4 hydride donor throughout the simulation, consistent with a geometry that facilitates hydride transfer. In contrast, (+)-pulegone is displaced downward towards Y257, forming a hydrogen bond at a distance of 4.0 Å. This repositioning has a pronounced effect on hydride transfer geometry. The β-carbon of (+)-pulegone is 5.9 Å from the NADPH C4 hydride donor, beyond the range required for productive nucleophilic attack. The combination of a weakened electrophilic activation at the carbonyl and a larger hydride transfer distance provides a structural rationale for why (+)-pulegone does not serve as a productive substrate for AaDBR1, despite possessing an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl moiety.

Experimental procedures

Chemicals

The following chemicals were used in enzymatic assays. Allo-ocimene, citronellal, dihydrolinalool, geranyl formate, rose oxide and ocimene were obtained from Vigon (East Stroudsburg). Carveol, carvone, citral, citronellene, citronellol, γ-terpinene, geraniol, hexadienal, hexanal, isopulegol, limonene, (−)-linalool, myrcene, myrtenol, nerol, (+)-Pulegone, terpinolene, and camphor were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Geranyl linalool was obtained from American Tokyo Kasei Inc. Piperitone was obtained from I.P. Callison & Sons. Piperitenone, piperitenone oxide, and pinocarveol were isolated in-house from plant material. Isoegomaketone was obtained from Synthonix.

Substrate docking and molecular dynamics simulations to assess the binding of potential substrates in the MpPulR active site

Substrate docking studies were performed using the YASARA Structure program (version 25.1.13; YASARA Biosciences, Nijmegen, The Netherlands). The initial model of MpPulR complexed with NADPH was constructed using the Boltzmann-weighted Boltz-1 homology modeling algorithm, and structural accuracy was confirmed by referencing the published crystal structure of PulR (PDB ID: 7EQL). Ligand structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and imported directly into YASARA. Prior to docking, both the protein and ligands underwent energy minimization using YASARA’s AMBER14-based force field to optimize geometry and relieve steric clashes. Local docking was then performed with the dock_runlocal macro, and docking results were evaluated based on Catalytically competent distances (e.g., hydride donor–acceptor distances <4.0 Å from the NADPH C4 position), active site contact residues, and calculated binding energies. MD simulations were conducted using the md_run macro in YASARA for each docked complex over 20 nanoseconds under explicit solvent conditions (water, pH 7.0). The substrate was constrained within 4.2 Å of the NADPH hydride to preserve the catalytic pose during equilibration. Key MD simulation outputs included the minimum solute energy structure (reflecting the most favorable ligand orientation and positioning), the trajectory of the ligand and active site residues over time (reflecting dynamic stability), and interaction persistence (reflecting hydrogen bonding, π-stacking, and hydrophobic contacts) (Fig. S5).

Site-directed mutagenesis to change putative MpPulR active site residues

Point mutations were introduced by PCR using QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis (26); a complete list of primers used in these reactions is provided in Table S8. PCR amplicons were DpnI-digested, transformed into E. coli Top10 cells (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and plated onto LB-agar plates containing 50 μg/ml kanamycin. Individual colonies were picked, and bacteria were grown under selection pressure. Plasmid DNA was then isolated using a commercial kit (GeneJet Mini Prep kit, Fermentas, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and the inserted gene sequence was confirmed by a commercial service provider (Eurofins Genomics, Huntsville, AL, USA).

Heterologous expression of MpPulR gene, purification of the corresponding recombinant protein, and enzyme assays

The coding for PulR was subcloned into the pET-28b vector (with N-terminal histidine tag; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) and transformed into E. coli BL21 cells. A single colony was picked and grown in a 5 ml tube of LB containing 50 mg/ml of kanamycin and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C. The 5 ml seed culture was added to a flask with 150 ml of fresh LB medium (containing 50 mg/ml kanamycin) and grown until an optical density of 0.5 at 600 nm was reached. Transgene expression was induced by addition of isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (final concentration 0.5 mM), and the culture was maintained for 24 h at 16 °C, while shaking at 250 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation for 10 min at 4 °C and 5000g. The supernatant was discarded, and to the remaining pellet 400 μl of 50 mM phosphate-citrate buffer (pH 6) was added. The mixture was sonicated three times for 20 s each (with resting intervals of 40 s on ice). The resulting suspension was centrifuged for 30 min at 4 °C and 13,000g, thereby generating a crude soluble protein extract. A 1 ml Nickel NTA column (ThermoFisher) was equilibrated with 10 column volumes of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 20 mM imidazole. The crude soluble protein extract was added to the column, followed by washing with the same buffer. Bound proteins were eluted with 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 200 mM imidazole; fractions were collected (0.5 ml each), and the purity of the target protein in each fraction was assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Enzyme assays were carried out with 100 μl of the protein-purified fraction (>90% PulR purity), 10 mM NADPH, 10 mM substrate in 50 mM phosphate-citrate buffer (pH 6), topped with n-hexane (containing 15 ng/μl camphor as internal standard for subsequent analyses) in a 2 ml glass vial by slowly rotating the vial for 16 h at 31 °C. The assay vials were transferred to a freezer, and the hexane fraction was removed for GC analysis as described below. Kinetic assays were performed with 25 μg of purified enzyme and varying concentrations of substrate (10–400 μm) by incubating for 5 to 30 min at 31 °C. To ensure accurate kinetic measurements, initial rate conditions were established by testing linearity with respect to both time and enzyme concentration. Linearity was assessed by performing assays at three enzyme concentrations (5, 25, and 100 μg) and sampling at multiple time points (5, 15, 30 min and 1 h). Reaction conditions were selected to ensure that substrate conversion remained within the linear range of product formation and that NADPH consumption was not rate-limiting. These optimized parameters were then used for all kinetic measurements, and kinetic values were fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation.

Substrates and products of enzyme assays were separated over a β-cyclodextrin column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm; J&W Scientific) using a model 7890A gas chromatograph equipped with a model 7693 autosampler and a flame ionization detector (Agilent Technologies). The operating conditions were as follows: He as carrier gas at 1 ml min−1, inlet temperature at 270 °C (split mode 1 : 20), injection volume of 1 μl, detector temperature at 230 °C, and an oven temperature gradient (40 °C on hold for 1 min, then 2 °C min−1 to 150 °C, hold for 2 min, then ramp at 50 °C min−1 to 200 °C, and final hold for 2 min. Quantitation was performed using the ChemStation B.03.02 software (Agilent Technologies) based upon calibration curves with known amounts of authentic standards and normalization to the peak area of the internal standard (camphor). Authentic standards for all products were available in our in-house chemical library.
