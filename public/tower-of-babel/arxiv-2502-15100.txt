Digitized counterdiabatic quantum critical dynamics
Anne-Maria Visuri, Alejandro Gomez Cadavid, Balaganchi A. Bhargava, Sebastián V. Romero, András Grabarits, Pranav Chandarana, Enrique Solano, Adolfo del Campo, Narendra N. Hegade

Abstract
We experimentally demonstrate that a digitized counterdiabatic quantum protocol reduces the number of topological defects created during a fast quench across a quantum phase transition. To show this, we perform quantum simulations of one- and two-dimensional transverse-field Ising models driven from the paramagnetic to the ferromagnetic phase. We utilize superconducting cloud-based quantum processors with up to 156 qubits. Our data reveal that the digitized counterdiabatic protocol reduces defect formation by up to 48% in the fast-quench regime—an improvement hard to achieve through digitized quantum annealing under current noise levels. The experimental results closely match theoretical and numerical predictions at short evolution times, before deviating at longer times due to hardware noise. In one dimension, we derive an analytic solution for the defect number distribution in the fast-quench limit. For two-dimensional geometries, where analytical solutions are unknown and numerical simulations are challenging, we use advanced matrix-product-state methods. Our findings indicate a practical way to control the topological defect formation during fast quenches and highlight the utility of counterdiabatic protocols for quantum optimization and quantum simulation in material design on current quantum processors.



The critical dynamics close to phase transitions has universal features that connect phenomena at vastly different energy scales: the polarization of magnetic materials, the superfluid transition of liquid helium, and the inflationary dynamics of the early universe
Zurek 1996
. Close to a continuous symmetry-breaking phase transition, the spatial correlation length and the relaxation time diverge.
As a result, a system driven through the phase transition at a nonzero rate fails to reach its instantaneous equilibrium with a uniform phase. Instead, domains of different broken-symmetry states are formed, leading to the excitation of topological defects such as domain walls and vortices.

The theoretical paradigm describing the formation of topological defects is known as the Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM). It relates the density of defects with the driving rate through a universal power-law dependence where the exponent is determined by the universality class of the phase transition
del Campo and Zurek 2014
. The mechanism, originally conceived for continuous phase transitions, was recently extended to phase transitions with tunable order
Rams et al. 2019
;
Suzuki and Zurek 2024
;
Sadhasivam et al. 2024
. The applicability of the KZM to both classical and quantum phase transitions
has been verified in numerous theoretical and experimental studies. In recent years, quantum simulation platforms based on Rydberg atoms
Keesling et al. 2019

and superconducting circuits
Weinberg et al. 2020
;
Bando et al. 2020
;
King et al. 2022
;
Miessen et al. 2024
;
Andersen et al. 2024
;
Teplitskiy et al. 2024
have been used to demonstrate the quantum Kibble-Zurek mechanism (QKZM) in new regimes. While most demonstrations have realized the one-dimensional transverse-field Ising model, recent work has also explored two-dimensional interacting systems
Ebadi et al. 2021
;
King et al. 2024
for which classical simulations are difficult
Schmitt et al. 2022
.
The Kibble-Zurek scaling is restricted to a low density of defects near the adiabatic limit. It breaks down in fast quenches, where the defect density no longer follows a power law as a function of the quench rate but saturates into a plateau. However, the value of the plateau has been shown to obey a power law as a function of the quench depth, extending nonequilibrium universality far from the adiabatic regime
Xia and Zeng 2021
;
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Grabarits et al. 2025
.

The QKZM and its extensions not only provide a universal description of nonequilibrium critical phenomena but are also relevant in the practical pursuit of quantum computing relying on the adiabatic theorem.
In adiabatic quantum computing,
an easy-to-prepare ground state of an initial Hamiltonian is evolved adiabatically to that of a final Hamiltonian encoding the solution
Albash and Lidar 2018
.
When the Hamiltonian parameters are changed sufficiently slowly, the system remains in its instantaneous ground state.
The time scale required for adiabaticity, however, typically exceeds the coherence time in current and near-term quantum computers, making adiabatic quantum computing infeasible. Control protocols called shortcuts to adiabaticity
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
offer a solution to this problem, allowing to shorten the evolution time at the cost of additional control fields.
One promising technique in this category is counterdiabatic driving (CD), where additional terms added to the Hamiltonian exactly cancel out transitions to excited states
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Berry 2009
. This approach has been successfully applied to a variety of problems
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Saberi et al. 2014
;
Hegade et al. 2021
;
Hegade et al. 2022
;
Chandarana et al. 2022
;
Chandarana et al. 2023
.

For systems with a finite gap between the energies of the ground state and the first excited state, a driving rate below the energy gap is known to preserve adiabaticity. Close to the critical point for a quantum phase transition, however, the energy gap closes in the thermodynamic limit, and excitations become inevitable.
This is relevant for quantum computing, for instance, optimization
Abbas et al. 2024
and material simulation
Clinton et al. 2024
;
Maskara et al. 2025
, since the excitations
along the adiabatic path deteriorate the accuracy of the solution
Suzuki 2011
.
While CD has been established as a way to speed up adiabatic dynamics, it has also been shown
to reduce the excitations created in traversing a critical point
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Saberi et al. 2014
. Recent theoretical work has further connected the phenomenology of the QKZM with the theory of quantum speed limits
Puebla et al. 2020
;
Carolan et al. 2022
. To our knowledge, however, the effects of CD on topological excitations in many-body systems have not yet been investigated experimentally.

Here, we verify experimentally, using IBM quantum processors, that the digitized counterdiabatic evolution leads to a reduction in the density of topological defects in the final state (see Fig.
1
a). We further characterize the breakdown of the Kibble-Zurek scaling and the saturation of the defect density in fast quenches experimentally and theoretically, both with and without CD.
Our results demonstrate the potential of counterdiabatic protocols for improved accuracy in quantum optimization, quantum simulation, and quantum state preparation.

Digitized counterdiabatic protocol in fast quenches

The KZM description of critical dynamics identifies a freeze-out time
\bar{t}
measured with respect to the time at which the transition occurs. Between
-\bar{t}
and
+\bar{t}
, the system cannot reach its instantaneous equilibrium due to the diverging relaxation time. The correlation length in the frozen regime corresponds to the average size of the broken-symmetry domains formed in the phase transition, and the inverse correlation length sets the average density of defects. According to the KZM,
the average density of point-like defects follows the universal scaling law
\langle\hat{n}_{\text{def}}\rangle\sim T^{-\frac{d\nu}{1+z\nu}}
,
where
T
is the total quench time,
d
the spatial dimension,
\nu
is the critical exponent of the correlation length and
z
the dynamical critical exponent. Recent works beyond the KZM paradigm have shown that not only the average density is universal but also the full defect number distribution, for which all cumulants share the same power-law with
T

del Campo 2018
;
Cui et al. 2020
;
Bando et al. 2020
;
King et al. 2022
.

Deviations from the Kibble-Zurek scaling are known to occur in fast quenches, where the defect density instead reaches a plateau and is independent of the quench rate. In this limit, the defect density and the critical quench time were proposed to follow a universal scaling with the quench depth—the distance of the final control parameter from its critical value
Xia and Zeng 2021
;
Zeng et al. 2023
. However, such scaling laws have not been investigated for counterdiabatic dynamics.
Rather than probing the scaling laws, we study the reduction of the defect density plateau with CD in different geometries at a fixed quench depth.
As a benchmark for the experiments in one dimension, we derive the exact solution for the defect number distribution.

To evolve the ground state of an initial Hamiltonian
H_{i}
into that of a final Hamiltonian
H_{f}
, one can introduce a time-dependent control parameter
\lambda(t)
in the total Hamiltonian

H(\lambda)=\left(1-\lambda(t)\right)H_{i}+\lambda(t)H_{f}.

(1)

The scheduling function
\lambda(t)
is chosen as a smooth function that changes from
\lambda(0)=0
to
\lambda(T)=1
at the final time
T
. If the change is sufficiently slow, the process is adiabatic. We use here a linear function
\lambda(t)=t/T
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
b.
Specifically, we implement the paramagnetic-to-ferromagnetic phase transition of the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) with the initial and final Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{i}=-g\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}_{j},\hskip 28.45274ptH_{f}=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\hat{Z}_{i}\hat{Z}_{j},

(2)

where
\hat{X}_{i}
and
\hat{Z}_{i}
denote the Pauli operators acting on lattice site
i
,
N
is the number of qubits, and
\langle i,j\rangle
indicates nearest neighbors. The transverse field is denoted by
g
and the Ising interaction by
J
, and we adopt natural units with
\hbar=1
. In our experiments, we set
g=J
and
J=1
. Thus, the energy and time scales are given by
J
and
1/J
, respectively. The initial state is the ground state of
H_{i}
:
\ket{+}^{\otimes N}=\left[(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\right]^{\otimes N}
.

Counterdiabatic driving makes it possible to implement and fast-forward an adiabatic reference trajectory in an arbitrarily short time. It relies on the geometric properties of eigenstates and does not require any dissipative processes. Diabatic excitations are canceled out by an auxiliary driving term added to the Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)=H(\lambda)+\dot{\lambda}A_{\lambda}
, where
A_{\lambda}
is known as the adiabatic gauge potential (AGP) or gauge connection
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Berry 2009
;
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017
.
Solving the AGP exactly requires diagonalizing the many-body Hamiltonian and is, therefore, only possible for small systems. Furthermore,
A_{\lambda}
generally consists of highly non-local many-body couplings, which prohibits its exact implementation. Local approximations are therefore often employed
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Saberi et al. 2014
;
Claeys et al. 2019
;
Takahashi and del Campo 2024
. To improve the performance of such local CD protocols in mimicking adiabatic dynamics through a phase transition, the use of additional control terms has been suggested to avoid traversing the critical point
Rams et al. 2019
;
Morawetz and Polkovnikov 2024
.

One possible local approximation is obtained through a nested-commutator (NC) series expansion
Claeys et al. 2019

A_{\lambda}^{l}=i\sum_{k=1}^{l}\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{O}_{2k-1}(t),

(3)

where
\hat{O}_{k}(t)=[H,\hat{O}_{k-1}(t)]
and
\hat{O}_{0}=\partial_{\lambda}H
.
Only odd orders of nested commutators appear in the ansatz. The sum is truncated at the order
l
, which controls the locality of the operators that are included. The variational parameters
\alpha_{k}
are found by minimizing the action
S_{\lambda}(A_{\lambda})=\text{Tr}\left(G_{\lambda}^{\dagger}G_{\lambda}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\right)
with
G_{\lambda}=\partial_{\lambda}H+i[A_{\lambda},H]
(see Supplementary Information).
For the time-dependent Hamiltonian defined by equations (
1
) and (
2
), we obtain the AGP of equation (
3
) as

A_{\lambda}^{1}=2gJ\alpha_{1}(\lambda)\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(\hat{Y}_{i}\hat{Z}_{j}+\hat{Z}_{i}\hat{Y}_{j}\right)

(4)

when truncated at order
l=1
. In one dimension, for open boundary conditions, the time-dependent coefficient
\alpha_{1}
reads

\alpha_{1}(\lambda)=-\frac{(N-1)}{16g^{2}(N-1)(\lambda-1)^{2}+4J^{2}[4(N-1)-3]\lambda^{2}}.

(5)

The two-body terms of equation (
4
) are not straightforward to implement on analog devices such as neutral-atom platforms or quantum annealers, and we perform the quantum simulations in a digitized manner. To implement the time-evolution operator resulting from
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
on gate-based quantum computers, we use the first-order Suzuki-Trotter decomposition to obtain the digitized time-evolution operator

U(T)=\prod_{m=0}^{M}\exp[-i\mathcal{H}(m\delta t)\delta t]+\mathcal{O}(M\delta t^{2})

up to
M
Trotter steps with
\delta t=T/M
. The quantum circuit implementing the digitized time evolution is illustrated in Fig.
1
c.

Defect statistics

To characterize the defect formation, we measure the expectation value of the density of defects

\hat{n}_{\text{def}}=\frac{1}{2N_{e}}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}(1-\hat{Z}_{i}\hat{Z}_{j}),

(6)

where
N_{e}
is the number of edges between qubits. In the Ising model, the defects correspond to kinks in the magnetization where
\langle Z_{i}\rangle
changes sign.
We consider various geometries with different numbers of edges: a one-dimensional chain, a three-leg ladder, a two-dimensional heavy-hexagonal lattice, and a two-dimensional square lattice, illustrated in Fig.
2
. We use open boundary conditions in all geometries.
For the one-dimensional chain, the number of edges is
N_{e}=N-1
, while for the square lattices,
N_{e}=N_{x}(N_{y}-1)+N_{y}(N_{x}-1)
, where
N_{x}
is the number of columns and
N_{y}
the number of rows. For the heavy-hexagonal geometry with 156 qubits considered here,
N_{e}=176
. We note that the definition of the kink number operator of equation (
6
) may count single spin-flip impurities as multiple kinks, depending on the geometry and the position of the spin. For instance, in the one-dimensional chain, an impurity of the type
\ket{\uparrow\uparrow\dots\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\uparrow\dots}
would be counted as two kinks. Different definitions of the kink operator were investigated in detail in a recent numerical study of the 1D TFIM
Garcia and Chepiga 2024
, where it was shown that for the final Hamiltonian considered here, with no transverse field, the kink definition did not have an effect on the Kibble-Zurek scaling exponent.

We analyze the first three cumulants of the defect density distribution. Given a probability distribution of the density of kinks
P(n)=\langle\delta(\hat{n}_{\text{def}}-n)\rangle
, its characteristic function is given by the Fourier transform

\tilde{P}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[e^{in\theta}]
. The logarithm of the latter is known as the cumulant generating function, which allows one to define cumulants
\kappa_{q}
through the identity
\log\tilde{P}(\theta)=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{q}(i\theta)^{q}/q!

The first three cumulants
\kappa_{1}=\langle\hat{n}_{\text{def}}\rangle
,
\kappa_{2}=N_{e}\left\langle\left(\hat{n}_{\text{def}}-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{def}}\rangle\right)^{2}\right\rangle
, and
\kappa_{3}=N_{e}^{2}\left\langle\left(\hat{n}_{\text{def}}-\langle\hat{n}_{\text{def}}\rangle\right)^{3}\right\rangle
,
respectively, correspond to the mean, variance, and the third central moment. This dictates the skewness of the distribution. Evaluating them in the initial state
\ket{+}^{\otimes N}
gives
\kappa_{1}=1/2
,
\kappa_{2}=1/4
, and
\kappa_{3}=0
.

In one dimension, for periodic boundary conditions, the transverse-field Ising model is exactly solvable through a Jordan-Wigner transformation to the free-fermion basis
Dziarmaga 2005
. To analyze kink formation, the model can be written as an ensemble of independent modes, each with excitation probability
p_{k}
. As a result, the
statistics of the defect formation can be described in terms of independent Bernoulli trials, where a defect is formed in quasimomentum state
k
with probability
p_{k}

del Campo 2018
.
We extend this solution to the fast-quench limit both in the absence and presence of CD and derive the momentum-dependent excitation probability
p_{k}
in both cases. The probabilities are obtained by solving numerically the corresponding time-dependent Schrödinger equation, as shown in the Supplementary Information.
The leading cumulants up to third order are functions of
p_{k}
given by
\kappa_{1}=\sum_{k}p_{k}
,
\kappa_{2}=\sum_{k}p_{k}(1-p_{k})
,
\kappa_{3}=\sum_{k}p_{k}(1-p_{k})(1-2p_{k})
.
In the limit of a sudden quench,
T\to 0
, we recover the simple expressions
\kappa_{1}=1/2
,
\kappa_{2}=1/4
, and
\kappa_{3}=0
when no CD is applied. In the presence of CD, the cumulants are found as
\kappa_{1}\approx 0.22
,
\kappa_{2}\approx 0.13
, and
\kappa_{3}\approx 0.04
. We remark that in the presence of CD, a discontinuity exists at
T=0
: The values of the cumulants in the initial state differ from those in the
T\to 0
limit (see Supplementary Information).

Discussion

In addition to measuring the predicted plateau of the mean defect density, we gain insight into the nonequilibrium dynamics of rapid quenches by measuring the variance and skewness of the defect density distribution. While in the KZ scaling regime, all cumulants are predicted to exhibit a common universal power-law scaling
del Campo 2018
;
Cui et al. 2020
;
Bando et al. 2020
;
King et al. 2022
, we find that the dependence of the skewness on the quench time differs from the mean and variance in the fast-quench regime. In particular, in the absence of CD, the skewness has a nonmonotonic dependence on the quench time in all geometries while the mean and variance show a plateau at
T\to 0
and a monotonic decrease for
T\gtrsim 1/J
. For the ladder and two-dimensional square lattice, the skewness increases as a function of the total evolution time while the mean and variance decrease.
Interestingly, in one dimension and the heavy-hexagonal geometry, the predicted deviations from a symmetric distribution are smaller by an order of magnitude compared to the ladder and the 2D square lattice and the distribution is skewed in the opposite direction. In the latter two cases, the rapid quench favors rare events with a defect density below the mean. This nontrivial dependence of the asymmetry of the distribution on geometry is an interesting avenue for further study.

Defect formation is well understood in the limit of slow quenches, as it is described by the QKZM and has been experimentally verified. However, the QKZM’s validity is restricted to a low density of kinks. We have demonstrated defect formation for fast quenches away from the adiabatic limit when the defect density of the uncontrolled system saturates to a plateau value independent of the quench time. In this regime, our results establish experimentally that the defect density can be tailored and suppressed by implementing approximate CD controls. This approach opens new avenues for quantum simulation and optimization assisted by CD. In the latter context, the paramagnet-to-spin-glass phase transition is of first-order, calling for an extension to the fast-quench regime of the dynamics of first-order quantum phase transitions
Rams et al. 2019
;
Suzuki and Zurek 2024
;
Sadhasivam et al. 2024
and its combination with approximate CD. Our study motivates the use of digital quantum computers for detailed simulations of quantum many-body systems in previously unexplored regimes.

Methods

Experiment

The experiments were realized on 156 qubit IBM Heron devices accessed through the cloud using qiskit
Javadi-Abhari et al. 2024
. We used both IBM Fez and IBM Marrakesh based on availability and calibration at the time of the experiment. Firstly, we considered nine different transpilation techniques and checked which one gave the best performance. This test was performed for a circuit including CD at
T=0.1/J
and
dt=0.1/J
for a 100-qubit 1D chain with open boundary conditions, run on IBM Fez with 20000 shots. These nine different transpilation techniques arise from the combination of three gate scheduling types: as-late-as-possible (ALAP), as-soon-as-possible (ASAP), and none, and using either standard basis gates (CZ, X, SX, RZ), fractional gates
IBM Quantum
, and standard basis gates plus dynamical decoupling (DD) with the
X_{p}X_{m}
sequence. In Table
1
, we observe that the best performance resulted from using the standard gates with DD (
X_{p}X_{m}
) and scheduling the gates ALAP. This scheduling is a suppression technique that delays the gates as much as possible, so qubits are not idle in the middle of the circuit. In case idle qubits are unavoidable, dynamical decoupling reduces possible cross-talk between qubits, further suppressing coherent errors. Therefore, in this paper, we work with standard basis gates.

Secondly, we build the circuits based on graph coloring to parallelize gates and have as few entangling layers as possible. Similar techniques have been applied in a heavy-hexagonal architecture
Kim et al. 2023
. Here, we create a conflict graph where each gate is a node and two nodes are connected if their associated gates overlap. Then, the graph is colored, resulting in layers of entangling gates that can be applied in parallel. Since the interactions between spins are short-range, greedy optimizers provide optimal solutions for the graph coloring problem. In particular, we use the default solvers of the Networkx library
Hagberg et al. 2008
. A trivial case is the 1D chain, whose efficient gate ordering is shown in the Supplementary Information.

Thirdly, due to current hardware limitations, the number of time steps in the digitized time evolution is held constant for
T\geq 0.8/J
in order to minimize device error. This causes the time step size
\delta t
to increase.
We set the maximum number of Trotter steps to six, which gave no more than 3000 entangling gates in the studied cases.

Simulations with matrix product states

For the ladder and two-dimensional geometries, we obtain reference solutions by time-dependent matrix product states (MPS)
Schollwöck 2011
simulations. We use the time-dependent variational principle
Haegeman et al. 2016
with a two-site update, provided in the open-source package
ITensors.jl
Fishman et al. 2022
. For the reference lines in Fig.
3
b-d, we set the time step to
\delta t=0.005/J
, the truncation cutoff to
10^{-20}
, and limit the maximum bond dimension to
\chi=250
. We have verified the convergence of the computed observables so that the numerical accuracy is given by the line width in the plots. While the short-time evolution of the systems studied here can be simulated accurately with MPS,
recent work using analog
King et al. 2024
and digital-analog
Andersen et al. 2024
quantum simulators has reported evolution times that are inaccessible with classical simulation methods at the same level of accuracy.

For the two-dimensional geometries, we find that the run times required for the level of accuracy chosen here are up to several hours.
For the heavy-hexagonal lattice, we also record the wall-clock run time of time evolution up to
T=2.7/J
, corresponding to a single data point in Fig.
3
b. To achieve similar accuracy for
\kappa_{1}
as the experiment, quantified by the difference between the measured
\kappa_{1}
and the converged value from the MPS simulation shown in Fig.
3
b, we observe a run time of TDVP of around
10
s. This number is obtained for TDVP with a two-site update, run on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M3 chip, 8 cores, and 16 GB of memory.
We note that the run times could potentially be reduced, depending on the geometry, by using different variations of time-evolution algorithms
Yang and White 2020
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Tindall et al. 2024
;
Patra et al. 2024
or parallelization
Secular et al. 2020
. For increasing system sizes, connectivities, and evolution times, the growth of entaglement will nevertheless eventually prohibit accurate classical simulations, a limitation inherently absent in quantum devices.
