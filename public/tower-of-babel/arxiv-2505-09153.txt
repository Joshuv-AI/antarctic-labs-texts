Neutrino Fluence influenced by Memory Burdened Primordial Black Holes
Arnab Chaudhuri, Koushik Pal, Rukmani Mohanta

Abstract
Primordial black holes (PBHs), formed in the early Universe, are capable of emitting both Standard Model (SM) and beyond-the-Standard-Model (BSM) particles via Hawking radiation. We investigate the impact of the recently proposed memory burden effect—a quantum gravitational backreaction that suppresses black hole evaporation once approximately half the PBH mass is lost—on the production of high-energy neutrinos. In particular, we examine how this suppression modifies the time-integrated muon neutrino flux, with and without the inclusion of heavy neutral leptons (HNLs) as BSM states. We compute the resulting neutrino fluence for different suppression strengths and analyze the prospects for detection in neutrino telescopes such as IceCube. Our results show that the memory burden significantly alters the neutrino energy spectrum, particularly at high energies, and that the presence of HNLs can counterbalance this suppression by introducing additional neutrino production channels. These findings have important implications for the detectability of PBH evaporation signatures and the parameter space of HNLs in cosmological settings.



I
Introduction

The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics has been remarkably successful in describing known elementary particles and their interactions through electromagnetic, weak, and strong forces. However, it leaves several important questions unanswered. For example, it cannot explain why the universe is dominated by matter rather than antimatter, nor does it incorporate gravity in a way that is consistent with general relativity. It also lacks any explanation for the accelerating expansion of the universe, which is usually attributed to dark energy and also does not provide a viable explanation for neutrino masses. Perhaps most significantly, the SM does not include a suitable candidate for dark matter (DM)
Aghanim et al. 2020
, which is believed to make up most of the matter in the universe. These gaps point to the need for new physics beyond the Standard Model—frameworks that can account for these missing pieces of our understanding of the cosmos.

A viable way to look into the shortcomings of the SM is to go beyond the standard model (BSM). Over time, multiple extensions to the standard model have surfaced, with scalar extensions being the most popular ones

These PBHs are formed in the very early universe due to quantum fluctuations and evaporate well before the onset of Big Bang Nucleosynthesis BBN, thus producing an early matter dominated (EMD) era. Even though they are evaporated well before the BBN, their effects can be still noticeable in the present universe. For example, the evaporation process can dilute the preexisting frozen out species through entropy injection, can produce dark matter and also be a viable source of neutrino production

Given the context of our work with IceCube, our attention is directed toward these small-scale PBHs that undergo Hawking evaporation, potentially emitting detectable neutrinos.

Evaporating PBHs are expected to emit all elementary particles lighter than their Hawking temperature. Among the SM particles, neutrinos serve as a particularly significant signature of such evaporation events. In the context of BSM physics, PBH evaporation also offers a potential window into new particles. Several BSM frameworks propose the existence of sterile neutrinos or heavy neutral leptons (HNLs) as a means to account for the observed neutrino masses and mixing

Despite extensive experimental efforts, no conclusive evidence for HNLs has been observed to date. Nonetheless, HNLs in the MeV–GeV mass range remain compelling candidates

Furthermore, the coexistence of PBHs and HNLs in the early universe could play a critical role in scenarios of leptogenesis, offering a possible explanation for the observed baryon asymmetry of the universe

We take into account the ”memory burden” effect on PBHs, a concept first introduced in
Dvali 2018a
. It is well known that Hawking’s semiclassical calculations break down when a black hole has lost approximately half of its initial mass. These results are strictly valid only in the theoretical limit
M\to\infty,G\to 0,r_{s}=\text{const.}
, where
G
represents the gravitational constant and
r_{s}=2GM/c^{2}
is the Schwarzschild radius. In this regime, the calculations neglect the backreaction of the emitted radiation on the black hole, an effect that becomes increasingly significant as the energy of the emitted quanta approaches the remaining energy of the black hole. The phenomenological and cosmological implications of the memory burden have been explored in recent works
Barman et al. 2024
;
Barman et al. 2025
;
Thoss et al. 2024
.
As emphasized in
Dvali et al. 2020
, an aged black hole that has lost half of its initial mass is fundamentally distinct from a young, classical black hole of the same mass. The critical insight from
Dvali et al. 2020
is that this backreaction leads to a universal phenomenon known as the ”memory burden”, which significantly alters the dynamics of black hole evaporation. This memory effect, originally introduced in
Dvali 2018a
, suppresses further evaporation, fundamentally altering the black hole’s evolution.

In this paper, we explore how an exploding memory-burdened PBH near Earth could help constrain HNL properties using neutrino telescopes
Coogan et al. 2021
;
Carr et al. 2016

\sim 1^{\circ}

Since photons from PBH explosions are much more abundant than neutrinos, we also estimate the expected photon signals at HAWC, which detects photons in the 100 GeV–100 TeV range
Ackermann et al. 2019
. By combining neutrino and photon observations, we can determine the PBH’s position more precisely. We assess how HNLs, if produced during memory burdened PBH evaporation, could modify the neutrino spectrum at IceCube and the photon spectrum at HAWC
Arbey and Auffinger 2019
.

This study examines the contributions of SM particles to the muon neutrino spectrum observed at IceCube
Abbasi et al. 2025
and further explores potential neutrino and photon signals arising from the decay of HNLs . A detailed statistical analysis is employed to evaluate the total time integrated muon neutrino flux for different channels within the SM and in presence of HNLs under the influence of the memory burden effect. The emitted flux is calculated for different suppression strength and it is shown that the flux decreases indeed varies as the strength of the memory burden is increased.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the consequences of quantum gravitational memory burden effects on the evaporation of PBHs and the resulting neutrino fluence. By incorporating a suppression factor that parametrizes the influence of backreaction on Hawking radiation, we demonstrated that memory burden leads to a systematic reduction in the high-energy muon neutrino flux. This suppression is particularly relevant for late-stage PBH evaporation and may substantially affect the prospects for detecting PBH signatures at neutrino telescopes.

Furthermore, we considered the impact of HNLs as additional BSM degrees of freedom. We found that HNL decays enhance the overall neutrino fluence, particularly in the MeV–GeV energy range, partially compensating for the suppression induced by the memory burden. This interplay introduces a novel observational signature that could distinguish between pure SM and extended BSM scenarios in future experiments.

Our findings underscore the importance of accounting for both quantum backreaction and new particle content in realistic PBH models. Future studies—including correlations with photon observatories (e.g., HAWC) and extended detector capabilities (e.g., IceCube-Gen2)—could provide further avenues to constrain or reveal the physics of PBH evaporation in the presence of quantum gravitational effects.
