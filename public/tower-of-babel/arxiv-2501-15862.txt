Hydrodynamic limit for an active-passive exclusion process
Deyue Li

Abstract
The collective non-equilibrium dynamics of multi-component mixtures of interacting active (self-propelled) and passive (diffusive) particles have garnered great interest in physics community. However, the mathematical understanding of these systems remains partial. In this work, we consider a lattice gas model of active-passive particle mixtures with exclusion, where the self-propulsion orientations of active particles undergo Brownian motion on a torus. We derive the hydrodynamic equations governing the particle densities. Due to the presence of two types of particles with continuous-valued orientations, further generalizations of non-gradient decomposition and spectral gap estimation developed for pure active case are necessitated, which entails novel challenges and new proofs. Key words : Hydrodynamic limit, Active matter, Active-passive mixtures, Non-gradient systems, Lattice gases, Exclusion processes, Statistical physics. AMS subject classification : 60K35, 82C22.



1
Introduction

Active matter systems, composed of self-propelled agents that extract energy from
their environment, are inherently out of equilibrium.
These systems exhibit a rich diversity of collective behaviors arising
from agent interactions, such as clustering of
chemically interacting particles
[
1
]
,
flocking of birds
[
29
]
,
self-organization of quorum sensing bacteria
[
23
]
,
and self-assembly of active materials from
catalytic colloids
[
21
]
.
Despite their diversity, these systems display
spontaneous condensate even without attractive or aligning
interactions
[
3
]
.
This phenomenon, known as

Motility Induced Phase Separation
(MIPS) in the physics community,
results from
interplay between particle crowding and velocity reduction:
as particles aggregate, their motion slows, further promoting aggregation.
Recently, there has been great practical interest in
the phase-separation behavior of active systems where the micro-swimmers are mixed
with passive particles.
Examples include active-passive segregation between rodlike particles
[
19
]
,
crystallization of glassy systems comprising mixtures of active and passive hard spheres
[
20
]
,
and collective dynamics in phase-separated mixtures of active and passive Brownian particles
[
30
]
.

Active matter systems also have drawn great attention from the mathematical community.
Many mathematical progress has been achieved under
mean-field assumption
[
9
,
8
,
2
]
.
While the mean-field assumption simplifies several complexities,
and allows explicit
derivation of macroscopic equations,
these derivations depend on the BBGKY hierarchy and molecular chaos assumption,
which generally fail to fully capture
the microscopic mechanism underlying the emergence of MIPS
[
2
]
.
A more rigorous and systematic approach is utilizing
hydrodynamic limit theory to derive hydrodynamic equations from microscopic dynamics.
One of the most fundamental and widely studied class of models
amenable to hydrodynamic limit theory is lattice gas models
[
11
]
,
where particles hop randomly on a discrete
lattice according to some local rules.
By taking the lattice spacing to zero and applying appropriate temporal and spatial rescaling,
one can rigorously derive
continuum equations that describe the large-scale behaviour of active systems
under relatively general assumptions.

The application of hydrodynamic limit theory to various classes
of active lattice gas (ALG) models has achieved remarkable success
[
6
,
16
,
2
]
,
providing valuable insights into the physics of active matter
[
13
]
.
However, while most of the existing studies focus on the hydrodynamic limit of
pure active lattice models,
mathematical progress for active-passive mixtures remains partial.
To the best of our knowledge, the first result on hydrodynamic limit of
active-passive lattice gas (APLG) model,
restricted to only two self-propelled directions,
was studied in
[
18
]
.

In this work, we remove such restriction on directions and
consider a more general APLG model
where active and passive particles interact via exclusion,
and the continuous-valued orientations of active particles undergo Brownian motion on a torus.
We extend the proof of the hydrodynamic limit for pure active exclusion process on lattice
[
6
,
16
]

to the active-passive mixed exclusion process.
This extension introduces additional degrees of freedom
into the set of measure-valued parameters characterizing local equilibrium
and necessitates a broader working function class for the non-gradient method.
Consequently, significant adaptations to the proof of the hydrodynamic
limit in pure active case are required to address these difficulties.

1.1
Previous works and our contributions

Starting from the seminal work of Vicsek
[
28
]
,
active matter has become a focal point of interest for both the physics and
mathematics communities.
Active lattice gas (ALG) models were first introduced through the
active Ising model in
[
24
]
,
where active self-propulsion was modeled by biased diffusion
and there are no exclusion interactions between particles.
Hard-core interactions, one of the key microscopic mechanisms
resulting the emergence of MIPS, were first incorporated into the
ALG framework by Erignoux in his PhD thesis
[
5
]

and later refined in
[
6
]
,
where the exclusion rules were used to model the hard-core interactions.
By incorporating the exclusion rule into active Ising model, valuable insights
into the physics of active matter were successfully provided in
[
13
]
.

The incorporation of the exclusion rule turns the system from gradient type to non-gradient type,
significantly increasing the mathematical complexity.
The first proof of a non-gradient hydrodynamic limit was established by Varadhan in
[
26
]
,
and Quastel
[
22
]
, where they extended the entropy method proposed in
[
10
]
to non-gradient systems.
In his seminal work
[
22
]
,
Quastel considered two-type mixed exclusion process
and constructed a suitable working function class on which the suitable spectral gap shrink rate can be reached.
This result was later partially extended to the weakly asymmetric exclusion process
to derive
Navier-Stokes equations via lattice gas
[
7
]
.
The weak asymmetry property was later broadly used to model self-propulsion in active matter systems.
A further mathematical breakthrough was achieved in
[
6
]
,
where the author extended the multi-typed symmetric exclusion process studied by Quastel
[
22
]

to an active (weakly asymmetric) exclusion process with continuous angular orientations.
If each orientation of a particle is treated as a
particle type in the sense of
[
22
]
,
the model in
[
6
]
involves uncountably many types due to the continuous-valued orientations.
Consequently, significant adaptations were made in
[
6
]
,
including the introduction of measure-valued parameters for local equilibrium and
the construction of a new working function class for non-gradient method.
Based on this mathematical breakthrough,
the authors of
[
16
]
studied an active exclusion process where self-propulsion orientations undergo diffusive dynamics,
instead of the Glauber dynamics considered in
[
6
]
.
However, all of the above mentioned works focus on purely active system.
Regarding active-passive lattice gas (APLG) models,
the only work to date, to the best of our knowledge, is
[
18
]
,
where the authors considered an APLG model restricted to only two orientations (left and right).
This restriction limits the model’s applicability to many realistic physical systems,
making its generalization both physically and mathematically meaningful.

In this work, we consider a more general APLG model
where active and passive particles interact via exclusion,
and the continuous valued orientations of active particles undergo Brownian motion on a torus.
Due to the presence of mixed active and passive particles,
which introduces additional degrees of freedom, the measure-valued parameters and
the working function class for non-gradient method introduced in
[
6
]

are no longer sufficient to establish the hydrodynamic limit in our case.
Drawing insights from
[
22
,
18
,
16
]
,
we further generalize the results of Erignoux
[
6
]
by introducing
new parameters of local equilibrium and new function class for the non-gradient method.
This generalization requires novel proofs for non-gradient decomposition and spectral gap estimation.

1.2
Structure of the article

In Section
2
, we introduce our model and present the main result

2.1
Particle configurations

The model involves two types of particles: active and passive.
The
Exclusion rule
ensures that at most one particle can occupy each site,
regardless of its type.
The
occupation configuration
at site
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}
is denoted by

\widetilde{\eta}_{x}=(\eta_{x}^{a},\eta^{p}_{x})
where
\eta^{\sigma}_{x}\in\{0,1\}
for
\sigma\in\{\text{a},\text{p}\}
,
indicates the number of particles of type
\sigma
at site
x
.
Let
\eta_{x}=\eta_{x}^{a}+\eta_{x}^{p}
denote the total number of particles at site
x
regardless of type.
To simplify notation, we write:
1^{a}\coloneqq(1,0)
,
1^{p}\coloneqq(0,1)
, and
\widetilde{0}\coloneqq(0,0)
,
which represent the three possible occupation configuration of a site.
For any occupied site
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}
, i.e.
\eta_{x}=1
,
we associate an angle
\theta_{x}\in\mathbb{S}
to indicate the direction of the particle
occupying that site. If the site
x
is unoccupied, i.e.
\eta_{x}=0
,
the angle is set to
\theta_{x}=0
by default.
The
complete configuration
(or simply
configuration
) is denoted by

\hat{\eta}=(\hat{\eta}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}
, where the pair
\hat{\eta}_{x}=(\widetilde{\eta}_{x},\theta_{x})
can
fully determines the scenario at site
x
.
The
configurations space
is given by

\Sigma_{N}=\Bigg\{\hat{\eta}=(\widetilde{\eta}_{x},\theta_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\in\big(\{1^{a},1^{p},\widetilde{0}\}\times\mathbb{S}\big)^{\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\Big|\theta_{x}=0\text{ if }\widetilde{\eta}_{x}=\widetilde{0}\Bigg\}

(1)

which will serve as the state space of our stochastic process.
In particular,
\Sigma_{\infty}
denotes
the set of infinite configurations, where
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}
in (
1
)
is replaced by
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

We will refer to any function
f:\Sigma_{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
that
depends on the configuration
through only a finite set of vertices as
local function
.
For such local function
f
, by slightly abusing notation, we denote this finite set of vertices by
\supp(f)\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Note that any local function admits a natural image as a function on
\Sigma_{N}
, for any sufficiently large
N
.
A local function
f
that is also
C^{2}
with respect to each
\theta_{x}
for every
x\in\supp(f)
is called a
cylinder function
.
The set of all cylinder functions will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}
.

2.2
Active-passive exclusion process

The dynamics of active-passive exclusion process consists of two parts:
random displacement and rotational diffusion.
The random displacement part governs the random walk of particles
and may differ between active and passive particles,
while the rotational diffusion part governs the angle update process
and applies equally to both particle types.

We begin by describing the random displacement dynamics.
All particles perform
a continuous time nearest-neighbor random walk with exclusion simultaneously.
A particle at site
x
waits an independent, exponentially distributed random time
before selecting a nearest-neighbor site
x+z
with
|z|=1
according to a specified jump law
that may depend on the particle type.
The jump is executed
if the destination site is unoccupied;
otherwise, the jump is canceled, and the particle
waits for another new exponential random time before attempting a new move.

The jump laws and the parameter of the exponential distribution
are chosen such that the active and passive jump rates from site
x
to a nearest-neighbor
site
x+z
with
|z|=1
are given by

R^{a}(x,x+z)=D_{T}+\frac{v_{0}}{2N}z^{\top}\mathbf{e}(\theta_{x}),\quad\text{ and }\quad R^{p}(x,x+z)=D_{T},

(2)

respectively.
In (
2
),
N
is sufficiently large to ensure that all quantities remain non-negative.
The vector
\mathbf{e}(\theta)=[\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)]^{\top}
represents the self-proposed direction,

D_{T}>0
is the
translational diffusion constant
,
and
v_{0}>0
is the
self-propulsion speed
.
Note the mean velocity of an active particle at site
x
is

\sum_{|z|=1}zR^{a}(x,x+z)=\frac{v_{0}}{N}\mathbf{e}(\theta_{x}).

The generator
\mathcal{L}^{\text{D}}_{N}
of the random displacement dynamics is given by

\mathcal{L}^{\text{D}}_{N}=D_{T}\mathcal{L}_{N}+\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N},

where
\mathcal{L}_{N}
is the generator of
the nearest-neighbor simple symmetric exclusion process (SSEP) defined in (
3
),
and
\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}
is the generator contribute to the weakly asymmetric motion defined in (
4
).

For a cylinder function
f\in\mathcal{C}
on
\Sigma_{N}
, the SSEP generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
acts as

\mathcal{L}_{N}f(\hat{\eta})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\sum_{|z|=1}\nabla_{x,x+z}f(\hat{\eta}),

(3)

where

\nabla_{x,x+z}f(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+z})\widetilde{\nabla}_{x,x+z}f(\hat{\eta})\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\nabla}_{x,x+z}f(\hat{\eta})=f(\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z})-f(\hat{\eta})

are referred to as
directed gradient
and
undirected gradient
of
f
respectively.
Here
\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z}
denotes the configuration obtained by swapping
\hat{\eta}_{x}
and
\hat{\eta}_{x+z}
:

\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z}_{y}=\begin{cases}\hat{\eta}_{x+z}&\text{if }y=x,\\
\hat{\eta}_{x}&\text{if }y=x+z,\\
\hat{\eta}_{y}&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

The generator
\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}
act on
f\in\mathcal{C}
as

\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}f(\hat{\eta})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\sum_{|z|=1}\frac{v_{0}}{2}z^{\top}\mathbf{e}(\theta_{x})\eta^{a}_{x}\nabla_{x,x+z}f(\hat{\eta}).

(4)

Remark 2.1
.

1.

The term
\eta^{a}_{x}
in the generator
\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}

indicates that the symmetric jump rate
D_{T}
at
x

is biased only if the site is occupied by an active particle.
Passive particles, on the other hand, only execute the SSEP with jump rate
D_{T}
.

2.

Although in our setting, the angles of passive particles do not affect their random displacement,
each passive particle is still assigned an angle.
This ensures our non-gradient method may cover a broader class of models,
such as multi-type particle systems with continuous orientations,
where each type follows its own distinct angle-dependent displacement.

3.

While
\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}
alone is not a markov generator, the combined generator

\mathcal{L}^{\text{D}}_{N}
is, provided
N
is sufficiently large.

Next, the rotational diffusion part is described as follows.
Each particles’s orientations undergoes independent Brownian motion on
\mathbb{S}
with a

rotational diffusion constant

D_{R}
.
The generator
\mathcal{L}^{\text{R}}_{N}
of the rotational diffusion acts on
f\in\mathcal{C}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{R}}_{N}f(\hat{\eta})=D_{R}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\eta_{x}\partial_{\theta_{x}}^{2}f(\hat{\eta}).

Now, the complete generator of the active-passive lattice gas (APLG) model is the superposition of
the accelerated displacement dynamics
and the rotational diffusion:

L_{N}=N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{\text{D}}_{N}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{R}}_{N}=N^{2}D_{T}\mathcal{L}_{N}+N\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}+\mathcal{L}^{\text{R}}_{N}.

(5)

Here we accelerate the displacement dynamic on a diffusive time scale,
so that in the hydrodynamic limit, the contributions from
\mathcal{L}_{N}
,
\mathcal{L}^{\text{WA}}_{N}
, and
\mathcal{L}^{\text{R}}_{N}

are balanced, capturing the relevant physical features.

In order to introduce the initial distribution of our process,
we define the
density profile

as a pair of maps

\bm{\hat{f}}=(\hat{f}^{a},\hat{f}^{p}):\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{S})
, where

\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{S})=\Big\{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}=(\hat{\alpha}^{a},\hat{\alpha}^{p})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{S})^{2}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{S}}\hat{\alpha}^{a}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+\int_{\mathbb{S}}\hat{\alpha}^{p}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq 1\Big\},

is referred to as the set of
grand canonical parameter
s.
If, for each
u
, the images measure
\hat{f}^{\sigma}(u,\mathrm{d}\theta)

has a density
f^{\sigma}(u,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
with
\sigma\in\{\text{a},\text{p}\}
,
we refer to the pair of density functions

\bm{f}=(f^{a},f^{p}):\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
as the
density profile functions
.

The initial configuration is chosen from
\mu_{\bm{\zeta}}^{N}
,
the
product measure associated with the density profile functions

\bm{\zeta}=(\zeta^{a},\zeta^{p})
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}
, which is defined as follows.
For macroscopic point
u\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
the integral

\rho_{0}^{\sigma}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\zeta^{\sigma}(u,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta

(6)

represent the local particle density of type
\sigma\in\{\text{a},\text{p}\}
at
u
.
Under
\mu_{\bm{\zeta}}^{N}
, each site
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}
is initially occupied by a type
\sigma\in\{\text{a},\text{p}\}
particle
with probability
\rho^{\sigma}(x/N)
, whose angle
\theta_{x}
is then sampled
from the probability distribution

\frac{\zeta^{\sigma}(x/N,\theta)}{\rho_{0}^{\sigma}(x/N)}\mathrm{d}\theta.

(7)

Starting from
\mu^{N}=\mu_{\bm{\zeta}}^{N}
, we define
\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{N}}
the path distribution of the Markov process

\hat{\eta}^{[0,T]}\in D([0,T],\Sigma_{N})
driven by the generator
L_{N}
defined in (
5
).
The corresponding expectation is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{N}}
.

Although the invariant measures of our process are not known, the symmetric displacement
happens at the fastest rate
N^{2}
.
Consequently, one can expect that the invariant measures of SSEP generator
\mathcal{L}

could serve as
important reference measures.
For any grand canonical parameter
\bm{\hat{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{S})
,
we denote by
\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}}
the

grand canonical measure with parameter

\bm{\hat{\alpha}}
, which
is a product measure associated with a constant density profile

\bm{\hat{\alpha}}\equiv(\hat{\alpha}^{a},\hat{\alpha}^{p})
.
Specially,
when the parameter
(\hat{\alpha}^{a},\hat{\alpha}^{p})
corresponds to uniform distributions,

\hat{\alpha}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\frac{\alpha^{\sigma}}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta,\quad\sigma\in\{\text{a},\text{p}\},

(8)

where
\bm{\alpha}\coloneqq(\alpha^{a},\alpha^{p})\in[0,1]^{2}
such that
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha^{a}+\alpha^{p}\leq 1
,
this special class of grand canonical measure is denoted by
\mu^{*}_{\bm{\alpha}}
.
The expectations associated with
\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}}
and
\mu^{*}_{\bm{\alpha}}

are denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}}

and
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, respectively.

Conventions.

For clarity, unless otherwise specified, through this paper,
\sigma

is used to refer to both active and passive particle cases in a given context.
Notations without subscripts are understood to represent the sum of active and passive quantities,

\alpha=\alpha^{a}+\alpha^{p}

2.3
Hydrodynamic limit

We now introduce our main result, the hydrodynamic limit of the orientation density for the APLG, whose
derivation is given in Sction
3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{S})
denote the space of non-negative measures on the macroscopic configuration space
endowed with the weak topology, and let
\mathcal{M}^{[0,T]}=D([0,T],\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}))
.
Each trajectory
\hat{\eta}^{[0,T]}\in D([0,T],\Sigma_{N})
of the process
admits a projection
\bm{\pi}^{N}=(\pi^{a,N},\pi^{p,N})
,
where
\pi^{\sigma,N}=(\pi^{\sigma,N}_{t})\in\mathcal{M}^{[0,T]}
denotes the trajectory of
empirical measure

\pi^{\sigma,N}_{t}(\hat{\eta}^{[0,T]})=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{N}}\eta^{\sigma}_{x}(t)\delta_{x/N,\theta_{x}(t)},

with
\delta_{u}
denoting the Dirac measure concentrated at
u
.
Furthermore, we define
Q^{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M}^{[0,T]}\times\mathcal{M}^{[0,T]})
as
the distribution of
\pi^{N}(\hat{\eta}^{[0,T]})
.

Heuristically, in the hydrodynamic limit, we will show that the trajectory
\bm{\pi}^{N}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}\theta)
of empirical measures
converges as
N\to\infty
, in probability, to a deterministic trajectory

\bm{\pi}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}\theta)=\big(f^{a}_{t}(u,\theta),f^{p}_{t}(u,\theta)\big)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}\theta

(9)

where
(f^{a}_{t},f^{p}_{t})
represents the local densities of active and passive particles with angle
\theta
.
Formally, they can be interpreted as

f^{\sigma}_{t}(u,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\approx\frac{1}{(2N\varepsilon+1)^{2}}\sum_{|x-Nu|\leq N\varepsilon}\eta^{\sigma}_{x}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\theta_{x}(t)\in[\theta,\theta+d\theta]\}},

where
\varepsilon
is the side length of a small macroscopic box.
We further define the
polarization
as

\mathbf{p}^{a}_{t}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathbf{e}(\theta)f^{a}_{t}(u,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta,

and similar to (
6
), the particle density as

\rho_{t}^{\sigma}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}f^{\sigma}_{t}(u,\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta.

(10)

Following the notations in
[
16
]
, we introduce two functions
\mathcal{D}
and

s
, defined on
[0,1]
as follows:

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\frac{1-d_{s}(\rho)}{\rho},\quad\text{ and }\quad s(\rho)=\mathcal{D}(\rho)-1,

where
d_{s}(\rho)
is the self-diffusion coefficient for the SSEP in dimension
2
,
defined in the Appendix
A
.
Note
\mathcal{D}(\rho)
has been continuously extended to
[0,1]
, see Remark
A.3
.

The main result of this paper (cf. Theorem
2.3
) states that density profile functions

(f^{a}_{t},f^{p}_{t})
, defined in (
9
), are
weak solutions (cf. Definition
2.2

3.3
Non-gradient decomposition and replacement lemma

Note that in (

Before stating the lemma precisely, we provide a heuristic explanation of the underlying ideas.

One main challenge in deriving the

\bm{\delta}_{i}h=\tau_{e_{i}}h-h,

To simplify the notations,

\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{\sigma,\omega}=\eta^{\sigma,\omega}_{e_{i}}-\eta^{\sigma,\omega}_{0},

\omega

\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{\sigma}

Next we perform the non-gradient decomposition of

where

to

\displaystyle J^{\omega}=J^{*}\cap T^{\omega}=\big\{u^{\top}j^{a}+v^{\top}j^{p}+m^{\top}j^{a,\omega}+n^{\top}j^{p,\omega}\mid u,v,m,n\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\big\}\subset\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}.

where

Next our goal is to define a semi-norm on the space

,

on the set

For any

Proposition 4.8

.

For any
\bm{\hat{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{S})

For each

\omega

Proposition 4.12

(Decomposition of currents)

For each fixed

it holds that for

\displaystyle\inf_{g\in\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}}\ll\!\!{j^{a}_{i}+d_{s}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{a}+\alpha^{a}\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta+\mathcal{L}g}\!\!\gg_{\hat{\alpha}}=0,

\displaystyle j^{a}_{i}+d_{s}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{a}+\alpha^{a}\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta\in\overline{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}},

\displaystyle j^{p}_{i}+d_{s}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{p}+\alpha^{p}\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta\in\overline{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}},

\displaystyle j^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{i}+d_{s}(\alpha)\bm{\delta}_{i}\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}\in\overline{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}}.

Proof.

First we clear out the trivial cases. When

5.2
Decomposition of closed forms

We introduce in this section the concept of discrete differential forms in the context
of particle systems. A key point of the non-gradient method is that any translation
invariant closed form can be decomposed as the sum of a gradient of a translation
invariant function and the current forms.

Recall that

\Sigma_{\infty}=\Bigg\{\hat{\eta}=(\widetilde{\eta}_{x},\theta_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\in\big(\{1^{a},1^{p},\widetilde{0}\}\times\mathbb{S}\big)^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\Big|\theta_{x}=0\text{ if }\widetilde{\eta}_{x}=\widetilde{0}\Bigg\}.

We consider the graph
\mathcal{G}=(\Sigma_{\infty},E)
with oriented edge set
E
:

E\coloneqq\Big\{(\hat{\eta},\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z})\in\Sigma_{\infty}^{2};\;\text{ for some }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|z|=1\text{ and }\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+z})=1\Big\}.

In other words,
there is an edge from
\hat{\eta}
to
\hat{\eta}^{\prime}
if and only if
\hat{\eta}^{\prime}
can be transformed
from
\hat{\eta}
by exerting exactly one particle jump.
We endow
\mathcal{G}
with the usual distance
d_{g}
on graphs, i.e.,

d_{g}(\widehat{\eta},\widehat{\eta}^{\prime})
is the minimal number of particle jumps necessary to go from one
configuration to the other.
If
\hat{\eta}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\eta}
is not connected,
we will adopt the usual convention
d_{g}(\widehat{\eta},\widehat{\eta}^{\prime})=\infty
.

We define
differential form
on
(\mathcal{G},d)
as a collection of
L^{2}(\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}})

functions
(\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|z|=1}
, satisfying

\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z}(\widehat{\eta})=\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+z})\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z}(\widehat{\eta}),

i.e.,
\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z}(\widehat{\eta})=0
if the jump from
x
to
x+z
cannot
be performed in configuration
\widehat{\eta}
.

We define
path
as a finite sequence of nearest-neighbor jumps
sites
\gamma=(x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}
.
For any path
\gamma=(x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}
, and configuration
\hat{\eta}
, by denoting

\hat{\eta}^{(0,\gamma)}=\hat{\eta}
, and
\hat{\eta}^{(i+1,\gamma)}=(\hat{\eta}^{(i,\gamma)})^{x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i}}
,
we define

\Gamma(\hat{\eta})\coloneqq\Big\{\gamma=(x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\mid\hat{\eta}_{x_{i}}^{(i,\gamma)}(1-\hat{\eta}_{x_{i}+z_{i}}^{(i,\gamma)})=1,\quad i=1,\ldots,n_{\gamma}\Big\}

the set of

licit paths
, i.e., paths such that
starting from
\hat{\eta}
, all the nearest-neighbor jumps on the path can be executed one by one.
We define

\Gamma_{c}(\hat{\eta})\coloneqq\Big\{\gamma=(x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\in\Gamma(\hat{\eta})\mid\big(\hat{\eta}_{x_{i}}^{(n_{\gamma},\gamma)}\big)^{x_{n_{\gamma}},x_{n_{\gamma}}+z_{n_{\gamma}}}=\hat{\eta}\Big\},

the set of

licit closed paths
, i.e., licit paths such that
after performing the last jump, the resulting configuration returns to to
\hat{\eta}
.
Given a differential form
\mathfrak{u}=(\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|z|=1}
,
and a path
\gamma=(x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}
,
we define
the integral of
\mathfrak{u}
along
\gamma
as

I_{\gamma,\mathfrak{u}}(\hat{\eta})=\mathbbm{1}_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\hat{\eta})}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\gamma}}\mathfrak{u}_{x_{i},x_{i}+z_{i}}(\hat{\eta}^{(i,\gamma)}).

With all above preparations,
we call a differential form
\mathfrak{u}
the
closed form
if

I_{\gamma,\mathfrak{u}}(\hat{\eta})=0,\forall\gamma\in\Gamma_{c}(\hat{\eta})
, for all
\hat{\eta}\in\Sigma_{\infty}
,
meaning that the integral along any closed path vanishes.
And we call a differential form
\mathfrak{u}

the
exact form
if there exists a cylinder function
f
such that

\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z}(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+z})(f(\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z})-f(\hat{\eta}))
, for all
\hat{\eta}\in\Sigma_{\infty}
.

We may now introduce the main object of this section.

Definition 5.4

(Germs of closed forms)
.

A pair of
L^{2}(\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}})
functions

\mathfrak{U}=(\mathfrak{u}_{1},\,\mathfrak{u}_{2}):\Sigma_{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a
germ of a closed form
,
if the closed form
\mathfrak{u}=(\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+z})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|z|=1}
defined by

\mathfrak{u}_{x,x+e_{i}}(\hat{\eta})=\tau_{x}\mathfrak{u}_{i}(\hat{\eta}),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{u}_{x,x-e_{i}}(\hat{\eta})=-\mathfrak{u}_{x-e_{i},x}(\hat{\eta}^{x,x-e_{i}}),

for
i=1,2
.

Definition 5.5

(Germs of exact forms)
.

A pair of
L^{2}(\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}})
functions

\mathfrak{U}=(\mathfrak{u}_{1},\,\mathfrak{u}_{2}):\Sigma_{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called a

germ of an exact form
, if there exists a cylinder function
h
such that

\big(\mathfrak{u}_{1},\,\mathfrak{u}_{2}\big)=\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{h}\coloneqq\big(\nabla_{0,e_{1}}\Sigma_{h},\nabla_{0,e_{2}}\Sigma_{h}\big).

Moreover, we denote the set of all germs of exact forms associated with cylinder functions by

\mathfrak{E}^{*}=\big\{\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{h}\big|h\in\mathcal{C}\big\}.

Note germs of exact forms are special class of germs of closed forms. Next we define another special class of germs of closed forms.

Definition 5.6

(Germs of current forms)
.

For any angular function
\omega
, and
i=1,2
,
the
germs of current forms

\mathfrak{{j}}^{\sigma,i},\mathfrak{{j}}^{\sigma,\omega,i}
are defined as

\mathfrak{{j}}^{\sigma,i}_{k}=\delta_{i,k}\eta_{0}^{\sigma}(1-\eta_{e_{i}})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{{j}}^{\sigma,\omega,i}_{k}=\delta_{i,k}\eta_{0}^{\sigma,\omega}(1-\eta_{e_{i}}),\quad\forall k=1,2.

Moreover, we denote the set of all germs of current forms by

\mathfrak{{J}}^{\omega}=\Span\big\{\mathfrak{{j}}^{\sigma,\Phi_{i}^{\sigma},i}\big|\Phi_{i}^{\sigma}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}),i=1,2\big\}.

We now define a linear mapping
\mathfrak{F}
, which establishes a connection between

\mathcal{LC}+J^{*}
and
\mathfrak{E}^{*}+\mathfrak{I}^{*}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}:\mathcal{LC}+J^{*}

\displaystyle\to\quad\mathfrak{E}^{*}+\mathfrak{I}^{*}

(80)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f+\sum_{i=1,2}j_{i}^{a,\Phi_{a}^{i}}+j_{i}^{p,\Phi_{p}^{i}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\quad\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{f}+\sum_{i=1,2}\mathfrak{{j}}^{a,\Phi_{i}^{a},i}+\mathfrak{{j}}^{p,\Phi_{i}^{p},i},

Remark 5.7
.

The linear mapping defined in (
80
) can be interpreted as a restriction of the following broader mapping:

\mathfrak{F}:f\mapsto\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{f}=\left(\nabla_{0,e_{1}}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{f},\nabla_{0,e_{2}}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{f}\right),

(81)

whose domain includes all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
such that
\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{f}
is well-defined.

To illustrate this, we examine the effects of

\mathfrak{F}
defined in (
81
) on
J^{*}
and
\mathcal{LC}
separately.
Formally, we define

W_{i}^{\sigma,\Phi}(\hat{\eta})=\sum_{x}x_{i}\eta_{x}^{\sigma,\Phi},

and then calculate

\mathcal{L}W_{i}^{\sigma,\Phi}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sum_{j=1,2}a(x,x+e_{j})\widetilde{\nabla}_{x,x+e_{j}}W_{i}^{\sigma,\Phi}=\Sigma_{j_{i}^{\sigma,\Phi}},

which yields

\mathfrak{F}(j_{i}^{a,\Phi})=\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{j_{i}^{a,\Phi}}=\bm{\nabla}W_{i}^{\sigma,\Phi}=\mathfrak{{j}}^{a,\Phi,i}.

Moreover, note that
\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Sigma_{f}=\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{\mathcal{L}^{-1}f}
,
we obtain
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{L}f)=\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{f}
.

Since most of functions that we are concerned with are in
T^{\omega}
,
we define the set of germs of closed forms with
components in
T^{\omega}
as:

\mathfrak{T}_{0}^{\omega}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathfrak{U}=(\mathfrak{u}_{1},\,\mathfrak{u}_{2})\mid\mathfrak{U}\text{ is a germ of a closed form, }\mathfrak{u}_{i}\in T^{\omega},i=1,2\Big\},

and define the set of germs of exact forms associated with functions in
T^{\omega}
as:

\mathfrak{E}_{0}^{\omega}\coloneqq\Big\{\bm{\nabla}\Sigma_{h}\mid h\in\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\omega}\Big\}\subset\mathfrak{T}_{0}^{\omega}.

We also define the set of current forms on
T^{\omega}
as:

\mathfrak{{J}}^{\omega}=\Bigg\{\sum_{i=1,2}u_{i}\mathfrak{{j}}^{a,i}+v_{i}\mathfrak{{j}}^{p,i}+m_{i}\mathfrak{{j}}^{a,\omega,i}+n_{i}\mathfrak{{j}}^{p,\omega,i}\Big|u,v,m,n\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\Bigg\}\subset\mathfrak{T}_{0}^{\omega}.

Finally, we let
\mathfrak{T}^{\omega}_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}}=\overline{\mathfrak{T}_{0}^{\omega}}
and
\mathfrak{E}^{\omega}_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}}=\overline{\mathfrak{E}_{0}^{\omega}}

be the closure of
\mathfrak{T}_{0}^{\omega}
and
\mathfrak{E}_{0}^{\omega}
in
L^{2}(\mu_{\bm{\hat{\alpha}}})
respectively.

We now state the main result of this section. Similar to Proposition
4.8
, we have the following direct sum decomposition of
\mathfrak{{T}}^{\omega}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

\mathfrak{{T}}^{\omega}=\mathfrak{{E}}^{\omega}\oplus\mathfrak{{J}}^{\omega}

Proof.

5.3
Spectral gap

This section aims to establish the spectral gap estimate of the symmetric exclusion generator
on the function class
T^{\omega}
, which is of order
n^{-2}
.
This is the reason why we focus on
T^{\omega}
.
We hope that
T^{\omega}
is rich enough to include all functions of interest in our model,
such as various kinds of local gradients and currents,
while still being simple enough to ensure that the spectral gap estimate holds

Recall that in (
51
) we define

T^{\omega}=\Span\Bigg\{f(\hat{\eta})=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(a\eta^{a}_{x}+b\eta^{p}_{x}+c\eta^{a,\omega}_{x}+d\eta^{p,\omega}_{x}\right)F_{x}(\eta)\Big|f\in\mathcal{C},F_{x}\in\mathcal{S},\forall x\Bigg\}.

Next, we define
\mathcal{C}_{n}
as the set of cylinder functions
whose supports are contained in
B_{n}
. With this notation, we define
T^{\omega}_{n}=T^{\omega}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}.

Recall that from the definition in (
42
) we encoded in the canonical state
\widehat{K}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}_{n}
the
number and angles of each type of particles in
B_{n}
. For each canonical state
\widehat{K}
, we define

\alpha^{a}=\frac{K^{a}}{|B_{n}|},\quad\alpha^{p}=\frac{K^{p}}{|B_{n}|},

representing the densities of each type of particles.
Finally, we define the Dirichlet form with respect to the canonical measure
\mu_{n,\widehat{K}}
as

\mathcal{{D}}_{n,\widehat{K}}(f)=\big<f,-\mathcal{L}_{n}f\big>_{n,\widehat{K}},

where
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the generator of SSEP restricted to jumps with both extremities in
B_{n}
.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Proposition 5.9

(The spectral gap estimate for the active-passive SSEP with angles)
.

For a fixed
\alpha^{\prime}\in[0,1)
, there exists a constant
C(\alpha^{\prime})
such that for any
\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
satisfying

\alpha^{a}+\alpha^{p}\leq\alpha^{\prime}
, and for any
f\in T_{n}^{\omega}
with
\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[f]=0
, the following holds:

\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[f^{2}]\leq C(\alpha^{\prime})n^{2}\mathcal{D}_{n,\widehat{K}}(f).

Before proving Proposition
5.9
, we need some preparations.
For any fixed
\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
such that
\alpha^{a}+\alpha^{p}\leq\alpha^{\prime}
.
We shorten

\overline{\omega}^{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[\omega(\theta_{0})|\eta^{\sigma}_{0}=1],\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\omega}^{\sigma}=\omega-\overline{\omega}^{\sigma}.

For any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{n}
,
we define the centered variables
\eta^{\sigma,\hat{\omega}^{\sigma}}=\widehat{\omega}^{\sigma}(\theta_{x})\eta^{\sigma}_{x}

and

\chi_{x}=\frac{\alpha^{p}}{\alpha}\eta^{a}_{x}-\frac{\alpha^{a}}{\alpha}\eta^{p}_{x}.

(84)

The centered variables
\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}
,
\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}
and
\chi
will play a crucial role
in the
L^{2}
orthogonal decomposition of functions in
T_{n}^{\omega}
.
To achieve this goal, we introduce some definitions.

Definition 5.10
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
fixed.

1.

We say a function
F
is
independent of type and angle at
x
(ITA-
x
)
,
if it can be written as

F(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}f(\hat{\eta})+(1-\eta_{x})g(\hat{\eta}),

where
f
is independent of
\hat{\eta}_{x}
.

2.

We say a function
F
is
independent of active angle at
x
(IAA-
x
)
,
if it can be written as

F(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}^{a}f(\hat{\eta})+\eta_{x}^{p}h(\hat{\eta})+(1-\eta_{x})g(\hat{\eta}),

where
f
is independent of
\hat{\eta}_{x}
.

3.

We say a function
F
is
independent of passive angle at
x
(IPA-
x
)
,
if it can be written as

F(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}^{p}h(\hat{\eta})+\eta_{x}^{a}f(\hat{\eta})+(1-\eta_{x})g(\hat{\eta}),

where
h
is independent of
\hat{\eta}_{x}
.

Remark 5.11
.

•

If a function
F
is ITA-
x
for every site
x
, we say
F
is
ITA-everywhere
.
If a function
F
is both ITA-
x
and ITA-
y
, we say
F
is
ITA-
xy
.
The same terminologies apply to the IAA and IPA cases.

•

ITA-everywhere is just another term for functions that are both angle-blind and type-blind.
Therefore,
\mathcal{S}
is just the set of all ITA-everywhere functions.

•

If
F
is ITA-
x
, heuristically, it can only feel whether the site
x
is occupied or not,
but it cannot distinguish the type or angle of the occupying particle.

•

A function
F
is IAA-
x
(or IPA-
x
) means that

F
distinguish not only whether the site
x
is occupied or not, but also the type of the occupying particle.
However, when the occupying particle is an active (or passive) particle

F
cannot see its angle.

•

Since we can decompose
\eta_{x}f=\eta^{a}_{x}f+\eta^{p}_{x}f
, An ITA-
x
function naturally is both IAA-
x
and IPA-
x
at the same time.

The following lemma illustrates that the ITA-
x
functions are orthogonal to
\chi_{x}
,
and that the IAA-
x
(resp. IPA-
x
) are orthogonal to
\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}
(resp.
\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{x}
).

Lemma 5.12
.

For any fixed canonical state
\widehat{K}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{K}}_{n}
, the following hold:

1.

For any ITA-
x
function
F
,

\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[\chi_{x}F]=0.

2.

For any IAA-
x
function
G
, IPA-
x
function
H
, and integrable function
\omega(\theta)
,

\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}G]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{x}H]=0.

Proof.

For an ITA-
x
function, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\eta^{\sigma}_{x}F\big]=\frac{\alpha^{\sigma}}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\big[\eta_{x}F\big],

so that

\mathbb{E}\big[\chi_{x}F\big]=\Big(\frac{\alpha^{p}\alpha^{a}}{\alpha^{2}}-\frac{\alpha^{a}\alpha^{p}}{\alpha^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big[\eta_{x}F\big]=0.

For the second part, by definition of IAA-
x
, we can write
G
as

G(\hat{\eta})=\eta_{x}^{a}f(\hat{\eta})+\eta_{x}^{p}h(\hat{\eta})+(1-\eta_{x})g(\hat{\eta})

where
f
is independent of
\hat{\eta}_{x}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta^{a,\omega}_{x}G]=\overline{\omega}^{a}\alpha^{a}\mathbb{E}[f]=\overline{\omega}^{a}\mathbb{E}\big[\eta^{a}_{x}G\big],

and similarly

\mathbb{E}[\eta^{p,\omega}_{x}H]=\overline{\omega}^{p}\mathbb{E}\big[\eta^{p}_{x}H\big].

Finally, using the fact that
\overline{\widehat{\omega}^{\sigma}}^{\sigma}=0
, the proof of Lemma
5.12
is complete.
∎

Now we state the following
L^{2}
orthogonal decomposition of functions in
T^{\omega}_{n}
.

Lemma 5.13

(
L^{2}
orthogonal decomposition of
T^{\omega}_{n}
functions)
.

For any fixed canonical state
\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
, and for any centered
f\in T_{n}^{\omega}

taking the form

f(\hat{\eta})=\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\left(a\eta^{a}_{x}+b\eta^{p}_{x}+c\eta^{a,\omega}_{x}+d\eta^{p,\omega}_{x}\right)F_{x}(\eta),

where
F_{x}\in\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}
and
\hat{\eta}\in\Sigma_{{n}}^{\hat{K}}
.
we have the
L^{2}(\mu_{n,\widehat{K}})
-orthogonal decomposition of
f
as follows:

f=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}+f_{4},

where
f_{1}
is centered and ITA-everywhere:

f_{1}(\hat{\eta})=\left((a+c\overline{\omega}^{a})\frac{\alpha^{a}}{\alpha}+(b+d\overline{\omega}^{p})\frac{\alpha^{p}}{\alpha}\right)\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta_{x}F_{x}(\eta),

f_{2}
is centered and both IAA-everywhere and IPA-everywhere:

f_{2}(\hat{\eta})=\left((a+c\overline{\omega}^{a})-(b+d\overline{\omega}^{p})\right)\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\chi_{x}F_{x}(\eta),

f_{3}
is centered and IPA-everywhere:

f_{3}(\hat{\eta})=c\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}F_{x}(\eta),

f_{4}
is centered and IAA-everywhere:

f_{4}(\hat{\eta})=d\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{x}F_{x}(\eta).

Proof.

Let
\tilde{a}=(a+c\overline{\omega}^{a})
and
\tilde{b}=(b+d\overline{\omega}^{p})
. We can rewrite
f
as

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\left(\tilde{a}\eta^{a}_{x}+\tilde{b}\eta^{p}_{x}\right)F_{x}(\eta)+\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\left(c\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}+d\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{x}\right)F_{x}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\left(\tilde{a}\frac{\alpha^{a}}{\alpha}+\tilde{b}\frac{\alpha^{p}}{\alpha}\right)\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta_{x}F_{x}(\eta)+\left(\tilde{a}-\tilde{b}\right)\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\chi_{x}F_{x}(\eta)

\displaystyle\quad+c\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}F_{x}(\eta)+d\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\eta^{p,\hat{\omega}^{p}}_{x}F_{x}(\eta)

\displaystyle=f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}+f_{4}.

Since for all
x
,
F_{x}(\eta)
is ITA-everywhere, and
f
is centered,
using Lemma
5.12
, we can conclude that
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
f_{3}
,
f_{4}
are all centered.
Furthermore, by Lemma
5.12
, we can see that
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}
are mutually orthogonal.
Thus, the proof of Lemma
5.13
is complete.
∎

Finally, we give a proof of Proposition
5.9
.

Proof of Proposition
5.9
.

Using
L^{2}
orthogonal decomposition from Lemma
5.13
,
we can split the variance and Dirichlet form of a centered
f
:

\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[f^{2}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[f_{i}^{2}\big],\quad\mathcal{D}_{n,\widehat{K}}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{D}_{n,\widehat{K}}(f_{i}).

Thus, to prove Proposition
5.9
,
we can break the problem into smaller parts by showing:

\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[f_{i}^{2}]\leq C(\alpha^{\prime})n^{2}\mathcal{D}_{n,\widehat{K}}(f_{i}),\quad\forall i=1,2,3,4.

(85)

Note that

f_{1}
satisfies the spectral gap estimate (
85
), which is a classical result, (see Lemma 8.1.4 in
[
6
]

or Lemma 8.2 in
[
22
]
). Since
f_{3}
and
f_{4}
are symmetric,
Proposition
5.9
is reduced to proving (
85
) for
f_{2}
, and
f_{3}
. Before we start, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.14
.

For any fixed canonical state
\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
, and for any ITA-
xy
function
F
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a}_{x}\eta^{a}_{y}F\big]=\frac{K^{a}(K^{a}-1)}{K(K-1)}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a}_{x}\eta^{p}_{y}F\big]=\frac{K^{a}K^{p}}{K(K-1)}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a,\omega_{1}}_{x}\eta^{a,\omega_{2}}_{y}F\big]=\frac{K^{a}}{K(K-1)}\big(K^{a}\overline{\omega_{1}}^{a}\overline{\omega_{2}}^{a}-\overline{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}^{a}\big)\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a,\omega_{1}}_{x}\eta^{p,\omega_{2}}_{y}F\big]=\frac{K^{a}K^{p}}{K(K-1)}\overline{\omega_{1}}^{a}\overline{\omega_{2}}^{p}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big].

Proof of Lemma
5.14
.

The first two equalities hold because:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{n,\widehat{K}}(\eta^{a}_{x}=\eta^{a}_{y}=1)}{\mathbb{P}_{n,\widehat{K}}(\eta_{x}=\eta_{y}=1)}=\frac{K^{a}(K^{a}-1)}{K(K-1)},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{n,\widehat{K}}(\eta^{a}_{x}=\eta^{p}_{y}=1)}{\mathbb{P}_{n,\widehat{K}}(\eta_{x}=\eta_{y}=1)}=\frac{K^{a}K^{p}}{K(K-1)}.

Next we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K^{a}}\omega_{1}(\theta_{i}^{a})\sum_{j\neq i}\omega_{2}(\theta_{j}^{a})=

\displaystyle K^{a}\sum_{i=1}^{K^{a}}\omega_{1}(\theta_{i}^{a})\Big(\overline{\omega_{2}}^{a}-\frac{1}{K^{a}}\omega_{2}(\theta_{i}^{a})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(K^{a})^{2}\overline{\omega_{1}}^{a}\overline{\omega_{2}}^{a}-K^{a}\overline{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}^{a}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a,\omega_{1}}_{x}\eta^{a,\omega_{2}}_{y}F\big]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{K^{a}}\omega_{1}(\theta_{i}^{a})\sum_{j\neq i}\omega_{2}(\theta_{j}^{a})\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K^{a}}{K(K-1)}\big(K^{a}\overline{\omega_{1}}^{a}\overline{\omega_{2}}^{a}-\overline{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}^{a}\big)\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big].

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a,\omega_{1}}_{x}\eta^{p,\omega_{2}}_{y}F\big]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{K^{a}}\omega_{1}(\theta_{i}^{a})\sum_{j=1}^{K^{p}}\omega_{2}(\theta_{j}^{a})\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K^{a}K^{p}}{K(K-1)}\overline{\omega_{1}}^{a}\overline{\omega_{2}}^{p}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big].

Thus, the proof of Lemma
5.14
is complete.
∎

In the rest of proof we denote
V^{a}(\omega)=\var_{n,\widehat{K}}\big(\omega(\theta_{0})|\eta^{a}_{0}=1\big)
.
Using Lemma
5.14
and definition of related quantities, direct computations yield the following corollary.

Corollary 5.15
.

For any fixed canonical state
\widehat{K}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
, the following hold:

1.

For any ITA-
x
function
F
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}^{2}F\big]=\frac{\alpha^{a}\alpha^{p}}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}F\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\big(\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}\big)^{2}F\big]=V^{a}(\omega)\frac{\alpha^{a}}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}F\big].

2.

For any ITA-
xy
function
F
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}\chi_{y}F\big]=\frac{-\alpha^{a}\alpha^{p}}{\alpha^{2}(K-1)}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{x}\eta^{a,\hat{\omega}^{a}}_{y}F\big]=-V^{a}(\omega)\frac{K^{a}}{K(K-1)}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\eta_{x}\eta_{y}F\big].

With all above preparations, we now prove the spectral gap estimate (

f_{2}
and
f_{3}

Note that multiplying

. Moreover,

First, we do computations related to
f_{2}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}[f_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}^{2}F_{x}^{2}\big]+\sum_{y\neq x}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}\chi_{y}F_{x}F_{y}\big]\Big)

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{a}\alpha^{p}K}{\alpha^{2}(K-1)}\sum_{x\in B_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[F_{x}^{2}\big].

Now we turn our attention to

Since we assumed that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}\big(\chi_{x}F_{x}\big)

\displaystyle=\chi_{x}\mathcal{L}_{n}F_{x}+\sum_{|z|=1}a(x,x+z)F_{x}(\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z})\big(\chi_{x+z}-\chi_{x}\big)

\displaystyle=\chi_{x}\mathcal{L}_{n}F_{x}+\sum_{|z|=1}\chi_{x+z}\big(1-\eta_{x}\big)F_{x}(\hat{\eta}^{x,x+z})

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big(f_{2}\big)=-\sum_{x,y\in B_{n}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}\chi_{y}F_{x}\mathcal{L}_{n}F_{y}\big]+\sum_{|z|=1}\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[\chi_{x}\chi_{y+z}(1-\eta_{y})F_{x}(\eta)F_{y}(\hat{\eta}^{y,y+z})\big]\Big)

Next, we do computations related to
f_{3}
.

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n,\widehat{K}}\big[f_{3}^{2}\big]
