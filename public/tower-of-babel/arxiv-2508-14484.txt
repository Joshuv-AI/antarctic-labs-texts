Abstract
. This paper presents an elementary introduction on K K -theoretic Q Q -functions, which were introduced by Ikeda and Naruse in 2013. These functions, which serve as K K -theoretic analogs of Schur Q Q -functions, are known to possess combinatorial and algebraic constructions. In a 2022 paper, the author introduced “ β \beta -deformed power-sums” to provide a simpler, more algebraic construction of these functions. Since the original approach relies on fermionic operators and vacuum expectation values, this paper presents a more accessible, purely algebraic treatment, following the exposition of Schur Q Q -functions in Macdonald’s standard textbook. We also show that the algebra of dual Q Q -cancellation property with integer coefficients is generated by dual K K - Q Q -functions associated with an odd row partition.

Lemma 2.4

Lemma 2.5

Let
\overline{Q}^{(\beta)}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{q}_{n}^{(\beta)}z^{n}

(7)

\displaystyle Q^{(\beta)}(z)\overline{Q}^{(\beta)}(z)=1,\qquad\overline{Q}^{(\beta)}(z)=\frac{Q^{(\beta)}(-z-\beta)}{Q^{(\beta)}(-\beta)}.

Proof.

The first equation follows directly from (

\displaystyle Q^{(\beta)}(-z-\beta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\frac{1-\overline{x_{i}}(-z-\beta)}{1-x_{i}(-z-\beta)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\frac{1+\frac{x_{i}}{1+\beta x_{i}}(z+\beta)}{1+x_{i}(z+\beta)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\frac{1-\beta\cdot\frac{x_{i}}{1+\beta x_{i}}}{1+\beta x_{i}}\cdot\frac{1-x_{i}z}{1-\overline{x_{i}}z}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\frac{1+\beta\overline{x_{i}}}{1+\beta x_{i}}\cdot\frac{1-x_{i}z}{1-\overline{x_{i}}z}

\displaystyle=Q^{(\beta)}(-\beta)\cdot\overline{Q}^{(\beta)}(z).

2.2.3.
Expansion by odd partitions

By repeatedly applying the recurrence relation (

q_{1}^{(\beta)},q_{3}^{(\beta)},q_{5}^{(\beta)},\dots

We introduce an ordering

\displaystyle q_{k_{1}}^{(\beta)}q_{k_{2}}^{(\beta)}\dots q_{k_{p}}^{(\beta)}\succ q_{l_{1}}^{(\beta)}q_{l_{2}}^{(\beta)}\dots q_{l_{r}}^{(\beta)}\qquad(k_{1}\geq k_{2}\geq\cdots,\;l_{1}\geq l_{2}\geq\cdots)

\displaystyle\iff\begin{cases}|k|>|l|,\quad\text{or}\\
|k|=|l|\text{ and }(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)\text{ is lexicographically smaller than }(l_{1},l_{2},\dots)\end{cases}

\displaystyle(q_{n}^{(\beta)})^{2}

3.
Algebra of the dual

Just like

3.1.

3.1.1.
An alternative

Here we introduce a new family of

Proof.

Since this expression is independent of

We have

Proof.

The inclusion

3.2.
Application: Nakagawa–Naruse’s bilinear form

We define a nondegenerate bilinear form on

\Gamma\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma\to\mathbb{Q};\qquad f\otimes g\mapsto\langle f,g\rangle,\qquad\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle=2^{-\ell(\lambda)}z_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda,\mu}.

Theorem 3.5
.

We have the identity

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{OP}}2^{\ell(\lambda)}z_{\lambda}^{-1}p_{\lambda}^{(\beta)}(x)p_{\lambda}^{[\beta]}(y)=\prod_{i,j}\frac{1-\overline{x_{i}}y_{j}}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}.

\prod_{i,j}\frac{1-\overline{x_{i}}y_{j}}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}.

We use the well-known identity:

\prod_{i,j}\frac{1+X_{i}Y_{j}}{1-X_{i}Y_{j}}=\exp\left(2\sum_{m=1,3,5,\dots}\frac{p_{m}(X)p_{m}(Y)}{m}\right).

We compute the right-hand side of the claimed identity as follows:

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j}\frac{1-\overline{x_{i}}y_{j}}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}\left(\frac{1+\frac{x_{i}}{1+\frac{\beta}{2}x_{i}}(y_{j}+\tfrac{\beta}{2})}{1-\frac{x_{i}}{1+\frac{\beta}{2}x_{i}}(y_{j}+\tfrac{\beta}{2})}\cdot\frac{1-\frac{x_{i}}{1+\frac{\beta}{2}x_{i}}\cdot\frac{\beta}{2}}{1+\frac{x_{i}}{1+\frac{\beta}{2}x_{i}}\cdot\frac{\beta}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2\sum_{m=1,3,5,\dots}\frac{p_{m}\left(\frac{x}{1+\frac{\beta}{2}x}\right)\left\{p_{m}(y+\tfrac{\beta}{2})-p_{m}(\tfrac{\beta}{2})\right\}}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1,3,5,\dots}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k}}{k!m^{k}}(p_{m}^{(\beta)})^{k}(p_{m}^{[\beta]})^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{OP}}2^{\ell(\lambda)}z_{\lambda}^{-1}p_{\lambda}^{(\beta)}(x)p_{\lambda}^{[\beta]}(y).

∎

The function
\prod_{i,j}\frac{1-\overline{x_{i}}y_{j}}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}

One general method to ensure the integrality of various constants is to provide their combinatorial interpretations.
For instance, Lewis–Marberg

However, in this section we avoid combinatorial arguments and instead present a few results that can be proved using only elementary algebraic computations.

4.1.

As noted in Section

Define the

Equivalently, we have

Lemma 4.1

For any positive integer

\mathbb{Z}[\beta]

Proof.

From (

is a symmetric polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.
Then from (

The key question is whether the coefficients actually lie in

4

This might seem straightforward, but in fact it is not trivial.

From (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}gp_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})z^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2+\beta z}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1+x_{i}\cdot\frac{z}{1+\beta z}}{1-x_{i}z}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+\beta z+x_{i}z)-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-x_{i}z)(1+\beta z)}{2+\beta z}\cdot\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1-x_{i}z)(1+\beta z)}.

The numerator vanishes at

4.2.

We define

The goal of this subsection is to prove that
g\Gamma_{\mathbb{Z}}
is actually generated by the odd-degree
gp_{m}
; we will show

(33)

g\Gamma_{\mathbb{Z}}=\mathbb{Z}[\beta][gp_{1},gp_{3},gp_{5},\dots].

From Proposition
3.4
and (
32
), we already know

g\Gamma=g\Gamma_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[\beta]}\mathbb{Q}(\beta)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta)[gp_{1},gp_{3},gp_{5},\dots],

which follows by the same arguments used to prove Theorem
2.8
.
The remaining question is whether the expansion of
gp_{2n}
in terms of
gp_{1},gp_{3},\dots
involves only coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

We now present a proof of (
33
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}gp_{n}z^{n}
, and define
F(z):=\frac{zf(z)}{1+f(z)}
. Then the identity

(34)

F(z)=F(\overline{z})

holds.

Proof.

From (
24
) and (
31
), we have
Q^{[\beta]}(z)=1+(2+\beta z)f(z)
.
Substituting this into (
26
), we have

(35)

\left\{1+(2+\beta z)f(z)\right\}\left\{1+(2+\beta\overline{z})f(\overline{z})\right\}=1.

Using
2+\beta\overline{z}=\frac{2+\beta z}{1+\beta z}
, we simplify (
35
) to obtain

(1+\beta z)\cdot\frac{f(z)}{1+f(z)}+\frac{f(\overline{z})}{1+f(\overline{z})}=0.

Multiplying both sides by
-\overline{z}=\frac{z}{1+\beta z}
, we obtain

\frac{zf(z)}{1+f(z)}-\frac{\overline{z}f(\overline{z})}{1+f(\overline{z})}=0,

which proves (
34
).
∎

From (
34
),
F(z)
can be regarded as a “
K
-theoretic even function.”
We call any function
F(z)
satisfying
F(z)=F(\overline{z})
a
K
-even function
.
If a
K
-even function
F(z)
has an expansion

F(z)=c_{p}z^{p}+c_{p+1}z^{p+1}+\cdots\quad(c_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}(\beta)),

then the leading exponent
p
must be even.
This follows immediately by comparing the leading terms in the identity
F(z)=F(\overline{z})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose a
K
-even function has an expansion

F(z)=c_{1}z^{2}+c_{2}z^{3}+\cdots.

Then, each coefficient
c_{2k}
can be expressed as a polynomial in
c_{1},c_{3},c_{5},\dots,c_{2k-1}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.

Proof.

Define

F_{1}(z):=F(z)-c_{1}\beta^{-1}(z+\overline{z})
.
Using the equation
z+\overline{z}=\frac{\beta z^{2}}{1+\beta z}
, we expand
F_{1}(z)
as

F_{1}(z)=(c_{2}+c_{1}\beta)z^{3}+(c_{3}-c_{1}\beta^{2})z^{4}+(c_{4}+c_{1}\beta^{3})z^{5}+\cdots.

Since
F_{1}(z)
is also
K
-even, we must have
c_{2}=-c_{1}\beta
.
Next, define
F_{2}(z):=F_{1}(z)-(c_{3}-c_{1}\beta^{2})\beta^{-2}(z+\overline{z})^{2}
.
Expanding
F_{2}(z)
, we have

F_{2}(z)=(c_{4}-c_{1}\beta^{3}+2c_{3}\beta)z^{5}+(c_{5}+2c_{1}\beta^{4}-3c_{3}\beta^{2})z^{6}+(c_{6}-3c_{1}\beta^{5}+4c_{3}\beta^{3})z^{7}+\cdots.

Again, since
F_{2}(z)
is
K
-even, we obtain
c_{4}=c_{1}\beta^{3}-2c_{3}\beta
.

For general
k>1
, define the
K
-even functions
F_{k}(z)
by the recursive formula
F_{k+1}(z)=F_{k}(z)-\alpha_{2k+2}\beta^{-k-1}(z+\overline{z})^{k+1}
, where
\alpha_{2k+2}
is a polynomial in
c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2k+1}
defined by the expansion
F_{k}(z)=\alpha_{2k+2}z^{2k+2}+\alpha_{2k+3}z^{2k+3}+\cdots
.
Since
F_{k+1}(z)
is
K
-even, we obtain the expression

c_{2k+2}=(\text{a polynomial in }c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2k+1}\text{ with coefficients in }\mathord{\mathbb{Z}}[\beta]).

By iterating this process, we obtain for all
k\geq 1
that
c_{2k}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
-polynomial in
c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2k-1}
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

The identity (
33
) holds. That is, we have

g\Gamma_{\mathbb{Z}}=\mathbb{Z}[\beta][gp_{1},gp_{3},gp_{5},\dots].

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
gp_{2n}
can be expressed as a
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
-polynomial in
gp_{1},gp_{3},\dots,gp_{2n-1}
.
Let
f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}gp_{n}z^{n}
and
F(z)=\frac{zf(z)}{1+f(z)}
be the functions introduced in Lemma
4.2
.
Consider the formal power series
\varphi(z)
defined by

\varphi(z):=\frac{F(z)}{z}=\frac{f(z)}{1+f(z)}=c_{1}z+c_{2}z^{2}+c_{3}z^{3}+\cdots.

Applying Lemma
4.3
to the
K
-even function
F(z)=c_{1}z^{2}+c_{2}z^{3}+\cdots
, we find that
c_{2k}
is a
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
-polynomial in
c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2k-1}
.
From the identity

(36)

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}gp_{n}z^{n}=\frac{\varphi(z)}{1-\varphi(z)}=\varphi(z)+\varphi(z)^{2}+\varphi(z)^{3}+\cdots,

it follows that the coefficient of
z^{2n+1}
in this expansion is of the form

gp_{2n+1}=c_{2n+1}+(\text{a polynomial in }c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2n-1}\text{ with coefficients in }\mathord{\mathbb{Z}}[\beta]).

Since
c_{2},c_{4},\dots,c_{2n}
are polynomials in
c_{1},c_{3},\dots,c_{2n-1}
, we deduce:

c_{2n+1}=gp_{2n+1}+(\text{a polynomial in }gp_{1},gp_{3},\dots,gp_{2n-1}\text{ with coefficients in }\mathord{\mathbb{Z}}[\beta]).

Substituting this into the expression for
f(z)
, we find that each
gp_{2n}
is a polynomial in
gp_{1},gp_{3},\dots,gp_{2n-1}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
.
∎

Example 4.5

(Calculation of
gp_{2}
and
gp_{4}
)
.

Repeating the procedure in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we obtain the sequence

(37)

(c_{2},c_{4},c_{6},\dots)=(-c_{1}\beta,c_{1}\beta^{3}-2c_{3}\beta,-3c_{1}\beta^{5}+5c_{3}\beta^{3}-3c_{5}\beta,\dots).

From (
36
), we obtain the expression

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}gp_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\lambda|=n\\
n\geq 1\end{subarray}}\binom{\ell(\lambda)}{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{\ell(\lambda)}}c_{\lambda}z^{n},

where
m_{i}=m_{i}(\lambda)=\#\{s\mid\lambda_{s}=i\}
,
c_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}c_{\lambda_{i}}
, and

\binom{a}{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{\ell}}=\frac{a!}{b_{1}!\cdot b_{2}!\cdots b_{\ell}!\cdot(a-b_{1}-b_{2}-\dots-b_{\ell})!}.

Therefore, we obtain the series of equations

\begin{gathered}gp_{1}=c_{1},\quad gp_{2}=c_{1}^{2}+c_{2},\quad gp_{3}=c_{1}^{3}+2c_{1}c_{2}+c_{3},\quad gp_{4}=c_{1}^{4}+3c_{1}^{2}c_{2}+2c_{1}c_{3}+c_{2}^{2}+c_{4}.\end{gathered}

From these equations and (
37
), we obtain

\begin{gathered}c_{1}=gp_{1},\quad c_{2}=-gp_{1}\beta,\quad gp_{2}=gp_{1}^{2}-gp_{1}\beta,\\
c_{3}=gp_{3}-gp_{1}^{3}+2gp_{1}^{2}\beta,\quad c_{4}=-2gp_{3}\beta+2gp_{1}^{3}\beta-4gp_{1}^{2}\beta^{2}+gp_{1}\beta^{3},\\
gp_{4}=-gp_{1}^{4}+2gp_{1}gp_{3}+3gp_{1}^{3}\beta-2gp_{3}\beta-3gp_{1}^{2}\beta^{2}+gp_{1}\beta^{3}.\end{gathered}
