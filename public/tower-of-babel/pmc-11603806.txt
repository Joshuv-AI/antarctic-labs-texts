Uncommon biphasic CAR-T expansion induces hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome and fatal multiple infections following BCMA CAR-T cell therapy: a case report
Authors: Wenqiang Yan, Yu Xiong, Rui Lv, Chenxing Du, Tengteng Yu, Shuaishuai Zhang, Weiwei Sui, Shuhui Deng, Jigang Xiao, Yan Xu, Dehui Zou, Lugui Qiu, Gang An
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractB-cell maturation antigen(BCMA)-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapy has significantly improved the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM). Nevertheless, the uncommon phenomenon of biphasic CAR-T cell expansion in vivo and its related severe toxicities have not been methodically described and studied. Herein, we report a case of patients with MM who experienced two CAR-T cell expansion peaks and subsequently developed multiple severe toxicities following BCMA CAR-T cell infusion. The first expansion peak occurred on Day 7, accompanied by grade 3 cytokine release syndrome. The second peak occurred on Day 28, associated with severe immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT), immune effector-cell associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome (IEC-HS), and polymicrobial infections. Both ICAHT and IEC-HS were refractory to our standard treatments; however, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell infusion exhibited some efficacy in improving cytopenia. Despite the administration of a broad-spectrum anti-infective regimen, cytomegalovirus viremia remained uncontrollable, resulting in the development of central nervous system infection, neurological symptoms, and ultimately death. Additionally, we also employed high-dimensional 33-color spectral flow cytometry to describe the dynamic changes in immune cell components and functions between the two expansion peaks. Collectively, this case provides novel insights into the biphasic CAR-T expansion and related immune effector cell-associated toxicities.

Insights

This case suggests that uncommon biphasic chimeric antigen receptor-T expansion may induce severe immune effector cell-associated toxicities and suggest the potential clinical value of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell for severe hematological toxicities.

Introduction

To date, B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapies have revolutionized the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM), producing superior and durable responses compared with standard salvage treatment.1 2 However, alongside the excellent therapeutic potential, CAR-T treatment is associated with significant toxicities. These include cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT), and infections, which practitioners are increasingly recognizing with more experience in patients receiving CAR-T treatment. Persistent cytopenia and increased incidence of multiple infections present ongoing challenges in the management of immunotherapies such as CAR-T.3 4 There lack enough studies on the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, some patients may develop hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-like syndrome manifestations subsequent to the CRS period, presenting with highly elevated serum ferritin levels, hypertriglyceridemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulation dysfunction, pulmonary impairment, and renal and/or hepatic dysfunction.5 This condition, formally defined as immune effector-cell-associated HLH-like syndrome (IEC-HS), is increasingly recognized but is infrequently observed in patients receiving BCMA CAR-T, and its underlying mechanisms remain unclear.6 While most CAR-T cell products reach their peak expansion shortly after infusion and subsequently struggle to sustain expansion in vivo, a small subset of patients may exhibit sustained persistence or multifactorial-induced expansion leading to a second peak.7 8 The precise mechanisms underlying this phenomenon also remain incompletely understood. Herein, we describe a rare case of delayed second CAR-T expansion that induced IEC-HS and severe polymicrobial infections in a patient who received academic BCMA CAR-T therapy. To gain insight into the underlying immunological dynamic, we also employed high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry (SFC) to illuminate changes in immune cell components and functions at two expansion peaks.

Case presentations

An adult patient who was healthy apart from hypothyroidism was diagnosed with MM at our hospital, characterized by light chain kappa-type M-spike, international staging system (ISS) stage II, and revised-ISS (R-ISS) stage II. Peripheral blood smear detected 3% plasma cells. FISH analysis of CD138-magnetics filtered tumor cells revealed chromosome 1q gain and IGH/CCND1 translocation. After six cycles of D-Pd (daratumumab, pomalidomide, and dexamethasone), the patient achieved stringent complete response (sCR) and minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity (NGS sensitivity: 10–6). Due to multiple failures of stem cell mobilization, our patient then received maintenance treatment with lenalidomide and regular follow-up. After 1 year of maintenance treatment, our patient opted for academic BCMA CAR-T (online supplemental method S1) to maintain remission. The IgG levels was 11.5 g/L prior to lymphodepletion.

The patients underwent standard Fc (fludarabine 40 mg, cyclophosphamide 400 mg, Day 5 to Day 3) lymphodepletion and received an academic CAR-T infusion with a dose of 6×106/kg at January 16, 2024. Post-infusion, the initial peak expansion of CAR-T cells occurred on Day 7, followed by a gradual decline (figure 1A). The patient developed grade three CRS, presenting with high fever and chills on Day 0 and hypotension on Day 3. These symptoms were resolved by Day 7 following treatment with tocilizumab, dexamethasone, and vasopressors (figure 1B). During this period, the patient experienced grade 4 neutropenia and was treated with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) (figure 1C). By Day 21, neutrophil levels had returned to near normal, but there was a rapid and significant reduction in platelet levels. Laboratory tests showed elevated levels of ferritin, lactate dehydrogenase, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), alanine transaminase, and aspartate transaminase, with decrease fibrinogen level (figure 1B, online supplemental figures S1 and S2). Bone marrow smear confirmed the presence of hemophagocytic cells (online supplemental figure S3). The patient met five of the eight HLH-2004 criteria9 and had an H-score10 of 215, confirming the diagnosis of IEC-HS. A chest CT scan revealed increased interstitial texture and new multiple ground-glass opacities in both lungs, predominantly in the subpleural regions. These findings were subsequently confirmed as a viral infection by a positive respiratory syncytial virus PCR test. During this time, a second delayed peak in peripheral blood CAR-T cell levels was observed (figure 1A). The patient was treated with G-CSF, eltrombopag olamine, ruxolitinib, and glucocorticoids, but these interventions did not result in significant improvement. In consideration of the patient’s refractory cytopenia and hyperinflammatory status, two infusions of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs, 4×10⁷, 40 mL each) were administered. Neutrophil levels gradually improved, but hemoglobin and platelet levels remained dependent on frequent transfusions (figure 1C). By Day 49, the patient developed recurrent high fevers again with elevated IL-8 and ferritin, despite multiple anti-infective treatments (figure 1B and D). Common pathogenic examination detected COVID-19 and cytomegalovirus (CMV) infections. Peripheral blood next-generation sequencing (NGS) test revealed multiple pathogens, including Campylobacter jejuni, Enterococcus faecalis, Aspergillus flavus, Candida tropicalis, CMV, and human herpesvirus 6B, indicating mixed bacterial, fungal, and viral infections. The patient was treated with a combined anti-infective regimen of meropenem, sulfamethoxazole, voriconazole, acicolovir, molnupiravir, and valganciclovir. While the patient’s temperature returned to normal, the inflammatory status and cytopenia persisted (figure 1B and D). At Day 56, the patient was in deep remission (MRD-negative and in sCR). By Day 70, the patient developed neurological symptoms, including vision disturbances, lower extremity weakness, and clumsy fingers. Examination revealed positive bilateral Babinski’s sign. The MRI of the brain indicated demyelination-like changes, and a lumbar puncture showed colorless cerebrospinal fluid with slightly elevated microalbumin protein levels. Flow cytometry did not detect tumor cells, but cerebrospinal fluid NGS detected 1,212 copies of CMV. High-dose steroids and immunoglobulin shock therapy were administered, but no significant improvement was observed. The patient eventually chose to discontinue treatment and passed away on Day 94 outside the hospital.

Identify diverse T-cell components at two CAR-T expansion peaks by spectral flow cytometry

To elucidate the potential mechanism underlying double CAR-T expansion peaks, we performed 33-color SFC on peripheral blood samples collected at two expansion peaks (Day 4–10 and Day 21–35) to characterize changes in immune cell composition and function (detailed in online supplemental methods S2 and table S1). Using computer-driven clustering with FlowSOM11 on cell type markers (CD3, CD8, CD4, CD56, BCMA-CAR, TCRγδ), we identified 15 clusters from CD45+lymphocytes, predominantly corresponding to classical natural killer (NK)/T lineages (CD8+T cells, CD4+T cells, γδT cells, NK cells, CAR+T cells) (figure 2A). Divergence analysis between the first and second expansion peaks revealed the main difference were in endogenous CD8+T and CAR+CD8+T populations (figure 2B). We further analyzed the features of these two clusters using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and FlowSOM algorithms, incorporating activation/memory T-cell markers and checkpoint receptors as variables. In CAR+CD8+ T cells, we identified eight clusters, each expressing different levels of checkpoints and exhibiting varying functions (figure 2C–D). Comparing the two expansion peaks, we observed that PD-1+T effctor cells were the major components during the first peak, whereas TCF1+Teffector/Naïve clusters were significantly more higher during the second CAR-T expansion peaks (p<0.05, figure 2E). For endogenous CD8+T components, we identified another eight clusters with diverse expression levels of related markers using t-SNE and FlowSOM algorithms (figure 2F–G). Similar to CAR+CD8+ T cells, the TCF1+Teff group maintained a higher proportion in the endogenous CD8+T population during the delayed second expansion peak compared with the first peak (p=0.009, figure 2H). Conversely, early T-cell clusters, including CTLA-4+Naïve and TIGIT+T central memory groups, were significantly more frequent during the first peak (p<0.05, figure 2H). The alterations of monocyte cluster proportions were described in online supplemental figure S4.

Discussion and conclusion

Our patient developed an unusual pattern of biphasic CAR-T expansion, resulting in complex immune effector-cell associated toxicities, including CRS, ICAHT, IEC-HS, and mixed infections, following academical CAR-T cell infusion. Despite the implementation of current standard management, these adverse effects were failed to be resolved, ultimately leading to the patient’s death.

To date, there is no effective treatment for high-grade ICAHT and IEC-HS. In this case, various pro-hematopoietic treatments, transfusion maintenance, and JAK inhibitor (ruxolitinib for IEC-HS6) therapy were employed, yet no significant improvement was observed. Notably, following two infusions of human umbilical cord MSCs, severe cytopenia, especially neutropenia, showed moderate recovery, suggesting the potential clinical value of MSCs in managing high-grade ICAHT. The observed improvement in cytopenia may be attributed to the immune modulatory effects of MSCs, which could mitigate the inflammatory microenvironment that contributes to hematopoietic stem cell dysfunction.12

Biphasic CAR-T expansion is another notable phenomenon in this patient, with the underlying mechanism of re-expansion remained unclear. Our high-dimensional SFC analysis revealed that the first peak was dominated by PD-1+Teff cells, whereas the second peak saw a significant increase in TCF1+Teff cells in both CAR+CD8+ T cells and endogenous CD8+T cells. This shift suggests a transition from an initial effector phase to a more prolonged memory/naïve phase during the second expansion. The persistence of TCF1+Teff cells during the two expansion peaks may indicate a continued activation response, possibly stimulated by the complex viral infectious milieu. Additionally, the secondary expansion of CAR-T cells in vivo creates a hyperinflammatory microenvironment, which can lead to the emergence of IEC-HS, persistent severe ICAHT, and immune dysfunction.

The patient’s demise was ultimately attributed to multiple infections, despite the administration of an aggressive anti-infective, broad-spectrum treatment. The aforementioned treatments were unable to prevent the CMV viremia from spreading to the central nervous system. Infections have emerged as an important complication of immunotherapy, frequently associated with immunosuppression due to persistent T-cell activation, an excessive inflammatory state, or impaired hematopoiesis.13 One recent study has indicated that herpesvirus infections after citla-cel infusion may promote the distant re-expansion of CAR-T cells.8 In our patient, we also observed a respiratory syncytial virus infection occurring prior to the second CAR-T expansion peak. Further investigation is required to elucidate the relationship between viral infection and CAR-T cell expansion.

In conclusion, our case report highlights the unusual phenomenon of biphasic CAR-T expansion and describes its associated immune cell dynamics following BCMA CAR-T therapy. Importantly, we report the coexistence of severe immunotoxicities including CRS, ICAHT, IEC-HS, and fatal mixed infections, in one patient as a consequence of CAR-T expansion, and confirm that umbilical cord MSCs infusion can effectively improve high-grade ICAHT.
