A study of genomic complexity underlying multidrug resistance in Candida auris strains in Thailand
Authors: Korawit Opassathian, Nawarat Boonmee, Saowanee Swangkaew, Piroon Jenjaroenpun, Thidathip Wongsurawat, Worarat Kruasuwan, Popchai Ngamskulrungroj, Chalermchai Mitrpant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Candida auris, one of the emerging multidrug-resistant Candida species, was first discovered in 2009 from the ear canal of a patient in Tokyo, Japan. Since then, several studies exhibit the possible threats of C. auris due to its versatile resistance to frontline antifungal agents. The number of studies focusing on genetic variants associated with multidrug resistance has risen over a decade. In this study, we retrieved eight C. auris strains, originated from clinical isolates in Bangkok, Thailand, and sequenced all genomes using long-read oxford nanopore technology. We have identified genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and found that the typical genetic variation, Y132F, in ERG11 was shared between fluconazole-resistant strains, as previously reported. The fluconazole-resistant strains also exclusively harboured other nonsynonymous SNPs suggesting new mutation candidates associated with fluconazole resistance. On the caspofungin resistance aspect, shared SNPs between caspofungin-resistant strains were also found in genes associated with various biological pathways, including G32E in CDC10, a gene encoding the protein related to cell wall integrity. The other mutation was found in fungal-specific transcription factors in the Zn (II)2-Cys6 binuclear cluster, which potentially plays a central role in regulating membrane efflux pumps or cell wall production. An amino acid substitution in FKS1, a well-known protein associated with echinocandin resistance, was found in only one of two caspofungin-resistant strains at the newly discovered position, I1361T. In short, the results of our study have confirmed the typical genetic variants underpinning multidrug resistance in C. auris strains and proposed the possible new pathways involved.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25386-6.

Introduction

Candida is a group of fungal species under the Ascomycota phylum. With many species, Candida is considered a non-monophyletic group spreading across the Saccharomycetales order1. Candida species exhibit a polymorphic trait, interchangeable forms during development under various environmental circumstances, including unicellular, hyphae, pseudohyphae and mating type in opaque form2. Many Candida species, such as C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, or Nakaseomyces glabratus (formerly C. glabrata), cause candidiasis in several parts of the human body, including the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and external reproductive organs3–5. Additionally, several Candida species can cause an invasive type of candidiasis, an infection of Candida in the bloodstream or internal organs6,7. An infection starts from the attachment of fungal cells to the host cell surface. Then, the invasion begins with the dimorphic yeast-to-hyphae stage and directed growth mediated by thigmotropic mechanisms. Finally, the accumulation of fungal cells forms a biofilm with single-cell form near the host cell surface and hyphal structure covering the upper surface5. The protection with heat-shock protein and pH-sensing mechanism are two processes of many Candida adaptive responses to host, which influence the survivability of Candida inside the host cells5. Despite all the adapting mechanisms, multi-antifungal drug-resistant feature has become alarming to healthcare systems around the world. Emerging multidrug-resistant Candida species include C. auris and other closely related species, C. haemulonii, C. duobushaemulonii, and Candida pseudohaemulonii8.

C. auris was first discovered in 2009 from an ear canal of a patient in Japan9. Several studies have shown that C. auris causes invasive candidiasis in the human bloodstream system10–12. C. auris was also found in the human urinary system13. Additionally, intra-hospital transmission of C. auris was also reported due to the persistent survival of C. auris on different surface materials and due to the tolerance of several disinfectants14,15. Several antifungal treatments, such as fluconazole and caspofungin, were discovered over the past two decades while amphotericin B and flucytosine have been in use since the 1950–1960 s16–19. Different types of antifungal agents target different biological pathways. For example, fluconazole, a basic antifungal drug in the triazole group, has a mechanism to intervene in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway18. Because fungal cells utilise ergosterol to maintain plasma membrane function, the disruption of ergosterol biosynthesis results in an increase in membrane permeability20. Caspofungin and anidulafungin, antifungal agents belonging to the echinocandin group, disrupt the cell wall component by targeting a biosynthesis process of β−1,3 glucan16,21,22. Amphotericin B, a primitive antifungal drug introduced around the middle of the 20th century, belongs to the polyene group of antifungal agents and functions by disrupting the fungal cell membrane, resulting in cell ion leakage17,23. Recently, the serious concern of C. auris is its ability to resist most frontline antifungal agents24,25. Several reports show that many clinical isolates of C. auris exhibit multidrug resistance or even pan-drug resistance to all antifungal agents8,24,26–28.

Genomic studies have revealed the genetic variants associated with multidrug resistance of Candida species at different levels. The mutations found in genes in ergosterol biosynthesis pathways, ERGs, were found in Candida species with fluconazole resistance8,28–31. Many studies have reported the common mutation Y132F in ERG11, a gene encoding lanosterol 14-α-demethylase, indicating that this gene is the central gene determining fluconazole resistance and susceptibility24,25,32. Moreover, mutations occur in FKS1 and FKS2, encoding 1,3-β-glucan synthase, and were found commonly in caspofungin-resistant Candida species24,29,33,34. Multiple mutations in FKS1 protein sequences were reported in caspofungin-resistant C. auris isolates, including the deletion at F635 (F635Δ) and amino acid substitutions F635Y, F635L, S639F, S639P, S639T, D642Y, M690I, R1354S, and R1354H24,27,31,35–38. Not only the gene level mutations, but a study from Munoz et al. (2018) also shows the expansion of mutated genes shared the protein families associated with antifungal resistance in the genomes of C. auris and its closely related species. The results suggest the pan-genome evidence underlying multidrug resistance traits beyond gene mutations8. Additionally, structural variants in the genome, such as insertions and deletions, are involved in several drug resistance traits39. Hence, several works have pointed out the connection between the multidrug resistance traits, or so-called pan-resistance in some works, of C. auris and the complexity of its genome.

In this study, we retrieved eight C. auris laboratory strains from the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. All C. auris strains were subjected to antifungal susceptibility tests (AST) and were categorised according to multidrug resistance profiles. The whole-genome sequencing was performed using long-read Oxford Nanopore technology. To elucidate the genomic complexity underlying resistance or susceptibility phenotype, we explored the phylogenetic relationship between strains using core SNPs alignment and identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genomic sequences and mutations in protein sequences that are associated with the different multidrug resistance profiles.

Methods

C. auris strains, DNA extraction, and whole-genome long-read sequencing

All C. auris strains used in this study were previously collected and stored in the department’s strain repository without any associated identifiable clinical or patient information. As no patient data were accessed or analyzed, this study was deemed exempt from ethical review by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board. All strains were identified at the species level using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometer (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker Daltonics GmbH, Bremen, Germany).

Genomic DNAs of the eight C. auris samples were extracted using the Nanobind CBB Big DNA Kit (Circulomics, NB900-001-01, United States) with a modified protocol based on Nanobind Saccharomyces cerevisiae High Molecular Weight DNA Extraction v0.5b (6/2021). Briefly, 1 mL of Spheroplasting Buffer 1 (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.5, and 10 mM DTT) was added to the yeast cell and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 15 min. Cell pellets were subsequently incubated with the Spheroplasting Buffer 2 (1 M sorbitol, 25 mM EDTA, 1.6 mM citric acid, 8.4 mM sodium citrate, 14 mM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 20 µL of 5 U µL⁻¹ lyticase at 35 °C for 30 min. Proteinase K and Buffer CLE3 were added to the cell lysate suspension. Next, RNase A (20 µL) was added to the cell lysate mixture and incubated at 55 °C for 1 h. Nanobind disk was applied to the cell lysate mixed with 400 µL of isopropanol (400 µL) and incubated at RT for 20 min. To capture the DNA in the suspension, place the tube on the magnetic tube rack. The supernatant was discarded, and the Nanobind disk was washed with Buffer CW1 (500 µL) and Buffer CW2 (500 µL). Finally, Buffer EB was incubated at RT overnight to elute the genomic DNA (gDNA). All extracted gDNA was qualified by a Qubit™ 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) using the 1X dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The purity of the extracted DNA with the A260/280 and A260/230 absorbance ratios was obtained using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA).

Antifungal susceptibility testing

Commercially available Yeast One Sensititre YO10 plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) were employed to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for eight strains of C. auris. All testing was conducted according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Briefly, the YO10 plates, pre-dosed with antifungal agents, utilise a colorimetric endpoint determination system to assess fungal growth. Fungal suspensions were prepared by culturing isolates on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) at 35 °C for 24 h, followed by adjustment to a 0.5 McFarland standard in sterile 0.85% saline solution. Wells displaying a pink colour were interpreted as indicating fungal growth. To specifically evaluate the susceptibility of Candida auris strains to caspofungin and fluconazole treatments, the broth microdilution (BMD) method was additionally performed. This testing followed the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M27-A3 guidelines.

MIC endpoints for fluconazole and caspofungin were determined as the lowest concentration resulting in a 50% reduction in growth compared to the drug-free control well, while amphotericin B was read at 100% inhibition. Interpretations of MIC values for fluconazole and caspofungin were based on the clinical breakpoints provided by the U.S. Centres for Disease Control and Prevention for C. auris (CDC Guidelines: https://www.cdc.gov/candida-auris/hcp/laboratories/antifungal-susceptibility-testing.html?CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/fungal/candida-auris/c-auris-antifungal.html).

Three biological replicates were performed to conclude the results for each experiment. Control experiments were conducted using Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019 and Candida krusei ATCC 6258 as susceptible reference strains.

Library preparation and sequencing

For long-read sequencing, the library was prepared from 400 ng input DNA using the Ligation Sequencing gDNA - Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD114.24, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, ONT, UK). A total of eight barcoded samples were pooled together, then loaded on the PromethION flow cell R10.4.1 (FLO-PRO114M; ONT, UK) and sequenced using PromethION P2 Solo (ONT, UK) with the default setting. Guppy (ONT, UK) v6.0.1 with the SUP (super accuracy) mode was used for base calling and quality control studies. Porechop v0.2.4 (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop40) was applied to remove adapters and barcodes from the readings.

Genome assemblies and gene annotations

Trimmed long-read sequences in fastq format were subjected to de novo genome assembly using Flye v2.9.3 (https://github.com/fenderglass/Flye41,). The resulting drafted genomes were polished using Medaka v1.11.3 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) resulting in genome sequences in FASTA format. BUSCO (https://github.com/metashot/busco) was used to evaluate the completeness of assembled genomes. Then, gene prediction was performed using BRAKER2 (https://github.com/Gaius-Augustus/BRAKER42–51) using publicly available protein sequences from NCBI database52 as a preset to train gene prediction (GCA_002759435.3, GCA_003014415.1, GCA_008275145.1, GCF_002775015.1, GCF_003013715.1, GCA_007168705.1, GCA_016809505.1, GCA_019332045.1, GCF_001189475.18,28,53–56). BRAKER combines two gene finders for gene prediction, including GeneMark (https://github.com/gatech-genemark/GeneMark-ETP57) and Augustus (https://github.com/Gaius-Augustus/Augustus58). The results from the gene-finding and predicting methods were used in the functional annotation step.

Gene functional annotation was performed following Funannotate v1.8.17 (https://github.com/nextgenusfs/funannotate59). In short, assembled genomes in FASTA format were masked, cleaned, sorted, and then used as an input in prediction steps with the pre-trained data from BRAKER. The results were then subjected to annotation by running the number sequentially. The annotation step also pulls the protein identification from several databases, including PFAM annotation60. EggNOG-mapper is also included in Funannotate as functional annotation pipelines61. Specific functional annotations in Funannotate include CAZYmes and SignalP, which are related to fungal secretome and signal peptide prediction, respectively62,63.

Genomic comparative analysis and variant identification

Assembled genomes of all C. auris strains were used as input files in the Snippy pipeline to identify genomic variants64. The genome of C. auris B8441 was used as a reference. After acquiring variants in VCF file format, only single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were collected as results. Clair3 (https://github.com/HKU-BAL/Clair365) was used as a validating pipeline for SNP identification using sequencing reads. In short, the analysis pipeline started with aligning reads with the reference genome with minimap2 (https://github.com/lh3/minimap266) resulting in a .sam file format, followed by converting and sorting the .sam file to a .bam file using samtools v1.1867. Sorted .bam files were used as input for Clair3 to perform the SNPs callings, yielding results in VCF file format, including reference chromosome and position of single SNPs together with quality assessment values. Only consensus SNPs between two pipelines were selected and used in downstream analysis.

To annotate the nonsynonymous mutation, amino acid substitution, in protein sequences, SNPs from each strain were mapped back to the reference genome and annotation file to translate the SNPs in DNA to amino acid changes in protein sequences. This was achieved by using the VariantAnnotation package in R and resulted in the positions and patterns of amino changes based on the reference protein (https://github.com/Bioconductor/VariantAnnotation68. The positions and patterns were then used as the key features to find the unique and shared mutations according to multidrug-resistant phenotypes using R scripts. The list of protein sequences with mutations in the reference genome was mapped to the annotated proteins of all sequenced C. auris genomes using BLASTP69. The results are collected in Supplementary File 1. Finally, the protein sequences carrying nonsynonymous mutations from all strains were aligned to confirm the pattern of mutations using the MSA package in R70.

Apart from calling SNPs separately from each strain, the snippy pipeline also performs the snippy-core workflow to extract core SNPs and construct the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using gubbins (https://github.com/nickjcroucher/gubbins) and FastTree (https://github.com/morgannprice/fasttree) with the general time reversible (GTR) substitution model and 1,000 bootstrap replicates71,72.

Analysis of hypothetical proteins with potentially responsible for multidrug resistance phenotype (R/R)

Protein sequences of hypothetical proteins, which were shared between multidrug-resistant strains (R/R), were collected along with associated protein family domains (PFAM domains). The sequences were then submitted to the InterProScan database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) to obtain different protein family classifications, so-called InterProScan domains. Next, lists of proteins containing matching PFAM and InterProScan domains from various infectious Candida species were acquired from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org), representing proteins with functional equivalents.

Results

Multidrug resistance profiles of C. auris strains

To observe the multidrug resistance profiles, all C. auris strains were subjected to AST (Thermo Fisher Scientific’s Sensititre YeastOne, YO10) with C. parapsilosis ATCC 20019 and C. krusei ATCC 6258 as standard control strains. Both SI-CAU-425 and SI-CAU-503, hereinafter referred to as strain 425 and strain 503, respectively, were considered sensitive to all fluconazole (FL), caspofungin (CS), anidulafungin (AND), and amphotericin B (AB), while the other strains exhibited resistance to FL, with the MIC values higher than 256 mg L⁻¹. A similar resistance profile was also found in tests with AB, with the resistance strains showing 2 mg L⁻¹ MIC values, equal to approximate resistance breakpoints from CDC guidelines. Strain 605 (> 8 mg L– 1) and strain 672 (> 8 mg L– 1) had MIC values beyond the breakpoints for caspofungin (CS), indicating resistance phenotype to CS. Only strain 605 (4 mg L⁻¹) exhibited the AND-resistant phenotype (Table 1). The results from the YO10 AST for FL and CS susceptibility tests were confirmed with the broth microdilution method (BMD). Strain 672 (16 mg L– 1) showed a higher caspofungin MIC value than strain 605 (8 mg L– 1), but both values were still higher than resistance breakpoints from CDC guidelines.

This study also conducted AST using YO10 on additional antifungal drugs including itraconazole (ITZ), voriconazole (VOR), posaconazole (PZ), micafungin (MF), and 5-flucytosine (5-FC). Strain 390, 605, and 672 exhibited reduced susceptibility to ITZ (16 mg L– 1), VOR (8 mg L– 1), and PZ (8 mg L– 1), while other strains MIC values were relatively low (Supplementary Table S1). The caspofungin-resistant strains, 605 and 672, also showed reduced susceptibility to MF, with MIC values greater than 8 mg L⁻¹.

However, due to the lack of established resistance breakpoints for these agents, our focus remained on resistance profiles for FL and CS. With this stand, all strains are classified into three subgroups: (1) sensitive to both FL and CS (S/S)—strains 425 and 503, (2) resistant to FL but sensitive to CS (S/R)—strains 390, 713, 757, and 1199, and (3) resistant to FL and CS (R/R)—strains 605 and 672.

Genome assemblies and features of C. auris strains

Eight C. auris genomes were subjected to long-read nanopore sequencing (ONT, UK). Nearly complete genome assemblies were generated using assembly pipelines for long-read sequences. Then, the completeness of assembled genomes was evaluated by BUSCO with the ranges of assembled genomes ranging from 11.6 Mb (strain 713) to 12.8 Mb (strain 425). We have found the percentage of similarity ranging from 85.4 to 94.8%. The number of contigs also varied among eight strains from 7 contigs (strain 605) to 157 scaffolds (strain 713). The number of contigs inversely reflects the quality of sequences indicated by the N50 score. Strains 713 and 757, with relatively high numbers of scaffolds, respectively, showed low N50 scores around 0.13 and 0.44 Mb, while other strains had N50 values around 1–2 Mb. Similar percentages of GC content were found across all C. auris strains (Table 2).

Assembled genomes were then subjected to the gene prediction and functional gene annotation process. The number of predicted genes was similar across all strains, from 5,388 genes in strain 713 to 5,675 genes in strain 425. Similarities in functional gene annotations were also found in all C. auris strains in all annotated protein-coding genes, hypothetical protein-coding genes, and tRNA categories (Table 2).

The differences in single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between C. auris strains associated with the multidrug resistance profiles

After the de novo genome assembly process, all assembled genomes were used as input files in the snippy variant calling workflow. We used the genome of the C. auris strain B8441 as a reference genome because this strain has been reported as sensitive to multiple antifungal treatments, including FL and CS8. Snippy workflow returns a variant calling result for each strain, which was used in the downstream analysis to identify mutations underlying different multidrug-resistant phenotypes. The number of identified SNPs was summarised and compared. We found that there were no clear differences in terms of the number of SNPs found between genomes of all C. auris strains. The distribution pattern of SNPs between genomes was also similar. The highest number of SNPs is located in reference chromosome CM076438.1, and the lowest number is in CM076444.1 (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Figure S1).

The Snippy workflow also runs the snippy-core command to define ‘core SNPs’, which can be used to define the genomic relatedness between assembled input genomes. The maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree constructed from core SNPs separates the group of genomes according to antifungal resistance profiles (Fig. 1). The genomes of strains 425 and 503 formed a distinct group of fluconazole-sensitive strains, while the other strains grouped together associated with fluconazole-resistant phenotypes. Among these fluconazole-resistant strains, genomes of strains 605 and 672 were closely related to each other, depicting the multidrug resistance (R/R). The phylogenetic tree based on core SNPs indicates that the differences in multidrug resistance profiles between C. auris strains are connected with the genetic variation between genomes.

Association between genetic variants and different multidrug resistance profiles

To check beyond the core SNP differences between genomes, we observed each SNP in more details. By this, the ID of each SNP was created from the chromosome, position of SNPs, and pattern of SNPs. Then, the shared SNP IDs between all strains were identified (Supplementary Figure S2). There were 349 SNPs shared between fluconazole-resistant strains and were classified as category I SNPs (Supplementary Figure S2, Table 3). Only nine SNPs were shared between strains 605 and 672. Four SNPs were found to be unique in the genome of strain 605, and seven SNPs were found to be specific to strain 672. All caspofungin-resistant SNPs were then classified as category II (Supplementary Figure S2, Table 3).

The next step was to identify amino acid substitutions from annotated SNPs, hereinafter referred to as nonsynonymous mutations in protein sequences. We used the VariantAnnotation package in R with the C. auris strain B8441 annotation as input. In short, each SNP in the coding sequence region was translated to an amino acid sequence. Only SNPs that caused the amino acid substitutions between the subject genome and reference genome were collected. Then, the location and pattern of amino acid change of each mutation were created and used to identify the shared mutation between C. auris strains. As shown in Fig. 2, there were 173 mutations shared between all C. auris genomes used in this study in comparison to the reference genome (black arrow). Of particular interest, 166 mutations were shared exclusively between fluconazole-resistant strains (green arrow), hereinafter referred to as category I mutations, and are summarised in Table 3. In contrast, 212 mutations were found in common between strain 425 and strain 503 (blue arrow). A relatively low number of mutations were found in caspofungin-resistant strains, with 5 mutations shared between strains 605 and 672. Only one mutation was found specific to strain 605, and 2 mutations were found specific to strain 672 (red arrows). All shared and strain-specific mutations found in caspofungin-resistant strains were classified as category II (Table 3). No mutation was found in common among caspofungin-sensitive strains.

The reference proteins with nonsynonymous mutations were then used as subjects to find the orthologous proteins in all annotated proteins from C. auris genomes with BLASTP. This step resulted in a data table consisting of mutation information, reference protein ID, and each annotated protein ID across all sequenced genomes (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). The list includes well-established FL-resistant variants such as Y132F in ERG11 and M192I in ERG4, confirming that the workflow used in the variant-calling workflow in this work yielded reproducible outcomes as previously reported. Apart from ERG4 and ERG11, the mutations were also identified in several other proteins. Among the list, there were two proteins with more than one mutation, which were CCH1 (E1478D and F1672V) and PRP22 (I82F and G354R). Several proteins with mutations were found to have possibly similar functions, such as RAD18 (Q145R), RAD23 (S99C), and RAD52 (L406S), which have functions related to DNA damage repair according to annotated PFAM and the Candida genome database (CGD, http://www.candidagenome.org/). The full description of proteins, including annotated protein family and gene ID in all genomes, was listed in Table 4 and Supplementary Table S3.

A protein with possible roles of multidrug-resistant regulator was found sharing SNPs between caspofungin-resistant strains

We, therefore, decided to investigate the hypothetical proteins with amino acid substitutions shared between strains 605 and 672 (K413T in KAK8441972.1, S275L in KAK8442458.1, and N330K in KAK8440507.1). This approach aimed to find in-depth details of these proteins to identify the possible functions involved in the caspofungin-resistant pathway. We used annotated protein family domains (PFAMs) of each protein as key functional identifiers to lead our observations.

Among all hypothetical proteins, ACI3LZ_001182.1 and ACI3LW_001907.1, orthologous proteins of KAK8442458.1, contained two PFAM domains associated with transcriptional activities, which are PF00172 (fungal Zn (2)-Cys (6) binuclear cluster domain) and PF04082 (fungal specific transcription factor domain). Protein sequences of both proteins were submitted to the InterProScan database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). The analysis resulted in four classes of protein families, including IPR051430, IPR036864, IPR001138, and IPR007219, all of which relate to the transcriptional activity of proteins, confirming the possible roles of these proteins as transcription factors (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S5). Finally, the list of proteins containing matching PFAM and InterProScan domains from infectious Candida species was obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/, Table 6, and Supplementary Table S5). The list indicates proteins from other Candida genomes containing equivalent protein domains, suggesting a possibly similar function. As shown in Table 6, most proteins are associated with generic names such as fungal specific transcription factor domain family protein or fungal zinc cluster transcription factors. However, there were several annotated names in different species, including multidrug resistance regulator 2 (MRR2) in C. albicans, HAP1 transcriptional regulatory protein (HAP1) in Candida californica, and ZCF34 transcription factor in C. orthopsilosis (Table 6, Supplementary Table S5). All of which are classified as transcription factors in the Zn(2)-Cys(6) binuclear cluster. The results suggest the association between the nonsynonymous mutations in one of the hypothetical proteins with the caspofungin-resistant phenotype.

A similar approach was also applied to ACI3LZ_000688.1 and ACI3LW_001403.1, orthologous proteins of KAK8441972.1. Both proteins contain PFAMs associated with cullin, a protein family with functions related to ubiquitin ligases (Supplementary Table S473,). However, the annotated PFAMs were too generic to be used to pinpoint the protein with possible matching functions. Additionally, for ACI3LZ_002944.1 and ACI3LW_000517.1, the orthologous protein of KAK8440507.1 has not been annotated with protein domains, resulting in the endpoint of this observation.

Discussion

Fungi in Candida species cause candidiasis spreading worldwide74–76. Species in Candida are considered diverse in terms of the genomic structure, ranging from diploid genome in C. albicans to haploid genome in C. auris and closely related species77,78. Additionally, some C. albicans isolates also exhibit aneuploidy and segmental aneuploid which correlate with the resistance phenotype to frontline antifungal treatment79. Alongside the discoveries of new species of Candida, treatments for candidiasis were also observed. Despite the novelty of the strain, C. auris was classified as multidrug, or even pan-drug, resistant strains, causing challenges to healthcare systems worldwide80–82. In this study, we retrieved eight C. auris strains from culture specimens from Thailand. We performed the antifungal susceptibility test and classified C. auris strains into three groups based on their response to fluconazole and caspofungin. Strains 425 and 503 were sensitive to both drugs (S/S), while strains 605 and 672 showed resistance phenotypes to both antifungal agents (R/R). The other strains were classified as FL-resistant and CS-sensitive (R/S). To elucidate possible genomic components underlying different multidrug resistance profiles, all genomes were sequenced, assembled, and annotated with a long-read sequencing platform. The drafted genome assemblies of strains 713 and 757 show relatively higher numbers of assembled contigs, reflecting the higher number of fragmented reads and lower quality in comparison to other assembled genomes. However, the endpoint number of predicted and annotated genes was still in range with other strains and acceptable when compared with publicly available data8. The first attempt to pinpoint the genetic component underpinning antifungal resistance profiles was by observing the differences in sequenced C. auris genomes based on a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree constructed from core SNP sequences. The phylogenetic tree approach is mostly used in C. auris research to identify the belonging clades from an epidemiological point of view83,84. However, several studies have applied the genetic variation approach, including copy number variation (CNV) and SNPs, to investigate associations with the multidrug resistance Candida isolates8. Our study adapted this approach to compare the laboratory specimens based on the hypothesis that the variation in genetic components is the key player in different antifungal resistance profiles, and the core SNPs phylogenetic tree supports this hypothesis. While our analysis identifies candidate mutations associated with resistance, functional validation through genetic manipulation, such as CRISPR-Cas9, is required to establish causal relationships.

The second step is to identify genetic variants underpinning resistance to FL and CS using SNPs. Similar SNPs distribution and number were found across all samples, indicating no SNPs hotspot in association with the multidrug resistance phenotype (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S2). Overlapping between SNPs from all sequenced genomes shows a common 394 SNPs referring to genome differences between C. auris strains in this study with the reference genome, a clinical isolate from Pakistan8. The number of SNPs could range from less than a hundred, when comparing different clinical isolates from the same patient, up to more than 200,000, when comparing between strains from different clades83,85. We decided to use C. auris B8441 as a reference due to the complete genome assembly at the chromosome level and the susceptibility to fluconazole and caspofungin, representing the suitable baseline of comparison8. With this antifungal susceptibility profile of a reference strain, a high number of SNPs, 349 SNPs, found in a shared group of fluconazole-resistant strains is not beyond expectation (Supplementary Figure S2, Table 3). Moreover, there were 476 SNPs found in common between fluconazole-susceptible strains (Supplementary Figure S2). By addition, this indicates that more than 800 SNPs cause differences in fluconazole resistance and susceptibility. Additionally, only 20 SNPs in total were found related to the caspofungin resistance phenotype, suggesting that only a limited number of genetic variants are involved in caspofungin resistance (Supplementary Figure S2, Table 3).

Then, we focused only on SNPs predicted to have amino acid substitutions in protein sequences. This step ruled out SNPs in non-coding sequences and SNPs that result in synonymous mutations. Of all 349 SNPs found in fluconazole-resistant strains, we identified 166 SNPs inducing amino acid substitutions. 76 mutations were found in 74 well-annotated proteins (Table 4, Supplementary Table S3). The list includes the canonical fluconazole-resistant mutation Y132F in ERG11, the amino acid change found in several fluconazole-resistant isolates of C. auris and other Candida species such as C. parapsilosis, confirming that our method is in line with previous reports8,24,28,86,87. Our data further confirms the azole cross-resistance in some fluconazole-resistant strains, 390, 605, and 672. These observations support previous reports of the ERG11 Y132F mutation mediating not only fluconazole resistance but also reduced voriconazole or posaconazole susceptibility24,88. Several studies also report the mutations in the ERG4 gene in different Candida species with different interpretations. In C. albicans, the mutations in ERG11 seem to have a stronger influence in azoles resistance than the mutations in ERG489. In C. lusitaniae, a nonsense mutation in ERG4 caused truncated protein associated with amphotericin B resistance but not fluconazole resistance34. A study from Munoz et al. (2018) found the mutation in ERG4 from a genome of the C. auris strain with resistance to both fluconazole and amphotericin B. Recently, 28 clinical isolates in Qatar were subjected to AST, and genomes were sequenced. The results have shown that, in comparison with the B8441 reference genome, all isolates possess the same mutation found in this study (M192I), and all isolates exhibit resistance to either fluconazole, amphotericin B, or both90. M192I in ERG4 and Y132F in ERG11 have also been reported in 19 clinical isolates with resistance to fluconazole in the United States25. There were other proteins with mutations shared exclusively between fluconazole-resistant strains. As far as we know, there is no direct connection between amino acid substitutions in most proteins on the list and the fluconazole resistant phenotype in Candida species.

Focusing on mutations shared between caspofungin-resistant strains, G32E mutation in CDC10 is shared exclusively between caspofungin-resistant strains. CDC10 is a protein subunit of septin, which is important for septum formation during bud morphogenesis91. CDC10 protein is also important in cell wall integrity by regulating chitin deposition91,92. In C. albicans, there is a connection between caspofungin and septin localisation mediated by CDC10. Null mutant of CDC10 is associated with increased caspofungin sensitivity92. Additionally, the result of this study also reported the Q198R as shared mutation between fluconazole-resistant strains. However, the association between CDC10 and antifungal resistance is well-reported in response to caspofungin but not to fluconazole92,93.

We also reported the shared mutation between caspofungin-resistant strains in BLM3, also known as BLM10. BLM3 has been reported to have transcriptional activity to genes involved in metabolic pathways of amino acid and carbohydrate94. The direct connection between this protein and caspofungin response has not been reported.

Three additional proteins with shared mutations among caspofungin-resistant strains were annotated as hypothetical proteins. One of these proteins, orthologous to reference protein KAK8442458.1, contains protein family domains associated with the Zn (2)-Cys (6) binuclear cluster domain, which is the transcription factor domain specific to fungal species95. In this study, we identified 20 functional equivalent proteins containing matching protein family domains. Interestingly, in other pathogenic Candida species such as C. albicans, C. orthopsilosis, and C. lusitaniae, the identified proteins were annotated as multidrug resistance regulator 2 (MRR2). Several studies have reported the function of MRR2 as the upstream regulator of CDR1, an ABC transporter responsible for the efflux pump of a wide range of chemicals, including multiple antifungal drugs96. A study from Niimi et al. (2006) reported slightly enhanced echinocandin resistance due to the ectopic expression of CDR1 and CDR2 in budding yeast. Moreover, the same study also reported that strains of C. albicans with fluconazole-resistant traits, with increased CDR1 and CDR2 expression, also exhibited a mild, non-significant enhancement in resistance97.

Focusing on proteins with unique amino acid substitutions in caspofungin-resistant strains, we identified a non-annotated gene specific to strain 605 (ACI3LZ_000731.1). No ortholog was found in C. albicans based on the EggNOG database, leaving its function unknown.

In strain 672, two unique mutations were observed in FKS1 and OST6. FKS1 is a well-known target of caspofungin. Most resistance-associated mutations occur at position S639 in C. auris, part of hotspot-1 (hs-1), equivalent to S645 in other Candida species. Variants at this position include S639F, S639P, S639T, and S639Y24,27,31,33,35–38,98–100. Mutations in hs-1 also include F635Y and F635L37. Additional mutation in the hotspot-2 (hs-2) region (D1350–L1357 in C. auris)—such as R1354H/S—have also been implicated27,37,101,102. A third region, hotspot-3 (hs-3), was proposed by Carolus et al. (2021), identifying M690I in an evolved resistant strain103. A non-hotspot mutation, W691L, was also linked to echinocandin resistance104. In our study, no known hotspot mutations were found. Instead, we identified a novel substitution in FKS1 at position I1361T, not previously reported in C. auris, suggesting a potential new mutation associated with caspofungin resistance.

The other strain 672-specific mutation is found in OST6, a protein subunit of the oligosaccharyltransferase transmembrane protein complex105. In C. albicans and S. cerevisiae, this enzyme complex functions in a protein glycosylation pathway as a crucial part for cell wall composition105. The chemical genomics approach reveals that the poacic acid treatment, a β−1,3-glucan-targeting antifungal similar to caspofungin, resembles the effect of deletion mutation of genes related to cell wall biogenesis and glycosylation pathways, including OST6106.

We also acknowledge the paradoxical growth effect (PGE), also known as Eagle effect, which could occur under caspofungin treatment. PGE was discovered in the early 2000 s and has been reported recently in several Candida species including C. auris100,107,108. There is an inconsistency in caspofungin resistance of C. auris between in vitro and in vivo studies; that is to say, several isolates exhibit resistance to caspofungin but show no significant results when infected in murine model100. However, the isolates harboring S639F exhibit caspofungin resistance in both experimental settings. These results suggest that the in vitro antifungal susceptibility test with caspofungins should be interpreted with caution and the genetic variation, such as mutations in FKS1, may serve as a more reliable indicator for resistance screening.

To minimize the impact of PGE during resistance screening, it may be beneficial to also assess susceptibility using micafungin and anidulafungin, as these echinocandins are reportedly less affected by PGE compared to caspofungin107,109. In this study, caspofungin-resistant strains also exhibited elevated MICs to micafungin when compared to non-resistant strains. Since no established clinical breakpoint exists for micafungin in C. auris, these MIC values cannot be definitively classified as resistant or susceptible. The elevated MICs to anidulafungin were also reported in these same strains but only one strain, 605, exhibited the MIC value equal to the standard breakpoint. The elevation of MICs across all three echinocandins supports the presence of a resistance phenotype when compared to other strains. To fully confirm the clinical significance of the observed genetic variants and to eliminate the potential confounding effect of PGE, in vivo validation should be pursued.

Collectively, we identified two caspofungin-resistant strains. Both strains shared mutations predominantly in genes encoding hypothetical proteins, as well as in CDC10, a gene involved in cell wall integrity. We hypothesize that mutations in CDC10 may represent key genetic variants distinguishing resistant from susceptible strains in our experimental setting. Additionally, a protein functional and structure analysis are also required to perform a comprehensive study as we have found one caspofungin resistance strain harbouring mutation in FKS1 protein in a non-hotspot region and several potential genetic variants underpinning the resistance phenotype.
