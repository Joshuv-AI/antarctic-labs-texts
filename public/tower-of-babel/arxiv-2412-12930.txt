Certified model predictive control for switched evolution equations using Model Order Reduction
Michael Kartmann † and Mattia Manucci ⋆ and Benjamin Unger ‡ and Stefan Volkwein †

Abstract
. We present a model predictive control ( MPC ) framework for linear switched evolution equations arising from a parabolic partial differential equation ( PDE ). First-order optimality conditions for the resulting finite-horizon optimal control problems are derived. The analysis allows for the incorporation of convex control constraints and sparse regularization. Then, to mitigate the computational burden of the MPC procedure, we employ Galerkin reduced-order modeling ( ROM ) techniques to obtain a low-dimensional surrogate for the state-adjoint systems. We derive recursive a-posteriori estimates for the ROM feedback law and the ROM - MPC closed-loop state and show that the ROM - MPC trajectory evolves within a neighborhood of the true MPC trajectory, whose size can be explicitly computed and is controlled by the quality of the ROM . Such estimates are then used to formulate two ROM - MPC algorithms with closed-loop certification.



Keywords:
model predictive control, linear switched systems, model order reduction, proper orthogonal decomposition, optimal control

Introduction

We introduce a framework for
model predictive control
(
MPC
) for linear switched parabolic
partial differential equations
(
PDE
s) using Galerkin
model order reduction
(
MOR
) techniques. In more detail, we focus on the first-order optimality conditions for the resulting finite-horizon
optimal control problems
(
OCPs
) with a proper, lower-semicontinuous, convex regularization function, which can then be used for gradient-based optimization techniques. Since gradients need to be computed many times in the receding horizon problems, we employ
MOR
to obtain a low-dimensional surrogate for the optimality systems to mitigate the computational burden conspiring against real-time applicability. Our main goal is to certify the approximation error of the
ROM
-
MPC
feedback law and closed-loop trajectory.

1.2.
Literature-review and state-of-the-art

The research subject at hand combines different research areas, namely optimal control and
MPC
for switched systems,
MPC
and
MOR
, and
MOR
for switched systems. To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first contribution combining all three areas while emphasizing the infinite-dimensional setting. We thus provide a literature review for the individual topics.

1.2.1.
Optimal control and
MPC
for switched systems

The literature on
OCPs
for switched systems is extensive; for this reason, we mention only some references closer to our framework and refer the interested reader to the references therein. The work
[
40
]
uses a maximum principle to derive the necessary conditions for a general class of hybrid control systems using a Hamiltonian function. In

[
14
]
authors consider an autonomous system, where the switching sequence is given a priori, and the goal is to find the optimal switching times. The possibility of modifying the switching signal to achieve some optimality is certainly an interesting topic, but it is not our focus in this work. Similarly, in
[
53
]
, a predefined sequence of the active modes is given, and then the authors study the problem of determining optimal switching times and an optimal control input. Noteworthy, the authors decompose the problem into a two-stage procedure, where, in the first stage, the optimal control problem is solved for given switching times, and then, in the second step, the optimal switching times are determined. We envision that such an extension is also possible for our framework. In
[
10
]
, a nonlinear switched system is considered. The authors construct sub-optimal controls by embedding the switched system in an extended non-switched system, where the switching signal is also used for the controller design. It is shown that the trajectories of the switched system are dense in the extended non-switched system. Finally,
[
49
]
(see also the thesis
[
50
]
) studies the finite-horizon linear quadratic regulator (
LQR
) problem for a switched linear differential-algebraic equation (
DAE
) with a given switching signal. The resulting optimal control is a feedback controller that can be computed as the solution of a Riccati differential equation. We highlight that all the works mentioned treat the control problem only from a finite-dimensional perspective. For the infinite-dimensional setting using a semigroup approach, we refer to
[
21
]
and the references therein. Again, the main focus in the context of optimal control is the derivation of optimal switching signals, and the optimal control computation is not considered. We exemplarily mention here
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
, where optimal switching is studied for an efficient simulation of gas flow in a pipe network using a model hierarchy. For future work, we want to combine these ideas with the two-stage approach of
[
53
]
and our certified
MOR
-
MPC
framework presented in this paper.

MPC
is an optimization-based control technique with a feedback mechanism for linear and nonlinear systems
[
17
]
, where a sequence of finite-horizon control problems substitutes the infinite-horizon optimal control problem over receding time intervals. We refer to
stabilizing

MPC
whenever the control objective is to drive the system (possibly unstable) to a predefined desired trajectory by penalizing deviations from the reference using a stage cost function, which is positive definite w.r.t. the reference. In contrast, in
economic

MPC
, one is interested in operating a system at a trajectory with minimal cost. Here, the cost function can model all kinds of quantities and does not need to be positive definite w.r.t to a given target. The stability of the closed-loop trajectory can be ensured by introducing terminal costs and terminal constraints, or by choosing a sufficiently large prediction horizon
[
17
,
33
,
5
]
. In the case of
MPC
specifically focusing on switched systems, we mention
[
57
,
39
,
55
,
56
]
and references therein.

1.2.2.
MPC
and
MOR

Since
MPC
represents a multi-query context for finite-horizon optimization problems, employing
ROM
techniques to accelerate the process is natural. We focus on Galerkin-
ROMs
, i.e.,
ROMs
obtained by projecting the dynamical system onto low-dimensional linear subspaces. Naturally, this leads to an interest in deriving conditions under which the
ROM
-
MPC
feedback law and the
ROM
-
MPC
closed-loop state exhibit correct behavior despite approximation errors.
The work
[
29
]
treats continuous-time, general stabilizable linear systems coupled with general projection-based
MOR
with control and state constraints. Stabilization is achieved by exploiting the feedback of the
MOR
error. This feedback, combined with tightened terminal constraints and terminal cost functions, ensures the stability of the
MPC
closed-loop.

[
11
]
provides a stabilizing
MPC
framework using the reduced basis method for parametrized linear-quadratic control problems with control constraints. The authors propose an algorithm that adaptively searches for the minimal stabilizing prediction horizon and estimates the suboptimality degree of the
ROM
-
MPC
feedback.
The
ROM
feedback stabilizes the original problem, where stability is guaranteed by extending the prediction horizon in the
MPC
problem. The theory is based on the
MPC
book
[
17
]
and employs a reduced suboptimality degree for stabilizing
MPC
in a discrete-time framework. The reduced suboptimality degree is based on a posteriori error bounds of the value function, optimal control, and optimal state of the finite-horizon linear quadratic subproblem. We emphasize that the bounds for the state and the adjoint we derive here are similar to those in
[
11
]
but adapted to our switched setting. In contrast, we are not interested in parametrized systems, but the control is the only parameter. We extend the estimates for linear-quadratic
OCPs
derived in
[
46
,
1
]
to the case of general convex, lower-semicontinuous regularization functions and perturbed initial values. This enables us to formulate estimates for the closed-loop state and the
ROM
feedback law, which account for the error from previous
MPC
iterations and are applicable in stabilizing and economic
MPC
settings. Another unique aspect of our optimal control error estimators is that they are expressed in terms of output bounds and can be linked to output bounds derived from
balanced truncation
(
BT
) model reduction.
In
[
30
]
, discrete-time linear systems with quadratic cost functional and control-state constraints are treated. The authors provide theoretical performance guarantees, computational efficiency, and output feedback for general projection-based
ROMs
. The work
[
16
]
concerns economic
MPC
using
POD
. A performance index for economic
MPC
is developed and used to evaluate the performance of the
MPC
controller online. However, the performance index is directly applied to the
ROM
system and does not quantify the error with respect to the
FOM
system. Economic
MPC
with state constraints on a time-varying parabolic equation is studied in
[
3
]
; the
POD
model is updated dynamically using both a-posteriori and a-priori error estimates, but there is no error estimate for the closed-loop state given. The work
[
2
]
introduces a stabilizing
MPC
framework in continuous time for semilinear parabolic
PDEs
without terminal constraints. A sufficient condition for stability of the
POD
feedback is given based on a priori analysis, and it is proven that the
ROM
feedback stabilizes the
ROM
but not the
FOM
.

1.2.3.
MOR
for switched systems

Several works have appeared in recent years addressing the topic of
MOR
for switched systems, such that we restrict our literature review to some selected references. In
[
52
]
, a method that uses a set of coupled linear matrix inequalities is proposed, which becomes infeasible in a large-scale context. Nevertheless, the matrix inequalities are important to guarantee quadratic stability of the reduced system and derive an error bound
[
37
]
. The works
[
15
,
45
]
solve a set of coupled Lyapunov equations to compute Gramians, which can then be used for a balancing-based model reduction. Unfortunately, there is no guarantee that the set of coupled Lyapunov equations is solvable
[
26
]
, and is not suitable for large-scale settings. In
[
44
]
, the switched system is recast as a linear system such that standard methods can be applied, while in
[
38
]
, the system is recast as a bilinear system, and a balanced truncation approach for such systems is employed. See also
[
31
]
for large-scale computation using the bilinear formulation. We emphasize that both methods provide an a priori error certification for the approximated input-output map and do not require a priori knowledge of the switching signal. This is crucial to develop a certified
MOR
-
MPC
framework. In
[
8
]
, interpolation-based techniques to construct a
ROM
for a switched system are discussed. The authors of
[
36
]
discretize the control variable to obtain a switched system of autonomous
PDEs
that they approximate with
ROMs
in an
MPC
framework. Finally, we refer to
[
24
]
for
MOR
of switched systems with state jumps and to
[
23
]
, where a similar technique is applied in the context of switched
DAE
s. The drawbacks of this approach are two: the switching sequence needs to be known a priori, and the method is not presented to deal with large-scale systems. Recent works
[
31
,
32
]
overcome these issues, presenting a
MOR
framework for large-scale systems that do not require a priori knowledge of the switching sequence.

Structure of the paper

We present the
MPC
problem in
Section

and discuss the well-posedness of the forward problem and the optimal control problem for which we also derive the first-order optimality conditions. In

For a given switching signal

(Energy norms)

(i)

(ii)

the energy norm

.

We write

if the space
W
is equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Similarly, for positive constants
\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{N-1}
,

, and

for some

, we define the time-dependent energy norm

for

where we used (

Remark 3.8
.

If there is no perturbation in the initial value, i.e.,
\Delta_{t_{n}}=0
, then the bound
\Delta_{A}
simplifies to

(3.28)

\displaystyle\|u_{n}^{\star}-\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{A}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star},0)=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\tilde{p}^{\star}-{\hat{p}})\|_{{\mathscr{U}}_{n}}

and can thus be interpreted as a perturbation bound (see, e.g.,
[
46
]
) with the perturbation
\xi=\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\tilde{p}^{\star}-{\hat{p}})
. However, our bounds hold for arbitrary
g_{n}
satisfying
Assumption

2.8
, while in the literature only the special case
g_{n}=\mathds{1}_{{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
has been considered so far. In the case
g_{n}\equiv 0
, the bound in (
3.28
) and the bound from
[
46
]
coincide. To see this, we add
\pm\lambda(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}-u_{\mathrm{d}})
in (
3.15
) and use the unconstrained optimality condition for the
ROM
-
MPC
subproblem (
3.3
), i.e.,

\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{n}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})=\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}^{\prime}\tilde{p}^{\star}+\lambda(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}-u_{\mathrm{d}})=0,

to obtain

\displaystyle\|u_{n}^{\star}-\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{A}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star},0)=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\tilde{p}^{\star}-{\hat{p}})\|_{{\mathscr{U}}_{n}}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\|\nabla\mathcal{J}_{n}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\|_{{\mathscr{U}}_{n}}.

Note that this bound is a standard residual-based coercivity bound (cf.
[
35
]
) for the unconstrained first-order optimality condition (
2.15
). The bilinear form represents the Hessian of
{\mathcal{J}}_{n}
, which possesses the coercivity constant
\lambda>0
, and the gradient
\nabla{\mathcal{J}}_{n}
acts as the residual operator, incorporating the reduced control
\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}
. In that sense, (
3.28
) extends the standard coercivity residual bound.

3.3.
A-posteriori error estimates for the state and adjoint

After the reduced control
\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}
is computed, the error bound
\Delta_{A}
in (
3.15
) (or
\Delta_{B}
) can be evaluated by solving the
FOM
equations for
{\hat{p}},\check{\theta}
(or for
\check{p},\check{\theta}
). Since the direct evaluation of these bounds depends on
FOM
calculations, we will call them
expensive-to-evaluate bounds
. These bounds may be suitable for certain scenarios, such as a single optimization of the open-loop problem. However, it can become costly in a multi-query context for open-loop problems, like
MPC
(see
Table

3
).

Therefore, in this subsection, we aim to derive an upper bound whose computation is based solely on the
ROM
level (see
Corollary

3.12
). We will refer to these estimates as
cheap-to-evaluate bounds
. Specifically, we focus on determining an upper bound for
\Delta_{B}
, noting that a similar approach can be applied to
\Delta_{A}
.
One way to achieve this is by incorporating output bounds for the primal and dual outputs, such as those derived from balanced truncation model reduction. Alternatively, one can estimate using the continuity constants of the output operators and introduce residual-based error estimates for the state and adjoint equations, which will be discussed in the next two results.
In the specific case of the switched system (
2.2
), we must account for the contributions arising from switching within the bilinear form
m_{\sigma}
. The proof involves iteratively estimating across the switching intervals, progressing from left to right (or vice versa for the adjoint), as the residual generated by the initial (or terminal) condition is known. Along the way, it is necessary to consider the jumps in the bilinear form
m_{\sigma}
, characterized by the constants
c_{i,j}
defined in (
3.5
).

Lemma 3.9

(A-posteriori estimate for the intermediate state)
.

Let
Assumptions

2.1
and
3.3
be satisfied. Let
\check{\theta}=\mathcal{S}(\check{\theta}_{n},\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\in{\mathscr{X}}_{n}
and
\tilde{\theta}^{\star}=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(\tilde{\theta}_{n},\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\in\tilde{{\mathscr{X}}}_{n}
be given.
Define the intermediate reduction error as
e\vcentcolon=\check{\theta}-\tilde{\theta}^{\star}
and the residual
R\colon\mathbb{T}_{n}\to V^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle R(t)\vcentcolon=\mathcal{B}_{\sigma(t)}\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}(t)-\mathcal{M}_{\sigma(t)}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\tilde{\theta}}(t)-\mathcal{A}_{\sigma(t)}\tilde{\theta}(t).

Then, for any
\tilde{N}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have

(3.29)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|e(t_{n,\tilde{N}})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{\tilde{N}})}}^{2}&+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\tilde{N}-1}\omega_{\tilde{N},i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|e(t)\|_{a_{\sigma(t)}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\omega_{\tilde{N},0}\|e(t_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma(t_{n})}}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\tilde{N}-1}\omega_{\tilde{N},i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|R(t)\|_{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\end{aligned}

with
\omega_{\tilde{N},i}=\prod_{k=i+1}^{\tilde{N}-1}c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,k}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,k})}\quad\text{for }i=0,\ldots,\tilde{N}-1
,
where
c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,k}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,k})}
is defined in (
3.5
). Note that
\omega_{\tilde{N}}=(\omega_{\tilde{N},i})_{i=0}^{\tilde{N}-1}
depends on
n
, but we have omitted the subscript for notational convenience. In particular, for
\tilde{N}=N
we define
\omega\coloneqq\omega_{N}
and (
3.29
) simplifies to

(3.30)

\displaystyle\|e(T_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma(T_{n})}}^{2}+\|e\|_{a_{\sigma},\omega}^{2}\leq\Delta^{2}_{\theta}(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\vcentcolon=\omega_{0}\|e(t_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma(t_{n})}}^{2}+\|R\|_{a_{\sigma}^{\prime},\omega}^{2},

with the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{a_{\sigma}^{\prime},\omega}
introduced in (
3.4
).

Proof.

Due to linearity, the error satisfies for
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}

\displaystyle\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} m_{\sigma(t)}(\dot{e}(t),\varphi)+a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),\varphi)&=\langle R(t),\varphi\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},&&\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\mathbb{T}_{n}$},\\
e(t_{n})&=\check{\theta}_{n}-\tilde{\theta}_{n},\end{aligned}\right.

for all
\varphi\in V
. Note that due to the switching in
m_{\sigma}
, the variables
\theta
,
\tilde{\theta}
, and
e
do not admit a weak derivative in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n},V^{\prime})
. Therefore, we have to estimate the error on each switching interval separately and concatenate the results later on. For almost all
t\in(t_{n,i},t_{n,i+1})
and for any
i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, testing against
e(t)\in V
results in

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}(e(t),e(t))+a_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}(e(t),e(t))\;=&\;m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}(\dot{e}(t),e(t))+a_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}(e(t),e(t))\\
=&\;\langle R(t),e(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}.\end{aligned}

Integrating from
t_{n,i}
to
t_{n,i+1}
leads to

\tfrac{1}{2}\|e(t_{n,i+1})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i+1})}}^{2}+\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\langle R(t),e(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\,\mathrm{d}t+\tfrac{1}{2}\|e(t_{n,i})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}}^{2}.

Next we apply Cauchy-Schwarz and the weighted Young’s inequalities to obtain

(3.31)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|e(t_{n,i+1})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i+1})}}^{2}+&\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|R(t)\|^{2}_{{a_{\sigma(t)}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|e(t_{n,i})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}}^{2}.\end{aligned}

Now we prove (
3.29
) using the induction principle. For
\tilde{N}=1
, we have from (
3.31
) and
i=0

\displaystyle\|e(t_{n,1})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,1})}}^{2}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n,1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n,1}}\|R(t)\|^{2}_{{{a_{\sigma(t)}}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|e(t_{n,0})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,0})}}^{2},

which is (
3.29
) for
\tilde{N}=1
since
\omega_{1,0}=1
.
Now let (
3.29
) be valid for
\tilde{N}=j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we need to show that it holds for
\tilde{N}=j+1
. From (
3.31
) for
i=j
we have

(3.32)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|e(t^{-}_{n,j+1})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j+1})}}^{2}+&\int_{t_{n,j}}^{t_{n,j+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\int_{t_{n,j}}^{t_{n,j+1}}\|R(t)\|_{{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|e(t_{n,j})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j})}}^{2}.\end{aligned}

From (
3.5
) we have
\|e(t_{n,j})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j})}}^{2}\leq c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}\|e(t_{n,j})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}^{2}
. Therefore, using this last relation, we insert (
3.29
) into (
3.32
) for
\tilde{N}=j
so that we get

(3.33)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|e(t^{-}_{n,j+1})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j+1})}}^{2}+\int_{t_{n,j}}^{t_{n,j+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\int_{t_{n,j}}^{t_{n,j+1}}\|R(t)\|^{2}_{a_{\sigma(t)}^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}t-c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{j-1}\omega_{j,i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(e(t),e(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
+&c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{j-1}c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}\|e(t_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma(t_{n})}}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{j-1}\omega_{j,i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|R(t)\|_{{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).\end{aligned}

Then, reordering the terms in (

) and using
\omega_{j+1,j}=1
,
c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}\omega_{j,i}=\omega_{j+1,i}
, we obtain (
3.29
) for
\tilde{N}=j+1
. Finally, (
3.30
) directly follows from (
3.29
) for
\tilde{N}=N
using the norms introduced in
Definition

3.4
. ∎

Lemma 3.11

(A-posteriori error estimate for the intermediate adjoint state)
.

Let
Assumptions

2.1
and
3.3
be satisfied. Let
\check{p}=\mathcal{A}(\tilde{y}^{\star})\in{\mathscr{X}}_{n,\sigma}
and
\tilde{p}^{\star}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{y}^{\star})\in\tilde{{\mathscr{X}}}_{n,\sigma}

and let us define the error
\epsilon\vcentcolon=\check{p}-\tilde{p}^{\star}
and the residuals:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Q_{T_{n}}\;\vcentcolon=&\;-\mathcal{M}_{\sigma(T_{n})}\tilde{p}^{\star}(T_{n})+\mu\mathcal{C}_{\sigma(T_{n})}^{\prime}(\tilde{y}^{\star}(T_{n})-y_{T}),\\
Q_{t_{n,i}}\;\vcentcolon=&\;-\mathcal{M}_{\sigma(t_{n,i}^{-})}\tilde{p}^{\star}(t_{n,i}^{-})+\mathcal{M}_{\sigma(t_{n,i}^{+})}\tilde{p}^{\star}(t_{n,i}^{+})\quad\text{for all }t_{n,i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\\

Then, for any
\tilde{N}\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, we have

(3.34)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\epsilon(t_{n,\tilde{N}}^{+})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,\tilde{N}})}}^{2}+&\sum_{i=\tilde{N}}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{\tilde{N},i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|\epsilon(t)\|^{2}_{a_{\sigma(t)}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\;2\sum_{i=\tilde{N}}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{\tilde{N},i}\|Q_{t_{n,i+1}}\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}}+\sum_{i=\tilde{N}}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{\tilde{N},i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|Q(t)\|_{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{aligned}

for
\tilde{\omega}_{\tilde{N},i}\vcentcolon=\prod\limits_{k=\tilde{N}+1}^{i}2c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,k}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,k})}
for
i=\tilde{N},\ldots,N-1
.
In particular, for
\tilde{N}=0
we define
\tilde{\omega}\coloneqq\tilde{\omega}_{0}=(\tilde{\omega}_{0,i})_{i=\tilde{N}}^{N-1}
and (
3.34
) reads as

(3.35)

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(t_{n,0}^{+})\|_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,0})}}^{2}+

\displaystyle\|\epsilon\|_{a_{\sigma},\tilde{\omega}}^{2}\leq\Delta^{2}_{p}(\tilde{y}^{\star})\;\vcentcolon=\;2\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{i}\|Q_{t_{n,i+1}}\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i+1})}}^{2}+\|Q\|_{a_{\sigma}^{\prime},\tilde{\omega}}^{2}.

Proof.

We proceed similarly as in the proof of
Lemma

3.9
. The error equation for
\epsilon
is given for all
\varphi\in V
and almost all
t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
as

\displaystyle\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} -m_{\sigma(t)}(\varphi,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\epsilon(t))+a_{\sigma(t)}(\varphi,\epsilon(t))&=\langle Q(t),\varphi\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},&\\
m_{\sigma(T_{n})}(\epsilon(T_{n}),\varphi)&=\langle Q_{T_{n}},\varphi\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},&\\
m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}(\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{-}),\varphi)&=\langle Q_{t_{n,i}},\varphi\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+m_{\sigma(t_{n,i}^{+})}(\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{+}),\varphi),&\forall t_{n,i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain for the terminal condition

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(T_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma}(T_{n})}^{2}\;=\;m_{\sigma(T_{n})}(\epsilon(T_{n}),\epsilon(T_{n}))\leq\|Q_{T_{n}}\|_{m_{\sigma}(T_{n})}\|\epsilon(T_{n})\|_{m_{\sigma}(T_{n})}.

For the switching points
t_{n,i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we get by testing with
\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{-})

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{-})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}\;\leq\;\|Q_{t_{n,i}}\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}+\sqrt{c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}\,\|\epsilon({t^{+}_{n,i}})\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}},

which implies, by squaring and then using Young’s inequality

(3.36)

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}\;\leq\;2\,\|Q_{t_{n,i}}\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}+2{c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i})}}\|\epsilon({t^{+}_{n,i}})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,i})}}.

We now test the error system with
\epsilon(t)\in V
for almost all
t\in(t_{n,i},t_{n,i+1})
and
i=0,\ldots,N-1
, to obtain

-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))+a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\;=\;\langle Q(t),\epsilon(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}.

Integration from
t_{n,i}
to
t_{n,i+1}
leads to

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\|\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,i}^{+})}}+\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\;=\;\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\langle Q(t),\epsilon(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\,\mathrm{d}t+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\epsilon(t_{n,i+1}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,i+1}^{-})}}

and by Cauchy-Schwarz in combination with Young’s inequality, we derive

(3.37)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\epsilon(t_{n,i}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,i}^{+})}}\;+&\;\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\;\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|\epsilon(t_{n,i+1}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,i+1}^{-})}}.\end{aligned}

Next, we prove (
3.34
) by induction and start with the case
\tilde{N}=N-1
. We obtain from (
3.37
) for
i=N-1

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(t_{n,N-1}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,N-1}^{+})}}\;+

\displaystyle\;\int_{t_{n,N-1}}^{t_{n,N}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\;\int_{t_{n,N-1}}^{t_{n,N}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|\epsilon(t_{n,N}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,N})}}.

By definition
\|\epsilon(t_{n,N}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,N})}}=\|\epsilon(T_{n})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma({T_{n}})}}
and therefore

\displaystyle\|\epsilon(t_{n,N-1}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,N-1}^{+})}}+\int_{t_{n,N-1}}^{t_{n,N}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{n,N-1}}^{t_{n,N}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{{a^{\prime}_{{\sigma(t)}}}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\|Q_{T_{n}}\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma({T_{n}})}},

which is (
3.34
) for
\tilde{N}=N-1
.
Assume (
3.34
) to be valid for
\tilde{N}=j
with
1\leq j\leq N-1
. We need to show that it holds for
\tilde{N}=j-1
. Choosing
i=j-1
in (

) gives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\epsilon(t_{n,j-1}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,j-1}^{+})}}+\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\;\leq\;\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{a^{\prime}_{\sigma(t)}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|\epsilon(t_{n,j}^{-})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}.\end{aligned}

Combining this with (

) for

we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|\epsilon(t_{n,j-1}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,j-1}^{+})}}\;+\;\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\leq&\;\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{{a^{\prime}_{{\sigma(t)}}}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+2\|Q_{t_{n,j}}\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}+2{c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}\|\epsilon(t_{n,j}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j})}}.\end{aligned}

Finally, using the induction hypothesis to bound

results in

(3.38)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\epsilon(t_{n,j-1}^{+})\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t_{n,j-1}^{+})}}\;+&\;\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}a_{\sigma(t)}(\epsilon(t),\epsilon(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\;\leq\;\int_{t_{n,j-1}}^{t_{n,j}}\|Q(t)\|^{2}_{{{a^{\prime}_{{\sigma(t)}}}}}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
+&\;2\,\|Q_{t_{n,j}}\|^{2}_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}+\;2{c_{\sigma(t^{+}_{n,j}),\sigma(t^{-}_{n,j})}}\bigg(2\sum\limits_{i=j}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{j,i}\|Q_{t_{n,i+1}}\|_{m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{n,i+1})}}^{2}\\
+&\;\sum\limits_{i=j}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{j,i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|Q(t)\|_{{{a^{\prime}_{{\sigma(t)}}}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t-\sum\limits_{i=j}^{N-1}\tilde{\omega}_{j,i}\int_{t_{n,i}}^{t_{n,i+1}}\|\epsilon(t)\|_{a_{\sigma(t)}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg).\end{aligned}

Reordering (

) gives (
3.34
) for
\tilde{N}=j-1
.

3.4.
Certification of the reduced
MPC
control

To utilize our certificates within the
MPC
algorithm, we proceed as follows. To derive the
ROM
-
MPC
feedback law in the
n
-th
MPC
iteration, we solve the
ROM
-
MPC
subproblem (
2.3
) to find the optimal control
\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}
. The certification framework outlined in the previous subsection can then be applied to assess the error in the reduced
MPC
schemes through the following two ways detailed here:

(i)

At each
MPC
iteration
n
, we can compute an a-posteriori bound on the error between the
FOM
-
MPC
trajectory
\theta_{\mathsf{mpc}}
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}
and its reduced counterpart. This approach ensures that the
ROM
-
MPC
trajectories remain within a bounded region around the
FOM
-
MPC
trajectory, with a radius of
\Delta_{t_{n+1}}
, where the quality of the
ROM
determines the size of this region. This concept is detailed in
Corollary

3.14
.

(ii)

The computation of
\Delta_{t_{n}}
inherently includes the calculation of the control error estimator
\Delta(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star},0)
, which measures how accurately the
ROM
approximates the
FOM
feedback law in the current
ROM
state. Controlling this error asymptotically results in an exact approximation; see
Lemma

3.15
.

If, during the
n
-th
MPC
iteration, the
ROM
-
MPC
trajectory deviates too far from the
FOM
-
MPC
trajectory or the current
FOM
-
MPC
feedback law is poorly approximated, in the sense that the error estimators
\Delta_{t_{n+1}}
or
\Delta(\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star},0)
exceed user-defined thresholds
\varepsilon_{n},\epsilon_{n}>0
, then the reduced feedback law
\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}
is rejected. In such cases, a
FOM
-
MPC
step is performed instead, the
ROM
is refined, and the procedure is restarted. This approach provides certification with respect to the last
FOM
-
MPC
step. The method for updating the
ROM
depends on the chosen
MOR
technique and is discussed in the following subsection for a
POD
-
ROM
(see
Remark

4.2
).
On the other hand, if the error estimator confirms that the
ROM
approximation is sufficiently accurate, the reduced optimal control
\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star}
is accepted. Subsequently, the
ROM
control is applied either to the
FOM
system (
2.2
) or the
ROM
system (
3.1
). These resulting approaches are referred to as
FOM
-
ROM
-
MPC
or
ROM
-
ROM
-
MPC
, respectively, and the details are summarized in
Algorithms

2
and
3
.

(i)

For the
FOM
-
ROM
-
MPC
approach (
Algorithm

2
), the controller is designed using the
ROM
but applied to the
FOM
, resulting in the following
FOM
-
ROM
-
MPC
iteration:

(3.44)

\check{\theta}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(0)=\theta_{0},\quad\check{\theta}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(t)=\big[\mathcal{S}(\check{\theta}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(t_{n}),\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\big](t)\quad\text{for }t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1}].

The certification of this algorithm can be entirely based on
ROM
methods that provide output bounds for the primal and dual input-output relationships (see
Theorem

3.7
and (
3.47
)). This approach can be particularly advantageous if the Kolmogorov
n
-widths or the Hankel singular values corresponding to the input-to-state map (see
[
25
,
48
]
) exhibit slow decay, while those of the input-to-output map decrease rapidly.

(ii)

Conversely, the
ROM
-
ROM
-
MPC
approach (
Algorithm

3
) designs and applies the controller directly at the
ROM
level, yielding the
ROM
-
ROM
-
MPC

(3.45)

{\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(0)=\Pi_{\tilde{V}}\theta_{0},\quad{\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(t)=\big[\tilde{\mathcal{S}}({\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathsf{mpc}}(t_{n}),\tilde{u}_{n}^{\star})\big](t)\quad\text{for }t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1}].

Unlike (

), this algorithm is entirely online-efficient, as it remains independent of the

dimension after the

is constructed
(see also the complexity analysis of both algorithms in

)

4.1.
Reduced model via proper orthogonal decomposition

The main idea of
POD
is to extract the most relevant information of the available data in a weighted
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n},V)
sense. Let snapshot data
\mathsf{s}_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n},V)
for
j=1,\ldots,M
be available (see
Remark

4.2
for possible choices).

POD
chooses basis functions
\bar{\psi}_{i}\in V
for
i=1,\ldots,r
solving the optimization problem

(4.1)

\displaystyle\min_{\{\psi_{i}\}\subseteq V}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}\bigg\|\mathsf{s}_{j}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\langle\mathsf{s}_{j}(t),\psi_{i}\rangle_{V}\,\psi_{i}\bigg\|^{2}_{V}\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{V}=\delta_{ij}\text{ for }i,j=1,\ldots,r,

where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.

Theorem 4.1

(​​
[
18
, Thm. 2.7 and Lem. 2.2]
)
.

The optimization problem (
4.1
) is solvable and a solution is given by the
r
eigenvectors
\{\bar{\psi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}\subset V
corresponding to the largest nonzero eigenvalues
\bar{\lambda}_{1}\geq\bar{\lambda}_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\bar{\lambda}_{r}>0
of the linear, compact, nonnegative, and self-adjoint operator

\mathcal{R}\colon V\to V,\qquad\psi\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{M}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}\langle\mathsf{s}_{j}(t),\psi\rangle_{V}\mathsf{s}_{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

The question that remains to be answered is the choice of
r
. It is common, see, e.g.,
[
18
]
, to use the information content of the basis about the whole snapshot set. In more detail, we are interested in the quantity

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(r)\vcentcolon=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\bar{\lambda}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\bar{\lambda}_{i}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\bar{\lambda}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|\mathsf{s}_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n},V)}^{2}},

where the identity is due to the Hilbert-Schmidt theorem that ensures the set of eigenfunctions forms an orthonormal basis of
V
.
Relation (
4.2
) can be used to determine a basis size
r
containing
e\%
of the snapshot information by choosing
r
with
\mathcal{E}(r)\approx{e}/{100}
. The associated
POD
subspace is then given as
\tilde{V}=\spann\{\bar{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}_{r}\}
.

Remark 4.2
.

(i)

We follow
[
12
]
for the numerical solution of (
4.1
).

(ii)

The
ROM
model update process in
Algorithms

2
and
3
for
POD
works as follows. Starting with an empty model (assuming that the update mechanism is triggered), the
POD
model update involves solving the current
FOM
-
MPC
subproblem (
2.3
), appending the optimal state and adjoint state to the set of snapshots, performing
POD
by solving (
4.1
), and selecting the reduced basis according to (
4.2
). A residual-based error estimator
\tilde{\Delta}_{B}
can then be constructed in an offline-online manner by computing the
V_{a_{\sigma}}
-Rietz representatives of the affine components of the state and adjoint residuals for
r
basis elements and each switching mode
L
(see
[
22
, Sec. 4.2.5]
). Recomputing (
4.1
) at every step may not always be necessary, as incremental

methods (see
[
13
]
) offer efficient alternatives.

(iii)

Our approach allows for incorporating other snapshot types, such as those associated with the control target
u_{\mathrm{d}}
or derivative information between switching points, ensuring that the

adapts dynamically along the

trajectory. To address outdated data in the snapshot set, a practical strategy is to retain only the most recent snapshots.

Conclusion

We presented an efficient framework for model predictive control of linear switched evolution equations based on Galerkin reduced-order modeling techniques. First, we provided optimality conditions for the
MPC
subproblems that allow box constraints and
L^{1}
regularization. We show that, due to switching in the operator in front of the time derivative, jumps at the switching times arise for the adjoint state. Based on these optimality conditions, we constructed a posteriori error estimates for the reduced
MPC
feedback law and the closed-loop state. These estimates show that the
ROM
-
MPC
trajectory evolves within a neighborhood of the true
MPC
trajectory, whose size is controlled by the quality of the
ROM
. Further, these estimates allow us to perform the
MPC
optimization procedure with a computational cost that, up to a few model updates through
FOM
-
MPC
subproblems resolution, is independent of the size of the
FOM
. Finally, we validate the theoretical findings with numerical experiments, emphasizing the contrast between expensive-to-evaluate estimates and their inexpensive-to-evaluate upper bounds.

In future work, we plan to integrate our error estimation procedure with balanced truncation approaches by incorporating a priori output error bounds. This should allow us to have sharper, inexpensive error estimates. We also want to extend our techniques with the concepts presented in
[
16
,
11
]
to directly assess the
MPC
performance in the economic and stabilizing setting. Finally, the
ROM
and
MPC
frameworks are well-suited to be coupled with contour integral methods (
CIM
)
[
19
,
20
]
for efficient and accurate time integration; our goal is to efficiently combine the
MPC
optimization with the
CIM
formulation for
ROM
.

Appendix A
Properties of the switched inner product

Lemma A.1

(Integration by parts)
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}
and
\theta,p\in{\mathscr{X}}_{n,\sigma}
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the set of switching times as in
Assumption

2.1

(i)
and assume that the bilinear forms
m_{i}
satisfy
Assumption

2.1

(iii)
. Then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}m_{\sigma}(\dot{\theta},p)\,\mathrm{d}t&=m_{\sigma(T_{n})}(\theta(T_{n}),p(T_{n}))-m_{\sigma(t_{n})}(\theta(t_{n}),p(t_{n}))\\
&+\sum_{t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\left(m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{i})}(\theta(t^{-}_{i}),p(t^{-}_{i}))-m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{i})}(\theta(t_{i}^{+}),p(t^{+}_{i}))\right)-\int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}m_{\sigma}(\dot{p},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{aligned}

Proof.

First, observe that if
m_{\sigma}
and the adjoint state are continuous, the sum in (
A.1
) would be zero, thus reducing the statement to standard integration by parts. Splitting
\mathbb{T}_{n}
into its sub-intervals with constant mode and applying integration by parts on each sub-interval, we can write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}m_{\sigma}(\dot{\theta},p)\,\mathrm{d}t\;=&\;\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}m_{\sigma}(\dot{\theta},p)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&\;\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\big(m_{\sigma(t^{-}_{i+1})}(p(t^{-}_{i+1}),\theta(t_{i+1}^{-}))-m_{\sigma(t^{+}_{i})}(p(t^{+}_{i}),\theta(t_{i}^{+}))\big)-\int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}}m_{\sigma}(\dot{p},\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t\end{aligned}

The claim follows by rearranging the sum and from the fact that
t_{n}
and
T_{n}
have no switching points (see
Assumption

2.1

(i)
).

Appendix B
Complexity analysis of the
MPC
-schemes

In this section, we compare the online complexity of the proposed
MPC
-schemes (
FOM
-
MPC
(
Algorithm

1
),
FOM
-
ROM
-
MPC
(
Algorithm

2
), and
ROM
-
ROM
-
MPC
(
Algorithm

3
) per
MPC
iteration
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which we denote by
C_{\textsf{FOM}},C_{\textsf{FOM}-\textsf{ROM}},C_{\textsf{ROM}-\textsf{ROM}}
, respectively. We restrict ourselves to evaluating the complexity as a function of (stationary)
FOM
linear system solves, which is denoted by
LS
, since the number of
LS
(and the computation of the
POD
, which is counted separately) is the most expensive part in each algorithm. Let
K_{\delta},K_{T}\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of discrete time steps corresponding to the sampling time
\delta
and prediction horizon
T
, respectively.

The computational cost of the
FOM
-
MPC
per
MPC
-step
n\in\mathbb{N}
consists of solving the
FOM
-
OCP
, which in turn consists (in the setting described in
Section

4.2
) of applying the proximal gradient method leading to one gradient evaluation
\nabla F_{n}(u_{k})
for the iterate
u_{k}
per inner iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consisting of the simulation of the state and the adjoint state over
K_{T}
steps (see (
2.14g
)). Assuming
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
proximal gradient steps until convergence, we obtain

(B.1)

C_{\textsf{FOM}}=k^{*}2K_{T}\textsf{LS}.

Before we consider the
ROM
schemes, we consider the cost of updating the
ROM
in
10
, which we call
C_{\textsf{update}}
. Updating the
POD
model consists of computing the
POD
basis in (
4.1
), which is done using singular value decomposition (
SVD
). Applying the economic SVD on a snapshots matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K_{s}}
for some the
FOM
dimension
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
K_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
has a cost of
C_{\textsf{POD}}\approx\mathcal{O}(NK_{s}^{2})
if
N\geq K_{s}
, which is satisfied, since in the
MPC
setting it holds
K_{s}=m_{s}K_{T}
, where
K_{T}
is small and
m_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
is also a small number indicating the number of snapshots of length
K_{T}
used (in the setting of
Section

4.2
we have
m_{s}\leq 14
,
K_{T}=20
, and
N=5304
). If the cheap error estimators
\tilde{\Delta}_{A},\tilde{\Delta}_{B}
are considered, an offline-online decomposition of the error estimator (see
[
22
]
) is considered, which depends on
LS
(for computing the Rietz representatives per affine component of the residual) that scale with the dimension
r\in\mathbb{N}
of the reduced space
\tilde{V}
, the number of switching modes
L
and the input and output dimensions
\rho,p\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, for the offline assembly of the state error estimator, we have
L\rho\textsf{LS}
for the operator
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
, and
2rL\textsf{LS}
for the operators
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
(see (
2.4
)). For the offline assembly of the adjoint error estimator, we have
Lp\textsf{LS}
for the operator
\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
, and
rL\textsf{LS}
for
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\sigma}
. If the expensive error estimators
\Delta_{A},\Delta_{B}
are considered, no offline-online decomposition is performed, leading to

(B.2)

C_{\textsf{update}}=\begin{cases}C_{\textsf{POD}}&\text{if }\Delta_{u}\in\{\Delta_{A},\Delta_{B}\},\\
C_{\textsf{POD}}+L(\rho+p+3r)\textsf{LS}&\text{if }\Delta_{u}\in\{\tilde{\Delta}_{A},\tilde{\Delta}_{B}\}.\end{cases}

Note that, for simplicity, we neglect the cost of constructing the
ROM
via projection onto
\tilde{V}
. After the model is constructed, the online cost for evaluating the error estimator is given as

(B.3)

C_{\textsf{estimate}}=\begin{cases}2K_{T}\textsf{LS}&\text{if }\Delta_{u}\in\{\Delta_{A},\Delta_{B}\},\\
0&\text{if }\Delta_{u}\in\{\tilde{\Delta}_{A},\tilde{\Delta}_{B}\}.\end{cases}

Thus, (
B.2
) shows that using the cheap estimators has a higher model update cost, while ensuring a cheaper evaluation after construction of the
ROM
in (
B.3
).

Next, turning to the
ROM
-
MPC
schemes, we have to distinguish whether the update criterion in
7
of both algorithms is triggered or not. First, we start with the
FOM
-
ROM
-
MPC
and assume that the
7
is not active. Using the cheap error estimators (
\tilde{\Delta}_{A},\tilde{\Delta}_{B}
),
C_{\textsf{FOM}-\textsf{ROM}}
consists of applying the reduced controller onto the
FOM
system in
12
(see also (
3.44
)) for
K_{\delta}
time steps, leading to
C_{\textsf{FOM}-\textsf{ROM}}=C_{\textsf{estimate}}+K_{\delta}\textsf{LS}=K_{\delta}\textsf{LS}
(by using (
B.3
)). Conversely, using the expensive error estimators (
\Delta_{A},\Delta_{B}
) leads with (
B.3
) to a cost of
C_{\textsf{FOM}-\textsf{ROM}}=C_{\textsf{estimate}}=2K_{T}\textsf{LS}
. In this case, there is no explicit cost for applying the reduced controller onto the full system, since for the evaluation of the expensive error estimator, we already compute the new
FOM
state at time step
K_{\delta}
(see the discussion at the beginning of
Section

3.3
). If
7
is active, then we add up the cost of error estimation,
FOM
-
OCP
