Multi-clocked Guarded Recursion Beyond ω \omega
Rasmus Ejlers Møgelberg

Abstract
Type theories with multi-clocked guarded recursion provide a flexible framework for programming with coinductive types encoding productivity in types. Combining this with solutions to general guarded domain equations one can also construct relatively simple denotational models of programming languages with advanced features. These constructions have previously been explored in the setting of extensional type theory through a presheaf model, which proves correctness of encodings of W-types. That model has been adapted to presheaves of cubical sets (functors into the category of cubical sets), where the model verifies correctness of encodings also of coinductive types whose definitions involve quotient inductive types such as finite powersets or finite distributions. Likewise the cubical model also verifies correctness of coinductive predicates defined using existential quantification and allows the results to be related to the global world of cubical sets. This paper looks at how to extend the extensional presheaf model of multi-clocked guarded recursion to higher ordinals, so that correctness of encodings of coinductive types can be extended from W-types to those involving finite powersets and finite distributions, as well as coinductive predicates involving existential quantification. This extension will allow results previously proved in Clocked Cubical Type Theory to be interpreted in a model based on set-theory, proving the correctness of these results as understood in their usual set theoretic interpretation.

Introduction

The complex numbers form a mathematical paradise in which all polynomials have roots. However, they are not just a dream world without connection to
the more earthly world of the real numbers. For example,
computing with complex numbers sometimes
leads to the construction of real solutions to polynomial equations. In the world of programming language semantics the equations of interest are
recursive domain equations
X\cong F(X)
. Many of these do not have solutions in the category of sets, but the topos of trees
[
6
]
serves as a mathematical paradise
in which guarded versions of these equations
X\cong F(\triangleright X)
all have solutions. One can therefore construct models of advanced programming languages using
the topos of trees, but can one then relate these results to the real world?

The answer depends on the type of results, and what is meant by the ‘real world’. For example, the original paper on the topos of trees
[
6
]

constructs a model of a programming language with higher order store in the internal language of the topos of trees (
\mathsf{Set}^{{\omega}^{\mathrm{op}}}
),
and then argues how these results relate to the real world of the category of sets. In some cases, this is not possible, however.
For example, a proof of an existential quantification of a predicate in the topos of trees does not always externalise
to a proof of an existential in sets. For this reason, Transfinite Iris
[
21
]
uses higher
indexing than
\omega
to reason about liveness properties. Likewise, as we shall explain below, passing to the real world does not preserve functors
like
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
used to model non-determinism and probabilistic choice, and this causes problems when relating models of languages
with these constructs to the real world.

The problem can be stated type theoretically using
multi-clocked guarded recursion
as in
Clocked Type Theory (CloTT)
[
5
]
and its
presheaf model
[
17
]
. In CloTT the delay modality
\triangleright^{\kappa}

is indexed by a clock
\kappa
, and one can quantify these clocks universally.
The model contains a copy of the standard set theoretic model of type theory as well as a copy of the topos of trees,
and universal quantification acts as a form of externalisation, relating results proved in the topos of tree to those in
the real world of set theory.
In this setting, the problem of concluding from existential quantification in the topos of
trees to actual existence in sets can be expressed as commutativity of quantification over clocks with existential quantification.

Multi-clocked guarded recursion also offers a way of programming with coinductive
types and predicates in type theory, using guarded recursion to express the requirement of productivity in types
[
4
,
7
]
.
Specifically, for functors
F
commuting with clock quantification, the solution to the guarded recursive domain equation

X(\kappa)\cong F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X(\kappa))
externalises to a terminal coalgebra with carrier

\forall\kappa.X(\kappa)
in the real world of set theory. Unfortunately, standard functors such as finite powerset functor
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}

or the finite distribution functor
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
do not commute with with clock quantification in the standard model, and so one cannot
use these tools to program with non-deterministic or probabilistic processes.

By varying the notion of ‘real world’, however, the situation can be improved. Kristensen et al.
[
16
]

construct a model of the combination of CloTT and
Cubical Type Theory
[
12
]
by considering presheaves valued in cubical sets. In this model, existential quantification
and functors like
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
, constructed as
quotient inductive types on the category of hsets
do
commute with clock quantification. This means that guarded recursion can be used
as a way of programming with coinductive types and predicates in Cubical Type Theory, including those involving
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}

and existential quantification. Clocked Cubical Type Theory has been used to model programming languages with non-determinism
[
19
]
and
probabilistic choice
[
22
]
. These models are constructed by solving guarded recursive domain equations
(also with negative occurences), and using the commutativity results above, the models can be related to the
operational semantics of these languages, defined as coinductive non-deterministic and probabilistic processes. Likewise,
Clocked Cubical Type Theory has been used to prove existence and non-existence of distributive laws between the coinductive delay monad and other monads
[
20
]
, and
to model programming languages with gradual typing
[
13
]
. Some of these results have been formalised in Guarded Cubical
Agda
[
24
]
, an extension of the Cubical Agda proof assistant
[
25
]
.

The question is now how tied these results are to cubical type theory. For example, the results proved about existence and non-existence of
distributive laws between monads as interpreted in the model of Kristensen et al. are results about these monads as seen as monads of hsets in the
cubical model. Do the same results hold for these monads on
\mathsf{Set}
? Likewise, results about contextual equivalence of programs proved using
the cubical model a priori only say something about their semantics as read in cubical sets. How do these relate to the ordinary set theoretic
interpretations?

In this paper we return to the set theoretic model of CloTT and look at how varying the indexing ordinal used in the model construction implies
commutativity of clock quantification with certain existential quantifications and with functors constructed as quotient inductive types
such as
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
. The contributions are as follows

•

We analyse existing applications of Cubical Clocked Type Theory
[
16
,
22
,
20
]
and
isolate the requirements on the model that will allow for these to be interpreted in the set theoretic model.

•

We state the conservativity of the model of Clocked Type Theory over the standard set theoretic model of type theory. This is an
easy consequence of the model construction of
[
17
]
, but has not previously been stated explicitly. We show how this allows us
to relate results proved in CloTT to the real world of set theory.

•

We give semantic conditions for finitary monads on sets to commute with clock quantification in the model of Clocked Type Theory
indexed by uncountable ordinals, and we give a simple condition on algebraic theories to generate such monads. This accounts for the functors

{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
mentioned above, among many others.

•

We give conditions allowing existential quantification to commute with quantification over clocks in the model

As a result we see that the applications mentioned above can all be expressed in the internal language of a variant of the set theoretic model
indexed by an ordinal of sufficiently high cardinality. The hope is that the results of this paper can form part of the semantic
basis for formalisation of applications as those mentioned above also in proof assistants
not based on cubical type theory.

We remark that the metatheory in which the models are constructed is classical set theory.
In particular, the proof of Theorem
8
is very classical.

This section gives a brief introduction to Clocked Type Theory. We refer readers to
[
17
]
for full details.

Clocked Type Theory is a dependently typed type theory with
\Pi
- and
\Sigma
-types, extensional
identity types, binary and nullary sums and products, as well as a modality
\triangleright
for delays and a guarded fixed point operator.
Delays are associated with clocks, so that
\triangleright^{\kappa}A
can be understood as a type of data of type
A
delayed by one time step
on clock
\kappa
. To program with clocks, the type theory has a special form of assumptions
\kappa:\mathsf{clock}
that can be placed in a context.
Note that
\mathsf{clock}
is not a type, but rather a pretype, similar to the status of the interval in Cubical Type Theory
[
12
]
. Introduction
and elimination for
\triangleright
is by abstracting and applying ticks on clocks, following the
Fitch-style rules for programming with modal types
[
10
,
11
]
. Selected typing rules can be found
in Figure
1
. The notation

\triangleright^{\kappa}A
is used for
{\triangleright}\,(\alpha\!:\!\kappa).A
when the tick
\alpha
does not appear free in
A
. Note that variables can be introduced
from anywhere in a context, also if they are behind ticks. This means that one can define a term
\nextop^{\kappa}\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\lambda x.\char 12\relax\alpha\kappa x

of type
A\to\triangleright^{\kappa}A
. Given
f:\triangleright^{\kappa}A\to A
, the fixed point operator computes the unique fixed point of
f\circ\nextop^{\kappa}
.
The language of ticks also allows one to define a dependent version of an applicative functor action for
\triangleright
:

\lambda f.\lambda x.\char 12\relax\alpha\kappa{f\,[\alpha](x\,[\alpha])}:\triangleright^{\kappa}(\Pi(x:A).B(x))\to\Pi(x:\triangleright^{\kappa}A).{\triangleright}\,(\alpha\!:\!\kappa).{B(x\,[\alpha])}

This is, for example, useful for reasoning about elements of modal type. It can also be
used to express an extensionality principle for
\triangleright
:

(t=_{{\triangleright}\,(\alpha:\kappa).A}u)\cong{\triangleright}\,(\alpha\!:\!\kappa).(t\,[\alpha]=_{A}u\,[\alpha])

For these applications, it is important
that the same ticks can be used on both the logical and term side, and this is one benefit of using
type theory rather than a language with a clear separation between term language and logic, such as topos logic.

2.1
Axioms

One of the main purposes of quantification over clocks it to allow for a controlled elimination of
\triangleright
. In Clocked Type Theory this is
done by an operation of application to a tick constant
\diamond
, with very special typing rules,
which can be used to construct a term of type
(\forall\kappa.\triangleright^{\kappa}A)\to\forall\kappa.A
. This, together with the

tick irrelevance axiom

\frac{\Gamma\vdash t:\triangleright^{\kappa}A}{\mathsf{tirr}^{\kappa}t:{\triangleright}\,(\alpha\!:\!\kappa).{\triangleright}\,(\alpha^{\prime}\!:\!\kappa).t\,[\alpha]=_{A}t\,[\alpha^{\prime}]}

(1)

allows one to prove the following

\mathsf{IsContr}(\lambda(x:\forall\kappa.A).\lambda\kappa.\char 12\relax\alpha\kappa{x\,[\kappa]})

(2)

where
\mathsf{IsContr}(-)
is defined precisely as in homotopy type theory
[
23
]
. In the present case of extensional
type theory, this axiom just states that the canonical map
(\forall\kappa.A)\to\forall\kappa.\triangleright^{\kappa}A

is an isomorphism.

The axiom of
clock irrelevance
states

\mathsf{IsContr}(\lambda x:A.\lambda\kappa.x)

(3)

Again, in our setting, this just states that the canonical map
A\to\forall\kappa.A
is an isomorphism when
\kappa
is not
free in
A
.
In Clocked Cubical Type Theory
[
16
]

clock irrelevance is not an axiom, but can be proved for many types. The other axioms mentioned can all
be given computational content in Clocked Cubical Type Theory, but it is unclear how to generalise this to
type theories not based on Cubical Type Theory. In this paper we are mostly concerned with properties
of the model, and so we will assume clock irrelevance and tick irrelevance as axioms, deriving (
2
) as a consequence.

3
Encoding Coinductive Types

One benefit of universes is that they allow for encoding guarded recursive types simply as fixed points for
endomaps on universes. Suppose, for example, that
A:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}
. Defining
\overline{{D^{\kappa}}}(A)

as the fixed point of the map

\lambda(x:\triangleright^{\kappa}\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}).A\overline{+}\overline{\triangleright}^{\kappa}x

then
{D^{\kappa}}(A)\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\operatorname{El}_{\Delta,\kappa}(\overline{{D^{\kappa}}}(A))
satisfies
{D^{\kappa}}(A)=A+\triangleright^{\kappa}{D^{\kappa}}(A)
. Note that
this is an actual judgemental equality of types. In Clocked Cubical Type Theory, fixed points only unfold up to propositional
equality, and the above type equation is only an equivalence of types. The type constructor
{D^{\kappa}}
is often referred
to as the
guarded delay monad
.

Guarded recursive types can be used as a stepping stone towards defining coinductive types. For example,
the more standard coinductive delay monad
D
can be encoded as the map that maps
A:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
to

\overline{\forall}\kappa.\overline{{D^{\kappa}}}(A)
where
\kappa
is a fresh clock. This gives the coinductive solution
to
D(A)\cong A+D(A)
. Using guarded recursion has the benefit that one can program with
D
using the guarded
fixed point operator, which allows productivity of coinductive definitions to be captured in types.

To describe the general setting of the encoding of coinductive types, note first that for any type
I
, the type
I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
describes
the objects of a category in the naive sense: Morphisms from
X
to
Y
are simply terms of the type
\Pi(i:I).X(i)\to Y(i)
. Here we
have left the ‘elements of’ operation
\operatorname{El}_{\Delta}
implicit as we shall do below also for operations of universe inclusions
to avoid clutter. If
\Delta\subset\Delta^{\prime}
, the universe inclusion
\mathsf{in}_{\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}}
induces a functor from
I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}

to
I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta^{\prime}}
. In the opposite direction, universal quantification over clocks defines a functor

(\forall\kappa.(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}))\to(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta})
(assuming
\kappa
fresh for
I
) by

(\forall\kappa.X)(i)\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\overline{\forall}\kappa.X\,[\kappa](i)

where
\forall\kappa.(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa})
is considered a category in the naive sense.

Definition 1
.

A functor
F:(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}))\to(J\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta})

commutes with clock quantification

if there exists a functor
F^{\prime}:\forall\kappa.((I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa})\to(J\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}))
extending
F

such that the canonical morphism

F(\forall\kappa.X)\to\forall\kappa.F^{\prime}\,[\kappa](X)

is an isomorphism in for all
X
in
\forall\kappa.(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}))
.

Suppose, for example, that
A:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
. Then
F(X)\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}A+X
defines a functor
\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
(omitting
I=1
),
which extends to a functor
F^{\prime}:\forall\kappa.(\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa})
because the universe inclusions
commute with binary sums. To prove that
F
commutes with clock quantification, one needs universal
quantification to distribute over sums
(
\forall\kappa.(B+C)\cong\forall\kappa.B+\forall\kappa.C
) and that
A
is clock irrelevant which is an axiom (
3
).
In the following we shall use the same symbol for the functor
F
and its extension
F^{\prime}
.

Theorem 2

(
[
4
,
18
]
)
.

Let
F:(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta})\to(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta})
be a functor commuting with universal quantification over clocks.
There is a unique object
\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
in
(I\to\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa})
satisfying

F(\triangleright^{\kappa}(\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)))\cong\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
. The
object
\forall\kappa.\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
is a final coalgebra for
F
.

The encoding was first proved with respect to a denotational model by Atkey and McBride
[
4
]
, and later internalised in type theory
by the author
[
18
]
. Although the setting above is slightly different from the original one, due to the indexing of universes, the proof carries over
verbatim from
[
18
]
. To get a feeling for the proof, note how the structure isomorphism of the final coalgebra can be easily
constructed:

\displaystyle\forall\kappa.\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)

\displaystyle\cong\forall\kappa.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}(\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)))

\displaystyle\cong F(\forall\kappa.\triangleright^{\kappa}(\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)))

\displaystyle\cong F(\forall\kappa.\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X))

A version of Theorem
2
can be proved also for the universes of propositions
\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta}
. This allows for
encoding of coinductive predicates.

For Theorem
2
to be useful, one needs a large collection of functors commuting with clock quantification. Many
functors have this property just by virtue of being defined by type constructors that commute with clock quantification. For example,

\forall\kappa
distributes over
\Sigma
-types just like
\Pi
does, and commutes with
\Pi
, so polynomial functors commute with clock quantification:

\displaystyle\forall\kappa.(\Sigma(a:A).\Pi(b:B(a).X))

\displaystyle\cong\Sigma(a:\forall\kappa.A).\forall\kappa.\Pi(b:B(a\,[\kappa]).X)

\displaystyle\cong\Sigma(a:A).\forall\kappa.\Pi(b:B(a).X)

\displaystyle\cong\Sigma(a:A).\Pi(b:B(a).\forall\kappa.X)

using clock irrelevance of
A
in the second line. In the model, universal quantification over clocks distributes over binary sums of types,
but this is not directly provable in the type theory unless one encodes sum as a
\Sigma
-type indexed by booleans and uses clock-irrelevance
for booleans. In this paper we will just consider this an axiom, and so it proves correctness of the encoding of the coinductive
delay monad. The model also verifies that
\forall\kappa
commutes with
\triangleright^{\kappa^{\prime}}
, and this can be proved internally
if the rule for tick application is extended as in
[
16
]
. In this paper we will look at conditions for
\forall\kappa
commuting with
quotient inductive types, in particular monads induced by algebraic theories.

On the propositional side, one can prove that
\forall\kappa
commutes with conjunction, and both forms of universal quantification.
Similarly to the case of types, the model also verifies that
\forall\kappa
commutes with disjunction and
\triangleright^{\kappa^{\prime}}
.
Existential quantification is more difficult. In Clocked Cubical Type Theory, existential quantification can be encoded using
\Sigma

and propositional truncation, and both of these can be proved to commute with clock quantification. In the extensional model
clock quantification does not commute with propositional truncation (see Example
7
below).
We will see, however, that by varying the indexing ordinal of
the model, it is safe to assume commutativity with quantification over sets of small cardinality. E.g., by indexing over
\omega_{1}

(the first uncountable ordinal), clock quantification commutes with existential quantification over countable set.
We first look at what is needed for applications.

4
Applications

This section recalls the applications of Clocked Cubical Type Theory to coalgebra
[
16
]
,
semantics of programming languages with probabilistic choice
[
22
]
and to
reasoning about weak bisimilarity
[
20
]
. The aim is to determine the precise requirements
needed to interpret these in a model of Clocked Type Theory.

4.1
Coalgebra

Coalgebras in general describe systems, and final coalgebras behaviours of such systems. Often
the systems of interest are non-deterministic or probabilistic, and can be described as coalgebras for functors involving powersets or distributions.
For example, coalgebras for the functor
F(X)={\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(A\times X)
, where
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
is the finite powerset functor, are finitely branching
labelled transition systems with labels in
A
. Similarly, coalgebras for
F(X)=A\times{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(X)
, where
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
is the finite distribution functor,
are Markov-processes with labels in
A
. Since clock quantification commutes with products, Theorem
2

will give encodings of final coalgebras for these functors, if just
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
commute with quantification over clocks. Note that
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
and

{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
are both generated by algebraic theories.
For example,
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(X)
is the free join semilattice on
X
, so generated by
\emptyset
, finite union,
and equations for associativity, commutativity, idempotency and a unit axiom for
\emptyset
.
(See Section
6.2
for details.) The natural way to program with these in type theory is
therefore as quotient inductive types, using the associated recursion and induction principles. As an example, we recall the
induction principle for
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(X)
just in the case of predicates:
Given
\phi:{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(X)\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta}
, in order to prove
\forall A:{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(X).\phi(A)
, it suffices to prove

\phi(\emptyset)
,
\phi(\{x\})
for all
x:X
, and that
\phi(A)
and
\phi(B)
implies
\phi(A\cup B)
.

Given a coalgebra
\xi:X\to F(X)
one would also like to define notions of similarity and bisimilarity as coinductive predicates. For example,
in the case of
F(X)={\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(A\times X)
,
R
is a simulation relation if whenever
R(x,x^{\prime})
holds, also the following holds

\forall a,y.(a,y)\in\xi(x)\to\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in\xi(x^{\prime})\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})

and simulation is the largest simulation relation. Here, the predicate
(a,y)\in\xi(x)
is defined by
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
-induction.
Supposing, for example, that
A,X:\operatorname{U}_{\emptyset}
, simulation should be
the final coalgebra for the functor mapping
R:X\times X\to\mathsf{Prop}_{\emptyset}
to

G(R)(x,x^{\prime})\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall a,y.(a,y)\in\xi(x)\to\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in\xi(x^{\prime})\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})

In order to define simulation, one first defines
\lesssim^{\kappa}
by guarded recursion to satisfy

x\lesssim^{\kappa}x^{\prime}\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall a,y.(a,y)\in\xi(x)\to\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in\xi(x^{\prime})\wedge\triangleright^{\kappa}(y\lesssim^{\kappa}y^{\prime})

then define
x\lesssim x^{\prime}
as
\forall\kappa.x\lesssim^{\kappa}x^{\prime}
. This definition will work if
G
commutes with clock quantification.
The crucial step in showing that, is to show that existential quantification and universal quantification over clocks commute. As we shall see,
this is not a property that holds generally in the model. However, in this particular case, the natural thing to do is to redefine the predicate

\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in\xi(x^{\prime})\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})
by
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
-recursion over
\xi(x^{\prime})
:

•

If
A=\emptyset
then
\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in A\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})
is false

•

If
A=\{(a^{\prime},y^{\prime})\}
then
\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in A\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})
is defined to be
a=a^{\prime}\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})

•

If
A=B\cup C
then
\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in A\wedge R(y,y^{\prime})
is defined as

(\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in B\wedge R(y,y^{\prime}))\vee(\exists y^{\prime}.(a,y^{\prime})\in C\wedge R(y,y^{\prime}))

Commutativity of the two quantifications
can then be proved by
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
-induction if just
\forall\kappa.(\phi\vee\psi)
is equivalent to
(\forall\kappa.\phi)\vee(\forall\kappa.\psi)

and
\forall\kappa.\mathsf{false}
is false. Both of these hold in the presheaf model of Clocked Type Theory independently of the indexing ordinal.

4.2
Modelling Programming Languages with Probabilistic Choice

Stassen et al.
[
22
]
construct a model of Probabilistic FPC, a programming language with recursive types and finite probabilistic choice, in
Clocked Cubical Type Theory. One of the basic ingredients in this interpretation is a monad modelling the combination of recursion and
probabilistic choice. In its guarded form, this monad maps a type
A:\operatorname{U}_{\emptyset}
to the guarded recursive type

{D^{\kappa}}A
satisfying
{D^{\kappa}}A\cong{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(A+\triangleright^{\kappa}({D^{\kappa}}A))
where
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
is the finite distribution monad. The coinductive version of
this monad satisfies
D^{\forall}A\cong{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(A+D^{\forall}A)
. Elements of
this monad are distributions of values of type
A
and computations that can run for one more step.
As before, the correctness of the definition
D^{\forall}A\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall\kappa.{D^{\kappa}}A

requires
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}
commutes with clock quantification.

The monads
{D^{\kappa}}
and
D
are used for both denotational and operational semantics. In order to prove an adequacy theorem,
the paper constructs a relation between syntax and semantics using guarded recursion.
One crucial ingredient in this definition is a notion of lifting of a relation on values
to one on computations. In terms of the universes of this paper, one can express this lifting as mapping a relation

R:A\to B\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
where
A:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}
and
B:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
to a relation

\overline{R}^{\kappa}:{D^{\kappa}}A\to D^{\forall}B\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
. Note the asymmetry:
A
and
B
live in different universes, and
different monads are applied to them. The definition of
\overline{R}^{\kappa}
is by case analysis of the first argument: An element in
{D^{\kappa}}A
is either
a distribution just of values of type
A
, one just of delayed computations, or a convex combination of the two:
\mu_{1}\oplus_{p}\mu_{2}
where

\mu_{1}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(A)
and
\mu_{2}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(\triangleright^{\kappa}({D^{\kappa}}A))
. Which of the three cases an input is in is decidable and, in the latter case

\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and
p
can be computed from the input. In the latter case the definition reads

(\mu_{1}\oplus_{p}\mu_{2})\overline{R}^{\kappa}\nu\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\exists\nu_{1}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(B),\nu_{2}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(D^{\forall}B).(\nu\leadsto^{\approx}\nu_{1}\oplus_{p}\nu_{2})\wedge\mu_{1}\overline{R}^{\kappa}\nu_{1}\wedge\mu_{2}\overline{R}^{\kappa}\nu_{2}.

where
\leadsto^{\approx}
is a form of reduction relation on
D^{\forall}
.

One important result of the paper (Lemma 7.3) states that if
A:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
,
the relation
\overline{R}:D^{\forall}A\to D^{\forall}B\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta}
defined as
\overline{R}(\mu,\nu)\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall\kappa.\overline{R}^{\kappa}(\mu\,[\kappa],\nu)

is equivalent to a coinductive definition of
\overline{R}
which is similar to the definition of
\overline{R}^{\kappa}
. The proof uses
that this coinductive definition can be defined via a guarded recursive definition, and this in turn relies on the existential quantifications of

\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
to commute with quantification over clocks. Unlike the existential quantifications used in the previous paragraph,
these quantifications cannot be rewritten to inductions over a quotient inductive type. In the models presented in this paper, existential quantification
will only commute with clock quantification if the existential quantification is of bounded size. The result as stated in this generality will therefore not be true
in these models. However, Stassen et al. only apply this theorem to the very specific case of
\nu:D^{\forall}1
, and the quantifications are therefore over

\nu_{1}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(1)
and
\nu_{2}:{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(D^{\forall}1)
, so of bounded size.

4.3
Weak Bisimilarity

The coinductive delay monad satisfying
D(A)\cong A+D(A)
,
is an intensional model of recursion because it counts computation steps. Often one wants to reason up to weak bisimilarity.
However, working with weak bisimilarity is quite subtle. For example, proving that the quotient of the coinductive delay monad by weak bisimilarity
is a monad seems to require countable choice
[
9
]
.
Rather than working with the quotient, one therefore often works in a category of setoids.
For example, Møgelberg and Zwart
[
20
]
prove that if
T
is a free model monad for an algebraic theory with no drop equations,
then there is a distributive law of T over the coinductive delay monad quotiented as a setoid.

Recall that coinductive delay monad can be defined as
D(A)\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall\kappa.{D^{\kappa}}(A)
where

{D^{\kappa}}(A)\cong A+\triangleright^{\kappa}{D^{\kappa}}(A)
. Given a setoid
(X,R)
, we would like to define the setoid
(D(X),\sim_{R})

capturing weak bisimilarity.
Writing

\displaystyle\now

\displaystyle:A\to D(A)

\displaystyle\step:D(A)\to D(A)

\displaystyle\now^{\kappa}

\displaystyle:A\to{D^{\kappa}}(A)

\displaystyle\step^{\kappa}:\triangleright^{\kappa}{D^{\kappa}}(A)\to{D^{\kappa}}(A)

for the left and right inclusions into the sum, the relation
\sim_{R}
should be defined by coinduction and cases:

\displaystyle\now(a)\sim_{R}y

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\exists(n:\mathbb{N},y^{\prime}:X).y=\step^{n}(\now(y^{\prime}))\wedge R(a,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle x\sim_{R}\now(a)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\exists(n:\mathbb{N},x^{\prime}:X).x=\step^{n}(\now(x^{\prime}))\wedge R(x^{\prime},a)

\displaystyle\step(x)\sim_{R}\step(y)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}x\sim_{R}y

This coinductive predicate can be encoded using guarded recursion, because the function mapping a relation
\sim_{R}
to
F(\sim_{R})

defined as above commutes with clock quantification. Indeed,
x\sim_{R}y
does not appear in the two first clauses,
and so in these cases the required condition follows from the axiom of clock irrelevance. In the last clause the case is immediate.

However, in order to use guarded recursion for programming with weak bisimilarity, Møgelberg and Zwart
[
20
]

use a different definition: They define

x\sim_{R}y\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\forall\kappa.x\,[\kappa]\sim_{R}^{\kappa}y\,[\kappa]

where
\sim_{R}^{\kappa}:{D^{\kappa}}X\to{D^{\kappa}}X\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
is defined also for

X:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta,\kappa}
and
R:X\to X\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
as the unique relation satisfying

\displaystyle\now^{\kappa}(a)\sim_{R}^{\kappa}y

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\exists(n:\mathbb{N},y^{\prime}:X).y=(\step^{\kappa}\circ\nextop)^{n}(\now^{\kappa}(y^{\prime}))\wedge R(a,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle x\sim_{R}^{\kappa}\now^{\kappa}(a)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\exists(n:\mathbb{N},x^{\prime}:X).x=(\step^{\kappa}\circ\nextop)^{n}(\now^{\kappa}(x^{\prime}))\wedge R(x^{\prime},a)

\displaystyle\step^{\kappa}(x)\sim_{R}^{\kappa}\step^{\kappa}(y)

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}{\triangleright}\,(\alpha\!:\!\kappa).{(x\,[\alpha]\sim^{\kappa}_{R}y\,[\alpha])}

Is this the same as the usual coinductive definition? When comparing the two we can assume
X:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
and

R:X\to X\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta}
. The critical cases are the first two, so suppose
x
is
\now(a)
. Then
\forall\kappa.x\,[\kappa]\sim_{R}^{\kappa}y\,[\kappa]

reduces to

\forall\kappa.\exists(n:\mathbb{N},y^{\prime}:X).y\,[\kappa]=(\step^{\kappa}\circ\nextop)^{n}(\now^{\kappa}(y^{\prime}))\wedge R(a,y^{\prime})

We would like to commute
\forall\kappa
past the two existential quantification, after which the equivalence follows by the relationship
between
\step
and
\step^{\kappa}
and the clock irrelevance axiom applied to
R(a,y^{\prime})
. The first quantification is over a countable
set, so will commute with
\forall\kappa
if the indexing in the model is over a sufficiently high ordinal. The other one is more difficult
because we want to define a generally applicable monad, and so should not put restrictions on the cardinality of
X
.
On the other hand, the
y^{\prime}
is essentially unique because

(\step^{\kappa}\circ\nextop)^{n}(\now^{\kappa}(y^{\prime}))=(\step^{\kappa}\circ\nextop)^{n}(\now^{\kappa}(y^{\prime\prime}))

implies
\triangleright^{n}(y^{\prime}=y^{\prime\prime})
. It therefore suffices to show that clock quantification commutes with existential
quantifications that are unique in this sense.

4.4
Summary Requirements

Having analysed the applications in detail, we now summarise the requirements needed for universal
quantification over clocks in order to express the applications above. They are

•

Universal quantification needs to commute with certain quotient-inductive type constructors such as finite powersets and finite distributions

•

Universal quantification needs to commute with bounded existential quantification, so over sets that are below a certain
cardinality

•

Universal quantification needs to commute with existential quantification that is essentially unique in the sense that if
X:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta}
and

\phi:X\to\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
is such that
\phi(x)\wedge\phi(y)
implies
(\triangleright^{\kappa})^{n}(x=y)
, then

\forall\kappa.\exists x:X.\phi(x)
implies
\exists(x:X).\forall\kappa.\phi(x)
.

\displaystyle\mathbin{\overset{\textsf{def}}{=}}\cup_{a\in A((\mathcal{E},\lambda);\delta[\lambda\mapsto\alpha];\iota\cdot\gamma)}B((\mathcal{E},\lambda);\delta[\lambda\mapsto\alpha];\iota\cdot\gamma;a)

\displaystyle=\cup_{a\in A(\mathcal{E};\delta;\gamma)}B((\mathcal{E},\lambda);\delta[\lambda\mapsto\alpha];\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot a)

\displaystyle=\cup_{a\in A(\mathcal{E};\delta;\gamma)}B(\mathcal{E};\delta;\gamma;a)

\displaystyle=\exists(A,B)(\mathcal{E};\delta;\gamma)

using invariance under clock introduction for
A
and
B
.
∎

5.2
Conservativity Over
\mathsf{Set}

One important consequence of the requirement that families be invariant under clock introduction
is that the fragment of the type theory that does not mention clocks, ticks or guarded
recursion is interpreted using constant presheaves only, and so the interpretation of this fragment agrees with that of the standard
model in
\mathsf{Set}
. In particular, any proof of such a statement in the model based on
\mathsf{GR}
gives rise to a proof
in the model based on
\mathsf{Set}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\Gamma\vdash A\,\operatorname{type}
be a wellformed statement in Clocked Type Theory
that can also be expressed in the internal type theory of
\mathsf{Set}
. If there is a
term
t
such that
\Gamma\vdash t:A
, then also the dependent type constructed by interpreting
\Gamma\vdash A\,\operatorname{type}
in
\mathsf{Set}

can be inhabited.

Note that
\Gamma
and
A
can be constructed using guarded recursion as long as they can be expressed in the internal language
of
\mathsf{Set}
. For example,
any endofunctor
F
on
\mathsf{Set}
mapping small sets to small sets induces a functor

F:\operatorname{U}_{\emptyset}\to\operatorname{U}_{\emptyset}
in the internal language
of our model. The type
\forall\kappa.\mu X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
is a closed type, so is interpreted as a set.
We can therefore view the statement that
\forall\kappa.\mu X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
is a final coalgebra for

F
(Theorem
2
) as a statement in the internal language of
\mathsf{Set}
, and since this statement has
a proof in Clocked Type Theory if
F
commutes with clock quantification, then,
by Theorem
5
, it also holds in the set-theoretic interpretation.
Of course, this statement will talk about the action of
F
as an endofunctor on
\operatorname{U}_{\emptyset}
, which, as mentioned above
is the set of all small objects of
\mathsf{GR}
that are invariant under clock introduction. However, the notion of functor expressed using
this universe in
\mathsf{Set}
is equivalent to the one expressed using the usual universe of small sets.
Many of the end results proved in the applications mentioned in Section
4
, are of this form, for example,
existence or non-existence of distributive laws of monads on
\mathsf{Set}
or setoids, and contextual equivalence of programs of Probabilistic
FPC. Their proofs use guarded recursion, but by Theorem
5
these proofs also prove their usual
set theoretic interpretation.

6
Algebraic data types

The construction of lifting a set functor F preserving smallness to an endomap on a universe
\mathcal{U}^{\emptyset}
extends to any of the universes

\mathcal{U}^{\Delta}
. For such a functor to commute with universal quantification over clocks means that the canonical map

F(\lim_{\alpha<\rho}A((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\cdot\gamma;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}))\to\lim_{\alpha<\rho}F(A((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\cdot\gamma;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}))

must be an isomorphism. Since
A
can be anything, this means that
F
needs to commute with
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed limits.
Theorem
2
is therefore
simply a synthetic restatement of Adámek’s fixed point theorem
[
1
]
:
The type
\forall\kappa.\mu^{\kappa}X.F(\triangleright^{\kappa}X)
is the limit of the terminal
sequence

1\leftarrow F(1)\leftarrow F^{2}(1)\leftarrow\dots\leftarrow F^{\omega}(1)\leftarrow F^{\omega+1}\leftarrow\dots

(where
F^{\beta}(1)=\lim_{\alpha<\beta}F^{\alpha}
for limit ordinals
\beta
), and Adámek’s theorem states
that this is a terminal coalgebra if
F
preserves
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed limits.

In this section we look at conditions for functors to commute with ordinal-indexed limits. We are particularly interested in the cases
of monads generated by algebraic theories, as in the examples of finite powersets and finite distributions. First a few examples.

Example 6
.

The finite powerset monad
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
does not commute with
{{\omega}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-limits. In fact the limit of the
{{\omega}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-long terminal sequence

1\leftarrow{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}(1)\leftarrow{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}^{2}(1)\leftarrow\dots

contains infinite sets
[
2
,
26
]
. Worrel
[
26
]
proved that the terminal sequence converges after
\omega+\omega
steps, but in general
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
does not commute with

{{(\omega+\omega)}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed limits. For a counter example, just append the terminal sequence with an
\omega
-long sequence of
1
.
In general, however,
{\mathsf{P}_{\mathsf{f}}}
does commute with

{{\omega_{1}}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-limits, where
\omega_{1}
is the first uncountable ordinal
[
2
]
.

The next example shows that some monads generated by algebraic theories do not commute with any
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-limits.

Example 7
.

Propositional truncation is the monad
T(X)=\{\star\mid\exists x:X.\mathsf{true}\}
. This monad does not commute with

{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-limits for any
\rho
. Consider, for example,
the sequence

X_{\alpha}=\{\beta\in\rho\mid\beta>\alpha\}

with maps
X_{\alpha}\to X_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\leq\alpha
given by inclusions. The limit of this sequence is empty, so also
T(\lim X)=\emptyset
,
but
T(X_{\alpha})=1
, so
\lim T(X)=1
.

6.1
A Semantic Condition

We now state conditions that imply that a functor preserves
{{\omega_{1}}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed limits.
Recall that
a functor
F:\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
is finitary if it preserves directed colimits. In particular, for any
X
, the map

\mathsf{colim}_{X^{\prime}\subseteq_{\mathsf{fin}}X}F(X^{\prime})\to F(X)

must be an isomorphism. In words, this means that any
x\in F(X)
lives already in
F(X^{\prime})
for some finite subset
X^{\prime}
of
X
.
Say that a functor
F
preserves pullbacks of monos, if, for any pullback diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}

where
m
is a mono, also
F
applied to the diagram is a pullback. In particular, this means that
F
preserves monos
also the non-split ones from the emptyset. This can be seen by considering the pullback of
\emptyset\to X
along itself.
It also means that
F
preserves binary intersections of subobjects.

Theorem 8
.

Let
F:\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
be a finitary functor which preserves pullbacks of monomorphisms.
If
\rho
is an uncountable regular ordinal, then
F
preserves
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed limits.

Proof.

First note that if
x\in F(X)
, then there exists a smallest subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq_{\mathsf{fin}}X
such that
x\in F(X^{\prime})
(leaving the action of
F

on the inclusion implicit): There exists an
X^{\prime}
because
F
is finitary, and the smallest is then the intersection of the finitely many
subsets
X^{\prime\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
, such that
x\in F(X^{\prime\prime})
.

Let
X
be a
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-indexed diagram. We must show that the canonical map

F(\lim X)\to\lim F(X)

is an isomorphism. To show surjectivity, suppose
x\in\lim F(X)
. Let
X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
be the smallest finite subset
of
X_{\alpha}
such that
x_{\alpha}\in F(X^{\prime}_{\alpha})
. We show that these form a subdiagram of
X
. Suppose
\beta\leq\alpha
and
let
r:X_{\alpha}\to X_{\beta}
be the restriction map. Then clearly
X^{\prime}_{\beta}\subseteq r[X^{\prime}_{\alpha}]
by minimality of
X^{\prime}_{\beta}
. Since
F

preserves pullbacks of monomorphisms, the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(r^{-1}(X^{\prime}_{\beta})\cap X^{\prime}_{\alpha})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(X^{\prime}_{\beta})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(X^{\prime}_{\alpha})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(r[X^{\prime}_{\alpha}])}

is a pullback, so
x_{\alpha}\in F(r^{-1}(X^{\prime}_{\beta}))
, and by minimality then
r^{-1}(X^{\prime}_{\beta})\cap X^{\prime}_{\alpha}=X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
. So

r[X^{\prime}_{\alpha}]=r[r^{-1}(X^{\prime}_{\beta})\cap X^{\prime}_{\alpha}]\subseteq X^{\prime}_{\beta}

and so
X^{\prime}_{\beta}=r[X^{\prime}_{\alpha}]
. So
X^{\prime}
is a subdiagram, and each map in
X^{\prime}
is surjective.

We next show that the maps
X^{\prime}_{\beta}\to X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
must be bijections from some point on. If not,
there exists a sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that each
X^{\prime}_{\alpha_{n}}
has at least
n
elements.
Then
X^{\prime}_{\vee\alpha}
must be infinite, which is a contradiction. Note that
\vee\alpha<\rho
because

\rho
is assumed regular and uncountable.

So there must be a stage
\alpha
such that all maps
X^{\prime}_{\beta}\to X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
are isomorphisms.
Then
F(\lim X^{\prime})\cong F(X^{\prime}_{\alpha})\cong\lim F(X^{\prime})
, and so
x
is in the image of the comparison map via the inclusion of
F(\lim X^{\prime})
into
F(\lim X)
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\lim X^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lim F(X^{\prime})\ni x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\lim X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lim F(X)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}

For injectivity, suppose
a,b\in F(\lim X)
map to the same element in
\lim F(X)
, so that
F(\pi_{\alpha})a=F(\pi_{\alpha})b
for any
\alpha
.
Let
X_{a}^{\prime}\subseteq\lim X
be the smallest subset such that
a\in F(X^{\prime}_{a})
and similarly for
X_{b}^{\prime}
. For each
x,y\in X_{a}^{\prime}

with
x\neq y
there is an
\alpha

such that
x_{\alpha}\neq y_{\alpha}
. Let
\alpha
be the upper bound of these as
x,y
range over all pairs of different
elements in
X^{\prime}_{a}
. Then
\pi_{\beta}:X^{\prime}_{a}\to\pi_{\beta}[X_{a}^{\prime}]
is
a bijection whenever
\beta\geq\alpha
. Similarly, there is a
\gamma
such that for all
\beta\geq\gamma
,

\pi_{\beta}:X^{\prime}_{b}\to\pi_{\beta}[X_{b}^{\prime}]
is a bijection. Let
\beta
the least upper bound of
\alpha
and
\gamma
.
Then
\pi_{\beta}[X_{a}^{\prime}]
is the smallest subset of
X_{\beta}
such that
F(\pi_{\beta})(a)
is in
F(\pi_{\beta}[X_{a}^{\prime}])
, and
similarly for
\pi_{\beta}[X_{b}^{\prime}]
. Since
F(\pi_{\beta})(a)=F(\pi_{\beta})(b)
, we must have
\pi_{\beta}[X_{b}^{\prime}]=\pi_{\beta}[X_{a}^{\prime}]
.
So then,
X^{\prime}_{a}\cong\pi_{\beta}[X_{a}^{\prime}]\cong X^{\prime}_{b}
via an isomorphism

such that
f(x)_{\beta}=x_{\beta}
from some
\beta

We conclude that
X^{\prime}_{a}=X^{\prime}_{b}
. So
a,b\in F(X^{\prime}_{a})
map to the same element in

by the isomorphism

, and therefore must be equal.
∎

6.2
A Syntactic Condition

We recall the notion of an algebraic theory. A signature consists of a set of operations
\Sigma
all associated with an arity, which is just a natural
number. Given a signature, one can form the set
\mathsf{Tm}(X)
of terms with free variables in
X
inductively in the standard way:

•

If
x\in X
then
x\in\mathsf{Tm}(X)

•

If
\mathsf{op}\in\Sigma
has arity
n
and
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is an
n
-vector of terms in
\mathsf{Tm}(X)
,
then
\mathsf{op}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathsf{Tm}(X)

If the arity is
0
, the vector in the second case is empty. An equation is a pair of terms
(t,s)
in some
\mathsf{Tm}(X)
. An algebraic
theory is a signature together with a set of equations over that signature.

An algebraic theory induces a monad on
\mathsf{Set}
by the definition

T(X)=\mathsf{Tm}(X)/\sim

where
\sim
is the smallest congruence relation generated by the equations of the theory. It is well-known (see e.g.
[
3
]
)
that the underlying functor of
T
is then finitary.

Example 9
.

The finite powerset monad is generated by the theory consisting of two operations
\bot
of arity
0
and
\vee
of arity
2
.
We write the latter as infix, The equations are

\displaystyle x\vee y

\displaystyle=y\vee x

\displaystyle(x\vee y)\vee z

\displaystyle=x\vee(y\vee z)

\displaystyle x\vee x

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle x\vee\bot

\displaystyle=x

Example 10
.

The finite distributions monad is generated by a family of binary operations
\oplus_{p}
indexed by
p
in the open interval
(0,1)

together with the equations

\displaystyle x\oplus_{p}x

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle x\oplus_{p}y

\displaystyle=y\oplus_{1-p}x

\displaystyle(x\oplus_{p}y)\oplus_{q}z

\displaystyle=x\oplus_{pq}(y\oplus_{\frac{(1-p)q}{1-pq}}z)

Example 11
.

The propositional truncation monad of Example
7
is generated by the algebraic theory of
no constructors and a single equation
(x=y)
.

We now give a syntactic condition for an algebraic theory ensuring that the generated monad satisfies the conditions of Theorem
8
.

Definition 12
.

An equation
(t,s)
is a drop equation if
\mathsf{fv}(t)\neq\mathsf{fv}(s)
.

Note that if a theory has no drop equations, then also the least congruence on terms generated by the equations has no drop equations. For example,
neither the theories of finite powerset, nor finite distributions, have drop equations.
On the other hand, the propositional truncation monad is generated by a single drop equation.

Theorem 13
.

Suppose
T
is a monad generated by an algebraic theory with no drop equations. Then
T
preserves
pullbacks of monomorphisms. As a consequence, if
\rho
is an uncountable regular ordinal
then
T
preserves
{{\rho}^{\mathrm{op}}}
-limits.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a map and let
Z\subseteq Y
. We must show that the following is a pullback

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(f^{-1}(Z))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(i)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(f)}

where
i:Z\to Y
is the inclusion. So suppose given
[s]_{Z}\in T(Z)
and
[t]_{X}\in T(X)
such that
T(i)[s]_{Z}=T(f)[t]_{X}
. Let
t_{f}
be
the result of substituting the free variables of
t
using
f
, so that
T(f)[t]_{X}=[t_{f}]_{Y}
. The assumption says that

t_{f}
and
s
are related in the congruence relation generated by the equalities of the algebraic theory. Since none of these
are drop equations,
t_{f}
and
s
have the same free variables. Since the free variables of
s
are in
Z
, the free variables of

t
must be in
f^{-1}(Z)
. So
[t]\in T(f^{-1}(Z))
and maps to
[s]_{Z}\in T(Z)
and
[t]_{X}\in T(X)
. This proves existence.

To prove uniqueness, it suffices to show that
T
preserves monomorphism. So suppose
A\subseteq B
,

j:A\to B
is the inclusion, and that
t
and
s

are terms with free variables in
A
such that
T(j)[s]=T(j)[t]
.
Since congruence of terms only equates terms with the same free variables,
there are no more equations between terms in
B
than there are between the same terms in
A
. So
[s]=[t]
.
∎

As a non-example, the pullback

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\emptyset}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{1\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0,1\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

witnesses that propositional truncation does not preserve pullbacks of monomorphisms.

7
Existential quantification

The previous section handled the first requirement listed in Section
4.4
. We now look at the last two, which both
concern commutativity of universal quantification over clocks and existential quantification. The property we need can be
stated in Clocked Type Theory as

\Delta,X:\operatorname{U}_{\Delta},\phi:X\to\forall\kappa.\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}\vdash\exists(x:X).\forall\kappa.\phi(x)=\forall\kappa.\exists(x:X).\phi(x)\,[\kappa]:\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta}

(4)

where
\Delta=\kappa_{1}:\mathsf{clock},\dots,\kappa_{n}:\mathsf{clock}
is a clock context of length
n
. We first note that this principle
does not hold in general.

Example 14
.

Let
\Delta
be the empty context and let
X=\rho
be the indexing ordinal. Let
\phi
be the prediate
on
\rho\times\mathrm{Clk}
defined as

\phi(\mathcal{E};\delta;\alpha;\lambda)=\{\star\mid\delta(\lambda)\leq\alpha\}

Since this predicate is invariant under clock introduction, it corresponds to a map
X\to\forall\kappa.\operatorname{Prop}_{\Delta,\kappa}
, but

\forall\kappa.\exists(\alpha:\rho).\phi(\alpha)\,[\kappa]=\{\star\}

and

\displaystyle(\exists(\alpha:\rho).\forall\kappa.\phi(\alpha)\,[\kappa])(\mathcal{E};\delta)

\displaystyle=\{\star\mid\exists\alpha:\rho.\forall\beta.\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\beta];\alpha;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})\}

\displaystyle=\emptyset

because the condition fails when
\beta>\alpha
.
Such
\beta
always exist, because
\rho
is assumed to be a limit ordinal, as it needs to be to interpret axiom (
2
).

The above example is a variant of the well-known phenomenon that existential quantification in the internal language of a presheaf
topos is local and does not imply global existence. This has been observed previously also in the setting of guarded
recursion
[
6
,
21
]
.

To understand the principle more generally, we look at substitutions from some object
\Gamma
in
\mathsf{GR}
into the context of (
4
).
A mapping
\Gamma\to\mathrm{Clk}^{\Delta}
corresponds to an object of
\mathsf{GR}/{\mathrm{Clk}^{\Delta}}
which is equivalent to
\mathsf{GR}[\Delta]
, so
a mapping into the context of (
4
) corresponds to an object
\Gamma
in
\mathsf{GR}[\Delta]
, a family
X
over
\Gamma
,
and finally, a predicate
\phi
on
X\times\mathrm{Clk}
. The equality (
4
)
then translates to equality of
(\exists(x:X).\forall\kappa.\phi(x))(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma)
which is

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\exists x:X(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma).\forall\alpha<\rho.\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})\}

(5)

and
(\forall\kappa.\exists(x:X).\phi(x))(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma)
which is

\displaystyle\{\star\mid\forall\alpha<\rho.\exists x:X((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma).\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})\}

(6)

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi)
and
\chi:\Delta\to\mathcal{E}
.

Theorem 15
.

If
\rho
is a regular ordinal and all fibres
X(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma)
are of cardinality strictly smaller than
\rho
, then
(
5
) and (
6
) are equal.

Proof.

Since
X
is invariant under clock introduction, (
6
) can be rewritten as

\{\star\mid\forall\alpha<\rho.\exists x:X(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma).\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})\}

(7)

Clearly the condition of (
5
) implies that of (
7
).
For the other direction, first note that the condition is downward closed in
\alpha
because, for
\beta\leq\alpha
, the identity on

(\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})
tracks a map
\sigma
which gives

\sigma\cdot(-):\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})\to\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\beta];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})

Now, arguing classically, suppose (
5
) is false. For each
x
there must exist an
\alpha_{x}
, such that

\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\beta];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})

is false for all
\beta\geq\alpha_{x}
. By the condition of the cardinality of the fibres of
X
and since
\rho
is regular, the least upper bound

\alpha
of all the
\alpha_{x}
is an ordinal which is smaller than
\rho
. This ordinal then proves (
6
) false.
∎

The above proof is essentially the same as the proof of the ‘existential property’ forming part of the basis
of Transfinite Iris
[
21
, Theorem 5.2]
, but adapted to the multi-clocked setting.
The existential property states that any existential proved in
the logic of Transfinite Iris externalises to an existential in the meta-logic. Transfinite Iris uses a large indexing ordinal
to ensure that the indexing ordinal is larger than the existentially quantified type. We will discuss this point further
in the conclusion.

Universal quantification over clocks also commutes with essentially unique existential quantifications.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\Gamma
be an object of
\mathsf{GR}[\Delta]
,

X
a family over
\Gamma
, and
\phi
a predicate over
X\times\mathrm{Clk}
, and let
n:\Gamma\to\mathbb{N}

be a map of presheaves. If
\phi(x,\kappa)\wedge\phi(y,\kappa)
implies
(\triangleright^{\kappa})^{n}(x=y)
then (
5
) and (
6
) are equal.

Proof.

The predicate

\Delta,\Gamma,x:X,y:X,\kappa:\mathsf{clock}\vdash(\triangleright^{\kappa})^{n}(x=y)

is true at
(\mathcal{E};\delta;\chi;\gamma;x;y;\lambda)
if and only if either
x=y
or
\delta(\lambda)<n(\gamma)
.
So, for
\alpha
larger than
n(\gamma)
, there exists at most a single
x
such that

\phi((\mathcal{E},\lambda_{\mathcal{E}});\delta[\lambda_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\alpha];\iota\chi;\iota\cdot\gamma;\iota\cdot x;\lambda_{\mathcal{E}})

Suppose now that (
7
) holds, and pick
\alpha
larger than
n(\gamma)
. By (
7
) there is an
x
such that
the above holds for that
\alpha
and
x
. By downward closedness, it then also holds at all
\beta\leq\alpha
. For
\beta\geq\alpha
, there is
a
y
such that the above holds at
\beta
for that
y
. By downwards closure and uniqueness, we then conclude
x=y
, so that
x
witnesses
(
5
).
∎

8
Conclusion

We have seen that by choosing the indexing ordinal of the model to be sufficiently big, all the requirements of the applications
studied in Section
4
can be met. For example, to program with finitely branching non-deterministic processes, and reasoning about
bisimilarity of these using guarded recursion, as done by Kristensen et al
[
16
]
the indexing ordinal can be
\omega_{1}

(the first uncountable ordinal) or higher. For modelling Probabilistic FPC and reasoning about contextual equivalence of programs,
the indexing ordinal needs to be larger than the cardinality of
{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{f}}}(D^{\forall}1)
, which is uncountable. Finally, for reasoning about the coinductive delay
monad up to weak bisimilarity as a monad on the category of setoids, one needs the indexing monad to be
\omega_{1}
or higher, such that
quantification over clocks commutes with existential quantification over
\mathbb{N}
. In all these examples, we can now interpret the results proved in
previous papers into the model and conclude the expected set theoretic results. For example, Theorem 7.7 of
[
20
]
now proves the existence
of a distributive law of the monad generated by an algebraic theory with no drop equations over the coinductive delay monad up to weak bisimilarity
as monads on the usual category of setoids. Likewise the results about contextual equivalence of Probabilistic FPC programs proved in
[
22
]

now hold the operational semantics given in that paper as interpreted using
D^{\forall}
as understood as a monad on the category of
sets in the usual way.

The syntactic requirements on algebraic theories to generate monads commuting with clock quantification are quite natural from a
type theoretic point of view: The arities should be finite and the equations non-drop. The latter corresponds to the equations being
typeable in a relevant type system (in the technical sense of substructural types).
The requirements on cardinality for existential quantifications, on the other hand, are less
natural in type theory. One way to avoid this is to use the ordinal
of all ordinals as indexing, as is done in Transfinite Iris
[
21
]
.
Since this ordinal is large (lives in the next universe), it suffices to commute
clock quantification with all small existential quantifications.
This simplification comes at a price, however: Universal quantification over clocks now raises the universe
level for small types. Monads such as
D^{\forall}
defined using universe quantification would therefore only be monads on the category
of sets in the next universe. In particular, the type
D^{\forall}1
would not be small, and so existential quantification over it would not
automatically commute with existential quantification over it, unless one could prove it isomorphic to a small set.

Future work includes extending Theorem
8
beyond the finitary case to more generally accessible functors.
Most of the proof of Theorem
8
can be generalised if the functor is assumed to preserve sufficiently large
intersections. The problem is the argument that proves that the diagram
X^{\prime}
must consist of bijections from some point. This argument
does not generalise from diagrams of finite sets to sets of larger cardinality.
It would also be good to extend beyond
\mathsf{Set}
, to slices of
\mathsf{Set}
or, more generally, presheaves. This would allow more general
quotient inductive types to be considered.

An alternative to proving type constructors commute with clock quantification is to use Worrels proof
[
26
]
that for
any
\kappa
-accessible endofunctor
F
on
\mathsf{Set}
, the terminal sequence converges in
\kappa+\kappa
steps, producing
a terminal coalgebra for
F
. This proves that the encoding of coinductive types for those functors is correct, externally. While this
result is more general (in the sense that it works for all cardinals
\kappa
), it is also less natural and flexible from the type theoretic
point of view. Also from the computational point of view, Kristensen et al.
[
16
]
develop a notion of induction under clocks
that provides computational content to axioms of higher-inductive types or quotient inductive types commuting with clock quantification.
It is not clear how to provide computational content to axioms of being a terminal coalgebra.

Multimodal Type Theory
[
14
]
gives a different approach to reasoning about the relationship between
\mathsf{Set}

and the topos of trees in one type theory. Each of these categories correspond to a mode of the type theory, and the functors of
constant presheaves and global elements are internalised as modalities between these two modes. While the use of modes
makes the relationship between these categories explicit in the language, as we saw in Section
5
the same two
models form submodels of the model of multiclocked guarded recursion. The results of Sections
6
and
7

can also be applied in the setting of Multimodal Type Theory.
