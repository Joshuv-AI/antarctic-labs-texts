Sad emotion processing-related theta oscillation patterns link suicide attempt to childhood trauma in depression: evidence from MEG
Authors: Yi Xia, Yishan Du, Moxuan Song, Junling Sheng, Rui Yan, Xiaoqin Wang, Zhijian Yao, Qing Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a severe mood disorder with genetic and environmental interactions. Subtypes with childhood trauma (CT) tend to have higher rates of suicide attempt (SA). The amygdala (AMY) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) are integral to the neurocircuitry underlying emotional processing, with dysregulation of these regions being posited as a pathophysiological hallmark both in CT and SA. However, the specific neural mechanisms linking CT and SA are unclear. Here, we examined local neural oscillatory patterns and interregional connectivity within mPFC and AMY during sad emotion processing. 88 MDD patients (44 with CT, 44 without CT) and 44 healthy control (HC) participants underwent an emotion recognition task during magnetoencephalography (MEG) recording. Time-frequency analysis was used to examine inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC) and neural oscillatory power at bilateral mPFC and AMY. In addition, directed connectivity between mPFC and AMY was examined using Granger causality. MDD without CT demonstrated less ITPC at bilateral mPFC and bottom-up dysconnectivity from the AMY.R to the mPFC.R compared to HC. Theta power (bilateral mPFC and left AMY), ITPC (bilateral AMY and mPFC) and top-down functional connectivity from the mPFC.L to the AMY.L were reduced in MDD with CT relative to MDD without CT. Furthermore, the mPFC.L theta ITPC mediated the association between SA and CT. Overall, the results suggest that abnormal theta power modulation, phase coherence, and hypo-connectivity between the mPFC and AMY may underlie impaired sad emotion processing in MDD with CT, partially explaining the increased SA rate in this subgroup.Subject terms: Depression, Neuroscience

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common and severe mental health condition that can significantly impair emotional regulation, cognitive function, and social interaction [1]. Some MDD patients may even engage in suicidal behavior, imposing a heavy burden on individuals, families, and society [2]. The etiology of MDD is multifaceted, with childhood trauma (CT) emerging as a significant distal risk factor for both the development and exacerbation of the disorder [3].

CT refers to adverse experiences or terrifying, dangerous, violent, or life-threatening incidents that occur in early life, including sexual abuse, physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect [4]. Previous research reported that MDD patients with cumulative adversity in childhood exhibit stronger resistance to treatment [5] and poorer social function [6]. Researchers proposed that MDD with CT should be regarded as a heterogeneous clinical phenotype of depression [7]. Furthermore, CT is linked to a heightened likelihood of suicide attempt (SA). Individuals who have attempted suicide tend to report more CT than those who have never made such attempts [8]. Aberrant functioning in the cortico-limbic circuit has been consistently observed in MDD and is thought to be a pathophysiological substrate for impaired emotional processing and heightened suicide risk [9–11]. However, the neurobiological mechanisms through which CT contributes to increased vulnerability to SA in MDD patients remain poorly understood.

CT often causes abnormalities in emotion regulation, which can make it difficult for individuals to get stuck in sad emotions when caught up in a bad event, leading to suicidal behavior. From a neurobiological perspective, the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and amygdala (AMY) are central to the neurocircuitry involved in emotion regulation [12]. The mPFC is crucial for the top-down modulation of limbic system activity [13], while the AMY is responsible for the detection and appraisal of emotionally salient stimuli [14]. A substantial body of evidence suggests that CT could impact neurodevelopment, influencing brain structure, function, and connectivity [15, 16]. Usually, from childhood to adulthood, the reactivity of AMY to emotional faces decreases as mPFC activity increases. However, early-life stress alters this developmental trajectory, resulting in persistent AMY hyperactivity and weakened mPFC-AMY connectivity [17, 18]. Notably, the neurophysiological mechanisms of these disturbances, and in particular their relationship to SA, remain unclear.

Theta band oscillations (4–8 Hz) have been identified as key neural rhythms supporting emotion processing, cognitive control, and interregional communication [19, 20]. Reduced theta power and synchrony, particularly in the mPFC, have been linked to deficits in emotional regulation and memory encoding in MDD patients [21, 22]. Inter trial phase coherence (ITPC), a measure of temporal phase coherence across trials, provides critical insights into the functional integrity of neural networks during task engagement [23], which may reflect alterations in neural activity and synchronization in the mPFC and AMY. In addition to local activities, interregional information communication is equally important for emotional processing. It is still unclear whether disrupted feedforward or feedback connectivity, or a combination of both, contributes to the altered emotional processing observed in MDD with CT. Investigating Granger causality (GC) between the mPFC and the AMY may provide a potential approach to explore this issue.

Magnetoencephalography (MEG), with its millisecond time resolution and excellent source localization ability, is very suitable for capturing the rapid neural dynamics related to emotional processing. In this study, we used MEG to examine changes in theta band power, ITPC, and effective connectivity within the mPFC and AMY in MDD patients with and without a CT history. In addition, we selected SA as a specific outcome variable, aiming to move beyond general depressive symptoms and focus on behavioral outcomes that carry greater clinical urgency. We explored whether the interruption of these neurophysiological markers mediated the emergence of SA and a history of CT in MDD patients. This approach facilitated a deeper understanding of how CT influences the risk of extreme behaviors through specific neural mechanisms, thereby providing evidence for targeted prevention and intervention strategies. We proposed a joint hypothesis of SA in MDD that incorporates both distal and proximal factors, including CT and sad emotion processing-related theta oscillation patterns.

Methods and materials

Ethics declarations

The authors assert that all procedures of this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (2011-KY027; 2018-KY021; 2021-KY021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

A total of 88 inpatients diagnosed with MDD were recruited from Nanjing Brain Hospital, including 44 MDD with a history of CT and 44 without CT (NCT) group. Additionally, 44 healthy controls were recruited from the same geographic region through community advertisements, and matched to the patient groups in age, sex, and education level. The power of our smallest group size (N = 44) was higher than 0.8.

All individuals were right-handed, either with normal or corrected vision, and were aged 18 to 60 years, having completed at least nine years of education. The diagnosis of MDD was made by two experienced psychiatrists through the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5).

Inclusion criteria for the patients group: a 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-17) score of ≥ 17; no neurological or psychiatric comorbidities; no electroconvulsive therapy and psychotherapy within the past year; no substance abuse; free of antipsychotics, mood stabilizers or antidepressants in the past 6 weeks. All the HC had no lifetime psychiatric diagnosis and were not taking any psychotropic medications.

Clinical assessments

Demographic details, including age, sex, education, family history and clinical characteristics (illness duration and number of depressive episodes) were recorded. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the HAMD-17. SA was defined if the patient had at least one documented self-injurious behavior with suicidal ideation during the current episode, which meant that the third-item (suicide) score of the HAMD-17 was rated 4. CT was evaluated using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [24]. The CTQ comprises 28 items, consisting of 25 clinical items and three validity items that cover five categories of childhood maltreatment: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Each subscale comprises five items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, resulting in a total score ranging from 5 to 25. For clinical samples, researchers typically use cut-off scores of ≥ 10 for physical abuse, ≥13 for emotional abuse, ≥8 for sexual abuse, ≥10 for physical neglect, and ≥ 15 for emotional neglect. In this study, participants who met the criteria for any indicator of childhood trauma were included in the CT group, while those who did not were assigned to the NCT group. All HC included in this study had no history of CT.

Experimental task

During the MEG scanning, all individuals completed a facial emotion recognition task. The video materials were sourced from the Chinese Facial Expression Video System (CFEVS), which includes neutral, happy, and sad facial expressions. The task consisted of two blocks, each containing 120 randomized trials. In a block, each type of emotional stimulus was presented by four male and female faces across a total of 30 trials. Each stimulus lasted for 3 s and was preceded by a jittered inter-trial interval of 0.5, 1, or 1.5 s. Subjects were required to quickly and accurately identify the emotional face of each stimulus with the hand response. Reaction time and accuracy were recorded. Subsequent analyses were conducted only in sad condition.

MEG and MRI data acquisition

MEG data were recorded using a whole-head 275-channel CTF MEG system (VSM MedTech, Canada) in a magnetically shielded room with a sampling rate of 1200 Hz. Structural T1 anatomical images were recorded with a Siemens Verio 3 T magnetic resonance imaging system through a high-resolution, T1-weighted, 3D gradient-echo pulse sequence (TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.48 ms, flip angle = 9◦, slices number = 176, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, FOV = 250 × 250 mm2). Three fiducial markers were positioned at the left and right ear canals as well as at the nasion to facilitate the co-registration of MEG with offline MRI data. During MEG scan, head position was tracked in real time via Head Localization Coil channel. The MEG data were required to meet the criteria of maximum head motion, with translations less than 5 mm and rotations under 2 degrees. In contrast, the structural MRI data adhered to stricter criteria, allowing for a maximum translation of less than 2 mm and a rotation of less than 2 degrees.

MEG preprocessing

MEG preprocessing was conducted using the FieldTrip toolbox in MATLAB [25]. The data was down-sampled to 600 Hz. A band-stop filter was used to eliminate 50 Hz line noise. Channels and trials with excessive artefacts were rejected based on variance thresholds. Artifacts associated with eye blinks, eye movements, muscle activity and heartbeat were eliminated through the application of independent component analysis (ICA) decomposition.

Source-level analysis

We focused on two regions of interest (ROIs): the bilateral AMY (left: MNI −22.94, −4.67, −18.06; right: 22.96, −3.82, −18.06) and bilateral mPFC (left: −5.13, 43.54, −18.33; right: 5.20, 43.23, −18.70). The method of linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer [26] was used to reconstruct the virtual channel time series of activity at these sources in each epoch.

Time-frequency analysis

Time-frequency representations (TFRs) of source-reconstructed signals were calculated for each ROI by a Hanning-taper sliding window approach (4–50 Hz in 1 Hz steps, 4-cycle window). Additionally, event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) was calculated and normalized in decibels relative to a baseline period (−400 to −100 ms). Oscillatory activity was categorized as theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (9–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (31–50 Hz). As shown in Fig. 1A, visual inspection of spectrographs displaying 4–50 Hz activity was conducted to identify the timeframe in which the specific frequency band was most prominently represented. A time window of 250–550 ms time window was chosen for theta analysis, while 350–600 ms was chosen for alpha and beta analysis, and 250–300 ms for gamma analysis. Typically, the power of the alpha/beta oscillations during the active window is decreased, i.e., event-related desynchronization (ERD). In contrast, increased power of the theta and gamma oscillations could be observed during emotion processing, i.e., event-related synchronization (ERS).

Inter trial phase coherence

As shown in Fig. 1B, we analyzed phase synchronization across trials (ITPC) in the frequency range of 4–50 Hz. ITPC measures the coherence of phase alignment at each time-frequency point across trials with values ranging from 0 (random phase) to 1 (perfectly aligned phase). Note that ITPC was analyzed exclusively within the active time window of 250–550 ms for theta activity. This focus is due to the fact that ITPC is generally only observable in low-frequency signals, as minor temporal jitter in stimulus presentation can disrupt the detection of ITPC in higher-frequency ranges.

Nonparametric granger causality

Nonparametric, frequency-resolved GC analysis was used to assess directional functional connectivity between the AMY and mPFC. Cross-spectral density matrices were estimated from source time series within the active time window (250–550 ms) using multi-taper spectral estimation with 5 Hz spectral smoothing. GC spectra were computed for frequencies ranging from 4–50 Hz to quantify directional information flow between ROIs (top-down feedback: mPFC→AMY; bottom-up feedforward: AMY→mPFC).

Statistical analyses

Demographic, clinical variables and task performance were analyzed using SPSS 22.0. Chi-square tests were applied for sex and SA, and one-way ANOVA was used for age and education. Independent-sample t-tests were used for HAMD-17, episodes, illness duration and CTQ.

Average values of ERSP in theta, alpha, beta, and gamma oscillation, along with average ITPC and GC values for theta in specific time-frequency windows, were obtained from ROIs for each participant. Extracted values for ERSP, ITPC and GC were subsequently analyzed to assess differences between groups. A mixed ANOVA was conducted to examine ERSP for each of the four frequency bands, along with ITPC and GC for theta activity. In this analysis, brain regions were treated as a within-subject factor, while group served as a between-subjects factor. Subsequently, false discovery rate (FDR) correction and all pairwise post-hoc tests were applied. For each metric (power/ITPC/GC), brain regions and frequency bands are jointly incorporated into the correction. We only reported FDR corrected analyses.

To further evaluate the independence of neurophysiological variables from essential demographic factors, the analyses above incorporated sex, age, education and drug use (use: 1, non-use: 0) as covariates.

Binary univariate logistic regression analysis was used to screen for MEG variables associated with SA, with sex, age, education and drug use as covariates. Features with P values less than 0.05 after FDR correction were included in subsequent analyses. Then, a mediation model was tested to examine whether oscillation patterns during sad emotion processing mediated the relationship between CT and SA. Bootstrapping (95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals [CIs] based on 5000 bootstrap resamples) was employed for analysis. If the 95% bias-corrected CI did not include zero, the indirect effect was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the neurophysiological mechanisms associated with CT in MDD, focusing on the neural oscillations and synchrony in the AMY and mPFC during sad emotion processing. Patients with MDD, particularly those with a history of CT, exhibited a significant reduction in theta band power modulation, ITPC and GC within mPFC and AMY. Furthermore, decreased theta ITPC mediated the relationship between CT and SA, suggesting a potential neural mechanism through which early life adversity influences suicidal behavior in MDD patients.

Theta activity in MDD demonstrated less ITPC (both in CT and NCT) compared with HC at bilateral mPFC. Theta-band activity is closely associated with numerous emotional and cognitive functions, including attention, memory, and emotion regulation [19, 27]. Specifically, theta oscillations recorded at the medial frontal site, also known as the midline frontal theta, are thought to reflect the activation of thalamocortical feedback loops [28, 29]. Theta oscillations are increasingly recognized as a direct neurophysiological indicator of certain aspects of cognitive control [30, 31]. Numerous studies reported that reduced frontal theta power is strongly associated with the severity of depression [32–34]. Mahato et al. found significant differences in theta wave power between the left and right hemispheres of HC, which were not observed in MDD patients [35]. Hansen et al. reported that the long-range temporal correlations in theta-band oscillations are a notable feature of a healthy human brain and could contribute to the diagnosis of psychiatric disorders [36]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies and further confirm the importance of theta-band activity in the pathophysiology of depression. Reduced mPFC ITPC in patients with MDD may reflect common impairments in these cognitive and emotional functions, which are consistent with clinical symptoms of depression, such as inattention, memory impairment, and difficulties in emotion regulation.

Within the MDD group, the subgroup with CT had more severe theta abnormalities compared to the subgroup without CT. First, theta power (ERS) was reduced in MDD with CT at both mPFC and left AMY. Second, MDD with CT showed lower theta ITPC at bilateral AMY and mPFC. This suggests that CT may have profound effects on the neurophysiological underpinnings of MDD patients. Clinically, these neurobiological changes manifest as poor cognitive control and emotional regulation. For example, maltreated adolescents often show deficits in executive/inhibitory functioning, consistent with a dynamic reduction in mPFC theta band activity [37]. This is consistent with our finding that MDD patients with CT may exhibit more pronounced theta band reductions in the mPFC and AMY, reflecting the compounding effects of trauma and depression on neuroplasticity and emotional control. In summary, CT appears to induce long-term functional alteration in the prefrontal-limbic network, with reduced mPFC neuronal integrity and regulatory influence, resulting in weaker theta-mediated connectivity.

Beyond its function in local neural processing, theta phase is believed to be essential for inter-areal communication within the brain. Our findings further revealed that bottom-up connectivity from the AMY.R to the mPFC.R was attenuated in MDD patients with or without CT compared to HC. This suggests that the transmission of emotional salience and threat-related signals from the AMY to the PFC regions responsible for higher-order emotional assessment and regulation is disrupted. In other words, the feedforward signal from AMY to the mPFC is diminished. This disruption in bottom-up signal transmission may lead to the exacerbation of sad affect and impaired emotional regulation commonly seen in MDD [38]. Additionally, we observed that compared to the NCT group, MDD patients with CT have reduced top-down functional connectivity from the mPFC.L to the AMY.L. In line with previous fMRI studies, Grant et al. found that, compared to those without early trauma, MDD individuals exposed to trauma displayed a lack of inhibitory mPFC to AMY flow during an emotional task (failure of inhibition), indicating that CT specifically disrupts frontal regulation of the AMY in MDD [39]. Cisler et al.‘s review explained that the weakened connectivity between the mPFC and AMY is conceptualized as a reduction in top-down control of the AMY by the mPFC [40]. This decoupling is theorized to underlie heightened sad affect in trauma survivors. A recent EEG study by Jaiswal et al. found that individuals with higher CT scores had lower connectivity in the theta-band prefrontal/insula salience network [41]. Early life threats lead to persistent prefrontal atrophy and increased AMY excitability [42]. In summary, these findings collectively emphasize the critical role of AMY-mPFC connectivity in the pathophysiology of MDD and highlight the exacerbating effect of CT on these neural circuits. Previous rat studies found restoration of mPFC-AMY information transfer could improve exploratory behavior [43]. In the future, we can develop in vitro non-invasive neuromodulation techniques targeting this loop to improve the prognosis of this subtype.

Another important finding of this study is that the theta-band ITPC in mPFC.L may serve as a neurophysiological bridge between CT and SA in individuals with MDD. A substantial body of previous neuroimaging evidence related to suicide points to the mPFC [44, 45]. However, constrained by traditional neuroimaging techniques, a trade-off between temporal and spatial resolution is required. Based on blood oxygen signals with relatively low temporal resolution, fMRI study reported functional changes in the mPFC related to SA in MDD [46]. Lee et al. found that theta-band activity in the central prefrontal region (limited spatial resolution) has potential clinical value as a biomarker for suicide using EEG [47]. Combining spatial and temporal resolution, current findings in this study provides new insight into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying suicidal behavior in MDD. mPFC is critical in participating in functions such as decision making, emotion regulation, and self-awareness. Early adverse experiences can result in lasting alterations in the structure and function of the mPFC. These changes may manifest as a decrease in theta band ITPC, indicating a diminished ability to synchronize the response activity of mPFC to emotional stimuli. Such deficits may impair an individual’s ability to effectively regulate sad emotion, leading patients to be more prone to suicidal thoughts and behaviors in the face of stress and sad emotion.

Although some findings were not central to our hypotheses, they are nonetheless noteworthy. First, beta ERD in mPFC.L was reduced in MDD with CT. Reductions in beta power are typically viewed as indicative of desynchronized underlying neuronal activity, which facilitates the processing of sensory input [48]. Furthermore, it has been proposed that reductions in beta power correspond to the amount of information that requires encoding [49]. Consequently, diminished beta ERD may impair information encoding and disrupt multimodal integration, which is essential for effective emotional processing. Second, the CT group displayed reduced gamma ERS in the AMY.L. Gamma oscillations are thought to play a role in various cognitive functions, including the processing of emotions. Prior research has indicated that patients with MDD exhibit increased gamma power in response to emotional stimuli, which may be linked to heightened arousal and cognitive reactivity [50]. This phenomenon is consistent with what we observed in the group without CT. Less gamma ERS might suggest a unique pattern underlying emotion processing about CT.

However, there are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the underlying etiology of mPFC and AMY hypofunction has not been identified. Further structural anatomical studies are needed to explore the relationship between structural and functional abnormalities. Secondly, although the present study identified an association between neurophysiological changes and suicidal behavior, the causality of this association is not yet fully established and needs to be explored in greater depth through longitudinal studies and animal models, among other methods. Thirdly, although the clinical subjects recruited in this study were either first-episode unmedicated patients or relapsing patients without mood stabilizers, antidepressants, or antipsychotics in the past 6 weeks, some participants took Z-drugs or benzodiazepines at night prior to the experiment. Benzodiazepines [51–53] and Z-drugs [54] will influence brain oscillations. Given that these medications are short-acting, their impact on MEG measurements was relatively minimal for an experiment conducted the subsequent afternoon. Additionally, drug use was included as a covariate in the analysis, and the findings remained unchanged. Finally, MEG was utilized to investigate the activities of deep subcortical brain structures including AMY. MEG’s signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) decreases with source depth. To minimize signal distortions and enhance the SNR, MEG recordings were carried out in a magnetically shielded room, head motion was restricted, ICA was applied and artifact components were removed. Data was also projected onto the source space using a LCMV beamformer equipped with an adaptive linear spatial filtering. This source localization technique can reveal weak sources of interest, offer a better localization accuracy and provide a superior detection sensitivity of local intracortical activity. The LCMV beamformer was previously applied to resting-state MEG data to explore the correlations between deep areas [55].

In conclusion, MDD patients with CT exhibited neurophysiological abnormalities, particularly in theta-band activity and neural connectivity. Furthermore, theta-band ITPC in mPFC.L, affected by CT, could increase the risk of SA. Taken together, these findings support our hypothesis that local theta abnormalities and dysconnection between brain areas underlie sad emotion processing deficits in MDD with CT, providing potential neurobiological targets for preventing and intervening in suicidal behavior in this subgroup.
