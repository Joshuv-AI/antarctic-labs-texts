Spatio-temporal envolutional graph neural network for traffic flow prediction in UAV-based urban traffic monitoring system
Authors: Wenming Ma, Zihao Chu, Hao Chen, Mingqi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the advancement of modern UAV technology, UAVs have become integral to creating traffic management monitoring systems. Additionally, UAV-based traffic monitoring systems can predict traffic flow by integrating machine learning methods. Specifically, traffic flow data contains both spatial and temporal information, which can be effectively processed by graph neural networks (GNNs). However, GNNs often face the challenge of oversmoothing, which hinders their ability to capture complex structures in the data. The Spatio-Temporal Graph Ordinary Differential Equations (STGODE) model addresses this issue by introducing Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) to construct deeper GNNs. Despite this, STGODE relies on initially predefined semantic neighborhood matrices, which do not adapt well to the dynamic nature of traffic information. To overcome this limitation, we propose an evolutionary graph neural network for traffic prediction, capable of continuously updating the semantic adjacency matrix throughout the training process. This dynamic evolution of the semantic adjacency matrix allows it to adapt to the features and semantic relations of the current data, enhancing its ability to capture the complexity and variability of traffic patterns. We validate our approach through experiments on several real-world datasets, demonstrating that our method outperforms state-of-the-art benchmarks.Keywords: Unmanned aerial vehicle, Traffic monitoring system, Graph neural network, Traffic flow predictionSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Computer science

Introduction

The rapid pace of urbanization and the continuous increase in automobile ownership have created a significant global challenge: traffic congestion. This pervasive issue not only imposes substantial economic costs and exacerbates environmental degradation but also profoundly affects the well-being of urban populations. As a result, mitigating traffic congestion has become a critical focus of research aimed at addressing these concerns and optimizing transportation systems. Traffic flow prediction is a key area of study that involves forecasting future traffic conditions based on historical traffic data and various relevant factors, such as vehicle speeds, travel times, and congestion levels. Effective management of urban transport networks relies heavily on comprehensive database support, with spatial distribution information of traffic being one of the most crucial components. This information is especially vital in atypical situations, such as road accidents, construction work, vehicle or infrastructure failures, or other incidents that necessitate the redirection of traffic flow.

In such scenarios, traffic data collected by remote sensing equipment over successive time intervals are used to dynamically construct and update the spatial distribution of traffic. This capability is essential for the effective management of urban road networks, enabling the identification of optimal routes in real-time and significantly contributing to the alleviation of traffic congestion. Remote sensing technology has a broad range of applications in traffic management. For instance, sensors and UAVs deployed on roads can monitor vehicle flow, speed, and traffic congestion in real-time, providing critical data support for traffic management and planning. By leveraging these technologies, we can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of urban transportation systems, ultimately enhancing the quality of life in urban areas.

The use of UAVs in traffic forecasting is revolutionizing traditional traffic monitoring and management methods1. UAVs can swiftly cover large areas and collect high-resolution traffic data in real-time, including traffic flow, speed, and lane occupancy, providing more accurate and timely inputs for traffic prediction models. Utilizing advanced image processing and machine learning methods, UAVs can automatically detect and classify different vehicle types, identify traffic accidents, and pinpoint congestion areas, enabling traffic management authorities to take swift actions to optimize traffic flow. Additionally, UAVs can rapidly reach the scene of a traffic accident to provide real-time video and imagery, aiding in quick assessment and decision-making, thereby enhancing accident handling efficiency and reducing secondary incidents and congestion. Data collected by UAVs can be integrated with existing traffic management systems to optimize traffic signal settings, dynamically adjust traffic flow, and predict future traffic patterns. By monitoring the environmental impact of traffic, UAVs support the development of eco-friendly transport policies and reduce traffic-related pollution. Furthermore, UAVs can regularly inspect transport infrastructure, identifying potential safety hazards and improving maintenance efficiency. As a crucial component of Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), UAVs achieve comprehensive, multi-level traffic monitoring and management when combined with other sensors and communication technologies. Overall, the application of UAVs2 in traffic prediction holds significant promise and potential. This approach not only enhances the efficiency and accuracy of data collection but also markedly improves traffic management and accident response capabilities through real-time monitoring and rapid response, providing robust support for the development of intelligent transport systems3.

The California Department of Transportation utilizes remote sensing technology to collect traffic data over time, resulting in the creation of the Freeway Performance Measurement System (PeMS4). This data includes extensive traffic information, such as traffic flow for each road and vehicle speed, making it invaluable for developing traffic flow prediction models. Traffic data is collected over time by sensors installed on the roads, and UAVs gather this information to construct spatiotemporal traffic maps during each data collection. These maps, rich in detailed information, are crucial for operations like traffic prediction. The research presented in this paper is also based on data collected through remote sensing technology.

The importance of accurate traffic flow prediction cannot be overstated, as it forms the foundation of efficient traffic management. By providing transportation authorities with actionable insights, precise traffic flow prediction enables proactive planning, judicious resource allocation, and informed decision-making related to traffic control and system optimization. Essentially, it is crucial for implementing multifaceted strategies aimed at alleviating congestion and promoting a more sustainable urban environment.

In recent years, the rapid development of data science, machine learning, and computational methods has led to significant advancements in traffic flow prediction techniques. Traditionally, traffic prediction relied on time-series analysis models such as Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA5)and Vector Autoregression (VAR6). However, these models often struggle with the complex nonlinear spatiotemporal dynamics of traffic data. To address these limitations, researchers have started using traditional machine learning models like Support Vector Regression (SVR7) and k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN8), achieving some improvements but still heavily reliant on manually engineered features.

Expanding on the integration of deep learning techniques into traffic prediction methods, recent advancements have witnessed a discernible trend towards the adoption of deep neural networks. These methodologies epitomize a paradigm shift towards a more data-centric approach in dynamic traffic modeling, thereby garnering substantial interest within the research community. Of particular prominence among these approaches is the spatiotemporal graph neural network (STGNN9), which has ascended as a frontrunner among the pantheon of frameworks for traffic prediction. Within the STGNN architecture, each node within the graph corresponds to a discrete traffic observation point, while edges delineate the interconnectedness between road traffic nodes, collectively imbuing the model with a network-centric structural framework. Crucially, STGNN harnesses the power of graph convolutional blocks to facilitate spatial processing, synergistically amalgamating this capability with forward computation functions that intricately simulate temporal dynamics. Notably, the efficacy of methods predicated upon STGNN hinges upon the choice of forward computation functions, delineating pathways that diverge into distinct trajectories within the landscape of deep learning. Specifically, these methods bifurcate into two primary categories based on their utilization of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or convolutional neural networks (CNNs), each conferring unique advantages and exhibiting nuanced performance characteristics.

A salient hallmark of these CNN-based methodologies is their intrinsic capacity to obviate the need for laborious manual feature engineering, circumventing the arduous task of handcrafting features that typifies traditional machine learning paradigms. Instead, these techniques harness the innate power of hierarchical feature learning, thereby endowing them with a heightened aptitude for discerning intricate patterns and latent correlations embedded within the complex fabric of traffic dynamics. Consequently, they engender superior prediction accuracy whilst concurrently mitigating the cognitive overhead associated with feature engineering, thereby constituting a seminal leap forward in the realm of traffic flow forecasting.

However, graph neural network (GNN) models have inherent limitations, particularly when applied to large-scale traffic networks with numerous nodes and edges. Traditional graph convolutional network (GCN) methods encounter computational and storage challenges with such extensive graphs, resulting in relatively shallow networks that struggle to capture long-distance spatial correlations. Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs10) represent a significant advancement in contemporary deep learning methodologies. Unlike conventional neural networks, which have a fixed number of layers and parameters throughout training, NODEs adopt a continuous-time framework, treating data as a dynamic process evolving over time. The key innovation of NODEs is viewing the model’s depth as a temporal continuum. Instead of being limited by a rigid architecture with a fixed number of layers, the depth of a NODE model evolves dynamically through the integration of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) over time. This adaptability allows NODEs to flexibly capture the complex temporal dynamics inherent in diverse datasets. By moving beyond the constraints of fixed-depth architectures, NODEs address the issue of oversmoothing commonly found in traditional GNN models. This enables the construction of deeper network architectures and the extraction of richer information embedded within the data.

STGODE11was the first to introduce ODE-based methods into spatiotemporal traffic prediction. However, it has limitations in the construction of the semantic adjacency matrix. The method constructs a fixed semantic adjacency matrix before the start of model training, but due to the complexity of traffic flow data, significant dynamic changes occur in both the temporal and spatial dimensions. A fixed semantic adjacency matrix is limited in its ability to capture these changes, potentially leading to suboptimal performance when the model is applied to predict the dynamic variations in traffic flow. This limitation highlights a critical gap in the existing research, underscoring the need for approaches that can dynamically adjust the semantic adjacency matrix to better capture the spatiotemporal variations in traffic flow data and improve prediction accuracy. To address this key issue, we propose an evolutionary graph neural network method, which offers a more effective solution for traffic flow prediction.

STGCN9 and ASTGCN12 are commonly used spatiotemporal graph convolutional network models that have achieved significant success in traffic flow prediction. However, both STGCN and ASTGCN rely on fixed semantic adjacency matrices, which present certain limitations in capturing the dynamic spatiotemporal variations in traffic flow data. Specifically, STGCN employs convolution operations to capture neighborhood information in the spatial dimension and uses convolutions to model temporal dynamics. However, its semantic adjacency matrix remains unchanged during training, making it difficult to adapt to the continuously evolving patterns in traffic flow data. ASTGCN attempts to address this issue by introducing spatiotemporal attention mechanisms, yet its semantic adjacency matrix also remains fixed, failing to fully capture the complex spatiotemporal variations inherent in traffic flow data.

To address this issue, we propose an evolutionary graph neural network for traffic prediction that continuously updates the semantic adjacency matrix during the training cycle. This dynamic adjustment process allows the matrix structure to evolve based on the features and semantic relations of the current data. This adaptability enables the model to better handle varying data distributions and feature changes, thereby improving prediction performance and generalization capability. Additionally, the evolving semantic adjacency matrix can promptly capture data changes, allowing the model to adapt more effectively to data distributions across different time points and spatial locations. During the matrix evolution process, we calculate the intersection between the current semantic neighborhood matrix and the initial matrix, ensuring that the evolution retains a degree of historical data information. This helps the model to remember past data while learning from new data, providing a better understanding of the data’s evolutionary trends and changes.

We modify a portion of the matrix by changing certain links from 1 to 0 after predefining the matrix, which can be interpreted as adding noise. This introduces a degree of randomness and ambiguity, reducing the model’s overfitting to the training data and enhancing its generalization ability, leading to better performance on unseen data. As the evolutionary process continues, we gradually and adaptively restore the elements that were initially set to 0, allowing the model to better accommodate changing traffic information. Related work

Traffic congestion stands as a pervasive challenge in urban environments, exerting profound impacts on various facets of society. Not only does it result in substantial economic losses due to productivity declines and increased fuel consumption, but it also exacerbates environmental pollution, contributing to greenhouse gas emissions and air quality deterioration. Moreover, the adverse effects of traffic congestion extend beyond economic and environmental realms, significantly impinging upon the quality of life for urban residents. Long commute times, heightened stress levels, and diminished accessibility to essential services are among the myriad consequences that underscore the urgency of addressing congestion in urban transportation systems.

By forecasting traffic conditions with greater accuracy and foresight, policymakers and transportation authorities can devise more effective strategies for managing traffic flow, deploying resources efficiently, and implementing targeted interventions to mitigate congestion hotspots. Moreover, accurate traffic flow prediction facilitates the development of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) equipped with real-time monitoring and adaptive control mechanisms, thereby enhancing overall system resilience and responsiveness to dynamic traffic conditions. In the subsequent sections, we present a comprehensive overview of the extant literature on traffic flow prediction, delineating recent advancements in traditional methodologies grounded in time series analysis, machine learning techniques, and the burgeoning field of deep learning.

How to collect more accurate traffic information is an important prerequisite for solving the problem of traffic congestion, and the development of remote sensing technology has made a great contribution to the more accurate collection of traffic information. Remote sensing technology is used to collect information on the ground through a variety of sensors, UAVs and other devices. In the field of transport, remote sensing technology is widely used to collect traffic data, including the number of vehicles, vehicle speed, traffic flow, road congestion and so on. These data are essential for urban traffic management, road planning, and traffic flow optimization. In terms of accuracy, traffic data collected by remote sensing technologies are usually highly accurate. This is because they can monitor the movement of vehicles on the road in real time and in some cases are able to identify the vehicle type and direction of travel.

However, the accuracy may be affected by a number of factors, such as weather conditions, equipment malfunctions, and data processing. Therefore, these factors need to be taken into account when using traffic data collected by remote sensing techniques, and data calibration and validation may be required to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data. The development of remote sensing technology in the transport sector is also advancing13,14. With advances in sensor and UAVs technology, the performance and accuracy of remote sensing equipment continue to improve. Meanwhile, the application of technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning has made the processing and analysis of traffic data more efficient and precise. In addition, integration with other information technologies, such as cloud computing, the Internet of Things and big data analysis, further expands the scope of application of remote sensing technology in the field of transport15,16, providing more possibilities for urban transport management and planning.

In the domain of traffic prediction, the academic community has traditionally relied on a range of conventional statistical methods and machine learning methods. Noteworthy among these are: (i) the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN8) paradigm elucidated by Zhang et al. (2013), which predicts the traffic flow at a given node by evaluating the traffic behavior of its k nearest topological neighbors; (ii) the ARIMA distribution structure described by van den17 which integrates autoregressive and moving average mechanisms for traffic flow prediction; (iii) the KNN paradigm elaborated by van den Berg et al. (1996), which predicts the vehicle flow at a specific node by assessing the traffic behavior of its k nearest topological neighbors; (iv) the KNN paradigm described by van den Berg et al. (1996), which integrates autoregressive and moving average mechanisms for traffic flow prediction; (v) the Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA18) technique detailed by Williams et al. (2003), which extends the prediction capabilities of ARIMA through an algorithm adapted to periodic changes. However, these machine learning frameworks, besides being constrained by manual feature derivation, also demonstrate a tendency towards time dependency while significantly overlooking spatial interconnectedness, thereby limiting their potential to autonomously extract predictive features relevant to the field of traffic prediction.

With the emergence of deep learning paradigms, there has been a notable surge in academic interest, primarily attributed to their inherent capability in delineating intricate spatiotemporal interdependencies central to data-centric challenges. Pioneering deep learning structural frameworks integrate spatial and temporal dimensions, including Fully Connected Long Short-Term Memory (FC-LSTM19), Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (ConvLSTM20), Spatiotemporal Residual Network (ST-ResNet21), Spatiotemporal Long Short-Term Memory (ST-LSTM22), and Spatiotemporal Convolutional Neural Networks (STCNN). These prototypes primarily employ convolutional and recurrent neural networks for analysis and inference, with their structures primarily designed for analyzing grid-like data topology, inadvertently disregarding the typical graph-like properties of traffic systems. In light of this, contemporary research has shifted towards utilizing graph-centric deep learning structures, particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs23), aimed at more intricately and comprehensively encapsulating spatial relationships existing within transportation infrastructure networks. For instance, a comprehensive review by Jiang24 explores the application of GNNs in traffic forecasting, highlighting their effectiveness in capturing complex spatial interactions between different road segments and nodes within a traffic network. This review discusses various GNN-based models, including Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks9, which integrate temporal convolutions to simultaneously model spatial and temporal dependencies. The review underscores the potential of GNNs to enhance traffic prediction accuracy by exploiting the structured nature of traffic data and improving the handling of dynamic traffic patterns.

For instance, STGCN9 is an exemplar model that significantly enhances traffic speed prediction by combining spatial and temporal information within a graph representation. Similarly, ASTGCN12 utilizes temporal and spatial attention mechanisms to gather critical information from data. DCRNN25 combines graph convolution with recurrent neural networks in an encoder-decoder manner. STG2Seq26 employs a multiple gated graph convolutional module and a seq2seq architecture with attention mechanisms to make multi-step predictions. DSANet27 is a correlated time series prediction system that uses CNN networks and a self-attention mechanism for spatial correlations. DHGNN28 leverages a hybrid graph-based neural network for data-driven traffic incident prediction. LRGCN29 introduces a framework for handling time-evolving graphs where the graph’s structure changes over time. SLTTCN30 leverages spatial linear transformers and a bidirectional temporal convolution network to predict future traffic flow by addressing dynamic spatial correlations and temporal dependencies. LSTSC31 captures long-short term sequence correlations using attention mechanisms in the proposed multi-modal attention neural network, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of traffic flow predictions. ASTGNN32 is able to effectively capture the dynamic changes, periodicity and spatial heterogeneity of traffic data in both temporal and spatial dimensions by designing new self-attention mechanisms and dynamic graph convolution modules to improve the prediction accuracy. Liu et al.33 proposed a multiscale spatio-temporal attention-based mechanism to capture actions, exemplifying the wide range of applications of spatio-temporal attention mechanisms. LFA-ConvLSTM is a model based on the spatio-temporal unit of interpretable local flow attention mechanism (LFA) for traffic flow prediction. The method explains the relationship between different traffic flows well. LFA-ConvLSTM34 combines the ConvLSTM module to learn traffic-specific features, the LFA module to model the correlation between traffic flows, and the feature aggregation module to merge the above two, which improves the prediction results well. Lastly, Z-GCNETs35 propose a new time-aware zigzag topological-based spatio-temporal model. However, these methods often handle temporal and spatial information in isolation, disregarding the interdependent relationship between these two aspects of the data. To address this shortfall, STSGCN36 introduces a novel approach, concurrently constructing models in both the temporal and spatial domains, thus unifying spatial and temporal components for a more comprehensive analysis and prediction. The emergence of graph-based models37,38 heralds a new era in traffic flow prediction, offering the ability to capture intricate spatiotemporal dependencies in real-world traffic data. This shift enables more accurate predictions, thereby bolstering traffic management, optimizing transportation systems, and ultimately enhancing people’s travel experiences.

In summary, graph-based models such as STGCN, ASTGCN, and STSGCN demonstrate the ability to capture complex spatio-temporal dependencies in real-world traffic data, offering a transformative approach to traffic flow prediction. These models hold promise for significantly enhancing traffic management, optimizing traffic systems, and improving the overall travel experience. Nevertheless, these models are not immune to the common issue of oversmoothing encountered in GNN models, which limits their ability to build deeper models and extract more profound spatio-temporal representations. While traditional neural networks tend to perform better with increased depth due to their capacity to learn intricate feature representations, graph neural networks (GNNs) often do not experience the same benefit. Research indicates that GNN performance peaks with just two layers, and additional layers can actually degrade performance39. The primary reason for this is oversmoothing, where increasing the number of layers causes node representations to converge to similar values, thereby impairing the model’s ability to capture rich spatial features. This issue severely restricts the depth of GNNs, making it challenging to capture deeper feature representations. In the context of traffic flow prediction, GNN models are similarly affected, and there is a notable lack of studies addressing the importance of network depth for capturing long-term dependencies in spatio-temporal forecasting.

Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) have seen growing interest in their application to machine learning, particularly for modeling continuous-time dynamics. A comprehensive survey by Rackauckas40 provides an in-depth overview of ODEs and their integration with numerical methods and machine learning. This review covers the theoretical foundations of ODEs, their numerical solutions, and their application in various fields, including machine learning. It highlights the development of Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) as a novel approach for continuous-time modeling, emphasizing their advantages in handling irregular time-series data and providing efficient memory usage. The survey also discusses various extensions and applications of ODEs in deep learning frameworks, illustrating their versatility and impact on modern machine learning techniques. Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs10) represents the forefront paradigm of deep learning, proposed by Ricky T. Q. Chen in a seminal research paper in 2018. Traditional neural networks employ fixed layers and parameters throughout the entire training process, whereas neural ODEs differ in that they adopt a continuous-time perspective, modeling data as a dynamic process evolving over time, thus fundamentally altering this approach. The fundamental concept of neural ODEs is to view the depth of the model as a continuous time process. The depth of neural ODE models is not strictly defined by pre-determined layers but dynamically evolves by integrating ordinary differential equations (ODEs41) that change over time. This adaptive adjustment of model complexity enables neural ODEs to flexibly capture the inherent complexity of the data. This approach addresses the oversmoothing issue frequently encountered in traditional GNN models, thereby enabling the construction of deeper network models and extraction of more information. NODEs have been applied in various research domains, including graph neural networks and traffic flow prediction. Specifically, as the only traffic prediction method based on ODEs, Spatial-Temporal Graph Ordinary Differential Equations (STGODE11) effectively leverages the advantages of ODEs, enabling the construction of deeper models to extract richer information. We introduced its shortcomings in the previous section, the semantic adjacency matrix generated at the initial stage is not well adapted to the complexity and variability of traffic data.

Recent approaches have increasingly employed neural differential equations (NDEs) to process traffic information. For example, STG-NCDE42 combines graph neural networks (GNN) and neural controlled differential equations (NCDE) for traffic prediction. This method excels at handling complex time-series data with external control inputs. However, it has limitations. Compared to our model, STG-NCDE trains temporal and spatial data separately and does not adequately address the rich semantic information in traffic data, resulting in potentially suboptimal predictions when semantic content is high. MTGODE43 combines dynamic graph neural networks and ordinary differential equations (ODEs). The model addresses the discontinuities, high complexity and dependence on predefined graph structures of discrete neural structures through continuous time aggregation and continuous graph propagation mechanisms. However, when MTGODE solves the problem of dependence on predefined graph structures based on continuous time aggregation and continuous graph propagation mechanism, it ignores the problem of dealing with the complex and changing semantic information of traffic information, which changes with time and other factors, which is overcome by our model. In contrast, GRAM-ODE44 utilizes the same neural ordinary differential equation (NODE) framework as our model. It incorporates multiple interconnected ODE-GNN modules to capture diverse views of complex local and global spatio-temporal dependencies, leading to improved representations. However, GRAM-ODE also lacks in leveraging the semantic information within traffic data. Our model, featuring the Envolutional Graph Neural Network, effectively exploits the rich semantic information inherent in traffic data, providing a more comprehensive solution for traffic prediction.

In the field of spatio-temporal prediction, the spatio-temporal generative graph sparse coding method is widely applied. Its primary function is to extract representative spatio-temporal features from dynamic traffic data, thereby providing more accurate information support for traffic flow prediction. The ST-SGCCaps45 method captures complex patterns within spatio-temporal graphs, effectively extracting key features that represent changes in the traffic system. These features include dynamic relationships of traffic flow variations over time and space. By leveraging sparse coding techniques, ST-SGCCaps compresses high-dimensional traffic data into low-dimensional sparse representations, thereby reducing computational complexity and enhancing model generalization capabilities. DTIGNN46models network-level traffic flow as a dynamic graph influenced by traffic signals and predicts future traffic flow data from generated spatio-temporal sparse graphs, effectively handling spatio-temporal traffic data. In summary, using spatio-temporal generative graph sparse coding to process traffic information effectively extracts important spatio-temporal features from the data, and our model, employing an evolving network, better adapts to changes in traffic information.

We propose an evolutional graph neural network for traffic prediction, which is able to update the semantic adjacency matrix according to the iteration of the training cycle, and the evolution process of the semantic adjacency matrix can dynamically adjust the structure of the matrix according to the features and semantic relations of the current data, while retaining the historical information of the data, which improves the ability of the model to adapt to the complex and variable traffic data. We also designed the initial matrix adjustment, which simply means replacing about 50% of the values in the semantic adjacency matrix with 0. This introduces a certain degree of randomness and ambiguity, which can reduce the degree of overfitting of the model to the training data and improve the generalization ability of the model, thus obtaining better performance on unknown data.

Preliminaries

In this section, we present the definition of the traffic prediction problem and the construction of two types of adjacency matrices.

Traffic flow forecast

We define the road network as a graph G=(V,E,A), where V denotes a collection of N nodes, E represents edges, and A∈RN×N signifies the adjacency matrix. The spatial characteristics of the traffic network are delineated by the topological arrangement of the graph G, while temporal details are encoded in the feature attributes of the nodes. At any given time t, each node i is associated with an observation vector xti∈RF, where F denotes the length of the observation vector. The observations across all nodes at time t are denoted as Xt=xt1,xt2,…,xtN∈RN×F. The collective observations of all nodes over all times are represented as X=X1,X2,…,XT∈RT×N×F. The primary aim in predicting traffic flow is to forecast future traffic conditions based on historical data within a defined temporal context, encapsulated by the input tensor X. This predictive task is achieved by defining a mapping function denoted as f. By analyzing past traffic data rigorously, the function f extracts implicit patterns and tendencies, thereby providing predictive insights into the potential trajectory of traffic flow. Through the development of analytical models that capture the complex dynamics of the traffic network G, the rich information contained within X is leveraged to facilitate the training of the mapping function f. This enables the model to generate accurate and reliable predictions of future traffic scenarios, thus enhancing our capacity to manage and optimize traffic flow. Given the tensor X recorded within a transportation network G, the objective of traffic forecasting is to establish a mapping function f that utilizes the historical T observations to forecast future traffic patterns for a subsequent duration T′.

Methods

In this section we describe how the data was collected, the use of the data and an introduction to our methodology.

Traffic flow prediction in UAV-based urban traffic monitoring system

In the previous section, we introduced the process of traffic information collection, involving the deployment of sensors on roads for a specified duration and utilizing UAVs to collect data from these sensors. The collected data enables UAVs to construct spatio-temporal information maps of traffic, which are rich in detail and crucial for subsequent operations such as traffic prediction. The data collection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. However, before this information can be used for traffic prediction, further processing is necessary. In addition to spatio-temporal information, the data contains rich semantic details such as traffic accidents and emergencies, which significantly influence traffic flow prediction. Therefore, after data collection by UAVs, additional processing is required to construct spatial adjacency matrices and semantic adjacency matrices for accurate traffic flow prediction. Given the complexity and variability of traffic data, the construction of semantic adjacency matrices is particularly pivotal in adapting to the dynamic nature of traffic conditions.

Our model’s traffic flow prediction process begins with the construction of spatial and semantic adjacency matrices using data from the historical traffic dataset. The input data is initially processed by the Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for temporal information processing. Following the solution, temporal information undergoes further integration and processing through TCN. Finally, prediction results are obtained via the maximum pooling layer and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP).

Experimental settings

The experimental procedures were carried out on a server running Windows, equipped with specific hardware components. The central processing unit (CPU) utilized was an AMD Ryzen 7 6800H, paired with Radeon Graphics to deliver significant computational capabilities. Additionally, the system featured a dedicated graphics processing unit (GPU), the NVIDIA®GeForce RTX 3060 laptop GPU, which supported efficient parallel processing and accelerated the computational tasks. Each network layer was designed with up to four blocks derived from the decisional constant micro-differential ADM-STNODE. The datasets were partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a 6:2:2 ratio. Historical data spanning one hour was employed to forecast future traffic conditions. The hidden dimensions for the TCN blocks were configured as 64, 32, and 64, respectively. The regularization hyperparameter α was set to 0.8, with spatial adjacency matrix thresholds σ and ϵ set to 10 and 0.5, respectively, and the semantic adjacency matrix threshold ϵ was set to 0.6. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01. The network depth consists of six layers, and the model integrates two TCNs and one NODE, effectively forming two stacked combinations of these components. Both spatial and semantic adjacency matrices were provided as separate inputs for model training.

Oversmoothing analysis

In the field of traffic flow prediction, traditional graph neural network (GNN) architectures have achieved impressive results. Nevertheless, these models still face a critical impasse, especially at advanced stages of deep training. This dilemma mainly stems from the oversmoothing problem, i.e., the representations of the nodes tend to converge after successive layers of graph convolution, thus obliterating the uniqueness of each node and ultimately inhibiting the improvement of the prediction accuracy during the iterative training process.

The Spatio-Temporal Graph Ordinary Differential Equation (STGODE) methodology emerges as a novel solution to the oversmoothing bottleneck problem by seamlessly integrating the Noodular Ordinary Differential Equation (NODE) into the GNN framework. Based on the STGODE methodology, we introduce EG-NODE, which not only supports the NODE-centric paradigm, but also provides an innovative treatment of the traffic information adjacency matrix. We perform a comprehensive empirical test by using the PeMSD4 traffic dataset, it maintains robustness to supersmoothing irrespective of the network depth without sacrificing its predictive power.

The evaluation metrics we use to quantify the performance of different architectural configurations are MAE and RMSE. Figure 5 graphically depicts the performance of the model as a function of network depth. As an example of loss MAE on PeMSD4 dataset, as the depth of the model is gradually increased from 2 to 6, the loss of our model does not increase with the depth, but rather the effect is getting better and better, from 20.1 to 19.68. It is noteworthy that EG-NODE consistently maintains the representation of the major nodes and shows no signs of performance degradation with increasing layering. The experimental results clearly demonstrate that our EG-NODE model can easily cope with the ultra-smoothing challenge and completely outperforms traditional deep GNN frameworks in prediction tasks. The resilience demonstrated by the model in refining complex and persistent spatio-temporal traffic patterns solidifies its value from both empirical and theoretical perspectives.

To analyze the phenomenon of over-smoothing related to random initialization, we removed the random initialization setting from our model. This means that in the initial semantic adjacency matrix, we did not set 50% of the ones to zero. The experimental results are clearly illustrated in Fig. 5, where “no-RI” represents the absence of random initialization. The results indicate that the addition or removal of random initialization does not affect the over-smoothing phenomenon.

Threshold analysis

Introducing a threshold in the adjacency matrix significantly impacts the model’s performance in several ways. By removing weaker connections, the adjacency matrix becomes sparser. This sparsity reduces the computational complexity of the model, as fewer connections need to be processed during training and inference. Consequently, this speeds up computation and lowers memory requirements, which is crucial for handling large-scale datasets such as PeMSD4. Real-world data often contains noise or irrelevant connections that can negatively affect model performance. By setting a threshold, these weaker and potentially noisy connections are filtered out, resulting in a clearer and more robust data representation.

To empirically analyze the impact of different threshold values on the performance of the semantic adjacency matrix constructed by our model, we conducted experiments to calculate the MAE, RMSE, and MAPE for various thresholds on the real datasets PeMSD4 and PeMSD3. The results are clear that in terms of the results on the dataset PeMSD4, the model achieves the best results at a threshold size of 0.6. As shown in Fig. 6, based on the data observation and analysis, it can be seen that at a threshold value of 0.6, the model achieves the best results under all three metrics. At this threshold, MAE is 19.25, RMSE is 31.37, and MAPE is 12.71. Therefore, the threshold value of 0.6 can be considered as the parameter setting that achieves the best results. This suggests that a well-chosen threshold can balance the trade-off between maintaining significant connections and removing noise, thereby optimizing the overall model performance.

Sample complexity analysis

To thoroughly evaluate the sample complexity of our proposed method, we designed a series of experiments aimed at observing the model’s performance under varying sample sizes. Specifically, we randomly selected data covering different continuous time spans-three, five, and seven days-within the PeMSD4 and PeMSD8 datasets. By using these datasets of different scales, we were able to assess the impact of sample size on the model’s performance. In our experiments, we employed multiple evaluation metrics to comprehensively measure the model’s performance, including MAE, MAPE, and RMSE. These metrics capture different aspects of prediction accuracy, reflecting the absolute error, relative error percentage, and the average squared error, respectively.

The experimental results are shown in Fig. 8. The experimental results show a significant improvement in model performance as the data volume increases. Specifically, when the sample size is smaller, the model exhibits relatively high errors, likely due to the insufficient data to fully capture the complexity of the dataset, which limits the model’s generalization capability. However, as the sample size increases to five or seven days, the model’s error decreases markedly, indicating a better understanding of data trends and patterns, leading to improved predictive accuracy. Further analysis reveals that data spanning longer periods not only includes more samples but also captures more short-term and long-term trends, which is particularly crucial for traffic flow prediction. Consequently, as the sample size increases, the model’s performance stabilizes and continues to improve. These results validate the robustness of our method when handling different sample sizes, demonstrating that in a big data environment, the method can more effectively learn and predict complex traffic flow patterns.

Ablation experiment

Our model consists of several modules, including random initialization, graph evolution, and Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN). In our experiments, we conducted ablation studies on our proposed model by removing or modifying some of these modules. Specifically, EG-NODE has four variants: (1) No Initial Matrix Adjustment (NOIMA): This variant removes the initial matrix adjustment. (2) No Grape Evolutional (NOGE): This variant eliminates the evolution of the semantic adjacency matrix. (3) No Front Temporal Convolutional Network (NOFTCN): In this variant, the TCN module before the NODE solver is removed. (4) No Back Temporal Convolutional Network (NOBTCN): Here, the TCN module after the NODE solver is removed. We evaluated these four model structures, along with our unmodified model, on four datasets. The performance of these models was quantitatively assessed using established metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). These metrics provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating and comparing the prediction accuracy of different configurations and datasets.

The experimental results, as depicted in Fig. 9, unequivocally substantiate the superior performance of the EG-NODE model over the NOGE model across four distinct datasets. Specifically, the EG-NODE model achieves a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) that is 1.60 lower than that of NOGE on the PeMSD3 dataset, 2.42 lower on the PeMSD4 dataset, and 2.29 and 2.55 lower on the PeMSD7 and PeMSD8 datasets, respectively. Despite demonstrating superior performance compared to NOGE, the NOIMA model still falls short of the EG-NODE model, thereby highlighting the latter’s efficacy.

Ablation experimental results.

The comparative analysis of each module further elucidates that NOGE yields the poorest performance, thereby underscoring the significant role that graph evolution operations play in enhancing the model’s effectiveness. Furthermore, alterations to the TCN modules in NOFTCN and NOBTCN lead to a detrimental impact on the model’s performance, thus validating the indispensable necessity of both TCN modules for optimal performance.

Conclusions

In this study, we introduce EG-NODE, a meticulously designed model adept at capturing the complex temporal fluctuations in vehicular traffic. Our methodology has been rigorously validated across five diverse real-world traffic datasets. This comprehensive series of experiments involved benchmarking our model against 16 carefully chosen baseline models. The empirical evidence from this rigorous analysis consistently confirms the superior predictive performance of EG-NODE, substantiating its reliability across various traffic conditions and scenarios. Certainly, our model has room for improvement, especially in the construction of the spatial adjacency matrix. In our method, the semantic adjacency matrix evolves iteratively during training, allowing it to adapt well to the complex and dynamic nature of traffic flow information. However, our spatial adjacency matrix is predefined and remains unchanged throughout the training process, which is a limitation. In future work, we believe that transforming the construction of the spatial adjacency matrix into a dynamic process, in tandem with the evolving semantic adjacency matrix, could better handle the complex and variable spatiotemporal traffic information.
