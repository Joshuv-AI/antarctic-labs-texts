Immersive versus non-immersive virtual reality in improving upper limb function among individuals with subacute hemiplegia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Athira George, Sathees Kumar Durairaj, Praveen Kumar Kandakurti, Animesh Hazari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVirtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising tool in neurorehabilitation to improve motor function in individuals with hemiplegia. VR applications are typically categorized into immersive and non-immersive types, both of which have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing upper limb function. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of immersive virtual reality (IMVR) and non-immersive virtual reality (NIVR) therapies in improving upper limb motor function in individuals with subacute hemiplegia.MethodsThis single-blinded randomized controlled trial included 30 participants (aged 25–40 years, both male and female) with subacute hemiplegia. Individuals with pre-existing musculoskeletal or neurological conditions affecting the upper limb, severe motion sickness, photosensitivity, or perceptual deficits were excluded. Participants were randomly allocated into two groups: Both groups initially received conventional physiotherapy, following which the immersive VR and non-immersive VR interventions were administered to the respective group. Each session lasted 60 min, with three sessions per week for six weeks. Assessments were conducted at baseline, post-intervention (6 weeks), and follow-up (6 months) using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), and Stroke-Specific Quality of Life Scale (SS-QOL). The System Usability Scale (SUS) and User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) were used to evaluate the safety and usability of both VR methods.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the groups. SUS and UEQ scores also showed no significant differences in user experience. However, IMVR demonstrated statistically significant improvements in FMA, WMFT, and SS-QOL scores compared to NIVR at both post-test and follow-up (p < 0.05).ConclusionBoth immersive and non-immersive VR therapies were effective in enhancing upper limb motor function and quality of life among individuals with subacute hemiplegia. However, IMVR showed greater clinical significance in improving motor function and quality of life. Future research should explore the integration of VR across different stroke stages and focus on developing simple, task-specific games to further support rehabilitation.Trial registrationThis clinical trial was registered in clinicaltrials.gov under the trial ID NCT06615141 on September 26, 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04588-5.Keywords: Stroke rehabilitation, Upper extremity, Physical therapy modalities, Virtual reality, Immersive, Non-immersive, Health-related quality of life

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of death and disability among adults. Approximately 14.8% of stroke incidents occur in young adults, with a notable increase in incidence between 2015 and 2021, primarily due to elevated blood pressure levels [1]. Stroke often results in impairments in sensory, motor, and cognitive functions. Lower limb dysfunction can limit participation in social and community activities, while upper limb impairments compromise daily self-care and reduce quality of life [2]. Rehabilitation aims to enhance motor function and promote early independence. Physiotherapists commonly employ task-specific upper limb exercises to meet these goals. Repetitive task-oriented training has shown superior results in post-stroke rehabilitation [3]. However, patient motivation and engagement are crucial to sustaining therapy, making it essential for physiotherapists to design adaptive, personalized therapy programs with measurable feedback.

Over the past two decades, the application of virtual reality (VR) in stroke rehabilitation has significantly improved motor outcomes. VR training involves computer-generated simulations delivered through specialized hardware and software, enabling users to interact with virtual environments that replicate real-life scenarios [4].

Non-immersive virtual reality (NIVR) was one of the first VR modalities introduced in rehabilitation [5]. It enables users to interact with virtual environments from a third-person perspective, providing limited sensory engagement. Originally developed for educational purposes, NIVR was later adapted for motor rehabilitation. Technologies such as the Nintendo Wii, Microsoft Kinect, ReHabgame, and joystick-driven VR systems demonstrated promising results [6, 7]. These systems are accessible and cost-effective, and they have shown efficacy in enhancing motor function, although their long-term effectiveness in neurological conditions remains mixed [8].

In parallel, immersive virtual reality (IMVR) technologies emerged, offering users a highly interactive experience that simulates real-life activities. Using devices such as head-mounted displays (HMDs), haptic feedback devices, and data gloves, IMVR provides enhanced sensory engagement [9]. Popular systems include IREX (Immersive Rehabilitation Exercise), Reaching APE, CAVE (Cave Automatic Virtual Environment), EyeToy-based platforms, and MIDAS (Multi-sensorial Immersive Dynamic Autonomous System). These systems have demonstrated consistent improvements in post-stroke motor functions [10].

Many RCT studies compared immersive VR with Non-immersive VR in stroke population, and a recent systematic review indicated that IMVR may offer superior outcomes compared to NIVR in stroke rehabilitation [8–10]. However, there remains a lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials directly comparing the two VR modalities, particularly in subacute stroke populations. This study aims to address that gap by evaluating the effectiveness of IMVR and NIVR in enhancing upper limb function among individuals with subacute hemiplegia.

Materials and methods

Study design, settings and ethical approval

This randomized controlled trial was conducted at Thumbay Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Hospital, Ajman, UAE. The data was collected between September 2024 and February 2025. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Gulf Medical University (Ref: IRB-COHS-STD-75-FEB-2024) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06615141). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Participants’ inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants aged 25 to 40 years, of both sexes, diagnosed with subacute hemiplegia (2 weeks to 6 months post-stroke) were included in the study [1, 11]. This set of age ranges was specifically taken to address the increasing prevalence of stroke in younger adults [1], to reduce the influence of age-related neuroplasticity, and comorbidities. Inclusion criteria were participants with upper limb pectoral, biceps and long wrist flexor’s spasticity level ≤ 2 in Modified Ashworth Scale, Brunnstrom Stages of Motor Recovery for upper limb greater than stage 2, and the Cognitive Assessment Scale for Stroke Patients (CASP) score ≥ 35 out of 36 [12]. Exclusion criteria included severe motion sickness, photosensitive epilepsy, impaired spatial awareness using Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE), use of thrombolytic agents, medical instability, and pre-existing neurological conditions affecting upper limb function (e.g., spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis).

Sample size, sampling method, and randomization

The sample size was calculated based on the FMA-UE — Fugl–Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity [13]. using the G*Power 3.1 software [14] assuming a medium effect (Cohen’s d = 0.50), two-tailed α = 0.05, and desired power (1–β) = 0.80, resulting in 24 participants. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, the total sample size of N = 30 was recruited via convenience sampling. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. Each number was sealed in an envelope and selected by the participants, determining their group allocation. A single-blinded design was adopted, with the outcome assessor blinded to group assignments to minimize assessment bias. Participants’ allocation details are given in Fig. 1.

Outcome measures

The Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremities (FMA-UE) consists of 33 items categorized into four subsets: shoulder/elbow coordination, wrist coordination, hand coordination, and coordination/speed. Each item is scored from 0 to 2, with a total score ranging from 0 to 66; higher scores indicate better upper limb motor function. The FMA-UE demonstrates high inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.98) and intra-rater reliability (ICC = 0.96) [15]. The reported MCID for clinical significance for FMA is 10 points [16].

The Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) assesses upper limb motor function, strength, and coordination through 17 tasks. This study utilized the time-based functional activities (first six tasks). The WMFT has high reliability, with inter-rater reliability of 0.97 and intra-rater reliability of 0.90 [17]. The reported MCID for clinical significance for WMFT is 17% [18].

The Stroke Specific Quality of Life (SS-QOL) is a self-reported questionnaire consisting of 49 items across 12 domains. It demonstrates excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC: 0.83–0.99), strong content and construct validity, and effectively assesses stroke-related quality of life [19]. The reported MCID for clinical significance for SS-QOL is a 1.2-point change [20].

Data collection procedure

Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the intervention. Baseline assessments included the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), and the Stroke-Specific Quality of Life (SS-QOL) questionnaire.

Initially, all participants were undergoing their routine conventional physiotherapy session, including stretching, strengthening, voluntary control training – active and task-specific exercise, and 15–20 min of upper-limb training targeting muscles of gross and fine motor functions. Then all participants were randomly allotted into two groups: group 1 received immersive virtual reality (IMVR), and group 2 received non-immersive virtual reality therapy (NIVR). The games in both groups were selected based on the MDA framework (Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics) guidelines [21, 22] to mainly encourage a multi-directional range of motion of the shoulder (flexion, extension, internal, external rotation, abduction, adduction and scaption), elbow (flexion, extension, supination, pronation), and wrist joints (flexion, extension, radial, ulnar deviation) to facilitate object manipulation such as grasp, release, and dexterity control activities of hand function. These details are given in Table 1. All participants were asked to complete the system usability scale (SUS) to evaluate the user’s ease of interacting with both groups’ VR-based games, and any side effects were noted during the delivery of VR games. SUS is a short 10-item questionnaire consisting of 5 positive questions and 5 negative questions. Each question was rated with a Likert scale of 0 to 4, and total scores converted into 100. The mean SUS score of 68 is a useful benchmark to measure the usability of the equipment [23].

In group 1, the immersive VR therapy was delivered using Oculus Quest 3. This device engages the participants in a 360-degree virtual environment that simulates real-world activities designed to improve upper limb motor function. The therapy utilized the Reach Shoulder Health app. For increasing upper limb mobility, two games, i.e., Firefly Assessment and Bull’s Eye Shooting from the app, were used for reactions and control of upper limb function (Fig. 2a, b, c). Each game was played for 20 min with a 5-minute rest period.

Group 1 (immersive VR group)

In group 2, Non-Immersive VR therapy was delivered using the Neofect Smart Board and Tyromotion device (MYRO). The reasons for selecting two devices in this group were to include all the functional tasks used in the Oculus Quest 3 for the immersive group, enabling a standard comparison considering the Mechanics-Dynamics-Aesthetics (MDA) framework. These devices provide an opportunity for participants to interact with a virtual environment displayed on a screen using motion-tracking devices. Initially, Tyromotion MYRO was used with the Star Assessment for 10 min and followed by Catch the Falling items for 10 min with a 5-minute rest period. Then, two games were selected from the Neofect Smart Board: Feed the puppies for 10 min and Tennis for 10 min to match the bimanual function and ADL relevance (Fig. 2d, e, f, g).

Group 2 (non-immersive VR group)

Participants in both groups underwent VR therapy for 45 min per session, with three sessions per week, and continued for six weeks. Once all participants completed the 6-week intervention program, the post-test assessments were collected by using the same outcome measures used at the baseline period. Participants in both groups were asked to fill in the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) to assess the user experience of VR interventions. The UEQ-S had 8 questions, 4 questions for Pragmatic Quality and 4 questions for Hedonic Quality. Each question is graded by 7-point ordinal scale − 3 (very negative) to + 3 (very positive). Overall score more than 1.58 is indicate the excellent user experience [24].

Statistical analysis

The chi-squared test and independent t-test were used to compare the baseline characteristics of both groups. The inferential analysis began with a test for Normality, following which a repeated-measure ANOVA was used for within and between-group comparisons. All the analysis was performed in IBM SPSS 30.0. P-value was set at < 0.05 for significance.

Results

This study recruited 30 subacute stage stroke patients. The IMVR group had 53% male participants, and the NIVR group had 60% female participants. Both groups had identical right and left-sided stroke participants, with 60% of participants having left-sided stroke. All the study participants were diagnosed with ischemic type stroke, and their cognitive screening score was 36/36 in CASP. Also, this study had none of the participants with 0 and 1 + grade in the modified Ashworth scale for upper limb muscles and stage V & VI in Brunnstrom stages of upper limb motor function. These details are given in Table 2, and all baseline characters showed no statistically significant difference between groups. Hence, this showed both group participants were homogeneous and comparable.

The IMVR group achieved the MCID threshold in both FMA and WMFT outcome measures at both post-test and follow-up levels. The interventions did not meet the MCID threshold in the SSQOL outcome measure for both groups. Discussion

This study is among the few randomised clinical trials to use Oculus Quest-3 immersive VR technology, designed to evaluate the effectiveness of game-based, non-immersive virtual reality compared with immersive virtual reality combined with conventional physiotherapy in young, sub-acute stage stroke patients. The participation in the study was 100% with zero drop-out. The reason could be the inclusion of participants from the inpatient department of the study settings, availability of transportation, insurance policy, and the most advanced rehabilitation centre in the region. Games in both immersive and non-immersive groups were selected based on the MDA framework to check whether they were able to engage and motivate the participants. This was reflected in SUS and UEQ, as participants were very happy about playing games and user-friendliness in relation to both VR methods. VR therapy methods, both IMVR and NIVR methods, are producing cybersickness and other practical challenges to handling the equipment [25, 26]. However, in our study, the systems showed no side-effect incidence in both VR methods, suggesting that the given VR delivery systems are safe. The continuous user-friendly experience on handling VR systems in both groups suggests a high adaptability in general, but the NIVR group showed higher variation in their practice experience than the IMVR group. This might be due to the NIVR system, which can be disturbed by real-time environmental influences that alter the user experience.

This study showed a statistically significant effect for both IMVR and NIVR methods to improve the motor function (FMA and WMFT) and quality of life (SS-QOL) in stroke patients. Conventional physiotherapy with VR therapy is task-specific, goal-oriented, repetitive functional training that encourages a more repetitive functional range of motion in the upper extremity. The repetitive functional training is one of the basic and main principles of motor learning and produces the reorganization at the cortical level to increase the motor function after a stroke [27].

After 6 weeks of application, Immersive VR showed significant improvement over Non-immersive VR on upper limb motor function in FMA & WMFT, and activities of daily living (ADL) in SSQOL. In addition, the IMVR effect was better retained at the 6th month of follow-up than the NIVR methods. This study’s statistical findings were compared with the MCID score for clinical significance. The reported MCID for clinical significance for FMA is 10 points [16], WMFT is 17% [18], and SS-QOL is a 1.2-point change [20]. Comparatively, in our study, the IMVR group showed the clinical significance in FMA and WMFT assessments, but not in SS-QOL. The NIVR group showed clinical significance only in WMFT. This showed that IMVR intervention is more efficient in improving upper limb motor function (FMA & WMFT scales) in young subacute stroke patients over NIVR intervention. Similar to this study’s findings, a previous systematic review found that the IMVR is more effective for improving upper limb function in the FMA scale than NIVR, gaming consoles, and conventional rehabilitation [28]. Even though both VR interventions improved upper limb function-related quality of life in the SS-QOL scale statistically, they did not produce a clinically significant difference. This needed more exploration and analysis of the stroke-specific quality of life improvements.

IMVR vs. NIVR

Studies report that the IMVR intervention has a potential impact on improving upper limb motor function and ADL as assessed in FMA, WMFT, and SS-QOL, respectively [29, 30]. The task-oriented training with IMVR creates more association between the somatosensory-motor cortex level to develop more attention, task control, and visual network during hand function [31]. This visuo-motor process creates more human mirror neurons to activate the brain region and produce better motor control [32]. A recent study showed promising changes in HbO2 level in normal human brain regions that are equal level with those during IMVR [33]. IMVR provides an opportunity to use both hand actions while performing a task, which also creates more mirror neuron action and provides better performance than NIVR [34]. The IMVR is additionally providing opportunity to feel they are in real time simulated environment, gives immediate feedback during the motor action, visual satisfaction on performing difficult real time tasks, enhancing the continuous alert on involved task, reduce the boring sense on doing repetitive activities are further augment the task oriented repetitive training’s effect without causing fatigue or pain to the extremity [34].

This study used rehabilitation-specific NIVR therapy with conventional physiotherapy intervention to improve upper limb function in the FMA scale and SSQOL questionnaire. Although the multiple, heterogeneous NIVR provides more learning opportunities by visualizing the simulated action on screen, while doing action and aesthetic feeling, tactile sensory feedback [35], it did not produce better performance than IMVR in our study. The gamification in NIVR training produced motivation through the repetition of movement. NIVR participants followed a specific task-specific training during a conventional physiotherapy session, which might be a cause for improved motor performance for NIVR, but it is not superior to IMVR intervention. In addition, the lessor bimanual movement and ADL components in NIVR could have affected the findings of this study. This result needs to be studied in detail in the future. Moreover, the updated versions of IMVR are commercially available, and easy to practice in any environment adds advantages for IMVR over NIVR, and this area needs to be explored in future studies.

Limitations

The main limitation of this study was additional a group with conventional physiotherapy alone, which could provide an opportunity to identify the specific effectiveness of the VR interventions. Despite this study followed standard sample size calculation, it was conducted in a relatively small sample size, which limits the generalizability of the study results. Hence, this study recommends a larger sample size and multicentric trials to validate the results.

In addition, given the relatively small sample size, applying strict corrections (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) may have increased the risk of type II error and reduced the ability to detect meaningful effects. Therefore, we did not apply formal multiple comparison corrections, which could be a potential risk of type I error due to repeated comparisons. Also, the small sample size may limit statistical power, especially for detecting between-group differences in quality of life. The NIVR involved fewer bimanual movements and less ADL relevance, which might contribute to the difference in treatment outcomes rather than the distinction between Immersive VR and Non-Immersive VR.

Future directions

Future studies can incorporate kinematic data (movement trajectories, velocity, etc.) using devices such Neofact Smart board. Also, this study only included subacute stage stroke patients and was limited to a variety of games. So, this study can be conducted in the future by including a wider range of the population and at different durations. Even future studies can consider developing an ADL task-specific, cost-effective VR protocol that could create a major impact on disability prevention in the stroke population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that IMVR with conventional physiotherapy is a promising rehabilitation method than NIVR with conventional physiotherapy for enhancing the recovery of upper limb functional capabilities of young, subacute stroke patients. In spite of the IMVR clinical significance effects, further studies are needed to explore and develop simple, task-specific gamification to enhance brain reorganization in neurological patients.
