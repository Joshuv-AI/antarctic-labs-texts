Phytofabricated zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) from a medicinal salt-tolerant Oxystelma esculentum (L.f.) Sm. extract and their multifunctional biological activities
Authors: Moona Nazish, Sura Rahimova, Muhammad Zubair, Muhammad Fiaz Khan, Muhammad Azhar Khan, Fozia Bibi, Muhammad Ishfaq Khan, Saleem Ulla, Jobborov Bakhrom, Raheek Alwaznah, Islem Abid, Muhammad Tahir Naseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study accounts for the green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) using Oxalis esculentum extract, aiming to evaluate their physicochemical properties and biomedical potential. The synthesized ZnO-NPs were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). XRD confirmed the hexagonal nanocrystalline nature of the ZnO-NPs, having a particle size of 13 nm, while FTIR indicated the existence of functional groups from plant phytochemicals. SEM revealed a quasi-spherical shape with agglomeration tendency, and EDX confirmed the presence of zinc (60.4%) and oxygen (22%) elements. The ZnO-NPs exhibited notable antifungal activity, with maximum inhibition rates of 72.3% against Aspergillus niger, 72.2% against Penicillium notatum, and 69.3% against Fusarium oxysporum at 4 mg/mL. Antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli peaked at a 13.9 mm inhibition zone at 8 mg/mL. The highest antileishmanial activity was 57.33% at 160 µg/mL, while hemolytic activity remained low at 1.41 ± 0.22% at 200 µg/mL, indicating biocompatibility. Additionally, the ZnO-NPs showed 79% anti-inflammatory activity at 800 µg/mL, 63.11 ± 0.22% alpha-amylase inhibition at 400 µg/mL, 17.66 ± 0.55% α-glucosidase inhibition at 400 µg/mL, and antioxidant activities, including 51.7 ± 1.2% TAC, 42.4 ± 0.02%TRP, and 55.6 ± 1.4%FRSA at 200 µg/mL of the NPs. In conclusion, the phyto-fabricated ZnO-NPs demonstrated significant antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antioxidant properties, highlighting their potential for future biomedical applications.Keywords: ZnO-NPs, Characterization, Oxystelma esculentum, Pharmacological potential, Antimicrobial activitiesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Nanotechnology, which focuses on materials at the 1–100 nm scale, is recognized for its exceptional properties that lead to a variety of applications in different fields like agriculture, medicine, and the environment1. The particles that measure less than 100 nm in size are the nanocrystals, nanotubes, nanowires, and nanoflowers. Nanoparticles are appreciated for their minute dimensions, extensive surface area, and various structural features. Nanoparticle synthesis through chemical and physical processes is common nowadays2. The organic preparation method involves the use of plants3, bacteria4, algae5, and fungi4. The synthesis of bio-inspired nanoparticles is garnering vast attention due to their enormous benefits, which stem from their cost-effective and eco-friendly nature. Bio-inspired synthesis utilizes naturally occurring phytochemicals such as phenolics, flavonoids, and proteins as reducing and stabilizing agents, thereby eliminating the need for toxic chemicals and making the process more sustainable5. The rate of synthesis of plant-based NPs is brisk compared to microorganisms and exhibits greater stability as well. The plant-based nanoparticles preparation is very effective because of the richness in the rich biological molecules. The capping elements have a significant role in the nanoparticles’ stabilization through hydration interaction and electrostatic and steric stabilization. Nanoparticles’ functions and stability are directly related to several biological assays6. Metal oxide nanoparticles (MO NPs) also play a vigorous role in farming, nanomedicine, and environmental fields7.

ZnO-NPs have the highest utilization among all types of NPs because they exhibit tremendous biological and physicochemical characteristics and display harmless behavior8. ZnO-NPs have several prodigious characteristics such as optical transparency, electric conductivity, piezoelectricity, non-toxicity, cost-effectiveness, stability, and wide availability9. Nanoparticles have multiple applications in many fields, such as in pharmaceutical items, varnishes, optoelectronics, catalysis, solar batteries, paints, and plastic apparatuses10. They act as protective agents in maquillages and sunscreen products because they can filter ultraviolet irradiations11. ZnO-NPs can alter the cell membrane’s structural configuration by interacting with the phospholipid bilayer, which compromises the membrane’s integrity and function that leading to microbial death; therefore, these are considered best for their antibacterial and antifungal activity12. The antifungal and antibacterial activities of ZnO-NPs prepared using aqueous plant extracts have been reported previously13. ZnO-NPs are also considered as exterior agents that inhibit the growth of microbes in ointments, lotions, mouth fresheners, food packages, and textile items nowadays14.

Medicinal plants serve as efficient reducing and stabilizing agents owing to their diverse phytochemical constituents and therapeutic properties15. Previously, various scientists have prepared nanoparticles from halophytes, including AgNPs from Sesuvium portulacastrumL16., ZnONPs from Withania somnifera,17, and ZnO-NPs from Salicornia brachiata Roxb18. Oxystelma esculentum (Linn. f.) R. Brown, an edible halophyte belonging to the family Apocynaceae19. This species demonstrates remarkable salt tolerance, which can be validated through growth performance, biochemical indicators such as proline accumulation and antioxidant enzyme activity, and maintenance of ionic balance (Na⁺/K⁺) under saline stress20. Unlike many other species, O. esculentum thrives under extreme saline conditions while maintaining elevated levels of phenolics, flavonoids, and antioxidant enzymes, reflecting a naturally evolved defense system capable of generating potent reducing biomolecules21. This intrinsic stress tolerance not only ensures sustainable biomass availability in marginal lands but also enhances its efficiency as a reducing, capping, and stabilizing agent, enabling the formation of stable, monodispersed ZnO-NPs with superior surface reactivity and biocompatibility. Furthermore, its long-established ethnomedicinal applications, ranging from the treatment of urinary inflammation, mouth ulcers, and throat infections to hepatoprotective and blood-purifying uses, demonstrate a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds with significant biomedical relevance. Therefore, O. esculentum stands out as a multifunctional, eco-resilient, and bioactive source ideally suited for the phyto-mediated green synthesis of ZnO-NPs in this study20.

This study aims to address the limited exploration of halophyte-derived ZnO-NPs by investigating O. esculentum, a salt-tolerant and phytochemically rich species that has not yet been utilized for nanoparticle synthesis, despite the use of other halophytes in previous studies. The prepared ZnO-NPs were systematically characterized using advanced approaches, including UV–Vis, XRD, FTIR, SEM, and EDX, revealing distinct structural features such as a hexagonal nanocrystalline phase and a rare clover leaf-like morphology, which may contribute to their enhanced bioactivity. Importantly, the study highlights the nanoparticles’ significant pharmacological effects, demonstrating broad-spectrum antifungal, antibacterial, antileishmanial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities, alongside potential applications in pollutant remediation and drug delivery. This work not only underlines the therapeutic promise of ZnO-NPs derived from O. esculentum but also plays a role in the development of environmentally friendly nanotechnological solutions with wide-reaching benefits for human health and ecological sustainability. Overall, the work aims to demonstrate the novelty, comparative advantage, and functional promise of O. esculentum as a sustainable source for green ZnO-NPs synthesis.

O. esculentum plant samples were collected during the flowering season, between March and April 2023, from the Soon Sakesar Salt Range in Punjab, Pakistan (32.5980°N, 72.3701°E). The wild specimens were gathered from publicly accessible, non-protected land within the Salt Range, and no destructive sampling was performed. Plant collection was conducted in compliance with national biodiversity conservation regulations and institutional ethical standards for plant research. The authenticated specimen was deposited at the Herbarium of Pakistan (ISL), Islamabad, under the voucher accession number MN-4477.

Plant extract preparation

The named institutional and/or licensing committee having reference no. AKBOT-2025 has approved all experimental protocols used in this study. The O. esculentum leaves extract was prepared using the previous standard protocol22. The leaves were painstakingly rinsed in tap water to remove debris and dust attached to the leaf surfaces. All washed leaves were dried in the shade, and a grinder was used to make their powder. For extract preparation, 10 g of the powdered leaves was mixed with 250 mL of double-distilled water (DDW) and heated in a water bath at 80 °C for 15 min under continuous stirring. The mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and subsequently filtered through Whatman No.1 filter paper to remove plant residues. The obtained filtrate was stored at 4 °C and used as the reducing and stabilizing agent for the synthesis of ZnO-NPs. The extract-to-precursor ratio, reaction temperature, and incubation time were carefully controlled to ensure reproducibility of nanoparticle synthesis.

Qualitative analysis of phytochemical constituents of plant extracts

Qualitative phytochemical analysis was performed, which constitutes the identification and recognition of various constituents within the different plant parts samples. The aim behind this analysis was to detect the presence of specific classes of compounds or phytochemicals, including protein, saponins, quinones, steroids, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, glycosides, tannins, phenolic compounds, oils, and anthocyanins. The previously published protocol was followed to perform the qualitative phytochemical evaluation23.

Preparation of ZnO-NPs

ZnO-NPs were synthesized using a modified standard protocol. Briefly, 100 mL of 1 mM zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O] solution was prepared in deionized water. Upon dissolution in water, zinc nitrate dissociates into Zn²⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions, providing the zinc precursor required for nanoparticle formation. Plant extract was prepared by boiling 10 g of dried plant powder in 100 mL of distilled water for 30 min, followed by filtration through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The aqueous plant extract contains various bioactive phytochemicals such as phenolics, flavonoids, terpenoids, and proteins, which possess hydroxyl (–OH), carbonyl (C = O), and carboxyl (–COOH) functional groups capable of interacting with metal ions. These biomolecules act as natural chelating, reducing, and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle synthesis.

The zinc nitrate solution was mixed with the plant extract in a 1:2 (v/v) ratio under continuous magnetic stirring. During this stage, Zn²⁺ ions interact with phytochemical ligands present in the extract to form intermediate zinc–phytochemical complexes through coordination bonding. This interaction facilitates controlled nucleation and prevents premature aggregation of zinc ions in the solution. The pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to 9 using 0.1 M NaOH, and the mixture was heated at 80 °C for 90 min. Under alkaline conditions, hydroxide ions (OH⁻) generated from NaOH react with Zn²⁺ ions to form zinc hydroxide [Zn(OH)₂], which serves as a key intermediate in the formation of ZnO nanoparticles. Upon heating, Zn(OH)₂ undergoes dehydration and conversion into ZnO nuclei, initiating nanoparticle formation. The reaction resulted in a visible color change indicating the formation of ZnO-NPs. Simultaneously, phytochemicals present in the plant extract adsorb onto the surface of the forming ZnO nanocrystals, acting as capping and stabilizing agents that control particle growth and prevent agglomeration. The reaction mixture was then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 35 min, and the obtained pellet was washed with distilled water to remove unreacted ions and residual organic compounds. The collected material was dried at 100 °C for 4 h and subsequently calcined at 400 °C for 2 h to obtain crystalline nanoparticles. During calcination, organic phytochemical residues decompose and zinc hydroxide intermediates are completely converted into highly crystalline ZnO nanoparticles, enhancing structural stability and purity. A primary stock suspension (10 mg/mL) was prepared by dispersing 100 mg of dried ZnO-NPs in 10 mL sterile distilled water, followed by ultrasonication for 20 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Working concentrations were subsequently prepared by serial dilution according to the requirements, and suspensions were briefly sonicated before use to maintain homogeneity.

Antioxidant assays

The DPPH-free radical scavenging, total reducing power, and antioxidant capacity were antioxidant assays that were carried out to study the antioxidant behavior of ZnO-NPs at different concentrations, varying from 25 to 200 µg/mL7.

Total antioxidant capacity determination (TAC)

A reagent was mixed with 100 µL ZnO-NPs to investigate the TAC following the previously published technique7. The mixture absorbance was noted at 660 nm with a microplate reader after its cooling process, which was done at 25 °C. DMSO and ascorbic acid were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Then, total antioxidant capacity was analyzed.

Total reducing power determination (TRP)

Potassium-ferricyanide method was utilized for the assessment of the TRP of prepared ZnO-NPs29. DMSO and ascorbic acid were considered negative and positive controls, respectively. The absorbance of the solution was noted at 620 nm. Total reducing power of synthesized ZnO-NPs was shown as AAE/mg (AA equivalents/milligrams).

Free radical scavenging assay (FRSA)

A mixture of 2, 2-diphenyl-1-picryl hydrazyl (DPPH) and methanol was utilized as a reagent solution to assess the FRSA29. Reagent solution (180 mL) was mixed with test concentrations of 20 mL, and incubation was done for 1 h. The free radical scavenging was determined at 515 nm in percentage. Ascorbic acid and DMSO were utilized as positive and negative controls. A given equation was used to calculate inhibition (%).

Antimicrobial applications

Antibacterial assays

ZnO-NPs antibacterial assay was performed against various bacterial strains, Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhi, and Staphylococcus aureus, utilizing the standard protocol with minor changes25. The bacterial strains were obtained from the preserved culture in the Plant Pathology Laboratory, National Agriculture Research Center, Islamabad. The incubation of bacterial strains was done at 37 °C for 24 h after subculturing in the nutrient broth media. The bacterial strains’ overnight culture was plated on agar medium that was already prepared and then dried for 5 min with the aim of examining the ZnO-NPs’ antibacterial activity. Before application, the ZnO-NPs were prepared by dispersing the appropriate amount of ZnO-NP powder into sterile distilled water to obtain final concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL. The nanoparticle suspensions were sonicated utilizing an ultrasonic bath for 15–20 min to ensure uniform dispersion and prevent aggregation. This step is crucial to maintain nanoparticle stability and maximize surface area for antimicrobial interaction.

Sterile filter paper discs of 6 mm in diameter were then loaded with 100 µL of each ZnO-NPs suspension. Loaded discs were air-dried under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood to allow the solvent to evaporate, ensuring accurate delivery of the nanoparticles onto the agar surface. After drying, the discs were put onto the agar plates previously inoculated with bacterial cultures carefully. The zone of inhibition was checked regularly during the incubation period of the plates at 37 °C for 24 h. DMSO (5%) and kanamycin-A were taken as negative and positive controls, respectively.

Kanamycin-A was utilized as a positive control, which is a similar concentration to that of NPs, while DMSO was taken as a negative control. Nanoparticles characterization

UV-Visible spectroscopy

The surface plasmon resonance measurements at 350 nm wavelength showed ZnO-NPs effective production as illustrated in Fig. 2. Shifting in color from yellow to white in cell-free cultures confirms the presence of secondary metabolites in the cell-free filtrate, which causes the reduction of zinc nitrate hexahydrate to yield ZnO-NPs33. The difference between the absorbance peak shapes for filtrate and zinc oxide also indicated the zinc nitrate hexahydrate salt reduction into the ZnO-NPs. It has been shown previously that ZnO-NPs absorb at 340 nm, which is a comparable peak34. The precursor material may be the cause of little difference in the peak. The band gap of the prepared material was determined to be 3.2 eV. This band gap value is consistent with the typical band gap range reported for ZnO nanoparticles, indicating the successful formation of crystalline ZnO with semiconducting properties35. Such optical characteristics suggest that the synthesized ZnO-NPs may exhibit enhanced photocatalytic and biological activities due to efficient electron–hole pair generation36.

UV-Visible Spectroscopy of phyto-synthesized ZnO-NPs.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum of the synthesized ZnO-NPs displayed several prominent peaks corresponding to different functional groups that contributed to nanoparticle stabilization and formation (Fig. 3). A broad absorption band noted at 3387 cm⁻¹ is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups, which may originate from adsorbed water molecules or phenolic compounds present in the plant extract. The peaks around 2920–2850 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic –CH₂/–CH₃ groups. The strong band at 1634 cm⁻¹ is assigned to C = O stretching of amide I or N–H bending of amide II, indicating the role of proteins or polyphenols in stabilizing and capping nanoparticles. Additional bands at 1450–1380 cm⁻¹ and 1050–1100 cm⁻¹ are ascribed to C–H bending and C–O stretching vibrations, ensuring the presence of organic biomolecules bind with the nanoparticle surface. Importantly, the sharp band noted in the 500–450 cm⁻¹ range corresponds to Zn–O stretching vibrations, which confirms the successful formation of ZnO-NPs.

FTIR spectrum of phyto-synthesized ZnO-NPs.

Although the synthesized ZnO-NPs were calcined at 400 °C for 3 h, which can lead to oxidation and partial removal of phytochemical capping agents, some residual organic functional groups were still detected in the FTIR spectra37. This suggests that a fraction of thermally stable biomolecules or their derivatives remained adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface even after calcination. These results clearly demonstrate that remaining phytochemicals present in the plant extract play a role in stabilizing and reducing during ZnO-NPs synthesis. In the current study, ZnO-NPs characterization confirmed their suitable size and stable nature. FTIR spectroscopy explains the presence of many functional groups in the redox reaction of nanoparticle preparation38.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) structural analysis

The XRD pattern shown above corresponds to ZnO-NPs and is used to confirm their crystalline structure as depicted in Fig. 4. The graph displays characteristic peaks at specific 2θ angles, which are matched with standard diffraction data to identify the crystal planes. Various peaks such as 21.44°, 29.90°, 30.08°, 31.92°, 36.33°, 43.3°, and 56.62°, were observed in XRD analysis. The intense diffraction peaks are observed at 31.92°, 36.33°, 43.3°, and 56.62°, corresponding to the (100), (101), (102), and (110) crystallographic planes, respectively. These peaks are in good arrangement with the ZnO-NPs hexagonal structure, as reported in the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card number 36–1451, confirming the pure crystalline ZnO-NPs formation.

XRD analysis of phyto-synthesized ZnO-NPs.

The average size of crystallite was calculated to be about 11.3 nm using the Debye-Scherrer method. The main diffraction peak at 2θ = 36.2° exhibited a β value of 0.0103 radians, resulting in an average crystallite size of 13 ± 1.2 nm. ZnO-NPs’ small size and high crystallinity, as characterized in this study, are significant since previous reports have shown that smaller nanoparticles tend to show improved antibacterial activity due to increased surface area and reactivity41.

FE-SEM analysis

FE-SEM is a highly developed method for obtaining topological information about nanoparticles through scanning electron microscopy. The SEM images of ZnO-NPs are shown in Fig. 5. FE-SEM images showed that the particles are mainly quasi-spherical and tend to agglomerate into a mass-like sponge. The average particle size of ZnO-NPs determined by Image J-software from the SEM micrograph was 55 nm. Densification leads to very close spacing between particles, which ultimately results in aggregation. Agglomeration is primarily attributed to the high surface energy and strong interparticle interactions of nanoscale ZnO, including van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding with surface-adsorbed phytochemicals from the plant extract, which promote clustering during drying and SEM sample preparation42. It has been reported similar ZnO-NP shapes when using Zinc acetate as a precursor; however, they observed a difference in particle size, which might be owing to different organic sources and synthesis conditions43. Structural morphology analyses using SEM revealed uniform spherical-shaped particles with a diameter from 25 to 60 nm from Azadirachta indica44, and an irregular morphology was observed when ZnO-NPs were prepared using an aqueous extract of Papaver somniferum45.

The hemolytic assay was carried out to analyze the phyto-bombarded nanoparticles’ toxic nature against RBCs. RBC and ZnO-NPs concentrations (25–200 µg/mL) were cultured in a buffer solution synthesized to emulate an extracellular environment. The hemolytic assay is based on hemoglobin release due to the RBCs’ lysis when ZnO-NPs are applied. The hemolytic assay findings are given in Table 4. According to the ASTM F756-17 standard (Standard Practice for Assessment of Hemolytic Properties of Materials), materials producing less than 2% hemolysis are considered non-hemolytic, values between 2 and 5% are slightly hemolytic, and values above 5% are hemolytic48. It is clear from the results (Table 4) that bio-fabricated ZnO-NPs exhibited biocompatibility even at the highest dose, which is 200 µg/mL. These findings make them suitable for biomedical utilization. Our findings aligned with the former work, and the synthesized ZnO-NPs can be subjected to useful applications27. The synthesized nanoparticles interact with cell membranes, which affect physiological properties and support immunity, aiding in autoimmune27. This integrative method assists in more effective and safer biomedical applications of ZnO-NPs in the case of combination with traditional medicine27. Furthermore, the low hemolytic activity observed highlights the compatibility of ZnO-NPs with blood-contacting systems, minimizing the risk of cytotoxic side effects. Such properties are especially advantageous in drug delivery, wound healing, and injectable formulations where hemocompatibility is essential49.

One of the effective therapeutic strategies to manage postprandial hyperglycemia involves the inhibition of carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes such as alpha-amylase (AA) and α-glucosidase (AG), which break down the complex carbohydrates into glucose in the gastrointestinal tract. The interaction of ZnO-NPs with α-amylase and α-glucosidase is thought to occur through several mechanisms50. Firstly, due to their reactive surface and high surface area, ZnO-NPs can adsorb onto the enzyme surface, possibly near or within the active site, resulting in steric hindrance or conformational changes that reduce enzymatic activity through surface adsorption and electrostatic interactions51. Secondly, ZnO-NPs are known to generate low levels of reactive oxygen species, which can oxidatively modify critical amino acid residues in the enzyme’s active site, thereby inhibiting catalytic function31. Additionally, ZnO-NPs can release Zn²⁺ ions in biological environments, which may competitively bind to key catalytic residues or metal-binding sites within the enzymes, disrupting their normal activity. Lastly, molecular docking studies from related literature suggest that ZnO-NPs may form hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions with amino acid residues within the enzyme structure, further contributing to enzyme inhibition.

Such multifaceted inhibitory interactions between ZnO-NPs and digestive enzymes demonstrate their potential as novel anti-diabetic agents17. The significant AA and AG inhibitory activities observed in this study indicate that ZnO-NPs could form the basis for new pharmacological strategies for the cure of diabetes mellitus45. The ability of nano-sized materials to modulate enzyme activity opens new avenues for nanomedicine-based diabetic therapies.

Anti-inflammatory potential

The O. esculentum plant extract and its synthesized ZnO-NPs exhibited notable anti-inflammatory activity, showing comparable efficiency to the standard drug Diclofenac sodium. Both the extract and ZnO-NPs effectively reduced in vitro inflammation (Table 7). The ZnO-NPs-mediated anti-inflammatory response increased in a concentration-dependent manner, with inhibition values of 46%, 59%, 66%, 71%, and 79% at 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 µg/mL, respectively. Beyond 800 µg/mL, no further enhancement in inhibition was observed. Although percent inhibition values demonstrate the anti-inflammatory potential of the synthesized nanoparticles, the present study did not determine IC₅₀ values or perform a statistical comparison with the standard control, which would be necessary for quantitative benchmarking. Future studies, including dose–response curve fitting and comparative analysis with Diclofenac sodium, could better validate the relative efficacy.

ZnO-NPs-antioxidant potential

Different concentrations varying from 25 to 200 µg/mL were used to examine the antioxidant activities (Table 8). The highest ZnO-NPs total antioxidant capacity was 51.7 ± 1.2% at 200 µg/mL, in terms of AA equivalent/milligrams. ROS (Reactive oxygen species) generated in living cells because of the oxidation process can disrupt the usually occurring cell metabolic activities. Lipid peroxidation, enzyme inactivation, protein and DNA destruction are some terrible consequences of inadequate antioxidants. ZnO-NPs exert antioxidant effects through multiple biochemical mechanisms. Primarily, they scavenge ROS by acting as electron or hydrogen donors, neutralizing free radicals before they can damage cellular components59. The oxygen vacancies and surface defects in ZnO-NPs increase their redox activity, making them efficient at disrupting radical chain reactions60. Moreover, their semiconducting nature facilitates electron transfer, contributing further to their antioxidant behavior61.

The antibacterial activity of ZnO-NPs arises from a complex interplay of multiple physicochemical and biological factors. Initially, ZnO-NPs interact electrostatically with the negatively charged bacterial cell surface, promoting strong adhesion and mechanical disruption of the cell wall and membrane, which increases membrane permeability and causes leakage of intracellular constituents. The nanoscale size, morphology, and high surface-to-volume ratio of ZnO-NPs further enhance bacterial contact and facilitate direct penetration into bacterial cells66. In addition, ZnONPs can induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anions (O₂⁻), hydroxyl radicals (OH⁻), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which lead to oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately impairing cellular function67. Alongside oxidative stress, the controlled release of Zn²⁺ ions plays a crucial role by disrupting enzymatic activity, inhibiting protein synthesis, and interfering with essential metabolic pathways68.

Other important factors influencing antibacterial efficacy include nanoparticle size distribution, surface charge, crystallinity, and dispersion stability, all of which govern bacterial attachment and toxicity. ZnO-NPs have also been reported to inhibit biofilm formation and destabilize pre-formed biofilms by interfering with quorum sensing and extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) production69. Furthermore, internalized nanoparticles may interact with bacterial DNA and ribosomes, leading to gene expression dysregulation and inhibition of cell division. The cumulative effects of membrane damage, oxidative and non-oxidative stress, metal ion toxicity, biofilm inhibition, and intracellular interference result in irreversible bacterial cell death70. Loss of membrane integrity has been identified as a particularly critical factor in the bactericidal action of ZnO-NPs against E. coli and other Gram-negative bacteria. The present findings are consistent with earlier studies reporting the broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy of ZnO-NPs against pathogenic microorganisms71.

The antifungal activity of ZnO-NPs is believed to arise from a combination of physicochemical interactions and intracellular stress responses. Due to their nanoscale dimensions and high surface reactivity, ZnO-NPs can adhere to fungal cell walls composed primarily of chitin, glucans, and mannoproteins72. This interaction may cause structural destabilization of the cell wall and plasma membrane, resulting in increased permeability and impaired barrier function. In addition, ZnO-NPs may penetrate fungal cells through damaged membranes or endocytic pathways, where they can interfere with intracellular processes essential for fungal growth and replication. Several studies have suggested that ZnO-NPs can induce oxidative stress through the generation of ROS and through the release of Zn²⁺ ions, which may disrupt mitochondrial function, inhibit key metabolic enzymes, and interfere with nucleic acid synthesis72,73. The variation in antifungal activity among different fungal strains may be attributed to differences in surface charge, as well as variations in antioxidant defense systems and metal ion tolerance. Additionally, strain-specific differences in spore structure, membrane lipid composition, and efflux mechanisms can further modulate sensitivity to ZnO-NPs.

The observed increase in antifungal activity with rising ZnO-NPs concentration up to 4 mg/mL is likely due to enhanced nanoparticle cell contact, greater surface coverage, and increased availability of reactive sites. However, the lack of further inhibition at higher concentrations may result from nanoparticle aggregation, which reduces effective surface area and limits interaction with fungal cells. Aggregation is influenced by physicochemical factors such as particle size, surface charge, and Brownian motion, particularly in nanoparticles with high surface-to-volume ratios74. In addition, excessive nanoparticle concentrations may lead to sedimentation or reduced diffusion in solid media, further diminishing antifungal efficacy. Unlike antibacterial activity, where nanoparticle action is primarily driven by interactions with peptidoglycan-rich cell walls and direct membrane disruption, antifungal activity involves additional challenges due to the complex chitin and glucan-based fungal cell walls and robust oxidative stress defense mechanisms, making fungal inhibition generally less pronounced and more strain-dependent. Interestingly, the nanoscale ZnO-NPs not only inhibit fungal growth but may also interfere with spore germination and hyphal elongation, suggesting a dual action that targets both the reproductive and vegetative stages of fungal pathogens, a feature less pronounced in their antibacterial effects75.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study reveals the successful green synthesis of ZnO-NPs using O. esculentum extract, with detailed characterization confirming their nanocrystalline structure, functionalization with plant biomolecules, and quasi-spherical morphology. The synthesized ZnO-NPs exhibited notable biological activities, including significant antibacterial, antifungal, and antileishmanial effects, along with promising anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antioxidant properties. The low hemolytic activity further indicates their biocompatibility, supporting safe biomedical use. Overall, these findings suggest that the phyto-fabricated ZnO-NPs possess multifunctional therapeutic potential. Future studies should focus on in vivo evaluations, mechanistic investigations, and formulation development to explore their practical applications in medicine and healthcare.
