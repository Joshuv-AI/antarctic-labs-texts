Deterministic Underlying States are incompatible with a Counterfactual account of Lüders’ rule
Alisson Tezzin, Bárbara Amaral, Jonte R. Hance

Abstract
In this work, we show that a counterfactual account of L”uders’ rule —– which we argue is naturally implied by the mathematical structure of the rule itself —– rules out underlying-state models of quantum mechanics (a type of hidden-variable model, typically used in the contextuality and nonlocality literature, where quantum states are treated as probability measures over “better-defined states”). This incompatibility arises because the counterfactual update requires ontological models to update their states according to conditional probability, which in turn establishes an equivalence between compatibility and the existence of such models.

Quantum state update—
We first argue that Lüders’ rule update states based on counterfactual propositions about subsequent measurements, rather than factual statements about a past observation. Note that this is an account of the theoretical quantum update mechanism taken as an axiom in standard quantum mechanics. It should not be misunderstood as a particular interpretation of any physical process which occurs when quantum systems are measured in actual experiments.

Let
\hat{A}
be an observable in a finite-dimensional quantum system
\mathfrak{S}
, and let
\alpha
be one of its eigenvalues. Consider the counterfactual proposition “If a measurement is made of
\hat{A}
, the result will be found to be
\alpha
”
[
29
]
, denoted
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
hereafter. Let
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\equiv\chi_{\{\alpha\}}(\hat{A})
be the corresponding projection (in which
\chi_{\{\alpha\}}
is the indicator function of
\{\alpha\}
and
\chi_{\{\alpha\}}(\hat{A})
is given by the functional calculus
[
36
]
), and let
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
be the subspace of
\mathcal{H}
onto which
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)
projects, i.e.,

\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})=\{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi:\psi\in\mathcal{H}\},

(1)

in which
\mathcal{H}
denotes the finite-dimensional Hilbert space associated with
\mathfrak{S}
.

Pure states that lie within the subspace
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
ensure the proposition is true, in that they assign probability
1
to
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
. This probability is strictly smaller than
1
for any other pure state. More broadly, a state
\hat{\rho}
(i.e., a density operator) assigns probability
1
to
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
if and only if it is a convex combination of pure states lying in the corresponding subspace.

The orthocomplement of
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
, namely the set

\begin{split}\hat{\Pi}&(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}\doteq\\
&\{\phi\in\mathcal{H}:\forall_{\psi\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})}\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=0\},\end{split}

(2)

is the subspace corresponding the proposition
[\hat{A}\neq\alpha]
, which asserts that “If a measurement is made of
\hat{A}
, the result will be found to be different to
\alpha
”. This is because a pure state
\psi
satisfies
P_{\psi}[\hat{A}\neq\alpha]=1
if and only if
\psi\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{S})^{\perp}
, in which, for any state
\hat{\rho}

P_{\hat{\rho}}[A=\alpha]\equiv\text{tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\right)

(3)

and
P_{\hat{\rho}}[A\neq\alpha]\doteq 1-P_{\hat{\rho}}[A=\alpha]
.

These two subspaces have trivial intersection (i.e., their intersection is the zero vector), and their direct sum is the entire Hilbert space
[
36
]
. This means that, for any vector
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a unique pair
\psi_{\alpha}\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
,
\psi_{\neg\alpha}\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}
, such that

\psi=\psi_{\alpha}+\psi_{\neg\alpha}.

(4)

Furthermore,
\psi_{\alpha}
is the unique element of
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
that minimises the distance from
\psi
, in that

\|\psi-\psi_{\alpha}\|=\min\{\|\phi-\psi\|:\phi\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})\},

(5)

and analogously for
\psi_{\neg\alpha}
. The vector
\psi_{\alpha}
is said to be the orthogonal projection of
\psi
on
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
. It follows by construction that

\psi_{\alpha}=\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi.

(6)

Projecting a pure state
\psi
onto
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
is the optimal way of reconstructing
\psi
to ensure the validity of the proposition
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
, i.e., to ensure — at the theoretical level — that, if a measurement is made of
\hat{A}
, the result
will be found to be
\alpha
. This is because, among all pure states (which we can consider as unidimensional subspaces) that assign probability 1 to
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
, the subspace spanned by
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi
is the closest to
\psi
, as the very notion of
orthogonal
projection implies. Hence,
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi
is the state in
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)(\mathcal{H})
that best approximates
\psi
in a mathematically precise sense. As shown in the Appendix, this reasoning naturally extends to the update of density operators.

We denote by
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}:\mathcal{S}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{0}
the mapping given by

T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)\doteq\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi}{\|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi\|}

(7)

for each
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{0}
consists of all pure states of the system.

Order-dependent predictions and incompatibility—
We will denote the distribution of an observable
\hat{B}
in the state
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)
by
P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\cdot\ |\hat{A}=\alpha]
. That is, for each
\beta\in\sigma(\hat{B})
we have

P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta|\hat{A}=\alpha]\equiv P_{T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)}[\hat{B}=\beta].

(8)

Let
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
be (not necessarily compatible) observables, and let
\psi
be a pure state. For each
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
and
\beta\in\sigma(\hat{B})
, let us define

P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}=\beta]\doteq P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta|\hat{A}=\alpha]

(9)

=\langle\psi|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)|\psi\rangle

(10)

(note that
\{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha):(\alpha,\beta)\in\sigma(\hat{A})\times\sigma(\hat{B})\}
is a POVM).
P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}=\beta]
can be thought of as the (theoretically constructed) probability of obtaining values
\alpha
and
\beta
by measuring
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
in sequence.
Compatibility is equivalent to order-independent predictions of sequential measurements:

Lemma 1

(Compatibility)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a finite-dimensional quantum system. Two observables
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
of
\mathfrak{S}
are compatible if and only if, for any pure state
\psi
and values
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
,
\beta\in\sigma(\hat{B})
,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}=\beta]=P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta,\hat{A}=\alpha].\end{split}

(11)

The proof can be found in the Appendix. Note that, due to Eq. (
9
), Eq. (
11
) is formally equivalent to Bayes’ rule.

State-updating Deterministic underlying-state models—
Deterministic underlying-state models, as often used in the contextuality and nonlocality literature
[
18
,
37
,
19
]
, are defined as follows:

Associated with a finite-dimensional system
\mathfrak{S}
is a measurable space
\bm{\Lambda}\equiv(\Lambda,\mathcal{A})

[
35
]
; the elements of the underlying set
\Lambda
are called “hidden variables”, “hidden states”, or “underlying states”. Each underlying state determines the value of all physical quantities of the system, thus observables must be represented by real-valued (measurable) functions on
\bm{\Lambda}
. The function
f_{\hat{A}}
representing an observable
\hat{A}
assigns, to each underlying state
\lambda
, the value
f_{\hat{A}}(\lambda)
that
\hat{A}
assumes when the system is in the underlying state
\lambda

[
4
]
. To use Bell’s terminology, underlying states are the “better defined states” over which states of
\mathfrak{S}
are averages
[
3
]
. Each state
\psi
of
\mathfrak{S}
therefore defines a probability measure
\mu_{\psi}
on
\bm{\Lambda}
. Given any measurable set
\Omega\subset\Lambda
,
\mu_{\psi}(\Omega)
is the probability that the system is in an underlying state lying in
\Omega

[
4
]
.

Underlying states assign truth-values to propositions. The proposition
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
(“If a measurement is made of
\hat{A}
, the result will be found to be
\alpha
”) is rendered true by
\lambda
if
\alpha=f_{\hat{A}}(\lambda)
(i.e., if
\alpha
is the value possessed by
\hat{A}
in the underlying state
\lambda
), and false otherwise
[
38
,
29
,
16
]
. This is because, whenever a physical quantity
\hat{A}
possesses a definite value, a measurement of
\hat{A}
must reveal, up to operational constraints such as limited accuracy of instruments, this value to the observer: if a quantity holds a value
\alpha
, the apparatus’s reported value must be as close to
\alpha
as the accuracy of the instrument permits. This has two important consequences.

First, the (theoretically constructed) probability
P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]
that a measurement of a quantity
\hat{A}
returns the value
\alpha
is the probability that, at that moment, the system lies in an underlying state that assigns this value to
\hat{A}
. This means

\displaystyle P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]

\displaystyle=\mu_{\psi}(\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)),

(12)

where
\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)
consists of all underlying states in which
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
holds, i.e.,

\begin{split}\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)&\doteq f_{\hat{A}}^{-1}(\{\alpha\})\\
&=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:f_{\hat{A}}(\lambda)=\alpha\}.\end{split}

(13)

Second, Bayesian inference (i.e., conditional probability) is the optimal way of reconstructing a state to ensure that a proposition
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
holds. If the system is described by a state
\mu_{\psi}
, the proposition
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
— which, under the assumption that underlying states exist, is equivalent to “the physical quantity
\hat{A}
has a value, and that value is
\alpha
” — conditions
\mu_{\psi}
on the set of underlying states for which this claim is true, namely the set
\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)
. Hence, the updated probability measure
\tau_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\mu_{\psi})
that reconstructs
\psi
to ensure that
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
holds is given by

\begin{split}\tau_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\mu_{\psi})(\ \cdot\ )&\doteq\frac{\mu_{\psi}(\ \cdot\ \cap\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha))}{\mu_{\psi}(\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha))}\\
&\equiv\mu_{\psi}(\cdot\ |\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)).\end{split}

(14)

For this to be consistent with the state update mechanism of
\mathfrak{S}
, the probability measure representing
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)
must be the measure
\mu_{\psi}
conditioned on
f_{\hat{A}}^{-1}(\{\alpha\})
, i.e.,

\mu_{T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)}=\tau_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\mu_{\psi}).

(15)

To summarise, let
\mathfrak{S}
be a finite-dimensional quantum system, and let
\mathcal{O}_{S}
be a non-empty subset of
\mathcal{O}
. A
state-updating deterministic underlying-state
model for
\mathcal{O}_{S}
consists of a measurable space
\bm{\Lambda}\equiv(\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
, a mapping
\Psi
assigning pure states of
\mathfrak{S}
to probability measures on
\bm{\Lambda}
, and a mapping
\Phi
assigning observables in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
to measurable functions on
\bm{\Lambda}
, such that, for each observable
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
, value
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
, and pure state
\psi
, Eqs. (
12
) and (
15
) are satisfied. This means that the distribution of
\hat{A}
w.r.t.
\psi
is that of the random variable
f_{\hat{A}}\equiv\Phi(\hat{A})
in the probability space defined by
\mu_{\psi}\equiv\Psi(\psi)
(Eq. (
12
)), and that Lüders’ rule translates to
\bm{\Lambda}
as conditional probability, as encapsulated by the following commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\psi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\psi)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}

(16)

(as before,
\tau_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}
denotes the mapping that conditions each probability measure
\mu:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]
) on
\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)
. When
\mathcal{O}_{S}
contains all observables of
\mathfrak{S}
, we say that
\mathfrak{M}\equiv(\bm{\Lambda},\Phi,\Psi)
is a model for the system
\mathfrak{S}
.

We define an underlying-state model for a set of observables
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, and not only for the entire system
\mathfrak{S}
, for practical reasons. The subscript
S
stands for “scenario” and is motivated by the concept of a “measurement scenario”, which appears in operational approaches to quantum foundations
[
39
,
20
,
19
]
.

Incompatibility obstructs state-updating underlying-state models—
We now show that incompatibility obstructs the existence of state-updating underlying-state models. If a deterministic state-updating underlying-state model exists for a set of observables
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, then for any pair of observables
A,B\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
, any pure state
\psi
and any values
\alpha\in\sigma(A)
,
\beta\in\sigma(B)
,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}&[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}=\beta]\doteq P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta|\hat{A}=\alpha]\\
&=P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]\frac{\mu_{\psi}(\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)\cap\Omega(\hat{B}=\beta))}{P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}\\
&=\mu_{\psi}(\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)\cap\Omega(\hat{B}=\beta))\\
&=P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta]P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha|\hat{B}=\beta]\\
&=P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta,\hat{A}=\alpha].\end{split}

(17)

When this is true, according to Lemma
1
,
A
and
B
are compatible. This proves that a state-updating underlying-state model exists for
\mathcal{O}_{S}
only if
\mathcal{O}_{S}
is a set of pairwise compatible observables. On the other hand, the most general version of the Kolmogorov extension theorem (see Theorem 2.4.3 of Ref.
[
35
]
) ensures that state-updating underlying-state models can be constructed for any set of pairwise compatible observables (see Appendix for details). This leads to the following result.

Proposition 1

(Compatibility and underlying states)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a finite-dimensional quantum system, and let
\mathcal{O}_{S}
be a non-empty set of observables in
\mathfrak{S}
. The following claims are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{O}_{S}
admits a state-updating deterministic underlying-state model.

(b)

The observables in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
are pairwise compatible.

As shown in Ref.
[
21
]
, a variant of Proposition
1
also applies to stochastic underlying-state models, whose underlying states assign probabilities — rather than values — to observables. It means that, arguably, incompatibility obstructs stochastic underlying-state models as well (or at least those for which state update corresponds to conditional probability á la Bayes’ rule). We explore the consequences of this result in Ref.
[
22
]
. Finally, recall that, regarding contextuality and nonlocality, there is no loss of generality in working with deterministic models
[
26
,
18
,
20
]
.

Discussion—
Under the counterfactual account of Lüders’ rule, Proposition
1
provides a straightforward method for ruling out (deterministic) underlying-state models of quantum mechanics. The proof, similar to those in Refs.
[
14
,
28
]
, is independent of specific states and observables, as well as space-time considerations; it establishes a direct connection between compatibility and underlying states, demonstrating that incompatibility is sufficient to obstruct such models.

State-updating underlying-state models are inherently Kochen-Specker noncontextual
[
40
,
18
]
and Bell-local
[
10
,
20
]
. This means that, if
\vec{A}\equiv(\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m})
are pairwise compatible, then, for any
\vec{\alpha}\equiv(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\hat{A}_{i})
and any state
\psi
,

P_{\psi}[\vec{A}=\vec{\alpha}]=\mu_{\psi}(\cap_{i=1}^{m}\Omega(\hat{A}_{i}=\alpha_{i})),

(18)

in which,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}[\vec{A}=\vec{\alpha}]&\doteq\\
\prod_{i=1}^{m}P_{\psi}&[\hat{A}_{i}=\alpha_{i}|\hat{A}_{1}=\alpha_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{i-1}=\alpha_{i-1}]\\
&=\left\langle\psi\left|\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}_{i}=\alpha_{i})\psi\right.\right\rangle\end{split}

(19)

(clearly,
P_{\psi}[\hat{A}_{i}=\cdot\ |\hat{A}_{1}=\alpha_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{i-1}=\alpha_{i-1}]
denotes the distribution of
\hat{A}_{i}
in the state
(T_{[\hat{A}_{i-1}=\alpha_{i-1}]}\circ\dots\circ T_{[\hat{A}_{1}=\alpha_{1}]})(\psi)
). Eq. (
18
) follow from straightforward calculations, as shown e.g., in Ref.
[
22
]
(in which it is also proved that state-updating models satisfy Fine’s consistency condition
[
27
]
). In particular, according to the counterfactual account of Lüders’ rule, contextual deterministic underlying-state models are inconsistent with the canonical postulates of quantum mechanics (which include the collapse postulate).

The view that Lüders’ rule assigns states to
post-measurement states
is particularly popular in the contemporary quantum foundations community, largely due to the influence of quantum information theory
[
41
]
and the prevalence of operational approaches in the field. This view permeates longstanding debates about the assumptions of “ideal measurements”, attempts to reconstruct quantum mechanics, and discussions related to hidden variables
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
19
,
37
]
. As shown in Ref.
[
21
]
, the alternative proposed here —– which agrees with multiple perspectives on quantum mechanics
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
44
]
— can shed light on important topics in quantum foundations, extending beyond the problem of hidden variables.

An important consequence of the correspondence between incompatibility, order-dependent predictions, and constraints on hidden-variable models we establish is that it makes it easier to identify classical parallels to our inability to form underlying-state models. We will show this in detail in a forthcoming paper. Our work therefore contributes to the debate about whether — and, if so, why — phenomena associated with such models, such as contextuality, can be considered a signature of non-classicality
[
37
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
.

To conclude, we would like to emphasise that Proposition
1
corroborates the traditional interpretation of the Kochen-Specker theorem
[
4
,
49
,
50
,
38
,
16
,
29
,
51
]
: quantum mechanics seems inconsistent with mechanistic realism, or naïve realism (as Isham and Butterfield
[
38
]
, and Döring
[
29
]
put it), where all physical quantities of a system are simultaneously possessed by that system.

Appendix A
Appendix

On the Update of Density Operators
. In the main text, we showed that the update of pure states under objective propositions (those that specify the measurement outcome) is, by construction, a theoretical mechanism for reconstructing states to ensure the validity of certain propositions about future (i.e., subsequent) measurements of physical quantities. Here, we extend this observation to density operators, which represent states where the experimentalist has limited knowledge of the system’s pure state.

As we know, the state (i.e., density operator) corresponding to the projected vector
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi
is the rank-1 projection

\begin{split}T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)&\doteq\left|\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi}{\|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\|}\right\rangle\left\langle\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi}{\|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\|}\right|\\
&=\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)}{P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}.\end{split}

(20)

With a slight abuse of notation, let’s denote also by
\mathcal{S}_{0}
the set of rank-1 projections on
\mathcal{H}
.

The straightforward way of extending the mapping
\mathcal{S}_{0}\ni|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mapsto T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
to the set
\mathcal{S}
of all states (i.e., density operators) is by defining

T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\hat{\rho})\doteq\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\rho}\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)}{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}

(21)

for each state
\hat{\rho}
. As well know, this is Lüders’ rule
[
17
]
, an improvement of von Neumann’s collapse postulate
[
52
]
. Let’s show that our analysis of the state update rule remains valid under this extension.

As one can easily check,
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\hat{\rho})
ensures that
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
is true, i.e., measurement of
\hat{A}
in the state
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\hat{\rho})
yields the value
\alpha
with probability
1
(at the theoretical level). Next, let
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
be any convex decomposition of the state
\hat{\rho}
in terms of pure states, with
p_{i}>0
for each
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1
(recall that
\mathcal{S}
is the convex hull of
\mathcal{S}_{0}
). The state
\hat{\rho}
is traditionally interpreted as asserting that the system lies in the state
|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
with probability
p_{i}

[
41
]
.

In the state
\hat{\rho}
, the probability
P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i},\hat{A}=\alpha]
that the system is in the state
|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
and that, in this pure state, a measurement of
\hat{A}
yields the value
\alpha
is given by

\begin{split}P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i},\hat{A}=\alpha]&\doteq p_{i}P_{\psi_{i}}[\hat{A}=\alpha].\end{split}

(22)

For simplicity, we will say that
P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i},\hat{A}=\alpha]
is the probability of
[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i}]
and
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
being “consecutively true” in the state
\hat{\rho}
. In the same state,
P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]
is the probability that
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
is true. The ratio

\frac{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i},\hat{A}=\alpha]}{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}

(23)

is thus the probability that
[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i}]
and
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
are consecutively true, provided that
[\hat{\rho}\in\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|,i=1,\dots,m\}]
and
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
are satisfied. Now note that

\begin{split}T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}&(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\frac{\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)}{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{p_{i}P_{\psi_{i}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i},\hat{A}=\alpha]}{P_{\hat{\rho}}[\hat{A}=\alpha]}T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|).\end{split}

(24)

Hence,
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(\hat{\rho})
asserts that the system is in one of the states
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|)
,
i=1,\dots,m
, which are pure states ensuring that
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
holds. The probability that the system is in the pure state
T_{[\hat{A}=\alpha]}(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|)
corresponds to the likelihood of
[\hat{\rho}=\psi_{i}]
and
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
being consecutively true, provided that
[\hat{\rho}\in\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|,i=1,\dots,m\}]
and
[\hat{A}=\alpha]
are satisfied. It shows that our analysis of the state update rule remains valid under Lüders’ extension.

Proof of Lemma
1
:

Let
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
be (not necessarily compatible) observables in a finite-dimensional quantum system, and let
\hat{A}=\sum_{\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})}\alpha\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)
and
\hat{B}=\sum_{\beta\in\sigma(\hat{B})}\beta\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)
be their spectral decompositions
[
16
]
. For any pure state
\psi
and values
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
,
\beta\in\sigma(\hat{B})
, we have

\begin{split}P_{\psi}&[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}\in\beta]=\\
&\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\psi\right.\right\rangle.\end{split}

(25)

On the other hand,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}&[\hat{B}=\beta,\hat{A}\in\alpha]=\\
&\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\psi\right.\right\rangle.\end{split}

(26)

Pure states separate observables in quantum systems, i.e., two self-adjoint operators
C,D
are equal if and only if
\left\langle\psi\left|C\psi\right.\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi\left|D\psi\right.\right\rangle
for each state pure
\psi
. Therefore, the probability distributions
P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\cdot\ ,\hat{B}=\cdot\ ]
and
P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\cdot\ ,\hat{A}=\cdot\ ]
are equal (up to a permutation) for every state
\psi
if and only if, for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\sigma(\hat{A})\times\sigma(\hat{B})
, the operators
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)
and
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)
are equal. As shown in Ref.
[
53
]
, this is equivalent to saying that
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)
and
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)
are compatible. We know that
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
are compatible if and only if
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}=\alpha)
and
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{B}=\beta)
are compatible for each
(\alpha,\beta)\in\sigma(\hat{A})\times\sigma(\hat{B})

[
41
]
. Hence,
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
are compatible if and only if, for each state
\psi
and each
(\alpha,\beta)\in\sigma(\hat{A})\times\sigma(\hat{B})
,

P_{\psi}[\hat{A}=\alpha,\hat{B}=\beta]=P_{\psi}[\hat{B}=\beta,\hat{A}=\alpha].

(27)

Proof of Proposition
1
:

All that remains for us to prove is that (a) follows from (b). Suppose thus that
\mathcal{O}_{S}
is a set of pairwise compatible observables. Let
\Lambda
be the Cartesian product
\prod_{\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}}\sigma(\hat{A})
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the product
\sigma
-algebra
\prod_{\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}}\mathfrak{P}(\sigma(\hat{A}))

[
35
]
, where
\mathfrak{P}(\sigma(\hat{A}))
denotes the collection of all subsets of
\sigma(\hat{A})
. Recall that elements of
\Lambda\equiv\prod_{\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}}\sigma(\hat{A})
are tuples
\lambda\equiv(\lambda_{\hat{A}})_{\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}}
satisfying
\lambda_{\hat{A}}\in\sigma(\hat{A})
for all
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
. For each
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
, let
f_{\hat{A}}
be the coordinate projection function
\Lambda\rightarrow\sigma(\hat{A})
, that is,
f_{\hat{A}}(\lambda)\doteq\lambda_{\hat{A}}
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. It follows from the definition of product
\sigma
-algebra that
f_{\hat{A}}
is a measurable function
[
35
]
. For each
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
, denote by
\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)
the set of all
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\lambda_{A}=\alpha
, i.e.,

\begin{split}\Omega(\hat{A}=\alpha)&\equiv f_{\hat{A}}^{-1}(\{\alpha\})\\
&=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\lambda_{\hat{A}}=\alpha\}.\end{split}

(28)

Thus far, we have constructed a space of deterministic underlying states
\bm{\Lambda}\equiv(\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
and measurable functions
f_{\hat{A}}:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
representing observables. To conclude, we need to define probability measures representing pure states and recover Eqs. ((
12
)) and (
15
). So let
\psi
be a pure state. For any finite subset
\{\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m}\}
of
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, let

P_{\psi}[\vec{A}=\cdot\ ]\equiv P_{\psi}[\hat{A}_{1}=\cdot\ ,\dots,\hat{A}_{m}=\cdot\ ]

(29)

be the joint distribution of
\vec{A}\equiv(\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m})
in the state
\psi
(see Eq.
19
). Let
\pi
be any permutation of
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Then for any
\vec{\alpha}\equiv(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\hat{A}_{i})
,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}&[\vec{A}=\vec{\alpha}]=P_{\psi}[\hat{A}_{1}=\alpha_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m}=\alpha_{m}]\\
&=\left\langle\psi\left|\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}_{i}=\alpha_{i})\psi\right.\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle\psi\left|\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\Pi}(\hat{A}_{\pi(i)}=\alpha_{\pi(i)})\psi\right.\right\rangle\\
&=P_{\psi}[\hat{A}_{\pi(1)}=\alpha_{\pi(1)},\dots,\hat{A}_{\pi(m)}=\alpha_{\pi(m)}].\end{split}

(30)

Now let
\hat{A}_{n_{1}},\dots,\hat{A}_{n_{M}}
be a sub-sequence of
\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m}
. The joint distribution of
\hat{A}_{n_{1}},\dots,\hat{A}_{n_{M}}
is the joint distribution of
\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m}
marginalised over all observables in
\{\hat{A}_{1},\dots,\hat{A}_{m}\}
except
\hat{A}_{n_{1}},\dots,\hat{A}_{n_{M}}
— this is known as the non-disturbance condition
[
18
]
. Hence, it follows from the Kolmogorov extension theorem
[
35
]
that there exists a unique probability measure
\mu_{\psi}
on
\bm{\Lambda}

and values
\vec{\alpha}\equiv(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\hat{A}_{i})
,

\begin{split}P_{\psi}[\vec{A}=\vec{\alpha}]&=\mu_{\psi}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}f_{\hat{A}_{i}}^{-1}(\{\alpha_{i}\})\right)\\
&=\mu_{\psi}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\Omega(\hat{A}_{i}=\alpha_{i})\right)\end{split}

(31)

(see Theorem 2.4.3 of Ref.
[
35
]
for details). In particular, for any
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
and
\alpha\in\sigma(\hat{A})
, Eq. (
12
) is satisfied. Finally, let’s show that, for any state
\psi
, any observable
\hat{A}
and any value

, the state

\mu_{\psi}

14

19

31

where, as usual,

It proves that
