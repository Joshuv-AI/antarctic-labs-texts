Quality of Life Changes in Respiratory Infection Trajectory with Point of Care Testing During Clinical Consultation
Authors: Paula Rojas-Garcia, Reyes Lorente, Marino J González, Carmelo A Juárez-Castelló, Fernando Antoñanzas, Pim W M Van Dorst, Clazinus Veijer, Simon Van der Pol, Christopher C Butler, Alike W Van der Velden, Herman Goossens, Antoinette D I Van Asselt, Maarten J Postma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluates the impact of a point-of-care (POC) testing strategy and subsequent treatments for respiratory tract infections (RTIs) on patients’ health-related quality of life (QoL).Materials and MethodsThe PRUDENCE trial, with a sample of 2639 patients, showed that a POC testing strategy for RTIs (C-reactive protein, group A streptococcus, and influenza A/B, with optional SARS-CoV-2 testing) did not reduce antibiotic prescribing nor had a measurable impact on time to patient recovery compared to usual care. However, differences in patient-reported QoL may still exist, as utility can capture more subtle changes in symptom burden and well-being. The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire was completed by PRUDENCE trial patients at days 1, 14, and 28 of follow-up. Quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gains were estimated. Static and dynamic analyses were performed to assess changes in utility values.ResultsNo statistically significant differences in utility scores were observed between the usual care and POC testing groups at any time point during the 28-day study period. However, both groups demonstrated statistically significant improvements in patient-reported utility scores over the 28-day period, corresponding to gains of 0.00502 QALYs in the usual care group and 0.00525 QALYs in the POC testing group. These improvements were already apparent by day 14 of follow-up.ConclusionImprovements in utility scores over time were similar in both groups and likely reflected the natural clinical recovery from RTIs rather than the effect of the diagnostic strategy itself. Measures such as symptom relief, full recovery and side effects may better capture the intervention’s impact.Trial Registration NumberISRCTN13336322.

Introduction

The rise of antibiotic resistance is a growing global concern. Overuse and inappropriate consumption of antibiotics, especially for viral respiratory tract infections (RTIs) where they are ineffective, contribute significantly to the development of resistant bacteria.1 RTIs represent a public health issue because they can lead to complications, particularly in older adults with compromised immune systems,2 and also constitute a significant economic burden through healthcare costs and resource utilisation.3

Point-of-care (POC) diagnostic technologies could provide a fast approach to appropriate (withholding of) treatment4,5 and improve healthcare efficiency.6,7 However, evidence shows that POC testing for RTI in primary care did not reduce antibiotic prescribing compared to usual care,8 although it remains unclear whether POC testing affects patients’ utility values in the management of RTIs.9

It is difficult to measure increases in utility for short illness trajectories, which can be managed without and with effective treatments, such as antibiotics,10 primarily because utility measures often use longer time scales, and are designed to assess how an intervention affects quality of life (QoL) over an extended period.11 In the case of an RTI, symptoms often resolve quickly with or without antibiotics. This makes it complex to capture significant changes in QoL, or in the patient’s perception of improvement, as the time to observe a notable effect is short.12

A Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) is a measure used in economic evaluations of medical interventions to quantify the effectiveness of different medical treatments.13 QALYs are better suited to long-term interventions, such as treatments for chronic illnesses, where changes in QoL and health status can be observed more gradually.

Utility values, often measured by tools such as the European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L), are a crucial component of health economics and outcomes research, as they capture patients’ perceptions regarding health state and quantify their overall well-being.14 The EQ-5D-5L comprises five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is assessed across five levels of severity, allowing the definition of distinct health states. These states can be converted into utility values using country-specific value sets, typically ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (full health), although negative values are possible and reflect health states considered worse than death.13 These utility values are then used to estimate QALYs,14 enabling comparisons between different medical interventions in terms of their impact on QoL.15 The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), an integral part of the EQ-5D-5L,16 is a tool used in medical research to measure the intensity of a subjective experience related to a health status. It consists of a horizontal line of fixed length, generally 10 centimeters, on which the patient is asked to mark a point that reflects the intensity of their experience at that moment.17 The VAS allows the subjective experience of the patient to be captured in a quantitative way, thus allowing statistical analysis to be performed.18

Previous evidence on patient-reported outcomes associated with POC diagnostics for RTIs is limited. A systematic review of 70 economic analyses found that fewer than half used generalisable outcomes such as QALYs. Two studies evaluating POC CRP testing incorporated EQ-5D data and reported reductions in antibiotic prescribing but no significant differences in health outcomes. Similarly, a trial in hospitalised adults with chronic or acute cardiopulmonary illness found no major differences in QALYs between POC testing, PCR and conventional diagnostic strategies.19 Although no difference in the antibiotic prescribing and time to recovery, it could be the case that some QoL differences may have happened between the two arms, driven by symptoms or perceptions about the recovery process.

Initially, the objective of this study is to compare utility values between patients with RTIs having undergone POC testing to direct their therapeutic plan, and those treated according to usual care. By assessing the differences in reported utilities between the two groups, this study aims to gather evidence on whether POC testing-directed treatment enhances patients’ QoL and if such benefits justify integrating POC testing into clinical practice. Additionally, the study aims to quantify the impact of an RTI on QoL until recovery, including the magnitude and timing of changes in utility and QALYs.

Materials and Methods

Data

This study used data from the PRUDENCE trial. This clinical trial was implemented in 13 countries and recruited over 2639 patients who sought medical care in primary care due to their RTI symptoms, namely, sore throat and cough. Patients were randomized to usual care or to the POC testing group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between the trial groups. Further details of the trial have been published elsewhere.8

Data for analysis included the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire and the VAS completed by patients at three time points: day 1 (i.e, the day of consulting and randomization), and days 14 and 28 of follow-up. Each patient assessed their own health state by responding to the five EQ-5D-5L dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and anxiety/depression) and then scoring their general condition through a VAS.16 Given the usually short, self-limiting course of RTIs, the 28-day follow-up was selected to capture changes in health status during the acute illness and early recovery period. The EQ-5D-5L was selected because it provides a standardised preference-based measure of patient-reported health and enables utility values and QALYs to be estimated for economic evaluation. This allowed us to assess whether POC-guided management produced any detectable difference in patient-reported health over the short-term disease trajectory.

To convert health states into utilities, value sets for scoring the EQ-5D-5L were applied.16 These value sets are different between countries, as perceptions of health and QoL vary between different cultures and societies: what is considered good health may differ between countries due to differences in cultural values, beliefs and social norms.16 Countries with published value sets can be found on the EuroQol website.20 To see the distribution of patients by country and randomized group check Supplementary Material.

To calculate the gain or loss in QALYs, the difference in utilities was multiplied by 14/365 (when the period between day 1 and day 14 or between day 14 and day 28 was analyzed), and by 28/365 (when the period between day 1 and day 28 was analyzed). The result was divided by two. In this way, it was assumed that the gain or loss of QALYs is progressive between the pairs of moments that are analyzed and, consequently, the QALY gain is assumed to grow uniformly within the analyzed period. Thus, we calculated the QALY gain as the area of a trapezoid whose base is the time period and the height is the difference of utilities between day 1 as the baseline reference and the specific moment considered (day 14 or 28).

Static and Dynamic Analysis

The static analysis allowed to analyze whether there are differences in the utilities between the usual care and POC testing groups on day 1, on day 14 and on day 28. The responses from all patients within each group were pooled. Normality of utility distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the assumption of normality was met, between‑group comparisons were performed using the independent‑samples Welch’s T‑test was applied as the assumption of homogeneity of variances was not satisfied.

The dynamic analysis allowed to analyze the evolution of the patients’ utility from day 1 to day 14, from day 1 to day 28 and between day 14 and day 28 to see whether there are statistically significant differences within each group between these three intervals. Paired comparisons were conducted, as the same patients were followed at each pair of time points. Normality of the paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the assumption of normality was met, comparisons were performed using the paired Student’s T‑test. Only patients with complete paired data for each comparison were included in the analysis.

Discussion

This study is one of the few21 to measure the loss of QoL resulting from RTI-like symptoms, providing valuable insights into the potential benefits of tailored management for patients experiencing these signs. It is the first to compare utilities perceived by a group of patients that was diagnosed using a POC test (POC testing group) and by another group receiving traditional diagnosis (usual care group) at three points of time of disease progression (day 1, 14 and 28 of follow-up).

From the static analysis, differences in the utilities between groups on days 1, 14 and 28 of follow-up, our findings reveal that differences in utilities gain were not statistically significant. From the dynamic analysis, evolution of the patients’ utility from day 1 to day 14, from day 1 to day 28 and between day 14 and day 28, our findings reveal a utility gain of 0.13097 in the usual care group and 0.13687 in the POC testing group from day 1 to day 28 of follow-up, or 25 points of gain in the VAS score.

The absence of significant between-group differences indicates that the POC testing strategy had no measurable positive or negative effect on QoL compared with usual care. Although this finding does not demonstrate a QoL benefit of POC testing, the analysis provides estimates of short-term changes in utility and QALYs for economic evaluations, offering a useful reference point for future RTI studies. VAS scores provided complementary information: utility values reflect preference-weighted health status, whereas the VAS captures patients’ direct assessment of their health. However, alternative outcome measures to QALYs may be more appropriate such as metrics of treatment efficacy and efficiency such as speed of symptom relief, rates of infection eradication, and minimization of side effects.22,23

The 28-day follow-up period is appropriate for assessing changes in patient-perceived utility given the rapid resolution of symptoms related to potential RTIs. However, it does present a limitation in terms of long-term implications. These RTIs typically follow brief, self-limiting courses, making a short-term horizon particularly suitable, as extending to a longer-term horizon would not affect utility outcomes significantly due to the short course of the illness. Thus, it would not be a limitation unless the long-term disutilities associated with bacterial resistance were to be incorporated. This highlights a broader limitation in applying QALYs to acute, quickly resolving illnesses. Furthermore, when analyzing utility in patients with RTI-like symptoms using POC diagnostics, it is essential to leverage adaptable, real-time data metrics that better capture the clinical effectiveness of such interventions, ultimately informing public health policy and clinical decision-making with precision.24

The final decisions made by physicians regarding antibiotic prescriptions were not included in this analysis. Although this study did not stratify patients based on actual antibiotic treatment to assess differences in QoL, a future study that specifically evaluates QoL outcomes in patients who receive antibiotics versus those who do not could provide deeper insights. Such an approach could help detect potential gaps in usual care practices and identify areas for improvement in appropriate antibiotic use.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that while POC diagnostics did not yield a utility difference compared to standard care, both approaches effectively supported recovery from RTI-type symptoms. Although this limits the direct clinical implications of the findings, the absence of a measurable QoL benefit provides useful evidence for the evaluation of POC testing strategies and highlights the need for more sensitive outcome measures in acute RTIs. Future research should focus on metrics specific to short-term outcomes and consider the broader public health implications of antibiotic use to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its value in clinical practice.
