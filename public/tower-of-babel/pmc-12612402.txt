Causal relationships between blood metabolites and glioblastoma risk: a large-scale Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Lin Huang, Yazhou Tang, Yali Tan, Wei Lei, Yuan Wang, Yongjie Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) represents the most common and lethal primary brain tumor with incompletely understood pathogenesis. This study aims to systematically evaluate the causal relationships between blood metabolites and glioblastoma risk using Mendelian randomization approaches, providing metabolic biomarker evidence for disease prevention and early diagnosis.MethodsThree Mendelian randomization analytical methods (inverse variance weighted, MR Egger regression, and weighted median) were employed to comprehensively assess causal relationships between multiple blood metabolites and glioblastoma. The study encompassed numerous metabolites including phospholipids, amino acid derivatives, vitamins, and carnitines. Multi-dimensional analytical approaches including forest plots, scatter plots, and funnel plots were used to evaluate effect estimates, dose-response relationships, and potential heterogeneity and pleiotropy bias.ResultsAmong the multiple blood metabolites examined, most showed no significant causal relationship with glioblastoma risk. However, the study identified three key metabolites with significant protective effects: 1-linoleoyl-GPI, tryptophan betaine, and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-GPE. Elevated blood levels of these metabolites demonstrated robust causal relationships with reduced glioblastoma risk, with consistent results across all three analytical methods and no significant horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity bias detected.ConclusionsThis study represents the first large-scale Mendelian randomization analysis to establish causal relationships between multiple blood metabolites and glioblastoma risk, particularly identifying protective factors within phospholipid and tryptophan metabolic pathways.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Blood metabolites, Mendelian randomization, Causal inference, Metabolic biomarkers

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) stands as the most prevalent and lethal primary brain malignancy in adult populations, comprising 54% of all gliomas and 16% of primary brain neoplasms. Current therapeutic interventions encompassing advanced surgical resection, radiotherapy protocols, and chemotherapeutic regimens have yielded only modest improvements in patient outcomes, with median survival remaining at 12–15 months and five-year survival rates below 5%, reflecting the exceptionally grave prognosis associated with this disease [1–3]. The hallmark features of GBM include pronounced cellular heterogeneity, aggressive invasive behavior, and remarkable therapeutic resistance, collectively creating substantial obstacles for both clinical diagnosis and effective treatment strategies. Consequently, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of GBM pathogenesis and discovering novel biomarkers alongside therapeutic targets remains critically important for enhancing patient survival and quality of life.

The emerging field of metabolomics has shown considerable promise in advancing our understanding of tumor biology. Metabolic rewiring within malignant cells constitutes a fundamental characteristic of carcinogenesis, serving not merely as an energy source and substrate provider for accelerated cellular proliferation and metastatic spread, but also functioning as a key regulator of the tumor microenvironment and mechanisms of immune system evasion. Circulating metabolites serve as valuable indicators of systemic metabolic states and may contribute significantly to both tumor initiation and disease progression. While earlier investigations have suggested correlations between specific blood metabolite concentrations and malignancy risk across various cancer types, such observational studies frequently suffer from confounding variables and issues of reverse causation, thereby limiting the establishment of definitive causal relationships [4–6].

Traditional observational epidemiological studies face methodological challenges such as residual confounding, reverse causation, and measurement errors when exploring relationships between metabolites and diseases. Mendelian randomization (MR), as an emerging causal inference method, utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables to simulate randomized controlled trials, effectively overcoming the aforementioned limitations of observational studies. This approach is based on Mendel’s laws of inheritance and leverages the random allocation characteristics of genetic variants to largely avoid interference from confounding factors, providing robust evidence for establishing causal relationships between exposure factors and outcome variables [7–9].

Currently, research on the causal relationships between blood metabolites and glioblastoma remains relatively limited, with most studies having small sample sizes and employing single analytical methods. With the continuous accumulation of large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data and rapid development of metabolomics technologies, important data foundations have been established for conducting high-quality Mendelian randomization studies. Systematically evaluating the causal relationships between multiple blood metabolites and GBM risk not only contributes to a deeper understanding of GBM metabolic mechanisms but may also provide new insights for early diagnosis, risk assessment, and personalized prevention strategies [10, 11].

Based on this rationale, our study employed multiple Mendelian randomization analytical methods to systematically evaluate the causal relationships between various blood metabolites including phospholipids, amino acid derivatives, vitamins, and carnitines and glioblastoma risk. Through multi-dimensional analyses including forest plots, scatter plots, and funnel plots, we aimed to identify key metabolic biomarkers with protective or risk effects, providing scientific evidence for glioblastoma prevention and early intervention.

Methods

Study design overview

This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization design to systematically evaluate the causal relationships between blood metabolites and glioblastoma risk. The Mendelian randomization approach utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables and, based on the random allocation principle of Mendel’s laws of inheritance, effectively simulates randomized controlled trial designs, thereby overcoming confounding factors, reverse causality, and selection bias inherent in traditional observational studies. This method infers causal relationships between exposure and outcomes by analyzing the effects of genetic variants on both exposure factors and outcome variables, providing a robust evidence base for causal inference regarding the relationship between metabolites and glioblastoma.

Data sources and sample characteristics

This investigation utilized genetic association data sourced from extensive genome-wide association study repositories. The exposure dataset comprised genetic variant information spanning diverse blood metabolites, encompassing phospholipid compounds (including 1-linoleoyl-GPI and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-GPE), amino acid derivatives (such as tryptophan betaine and sphingomyelin), vitamin compounds (including retinol), carnitine derivatives (such as eicosanoylcarnitine), along with additional lipid species, organic acids, and metabolic pathway intermediates. Glioblastoma outcome data were extracted from corresponding large-scale case-control investigations, with careful attention to ensuring that exposure and outcome datasets originated from distinct study cohorts to prevent bias arising from potential participant overlap. To minimize population stratification effects on findings, all incorporated studies were restricted to European ancestry populations.

Instrumental variable selection and validation

The selection of instrumental variables followed rigorous adherence to the fundamental three assumptions underlying Mendelian randomization methodology. Initially, we verified that chosen genetic variants demonstrated robust associations with blood metabolite concentrations, mandating that single nucleotide polymorphisms reach genome-wide significance thresholds for their metabolite associations. Subsequently, F-statistic calculations were performed to evaluate instrumental variable strength, eliminating potential weak instrument bias by requiring F-statistics to exceed 10. Linkage disequilibrium assessment was conducted to confirm independence among selected SNPs, preventing information redundancy due to linkage disequilibrium patterns. Furthermore, through systematic database consultation and literature review, genetic variants linked to recognized confounding variables were eliminated to satisfy the independence criterion. Lastly, we verified that genetic variants influenced glioblastoma risk exclusively through their effects on metabolite concentrations, without alternative direct pathways [12–14].

Mendelian randomization analysis strategy

Three complementary Mendelian randomization approaches were implemented for causal effect estimation to strengthen result reliability and analytical robustness. The inverse variance weighted approach constituted the primary analytical framework, operating under the assumption that all genetic variants represent valid instrumental variables and deriving overall causal effects through weighted integration of individual SNP effect estimates. MR Egger regression methodology permits causal inference despite horizontal pleiotropy presence, evaluating pleiotropy bias through testing whether the regression intercept significantly differs from zero, with the regression slope providing the pleiotropy-corrected causal effect estimate. The weighted median approach exhibits substantial robustness, delivering consistent causal effect estimates even under conditions where 50% of instrumental variables are invalid. Cross-method comparison of result consistency strengthens causal inference credibility.

Sensitivity analysis and quality control

Multiple sensitivity analyses were implemented to guarantee study result robustness and reliability. Heterogeneity assessment employed Cochran’s Q statistic to evaluate effect estimate consistency across different SNPs, with significant heterogeneity indicating potential pleiotropy or additional biases. Pleiotropy evaluation was conducted through MR-Egger intercept testing to assess horizontal pleiotropy, while funnel plot examination evaluated potential publication bias and pleiotropy patterns. Leave-one-out analysis identified genetic variants with disproportionate influence on overall findings by systematically excluding individual SNPs and conducting reanalysis, confirming that results were not dependent on any single SNP. Furthermore, multivariable Mendelian randomization analysis was executed to simultaneously control for multiple related metabolites, yielding more precise independent effect estimates.

Statistical methods and visualization

All causal effects were expressed as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Considering multiple comparison issues, Bonferroni correction was applied to significant results. Study results were presented through various visualization methods, with forest plots intuitively displaying effect estimates and confidence intervals for each metabolite, facilitating comparison of effect sizes and directions across different metabolites. Scatter plots showed the distribution of SNP effects on exposure and outcomes, along with fitted lines from three Mendelian randomization methods, helping evaluate dose-response relationships and consistency among methods [15, 16]. Funnel plots assessed methodological quality and potential bias by displaying distribution patterns of individual SNP effect estimates. All statistical analyses were completed using R software, primarily utilizing specialized packages such as TwoSampleMR and MendelianRandomization, with plots generated using the ggplot2 package.

Ethical considerations and data management

This study conducted secondary analysis based on publicly available genome-wide association study summary statistics, without involving individual-level genetic information or clinical data. All original studies had obtained appropriate ethics committee approval and participant informed consent, with data usage complying with relevant ethical standards and legal requirements. The research process strictly adhered to data protection principles, ensuring scientific rigor in data analysis and objectivity in result interpretation. All analysis codes and supplementary data will be made publicly available upon study publication to promote reproducibility and transparency of research results.

Discussion

Building upon the protective metabolites identified in our study, analysis of dopamine metabolic pathways provides us with a more complete picture of neuro-metabolic-glioma relationships. The three protective metabolites we identified—1-linoleoyl-GPI, tryptophan betaine, and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-GPE—may have important synergistic mechanisms with dopamine metabolites. Tryptophan betaine, as a product of the tryptophan metabolic pathway, belongs to the same neurotransmitter-related metabolic network as dopamine, and both may exert synergistic protective effects in maintaining central nervous system homeostasis. Tryptophan is not only a precursor for serotonin synthesis but also participates in neuroinflammatory regulation through the kynurenine pathway, while dopamine primarily regulates neuronal excitability and reward mechanisms [17, 18]. Normal function of these two metabolic pathways may collectively constitute a metabolic protective barrier against glioblastoma by maintaining the stability of the neural microenvironment to suppress tumorigenesis. Our Mendelian randomization analysis revealed an important phenomenon: products from multiple different metabolic pathways all showed protective effects against glioblastoma, suggesting the existence of a systemic neural metabolic protection network. Phospholipid metabolites 1-linoleoyl-GPI and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-GPE primarily participate in membrane stability and signal transduction, while tryptophan betaine and dopamine metabolites are more involved in neurotransmitter function and neuroprotection. This multi-level, multi-pathway protective mechanism indicates that glioblastoma development may require overcoming the failure of multiple metabolic defense lines. Under normal physiological conditions, phospholipid metabolism maintains membrane integrity and normal intercellular communication, neurotransmitter metabolism ensures normal neural network function, and the coordinated action of these metabolic pathways provides the brain with powerful anti-tumor barriers. When multiple metabolic pathways simultaneously develop functional disorders, they may create a metabolic environment favorable for glioma development [19–21].

Our findings indicate that protective metabolites may function through interconnected networks rather than isolated pathways. The intersection between dopamine and phospholipid metabolism occurs at the cellular membrane level, where dopamine receptor functionality relies on intact phospholipid composition. Disrupted phospholipid metabolism can compromise dopamine receptor expression and signaling cascades. Tryptophan betaine contributes to this protective network through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, supporting neuronal and glial cell health while maintaining optimal neurotransmitter metabolic conditions. This network amplification effect may explain the robust statistical significance observed in our Mendelian randomization analyses, emphasizing the importance of adopting systems biology approaches over single-target methodologies when investigating metabolite-tumor relationships.

The identification of multiple protective metabolites enables the development of comprehensive glioblastoma risk assessment frameworks. Multi-metabolite panels may demonstrate superior predictive accuracy compared to individual biomarkers, enhancing both sensitivity and specificity in glioma risk evaluation. Therapeutic interventions targeting diverse metabolic pathways could yield synergistic benefits combining nutritional supplementation to optimize phospholipid levels with lifestyle modifications to enhance neurotransmitter metabolism. Given the connection between dopamine metabolism and mood regulation, effective prevention strategies should incorporate psychological wellness and stress management components. These discoveries also present multiple therapeutic targets for novel drug development, where simultaneous modulation of protective metabolic networks may achieve enhanced treatment efficacy.This investigation demonstrates the superior value of integrated multi-pathway metabolomics analysis over traditional single-metabolite approaches in complex disease causal inference. By synthesizing findings across phospholipid, amino acid, and neurotransmitter metabolism within a unified analytical framework, we identified critical associations and interactions that compartmentalized analyses might overlook. This systematic methodology not only strengthens research credibility but also provides deeper mechanistic insights into glioblastoma pathogenesis.

Several research priorities emerge from these findings. Longitudinal cohort studies should track dynamic changes in protective metabolite profiles throughout glioma development to validate biomarker temporal stability and predictive utility. Functional genomics investigations must elucidate how genetic variants regulating these metabolites exert protective effects through gene expression and protein function modulation. Multi-omics integration combining genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic datasets will enable comprehensive systems biology modeling of glioblastoma pathogenesis. Environmental factor evaluation should assess how external influences modulate protective metabolite levels and functions, informing personalized prevention strategies. Translation of these metabolic discoveries into therapeutic development and clinical trials represents the ultimate goal for advancing glioblastoma prevention and treatment.

Limitations

Several constraints limit our findings. The analysis primarily utilized European population genetic data, requiring validation across diverse ethnic groups to address potential population-specific biases. Mendelian randomization assumes linear dose-response relationships, potentially missing complex nonlinear metabolite-disease interactions. GWAS data limitations prevented detailed analysis of metabolite subtypes, concentration thresholds, or temporal pathway dynamics. Gene-environment interactions affecting metabolite regulation and complex inter-metabolite network effects remained inadequately addressed. While causal relationships were established, specific biological mechanisms require additional functional validation studies.

Conclusions

This study, through large-scale Mendelian randomization analysis, represents the first systematic establishment of causal relationships between blood metabolites and glioblastoma risk. We identified three key metabolites with significant protective effects: 1-linoleoyl-GPI, tryptophan betaine, and 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-GPE, findings that involve multiple important pathways including phospholipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and neurotransmitter metabolism.
