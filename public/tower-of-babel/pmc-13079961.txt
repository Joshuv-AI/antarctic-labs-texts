Landscape of Aging Policies in Bangladesh: A Perspective Study
Authors: Md Jamil Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAging is currently a global concern, as there has been a massive transition in life expectancy and lower mortality rates because of advances in medical science. Bangladesh is experiencing a demographic transition with its elderly population, projected to reach 21% by 2050. This demographic shift raises significant social, economic, healthcare, and social inclusion challenges. This prospective study aimed to review the current landscape of aging policies in Bangladesh and assess their implementation, existing gaps, and opportunities.MethodsThis study utilized a narrative review approach to search existing literature on aging policy in Bangladesh on PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and government and non‐governmental policy documents. The analysis focused on essential objectives, including our demographic trends regarding the aging population, existing policies and programs, legal frameworks, and their implementation and gaps. The perspective was written based on the available information from literature and policy documents.ResultsBangladesh already has some key policy and legal frameworks, such as the National Policy on Older Persons (2013), the National Social Security Strategy (2015), and the Old‐Age Allowance Program, alongside legal measures like the Maintenance of Parents Act (2013). These frameworks showed a growing commitment to older people's welfare and overall well‐being. However, the actual impact of this policy is limited by insufficient funding, poor inter‐ministerial coordination, inadequate healthcare infrastructure and facilities tailored to the needs of older adults. Additionally, persistent disparities, particularly along rural–urban and gender lines, further limit equitable access to these benefits and services.ConclusionWhile Bangladesh has established some policy foundations and a legal framework for addressing aging, their effective implementation remains a significant challenge. Strengthening inter‐ministerial coordination, expanding financial and healthcare support, improving legal enforcement, and developing robust data systems are essential to eradicate this problem. Political commitment and inclusive strategies are crucial to ensure dignity, equity, and well‐being for the country's growing older population.

Introduction

Aging is the natural and inevitable process that causes deterioration of biological functions over time. It leads to a decline in health, increased vulnerability, and a greater risk of illness and death over time [1]. Aging has become a global concern that shapes social, economic, and health policy agendas across nations [2]. With declining fertility rates and improvements in life expectancy, the world is experiencing a significant demographic transition to an aging population [2]. According to a report by the United Nations, people aged 60 and over will more than double, from 1.2 billion in 2024 to 2.1 billion by 2050 [3]. The rapid demographic change will create global challenges and opportunities for societies, impacting areas like healthcare, social welfare, and economic growth [3].

Bangladesh became independent in 1971, and as of 2022, its population has increased from 75 million to 169.83 million as per the last census [4]. The average annual growth rate of the population is 1.22% per year [4]. In the early 1970s, the total fertility rate was 6.9 births per woman in Bangladesh. In 2020, that number had declined to 2.0 births per woman [5]. In Bangladesh, the population aged 60 and over was over 15.3 million, or 9.28% of the total population [6]. By 2050, it is projected that Bangladesh will have 44 million elderly citizens, constituting approximately 22% of the total population, which is a significant increase from just 1.94 million in 1951 [7, 8].

As a result, there has been a pressing need to comprehend the security and well‐being of older people, particularly for elderly poor women and those who reside in rural areas [6]. The 2013 National Policy for Older Persons recognized the importance of senior persons’ well‐being and worked to improve their circumstances by guaranteeing their safety and promoting their welfare in society [9]. Formulating and implementing policies for older people is crucial in ensuring social justice, equity, and human rights. Globally, commitments such as the Madrid International Plan of Action on Ageing (2002) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) call for inclusive development that leaves no one behind, including older populations [10]. For Bangladesh, aligning national policies with these global frameworks ensures that older citizens are recognized as an integral part of national development rather than as a dependent group [11, 12]. Good policies also make life easier for families, bring people together, and protect people's dignity as they get older.

This prospective study aims to review and analyze the landscape of aging policy in Bangladesh. It has provided an overview of demographic transitions, evaluated the national policy framework, and assessed the implementation and gaps of programs designed for older people. In addition, it highlights strengths, weaknesses, and barriers in existing policies, while identifying critical gaps in policies for the elderly population. Overall, there have been limited studies on the aging population in Bangladesh compared to other groups. Thus, the study aims to inform future policy directions and recommend strategies for building a more inclusive, equitable, and sustainable aging policy framework.

Context of Aging in Bangladesh

Current Elderly Population Statistics

Bangladesh, home to over 170 million people, is experiencing a steady demographic shift with a growing elderly population [4]. The proportion of people aged 60 years and above has increased from 6.9% in 2001 to nearly 9% in 2022, representing more than 15 million individuals [12]. Projections by the United Nations and the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) suggest that this share will continue to rise, reaching approximately 21% of the total population by 2050 [3, 4, 12]. This means that by mid‐century, one out of every five Bangladeshis will be elderly. The majority of this population resides in rural areas, where access to healthcare, employment, and social protection mechanisms is often limited [13]. Furthermore, the feminization of aging is becoming increasingly relevant as women generally live longer than men, resulting in a higher proportion of widowed and single elderly women who face unique socio‐economic vulnerabilities such as increased risk of poverty, social isolation, and limited access to healthcare services [4, 13].

Life Expectancy Trends

Life expectancy in Bangladesh has significantly improved over the past five decades, reflecting advances in healthcare, nutrition, and public health interventions. According to a report by the World Bank, a newborn is anticipated to live over 70 years, whereas in 1972, life expectancy was less than 50 years [14]. According to our latest census in 2022, it had risen to approximately 73 years [4]. This remarkable achievement, however, is accompanied by new challenges. As people live longer, the likelihood of chronic illnesses, disabilities, and the need for long‐term care also increases [15]. Non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and hypertension are becoming more prevalent among older populations, often complicated by limited geriatric care infrastructure [15].

Major Challenges

The elderly population in Bangladesh faces a constellation of interrelated challenges that threaten their well‐being and quality of life. Many older individuals suffer from poverty and income insecurity. Many older people lack access to formal employment‐based pensions or retirement savings [12]. Although the government's Old Age Allowance program provides some financial support, its coverage and benefit levels remain insufficient to address widespread income insecurity [12]. In addition, older people have limited access to healthcare services. The healthcare system in Bangladesh is mostly oriented toward maternal and child health, neglecting geriatric services. Older people often face high out‐of‐pocket expenditures, limited availability of specialized care, and a lack of affordable medicines [6, 8]. Elderly individuals, particularly women and those without family support, often experience social isolation and neglect [16]. Older people are at risk of exclusion from decision‐making processes and community participation [16, 17].

Cultural Context: Role of Family and Community

Traditionally, the family has been the primary source of care and support for older people in Bangladesh [18]. Respect for elders and filial responsibility are deeply rooted in cultural and religious values, and older adults have historically relied on their children, particularly sons, for financial, emotional, and physical support [19, 20]. Community networks and religious institutions have also played important roles in providing informal care and social integration [21]. However, these traditional safety nets are becoming weaker day by day. Nowadays, younger generations are increasingly moving to cities or abroad in search of work, leaving many elderly parents behind in rural areas with limited support [22]. As a result, although cultural norms still value respect and care for the elderly, the reality often reflects a gap between expectation and practice, as many elderly parents are left without adequate support systems due to their children's migration for work.

Policy Framework

National Policy on Older Persons (2013)

The Constitution of the People's Republic of Bangladesh guarantees equality, non‐discrimination, and access to basic necessities, including food, shelter, healthcare, education, and social security [23]. The National Policy on Older Persons (NPOP), 2013, was formulated to safeguard the rights and dignity of its elderly population. Furthermore, this policy was developed following the international frameworks such as the Vienna International Plan of Action on Ageing (1982) [24] and the Madrid International Plan of Action on Ageing in 2002 [10]. In the years preceding the NPOP, the government initiated several programs, such as the Old Age Allowance Program (OAAP) in 1998, and integrated aging issues into broader policies, including the Health Population & Nutrition Sector Development Plan (2011), the National Population Policy (2012), and the Social Welfare Policy (2012). However, these policies were found to be inadequate to comprehensively address the diverse needs of the elderly, particularly in the context of demographic shift, rapid urbanization, weakening family support systems, and rising life expectancy [9].

The NPOP defined elderly persons as individuals aged 60 years and above and outlined 11 key objectives to ensure their welfare, as per the Ministry of Social Welfare [9]. These included mainstreaming aging concerns into national development policies, recognizing the contributions of senior citizens to society, and ensuring their active participation in local governance and development initiatives. It also emphasizes pension plans and social safety nets for older people who are most in need. A strong emphasis was placed on healthcare, urging government and non‐government institutions to prioritize geriatric services. Legal protection for older people was also emphasized. Additionally, its priority is the elimination of discrimination against elderly women and persons with disabilities, as well as the promotion of research and awareness to strengthen intergenerational solidarity. The policy outlines 11 key objectives aimed at ensuring the welfare, rights, and social protection of older people [9, 12] (Table 1).

National Social Security Strategy (2015)

The National Social Security Strategy (NSSS), 2015, is a big step forward for Bangladesh's overall social protection. It gives a basic framework for dealing with poverty, vulnerability, and social exclusion [25, 26]. The NSSS was developed under the Seventh 5‐Year Plan (FY2016–2020) and aligns with both constitutional provisions and international commitments, including the SDGs, which call for inclusive development and protection of vulnerable groups [26].

A central feature of the NSSS is its life‐cycle approach, which aims to provide targeted interventions for different stages of life, starting from childhood to old age [26]. Within this framework, older people are explicitly recognized as a vulnerable group requiring dedicated programs to ensure income security, access to health services, and social dignity. The strategy emphasizes strengthening and expanding the OAAP and gradually increasing its coverage and benefits to reach the poorest and most marginalized elderly citizens [25]. In addition, the NSSS calls for better coordination among ministries, improved targeting mechanisms, and the establishment of monitoring systems to enhance program efficiency [25].

Old‐Age Allowance Program

In Bangladesh, the Old‐Age Allowance (OAA) program is a government‐funded social safety net that was implemented in FY 1997–1998 to assist older people who are impoverished, like women, who are eligible at age 62, while males are at age 65 [27]. Initiated by the Ministry of Social Welfare, the program was intended to provide financial assistance to poor and marginalized elderly citizens who lacked reliable sources of income or family support. Over time, the program has expanded significantly in terms of both coverage and allocation. By 2024, over 60 million elderly individuals were beneficiaries of this program, and the monthly benefit amount had been gradually increased in line with inflation and cost‐of‐living considerations over the decades [27]. The program prioritizes vulnerable groups such as elderly women, widows, and individuals living in extreme poverty, particularly in rural areas.

Healthcare Services (Geriatrics, Clinics, NGO Initiatives)

Healthcare provision for older persons in Bangladesh remains a critical component of the policy landscape, given the growing prevalence of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), disabilities, and age‐related conditions among the elderly. Although the country has made progress in expanding health coverage, specialized geriatric services are still limited and unevenly distributed. Public healthcare facilities rarely offer dedicated geriatric units, and most elderly patients receive care within general outpatient and inpatient departments [28].

Some steps have been taken to meet these needs. For instance, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare has been trying to introduce geriatric corners in selected district hospitals and urban health centers [29]. Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMCH) opened a special geriatric unit in October 2014 [30]. At the community level, Upazila Health Complexes and community clinics play an important role in delivering primary healthcare to elderly populations. However, constraints such as shortages of trained professionals, inadequate infrastructure, and limited supply of essential medicines hinder the quality of services provided [31]. Non‐governmental organizations (NGOs) and private sector actors also contribute to elderly healthcare [32].

Legal Frameworks and Institutional Arrangements

The legal and institutional framework for aging in Bangladesh reflects a gradual recognition of the rights and needs of older citizens. The most prominent legal provision is Bangladesh's Parents' Care Act (2013). The Shantan Kotrik Pita Matar Voron Poshon Ain (2013) law, which obligates adult children to provide financial and social support to their aging parents, reinforces the traditional family‐based care system [33]. According to Section Results, 3 of this Act, each child is obligated to pay their parents’ maintenance. When multiple kids live with their parents, they discuss and split the costs. Furthermore, children are also allowed to live with their parents and are not allowed to force them to relocate or go somewhere else against their will. While this law formalizes filial responsibility, it has faced challenges in enforcement, particularly where family ties are weak or financial constraints limit support [12].

Implementation and Gaps

Implementing aging‐related policies and programs in Bangladesh has brought notable progress, but several gaps remain in ensuring a comprehensive support system for the country's older population. One of the major achievements has been the gradual expansion of coverage under social protection schemes such as the OAAP, which has reached millions of beneficiaries across the country. The adoption of the NPOP and NSSS consolidated various programs that promote social participation, access to healthcare, and protection from discrimination and helped maintain stress on the national development agenda.

Despite these advances, implementation has been constrained by limited financial resources. For instance, although the OAAP has expanded, the monthly allowance remains inadequate to cover basic living costs. The Bangladeshi government's development budget does not properly account for the substantial funding and investment needed to implement the policy. Policies require detailed operational rules for effective implementation, which have not yet been formulated. Moreover, inter‐organizational coordination is one of the main reasons for the poor and ineffective application of the current policy. Due to a lack of such inter‐organizational collaboration and coordination of approaches, the policy lacks clear operational directions.

Furthermore, this policy didn't emphasize the concerns of elderly women, especially rural women. There are also a number of structural and social barriers that make it even harder to put the plan into action. A key challenge is the shortage of trained professionals in geriatric medicine, nursing, and social work, which limits the capacity of institutions to provide comprehensive care. Cultural stigma around aging, particularly the perception of older people as dependent and non‐productive, also contributes to their marginalization and weakens advocacy for more vigorous policy enforcement. Additionally, weak monitoring and evaluation mechanisms reduce accountability in the delivery of programs.

Recommendations

To effectively strengthen aging policies in Bangladesh, it is recommended that the government establish inter‐ministerial coordination among relevant ministries to eliminate policy fragmentation and enhance collaboration. A national database and Management Information System (MIS) should be developed for the elderly population to improve program planning, targeting, and monitoring. The Old‐Age Allowance Program also needs to be expanded in terms of coverage and monthly benefits. Geriatric care services also need to be expanded by adding more care units and specialized healthcare services. Training for healthcare professionals in geriatric medicine is essential, as is the reinforcement of legal and institutional frameworks to ensure the effective enforcement of the Maintenance of Parents Act (2013). Quick trials should be introduced for elderly rights violations, and the authority and accountability of national aging committees need to be enhanced to ensure timely justice and effective advocacy for the rights of the elderly population. Finally, it is crucial to secure political and financial commitment by allocating sufficient funding for elderly programs and integrating aging issues into national development and poverty reduction strategies.

Conclusion

Bangladesh is experiencing a demographic shift with a rapidly growing elderly population, which has been recognized through policies such as the National Policy on Older Persons (2013), the National Social Security Strategy (2015), and the Old‐Age Allowance Program. These initiatives, alongside legal measures like the Maintenance of Parents Act (2013), demonstrate a commitment to promoting the rights, welfare, and inclusion of older citizens. They provide a foundation for social protection, healthcare, and legal safeguards for senior citizens. However, limited funding, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, rural‐urban disparities, and weak institutional coordination continue to undermine progress. Addressing these gaps requires stronger inter‐ministerial collaboration, increased financial and healthcare support, robust data systems, and efforts to combat aging‐related problems. With adequate resources and political commitment, Bangladesh can ensure that its aging population lives with dignity, security, and meaningful social participation.
