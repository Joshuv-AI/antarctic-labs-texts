Lumbar Decompression is Associated With Changes in Functional Transition Biomechanics and Neuromuscular Control During Sit-to-Stand in Degenerative Lumbar Stenosis
Authors: Ye Shu, Prasanth Romiyo, Andrew Gansas, Paul Rubery, Mark Lawlor, Ashley Rogerson, Andrew Megas, Varun Puvanesarajah, Ram Haddas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignProspective cohort study.ObjectivesTo compare sit-to-stand (STS) movement patterns and characterize neuromuscular activation and compensatory strategies in patients with degenerative lumbar stenosis (DLS) before and after lumbar decompression, relative to healthy controls.MethodsThirty-five DLS patients undergoing central decompression and sixteen healthy controls were included in the study. Patients were evaluated one week preoperatively and three months postoperatively; controls were tested once. STS kinematics and muscle activation were quantified using three-dimensional motion capture and surface EMG across three trials without upper-extremity assistance. Outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects regression models adjusted for age and body mass index, with subject-level random intercepts.ResultsAt peak trunk forward lean, postoperative patients shifted toward a lower-extremity–driven strategy, with greater knee flexion, reduced pelvic anterior tilt, and reduced thoracic flexion (25.9° vs 30.4°, p < 0.001). Neuromuscular coordination improved postoperatively. Normalized EMG findings reflect relative activation patterns and should not be interpreted as absolute muscle effort. Tibialis anterior activation occurred earlier (4.6% vs 10.1%, p = 0.002) with lower mean activity, suggesting improved anticipatory ankle control. Gluteus maximus showed earlier onset (2.9% vs 9.0%, p = 0.018), longer activation duration (p = 0.001), and lower mean amplitude (0.9 vs 1.8, p = 0.002), reflecting more efficient hip extension. Erector spinae activation was delayed (9.3% vs 4.3%, p = 0.031) with reduced duration (p = 0.003), indicating decreased reliance on protective trunk co-contraction. Patient-reported outcomes improved significantly, including disability (ODI: 24.8 vs 44.4), fear of movement (TSK: 36.2 vs 43.4), physical function (PROMIS PF: 42.3 vs 35.1), and pain interference (PROMIS PI: 57.3 vs 66.2) (all p < 0.001).ConclusionLumbar decompression was associated with measurable changes in functional transition biomechanics and neuromuscular coordination during sit-to-stand. Postoperatively, patients demonstrated reduced reliance on trunk-dominant compensatory strategies and protective extensor co-contraction, with greater lower-extremity contribution and improved anticipatory neuromuscular control. Importantly, these objective recovery signatures were detected despite minimal differences in task completion time, suggesting that conventional time-based assessments may underestimate meaningful postoperative functional recovery. Objective biomechanical assessment of activities of daily living may provide a sensitive measure of recovery following lumbar decompression and may help guide postoperative rehabilitation and functional optimization in patients with degenerative lumbar stenosis.

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar stenosis (DLS) is one of the most common causes of functional debilitation in older adults and affects an estimated 11% of adults in the United States. 1 Patients commonly present with symptoms of radiculopathy and neurogenic claudication, resulting in impaired mobility, functional dependence, and reduced quality of life.2,3 Patients with DLS frequently adopt compensatory movement strategies during activities of daily living (ADLs). These are characterized by altered trunk posture, reduced lower-extremity contribution, and modified neuromuscular activation patterns intended to minimize symptom provocation during weight-bearing tasks.4,5 Lumbar decompression surgery is commonly performed to relieve neural compression and reduce symptoms associated with DLS. 4 Current postoperative evaluation in spine surgery relies predominantly on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), pain scores, and basic performance metrics, which may not fully characterize persistent deficits in movement coordination and motor control.6-9 Postoperative improvement in pain and walking tolerance has been widely reported after lumbar decompression.4,7,10,11 Less is known about how surgery alters movement quality, neuromuscular coordination, and compensatory motor behavior during functional transitions like sit-to-stand (STS).

The STS task is a fundamental component of daily life. 12 It is a functional maneuver that integrates trunk, lower-extremity, and neuromuscular control and is commonly compromised in patients with lumbar pathology presenting with pain and disability. 13 While conventional clinical assessments often demonstrate postoperative improvement, they do not quantify residual abnormalities in movement coordination and neuromuscular control during functional transitions such as STS. Biomechanical analysis using motion capture and dynamic surface electromyography (EMG) provides an objective method for quantifying movement strategies, dynamic compensation, and neuromuscular coordination during functional activities. 14 By examining both joint kinematics and neuromuscular activation patterns, it is possible to better understand how patients adapt their movement strategies following lumbar decompression. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to characterize objective biomechanical and neuromuscular recovery during sit-to-stand following lumbar decompression in patients with degenerative lumbar stenosis. Sit-to-stand was selected because it represents a clinically meaningful functional transition integrating trunk control, lower-extremity force generation, postural regulation, and anticipatory neuromuscular coordination. We hypothesized that preoperatively, patients with DLS would demonstrate a trunk-dominant compensatory strategy with altered neuromuscular activation relative to controls, and that these abnormalities would partially improve following decompression but may not fully normalize at early postoperative follow-up.

Methods

Study Design

This was a prospective cohort study that included data from 2024 to 2026 within a single-center, concurrent cohort design, approved by the University of Rochester Institutional Review Board (STUDY00008419), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Surgical patients were assessed within 1 week before surgery and 3 months after surgery. Asymptomatic controls underwent a single assessment during the same study period and served as a comparison group.

Preparatory and testing procedures

DLS patients were tested one week before surgery and 3 months after surgery. Asymptomatic controls were tested at a mutually convenient time. Before each visit, DLS patients completed the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), and Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK). Preoperative standing radiographs were obtained to assess spinopelvic alignment parameters, including pelvic tilt, sacral slope, pelvic incidence, lumbar lordosis, and pelvic incidence–lumbar lordosis mismatch (PI–LL) 10 (Figure 1).Figure 1.Upright pre-operative (A) and three month post-operative (B) lateral radiographs of an L3-5 decompression

Upright pre-operative (A) and three month post-operative (B) lateral radiographs of an L3-5 decompression

All subjects were fitted with full-body reflective markers for three-dimensional (3D) kinematic analysis using a Vicon motion capture system (VICON Nexus 2.19, Denver, CO) at a sampling rate of 100Hz. 10 Surface electromyography (EMG) (Trigno Wireless EMG System, Delsys Inc., Natick, MA, USA) was placed on the symptomatic side or the dominant side for bilateral symptoms to quantify the activation of key muscles involved in the transition from sitting to standing, including tibialis anterior, rectus femoris, semitendinosus, gluteus maximus, erector spinae, external oblique, and medial gastrocnemius. When symptoms were bilateral, the dominant side was selected because no single symptomatic side could be identified. Surface EMG leads were placed on the dominant side for healthy controls to provide a consistent reference in the absence of symptoms. The same side was assessed at each postoperative visit to support within-patient longitudinal comparisons. Limb dominance was defined as the preferred leg for kicking a ball.

To analyze muscle activation patterns, electromyography (EMG) signals were normalized to submaximal voluntary contractions (sMVCs) to allow meaningful comparisons across participants.15,16 Submaximal normalization was selected to minimize pain provocation and ensure feasibility and consistency in this symptomatic cohort. Subjects performed 3 repetitions of 5-second contractions to activate the muscle. The tibialis anterior was activated through ankle dorsiflexion performed in sitting or standing, the medial gastrocnemius through bilateral heel raise, the rectus femoris by performing a squat at the furthest comfortable extent, and the semitendinosus was activated by knee flexion while standing. The gluteus maximus and erector spinae were engaged by trunk flexion followed by controlled trunk extension, with concurrent hip extension. The external oblique was activated by sitting on the edge of the chair and lifting both legs. After completing these tasks, all subjects performed three functional transitions from sitting on an adjustable stool standardized to a 90-degree knee flexion, with hands on their laps, to complete standing with hands at their sides. This functional motion was performed with specific instruction to not use the upper extremities to push on the lower extremities to support the transition.

Data acquisition

All marker trajectories were smoothed using a fourth-order, no-phase-shift Butterworth low-pass digital filter with a cut-off set to 6 Hz, and a Woltering filter with a smoothing of 10 mm2 in the MSE filter mode. The data were then exported to MATLAB for joint-angle calculation. The joint angles were calculated using the plug-in-gait model. The peak trunk forward lean was identified using the minimum vertical position of the shoulder markers. STS initiation was defined as the first sustained anterior-inferior displacement of the trunk markers from the seated position, and task completion was defined as achievement of upright standing.

EMG signals were bandpass filtered between 20–450 Hz and processed using a root mean square (RMS) calculation with a 100-sample sliding window (equivalent to 50 ms at a 2 kHz sampling rate). Subject-specific muscle activation values were determined for each muscle using sMVC trials by identifying local maxima between distribution peaks. The sMVC is calculated as the average of all activated regions across the sMVC trials. The EMG signal during the trial was normalized to sMVCs for calculations. The onset and offset timings are the percentages of activity, defined as the first time the muscle activation surpasses 10% of the sMVC value and the first time it falls below 10% after the first activation. Percent phase active is the percentage of the trial when the muscle is active. Mean activity is the average of sMVCs during the trial when the muscle is active.

Statistical Analysis

Kinematic measurements, muscle activation patterns, and patient-reported measures were compared between preoperative and postoperative assessments using mixed-effects linear regression models in R (version 2025.5.1.513). Cohort (DLS patient or control), time point (preoperative vs postoperative), age, and BMI were included as fixed effects to control for potential influence on movement and muscle activation patterns, with subject-level random intercepts to account for repeated measures. Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, and results are reported as model adjusted means with 95% confidence intervals. All trials were retained within the mixed-effects framework rather than averaged to preserve within-subject variability and improve sensitivity to detect subtle changes in movement coordination and neuromuscular behavior during functional transition performance. This approach preserves within-subject variability, avoids pseudo-replication, and appropriately accounts for correlation across repeated trials. Model fit was assessed using Akaike and Bayesian information criteria. To account for multiple testing, families of related variables, segmental/joint kinematics and EMG variables, were prespecified based on biomechanical domains. Within each family, the applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. All reported p-values remained significant after FDR adjustment.

Because the DLS and control groups differed substantially in age and BMI, two post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of between-group findings. First, we excluded subjects with BMI >30 kg/m2. This analysis included 12 controls and 17 patients. BMI was similar between groups after restriction (mean 26.4 vs 26.4 kg/m2), although the patients remained older than the controls (mean 69.8 vs 53.5 years). Second, we performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching for age and BMI. This produced a matched sample of 10 controls and 10 patients. BMI balance was excellent after matching (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.008), while age imbalance was markedly reduced but remained slightly higher (SMD = 0.127). The age and BMI adjusted mixed-effects models were repeated. The estimated between-group differences for both kinematic and EMG variables remained similar in direction and magnitude across both sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of this finding to measured age and BMI imbalances.

Data were reported as model adjusted estimated means and 95% confidence intervals. Because EMG outcomes were analyzed using an unconstrained linear model, the adjusted estimates and their confidence intervals were not restricted to nonnegative values and could extend below zero when activation was low or statistical uncertainty was high. Negative estimates do not represent physiologically negative EMG activity. These findings should be interpreted with caution.

Results

Demographics

Thirty-five patients with DLS underwent central decompression, and sixteen healthy controls were included in the analysis. Controls were numerically younger and had lower body weight and BMI. Although these differences were not statistically significant, age and BMI were included in the mixed models to reduce potential confounding (Table 1). At 3 months, 23 patients continued to report residual low back pain, and 9 patients initiated physical therapy at approximately 2 months postoperatively, with sessions conducted once weekly. Because of the small and unequal subgroup sizes, the study was not adequately powered to compare biomechanical and EMG trajectories between participants who received postoperative physical therapy and those who did not.

Sit-to-Stand Performance

STS completion time did not differ significantly between groups, although controls completed the task approximately 0.5 seconds faster on average than both preoperative and postoperative patients. Accordingly, time-based performance alone did not distinguish the observed postoperative changes in movement strategy and neuromuscular coordination (Table 1).

Patient Reported Outcome Measures and Radiographic Characteristics

Patient-reported outcomes improved significantly, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), including disability (ODI: 24.8 vs 44.4, p < 0.001), fear of movement (TSK: 36.2 vs 43.4, p < 0.001), physical function (PROMIS PF: 42.3 vs 35.1, p < 0.001), and pain interference (PROMIS PI: 57.3 vs 66.2, p < 0.001) (Table 1).17,18 Pelvic tilt averaged 22.0° (SD 16.8), slightly higher than the typical value of 15° calculated from Pelvic incidence, 19 suggesting a trend toward mild pelvic retroversion in this cohort. The mean PI–LL mismatch was 6.9° (SD 17.3), indicating overall acceptable sagittal alignment at the group level; however, the substantial variability suggests that some individuals may exhibit clinically relevant spinopelvic mismatch (Table 1).

Neuromuscular Activity

Following surgery, patients demonstrated significant improvement in neuromuscular coordination, as evidenced by earlier tibialis anterior activation (4.7% vs 10.1%, p = 0.002) and lower mean activity (0.6 vs 0.7, p < 0.001). The medial gastrocnemius showed increased mean activity (1.3 vs 0.4, p = 0.023) (Figure 3). Erector spinae activation was delayed (9.3% vs 4.3%, p = 0.031) with reduced percent phase active (73.0 vs 85.9, p = 0.003). Preoperative Impairment (Pre-surgical vs Control)

No significant differences were observed in kinematics or movement strategies compared to controls.

Neuromuscular Activity

Compared to control patients, surgical patients preoperatively demonstrated later tibialis anterior onset timing (10.1% vs 0.2%, p = 0.003), and greater mean activity (0.7 vs 0.5, p = 0.001). Patients also had earlier semitendinosus onset (18.4% vs 45.2%, p = 0.006), and earlier erector spinae onset (4.3% vs 17.3%, p = 0.005) and offset (10.8% vs 48.1%, p = 0.001) timing, along with greater erector spinae percent phase active (85.9% vs 65.9%, p = 0.035) (Table 4, Figures 3 and 4).

Residual Deficiency (Post-surgical vs Control)

No significant differences were observed in kinematics or movement strategies compared to controls.

Neuromuscular Activity

Despite these postoperative improvements, semitendinosus (27.1% vs 45.2%, p = 0.050) and erector spinae (21.4% vs 48.1%, p = 0.014) continued to show earlier offset timing than controls (Table 4, Figures 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that lumbar decompression is associated with measurable changes in functional transition biomechanics and neuromuscular coordination during sit-to-stand in patients with degenerative lumbar stenosis. The principal findings were: (1) clinically meaningful improvement in disability, pain interference, fear of movement, and physical function; (2) reduced reliance on trunk-dominant compensatory movement strategies during STS; (3) improved anticipatory ankle and hip neuromuscular coordination; and (4) persistent residual abnormalities in muscle activation timing despite substantial symptomatic recovery. Collectively, these findings suggest that recovery following lumbar decompression extends beyond symptom reduction alone and involves progressive improvements of movement efficiency and motor control during activities of daily living. These associations are consistent with improved movement efficiency following lumbar decompression; however, because this was an observational longitudinal study, the mechanisms underlying these changes cannot be established definitively.

Despite a substantial proportion of patients continuing to report residual low back pain at 3 months, objective biomechanical and neuromuscular measures demonstrated significant postoperative improvements during STS. However, medication use and timing relative to testing timepoints were not systematically documented, and circulating drug exposure was not measured. Medication use may have influenced pain, muscle activation, and movement patterns. Therefore, the effect of analgesics, muscle relaxants, or anti-inflammatory medications could not be separated from those of surgery.

Patients demonstrated reduced trunk flexion and pelvic anterior tilt at peak trunk forward lean after surgery, suggesting decreased reliance on a compensatory trunk-driven momentum generation, where patients swing their trunk forward to initiate the rise, and transitioning toward a movement strategy characterized by relatively greater lower-extremity contribution to generate the vertical propulsion. Previous studies have shown that greater trunk flexion is a compensatory response to diminished lower-extremity strength and impaired balance regulation.20,21 Individuals with DLS often adopt exaggerated trunk flexion during STS to alleviate symptoms associated with neural compression. 22 The postoperative reduction in trunk forward lean may reflect reduced reliance on compensatory trunk-dominant movement strategies during STS. In this study, patients also demonstrated increased knee flexion at max trunk forward lean and reduced knee adduction range of motion, indicating a shift from a trunk-dominant to a lower-extremity-dominant strategy. Postoperatively, forward momentum and vertical lift are generated more by knee flexion, and the reduction in knee adduction reflects improved stability.

The postoperative findings suggest improved neuromuscular coordination and movement efficiency during functional transition performance. Because the EMG signal was normalized to sMVC rather than MVC to minimize pain and ensure consistent testing in DLS patients, the normalized amplitude cannot be interpreted as an absolute measure of muscle effort but should be interpreted as a relative activation pattern. Preoperatively, DLS patients demonstrated substantial alterations in neuromuscular activation patterns compared with controls. Earlier activation of the semitendinosus and erector spinae muscles, and the prolonged activation of the erector spinae, indicate reliance on protective trunk extensor co-contraction and less efficient hip extension, further supporting the compensatory trunk-dominant strategy before surgery. Prior studies have shown that the trunk-dominant strategy may require greater lower-extremity muscle co-contraction to maintain balance.20,23,24 We observed delayed tibialis anterior activation and increased mean activity, suggesting impaired anticipatory postural adjustments and greater reliance on ankle stabilization during the movement. This is further compounded by increased trunk flexion to generate forward and upward momentum, thereby shifting the center of mass anteriorly and increasing ankle loading. As a result, greater tibialis anterior mean activity is required to control the forward progression and maintain balance. Postoperatively, tibialis anterior activation occurred earlier and with reduced amplitude, suggesting the anticipatory use of the lower extremity and more efficient control during movement initiation. Increased medial gastrocnemius activity and earlier gluteus maximus activation reflect improved lower-limb contribution during the rising phase of the task. Additionally, delayed activation and reduced phase activity of the erector spinae indicate decreased reliance on trunk stabilization strategies. Together, these findings suggest a transition from a trunk-dominant compensation pattern toward a more coordinated lower-extremity-driven movement strategy after surgery.

Despite these changes after surgery, both the semitendinosus and erector spinae continued to demonstrate earlier offset timing compared to controls, suggesting that complete normalization of muscle coordination may require additional rehabilitation or longer recovery periods, and reflecting residual neuromuscular adaptations developed during prolonged periods of pain and functional limitation prior to surgery. 25 Based on these residual deficiencies, targeted rehabilitation should focus on restoring posterior chain activation and enhancing trunk control, including gluteal strengthening, bridges, and plank variations, to support more efficient, controlled STS performance.26,27

These findings suggest that functional recovery may precede complete symptom resolution and that improvements in motor control and movement efficiency can occur even in the presence of persistent pain, also supporting the clinical value of incorporating objective functional biomechanical testing of ADLs into postoperative evaluation. PROMs improved substantially after surgery, but they did not reveal the specific movement adaptations and residual deficits identified by motion analysis and EMG. Similarly, STS completion time alone did not meaningfully distinguish postoperative movement quality. For spine surgeons, this suggests that objective assessment of functional transitions may complement conventional outcome measures by identifying persistent motor deficits that could inform rehabilitation referral, patient counseling, and recovery monitoring.

These findings also support the emerging role of objective digital biomechanical biomarkers in spine surgery. While PROMs remain essential for capturing patient perception of recovery, objective biomechanical assessment provides complementary information regarding movement quality, compensatory motor behavior, and neuromuscular coordination that may not be detectable through subjective measures alone. As spine care increasingly moves toward precision rehabilitation and objective functional assessment, quantifiable movement biomarkers derived from activities of daily living may help identify patients with persistent functional impairment despite symptomatic improvement and may facilitate individualized postoperative rehabilitation strategies. Importantly, the purpose of the control cohort was not to establish normative thresholds but to provide physiologic context for the observed movement strategies. The principal findings of this study derive from the within-patient longitudinal comparison before and after surgery, which is less susceptible to demographic confounding than cross-sectional comparisons.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that recovery after lumbar decompression should not be interpreted solely through pain reduction or PROM improvement. Persistent alterations in neuromuscular coordination may remain despite substantial symptomatic recovery and may contribute to residual functional limitation, impaired balance regulation, or delayed return to higher-level activities. Incorporating objective functional transition assessment into postoperative spine evaluation may help identify patients who could benefit from targeted rehabilitation focused on trunk control, posterior chain activation, balance regulation, and movement retraining.

This study has several limitations. First, the present study was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, which may limit generalizability and preclude stratified analyses based on the number of operated levels or baseline sagittal alignment. The number of decompressed levels was not included as a stratification variable in the analysis. Although 8 patients underwent decompression at three or more levels, the sample size within the surgical-level subgroups was insufficient to support adequately powered and reliable subgroup comparisons. Because the extent of decompression may influence postoperative recovery trajectories, the possibility that this variable contributed to differences in outcomes cannot be excluded. Future studies with larger cohorts should stratify patients by the number of decompressed levels or include surgical extent as a covariate in the analysis. Moreover, nine participants began physical therapy approximately two months after surgery. Therefore, the observed postoperative changes cannot be attributed exclusively to lumbar decompression, as physical therapy may have contributed to recovery in this subgroup. Future studies should systematically document rehabilitation timing, frequency, duration, and content and evaluate its independent and combined effects on postoperative biomechanical and neuromuscular outcomes. Second, the follow-up period was limited to three months. Longer-term evaluation is needed to evaluate changes in neuromuscular control over time. Third, the DLS and the control group differed substantially in age and BMI, which may independently affect biomechanical and EMG outcomes. Although age and BMI are included as fixed effects in the mixed-effects models, the models assumed a linear relationship between these covariates and outcomes. The statistical adjustment does not fully compensate for the difference residual confounding may remain. Therefore, the between group comparisons should be considered as exploratory association. Fourth, motion capture and marker placement are subject to human error and soft tissue artifact, which may introduce variability in joint kinematic measurements. Fifth, unilateral EMG placement may have introduced a between-group confound because the symptomatic side in DLS patients was not always the dominant side. Future studies should incorporate bilateral lower extremity EMG recordings. The surface EMG signals are susceptible to adjacent muscle activity and signal attenuation by subcutaneous adipose tissue. However, the electrodes were placed by trained physical therapists to improve consistency, and EMG signals were normalized to sMVCs. While sMVC normalization is commonly used when maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs) testing is not feasible or may cause discomfort in clinical populations, it may limit the ability to compare absolute muscle activation levels across participants. 28 As a result, the reported EMG amplitudes should be interpreted primarily as relative activation patterns rather than absolute measures of muscle effort. 29 Accordingly, isolated negative adjusted estimates should be interpreted as statistical artifacts of model estimation rather than physiologically meaningful findings. Sixth, direct kinetic measurements like ground reaction forces were not available; therefore, inferred changes in load-sharing, spinal loading, and joint moments cannot be directly quantified. Seventh, residual differences between groups, despite adjustment and sensitivity analyses, may have reduced the ability to detect subtle between-group effects and should be considered when interpreting comparisons with controls. Finally, direct lower-extremity strength measurements were not obtained; therefore, postoperative improvements in movement strategy should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of strength recovery. Future studies should include a longer follow-up to fully characterize the recovery trajectory after surgery. Furthermore, future work should examine the effect of postoperative rehabilitation on biomechanical and neuromuscular recovery. Investigating how rehabilitation interventions influence recovery may help identify strategies to optimize functional outcomes after surgery. Incorporating kinetic data and stratification by surgical factors or sagittal alignment may further clarify which patients are most likely to demonstrate persistent functional deficits despite symptomatic improvement.

Conclusion

Lumbar decompression was associated with measurable changes in functional transition biomechanics and neuromuscular coordination during sit-to-stand in patients with degenerative lumbar stenosis. Postoperatively, patients demonstrated reduced trunk and pelvic compensation, enhanced lower-extremity contribution, and less reliance on protective trunk extensor activation, consistent with improved movement efficiency during functional transition performance. Importantly, persistent residual abnormalities in muscle activation timing remained despite clinically meaningful improvements in pain and disability, suggesting that improvements of motor control may lag behind symptomatic recovery. These findings support the value of objective biomechanical assessment of activities of daily living as a complementary biomarker of postoperative recovery that may help guide rehabilitation and optimize functional outcomes following lumbar decompression.
