A comparison of borderline personality features and level of personality functioning in explaining mentalizing capacity
Authors: Charlotte S. Zell, Kennedy M. Balzen, Joseph Boudreaux, Francesca Penner, Carla Sharp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNumerous studies have demonstrated that the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) outperforms traditional categorical nosology (i.e., Section II) in predicting an array of salient clinical outcomes. However, despite the centrality of social-cognitive impairments in personality disorder, few studies have examined the superiority of the AMPD over Section II in predicting social-cognitive deficits. The current study addresses this gap by evaluating the incremental validity of AMPD-defined level of personality functioning (LPF) versus borderline personality features in predicting mentalizing – a social-cognitive construct proposed to underlie the development of personality disorder.MethodParticipants included 267 university students (Mage = 20.49, SD = 1.74) who completed self-report measures of LPF, borderline personality features, and two measures of mentalizing: the Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (RFQ) and the Mentalization Scale (MentS).ResultsHierarchical regressions revealed that LPF explained additional variance in the RFQ beyond borderline features and internalizing psychopathology. Although borderline features also incremented LPF in predicting the RFQ, additional variance explained was less than half that contributed by the LPF over borderline features. LPF also incremented the variance explained in the MentS total score and subscales over and above borderline features and internalizing symptoms, whereas borderline features did not increment LPF in predicting any of the MentS variables.ConclusionsThese results strengthen the evidence base for the superiority of the AMPD over Section II and suggest that mentalizing deficits are better captured by LPF than borderline personality disorder symptoms. Findings support the extension of mentalization-based treatment to AMPD-defined personality disorder, which should be explored in future research.

Background

In response to concerns regarding the categorical framework for personality disorders, the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) was proposed as a replacement for the Section II model in DSM-5 [1, 2]. The AMPD offers a dimensional approach to personality pathology, with Criterion A assessing Level of Personality Functioning (LPF), defined as self (identity and self-direction) and interpersonal (empathy and intimacy) functioning, and Criterion B evaluating the specific style or manifestation of personality pathology through maladaptive traits [3]. Despite substantial evidence supporting the validity of dimensional nosology for personality disorder (PD), the American Psychiatric Association ultimately rejected the inclusion of the AMPD in Section II, placing it in Section III (emerging measures and models) due to concerns regarding limited empirical evidence establishing superiority over the categorical model [4, 5]. Indeed, the American Psychiatric Association requires that any newly proposed diagnostic system shows superiority over the existing system in terms of key validators [6]. These criteria include antecedent validators such as environmental risk factors or temperament, concurrent validators such as sociodemographic, cognitive, and emotional correlates, and predictive validators such as treatment responses and diagnostic stability.

Since the publication of the DSM-5, there have been several head-to-head studies that have shown superiority of the AMPD over the traditional categorical nosology (see 5 for an overview). For instance, studies have found the AMPD performs better than Section II in predicting outcomes including suicidal ideation [7, 8], suicidal behaviors [8], psychosocial functioning [9, 10], dispositional stress [11], problematic substance use [7], quality of life [9], interpersonal problems [11, 12], and response to psychological treatment [13]. Of note is the relative lack of attention to social-cognitive correlates of PD (see 4 for a review). The only social-cognitive outcome (or cognitive correlate of any kind, to our knowledge) that has been investigated in a head-to-head comparison of Section II versus AMPD-defined PD is social reward processing, with a recent study demonstrating that LPF (AMPD) predicts neural response to social acceptance over and above borderline features (Section II) [14]. Given the known association between impairment in social cognition and personality pathology [15], this relative reduced focus on social-cognitive correlates represents an important limitation of the current literature examining incremental validity of the AMPD.

A major social-cognitive construct that has been studied in relation to PD is mentalizing, or the capacity to understand the self and others in terms of mental states (i.e., feelings, beliefs, intentions, desires) [16]. Theoretical and empirical work on mentalizing has focused particularly on borderline PD [17]. This work identifies mentalizing, particularly complex forms of mentalizing requiring the integration of multiple perspectives, as a central impairment in borderline PD [18–21]. Mentalizing is conceptualized as a critical impairment core to personality pathology in general as defined by the AMPD: it is theorized to represent the intrapsychic process underlying the development of self and interpersonal functioning [22–24], the two primary domains constituting LPF. Two recent empirical investigations indicate moderate to strong association between LPF and mentalizing deficits [25, 26]. Despite extensive research showing mentalizing deficits in borderline PD [27, 28] and clear theoretical and emerging empirical evidence linking mentalizing and LPF, studies have yet to directly compare these two personality constructs across the traditional model and the AMPD in association with mentalizing [17]. Establishing superiority of LPF over borderline PD in relation to mentalizing ability would provide important justification in extending the literature base for mentalization-based treatment – originally developed and still primarily studied for the treatment of borderline PD [29] – to AMPD-defined personality pathology.

Examining maladaptive traits – Criterion B of the AMPD – versus borderline PD in association with mentalizing may also be of interest in the evaluation of social cognition across personality models. While borderline PD has been shown to be a relatively good indicator of the general factor for PD [30, 31], and is thus the Section II PD construct that most closely mirrors LPF, some symptoms of borderline PD additionally capture a specific trait manifestation of personality pathology: one characterized by emotional liability, anxiousness, separation insecurity, depressively, impulsivity, risk-taking, and hostility [3]. Comparing these Criterion B traits to borderline PD symptoms (and potentially to other maladaptive traits and categorical PDs) could provide insight into how the “flavor” of personality pathology may influence mentalizing, and how this is captured across Section II versus Section III. However, an investigation of the role of maladaptive traits was outside the scope of the current paper, which focuses on comparing borderline PD to LPF given the theoretical and clinical relevance of this comparison, as laid out above.

The aim of the present study was to examine the incremental validity of LPF versus borderline features in explaining mentalizing capacity as reported on two established measures, the Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (RFQ-8; [32]) and the Mentalization Scale (MentS; 33). The RFQ is the most widely used self-report measure in existing mentalizing research, while the MentS was developed more recently to address limitations of the RFQ and other extant mentalizing measures including underrepresentation of other-related mentalizing [33]. The use of both the RFQ and the MentS allows us to examine whether the relationship of LPF versus borderline features to mentalizing is consistent across measures. This study uses data collected from young adults, given (1) the onset of personality disorder tends to be in adolescence and young adulthood [3, 34] and (2) the substantial changes in the social brain over the adolescent-young adult years [35] which subserves social cognitive functioning. Evaluating personality and mentalizing in young adulthood therefore has particular developmental relevance. Symptoms of depression and anxiety were included as covariates due to their frequent cooccurrence with PD [36, 37] and associations with mentalizing impairment in prior research [38–40]. We hypothesized that LPF would demonstrate incremental validity over borderline features in explaining variance in both measures of mentalizing given its explicit focus on mental representations of self and other. Furthermore, we expected LPF to be more strongly associated with mentalizing than depressive and anxiety symptoms given that mentalizing impairment, while a potential transdiagnostic risk factor for psychopathology, is suggested to play a particularly critical role in personality pathology [41].

Methods

Participants and procedures

The sample comprised 267 emerging adults aged 18–25 from a larger study investigating relationships between personality and positive psychology constructs. This larger study recruited participants from a college campus in the Southwestern United States. Students were recruited for the study through the SONA subject pool system and earned extra course credit for participation. Inclusion criteria required participants to be between the ages of 18 and 25, fluent in English, and enrolled as a student at the university. After reviewing a consent cover letter outlining study procedures, goals, risks and benefits, confidentiality, rights to withdraw, and how to contact the PI and university IRB, respondents indicated whether they agreed to participate in the study. Those who agreed to participate completed a series of online questionnaires in Qualtrics at one timepoint about their childhood, mental health, personality, and outlook on life. Participants were excluded from the final dataset for poor data quality, operationalized as missing at least three out of nine attention/quality check questions (e.g., “Choose item C for this question”). The sample for the current study was limited to the 267 participants in the original dataset who completed all of the measures described below. The final sample was racially and ethnically diverse and predominantly (80.1%) female. Just over one third of the sample (33.7%) endorsed a history of mental health treatment. Measures

Level of personality functioning

Level of personality functioning scale-brief form 2.0 (LPFS-BF 2.0; 42)

The LPFS-BF 2.0 is a 12-item self-report instrument that assesses LPF, as outlined in Criterion A of the AMPD. It measures the 12 facets of personality functioning within the two higher order domains of self- (identity and self-direction) and interpersonal (empathy and intimacy) functioning. Example items measuring impairments in self-functioning include “I often do not know who I really am” and “I often make unrealistic demands on myself”. Items in the interpersonal functioning domain include “I often find it hard to stand it when others have a different opinion” and “I often feel very vulnerable when relations become more personal”. Response options range from 1 (completely untrue) to 4 (completely true). Item scores are summed to produce a total score, which represents a unidimensional severity continuum of LPF impairment, with higher scores indicating greater impairment. The LPFS-BF 2.0 has demonstrated good psychometric properties in previous studies [42–45]. In the current sample, it showed strong internal consistency (α = 0.86).

Borderline features

Personality Assessment Inventory - Borderline Features (PAI-BOR; 46)

The PAI is a self-report measure designed to assess various forms of psychopathology. The Borderline Features (BOR) scale comprises 24 items across the domains of affective instability, identity disturbance, negative relationships, and self-harm. Items are rated on a four-point scale from 0 (false) to 3 (very true). In previous research, the PAI-BOR has shown strong criterion, concurrent, and external validity and good internal consistency reliability [46–48]. In our sample, the PAI-BOR demonstrated very good internal consistency (α = 0.88).

Depressive symptoms

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES-D; [49])

The CES-D is a 20-item self-report measure designed to assess depressive symptoms in the general population. Respondents rate how often they have felt or behaved as described by each item (e.g. “I felt that everything I did was an effort”) within the past week. Response options range on a four-point Likert scale from rarely or none of the time (less than 1 day) to most or all of the time (5–7 days). The CES-D has demonstrated good psychometric properties in prior research [50, 51]. In the current study, it had excellent internal consistency (α = 0.91).

Anxiety symptoms

Generalized anxiety disorder-7 (GAD-7; 50)

The GAD-7 is a seven-item self-report scale that assesses symptoms of anxiety. The measure asks respondents how often they have experienced problems with anxiety (e.g. “not being able to stop or control worrying”) over the last two weeks. Response options range from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The GAD-7 has strong psychometric properties [52, 53]. Its internal consistency in the present sample was excellent (α = 0.92).

Mentalizing capacities

Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (RFQ-8; 32)

The RFQ-8 is an 8-item self-report instrument that assesses mentalizing abilities. Example items include “I don’t always know why I do what I do” and “people’s thoughts are a mystery to me”. Response options range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The measure was originally designed to yield two subscales capturing excessive certainty and uncertainty about mental states. However, recent investigations into the factor structure and psychometric properties of the RFQ have shown that it appears to measure a unidimensional construct of maladaptive uncertainty about mental states (hypomentalizing) and does not effectively assess excessive mentalizing certainty [54–56]. Furthermore, the RFQ demonstrates superior psychometric properties when scored by summing all eight items (after reverse scoring item 7, “I always know what I feel”), instead of with the original, more complex, scoring scheme (54, 56). Therefore, we used this updated scoring method in the current study to obtain a total RFQ score, with higher scores reflecting greater uncertainty about mental states. The internal consistency of the total score in the current sample was good (α = 0.84).

Mentalization Scale (MentS; 33)

The MentS is a 28-item self-report measure of mentalizing capacity comprising three subscales that assess self-related mentalization (MentS-S; 8 items), other-related mentalization (MentS-O; 10 items), and motivation to mentalize (MentS-M; 10 items). Example items on this measure include “I can easily describe what I feel” (MentS-S subscale), “Usually I can recognize what makes others uneasy” (MentS-O subscale) and “When someone annoys me, I try to understand why I react in that way” (MentS-M subscale). Response options are on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (completely incorrect) to 5 (completely correct). Item scores on each subscale are summed to create subscale scores, with higher scores indicating more advanced mentalizing capacity. A total score reflecting global mentalization abilities is obtained by summing all items across the three subscales. Prior studies support the convergent and discriminant validity, factor structure, and test-retest and internal consistency reliability of the MentS [33, 57, 58]. Internal consistency of the MentS subscales in the current study ranged from satisfactory to good (α = 0.77-0.82). The reliability of the full scale was good (α = 0.87).

Data analytic strategy

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2). Bivariate correlations were calculated among all study variables. Demographic characteristics, specifically age and gender, were included to inspect associations with mentalizing; if they were correlated with mentalizing (outcome) variables, we included them as covariates in subsequent analyses. Next, we conducted five sets of hierarchical linear regressions to compare the relative contribution of LPF and borderline features in explaining each of the five mentalizing variables (RFQ total, MentS total, MentS-S, MentS-O, and MentS-M). In the first hierarchical regression within each set, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and any demographic covariates were included as predictors in step 1. Borderline features were added as a predictor in step 2, and LPF was added in step 3. In the second hierarchical regression within each set, step 1 remained the same, but LPF was entered in the second step and borderline features was added in the final step. We examined variable inflation factor (VIF) values to assess multicollinearity among predictors. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, p-values for ΔR² tests were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. FDR-adjusted p-values (q-values) are reported.

LPF increments anxiety, depression, and borderline features in association with the RFQ

In step 1 of the first set of hierarchical regression models, depressive symptoms (β = 0.410, p < .001) and anxiety symptoms (β = 0.227, p < .001) were both significant predictors and explained a combined 34.3% of variance in the RFQ total score. The addition of borderline features to the model in step 2 significantly increased variance explained (ΔR2 = 0.128, q < 0.001). The addition of LPF in step three further increased the variance explained by the model (ΔR2 = 0.112, q < 0.001). In the second hierarchical regression in the set in which LPF was added in step 2, it explained a significant and large proportion of additional variance in RFQ scores (ΔR2 = 0.192, q < 0.001). In step 3, the inclusion of borderline features also significantly increased variance explained (ΔR2 = 0.048, q < 0.001), though this increase was less than half the magnitude of the increase observed in the first hierarchical regression when LPF was added in the third step. In the final step with all internalizing psychopathology and personality variables included in the same model, LPF and borderline features were both significant predictors of RFQ scores, while anxiety and depressive symptoms were not. LPF (β = 0.465, p < .001) was more strongly related to RFQ scores in this final model than was borderline features (β = 0.322, p < .001).

LPF uniquely explains ments total, self-related mentalizing, other-related mentalizing, and motivation to mentalize

In the first step of both hierarchical regressions predicting the MentS global score, depressive symptoms (β = − 0.239, p = .003) and age (β = 0.151, p = .011) were significant predictors, while anxiety symptoms were not. These three variables explained a total of 9% of variance in MentS scores. Adding borderline features in step 2 increased the variance explained, although minimally (ΔR2 = 0.020, q = 0.021). The addition of LPF in step 3 substantially increased variance explained (ΔR2 = 0.147, q < 0.001). In the second hierarchical regression in the set, the inclusion of LPF in step 2 significantly increased variance explained (ΔR2 = 0.167, q < 0.001), while the addition of borderline features in step 3 did not (ΔR2 = 0.000, q = 0.949). In the final model with depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, age, LPF, and borderline features, age (β = 0.125, p = .021) and LPF (β = − 0.536, p < .001) were the only variables significantly related to the MentS global score.

In step 1 of the next set of hierarchical regressions, depressive symptoms (β = − 0.344, p < .001) and anxiety symptoms (β = − 0.189, p = .009) were each independently associated with self-related mentalization abilities as measured with the MentS-S, accounting for a combined 24% of variance. Borderline features explained additional variance when added in step 2 (ΔR2 = 0.027, q = 0.003), and LPF further incrementally explained MentS-S scores in step 3 (ΔR2 = 0.171, q < 0.001). When added in the opposite order in the second hierarchical regression in the set, LPF accounted for a large amount of additional variance in step 2 (ΔR2 = 0.198, q < 0.001), while borderline features did not incrementally explain the MentS-S in step 3 (ΔR2 = 0.000, q = 0.844). In the final model with all predictors, only LPF (β = − 0.575, p < .001) was significantly associated with self-related mentalization capacities.

Anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and age did not explain significant variance when included as independent variables in step 1 of the hierarchical regressions predicting other-related mentalization on the MentS-O (R2 = 0.022, p = .122). Borderline symptoms did not significantly add to variance explained in step 2 (ΔR2 = 0.015, q = 0.055). However, LPF increased variance explained substantially in step 3 (ΔR2 = 0.083, q < 0.001). In the second hierarchical regression in the set, the change in variance was significant when LPF was added in step 2 (ΔR2 = 0.099, q < 0.001), but not when borderline features were added in step 3 (ΔR2 = 0.000, q = 0.889). In the step 3 model, anxiety symptoms (β = 0.168, p = .047) were associated with higher other-related mentalization abilities, while LPF was associated with lower abilities (β = − 0.404, p = < 0.001). Other variables were not significantly related to the MentS-O.

In step 1 of the final set of hierarchical regressions, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and age accounted for substantial variance in motivation to mentalize as measured on the MentS-M (R2 = 0.053). Age (β = 0.202, p < .001) exhibited a positive association with the MentS-M score while other step 1 predictors were nonsignificant. The addition of borderline features in step 2 did not explain additional variance (ΔR2 = 0.002, q = 0.635), but the addition of LPF in step 3 did (ΔR2 = 0.037, q = 0.001). Similarly, LPF incrementally explained motivation to mentalize when added in step 2 (ΔR2 = 0.038, q = 0.002), while borderline features did not add incremental explanatory value in step 3 (ΔR2 = 0.001, q < 0.698). In the final model, anxiety symptoms (β = 0.183, p = .033) and age (β = 0.186, p = .002) were associated with significantly greater motivation to mentalize, while LPF (β = − 0.269, p = .001) was associated with substantially lower motivation. Borderline features and depressive symptoms were not significantly related to motivation to mentalize.

Post-hoc analyses

Given that the LPFS-BF 2.0 taps some aspects of mentalizing, we reran all analyses with a modified version of the measure to ensure that results were robust when accounting for potential construct overlap. Specifically, we removed the three items of the LPFS-BF 2.0 that appear to tap mentalizing capacity: “I often do not understand my own thoughts and feelings”, “I often have difficulty understanding the thoughts and feelings of others”, and “I often do not fully understand why my behavior has a certain effect on others”. Findings were largely unchanged. Among mental health and personality variables, LPF still exhibited the largest bivariate relationship with all measures of mentalizing. Although both LPF and borderline features demonstrated incremental value in explaining the RFQ, the variance explained by LPF was larger. LPF incrementally explained the MentS global score and all of its subscales over borderline features, while borderline features did not explain significant variance in MentS scores beyond what was accounted for by LPF. The only differences in results using the modified version of the measure (compared to the original, full measure) were a decrease in bivariate correlations between LPF and mentalizing variables by 0.020-0.032 and a decrease in R2 by 0.009-0.036 across all models.

Discussion

Since its publication in Section III of the DSM-5 over a decade ago, a large body of evidence has emerged supporting the validity and clinical utility of the AMPD, with a growing literature demonstrating its superiority over the current Section II model [4]. Social-cognitive correlates of PD, however, remain under investigated in head-to-head comparisons of Section II versus Section III PD nosology, despite the central role of social-cognitive impairments underlying personality pathology. The current study sought to fill this gap by comparing LPF – the common core of PD as defined by the AMPD – to borderline personality disorder (a Section II personality construct that has received considerable research attention in relation to social-cognitive impairment) in predicting mentalizing. Specifically, we used self-report data from a non-clinical sample of young adults to investigate the incremental validity of LPF versus borderline personality features in association with the RFQ and the MentS, controlling for symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Results from hierarchical linear regression analyses revealed that LPF explained additional variance in the RFQ beyond what was accounted for by borderline features and internalizing psychopathology. While borderline features also incrementally explained variance in the RFQ when added in the final step, the increase in variance was less than half that observed when adding LPF in the final step. LPF incremented borderline features (and internalizing psychopathology) in associations with the MentS total score and each of its three subscales capturing self-related mentalization, other-related mentalization, and motivation to mentalize. The opposite was not true: borderline features did not explain additional variance in the full scale MentS or any of its subscales beyond what was accounted for by LPF. Notably, these findings held even when re-running the analyses without including the LPFS-BF 2.0 items that tap mentalizing. These results provide evidence for the superiority of LPF over the borderline personality construct as an indicator of mentalizing capacity.

Minor differences in results between the MentS and the RFQ, specifically that LPF provided virtually full coverage of the variance explained by borderline features on the former but not the latter, may be due to differences in the content coverage of these two measures. It has been suggested that several of the RFQ’s eight items (e.g. item 3, “when I get angry, I say things without really knowing why I am saying them” and item 4, “when I get angry, I say things that I later regret”) may tap into constructs like impulsivity and negative emotionality as well as mentalizing [52]. These personality traits may be more closely related to the negative affectivity and disinhibition manifestations of borderline PD than the maladaptive self-and interpersonal features of borderline PD that more closely mirror LPF. Other items on the RFQ (e.g. item 5, “if I feel insecure I can behave in ways that put others’ backs up”) may inflate the apparent correlation between mentalizing and both borderline features and LPF by capturing difficulties in identity and interpersonal functioning not specific to mentalizing. Concerns about the RFQ’s discriminant validity, particularly in relation to negative urgency and personality pathology, have been raised in previous research [52]. Bivariate correlations in the current study also suggested potential problems with discriminant validity, with the RFQ associating more strongly with both LPF and borderline features than with the MentS, and LPF and borderline features both associating more strongly with the RFQ than with each other.

The item content of the MentS, on the other hand, appears to overlap less with other personality constructs, while also providing more complete construct representation of mentalizing [33]. For instance, while the RFQ focuses mostly on self-related mentalizing (with just a single item, “people’s thoughts are a mystery to me”, explicitly focused on mentalizing others), the MentS includes an eight-item other-related mentalization subscale, as well as a 10-item subscale designed to capture motivation to mentalize. For the purpose of the current study, this allowed for the examination of how measures of personality pathology relate to different aspects of mentalizing; our results indicated that while LPF incrementally explains all categories of mentalizing, it is strongly associated with self-mentalizing impairment, moderately linked to deficits in other-related mentalizing, and modestly related to reduced interest in understanding mental states. These findings align with those from the original validation study of the MentS, in which individuals with borderline PD exhibited the largest differences from healthy controls on the self-mentalization subscale [33]. They may also partially explain the RFQ’s very high correlation with measures of personality pathology (given its emphasis on self-mentalizing). From a broader conceptual perspective, that LPF (as well as borderline features) is particularly linked to deficits in self-related mentalizing aligns with the notion that self-pathology may be core to personality pathology [59]. The small relationship between motivation to mentalize and LPF (and lack of significant relationship between this subscale and borderline features), on the other hand, suggests that impairment in mentalizing in individuals with personality pathology may primarily lie in their ability rather than their motivation to understand mental states.

Results from the present study also provide evidence about the relationship of mentalizing to PD relative to other forms of psychopathology. Bivariate analyses revealed that all measures of mentalizing were much more strongly linked to LPF than to anxiety and depressive symptoms. When all psychopathology variables were included in the same model, depressive and anxiety symptoms no longer predicted mentalizing deficits (in fact, anxiety was associated with greater other-related mentalization capacity and motivation to mentalize after accounting for borderline features and LPF). Therefore, the bivariate relationships observed between internalizing psychopathology and mentalizing impairment may be largely accounted for by the covariation of internalizing symptoms with personality pathology. These results support the notion that mentalizing plays a more central role in PD relative to other forms of psychopathology.

Findings from this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Most notably, all constructs in the study were measured through self-report. Shared method variance may therefore have inflated observed relationships between personality and mentalizing. Future research should utilize tasks that directly test mentalizing ability, which may differ from the degree to which this ability is experienced in everyday life (i.e., what is captured by self-report measures; [60]) [33]. Secondly, the study’s cross-sectional design precludes inferences about the nature of the relationship between personality and mentalizing. Mentalizing is conceptualized as the process underlying the development of self and interpersonal functioning [22]. To better test this theory, however, future research should longitudinally track mentalizing and self and interpersonal functioning across childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood. Finally, while racially and ethnically diverse, the study’s sample was recruited from a college campus and just over 80% female. Despite the developmental relevance of this age group for the onset of personality pathology and changes in social-cognitive function, future studies should also assess these associations in other age groups and clinical samples. Further, it is possible that the relationship between personality pathology and mentalizing differs across men, women, and gender minorities. The link between LPF and mentalizing should be explored in more gender-diverse samples and potentially with gender as a moderator.

Conclusions

Notwithstanding these limitations, the current study makes important contributions to the literature on personality pathology and mentalizing and adds to the evidence base for the superiority of the AMPD over the current Section II PD nosology. The incremental validity of LPF over borderline personality features in predicting mentalizing capacity is particularly clinically relevant, as it supports the extension of mentalization-based treatment approaches to AMPD-defined PD. Mentalization-based treatment was initially developed for borderline PD given the substantial mentalizing deficits observed in borderline patients [61]. Clinical trials of mentalization-based treatment have remained focused on borderline PD and demonstrated its efficacy in decreasing borderline symptoms, suicidal behaviors, and nonsuicidal self-injury, and improving mentalizing and general functioning in borderline patients [62]. Results of the present study indicate that mentalizing deficits, and thus the promise of mentalization-based interventions, are not specifically related to borderline PD, but rather the severity of personality impairment in general. This view is consistent with evidence that suggests that in contrast to more behaviorally focused criteria of other section II PDs, many of the symptoms of borderline PD capture general features of self and interpersonal impairment shared among all forms of personality pathology [30, 31, 63, 64]. Borderline PD and LPF are thus closely related constructs, with LPF simply offering a more focused, complete, and fully dimensionalized representation of the core features of personality functioning. LPF, and the AMPD more broadly, was intended to improve the clinical utility of PD diagnosis, in part by facilitating the identification of relevant treatment targets for all manifestations of personality pathology; this study adds to the evidence that mentalizing represents such a target. Therefore, we urge researchers to investigate the efficacy of mentalization-based treatment for AMPD-defined personality pathology.
