Generators of H 1 ​ ( Γ , ∂ Γ c ) H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c}) with ∂ Γ c ⊂ ∂ Γ \partial\Gamma^{c}\subset\partial\Gamma for Triangulated Surfaces Γ \Gamma : Construction and Classification of Global Loops
Silvano Pitassi

Abstract
Given a compact surface Γ \Gamma embedded in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with boundary ∂ Γ \partial\Gamma , our goal is to construct a set of representatives for a basis of the relative cohomology group H 1 ​ ( Γ , ∂ Γ c ) H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c}) , where Γ c \Gamma^{c} is a specified subset of ∂ Γ \partial\Gamma . To achieve this, we propose a novel graph-based algorithm with two key features: it is applicable to non-orientable surfaces, thereby generalizing the construction of Hiptmair and Ostrowski [SIAM J. Comput., 31 (2002)], and it has a worst-case time complexity that is linear in the number of edges of the mesh 𝒦 \mathcal{K} triangulating Γ \Gamma . Importantly, this algorithm serves as a critical pre-processing step to address the low-frequency breakdown encountered in boundary element discretizations of integral equation formulations. Keywords : relative cohomology; loop-star decomposition; global loops; electrical field integral equation; discrete Morse theory



1
Introduction

This paper bridges the fields of algebraic topology and computational electromagnetics.
Specifically, it addresses a challenge arising from boundary element method computations for an electromagnetic scattering problem: computing a set of representatives for a basis of the
first relative cohomology group

H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c})
, where
\Gamma
is a triangulated surface embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\partial\Gamma^{c}\subset\partial\Gamma
.
To tackle this, we propose a novel graph-based algorithm with a worst-case linear-time complexity.

Background and motivation.

Throughout this paper, we consider the following prototypical electromagnetic scattering problem, which serves as the primary motivation for the algorithm discussed here.
Specifically, an incident harmonic electric field
\bm{E}^{i}
, propagating in a space with permittivity
\epsilon
and permeability
\mu
, impinges upon a triangulated surface
\Gamma
without boundary, which is a perfect conductor.
This results in a scattered electric field
\bm{E}^{s}
. To determine
\bm{E}^{s}
, we solve the
Electric Field Integral Equation (EFIE)

\gamma_{\mathrm{T}}\circ\mathfrak{U}_{k}(\bm{J})=-\gamma_{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}^{i},

which involves finding the induced surface current density
\bm{J}
on
\Gamma
determining
\bm{E}^{s}
via the
EFIE integral operator

\gamma_{\mathrm{T}}\circ\mathfrak{U}_{k}

[
1
]
.
Here,
\gamma_{\mathrm{T}}
denotes the tangential trace operator on
\Gamma
.

Crucially, when the EFIE is discretized using the
Boundary Element Method (BEM)
, two numerical challenges arise as the frequency of the involved harmonic fields approaches zero—a well-documented phenomenon referred to as the
low-frequency breakdown
.
The first challenge is the growth in the condition number of the resulting linear system after BEM discretization, while the second is the occurrence of round-off errors due to finite precision in the numerical integration of the EFIE’s source term.
These two effects can lead to inefficiencies in standard numerical solvers, requiring significantly higher computational resources (e.g., more memory or more solver iterations), or even causing the solution to fail to converge
[
2
]
.

To address the low-frequency breakdown of the EFIE, the key idea is to decompose the current density
\bm{J}
as the sum
\bm{J}=\bm{J}_{\Lambda}+\bm{J}_{\Sigma}
, where
\bm{J}_{\Lambda}
is
solenoidal
(i.e.,
\div_{\Gamma}\bm{J}_{\Lambda}=0
, with
\div_{\Gamma}
denoting the surface divergence operator on
\Gamma
) and
\bm{J}_{\Sigma}
is non-solenoidal.
This decomposition is essentially derived from the Hodge decomposition theorem applied to the
L^{2}
-based
de Rham complex
on
\Gamma
, along with the definition of the first (absolute) cohomology group
H^{1}(\Gamma)
as a quotient space, so that we can write:

\bm{J}=\underbrace{\curl_{\Gamma}\bm{\psi}+\bm{g}}_{\eqqcolon\bm{J}_{\Lambda}}+\underbrace{\grad_{\Gamma}\varphi}_{\eqqcolon\bm{J}_{\Sigma}},

(1)

where
\grad_{\Gamma}
is the
L^{2}
-adjoint of
\div_{\Gamma}
, and
\bm{g}
represents a cohomology class in
H^{1}(\Gamma)
.
The vector field
\bm{\psi}
is referred to as the
stream function
or
local loop
, and
\bm{g}
is called a
(cohomology) generator
or
global loop
.

Two approaches for computing this decomposition have been proposed in the engineering literature.
Initially, the
loop-star
decomposition was introduced
[
3
,
4
]
. This approach requires computing a set of generators for the image spaces
\mathrm{Im}\,\curl_{\Gamma}
and
\mathrm{Im}\,\grad_{\Gamma}
, as well as a set of generators for the cohomology space
H^{1}(\Gamma)
.
More recently,
quasi-Helmholtz projectors
have been proposed as an alternative to avoid explicitly computing cohomology generators
[
5
]
.
This method computes the non-solenoidal part
\bm{J}_{\Sigma}
via an orthogonal projection
P_{\Sigma}
onto
\mathrm{Im}\,\grad_{\Gamma}
, while the solenoidal part
\bm{J}_{\Lambda}
is found using the complementary orthogonal projection
P_{\Lambda}
.

However, we emphasize that the computation of generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma)
is, in practice, strictly necessary to address the low-frequency breakdown of the EFIE, even when quasi-Helmholtz projectors are used.
This point is supported by recent work
[
6
]
, which introduces a technique for reducing round-off errors in the numerical evaluation of the EFIE’s source term.
Notably, the methodology outlined in Sections IV A and B of that paper clearly shows the need for a set of generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma)
, as they appear explicitly in the definition of the proposed quasi-Helmholtz projectors (cf.
[
6
, Equations (24)–(28)]
).

Review of cohomology computations in the numerical analysis community.

We begin by pointing out early works by Hiptmair and Ostrowski in
[
7
]
(which we refer to as the
HO-algorithm
hereafter), along with a non-exhaustive list of related works in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
These articles focus on a surface
\Gamma
without boundary and aim to compute generators of the first homology group
H_{1}(\Gamma)
.
However, in numerical analysis and engineering applications, what is typically needed are generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma)
, rather than those of
H_{1}(\Gamma)
.

Since
\Gamma
is orientable, it is possible to recover generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma)
from those of
H_{1}(\Gamma)
using the Poincaré-Lefschetz Duality Theorem
[
11
]
.
This approach is mentioned in the introduction of
[
7
]
, though the actual implementation details are missing.
Instead, an algorithm that implements this construction was first developed by Dłotko in
[
12
]
and later reorganized by Dłotko and Specogna in the series of works
[
13
,
14
]
(referred to as the
DS-algorithm
).

Now, consider the case where
\Gamma
is a surface with a non-empty boundary
\partial\Gamma
.
In this case, note that the DS-algorithm can still be applied with a simple modification of the construction, as described in
[
12
, Section 6]
.
However, in the context of our electromagnetic application, the cohomology space
H^{1}(\Gamma)
is no longer appropriate.
We must instead account for the boundary condition
\bm{J}\cdot\bm{n}_{\partial\Gamma}=0
on
\partial\Gamma
, which arises from the EFIE model.

To satisfy this boundary condition, it can be shown that the vector field
\bm{g}
in
Eq.

1
must belong to the first
relative cohomology space

H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma)

[
11
]
.
Therefore, algorithms like the DS-algorithm or the HO-algorithm are unsuitable for computing generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma)
, since
H^{1}(\Gamma)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma)
are generally quite different spaces;
see
Fig.

1
.

As far as we are aware, no combinatorial algorithm has been devised to address this case.

:
A triangulation of an annulus
\Gamma
.
The support of the representative of the
H^{1}(\Gamma)
generator, as formally defined in
Section

2.2
, is shown in red, while the support of the representative of the

The green part shows the intersection of the two supports.
The shaded grey triangles indicate the support of the corresponding generators as functions in

, obtained by reconstruction using the standard Raviart-Thomas basis functions.

In this paper, we build upon this observation and consider the more general problem of computing generators of
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c})
, where
\partial\Gamma^{c}
is a suitable subset of the boundary
\partial\Gamma
of
\Gamma
.
This general version arises in the EFIE model, specifically for cases where the surface
\Gamma
is connected to external lumped-element circuits through suitable
contacts
or
ports
, such as in antennas and their connections with electronic circuits.

From a physical perspective, these connections result in net currents flowing through the body from one electrode to another, a phenomenon that would otherwise be absent.
Crucially, this modifies the topology of the surface. For example, in the simplest case of a simply connected surface
\Gamma
, these connections make the surface non-simply connected.
We discuss the details of the EFIE model with contact regions in
Section

5
.

The main results and their novelty w.r.t.
literature.

The primary objective of this paper is to provide an efficient algorithm for constructing and classifying the generators of the first relative cohomology group
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c})
, where
\Gamma
is an arbitrary triangulated surface embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\partial\Gamma^{c}
is a subset of its boundary.
Specifically, we present two key contributions: one for the mathematical community and the other for the engineering community.

On the mathematical side, a significant novel aspect is that our algorithm is provably correct for non-orientable surfaces, such as the
Möbius strip
.
Since we assume that
\Gamma
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it follows that
\Gamma
must have a non-empty boundary. This is because a non-orientable surface without boundary cannot be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(e.g., the Klein bottle; see
[
15
, Theorem 27.11]
for the general statement of this fact), while every surface with boundary can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The key challenge for non-orientable surfaces is that a generator of the
torsion subgroup

appears when considering relative homology with integer coefficients (a fact evident from a canonical representation of the Möbius strip).
The problem, then, is how to distinguish the torsion generator from other homology generators.

Crucially, in the proof of correctness presented in
Section

4
, we explicitly identify the torsion generator.
In doing so, we also resolve an open problem mentioned in
[
12
, Section 7]
.
The proof relies on concepts from
Discrete Morse Theory
, which has several far-reaching implications.
First, it generalizes the construction used in the HO-algorithm, making our algorithm a natural extension of the HO-algorithm to the case of non-orientable surfaces.
Second, it provides an alternative proof of the classical Classification Theorem for Compact Surfaces
[
16
]
, which follows as a corollary of our specific constructions.

Regarding the engineering contribution, the first aspect is a rigorous definition of
global loops

[
17
]
within the framework of
cohomology theory
.
In
Definition

5.1
, at the end of
Section

5
, we show that when global loops are defined as generators of the first relative cohomology group
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c})
of a triangulated surface that excludes contact regions, the decomposition of the current density
\bm{J}
in
Eq.

1
holds.
This formal definition has several important implications. First, it covers cases in which
\Gamma
has no contact regions and no boundary.
In these situations, the space
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma^{c})
reduces to both
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma)
, since we have
\partial\Gamma^{c}=\partial\Gamma
and
\partial\Gamma=\emptyset
, respectively.
Thus, our definition naturally encompasses a broad range of modeling contexts, correctly identifying the appropriate cohomology space, relative or absolute, in each case.

Second, our definition is based directly on the
cochain complex
(see
[
18
, Section 2.6]
) supported on a triangulation
\mathcal{K}
of
\Gamma
, rather than on the standard
L^{2}
de Rham complex typically used in the engineering literature (see
[
2
, Section IV]
).
This perspective provides new insights into algorithmic construction, enabling implementations based on efficient graph-theoretic techniques.
In particular, our algorithm has a worst-case time complexity that is linear in the number of edges of the triangulation, making it highly efficient for engineering applications such as those involving the EFIE model.
It is worth noting that, in the context of engineering computations of global loops, it has even been suggested that “it is impossible to provide a complete description of this family of functions”
[
5
]
.

Another important aspect is the treatment of contact regions. While the EFIE model in the presence of contacts has been studied in engineering works
[
19
,
20
]
, the focus has primarily been on the design of the coupling between the EFIE model and external circuit equations, as well as the quasi-static assumptions near contact regions.
Mathematically, contributions have been made to establish links with relative cohomology, beginning with seminal ideas in
[
11
]
and revisited in works such as
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, among others.
However, these contributions have largely focused on orientable domains, where the Poincaré-Lefschetz Duality Theorem can be applied.

In contrast, when a torsion generator is present, an additional complication arises, particularly the introduction of an extra generator associated with contact regions.
This stands in stark contrast to the orientable case, where a clear physical interpretation is also available.
Indeed, techniques that rely on duality theorems do not necessarily apply in non-orientable cases, as the hypotheses underlying these theorems are not satisfied.

We believe that the combination of all these features opens up new possibilities for efficient numerical procedures to solve the EFIE.

2
Preliminaries from algebraic topology

In this preliminary section, we introduce concepts from algebraic topology and provide specific definitions tailored to our purposes.
In particular, we define the concept of a cochain as a model for a discrete vector field.

In this paper, we consider two types of cochains. The first are integer-valued cochains, such as representatives of the generators of the first cohomology group over the integers.
The second are real-valued cochains, which are used to model physical variables.

2.1
CW complex and triangulations

A
2
-manifold
\Gamma
with boundary
is a compact Hausdorff topological space in which every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to a relatively open subset of either
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or the half-plane
\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid a\geq 0\}
.
The
boundary
of
\Gamma
, denoted by
\partial\Gamma
, consists of all points with neighborhoods homeomorphic to a relatively open subset of the half-plane.
We say that
\Gamma
is a
2
-manifold without boundary
, or simply a
2
-manifold, if
\partial\Gamma
is empty.

An (
open
)
m
-cell, for
m\in\{0,1,2\}
, is a topological space homeomorphic to the interior
\mathrm{int}\,B^{m}
of an
m
-ball
B^{m}
(note that
B^{0}
is a single point).
Let us now introduce the concept of a CW complex
\mathcal{K}
defined on
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1

(CW complex)
.

(See
[
24
, Section 4]
.)

A (
2
-dimensional)
CW complex

\mathcal{K}
on
\Gamma
is a partition of
\Gamma
into
m
-cells
x
such that the following holds:
for each
m
-cell
x
, with corresponding closure
\mathrm{cl}\,x
, there exists a continuous surjection (the
gluing map
)
g_{x}:B^{m}\to\mathrm{cl}\,x
, satisfying:

(i)

The restriction of
g_{x}
to the interior,
g_{x}:\mathrm{int}\,B^{m}\to x
, is a homeomorphism.

(ii)

The image of the boundary,
g_{x}(\partial B^{m})
, is contained in the union of a finite number of cells of dimension
k<m
.

For
m\in\{0,1,2\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{K}^{(m)}
the set of all
m
-cells of
\mathcal{K}
.

A CW complex
\mathcal{K}
is called
regular
if, for every
m
-cell
x
, the restriction of the gluing map
g_{x}:\partial B^{m}\to g_{x}(\partial B^{m})
is a homeomorphism.

A
regular
CW complex
\mathcal{K}
where each
m
-cell is an
m
-simplex is also referred to as a
triangulation
.
In this case, we adopt a more familiar notation and terminology for the
m
-cells.
Specifically: a
node

n
is a
0
-cell in
V\coloneqq\mathcal{K}^{(0)}
, an
edge

e
is a
1
-cell in
E\coloneqq\mathcal{K}^{(1)}
, and a
face

f
is a
2
-cell in
F\coloneqq\mathcal{K}^{(2)}
.
Accordingly, we denote a triangulation
\mathcal{K}
by
\mathcal{K}=(V,E,F)
.

A
CW subcomplex

\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{K}
is a union of
m
-cells
x\in\mathcal{K}
such that, if
x\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, then the closure of
x
is also a subset of
\mathcal{S}
.

If
\Gamma
has a non-empty boundary
\partial\Gamma
, we denote by
\partial\mathcal{K}
the subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
induced on
\partial\Gamma
(note that
\partial\Gamma
is itself a manifold because
\Gamma
is).

The
1
-skeleton
of
\mathcal{K}
is the union
\mathcal{K}^{(0)}\cup\mathcal{K}^{(1)}
, i.e., the set of all
0
- and
1
-cells.
It is straightforward to observe that the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{K}
can be regarded as a graph whose vertices and edges are
\mathcal{K}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{K}^{(1)}
, respectively.

Using this identification, a
tree

T=(V_{T},E_{T})
of
\mathcal{K}
refers to a tree of the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{K}
, viewed as a graph.
We then say that
T
is a
spanning tree
if the set
V_{T}
includes all the
0
-cells of
\mathcal{K}
, that is, if
V_{T}=\mathcal{K}^{(0)}
.

2.2
Chains and cochains

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a CW complex, and let
G
be either the commutative ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
or reals
\mathbb{R}
.
We start by fixing an orientation of all its
m
-cells (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Chapter 2]
).

The
m
-th
chain group

C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
is the group of all formal sums
c=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}x
with
a_{x}\in G
.
We call each element of
C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
a
m
-chain
.
For two
m
-chains
c=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}x
and
c^{\prime}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}b_{x}x
, we define their
scalar product
by

<c,c^{\prime}>\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}b_{x}.

The
m
-th
cochain group

C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
is the group of all (linear) maps
\mathbf{c}:C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\to G
such that
\mathbf{c}(c)=\mathbf{c}(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}x)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}\mathbf{c}(x)
for every
c\in C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
.
We call each element of
C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
a
m
-cochain
.

For a
m
-cochain
\mathbf{c}\in C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
, its
support
is the set of all
m
-cells
x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}
such that
\mathbf{c}(x)\neq 0
.

Notice that to compute the value
\mathbf{c}(c)
it is sufficient to know the values
\mathbf{c}(x)
on every
m
-cell.
Therefore, we can associate to the
m
-cochain
\mathbf{c}
the
m
-chain
\phi(\mathbf{c})\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}x
with
a_{x}=\mathbf{c}(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}
in such a way that, for each
m
-chain
c^{\prime}\in C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
, it holds

\mathbf{c}(c^{\prime})=<\phi(\mathbf{c}),c^{\prime}>

In what follows, we will simply write
<\mathbf{c},c^{\prime}>
in place of
<\phi(\mathbf{c}),c^{\prime}>
so that, by the above equality, this expression denotes also the value
\mathbf{c}(c^{\prime})
.

For
m\in\{1,2\}
, let
\iota:\mathcal{K}^{(m)}\times\mathcal{K}^{(m-1)}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the
incidence number
between a
m
-cell and a
(m-1)
-cell (see
[
26
, Section 2.2]
, and, in particular, the cellular boundary formula on p. 140; equivalently, see
[
25
]
).
If
\iota(x,x^{\prime})\neq 0
, then we say that
x
is
incident
on
x^{\prime}
, or simply that
x
and
x^{\prime}
are incident.

We use incidence numbers to define the
boundary map

\partial_{m}:C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\to C_{m-1}(\mathcal{K};G)
for
m\in\{1,2\}
.
For each
m
-cell
x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}
we define
\partial_{m}x\coloneqq\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{(m-1)}}\iota(x,x^{\prime})x^{\prime}
, and for a
m
-chain
c=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}x\in C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
, by linearity, we define
\partial_{m}c\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{K}^{(m)}}a_{x}\partial_{m}x
.
It can be checked that
\partial_{m+1}\circ\partial_{m}=0
for
m\in\{0,1\}
.

The
coboundary map

\delta^{m}:C^{m-1}(\mathcal{K};G)\to C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
, for
m\in\{0,1\}
, is defined as follows.
For a
m
-cochain
\mathbf{c}\in C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
,
\delta^{m}\mathbf{c}
is the (unique)
(m+1)
-cochain such that

<\delta^{m}\mathbf{c},c^{\prime}>=<\mathbf{c},\partial_{m+1}c^{\prime}>

for all
c^{\prime}\in C_{m+1}(\mathcal{K};G)
.
Using
\partial_{m+1}\circ\partial_{m}=0
, it can be checked that
\delta^{m}\circ\delta^{m+1}
for
m\in\{0,1\}
, so that the (graded) family
(C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G),\delta^{m})
with
m\in\{0,1,2\}
is a
(cochain) complex
once we also set
\delta^{2}\coloneqq 0

[
18
]
.

2.3
Homology and cohomology

The boundary operator allows us to classify
m
-chains in
\mathcal{K}
into key subgroups.

We define the subgroup of
m
-cycles
as the kernel of the boundary operator,
Z_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq\ker\partial_{m}=\{c\in C_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\mid\partial_{m}c=0\}
, and the subgroup
B_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq\mathrm{im}\,\partial_{m+1}=\{\partial_{m+1}c\mid c\in C_{m+1}(\mathcal{K};G)\}
, which consists of elements that are the image of the
(m+1)
-th boundary operator.
An essential property is that
B_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
is a subset of
Z_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
.

The
m
-th
homology group
is then the quotient group
H_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq Z_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)/B_{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
.

When the coefficient ring is the integers
G=\mathbb{Z}
, the homology group
H_{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
is a finitely generated Abelian group.
By the Fundamental Theorem for Finitely Generated Abelian Groups (see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 3.61]
), it is isomorphic to the direct sum of a free Abelian group and a
torsion subgroup
.

In a similar manner, we define the subgroup of
m
-cocycles

Z^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq\ker\delta^{m}=\{\mathbf{c}\in C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\mid\delta^{m}\mathbf{c}=0\}
, and the subgroup
B^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq\mathrm{im}\,\delta^{m-1}=\{\delta^{m-1}\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in C^{m-1}(\mathcal{K};G)\}
.
The
m
-th
cohomology group
is the quotient group
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)\coloneqq Z^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)/B^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
.

In the following, we will also need the concept of
relative cohomology
, which focuses on a complex relative to a subcomplex.
Specifically, we will focus on the CW subcomplex
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
induced by
\mathcal{K}
on a closed subset
\partial\Gamma^{c}
of
\partial\Gamma
.

The group of
m
-th
relative cochains
, denoted
C^{m}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};G)
, is defined as the subgroup of
C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
consisting of
m
-cochains that vanish on all
m
-cells of
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
.
This means that a
m
-cochain
\mathbf{c}\in C^{m}(\mathcal{K};G)
is a relative
m
-cochain if its support does not contain any
m
-cells in
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
.

The coboundary operator
\delta^{m}
restricts to a map on these groups, allowing the definition of the
m
-th
relative cohomology group

H^{m}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};G)
analogously to the absolute case.

When the coefficient ring is the reals,
G=\mathbb{R}
, the
m
-th cohomology group
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
is a real vector space, and its dimension is denoted by
\beta_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
.
A basis for
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
is a set of equivalence classes
\{[\mathbf{g}_{1}],\dots,[\mathbf{g}_{\beta_{m}}]\}\subset H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
such that every element in
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
can be uniquely written as a linear combination
\sum_{i=1}^{\beta_{m}}\alpha_{i}[\mathbf{g}_{i}]
with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The set of
m
-cocycles
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{\beta_{m}}\}
is then called a set of
cohomology generators
.
These considerations and terminologies also apply to the relative cohomology group
H^{m}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 1

(Relationship between cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{R}
)
.

The relationship between cohomology with integer coefficients
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
and real coefficients
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
is crucial for our application.
For the numerical approximation of physical fields, we use generators of
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
, while computational topology algorithms typically provide generators of
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
.

Fortunately, generators for
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
can be derived from those for
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
.
The basis for the real vector space
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
corresponds to the free part of the abelian group
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
, effectively disregarding its torsion subgroup.
This connection is formalized by the Universal Coefficient Theorem; see
Section

4.5
, and in particular
Corollary

1
, for its implications in our specific constructions.

Remark 2

(Notation for cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
)
.

Unless otherwise specified, cohomology groups will be taken with real coefficients.
In particular, for the remainder of the paper we write
H^{m}(\mathcal{K})=H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
, while we continue to write
H^{m}(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{Z})
when integer coefficients are intended.
The same convention applies to relative cohomology, e.g.
H^{m}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})=H^{m}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.

Let

Definition 4.1

(Matching, acyclic matching)

A

.

4.5
Characterization of generators of
H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})

Let us remind the Universal Coefficient Theorem for cohomology.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

If a chain complex
C
of free Abelian groups has homology groups
H_{n}(C;G)
, then the cohomology groups
H^{n}(C;G)
of the cochain complex
Hom(C;G)
are determined by the exact sequence

0\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{Ext}(H_{n-1}(C;G);G)\xrightarrow{}H^{n}(C;G)\xrightarrow{h}\mathrm{Hom}(H_{n}(C;G);G)\xrightarrow{}0.

For our purposes, we do not need to delve into the definition of the
\mathrm{Ext}
functor.
It suffices to note the following property:
\mathrm{Ext}(Q;G)=0
when
Q
is a free group.
For further details and a proof of this property, consult
[
26
]
.

We now apply
Theorem

4.2
to
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
with
n=1
and
G=\mathbb{Z}
.
In
Theorem

4.2
, the map
h:H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z});\mathbb{Z})
assigns to each generator
\mathbf{g}\in H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})
the function
<\mathbf{g},\cdot>
, which maps an element
c\in H_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})
to the value
<\mathbf{g},c>
.

Note that if
\mathrm{Ext}(H_{0}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z});\mathbb{Z})\cong 0
, then
h
is an isomorphism.
Since we assume
\mathcal{\mathcal{K}}
is connected,
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
is also connected, implying that
H_{0}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
.
Consequently,
\mathrm{Ext}(H_{0}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z});\mathbb{R})\cong 0
, and from the exactness of the sequence in
Theorem

4.2
, we conclude that
h:H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z});\mathbb{Z})
is an isomorphism.

As a result, we obtain the following characterization of generators of

with real coefficients in terms of a set of generators of
H_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})
.

Corollary 1

(Characterization of cohomology generators)
.

Let
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\beta_{1}}
be a set of homology generators of
H_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}};\mathbb{Z})
, and let
\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\beta_{1}}
be a set of
1
-cocycles of
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
such that

\displaystyle<\mathbf{g}_{i},c_{j}>

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,j},

(10)

for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\beta_{1}\}
, where

Then, the set

.
In particular, the same set yields a basis for

.

Lemma 6

(Dimension calculation via long exact sequences)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
a
2
-dimensional CW complex with a non-empty boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}
.
Then, the relationship between the dimensions of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
and
H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})
is given by the following expression

\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})}-\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}=N^{\mathrm{ho}}-1.

(14)

Proof.

The proof consists in expressing the dimensions of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
and
H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})
in terms of that
H^{1}(\mathcal{K})
and in computing the dimension of some elementary cohomology spaces.

We start by considering the Mayer-Vietoris long exact sequence
[
30
, Theorem 3, Chapter 11]
for the triad
(\bar{\mathcal{K}}
,
\mathcal{K}
,
D)
, where
D
is defined as
D\coloneqq\bigcup_{k=1}^{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}D_{k}
.
The exact sequence is as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\longrightarrow\underset{1}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{1}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K})}}\oplus\underset{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}{\underbrace{H^{0}(D)}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K}\cap D)}}\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\mathcal{K})\oplus\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{1}(D)}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}{\underbrace{H^{1}(K\cap D)}}\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}H^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\mathcal{K})}}\oplus\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(D)}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\mathcal{K}\cap D)}}\longrightarrow 0,\end{split}

(15)

where the dimensions of the corresponding cohomology groups are indicated under the braces.

Next, we apply the long exact sequence in relative cohomology
[
30
, Theorem 13]
for the pair
(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\longrightarrow\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{1}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\partial\mathcal{K})}}\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{}\underset{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}{\underbrace{H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K})}}\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}H^{2}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\mathcal{K})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\partial\mathcal{K})}}\longrightarrow 0,\end{split}

(16)

where the dimensions of the cohomology groups are indicated under the braces.
In particular,
N^{\mathrm{ho}}
denotes the number of connected components of
\partial\mathcal{K}
.

Finally, applying the Euler characteristic formula
[
26
, Theorem 2.44]
to the exact sequences
Eq.

15
and
Eq.

16
, and noting that in
Eq.

16
, the dimension of
H^{2}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
equals that of
H^{2}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})
in
Eq.

15
, regardless of whether
\mathcal{K}
is orientable, we obtain the desired formula
Eq.

14
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the second decomposition of global loops, which demonstrates the correctness of
Algorithm

3
.

Lemma 7

(Second decomposition of global loops)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}=\{\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{ho}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}}|}
be the set of
1
-cochains computed by
Algorithm

3
, and define
\langle{[\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}]}\rangle\coloneqq\{\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}}|}\beta_{k}[\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{ho}}_{k}]\mid\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\}
as the linear span of the equivalence classes associated with each generator
\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{ho}}_{k}\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}

Then, the following group isomorphism holds

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})\cong H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\oplus\langle{[\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}]}\rangle.

(17)

Proof.

We begin by recalling the definition of
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\mathrm{ho}}
in
Algorithm

3
, specifically at lines 2–3.
Each
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\mathrm{ho}}\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}
is a

-cocycle; in particular

\langle{[\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}]}\rangle\subset H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})

Moreover, since each

, the classes represented by

are trivial in

Since each

As

\mathrm{dim}\,{\langle{[\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{ho}}]}\rangle}=N^{\mathrm{ho}}-1=\mathrm{dim}\,H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})-\mathrm{dim}\,H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}}),

∎

Lemma 8

(Dimension computation via long exact sequences)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
a
2
-dimensional CW complex with a non-empty boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}
.
Recalling that
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{K}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{N^{\mathrm{co}}}\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}_{j}
, where each
\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}_{j}
is a connected component of
\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}
, the following expression relates the dimensions of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
and
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
:

\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}-\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})}=\begin{cases}N^{\mathrm{co}}-1&\text{if $\mathcal{K}$ is orientable},\\
N^{\mathrm{co}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(18)

Proof.

We proceed in a similar manner as in the proof of
Lemma

6
, by expressing the dimensions of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
and
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
in terms of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K})
.

First, let us consider the long exact sequence in relative cohomology
[
30
, Theorem 12]
for the pair
(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&\longrightarrow\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{1}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\mathcal{K})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{N^{c}}{\underbrace{H^{0}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}}\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{}H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})\rightarrow\\
&\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\mathcal{K})}}\xrightarrow{}\underset{0}{\underbrace{H^{2}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}}\longrightarrow 0,\end{split}

(19)

where
N^{c}
represents the number of connected components of
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
.

Next, let us note the following two facts:

1.

In the exact sequence for
(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
given in
Eq.

16
of
Lemma

6
, the dimension of
H^{2}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})
is
1
if
\mathcal{K}
is orientable and
0
if
\mathcal{K}
is non-orientable.

2.

In the exact sequence for
(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
in
Eq.

19
, the dimension of
H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
satisfies the formula

\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}=N^{c}-N^{\mathrm{co}},

(20)

which will now we prove.

Consider the set of all connected components
\partial\mathcal{K}_{k}
of
\partial\mathcal{K}
, where
k\in\{1,\dots,N^{\mathrm{ho}}\}
.
After potentially rearranging the indices, we assume that there is a number
p\in\{1,\dots,N^{\mathrm{ho}}\}
such that the first
p
connected components,
\{\partial\mathcal{K}_{1},\dots,\partial\mathcal{K}_{p}\}
, contain some contact regions
\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,N^{\mathrm{co}}\}
, while the remaining components
\{\partial\mathcal{K}_{p+1},\dots,\partial\mathcal{K}_{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}\}
do not.
If
p=N^{\mathrm{ho}}
, then the second set is empty.

For each
k\in\{1,\dots,N^{\mathrm{ho}}\}
, define
J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}
as the set of indices
j\in\{1,\dots,N^{\mathrm{co}}\}
such that
\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}_{j}\subset\partial\mathcal{K}_{k}
.
Let
|J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}|
denote the cardinality of
J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}
, and define
N^{c}_{k}
as the number of connected components of the set
\partial\mathcal{K}_{k}\setminus\bigcup_{j\in J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}}\partial\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{co}}_{j}
.

It is easy to check that
|J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}|=N^{c}_{k}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, and that
|J^{\mathrm{co}}_{k}|=0
and
N^{c}_{k}=1
for all

Hence, we have the relation

N^{\mathrm{ho}}-p=\sum_{k=p+1}^{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}N^{c}_{k}.

Also, since
\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})}=N^{\mathrm{ho}}-p
, we obtain the formula:

N^{c}=\sum_{k=1}^{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}N^{c}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}N^{c}_{k}+\sum_{k=p+1}^{N^{\mathrm{ho}}}N^{c}_{k}=N^{\mathrm{co}}+\mathrm{dim}\,{H^{1}(\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})},

which is the desired result
Eq.

20

Finally, applying the Euler characteristic formula
[
26
, Theorem 2.44]
to the exact sequences
Eq.

19
and
Eq.

16
in
Lemma

6
, and using Facts 1 and 2, we derive the formula
Eq.

.

Lemma 9

(Third decomposition of global loops)
.

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})\cong H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})\oplus\langle{[\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{co}}]}\rangle.

Proof.

Let

be the set defined in

2
at line 14, and used in

4
at line 9.

4

On the other hand, if

5
Application to BEM discretization of the EFIE

In the previous sections, we established a rigorous algorithmic framework for computing a set of cohomology generators of
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c})
for a generic CW complex.
In this section, we place this mathematical result in the context of the computational electromagnetics application mentioned in the introduction.
Specifically, we show how these cohomology generators, referred to in engineering literature as
global loops
, appear naturally in the discretization of the EFIE for conducting surfaces with contacts.

5.1
EFIE modeling of screens with contacts

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a compact surface representing the computational domain of a thin conducting body (a
screen
).
We assume
\Gamma
is a
2
-manifold with boundary, satisfying the Lipschitz regularity conditions detailed in
[
1
, Section 1.1]
.
Note that if the physical model includes
2
-dimensional contact regions (patches),
\Gamma
is defined as the surface obtained after removing the interior of these patches (see, e.g.,
[
19
, Section II]
).

An induced surface current density
\bm{J}
flows on
\Gamma
, driven either by an incident electromagnetic field or by external voltage sources.
To model these connections, the boundary
\partial\Gamma
is partitioned into two disjoint subsets as

\partial\Gamma=\partial\Gamma^{c}\cup\partial\Gamma^{\mathrm{co}}.

(22)

Here,
\partial\Gamma^{\mathrm{co}}
represents the union of
N^{\mathrm{co}}
disjoint connected components (the
contacts
or
ports
).
These components may correspond either to the boundaries of removed patches (interior ports) or to specific subsets of the original boundary (edge ports).
The remaining part,
\partial\Gamma^{c}
, represents the insulating boundary.
See
Fig.

3
for an illustration.

Figure 3
:

A screen
\Gamma
whose boundary
\partial\Gamma
decomposes into two connected components.
In red, the (open) subset
\partial\Gamma^{co}
of
\partial\Gamma
represents the contact region, and it decomposes into two connected components.
The dashed line indicates an example of a possible interior port,
whose associated patch must then be removed from
\Gamma
.

The physical boundary conditions for the current density
\bm{J}
differ on these two subsets.
Specifically, on the insulating boundary
\partial\Gamma^{c}
, no current can flow out of the surface, imposing the condition

\bm{J}\cdot\bm{n}_{\partial\Gamma}=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Gamma^{c},

(23)

where
\bm{n}_{\partial\Gamma}
is the unit outer normal tangent to
\Gamma
.
Conversely, on the contact interfaces
\partial\Gamma^{\mathrm{co}}
, current is allowed to flow in and out to connect with the external circuit.

5.2
Formal definition of global loops

To solve the EFIE numerically, we introduce a triangulation
\mathcal{K}
covering the computational domain
\Gamma
.
The standard Galerkin BEM seeks the solution
\bm{J}
in the finite element space
W^{1}_{h}
spanned by Raviart-Thomas basis functions, subject to the boundary condition
Eq.

23
on
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
.

The connection to the cochain framework is established via the
de Rham map

[
18
,
31
]
.
This map provides a structural isomorphism between the finite element space
W^{1}_{h}
and the space of relative
1
-cochains
C^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.
Under this mapping:

•

The action of the surface divergence operator
\div_{\Gamma}
corresponds to that of the coboundary operator
\delta^{1}
.

•

The subspace of divergence-free currents
\{\bm{J}\in W^{1}_{h}\mid\div_{\Gamma}\bm{J}=0\}
is isomorphic to the space of relative
1
-cocycles
Z^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.

•

The subspace
\mathrm{Im}\,\curl_{\Gamma}
is isomorphic to
B^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.

Therefore, the space of solenoidal vector fields
\bm{g}
required for the decomposition in
Eq.

1
is exactly isomorphic to the first relative cohomology group
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.

This physical reasoning leads directly to the formal definition used throughout this paper.

Definition 5.1

(Definition of global loops)
.

The set
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\beta_{1}}
of
global loops
on the screen
\Gamma
is defined as a set of representatives for a basis of the first relative cohomology group
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.
Here,
\mathcal{K}
is the triangulation of the computational domain
\Gamma
(excluding interior ports), and
\partial\mathcal{K}^{c}
is the subcomplex of the boundary where the insulation condition
Eq.

23
holds.

This definition clarifies that finding “global loops” is not merely a heuristic geometric search, but a rigorous algebraic task: identifying generators for the relative cohomology space
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K}^{c};\mathbb{R})
.
The algorithm proposed in
Section

3
is specifically designed to
address this task efficiently, addressing the complex topological configurations that arise in real-world engineering applications.

6
Conclusions

In this final section, we provide an overview of the algorithm’s key features, its impact on engineering applications, and potential extensions that could guide future work.

We first emphasize that the algorithm’s foundation in Discrete Morse Theory ensures its broad applicability.
The algorithms presented here are effective as long as an
optimal discrete Morse function
can be computed.
For example, such functions can be determined even for pathological cases, such as surfaces connected by a single vertex or edge
[
32
]
.
Additionally, these ideas can extend to handle
non-manifold surfaces
(e.g., screens with junction points, as discussed in
[
33
,
34
]
).
We propose that slight modifications to our algorithms could extend their applicability to these cases while maintaining linear worst-case complexity.
For instance, identifying manifold parts can be achieved in linear time by analyzing the coboundary elements of each edge.
By orienting a random polygon in each manifold part and iteratively orienting others using a
breadth-first search

[
35
]
, we expect the algorithm’s linear time complexity to remain intact.

Another consideration is that the algorithm does not aim to produce a minimal or shortest set of cohomology generators.
This means that the generated set of cohomology generators may not have the most compact support (e.g., minimal length or the smallest number of edges).
For further insights, readers may consult Erickson et al.
[
36
]
for homology and Dłotko et al.

[
14
]
for cohomology, which are particularly relevant to our applications.
Achieving a truly minimal basis is often computationally prohibitive, necessitating a trade-off between runtime and the quality of the basis obtained.

Regarding the impact on engineering applications, the first aspect to highlight is the algorithm’s ability to automatically compute global loops for surfaces, both with and without boundaries, and for orientable as well as non-orientable surfaces.
The primary algorithm, presented in
Section

3.5
, utilizes a three-step procedure to partition global loops into distinct disjoint classes.
Informally, these classes correspond to the presence of “handles,” “holes,” or contact regions.
The algorithm handles all these cases automatically, adapting to each situation to produce the correct output.
This is a crucial feature for engineering applications where manually verifying the explicit form and count of global loops is challenging.
Furthermore, this reliability is underpinned by the robust theoretical analysis provided earlier in the paper.

For example, consider the case where
\Gamma
is a triangulated Möbius strip. Our algorithm outputs no global loop, understood as a generator of
H^{1}(\Gamma,\partial\Gamma)
. This result is easily validated theoretically via
Lemma

7
. Since the Möbius strip has only one connected component,
N^{\mathrm{ho}}=1
, it follows that
H^{1}(\mathcal{K},\partial\mathcal{K})\cong H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})
.
However,
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
, obtained by gluing a disk to the Möbius strip, is homeomorphic to a
projective plane
(cf.
[
16
, pg. 19]
), and from the homological characterization of the projective plane, we know that
H^{1}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\cong 0
.

This observation corrects an error in
[
6
]
, where it is claimed that the Möbius strip supports a global loop (see the caption of Fig. 2 in
[
6
]
).
This figure refers to their proposed procedure for computing global loops of the surface under consideration.
To avoid such pitfalls and ensure provably correct results, the algorithm proposed in this work is therefore explicitly required.

The second crucial practical aspect of the algorithm is its linear time worst-case complexity.
Specifically, the algorithms described in
Section

3
require only a list-based representation of a triangulation
\mathcal{K}
of the surface
\Gamma
.
In this representation, each cell in
\mathcal{K}
has an associated data structure that stores its neighboring cells.
In practical applications, it is generally assumed that the number of neighboring cells for each cell in
\mathcal{K}
is bounded by a constant, reflecting common mesh regularity assumptions in boundary element methods.
Considering this constant, the use of standard graph algorithms for spanning trees results in a worst-case linear time complexity
[
35
]
.
Additionally, all algorithms in Section
3
are entirely graph-based, which makes them straightforward to implement without requiring external libraries or specialized procedures.
