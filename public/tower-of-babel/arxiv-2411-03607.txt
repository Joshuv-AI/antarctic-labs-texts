Upper bound of high-order derivatives for Wachspress coordinates on polytopes Thanks: This work was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China under Grant No. 12171244.
Pengjie Tian, Yanqiu Wang

Abstract
The gradient bounds [ 19 , 27 , 14 ] of generalized barycentric coordinates play an essential role in the H 1 H^{1} norm approximation error estimate of generalized barycentric interpolations. Similarly, the H k H^{k} norm, k > 1 k>1 , estimate needs upper bounds of high-order derivatives, which are not available in the literature. In this paper, we derive such upper bounds for the Wachspress generalized barycentric coordinates on simple convex d d -dimensional polytopes, d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 . The result can be used to prove optimal convergence for Wachspress-based polytopal finite element approximation of, for example, fourth-order elliptic equations. Another contribution of this paper is to compare various shape-regularity conditions for simple convex polytopes, and to clarify their relations using knowledge from convex geometry.



keywords

polytopal mesh, generalized barycentric coordinates, Wachspress coordinates, finite element method, interpolation error.

AMS

65D05, 65N30, 41A25, 41A30.

Introduction

Generalized barycentric coordinates (GBCs)
[
13
,
22
,
36
]
have been used to construct various finite element methods (FEMs) on polytopal meshes
[
17
,
18
,
24
,
25
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
39
]
.
Following the standard FEM theory
[
2
,
6
]
, FEM approximation error analysis relies on the interpolation error analysis.
For second-order elliptic equations, Gillette, Rand and Bajaj
[
19
]
have shown that the
H^{1}
norm
GBC interpolation error estimate can be derived using the gradient bounds of GBCs.
They also gave the gradient bounds of three GBCs: the Wachspress coordinates, the Sibson coordinates and the harmonic coordinates, in two dimensions.
The same authors then proved the gradient bound of the mean value coordinates in
[
27
]
.
Later, Floater, Gillette and Sukumar
[
14
]
re-investigated the Wachspress coordinates, where
they extended the gradient bound in
[
19
]
from
2
D planar polygons to simple convex
d
-dimensional polytopes.
These results helped to establish the approximation error estimate of GBC-based FEM for second-order elliptic equations.

With the development of high-order GBC-based polygonal elements
[
28
,
16
,
5
]
,
it is possible to construct GBC-based FEM for fourth-order or even higher order elliptic partial differential equations.
A Morley-type GBC-based non-conforming FEM on polygonal meshes
[
34
]
has been developed for solving fourth-order plate bending problems.
Its FEM approximation error analysis relies on the interpolation error analysis,
which in turn relies on the upper bounds of second-order derivatives of GBCs.
Such upper bounds were not available by the time when
[
34
]
was completed,
which has left a gap in the theoretical error analysis. This motivates the current work.
In this paper, we derive upper bounds of arbitrary order derivatives for Wachspress coordinates on simple convex
d
-dimensional polytopes.
The result can be used to prove optimal convergence for Wachspress-based polytopal finite element approximation of fourth-order elliptic equations.
Moreover, through this work,
we hope to reveal more properties of the GBCs, which have found interesting applications in computer graphics and computational mechanics
[
22
]
.

To derive the upper bounds,
one may naturally think of extending the analysis of the gradient bound in
[
14
]
to high-order derivatives.
Unfortunately, this turns out to be almost impossible, as the analysis in
[
14
]
is tailored to retrieve a sharp gradient bound, and thus highly technical.
We propose different techniques to reach the goal.
Similar to
[
14
]
, our upper bounds depend on the diameter
h_{K}
of polytope
K
,
and the minimal distance
h_{*}
between vertices and non-incident facets (
(d-1)
-dimensional faces) of
K
.
The bounds are sharp in asymptotic orders when
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
, but not when
h_{*}\ll h_{K}
.
We shall compare various shape regularity assumptions for simple convex polytopes, and
show that
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
is a practically reasonable assumption in applications such as FEM.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the Wachspress coordinates in detail, and present some preliminary tools.
In Section 3, we derive the upper bounds of arbitrary-order derivatives for Wachspress coordinates.
In Section 4, we discuss the relation of geometric conditions for simple convex polytopes.
Supporting numerical results, including an application on solving fourth-order plate bending problems, are presented in Section 5.

2.1
Convex polytope

We follow Def 0.1 in
[
41
]
to define a
d
-dimensional,
d\geq 1
, convex polytope
K\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
which is closed and hence compact.
By “
d
-dimensional” we mean that the affine hull of
K
fills the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Following Def. 2.1 in
[
41
]
, define
faces
of
K
to be the intersection of
K
with its supporting hyperplanes.
Again, each face of
K
is a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Faces with dimension
0
,
1
and
d-1
are called
vertices, edges
and
facets
, respectively.
Denote by
V
and
F
, respectively, the set of all vertices and the set of all facets of
K
. When
d=2
,
K
is a polygon and the facets are just edges of the polygon.

A vertex
\boldsymbol{v}
and a facet
f
are
incident
to each other if
\boldsymbol{v}\in f
. Otherwise, we say they are non-incident.
For each facet
f\in F
, denote by
V_{f}
the set of all vertices of
K
incident to
f
.
For each vertex
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
, denote by
V_{\boldsymbol{v}}
the set of all vertices of
K
connected to
\boldsymbol{v}
through an edge, and by
F_{\boldsymbol{v}}
the set of all facets of
K
incident to
\boldsymbol{v}
.
We use
|\cdot|
to denote the cardinality of a given set. For example,
\left|V\right|
is the number of vertices of
K
, and
\left|F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|
is the number of facets incident to
\boldsymbol{v}
.

Throughout the entire paper, we assume that the polytope
K
is

•

convex
;

•

non-degenerate:
adjacent facets do not lie in the same hyperplane;

•

simple:
each vertex is incident to exactly
d
facets, i.e.,
\left|F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|=d
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
.

Remark 2.1
.

Convex polytopes defined according to Def 0.1 in
[
41
]
are automatically non-degenerate.
In case that readers may follow other possible definitions of “convex polytopes” which can be degenerate, we explicitly require polytopes to be non-degenerate in this paper.
In
2
and
3
dimensions, polytopes in a centroidal Voronoi tessellation
[
9
]
are convex and non-degenerate.

Remark 2.2
.

According to the properties of simple convex polytopes (see Thm 12.12 in
[
3
]
), we have
\left|V_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|=d
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
.

Let
h_{K}
be the diameter of
K
. For each point
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
and facet
f\in F
, denote by
h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
the distance from
\boldsymbol{x}
to the hyperplane containing
f
.
Define
[
14
]

h_{*}=\min_{f\in F}\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus V_{f}}h_{f}(\boldsymbol{v}),

which is the minimum distance between non-incident vertices and facets.
It is clear that
h_{*}
is strictly greater than
0

as long as
K
is convex and non-degenerate.

2.2
Wachspress coordinates

A set of functions
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
, is called a set of
generalized barycentric coordinates
(GBCs) on
K
, if it satisfies
[
13
]

1.

(Non-negativity)
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
on
K
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
;

2.

(Linear precision) for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in K

\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=1,\qquad\quad\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{x}.

(2.1)

When
\left|V\right|=d+1
, i.e.,
K
is a simplex, the set of GBCs is uniquely determined by (
2.1
).
When
\left|V\right|>d+1
, the choice of GBCs is no longer unique. In this paper, we focus on the Wachspress GBCs, and will give a description below.

For each facet
f\in F
, denote by
\boldsymbol{n}_{f}
the unit outward normal vector on
f
. Then for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, we can express
h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
by

h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f},\qquad\forall\ \boldsymbol{v}\in V_{f},

(2.2)

which is just a linear polynomial.
Function
h_{f}
is non-negative in
K
, with
h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
only when
\boldsymbol{x}\in f
.

Given
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
, let
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{d}
be the
d
facets in
F_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, ordered such that the matrix

M_{\boldsymbol{v}}=[\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{1}},\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{d}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

(2.3)

has a positive determinant
(note that
{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})
is non-zero as long as
K
is non-degenerate).
The Wachspress coordinates are then defined by

\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in V}w_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{x})},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in K,

where

w_{\boldsymbol{v}}={\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})\prod_{f\in F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}}}h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2.4)

Note that
F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}}
can not be empty, thus (
2.4
) is well-posed.
For simplicity, denote
W=\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in V}w_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and consequently we can write

\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{W(\boldsymbol{x})},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in K.

(2.5)

Clearly, each
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is non-negative in
K
. Moreover, we have

Lemma 1
.

Function
W
is positive in
K
.

Proof.

Since each
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is non-negative in
K
and
W=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, we only need to show that for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, there exists a
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
such that
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
.
If
\boldsymbol{x}
lies in the interior of
K
, then by definition
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
.
Consider the case when
\boldsymbol{x}
lies on the boundary of
K
.
The intersection of all facets containing
\boldsymbol{x}
is a face with the smallest dimension that contains
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Denote this face by
\sigma
.
Then we have
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in V\cap\sigma
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

By Lemma
1
, function
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is well-defined and has non-negative value in
K
.
They also satisfy (
2.1
), and hence form a set of GBCs.

Remark 2.3
.

The Wachspress coordinates defined in
[
37
,
38
]
uses

w_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})}{\prod_{f\in F_{\boldsymbol{v}}}h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})},

(2.6)

instead of (
2.4
). It is clear that they are equivalent as
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}
remains the same.
The formula (
2.6
) may be easier to implement, as illustrated in
2
-dimension
[
13
]
.
However, unlike (
2.4
), formula (
2.6
) is not well-defined on
\partial K
.
Besides, its non-polynomial structure has caused much difficulty when estimating the gradient bounds of
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, as seen in
[
14
]
.
In this paper, we find it easier to use (
2.4
) to derive upper bounds of higher order derivatives.

Remark 2.4
.

The case
d=1
is trivial, with
K
being a line segment.
All notation and results in this paper apply trivially to the case
d=1
.
Readers shall focus on the case with
d>1
.

2.3
Preliminary tools

We introduce the
general Leibniz formula
for computing partial derivatives of products,
the
multivariate Faà di Bruno formula

[
7
]
for computing partial derivatives of composite functions,
and some notation.

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the set of non-negative integers, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be a multi-index. Define

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}!

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!\cdots\alpha_{d}!,

\displaystyle D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}.

For
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, we say
\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
if
\beta_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
.
Similarly, one can define
<
,
\geq
and
>
.
When
\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, define

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{array}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{i}\end{array}\right)=\frac{\boldsymbol{\alpha}!}{\boldsymbol{\beta}!(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})!}.

We say
\boldsymbol{\beta}\prec\boldsymbol{\alpha}
provided that one of the following holds:

(1)

|\boldsymbol{\beta}|<|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
;

(2)

|\boldsymbol{\beta}|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
and
\beta_{1}<\alpha_{1}
;

(3)

|\boldsymbol{\beta}|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
,
\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k+1}<\alpha_{k+1}
for some
1\leq k<d.

General Leibniz formula.
For sufficiently smooth functions
f,\,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, one has

D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(fg)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{array}\right)\left(D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}f\right)\left(D^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}g\right).

Multivariate Faà di Bruno formula

[
7
]
.
For sufficiently smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and multi-index
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
\boldsymbol{\beta}>\boldsymbol{0}
, one has

D^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}f(g(\boldsymbol{x}))=\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}f^{(\lambda)}(g)\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}\boldsymbol{\beta}!\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\left(D^{\boldsymbol{l}_{j}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{k_{j}}}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}},

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)=

\displaystyle\bigg\{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s};\boldsymbol{l}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{l}_{s}):k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\textrm{ and }\boldsymbol{l}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\textrm{ satisfy}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\textbf{0}\prec\boldsymbol{l}_{1}\prec\cdots\prec\boldsymbol{l}_{s},\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}=\lambda\ and\ \sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}\boldsymbol{l}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigg\}.

Remark 2.5
.

According to (
2.8
), the summation in (
2.7
) is a finite sum, in which the total number of terms depends only on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Consider the tensor product of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}=[x_{1}^{(i)},x_{2}^{(i)},\ldots,x_{d}^{(i)}]^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
i=1\ldots k
,

\boldsymbol{X}\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}^{(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)},

with elements
X_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{k}}=x_{i_{1}}^{(1)}x_{i_{2}}^{(2)}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{(k)}
. Define the norm

\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|=\left(\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}=1}^{d}X_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 2.6
.

The norm
|\cdot|
is an extension of the Euclidean length of vectors, which is in turn an extension of the absolute value of scalars.
Thus it is natural to use the same notation
|\cdot|
for all of them. This is not to be confused with the cardinality of sets such as
\left|V\right|
and
\left|F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|
.

Lemma 2
.

For
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,k,

\left|\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}^{(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}|.

Proof.

The case
k=2
can be calculated directly.
The rest can be proven by induction.
∎

Denote by
\nabla^{k}
the
k
th order differential operator, which can be viewed formally as the
k
th order tensor product of the gradient operator
\nabla=[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}]^{t}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\tiny k\;\mathrm{times}}

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}=\overbrace{\nabla\otimes\nabla\otimes\cdots\otimes\nabla}.

3
Derivatives of Wachspress coordinates and the upper bound

We first derive the general form of arbitrary derivatives for Wachspress coordinates. By the
general Leibniz formula
and (
2.5
), for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we have

D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}=D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(w_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\frac{1}{W}\right)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{array}\right)\left(D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)\left(D^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\frac{1}{W}\right).

(3.1)

Then we treat
\frac{1}{W}
as the composite of an inverse proportional function with
W
, and
use the
multivariate Faà di Bruno formula
to get, for
\boldsymbol{\beta}>\boldsymbol{0}
,

\displaystyle D^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\frac{1}{W}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}(-1)^{\lambda}\lambda!W^{-\lambda-1}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}\boldsymbol{\beta}!\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\left(D^{\boldsymbol{l}_{j}}W\right)^{k_{j}}}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}\frac{(-1)^{\lambda}\lambda!\boldsymbol{\beta}!}{W^{\lambda+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\left(D^{\boldsymbol{l}_{j}}W\right)^{k_{j}}}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}.

When
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
, one simply has
D^{\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{1}{W}=\frac{1}{W}
.
Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) gives

\displaystyle D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{array}\right)D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}\frac{(-1)^{\lambda}\lambda!\boldsymbol{\beta}!}{W^{\lambda+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\left(D^{\boldsymbol{l}_{j}}W\right)^{k_{j}}}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{W}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}<\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}\end{array}\right)(-1)^{\lambda}\lambda!\boldsymbol{\beta}!\frac{D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}}{W^{\lambda+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{\left(D^{\boldsymbol{l}_{j}}W\right)^{k_{j}}}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}.

Next, we estimate the upper bound of
|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}|
.
For any given
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, the (
4
-layer) summation in (
3.3
) is a finite sum with the total number of terms depending only on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
Hence it suffices to estimate (1) the upper bounds of
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}|
and
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}W|
, for any multi-index
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
;
(2) the lower bound of
W
. According to Lemma
1
, we know that
W
is positive on the compact set
K
.
Therefore its lower bound must be strictly above
0
.

3.1
Upper bound of
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}|
and
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}W|

Note that
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}|\leq|\nabla^{k}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}|
, where
k=|\boldsymbol{\nu}|
.
We shall estimate
|\nabla^{k}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}|
.
By (
2.2
) and (
2.4
), the weight function
w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is a polynomial of degree no greater than
\left|F\right|-d
.
Thus we only need to consider the case
k\leq\left|F\right|-d.

Lemma 3
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
For each
\boldsymbol{v}\in V
, the matrix
M_{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined in (
2.3
) satisfies

\left(\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}\right)^{d}\leq{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leq 1.

Proof.

For
F_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{d}\}
, consider a
d
-dimensional parallelepiped
P
spanned by vectors
\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{1}},\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{d}}
as follows

P(\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{1}},\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{d}})=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}t_{i}\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{i}},0\leq t_{i}\leq 1\right\}.

In (
2.3
) we have assumed that
\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{d}\}
is ordered to ensure that
M_{\boldsymbol{v}}
has a positive determinant. Hence
{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})
is just the volume of
P
.
Since each edge of
P
has length equal to
1
, we have
{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})\leq 1
.

We then prove the lower bound for
{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})
.
Denote
V_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{d}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is the only vertex of
K
that is adjacent to
\boldsymbol{v}
but non-incident to
f_{i}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\notin V_{f_{i}}
.
Denote by
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
be the edge pointing from
\boldsymbol{v}
to
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
. We conveniently write
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\boldsymbol{v}
.
Then the facet
f_{i}
lies in the
d-1
dimensional hyperplane

\boldsymbol{v}+{\rm span}\{\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{i-1},\boldsymbol{e}_{i+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\}.

Thus
\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=0
, for all
j\neq i.

Define a square matrix
E_{\boldsymbol{v}}=[\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Then clearly

E_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}M_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{1}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{2}}&\cdots&0\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&0&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{d}}\end{bmatrix},

which further implies that

|{\rm det}(E_{\boldsymbol{v}})|\ {\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})=|{\rm det}(E_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}M_{\boldsymbol{v}})|=\left|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{i}}\right|=\left|\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i})\right|\geq h_{*}^{d}.

(3.4)

Next, let
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be the
d
-dimensional parallelepiped spanned by
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{d}
so that the volume of
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
is
|{\rm det}(E_{\boldsymbol{v}})|
.
Note that each edge of
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
has length no greater than
h_{K}
, while the distance from each vertex
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
to a non-incident facet of
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
has length no less than
h_{*}
.
By induction one easily gets

h_{K}^{d}\geq|{\rm det}(E_{\boldsymbol{v}})|\geq h_{*}^{d}.

(3.5)

Combining (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) gives the lower bound of
{\rm det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
For
0\leq k\leq\left|F\right|-d
, we have

|\nabla^{k}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\frac{(\left|F\right|-d)!}{(\left|F\right|-d-k)!}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-k},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in K.

(3.6)

Proof.

Using induction and noticing that
\nabla h_{f}
is constant for every facet
f
, we easily get

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})\,\nabla^{k}\left(\prod_{f\in F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}}}h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle det}(M_{\boldsymbol{v}})\sum_{f_{j_{1}}\in F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\sum_{f_{j_{2}}\in F\setminus(F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cup\{f_{j_{1}}\})}\ldots\sum_{f_{j_{k}}\in F\setminus(F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cup\{f_{j_{1}},\ldots,f_{j_{k-1}}\})}

\displaystyle\nabla h_{f_{j_{1}}}\otimes\nabla h_{f_{j_{2}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\nabla h_{f_{j_{k}}}\prod_{f_{\in}F\setminus(F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cup\{f_{j_{1}},f_{j_{2}},\ldots,f_{j_{k}}\})}h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x}).

When
k=\left|F\right|-d
, the set
F\setminus(F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cup\{f_{j_{1}},f_{j_{2}},\ldots,f_{j_{k}}\})
is empty and we need to formally define

\prod_{f\in\emptyset}h_{f}=1
.
Note that

\quad|\nabla h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})|=|-\boldsymbol{n}_{f_{i}}|=1
.
Using (
3.7
), Lemma
3
and Lemma
2
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{f_{j_{1}}\in F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\ldots\sum_{f_{j_{k}}\in F\setminus(F_{\boldsymbol{v}}\cup\{f_{j_{1}},\ldots,f_{j_{k-1}}\})}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\left|F\right|-d)!}{(\left|F\right|-d-k)!}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-k}.

∎

Corollary 3.1
.

For any multi-index
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
such that
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k\leq|F|-d
, we have

|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}W(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\frac{\left|V\right|(\left|F\right|-d)!}{(\left|F\right|-d-k)!}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-k},\qquad for\ all\ \boldsymbol{x}\in K.

Proof.

The result follows from
|D^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}W(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq|\nabla^{k}W(\boldsymbol{x})|
and
W=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}
.
∎

3.2
Lower bound of
W

From Lemma
1
we know that
W
must have a lower bound strictly above
0
in the compact set
K
.
Our next goal is to estimate this lower bound in terms of
h_{K}
and
h_{*}
.
To this end, we first present two lemmas.

Lemma 5
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, there are at most
d
facets of
K
, such that the distance from
\boldsymbol{x}
to these facets are strictly less than
\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by contradiction. Fix
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
. Assume that there exist
d+1
facets, denoted by
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{d},f_{d+1}
, such that

h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\frac{h_{*}}{d+1},\qquad for\ i=1,2,\ldots,d+1.

(3.8)

By the linear precision property of GBCs, we have

h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})h_{*},

(3.9)

where in the last step we have used the fact that
\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}
s are non-negative.
Combining inequalities (
3.8
)-(
3.9
) and using
\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}=1
give

\frac{1}{d+1}>\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=1-\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Summing over
i=1\ldots,d+1
and noticing that every vertex is adjacent to exactly
d
facets, we have

1>d+1-\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq d+1-\sum_{f\in F}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{f}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=d+1-d\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Since
\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
, the above inequality yields
1>1
, which is impossible. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
Suppose the distance from a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
to
k
facets,
k\geq 1
, are strictly less than
\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}
, then these
k
facets have a common vertex.

Proof.

Denote the
k
facets by
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}
. By Lemma
5
, we have
k\leq d
.
We prove the lemma by contradiction. Suppose that these
k
facets have no common vertex, i.e
\cap_{i=1}^{k}V_{f_{i}}=\emptyset
.
Note that (
3.9
) still holds. Summing (
3.9
) over
i=1,\ldots,k
and using the assumption
\cap_{i=1}^{k}V_{f_{i}}=\emptyset
, one has

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus V_{f_{i}}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})h_{*}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\geq h_{*}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\cup_{i=1}^{k}\left(V\setminus V_{f_{i}}\right)}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=h_{*}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V\setminus\left(\cap_{i=1}^{k}V_{f_{i}}\right)}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=h_{*}\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})=h_{*}.

On the other hand, we also have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\frac{kh_{*}}{d+1}<h_{*}.

(3.11)

Combine (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) gives
h_{*}>h_{*}
, which can not be true.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we are able to derive a lower bound for
W
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
For
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, one has

W(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\frac{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}{\left(d+1\right)^{\left|F\right|-d}h_{K}^{d}}.

(3.12)

Proof.

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, if
h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}
for all
f\in F
, then by (
2.4
) and Lemma
3

W(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}w_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}\left(\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}\right)^{d}\left(\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}\right)^{\left|F\right|-d}=\left|V\right|\frac{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}{\left(d+1\right)^{\left|F\right|-d}h_{K}^{d}},

and (
3.12
) follows from
|V|\geq 1
.

If there exists exactly
k
facets,
k\geq 1
, denoted by
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}
, such that
h_{f_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})<\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}
, and

h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\frac{h_{*}}{d+1},\qquad\forall f\in F\setminus\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}\}.

By Lemma
5
, we know that
k\leq d
. By Lemma
6
, these
k
facets have a common vertex, which is denoted by
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\cap_{i=1}^{k}V_{f_{i}}
. It is apparent that

\{f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}\}\subset F_{\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}
.
Therefore

h_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\frac{h_{*}}{d+1},\qquad\forall f\in F\setminus F_{\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}.

Combining the above and using Lemma
3
, we get

W(\boldsymbol{x})\geq w_{\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\left(\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}\right)^{d}\left(\frac{h_{*}}{d+1}\right)^{\left|F\right|-d}=\frac{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}{\left(d+1\right)^{\left|F\right|-d}h_{K}^{d}}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

.

Prop. 8 in

]
gives a similar lower bound of
W
for
2
-dimensional convex polygons, where the proof also uses

W(\boldsymbol{x})\geq w_{\boldsymbol{v}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
However, Prop. 8 in

picked
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}

, breaking any tie arbitrarily.

shows that such a choice may only lead to a

lower bound.

,
K
is a quadrilateral with vertices

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(1,\,1),\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=(0,\,0),\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{3}=(2,\,0),\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{4}=(2,1),

and satisfies all shape regularity conditions in
[
19
]
.
Draw the line passing through
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and perpendicular to the line
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
.
For any point
\boldsymbol{x}
in this line, the closest vertex is
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}

.

3.3
Main result: the upper bound of
|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}|

We are now ready to present the main result on upper bound of arbitrary derivatives of the Wachspress coordinates.

Lemma 8
.

Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope.
For
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
, one has

\left|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|\leq C(\left|F\right|,\left|V\right|,\boldsymbol{\alpha},d)\sum_{\tiny\begin{matrix}0\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq|F|-d\end{matrix}}\sum_{\textrm{min}\{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|,1\}\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\frac{h_{K}^{\left|F\right|(1+\lambda)-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|(1+\lambda)}},

where
C(\left|F\right|,\left|V\right|,\boldsymbol{\alpha},d)
is a positive constant depending only on
\left|F\right|
,
\left|V\right|
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
d
.

Proof.

Using (
3.3
), Lemma
4
, Corollary
3.1
and Lemma
7
, we get

\displaystyle|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\left|F\right|,\left|V\right|,\boldsymbol{\alpha},d)\bigg[\sum_{\tiny\begin{matrix}0<\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq|F|-d\end{matrix}}\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\left(\frac{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}{h_{K}^{d}}\right)^{-(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{1}{k_{j}!(\boldsymbol{l}_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}h_{K}^{(\left|F\right|-d-|\boldsymbol{l}_{j}|)k_{j}}+\frac{\textrm{max}\{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|,0\}\,h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\left|F\right|,\left|V\right|,\boldsymbol{\alpha},d)\bigg[\sum_{\tiny\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq|F|-d\end{matrix}}\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\sum_{p_{s}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\lambda)}h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-d-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\left(\frac{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}{h_{K}^{d}}\right)^{-(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle\cdot h_{K}^{(\left|F\right|-d)\lambda-|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}+\frac{\textrm{max}\{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|,0\}\,h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}\bigg],

where in the last step we have used
(\left|F\right|-d)(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{s})-\sum_{j=1}^{s}k_{j}|\boldsymbol{l}_{j}|=(\left|F\right|-d)\lambda-|\boldsymbol{\beta}|
.
Notice that all summations in the above are finite sums and
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|-|\boldsymbol{\beta}|
when
\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we further get

\displaystyle|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}|

\displaystyle\leq C(\left|F\right|,\left|V\right|,\boldsymbol{\alpha},d)\bigg[\sum_{\tiny\begin{matrix}0<\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\\
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}|\leq|F|-d\end{matrix}}\sum_{1\leq\lambda\leq|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\frac{h_{K}^{\left|F\right|(1+\lambda)-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|(1+\lambda)}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\textrm{max}\{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|,0\}\,h_{K}^{\left|F\right|-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}}{h_{*}^{\left|F\right|}}\bigg].

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma
8
immediately implies that:

Theorem 9
.

When
\left|F\right|
,
\left|V\right|
are bounded and
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
, we have

\left|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|\leq Ch_{K}^{-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|},

where
C
depends only on
\left|F\right|
,
\left|V\right|
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
d
.

Remark 3.2
.

One may wonder if the result in Theorem
9
can be simply proved through a scaling argument instead of the long process presented in this section.
We point out that there is no affine scaling for general polytopes. Though one can still use a uniform scaling to map
K
into a “reference polytope” with characteristic size of
O(1)
,
and then perform a uniform scaling argument to derive
\left|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|\leq Ch_{K}^{-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|}
. However in this case the constant
C
will depend on the shape of
K
.

Remark 3.3
.

When
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
, the upper bound in Theorem
9
is obviously sharp, in the asymptotic order, according to a uniform scaling argument.
Unfortunately, when
h_{*}\ll h_{K}
, the upper bound in Lemma
8
is far from sharp, as can be easily seen by examining
d
-dimensional simplices.

When
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=1
, a sharp (in asymptotic order) upper bound
\left|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|\leq Ch_{*}^{-1}
has been proved in
[
14
]
.
However, the proof in
[
14
]
is highly technique and thus can hardly be generalized to cases with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|>1
.

The result in this section is mostly useful in the scenario when
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
and higher order derivative bounds are needed.
In Section 5, we shall further explore this through numerical experiments.

4
Geometric assumptions

According to Theorem
9
, for given dimension
d
and multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
the upper bound of the derivatives for Wachspress coordinates can be characterized solely by
\left|F\right|
,
\left|V\right|
,
h_{K}
and
h_{*}
.
In this section, we discuss conditions on
K
that ensure these characteristic quantities be bounded.

Let
\rho_{K}
be the radius of the largest inscribed ball in
K
and
R_{K}
be the radius of the smallest ball containing
K

(not to be confused with the circumsphere of simplices which must pass through all vertices).
Let
w_{K}
be the width of
K
, i.e. the minimal distance of two parallel supporting hyperplanes of
K
.
Here by “supporting hyperplane” we mean a
d-1
dimensional hyperplane whose intersection with
K
is a face of
K
.
An illustration in
2
-dimension is given in Figure
2
.

The relation of
\rho_{K}
,
R_{K}
,
w_{K}
and
h_{K}
has been studied thoroughly by the convex geometry community.
First, it is obvious that

2\rho_{K}\leq w_{K}\leq h_{K}\leq 2R_{K}.

The upper bounds of
R_{K}/h_{K}
and
w_{K}/\rho_{K}
are given by
the Jung’s theorem and the Steinhagen’s Theorem, respectively
[
1
,
8
,
10
,
21
,
40
]
.

Jung’s theorem
(see (2.6) in
[
8
]
). Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
h_{K}
and
R_{K}
satisfy

h_{K}\geq R_{K}\sqrt{\frac{2(d+1)}{d}}.

Steinhagen’s theorem
(see (1.1) in
[
1
]
). Let
K
be a simple, non-degenerate, convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
w_{K}
and
\rho_{K}
satisfy

\displaystyle w_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{K}(2d^{\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad

\displaystyle d\ \textrm{odd},

\displaystyle w_{K}

\displaystyle\leq 2(d+1)\rho_{K}/(d+2)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle d\ \textrm{even}.

Fig. 2:
A convex polygon is located between two parallel lines (supporting hyperplanes). The minimum distance between all pairs of parallel supporting hyperplanes is the width of the polygon.

Combining the above, one gets the following proposition.

Proposition 10
.

On
d
-dimensional simple, non-degenerate, convex polytopes, one has
h_{K}\approx R_{K}
and
w_{K}\approx\rho_{K}
.
That is, there exist positive constants
C_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, depending only on
d
such that

C_{1}R_{K}\leq h_{K}\leq C_{2}R_{K},\qquad C_{3}\rho_{K}\leq w_{k}\leq C_{4}\rho_{K}.

We then list some commonly used geometric assumptions on the shape of the polytope
K
and study their relations.
Constants in the following assumptions may depend on
d
but not on the shape of
K
.
For readers not familiar with the finite element language, we emphasize that these constants are not meant for one single polytope,
but for multiple polytopes with various shapes.

(H1)

There exists a positive constant
C_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h_{*}\geq C_{*}h_{K}
.

(H2)

(Minimum edge length) There exist a positive constant
D_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}|\geq D_{*}h_{K}
for any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in V
,
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{v}
.

(H3)

(Maximum vertex count) There exists a positive integer
N_{V}^{*}
such that
\left|V\right|\leq N_{V}^{*}.

(H4)

(Maximum facet count) There exists a positive integer
N_{F}^{*}
such that
\left|F\right|\leq N_{F}^{*}.

(H5)

There exists a positive constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that
Ch_{K}\leq w_{K}
.

According to Proposition
10
, it is not hard to see that Assumption
(H5)
is equivalent to

(H5’)

(Chunkiness parameter) There exists a positive constant
\gamma^{*}
, such that

\frac{h_{K}}{\rho_{K}}\leq\gamma^{*}.

Remark 4.1
.

For convex polytopes which are always star-shaped with respect to its inscribed ball, Assumption
(H5’)
is just the well-known
chunkiness parameter assumption

in the finite element literature (see definition 4.2.16 in
[
2
]
).

We now state an important observation.

Lemma 11
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-degenerate simple convex polytope, then

\textbf{(H1)}\Rightarrow\textbf{(H2)}\Rightarrow\textbf{(H3)}\Leftrightarrow\textbf{(H4)}\quad and\quad\textbf{(H1)}\Rightarrow\textbf{(H5)}\Leftrightarrow\textbf{(H5')}.

Proof.

We first prove
(H1)

\Rightarrow

(H2)
. It is clear that for any facet
f\in F_{\boldsymbol{v}}
that does not contain
\boldsymbol{u}
(such an
f
does exist), one has

|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}|\geq|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{f}|=h_{f}(\boldsymbol{u})\geq h_{*}\geq C_{*}h_{K}.

(H2)

\Rightarrow

(H3)
has been proved, for
2
-dimension, in Proposition 4 of
[
19
]
.
It is straight forward to extend to arbitrary dimensions. For reader’s convenience, we present it below.
Let
\{B(\boldsymbol{v},\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2})\}_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}
be the set of open balls centered at each vertex

\boldsymbol{v}\in V
and with radius
\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2}
. By Assumption
(H2)
, the balls are disjoint.
By the definition of
R_{K}
, there exists a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
such that
K\subset\overline{B(\boldsymbol{x},R_{K})}
. One has

\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V}B(\boldsymbol{v},\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2})\subset B(\boldsymbol{x},R_{K}+\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2}).

Comparing the volume of the balls gives

\left|V\right|\left(\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2}\right)^{d}\leq\left(R_{K}+\frac{D_{*}h_{K}}{2}\right)^{d}.

Using the Jung’s theorem, we arrive at

\left|V\right|\leq\left(\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}}+D_{*}}{D_{*}}\right)^{d}.

Next, we prove
(H3)

\Leftrightarrow

(H4)
. For
d
-dimensional simple polytopes, one clearly has
\left|F\right|\leq d\left|V\right|
. Thus
(H3)

\Rightarrow

(H4)
.
By setting
j=0
in Theorem 18.1 of
[
3
]
, one gets
(H3)

\Leftarrow

(H4)
.

Finally, we prove
(H1)

\Rightarrow

(H5)
.
By (
3.5
), we know that the volume of
K
satisfies

vol(K)\geq\frac{1}{d!}|{\rm det}(E_{\boldsymbol{v}})|\geq\frac{1}{d!}h_{*}^{d}\geq\frac{C_{*}^{d}}{d!}h_{K}^{d},

(4.1)

where the last step follows from Assumption
(H1)
.
By the definition of
h_{K}
and
w_{K}
, one can always fit a polytope
K
in a hypercube with size
[0,h_{K}]^{d-1}\times[0,w_{K}]
. Hence

vol(K)\leq w_{K}h_{K}^{d-1}.

(4.2)

Combining (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) gives Assumption
(H5)
.

Since
(H5)
is equivalent to
(H5’)
, this completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Assumptions
(H5)
and
(H5’)
exclude “thin” polytopes, while Assumption
(H1)
is stronger.
An illustration is given in Fig.

When
K
is a non-degenerate, convex polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, more geometric assumptions can be considered:

(H6)

(Maximum interior angle) There exists
\alpha^{*}<\pi
such that all interior angles of
K
are bounded above by
\alpha^{*}
.

(H7)

(Minimum interior angle) There exists
\alpha_{*}>0
such that all interior angles of
K
are bounded below by
\alpha_{*}
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
K
be a non-degenerate, convex polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In addition to the relations presented in Lemma
11
, one has

\textbf{(H6)}\Rightarrow\textbf{(H3)},\qquad\textbf{(H5')}\Rightarrow\textbf{(H7)},

and

\textbf{(H1)}\Leftrightarrow\bigg(\textbf{(H2)}+\textbf{(H6)}+\textbf{(H7)}\bigg).

Proof.

(H6)

\Rightarrow

(H3)
and
(H5’)

\Rightarrow

(H7)
have been proved in
[
19
]
.
It has been proved in Corollary 2.4 of
[
14
]
that

\bigg(\textbf{(H2)}+\textbf{(H6)}+\textbf{(H7)}\bigg)\Rightarrow\textbf{(H1)}.

Combining the above with Lemma
11
, one only needs to prove
(H1)

\Rightarrow

(H6)
.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
the vertices of
K
, ordered counter-clockwisely. Let
f_{i}
be the edge pointing from veterx
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
to vertex
\boldsymbol{v}_{i+1}
.
Let
\theta_{i}
be the interior angle at vertex
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
. Since
K
is non-degenerate and convex, we have
\theta_{i}<\pi
.
Moreover,

h_{K}\sin\theta_{i}\geq|\boldsymbol{v}_{i+1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}|\sin\theta_{i}=h_{f_{i-1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i+1})\geq h_{*}\geq C_{*}h_{K}.

Hence
\sin\theta_{i}\geq C_{*}>0
, which imples both
(H6)
and
(H7)
.
∎

From Lemma
11
, we know that Assumption
(H1)
ensures that
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
and
\left|F\right|
,
\left|V\right|
being bounded,
which implies that Theorem
9
holds.

5.1
Can one easily obtain high quality polygonal meshes with
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
?

The answer is definitely yes. Consider the centroidal Voronoi tessellations (CVTs)
[
9
]
(see Fig.
4
).
All polygons in a CVT mesh are non-degenerate and convex.
By definition, it is not hard to prove that a quasi-uniform CVT polygonal mesh must satisfy maximum interior angle condition
(H6)

and the minimum interior angle condition
(H7)
. Then, according to Lemma
12
, condition
(H1)
holds as long as condition
(H2)
holds,
i.e.,
h_{*}=O(h_{K})
as long as there is no “short” edge in the mesh. There is no built-in mechanism in CVT to prevent the existence of “short” edges.
However, we can easily “eliminate” the “short” edges from a CVT, by simply shrink a “short” edge into a vertex.

In practice, we first generate a CVT mesh with characteristic mesh size
h
using the code
polymesher
written by Talischi et al.
[
33
]
,
and then check each edge in the mesh. If the edge has length less than
0.15h
, we simply remove one end point of the edge, so that the “short” edge vanishes.
The result turns out to be satisfying, as one can see from Fig.
4
that
\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}
is now controlled in a reasonable range of
[0.1335,\,0.6681]
.
Another interesting fact about CVT, which can be observed in Fig.
4
, is that it appears to consist of mostly hexagons.

As a comparison, we also examine
\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}
and the distribution of
n
-gons of
10000
random convex polygons. “Short” edges are not eliminated this time.
In a test run presented in Fig.

, the majority of polygons has
\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}\in[0.0024,\,0.6810]
,
with only one polygon having
\frac{h_{*}}{h_{K}}=6.3552e-04
.
The distribution of
n
-gons appears to be a normal distribution with respect to
n
.

5.4
When does one need a higher (
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|>1
) derivative bound?

The present research was motivated by the application of GBC-based FEM to fourth-order elliptic equations.
Let
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)
.
Consider the clamped plate bending problem: Find
u\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
such that for all
v\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta u\Delta v+(1-\sigma)(2\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial y}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x\partial y}-\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial y^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}})\right)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(5.1)

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a transverse force and
0<\sigma<\frac{1}{2}
is the Poisson ratio.
One may also consider the simply supported plate model, with a little modification on the boundary conditions.
In
[
34
]
, we proposed a Morley-type, GBC-based polygonal FEM for Problem (
5.1
).

We use the second order GBC-based element constructed by Floater and Lai
[
16
]
to define the local finite element space,
while the degrees of freedom are Morley-type, i.e., consisting of the nodal value at each vertex and the momentum of normal derivative on each edge.
The element constructed in
[
34
]
is a natural extension of the Morley element
[
26
]
to polygonal meshes.
To analyze its FEM approximation error, one needs the upper bound of
\nabla^{2}\phi_{i}
.

In
[
34
]
, we were not able to prove this upper bound at that time, but making it an assumption.
Under this assumption,
optimal FEM approximation error
|u-u_{h}|_{2,\Omega}=O(h)
and
|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}=O(h^{2})
are proved in
[
34
]
.
The present work fills this missing theoretical step.

For reader’s convenience, we attach below some numerical results from
[
34
]
.
Consider the clamped plate with exact solution
u(x,y)=x^{2}y^{2}(1-x)^{2}(1-y)^{2}
,
u|_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}|_{\partial\Omega}\equiv 0
,
and the simply supported plate with exact solution
u(x,y)=e^{x}\sin(2\pi x)\sin(2\pi y)
,
u|_{\partial\Omega}\equiv 0,\frac{\partial u}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}|_{\partial\Omega}\not\equiv 0
.
We test the problems on five different types of meshes, as shown in Fig.
22
.
Numerical results are reported in Fig.
23
-
24
, where
one observes an
O(h)
convergence in the
H^{2}
-seminorm and an
O(h^{2})
convergence in the
H^{1}
-seminorm.
Both agree well with the theoretical prediction.
