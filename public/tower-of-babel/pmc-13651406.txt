Associations between smoking, tobacco types, and depression in middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals: a cross-sectional analysis from the CHARLS study
Authors: Guozhong Chen, Hongmiao Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveDepression is a major public health issue with a profound impact on quality of life and mortality. Tobacco smoke is a leading risk factor for various health conditions. However, the relationship between smoking, types of tobacco and depression remains underexplored, especially in middle-aged and elderly populations in China. This study mainly examines the associations between smoking status, types of tobacco products, and depression in middle-aged and elderly individuals in China.MethodsData were derived from all five waves (2011–2020) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) in a pooled cross-sectional design. A total of 34,215 observations from adults aged 45 years and older were analysed. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), with a score of 10 or higher indicating elevated depressive symptoms. Smoking status was categorized into current smokers, ex-smokers, and non-smokers, and tobacco types included conventional cigarettes, self-rolled cigarettes, pipes, and others (cigars and hookahs). Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of smoking and tobacco type with depressive symptoms. Generalized estimating equations and mixed-effects models were used as sensitivity analyses to account for repeated observations from the same individuals.ResultsThe prevalence of depressive symptoms (CES-D-10 ≥ 10) was 34.06%. Current smokers had an OR of 1.25 (95% CI: 1.15, 1.35; p < 0.001) and ex-smokers an OR of 1.15 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.25; p < 0.001) relative to non-smokers. Among tobacco types, self-rolled cigarette smokers (OR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.35, 1.74; p < 0.001) and pipe smokers (OR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.35, 1.81; p < 0.001) showed the strongest associations, followed by users of cigars and hookahs (OR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.15, 1.68; p < 0.001) and conventional cigarettes (OR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.19; p = 0.010).ConclusionsIn this middle-aged and elderly Chinese population, smoking and all examined tobacco types were associated with a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms, with the strongest associations observed for self-rolled and pipe tobacco. Because the design is cross-sectional, these findings indicate associations rather than causal effects, and underscore the need to integrate mental-health considerations into tobacco-control efforts, including attention to traditional and non-cigarette tobacco products.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13011-026-00750-5.

Introduction

Depression is a major public health concern, affecting approximately 350 million individuals globally [1]. Characterized by persistent low mood, depression significantly contributes to the global disease burden, consuming extensive medical and social resources and markedly reducing social behavior and quality of life. Additionally, depression is a leading cause of non-fatal health loss, disability, and mortality worldwide [2]. The development of depression is recognized as a multifaceted process influenced by genetic, psychological, environmental, and biological factors [1, 3, 4]. Tobacco smoke has been identified as the second leading risk factor for mortality worldwide [5]. Tobacco-related deaths already number in the millions each year [6]. The burden falls increasingly on low- and middle-income countries, where tobacco-related deaths are projected to rise over the coming decades [7]. China has one of the countries with the highest male smoking rates, with over 300 million smokers and 740 million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke [8]. Despite mounting evidence suggesting smoking may lead to mental health issues, the relationship between smoking and depression risk remains contentious.

A study employing Mendelian randomization (MR) indicated that smoking is associated with an increased risk of depression [9]. Smoking and alcohol consumption, as two major health risk factors contributing to morbidity and premature mortality, are closely linked to depression both in the United States and globally [10, 11]. In a cross-sectional study by Boden et al., data on nicotine dependence and depressive symptoms were collected from approximately 1,000 New Zealand residents [12]. The study found that nicotine dependence could predict future depressive episodes. Additionally, a systematic review by Park and Romer reported significant associations between smoking and depression in 47 out of 57 retrospective studies on adolescents [13]. Contrarily, a systematic review of mental health professionals’ attitudes towards smoking and cessation among mental illness patients, encompassing 38 small studies with 16,369 participants, suggested that smoking could alleviate certain mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and stress, while cessation could exacerbate these symptoms [14, 15]. Furthermore, the health impact of non-cigarette combustible products such as cigars and pipes has drawn growing attention [16], and their consumption has risen even as cigarette use has declined [17]. These products are not harmless: cigar smoking causes cancers of the oral cavity, larynx, oesophagus, and lung, and cigar smokers face cardiovascular and pulmonary risks comparable to those of cigarette smokers [18, 19].

Gender, age, and racial/ethnic differences in smoking and depression have been extensively documented. Guo and Yan, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), found significant associations between smoking and the incidence and progression of depression among Americans [9]. An and Xiang further confirmed a positive correlation between smoking and depression in middle-aged and elderly Americans using NHANES data [20]. The potential link between smoking and depression is crucial for health promotion interventions in middle-aged and elderly populations. However, large-scale evidence on this question in middle-aged and older Chinese adults remains scarce. In particular, it is unclear whether the association with depressive symptoms differs across tobacco product types (such as the self-rolled cigarettes and pipe tobacco that remain common in this population) rather than reflecting cigarette smoking alone.

Given the potential significance of the relationship between smoking, tobacco types, and depression for health promotion interventions among middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals, we conducted a study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). This study investigated the relationship between smoking, tobacco types, and depression among middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals, providing a new data point to the literature.

Methods

Study participants

Data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey. CHARLS employs a multi-stage probability sampling strategy with proportional sampling to size, covering 150 counties across 28 provinces, cities, and autonomous regions in China [21].

The CHARLS baseline survey was conducted in 2011, with national follow-up waves in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The 2020 wave was fielded during the COVID-19 pandemic and, at the time of analysis, was the most recent publicly released national wave of CHARLS. The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Peking University Ethics Review Committee, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

In this pooled cross-sectional analysis, we drew on data from all five CHARLS waves. Because CHARLS re-interviews the same individuals over time, a given participant could contribute observations to more than one wave. Accordingly, the unit of analysis was the observation (person-wave) rather than the unique individual. From 87,731 observations of adults in the participating waves, we restricted the sample to those aged 45 years or older and excluded 8,089 observations with unavailable smoking or tobacco-type data, 945 observations with missing depression data, and 44,482 observations with missing covariate data, yielding a final analytic sample of 34,215 observations (Fig. 1).

Covariates

To account for potential confounding in the smoking–depression association, we a priori selected covariates that are plausibly related to both smoking behaviour and depressive symptoms. These were grouped into three domains. (1) Sociodemographic factors: age (continuous, years), sex (male/female), marital status (married versus non-married, the latter comprising never-married, separated, divorced, and widowed participants), and residence (rural versus urban). (2) Health behaviour: drinking status (yes versus no). (3) Chronic conditions, each coded as present versus absent on the basis of self-reported physician diagnosis: hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, and stomach disease. Adjusting for these variables addresses confounding by socioeconomic position, lifestyle, and comorbid physical illness, each of which is plausibly associated with both tobacco use and depressive symptoms.

Exposure factors

In each wave, smoking behaviour was assessed within the CHARLS health-status module (items DA059, DA061, and DA060). Participants were first asked whether they had ever smoked tobacco (for example, manufactured cigarettes, hand-rolled tobacco, pipe tobacco, cigars, or water pipe). Those who reported ever smoking were then asked whether they still smoked or had quit, allowing classification into three mutually exclusive groups: current smokers (still smoking at the time of the survey), ex-smokers (had smoked previously but had quit), and non-smokers (never smoked). Current and former smokers were further asked which type of tobacco product they (had) used. Responses were grouped into conventional cigarettes (both filtered and unfiltered manufactured cigarettes), self-rolled (hand-rolled) cigarettes, pipe tobacco, and other products (cigars and water pipe/hookah). The same items were administered in each wave. For participants contributing more than one observation, smoking status and tobacco type were taken from the corresponding wave for each observation.

Outcome factors

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) [22]. The CES-D-10 is scored from 0 to 30, with 10 items rated on a four-point Likert scale (0 = rarely or none of the time (< 1 day); 1 = some or a little of the time (1–2 days); 2 = occasionally or a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days); 3 = most or all of the time (5–7 days)) [23]. The items “I felt hopeful about the future” and “I was happy” were reverse scored. Participants with a CES-D-10 score of 10 or higher were classified as having elevated depressive symptoms. This validated screening threshold indicates a positive screen for depressive symptoms and does not constitute a clinical diagnosis of depression [23].

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3). Continuous variables were winsorized, with values below the 2.5th percentile and above the 97.5th percentile set to the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, respectively. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (x ± s), and categorical variables were reported as percentages (n [%]). The two-sample t-test and the χ² test were used to analyze continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Variables not normally distributed were expressed as medians (P25, P75), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to analyze differences between depression and non-depression groups.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of smoking status and tobacco type with depressive symptoms. The crude model was unadjusted; Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, residence, marital status, and drinking status; and Model 2 was further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, and stomach disease.

Stratified analyses were performed by sex (male vs. female), residential areas (rural vs. urban), marital status (married vs. non-married), drinking status (yes vs. no), hypertension (yes vs. no), diabetes (yes vs. no), liver diseases (yes vs. no), kidney diseases (yes vs. no), and stomach diseases (yes vs. no).

Several sensitivity analyses were performed. CES-D-10 was modelled as a continuous outcome using linear regression. Second, to account for the within-individual correlation arising from participants who contributed observations to more than one wave, the fully adjusted models were re-estimated using (i) generalized estimating equations with a binomial distribution, a logit link, and an exchangeable working correlation structure clustered on participant ID, and (ii) mixed-effects logistic regression with a random intercept for participant ID. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics

In this pooled cross-sectional analysis, 34,215 observations with complete data were included, with a mean age of 59.30 ± 9.95 years, 18,420 (53.84%) from women, 20,178 (58.97%) from rural residents, and 29,997 (87.67%) from married participants. Among all observations, 21,117 (61.72%) were from non-smokers, 5,832 (17.05%) from ex-smokers, and 7,266 (21.24%) from current smokers. Regarding tobacco type, 10,254 (29.97%) involved conventional cigarettes, 1,336 (3.90%) self-rolled cigarettes, 978 (2.86%) pipe tobacco, and 530 (1.55%) other types (cigars and hookahs). A total of 11,652 observations (34.06%) reflected elevated depressive symptoms (CES-D-10 ≥ 10). Significant differences (p < 0.050) were observed between observations with and without elevated depressive symptoms in age, sex, marital status, residence, smoking status, tobacco type, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, and stomach disease (Table 1).

Association between smoking and depression

The prevalence of depressive symptoms was significantly higher among current smokers and ex-smokers than among non-smokers (p < 0.001; Table 2). In Model 1 (adjusted for age, sex, residence, marital status, and drinking status), ex-smokers (OR: 1.13; 95% CI: 1.04, 1.23; p = 0.001) and current smokers (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.17, 1.36; p < 0.001) had higher odds of depressive symptoms. These associations persisted in Model 2, which additionally adjusted for chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, and stomach disease), with ORs of 1.15 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.25; p < 0.001) for ex-smokers and 1.25 (95% CI: 1.15, 1.35; p < 0.001) for current smokers (Table 2).

Association between cigarette type and depression

Significantly higher prevalence of depressive symptoms was observed for all tobacco types relative to non-smokers. In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), the odds of depressive symptoms were 1.10 times higher for conventional cigarette smokers (95% CI: 1.02, 1.19; p = 0.010), 1.53 times higher for self-rolled cigarette smokers (95% CI: 1.35, 1.74; p < 0.001), 1.56 times higher for pipe smokers (95% CI: 1.35, 1.81; p < 0.001), and 1.39 times higher for cigar and hookah users (95% CI: 1.15, 1.68; p < 0.001), compared with non-smokers (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Stratified analyses by age, sex, BMI, residence, marital status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, and stomach disease showed broadly consistent associations across subgroups. In particular, the associations of smoking status and tobacco type with depressive symptoms did not differ significantly by sex, and no significant effect modification was detected for the other stratifying variables (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

We further investigated the association between smoking status, types of tobacco use, and CES-D-10 scores, with results consistent across analyses. After full adjustment for covariates, compared to non-smokers, ex-smokers had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 0.48 points (95% CI: 0.27, 0.70; p < 0.001), while current smokers had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 0.61 points (95% CI: 0.40, 0.82; p < 0.001). Regarding the types of tobacco use, after full adjustment for covariates, compared to non-smokers, conventional cigarette users had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 0.33 points (95% CI: 0.13, 0.52; p < 0.001). Self-rolled cigarette users had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 1.17 points (95% CI: 0.82, 1.52; p < 0.001), and pipe cigarette users had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 1.55 points (95% CI: 1.15, 1.95; p < 0.001). Users of other forms of tobacco, such as cigars and hookahs, had an average CES-D-10 score higher by 0.92 points (95% CI: 0.40, 1.44; p < 0.001). These continuous-outcome estimates are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

To address the within-individual correlation introduced by the pooled design, the fully adjusted models were re-estimated using generalized estimating equations and mixed-effects logistic regression. The direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of all associations were preserved under both approaches: self-rolled and pipe tobacco continued to show the strongest associations with depressive symptoms, and all smoking categories and tobacco types remained significantly associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms relative to non-smoking (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between smoking status, types of tobacco use, and depression using cross-sectional data from a representative Chinese population. This is the first large-sample observational study to analyze the relationship between smoking status, types of tobacco use, and depression in middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals.

Our findings indicate that both current smokers and ex-smokers have a significantly higher risk of depression compared to non-smokers, with the risk being higher among current smokers. Regarding the types of tobacco use, we observed that all types of tobacco users have a significantly higher risk of depression compared to non-smokers. Specifically, although every tobacco type was associated with significantly higher risk than non-smoking, the association was strongest for pipe and self-rolled cigarette users. Additionally, in stratified analyses, we did not observe significant interactions between age, sex, BMI, residential area, alcohol consumption, chronic disease, and smoking status in relation to the risk of depression. Furthermore, our sensitivity analyses confirmed that smoking status and types of tobacco use were associated with higher CES-D-10 scores, further validating our findings.

The relationship between smoking and mental health conditions remains controversial. Individuals with mental health issues might perceive smoking as a means to alleviate stress and anxiety. Some report that traditional smoking cessation interventions do not address their mental health concerns [15]. However, there is evidence suggesting that continued smoking exacerbates mental health conditions, while smoking cessation can improve mental health. A meta-analysis of smokers indicated that quitting smoking is associated with reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as improvements in mood and quality of life [24].

Our results clearly demonstrate a positive correlation between smoking status and the risk of depression. This aligns with previous findings by Guo et al., which showed that both former and current smokers have an increased risk of depression in a US population [9]. The mechanisms underlying this relationship are still under investigation. Some scholars suggest that smoking-induced anxiety or depression may be mediated by its effects on neural circuits, increasing sensitivity to environmental stressors. In animal models, prolonged nicotine exposure disrupts the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to excessive cortisol secretion and alterations in the monoaminergic neurotransmitter system, which regulates responses to stressors [25]. These effects appear to normalize after nicotine withdrawal. Although the risk of depression is lower in ex-smokers than in current smokers, it remains higher than in those who have never been exposed to nicotine, possibly due to the long-term neurotoxic effects of nicotine [26]. While the short-term pharmacological effects of nicotine involve brain stimulation and relief from stress, anxiety, and depression, long-term nicotine use can lead to addiction, mental and psychological harm, increased sensitivity and vulnerability, and a heightened susceptibility to developing depression or anxiety [26].

Although recent years have seen an increased focus on the health risks of tobacco use globally, relatively few studies have analyzed the possible differences in the effects of various types of tobacco. Even in large epidemiological studies, most research focuses on cigarette smoking, leaving the impacts of other tobacco types underexplored. Christensen et al. found that cigarette smokers had a higher risk of dying from tobacco-related cancers compared to cigar and pipe smokers [19]. Similarly, a study by Primack et al. on the effects of smoking on mental health among college students showed a very strong relationship between cigarette smoking and mental health variables, but only a moderate relationship between smoking and hookah use [27].

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to explore the relationship between smoking type and depression in the middle-aged and elderly population in China. Every tobacco type examined, including conventional manufactured cigarettes, was associated with significantly higher odds of depressive symptoms than non-smoking, so no form of tobacco use appeared benign. What differed was the magnitude of the association, which was greatest for self-rolled and pipe tobacco. Several non-exclusive explanations are plausible. First, self-rolled and pipe products are typically unfiltered and may deliver higher concentrations of nicotine, tar, and other combustion toxicants, and they are often smoked with deeper or more prolonged inhalation, all of which could amplify the neurobiological pathways linking tobacco exposure to mood disturbance. Second, in China these traditional products are used disproportionately by older, rural, and socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals (groups that also carry a higher burden of depressive symptoms), so residual confounding by socioeconomic position and related exposures may contribute to the stronger associations despite covariate adjustment. Third, differences in smoking intensity, nicotine dependence, and cessation patterns across product types may also play a role. Because these mechanisms cannot be disentangled in cross-sectional data, the product-specific differences should be interpreted as associations rather than as evidence that any product is comparatively safe.

Our stratified analyses showed that the associations between smoking, tobacco type, and depressive symptoms were broadly similar across subgroups, including by sex. This contrasts with some studies in Western populations that have reported stronger smoking-depression associations in one sex. The absence of significant sex differences here may partly reflect the very low prevalence of smoking among women in this cohort, which limits statistical power to detect effect modification by sex and concentrates the female sample among non-smokers. The consistency of the associations across age, residence, drinking status, and chronic-disease strata nonetheless suggests that the link between tobacco use and depressive symptoms is broadly generalisable within this middle-aged and elderly Chinese population.

These findings carry several implications for policymakers and health professionals. First, because smokers of every product type showed elevated depressive symptoms, tobacco-control and mental-health services should be integrated rather than addressed in isolation: cessation programmes for middle-aged and older adults should incorporate routine screening for depressive symptoms, and mental-health care for this population should include assessment of tobacco use. Second, the markedly stronger associations for self-rolled and pipe tobacco highlight the need to extend tobacco-control efforts beyond manufactured cigarettes to traditional and non-cigarette products, which remain common in rural China yet are often overlooked in surveillance and cessation messaging. Third, clinicians should recognise that the mental-health burden associated with smoking is not confined to heavy cigarette use, and that users of traditional products, who are frequently older and rural, may warrant particular attention.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Smoking, depressive symptoms, and other variables were based on participant self-report, which may introduce information bias, and depressive symptoms were measured with a screening scale (CES-D-10) rather than a diagnostic interview. Second, the design was a pooled cross-sectional analysis, so the associations cannot be interpreted as causal and the temporal ordering of smoking and depressive symptoms cannot be established. Third, because the same individuals could contribute observations to more than one wave, the observations are not fully independent, and conventional logistic regression may underestimate standard errors. To address this, we re-estimated the fully adjusted models using generalized estimating equations and mixed-effects logistic regression, which account for within-individual correlation and yielded materially unchanged results. Fourth, we did not examine dose-response relationships, because consistent data on smoking intensity, duration, and quantity (e.g., cigarettes per day) were not available across waves. This is an important direction for future research. Fifth, the 2020 wave was collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period of heightened psychological distress that may have influenced the prevalence of depressive symptoms. We nonetheless included all available waves, and the 2020 wave represents the most recent publicly released CHARLS data. Finally, residual confounding by unmeasured factors, such as socioeconomic status and other lifestyle exposures, cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In this study, both former and current smoking were associated with a higher prevalence of elevated depressive symptoms than never smoking, and every tobacco type examined was significantly associated with depressive symptoms. The associations were strongest for self-rolled cigarettes and pipes; no tobacco type was free of association, so these findings should not be interpreted as indicating that any product is safe. The key message for policymakers and health professionals is that tobacco-control efforts in this population should be coupled with routine screening for depressive symptoms, and that prevention and cessation messaging should extend beyond conventional cigarettes to traditional products such as self-rolled cigarettes and pipes. Future longitudinal studies that capture smoking intensity, duration, and quantity are needed to establish temporality, test dose-response relationships, and clarify the mechanisms underlying these associations.
