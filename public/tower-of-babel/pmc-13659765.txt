Combined interleukin-1 blockade for central nervous system inflammation in children with severe cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes
Authors: Özlem Satirer, Anton Hospach, Nadiya Parczyk, Susanne M. Benseler, Jasmin B. Kuemmerle-Deschner
Published: 2026

Cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS) are rare NLRP3-mediated autoinflammatory disorders driven by excessive interleukin-1 (IL-1) signalling [1]. Central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, including aseptic meningitis, papilledema and sensorineural hearing loss, characterises severe CAPS and can cause irreversible damage [2]. Although both anakinra and canakinumab inhibit IL-1, anakinra is often preferred for CNS disease because of superior blood-brain barrier penetration; cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflammation may persist under canakinumab [3,4]. In Europe, anakinra and canakinumab are licensed for CAPS [5,6]; rilonacept is US Food and Drug Administration (US FDA)-approved for familial cold autoinflammatory syndrome/Muckle-Wells syndrome (FCAS/MWS), and its prescribing information advises against concomitant administration with another IL-1 blocker [7]. Severe CNS disease may require anakinra escalation up to 8 mg/kg/d, sometimes twice daily, with up to 10 mg/kg/d reported in expert cohorts [5,6,8,9]. High-dose daily anakinra increases injection volume, and prolonged high-dose exposure has recently been associated with drug-derived systemic amyloidosis [10]. Concurrent anakinra and canakinumab is used empirically by some experts, but published evidence is lacking. We reported 3 children in whom low-dose anakinra was added to optimised canakinumab because anakinra escalation was not feasible.

Patients with severe CAPS and active CNS inflammation—CSF pleocytosis, papilledema or abnormal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan—persisting despite optimised canakinumab were identified from a prospective cohort [11]. Following ethics approval (No 040/2025BO2), anakinra was added to ongoing canakinumab. Response was assessed using clinical features, Physician Global Assessment (PGA) scale, serum amyloid A (SAA) level, CSF cell counts, brain MRI scan, and ophthalmological and audiological assessments [2,3,8].

Three children (1 girl and 2 boys) with severe CAPS and pathogenic or likely pathogenic NLRP3 variants were included. Initially, all received anakinra 2 mg/kg/d for a median of 4 months. None was refractory: treatment was switched to canakinumab because of severe injection-site reactions and intolerance of daily injections, not lack of efficacy. CNS and systemic disease activity subsequently emerged or recurred. Canakinumab was optimised by interval shortening and/or dose escalation, but CNS inflammation remained inadequately controlled (Table). Anakinra escalation to 3 to 8 mg/kg/d was considered but not pursued because greater injection volumes were expected to aggravate the reactions that had limited adherence; recently described amyloidosis after prolonged high-dose anakinra was an additional safety consideration [10]. Low-dose anakinra was therefore reintroduced with optimised canakinumab: 2 mg/kg/d in patients 1 and 3 and 2 mg/kg every other day in patient 2, with canakinumab 4 to 6 mg/kg every 4 to 8 weeks (Table). PGA scale score decreased to remission-level activity, SAA level fell markedly, and CSF cell pleocytosis improved in all patients, normalising in 2 and falling to 4 cells/µL in one (Fig, A,B). Inner-ear contrast enhancement resolved in patient 3 (Fig, C). Adding low-dose anakinra to optimised canakinumab controlled CNS inflammation that had persisted under canakinumab alone; to our knowledge, this is the first published description of combined IL-1 blockade in CAPS, although the approach has been used empirically. Our observations do not establish superiority over adequately escalated anakinra monotherapy, which remains the licensed and guideline-supported option for severe CAPS with neurological disease and should be pursued first [5,6,9]. Concurrent anakinra plus canakinumab is off-label and neither established nor guideline-recommended; here it was an individualised rescue strategy because anakinra escalation was not feasible. Drug-derived anakinra amyloidosis has been described after prolonged high-dose exposure [10], but whether lower cumulative exposure reduces this risk is unknown. Injection-site surveillance, site rotation, and monitoring for proteinuria remain essential [5,6,10]. The long-term safety of dual IL-1 blockade, including potentially additive immunosuppression, is unknown; 3 children followed for 7 to 18 months cannot establish safety. Pooling and sharing of concurrent clinical observations and prospective registry-based studies comparing combination therapy with optimised anakinra monotherapy are needed.
