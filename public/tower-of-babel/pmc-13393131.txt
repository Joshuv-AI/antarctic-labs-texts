Rebound Pain and Patient Satisfaction With Dexamethasone as an Adjunct for Peripheral Nerve Blocks. A Substudy Based on Two Randomised Clinical Trials
Authors: Laura B. Madsen, Jakob H. Andersen, Anders K. Nørskov, Janus C. Jakobsen, Ole Mathiesen, Mathias Maagaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeripheral nerve blocks (PNBs) are a cornerstone in postoperative pain management, reducing pain, opioid consumption, and opioid‐related adverse effects. However, the duration of PNBs is limited and might result in rebound pain after block cessation. Dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid, when administered in addition to PNBs, prolongs block duration and might mitigate rebound pain. We assessed the effect of adjunct dexamethasone on pain after block cessation and rebound pain.MethodsThis is a substudy pooling data from two randomised, placebo‐controlled three‐arm clinical trials conducted between June 9, 2022 and May 17, 2023. Participants underwent osseous surgery of either the lower or upper limb under peripheral nerve block. Participants were allocated to dexamethasone (12 mg oral, 24 mg oral, or 12 mg intravenous) or placebo (oral or intravenous). The primary outcome was pain immediately after block cessation (Numerical Rating Scale, NRS, 0 to 10) and rebound pain (NRS ≥ 7 immediately after block cessation).ResultsData from 159 participants were included. Fifty‐six participants received placebo and 103 participants received dexamethasone. The median pain score after block cessation was 5.0 points (IQR 3.0 to 8.0) in the placebo group and 3.0 points (IQR 1.0 to 6.0) in the dexamethasone group. Rebound pain occurred in 20 of 56 participants (35.7%) in the placebo group and in 19 out of 103 participants (18.4%) in the dexamethasone group. Dexamethasone significantly reduced pain after block cessation (mean difference: −1.7 points, 95% CI: −2.6 to −0.8, p < 0.001) and the risk of rebound pain (risk ratio: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.30 to 0.88, p = 0.014) when compared to placebo. We found no association between patient satisfaction and rebound pain. However, the satisfaction measure exhibited a ceiling effect with most participants reporting maximum satisfaction and therefore the ability to detect a difference may have been limited.ConclusionsDexamethasone reduced pain after block cessation and the risk of rebound pain when compared with placebo.Editorial CommentThis study combined data from two randomised controlled trials, which included patients who underwent osseous surgery on either the upper or lower limb under peripheral nerve block. The trials investigated rebound pain and patient satisfaction. When administered intravenously or orally, dexamethasone reduces pain after block cessation and the occurrence of rebound pain. No significant difference in satisfaction scores was found between patients who received systemic dexamethasone and those who received a placebo.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve blocks (PNBs) have become integral to multimodal analgesic strategies for managing postoperative pain. The use of PNBs is associated with reduced postoperative pain, opioid consumption and opioid‐related adverse effects [1, 2]. Moreover, PNBs have been shown to enhance patient satisfaction following arthroscopic shoulder surgery compared to patients who did not receive a PNB [3].

However, the analgesic duration of PNBs is limited to 12 to 16 h for extremity blocks using long‐acting local anaesthetics [4, 5]. As the block wears off, patients may experience severe pain, a phenomenon that is termed ‘rebound pain’. Rebound pain can be defined as pain severity of ≥ 7 on the numerical rating scale (NRS) following block cessation [6, 7, 8] and has been negatively associated with patient satisfaction [9]. The exact mechanism behind rebound pain is not clear, but proposed mechanisms are unopposed nociceptive input in the absence of multimodal analgesia (i.e., paracetamol, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids), neuroinflammatory sensitisation and primary hyperalgesia uncovered after PNB resolution [10, 11, 12].

Numerous trials have investigated adjunct drugs to prolong the analgesic duration of PNBs [13, 14, 15]. Adjuncts can be administered either systemically or perineurally. Among these, dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine are the most promising [14]. Dexamethasone has been reported to prolong the duration of PNBs by up to 70% when used as an adjunct. Previous trials indicated that dexamethasone reduced the risk of rebound pain [8]. The potent anti‐inflammatory action of dexamethasone on neuroinflammatory sensitisation might be a mechanistic explanation unrelated to block prolongation, but the effect may also be mediated simply by block prolongation potentially explained by direct inhibition of nociceptive C‐fibres and inhibitory potassium channels [16, 17].

Prior trials used different definitions of rebound pain, resulting in considerable heterogeneity, limiting comparisons across trials [8]. Consequently, the effect of dexamethasone on rebound pain warranted further evaluation. We revisited and pooled data from two randomised clinical trials [18, 19]. By pooling data from two trials, we increased statistical power for evaluating the effects of dexamethasone on pain after block cessation and rebound pain using standardised definitions, but at the expense of increased statistical heterogeneity from differences in block type, route of administration and surgical population. However, we considered this trade‐off acceptable as both trials investigated similar perioperative analgesic strategies and assessed comparable outcomes, allowing assessment of pain after block cessation and rebound pain across a broader range of peripheral nerve blocks.

The primary aim and contribution of this substudy was to provide a more precise assessment of the effects of dexamethasone versus placebo on pain immediately after block cessation and rebound pain through pooled participant‐level analysis of two randomised trials using a uniform, standardised rebound pain definition, thereby complementing the individual trial reports and addressing limitations arising from heterogeneous rebound pain definitions in the existing literature.

Method

Study Design

This study was based on data from two randomised, blinded, placebo‐controlled, three‐arm trials [14, 18, 19]. The two trials were not originally protocolised to collect data on pain immediately after block cessation, rebound pain and patient satisfaction. We amended the protocol and applied for permission to collect data related to these outcomes. Consequently, we did not collect data on these outcomes before halfway through both trials. Therefore, there is missing data for both the active and the placebo groups in both trials.

The trial methodologies have been described in detail previously [18, 19, 20, 21]. Briefly, both trials were superiority trials with a 1:1:1 allocation between active and placebo arms. Randomisation was conducted by computer‐generated allocation sequences stratified by site with random blocks of variable sizes. Allocation concealment was ensured by online allocation in both trials. In ADJUNCT2, the allocation was concealed in opaque envelopes that were only opened by personnel outside the trial framework who were responsible for preparing the indistinguishable trial medication. In ADJUNCT1, trial medication was prepared by a pharmacy and packed in indistinguishable capsules. Participants, personnel, outcome assessors, and statisticians were blinded to allocation in both trials. Both trials were prospectively registered at clinicaltrials.gov and conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. The ADJUNCT1 trial was prospectively registered on April 19, 2021 (NCT04853446) and the ADJUNCT2 trial was prospectively registered on March 24, 2021 (NCT04818749) [20, 21]. Both trials were approved by the Committees on Biomedical Research Ethics in the Zealand Region of Denmark (ADJUNCT1 on May 5, 2021; ADJUNCT2 on May 6, 2021), the Danish Data Protection Agency and the Danish Medicines Agency prior to initiation. Protocol amendments for including data on pain after block cessation, rebound pain, and patient satisfaction were approved by the committees on June 9, 2022. The protocol for this substudy was retrospectively registered on October 17, 2024, at osf.io (Open Science Framework) after unblinded data became available [22]. However, we did not inspect data, nor perform any analyses prior to registering the protocol.

The ADJUNCT1 trial investigated the effects of oral dexamethasone on block duration following an infraclavicular nerve block with 30 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either oral placebo, 12 mg oral dexamethasone or 24 mg oral dexamethasone. All participants received multimodal analgesia with paracetamol, ibuprofen, and oxycodone as needed in addition to their PNB. The trial demonstrated that, compared to placebo (mean duration 14 h), oral dexamethasone prolonged the duration of analgesia by approximately 6 to 7 h in patients undergoing forearm or hand surgery. The difference between 12 and 24 mg oral dexamethasone was not significant [19].

The ADJUNCT2 trial investigated the effects of dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine on block duration following popliteal (sciatic) and saphenous nerve blocks with 20 mL and 10 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine, respectively. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either intravenous placebo, 12 mg intravenous dexamethasone, or 12 mg intravenous dexamethasone combined with 1 mcg/kg intravenous dexmedetomidine. All participants received multimodal analgesia with paracetamol, ibuprofen, and oxycodone as needed in addition to their PNBs. The trial showed that 12 mg dexamethasone, with or without 1 mcg/kg dexmedetomidine, when compared with placebo (median duration of analgesia 15 h), increased the duration of analgesia in patients undergoing foot or ankle surgery by 8 to 9 h. Notably, the prolongation of block duration was similar in the dexamethasone‐only and the dexamethasone‐plus‐dexmedetomidine groups [18].

There was no public involvement in the design of either trial.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the trials.

We did not use any artificial intelligence to generate content for this manuscript.

Data Collection

Data were collected through in‐person or telephone interviews based on a standardised study diary consisting of questions related to pain immediately after block cessation, rebound pain and patient satisfaction (see below). We instructed the participants to note their outcome measures as they occurred, that is, participants rated their pain immediately after block cessation and noted the result in the diary. In the ADJUNCT1 trial, participants were contacted at 24 and 48 h postoperatively. In the ADJUNCT2 trial, participants were contacted at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To be included in this study, participants were enrolled in either the ADJUNCT1 or ADJUNCT2 trial and had completed the questionnaire on pain immediately after block cessation, rebound pain, and patient satisfaction.

Statistical Analysis

To assess the effects of dexamethasone on pain immediately after block cessation, rebound pain and patient satisfaction, we pooled data from the two trials into two groups: DEXA: participants who received dexamethasone (i.e., oral dexamethasone 12 or 24 mg from ADJUNCT1; intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg or intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg with intravenous dexmedetomidine (1 mcg/kg) from ADJUNCT2).PLACEBO: participants who received placebo (i.e., oral placebo from ADJUNCT1 or intravenous placebo from ADJUNCT2).

DEXA: participants who received dexamethasone (i.e., oral dexamethasone 12 or 24 mg from ADJUNCT1; intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg or intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg with intravenous dexmedetomidine (1 mcg/kg) from ADJUNCT2).

DEXA: participants who received dexamethasone (i.e., oral dexamethasone 12 or 24 mg from ADJUNCT1; intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg or intravenous dexamethasone 12 mg with intravenous dexmedetomidine (1 mcg/kg) from ADJUNCT2).

PLACEBO: participants who received placebo (i.e., oral placebo from ADJUNCT1 or intravenous placebo from ADJUNCT2).

PLACEBO: participants who received placebo (i.e., oral placebo from ADJUNCT1 or intravenous placebo from ADJUNCT2).

We assessed the underlying distribution of continuous outcomes using raincloud plots. For continuous outcomes, we summarised within‐group results using means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges depending on the distribution of the data. For dichotomous and ordinal outcomes, we summarised within‐group data using proportions and percentages.

For all outcomes, we performed an overall analysis based on the aforementioned grouping in which we assessed if there was interaction between the treatment group (DEXA or PLACEBO) and the trial from which the data was collected (ADJUNCT1 vs. ADJUNCT2). This allowed us to assess whether potential treatment effects depended on the trial from which the participants were included. If there were signs of a significant interaction (p < 0.05), we reported the results from each trial separately, respecting the original three‐arm designs. Regardless of the test of interaction, the results of each trial were presented separately in the Supporting Information, respecting the three‐arm designs. We also performed sensitivity analyses for the primary outcomes in which participants receiving dexmedetomidine in addition to dexamethasone were excluded and we performed sensitivity analyses based on mixed‐effect models in which we modelled ‘intervention’ as a fixed‐effect and ‘study’ as a random‐effect.

For continuous outcomes, we primarily performed linear regression to assess between‐group differences. We assessed whether the underlying assumptions of linear regression were met (i.e., normally distributed residuals, constant variance of errors, low multicollinearity) using diagnostic plots. If the underlying assumptions were violated, we assessed between‐group differences using quantile regression.

For dichotomous outcomes, we assessed between‐group differences using log‐link binomial regression and we reported the results using risk ratios.

Ordinal outcomes (i.e., block duration too short, adequate, or too long) were assessed using proportional odds logistic regression. We reported the result as the probability of achieving each ordinal level for each allocation group.

We performed subgroup analyses for the primary outcomes based on the baseline characteristics: age (below 65 vs. 65 or above), sex (male vs. female), body mass index (below 25 vs. 25 or above), type of surgery (fracture vs. non‐fracture) and baseline pain (below 3 vs. 3 or above). A p‐value of 0.05 or less from the test of interaction was considered as a significant subgroup difference.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3 (R Core Team, 2026) with RStudio version 2024.04.2‐764 (Posit Team 2024) [23, 24].

Finally, 35 participants in the placebo group, 35 in the 12 mg group and 28 in the 24 mg dexamethasone group provided data for this substudy (Figure 1).

A total of 141 participants were excluded from this substudy: 95 who received dexamethasone and 46 who received placebo. Both trials had two arms receiving dexamethasone and one arm receiving placebo, why the distribution of participants was not equal. The missingness of outcome data was balanced across the intervention groups.

Primary Outcomes

Pain Immediately After Block Cessation

Test‐of‐interaction did not indicate a significant difference between treatment and trial (p = 0.67). The median pain score on the NRS immediately after block cessation was 5.0 points (IQR 3.0 to 8.0) in the placebo group and 3.0 points (IQR 1.0 to 6.0) in the dexamethasone group. Dexamethasone reduced pain immediately after block cessation compared to placebo with a mean difference of −1.7 points (95% CI −2.6 to −0.8 points, p < 0.001, estimated via linear regression after verifying the underlying assumptions were met; the mean and median difference may differ due to the skewed distribution of the raw data).

When analysing data from the trials individually, respecting the original intervention groups, dexamethasone reduced pain after block cessation and reduced pain after block cessation regardless of route (intravenous or oral) and dose (12 mg or 24 mg) (Table S1).

Rebound Pain

Test‐of‐interaction did not indicate a significant difference between treatment and trial (p = 0.88). Rebound pain occurred in 20 of 56 participants (35.7%) in the placebo group and in 19 out of 103 participants (18.4%) in the dexamethasone group. The absolute risk reduction in rebound pain with dexamethasone was 17.3%, while the relative risk reduction in rebound pain with dexamethasone versus placebo was 0.51 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.88, p = 0.014). The number needed to treat was six.

When analysing data from the trials individually, respecting the original intervention groups, dexamethasone reduced the risk of rebound pain and reduced the risk regardless of the route (intravenous or oral) and dose (12 or 24 mg) (Table S2).

Subgroup Analyses

None of the subgroup analyses related to age (below 65 vs. 65 or above), sex (male vs. female), body mass index (below 25 vs. 25 or above), type of surgery (fracture vs. non‐fracture) and baseline pain (below 3 vs. 3 or above) were associated with significant subgroup differences for pain after block cessation or rebound pain. See Table S1, Table S2, Figures S1–S10.

Sensitivity Analyses

Excluding participants receiving dexamethasone combined with dexmedetomidine from the analyses of the primary outcomes did not alter the results of dexamethasone versus placebo for neither pain after block cessation (MD −1.8 points, 95% CI −3.0 to −0.7) nor rebound pain (RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.79).

Using a mixed‐effect model also showed that dexamethasone reduced pain after block cessation (mean difference −1.7 points, 95% CI −2.6 to −0.8). The variance of the random intercept for study was small (0.1, SD 0.30), suggesting a limited between‐study variability relative to the residual variance (7.4, SD 2.7).

Using a mixed‐effects model also showed that dexamethasone reduced the risk of rebound pain (Odds ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.84). The between‐study variance was very small (< 0.001).

For satisfaction with duration of the block, there was a higher probability of rating block duration as adequate in the dexamethasone group compared with the placebo group. The probability of rating the block duration as too long was higher in the dexamethasone group, while the placebo group had a higher probability of rating the block duration as too short (Table S4 and Figure S11).

For discomfort related to having a blocked extremity, the test of interaction did not show a significant difference between patients included in the ADJUNCT1 and ADJUNCT2 trials. However, discomfort was approximately 2 points lower on the NRS for participants included in the ADJUNCT2 trial compared to participants included in the ADJUNCT1 trial. This suggests that patients find it less uncomfortable to have a blocked leg (ADJUNCT2) compared to having a blocked arm (ADJUNCT1) (Table 3 and Table S5).

Patients in both groups reported equal overall satisfaction with their nerve block, regardless of treatment allocation (Table 3, Table S6) and they were equally likely to accept a block in the future and recommend receiving a block to other participants (Table 3, Tables S7 and S8).

Patients in both the placebo group and the dexamethasone group were equally satisfied with the content and amount of information regarding the block (Table 3, Tables S9 and S10).

Discussion

In this substudy based on two randomised controlled trials, we found that adding systemic dexamethasone to a nerve block significantly reduced pain after block cessation by 1.7 points on the NRS. The absolute and relative risk reductions in rebound pain were 17.3% and 49%, respectively, with a number needed to treat to prevent one occurrence of rebound pain of six. There were no important subgroup differences. Notably, there were no important differences between patients receiving sciatic/popliteal block and intravenous dexamethasone and patients receiving infraclavicular block and oral dexamethasone. There appeared to be no difference between 12 and 24 mg dexamethasone, suggesting that 12 mg represents a sufficient dose for preventing rebound pain.

Overall, patient satisfaction with the peripheral nerve block was high in both groups with no significant between‐group differences. Regarding block duration, participants in the dexamethasone group had a higher probability of rating the block duration as adequate compared to those in the placebo group. Additionally, participants who received a popliteal (sciatic) and saphenous nerve block reported less discomfort from having a blocked extremity than those who received a lateral infraclavicular brachial plexus block.

Comparison With Other Studies

A previous systematic review has investigated the effect of dexamethasone to prevent rebound pain in patients undergoing surgery with a peripheral nerve block [8]. The review showed that dexamethasone versus placebo resulted in a relative risk reduction in rebound pain by 0.36 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.49, inverse‐variance fixed‐effect estimate re‐calculated from the data available from the systematic review). Thus, it suggests a larger reduction in the risk of rebound pain with dexamethasone than suggested in this substudy. This difference may be due to the trials included in the systematic review employing different definitions of rebound pain while also being relatively small and with large clinical heterogeneity, or by the more homogenous patient population in this substudy with similar basic analgesic regimens and outcome definitions.

Another systematic review investigated different routes of dexamethasone administration for preventing rebound pain following peripheral nerve blocks and found that systemic dexamethasone as an adjunct to a peripheral nerve block reduced the incidence of rebound pain, similar to this substudy. Yet again, the randomised controlled trials included in this systematic review employed different definitions of rebound pain, again contributing to heterogeneity in the study [25].

Previous research has indicated that rebound pain is negatively associated with patient satisfaction [9]. This substudy did not find significant differences in patient satisfaction between the dexamethasone and placebo groups, despite higher pain after block cessation and a higher incidence of rebound pain in the placebo group.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this substudy is that it is based on data from two randomised, placebo‐controlled clinical trials, which were originally designed to minimise bias. This substudy was, however, retrospectively registered after unblinded data became available, which increased the risk of reporting and analytical bias.

The primary limitation of this substudy is the late protocol amendment and therefore late addition of the outcomes related to this substudy. Only a subset of participants from each trial contributed data and in total, 141 were excluded as they did not provide data.

We pooled data from two randomised trials that used a different route of administration (oral vs. intravenous) and different peripheral nerve blocks (brachial plexus vs. popliteal sciatic and saphenous). We included participants receiving combined dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine in the primary analysis, introducing a co‐intervention, namely dexmedetomidine, that was not the primary focus of this substudy. However, sensitivity analyses excluding participants receiving combined dexamethasone and dexmedetomidine did not indicate meaningful differences between patients receiving dexamethasone only and those also receiving dexmedetomidine on pain immediately after block cessation and rebound pain.

Our subgroup analyses did not show important subgroup differences. The subgroup cut‐off points for age, BMI and baseline pain levels were selected after study completion and should therefore be considered exploratory. Most importantly, there did not appear to be meaningful subgroup differences in the effects of dexamethasone on pain immediately after block cessation and rebound pain between the two studies. This was also the case in mixed‐effect models. However, the mixed‐effect models should be interpreted with caution as there were only two levels for the random intercept for ‘study’ whereas usually 5 to 10 are required to obtain precise variance estimates of random effects. The estimated between‐study variance was < 0.001 for rebound pain. However, as only two studies contributed data, estimation of random effects variance was unstable and this value should not be interpreted as evidence of negligible between‐study heterogeneity. Accordingly, the mixed‐effects analysis should be regarded as supportive of the primary analysis rather than confirmatory evidence of robustness.

We did not adjust the threshold for statistical significance despite using two co‐primary outcomes and this has increased the risk of type 1 errors. There was an imbalance between the treatment groups (103 dexamethasone vs. 56 placebo participants) due to the original three‐arm designs. The unequal group sizes have reduced statistical precision, particularly for the placebo group.

Another limitation is related to the satisfaction outcomes, which were based on the Numerical Rating Scale rather than validated tools like the Quality of Recovery scales. We did not find an association between rebound pain and patient satisfaction. The lack of an observed association between rebound pain and satisfaction should be interpreted cautiously, as satisfaction scores exhibited a pronounced ceiling effect, with most participants reporting maximal scores. Consequently, the instruments may have lacked sufficient discriminatory capacity to detect modest differences in satisfaction attributable to rebound pain. Further, the lack of crossover meant that participants could not compare the treatment they received with the alternative treatment. The observed absence of association therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that rebound pain has no influence on patient satisfaction. Several studies, including this substudy, have reported high levels of patient satisfaction even in the presence of rebound pain, suggesting that factors, other than rebound pain, may influence overall patient satisfaction [6, 26].

Following block cessation, the point estimate indicated a reduction in pain of 1.7 points with dexamethasone compared to placebo. The minimal important difference for pain remains a topic of debate and may vary based on baseline pain levels and the population of interest. A systematic review showed that minimal important differences used in pain research ranged from 8 to 40 mm on the Visual Analogue Scale [27]. A commonly used minimal important difference for acute pain is a 1‐point reduction on the NRS, which corresponds to a 10 mm reduction on the visual analogue scale [28, 29]. Although the minimal important differences were developed in other populations not directly comparable to our population, our observed point estimate exceeded one point on the NRS, suggesting that the reduction in pain after block cessation with dexamethasone was clinically meaningful for patients. However, the 95% confidence interval is also compatible with a smaller effect. The effect appears modest and should be considered in the light of the patient population.

Perspectives

Future trials should investigate whether patient satisfaction with nerve block duration varies based on the type of extremity and the specific nerve block employed. To gain deeper insights into patient satisfaction with peripheral nerve blocks, future trials should incorporate more comprehensive assessment tools, such as the Quality of Recovery 15, rather than relying solely on single item measures like the NRS. Reporting on Quality of Recovery 15 was recently recommended in a core outcome set developed for regional anaesthesia [30]. Additionally, we encourage future trials to standardise the definition of rebound pain—such as pain ≥ 7 after block cessation—to facilitate meaningful comparisons across trials.

In this substudy, dexamethasone significantly reduced pain immediately after block cessation and the risk of rebound pain. Thereby strengthening the hypothesis that dexamethasone is not only beneficial for block duration, but also for recovery following block cessation. However, given the relatively sparse previous evidence and the limitations of this substudy discussed above, additional trials investigating the effects of dexamethasone on rebound pain are warranted before recommendations for the routine administration of dexamethasone specifically for reducing rebound pain can be made.

Conclusion

In this substudy of two randomised trials, adding dexamethasone to a peripheral nerve block reduced pain after block cessation and the risk of rebound pain when compared with placebo.
