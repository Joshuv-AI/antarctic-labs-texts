Identification of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitors from marine actinomycetes through integrated phylogeny-based metabolomics with functional screening and bioinformatic analysis
Authors: Doralyn S Dalisay, Jomari C Mateo, Jade Joshua R Teodosio, Lucille Marie S Jusa, Diane Monique S Baladjay, Leighiara S de Guzman, Lex Aliko P Balida, Jamia Azdina Jamal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the acute threat of the COVID-19 pandemic diminishes globally, advancing the research and development of antiviral agents remains crucial for ensuring preparedness for future outbreaks. This study investigates the inhibitory effects of marine sediment-derived actinomycetes on the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (3CLpro), a key enzyme involved in viral replication and transcription. Extracts of 75 actinomycete strains from two locations, Nogas Island in Antique and Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park in Palawan, Philippines, were evaluated for their ability to inhibit 3CLpro, with 36% of the extracts showing more than 90% inhibition. Further examination of 27 extracts revealed IC50 values between 2 and 8 µg/mL, suggesting notable inhibitory strength. Phylogenetic analysis grouped the active strains into seven distinct clades, with Clades 1, 5, and 6, closely related to Streptomyces enissocaesilis, S. geysiriensis, S. ardesiacus, and S. indicus, exhibiting antiviral activity. Metabolomic profiling using LCMS-QTOF, coupled with principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering, revealed that metabolic signatures were consistent with clade classification. Shared features among the active clades included a biohopanoid, fatty acid amides, diketopiperazines, lipid derivatives, and aromatic compounds. Supervised analysis using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) identified 17 discriminant metabolites produced by Streptomyces strains associated with antiviral activity, including a bicyclic sesquiterpenoid, an aromatic polyketide, an alkylphenol, fatty acid derivatives, alkylated aromatic amines, lipid-derived metabolites, and a carbazole alkaloid. Among the identified compounds, streptocarbazole C showed a strong predicted binding affinity to SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro (− 9.8 ± 0.30 kcal/mol) in molecular docking analysis, with favorable interactions at the enzyme’s catalytic dyad (HIS41 and CYS145). In silico toxicity predictions suggested a generally favorable safety profile, although some limitations were noted. These findings highlight the biosynthetic and functional potential of marine sediment-derived Streptomyces against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro, providing a framework for the discovery of antiviral natural products through the integration of ecological, phylogenetic, metabolomics, and computational analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25354-0.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitors, Marine actinomycetes, Phylogenetic analysis, Metabolomics, Carbazole, Diketopiperazines, Fatty acid amide, Antivirals, SesquiterpenoidSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemical biology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Ecology, Microbiology, Systems biology, Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences, Diseases, Chemistry

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2, identified in Wuhan, China, at the end of 2019, is a betacoronavirus thought to have originated from animals and was initially associated with cases of severe pneumonia of unknown cause1. Because of its rapid human-to-human transmission, mainly through droplets and aerosols during close interactions, the World Health Organization (WHO) later recognized the outbreak as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and declared it a pandemic in March 20202. SARS-CoV-2 has spread over the world as of August 24, 2025, resulting in over 7.1 million fatalities and around 778 million recorded cases3. The pandemic has resulted in direct health consequences and has also caused considerable disturbances in healthcare systems, economic instability, strain on social structures, and changes in cultural practices globally.

The virus has steadily accumulated mutations during its ongoing global spread, leading to variants such as alpha4, beta5, gamma6, delta7, and omicron8. The observed variants display various patterns of genomic diversity that contribute to increased transmissibility, virulence, and the capacity to escape immune responses9. Furthermore, concerns have also been raised about how these variants might affect the effectiveness of vaccines. Hence, the emergence of new variants has underscored the need for ongoing research to develop anti-SARS-CoV-2 drugs that target specific treatment strategies, such as inhibiting viral replication and transcription, to enhance treatment outcomes.

The tireless efforts made in recent years for aggressive drug discovery and development programs aimed at viral disorders yielded a wide spectrum of promising drug candidates targeting various viral components such as proteases, polymerases, and helicases10. One of the most well-investigated therapeutic targets in SARS-CoV-2 is the major protease, 3CLpro11. The processing of the polyproteins produced from viral RNA into non-structural proteins predominantly relies on this essential enzyme, which significantly impacts viral replication and transcription11. An exciting approach to halt viral replication is to inhibit the activity of this enzyme. Moreover, the unique cleavage selectivity of the primary protease in SARS-CoV-2, which lacks equivalents in human proteases, makes inhibitors targeting this enzyme unlikely to exhibit harmful effects on human cells. These results emphasize the need to focus on the identification and development of antiviral medicines targeting the SARS-CoV-2 major protease.

Natural products and synthetic or semisynthetic compounds inspired by them continue to serve as valuable structural frameworks for the identification of novel therapeutics, particularly in the area of antiviral agents. About 53% of drugs approved by the FDA that are based on natural products originate from microbes, including those with antiviral properties12. Ara-A, or vidarabine, has been recognized as one of the first antiviral nucleoside analogs identified from Streptomyces antibioticus13–15. Following this, a number of nucleoside-derived antiviral compounds originating from microbial sources were created16. Notably, the semi-synthetic anti-parasitic drug ivermectin, sourced from the soil bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis and approved by the FDA, has shown effectiveness as an in vitro inhibitor of SARS-CoV-2 replication17,18 by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of viral proteins19. Recent meta-analyses, however, show that this mechanism has limited efficacy in a clinical setting20,21. Additionally, this mechanism has not been unequivocally proven in vivo. The findings indicate that while ivermectin demonstrates potential in preclinical set-up, its application in clinical settings as an antiviral is still not fully established.

Our recent discovery reveals a diverse array of bioactive actinomycetes thriving in the marine sediment collected from the Philippine seabed at a depth of 1 m below the seafloor22–26. These microorganisms, buried for extended periods in accumulating particulate matter, have developed unique secondary metabolites in marine environments27,28. These metabolites hold significant potential for drug discovery and biotechnology applications. As part of our ongoing research in bioprospecting for bioactive metabolites, we examined 75 actinomycete strains derived from marine sediments at two distinct sampling sites: Nogas Island, Antique and Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park, Palawan. We selected these sites from our collection of actinomycetes based on their rich phylogenetic diversity22,23. These species may target the SARS-COV-2 3CLpro. Specifically, this study encompasses the identification of actinomycetes exhibiting anti-3CLpro bioactivities, the determination of their IC50 values, metabolite profiling, the identification of potential metabolites responsible for bioactivity, and the correlation of these findings with phylogenetic and metabolomic analyses. Additionally, bioinformatics approaches such as molecular docking against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro were employed to predict the binding interactions and affinities of the identified metabolites, while in silico organ toxicity prediction was conducted to evaluate their safety profiles. This study investigates microbial and chemical diversity in search of metabolites with potential inhibitory effects against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro.

Experimental procedures

General experimental procedures

Fluorescence measurements were conducted at λexcitation = 360 nm and λemission = 460 nm using a microplate reader (BMG Labtech CLARIOstar® Ortenberg, Germany). Metabolomic analysis was performed in a Waters™ Synapt XS QTOF mass spectrometer coupled to a Waters™ Acquity UPLC Class I and an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. PCR amplification was performed in a BIOER LineGene 9600 Real Time Thermal Cycler (Hangzhou, China).

Chemicals and reagents

Media ingredients, solvents (analytical to LC–MS grade), DNA extraction and PCR reagents, primers, and molecular biology buffers were purchased from Qiagen Ltd (Hilden, Germany), RCI Labscan Ltd (Bangkok, Thailand), Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), Bio-Rad, Promega, and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively. The 3 chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro) MBP-tagged assay kits were obtained from BPS Bioscience Inc (San Diego, California, USA).

Revival of actinomycetes from Nogas and Tubbataha Reefs collection sites

Seventy-five (75) marine sediment-derived actinomycetes from Nogas Island (n = 57) and Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park (n = 18) collection sites were retrieved from our actinomycetes biobank. Following the previously reported method22–26, the strains were thawed and inoculated with 200 µL glycerol stock into 15 mL marine media 1 (MM1) broth supplemented with artificial seawater (ASW) and incubated for 7 days at room temperature. Colonies were sub-cultured onto MM1 agar and subsequently re-streaked to achieve pure isolates.

Biomass production and extraction

Pre-cultures of seventy-five (75) actinomycete strains were grown in MM1 broth for 3–7 days, then transferred to MM1 agar for biomass production using the previously described method22–26. The harvested material was extracted with ethyl acetate for 24 h, concentrated under reduced pressure, partitioned against water, and the ethyl acetate extract was cleaned up using C18 solid-phase extraction using a Sep-Pak® C18 cartridge (360 mg, 55–105 µm, 125 Å, 0.7 mL)22–26. Dried extracts were reconstituted in DMSO (10 mg/mL) and stored at − 80 °C until further use in bioassays targeting SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro.

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay for SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibition

A set of assay kits was used consisting of recombinant 3CLpro, 3CLpro assay buffer, GC376, a known 3CLpro inhibitor29–31 as positive control, dithiothreitol (DTT), and 3CLpro substrate. The fluorogenic substrate was an internally quenched 14-mer fluorogenic peptide (KTSAVLQSGFRKME) with DABCYL and EDANS attached on its N- and C-terminals, respectively, wherein once inhibited the fluorescence of the substrate was quantified by a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay mechanism32. The manufacturer’s procedure with modifications was followed to measure the 3CLpro activity of the 75 actinomycete crude extracts.

Working solutions of recombinant 3CLpro (target, 5 ng/µL), 3CLpro substrate, GC376 (positive control), water (negative control), vehicle control for GC376 (assay buffer), crude extracts (test inhibitors), and vehicle control for inhibitors (which is 0.5% DMSO in assay buffer) were freshly prepared prior to assay. Briefly, recombinant 3CL protease (2.1 mg/mL) and substrate (10 mM) were diluted with 1 mM DTT to 5 ng/µL and 250 µM solutions, respectively. The positive control, GC376 was diluted in 1 mM DTT to 500 µM solution. A 0.5 µL aliquot of a 10 mg/mL crude extract stock solution in DMSO was diluted with 1 mM DTT to a final volume of 100 µL, yielding a working solution containing 50 µg/mL extract in 0.5% DMSO.

In the wells designated as positive control, negative control, and test inhibitors, 30 µL of a diluted 3CLpro solution was added. This was followed by adding 10 µL of vehicles, GC376, and samples to the appropriately marked wells. An additional 30 µL of 1 mM DTT was added to the wells labeled blank 1, and 10 µL of inhibitor buffers 1 and 2 was added to the wells labeled blank 2. The treated plates were kept at room temperature and subjected to gentle agitation for a duration of 30 min. To initiate the reaction, 10 µL of 3CLpro substrate was added to each well in the dark. This resulted in a final concentration of 10 µg/mL, along with 0.1% DMSO sample and 100 µM GC376. The plate was kept in the dark at room temperature for 8 h. The experiment was conducted in duplicates. A microplate reader (BMG Labtech CLARIOstar®﻿, Ortenberg, Germany) was used to measure fluorescent intensity at λexcitation = 360 nm and λemission = 460 nm. Values from blank 1 were subtracted from GC376 and vehicle 1, while values from blank 2 were subtracted from the sample and vehicle 2. IC50 inhibition assay

To determine the IC50 values for SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibition, different concentrations of crude extracts were prepared in DMSO. To prepare the samples, 0.5 µL of the crude extract solutions in different concentrations was mixed with 99.5 µL of assay buffer with DTT. Thirty (30) µL of 3CLpro solution was added to the designated wells treated with 10 µL of crude extracts at varying concentrations and pre-incubated for 30 min with orbital shaking of 100 rpm. After incubation, 10 µL of 3CLpro substrate was added to the wells in a dark environment to initiate the reaction, resulting in final concentrations of 40, 20, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 1, and 0.5 µg/mL in 0.1% DMSO. The plate underwent incubation at room temperature for a duration of 8 h. The experiment was performed in duplicates as described above. IC50 values were determined through nonlinear regression analysis of dose–response inhibition curves using GraphPad Prism 9 (California, USA). The heatmap package in RStudio® version 4.4.1 software was utilized for clustering heatmap plotting of the IC50. Extracts with IC50 values < 3.17 µg/mL were considered high inhibitory activity, IC50 values between 3.17 and 6.45 µg/mL were considered moderate activity, and low activity was considered for IC50 values > 6.45 µg/mL.

Cultivation of Streptomyces strains, genomic DNA Extraction, and 16S rRNA sequencing

The actinomycete strains identified as hits against the SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro were cultivated on MM1 agar plates to obtain pure isolates. These pure isolates were then transferred and incubated in MM1 broth for a period ranging from 3 to 7 days to obtain sufficient cells for DNA extraction23.

Genomic DNA extraction and PCR amplification methods were performed according to the established methods23,24. The 16S rRNA gene amplicons were sequenced (1st BASE, Malaysia) and a phylogenetic analysis was carried out based on the obtained 16S rRNA gene sequences, adhering to the methodology outlined in prior studies23,24. The partial 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained were validated by BLAST and MEGA 11.0.1333. The construction of phylogenetic trees was performed utilizing neighbor-joining34 and maximum likelihood35 methodologies with bootstrap analysis based on 1,000 replications. Phylogenetic trees were annotated with iTOL 6.9.1 (Biobyte Solutions, Heidelberg, Germany)36. The 16S rRNA sequences of 27 isolates were deposited in GenBank with accession numbers PQ056056 until PQ056083. (Supplementary Table S3).

Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry quadrupole time-of-flight (LCMS-QTOF) analysis

Analysis using LCMS was performed on a Waters™ Acquity UPLC system equipped with a C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 µm), coupled to a Waters™ Synapt XS High-Definition Mass Spectrometer (HDMS). The acquisition and processing of data were conducted utilizing MassLynx® 4.2 software, following the previously described method with minor modifications23. The mobile phase was H2O/MeCN (Solvent A/Solvent B) with 0.1% HCOOH pumped at 0.3 mL/min as follows: 20% B (0 to 0.55 min), 20% to 100% B (0.55 to 5.55 min), 100% B (5.55 to 7.00 min), 100% to 20% B (7.00 to 7.45 min), and 20% B (7.45 to 8.50 min). Mass spectrometry was performed using positive ESI mode at a voltage of 3 kV. The source temperature was established at 100 °C, accompanied by a desolvation temperature of 250 °C, and a nitrogen flow rate maintained at 600 L/h. Twenty micrograms in 10 µL of the crude extract in 100% MeOH were injected, and three replicates were run to ensure reproducibility in the metabolomics analysis. Calibration was performed using sodium formate, while lock-mass correction was applied with leucine-enkephalin (m/z 556.2771 [M + H]+, 554.2615 [M − H]−). MSE data were collected from m/z 100–1200 (scan time 0.15 s, CID 20–75 mV). Targeted MS/MS was acquired for precursor ions of interest at CID 10–80 mV.

Metabolomics data analysis and multivariate statistical analysis

The metabolomic profiles of actinomycetes active against 3CLpro were analyzed using Progenesis QI (version 3.0, Waters™). Data were aligned, peaks detected and deconvoluted, and metabolites identified using the software’s integrated pipeline. Strain DSD454, which exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity, was used as the reference for alignment. Positive ionization mode adduct [M + H]+ was considered. Subsequently, a workflow was implemented to filter the data using the following parameters: RT range of 0.55–7.00 min, ANOVA p-value ≤ 0.05, and maximum fold change ≥ 2. By restricting the analysis to the 0.55–7.00 min window, the study ensures that the detected m/z values are more likely to correspond to meaningful analytes, reducing the potential for interference from background noise or non-specific signals during the column equilibration phases.

EZinfo® version 3.0 (Umetrics AB, Umeå, Sweden) and SIMCA® P+ version 18.0 (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB) were used to perform multivariate statistical analyses (Principal Component Analysis, PCA and Orthogonal Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis, OPLS-DA). For PCA, features with raw intensities above 1 × 107 were retained, and Pareto scaling was applied to reduce the dominance of high-variance variables while preserving overall data structure. Hierarchical Clustering Analysis (HCA) was conducted utilizing Ward’s linkage method, organizing clusters based on their size.

A manual curation step was introduced to enhance feature quality and eliminate false positives linked to automated peak detection. Extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) and total ion chromatograms (TICs) were analyzed for each feature across all samples. Features were retained only if they surpassed an intensity threshold of 1 × 107, demonstrated consistent detection across biological replicates, exhibited reproducible peak shapes, and showed no retention time drift or alignment artifacts. The features were additionally filtered based on their occurrence within phylogenetic clades. Only those identified in all strains of a given clade and fulfilling all specified quality criteria were included in the final dataset. A Venn diagram was created to illustrate the overlap of high-intensity metabolite features (≥ 1 × 107) across the specified sample groups. Data visualization and processing were performed in the R environment using RStudio® (version 2023.12.1), utilizing the ggplot2 package for plotting and the ggvenn package for constructing the Venn diagram.

OPLS-DA was used to assess metabolic variation among Streptomyces strains grouped by inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro (high, moderate, low, based on IC50 values). Metabolite intensity data that had been pre-processed (> 1 × 10⁷) underwent Pareto scaling and was subjected to analysis using a single predictive component in combination with one orthogonal component. Model performance was evaluated using R2Y and Q2, with reliability assessed by the 95% Hotelling’s T2 ellipse. Variables contributing to class separation were ranked by VIP scores, and features with VIP > 1 were further filtered by fold change (≥ 2) and ANOVA significance (p ≤ 0.05). The features meeting all criteria were considered candidates for SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitor biomarkers. Metabolite identification involved matching MS and MS/MS fragment ions against databases such as ChemSpider37, PubChem38, GNPS39, MarinLit, Natural Products Atlas40, MetFrag41, and StreptomeDB42, with the following criteria: a mass error tolerance of 5 ppm for precursor ions and 10 ppm for fragment ions, an isotope similarity score of 80% for precursor ions, and manual inspection of fragmentation spectra for each putative biomarker.

In silico molecular docking analysis

To determine the molecular interactions of compounds present in the highly active Streptomyces strains extracts against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro, we investigated their binding affinities using previously reported molecular docking settings43. The chemical structures of the identified compounds, including the positive control GC376 (in .sdf format), were obtained from Pubchem38. Ligand geometries were optimized using Avogadro v1.2.0 by applying the General Amber Force Field (GAFF) and performing 5,000 steps with the conjugate gradient algorithm. The protein structure of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro, available in .pdb format, was obtained from the Protein Data Bank at the following URL: https://www.rcsb.org/, corresponding to PDB ID: 6YB7. The structure was cleaned using PyMOL by removing water molecules, followed by the addition of polar hydrogens and Kollman charges to enhance docking efficiency. The optimized configurations of the ligands and the protein were used and exported in .pdbqt format. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.5.7, by targeting the active site of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The docking grid was centered at coordinates x = 13.707, y = 1.456, and z = 21.418, with a grid box size of 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å and a spacing of 0.5 Å43. Each docking experiment was repeated three times to calculate the standard error of the mean (SEM), and the docking score with the lowest root mean square deviation (RMSD) value was converted to .mol2 file using Open Babel v.2.4.1 and visualized their binding interactions using BIOVIA Discovery Studio v25.1.0. The parameter for exhaustiveness was established at 32 for each docking run43.

In silico prediction of organ-specific toxicities

Organ-specific toxicities (hepatic, neural, renal, respiratory, cardiac) of candidate metabolites were predicted using ProTox-II44, based on canonical SMILES retrieved from the PubChem38. These SMILES were entered into the ProTox-II interface, which applies machine learning models trained on curated toxicological datasets to classify compounds as toxic or non-toxic for each organ system44. Each metabolite was assessed individually. The predicted toxicity outcomes were recorded and compiled to generate an organ-specific toxicity profile for each metabolite.

Results

Marine sediment-derived Streptomyces as sources of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitor

In this study, crude ethyl acetate extracts from 75 marine sediment-derived Streptomyces were evaluated for their inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The library of the 75 marine sediment-derived Streptomyces strains, including their corresponding strain codes and descriptions, is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Table S1 lists 75 actinomycete isolates collected from marine sediments in Nogas Island and the Tubbataha Reefs. For each strain, we noted the sampling location, depth below the seafloor, and observable traits like mycelium color and pigment production. These details highlight the wide diversity of the isolates and help provide context for their potential as sources of 3CLpro inhibitors. The strains were obtained from two locations: Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park (n = 18) and Nogas Island, Antique (n = 57). The initial screening revealed that 58 strains exhibited more than 50% inhibition, resulting in a hit rate of 77% (58/75). Given the significant number of strains showing activity (Fig. 1), the threshold for selecting bioactive extracts was raised to 90% inhibition. With the > 90% inhibition threshold, 27 strains were identified as highly active, corresponding to a hit rate of 36% (27/75) (Fig. 1).

Notably, the samples showing greater than 90% inhibition exhibit no significant difference when compared to the positive control, suggesting that their activity levels are comparable to those of the positive control, as indicated in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S2.

IC50 determination of 3CLpro active marine Streptomyces

To validate the findings from the initial screening for 3CLpro inhibition, the inhibitory activity of these 27 active extracts was further evaluated using an endpoint 3CLpro IC50 FRET assay as a confirmatory screening method. The extracts were tested at concentrations ranging from 40 to 0.5 µg/mL to assess their inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The results confirmed that the actinomycete crude extracts preserved their bioactivity, consistently showing > 90% inhibition of 3CLpro at concentrations between 40 and 10 µg/mL (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Fig. S2). A clear dose-dependent inhibition was observed at concentrations below 10 µg/mL (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S2), with Streptomyces sp. DSD454 displaying the lowest IC50 of 2.43 µg/mL, compared to the control showing 1.57 µg/mL (3.09 µM) for the known 3CLpro inhibitor, GC376. Notably, seven of the top ten extracts (Streptomyces spp. DSD454, DSD149, DSD236, DSD375, DSD735, DSD235, DSD371) originated from Nogas Island (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2). To classify the potency of inhibitory activity, the IC50 value distribution and stratification threshold were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. S3). Extracts with IC50 values < 3.17 µg/mL were considered to have high inhibitory activity, IC50 values between 3.17 and 6.45 µg/mL were deemed to have moderate activity, and low activity was assigned to IC50 values > 6.45 µg/mL. Five extracts with high inhibitory activities were Streptomyces spp. DSD454, DSD149, DSD375, DSD360, and DSD735 (Fig. 2a,b).

Phylogenetic analysis and inhibitory potential of Streptomyces strains against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro

We performed a phylogenetic analysis utilizing 16S rRNA gene sequences to elucidate the evolutionary relationships among 27 Streptomyces strains that demonstrated notable inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro (Fig. 3). This method allowed us to classify the strains into seven distinct clades and assess their genetic relatedness. Clade 1 (Fig. 3, shown in red), the largest cluster identified in the analysis, received strong support from bootstrap values of 94% in both the maximum likelihood and 99% in the neighbor-joining trees (Supplementary Fig. S4-5) at critical nodes. This clade consists of marine sediment-derived strains from Nogas Island, Antique: Streptomyces spp. DSD483, DSD454, DSD371, DSD368, DSD236, DSD235, and DSD193. These strains displayed a close genetic relationship to S. enissocaesilis (NR 115668.1) with 100% sequence identity, indicating a highly conserved lineage. Streptomyces sp. DSD377 also showed a close alignment with S. enissocaesilis but exhibited a slightly lower identity of 99.93%. The strains presented grey aerial mycelium and a yellowish-brown substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1), with IC50 values ranging from 2.43 to 5.80 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2). Within this cluster, Streptomyces spp. DSD149, DSD357, DSD360, and DSD2604, showed 100% sequence identity with S. geysiriensis (NR 043818.1), exhibit moderate phylogenetic support, featuring bootstrap values of 59% in the neighbor-joining tree and 49% in the maximum likelihood tree (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4-5). These strains, primarily from Nogas Island, with Streptomyces sp. DSD2604 originating from Tubbataha Reef, displays IC50 values ranging from 2.47 to 4.80 µg/mL (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2). Additionally, this clade includes a strain with low IC50 values, particularly Streptomyces sp. DSD454 (2.43 µg/mL), indicating strong inhibitory activity against 3CLpro (Fig. 2a,b).

DSD287 demonstrates a 99.8% sequence identity with S. physcomitrii (NR 180163.1), forming a distinct branch with strong bootstrap support of 67% in both the neighbor-joining and 89% in the maximum likelihood trees (Supplementary Fig. S4-5), and belongs to Clade 2 (Fig. 3, shown in brown). Streptomyces spp. DSD88, DSD934, and DSD1554 demonstrate close genetic similarity to S. malachitospinus (NR 041423.1) and S. olivaceus (NR 041202.1). These strains belong to Clade 3 (Fig. 3, highlighted in yellow). Streptomyces sp. DSD88, with an IC50 of 4.68 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2), exhibits a 99.93% sequence identity with S. malachitospinus, supported by a strong bootstrap value of 96% in the neighbor-joining tree and 98% in the maximum likelihood tree (Supplementary Fig. S4-5). The strain, collected from Nogas Island, displays grey aerial mycelium and a pale-yellow substrate mycelium (Supplementary Table S1), while Streptomyces spp. DSD934 and DSD1554 closely align with S. olivaceus. These strains exhibit grey aerial sporulation and a brown substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1). The presence of other strain with higher IC50 values in this clade suggests that, while there may be some potential, it is not as strong as that in Clade 1.

The fourth clade (Clade 4, Fig. 3, shown in beige) includes Streptomyces strains closely related to S. griseorubens (NR 041066.1), S. althioticus (NR 112254.1), and S. ambofaciens (NR 041079.1). Streptomyces sp. DSD1050, with an IC50 of 4.63 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2), is aligned with S. griseorubens with 99.86% identity and demonstrates strong phylogenetic support with bootstrap values of 93% in the neighbor-joining tree. Originating from Nogas Island, this strain shows grey aerial mycelium and a yellowish-brown substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, Streptomyces sp. DSD848, also obtained from Nogas Island, is closely related to S. ambofaciens with 99.52% identity. It exhibits white aerial mycelium and a white substrate mycelium, highlighting morphological variation within the clade. Related to S. althioticus, Streptomyces sp. DSD2954 was isolated from Tubbataha Reefs. Supported by bootstrap values between 62 and 86% (Supplementary Fig. S4-5), the phylogenetic links in this clade point to a common evolutionary history impacting their bioactive potential. For Streptomyces strains in this clade, the IC50 values show moderate inhibitory potential, similar to those in Clade 3. Though it does not achieve the potency seen in Clade 1, Streptomyces sp. DSD1050, with an IC50 of 4.63 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2), shows the most promise among this clade.

A high degree of sequence identity (99.45% to 99.59%) characterizes the relationship among Streptomyces spp. DSD375, DSD670, and DSD1735 with S. ardesiacus (NR 112454.1), as they belong to Clade 5 (Fig. 3, shown in light blue). Substantial bootstrap support (100% in the neighbor-joining tree and 63% in the maximum likelihood tree (Supplementary Fig. S4-5) underscores the reliability of this grouping. These strains, all collected from Nogas Island, display grey aerial mycelium with a red substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1). This clade contains strains with low IC50 values; for instance, DSD375 has an IC50 of 2.75 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2). The overall low IC50 range suggests that this clade has a high potential for producing potent 3CLpro inhibitors, similar to Clade 1.

Streptomyces sp. DSD735 with an IC50 value of 3.15 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2) shows a close relationship with S. indicus (NR 108182.1), exhibiting 99.8% sequence identity. Clade 6 (Fig. 3, represented in blue) is supported by 100% bootstrap values in both the neighbor-joining and maximum likelihood trees (Supplementary Fig. S4-5), indicating a stable evolutionary lineage. DSD735, collected from Nogas Island, displays yellow aerial mycelium and a yellow substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1). This clade, although small, holds high potential based on the low IC50 values.

Distinct from other groups, Clade 7 (Fig. 3, shown in dark blue) comprises Streptomyces spp. DSD2893, DSD2939, DSD2941, and DSD2890, which are closely related to S. ginkgonis (NR 181625.1). The clade exhibits strong support, evidenced by 100% bootstrap values in both the neighbor-joining tree and the maximum likelihood tree (Supplementary Fig. S4 and S5). These strains, sourced from Tubbataha Reef, exhibit grey aerial mycelium and a black substrate mycelium (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, Streptomyces sp. DSD2893 indicates significant inhibitory potential with an IC50 of 3.92 µg/mL (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2). However, the presence of strains with higher IC50 values (up to 7.28 µg/mL, Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S2) suggests variability within this clade.

Metabolomics analysis of Streptomyces strains for 3CLpro inhibitory metabolites

Data from LCMS-QTOF was utilized for a PCA to enhance the understanding of the metabolic diversity among the 27 Streptomyces strains and their capacity to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The first principal component (PCA1) accounted for 22.7% of the variance, while the second principal component (PCA2) accounted for 17.4% of the variance, thus capturing a significant portion of the metabolic variability among the strains. The PCA plot (Fig. 4a) generated from the LCMS-QTOF data reveals distinct groups that closely match the evolutionary clades and highlights the metabolic coherence within the clades (Fig. 3). In accordance with their low IC50 values for 3CLpro inhibition (Fig. 2b), Clades 1 and 5, represented by red and light blue points, exhibited tight clusters suggesting highly comparable metabolite profiles. However, as indicated by the PCA plot and a wider range of IC50 values (Fig. 2b), Clade 3 (yellow points) and Clade 7 (dark blue points) displayed a more dispersed pattern, suggesting a distinct metabolic profile compared to other groups. To further explore the grouping patterns observed in our PCA, we followed it with hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA). HCA was chosen for its ability to reveal relationships and similarities among samples in an unsupervised manner, helping us better understand how metabolite profiles vary across different strains. Our analysis confirmed and refined the PCA groupings. Strains from Clade 1 (shown in red) and Clade 5 (shown in light blue) exhibited tight clustering in both HCA (Fig. 4b) and PCA (Fig. 4a), indicating high metabolomic similarity. In contrast, Clade 7 (shown in dark blue) and Clade 3 (shown in yellow) displayed more dispersed clustering, reflecting greater metabolic variability (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. S6).

Metabolomic overlap among Streptomyces strains of clades 1, 5, and 6

To complement the insights gained from the HCA and PCA, a Venn diagram (Fig. 4c) was created to examine the metabolomic overlap among Clades 1 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. enissocaesilis and S. geysiriensis, shown in red), 5 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. ardesiacus, shown in light blue), and 6 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. indicus, shown in blue). These clades were previously identified as having significant potential for inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro (Fig. 2a and 4a,b). The Venn diagram (Fig. 4c) illustrates the distribution of unique and shared metabolites across Clade 1, Clade 5, and Clade 6.

Using Progenesis QI’s automated feature alignment, a total of 339 metabolite features (m/z values) were initially classified as present across the dataset, based on detection in at least one strain from any of the three clades (Supplementary Table S8). However, to assess the reliability of these assignments, we conducted a manual validation process. Each feature was re-evaluated using a minimum intensity threshold of 1 × 10⁷, and both extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) and total ion chromatograms (TICs) were examined across all strains. During this step, 20 features were excluded due to low-abundance signals, inconsistent peak presence, or alignment artifacts likely introduced by peak warping, retention time drift, or missing value imputation. This refinement reduced the working dataset to 319 m/z features (Supplementary Table S9 and S15). Further analysis was performed to assess feature presence across all strains within each clade. Only 206 m/z features met the combined criteria of consistent detection in every strain per clade, above-threshold intensity, and reproducible peak shape across replicates (Supplementary Table S14).

This reclassification substantially adjusted the clade-level metabolite distribution, as reflected in the Venn diagram (Fig. 4c). A total of 101 features (49.03%) were found to be truly shared among the strains of Clades 1, 5, and 6. Clade 1 contained one unique feature (0.49%), Clade 5 retained 36 unique features (17.48%), and Clade 6 exhibited 41 unique features (19.90%). Additionally, 6 features (2.91%) were shared exclusively between Clades 1 and 5, 2 features (0.97%) between Clades 1 and 6, and 19 features (9.22%) were shared only between Clades 5 and 6. These revised distributions offer a more accurate view of clade-specific and shared chemical space, enhancing the interpretability of metabolomic variation across phylogenetic groups (Supplementary Tables S4 and S10-14).

Metabolite profiling reveals shared metabolic signatures and clade-specific features among bioactive Streptomyces strains

PCA of the LCMS-QTOF metabolomic data revealed distinct clustering of Streptomyces strains belonging to Clades 1, 5, and 6, all of which demonstrated high inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. Examination of the PCA loading plot identified 18 high-abundance m/z features (Supplementary Tables S4 and S14) consistently present across these bioactive clades. To further refine the identification of shared metabolic features, a Venn diagram analysis of high-intensity m/z values (≥ 5 × 10⁷) across Clades 1, 5, and 6 was conducted. This analysis confirmed the presence of overlapping m/z features unique to the active strains (Fig. 4c), consistent with the PCA-derived clustering pattern.

Dereplication of the corresponding m/z features resulted in the identification of 12 known compounds, representing distinct chemical classes. These metabolites were ranked by relative abundance, with compound numbers reflecting their intensity from highest to lowest (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S7). The most abundant metabolite, 35-aminobacteriohopane-32,33,34-triol 1, was identified as a biohopanoid (pentacyclic triterpenoid). Fatty acid derivatives included 9-octadecenamide 2, ethyl 8-oxooctadec-9-enoate 10, and palmitamide 12, comprising both amides and esters. Four compounds, namely cyclo(Pro-Ile) 3, cyclo(Phe-Pro) 6, cyclo-(phenylalanine-4-hydroxyproline) 7, and cyclo-(leucine-4-hydroxyproline) 8, were annotated as diketopiperazines (cyclic dipeptides). In addition, 4-vinylphenol 4 and N-acetyltyramine 9 were identified as phenolic and aromatic amine derivatives, respectively. Piptamine 5 was annotated as an alkylated aromatic amine, while calamenene 11 was classified as an aromatic sesquiterpenoid (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. S8–19). The remaining six features were not matched to known metabolites in the databases but exhibited fragmentation patterns and retention characteristics consistent with lipid-derived metabolites (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. 33-38).

Metabolite biomarkers linked to SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitory activity

OPLS-DA was conducted to evaluate metabolic variation among strains classified as high (green), moderate (yellow), and low (red) activity (Fig. 4d). The score plot demonstrated distinct clustering of Streptomyces strains with high inhibitory activity, showing some overlap between the moderate and low inhibitory activity groups. The model demonstrated a good fit with a cumulative R2Y of 0.999 and a cross-validated Q2 of 0.483. The predictive component explained 3.84% of the total variance in the X variables (R2X[1] = 0.0384), while the first orthogonal component captured 18.9% of the variance (R2Xo[1] = 0.189), which is linked to variation unrelated to group separation. All observations fell within the 95% confidence limit defined by Hotelling’s T2 ellipse.

Feature selection based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP > 1), fold change ≥ 2, and ANOVA (p ≤ 0.05) identified 17 metabolite features (Supplementary Table S5, S15-16, Supplementary Fig. S23). These features were ranked by decreasing VIP scores. The top feature (VIP = 5.2) corresponded to an unknown compound with m/z 528.1637 [M + H]⁺, molecular formula C30H30N3OBr, and a double bond equivalent (DBE) of 16.5. The isotopic distribution indicated the presence of a bromine atom (Supplementary Fig. S40-41). This compound showed no match in existing spectral or compound databases for natural products. The next discriminant metabolite (m/z 563.5524 [M + H]⁺, C36H70N2O2, DBE = 2.5) was also unannotated but exhibited fragmentation patterns consistent with a lipid derivative (Supplementary Fig. S42). The remaining features included metabolites belonging to various structural classes: albaflavenone 16 (bicyclic sesquiterpenoid), streptokordin 17 (aromatic polyketide), anaephene A 18 (alkylphenol), and 4-hydroxyphenylglycine 22 (non-proteinogenic aromatic amino acid). Fatty acid derivatives such as heptadecenamide 19, linoleamide 23, and linoleoyl ethanolamide 24 were also detected, along with alkylated aromatic amines including N-benzyl-N-methylheptadecan-1-amine 20, and the carbazole alkaloid streptocarbazole C 21 (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. S24-32). Interestingly, there are three remaining features that could not be matched to known metabolites in existing databases but displayed fragmentation patterns and retention behavior consistent with lipid-derived metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S38, S43-44). Interestingly, a subset of compounds consistently emerged as top features in both the supervised (OPLS-DA) and unsupervised (PCA) analyses, highlighting their potential relevance to the observed antiviral activity. Specifically, piptamine 5, calamenene 11, and palmitamide 12 were identified across both models (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S6). Their repeated detection in active extracts suggests a potential functional role in SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibition. Altogether, these metabolites represent a diverse array of candidates that may exhibit anti-SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro.

To evaluate their inhibitory potential, binding affinity, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions with catalytically important residues were examined (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S45, and Supplementary Table S17). Among the compounds evaluated, streptocarbazole C 21 exhibited the strongest binding affinity (− 9.80 ± 0.30 kcal/mol), outperforming the positive control GC376 (− 7.67 ± 0.03 kcal/mol). Compound 21 formed an extensive network of hydrogen bonds with critical catalytic and substrate-binding residues. In the 2D interaction diagram (Fig. 6a), compound 21 formed six conventional hydrogen bonds with residues within the active site: LEU141, GLY143, SER144, CYS145, and GLN189 (2 hydrogen bonds). Additionally, a π-donor hydrogen bond with GLU166 and a carbon-hydrogen bond with SER46 were noted. Also, π-sigma bonding was observed in LEU27 and MET165, while π-lone pair was observed in ASN142. The compound 21’s central polyaromatic system was engaged in a π-cation interaction with HIS41 and π-donor hydrogen interactions with CYS145, both components of the catalytic dyad. Additional van der Waals interactions were detected with THR25, THR26, MET49, PHE140, and HIS163. In silico prediction of organ-specific toxicities

To evaluate the safety profile of the candidate SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitors identified through OPLS-DA, organ-specific toxicity was assessed using the ProTox-II44 platform. Predictions focused on neurotoxicity, respiratory toxicity, nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. S46-57). Neurotoxicity was the most commonly predicted adverse effect, associated with 9 out of the 12 compounds. These included compounds 5, 11, 12, 16, 17, 19, 20, 21, and 23. In addition to neurotoxicity, 5, 12, 20, and 21 were also predicted to induce respiratory toxicity, indicating potential for multi-organ involvement. Compound 22 was predicted to exhibit both nephrotoxicity and respiratory toxicity, while 18 and 24 showed no predicted organ toxicity, suggesting a potentially safer profile. No compounds were predicted to exhibit hepatotoxicity or cardiotoxicity under the current in silico parameters. These results provide an initial toxicity screen to guide the prioritization of candidate metabolites for further pharmacological and toxicological evaluation.

Discussion

Streptomyces species are recognized for their capacity to synthesize a wide variety of secondary metabolites23–26,42,48. To recreate conditions that facilitate the activation of secondary metabolism, a cultivation strategy aligned with the morphological and physiological characteristics of Streptomyces was employed. Solid-state fermentation on MM1 agar under static conditions was employed in this study, following protocols commonly used in our laboratory for cultivating Streptomyces species. This approach has reliably enabled the isolation of both known and novel secondary metabolites22,23,26,45–47. Cultivation on solid media encourages morphological differentiation, including the development of substrate and aerial mycelium, which is frequently associated with the activation of secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters48,49. Moreover, static conditions closely resemble the natural habitats of Streptomyces, such as soil or marine sediments, where environmental gradients affect secondary metabolism. This cultivation method has proven successful in inducing the production of various bioactive compounds and remains our preferred strategy for discovering microbial secondary metabolites. In addition, the 7-day incubation period was chosen based on our preliminary observations, where consistent biomass production and measurable 3CLpro inhibitory activity were observed across several Streptomyces strains.

In our initial screening of marine sediment-derived Streptomyces strains extracts for 3CLpro inhibition, many strains exhibited promising activity (Fig. 1). Rather than including samples with moderate activity (over 50% inhibition, 58 samples, 77% hit rate), we opted to raise our threshold, focusing solely on strains that demonstrate over 90% inhibition (27 samples, 36% hit rate). This approach reduced the likelihood of false positives, enabling us to concentrate on the most promising candidates for subsequent testing, such as IC50 determination and metabolomic analysis.

The 27 Streptomyces strains extracts exhibited significant dose-dependent inhibition at concentrations below 10 µg/mL (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. S2) in the IC50 assay. This dose–response relationship indicates that metabolites derived from actinomycetes are effective as 3CLpro inhibitors, even at lower concentrations. The extract from Streptomyces sp. DSD454 exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect, demonstrating an IC50 value of 2.43 µg/mL. The positive control compound GC376, a dipeptide recognized for its inhibitory effects on 3CLpro, exhibited an IC50 value of 1.57 µg/mL (3.09 µM)29. We suggest isolating the active constituent from this extract, as purification could yield a more concentrated and potent inhibitor, potentially improving the IC50. Nonetheless, Streptomyces sp. DSD454 displayed significant inhibitory activity, warranting further investigation for metabolite profiling. These findings support the initial screening results and demonstrate that marine sediment-derived actinomycetes are promising sources of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibitors. It is particularly noteworthy that the IC50 for the positive control GC376 is relatively high in this study, in contrast to previously reported values ranging from 0.0015 to 17 µM29,32,50,51. The differences observed can be linked to the differing concentrations of enzymes and substrates employed in various studies (Supplementary Table S7). While the ethyl acetate extracts demonstrated strong 3CLpro inhibition, the observed activity cannot yet be attributed to specific metabolites due to the complex nature of the crude mixtures. The process of isolating and determining the structure of active constituents is important for establishing direct connections between specific metabolites and their bioactive properties. In addition, the FRET-based enzymatic assay used in this study, although suitable for identifying inhibitors of 3CLpro enzymatic function, does not capture key biological parameters such as cellular uptake, metabolic stability, and cytotoxicity. These factors must be validated in cell-based infection models, which are necessary to evaluate the practical antiviral potential of these extracts or compounds.

Approximately 33% of the recorded hits were traced back to Tubbataha Reefs Natural Park, a site valued by UNESCO for its rich biodiversity and unspoiled marine ecosystem52–54. This indicates that the distinct ecological interactions present in this area play a role in the bioactivity of secondary metabolites. A new species, designated S. tubbatahensis DSD3025T, has been identified, supporting this hypothesis22, as it produces rare halogenated antibiotic carbazole alkaloids. In contrast, Nogas Island in Antique, although a lesser-known collection site, shows a slightly higher hit rate of 37% (21/57). The differences in activity between Tubbataha Reefs and Nogas Island may arise from the varying adaptations of Streptomyces species to their specific environments, resulting in the production of distinct secondary metabolites that facilitate survival.

We constructed a phylogenetic tree using 27 Streptomyces strains, employing maximum likelihood and neighbor-joining methods (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4-5). Clear tree branching patterns, high bootstrap values, and close genetic relationships supported the seven clades in the tree55–57. The 16S rRNA gene-based phylogeny provided well-supported clades and insights into the evolutionary relationships among the strains. However, this single-locus approach lacks the resolution needed to distinguish closely related Streptomyces species with confidence. Therefore, more detailed phylogenetic analyses, such as those using whole-genome sequencing or multi-locus sequence typing, would enable more accurate taxonomic placement. Nonetheless, numerous bioactive metabolites were identified in strains from Clades 1, 5, and 6, corroborating previous discoveries and indicating possible antiviral properties. Research shows that S. enissocaesilis, closely related to Streptomyces strains in Clade 1, produces bioactive compounds with antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anticancer properties. These compounds include indolocarbazole alkaloids, decoyinine, cycloheximide, daunorubicin, and hygromycin B58. While the antibacterial properties of these metabolites are well known, their varied bioactivity suggests that S. enissocaesilis may also possess unknown antiviral potential. There are not many studies on the antiviral capabilities of S. geysiriensis, closely related to Streptomyces strains in Clade 1, but the genus is recognized for producing various complex secondary metabolites, implying that further research is necessary. Conversely, S. ardesiacus, closely related to Streptomyces strains in Clade 5, produces angucycline glycosides, which have shown strong antibacterial and anticancer properties59. It also produces rare antibacterial phenazine metabolites60. Given the range of bioactive metabolites found in S. ardesiacus, it is plausible that this species also produces antiviral metabolites. S. indicus, closely related to Streptomyces strains in Clade 6, produces two fatty acids: isopalmitic acid and 13-methyltetradecanoic acid61. S. indicus presents as an attractive candidate for additional antiviral research due to reports indicating that these fatty acids have anticancer qualities and antiviral effects against spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV)62,63. Clades 1, 5, and 6 may be promising for developing antiviral drugs based on their combined phylogenetic information and known bioactivities.

We focused on mass spectrometry data generated in the positive polarity because this mode provides enhanced ionization efficiency for various metabolites relevant to this study, including alkaloids, macrolides, peptides, and certain lipids commonly found in marine microbes64,65. The PCA (Fig. 2a) of LCMS-QTOF data shows that Streptomyces strains exhibit significant metabolic diversity and cluster according to their phylogenetic clades. Clade 1 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. enissocaesilis and S. geysiriensis, red) and Clade 5 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. ardesiacus, light blue) cluster tightly, indicating significant 3CLpro inhibition. Clade 7 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. ginkgonis, dark blue) and Clade 3 (Streptomyces strains closely related to S. malachitospinus and S. olivaceus, yellow) show greater metabolic variance, suggesting a wider bioactivity range that warrants further study to identify new 3CLpro inhibitors. The HCA supports these findings by confirming Clades 1 and 5 as promising 3CLpro inhibitors. It also identifies outliers that may represent unique bioactive metabolites with different processes. HCA dendrograms (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. S6) illustrate the distinct metabolomic profiles of the strains as indicated by branch height. The strong 3CLpro inhibitory activity of strains with shorter branch lengths, such as those in Clades 1 and 5, correlates with substantial similarity in metabolite biosynthesis (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. S6). In contrast, strains from Clades 7 and 3, particularly those with longer branch lengths (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. S6), exhibit greater dissimilarity, indicating a broader range of metabolites, some of which may be less bioactive (Fig. 2b).

This study highlights the strengths and limitations of using automated workflows for untargeted metabolomic analysis, especially when comparing microbial taxa with diverse metabolic outputs. Software platforms like Progenesis QI streamline data processing by automating peak detection, alignment, and statistical comparisons across large datasets. However, the results show that automated pipelines, while effective for data handling, may fall short in analyses that require high biological accuracy. Several metabolite features initially identified as shared across clades were found, upon manual inspection, to be inconsistently detected or absent in certain strains. These inconsistencies were linked to known limitations in automated processing, including retention time shifts, m/z misalignment, and feature grouping errors. Such artifacts, if left uncorrected, may result in false interpretations of metabolomic similarity. To address these limitations, a manual validation step was implemented after intensity-based filtering. This approach refined the dataset by retaining only features consistently observed with high signal quality across replicates. The resulting feature set diminished the influence of processing artifacts and provided a more accurate representation of metabolite distribution across the studied clades. This refinement enhanced the quality of comparative analyses and improved dereplication efforts. The findings underscore the importance of integrating automated data processing with manual curation to enhance analytical reliability and support accurate biological interpretation.

Metabolite profiling of Streptomyces strains revealed distinct chemical signatures that correlated with clade classification and antiviral activity. PCA of LCMS-QTOF data showed clear clustering of Clades 1, 5, and 6, all of which exhibited high inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The consistent separation of these clades in both PCA and HCA suggests that metabolic profiles reflect both phylogenetic relationships and functional traits. To identify features contributing to this clustering, high-abundance m/z values (≥ 5 × 10⁷) were analyzed across these clades. However, we recognize that low-intensity metabolites may exhibit significant biological activity despite their reduced detectability on the current analytical platform. By focusing on metabolites more likely to demonstrate notable and meaningful bioactivity while reducing noise or the risk of false positives, applying the mass intensity threshold of 5 × 10⁷ enhanced the reliability of the analysis.

The Venn diagram (Fig. 4c) illustrates the similarities and differences among metabolites across three groups of Streptomyces strains related to S. enissocaesilis and S. geysiriensis in Clade 1, S. ardesiacus in Clade 5, and S. indicus in Clade 6. This metabolic distribution reflects evolutionary and biosynthetic principles, such as the role of conserved biosynthetic gene clusters66, the evolutionary dynamics of natural product diversity67, and the integration of primary metabolic pathways into secondary metabolism for ecological adaptation67,68. These studies emphasize how niche-specific adaptations and conserved pathways operate together as the main drivers of microbial metabolic diversity. The Venn diagram reveals that Clades 1, 5, and 6 collectively share 101 metabolites, accounting for 49.03% of the total. This finding suggests that the fundamental biosynthetic pathways in these groups have likely been preserved over time, possibly due to shared BGCs66. These metabolites frequently play roles in ecological processes such as interspecies communication, nutrient intake, and defense against competitors or predators66, making them promising candidates for broad antiviral efficacy, including anti-SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro inhibition. Unique metabolites in Clades 1 (0.49%), 5 (17.48%), and 6 (19.90%) signify niche-specific adaptations and specialized biosynthetic pathways, likely resulting from evolutionary processes like gene duplication and horizontal gene transfer (HGT)66,68.

Among the 18 most intense metabolites, 12 were annotated based on database matching and MS/MS spectral data, which include several structurally distinct classes. The most abundant compound, 35-aminobacteriohopane-32,33,34-triol 1, belongs to the biohopanoid class comprised of pentacyclic triterpenoid lipids originating from the bacterial hopane core69–71. This class of bacterial triterpenoids is known to contribute to membrane integrity, environmental stress tolerance, and cellular development in actinomycetes72. Its consistent presence in bioactive strains supports a role in physiological adaptation, particularly under nutrient-limited or variable oxygen conditions common in marine sediments73. The breakdown products of hopanoids are commonly found in sediments and serve as useful biomarkers in geochemistry71,74. These observations support the hypothesis that the production of 1 could be affected by the distinct ecological conditions present in the marine sediment environment. Notably, each strain of Streptomyces species examined in this study was sourced from marine sediment samples, an origin that provides substantial context for the production of hopanoids. Fatty acid derivatives such as 9-octadecenamide 2, ethyl 8-oxooctadec-9-enoate 10, and palmitamide 12 were also prominent. These molecules, which comprise amides and esters, likely serve structural roles in membrane lipid composition or act as signaling compounds75–78. Their abundance in active clades may reflect adjustments in membrane dynamics in response to environmental pressure.

Four diketopiperazines, namely cyclo(Pro-Ile) 3, cyclo(Phe-Pro) 6, and two hydroxylated derivatives 7 and 8, were also identified. Cyclic dipeptides, or 2,5-diketopiperazines (DKPs), are small, naturally occurring cyclic peptides formed from the condensation of two amino acids79. These cyclic dipeptides are commonly produced by Streptomyces and other bacteria and have been associated with antimicrobial activity and signaling functions80–83. DKPs are involved in regulating developmental processes, including sporulation and biofilm formation, and can influence the expression of secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters81,84. Their presence in these Streptomyces strains supports their role in intercellular signaling and microbial competition or communication. Additional shared compounds included 4-vinylphenol85 4, a phenolic compound; N-acetyltyramine86,87 9, an aromatic amine; piptamine88 5, an alkylated aromatic amine; and calamenene89 11, an aromatic sesquiterpenoid. These compounds are structurally diverse but share reported biological activities, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, and membrane-modulating effect85,86,88,89. Their repeated detection in strains with antiviral activity suggests that Streptomyces species may rely on chemically diverse secondary metabolites to adapt to competitive environments and modulate host–pathogen interactions.

Clade-specific metabolites provided further evidence of ecological and functional differentiation. Clade 5 uniquely produced prodigiosin derivatives such as metacycloprodigiosin90 13 and undecylprodigiosin91 14, compounds known for their cytotoxic, immunosuppressive, and antiparasitic activities90. Clade 6 was characterized by coproporphyrin III 15, a porphyrin-like intermediate associated with tetrapyrrole biosynthesis92–94. The presence of this metabolite may reflect a physiological response to redox conditions or involvement in iron acquisition pathways. Clade 1 was distinguished by an uncharacterized metabolite at m/z 296.1180 [M + H]+ (C20H13N3, DBE = 15.5), for which no database match was found. Its high unsaturation suggests a polyaromatic structure, which may represent a previously unreported class of secondary metabolites.

The identification of both shared and clade-specific metabolites has important implications for understanding natural product biosynthesis in Streptomyces. The presence of structurally diverse metabolites across phylogenetically distinct but bioactive clades suggests that key biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) are either conserved or horizontally transferred among marine Streptomyces lineages occupying similar ecological niches66. The detection of core metabolites such as hopanoids, diketopiperazines, and fatty acid amides across Clades 1, 5, and 6 implies that these biosynthetic pathways are central to cellular adaptation and survival in sediment environments66–68,73. In contrast, the clade-specific production of specialized metabolites like prodigiosins and coproporphyrins suggests the existence of unique regulatory or enzymatic modules that may be influenced by environmental factors or niche-specific pressures66,67. Mapping these metabolite signatures to genomic data will be a crucial next step in linking specific BGCs with observed metabolite profiles and exploring their potential for drug discovery.

One of the most striking findings from the OPLS-DA was the detection of a metabolite with the highest VIP score that could not be matched to any known compound in current databases. This feature, consistently present in antiviral-active strains, exhibited spectral properties indicative of halogenation (Br), an uncommon but notable trait among microbial natural products. Its strong statistical weight and absence from reference libraries point to the possibility that it may be a previously unreported compound with potential biological relevance. Additional unannotated features were also observed among the top discriminant metabolites. Some exhibited fragmentation patterns suggestive of lipid-derived structures, and like the halogenated feature, were consistently enriched in active clades. While their precise identities remain unknown, their presence across multiple active strains and contribution to group separation suggest they may play a role in the observed antiviral activity. These findings underscore both the chemical novelty within marine sediment-derived Streptomyces and the limitations of relying solely on existing databases for metabolite identification. To move forward, targeted isolation and structural elucidation will be essential, along with genome mining to explore the biosynthetic origins of these compounds. Clarifying the identity and function of these unknown features could lead to the discovery of new scaffolds with antiviral potential and may help expand our understanding of secondary metabolism in marine actinomycetes.

Alongside these unknown features, the analysis also revealed several metabolites that align with well-established biosynthetic pathways and known biological functions. Albaflavenone 16 is a bicyclic sesquiterpenoid produced through the mevalonate-independent terpenoid biosynthesis pathway95,96. It has been previously reported from terrestrial Streptomyces and is known for its antibacterial activity97. Streptokordin 17, an aromatic polyketide with a naphthoquinone core, is likely synthesized by type II polyketide synthases and represents a class of compounds that often exhibit cytotoxicity against cancer cells98,99. Anaephene A 18, an alkyl-substituted phenol, represents a compound class recognized for disrupting membrane integrity and inhibiting microbial growth100,101. Additionally, a cluster of biologically active fatty acid amides, namely heptadecenamide 19, linoleamide 23, and linoleoyl ethanolamide 24102,103, was detected. These metabolites, either amides or ethanolamides, are typically synthesized through the amidation of long-chain fatty acids and may reflect membrane remodeling or energy storage processes in Streptomyces species77.

Streptocarbazole C 21 is a carbazole alkaloid structurally related to known microbial metabolites produced via tryptophan-derived pathways104–106. Carbazole alkaloids are documented in actinomycetes and are linked to various biological activities, including cytotoxicity and antimicrobial effects106,107. The presence of this compound in antiviral-active strains highlights the chemical richness of Streptomyces secondary metabolism and the potential relevance of nitrogen-containing aromatic scaffolds. Additionally, 4-hydroxyphenylglycine 22, a non-proteinogenic aromatic amino acid, is often found in the biosynthetic gene clusters of glycopeptide antibiotics108,109. Its presence in active strains may reflect either a precursor function or direct biological activity. Besides known metabolites, three features with no database matches were retained based on their high VIP scores and spectral characteristics consistent with lipid derivatives. Their consistent occurrence in bioactive strains suggests that they could signify compounds that have not been previously characterized, yet may hold biological significance. It is important to note that metabolite identification in this study was primarily based on dereplication using accurate mass measurements and spectral matches from databases such as GNPS, PubChem, and StreptomeDB. For features not represented in these databases, we inferred structural annotations from MS/MS fragmentation and chromatographic behavior. While these approaches provide reasonable confidence, we have not confirmed these identifications using authentic standards. We recognize that standard-based validation and further structural confirmation through isolation and NMR spectroscopy are needed, and we consider this an important next step to confirm the bioactive compounds and clarify their role in 3CLpro inhibition.

The identification of piptamine 5, calamenene 11, and palmitamide 12 in both PCA and OPLS-DA underscores their statistical robustness and biological relevance within the metabolomic profiles of antiviral Streptomyces strains. PCA, an unsupervised method, captures the overall variance in high-dimensional data without incorporating class labels and is useful for revealing intrinsic sample groupings based on shared features110,111. The presence of these three metabolites among the high-loading variables in the PCA model indicates that they are not only abundant but also contribute significantly to the natural variance observed across samples, particularly among the bioactive clades. In contrast, OPLS-DA is a supervised method that maximizes separation between predefined groups112,113, which in this case, are Streptomyces strains classified by antiviral activity. The appearance of the same three metabolites as top discriminant features in the OPLS-DA model suggests that they are also strongly associated with the biological phenotype of interest. This dual occurrence across both unsupervised and supervised models provides cross-validation within the statistical framework and reduces the likelihood that these features are artifacts of model overfitting, a known concern in supervised approaches when applied to complex datasets with limited sample sizes110–113. From a statistical standpoint, such overlap enhances confidence in the reliability of the selected features. It reflects consistency in their contribution to both intrinsic data structure (PCA) and class-specific variance (OPLS-DA). In the context of untargeted metabolomics, where thousands of features may be detected and inter-feature correlations are high, the recurrence of specific metabolites across different analytical models provides a powerful criterion for prioritization. These findings support the utility of combining unsupervised and supervised multivariate analyses to extract meaningful biological information from complex chemical data and to identify metabolite markers that are both structurally conserved and functionally relevant. More importantly, attention should be drawn to the fact that the performance of multivariate models such as PCA and OPLS-DA is highly dependent on data quality and consistency. Variability in ionization efficiency, chromatographic retention, or sample preparation may introduce technical bias and affect feature selection. Collectively, metabolites identified through PCA and OPLS-DA constitute the metabolic signature of antiviral Streptomyces, highlighting a strong association between phylogenetic identity and secondary metabolite production. The recurrence of high-abundance features across multiple active clades suggests conserved biosynthetic pathways linked to antiviral activity, whereas clade-specific metabolites point to functional diversification likely shaped by environmental adaptation and ecological factors. Hence, integrating statistical modeling with metabolomic and phylogenetic data enabled the delineation of both conserved and lineage-specific metabolic traits with antiviral relevance.

While these multivariate statistical tools reveal meaningful patterns in metabolomic data, their interpretation is enhanced when supported by complementary computational approaches. The complexity of microbial metabolomes, combined with limitations in dereplication and metabolite annotation, presents challenges in directly linking specific features to observed bioactivity. In this context, computational tools such as molecular docking and toxicity prediction were utilized to strengthen the interpretation of the metabolomic data. Our molecular docking analysis provided further support for bioactivity. Streptocarbazole C 21, a carbazole alkaloid104,106, demonstrated the strongest predicted binding affinity to 3CLpro (− 9.80 ± 0.30 kcal/mol), surpassing the known covalent inhibitor GC376 (− 7.67 ± 0.03 kcal/mol). This high binding energy is notable, particularly considering that GC376 is a peptidomimetic inhibitor specifically designed to target the catalytic dyad of 3CLpro29. In contrast, 21 is a naturally occurring small molecule with a rigid polyaromatic core104. The compound formed an extensive hydrogen bonding network with catalytically essential residues (CYS145, SER144, GLY143, LEU141, and GLN189) and engaged HIS41 through π-cation interaction, which is a crucial residue in the catalytic mechanism of 3CLpro. Additional conventional hydrogen bonding and π-donor hydrogen bonding interactions with CYS145, as well as van der Waals contacts with residues such as THR25, THR26, MET49, HIS163, and PHE140, further stabilized its binding conformation.

The strong binding observed for 21 may be attributed to its planar aromatic scaffold, which enables favorable stacking interactions, and its polar functional groups that facilitate hydrogen bonding deep within the substrate-binding pocket114,115. These features allow the compound to occupy both the S1 and S2 subsites of the protease, mimicking substrate engagement and potentially disrupting catalysis117–119. Although docking does not account for enzyme flexibility or solvent effects, the consistent and robust interaction profile of 21 provides compelling evidence for its potential as a non-peptidic 3CLpro inhibitor. This finding supports the hypothesis that metabolites, particularly those with indole and carbazole systems, could serve as promising scaffolds for antiviral drug development114–116. Other compounds, including 11, 12, and 16, also exhibited relevant binding interactions, although with lower affinity. These results are consistent with their identification as discriminant features in the OPLS-DA model. It is important to highlight, however, that the docking studies offer a static view of ligand–protein interactions and do not account for important factors such as solvation, protein flexibility, or compound bioavailability. Binding affinity alone does not equate to biological activity and must be confirmed in experimental systems.

In order to predict potential toxicity, organ-specific effects were assessed using ProTox-II44. Neurotoxicity emerged as the predominant anticipated adverse effect among the compounds evaluated. Streptocarbazole C 21 has been predicted to demonstrate respiratory toxicity, potentially associated with its polyaromatic structure. Only two compounds, anaephene A 18 and linoleoyl ethanolamide 24, lacked predicted organ-specific toxicity, suggesting a more favorable safety profile. Importantly, no compounds were associated with hepatotoxicity or cardiotoxicity under the tested parameters. While computational tools offer a preliminary assessment of safety, their predictions rely on training datasets that may not adequately represent the complex nature of natural products. Experimental validation in cellular models will be needed to confirm both antiviral activity and cytotoxicity.

Taken together, the integration of phylogenetics, metabolomics, statistical modeling, docking, and toxicity prediction enabled the identification and prioritization of candidate antiviral metabolites from marine sediment-derived Streptomyces species. Streptocarbazole C 21 stands out as a lead compound with a high predicted affinity and a mechanistically plausible interaction profile with SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. Future work will emphasize the isolation, structural confirmation, in vitro enzyme inhibition, in vivo assays, and toxicity testing of this and its related compounds to evaluate their therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that marine sediment-derived Streptomyces species are a valuable source of metabolites with inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. The most active strains were confined to Clades 1, 5, and 6, associated with S. enissocaesilis, S. geysiriensis, S. ardesiacus, and S. indicus. Untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed that these clades exhibited distinct metabolomic signatures, particularly enriched in biohopanoids, diketopiperazines, fatty acid amides, and aromatic compounds. Concurrently, specific metabolites with putative antiviral activity were identified, including a bicyclic sesquiterpenoid, an aromatic polyketide, an alkylphenol, fatty acid derivatives, alkylated aromatic amines, lipid-derived metabolites, and a carbazole alkaloid. The combined findings reveal a consistent association between defined metabolic features and antiviral bioactivity, reinforcing the utility of phylogeny-informed metabolomics for identifying bioactive natural products.

To prioritize candidate metabolites from complex untargeted datasets and evaluate their therapeutic potential, molecular docking and in silico toxicity assessments were applied. Streptocarbazole C, a well-characterized aromatic carbazole alkaloid, demonstrated a stable and energetically favorable binding pose within the catalytic dyad residues HIS41 and CYS145 of SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro. These findings support its candidacy as a selective 3CLpro inhibitor and provide a rational starting point for subsequent structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies and medicinal chemistry optimization.

Overall, our findings underscore the value of combining phylogeny-guided metabolomics with functional screening and bioinformatics to elucidate relationships between microbial taxonomy, chemical diversity, and bioactivity. This multidisciplinary approach establishes a robust framework for future natural product discovery by leveraging evolutionary and ecological context alongside computational analysis to access the largely untapped biosynthetic potential of marine actinomycetes.
