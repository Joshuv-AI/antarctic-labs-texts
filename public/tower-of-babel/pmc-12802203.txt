USP18 confers protection against allergic asthma by suppressing CCL8 production in alveolar type II epithelial cells
Authors: Huihui Zhang, Zhi Rao, Xiaoke Liu, Yahai Shu, Wuju Zhang, Jiachun Cai, Zehui Yao, Ying Lin, Zhiqing Cai, Zhimin Zhang, Pinglin Lai, Sheng Zhang, Yuxin Wang, Hong Wang, Wenping Chen, Wenjun Mao, Ran Wei, Xiaochun Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlveolar epithelial type II (AT2) cells participate in epithelial repair and lung immune defense, while the detailed molecular mechanisms through which AT2 cells regulate inflammatory immune responses in asthma remain unclear. Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18) has been implicated in immune regulation, but its role in asthma pathogenesis is not fully understood.MethodsUSP18 expression in AT2 cells was analyzed in both mouse models of allergic asthma and asthmatic patients. Functional studies were conducted using USP18 knockout mice, AT2 cell-specific chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 8 (CCL8) knockdown, and exogenous CCL8 treatment. Th2 responses, eosinophil recruitment, and chemokine production were assessed. Mechanistic investigations focused on USP18-mediated regulation of SOCS1 stability and downstream ERK-STAT3 signaling.ResultsUSP18 is increased in AT2 cells from both mouse models of asthma and asthmatic patients, and played a protective role in the progression of allergic asthma by suppressing Th2 responses and reducing CCL8 production. Notably, USP18 deficiency causes increased CCL8 in AT2 cells, which in turn recruits Th2 cells and eosinophils, thereby exacerbating allergic asthma. Consistently, CCL8 treatment induced asthmatic inflammation and knocking down CCL8 in AT2 cells of USP18 knockout mice alleviates asthma symptoms. Mechanistically, USP18 stabilizes SOCS1 by inhibiting its ubiquitination and degradation, leading to reduced CCL8 production through the ERK-STAT3 signaling pathway in a negative feedback loop.ConclusionsOverall, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized role for USP18 in regulating CCL8 production during asthma progression, highlighting USP18 and CCL8 as potential therapeutic targets for asthma treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03433-0.Keywords: Asthma, USP18, AT2 cells, CCL8, SOCS1

Background

Asthma, a prevalent chronic inflammatory respiratory disease, is characterized by chest tightness, breathlessness, wheezing, and cough. Patients with asthma suffer from impaired lung function because of airway hyperresponsiveness, epithelial cell damage, airway remodeling, mucus hypersecretion, and inflammatory mediator production [1]. Current treatments such as inhaled corticosteroids, anti-inflammatory drugs, antiallergics, and rapid-acting bronchodilators often do not produce a consistent effect because of the diverse endotypes of asthma, each with its distinct primary inflammatory cell types and varying treatment responses [2]. During the development of asthma, immune cells infiltrate the airways, and lead to the production of mast cells and immunoglobulin (Ig) E through type 2 helper (Th2) cytokine secretion, thereby producing the symptoms of asthma [3]. Thus, targeting and reducing inflammation in the respiratory system is a promising approach for asthma management. Despite considerable efforts to elucidate its pathogenesis and improve management, the molecular immune mechanisms remain poorly understood, and current therapeutic options often yield unsatisfactory results. Consequently, it is necessary to explore the pathophysiological mechanisms of asthma, and discover novel therapeutic targets.

Asthma progression involves various pulmonary cells, including alveolar epithelia, T cells, dendritic cells, B cells, and alveolar macrophages (AMs). Alveolar epithelia maintain pulmonary homeostasis, and also contribute to asthma pathogenesis. The distal alveolar microenvironment is complex and dynamic, supporting efficient gas exchange, orchestrating immune responses to environmental stimuli, and maintaining a tight barrier to prevent fluid accumulation in the alveoli [4]. The alveolar epithelium consists of alveolar epithelial type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) cells. AT1 cells primarily facilitate gas exchange, whereas AT2 cells serve as stem cells for epithelial repair and secrete surfactant proteins to protect the lungs [5, 6]. In normal conditions, the intercellular junctions tightly link AT2 cells, enabling effective cell-cell communication [7]. Impairment of the alveolar epithelial barrier can lead to acute and chronic respiratory diseases, such as asthma [8], pulmonary fibrosis [9], and emphysema [10], which are often diagnosed at advanced stages and require complex treatments. Moreover, AT2 cells secrete various proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines to attract and activate macrophages, Th2 cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils, exacerbating asthma severity [11]. Given their functional versatility, AT2 cells are considered a promising target for asthma treatment. However, the specific genetic regulation and immune mechanisms through which AT2 cells influence cellular interactions within the inflammatory microenvironment of asthma are not completely understood, and require extensive investigation.

Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18), is primarily a deubiquitinating enzyme that plays a critical role in regulating type I interferon signaling. Dysregulation of USP18 has been associated with numerous diseases, including autoimmune diseases [12], cancers [13], viral infections [14], and inflammatory diseases [15]. Chemokines, produced locally at inflammatory sites, regulate immune cell migration to damaged tissues. Although airway inflammation is a primary pathological feature of asthma, the key inflammatory regulators remain unidentified. Chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 8 (CCL8), also known as monocyte chemoattractant protein-2, participates in monocyte migration and inflammatory response. It has been implicated in autoimmune disorders [16], cancer [17], and more recently, coronavirus disease 2019-related complications [18]. This chemokine is involved in the regulation of Th2 cell responses, which are pivotal in the pathogenesis of asthma [19]. In the context of asthma, elevated levels of CCL8 have been reported in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and serum of asthmatic patients, suggesting its potential role in the disease’s inflammatory processes [20]. Studies have shown that CCL8 can promote the recruitment of eosinophils and Th2 cells to the airways, contributing to the exacerbation of asthma symptoms [21]. Furthermore, CCL8 has been implicated in the modulation of airway hyperresponsiveness and the production of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby amplifying the asthmatic response [22]. However, whether USP18 regulates CCL8 production in asthma remains unknown. In this study, we found that USP18 expression in AT2 cells was elevated in asthma and played a protective role in the progression of allergic asthma by suppressing Th2 responses and reducing CCL8 production. Furthermore, we identified a novel molecular pathway in which USP18 functions as a negative regulator of CCL8 expression through the stabilization of suppressor of cytokine signaling 1(SOCS1) and subsequent inhibition of ERK-STAT3 signaling. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated knockdown of CCL8 ameliorated allergic asthma in USP18 knockout mice. Overall, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized role for USP18 in regulating CCL8 production during asthma progression, highlighting USP18 and CCL8 as potential therapeutic targets for asthma treatment.

Methods

Mice

The Usp18+/− mice used in this study were kindly provided by Prof. Chengjiang Gao (Shandong University, China) and were originally established by Prof. Dong-er Zhang from the University of California, San Diego. The Usp18+/− mice were established on a mixed Sv129-C57BL/6 background and subsequently crossed to breed Usp18+/+ and Usp18−/− mice [23]. All mice were housed in specific pathogen-free animal facilities at Southern Medical University (Guangdong, China). All mice used in this study were female. The animal experiments (SYXK2021-0167) were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Research of Southern Medical University.

Reagents

OVA (A5503), DNase I (10104159001), and collagenase D (11088866001) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, and HDM protein extract (XPB82D3A2.5) was obtained from Greer Laboratory. Imject Alum adjuvant (77161), Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015), and the mouse IgE ELISA kit (88–50460-22) were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific, the mouse CCL8 ELISA kit (DY790) was purchased from R&D Systems, and recombinant murine CCL8 (CS45) was sourced from NovoProtein. The BCA protein assay kit (P0010S), RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B), and cell lysis buffer for western and IP (P0013) were obtained from Beyotime. The antifade mounting medium (containing DAPI, G1407-25ML) was purchased from Servicebio, and MitoTracker Deep Red FM (8778) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. The TSA Multi-color IF System Kit (10001100020) was obtained from PANOVUE, multicolor prestained protein ladder (WJ103) was sourced from EpiZyme, and True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (424401) and Zombie UV Fixable Viability Kit (423108) were purchased from BioLegend. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture

Human HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063), HeLa (RRID: CVCL_0030), and BEAS-2B cells (RRID: CVCL_0168) as well as mouse MLE-12 cells (RRID: CVCL_3751) were obtained from ATCC, and cultured in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) or DMEM/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT) with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Bio, FSP500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo, 15140122). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All experiments were performed with mycoplasma-free cells.

Animal models

For the OVA-induced asthma model, 6–8-week-old mice were immunized intraperitoneally on days 0 and 10 with 75 µg OVA in 100 µL PBS emulsified in 100 µL Imject Alum adjuvant for a total volume of 200 µL. On days 21–23, the mice were intratracheally challenged with 50 µg OVA in 40 µL PBS. On day 25, the mice were sacrificed, and their BALF, peripheral blood, and lung tissues were collected. Meanwhile, for the HDM-induced asthma model, mice (6–8 weeks old) were first sensitized with HDM (10 µg in 40 µL PBS) intranasally on day 0 and then intranasally challenged with 10 µg HDM in 40 µL PBS from days 7 to 11. On day 14, the mice were sacrificed and samples were collected [24, 25].

Transfection of AAV6-shCCL8 in mice

The AAV6 vectors encoding the green fluorescence protein reporter and incorporating a DNA sequence to silence CCL8 was produced by GenePharma (China). Mice were intratracheally injected with AAV at a dose of 2 × 1011 vg in 40 µL PBS, as described previously [26]. Thereafter, CCL8 expression in the infected lung AT2 cells was assessed 21 days after AAV injection. Subsequently, the mice were sensitized and challenged with OVA according to a standard protocol [27], and ultimately sacrificed 48 h after the final challenge.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

For collecting BALF cells, lung lavage was performed by flushing with 1 mL PBS twice. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was analyzed for cytokine and total protein levels. The cell pellet, after red blood cell (RBC) lysis, was resuspended in PBS to assess the total cell number and to be stained for flow cytometry. In addition, the lung tissues were perfused with sterile PBS, followed by the removal of lung lobes, and digestion with collagenase D (1.0 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) on a shaking incubator at 180 rpm for 1 h at 37 °C. To obtain single cells, digested lung tissues were filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer. The cells were stained with indicated antibody combinations after RBC lysis. Next, the cells were pretreated with Fc-blocking anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody and stained with flow antibodies for 1 h at 4 °C for surface labeling. For the intranuclear staining of GATA3, lung tissue cells were first surface-stained with antibodies and then treated with the True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set and an anti-GATA3 antibody. Dead cells were removed using the Zombie UV Fixable Viability Kit. Finally, all samples were analyzed using flow cytometry (LSR Fortessa SORP for cytometry and FACS Aria IIU for sorting; BD Biosciences), and data were evaluated using the FlowJo software. Each cell type in BALF was characterized as AMs (CD11c+Siglec-F+), eosinophils (Eos, CD11c−Siglec-F+), and neutrophils (Neu, Ly6G+), whereas each cell lineage in the lung tissue was determined as AMs (CD45+CD64+CD11c+Siglec-F+), neutrophils (CD45+CD64−Ly6G+CD11b+), eosinophils (CD45+CD64−CD11c−Siglec-F+), leukocytes (CD45+), endothelial cells (CD45−CD31+Epcam−), mesenchymal cells (CD45−CD31−Epcam−), airway epithelial cells (CD45−CD31−Epcam+CD104+ MHCⅡ−), AT2 cells (CD45−CD31−Epcam+ CD104−MHCⅡ+), and Th2 cells (CD45+CD3+CD8−CD4+GATA3+).

RNA isolation and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was quality-checked, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and circularized. Sequencing was then performed using combinatorial probe–anchor synthesis. Both RNA-seq and analysis were performed by BGI Genomics Co., Ltd.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and immunoblot (IB) analyses

IP and IB assays were performed according to previously established methods [27]. First, cells were lysed using Cell lysis buffer for Western and IP (Beyotime, P0013) supplemented with a protease inhibitor (Roche, 5892791001). After centrifugation at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected and incubated with protein A/G agarose beads (Smart-Lifesciences, SA032025) for 1 h to remove any nonspecific protein binding. The protein extracts were then incubated overnight with continuous rotation at 4 °C with the relevant antibody and protein A/G agarose beads. The immune complexes were washed and analyzed using IB, for which proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. After incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, the membrane blots were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection kit (PerkinElmer, NEL105001EA) and imaged with the Tanon chemiluminescence imaging system.

Cell IF staining

The cells were fixed with 4% buffered paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with Triton X-100. Next, they were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin to prevent nonspecific binding, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by treatment with fluorescent secondary antibodies. The nuclei and mitochondria were stained with DAPI and MitoTracker, respectively, and images were acquired using the laser confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200).

Histopathology analysis

After paraformaldehyde fixation, dehydration and paraffin embedding, lung tissues were sectioned into 5 μm slices, and placed on adhesive microscope slides. Next, following deparaffinization, the samples were stained with H&E, PAS, and Masson’s trichrome according to the manufacturer’s instructions to assess inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus production, and collagen deposition. For IHC and IF of tissue analyses, the deparaffinized lung sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Finally, images were captured using the upright fluorescence microscope (Olympus, BX43) or the laser confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from lung tissues or cells using an RNA extraction kit (EZBioscience, RN001-plus or B0004D), and reverse-transcribed into cDNA via the HiScript QRT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) Kit (Vazyme, R123-01) as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. Gene-specific qRT-PCR was performed using primers and probes from ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (High ROX Premixed) (Vazyme, Q341-02) on Roche LightCycler 96. The results were normalized to GAPDH expression, and mRNA levels relative to the control group were evaluated using the 2−△△Ct method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Plasmid construction and transduction

The cDNA of USP18 was extracted from the BEAS-2B cell line using standard PCR, and subsequently cloned into pcDNA3.1-Myc eukaryotic expression vectors, as previously described [27]. Several truncated mutant vectors, including Myc-USP18 51–372 amino acids, Myc-USP18 112–372 amino acids, Myc-USP18 150–372 amino acids, and Myc-USP18 C64S, were constructed using the KOD-Plus-Mutagenesis Kit (SMK-101; Toyobo) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The plasmids encoding SOCS1, SOCS2, SOCS3, Ub, and their mutants are detailed in a previous study [27]. These plasmids were transfected into HEK293T or HeLa cells using Lipofectamine 3000 as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

SiRNA constructs and transduction

The siRNA sequences of USP18 are consistent with those reported in the previous study [28], while the siRNA sequences of CCL8 is screened and confirmed through qRT-PCR. These siRNA sequences, synthesized by GenePharma (China), are listed in Table S3. The siRNAs were transfected into BEAS-2B or MLE-12 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s manual.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (n ≥ 3). The mean values of paired groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test. For comparing the means of multiple groups, two-way analysis of variance was employed, followed by Sidak’s post-hoc test. All data analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8 software.

Results

USP18 is increased in AT2 cells of asthmatic model mice induced by OVA and HDM

To uncover the mechanism of allergic asthma, murine models were established with ovalbumin (OVA) (Fig. 1a) or house dust mite (HDM) (Fig. 1b) through repeated exposure. Subsequently, AT2 cells, characterized by the markers CD45⁻ CD31⁻ EpCAM⁺ CD104⁻ MHC II⁺ [29], were isolated from lung tissue using flow cytometry sorting (Fig. S1a and Slb), followed by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. The analysis identified 1,934 DEGs between the OVA and PBS groups, with 1,410 upregulated and 524 downregulated genes (Fig. 1c and Table S4). In the HDM-induced model, 7,524 DEGs were identified, of which 4,990 were upregulated, and 2,534 were downregulated (Fig. 1 d and Table S5). To elucidate the underlying ubiquitin-related mechanisms, DEGs from OVA and HDM models were overlapped with ubiquitin-, alveolar epithelial cell-, and lung inflammation-related genes from the GeneCards database (Table S6−8). The analysis identified USP18 as a DEG among all groups, with its expression significantly increased in both the OVA and HDM groups (Fig. 1c-e). Likewise, USP18 was significantly higher in AT2 cells than in leukocytes, endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells, and airway epithelial cells (Fig. 1f). Western blot analysis confirmed the elevated USP18 expression in AT2 cells from the OVA and HDM groups (Fig. 1 g h). In addition, USP18 expression was significantly higher in AT2 cells, noted by the AT2 marker surfactant protein C (SFTPC), in the OVA and HDM groups than in PBS controls (Fig. 1i). immunohistochemical (IHC) staining validated the upregulated expression of USP18 in both the OVA and HDM models (Fig. 1j). These results demonstrate that USP18 expression is predominantly upregulated in AT2 cells.

USP18 is upregulated in AT2 cells of allergic asthmatic patients

Given the observed upregulation of USP18 in AT2 cells from mouse models of asthma, we further investigated its role in human allergic asthma. To this end, we reanalyzed a previously published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE193816), which included samples from four allergic asthmatic patients and four allergic non-asthmatic controls (Fig. 2a) [30]. The background information about the donors, including age, sex, and baseline spirometry in Table S9. As shown in Fig. 2b, we identified eight distinct subclusters based on their transcriptional profiles. Cell-type annotation was performed by comparing canonical marker genes with known lineage-specific markers (basal/club/goblet cells were not rigorously evaluated in the analysis but are biologically plausible in this sampling context), supported by differential gene expression analysis. Specific clusters were manually assigned to cell identities, including alveolar type I (AT1), type II (AT2), ciliated epithelial cells, mast cells, T cells, B cells, and mononuclear phagocytes (MNPs), based on the expression of marker (Fig. 2c). Subsequent analysis of USP18 expression in these clusters revealed a significantly elevated expression level in AT2 cells from allergic asthmatic patients compared to allergic non-asthmatic controls (Fig. 2 d and e), consistent with findings in asthmatic mouse models. These results collectively suggest that USP18 is specifically upregulated in AT2 cells in human allergic asthma.

CCL8 in AT2 cells is upregulated in asthma

The current study also aimed to identify specific molecular characteristics involved in regulating AT2 cell function during asthmatic inflammation using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Fig. 3 and Fig. S2) and KEGG analysis (Fig. S3). As depicted in Fig. 3a and b, the molecular function categories of “protein-containing complex binding,” “cytokine receptor activity,” “cytokine binding,” “chemokine activity,” “signaling receptor binding,” and “ubiquitin protein ligase binding” were significantly enriched in both the OVA and HDM models. These findings suggest that USP18 may play a role in regulating cytokine and chemokine production in asthma. In order to screen out the target cytokine or chemokine, DEGs identified from the OVA and HDM models were intersected with DEGs in the public GSE74075 dataset of patients with asthma (Table S10), as well as with genes encoding secreted proteins from the GeneCards database (Table S11 and Fig. 3c). This analysis highlighted several genes, among which Ccl8 was selected because it was significantly upregulated in AT2 cells (Fig. 3c and d). Similarly, CCL8 expression in the induced sputum of patients with asthma was also increased compared with healthy individuals (Fig. 3e). To further explore the potential regulatory relationship between USP18 and CCL8 in asthmatic patients, we examined another human transcriptomic dataset (GSE69497, as GSE74075 did not show the expression of USP18). The analysis revealed a positive correlation between USP18 and CCL8 expression levels in individuals with asthma (Fig. 3f). Meanwhile, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) revealed that Ccl8 mRNA expression was significantly higher in AT2 cells than in other cell types after OVA or HDM treatment (Fig. 3 g). More importantly, CCL8 levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was increased following OVA and HDM challenge (Fig. 3 h). Immunofluorescence (IF) confirmed the colocalization of CCL8 with SFTPC, showing higher expression levels of CCL8 in AT2 cells from both OVA- and HDM-induced murine asthma models (Fig. 3i and Fig. S4a), and IHC results showed increased CCL8 expression in the lung tissue of mice with OVA- and HDM-induced asthma (Fig. 3j). These findings indicate that CCL8 expression in AT2 cells are significantly elevated and positively correlates with USP18 in asthma.

USP18 deficiency potentiates CCL8 production and Th2 response in the allergic asthma induced by OVA or HDM

To assess the impact of USP18 in OVA- and HDM-induced airway inflammation, USP18-deficient mice (referred to as Usp18−/−) were generated by crossbreeding Usp18 +/− mice. PCR confirmed the presence of wild-type (WT) and recombined alleles (Table S2 and Fig. S5a), and western blot verified the complete absence of USP18 protein in the lung tissues of Usp18−/− mice (Fig. S5b). The IF staining revealed that CCL8 in AT2 cells from Usp18−/− mice were more abundant than those from Usp18+/+ mice after OVA or HDM challenge (Fig. 4a and Fig. S6a). The results showed that OVA or HDM stimulation significantly increased the levels of BALF cells and protein (Fig. S6b–d), and elevated IgE levels in serum (Fig. 4b) and BALF (Fig. S6e) in Usp18+/+ mice, with these parameters being even higher in Usp18−/− mice. Flow cytometry revealed that eosinophils, the key indicators of allergic asthma, were significantly increased both in percentage and absolute numbers in Usp18−/− mice in the OVA-induced asthma model (Fig. 4c–e). Similar trends were observed in the HDM asthma model (Fig. S6f–i). Meanwhile, USP18-knockout mice exhibited increased Th2 cell infiltration in the lung tissues (Fig. 4f g) in both the OVA and HDM asthma mouse models. MUC5AC, associated with mucus thickness and deposition, is elevated in patients with asthma [31]. Consistent with this, USP18 deficiency increased MUC5AC production in the airways of mice from both the OVA and HDM groups (Fig. 4 h), suggesting that USP18 prevents goblet cell metaplasia. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), and Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus production, and collagen deposition in Usp18−/− mice compared with Usp18+/+ littermates (Fig. 4i). These findings highlight the critical role of USP18 in suppressing asthmatic inflammation, possibly by reducing the abundance of CCL8 in AT2 cells, which in turn inhibits Th2 responses in allergic asthma.

CCL8 drives the pulmonary recruitment of Th2 cells and eosinophils

To investigate whether CCL8 drives pulmonary asthma-like inflammation in vivo, a murine model was established by administering recombinant CCL8 via repeated intranasal challenges (Fig. 5a). The IF staining of the lung tissues from CCL8-treated mice demonstrated a higher number of CCL8 in AT2 cells marked by both CCL8 and SFTPC compared with the PBS controls (Fig. 5b and Fig. S7a). Immune cell changes in the lung tissues after CCL8 treatment were assessed using flow cytometry (Fig. 5c). Although the percentage of AMs remained unchanged, both the percentages of neutrophils and eosinophils increased upon CCL8 stimulation. The increase in eosinophils was more pronounced, rising approximately threefold compared with controls, with neutrophils increasing nearly twofold (Fig. 5d–g). These results suggest that CCL8 in AT2 cells predominantly induces eosinophil-driven infiltration. Meanwhile, exogenous CCL8 increased the total cell number (Fig. 5 h) and protein level (Fig. 5i) in the BALF, indicating severe microvascular permeability. Flow cytometry results also showed that CCL8 treatment elevated Th2 cells in the lung tissue (Fig. 5j and k). MUC5AC expression in the mouse airway was also increased after CCL8 challenge (Fig. 5 l). Moreover, CCL8 treatment caused asthmatic changes in the mouse lung tissues, characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus production, and collagen deposition (Fig. 5 m). These results suggest that CCL8 can induce pulmonary eosinophil and Th2 cell accumulation.

Reducing CCL8 in AT2 cells alleviates Th2 inflammation and maintains epithelial integrity in the USP18-knockout mice

To identify crucial regulatory elements in OVA- and HDM-induced airway inflammation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed on DEGs identified from these models. The findings revealed several enriched pathways in addition to “asthma,” including “Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation,” and “Th17 cell differentiation.” Of note, the top pathways associated with these DEGs included “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “cell adhesion molecules,” “focal adhesion,” “adherens junction,” “tight junction,” and “gap junction” (Fig. S8a and S8b). Tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, and claudin, along with the adherens junction protein E-cadherin maintain epithelial barrier integrity. Consistent with the data on tight and adherens junctions, exposure to OVA or HDM decreased the protein levels of ZO-1, claudin1, and E-cadherin in AT2 cells, with USP18 deficiency exacerbating these reductions (Fig. 6a). By contrast, USP18-knockout mice exhibited a more pronounced increase in vimentin and fibronectin expression in AT2 cells compared with WT mice after OVA or HDM challenge (Fig. 6b). These findings suggest that USP18 deficiency disrupts lung epithelial integrity.

To further evaluate the causal role of USP18 deficiency exacerbating asthma through increasing CCL8 production in AT2 cells, additional experiments were conducted by intratracheally administering AAV6-shCCL8 to knockdown CCL8 expression in AT2 cells of Usp18−/− mice. To investigate this point, three CCL8 siRNAs (CCL8 siRNA 1, 2, and 3) were designed and synthesized, as detailed in Table S3. The results showed that CCL8 siRNA 1 significantly reduced CCL8 mRNA expression levels compared with the scrambled (Scr) siRNA (Fig. S8c). Therefore, the sequence of CCL8 siRNA 1 was used for AAV6-shCCL8. At day 21 post-AAV injection, infection efficiency was evaluated, which revealed a significant decrease in CCL8 expression in lung AT2 cells treated with AAV6-shCCL8 (Fig. S8d). Next, mice were challenged with OVA to assess the impact of CCL8 knockdown (Fig. 6c). After OVA challenge, Usp18−/− mice treated with CCL8-knockdown AAV exhibited decreased OVA-induced CCL8 expression in AT2 cells (Fig. 6 d and Fig. S8e). CCL8 knockdown also alleviated Th2 inflammation, as evidenced by significantly reduced total cell numbers (Fig. S8f), lower total protein levels in BALF (Fig. S8g), and decreased infiltration of eosinophils (Fig. 6e–g) and Th2 cells (Fig. 6 h and i) in the Usp18−/− mice upon OVA stimulation. Additionally, AAV6-mediated shCCL8 significantly reduced serum IgE levels (Fig. 6j), and airway MUC5AC expression (Fig. 6k) in the OVA-challenged Usp18−/− mice. Meanwhile, after OVA stimulation, CCL8 knockdown Usp18−/− mice exhibited higher ZO-1, claudin1, and E-cadherin levels (Fig. 6 l) and lower vimentin and fibronectin levels (Fig. 6 m) in AT2 cells, indicating reduced lung tissue damage compared with control Usp18−/− mice. Similarly, after the OVA challenge, Usp18−/− mice with CCL8 knockdown showed decreased mucus production, and lower collagen deposition than Usp18−/− mice without CCL8 knockdown (Fig. 6n). These findings illustrate that USP18 modulates Th2 inflammation by maintaining epithelial cell integrity and reducing CCL8 in AT2 cells.

USP18 positively regulates CCL8 in AT2 cells by targeting SOCS1 in the asthma

To elucidate the mechanism through which USP18 regulates allergic asthma development, the GO analysis of DEGs identified from OVA and HDM models was performed. As shown in Fig. 7a and b, the following biological process categories were enriched: “immune response,” “inflammatory response,” “positive regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade,” “positive regulation of chemokine production,” “protein phosphorylation,” and “cell-cell adhesion.” These results suggest that DEGs involved in ERK signaling regulate CCL8 in AT2 cells in the asthma. In line with this, higher levels of phosphorylated STAT3 and ERK were detected in USP18-knockdown mouse lung epithelial (MLE)−12 cells than in WT cells upon HDM stimulation (Fig. 7c). As STAT3 activation is negatively regulated by stabilizes SOCS family proteins (SOCS1, SOCS2, and SOCS3), the expression of these proteins was assessed. The results revealed that HDM significantly decreased SOCS1 levels in USP18-knockdown cells, while the levels of SOCS2 and SOCS3 levels remained unchanged compared with WT MLE-12 (Fig. 7c). Supporting these data, patients with asthma exhibited elevated SOCS1 gene expression compared with the healthy individuals (Fig. 7 d). To validate the role of USP18 in STAT3 activation, the subcellular localization of USP18 was assessed in HDM-stimulated MLE-12 cells using IF microscopy. As shown in Fig. 7e and Fig. S9a, the level and proportion of the nuclear translocation of STAT3 were enhanced in USP18-knockdown cells. Moreover, USP18 knockdown worsened the reduction in epithelial ZO-1, claudin1, and E-cadherin protein levels induced by HDM in MLE-12 cells (Fig. 7f). These results indicate that USP18 potentially suppresses the phosphorylation and activation of STAT3 and ERK by upregulating SOCS1 expression, thereby maintaining the integrity of tight junction components, and coordinating epithelial cell and immune cell interactions during asthma inflammation.

USP18 interacts with SOCS1 and mediates its K48-linked deubiquitination

It was unclear how the USP18-induced upregulation of SOCS1 expression affected asthma inflammation. As USP18 is a mitochondria-resident DUB and reduced HDM-induced SOCS1 accumulation was observed in USP18-deficient conditions (Fig. 7c), we hypothesized that USP18 counteracts the ubiquitin system by targeting SOCS1. To test this hypothesis, the interaction between USP18 and SOCS1 was first assessed. The results demonstrated that USP18 specifically bound to SOCS1 in the HEK293T cell overexpression system, whereas no interaction was observed with SOCS2 or SOCS3 (Fig. 8a). To identify the precise interaction region, three USP18 mutants were constructed based on their functional motifs. As shown in Fig. 8b, the interaction between USP18 and SOCS1 was disrupted in both the USP18 (112–372) and USP18 (150–372) mutants, highlighting the critical role of amino acids 51–112 in USP18 for this interaction. Meanwhile, confocal microscopy revealed that the overexpression of WT USP18 and USP18 (51–372) in HeLa cells resulted in their colocalization with SOCS1, whereas the overexpression of the USP18 (112–372) and USP18 (150–372) mutants did not exhibit such colocalization (Fig. 8c). Since USP18 is known to localize to mitochondria, the subcellular localization of USP18 and SOCS1 was evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 8e, WT USP18 and the USP18 (51–372) mutant colocalized with SOCS1 in the mitochondria, with no such colocalization observed with the USP18 (112–372) and USP18 (150–372) mutants. These findings indicate that the amino acids 51–112 of USP18 are important for its interaction with SOCS1, forming a USP18-SOCS1 complex in the mitochondria.

As USP18 regulated HDM-induced SOCS1 protein accumulation and directly interacted with SOCS1, whether USP18 regulates SOCS1 degradation through the proteasome pathway was next evaluated. The reduction in SOCS1 levels was effectively blocked by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 in USP18-knockdown BEAS-2B cells, whereas the lysosome inhibitor NH4Cl did not produce a similar effect (Fig. 8 d). These results suggest that USP18 prevents the proteasome-dependent degradation of SOCS1 protein. Additionally, the direct impact of USP18 on SOCS1 polyubiquitination was examined, and the results showed that USP18 overexpression significantly promoted SOCS1 deubiquitination. By contrast, the transfection of the three truncated USP18 mutants did not reduce SOCS1 polyubiquitination (Fig. 8f). Because distinct ubiquitin linkages are associated with different functions, the specific polyubiquitin linkage on SOCS1 regulated by USP18 was assessed by transfecting HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding WT ubiquitin (containing all seven lysine residues), sequential ubiquitin substitution mutants (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63, in which each lysine residue except one is replaced with arginine), SOCS1, and USP18. The results revealed that USP18 significantly reduced the K48-linked polyubiquitination of SOCS1 (Fig. 8 g). Correspondingly, the USP18 (C64S) mutant, which lacked the DUB activity, did not remove polyubiquitin chains from SOCS1 (Fig. 8 h). These data suggest that USP18 mediates the K48-linked deubiquitination of SOCS1 via its DUB activity.

Discussion

Asthma involves disruptions in the local immune responses against chronic lung damage, affecting both epithelial and immune cells. These processes result in cytokine release and efforts to restore lung function. Recent research has emphasized the role of certain alveolar changes in asthma, such as inflammatory cell infiltration, small airway obstruction, and pulmonary fibrosis [8]. AT2 cells are important for maintaining the respiratory barrier and regulating immune responses in asthma [5]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which these cells influence inflammatory responses in asthma remain unclear. The current study revealed that USP18 expression was increased in AT2 cells from both mouse models of asthma and asthmatic patients. Furthermore, CCL8 expression was shown to increase in AT2 cells when alveolar epithelial integrity was disrupted following OVA or HDM stimulation, resulting in eosinophil infiltration and enhanced Th2 immune responses. In the process of negative feedback, USP18 stabilized SOCS1 by inhibiting its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby fine-tuning the ERK-STAT3 pathway and maintaining epithelial barrier function. This modulation decreased CCL8 in AT2 cells, and suppressed Th2 responses and eosinophil accumulation (Fig. 9). Therefore, studying the effects of USP18 regulating CCL8 production in asthma may provide valuable insights into their potential as therapeutic targets for human asthma.

Diagram illustrating the role of USP18 in negatively regulating CCL8production by AT2 cells in allergic asthma

Several studies have underscored the significance of post-translational modification in regulating cytokine production [32, 33]. Our study, alongside findings from other groups, demonstrated that ubiquitination and deubiquitination—orchestrated by E3 ligases and DUBs—are crucial for regulating protein activity and stability [9, 10]. This finely tuned mechanism is critical for maintaining immune homeostasis and tolerance across different cell types. However, the specific mechanisms through which ubiquitination influences AT2 cell function, particularly in modulating CCL8 production in asthma, are poorly understood and warrant further investigation. Among DUBs, USP18 plays a key role in modulating innate immune responses and several biological processes, including inflammatory reactions [34], innate antiviral immunity [28], autophagy [35], and tumorigenesis [36]. However, it is not clear how USP18 regulates CCL8 in AT2 cells and Th2-driven lung inflammation upon allergen exposure. The current study revealed that USP18 deficiency led to increased CCL8 in AT2 cells, eosinophil accumulation, and heightened Th2 inflammation after OVA or HDM treatment. Importantly, CCL8 knockdown in AT2 cells reversed the enhanced CCL8 production and Th2 inflammation in USP18-deficient mice after OVA challenge. Although USP18-deficient mice did not develop inflammation or spontaneous lung injury under normal conditions, the disruption of AT2 cell homeostasis may heighten susceptibility to environmental stimuli, potentially worsening immune responses. Thus, it can be hypothesized that USP18 regulates Th2 inflammation by decreasing CCL8 in AT2 cells.

The traditional asthma treatment options including glucocorticoids, beta-2 agonists, and newer options such as omalizumab (anti-IgE) provide only partial relief, and are hampered by side effects and limited specificity [37, 38]. It is necessary for a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying asthma pathogenesis to develop more effective treatments. Resident alveolar epithelia, particularly AT2 cells, play a dual role in asthma by both producing and responding to inflammatory mediators, initiating immune responses and managing external damage. Epithelial cells release cytokines such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin, IL-33, and IL-25 in response to allergens, thereby activating Th2 cells and intensifying airway inflammation [39]. However, the exact mechanisms underlying allergen-induced epithelial cell activation and the subsequent Th2-associated inflammation in asthma are not clearly understood [40]. Recent research has uncovered the role of CCL8 in several immune-related diseases [41, 42], but its role in asthma remains unclear. Here, we show that intratracheal CCL8 administration mimics its local airway elevation and drives CCL8 expression in AT2 cells. This effect may involve autocrine feedback or indirect activation via local immune cells. Similar autocrine circuits have been reported in airway epithelial cells, where cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1α, and IL-1β amplify pro-inflammatory mediator release [9, 43]. Moreover, Th2 cells and eosinophils were identified as key effector cells interacting with CCL8 in AT2 cells. Although CCL8-targeted therapies are not yet available, the development of inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies may offer novel strategies for asthma treatment.

The epithelial barrier is essential for protection against pathogens and allergens, thereby participating in the defense against allergic asthma. In patients with asthma, impaired epithelial barrier function increases mucosal permeability, causing abnormal immune responses and facilitating rapid inflammatory cell infiltration after allergen exposure [44, 45]. Tight junctions and adherens junctions maintain epithelial barrier integrity. Tight junctions, located at the apical end of the junctional complex, comprise proteins such as ZO-1, claudins, and occludins, whereas adherens junctions, featuring the transmembrane protein E-cadherin, are vital for cell-cell adhesion and apical-basal polarization [46]. These proteins help organize transmembrane proteins, and connect them to the cytoskeleton to form a selective barrier that regulates epithelial permeability. Our results showed that USP18 knockout significantly worsened epithelial barrier damage with reduced ZO-1, claudin1, and E-cadherin protein expression, whereas CCL8 knockdown restored barrier integrity after OVA or HDM challenge. These findings suggest that CCL8 exacerbates damage to epithelial cells in a USP18-dependent manner. Due to the complexity of asthma pathogenesis, in addition to epithelial barrier dysfunction, factors such as genetics, innate immune responses, and comorbidities can significantly influence inflammation, thereby challenging the established predominant pathophysiological mechanisms [38]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of each inflammatory subgroup is necessary for advancing personalized and precision medicine, and future research should explore whether CCL8 in AT2 cells is linked to allergic sensitization.

A recent clinical study has underscored the function of USP18 in maintaining immune homeostasis [47]. The Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor ruxolitinib has been reported to alleviate severe clinical consequences resulting from USP18 loss-of-function mutations [48], suggesting that the clinical manifestations of USP18 deficiency can be reversed by modulating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Epithelial STAT3 signaling regulates barrier integrity in chronic inflammation [49], and SOCS1 has been shown to inhibit allergic inflammation via a negative feedback loop [50, 51]. However, the relationship of USP18 with CCL8 via epithelial SOCS1-STAT3 signaling in asthma is unknown. Our results demonstrated that USP18 knockdown decreased SOCS1 levels, and increased STAT3 and ERK phosphorylation after HDM stimulation. Although various E3 ligases are known to regulate SOCS1 activation and stability [52], there are limited data on DUBs affecting SOCS1 ubiquitination and stability. As a DUB, USP18 can deubiquitinate and stabilize Snail1 to promote the proliferation and invasion of colorectal cancer cells [53]. In addition, USP18 functions as a scaffold protein to recruit TRIM31 for MAVS ubiquitination [54], and interact with USP20 for STING deubiquitination [55], independent of its enzymatic activity. These findings suggest that USP18 is involved in various signaling pathways, either dependent on or independent of its enzymatic activity. In this study, USP18 was identified as a DUB that directly interacted with SOCS1, particularly within amino acids 51–112, but not with SOCS2 or SOCS3. Moreover, USP18 predominantly deconjugated the K48-linked ubiquitination of SOCS1, preventing its proteasome-dependent degradation. Of note, the USP18 (C64S) mutant, which contains a critical mutation in the protease domain, interacted with SOCS1 but failed to induce SOCS1 deubiquitination and accumulation to the extent observed with WT USP18. These results indicate that USP18 depends on its enzymatic activity to participate in SOCS1-mediated the activation of CCL8 in AT2 cells. Because SOCS1 is also involved in IL-2, IL-4, and IFN-γ signaling pathways of Th1 response and autoimmunity [56, 57], investigating whether USP18 regulates SOCS1 degradation in these contexts would be valuable. In addition, CCL8 appears to be regulated by a complex network of both positive and negative regulators, suggesting that its regulation cannot be attributed to a single isolated mechanism. It is therefore important to investigate whether the USP18-SOCS1 complex activates CCL8 in AT2 cells through additional signaling pathways. Moreover, further research is needed to determine whether these molecular interactions are stable or transient, as well as to explore their stoichiometry. Given the differences in the pathophysiology and CCL8 activation between humans and mice, clinical validation of these findings may pave the way for effective, personalized CCL8-targeting treatments, ultimately improving the management of allergic asthma.

There were several limitations of this study. Firstly, one of the major limitations of this study was that human - related data was entirely sourced from public databases and the relatively small sample size. The single-cell RNA sequencing dataset used in our analysis was obtained from only four asthmatic and four control patients derived from public dataset. Asthma is a complex and heterogeneous disease with multiple subtypes and varying clinical manifestations. A small sample size may not adequately represent the full spectrum of asthma, which could limit the generalizability of our findings. The limited number of cases also reduces the statistical power of our analysis, making it more challenging to detect subtle differences between asthmatic and control groups. We acknowledge that a larger and more diverse cohort would be necessary to validate our results and draw more definitive conclusions. Future studies should aim to expand the sample size by collaborating with other research groups and accessing more single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from asthmatic patients and healthy controls. Secondly, another limitation of our study is the absence of validation in primary human cells, which restricts the direct translational applicability of our findings on USP18 and CCL8 as therapeutic targets for allergic asthma, as the murine models used may not fully capture the complexities of human asthma pathology. Future research should prioritize the inclusion of human - relevant experimental systems to confirm and extend our findings, thereby facilitating the translation of basic research into clinical applications. Thirdly, the last one is the use of global USP18 knockout mice, which may introduce confounding effects due to USP18 deletion in non-AT2 cell types. While our findings strongly implicate AT2 cells as a critical site of USP18 action, we cannot fully exclude contributions from other cell populations (e.g., immune cells or airway epithelium). Future studies employing AT2 cell-specific conditional knockout models would enhance specificity and strengthen conclusions about the direct role of USP18 in alveolar epithelial-driven asthma pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, USP18 inhibits CCL8 production in AT2 cells, resulting in alleviating Th2 responses in the allergic asthma. USP18 stabilizes SOCS1 by inhibiting its ubiquitination and degradation, ultimately reducing CCL8 in AT2 cells through ERK-STAT3signaling in a negative feedback manner. Therefore, our work provides novel insights into the pathogenesis of allergic asthma, and indicates that targeting USP18 and CCL8 may offer promising clinical therapeutic strategies for asthma treatment. However, validation in human-relevant systems is essential before clinical translation.

Under allergen stimulation, AT2 cells suffer tight junction damage, and produce abundant CCL8, which triggers Th2 immune responses characterized by the recruitment of Th2 cells and eosinophils. Mechanistically, USP18 accumulates in the mitochondria, in which it stabilizes SOCS1 by inhibiting its K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent degradation following allergen exposure. This interaction precisely modulates ERK-STAT3 signaling in a negative feedback loop, preserves alveolar epithelial integrity, and inhibits CCL8 production by AT2 cells.
