A multi-stage prospective study evaluates serum metabolomic signatures for the differential diagnosis and prognostic stratification of PDAC
Authors: Zhouyu Ning, Ying Zhu, Hui Li, Zhiqiang Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Serum metabolomics identifies novel biomarkers for PDAC diagnosis and prognosis.•A specific 4-metabolite panel distinguishes PDAC from chronic pancreatitis.•Combining these four metabolites with CA19-9 achieves a diagnostic AUC of 0.999.•The metabolic signature offers prognostic value independent of clinical stage.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Metabolomics, Biomarkers, Differential diagnosis, Prognosis

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy whose global and regional burden continues to increase [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Its poor prognosis reflects aggressive tumor biology, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and frequent diagnosis at an advanced stage, when curative surgery is often not feasible [5,6]. These features underscore the need for more effective strategies for disease detection and clinical stratification.

The clinical management of PDAC is further complicated by two critical diagnostic dilemmas. First, the differentiation of PDAC from other periampullary malignancies, such as distal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA) or duodenal adenocarcinoma, can be challenging based on imaging alone. This difficulty arises from the complex anatomic intimacy of the pancreatic head, the distal common bile duct, and the duodenum. A large mass originating in the pancreatic head can obliterate the normal anatomic planes and present similarly to a dCCA that has invaded the pancreas, or vice versa, both frequently presenting with pancreatic and biliary ductal dilation and an ill-defined mass on cross-sectional imaging [7,8]. Second, and more critically, distinguishing PDAC from chronic pancreatitis (CP) remains a significant clinical hurdle. Both conditions can lead to parenchymal fibrosis and inflammation, resulting in similar imaging findings such as focal enlargement, parenchymal heterogeneity, and loss of normal lobulation [9]. They also share clinical symptoms like abdominal pain and weight loss, and risk factors such as smoking, yet their management and prognosis are vastly different. In this context, the serum biomarker carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) is the current clinical standard. However, its utility is limited by suboptimal specificity, as elevated levels are frequently observed in benign biliary obstruction and CP due to cholestasis and inflammation [[10], [11], [12]]. Furthermore, it is not expressed in approximately 5-10% of the population who are Lewis antigen-negative [13]. Consequently, there is an urgent, unmet need for more specific biomarkers to improve the diagnostic accuracy for PDAC.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer and is particularly prominent in PDAC, including altered glycolysis, glutamine use, and lipid metabolism [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. These tumor-associated and systemic changes can be detected in circulation, although the serum metabolome also integrates nutritional, medication, comorbidity, and other physiological influences [20,21]. Mass-spectrometry metabolomics enables broad characterization of these perturbations [22]. Several serum and plasma studies have reported candidate PDAC signatures, including multicenter panels for PDAC versus chronic pancreatitis, untargeted discovery followed by targeted validation, and prospective real-world evaluation [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Across these studies, amino-acid, lipid, creatine, and betaine-related signals recur, but the exact panels and their performance vary with cohort composition, analytical platform, and validation design. Untargeted metabolomics offers broad discovery coverage without restricting analysis to a prespecified analyte list, whereas targeted assays provide stronger compound confirmation, absolute quantification, and inter-laboratory transferability. Together, these complementary approaches support the discovery of compact serum metabolite panels across clinically relevant comparator groups and their subsequent translation into targeted quantitative assays.

The prognostic potential of circulating metabolomics in PDAC remains less established. Recent reports have linked metabolic profiles with survival [[27], [28], [29], [30]], but sample size, event count, clinical-covariate completeness, and external validation remain important constraints. These observations motivated our evaluation of whether circulating metabolic heterogeneity is associated with survival and could contribute to future prognostic stratification.

To address these gaps, we conducted a prospective, single-center, multi-cohort serum metabolomics study involving 221 participants: two separately enrolled PDAC cohorts, healthy controls, patients with other cancers, and patients with chronic pancreatitis. The design enabled characterization of PDAC-associated metabolic changes, exploratory identification of candidate panels for clinically relevant differential diagnoses, and investigation of within-PDAC metabolic heterogeneity. This design links metabolic characterization with clinically relevant diagnostic comparisons and survival analysis, providing a basis for targeted assay development and prospective multicenter evaluation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Unless otherwise stated, all solvents and reagents were of at least LC-MS grade. Ultrapure water was sourced from a Millipore Milli-Q Integral 5 system (Billerica, USA). Acetonitrile, methanol, 2-propanol, and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) were supplied by SCRC (Shanghai, China). Formic acid, ammonium hydroxide, and ammonium formate were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Standard compounds were procured from a range of vendors, including Tansoole (Shanghai, China), ThermoFisher (Waltham, USA), Merck, Aladdin (Shanghai, China), and Macklin (Shanghai, China).

Study design and patient cohorts

This prospective metabolomics study enrolled participants in two separately recruited cohorts at the Department of Integrative Medicine, Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, with approval from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee (No. 1904200-13). Both cohorts were recruited at the same institution and were designed to address complementary metabolic characterization, differential-diagnosis, and prognostic questions. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cohort 1 (PDAC Metabolic Characterization): We prospectively enrolled 33 treatment-naïve patients with PDAC (PC-a) at initial diagnosis and 92 healthy controls (HC). Cohort 2 (Diagnostic Specificity Assessment): In a separate enrollment at the same center, we prospectively recruited 33 additional patients with PDAC (PC-b), 40 patients with other cancers (OC), and 23 patients with chronic pancreatitis (CP). Samples were collected and processed using the same protocol, and the diagnostic comparisons were conducted within Cohort 2. Combined Prognostic Analysis: PC-a and PC-b (total n = 66) were combined after batch correction to investigate metabolic heterogeneity and survival associations. As an exploratory study, no a priori power calculation was performed because a reliable prespecified effect size was unavailable; enrollment was based on eligible participants and available samples during the prospective collection period.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria: PDAC patients were enrolled at initial diagnosis based on histopathological confirmation or characteristic imaging features combined with elevated CA19-9 levels and clinical presentation. All patients were treatment-naïve at enrollment. Chronic pancreatitis was diagnosed according to established clinical, imaging, and laboratory criteria. The other cancers cohort included patients with histologically confirmed malignancies from various organs—specifically, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), gallbladder carcinoma (GBC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), esophageal carcinoma (ES), and colorectal carcinoma (CC). Healthy controls were volunteers with no history of cancer, inflammatory diseases, or metabolic disorders, confirmed by physical examination and routine laboratory tests. Exclusion criteria included acute infection, inflammatory conditions unrelated to the assigned study group, concurrent autoimmune disease, severe liver or kidney dysfunction, and use of medications known to markedly affect metabolism within 4 weeks before enrollment.

Clinical Data Collection and Survival Analysis. Clinical and pathological information was obtained from medical records and follow-up, including age, sex, disease stage, CA19-9 levels, and survival outcomes. PDAC staging was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition TNM classification. Overall survival was defined as the interval from diagnosis to death or last contact. Survival associations were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional-hazards regression. Cox models included an unadjusted model, a stage-adjusted model, and an expanded model adjusted for tumor stage, age, sex, and log2-transformed CA19-9.

Sample collection and processing

Fasting peripheral venous blood samples (5-10 mL) were collected from all participants in the morning (8:00-10:00 AM) at the time of enrollment using standard venipuncture techniques. Blood was collected into serum separator tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum was carefully aliquoted into cryovials (200–500 μL per aliquot), immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. All samples were processed within 2 h of collection to minimize pre-analytical variability. Samples were maintained at −80 °C until metabolomic analysis. Freeze-thaw cycles were strictly controlled, with each aliquot thawed only once for analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Sample Preparation and LC-MS Analysis: Serum samples were prepared for untargeted metabolomic analysis using a cold extraction method. Briefly, 100 μL of serum was combined with 400 μL of ice-cold methanol containing an internal-standard mixture, vortexed, incubated, and centrifuged; the supernatant was transferred for LC–MS analysis. Pooled QC samples were prepared by combining equal serum volumes from all participants.

Metabolomic analysis was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS) on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system interfaced with an Agilent 6545 QTOF mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, with a gradient elution as follows: 0–2 min, 1% B; 2–10 min, 1–99% B; 10–12 min, 99% B; 12–12.1 min, 99–1% B; 12.1–15 min, 1% B. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and injection volume was 5 μL. To ensure data quality and minimize systematic bias, all samples were analyzed according to a randomized injection sequence. Pooled QC samples and mixed standard compound solutions were interspersed every 8 sample injections throughout the analytical queue to rigorously monitor instrument stability and signal drift. The analytical reliability was further evaluated based on the Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) of the internal standards and detected features in the QC samples.

Metabolite annotation confidence

Metabolite names are reported as annotations under the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) framework[31]. MSI level 1 requires comparison with an authentic standard analyzed in the same laboratory using at least two orthogonal properties, such as retention time and MS/MS fragmentation; MSI level 2 denotes a putative annotation supported by spectral or database evidence. Accordingly, the reported features are described as annotated or candidate metabolites, and pathway-level and mechanistic interpretations are presented as hypothesis-generating.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw LC-MS data were processed using Agilent MassHunter Profinder software (version B.08.00) for peak detection, alignment, and normalization. Features were retained when detected in ≥80% of samples in at least one group and when their coefficient of variation was <30% in pooled QC samples. Missing values were imputed with one-half of the minimum detected value for the corresponding feature. Normalized peak areas were log2-transformed and autoscaled. For analyses combining the separately acquired PC-a and PC-b datasets, batch correction was applied before merging.

Univariate Analysis: For comparisons between two groups, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first applied to assess normality. Student's t-test was used for normally distributed variables, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed data. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA (parametric data) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric data) was performed, followed by Tukey's HSD or Dunn's test for pairwise comparisons. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Comparison-specific fold-change thresholds were FC>2 or <0.5 for PC-a versus HC and FC>1.5 or <0.677 for the other pairwise comparisons, together with FDR<0.05; VIP>1 was additionally required where specified.

Multivariate Analysis: Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize overall metabolic variation and identify potential outliers. Supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) or orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), as appropriate, was used to maximize separation between groups and identify discriminatory metabolites. Model validity was assessed using 7-fold cross-validation and permutation testing (200 permutations) to guard against overfitting. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores was calculated, with VIP > 1 indicating significant contribution to group discrimination.

Machine Learning and Feature Selection: For diagnostic model development, we employed multiple machine learning algorithms including Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and logistic regression. To mitigate the impact of multicollinearity, a pre-filtering step was applied using a Pearson correlation matrix; for feature pairs with a correlation coefficient |r| > 0.9, the metabolite with lower biological relevance or analytical signal intensity was removed to reduce redundancy.

Model hyperparameters were optimized by grid search with internal five-fold cross-validation. Stratified resampling preserved the class ratio in each split, and no synthetic oversampling was used. For SVM, the kernel type, regularization parameter C, and kernel coefficient gamma were tuned; for Random Forest, the number of trees and the number of variables considered at each split were optimized using out-of-bag error. Because the diagnostic analyses were small discovery studies, model output was interpreted primarily as discrimination rather than as calibrated clinical probability.

Feature selection used: (1) recursive feature elimination with cross-validation; (2) Random Forest importance ranking; and (3) Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) with linear SVM. For MCCV, stratified training (two-thirds) and validation (one-third) splits were repeated 100 times; features appearing in >50% of iterations with stable importance were retained. Label-permutation testing (n = 1000) compared observed AUCs with a null distribution. As a leakage-resistant sensitivity analysis for PC-b versus CP, we additionally used 20 repeats of stratified five-fold outer cross-validation. Within each outer training set, standardization and feature ranking were repeated de novo, and four-fold inner cross-validation tuned the number of retained features (2, 3, 5, 8, or 10) and L2 penalty (0.1, 1, or 10) for logistic regression. Only the untouched outer fold was used for evaluation. The mean, SD, and empirical 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of outer-fold AUCs were reported (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Metabolic Pathway Analysis: Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA) was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) based on the KEGG pathway database. Pathways were considered significantly enriched with FDR 〈 0.05 and pathway impact score 〉 0.1. Pathway topology analysis was conducted using the relative betweenness centrality algorithm to assess the biological relevance of identified pathways.

Results

Serum metabolomics reveals profound disturbances in PDAC

To investigate the metabolic alterations associated with PDAC, we first conducted an untargeted metabolomic analysis on serum samples from a cohort comprising 33 PDAC patients (PC-a) and 92 healthy controls (HC) (Fig. 1A, Table 1). As detailed in Table 1, the PC-a group was significantly older than the healthy controls and presented with predominantly advanced-stage disease, while gender distribution was comparable between the two groups. The unsupervised PCA model demonstrated a clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 1B), indicating a significant reprogramming of the systemic metabolome in PDAC patients. We identified 20 metabolites with significant differences (FC > 2 or FC < 0.5, FDR < 0.05) between the PC-a and HC groups, including 16 with elevated levels and 4 with lower levels (Fig. 1C). Applying a more stringent criterion (FC > 2 or FC 〈 0.5, VIP 〉 1, and FDR < 0.05) to enhance biomarker confidence yielded a refined list of 17 differential metabolites. To ensure these markers were not confounded by the observed age imbalance between cohorts (P < 0.001), we performed a limma-based covariate-adjusted analysis. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, the significance levels for all 17 metabolites remained highly significant (P < 10-10) and aligned closely with the y=x diagonal after adjustment. All 17 metabolites remained significant after age adjustment, indicating that the observed associations were not explained by the between-group age difference. The hierarchical clustering of these 17 metabolites effectively distinguished PDAC patients from healthy individuals, as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 1D). To place these findings in a biological context, we performed Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA). The results highlighted significant enrichment in several key metabolic pathways (Fig. 1E), including Riboflavin metabolism, Cysteine and methionine metabolism, Starch and sucrose metabolism, Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, Galactose metabolism, and the Citrate cycle (TCA cycle).

PDAC exhibits a distinct metabolic profile from other cancers and chronic pancreatitis

To identify metabolic markers with higher specificity for PDAC, we extended our study by analyzing serum samples from a new cohort of 33 PDAC patients (PC-b), 23 chronic pancreatitis patients (CP), and 40 patients with various other cancers (OC, including ICC, GBC, HCC, ES, and CC) (Fig. 2A, Table S1).

(A) Single-center cohort design: PC-b (n = 33), CP (n = 23), and OC (n = 40; ICC n = 9, GBC n = 4, HCC n = 22, ES n = 3, colorectal cancer n = 2). (B) PCA score plot. We first performed a comprehensive comparison among the three groups (PC-b, CP, and OC). The unsupervised PCA model demonstrated clear metabolic separation and distinct clustering for each group (Fig. 2B). An ANOVA analysis further confirmed significant metabolic differences. Using the criteria of P < 0.05 and VIP > 1, we identified several differential metabolites whose expression patterns, as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 2C), effectively distinguished the three groups. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis of these metabolites revealed significant enrichment in several pathways, including Arginine biosynthesis, Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, Arginine and proline metabolism, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, Purine metabolism, Phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, and Riboflavin metabolism (Fig. 2D).

Exploratory metabolite-based model for distinguishing PDAC from other cancers

We next explored the distinction between PDAC and other malignancies. PLS-DA showed separation between the OC and PC-b groups (Fig. 2E), and four candidate annotated metabolites—Aspartate, Glucose-1-phosphate, Nicotinamide, and Taurine—met the prespecified FC, VIP, and FDR criteria (Fig. 2F,G). Individual AUCs exceeded 0.7 in this dataset (Fig. 2H). The two-metabolite model comprising Glucose-1-phosphate and Nicotinamide yielded an internal AUC of 0.894, compared with 0.761 for CA19-9 (Fig. 2H,I). An exploratory combined model yielded an internal AUC of 0.905 (Fig. 2J). The increase in AUC after combining the metabolite panel with CA19-9 supports the complementary discriminatory information captured by the two marker types.

Exploratory candidate metabolic signature for PDAC versus chronic pancreatitis

Distinguishing PDAC from chronic pancreatitis poses a significant clinical challenge. We therefore conducted a focused metabolomic comparison between these two groups. Unsupervised PCA revealed a clear separation between the PC-b and CP cohorts (Fig. 3A), indicating distinct underlying metabolic states. A supervised analysis using stringent criteria (FC > 1.5 or 〈0.677, VIP 〉 1, FDR < 0.05) identified 15 significantly dysregulated metabolites (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, most of these metabolites, including several amino acids (alanine, arginine, glutamine, tryptophan) and compounds involved in osmoregulation and energy metabolism (betaine, creatine, creatinine), were significantly downregulated in the PC-b group. A singular and notable exception was D-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, an intermediate of the pentose phosphate pathway, which was highly elevated in PDAC patients.

(C,D) Linear-SVM/MCCV five-metabolite candidate panel comprising D-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, betaine, creatinine, creatine, and glutamine (AUC=0.998; 95% CI, 0.993–1.000). Using linear SVM with MCCV-based feature ranking, we identified a five-metabolite candidate signature comprising d-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, betaine, creatinine, creatine, and glutamine. The SVM model yielded an internally estimated AUC of 0.998 (95% CI, 0.993–1.000) (Fig. 3C, D). CA19-9 yielded an AUC of 0.773 in the same comparison (Fig. 3E). In the leakage-resistant nested sensitivity analysis, the mean outer-fold AUC was 0.955 (SD 0.020; empirical 2.5th–97.5th percentiles, 0.909–0.978), indicating a persistent diagnostic signal but lower performance than the full-data estimate (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Table S3).

Combining CA19-9 with four frequently ranked metabolites (D-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, betaine, creatinine, and creatine) yielded an internally estimated AUC of 0.999 (Fig. 3F). The combined result further supports complementary discrimination from the metabolite features and CA19-9 within the present cohort.

We compared metabolite profiles between PC-a and PC-b and observed differences involving central energy, nicotinamide, and amino-acid pathways (Fig. 4B,C). Intersecting the Random Forest top 20 features with the prespecified differential-abundance criteria yielded 18 candidate metabolites (Fig. 4D). Several candidates, including isocitrate, cysteine, glutamine, and serine, were negatively associated with survival measures in the combined cohort (Fig. 4E). These associations identify circulating metabolic features linked to survival and informed construction of the exploratory risk score.

The 18 candidate metabolites were further evaluated as an exploratory prognostic signature in 66 patients with available follow-up data, including 59 deaths. A Cox proportional-hazards model incorporating the 18-metabolite signature was visualized as a nomogram for estimating survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 4F). The model had a C-index of 0.601 (95% CI, 0.522–0.680), and calibration curves were used to compare the predicted and observed survival probabilities (Fig. 4G). Time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUCs of 0.64, 0.72, and 0.85 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 4H). Using the original cut-off of 0.110 and a strict greater-than rule, 19 patients were classified as high risk and 47 as low risk. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly shorter overall survival in the high-risk group (Fig. 4I). The high-risk group had an unadjusted HR of 3.80 (95% CI, 2.01–7.18; P < 0.001). The association remained significant after adjustment for stage (HR, 3.69; 95% CI, 1.91–7.11; P < 0.001) and in the expanded model adjusted for stage, age, sex, and log2-transformed CA19-9 (HR, 3.94; 95% CI, 2.05–7.58; P < 0.001; Table S2). These findings indicate that the 18-metabolite risk score provided prognostic information beyond the clinical variables included in the multivariable models.

Discussion

This study systematically characterized serum metabolic alterations relevant to PDAC detection, differential diagnosis, and prognostic stratification. Comparison with healthy controls identified 17 annotated differential metabolites and revealed coordinated changes in pathways related to energy metabolism, redox homeostasis, and amino acid metabolism. In a separately enrolled clinical cohort, compact metabolite panels distinguished PDAC from other cancers and chronic pancreatitis and provided additional discriminatory information when combined with CA19-9. Analysis of the combined PDAC cohorts further demonstrated metabolic heterogeneity associated with disease stage and survival and identified an 18-metabolite risk score associated with overall survival after adjustment for established clinical variables. Collectively, these findings support serum metabolomics as a promising approach for improving the clinical characterization of PDAC and provide insights into the systemic metabolic alterations accompanying disease progression.

Serum metabolite panels provide complementary information for differential diagnosis

Accurate differentiation of PDAC from chronic pancreatitis and other malignancies remains clinically challenging because CA19-9 can be elevated in both malignant and benign pancreaticobiliary conditions [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. In the present study, the two-metabolite panel comprising glucose-1-phosphate and nicotinamide distinguished PDAC from the heterogeneous other-cancer cohort with an AUC of 0.894, compared with 0.761 for CA19-9. The five-metabolite panel comprising d-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, betaine, creatinine, creatine, and glutamine achieved an AUC of 0.998 for PDAC versus chronic pancreatitis, with a corresponding nested cross-validation mean AUC of 0.955. The persistence of strong discrimination under the more conservative nested procedure supports the robustness of the metabolic signal observed in this comparison.

The addition of the metabolite panels to CA19-9 increased the AUCs to 0.905 for PDAC versus other cancers and 0.999 for PDAC versus chronic pancreatitis. This improvement suggests that circulating metabolites and CA19-9 capture partially distinct aspects of PDAC biology. Whereas CA19-9 primarily reflects altered glycosylation and tumor-associated antigen release, the metabolite panels may reflect systemic changes in nutrient utilization, energy metabolism, and redox regulation. Their combination may therefore offer a broader representation of disease-associated biology than either type of marker alone.

Previous multicenter and targeted metabolomics studies have also demonstrated the potential of circulating metabolites for PDAC detection and differential diagnosis [23,24,26,32]. A previously reported nine-metabolite-plus-CA19-9 signature achieved a test-set AUC of 0.94, whereas the prospective METAPAC study reported an AUC of 0.846 in a real-world multicenter cohort [26] (Supplementary Table S4). Differences in study populations, comparator composition, analytical platforms, and validation strategies likely contribute to variation in performance across studies. Nevertheless, the recurrent identification of metabolites related to creatine, betaine, amino acid, and energy metabolism supports the biological relevance of these pathways and highlights their potential value in PDAC-associated metabolic profiling.

Biological interpretation of the PDAC-Associated metabolic alterations

The separation between PDAC and healthy controls, together with the 17 annotated differential metabolites, indicates extensive systemic metabolic alteration in patients with PDAC. Enrichment of the TCA cycle, riboflavin metabolism, and cysteine–methionine metabolism suggests coordinated changes in mitochondrial energy production, redox regulation, and one-carbon metabolism [17,18,37,38]. Increased demand for biosynthetic intermediates and reducing equivalents is a well-recognized feature of proliferating cancer cells. Riboflavin-derived cofactors support mitochondrial oxidation–reduction reactions, whereas cysteine and methionine contribute to glutathione synthesis, methyl-group metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, and epigenetic regulation. The enrichment of these pathways therefore provides a biologically coherent representation of the metabolic demands associated with PDAC.

The metabolic pattern distinguishing PDAC from chronic pancreatitis was characterized by reduced circulating amino acids and energy-related metabolites, including alanine, arginine, glutamine, betaine, creatine, and creatinine, together with increased d-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate. Depletion of circulating nutrients may reflect increased utilization by the tumor and surrounding tissues, altered hepatic metabolism, or systemic catabolic responses associated with PDAC. In contrast, d-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate is related to pentose phosphate metabolism, which supports nucleotide synthesis and NADPH production for redox homeostasis [39,40]. The coexistence of systemic nutrient depletion and increased pentose phosphate pathway-related signals may therefore reflect the balance between enhanced anabolic requirements and host metabolic consumption in PDAC.

Aspartate, glucose-1-phosphate, and nicotinamide were elevated in PDAC relative to the pooled other-cancer cohort. These metabolites connect several processes relevant to PDAC biology. Aspartate contributes to anaplerosis and nucleotide biosynthesis, glucose-1-phosphate links glycogen turnover with glycolytic metabolism, and nicotinamide supports NAD-dependent energy metabolism and stress responses [41,42]. Their combined elevation may reflect the metabolic adaptations required for PDAC cells to survive in a hypoxic, nutrient-restricted, and desmoplastic microenvironment. These observations also support the concept that systemic metabolic profiles retain information capable of distinguishing PDAC from other malignancies, despite shared features of cancer-associated metabolism.

Metabolic heterogeneity is associated with PDAC prognosis

The metabolic differences between the PC-a and PC-b cohorts were accompanied by differences in disease stage and survival. PC-a contained a higher proportion of patients with stage III–IV disease and had a shorter median survival than PC-b, whereas PC-b contained more patients with stage II disease. Metabolites distinguishing the two cohorts were enriched in central energy metabolism, nicotinamide metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis. These findings suggest that the circulating metabolome changes with disease burden and may capture clinically relevant aspects of PDAC progression that are not fully represented by individual conventional biomarkers.

The 18-metabolite risk score further supported an association between systemic metabolic status and patient outcome. Patients in the high-risk group had significantly shorter overall survival than those in the low-risk group, with an unadjusted HR of 3.80. The association remained significant after adjustment for stage alone and after additional adjustment for age, sex, and CA19-9, with an adjusted HR of 3.94. Although the C-index of 0.601 indicates modest overall ranking performance, the consistent survival separation across the Cox models suggests that the metabolic signature captures prognostically relevant information beyond the included clinical variables.

Several metabolites contributing to the prognostic signature, including isocitrate, cysteine, glutamine, and serine, have established roles in PDAC-associated metabolism [16,17]. Glutamine supports anaplerosis, nitrogen metabolism, redox balance, and biosynthesis, whereas serine and cysteine contribute to one-carbon metabolism and antioxidant defense. Isocitrate reflects TCA-cycle activity and the exchange of carbon between mitochondrial metabolism and biosynthetic pathways. The associations between these circulating metabolites and survival may therefore reflect differences in metabolic activity, nutrient dependence, redox capacity, and systemic disease burden among patients with PDAC. These results provide a rationale for investigating metabolic phenotypes as prognostic biomarkers and as indicators of biologically distinct PDAC states.

Clinical implications, study limitations, and future directions

The present findings have several potential clinical implications. First, compact serum metabolite panels may complement CA19-9 when differentiating PDAC from chronic pancreatitis or other malignancies, two settings in which conventional biomarkers frequently lack specificity. Second, the combination of metabolites with CA19-9 may improve the representation of complementary biological processes and support the development of multimodal blood-based diagnostic strategies. Third, the association between the 18-metabolite risk score and overall survival suggests that circulating metabolic profiles could contribute to future prognostic assessment and patient stratification. The relatively small number of metabolites in the diagnostic panels may also facilitate their conversion into targeted quantitative assays suitable for prospective clinical evaluation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cohorts were recruited at a single center, the chronic pancreatitis group was relatively small, and the other-cancer cohort comprised several cancer types with limited numbers in each subgroup. The diagnostic models were evaluated internally, and the prognostic analysis included 18 metabolites in 66 patients with 59 events and did not include complete information on treatment, ECOG performance status, or resection. In addition, the untargeted metabolomics platform provided relative measurements and metabolite annotations that will benefit from targeted analytical confirmation. These considerations define the next stage of the research, including targeted quantitative LC–MS/MS assay development, prospective multicenter evaluation, cancer-specific comparator cohorts, and prognostic modeling with comprehensive clinical covariates. Such studies will determine the reproducibility, incremental clinical value, and biological relevance of the identified metabolic signatures.

Conclusion

This prospective single-center study identified serum metabolomic signatures associated with PDAC characterization, differential diagnosis, and prognosis. Compact metabolite panels demonstrated strong discrimination between PDAC and other cancers or chronic pancreatitis and provided complementary information to CA19-9, while the 18-metabolite risk score was independently associated with overall survival after adjustment for clinical variables. These findings support the potential of serum metabolomics to improve the clinical characterization of PDAC and provide a foundation for targeted assay development and prospective multicenter evaluation.
