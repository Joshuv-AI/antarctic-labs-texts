Hospitalized Older Patients’ Readiness to Use mHealth Mobility Apps
Authors: Trevor Denton, Shuo Tian, Sachita Shrestha, Liana Marquis, Anupama Goyal, Stephanie Parks Taylor, David Paje
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesHospitalization‐associated disability (HAD) is prevalent among older adults, impacting their recovery and independence post‐discharge. Assessing the readiness and willingness of older adults to use mobile health (mHealth) apps can provide new opportunities to mitigate HAD by promoting physical activity during hospitalization.MethodsWe performed a cross‐sectional study of patients aged 65 years and older, admitted to the general medical unit of a large academic hospital. In‐person surveys assessed participants’ perceptions of physical activity, mobile device familiarity, and willingness to use a hypothetical mHealth mobility app that would provide reminders to be active during hospitalization. Participants also completed a practical digital literacy assessment by setting an alarm on their personal mobile device. Demographic and clinical data were abstracted from electronic medical records.ResultsOf 80 eligible patients invited, 69 (86%) were enrolled. The median age of participants was 74 years (interquartile range, 69–77 years). The majority of participants preferred to stay physically active during hospitalization (81%), owned a mobile device (83%), and expressed willingness (55%) to use an mHealth app that provides notifications to perform physical activities. Participants willing to use such an app had a higher median Charlson comorbidity score (8 vs. 6, p = 0.005), more likely completed education beyond high school (84% vs. 57%, p = 0.016), and reported greater comfort using mobile devices in general (90% vs. 67%, p = 0.033) compared to those unwilling. Willing participants were more successful in the alarm‐setting task compared to those unwilling (63% vs. 13%, p = 0.001).ConclusionsIn this single‐center study, most older adults prefer to stay physically active, and a majority are willing and capable of using an mHealth app to receive mobility reminders during hospitalization. These findings support the development of targeted mHealth interventions to decrease HAD in geriatric acute care settings.Keywords: digital health, hospitalization‐associated disability, mHealth apps, physical activity

Introduction

Hospitalization‐associated disability (HAD) is prevalent among older adults, affecting 1 in 3 of those admitted for an acute medical illness [1]. Recovery from HAD can take a long time and may be incomplete, hindering many previously independent community‐dwelling adults from performing basic activities of daily living (ADL) as late as 6 months after hospital discharge [2]. Patients who develop HAD are also more likely to be readmitted to the hospital or experience complications that may lead to death [3, 4, 5].

A strong independent risk factor for HAD is low mobility or relative inactivity during hospitalization, which is common among older patients [6]. Even when capable of walking independently, the average older adult spends most of their hospital stay lying in bed [7]. Conversely, simple physical activity interventions, such as slow‐paced walking for about 25 min a day, can lower the risk of functional decline [7, 8]. In‐hospital mobility interventions, including those that utilize digital technology, have been shown to increase physical activity and improve aerobic functional capacity [9].

However, existing digital health interventions on mobility have not been broadly implemented as they mostly rely on wearable activity trackers (WAT) and require digital skills that are challenging for older adults [10]. Alternative interventions using mobile health (mHealth) mobility apps installed on the patient's own personal device might improve adoption, implementation, and maintenance of digital interventions. As formative work for developing such interventions, we evaluated demographic, clinical, and behavioral characteristics of hospitalized older adult patients that could influence their readiness to use an mHealth mobility app. We hypothesize that most contemporary older adults would prefer to engage in physical activity during hospitalization and that they would be willing and capable of using an mHealth mobility application.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Identification

In August 2024, we conducted a cross sectional survey of older adult patients admitted to the University Hospital of Michigan Medicine, a 1000‐bed referral academic medical center. The institutional review board at the University of Michigan Medical School approved this study (HUM00256345). We screened adult patients admitted to the general medical units using the electronic medical records (EMR). We included patients who were 65 years and older and had been admitted for more than 48 h. We excluded patients on isolation precautions and those with documented significant cognitive, physical, auditory, or visual impairment that would limit interaction with a mobile communication device. Eligible patients were approached consecutively by room number at bedside, invited to participate, and surveyed if verbal consent was provided.

Data Collection

Study participants were surveyed in‐person by two investigators, DP and TD, using a structured form created in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) compliant browser‐based data capture software. The survey form was created by the investigators based on a review of relevant literature and expert input, and included items in the following areas: level of education completed; self‐reported ability to perform basic ADL prior to hospitalization (to calculate Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living score [Katz ADL]); expectation, preference, and barriers to performing physical activity while hospitalized; perceived amount of information or education received from hospital staff about staying physically active; and experience with mobile communication devices [11, 12]. While the instrument was reviewed for content validity, it has not undergone other formal validation testing. The full survey is provided as a supplemental file S1. We asked participants if they would use a hypothetical mHealth app that provides auditory and visual reminders to perform physical activities during the hospital stay. If participants had their own mobile device available, they were also asked to demonstrate their ability to set the alarm function, which is a pragmatic assessment of the digital skill needed to use a notification‐based mHealth app. A successful demonstration was defined as setting the alarm function for 10 am. independently within 5 min. Participants’ demographic and clinical data were abstracted from the EMR, including age, comorbidities, medications, physician orders, and functional assessment scores (e.g., Activity Measure for Post‐Acute Care [AM‐PAC] Basic Mobility Inpatient Short Form [“6‐clicks”] score, and Johns Hopkins Highest Level of Mobility [JH‐HLM] score) [13, 14, 15].

Data Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize participants based on demographic and clinical data. Bivariate analyses were used to assess relationships between participant characteristics and willingness to use the mHealth app for mobility and responses to other survey items. We also evaluated differences in successful demonstration of setting the alarm function across participant characteristics. Welch's t‐test or Fisher's exact test was performed to compare the characteristics of the two groups. Statistical significance was presented as p < 0.050. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software version 2024.09.0 (Boston, MA, United States).

Results

Of 480 general medical patients screened, 17% (80/480) were eligible, of which 86% (69/80) agreed to participate in the study. The median age was 74 years (interquartile range [IQR], 69–77 years), and 36 (52%) were female. Most participants (72%) completed more than high school education.

The Katz ADL median score was 5 (IQR, 5–5), the initial AM‐PAC “6‐Clicks” median score was 21 (IQR, 15–24), and the initial JH‐HLM median score was 6 (IQR, 3–7), which are all reflective of the ability to walk 10 steps or more upon admission. Physical therapy evaluated 49 (71%) participants, and occupational therapy evaluated 36 (52%) participants. Almost all participants (99%) were placed on fall precautions, but only 5 (7%) participants had a history of falls. The median hospital length of stay (LOS) was 9 days (IQR, 6–15 days).

Although many participants (67%) expected to be less active during the hospital stay, most of them (81%) preferred to keep their physical activity the same or more than their usual baseline. Participants indicated the following as perceived barriers to physical activity: lack of energy (30%), poor health (26%), need for rest (17%), pain (13%), fear of injury (10%), and lack of time (10%). Regarding expectations upon leaving the hospital, 38% of participants thought they would be weaker compared to their baseline prior to acute illness. About half of participants (51%) indicated they did not receive enough information or education from their healthcare team about staying physically active while in the hospital.

Mobile device ownership was prevalent, and most participants used the common functions of mobile devices. A total of 57 (83%) participants owned a smartphone, and 36 (52%) participants owned a tablet device. Of the 34 participants who owned both mobile devices, 20 (59%) preferred to use the smartphone because of its portability, while 14 (41%) participants preferred the tablet for its larger screen size. The most common functions used by those with mobile devices included making voice calls (93%), looking up general information (88%), taking or viewing pictures (85%), finding locations and directions (81%), and exchanging text messages (80%). Many participants used the mobile device for health‐related activities, such as looking up health information (80%) and accessing the patient portal for healthcare (63%). Some (15%) tracked their physical activity or fitness data on their mobile device. More than half (55%) of the participants were willing to use an mHealth application that provided scheduled notifications to perform physical activities. Of the 55 participants who had a mobile device available at bedside, 28 (51%) were able to successfully set the alarm on the phone without assistance.

Other demographic characteristics, including initial functional assessment scores, were similar between participants willing versus unwilling to use the mHealth app. Participants willing to try the app more frequently reported comfort with using a mobile device in general (90% vs. 67%, p = 0.033), viewing informational or educational content (92% vs. 55%, p < 0.001), or following instructions on performing physical activities (84% vs. 36%, p < 0.001) compared to participants unwilling to use the mHealth app.

Discussion

This study conducted in‐person surveys to assess patient perspectives on physical activity during hospitalization and potential digital health technology solutions, leading to several key findings. First, older patients express a desire to be as active or more active while hospitalized compared to their baseline. This preference is important to note because it may conflict with assumptions from clinicians or family members that older patients prefer to rest in the hospital and differs from earlier studies that showed many older adults believed staying in bed was necessary to recover from an acute illness [16].

Second, we elicited several barriers to achieving the desired level of mobilization, which are consistent with those found in the literature [16, 17, 18]. Importantly, almost all our participants were placed on fall precautions despite a low prevalence of prior falls and most participants having the ability to walk upon admission. In prior studies, patients identified fear of falling as influential to their walking activity in the hospital [17]. Some patients do not view the hospital as a safe place and point to institutional factors, such as lack of support from their care providers and active discouragement, as reasons they curtail their physical activity [16, 18]. Indeed, the designation of falls as a “never event” by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services in 2008 was followed by a decline in hospital mobility and a heightened focus on falls, with higher utilization of preventative interventions such as bed alarms [19, 20]. While fall prevention protocols are designed to enhance safety, their routine or overly cautious use can inadvertently discourage physical activity, potentially contributing to functional decline. Future fall prevention efforts should incorporate the contemporaneous need to prevent HAD by encouraging physical activity while identifying and supporting the mobility needs of patients at risk for falls.

Third, we identified a digital health solution as a promising innovation to help promote physical activity for older hospitalized patients without significant cognitive, physical, auditory, or visual impairment. Most surveyed patients owned mobile devices and were comfortable using multiple digital functions. A majority were willing to use an mHealth app that would provide notifications to perform physical activities, and about half were able to demonstrate the minimum digital proficiency required for such an app (setting an alarm). Educational attainment beyond high school and a high comfort level with using mobile devices were associated with willingness to use the mHealth app. Although a growing proportion of older adults will have significant digital experience as the population ages, their individual health status, particularly cognitive function, could be a key factor in their ability to navigate digital interventions [21]. Therefore, optimizing usability and accessibility would be critical in the design of mHealth apps.

Recent research highlights that for older adults, additional factors including familiarity, advice acceptance from healthcare providers, compatibility with daily routines, trust, and perceived personal need substantially affect willingness to adopt healthcare technologies [22]. These insights underscore that mHealth interventions for older adults should not only maximize usability and utility, but also address the context of introduction, individual health priorities, trusted sources of advice, and support for building confidence and digital familiarity. Incorporating these broader psychosocial and contextual factors into future mHealth intervention design may help close the gap between willingness and sustained adoption in older patient populations.

Although 45% of our cohort were not willing to use an mHealth app, it is encouraging that the majority expressed willingness or interest. This suggests meaningful potential for digital health interventions among hospitalized older adults. Strategies to increase engagement among less willing patients may include enhanced digital literacy support, targeted education on the benefits and safety of mHealth tools, involvement of family members or caregivers in technology adoption, and personalized, patient‐centered approaches that address individual concerns or preferences [23].

Interestingly, we observed that patients who expressed willingness to use an mHealth mobility app were more likely to have more comorbid conditions than those who were unwilling. This contradicts findings from a prior study that showed an association between increased comorbidity and lower digital health engagement and health literacy [24]. Our findings suggest that patients with a greater comorbidity burden may be more interested in additional resources and support to manage their health during hospitalization. Conversely, patients with fewer comorbidities may be more likely to feel sufficiently independent and therefore less motivated to adopt new tools for activity promotion.

Given the limited success of interventions that promote physical activity for hospitalized patients, the potential for an mHealth app that provides education, motivation, and reminders for patients to engage in physical activity is appealing. For older adults, recommendations and reminders from their physicians or nurses are important motivators to be physically active while in the hospital [18]. However, some patients experience inconsistent and confusing messaging about getting up from bed and walking [17]. Patients also describe the paucity of physical, cognitive, and social stimulation and constraints from the hospital staff as contributors to their inactivity [25]. Indeed, in our study, half of our participants said they did not receive enough information or education about physical activity from their physicians or nurses. Addressing these barriers through digital health innovation is a promising approach.

Our findings align with recent hospital‐based studies of digital mobility interventions, which identified limited digital skills, comfort with technology, and technical barriers as major challenges to their adoption and sustained use [9, 10]. Similar to van Dijk‐Huisman et al., we found that participants’ willingness to use an mHealth app was closely linked to their practical digital skills and previous device experience. By assessing patients’ readiness on their own devices, our study addresses a gap from previous interventions that relied on unfamiliar, study‐provided technology [9, 10] The high rates of device ownership and comfort observed in our cohort suggest that “bring your own device” models may facilitate greater uptake. Our results underscore the need to assess digital skills and comfort, provide targeted support, and design interventions that leverage familiar devices to enhance adoption and effectiveness among hospitalized older adults.

Our findings must be interpreted within the limitations of the study. First, our participants were admitted to a single site, an academic referral center in a small affluent urban location in the Midwest, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations and settings. Second, as we conducted an in‐person survey, the participants’ responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Third, we collected clinical data from the EMR, which could have incomplete information resulting in nondifferential misclassification bias. Fourth, we relied upon the participants' recall when describing their functional ability prior to hospitalization to calculate the Katz ADL score, which could be another source of information bias. Fifth, we did not have information on social determinants other than educational attainment, which limited our ability to assess their influence on mobility behavior and access to mHealth. Finally, the small study sample size could have limited the power to detect other meaningful differences and associations between variables.

In conclusion, most older adults in this single‐center cohort prefer engaging in physical activity during their hospital stay for acute medical illness, and a majority appear to be willing and capable of using an mHealth app that provides prompts to perform physical activities. These findings could help inform the development, implementation, and assessment of mHealth mobility interventions tailored for hospitalized older adults.
