Physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors in predicting outcomes for neck and thoracic pain: a systematic review
Authors: Rabea Begum, Alaa El Chamaa, Paul Parikh, David Walton, Alison Rushton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo summarize evidence for physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors in predicting outcomes in people with neck and thoracic pain.MethodsA comprehensive search was performed on 01/09/2025 across key databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science), the grey literature (ProQuest), handsearches of key journals, and reference lists. Eligible studies were prospective longitudinal cohort studies. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, risk of bias (RoB) assessment using Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool, and quality of evidence using GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation). Owing to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, findings were synthesized narratively by grouping studies according to outcome type, physical measure, and follow-up timepoints.ResultsTwenty-two studies (10 high, 7 moderate, 5 low RoB) were included. Low level evidence supports higher cervical movement velocity in the frontal plane predicting lower short-term (< 3 months) disability for people with neck pain. Low to very low evidence suggests cervical active range of motion (frontal and transverse planes), cervical movement velocity (sagittal and transverse planes), spring test, joint position error (transverse plane), and endurance of deep neck flexors were not predicting pain, disability and good overall changes in the short-term and mid-term (3 to 12-month) for neck pain. Inconsistent associations were also found for multiple measures in the short-and mid-term outcomes, with very low evidence. No studies addressed any measures for thoracic pain.ConclusionThe overall evidence was consistently low, precluding strong conclusions. Cervical movement velocity may be a potential predictor of short-term disability for neck pain to inform clinical decision making. There is an urgent need for low RoB prospective longitudinal cohort studies with standardized prognostic factors and outcomes measurement to further investigate physical measures of physical functioning as candidate prognostic factors for people with neck pain. Evidence for thoracic pain was entirely lacking, highlighting the need for a future prognostic studies for thoracic pain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09300-9.

Introduction

Neck pain is the fourth leading cause of disability [1], affecting 203 million people globally in 2020, and projected to reach 270 million people by 2050 [2]. Neck pain significantly affects physical functioning [3–9], imposing a considerable personal and socioeconomic burden [10–13], while the burden of thoracic pain has not been adequately studied [14]. Thoracic and cervical dysfunction often co-exist [15], making the thoracic region a critical component of examination, classification, diagnosis, and management of neck pain, as per clinical practice guidelines of neck pain [16, 17]. Compared to cervical and lumbar spine, the thoracic spine has received less attention in clinical and epidemiological research [14, 18]. Thoracic spine pain can be equally disabling, imposing similar burdens on individuals and communities [19, 20]. The prevalence of thoracic pain among health adolescent is increasing [14, 20–22], indicating a potential emerging burden on future adult populations [23].

As cervical and thoracic dysfunction can influence multiple aspects of physical functioning, it is necessary to assess limitation of physical functioning. Physical functioning is a multidimensional construct [24] that evaluates ability to carry out daily activities such as household chores, walking, work, travel, and self-care, as well as strength and endurance [25]. Physical functioning can be assessed using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [26] to reflect perceptions from the patient’s perspective [27] or physical measures of physical functioning [26] including impairment-based measures, performance-based measures, or activity measures in a real environment [26, 28].

Physical measures focus on impairments in structural or functional limitation [e.g., limited range of motion measured by a goniometer or inclinometer] [29], performance [e.g., standardized tests of task performance such as Functional Capacity Evaluation, FCE] [30]), or physical function in real environments typically captured through unobtrusive devices like accelerometers to measure physical activity or sleep-related behaviors [26, 31].

Six prior systematic reviews [32–37] have investigated physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors, including range of motion (ROM) [32–37], joint position error (JPE) [32–34], or muscle strength, endurance [32, 33] for prediction of neck pain outcomes. Most reviews have focused on acute [< 6 weeks] whiplash injuries [32–34, 37]. There has been considerably less attention paid to sub-acute (6 to 12 weeks] and chronic (> 12 weeks) neck pain including radicular pain. Several previous reviews [32, 33, 36, 37] included only English language studies which may introduce language bias [38]. According to A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews-2, AMSTAR-2, only one systematic review [32] meets the criteria of low RoB, while the remaining reviews [33–37] exhibit multiple methodological limitations aligned with a high RoB. They failed to meet common critical domains of AMSTAR-2, including protocol registration, comprehensive literature search, providing a list of excluded studies, using standardized RoB assessment tools, and incorporating risk of bias into their synthesis and interpretation.

Research on prognostic factors for thoracic pain is even more limited. Two systematic reviews [14, 39] were identified. The first investigated risk factors for thoracic pain, while the second concluded that knowledge about prognostic factors for thoracic pain is limited, as no studies specifically assessed these factors. Therefore, there is a need for a systematic review focusing on prognostic factors for thoracic pain. Moreover, owing to the methodological issues outlined above, and insufficient inclusion of physical measures in existing systematic reviews, a significant gap remains for neck pain population as well.

The objective of this study was to systematically identify, appraise, and synthesize the evidence for physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors for predicting outcomes in people with acute, sub-acute, or chronic neck and thoracic pain, over short-, medium-, and long-term follow-up.

Methods

Design

This systematic review was conducted according to a pre-defined, registered (CRD42024574473) in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [40] and published [41] protocol, reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis [42] statement [43] and followed the Cochrane Back Review Group guidelines [44], Cochrane Prognosis Methods Groups [45] and Cochrane Handbook [46]. There was no deviation from the protocol.

Eligibility criteria

The PECOS (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome and Study design) framework was used to develop the inclusion criteria [42].

Population (P)

People aged 18 and above with neck and thoracic pain for any duration (acute, sub-acute or chronic) of neuromusculoskeletal origin.

Exposure (E)

Physical measures of physical functioning, including impairment-based physical measures (e.g., strength, ROM) [28], performance-based physical measures (e.g., FCE) [30], and activities in the natural environment (e.g., accelerometry) [26].

Comparator (C)

Not applicable due to the nature of this review.

Outcome (O)

No limitation was placed on the outcomes evaluated. The preliminary scoping search identified a limited number of studies, which emphasized the need to include a wide range of outcomes. Both self-reported outcomes for example PROMs (e.g., neck disability index) or any physical measures such as ROM measured by using cervical range of motion (CROM) device were included. Any time point of outcome assessment was included.

Study design (S)

Only prospective longitudinal cohort studies were included as they are the preferred design for establishing a clear temporal sequence between exposure and outcome [47].

No limitation on language and publication year was applied. It was planned to include non-English studies using openly accessible natural language processing models to do the initial translation, followed by validation from bilingual reviewers.

Exclusion criteria

Studies where pain was a result of a space-occupying lesion (e.g., tumors, cysts), any serious neurological disorders (e.g., spinal cord injury, myelopathy), disc infection, inflammatory disorders (e.g., ankylosing spondylitis), fibromyalgia, any metabolic bone diseases (e.g., osteoporosis), deformities (e.g., congenital scoliosis), spinal dislocation or fracture and other studies related to the non-neuromusculoskeletal origin of pain were excluded.

Information sources

A comprehensive systematic search was performed in electronic databases from inception to September 1, 2025, including MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science and ProQuest. Handsearches were performed in key journals (Spine, European Spine Journal and The Spine Journal). Reference lists of all included studies were also screened.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in MEDLINE (Ovid) in collaboration with a library information specialist. The MEDLINE search comprised medical subject heading [48] terms and text words. MESH terms and keywords of similar concepts were combined with the Boolean operator OR and then all concepts were combined with AND following Cochrane guideline for searching studies [49]. The search strategy was adapted for other databases by modifying syntax and conducting preliminary scoping searches to refine text words selection. The MEDLINE (Ovid) search is presented in Supplementary material 1.

Selection process

Covidence [50] was used to manage data and identify duplicates. Screening occurred in two stages. First, title and abstract were screened to remove clearly irrelevant citations. Second, the full text of remaining articles were screened. Two independent reviewers (RB, AE) performed both stages. Reasons for exclusion were reported. Disagreements were planned to be addressed through discussion, and a third experienced reviewer (PP) was available to resolve any disagreements.

Data extraction process

The same two independent reviewers extracted data according to the Checklist for critical appraisal and data extraction for systematic reviews of prediction modelling studies (CHARMS) [51]. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, a third reviewer (PP) was consulted.

Data items

The extracted data included: study characteristics (e.g., author, year of publication, country); participant characteristics (sample size, mean age, sex and gender, duration symptoms, types of clinical conditions); physical prognostic factor(s) (PFs) including level of data (continuous, dichotomized); domain of measurement (impairment, performance, and activity measure); method of PFs measurement; outcome measure; length of follow-up (short-term: less than 3 months, medium-term: 3 to 12 months, and long-term: more than 12 months) [52]; results of univariate and multivariate analyses (including effect estimates, significance levels, confidence intervals, and other variables); effect estimates: odds ratio (OR), beta coefficient (B for unstandardized, β for unstandardized).

Risk of bias assessment

The two reviewers independently assessed RoB for each study independently using the Quality in Prognostic Studies (QUIPS) tool which contains 6 domains: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, study confounding and statistical analysis, and reporting. Each domain was rated as low, moderate, or high RoB [53]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (PP) consulted as necessary. An overall RoB judgement was derived according to the following criteria: studies were rated as low RoB if all domains were judged low or if there was only one domain rated moderate; moderate RoB if two domains were rated moderate and the remaining four domains low; and high RoB if one or more domains were rated high or if three or more domains were rated moderate.

Data synthesis

A meta-analysis was planned if studies were sufficiently homogenous to enable meaningful statistical pooling [54]. However, due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, a meta-analysis was not performed. Clinical heterogeneity resulted from variation in study population including type of clinical condition (e.g., whiplash injuries, cervical radiculopathy) and duration of symptoms (e.g., acute, chronic). Methodological heterogeneity reflected high number of high RoB studies, and variation in statistical approaches (univariate vs. multivariate analyses), differences in follow-up timepoints, and variation in measurement methods for prognostic factors (e.g., cervical ROM was assessed using CROM device, inclinometer or other devices) and outcomes (e.g., pain was measured using numeric pain rating scale or visual analogue scale). Findings were synthesized narratively based on the Cochrane guidelines [54, 55]. For synthesis and interpretation, studies were grouped by similar prognostic factor, outcome and follow-up timepoint. Effect estimates for both univariate and multivariate findings were tabulated. The synthesis was based on the RoB assessment of the included studies, the strength and direction of association between identified prognostic factors with outcomes based on categorized follow-up timepoints, and the overall quality of evidence for each candidate prognostic factor, evaluated using the GRADE approach.

GRADE assessment of evidence

The two independent reviewers rated overall level of evidence according to an adapted version of GRADE specially design for prognostic research [56]. The starting level of evidence was low based on the GRADE guideline for observational studies, and 5 factors were assessed to downgrade and 2 factors to upgrade the quality of evidence. Evidence was downgraded based on 5 factors: RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The evidence was upgraded when: the magnitude of associations were moderate or large (0.3 is moderate effect and 0.5 is large effect for standardized beta coefficient) and consistent across all/most of included studies for a single PF and outcome; and there was a significant exposure-response gradient reported between PF and outcome in a specified direction [56]. GRADE summary statements were created to categorize the quality of evidence into 4 levels: high, moderate, low, and very low [57]. For each PF examined in ≥ 2 studies, a GRADE summary was created that integrated the magnitude of association and level of evidence.

Results

Study selection

The search identified 46,988 records from electronic databases and 348 records from other sources. After removing duplicates, 39,090 titles and abstracts were screened, and then 232 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility by 2 independent reviewers. All identified eligible studies were in English. 22 studies (25 articles) were included. The PRISMA flow 2020 diagram illustrates the study selection process at each stage (Fig. 1).

Participants characteristics

The 22 studies included a total of 2,784 participants for neck pain, ranging from n = 31–599. Mean age was 35–50 years, and percentage of females was 36–73%. This review identified 13 studies with acute symptoms, 8 with sub-acute, and 10 with chronic. The most frequently studied condition was neck pain with or without arm symptoms (n = 10), followed by whiplash associated disorder (WAD) (n = 8), cervical radiculopathy (n = 4), cervical spondylosis (n = 2), and other neck conditions (n = 2). No studies investigated thoracic pain.

Physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors

Most prognostic factors (PFs) were impairment-based, with cervical active range of motion (AROM) investigated in 17 studies [59–67, 70–76, 79–81] and cervical passive range of motion (PROM) examined in 13 studies for neck pain. PROM in sagittal, frontal and transverse plane for neck pain was assessed by CROM device (1 study [81]) the spring test (5 studies [59, 60, 66, 70, 71], the cervical distraction test (4 studies [58, 59, 71, 79]), the cervical rotation lateral flexion (CRLF) test (1 study [66]), the side gliding test (1 study [79], and the quadrant test (1 study [70]). Other impairment-based measures were investigated for neck pain, including JPE (5 studies [67, 69, 73–76]), cervical movement velocity (2 studies [67, 79]), and handgrip strength (1 study [82]). Performance-based measures were examined for people with neck pain in 9 studies, including an endurance test (5 studies [59, 68, 70–72]), the workload test measured by strength and endurance (1 study [63–65]), the fly test as motor control test (2 studies [67, 69]), functional capacity evaluation (FCE) (1 study [77]), and other motor control tests including the figure of eight (FOE), standing balance test with eyes open (EO), eyes closed (EC) and eyes open on a board (EOB) (1 study [67]). No studies investigated activity measures in a real environments in the neck pain population.

Outcomes and outcome measures

A wide range of outcomes were measured using a variety of outcome measures. For pain, the Numeric Pain Rating scale (NPRS) was used in 7 studies [58, 60, 66, 67, 72, 78, 79], the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) in 5 studies.

[61, 63–65, 68, 69, 82], the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire [62], and North American Spine Society (NASS) pain scale [80] used in single studies for neck pain. For disability, the Neck Disability Index (NDI) was used in 11 studies [58, 60, 66, 67, 69, 73–76, 78, 81, 82], and single studies used the Northwick Park neck pain Questionnaire (NPQ) [62], Neck Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) [72], and NASS disability [80]. For overall change, the Global Rating Of Change (GROC) was used in 6 studies [59, 60, 66, 70, 71, 79], and single studies used the Global Rating of Perceived Improvement [58], Global Perceived Effect (GPE) [72], and Patient Reported Recovery [81] for people with neck pain. Single studies measured additional outcomes using the following measures: Patient Specific Functional Scale (PSFS) [60], medical care seeking (yes/no) [62], sickness absence (yes/no) [62], work capacity and handicap questionnaire [63–65], work disability by return to work [63–65], Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) [69], General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) [69], work capacity by ability to work (0–100%) [77], handgrip strength (Hand-held Dynamometer) [82].

Results of synthesis and individual studies

Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was conducted only when ≥ 2 studies were available. No synthesis was possible for long-term outcomes (> 12-month) as less than 2 studies examined prognostic factor at time point.

According to the strength of association and level of evidence, there is low level evidence suggests that higher cervical movement velocity in the frontal plane significantly predicts lower disability in the short-term follow-up for people with neck pain (Table 3 and Supplementary material 2). Several physical measures consistently demonstrated no association with short and medium-term outcomes for neck pain with limited evidence (low to very low level). Low level evidence indicates that cervical AROM in the transverse plane is not predictor of overall change in the medium-term follow-up for neck pain. For other impairment-based measures, very low level evidence shows that cervical AROM in the frontal plane is not predictor of short-term pain and overall changes; cervical AROM in the transverse plane is not predictor of short-term overall changes; cervical movement velocity in the sagittal plane is not predictor of short term pain and disability; cervical movement velocity in the frontal plane is not predictor of short-term pain; spring test is not predictor of short-term pain; cervical JPE in the transverse plane is not predictor of short-term pain and medium-term disability for people with neck pain (Table 3 and Supplementary material 2)

Inconsistent findings with very low level evidence were identified in this narrative synthesis for several impairment-and performance-based measures with pain, disability and overall changes in the short-and medium-term follow-up for people with neck pain. For impairment-based measures, no consistent associations were observed for cervical AROM in the sagittal plane with short-term pain intensity and disability, medium-term disability, and short-term overall changes; cervical AROM in the frontal plane with short-term disability; and cervical AROM in the transverse plane with short-term pain intensity, and short-and medium-term disability (very low level evidence). There was an inconsistent association between cervical total AROM and short-term pain intensity for neck pain (very low level evidence). Inconsistent associations were also found for cervical movement velocity in the sagittal and transverse plane with short-term disability; cervical distraction test with short-term pain intensity and overall changes; spring test with short-term disability and overall changes; and JPE in the transverse plane with short-term disability for people with neck pain (very low level evidence). For performance-based measures, very low level evidence shows no consistent associations were found for endurance test of deep neck flexors with short-term overall changes; and the motor control test (fly test) with short-term pain and disability for people with neck pain (Table 3 and Supplementary material 2).

Several individual studies demonstrated mixed findings regarding the prognostic value of specific physical functioning measures. Some impairment-based measures including total cervical AROM [63–65] and grip strength [82], were significantly associated with improvements in pain, disability, or work outcomes in the short- and medium-term. However, other impairment-and performance-based measures including PROM [66, 81], JPE (sagittal plane) [75], quadrant test [70], side gliding [79], workload [63–65], balance [67], and FCE [77] showed no predictive significance in the short and medium-term follow-up across studies for neck pain (Supplementary material 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the first systematic review focusing specifically on physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors in predicting outcomes following neck and thoracic pain. This review included 22 prospectively designed cohort studies for neck pain, with only five of these identified as low RoB. The lack of high quality studies for neck pain and the absence of thoracic pain studies highlights a notable gap in the current evidence. Future research is required to explore and identify physical prognostic factors in predicting outcomes for neck and thoracic pain.

Our current understanding on physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors in predicting neck pain outcomes is limited. These findings are consistent with a recent systematic review by Alalawi et al. [32], which synthesized evidence on physical function measures as prognostic factors following whiplash injuries. While that review [32] was limited to individuals with acute whiplash injuries and focused largely on impairment-level measures (e.g., ROM, muscle activity) concluded that the overall quality of evidence was low to very low. Our review expands on this by including other types of neck pain of any duration, and incorporating a wider range of physical functioning measures including impairment-based, performance-based and activity measure in a real environment.

To our knowledge, no prior systematic reviews [32–37] have examined cervical movement velocity as a prognostic factor in individuals with neck pain, making these novel contribution of our review. This systematic review contributes to emerging evidence supporting cervical movement velocity as a potential predictor of disability in the short-term follow-up (less than 3-month) for neck pain, although the quality of this evidence remains low. This highlights the potential value of cervical movement velocity in guiding clinical decision-making for neck pain populations and the need for further high-quality prospective longitudinal cohort studies on physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors in predicting outcomes for neck pain. Additional potential prognostic factors were identified in single studies. Significant associations were observed for total cervical AROM, handgrip strength, endurance, FOE test, and standing balance (Supplementary material 3). Although limited to individual studies, these findings warrant further investigation for neck pain.

This review identified no association of several prognostic factors for neck pain outcomes with low to very low level evidence that necessitates cautious interpretation of these findings. The findings of cervical JPE in the transverse plane was not associated with short-term pain intensity and medium-term disability, consistent with prior systematic reviews [32, 33]. Regarding performance-based measures, this review consistently identified that an endurance test of deep neck flexor was not predicted pain intensity in the short-term follow-up for neck pain [68, 72] with limited evidence. Notably, no previous systematic reviews [32–37] have investigated this association for neck pain, highlighting another novel investigation for neck pain populations. Additionally, the spring test as a physical measure and overall changes as an outcome provide new insights on prognostic studies for neck pain. In this review, most included studies [58–61, 66, 67, 70, 71, 79] examined short-term outcomes for neck pain, future studies are warranted to measure medium and long-term outcomes for neck pain.

Additional individual studies examined several impairment-and performance-based measures include CRLF, PROM, JPE, quadrant test, side gliding, workload, FCE, and standing balance with eyes closed or on board, and found no association with pain, disability, overall change, or work capacity in individuals with neck pain (Additional file 3). These findings are based on single studies with varying methodological quality, highlighting the need for further high-quality cohort studies to clarify the prognostic value of physical functioning in the short-term, medium-and long-term follow-up for neck pain.

Several inconsistencies in the impairment-and performance-based measures observed across multiple included studies require cautious interpretation. These inconsistencies, particularly for AROM, JPE and endurance are consistent with findings from previous systematic reviews [32–37]. Cervical distraction test, spring test and motor control test were not examined in prior systematic reviews, representing an important addition of this review. These inconsistencies may be due to methodological limitations, including use of different PFs and outcome measurements and unclear reporting of findings, highlighting the need for consistent use of standardized PFs and outcome measurements with clear reporting of analyses in future studies for neck pain.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first low-RoB systematic review (self-assessed using AMSTAR 2) only included prospective longitudinal cohort studies which is considered as the gold standard in prognostic research. Another key strength is the broad inclusion criteria. There was no restrictions on types of clinical conditions, duration of symptoms, outcomes, follow-up timepoints and the inclusion of a wide range of physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors, enhancing the generalizability and comprehensiveness of the findings. This systematic review has several limitations. First, several included studies did not provide sufficient detail on how prognostic factors and outcomes were assessed, and used unreliable and non-standardized prognostic factors and outcome measurement (e.g., use NPRS for disability), limiting confidence in the findings. Second, substantial heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis, limiting the comparability of between included studies. Third, in some cases, the interpretation of findings was restricted to univariate analyses, as multivariate analyses were not conducted in included studies, while univariate results were still included but interpreted with greater caution.

Conclusion

This systematic review identified low level evidence that higher cervical movement velocity in the frontal plane predicts lower disability in the short-term follow-up for neck pain. Other physical measures of physical functioning as prognostic factors were not predictor of pain, disability or overall changes. As strong recommendations cannot be made based on current evidence, cervical movement velocity may be a valuable predictor of disability to inform clinical decision-making in the management of neck pain. There is an urgent need for adequately powered prospective longitudinal cohort studies using standardized prognostic factor and outcome measurements for people with neck pain. The lack of studies on thoracic pain and activity measures in a real environment for neck pain limit inference and warrant focused future research.
