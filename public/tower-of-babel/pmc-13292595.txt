Integrating genomics and transcriptomics to dissect genetic variants associated with feed efficiency and growth traits in chicken
Authors: Yapeng Zhang, Yin Zhang, Na Luo, Keqi Cai, Xiaochen Liang, Yi Lan, Ranran Liu, Guiping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeed efficiency (FE) and growth traits are key determinants of profitability in the chicken industry. Using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in 432 Wenchang chickens, we identified 28 significant and 1,063 suggestive genes associated with 6 FE and growth traits. We conducted colocalization analyses between GWAS loci and cis-eQTLs and detected 4 GWAS loci with 4 unique eGenes including LAP3, PDS5A, KLHL5 and KLB. We also examined the cis-eQTL analysis results of ChickenGTEx and integrated them with the results of our GWAS, which detected two SNPs (4_75971034, 4_75971177) regulating LAP3. Moreover, two significant InDels (4_75971045, 4_75971140) are identified in the LAP3 gene using GWAS, three genotypes significantly affecting the expression levels of this gene in transcriptomic analyses of 114 liver tissues, and highly linked to two SNPs, so these InDels may also affect LAP3. Our findings reveal key genetic variants and genes, contributing to a deeper elucidation of the molecular mechanisms that drive FE and growth traits in chickens.Keywords: WGS, GWAS, InDel, Colocalization analyses, Linkage disequilibrium

Introduction

As a primary source of animal protein for humans, chicken and their products are a cornerstone of global food security (Smith, et al., 2013). With feed accounting for over 70% of total production costs, enhancing feed efficiency (FE) remains a primary driver of genetic improvement in chicken breeding programs. (Zampiga, et al., 2018). In poultry, FE capture the dynamic between feed intake (FI) and growth, commonly quantified via feed conversion ratio (FCR) and residual feed intake (RFI). The RFI metric was first introduced by Koch (1963), later adapted to chickens by Luiting (1991) covering the calculation methodologies, heritability assessments (Koch, et al., 1963; Luiting and Urff, 1991). Growth traits are key in poultry breeding, and the properties of growth traits need to be considered in breeding for FE (Cai, et al., 2024). FE and growth traits are critical determinants of productivity and have been extensively studied (He, et al., 2023; Lan, et al., 2025; Li, et al., 2020). Unraveling the molecular genetic basis of these traits holds the key to unlocking the full genetic potential for more profitable and sustainable poultry production.

GWAS leveraging SNPs to dissect the genetic architecture have been widely adopted in livestock, particularly in cattle (Higgins, et al., 2018; Seabury, et al., 2017; Zhang, et al., 2025), pig (Bertolini, et al., 2026; Godinho, et al., 2018; Lin, et al., 2024; Silva, et al., 2019) and chicken (Marchesi, et al., 2021; Tan, et al., 2024; Wang, et al., 2024; Zhang, et al., 2021b). These GWAS have identified numerous candidate genes and laid the groundwork for genomic breeding. After SNPs, InDels represent the second most abundant and widely distributed class of genetic polymorphisms in the genome (Zhao, et al., 2024). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has greatly facilitated detection of InDels, consequently revealing their significant associations with multiple complex traits (Craven, et al., 2022). In pig and cattle, InDels have been reported to be related to milk composition, female fertility, backfat thickness and other traits (Crespo-Piazuelo, et al., 2019; Jiang, et al., 2016; Li, et al., 2021; Liu, et al., 2024; Xu, et al., 2020; Yuan, et al., 2025; Zang, et al., 2016). In chicken, InDels were found to affect body size, growth, comb and other traits (Fu, et al., 2020; Jiang, et al., 2025; Yang, et al., 2021; Zhang, et al., 2023). These reports underscore the importance of InDels as functional variants shaping genetic architecture underlying complex traits.

Here, we conducted GWAS to analyze the FE and growth traits of Wenchang chicken. To further validate the candidate genes identified by GWAS, we performed colocalization analyses to fine-map causal variants and transcriptomic analyses to examine gene expression, thereby providing biological evidence for their roles in FE and growth traits. Our research aimed to identify genetic variants associated with FE and growth traits, thereby enhancing understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Material and methods

Animals and sample collection

This study involved 546 Wenchang chickens obtained from a commercial line at Hainan (Tanniu) Wenchang Chicken Co., Ltd. All chickens were housed individually in three-tier battery cages under uniform feeding and management regimes. A cohort of 432 chickens was used for phenotypic data collection from 87 to 113 days of age. For transcriptome analysis, liver samples were harvested from 114 birds, which were from a different batch than the 432 individuals used for phenotypic data collection, immediately after slaughter, rinsed with sterile PBS (pH 6.8), transferred to 2 mL tubes, and rapidly freeze in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction.

Phenotyping and quality control

Six traits were assessed, comprising body weight at 87 d (BW87) and 113 d (BW113), average daily feed intake (ADFI), RFI, metabolic body weight in the middle of the test (MWT), and FI. FI for each bird was recorded by summing the feed consumption from 87 to 113 days, and ADFI was subsequently derived by dividing this total by the number of test days. ADG was calculated from the body weight difference between 87 d (BW87) and 113 d (BW113) divided by the interval days. Genotyping, variant calling and controlling

At 112 days of age, blood samples (2 mL) were collected from the wing vein into EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes, gently mixed, and stored at -20°C for subsequent analysis. DNA was isolated using the TIANamp Blood DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). DNA quality was evaluated with a NanoPhotometer N50 (Implen, Munich, Germany), and samples with an A260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were deemed qualified for sequencing. Paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were constructed for each qualified individual and subsequently sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

Trimmomatic (v0.39) was employed to filter out adapter-contaminated reads, low-quality sequences, and reads with a mean quality score below Q20 (Bolger, et al., 2014). Clean reads totaling 414.66 billion were obtained, corresponding to 39.03–166.80 million reads per sample. High sequencing fidelity was confirmed by average Q20 (96.76%) and Q30 (91.41%) scores (Supplementary Table 1). Clean reads were then mapped to GRCg6a using bwa (v 0.7.18)(Li and Durbin, 2009), followed by sorting with SAMtools (v1.21) (Li, et al., 2009), and duplicate removal with Picard (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). SNP and InDel were obtained with GATK (v4.05)(McKenna, et al., 2010), and filtered separately using the parameters listed in Supplementary Table 2. For the final variant filtration, we used PLINK (v1.90)(Purcell, et al., 2007) with the parameters –geno 0.1 –hwe 0.000001 –maf 0.05 –mind 0.1. These steps yielded a final dataset of 12,263,342 SNPs and 1,017,295 InDels for subsequent analysis.

The suggestive significance threshold was corrected using the Bonferroni method and calculated as 1/N, where N denotes the effective number of independent variants. This resulted in thresholds of 8.15 × 10⁻⁸ for SNPs and 9.83 × 10⁻⁷ for InDels. Alternative significance threshold was set at 1 × 10⁻⁵. In order to better understand variants, we combined SNP and InDel together with bcftools (v1.17) (Genovese, et al., 2024) and subsequently determined LD blocks in candidate regions with Haploview (v4.2) (Barrett, et al., 2005).

RNA extraction and sequencing

RNA was isolated from liver samples with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Life Technologies), integrity was evaluated using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and samples with a high-quality (RIN > 7) were deemed qualified for cDNA library construction using the Illumina TruSeq™ RNA Kit. Library concentration and purity were assessed with a Qubit fluorometer and NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), respectively. The libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads.

RNA-Seq data analysis and colocalization analysis

Fastp (v0.21.0)(Chen, et al., 2018) was employed to filter raw reads, after which reads were then mapped to GRCg6a with STAR (v2.7.11a)(Dobin, et al., 2013), followed by converting and sorting with SAMtools (v1.21)(Li, Handsaker, Wysoker, Fennell, Ruan, Homer, Marth, Abecasis and Durbin, 2009). FeatureCounts (v2.1.1)(Liao, et al., 2014) and Stringtie (v3.0.0)(Pertea, et al., 2015) were subsequently applied for read counting and per kilobase per million mapped reads (TPM) normalization across samples. Obtain the three genotypes of candidate variations, along with their corresponding phenotypes and gene expression levels, and explore the relationships between genotypes, phenotypes, and gene expression levels using R. Using cis-eQTL result of liver tissue from chickenGTEx project, we performed colocalization analysis with GWAS summary statistics for FE and growth traits using the COLOC package (Giambartolomei, et al., 2014). A posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PP.H4) greater than 0.8 was considered strong evidence that a single shared causal variant influences both the gene expression level and the complex trait.

Colocalization, linkage disequilibrium and transcriptome gene expression

To further explore the genetic factors underpinning complex traits in chickens, we carried out colocalization analyses and identified four GWAS loci colocalizing with cis-eQTLs of unique eGenes including KLB, PDS5A, KLHL5 and LAP3 (Table 3). Moreover, we identified the significant SNP (4_75971177) and InDels (4_75971140 and 4_75971045) are located in the same region and annotated same genes (Fig. 5). In order to better understand these variants, we merged SNPs and InDels and identified four blocks ranging from 2 to 12 variants (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 6). SNP (4_75971177) had a high correlation with InDel (4_75971140, R2=0.88), InDel (4_75971045) had a high correlation with SNP (4_75971034, R2=0.96). We also examined the genes in the ChickenGTEx database and SNP (4_75971177 and 4_75971034) that affect the gene of LAP3 (Fig. 5D, Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4). The gene expression of KLB, RPL9, KLHL5 and LAP3 in 114 liver tissue is shown in Supplementary Table 7. The identified variants act as cis-eQTLs, with different genotypes significantly affecting the expression levels of their corresponding genes (Fig. 2; Fig. 4C; Fig. 5G and Fig. 5H).

Discussion

Whole-genome sequencing provides access to a wider array of potentially causal variants, enabling QTL detection that can deepen insights into the genetics of FE and growth traits. Accurate variance component and genetic parameter estimation is equally vital for interpreting phenotypic inheritance and deriving breeding values (Cavani, et al., 2015; Visscher, et al., 2008). Phenotype data were collected from day 87 to 113, consistent with the experimental timeline used in previous studies on yellow-feathered chickens (Cai, Liu, Wei, Wang, Cui, Luo, Wen, Chang and Zhao, 2024; Luo, et al., 2025). Heritability estimates for FE and growth traits using SNP data ranged from 0.11 to 0.29, reflecting low to moderate inheritance and corroborating prior studies (Cai, Liu, Wei, Wang, Cui, Luo, Wen, Chang and Zhao, 2024; Li, Liu, Zheng, Feng, Liu, Guo, Zhao and Wen, 2020). The genetic correlation with the RFI trait exhibits a notably large standard error, likely due to the relatively low heritability of RFI, which impedes reliable decomposition of genetic variance components. As a result, the estimation of the genetic covariance structure becomes highly unstable. Furthermore, our sample size is relatively small (n = 432), contributing to the increased standard error.

We identified 603 and 53 candidate genes related to FE traits from SNP and InDel data, respectively. In particular, 4_69301432_InDel and 4_69295075_InDel were annotated within the genes KLB and RPL9, respectively. The candidate genes RPL9 and KLB were mapped to a genomic region spanning 69.26–69.41 Mb on GGA4. The fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), whose demethylase activity is crucial for adipocyte differentiation (Ben-Haim, et al., 2015; Zhang, et al., 2015), has been shown to regulate RPL9 expression (Li, et al., 2025; Zhuang, et al., 2023a). Given this regulatory relationship, RPL9 may play a role in fat deposition through its effects on adipocytes. KLB can affect muscle development and human obesity (Cortes-Araya, et al., 2022; Ji, et al., 2019). These two genes are thus implicated as putative regulators of FE traits based on our evidence.

The 74.65–76.17 Mb interval on GGA4 contained a significant QTL for BW87, BW113, and MWT. Nine candidate genes within this colocalized region—MED28, QDPR, NCAPG, FAM184B, LAP3, LCORL, SLIT2, LDB2, and KCNIP4—were implicated in growth trait regulation. NCAPG and LCORL are considered pleiotropic genes in cattle, as they have been repeatedly associated with ADG (Zhang, et al., 2016), FI (Lindholm-Perry, et al., 2013) and live weight(Niu, et al., 2025). Furthermore, LCORL loci have been found to contain sweep-driving variants underlying recent selection signatures in beef cattle breeds, exerting notable effects on body size and growth rate(Bai, et al., 2025). NCAPG and LCORL also emerged as candidate genes for body weight in chicken (Jiang, Chu, Wei, Gu, Zhang, Ren, Chen, Miao, Yu, Muhatai, Liu and Qu, 2025; Wang, et al., 2023). Moreover, NCAPG was reported to be a functional gene for the eviscerated weight phenotype in chicken(Zhu, et al., 2025). LAP3 as a candidate gene involved in growth traits (Doyle, et al., 2020; Liu, et al., 2013), muscle development (Ge, et al., 2022), body weight (La, et al., 2019). The MED28 gene is located next to LAP3, has been reported to be related to intramuscular fat content (Anton, et al., 2018), live weight (Ramos, et al., 2023) and muscle development (Zhuang, et al., 2023b). By regulating tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), a cofactor critical for neurotransmitter synthesis and blood pressure regulation, QDPR is positioned as an important gene participated in growth regulation.(Girish, et al., 2024). In chicken, LDB2 is an important gene for tissue organ development and its expression is closely associated with late-stage growth (Becker, et al., 2002; Gu, et al., 2011; Liu, Sun, Zhao, Wang, Wu, Zheng, Chen, Zhang, Hu and Wen, 2013). KCNIP4 and SLIT2 have been implicated as candidate genes influencing body weight in chicken (Cha, et al., 2021; Jiang, Chu, Wei, Gu, Zhang, Ren, Chen, Miao, Yu, Muhatai, Liu and Qu, 2025; Wang, et al., 2022; Wang, et al., 2020). Moreover, KCNIP4 and FAM184B have been reported can affect the growth in chicken (Cha, Choo, Srikanth, Lee, Son, Park, Kim, Jang and Park, 2021; Jin, et al., 2015; Wang, Wang, Li, Xiao, Guo, Gao, Wang, Zhang and Li, 2022). Our GWAS findings corroborate the evidence presented in these published reports.

The liver serves as a central hub for numerous physiological processes, mediating blood detoxification, hormone regulation, carbohydrate metabolism, and protein synthesis (Ahn, et al., 2019). In addition, de novo lipogenesis, the metabolic process by which glucose is converted into triglycerides, occurs primarily in the liver of chickens. (Walzem, et al., 1999). The ChickenGTEx aims to investigating context-specific regulatory effects and understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying complex phenotypes (Fang, et al., 2025). Compared with ChickenGTEx (n=741), the number of our liver tissue samples is limited (n = 114)(Guan, et al., 2025). For deeper insight into the SNPs detected via GWAS, we downloaded the cis-eQTL result of liver tissue from the chicken GTEx project, then conducted colocalization analysis between GWAS loci and cis-eQTLs, and detected 4 SNPs with 4 unique eGenes including LAP3, PDS5A, KLHL5 and KLB. KLB and LAP3 can affect muscle development (Cortes-Araya, Stenhouse, Salavati, Dan-Jumbo, Ho, Ashworth, Clark, Esteves and Donadeu, 2022; Ge, Su, Wang, Gu, Cao, Lv, Wang, Getachew, Mwacharo, Haile, Yuan and Sun, 2022). Deficiency in PDS5A resulted in growth retardation, cleft palate, renal developmental defects, as well as an atypical skeletal pattern (Zhang, et al., 2009; Zhang, et al., 2021a). KLHL5, belongs to Kelch-like family, plays a role in signal transduction, protein degradation, and cytoskeletal dynamics (Di Giulio, et al., 2025). Furthermore, KLHL5 was identified as a candidate gene for 45-day and 90-day body weight, week 5, week6, week7, week8, week10, week11, week12, week13 feed intake, week 10-13 daily feed intake, week 5-7 daily gain, feed efficiency and total feed intake for liver tissue using TWAS from ChickenSexGTEx (Zhang, et al., 2026). Access the ChickenGTEx database, we found that there were 2 SNPs (4_75971177, 4_75971034) regulating LAP3 in our GWAS and cis-eQTL analysis. ChickenGTEx mainly analyzed SNP data and did not conduct research on InDel. Moreover, InDel (4_75971045) and InDel (4_75971140) were annotated to the LAP3 gene in our GWAS analysis and highly correlated with SNP (4_75971177, R2=0.88) and SNP (4_75971034, R2=0.96), which indicated two InDels are also affect LAP3 gene. These findings partially elucidate the genetic influence on the traits under investigation.

Conclusion

Our integrated GWAS and eQTL mapping approach provides novel insights into the genetic architecture underlying FE and growth traits in chickens. By pinpointing candidate genes such as LAP3 and the regulatory variants (two SNPs and two InDels) that control its expression, our research provides a valuable resource for dissecting the underlying biological mechanisms and guiding future breeding strategies aimed at improving poultry FE and growth traits.
