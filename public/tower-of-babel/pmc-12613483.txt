Early experience with ambulatory laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a regional hospital with limited ambulatory infrastructure: a retrospective cohort study using propensity score matching
Authors: Suppadech Tunruttanakul, Jeena Singhanat, Wanchai Manakijsirisuthi, Thanin Pinyopornpanit, Putticha Keawmanee, Kwanhathai Sakulsansern
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on outcomes of ambulatory surgery in Thailand remain limited. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the first procedure included in Thailand’s national policy for ambulatory surgery requiring more than local anesthesia or intravenous sedation. This study aimed to compare outcomes of ambulatory versus conventional inpatient laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a regional hospital lacking dedicated ambulatory surgery infrastructure.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy between May 2022 and June 2024. Data were obtained from the hospital’s ambulatory surgery registry and electronic medical records. The hospital did not have an established ambulatory unit, and ambulatory laparoscopic cholecystectomy had been implemented less than one year prior to the study period. Treatment allocation (ambulatory vs. inpatient) was determined by the attending surgeons and patient preference. To reduce bias, 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching was performed based on age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists class III status, obesity, and complicated gallstone disease. The primary outcomes were readmission and postoperative morbidity or mortality.ResultsA total of 570 eligible patients were included. After matching, 358 patients (179 per group) remained for analysis. Readmission and complication rates were comparable between groups, with no observed mortality. Risk ratios (95% confidence intervals) for readmission and complications were 2.00 (0.51, 7.89) and 0.67 (0.19, 2.32), respectively. Although the ambulatory group showed a slightly higher readmission rate, length of hospital stay was significantly shorter by approximately two days.ConclusionsIn the early implementation phase, among predominantly healthy patients, ambulatory laparoscopic cholecystectomy appeared safe and feasible in a resource-limited setting, with comparable morbidity and a non-significant increase in readmissions—likely attributable to the learning curve and limited patient familiarity with the ambulatory pathway.Trial registrationNot applicable as this study is a retrospective cohort study and not a clinical trial.Keywords: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Ambulatory surgical procedures, Cohort studies, Propensity score, Comparative study, Resource-Limited settings

Background

As growing surgical demand outpaces available hospital capacity [1]—particularly in aging and resource-constrained settings [2]—ambulatory surgery has emerged as a strategy to optimize surgical access and alleviate inpatient pressure. Defined as surgery performed without overnight admission, this model helps optimize healthcare efficiency while maintaining patient safety [3].

While many high-income countries have widely adopted ambulatory surgery programs across various specialties [4], implementation in Asia—particularly in low- and middle-income countries—remains limited [5–7]. In Thailand, ambulatory surgery has historically been confined to minor procedures performed under local or intravenous anesthesia.

To address hospital overcrowding, Thailand’s Ministry of Public Health has promoted one-day surgery (ODS) as a national strategy. In 2022, this initiative was expanded to include more complex procedures by integrating ODS with the national minimally invasive surgery service platform. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), the country’s most frequently performed minimally invasive operation, became the first procedure under this expanded model—referred to as ODS + or ambulatory LC (ODS-LC).

Despite this policy advancement, regional hospitals often lack dedicated ambulatory infrastructure, such as same-day units or formal anesthesia protocols, and local experience with ambulatory surgery remains limited. Moreover, to our knowledge, only one published study has examined ambulatory LC in Thailand—conducted at a university hospital with a relatively small sample size [8].

To address this gap, the present study reports a two-year experience of ambulatory LC at a regional tertiary hospital, offering real-world evidence from a non-academic, resource-constrained setting. These findings aim to inform future expansion of the ODS + model and support safe, scalable adoption of ambulatory surgery nationwide.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed an observational cohort design. Clinical records were reviewed for all patients who underwent elective LC between May 2022 and June 2024 at Sawanpracharak Hospital, a 700-bed tertiary care center in Nakhon Sawan, Thailand. Although the data covered this two-year period, access for research and analysis commenced only after ethical approval was obtained in August 2024.

Elective LC cases analyzed in this study were mostly from within the provincial area, as laparoscopic procedures are widely available in local hospitals, and referrals for uncomplicated cases are generally unnecessary. Given that ambulatory LC was newly introduced in Thailand, our surgical department maintained an institutional ODS registry to monitor implementation and safety. Data for ambulatory LC cases, including demographics, complications, readmissions, and three-day follow-up outcomes (including external care or admission), were retrieved from this registry. Additional ambulatory-related details and all conventional LC data were retrospectively obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system (EMR).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Sawanpracharak Hospital Ethical Committee for Research in Human Subjects (COA.50/2024). All data were de-identified, and confidentiality was maintained in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Study population

Inclusion criteria comprised all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent planned elective LC during the study period. We included cases with various gallstone-related indications to reflect real-world clinical practice. Exclusion criteria were: patients aged < 18 years, emergency/unplanned surgeries, and those undergoing complex additional interventions during the same admission, such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERC) or laparoscopic bile duct exploration. However, procedures such as intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) and drainage tube insertion were not excluded. If IOC identified common bile duct stones requiring further treatment, the case was excluded. We decided to include LC with IOC because it is a relatively simple procedure with minimal additional time requirements [9].

Patients lost to follow-up were managed as follows: if missing data accounted for < 5% of the sample, they were excluded under the assumption of minimal bias [10]. If >5% were missing, patients were contacted by telephone to verify outcomes.

Variables and definitions

Collected variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status, LC indications, operative time (in minutes), estimated blood loss, and conversion to open surgery. BMI was categorized using Asian-specific cutoffs: <27.5, 27.5 to < 32.5, and ≥ 32.5 kg/m² [11]. ASA status was determined by anesthesiologists at the indexed operation. LC indications were dichotomized as follows: symptomatic gallstones (binary code = 0) and more complex indications (binary code = 1), including resolved acute cholecystitis, previously treated common bile duct stones (via ERC), and gallstone pancreatitis. Only patients with resolved symptoms (for acute cholecystitis or gallstone pancreatitis) and successful prior ERC, when applicable, were included.

Interventions

Conventional LC

Patients received outpatient preoperative evaluation from both the surgeon and anesthesiologist. Internal medicine consultation was performed if deemed necessary. Patients were admitted one day prior to surgery. After the operation, they remained in the recovery room until cleared by the anesthesiology team, then transferred to the inpatient ward. Discharge was determined by the attending surgeon based on general criteria: stable vital signs, oxygen saturation > 90% on room air, adequate mobility, and manageable pain, nausea, and vomiting.

Ambulatory LC

This pathway followed the Thai National ODS platform protocol (Fig. 1). Patients received similar outpatient evaluations, with an additional visit to the ODS unit, where ODS nurses coordinated the process. Eligibility for ambulatory LC was confirmed by the anesthesiologist during the pre-anesthetic visit. If unsuitable, patients were referred back to the conventional pathway. Eligible patients arrived on the day of surgery. After the operation, they were monitored in the recovery area until approved for transfer to the regular inpatient ward. An ODS nurse assessed patients 4–6 h postoperatively using discharge criteria from the Modified Aldrete Scoring System and the Postanesthetic Discharge Scoring System [12], with discharge decisions based on these assessments. Follow-up included telephone calls within 24 h by the anesthesiologist and ODS nurse, and again at approximately 48 and 72 h by ODS nurses to assess symptoms such as pain, nausea/vomiting, activity level, wound condition, sore throat, and headache. All patients had a one-month in-person follow-up with the surgeon.

Patient allocation

As this was an observational study, the choice of surgical approach (ambulatory vs. conventional LC) was determined by the in-charge surgeons, in consultation with the patients. During the study period, seven surgeons performed LC at our institution.

Of these, three surgeons actively practiced ambulatory LC, provided that both they and the pre-anesthesia evaluation deemed it appropriate. All three had more than five years of LC experience.

Two surgeons consistently performed conventional inpatient LC, without consideration for the ambulatory approach. Both also had over five years of LC experience.

The remaining two surgeons generally preferred conventional inpatient LC, opting for ambulatory LC only in very young and fit patients. One of them had more than five years of experience, while the other had approximately two years of LC experience.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were 30-day readmission and morbidity/mortality. Morbidity included any postoperative complications, including those requiring readmission or medical intervention. Complications were classified according to Clavien–Dindo classification [13]. Minor versus major bile duct injury was defined by the extent of common bile duct circumferential involvement (≥ 25% = major; <25% = minor) [14]. Complications were identified from the hospital EMR for both groups. For the conventional (inpatient) cohort, events recorded during the index admission and any subsequent unplanned emergency department visits, returns to theatre, or readmissions documented in the EMR were abstracted. For the ambulatory cohort, structured telephone follow-up at 24, 48, and 72 h was conducted by ODS nurses; any reported adverse symptoms prompted EMR review. To reduce detection bias, comparative analyses of complications were based on EMR-documented events requiring healthcare utilization (e.g., emergency department visit, reoperation, or readmission); transient symptoms reported only by telephone are presented descriptively and were not counted as complications in matched analyses.

Secondary outcomes were early outpatient visits (unplanned visits before scheduled follow-up), length of hospital stay (in days), and hospital costs (in Thai Baht). Hospital costs were calculated by the hospital’s financial officers and included expenses for instruments, medications, anesthesia, surgery, nursing care, and room and food services. Lastly, we also report the outcomes from post-discharge telephone follow-ups conducted at 24, 48, and 72 h. These follow-ups assessed the following parameters, as outlined by Thailand’s National ODS Service Plan:

There is no standardized home medication protocol for either ambulatory or conventional LC in our department. However, patients are typically prescribed a combination of paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and antiemetics, with or without tramadol.

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was based on a non-inferiority hypothesis for readmission and morbidity rates. According to the latest meta-analysis, readmission rates were 2.4% for overnight stay and 2.0% for day surgery, while morbidity rates were 6.0% and 5.2%, respectively [15]. Using a non-inferiority test with 80% power and a significance level (alpha) of 0.05, the required sample sizes were calculated to exceed 30,000 for readmission and 20,000 for morbidity—both of which were unfeasible. Accordingly, we did not pursue a formal non-inferiority hypothesis. Instead, we conducted a two-year exploratory cohort study to generate preliminary, locally relevant estimates and assess the feasibility of ambulatory LC. Comparative analyses are interpreted as hypothesis-generating; we report effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) without claims of non-inferiority.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. For univariable comparisons, t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to continuous data, while Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data.

Missing data were managed using complete case analysis when missingness was below 5%, a threshold generally considered to introduce minimal bias [10]. For variables with a higher proportion of missingness, mean imputation was applied.

Propensity score methods

To reduce confounding in this observational study [16, 17], propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression based on the following covariates: age, sex, categorized BMI, ASA III status, and LC indication. 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.2 on the logit of the propensity score was employed. Operative time and estimated blood loss were treated as post-treatment mediators (i.e., variables occurring after treatment allocation [18]), and conversion rate was considered an intraoperative process measure; therefore, none of these variables were included in the propensity-score model. Because operative time and estimated blood loss may reflect surgeon performance, we adjusted for them in the post-matching outcome model as a sensitivity analysis. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD) and visualized through overlaid histograms of the propensity score distributions for each group. To further address residual confounding, covariates with SMDs ≥ 10% after matching were included in the outcome regression models as part of a double-adjustment approach [19].

Following matching, binary regression (generalized linear models for the binomial family) was used to analyze binary outcomes (readmission, morbidity/mortality, and early outpatient visits), while linear regression was used for continuous outcomes (length of stay and hospital costs). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Notably, the hospital costs reported were approximate and were not derived from a formal cost analysis; they reflect direct in-hospital costs only, and indirect costs were not captured.

As a sensitivity analysis to approximate surgeon skill [20], we fit outcome regression models in the matched sample further adjusted for operative time and estimated blood loss. We also conducted an elderly subgroup analysis (age ≥ 65 years [21]): the cohort was restricted to these patients, re-matched using the same 1:1 nearest-neighbor procedure (caliper = 0.2), and analyzed with the same post-matching adjustments.

Results

Patient recruitment is illustrated in Fig. 2. Over the two-year study period, a total of 570 eligible patients were included. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 358 patients remained (179 in each group). No patients were lost to follow-up in the ambulatory LC group, likely due to the structured follow-up protocol that included scheduled telephone calls. In the conventional LC group, eight patients (4.6%) were lost to follow-up. Since this was below the predefined 5% threshold, no follow-up contact was initiated, and these cases were excluded. Other than these eight cases, no missing data were identified for clinical or perioperative variables, as all necessary data were routinely documented in the hospital’s ODS registry and electronic medical records system.

A sensitivity analysis—hypothesized to account for surgeon skill—adjusted for operative time and estimated blood loss in the matched sample, is also presented in Table 2. Overall, the sensitivity analysis yielded findings consistent with the main analysis. Although not statistically significant, ambulatory LC continued to show a trend toward higher readmission rates compared to conventional LC, with RR for readmission 2.30 (95% CI: 0.58, 9.10).

With regard to readmission events, six patients (3.2%) in the ambulatory LC group and seven (1.8%) in the conventional LC group were readmitted during the study period. After matching, the number of readmissions was six (3.4%) in the ambulatory group and three (1.7%) in the conventional group. In the ambulatory group, readmissions were due to tramadol-induced anaphylaxis (n = 1), severe pain (n = 2; one with bowel ileus), severe anorexia (n = 1), a retained common bile duct (CBD) stone (n = 1), and cholangitis from an idiopathic distal bile duct stricture requiring ERC (n = 1). In the conventional group, three readmissions were due to surgical wound bleeding, postoperative ileus (not requiring surgery), and severe pain.

Postoperative complications for both the unmatched and the matched cohort are presented in Table 3. In the matched cohort, conventional and ambulatory LC had similar complication rates (3.4% vs. 2.2%) and comparable distributions by Clavien–Dindo grade and type.

Regarding secondary outcomes, early outpatient visits and hospital costs were similar between the two groups. As expected, the ambulatory group had a significantly shorter hospital stay, with an average reduction of approximately two days. The sensitivity analysis produced consistent findings.

To expand generalisability of the study results to include the elderly, a subgroup analysis was performed and is also presented in Table 2. The readmission rate in the elderly was comparable between groups (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.07, 14.9), with a benefit of an approximately two-day shorter hospital stay with ambulatory LC. Notably, these results are limited to relatively healthy elderly patients, as ambulatory LC required anaesthesiologist approval, and the sample available for analysis was small (n = 40; 20 per group). Comparisons for complications and early outpatient visits were not estimable because there were no events. Nonetheless, this subgroup analysis suggests that ambulatory LC may likewise benefit in healthy elderly patients.

Table 4 presents the results of postoperative telephone follow-ups conducted by ODS nurses to assess patients’ conditions after returning home. Of the total 185 patients, follow-up data were available for 171, as no calls were made to patients with extended hospital stays (e.g., those who underwent conversion to open surgery). Most patients tolerated pain well during the first 24 h, with 20.5% reporting moderate pain and only 1.2% reporting severe pain. Pain levels improved markedly by 48 and 72 h post-discharge. Within 24 h, the majority of patients were able to resume some level of activity, although a small proportion required assistance. The incidence of nausea/vomiting, wound oozing, fever, and headache was low. However, approximately half of the patients experienced sore throat, which gradually improved over subsequent days.

Discussion

Despite our limited ambulatory surgery experience and lack of dedicated infrastructure, this study demonstrates that ambulatory LC is a safe approach, with morbidity rates comparable to those of conventional inpatient LC (RR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.19, 2.32). The principal benefit of ambulatory LC was a shorter length of stay—approximately two days less than conventional LC. In the conventional pathway, this approximately two days shorter stay typically reflects one preoperative admission day and one postoperative observation day. Our prior work indicates that preoperative admission is non-essential for most LC patients [23]; if omitted, most patients in the conventional group (74.3% in the unmatched cohort) would be expected to stay about one day. Moreover, the ambulatory cohort in our study shows that the postoperative day can also be omitted in appropriately selected, low-risk patients, enabling same-day discharge. This two-day reduction benefits patients and families (less time off work) and helps hospitals with limited capacity by allowing staff and beds to be redirected to those who truly require inpatient care.

Although our data showed a non-significant trend toward a higher readmission rate (RR 2.00; 95% CI 0.51, 7.89), the overall incidence over the two-year period remained low and clinically acceptable at 3.4% (6 patients). Our postoperative telephone follow-ups indicated that most patients tolerated recovery well, with acceptable levels of pain, nausea, fever, and wound appearance. However, a notable proportion of patients reported sore throat, a symptom often overlooked [24]. Based on these findings, providing appropriate medication to alleviate sore throat symptoms prior to discharge might be beneficial [24].

Our observed trend toward a higher readmission rate contrasts with findings from published meta-analyses, which generally report comparable or lower readmission rates for ambulatory LC [15, 25]. We hypothesize that this difference may be due to lower physician experience with the ambulatory approach and the absence of a standardized home-medication protocol [26]. Additionally, patients in our setting may be less familiar with ambulatory care, leading to uncertainty about their postoperative status [27]. Furthermore, the relatively low hospital costs and easy access to medical services in our country [28] may lower the threshold for seeking readmission [29]. Among the six readmissions, approximately half (three cases involving severe pain and nausea) may have been avoidable. With increased provider experience and greater patient familiarity with the ambulatory model, readmission rates could potentially decrease. Notably, the occurrence of readmissions in the conventional inpatient LC group suggests that routine hospital admission does not necessarily prevent post-discharge complications, and inpatient observation alone may not guarantee avoidance of adverse events once patients return home.

In terms of hospital cost, our study did not demonstrate a financial advantage for ambulatory LC within the Thai healthcare context. This likely reflects Thailand’s relatively low inpatient care costs, driven by relatively low nursing wages and overnight hospital charges. However, our analysis did not account for indirect costs borne by patients and their families, such as lost income from absenteeism, travel and lodging expenses, and food costs during hospital stays [30]. These unmeasured burdens likely contribute to inefficient use of hospital resources and may place further strain on an already overstretched healthcare system. Additionally, inefficient resource allocation—particularly of human resources—may compromise the quality of care for patients who truly require inpatient hospitalization [31]. Thailand is also undergoing demographic aging, with a growing proportion of elderly individuals and a shrinking working-age population [32]. If this trend continues, hospital overcrowding is likely to become increasingly severe, exacerbating the strain on human resources, particularly nursing staff.

In our patient cohort, ambulatory LC accounted for only approximately one-third of all cases (185 out of 570 patients), indicating a relatively low adoption rate. A large proportion of ASA I and II patients—who would typically be considered suitable candidates for ambulatory surgery [33]—still underwent conventional inpatient LC. In contrast, over 80% of LC cases in high-income countries are performed in ambulatory settings [4]. This discrepancy suggests that full institutional adoption of the ambulatory approach has yet to be achieved. Although the national surgical service plan has promoted the ODS + protocol, its implementation remains voluntary. Moreover, the financial benefit of ambulatory LC at the hospital level appears limited, as demonstrated by our cost comparison. Currently, there are no national budget incentives specifically designed to promote the use of ambulatory LC. More than five years after the introduction of ODS+, many hospitals in Thailand have yet to adopt this approach. To accelerate implementation, stronger engagement from the Ministry of Public Health and the national service plan is needed. This may include, for example, enhanced public and professional education on the benefits of ambulatory surgery, financial incentives for participating hospitals, collaboration with specialist medical colleges, national benchmarking, and other supportive measures [3, 4].

A key strength of our study is that it reports real-world outcomes in a setting lacking dedicated ambulatory surgery infrastructure. By demonstrating that ambulatory LC can be safely performed with acceptable readmission rates, we hope this evidence will support other hospitals—particularly those still struggling to develop ambulatory surgery programs or awaiting infrastructure improvements—in adopting ambulatory protocols with greater confidence.

There were several limitations in this study that should be considered. First, due to its retrospective observational design, the study is subject to inherent biases and confounding [34]—particularly in how patients were allocated to ambulatory or conventional LC groups. Patients undergoing ambulatory LC tended to be younger and had fewer comorbidities. However, this reflects real-world practice, as younger and healthier patients are typically the first candidates for ambulatory surgery during the early phase of program development. Our sensitivity analysis, which applied stricter matching criteria, provides further support for the safety of ambulatory LC in this low-risk population.

Second, surgeon preference played a role in group allocation, with some surgeons consistently choosing a particular approach. Differences in surgical skill may have influenced the outcomes [20]. In our data (Table 1), the matched cohort still showed differences in operative time and estimated blood loss, likely reflecting residual variation in surgical skill. To address this, we conducted a post-matching sensitivity analysis adjusting for these variables, which yielded results broadly similar to the non-adjusted model. To more robustly adjust for surgeon skill, surgeon identifiers should be incorporated into the analytic model. However, these identifiers were not available in our dataset because they are considered sensitive at our center; therefore, residual confounding by surgeon skill may persist. Nevertheless, this highlights the importance of adequate surgical experience when implementing ambulatory protocols. We recommend that surgeons adopting this approach have sufficient experience to ensure patient safety.

Third, the absence of standardized protocols for anesthesia, surgical technique, and postoperative care at our institution likely contributed to variations in postoperative outcomes, as observed in the telephone follow-ups. Because analgesic prescribing was not protocolized, data on drug types and dosages were not systematically captured and therefore could not be included in the analysis. This limitation may also have affected readmission patterns: in the matched ambulatory LC cohort, three readmissions (50%) were related to pain and/or nausea/vomiting. While standardized protocols can help establish consistent care and improve outcomes [35], enforcing strict protocols too early in the implementation phase may hinder collaboration among healthcare providers. A more flexible approach may initially be necessary to facilitate adoption and engagement.

Fourth, for complication outcomes, structured telephone follow-up was applied to ambulatory patients but not to inpatient controls, likely increasing capture of minor, self-limited symptoms in the ambulatory group and introducing differential detection bias. To mitigate this, primary comparative analyses were restricted to EMR-documented events requiring healthcare utilization (e.g., emergency department visit, reoperation, or readmission) in both groups, and complication details are presented separately in Table 3 for transparency. Residual detection bias for minor complications remains possible and should be considered when interpreting the results. Notably, in the propensity score–matched cohort, the ambulatory group had a lower overall complication rate, lending support to the robustness of these findings.

Finally, the calculated sample size for this study was not feasible to achieve. As a result, the study may have been underpowered to detect statistically significant differences. However, compared to previous studies conducted in our country [8] and the sample sizes reported in relevant meta-analyses [15, 25], the number of participants in this study is considered moderate and likely reflects real-world clinical practice.

Conclusion

Ambulatory LC appears to be a safe and well-tolerated approach, even in settings without dedicated ambulatory infrastructure. During the early implementation phase, a non-significant trend toward higher readmission may occur, likely reflecting the learning curve, lack of system support, and limited patient familiarity with the ambulatory pathway. These findings primarily reflect a selected, low-risk cohort in an early-implementation setting and should not be extrapolated to higher-risk patients.
