Perceived social support and psychological resilience as mediators between social frailty and the frailty of older patients with heart failure: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Junting Huang, Xiaobo Liu, Duolao Wang, Xiaorong Luan, Wanxia Yao, Zhengzhou Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study explores the level of frailty and the factors that influence elderly HF inpatients. It also examines the correlation between social frailty, perceived social support, and psychological resilience. It verifies psychological resilience and perceived social support as mediators between social frailty and frailty.MethodThis cross-sectional study collected four hundred fifty-six questionnaires from Northeast China, Northwest China and South China. The research assessment tools include the FRAIL scale (frailty), MSPSS scale (perceived social support), CD-RISC10 (psychological resilience), and HALFT scale (social frailty). Data analysis was performed using multiple regression analysis and the SPSS PROCESS Macro plug-in Model 6 and Model 4.ResultsFemale (β = 0.085, p = 0.036), number of hospitalizations (β = 0.342, p < 0.001), and NYHA class (β = 0.325, p = 0.004) were risk factors for frailty in elderly HF inpatients. Frailty was found to be at a medium-high level, negatively correlated with perceived social support (ρ=-0.471, p < 0.01) and psychological resilience (ρ=-0.670, p < 0.01), and positively correlated with social frailty (ρ = 0.596, p < 0.01). Psychological resilience and perceived social support mediated the relation between social frailty and frailty, with an indirect effect of 27.18%, 10.49%, and 24.19%, respectively, all partial mediation.ConclusionNYHA class, number of hospitalizations, and female sex are indicators of the frailty level of elderly HF patients. Social frailty, psychological resilience, and perceived social support are interrelated. Psychological resilience and perceived social support are mediators in the relation between social frailty and frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13030-025-00345-9.

Introduction

Heart failure [1] is a clinical syndrome defined by symptoms or signs resulting from structural or functional cardiac abnormalities, frequently accompanied by elevated natriuretic peptide levels or evidence of fluid accumulation in the lungs or peripheral tissues [2, 3]. HF represents a significant public health issue due to its high prevalence and association with substantial morbidity and mortality, even over short timeframes. Globally, HF affects more than 64 million individuals, including approximately 8.9 million patients in China [4]. Elevated hospital readmission rates and substantial hospitalization costs impose considerable burdens on families affected by HF. Anxiety and depression are prevalent among individuals with HF, largely due to fear of impaired cardiac function [5]. Additionally, the physical limitations, required lifestyle modifications, and uncertainty inherent to HF frequently contribute to the onset or worsening of anxiety and depression. Consequently, prioritizing both the physical and psychological health of elderly patients with HF is essential.

Frailty is an adverse clinical state caused by a variety of factors, and the level of frailty spikes during stressful states in older adults, inducing adverse cardiovascular health outcomes, of which social and physical frailty are common in patients with HF [6, 7]. Frailty is a common predictor of disability, hospitalization and death, and social frailty is also strongly linked to rising rates of disability and mortality [8–10]. The coexistence of physical and social frailty significantly elevates the likelihood of disability onset. A study showed that more than one in five adults aged 65 years and above experience frailty, with women making up a larger share of this group [11]. Another study revealed that about 48% of elderly individuals were pre-frail [10]. However, the prevalence of frailty in older inpatients ranges from 27% to 80% [11]. A study noted that individuals who began with social frailty were four times more likely to develop physical or pre-existing frailty within four years compared to their peers [12]. Another study claimed that frailty affects social frailty after conducting a longitudinal analysis that verified that slower steps and waning muscle strength, the hallmarks of physical frailty, can quietly set the stage for future struggles in loss of social functions [6].

Social frailty (SF) is a vital indicator of the social aspect of health. It describes a person’s lack of social resources to connect, participate, and seek support in the social world [13]. Social frailty is the lack of a person’s ability to access social resources. The social frailty rate in elderly individuals in Spain is 8.9%, while in Japan, it is 23.26% [14]. However, the social frailty rate in hospitalized older individuals is as high as 53.13% [13]. Research revealed that SF is not only closely linked to the emergence and progression of overall frailty, but may even appear before frailty itself takes hold [6]. A cohort study revealed that SF is closely related to depression, anxiety, obesity, and cardiovascular disease’s other physical symptoms and can independently predict mortality in older adults [7]. In addition, hospitalization rates, disability, and cognitive impairment in older patients were also relatively linked with SF [7]. Chinese families have indeed experienced a significant shift from multi-generational living to alternative living arrangements such as living with spouse only or living alone, which has greatly increased the likelihood of social frailty in older HF individuals. Furthermore, as older adults age, they gradually retire, become physically weaker, and experience irreversible changes in their physical and mental health, leading to functional loss. This puts them at high risk for loneliness, significantly increasing the incidence of social frailty. Notably, social frailty in older individuals is a universal challenge that affects lives across countries, cultures, and backgrounds.

Perceived social support (PSS) includes family social support (SS), friends SS, and others SS [15]. When PSS is scarce, it leaves individuals more vulnerable [16]. According to the social relationship escort theory, when older people actively construct and expand their friend relationship networks, these support networks can make up for the lack of social resources in the elderly, bring positive emotional experiences to the elderly, and reduce their sense of loneliness and helplessness [17]. A study found that increased PSS is related to a more minor increase in frailty for older individuals who are already frail [18]. A shortage of PSS stands among the many biological forces that quietly accelerate the ageing process. Communities need to actively promote community interaction, organize community activities, and guide older people to understand and use community resources to increase their sense of social participation. In addition, family members are encouraged to communicate more with older people to understand their needs and feelings [19]. The combined effect of the above measures can reduce the risk of frailty.

Psychological resilience (PR) is the inner strength to ‘bounce back’ and adapt positively to adverse events [20]. Ungar sees PR as an interaction between psychological, biological, social and ecological systems [21]. Currently, our understanding of PR is largely shaped by studies focused on conditions like mental illness, HIV/AIDS, cancer, and intestinal diseases. However, the PR of HF patients, particularly within hospital walls, remains a largely uncharted territory [22]. A sweeping review of twelve clinical trials brought together people facing chronic illnesses like tumor, heart disease, and diabetes, all to explore new ways to encourage PR [23]. While the heterogeneity among the studies slightly limited the overall strength and reach of the results, programs designed to boost PR still offered clear benefits. They eased depression, anxiety, and frailty, empowered patients to notice meaningful changes in their lives, and encouraged better commitment to treatment plans.

Each episode process of HF is a source of stress for patients. Drawing from the theory of unpleasant symptoms (TOUS) [24] and Roy’s adaption model [25, 26], resilience emerges as a powerful psychological asset, enabling individuals to navigate tragedy, adversity, trauma, and other challenges with strength and adaptability. When the heart’s ability to pump falters in elderly HF patients, the body swiftly rallies its defenses, activating the sympathetic nervous system, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and other pathways to preserve vital balance. Heart failure is a clinical syndrome characterized by systemic effects; compensatory mechanisms extend beyond biological processes and encompass psychosocial factors influencing individual functioning. When older HF patients over the long term suffer from SF and lack support from friends, family, and significant others, PR plays a vital role in helping individuals adapt to disease and against frailty. Therefore, we propose the four hypotheses below. Hypothesis 1: SF is related to frailty. Hypothesis 2, PSS (family, friends, other), PR, SF and frailty are interrelated. Hypothesis 3, PSS and PR multiple chain-mediate between SF and frailty. Hypothesis 4, family SS, friends SS and other SS multiply mediate SF and frailty. Figure 1 and 2 shows the mediating models. Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study recruited eligible older HF individuals from three Class A tertiary hospitals in Northeast China (Shandong Province), Northwest China (Shaanxi Province), and Southwest China (Guizhou Province) between January and December 2023. Inclusion criteria included: patients diagnosed with HF by a cardiologist; aged 60 years or older; voluntary participation; and fluent listening, speaking, reading, and writing skills. Exclusion criteria included: individuals with comorbid neurological illness like dementia, stroke, and epilepsy; individuals with serious mental diseases; for example, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia; and individuals with functional hearing or visual impairment.

This study employed a basic yet practical approach to estimate the sample size, selecting a range of 10 to 20 participants for each variable examined. Because 14 variables were examined in this study, sample sizes of 140–280 were required. To account for invalid questionnaires, the sample size was increased by 15%, resulting in 161–322 participants. A total of 456 patients with HF returned the questionnaire. Ethical approval and procedures

Ethical approval was granted by the host university’s ethics committee (approval number: 2023-R-004). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant was personally informed about the study’s voluntary nature, the promise of confidentiality, the commitment to fairness, and the assurance of safety. Only after these assurances did participants provide their written consent.

This study employed a rigorous data collection process. Prior to data collection, two nursing PhD students completed a data collection training course and received approval from hospital management. Data collection was conducted entirely on-site using paper questionnaires, with each participant completing each questionnaire independently. If elderly HF individuals had any confusion about data entry, the nursing students aided them without interfering with the content of their responses. Data validity and completeness were immediately verified after data collection. Any questions were cross-checked with the patients by the nursing students to ensure data authenticity.

Assessment tools

General information scale

A general information scale was applied to acquire demographic and medical information. Demographic information included age, sex, marital condition, location, education level, and monthly income. Medical information included number of hospitalizations, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, and number of chronic diseases. Supplementary Table 1 gives the criteria and characteristics of each NYHA classification. Supplementary Table 3 gives the coding of the category variables.

Help, participation, loneliness, financial, and talk scale

The Help, Participation, Loneliness, Finance, and Talk (HALFT) scale was used to assess social frailty [27, 28]. The five dimensions of HALFT scale are inability to help others, limited social participation, loneliness, financial difficulties, and having no one to confide in. The five items on the HALFT are: In the past year, have you helped friends or family (Item 1)? In the past year, have you participated in social or leisure activities (Item 2)? Was your income last year sufficient to cover your living expenses for the year (Item 3)? Do you have someone to confide in every day (Item 5)? Answering “no” to any of these items is scored as 1 point. In the past week, have you felt lonely (Item 4)? Answering “yes” to this item is scored as 1 point. Total scores on this scale range from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social frailty. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the HALFT scale is 0.736.

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) was used to assess perceived social support [29, 30]. This scale encompasses three dimensions: family social support, friends social support, and others social support. Each dimension consists of four items, for a total of 12 items. The MSPSS uses a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating strong disagreement and 7 indicating strong agreement. Total scores range from 12 to 84, with higher MSPSS scores indicating higher levels of perceived social support. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the Chinese version of the MSPSS is 0.906.

Ten-item Connor-Davidson resilience scale

The Ten-Item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC10) was used to assess psychological resilience [31, 32]. This scale consists of 10 statements, and respondents rate the extent to which each statement applied to them over the past month. The CD-RISC10 uses a 5-point Likert scale, with the total score ranging from 0 to 40. Higher scores indicate greater psychological resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Chinese version of the CDRISC-10 is 0.965.

Fatigue, resistance, aerobic, illnesses, loss of weight scale

The Fatigue, Resistance, Aerobic, Illnesses, and Loss of Weight (FRAIL) Scale was used to assess frailty [33, 34]. The scale consists of five dimensions and items: Fatigue: Do you feel tired? Resistance: Unable to climb one flight of stairs? Aerobic: Unable to walk one block? Illness: Do you have five or more illnesses? Weight Loss: Have you lost more than 5% of your weight in the past six months? A “yes” answer is scored as 1 point, with a total score of 0 to 5 and higher scores indicating a higher level of frailty. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Chinese version of the FRAIL is 0.87.

Data analysis

This study used IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) for data analysis. Categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables are given as means (M) and standard deviations (SD). Because some variables were not normally distributed, as indicated by skewness and kurtosis tests, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used to examine relations. For univariate analysis of frailty, independent samples t-tests and ANOVA were applied. It used regression analysis to identify factors influencing frailty in multivariate analysis. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was below 5, indicating no multicollinearity. In the regression models, variables significant in univariate analysis were included in Model 1, social frailty (SF) in Model 2, and perceived social support (PSS) and psychological resilience (PR) in Model 3.

The SPSS PROCESS Macro Plugin Model 6 was used for multiple chain mediation analyses, with SF as the independent variable (X), PSS (M1), PR (M2), and frailty as the outcome (Y) [35]. Indirect effects were examined through PSS (a1b1), PR (a2b2), and both combined (a1d21b2); the total indirect effect was their sum. The direct effect (c’) and total effect (c) were calculated accordingly. All covariates were controlled for, and significance was tested using a bootstrap CI (5000 samples); effects were significant if the 95% CI did not include zero.

Multiple mediation analyses also used the SPSS PROCESS Macro Plugin Model 4, with SF as X, family SS (M1), friends SS (M2), other SS (M3), and frailty as Y [27]. Indirect effects were assessed through each mediator (a1b1, a2b2, a3b3), with the total indirect effect being their sum. Direct (c’) and total (c) effects were defined as before. All covariates were included, and effect significance was determined using a bootstrap CI (5000 samples); significance was determined when the 95% CI excluded zero.

Multiple-factor analysis of frailty

Stepwise regression analysis revealed three models. In Model 1, the adjusted R-squared value was 0.296. The number of hospitalizations, NYHA class, and gender explained 29.6% of the variation in frailty. In Model 2, including social frailty increased the adjusted R-squared value to 0.392. In Model 3, the R-squared value increased to 0.652. Perceived social support and psychological resilience explained an additional 26% of the variance. The effect of SF on frailty was significantly smaller in this model. This suggests that PSS and PR may mediate the link between SF and overall frailty. Table 4 has more details.

Mediation analysis of PSS and PR between SF and frailty

The bootstrap results showed that the standardized coefficient for the effect of SF on PSS (path a1) was − 0.6323 (95% CI: −12.2012, −7.0785). The coefficient for PSS on frailty (path b1) was − 0.1967 (95% CI: −0.0181, −0.0084). For PSS on PR (path d21), the coefficient was 0.1491 (95% CI: 0.0182, 0.1269). The effect of SF on PR (path a2) was − 0.2175 (95% CI: −3.1894, −0.0374). The effect of PR on frailty (path b2) was − 0.5092 (95% CI: −0.0786, −0.0620). The total effect (path c) of SF on frailty was 0.7408 (95% CI: 0.5848, 0.9329). The direct effect (path c’) was 0.4577 (95% CI: 0.3288, 0.6089). The total indirect effect of social frailty on frailty (path a1*b1 + path a1*d21*b1 + path a2*b2) was 0.2831 (95% CI: 0.1642, 0.4178). The indirect effect of the sequential mediation by PSS and PR was 10.49%, calculated as (path a1*d21*b1) divided by (path c’). This indicates partial mediation. The mediating effect size of PSS was 27.18%, calculated as (path a1*b1) divided by (path c’). The mediating effect size of PR was 24.19%, calculated as (path a2*b2) divided by (path c’). Discussion

This study looked at how SF relates to the frailty of older Chinese patients with HF. We also considered how demographic and disease factors, PSS, and PR influence the development of frailty. Our results indicated that SF, PSS, PR were related to frailty. It also showed that PSS and PR act as mediators in the relation between SF and frailty. These findings suggest that lowering SF may help reduce the frailty of older HF patients.

We found that the number of hospitalizations, being female, and NYHA class are important factors linked to the frailty of older individuals with heart failure. Our study showed that as the number of hospitalizations increased, so did the degree of frailty. Frequent hospital stays can add to the psychological and financial stress of patients and their families [36]. During these stays, patients often need various treatments and medications. These can also contribute to frailty. Re-hospitalization often is the result of disease progression, infections, hypertensive crises, arrhythmias, not following medical advice, or other health problems. These may further worsen frailty. The NYHA class helps assess the severity of frailty in these patients. Its criteria are similar to those of heart failure symptoms. A higher NYHA class indicates that the heart pumps less effectively, symptoms are more severe, and frailty is more likely. Leong’s results showed that NYHA class is an independent adverse element for frailty in elderly people with heart failure [1]. The risk is higher in NYHA classes III and IV than in class II. This matches our findings. Female individuals are more likely to experience frailty. This may be because the loss of estrogen after menopause can weaken muscles and nerves, raising the possibility of frailty. A comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that, across all age groups, older women consistently had greater frailty scores than their male counterparts [37].

Social frailty and frailty are positively correlated, meaning that as the level of social frailty increases, so does the level of frailty in older HF patients. Ma et al. pointed out that sex, education level, physical activity and comorbidities are common adverse elements for SF and frailty [12]. Tsutsumimoto found that when physical, psychological and social frailty intertwine, their combined impact increases the risk of disability and death far more than any single type of frailty on its own [38]. This research applied the HALFT to assess the SF level of elderly HF patients, including help, participation, loneliness, finance, and talking; and the FRAIL scale to assess frailty, including fatigue, activities of daily living, physical activity, comorbidity and weight change. Different dimensions may shape and influence each other in unexpected ways. Our study revealed a significant association between feelings of loneliness and frailty. Similarly, a prospective cohort study in the UK revealed that, for the elderly, frailty and loneliness can intertwine and directly shape their ability to engage socially [39]. Across China, a wave of low birth rates, an aging society, and smaller families is leaving more older adults to live alone. This growing solitude can heighten both social frailty and vulnerability among these individuals [6, 13]. Physical activity among elderly people is indirectly related to social and physical frailty through chronic disease, nutritional status and depression [40]. The main reason is that regular physical activity by elderly individuals can improve their disease resistance and adaptability and maintain a dynamic balance in energy metabolism. In addition, regular activity can cause the body to secrete beta-endorphins, increase neurotransmitters, and induce positive subjective experiences so that older people can maintain good physical and mental health and integrate into society.

Low PSS levels are related to an increased incidence of frailty. Fang has shown that PSS is related to a lower level of frailty among community older individuals, consistent with our study [41]. Another study reveals that the PR of older adults can be safeguarded when they receive meaningful social support from both family and their wider community [16]. Hailey found that PSS outside the family can help protect against weak grip strength [42]. Another study showed that PSS can enhance sports performance by decreasing perceived hardship and increasing physical output [43]. According to the ‘’main effect model’’ in social support theory, PSS, as a protective factor, can promote individuals to have a positive self-evaluation, keep brain energy metabolites and amino acid levels stable, reduce the damage of SF in older individuals, keep optimistic attitude, increase the elderly’s belief in active participation in activities, and reduce the occurrence of frailty [44].

Psychological resilience and frailty are inversely related. Specifically, the greater the level of PR, the lower the degree of frailty in elderly patients with HF. In a hospital environment with a heavy disease burden, where elderly patients with HF are the mainstay, both are related to dependency, cognitive level, and ageing [23]. On the one hand, ageing can damage the function of major organ systems and lead to a decline in adaptive capacity. On the other hand, dependency is usually on account of a decrease in functional reserves (i.e., ageing), leading to a decline in coping with stress. The secretion of pro-inflammatory factors and hypothalamic-endocrine axis disorders often occur in older patients with HF. The severity of the disease can easily lead to psychological issues such as anxiety, depression, and fear, reducing psychological resilience [45, 46]. Lenti points out a significant correlation between PR, its changes, future physical frailty, and the progression of physical frailty [22]. It highlights the non-linear association between these variables and emphasizes that different effects are observed in older people with different baseline frailty levels. Early identification of low PR and frailty is essential for designing and implementing targeted interventions that improve health outcomes in affected populations. Physical activity and nutritional strategies have been shown to enhance both physical and mental functioning, as well as reduce the risk of frailty. Corrective measures may have therapeutic advantages as resilience and frailty can be improved. In addition, psychological therapies such as mindfulness therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy can also be provided to patients to enhance the PR and promote mental conditions [47, 48].

PSS and psychological resilience mediate the link between SF and frailty, while the three dimensions of PSS – family SS, friend SS and other SS – play a multiple mediating role between SF and frailty. In elderly HF patients, social frailty—marked by reduced social support networks over time—leads to a higher level of frailty and is independently linked to greater risk of disability in the functional activities [49], consistent with the finding of our study. Adequate social support depends on the balance between the needs of elderly HF patients and what they receive from the social support system. Psychological distress ensues when there is an imbalance between demands and what is accepted. Strong social relationships enable older adults to participate more actively in society, broaden their social circles, and enhance their psychological resilience. A Chinese study of nursing homes found that PR is an indispensable positive element in ameliorating the adverse emotions of older people, helping to improve their level of PSS and social participation and reduce the incidence of frailty [50]. Chronic social stress can erode the vital tight junction protein claudin-5 in the blood-brain barrier. Meanwhile, greater PR acts as a shield, helping to preserve the delicate architecture of white matter and guard against negative health effects. Elderly patients may experience a decline in psychological resilience in the face of heart failure symptoms. Men show better psychological resilience than women when faced with heart failure. Female patients may be more sensitive despite receiving more social support, while men have stronger self-esteem and personal endurance [51]. In Chinese culture, the tradition of Confucian filial piety shapes how families care for one another, leading older people to look to their children first and foremost for support [52]. This also explains the mediating role of family SS between SF and frailty in this study. In addition, emotional support and instrumental support are also significant social and health needs of older HF patients. In many Chinese families, older persons often look to their sons to be the pillars of financial support, while daughters are seen as the heart of daily care and emotional comfort [53].

Limitations

This study has the following limitations: First, because this is cross-sectional, it is impossible to determine causality. In the future, longitudinal studies are needed to verify causality. Second, the variables in this study, such as ‘social frailty’, ‘perceived social support’, ‘psychological resilience’ and ‘frailty’, were subjectively reported by the participants, which may have information bias. However, all measurement tools are reliable and valid. Third, we only verified psychosocial variables as mediating variables. Demographic and disease-related data can also be explored as mediating variables in the future. Fourth, all samples were from China, limiting the generality to different cultural backgrounds and social and medical systems. Finally, considering that some potential psychological variables (such as anxiety and depression) may also have a certain impact on frailty, it will be necessary in future studies to expand the scope of psychological variables to further increase the rigor of the research.

Implications for practice

Our findings are of practical significance. They suggest that a multidimensional and personalized approach should be adopted to reduce frailty and its negative impact on elderly inpatients with HF. When providing care or designing interventions for elderly HF patients with frailty, psycho-social factors such as social frailty, perceived social support (family, friends, and others) and psychological resilience should be considered, not only to prevent mortality but also to reduce adverse health outcomes. During the 14th Five-Year Plan, China is poised to see its elderly population soar past 300 million, marking a profound demographic shift. The elderly have a long average survival period with illness, and many of them suffer from heart failure. Therefore, the construction and urban and rural coverage of a comprehensive and integrated ‘hospital-community-home’ health service platform is urgently needed. Finally, medical staff and family members should provide ‘dual-heart’ care for heart failure patients, reduce their level of social frailty, give full play to the health-promoting function of the family, and decrease the level of frailty in elderly patients with HF.

Conclusions

The number of hospitalizations, NYHA class, and female sex are indicators of the frailty level of older HF patients. Social frailty, perceived social support (family, friends, and others), psychological resilience, and frailty are interrelated. Perceived social support and psychological resilience are multiply chain-mediated between social frailty and frailty; family SS, friends SS, and other SS are multiple-mediated between social frailty and frailty. It is recommended that healthcare professionals pay careful attention to the physical, mental, and social health of elderly HF inpatients.
