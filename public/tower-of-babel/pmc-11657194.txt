CD69+CD103+CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells possess stronger anti-tumor activity and predict better prognosis in colorectal cancer
Authors: Zi-Xin Wu, Tian-Tian Da, Chuan Huang, Xiao-Qing Wang, Liang Li, Zhi-Bin Zhao, Ting-Ting Yin, Hai-Qing Ma, Zhe-Xiong Lian, Jie Long, Fei Wang, Jie Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent cancers worldwide. Despite advancements in therapeutic methodologies, it still causes a high rate of patient mortality. CD8+ tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells are strategically positioned to mediate effective anti-tumor responses. However, the characteristic surface molecules and functions of CD8+ TRM cells exhibit significant heterogeneity.MethodsThe roles and anti-tumor biological functions of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC were determined by clinical CRC samples, bioinformatics analysis, and in vitro experiments including co-culture experiments and transwell migration assays. The signaling pathways that synergistically regulate the differentiation of CD8+ TRM cells were identified by in vitro CD8+ T cell activation and inhibition assays, and the functioning transcription factors were predicted using the UCSC and JASPAR databases.ResultsWe found that different CD8+ TRM subsets existed in CRC tumor tissues, which were identified as CD69−CD103−CD8+ TRM, CD69+CD103−CD8+ TRM (SP CD8+ TRM), and CD69+CD103+CD8+ TRM (DP CD8+ TRM) subsets. Compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, increased infiltration of DP CD8+ TRM cells predicted better prognosis and played a protective role mainly in tumor invasion and lymph node metastasis of CRC. DP CD8+ TRM cells expressed higher levels of effector molecules and exerted stronger anti-tumor effects in a FAS/FASL pathway-dependent manner. Additionally, DP CD8+ TRM cells secreted higher levels of CXCL13 and recruited B cells into tumor tissues through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis to form tertiary lymphoid structures, participating in anti-tumor immune responses. Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways synergistically regulate the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells.ConclusionsWe clarified the roles and mechanisms of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC and identified that DP CD8+ TRM cells exert stronger anti-tumor effects and predict better prognosis, which provides ideas for developing new clinically available therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01990-3.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Tissue-resident memory T cell, CD69, CD103, Anti-tumor effect, Prognosis

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract, ranking third and second in terms of global incidence and mortality among all malignant tumors, respectively [1]. Approximately 30% of patients with CRC present with synchronous metastatic diseases, and the five-year survival rate of patients with advanced CRC is only about 50–60% [2, 3]. Currently, CRC treatment primarily involves surgery and chemotherapy. In recent years, immunotherapy has brought hope to the treatment of many tumors, but unfortunately, immune checkpoint drugs are only effective for some patients with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H), and their effect is still very limited [4]. Therefore, it is urgent for CRC to find more effective therapeutic targets and develop new therapeutic approaches.

Tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells are primarily found in peripheral lymphoid organs and non-lymphoid tissues, such as the lung, skin, and gastrointestinal tract, and do not participate in the peripheral circulation [5–7]. Studies have shown that dendritic cells (DCs) present antigens to naive CD8+ T cells in the draining lymph nodes. In response to a variety of cytokines, naive CD8+ T cells progressively differentiate into CD8+ TRM cells and reside in tumor tissues [8]. CD8+ TRM cells can participate in anti-tumor immune responses by secreting effector cytokines to exert cytotoxicity and by killing target cells through direct contact [9]. Infiltration of CD8+ TRM cells in various tumors is associated with better clinical prognosis, and CD8+ TRM cells have emerged as predictors of survival in patients with multiple epithelial tumors, including breast cancer, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, and melanoma [10–13]. However, the role and mechanism of CD8+ TRM cells in CRC remain unclear.

CD69 and CD103 are two major markers of TRM cells. High expression of CD69 promotes the internalization of sphingosine 1-phosphate receptors (S1PRs) into cells and suppresses their reactivity to S1P, thereby attenuating the migration of cells outside the tissue and allowing them to reside in the tissue [14, 15]. CD103, also known as integrin αE (ITGAE), is a transmembrane heterodimer complex involved in cell to cell or cell to matrix interactions [16]. CD103 mediates the tight adhesion of TRM cells to epithelial cells by binding to E-cadherin expressed on epithelial cells, allowing TRM cells to reside in the tissues. However, not all TRM cells in the organs express CD103, such as those in the liver [17–19]. The characteristic surface molecules of TRM cells in different tissues exhibit significant heterogeneity, which is closely related to the type of tissue in which they reside. Moreover, TRM cells in different tissues, as well as TRM subsets with different expression patterns of surface molecules in the same tissue, may have different functions. Based on the expression of CD69 and CD103, we found that different CD8+ TRM subsets did exist in CRC tumor tissues, which were identified as CD69−CD103−CD8+ TRM (double negative CD8+ TRM, DN CD8+ TRM), CD69+CD103−CD8+ TRM (CD69-single positive CD8+ TRM, SP CD8+ TRM), and CD69+CD103+CD8+ TRM (double positive CD8+ TRM, DP CD8+ TRM) subsets. Therefore, further exploration of the roles and mechanisms of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC is beneficial for identifying novel therapeutic targets and advancing CRC treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Guangzhou First People’s Hospital and Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (No. K-2019-070-01 and KY2023-453-02) and conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. 12 peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy donors, and 109 freshly resected colorectal tumors and 64 formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissues were obtained from patients with CRC who underwent surgery. All the patients with CRC didn’t receive preoperative chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. The demographics and clinicopathological characteristics of all the patients with CRC are shown in Table S1.

Tissue digestion and cell isolation

Freshly resected CRC tumor tissues were rinsed with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and cut into small pieces, and transferred to 15 mL of digestion medium containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL, C5138, Sigma-Aldrich) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F12 1:1 (1×) (C11330500BT, Gibco). Tissues were digested for 40 min at 37 ℃ under agitation at 150 rpm. Subsequently, the cell suspensions were filtered using a 150-µm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 450 × g for 5 min. Mononuclear cells were separated by centrifugation with 8 mL of 40% Percoll (17089109, Cytiva). Following the centrifugation at 800 g for 20 min at room temperature (RT), the supernatants were discarded and the cell pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (C3702-500 ml, Beyotime) at 4 °C for 10 min for depletion of red blood cells. After washing with 1×PBS, the cell pellets were suspended in 1×PBS containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA, 4240GR500, BioFroxx) and filtered using a 74-µm nylon mesh. Cells were counted after treatment with trypan blue.

Flow cytometry and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis

Single-cell suspensions were incubated with the human Fc receptor blocking reagent and stained for 20 min at 4 °C using a cocktail of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. The information of antibodies used was listed in Table S2. Following surface marker staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (554714, BD Biosciences) and Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523-00, eBioscience) to detect cytokines and transcription factors, respectively. Data were obtained using the BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and Cytek Aurora™ spectrum flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (V10, BD Biosciences, RRID: SCR_008520). T-SNE analysis of multiparameter flow cytometry data of CD45+DAPI− cells was performed using the t-SNE (RRID: SCR_024305) plug-in in the FlowJo software.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining

A PANO 7-plex IHC kit (TSA-RM) (0004100100, Panovue) was used for mIHC staining. FFPE tumor tissue slides from patients with CRC were dewaxed and rehydrated. After antigen retrieval, slides were blocked with goat serum at RT for 20 min. The primary antibody, CD69 (1:500, ab233396, Abcam) was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Slides were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (0004100100, Panovue) at RT for 10 min. Finally, tyramide signal amplification (TSA) dye690 (1:100) was applied for 10 min after washed. The second antibody, CD8α (1:400, 70306 S, CST) was incubated at RT for 2 h, and TSA dye520 (1:100) was applied. The third antibody, CD103 (1:200, ab224202, Abcam) was incubated overnight at 4 °C, and TSA dye620 (1:100) was applied. The fourth antibody, CD4 (1:500, ab133616, Abcam) was incubated at RT for 2 h, and TSA dye570 (1:100) was applied. The last antibody, CK19 (1:5000, ab52625, Abcam) was incubated at RT for 1 h, and TSA dye480 (1:100) was applied. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime) at RT for 5 min. In another mIHC staining protocol, the primary antibody, CD69 (1:500, ab233396, Abcam) was incubated overnight at 4 °C, and TSA dye690 (1:100) was applied. The second antibody, CD8α (1:400, 70306 S, CST) was incubated at RT for 2 h, and TSA dye570 (1:100) was applied. The third antibody, CD103 (1:200, ab224202, Abcam) was incubated overnight at 4 °C, and TSA dye620 (1:100) was applied. The last antibody, CD20 (1:2000, ab78237, Abcam) was incubated at RT for 1 h, and TSA dye520 (1:100) was applied. Then, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1006) at RT for 5 min. Slides were imaged and scanned using a Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), and multispectral images were acquired using Phenochart software, (V1.0.12, Akoya Biosciences) to unmix and remove autofluorescence. And slides were imported into HALO software (V3.2, Indica Labs) for subsequent analysis, including annotation, training, and classification of multispectral slides.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset analysis

Gene expression and clinicopathological data of 526 cases of patients with CRC were downloaded from TCGA (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas). We analyzed the survival of patients with CRC according to the expression levels of CD69 and ITGAE. The R package ‘survminer’ was used to determine the ideal point to divide groups. The surv_cutpoint() function, which uses the maximally selected rank statistics from the ‘maxstat’ R package, was used to split each of the genes into high- and low-expression groups. The survival and ggsurvplot R packages were used to create Kaplan-Meier plots for each of the selected groups including p-values determined by log-rank test.

Analysis of GSE108989 dataset

GSE108989 dataset was obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, RRID: SCR_005012) database. The data processing methods were described in the original article [20]. On acquiring the dataset, we excluded cells in which the number of unique genes was more than 5000 or less than 200. Only genes expressed in 10 or more cells were used for further analysis. We also excluded cells with mitochondrial gene percentages exceeding 10%. Eventually, we obtained a data matrix of 22,833 genes and 11,138 cells. Dimensionality reduction analysis was performed using the Seurat R package (version 3.1.1, RRID: SCR_016341). The matrix was normalized using the method LogNormalize in Seurat package. We identified 2000 highly variable genes using a ‘vst’ method embedded in the FindVariableFeatures function. We then performed principal component analysis (PCA) using the variable genes and determined significant principal components (PCs) based on the ElbowPlot function from the Seurat package. The top 20 PCs were selected for t-SNE analysis. To cluster cells, we used the FindClusters function and set the resolution parameter at 0.4. The CD8+ T cell clusters were then separated for further analysis. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed to identify pathways that were up- and down-regulated between the different groups using the R package clusterProfiler (RRID: SCR_016884).

Cell sorting

Single-cell suspensions of CRC tumor tissues were stained with antibodies against CD45, CD8α, CD69, CD103, CD19, CD326 (EpCAM), and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD, B226396, BioLegend) for fluorescence-activated cell sorting using BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences). The information of antibodies used was listed in Table S2. SP CD8+ TRM cells were sorted as 7-AAD−CD45+CD8+CD69+CD103−, and DP CD8+ TRM cells were sorted as 7-AAD−CD45+CD8+CD69+CD103+. B cells were sorted as 7-AAD−CD45+CD19+, and tumor cells were sorted as 7-AAD−CD45−CD326+.

The establishment of CD8+ TRM cell-tumor cell co-culture system

Cells were sorted and cultured in complete RPMI-1640, supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (C11875-500BT, Gbico), 1% sodium pyruvate (11360-070, Gbico), 1% HEPES free acid (15630-080, Gbico), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco), 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol (21985023, Gbico), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FSD500, ExCell Bio). Different CD8+ TRM subsets sorted from CRC tumor tissues were cultured either alone or with tumor cells. In some conditions, CD8+ TRM cells were pre-incubated with 10 µg/mL of anti-FasL (CD178) blocking antibody (306402, BioLegend) for 1 h prior to the addition of tumor cells. Following the preparation described above, CD8+ TRM cells were directly added to tumor cells at a 5:1 ratio to establish the CD8+ TRM cell-tumor cell co-culture system. After co-culturing for 4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2, cells were collected and labeled with ANNEXIN V Alexa Fluor 647 (640912, BioLegend), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were analyzed using flow cytometry. Experiments were performed at least three times with different donors.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell migration assay was performed to investigate whether B cells from patients with CRC were recruited by CD8+ TRM cells and to clarify the underlying mechanism. Twenty-four-well transwell chambers (3422, Corning) with 8-µm-pore filters were used. Firstly, 2 × 104 different CD8+ TRM subsets in 600 µL of the culture medium were added to the bottom chamber. To disrupt CXCL13/CXCR5 interactions during chemotaxis, neutralizing antibody against CXCL13 (2 µg/mL, MAB801, R&D Systems) was added to the culture medium. Then, a total of 1 × 105 B cells in 100 µL of the culture medium were added to the upper chamber. After 3 h of migration, cells in the lower chambers were collected, counted, and evaluated by flow cytometry. Experiments were performed at least three times with different donors.

In vitro CD8+ T cell activation and inhibition assay

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll (Lymphoprep, AS1114546, Axis-Shield) density gradient centrifugation method [21], and CD8+ T cells were sorted using human CD8 microbeads (130-045-201, Miltenyi). Peripheral blood CD8+ T cells were cultured in 96 well flat bottom plate with 1 × 105 cells per well in 200 µL complete RPMI-1640, supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (C11875-500BT, Gbico), 1% sodium pyruvate (11360-070, Gbico), 1% HEPES free acid (15630-080, Gbico), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco), 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol (21985023, Gbico), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FSD500, ExCell Bio). CD8+ T cells were stimulated with anti-human CD3 antibody (0.5 µg/mL, 317348, BioLegend), anti-human CD28 antibody (5 µg/mL, 302944, BioLegend), and with or without recombinant human TGF-β1 (100 ng/mL, 100–21 C, PeproTech), RBPJ inhibitor1 (RIN1, 10 µM, S3376, Selleck), and recombinant human Delta-like ligands 4 (DLL4, 4 µg/mL, 10185-D4-050, R&D). All conditions were plated in triplicate. Plates were incubated for 96 h in the presence of IL-2 (20 IU/mL, 200-02-250, Proteintech) or as other-wise indicated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 incubator with humidified air. Then, cells were collected and labeled with a viability dye, followed by anti-CD45, anti-CD8α, anti-CD69, and anti-CD103. Cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Gene expression correlation analysis

The correlation analysis module of Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2, RRID: SCR_018294) database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) was used to explore the gene expression correlation between RBPJ and SMAD3 in CRC tumor samples from TCGA dataset. The p value and the correlation coefficient (R) were generated. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to calculate the relationships between different genes.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

The PPI network of RBPJ and SMAD3 was constructed using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING V12.0, RRID: SCR_005223) database (http://string-db.org). The minimum required interaction score was set at high confidence (0.700).

UCSC and JASPAR bioinformatics analyze

Online promoter analysis software UCSC (http://genome.ucsc.edu/, RRID: SCR_005780) and transcription factor database JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/, RRID: SCR_003030) were used to predict the transcription factors that may interact with the promoter region of ITGAE gene. And transcription factor database JASPAR was used to predict the binding sites of transcription factors RBPJ and SMAD3 to the promoter region of ITGAE gene, with a relative profile score threshold of 80%.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between unpaired groups was determined by the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance between paired groups was determined using non-parametric Wilcoxon’s matched-pairs tests and Friedman test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to calculate the relationships between different groups. All analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 8, GraphPad Software, RRID: SCR_002798), SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc., RRID: SCR_002865), and the R software (version 3.5.3). All p values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Increased infiltration of DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC predicts better prognosis

To explore the prognostic value of CD69 and CD103, which are two major markers of TRM cells, we divided patients with CRC from TCGA cohort into high- and low-expression groups and found that the gene expressions of CD69 and ITGAE can jointly predict the prognosis of patients with CRC. Patients with CRC with high expressions of both CD69 and ITGAE in tumor tissues had significantly better disease-free survival rate and overall survival rate (Fig. 1A-C). To identify cells that highly express both CD69 and CD103, t-SNE analysis was performed on CD45+DAPI− cells from fresh CRC tumor tissues. We categorized these cells into subclusters and identified each subcluster using several specific surface markers, including CD56, CD3, CD19, CD8, CD4, CD45RO, CD45RA, CD25, and CD127. We identified 8 immune cell clusters among CD45+DAPI− cells, including NK cells (CD3−CD56+), B cells (CD19+), regulatory T (Treg) cells (CD4+CD25+CD127−), memory CD4+ T cells (CD4+CD45RO+), naive CD4+ T cells (CD4+CD45RA+), memory CD8+ T cells (CD8+CD45RO+), naive CD8+ T cells (CD8+CD45RA+), and other cells (Fig. 1D-E). We observed that the main subcluster with high expressions of both CD69 and CD103 was memory CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1D-E). Moreover, we analyzed the expression of CD69 and CD103 in fresh CRC tumor tissues using flow cytometry (Figure S1). The results showed that CD69+CD103+ cells were mainly CD8+ T cells, especially memory CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings suggest that increased infiltration of DP CD8+ TRM cells in tumors of patients with CRC indicates a better prognosis.

DP CD8+ TRM cells play a protective role in depth of invasion and lymph node metastasis of CRC

We further analyzed different CD8+ TRM subsets infiltrated in CRC tumor tissues by flow cytometry (Figure S2A). Based on the expression of CD69 and CD103, we discovered that different CD8+ TRM subsets existed within the CRC tumor tissues, which were identified as DN CD8+ TRM, SP CD8+ TRM, and DP CD8+ TRM subsets (Fig. 2A). The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) staging system was used in our study, and we investigated the association between the infiltration of different CD8+ TRM subsets and the tumor stage in patients with CRC. We observed that the percentage of DP CD8+ TRM cells was significantly higher in patients with CRC showing T1-T3 stage disease than those with T4 stage disease (Fig. 2B-D). Moreover, our results showed that the percentage of DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC tumor tissues was higher in patients without lymph node metastasis (N0 stage CRC) than in those with lymph node metastasis (N2 stage CRC) (Fig. 2E-J). Additionally, the characteristic of CD8+ TRM subsets in the FFPE tumor tissue slides from patients with CRC were stained by mIHC, including DAPI (blue), CD8 (green), CD69 (red), CD103 (orange), and CK19 (cyan) (Fig. 2K). The findings of flow cytometry were further validated by mIHC in CRC tumor tissues (Fig. 2L). These results suggest that DP CD8+ TRM cells play a protective role against tumor invasion in situ and lymph node metastasis.

DP CD8+ TRM cells express high levels of immune checkpoint and effector molecules

To further investigate the molecular characteristics of DP CD8+ TRM cells, differential gene expression was determined using GSE108989 dataset obtained from GEO database. Among CD8+ TRM cells, 3 clusters (DN CD8+ TRM cells, SP CD8+ TRM cells, and DP CD8+ TRM cells) were identified, and the top 5 up-regulated genes for each cluster were exhibited in heatmap (Fig. 3A). We then performed an in-depth bioinformatic analysis to determine the functions of different CD8+ TRM clusters within biological systems, followed by GO analysis. The most enriched pathways of DP CD8+ TRM cells were mainly related to regulation of T cell activation, cell-cell adhesion, leukocyte proliferation, cell chemotaxis, mononuclear cell differentiation, response to external stimulus, leukocyte mediated cytotoxicity, cell killing, and B cell activation (Fig. 3B). Additionally, compared with SP CD8+ TRM subset, DP CD8+ TRM subset expressed higher levels of immune checkpoint molecules, including HAVCR2, PDCD1, TIGIT, and CTLA4, and effector molecules, including PRF1, GZMB, GZMA, and FASLG (Fig. 3C-D). We further verified PD-1, TIGIT, TIM-3, granzyme A, perforin, and Fas ligand (FasL) by flow cytometry and obtained consistent results (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these findings indicate that compared with SP CD8+ TRM subset, DP CD8+ TRM subset may preserve stronger anti-tumor function through perforin-granzyme-dependent and FAS-mediated mechanisms.

DP CD8+ TRM cells are closer to tumor cells and exert stronger anti-tumor effects via FAS/FASL pathway

By analyzing the spatial distance between different CD8+ TRM cells and tumor cells by mIHC of CRC tumor tissues, we found that DP CD8+ TRM cells were closer to tumor cells than SP CD8+ TRM cells (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the spatial distance between DP CD8+ TRM cells and tumor cells was shorter in patients without lymph node metastasis (N0 stage CRC) than in those with lymph node metastasis (N1 and N2 stage CRC) (Fig. 4B-J). These results suggest that DP CD8+ TRM cells may exert anti-tumor effects by killing tumor cells via direct contact and the FAS-mediated mechanism. We then established a CD8+ TRM cell-tumor cell co-culture system (Fig. 4K). The proportion of ANNEXIN V+ tumor cells was higher when tumor cells were co-cultured with DP CD8+ TRM cells and decreased with the addition of anti-FASL antibody (Fig. 4L-N). In other words, compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, the CD8+ TRM cell-tumor cell co-culture system verified the stronger anti-tumor effects of DP CD8+ TRM cells in a FAS/FASL pathway-dependent manner.

The proportion of DP CD8+ TRM cells are positively correlated with the proportion of type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1s)

The direct cytotoxic effect of CD8+ T cells on tumor cells relies on their ability to recognize tumor-associated antigens. Additionally, cross-presentation by specialized antigen presenting cells, especially DCs, is required for the ability of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [22]. We further analyzed the correlation between infiltrating DCs and CD8+ TRM cells in CRC tumor tissues (Figure S2B). The proportion of DP CD8+ TRM cells was found to be positively correlated with the proportion of cDC1s, suggesting that DP CD8+ TRM cells are tumor-specific killers (Fig. 5A). The expression levels of Ki-67 in DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC tumor tissues were higher than those in other CD8+ TRM cells, whereas there was no difference in the expression levels of ANNEXIN V (Fig. 5B-C). These findings suggest that cell proliferation capacity of DP CD8+ TRM cells is higher than that of other CD8+ TRM cells, with no difference in apoptosis levels, further verifying that DP CD8+ TRM cells are tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells. Taken together, naive CD8+ T cells differentiate into DP CD8+ TRM cells upon encountering tumor antigens presented by cDC1s, exhibiting stronger cytotoxicity and playing a protective role against tumor invasion in situ and lymph node metastasis.

DP CD8+ TRM cells recruit B cells to tumor tissues and form tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) by secreting CXCL13

We further analyzed the transcriptional characteristics of SP CD8+ TRM and DP CD8+ TRM cells. Compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, DP CD8+ TRM cells upregulated the expression of CXCL13 (Fig. 6A). It has been reported that CXCL13 plays a dominant role in B cell recruitment and TLS formation through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis, thereby activating immune responses against tumors [23]. Flow cytometry and mIHC were used to detect the relationship between different CD8+ TRM cells, B cells and TLS in CRC tumor tissues (Fig. 6B, Figure S2A and S3). The results showed that the proportion of DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC tumor tissues was positively correlated with the proportion and count of B cells, and the count and area of TLS (Fig. 6B-C and Figure S3). Moreover, compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, transwell migration assay verified that DP CD8+ TRM cells were more capable of recruiting B cells, and the chemotactic effect on B cells could be attenuated by a CXCL13 neutralizing antibody (Fig. 6D-E). Together, these findings suggest that DP CD8+ TRM cells secrete CXCL13 and recruit B cells into tumor tissues through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis to form TLS and participate in anti-tumor immune response.

Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways synergistically regulate the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells

Studies have demonstrated that TGF-β is absolutely required for CD103 upregulation and CD103+CD8+ TRM cell differentiation [24]. And we observed that DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC tumor tissues upregulated the expression of RBPJ, a crucial Notch signaling pathway transcription factor, and Notch receptor (Notch2), compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells (Figs. 6A and 7A-B), suggesting that the Notch signaling pathway plays an important regulatory role in DP CD8+ TRM cells. However, it is unclear whether Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways have synergistic effects on DP CD8+ TRM cell differentiation. Peripheral blood CD8+ T cells were isolated and incubated in vitro in media containing TGF-β1, RIN1 (RBPJ inhibitor) and DLL4 (Notch ligand) (Fig. 7C). We identified that the expression of CD103 was significantly increased by the addition of DLL4 and TGF-β1 and decreased by the addition of RIN1 (Fig. 7D-E), suggesting that Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways synergistically regulate the expression of CD103 and the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells. Furthermore, with the aid of STRING database, we found a possible interaction between the transcription factors RBPJ and SMAD3, downstream of the TGF-β signaling pathway (Figure S4A). Positive correlation between RBPJ and SMAD3 gene expression was found in CRC tumor tissue samples from TCGA dataset using the GEPIA2 database (Figure S4B). Moreover, according to the predictions of the UCSC and JASPAR databases, the transcription factors RBPJ and SMAD3 can bind to the ITGAE promoter region, and the binding sites of RBPJ and SMAD3 to the ITGAE promoter were predicted (Figure S4C-E and Table S3). These results suggest that Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways may be integrated via protein-protein interactions between Smad3 and RBPJ to synergistically regulate the expression of CD103 and the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells. Discussion

CD8+ T cells are key players in anti-tumor immune responses and considered as the major targets of immune checkpoint blockade. The ability of CD8+ T cells to mediate tumor eradication depends on their capacity to infiltrate and reside within solid tumors. CD8+ TRM cells are conveniently positioned to mediate anti-tumor responses. However, CD8+ TRM cells are not a uniform subset but a heterogeneous population composed of cells with diverse gene expression profiles. The characteristic surface molecules of TRM cells in different tissues have great heterogeneity, and CD8+ TRM cells can be identified as DN CD8+ TRM, SP CD8+ TRM, and DP CD8+ TRM cells based on the expression of CD69 and CD103. Our data showed that different CD8+ TRM subsets did exist in all CRC tumor tissues, and the proportion varied from person to person. Studies have shown that TRM cells in different tissues, as well as TRM subsets with different expression patterns of surface molecules in the same tissue, may have different functions. Therefore, further exploration of the role and mechanism of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC is beneficial for developing new therapeutic targets and for providing ideas for the treatment of CRC.

In this study, we found that compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, increased infiltration of DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC predicted better prognosis, and DP CD8+ TRM cells played a protective role in CRC, mainly in tumor invasion in situ and lymph node metastasis. Notably, not only the number of immune cells, but also their location and spatial interaction with tumor cells contribute independently to prognostic information on clinical outcomes [25, 26]. The spatial distance between DP CD8+ TRM cells and tumor cells was significantly shorter in patients without lymph node metastasis than in those with lymph node metastasis, although this did not contribute to the extent of local progression. This discrepancy may be attributed to the varying degrees of disease progression. During the development of CRC, local progression typically occurs earlier, while lymph node metastasis generally develops at a later stage. Therefore, the differences in the distance between DP CD8+ TRM cells and tumor cells across different T stages may not be as pronounced as those observed between different N stages. DP CD8+ TRM cells might play a more prominent role in preventing lymph node metastasis.

Furthermore, studies have shown that only two main mechanisms accounted for T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells, namely, the FAS-mediated mechanism and the perforin-granzyme-dependent granule exocytosis mechanism [27]. Yenyuwadee et al. [28] and Byrne et al. [9] indicated that despite expressing high levels of immune checkpoint markers, CD8+ TRM cells maintain cytolytic capacity and poly functionality, express high levels of cytotoxic molecules, and respond to PD-1-blocking immunotherapy. And we found that DP CD8+ TRM cells expressed not only higher levels of immune checkpoint molecules, but also higher levels of effector molecules and FASL, and were closer to tumor cells than SP CD8+ TRM cells, suggesting that DP CD8+ TRM cells may exert stronger anti-tumor effects by killing tumor cells via direct contact and the FAS-mediated mechanism. Almost all known cancer cells express FAS [29], and the interaction between FAS and its ligand (FasL) leads to FAS-positive cell apoptosis [30]. Therefore, we constructed a CD8+ TRM-tumor cell co-culture system, and further validated the stronger anti-tumor ability of DP CD8+ TRM cells, which was dependent on the FAS/FASL pathway. In tumor microenvironment, the key first step of the anti-tumor immune responses of CD8+ T cells is MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation and recognition, and impeding the interaction between MHC-I and TCR molecules attenuates the anti-cancer effect [31]. In fact, in some conditions of our CD8+ TRM-tumor cell co-culture system, tumor cells were pre-incubated with 50 µg/mL of anti-MHC-class I blocking antibody (311428, BioLegend) for 1 h prior to adding DP CD8+ TRM cells as previously described [32]. However, there was no significant change in the killing effect of DP CD8+ TRM cells on tumor cells (data not shown), which may be due to tumor cells down-regulated the expression of MHC-I in order to achieve immune escape [33].

DCs are important antigen-presenting cells for CD8+ TRM cell differentiation [34], among which cDC1s are considered to excel in the activation of cytotoxic lymphocytes including CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes [35]. And we found that the presence of cDC1s may be related to the production of DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC, that is, naive CD8+ T cells may differentiate into DP CD8+ TRM cells after receiving the tumor antigen presented by cDC1s. Furthermore, the stronger cell proliferation capacity and cytotoxicity of DP CD8+ TRM cells indicated that they were tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells. We then analyzed the transcriptional characteristics of different CD8+ TRM cells and found that DP CD8+ TRM cells upregulated the expression of CXCL13. It has been reported that CXCL13 has a dominant role in B cell recruitment and TLS formation through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis, thereby activating immune responses against tumors and predicting better prognosis [23, 36]. And CXCL13+CD8+ T cells were found to be positively correlated with TLS [37]. We verified that DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC were positively correlated with the infiltration of B cells and TLS, and DP CD8+ TRM cells were more capable of recruiting B cells in a CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis-dependent manner, indicating that DP CD8+ TRM cells secrete CXCL13 and recruit B cells into tumor tissues through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis to form TLS and participate in anti-tumor immune response.

The Notch signaling pathway plays an important role in T cell development and differentiation [31], and the Notch transcription factor RBPJ is a master regulator of the induction and maintenance of CD8+ TRM cells [10]. We noticed that DP CD8+ TRM cells upregulated the expression of RBPJ compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, suggesting that the Notch signaling pathway plays an important regulatory role in DP CD8+ TRM cells. Furthermore, it has been shown that TGF-β is absolutely required for CD103 upregulation and CD103+CD8+ TRM cell differentiation [24]. To clarify whether Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways have synergistic effects on DP CD8+ TRM cells, we performed in vitro CD8+ T cell activation and inhibition assays, as well as the prediction of functioning transcription factors. We verified that Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways may be integrated via protein-protein interactions between Smad3 and RBPJ to synergistically regulate the expression of CD103 and the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells.

There were some limitations of our study. The specific mechanism by which Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways synergistically promote the differentiation of CD8+ TRM subset and how they synergistically regulate the function of CD8+ TRM subset remain to be further identified and explored. And indeed, the heterogeneity of CD8+ TRM subsets is manifested in many ways beyond the use of CD69 and CD103 to define CD8+ TRM subsets, and more distinct CD8+ TRM subsets in different therapeutic modalities or different tumors tissues deserve further investigation. This is currently an area of active study.

Taken together, this study clarified the roles and mechanisms of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC and identified DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC predict better prognosis and exert stronger anti-tumor effects through clinical samples, which is closer to the clinic and more conducive to the transformation of research results. In addition, we further elucidated the mechanisms that regulate the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells. These findings have important theoretical value for revealing the significance of different CD8+ TRM subsets in CRC, and provide ideas for developing clinically available new therapeutic targets and advancing CRC treatment strategies.

Conclusions

Compared with SP CD8+ TRM cells, DP CD8+ TRM cells in CRC predict better prognosis and exert stronger anti-tumor effects, which may play a role in three aspects: firstly, DP CD8+ TRM cells kill tumor cells by secreting effector molecules; secondly, after being sensitized by cDC1s, DP CD8+ TRM cells kill tumor cells in a FAS/FASL pathway-dependent manner; thirdly, DP CD8+ TRM cells secrete CXCL13 and recruit B cells into tumor tissues through the CXCL13/CXCR5 signaling axis to form TLS and participate in anti-tumor immune response. Lastly, Notch and TGF-β signaling pathways synergistically regulate the differentiation of DP CD8+ TRM cells.
