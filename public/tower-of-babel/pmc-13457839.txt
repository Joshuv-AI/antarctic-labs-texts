Factors associated with circulating levels of IL-17A in a high HIV-burden population
Authors: David Chisompola, Emmanuel Luwaya, Joseph M Chalwe, Joreen P Povia, Annet Kirabo, Sepiso K Masenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin-17A (IL-17A) is a key cytokine in inflammation and autoimmunity. However, its systemic correlates, particularly in a high HIV burden population, are not fully elucidated. This study aimed to identify the sociodemographic, clinical, inflammatory, metabolic, and renal factors independently associated with plasma IL-17A levels. This was a cross-sectional study of 226 adults recruited from Livingstone University Teaching Hospital, Zambia. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using standardized methods, and plasma biomarkers were measured using validated assays. Associations with IL-17A were assessed using multivariable linear regression models constructed using a theory-driven approach. A base clinical model was specified a priori, followed by models incorporating metabolic and inflammatory variables. A final parsimonious model was developed, and sensitivity analyses were conducted, separating by HIV status.The cohort was predominantly female (68.6%) and included a high proportion of people living with HIV (64.2%), most of whom were receiving integrase inhibitor-based therapy. In the base clinical model, HIV status was positively associated with IL-17A; however, this association was no longer significant after adjustment for inflammatory markers. In the final model, IL-1 (β: 1.32, 95% CI: 0.60–2.04, p < 0.001) and IL-6 (β: 16.02, 95% CI: 8.19–23.84, p < 0.001) were positively associated with IL-17A, while soluble ST2 (β: -23.86, 95% CI: -41.99 to -5.73, p = 0.010) was inversely associated. Plasma potassium showed a modest positive association (β: 5.83, 95% CI: 0.37–11.29, p = 0.036), but this was not retained in analyses restricted to people living with HIV and in HIV negative individuals. Clinical and metabolic variables were not consistently associated with IL-17A after adjustment. In this high HIV-burden population, IL-17A levels were primarily associated with inflammatory cytokines rather than HIV status or metabolic factors. The attenuation of the HIV association after adjustment suggests that IL-17A was more closely associated with broader inflammatory activity than with HIV status itself. The observed association with plasma potassium was not robust and should be interpreted as exploratory. These findings highlight the role of IL-17A within a coordinated inflammatory network and underscore the need for longitudinal studies to clarify causal pathways.Keywords: Interleukin-17A, HIV, Inflammation, Potassium, Metabolic syndrome, Cross-sectional studiesSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Interleukin-17A (IL-17A) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine produced primarily by T helper 17 (Th17) cells and is recognized as a mediator linking immune dysregulation to a broad spectrum of chronic diseases1. Its production is potently stimulated by upstream cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, which promote the differentiation and expansion of Th17 cells2. While the role of IL-17A in conditions like psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis is well-defined, its regulation in the context of chronic viral infections and associated metabolic disturbances is less understood3.

Zambia represents a high HIV-burden setting, with an estimated national adult HIV prevalence of 11.9%4, substantially exceeding the global average of 0.7%5. In the Southern Province, where this study was conducted, prevalence estimates range from 7.4–15%6, placing the catchment population among those with the highest HIV burden in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), Human Immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection persists as a state of chronic immune activation and CD4 + T-cell dysregulation7. A key feature of this dysregulation is the disruption of the Th17/regulatory T cell (Treg) balance, which compromises mucosal integrity and fuels systemic inflammation8. This persistent inflammatory state has been strongly associated with non-AIDS-defining comorbidities, including metabolic and renal diseases9. With the global shift towards integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTI)-based ART regimens, which have distinct immunomodulatory effects, there is a need to re-evaluate correlates of inflammation, such as IL-17A, in contemporary treated HIV infection10.

Epidemiological evidence supports that people living with HIV have a higher prevalence and faster progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) compared with uninfected individuals, driven by the interplay of chronic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and direct viral and treatment-related kidney injury9,11–13. IL-17A is increasingly recognized as being associated with renal pathology in this context14. Moreover, experimental studies demonstrate that IL-17A promotes renal fibrosis through activation of fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition15–17, while also amplifying local inflammation by recruiting neutrophils and macrophages to kidney tissue18. In HIV-associated nephropathy specifically, Th17 cells have also been identified within renal infiltrates, suggesting direct involvement of the IL-17 pathway in HIV-related kidney damage19.

This synergy creates a significant public health challenge, contributing to elevated rates of cardiovascular morbidity, progression to dialysis‑dependent kidney disease, and premature mortality among people living with HIV12,20. Understanding the factors associated with IL-17A levels in high HIV-burden populations may therefore identify novel targets for preserving renal function and reducing inflammation-related comorbidities. Beyond infectious disease, emerging evidence suggests IL-17A is intertwined with metabolic homeostasis. It has been linked to insulin resistance, atherosclerosis, and, intriguingly, kidney function, where it can promote fibrosis and inflammation15,16. However, most studies investigate IL-17A in isolation. A comprehensive analysis examining its simultaneous relationship with sociodemographic, clinical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors, particularly in a population with a high burden of chronic inflammation like people living with HIV, is lacking.

Therefore, this study primarily aimed to determine whether circulating IL-17A levels are independently associated with inflammatory and clinical factors in a high HIV-burden population characterized by treated HIV infection and persistent immune activation. We hypothesized that IL-17A levels would be most strongly associated with upstream inflammatory cytokines involved in Th17 biology. Metabolic, renal, and electrolyte variables were evaluated as secondary exploratory correlates to assess whether broader systemic factors were also linked to IL-17A levels and HIV status.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This was a cross-sectional study involving 226 adults recruited from the outpatient medical clinic at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital who were attending routine medical check-ups between 1 October 2023 and 1 June 2024.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the outpatient medical clinic of Livingstone University Teaching Hospital, a tertiary care referral center serving the Southern Province of Zambia. The clinic provides comprehensive outpatient services, including non-severe management of chronic conditions and routine medical check-ups, with an average monthly attendance of approximately 1200 adult patients.

According to recent regional health surveillance reports, the estimated adult HIV prevalence in the hospital’s catchment area is 11.9%4, which is consistent with national figures for urban settings in Zambia21. This setting provided an appropriate environment for recruiting a cohort with a high burden of HIV, as reflected in our study population.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older, provided written informed consent before enrolling on the study, living with (PWH) and people without HIV (PWTH) and both hypertensive and non-hypertensive individuals within each HIV category. Those with HIV were on stable antiretroviral therapy for at least 12 months and were virologically suppressed (viral load < 50 copies/mL). People without HIV had a negative HIV test. To minimize confounding effects on inflammatory markers, we excluded individuals with diabetes, pregnancy, severe renal or hepatic disease, active opportunistic infections, or other serious (debilitating) comorbidities.

Sample size and sampling technique

A convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit participants. This non-probability method was chosen to efficiently enroll as many participants as possible. A sample size calculation was performed for a multiple linear regression model. To detect a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90% for a model testing up to 12 predictors, a minimum sample size of n = 158 was required22. To account for potential data issues and to ensure robust subgroup analysis, we targeted a larger sample. The final recruited cohort of n = 226 participants therefore provided ample statistical power to reliably identify the independent correlates of IL-17A reported in this analysis.

Participant recruitment and study procedures

Potential participants were identified during routine outpatient medical visits at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital. Trained research staff approached individuals in the waiting area, provided brief study information, and assessed initial interest.

Screening and enrollment visit (visit 1)

Interested individuals underwent formal screening to confirm eligibility. After providing written informed consent, participants completed a structured interview collecting sociodemographic and clinical data. At this visit, each participant was given both verbal and written instructions regarding the required 10–12 h overnight fast prior to their subsequent blood collection appointment. A standardized instruction sheet (translated into Nyanja, Tonga, and English) was provided, specifying that only water was permitted after 8:00 PM on the evening before sampling.

Blood collection visit (visit 2)

Participants returned to the study clinic between 7:00 AM and 9:00 AM on a scheduled morning, typically within 3–7 days of the enrollment visit. Upon arrival, research staff verbally confirmed compliance with the overnight fast by asking: "Have you had anything to eat or drink (other than water) since 8:00 PM last night?" Participants who reported any food or caloric beverage intake were rescheduled for a subsequent visit. Only participants who confirmed adherence to the fast proceeded to blood collection.

This two-visit approach was implemented to separate the informed consent and interview process (which could be lengthy and inconvenient if performed fasting) from the early-morning blood collection, thereby optimizing participant comfort and compliance with fasting requirements.

Sociodemographic and clinical data

Sociodemographic and clinical data were systematically collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform. Trained research personnel gathered information on age, sex, marital status, employment status, and medical history (including HIV, hypertension, and cardiovascular conditions) through standardized structured interviews conducted in Nyanja, Tonga, or English, as appropriate for the participant. All responses were translated and recorded in English. Medical record reviews were also conducted on HIV status, history of tuberculosis (TB) and ART regimen details.

Biochemical measurements

Following an overnight fast, approximately 12 mL of venous blood samples was drawn from each participant via standard venipuncture into appropriate vacutainers including EDTA tubes where blood for hematological assays and plasma cytokine assays, heparin tubes for certain metabolic panels, and serum separator tubes for lipid and electrolyte analysis. For cytokines plasma was separated and stored at -80 °C until analysis. IL-17A, IL-1, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, d-dimer and IFN-γ were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) commercially available kits, following manufacturer protocols. The concentrations of IL-17A, IL-1, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, USA; Catalog No: E-OSEL-H0067), with a detection range of 7.81–4000 pg/mL. The assay demonstrated acceptable analytical performance, with intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) < 8% and inter-assay CVs < 10%, consistent with established reliability thresholds for cytokine biomarker measurement (typically CV < 10%). D-dimer levels were assessed using the iChroma-II system (Boditech Med Incorporated, Republic of Korea). Fasting glucose, lipid profile (triglycerides, LDL-cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, VLDL), and plasma potassium and sodium were measured using standard automated clinical chemistry analyzers. Plasma sodium and potassium were measured as part of a comprehensive metabolic panel, given emerging evidence of their role in immune modulation and their relevance to renal and cardiovascular health23.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from REDCap and cleaned prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using StatCrunch and GraphPad prism 10.3.0. Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

The distribution of plasma IL-17A was assessed using graphical methods and was found to be right-skewed; therefore, IL-17A was analyzed as a continuous variable in linear regression models. Because residual diagnostics from linear regression models indicated no major violations from model assumptions, untransformed IL-17A values were retained for analysis. In addition to conventional linear regression, robust regression analyses were performed to assess the influence of non-normality and potential outliers. Model assumptions, including linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity, were evaluated using residual plots and diagnostic tests.

Associations between IL-17A and candidate variables were first explored using simple linear regression. Multivariable linear regression models were then constructed using a theory-driven approach based on biological plausibility and potential confounding, rather than relying solely on statistical significance in bivariate analyses. Core clinical variables (age, sex, HIV status, BMI, hypertension, and eGFR) were retained a priori across models (3A-3D) because of their established relevance to systemic inflammation and HIV-related comorbidity. Additional metabolic, renal, hematologic, and inflammatory variables were introduced sequentially to evaluate specific domains of association with IL-17A. Variables were retained in the final parsimonious model if they demonstrated biologically plausible and stable associations across models or contributed meaningfully to interpretation of IL-17A-related inflammatory pathways. Variables showing unstable estimates, overlapping biological domains (collinearity), attenuation after adjustment, or limited contribution to model interpretability were not retained.

Both categorical and continuous variables were included in regression models24,25. Categorical variables were coded as binary indicator variables with prespecified reference categories, whereas continuous variables were analyzed on their original measurement scales. Regression coefficients for categorical variables represent differences in mean IL-17A relative to the reference category, while coefficients for continuous variables represent the expected change in IL-17A per unit increase in the variable.

A base clinical model (Model 3A) was specified a priori and included age, sex, smoking status, HIV status, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR).

To evaluate metabolic and renal correlates, a second model (Model 3B) extended the base model by including fasting glucose, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, plasma potassium, microalbuminuria, D-dimer, and lymphocyte count.

To assess inflammatory correlates, a third model (Model 3C) included the base clinical variables together with inflammatory biomarkers (IL-1, IL-5, IFN-γ, IL-6, TNF-α, and soluble ST2 [sST2]).

A final parsimonious model (Model 3D) was then constructed by retaining a priori confounders and variables from prior models that showed biological relevance and stable associations across models. Variables representing overlapping domains or showing unstable or inconsistent effects were not retained to improve interpretability.

Multicollinearity was assessed in each model using variance inflation factors (VIF) and correlation matrices, with VIF values < 5 considered acceptable.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings. Specifically, plasma potassium was included in the final model to evaluate its independent association with IL-17A (Table 6). This analysis was then repeated separately among people living with HIV and HIV-negative individuals to determine whether the observed associations varied by HIV serostatus (Table 7 and Table 8).

Results

Participants characteristics

A total of 271 individuals were screened for eligibility during the study period. Of these, 45 were excluded: 18 declined participation, 14 did not provide informed consent, 3 had an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 10 were excluded due to incomplete data collection. The final analytic sample comprised 226 participants.

The cohort was predominantly female (n = 155/226, 68.6%), with a median age of 48.5 (40.0–59.0, IQR) years, and (n = 145/226, 64.2%) participants were living with HIV (Table 1). Among participants living with HIV, ART regimen data were available for 126 individuals (86.9% of the 145 PWH). Of these, the vast majority (n = 120/126, 95.2%) were on an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based regimen, primarily tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir (TLD) or tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine/dolutegravir (TafED). A small proportion were on non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based regimens (n = 4/126, 3.2%) or protease inhibitor (PI)-based regimens (n = 2/126, 1.6%). Most participants had no prior history of tuberculosis (n = 158, 85.9%). Hypertension was the most frequently reported comorbidity, affecting 66 participants (n = 66/226, 29.2%). Plasma IL-17A concentrations had a median value of 22.9 pg/mL, with an interquartile range of 18.8–491.3 pg/mL (Table 1).

Relationship between Il-17A and covariates in simple linear regression

Simple linear regression analyses revealed statistically significant positive correlations between plasma IL-17A levels and a range of clinical, inflammatory, metabolic, and renal variables (Fig. 2A–L, Fig. 3A-L and Fig. 4A-F). Although patterns varied across parameters, most variables showed a weak or inconsistent relationships with IL-17A, indicating considerable inter-individual variability. Univariable linear regression analysis of factors associated with IL-17A levels

To evaluate the unadjusted associations between demographic, clinical, and inflammatory variables with IL-17A levels, a series of univariable linear regression analyses were performed (Table 2). HIV status showed a strong positive association with IL-17A, with HIV-positive individuals exhibiting significantly higher IL-17A concentrations (β: 685.2, 95% CI: 368.4–1002.0, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Among metabolic and biochemical parameters, plasma potassium (β: 10.7, 95% CI: 4.8–16.6, p < 0.001), fasting glucose (β: 134.3, 95% CI: 17.1–251.5, p = 0.025), triglycerides (β: 415.8, 95% CI: 97.1–734.6, p = 0.011), LDL cholesterol (β: 186.9, 95% CI: 12.4–361.4, p = 0.036), D-dimer (β: 0.004, 95% CI: 0.001–0.008, p = 0.026), and microalbuminuria (β: 414.5, 95% CI: 31.8–797.1, p = 0.034) were all significantly associated with higher IL-17A levels. Additionally, lymphocyte count showed a positive association (β: 1.2, 95% CI: 0.4–2.0, p = 0.005) (Table 2). Inflammatory cytokines and biomarkers revealed a consistent and strong associations with IL-17A. Significant positive relationships were observed for IL-1 (β: 0.02, p = 0.008), IL-5 (β: 0.5, p < 0.001), IFN-γ (β: 0.07, p = 0.004), IL-6 (β: 14.2, p < 0.001), TNF-α (β: 0.05, p < 0.001), and sST2 (β: 20.6, p < 0.001), indicating a coordinated pro-inflammatory profile. In contrast, no statistically significant associations were identified for age, sex, body mass index, hypertension status, smoking, or renal function (eGFR), suggesting that these factors were not independently related to IL-17A levels in unadjusted analyses.

Factors associated with IL-17A

Base clinical model

A final multivariable model was constructed incorporating clinically relevant covariates and key inflammatory markers (Table 6). In this model: IL-1 (β: 1.32, 95% CI: 0.60–2.04, p < 0.001), IL-6 (β: 16.02, 95% CI: 8.19–23.84, p < 0.001) and plasma potassium (β: 5.83, 95% CI: 0.37–11.29, p = 0.036) remained positively associated with IL-17A, while sST2 (β: -23.86, 95% CI: -41.99 to -5.73, p = 0.010) was negatively associated. HIV status, IFN-γ, D-dimer, and clinical variables were not independently associated after adjustment.

Sensitivity analysis (HIV only)

A sensitivity analysis restricted to participants living with HIV was conducted to assess the robustness of associations (Table 7). In this subgroup: IL-1 (β: 1.156, 95% CI: 0.063–2.249, p = 0.039) and IL-6 (β: 20.1, 95% CI: 7.5–32.6, p = 0.002) remained positively associated with IL-17A. Plasma potassium was not significantly associated (β: 5.6, p = 0.157). sST2 showed a negative but non-significant trend (Table 7).

Sensitivity analysis (HIV negative only)

A sensitivity analysis restricted to HIV negative individuals was conducted to assess the robustness of associations (Table 8). In this subgroup: IFN-γ (pg/mL) was positively associated with IL-17A (β = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.13–0.78; p = 0.007), indicating that higher IFN-γ levels corresponded to an increase in IL-17A (Table 8).

Discussion

In this study of a high HIV-burden population, we found that circulating IL-17A levels were primarily associated with inflammatory biomarkers rather than clinical or demographic factors. While HIV status was strongly associated with higher IL-17A levels in the base clinical model, this association was no longer observed after adjustment for inflammatory markers, suggesting that IL-17A variability was more closely associated with systemic inflammatory markers than with HIV status itself. In fully adjusted models, IL-1 and IL-6 emerged as consistent positive correlates, while sST2 showed an inverse association, highlighting a coordinated inflammatory network linked to IL-17A. These findings are consistent with established Th17 biology, in which IL-17A is closely linked to upstream cytokines such as IL-1 and IL-6, which play central roles in Th17 differentiation and immune activation26. The persistence of these associations in sensitivity analyses restricted to people living with HIV further supports the robustness of this inflammatory signature. In contrast, the association between plasma potassium and IL-17A, observed in the final model, was attenuated in HIV-restricted analyses, suggesting that this finding may be context-dependent and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, our results indicate that IL-17A reflects broader immune activation pathways rather than acting as an independent marker of HIV status, consistent with emerging evidence that IL-17A operates within a complex cytokine network in treated HIV populations27.

The positive association of IL-1 with IL-17A is especially credible in light of established Th17 biology. IL-1β has been associated with differentiation, expansion, and pathogenic activation of Th17 cells, and may amplify IL-17A-driven inflammation through feed-forward immune signaling28,29. Likewise, IL-6 is a canonical polarizing cytokine for Th17 responses and has repeatedly been linked to chronic inflammatory activity in HIV and other immune-mediated conditions9,30. The persistence of IL-1 and IL-6 in the HIV-restricted sensitivity analysis strengthens the view that these are not merely reflections of case-mix differences between participants with and without HIV, but rather represent a more stable inflammatory signature associated with IL-17A in this cohort. More broadly, these results fit with current models of treated HIV in which residual immune activation persists despite ART and contributes to non-AIDS comorbidity risk, including cardiovascular and renal disease8,9.

The inverse association between sST2 and IL-17A is noteworthy and may reflect more complex immune regulation. sST2 is the soluble decoy receptor for IL-33 and is increasingly recognized as an immune-modulating and tissue-injury marker rather than a simple pro-inflammatory signal. Depending on context, the IL-33/ST2 axis can either amplify or dampen downstream inflammatory pathways, including IL-1-, IL-6-, and IL-17-related responses31,32. In that framework, the negative association observed here may indicate that higher circulating sST2 reflects compensatory regulation or altered IL-33 signaling in individuals with lower IL-17A activity, although the cross-sectional design does not allow mechanistic inference. Because sST2 is also linked to tissue remodeling, fibrosis, and cardiovascular risk, its association with IL-17A may be particularly relevant in populations with chronic inflammatory and cardiometabolic stress, including people living with HIV33,34.

Although several associations reached statistical significance, their potential clinical importance differs. IL-1 and IL-6 showed relatively stable associations across multivariable and sensitivity analyses and are biologically consistent with established Th17 inflammatory signaling, suggesting that these findings may reflect meaningful inflammatory relationships rather than isolated statistical associations. In contrast, the association observed for plasma potassium was modest and not retained in HIV-restricted analyses, limiting confidence in its clinical relevance. Similarly, while sST2 showed an inverse association with IL-17A, the absence of established clinical thresholds for these biomarkers in this population makes interpretation of absolute effect size difficult. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted primarily in terms of biological plausibility and consistency rather than immediate clinical applicability.

An important finding of this study is that HIV status was not independently associated with IL-17A after adjustment for inflammatory biomarkers. This result suggests that HIV may influence IL-17A indirectly through persistent immune dysregulation rather than through a direct independent effect on circulating IL-17A. That interpretation is consistent with work showing that Th17-polarized CD4 + T cells are deeply involved in HIV pathogenesis, mucosal dysfunction, and reservoir biology, but that the downstream circulating cytokine profile is shaped by a broader network of inflammatory mediators and comorbid processes35,36. It is also compatible with evidence that ART-treated HIV remains associated with chronic immune activation and elevated IL-6/TNF-related pathways even when viral replication is controlled9. Taken together, our data suggest that IL-17A may be better understood as a marker of residual inflammatory milieu in treated HIV populations rather than as a stand-alone biomarker of HIV status.

To assess the robustness of these findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by including plasma potassium in the final model to evaluate association with IL-17A. This model was then repeated after stratifying by HIV status. Notably, while plasma potassium remained independently associated with IL-17A in the overall group, this association was not retained in either people living with HIV or HIV-negative subgroups when analyzed separately, suggesting that the observed overall potassium effect may be associated with confounding factors or interactions present only when both subgroups are combined. These sensitivity analyses indicate that, irrespective of potassium adjustment, the core findings persist: IL-1 and IL-6 are independently associated with IL-17A in people living with HIV, whereas IFN-γ is the primary correlate in HIV-negative individuals. These results highlight that HIV status may modify cytokine correlates of IL-17A, with the broader systemic signals observed in the combined cohort likely suggesting the confounding influence of HIV-associated inflammation rather than universally applicable pathways.

The association between plasma potassium and IL-17A should be interpreted more cautiously. Potassium remained positively associated with IL-17A in the overall combined model, but this relationship was not retained in the HIV-status sensitivity analyses. This pattern suggests that the potassium finding is context-dependent and hypothesis-generating rather than robust. Experimental literature supports the idea that potassium flux can influence immune cell activation and T-cell differentiation, including downstream inflammatory signaling, but the direction and relevance of these effects are highly context-specific19,37–40. It is therefore possible that plasma potassium in our study may indicate unmeasured metabolic, renal, or inflammatory states rather than directly associating with IL-17A. Future work should evaluate this relationship in larger cohorts with repeated measurements and more detailed renal, dietary, and medication data before drawing stronger conclusions.

This study has several strengths. It was conducted in a clinically relevant, high HIV-burden African setting and included a substantial proportion of participants receiving contemporary INSTI-based ART, improving the relevance of the findings to current HIV care. The modeling strategy was also stronger than that used in many exploratory biomarker studies, because it progressed through theory-driven clinical, metabolic, and inflammatory models before arriving at a final parsimonious model, and the key results were further examined in an HIV-restricted sensitivity analysis. In addition, the inclusion of inflammatory, metabolic, renal, and hematologic measures allowed a broader assessment of IL-17A correlates than is typical in studies focused on a single pathway.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporality or causality. Second, although model diagnostics were acceptable, IL-17A was right-skewed, and some residual non-normality cannot be completely excluded. Third, the cohort was predominantly female and largely composed of ART-treated, virologically suppressed participants, which may limit generalizability to untreated populations, men, or people with advanced HIV disease.

Several additional sources of residual confounding should be acknowledged. Although participants with major comorbidities were excluded and core clinical variables were adjusted for, detailed information on medication use beyond ART regimen category, including antihypertensive agents, diuretics, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, corticosteroids, and other renal or cardiovascular medications—was not systematically incorporated into analyses. These therapies may influence inflammatory biomarkers, electrolyte balance, and kidney function. Dietary factors, including salt and potassium intake, were also not measured and may have contributed to variability in plasma potassium and inflammatory profiles. Furthermore, HIV-specific variables such as ART duration, cumulative treatment exposure, and immunologic history (e.g., CD4 count trajectories) were not included in final models and could contribute residual confounding.

Finally, while history of tuberculosis was collected, systematic screening for latent or active tuberculosis and other chronic infections was not performed. Given the high HIV/TB burden in this setting, unrecognized co-infections may have influenced inflammatory pathways, including IL-17A signaling. Therefore, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical, infectious, dietary, and treatment-related factors cannot be excluded. Finally, the cytokine panel did not include TGF-β, IL-23, or other upstream Th17-polarizing mediators, which limits more complete mapping of the pathway regulating IL-17A.

Our findings suggest that in a high HIV-burden, largely ART-treated population, circulating IL-17A is most strongly linked to an IL-1/IL-6-centered inflammatory axis, with sST2 showing an opposing association and potassium representing a tentative, non-robust signal. These data support the view that IL-17A reflects broader immune activation rather than HIV infection alone. Longitudinal studies that incorporate repeated cytokine profiling, HIV-specific clinical variables, and more detailed metabolic and renal phenotyping are needed to clarify the temporal and mechanistic basis of these associations.

Conclusion

This study reveals that IL-17A levels are independently associated with key inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1 and IL-6, with an inverse association observed for soluble ST2. HIV status and clinical variables were not independently associated after adjustment, suggesting that IL-17A reflects broader immune activation rather than HIV infection per se. The association with plasma potassium was not consistent across sensitivity analyses and should be interpreted as exploratory. These findings highlight the role of IL-17A within a coordinated inflammatory network and underscore the need for longitudinal studies to clarify causal pathways.
