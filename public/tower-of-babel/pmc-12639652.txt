Occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture inducing recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess in a pediatric patient: a case report
Authors: Ligang Jiang, Xin Jiang, Wencan Wu, Fangzheng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn pediatric populations, rhinogenic intraorbital abscess is frequently encountered, typically resulting from the direct extension of acute sinusitis. However, recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess secondary to an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture is extremely rare and often overlooked due to atypical trauma history and subtle imaging features.Case presentationAn 11-year-old male pediatric patient experienced recurrent nasal congestion and rhinorrhea with redness, swelling, and pain of the left eye. He was first diagnosed with acute sinusitis, yet conventional anti-infective treatment failed to provide improvement. Radiological assessment indicated a left intraorbital abscess, and further history disclosed repeated facial injuries caused by basketball strikes. Although the preoperative Computed Tomography(CT) did not clearly show a fracture, the integration of clinical presentation and multidisciplinary evaluation indicated that an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture was highly likely to have resulted in an anatomical vulnerability: on the one hand, it might impair normal sinus drainage; on the other hand, it could create subtle fissures, serving as a conduit for recurrent dissemination of sinus infection into the orbit, eventually leading to abscess development. The pediatric patient was treated with endoscopic total ethmoidectomy, frontal and sphenoid sinusotomy, and concurrent intraorbital abscess drainage, leading to significant symptomatic relief and no recurrence over a six-month follow-up period.ConclusionsOccult medial orbital wall trapdoor fractures may underlie recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess in pediatric patients, underscoring the importance of thorough trauma history assessment and high-resolution radiological evaluation. In the presence of ocular warning signs or recurrence, prompt endoscopic sinus surgery with intraorbital abscess drainage is recommended to control the etiology, remove the lesion, and optimize clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06331-y.

Background

The medial orbital wall is primarily composed of the lamina papyracea of the ethmoid bone. Due to its anatomical fragility, it is one of the common sites of isolated orbital fractures, particularly prone to occult trapdoor fractures in children [1–3]. Clinically, such injuries often present with subtle external appearance and atypical signs, thereby increasing the risk of misdiagnosis [4]. Especially following craniofacial trauma, if the integrity of the medial wall is compromised, the barrier between the orbit and paranasal sinuses may be disrupted or form a communication channel. This anatomical alteration facilitates the spread of sinus infections or sinonasal pathogens through the fracture margin or bony defect into the orbital cavity, predisposing patients to orbital infections such as orbital cellulitis, intraorbital abscess, or, most commonly, subperiosteal abscess [5, 6].

In pediatric patients, the anatomical configuration of the ethmoid sinus and the unique characteristics of their bone structure facilitate the spread of infection toward the medial orbital wall [7, 8]. Sinusitis remains the predominant cause of orbital cellulitis and subperiosteal abscess in this population [9–11]. If diagnosis and treatment are delayed, orbital infections may lead to irreversible complications such as vision loss, proptosis, and restriction of extraocular motility. In severe cases, disruption of natural barriers allows pathogens to spread intracranially, resulting in brain abscess or venous sinus thrombosis, which are potentially life-threatening [12, 13]. Accordingly, when orbital infection is suspected, prompt imaging evaluation within a multidisciplinary setting, followed by timely surgical or medical intervention, is essential to prevent the development of severe sequelae [14].

Although sinonasal-related intraorbital abscess has been widely reported [15–18], recurrent cases in pediatric patients triggered by occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fractures remain exceptionally rare in the literature. These cases are often misdiagnosed, with the underlying etiology difficult to establish, which may consequently lead to delayed treatment. In this case, the patient had long been diagnosed as having acute sinusitis alone, with recurrent relapses and limited response to conventional treatment. Such cases offer important reference value for identifying complex etiologies and optimizing management strategies in clinical practice. Through a comprehensive analysis of the clinical presentation, imaging findings, and treatment course, this report seeks to raise awareness among clinicians and guide refinement of diagnostic and therapeutic pathways in comparable situations.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old male pediatric patient initially sought care at an outside otolaryngology department on multiple occasions due to recurrent left-sided nasal obstruction and rhinorrhea. According to the patient and his parents, one month earlier he had developed nasal obstruction and rhinorrhea following a cold, accompanied by left eye tearing, mild redness, swelling, and pain, with intermittent dizziness, headache, and nausea. He experienced four episodes of vomiting, all consisting of gastric contents. At that time, he was diagnosed with acute sinusitis at a local hospital and treated with intravenous cephalosporins and dexamethasone, which provided only slight relief.Three days later, his ocular symptoms worsened and he presented urgently to our ophthalmology outpatient department. He reported marked aggravation of left-sided nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, and ocular pain and swelling, with mildly blurred vision, accompanied by dizziness, headache, and nausea with vomiting of gastric contents. He denied diplopia, visual field loss, or scotoma.He had a history of chronic sinusitis, characterized by frequent nose blowing, though symptoms were mild and typically resolved within one week.According to the parents’ report, the pediatric patient had no apparent history of trauma and was otherwise healthy, with no history of similar conditions or other notable medical history.He had no regular medication use apart from antibiotics, no history of toxic exposure, and no family history of glaucoma. On general examination, he was in good condition with normal body temperature. Ophthalmic examination revealed a best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 1.2 in the right eye and 0.8 in the left eye. The left eye demonstrated mild exotropia, mild proptosis, and restricted adduction. Palpation indicated increased orbital pressure on the left compared to the right. Intraocular pressure measured 16 mmHg in the right eye and 23 mmHg in the left eye. Both eyes showed quiet conjunctivae, smooth and edema-free corneas, and clear anterior chambers without flare. Pupils were round, 3 mm in diameter, and briskly reactive to light. Lenses were clear, vitreous was normal, and fundus examination revealed flat retinas with preserved foveal reflex and normal retinal vasculature. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis (13.8 × 10⁹/L), elevated absolute neutrophil count (8.28 × 10⁹/L), raised C-reactive protein (25.8 mmol/L), and increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (24 mm/h), while other laboratory parameters were normal. Nasal endoscopy (Fig. 1) demonstrated purulent secretions in the posterior segment of the left middle nasal meatus and nasopharynx. Orbital Computed Tomography(CT) (Fig. 1) revealed a soft tissue density lateral to the left medial rectus muscle with well-defined borders and small intralesional gas.The estimated abscess volume was approximately 3.28 mL. The left maxillary, ethmoid, sphenoid, and frontal sinuses exhibited mucosal thickening and soft tissue density filling. Magnetic Resonance Imaging(MRI) (Fig. 2) demonstrated mucosal thickening of the left maxillary, ethmoid, frontal, and sphenoid sinuses, with patchy abnormal signals in the sinus cavities. Isointensity was observed on T1-weighted imaging, heterogeneous hyperintensity on T2-weighted imaging, and marked mucosal enhancement on contrast-enhanced sequences. Based on radiological and systemic findings, a left intraorbital abscess was confirmed. Given the recurrent nature of the infection and its resistance to standard medical therapy, a more detailed history was obtained, revealing multiple prior mild facial impacts from basketball that had not prompted parental concern or medical evaluation. Although the preoperative CT failed to definitively visualize a fracture line, the integration of the refractory clinical course, the new trauma history, and the imaging findings suggested that an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture was the most plausible explanation. After a multidisciplinary consultation and ruling out other lesions, this was considered the most plausible underlying etiology.Importantly, in the absence of tissue entrapment, trapdoor fractures frequently lack characteristic symptoms, making them easily overlooked and delaying diagnosis.

After ruling out surgical contraindications, the pediatric patient underwent endoscopic left complete ethmoidectomy, frontal, maxillary, and sphenoid sinus openings, and intraorbital abscess drainage under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Intraoperatively, the nasal mucosa appeared markedly congested and edematous (Fig. 3a). The openings of the frontal and maxillary sinuses showed disorganized tissue architecture and abundant soft tissue proliferation.The mucosa over the left uncinate process was incised (Fig. 3a), and the uncinate process and ethmoid bulla were removed to access the maxillary sinus. The anterior and posterior ethmoid sinuses were sequentially resected (Fig. 3b), and following thorough hemostasis, the frontal sinus was opened. Under navigation guidance, the natural ostium of the sphenoid sinus was located and opened, diseased tissue was excised, and the intraorbital abscess was completely drained (Fig. 3c). Postoperatively, the pediatric patient was administered empirical cefazolin for infection control, dexamethasone for anti-inflammatory and anti-edema purposes, and supportive measures including hemostasis, calcium supplementation, gastroprotection, and potassium supplementation. Pus culture revealed methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, and based on susceptibility testing, cefazolin therapy was continued [19].On postoperative day 3, the pediatric patient was clinically stable, with alleviation of left ocular swelling and pain, and marked improvement in nasal obstruction and rhinorrhea. BCVA was consistent with preoperative measurements. Inflammatory indices demonstrated significant improvement relative to preoperative values.Orbital CT (Fig. 4) demonstrated a significant decrease in the soft tissue density lateral to the left medial rectus muscle, with a slight increase in soft tissue density in the ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses, likely reflecting drainage of the intraorbital abscess into the sinuses. The maxillary sinus soft tissue density showed minimal change compared with preoperative findings.At six-month follow-up, the pediatric patient had no left-sided nasal obstruction or rhinorrhea and no ocular distension or pain. Bilateral BCVA was 1.0, with improved vision in the left eye, corrected exotropia, reduced proptosis, and normal ocular motility. All hematological parameters were within normal limits.Orbital CT (Fig. 5) demonstrated near-complete absorption of the soft tissue density lateral to the left medial rectus muscle, as well as in the ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses. Minimal residual soft tissue density persisted in the maxillary sinus but had significantly decreased compared with preoperative imaging. Drainage of the intraorbital abscess was highly effective, with no signs of recurrence over six months.Subtle medial orbital wall fractures were assessed on orbital CT by measuring the distance from the nasal septum to the medial orbital wall on the injured versus uninjured side [20]. Shortening of the distance on the fractured side was noted. Discussion and conclusions

This rare case demonstrates how an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture in a pediatric patient resulted in recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess, emphasizing the necessity of comprehensive assessment of trauma history and imaging findings in clinical practice. In pediatric patients, the medial orbital wall is extremely thin and not fully ossified, rendering it prone to trapdoor or minimally displaced fractures following trauma. Nevertheless, such fractures frequently lack obvious external signs and may be accompanied by transient symptom resolution, leading to under-recognition by the pediatric patient and caregivers, and are often missed due to the limited sensitivity of standard imaging modalities.As shown in this case, despite imaging failing to definitively identify a medial orbital wall fracture, the pediatric patient had a history of chronic sinusitis and frequent nose blowing. Chronic sinusitis may erode adjacent orbital walls or transmit nasal inflammation into the orbit through natural grooves, foramina, fissures, or gaps at post-traumatic fracture sites. When the displacement of an occult fracture is significant, it can mechanically obstruct the frontal-ethmoid recess or the maxillary sinus ostium [21], leading to impaired sinus drainage, localized negative pressure fluid accumulation, bacterial overgrowth, and recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess formation. Compared with the typical mechanism of acute sinusitis directly spreading to the orbit, this pathological process is more insidious and prone to recurrence.

Previous studies have confirmed that sinusitis is the most common cause of intraorbital abscess and orbital cellulitis. Jabarin et al. [22] indicated that intraorbital abscess and preseptal cellulitis secondary to sinusitis are frequently encountered clinically, highlighting sinusitis as a key predisposing factor for orbital infections. Eufinger et al. [23] conducted a retrospective analysis of 36 patients with acute suppurative sinusitis and found that most cases of intraorbital abscess were closely linked to sinusitis, with some exhibiting intracranial complications, emphasizing the significance of early surgical intervention and multidisciplinary care. Chaudhry et al. [24] retrospectively reviewed 218 patients with orbital cellulitis and reported that sinusitis was the most common etiology, with roughly half of the cases complicated by intraorbital abscess. Most required surgical drainage, and delayed management could lead to significant vision loss. Furthermore, in the current literature, cases explicitly describing occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fractures leading to recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscesses are exceedingly rare. Only a few reports have discussed the potential association between medial orbital wall fractures and rhinogenic intraorbital infections. Simon et al. [25] reported four cases of orbital blowout fractures that subsequently developed orbital cellulitis and intraorbital abscesses; all patients had a history of sinusitis or developed sinusitis shortly after the fracture, suggesting that the fracture may disrupt the bony barrier and create a pathway for infection. Takesue et al. [26] described a patient with orbital fracture accompanied by chronic sinusitis who developed severe intraorbital infection and panophthalmitis postoperatively, indicating that local barrier disruption following fracture may facilitate bacterial invasion. Similarly, Park et al. [27] emphasized that orbital wall fractures associated with sinus pathology or unhealed bony defects could serve as potential risk factors for delayed orbital infection. Kemps et al. [28] reported an 8-year-old girl who developed an orbital floor trapdoor fracture with muscle entrapment after being struck in the face by a soccer ball, highlighting that potential fractures should be highly suspected in pediatric patients even with mild post-traumatic clinical signs. Furthermore, we previously reported three cases of definitively diagnosed medial orbital wall fractures [29], in which imaging and intraoperative observations confirmed obstruction of the sinus ostia by the fractures, resulting in spread of sinus infection into the orbit. Overall, these studies support our hypothesis that an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture may provide a potential pathway for recurrent invasion of rhinogenic pathogens into the orbit, leading to persistent or recurrent intraorbital abscesses. In the present case, the initial history of facial trauma was overlooked, and the condition was misdiagnosed as acute sinusitis, resulting in a protracted and relapsing course. The uniqueness of this case lies in its chronic, recurrent presentation with subtle radiologic findings, highlighting that occult trapdoor fractures should be considered in pediatric patients with recurrent orbital infections. Clinicians should proactively inquire about prior facial trauma and, when necessary, perform high-resolution bone-window CT to avoid misdiagnosis.

Regarding management, although some pediatric intraorbital abscess may temporarily improve with early medical therapy [8, 30], based on the Chandler staging system [31, 32] and contemporary expert consensus, patients with intraorbital abscess should undergo surgical intervention within 24 h. For preseptal and orbital cellulitis, conservative management is preferred initially, with surgical drainage considered if the condition deteriorates [33, 34]. Pediatric patients with subperiosteal orbital abscess showing inadequate response to 48 h of conservative therapy should similarly proceed to surgical intervention [35]. Concerning surgical timing, it has been proposed that pediatric patients with abscess volumes exceeding 1.18 mL should be considered for operative management [36]. In our case, the estimated abscess volume was 3.28mL, further supporting the decision for prompt surgical intervention. Compared with conventional external orbital incision drainage, endoscopic transnasal surgery enables precise localization [37], safe and effective drainage under direct visualization [38], and concurrent treatment of the primary sinus pathology, including removal of bone fragments, osteophytes, or hyperplastic tissue that mechanically obstruct the sinus ostia, thereby restoring sinus drainage and lowering recurrence risk [39, 40]. In the present case, the pediatric patient underwent endoscopic transnasal complete ethmoidectomy, frontal and sphenoid sinus opening, combined with intraorbital abscess drainage. During nearly six months of follow-up, no recurrence was observed, further demonstrating the effectiveness of early, targeted surgical management.

The main limitation of this case report lies in the absence of direct radiologic or intraoperative confirmation of a medial orbital wall fracture. Therefore, the diagnosis of an occult trapdoor fracture was essentially a clinical inference based on multiple indirect lines of evidence. These include the patient’s definite history of minor facial trauma, the recurrent infection refractory to standard antibiotic therapy, and the CT-based asymmetry suggesting subtle orbital wall deformity. This diagnostic uncertainty underscores a critical challenge in clinical practice: in the absence of definitive imaging findings, clinicians must rely on meticulous history-taking, careful analysis of disease progression, and the integration of indirect signs to reach the most reasonable judgment.

In summary, an occult medial orbital wall trapdoor fracture represents a rare but clinically significant underlying cause of recurrent rhinogenic intraorbital abscess in pediatric patients. Because of its atypical trauma history and inconspicuous radiologic features, this condition is easily overlooked in clinical settings.The core clinical implication of this case is that ophthalmologists and otolaryngologists should maintain a high index of suspicion when encountering Pediatric patients with recurrent or unexplained periorbital infections. First, even in the absence of a definite history of major facial trauma, clinicians should carefully and repeatedly inquire about possible minor injuries that may have been forgotten. Second, the inherent limitations of routine CT in detecting subtle or occult fractures must be recognized. A comprehensive assessment integrating clinical history with indirect orbital morphologic asymmetry is essential for early diagnosis, timely intervention, and improved prognosis. Furthermore, endoscopic orbital abscess drainage has proven to be an optimal therapeutic approach. This procedure not only allows effective evacuation of the abscess but also addresses the underlying sinonasal pathology by restoring adequate sinus drainage. By doing so, it significantly reduces the risk of recurrence and highlights the pivotal role of multidisciplinary collaboration in managing complex orbital infections.
