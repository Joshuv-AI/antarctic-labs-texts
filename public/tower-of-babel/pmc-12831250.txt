Identifying high-risk subdistricts for targeted dengue interventions: a spatio-temporal study in Mueang Chonburi district, Thailand, 2011-2020
Authors: Monot Rattananen, Naiyana Sahavechaphan, Darin Areechokchai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Dengue fever remains a critical public health threat in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide [1–3]. Reported cases have risen from 505,430 in 2000 to over 2.4 million in 2010 and reached 5.2 million by 2019, while deaths increased from 960 in 2000 to 4,032 by 2015. Thailand documented its first dengue cases in 1949, and outbreaks have escalated over the past 60 years. More than 50,000 cases are annually reported [4–6], with pandemic years exceeding 100,000 cases and more than 100 deaths. The epidemic patterns vary, including every other year, two years apart, and a two-year epidemic with one- or two-year intervals, consistent with observations in Thailand [7] and global evidence of cyclic dengue outbreaks [8].

Dengue fever is primarily transmitted by the bites of female Aedes mosquitoes [9, 10], which breed in a wide range of natural and artificial water-holding containers [11–13]. These containers can support larval and pupal development even during periods of low rainfall [14]. Effective prevention and control of dengue outbreaks, therefore, depend on reducing human-mosquito contact and eliminating breeding sites [15–21].

In Thailand, the Department of Disease Control (DDC) under the Ministry of Public Health typically implements dengue interventions at the district level, focusing on areas classified as high-risk. These include urban districts – designated by the Ministry of Interior, one per province – and districts with recurrent outbreaks, identified based on five-year incidence trends and specific evaluation criteria set by the DDC [22]. However, such district-level assessments often encompass diverse subdistricts with varying levels of risk. Consequently, more granular, subdistrict-level interventions may offer greater precision and effectiveness than uniform, district-wide approaches.

This study, therefore, aims to analyze dengue endemicity, spatio-temporal patterns, and key risk factors at the subdistrict level. The Mueang Chonburi district, the urban district of Chonburi province, was selected as a case study. Dengue incidence data from 2011 to 2020 were used to develop a more granular understanding of localized transmission dynamics and to identify subdistricts at elevated risk that may need targeted public health interventions.

Methods

Study area

Chonburi Province is located in the eastern region of Thailand, with the Mueang Chonburi District as its central area. This district is home to an industrial estate that houses 676 factories and employs approximately 215,000 people, including residents of diverse nationalities. Geographically, Mueang Chonburi district spans from approximately 13.23° to 13.47° N in latitude and 100.89° to 101.07° E in longitude [23]. It is administratively divided into 18 subdistricts, as depicted in Fig. 1, covering a total area of 225 square kilometers and housing a population of around 343,000 people. The district experiences three distinct seasons, as classified by the official Thai climate classification [24]: summer, rainy, and winter. The summer season lasts from mid-February to mid-May, with April and May being the hottest months of the year. The rainy season occurs from mid-May to mid-October, while the winter season lasts from mid-October to mid-February. The annual rainfall exceeds 1,200 mm, and the mean temperature varies from 27.79 to 29.73°C.

Study area: the Mueang Chonburi district

Data source

This study utilized daily dengue incidence data reported from all hospitals nationwide between 2011 and 2020, obtained directly from the TanRabad system [25, 26], the dengue surveillance platform of the DDC. The dataset comprised only laboratory-confirmed dengue cases, not restricted to severe forms such as Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF) or Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS). A confirmed case was defined as a hospital-attended patient who met the clinical criteria for dengue according to WHO guidelines and was subsequently verified by laboratory testing (NS1 antigen detection, IgM/IgG serology, or PCR). The DDC case definition remained consistent throughout the study period, and robable cases based solely on clinical symptoms were excluded to ensure comparability across years and minimize bias from diagnostic varieability. It should be noted that TanRabad does not record hospital identifiers; therefore, cases were assigned based on the patient’s subdistrict of residence rather than the treatment location. Accordingly, the analysis included all dengue cases from Mueang Chonburi district, irrespective of where care was received.

In Thailand, reporting of dengue cases is mandatory for all public hospitals but not for private hospitals. As a result, the dataset includes complete coverage from the public sector but only partial coverage from private hospitals. This limitation may result in some underestimation of absolute incidence. However, the DDC itself relies on the same dataset to produce official national and provincial dengue reports, which are widely used for surveillance and intervention planning [27, 28]. In addition, local health officials actively work to collect and input dengue cases from private hospitals into the DDC system whenever possible, which helps reduce gaps in private-sector reporting. Because public hospitals remain the primary providers of dengue care, and private cases are partly incorporated through these efforts, the dataset is considered sufficiently representative for assessing trends, endemicity, and identifying high-risk areas. While some selection bias is possible, it is unlikely to substantially affect the detection of relative spatio-temporal clustering patterns, which rely more on geographic distribution than on exact case counts.

Population denominators were obtained from the Department of Provincial Administration (DOPA) [29] for 2011–2020. These data are updated annually, and the corresponding year’s population figures were used to calculate incidence rates. The figures represent the registered resident population and do not capture hidden or transient groups such as migrant workers associated with the industrial estate in Mueang Chonburi. This limitation may lead to overestimation of incidence rates in certain subdistricts, but DOPA data are the standard source for population statistics in Thailand. Geospatial boundary data were obtained from the Geo-Informatics and Space Technology Development Agency (GISTDA), with the centroid of each subdistrict used as its geographic reference point.

Data analysis

To account for annual variations in epidemic patterns, the DDC typically relies on dengue cases from the past five years as a basis for assessing risk or establishing a baseline for the current year [22]. Consistent with this practice, this observational ecological study with spatio-temporal components adopted a 5-year time frame. Beyond operational considerations, the use of consecutive 5-year windows has important epidemiological advantages. Multi-year averaging stabilizes incidence rates by reducing the influence of short-term anomalies, localized outbreaks, and reporting irregularities, while also capturing the natural cyclical behavior of dengue epidemics. Previous studies have reported that dengue incidence in Thailand typically exhibits epidemic peaks every 3–5 years [7], and global assessments similarly note that large dengue outbreaks recur every 3–5 years [8]. These findings underscore that multi-year windows, such as 5 years, provide a more stable and epidemiologically meaningful basis for assessing endemic intensity and detecting spatio-temporal clusters compared to annual analyses or simple rolling averages.

Endemic level analysis

The 5-year average incidence rate of dengue virus infection was determined for each subdistrict by analyzing the proportion of its local population affected per 100,000 population. This rate serves as the foundation for assessing the dengue endemic level, which is classified into six categories: “safe”, “very low”, “low”, “moderate”, “high”, and “very high”, as outlined in Table 1. These thresholds were adapted from the practice of the DDC, which classifies dengue incidence rates at the provincial and district levels using cutoffs of 0–5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, and >20 cases per 100,000 population over a 4-week period. Because our analysis focused on subdistricts and used 5-year average incidence rates, the thresholds were modified to reflect the longer temporal window and finer spatial resolution. We also examined the empirical distribution of dengue incidence across the study area (2011–2020) and selected cutoffs that maintained alignment with the DDC framework while providing sufficient granularity to distinguish persistent endemicity levels at the subdistrict level. This approach ensured that the endemicity categories are both consistent with national surveillance practices and epidemiologically meaningful for local transmission dynamics.

Spatial cluster analysis

SaTScan software version 10.0, developed by Kuldlorff [30, 31], was utilized to detect and evaluate statistically significant spatial dengue clusters. These clusters are defined as “geographically bounded groups of occurrences of sufficient size and concentration to be unlikely to have occurred by chance” [32]. SaTScan offers four scanning methods: “purely spatial”, “purely temporal”, “space-time” and “seasonal”, each with various probability models such as “Poisson”, “Bernoulli”, “Multinomial” and others. Among these four scanning methods, in this study, we employed the “purely spatial” scanning method and the Poisson-based model to assess geographical areas with the highest dengue risk, focusing exclusively on the spatial dimension and neglecting the temporal aspect.

The spatial scan statistic, as described by Kulldorff [33], involves placing a circular window on the map and systematically shifting the circle’s center over each point location. At each point location, the circle’s radius is progressively increased from zero to a user-defined maximum radius, which sets the size of the circular windows and determines the sets of neighboring points considered within them. The maximum spatial cluster size parameter can be defined in one of two ways: (1) by specifying the maximum percentage of the total population at risk contained within the circle or (2) by specifying the geographic extent of the circle. In this study, we followed the default approach, which utilizes the maximum percentage of the total population at risk within the circle. As per the SaTScan user guide recommendations, the default maximum-size value was set to 50% of the total population at risk. This means that any reported cluster identified by the analysis could encompass, at most, 50% of the total population at risk.

SaTScan identifies potential clusters by calculating a likelihood ratio for each circular window, and this likelihood ratio is directly proportional to Equation 3.

where C is the total number of cases, c the observed number of cases within the circular window, e the covariate adjusted expected number of cases within the circle, and I() a binary indicator function. When SaTScan is set to scan for high-risk clusters, I() is set to equal 1 when c exceeds e. Conversely, for low-risk clusters, I() is equal to 0 when c is less than e. To scan for both high- and low-risk clusters, the indicator function I() is set to equal 1. For each identified cluster, SaTScan reported the relative risk (RR), log-likelihood ratio (LLR), and p-value. Statistical significance was evaluated using 999 Monte Carlo replications, with clusters considered significant at p < 0.05.

SaTScan evaluates all possible point locations and radius sizes to identify the circular windows with the highest likelihood ratios as the most likely clusters. To conduct the spatial scan statistic, dengue case counts aggregated at the subdistrict level (2011–2020) were used as input, with corresponding subdistrict population data from the Department of Provincial Administration (DOPA) applied as the underlying population at risk. The centroid of each subdistrict, derived from official GISTDA boundary shapefiles, was used as its geographic reference point. The purely spatial Poisson model compared the observed number of dengue cases within each subdistrict to the expected number based on its share of the total population at risk. No additional covariates (e.g., density, environmental or demographic factors) were incorporated into the SaTScan model; these were instead evaluated separately through the Key Risk Factor Analysis.

Although widely used for spatial scan statistic analyses, SaTScan’s results can be sensitive to scaling parameters, and no direct guidance exists for their optimal selection [34]. In this study, we tested various maximum cluster sizes — 4%, 6%, 8%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% of the population at risk for spatial scanning — using the data described above. Based on these experiments, a 6% cluster size was selected, as it resulted in the detection of the highest number of non-overlapping clusters, each corresponding to a single subdistrict. To assess the robustness of this choice, we conducted a sensitivity analysis across the same range of maximum cluster sizes. The results, presented in Supplementary Table S1, indicate that larger maximum sizes tended to merge multiple subdistricts into broader clusters, whereas smaller sizes occasionally failed to detect certain high-risk subdistricts.

It should be noted that SaTScan used only registered population figures from DOPA as the denominator for calculating expected case counts, and these values did not include hidden or transient populations such as migrant or mobile workers. In contrast, the observed dengue cases reported to TanRabad included all affected individuals, regardless of registration status. This mismatch may have distorted SaTScan’s relative risk (RR) and log-likelihood ratio (LLR): subdistricts with large hidden populations may have appeared to have inflated risk due to underestimated denominators, while subdistricts with more accurate or stable resident populations may have appeared to have comparatively lower risk.

Similar to the calculation of the overall endemic score, we assessed the recurrence of each subdistrict being identified as a statistically significant spatial cluster across consecutive 5-year intervals from 2011 to 2020. Temporal cluster analysis

SaTScan was also used to identify and evaluate statistically significant temporal clusters of dengue cases. To determine the period of highest dengue risk, we employed the “seasonal” scanning method and applied a Poisson-based model. Particularly, the seasonal scan statistic is a purely temporal scan statistic where all the data is on a connecting loop, such as the year, where December 31 is followed by January 1. The key distinction is that the seasonal scan statistic focuses solely on the day and month of the observations, completely disregarding the year.

The seasonal scan statistic, as outlined by Kulldorff [33], involves positioning a time window over the temporal data and systematically moving it across the observation period. For each point in time, the size of the time window is incrementally expanded from the user-defined minimum to maximum temporal cluster sizes. Similar to the spatial scan statistic, SaTScan analyzes all possible time windows to identify those with the highest likelihood ratios as the most likely clusters. The likelihood ratio (LLR) equation for the seasonal scan statistic is similar to that of the spatial scan statistic, with the primary difference being the use of a time window instead of a circular window.

In this study, input data were prepared in consecutive 5-year intervals (2011–2015, 2012–2016, etc.), with the year field removed so that SaTScan evaluated clustering by day and month only. This approach disregards year-to-year variation and focuses on seasonal recurrence across pooled years. We defined 28 and 84 days as the minimum and maximum temporal cluster sizes, respectively. The lower bound of 28 days (4 weeks) reflects the approximate length of a complete dengue transmission cycle, which includes the intrinsic incubation period in humans (4–10 days), the extrinsic incubation period in mosquitoes (8–12 days), and subsequent local transmission. The upper bound of 84 days (12 weeks) corresponds to the typical duration of dengue epidemic waves in Thailand, which often persist for 2–3 months during the rainy season. This range therefore allowed detection of both short-term outbreak clusters and longer seasonal clusters, consistent with the local epidemiology of dengue. It also filtered out irrelevant clusters and those associated with declining outbreaks.

Statistical significance was evaluated using 999 Monte Carlo replications, which adjust for the multiple testing inherent in scanning across numerous temporal windows, with clusters considered significant at p < 0.05. The analysis was configured to allow detection of multiple non-overlapping clusters; however, only one statistically significant temporal cluster was identified in each 5-year interval. Temporal patterns were further examined for each significant and recurrent spatial cluster (subdistrict) across all consecutive 5-year intervals from 2011 to 2020.

Key risk factor analysis

To explore the underlying causes of varying dengue endemicity, we selected five potential key risk factors from the report [35] related to the development of local administrative organizations. Thresholds for these factors were chosen using an evidence-based, pragmatic approach combining (1) national or local operational guidance, (2) peer-reviewed literature where available, and (3) the empirical distribution of each variable across the 18 study subdistricts:

Hidden population. The Mueang Chonburi district hosts an industrial estate, attracting a substantial transient population. This hidden population moves in and out of the area, potentially carrying the dengue virus into local communities. For each subdistrict, the hidden population proportion was calculated as y/x, where y is the number of hidden individuals and x is the total population (registered + hidden), as reported in [35]. Thresholds of 12% and 22% were defined based on the empirical distribution across subdistricts.

The analysis was conducted descriptively, overlaying threshold exceedances with endemicity and cluster results to identify potential associations. Although this provides useful insights, it does not quantify the independent contribution of each factor. A more formal multivariate approach (e.g., Poisson or Negative Binomial regression) could be applied in future work. Moreover, other determinants such as climatic variables, socioeconomic status, and vector control intensity were not included (only one weather station covers the district and vector control is implemented unevenly across subdistricts). These constraints limit the scope of the present analysis, which should therefore be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

Results

Endemic level analysis

Figures 3 and 4 illustrate 5-year average dengue incidence rates (per 100,000) for overlapping periods from 2011 to 2020 at the subdistrict level within Mueang Chonburi district. All 18 subdistricts had endemic dengue levels, ranging from very low to very high, across these overlapping periods. High-risk areas were generally located in the northern and southeastern parts of the district, with occasional cases in the central region.

As detailed in the Methods section, we evaluated spatial clusters under a range of maximum spatial cluster sizes (4–50%). Building on that analysis, we synthesized the detections across all settings to assess the stability of spatial patterns. Across the full sensitivity analysis over all consecutive 5-year periods from 2011 to 2020, three subdistricts – Nong Mai Daeng, Nong Ri, and Nong Khang Khok – consistently emerged as significant clusters. These areas were repeatedly detected either as independent clusters at smaller window sizes or as core components of larger merged clusters when broader spatial thresholds were applied. Don Hua Lo also appeared frequently, though with slightly less consistency. Overall, the spatial clustering patterns proved highly robust across all cluster-size settings, indicating that the primary high-risk areas remained stable regardless of parameter specification.

Temporal cluster analysis

Figure 7 presents all consecutive 5-year temporal clusters from 2011 to 2020 for the top three subdistricts in the Mueang Chonburi district with recurrent spatial clusters: Nong Mai Daeng, Nong Ri, and Nong Khang Khok. These subdistricts exhibited significant temporal clusters with varying start and end periods. While most clusters occurred during the rainy season, spanning from mid-May to mid-October, Nong Mai Daeng was the only subdistrict with clusters extending beyond the rainy season.

Significant temporal clusters of dengue cases across consecutive 5-year periods (2011–2020) (The red dots represent dengue cases, while the blue bars indicate the temporal clusters)

The Nong Mai Daeng subdistrict (LLR=8.712 - 22.726, RR=2.31 - 3.25, p=0.001 - 0.005) showed statistically significant temporal clusters in all six consecutive 5-year periods. The timing of these clusters varied, with four periods displaying similar patterns, but differing in duration. Clusters typically began at the start of August and ended either in September, or extended into early to mid-November. In the remaining two periods, the clusters either began in June and ended in mid-July, or started in mid-June and continued until the end of August.

The Nong Ri subdistrict (LLR=11.316 - 21.946, RR=2.29 - 2.98, p=0.001) exhibited statistically significant temporal clusters in five out of the six consecutive 5-year periods. Most clusters began in mid-July and ended between early to late September. However, one period saw the cluster starting earlier, from mid-June. The average duration of these clusters was approximately 80 days.

The Nong Khang Khok subdistrict (LLR=6.535 - 8.477, RR=2.64 - 3.41, p=0.001 - 0.027) exhibited statistically significant temporal clusters in three out of the six consecutive 5-year periods. These clusters started in late May and ended between mid to late July, with an average duration of about 60 days.

Overall, the temporal clustering patterns showed a clear seasonal structure across all periods and subdistricts. Most clusters occurred from July to September, aligning with the core of the rainy season. Average cluster duration ranged from approximately 60 days in Nong Khang Khok to about 80 days in Nong Ri, while Nong Mai Daeng exhibited the longest and most variable temporal windows, in some periods extending into October or November. This integrated pattern indicates that, despite differences in duration and timing among recurrent high-risk subdistricts, dengue activity consistently intensified during the mid-rainy season across all 5-year intervals.

Key risk factor analysis

Figure 8 presents the distribution of five key risk factors—hidden population proportion, population density, number of temples and schools, number of cemeteries, and waste management—across all subdistricts, along with their corresponding thresholds. Each subdistrict exhibits a distinct pattern across these factors, which may contribute to varying levels of dengue outbreak risk. The Samnak Bok and Saen Suk subdistricts had favorable conditions across all five factors. In contrast, the remaining subdistricts exhibited one to three risk factors, indicating elevated dengue risk. Specifically, the Nong Mai Daeng, Khlong Tamru, Don Hua Lo, and Ang Sila subdistricts had hidden population proportions above the higher threshold, while Nong Ri, Ban Puek, Bang Sai, and Na Pa subdistricts exceeded the lower threshold, reflecting increased vulnerability. Population density surpassed the threshold in the Ban Khot, Bang Sai, Makham Yong, Bang Pla Soi, Nong Mai Daeng, Ban Suan, and Samet subdistricts. The number of temples and schools exceeded the higher threshold in the Nong Ri, Nong Khang Khok, Mueang, and Huai Kapi subdistricts and surpassed the lower threshold in Ang Sila, Samet, and Nong Mai Daeng subdistricts. The number of cemeteries exceeded the higher threshold in the Nong Ri subdistrict and the lower threshold in the Na Pa subdistrict. In terms of waste management, lower values indicate inadequate handling; accordingly, the Nong Mai Daeng, Khlong Tamru, Ang Sila, Ban Puek, and Ban Suan subdistricts recorded values below the benchmark.

Discussion

This study used data collected from hospitals nationwide through the DDC. Several limitations should be considered:

Incomplete reporting: Dengue reporting is mandatory for public hospitals but only partial for private facilities, potentially underestimating absolute incidence.

Despite these limitations, the study provides robust insights into the spatial and temporal dynamics of dengue at the subdistrict level.

The findings reveal that all subdistricts in the Mueang Chonburi district experienced varying degrees of dengue endemicity over time, with some consistently exhibiting either higher or lower levels than others. Nong Mai Daeng, Nong Ri, and Nong Khang Khok subdistricts recorded the top three highest endemicity scores and were repeatedly identified as statistically significant spatial clusters across all consecutive 5-year periods from 2011 to 2020 using the SaTScan spatial scan statistic method [30, 33]. These persistent clusters are consistent with observations from other countries where spatial heterogeneity in dengue risk has been documented, including Brazil and Sri Lanka [40, 41]. In contrast, the Samnak Bok and Saen Suk subdistricts consistently maintained low endemic levels and showed no significant spatial or temporal clustering, reflecting a relatively stable and lower-risk profile.

The key risk factor analysis provided further insight into these patterns. Subdistricts exhibiting multiple unfavorable conditions – such as high proportions of hidden populations, elevated population density, numerous temples and schools, a high number of cemeteries, and inadequate waste management – often corresponded to those identified as spatial and temporal clusters [42]. Similar associations have been reported in other settings: high-density populations were strongly correlated with dengue clustering [36], poor waste management contributed to persistent hotspots [43, 44], and cemeteries created risk environments due to unmanaged containers and water storage practices [45]. Our findings in Nong Mai Daeng and Nong Ri extend these observations by showing that the cumulative presence of multiple risk factors, rather than any single factor, is most strongly associated with persistent clustering over a decade. Conversely, the consistently low-risk profiles of Samnak Bok and Saen Suk suggest that maintaining favorable conditions across several domains can substantially reduce outbreak likelihood, consistent with the protective effect of coordinated urban planning [46].

To place these findings in a broader context, we compared patterns in Mueang Chonburi with dengue cluster studies from other Thai provinces. Studies conducted in Bangkok (2003–2017) demonstrated that urbanization, population density, and human movement are key drivers of dengue transmission in the capital, where dense residential patterns and daily commuting sustain virus circulation throughout the year despite seasonal fluctuations [7]. Investigations in Chiang Mai during the 2013 outbreak reported that dengue transmission peaked between July and September, with spatial clusters concentrated in densely populated urban neighborhoods, areas with numerous temples, and sites hosting large transient or mobile populations [47]. Analysis in Khon Kaen Province (2006–2016) identified persistent clusters in both the urban city core and surrounding rural zones [48]. The rural clusters may be influenced by nearby lakes and swamps that provide mosquito habitats, combined with human movement and rural–urban migration. These regional patterns align closely with findings from Mueang Chonburi, where the most persistent high-risk subdistricts share similar risk characteristics. Nong Mai Daeng hosts a major industrial estate with large transient worker populations, while Nong Ri and Nong Khang Khok feature numerous temples and densely populated mixed-use areas. Collectively, these studies underscore that mobility, urban expansion, and mixed-use landscapes are consistent determinants of enduring dengue hotspots across Thailand.

Importantly, these risk factors also reveal subdistricts that may not yet exhibit consistently high endemicity but display latent risk due to unfavorable environmental or demographic characteristics. Subdistricts such as Ang Sila and Samet, which exceeded multiple risk thresholds, warrant proactive public health interventions despite their currently moderate outbreak levels. This highlights the potential of early-warning approaches, as monitoring environmental and demographic indicators can help anticipate dengue surges before incidence peaks [49].

Overall, these findings underscore the need for multifaceted and localized public health strategies that consider demographic, environmental, and behavioral contributors to dengue transmission [46]. Targeted interventions—such as improving waste management, monitoring mobile populations, and controlling breeding sites around public spaces—are crucial in high-risk subdistricts. Moreover, the identification of persistent high-risk areas like Nong Mai Daeng, Nong Ri, and Nong Khang Khok reinforces the importance of spatially disaggregated data to guide resource allocation and prioritize control efforts. Consistent with studies in Mexico and Colombia emphasizing hotspot-focused interventions [50, 51], our results suggest that resources should be concentrated in persistently high-risk subdistricts for maximum impact. While good performance in a single domain (e.g., waste management in Nong Ri) is beneficial, it cannot fully offset risks posed by other factors, underscoring the importance of integrated and sustained community-level strategies.

Finally, building on the results from Mueang Chonburi, the methodology is broadly adaptable. The spatio-temporal clustering and endemicity framework relies on routinely collected surveillance and population data, which are available nationwide in Thailand. With minor adjustments to thresholds and parameter settings to reflect local epidemiological contexts, this approach could be applied in other provinces or scaled nationally to support dengue risk assessment and guide public health planning.

Conclusions

This study examined dengue endemicity and spatio-temporal patterns at the subdistrict level within Mueang Chonburi district, utilizing long-term data from 2011 to 2020 collected from hospitals nationwide. The analysis revealed that all subdistricts experienced dengue outbreaks to varying degrees, with Nong Mai Daeng, Nong Ri, and Nong Khang Khok consistently emerging as high-risk areas. This recurrent pattern highlights the need for subdistrict-specific interventions, moving beyond uniform, district-wide approaches.

To explore potential drivers of these patterns, an evidence-based framework was employed to evaluate five key risk factors: hidden population proportion, population density, number of temples and schools, number of cemeteries, and waste management practices. Subdistricts where multiple factors exceeded defined thresholds were more likely to exhibit persistent or severe outbreaks. These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of dengue transmission and reinforce the importance of spatially disaggregated, data-driven public health strategies tailored to local conditions.
