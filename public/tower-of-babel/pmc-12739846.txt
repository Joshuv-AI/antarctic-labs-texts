Conclusions about a link between SAPHO syndrome, IgA nephropathy and dental amalgams can only be drawn after extensive analysis
Authors: Julien Delrieu, Alison Prosper, Rémi Esclassan, Thibault Canceill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComment on “Dental amalgam associated with SAPHO syndrome and IgA nephropathy: a case report and literature review” by Cai et al. (2025)Keywords: SAPHO syndrome, Dental amalgam, IgA nephropathy, Case report, Commentary

Main text

The recent case report published by Cai et al. is, to our knowledge, the first to describe a potential link between Synovitis Acne Pustulosis Hyperostosis Osteitis (SAPHO) syndrome, IgA nephropathy (IgAN), and dental amalgam exposure [1]. The authors’ objective of exploring the intersection between systemic autoimmune manifestations and oral health factors is commendable. However, we believe that several critical limitations in the methodology and interpretation warrant further discussion. Drawing hasty conclusions about causal links between several factors, without providing the necessary analyses, can lead to serious diagnostic and treatment errors for future patients.

The authors’ initial idea was innovative for two reasons. On the one hand, it is increasingly clear that general pathologies can be correlated with oral problems [2], and, on the other hand, patients suffering from SAPHO syndrome may exhibit oral symptoms and manifestations involving the jaw bones [3]. Therefore, exploring new risk factors potentially associated with certain dental biomaterials appeared worthwhile. Even if SAPHO syndrome is a rare condition, clinical studies with wider samples of patients have already been performed in the past [4] and they represent the only way to conclude that a link exists between the disease and a factor exposure.

In a case report such as this, it would have been interesting to specify that not all classical features of SAPHO syndrome were present in the patient. In particular, the authors do not report any manifestation of acne, which constitutes one of the core components of the SAPHO acronym and is frequently severe in typical cases. Here, the authors described the presence of dental amalgams in the mouth of a 50-year-old patient. However, this is a highly common clinical finding in dental practice, although not all 50-year-old men with amalgam fillings develop SAPHO syndrome. It is entirely possible that these dental fillings are a purely random confounding factor. In order to draw further conclusions from this clinical case, it would have been useful to provide a complete dental assessment of the patient, including intraoral photographs, a panoramic X-ray, or even volume-rendered computed tomography as recently recommended [5]. This would allow readers to assess the number and volume of amalgam fillings in his oral cavity, their proximity to each other, and the presence of other alloys that could cause corrosion, which would then justify concern about the release of metal ions into the body. Given the central role of dental amalgam in the authors’ hypothesis, this lack of documentation represents a major omission and undermines the strength of the case presentation.

When discussing differential diagnoses, the authors also mentioned possible allergic reactions. It has already been shown in the literature that a dental alloy allergy could occur in a patient with a SAPHO syndrome but with no link between the two [6]. In the case report published by Cai et al., despite referencing hypersensitivity as a possible mechanism, the authors did not perform any allergy testing, nor did they measure mercury or other metal concentrations in the patient’s blood or urine. These are essential steps to substantiate the suspicion of metal-related pathology. Moreover, in dermatology, there is well-established evidence that dental amalgams can induce oral or cutaneous lichenoid reactions, which can be associated with a positive patch test and mediated by a type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction [7]. The lack of such testing represents a significant methodological weakness, especially considering the central hypothesis of a metal-induced immune reaction. It is important to highlight that the authors themselves acknowledged these limitations in their conclusion, but they did not critically assess the implications of their conclusion.

The only information presented in the article that supports the authors’ conclusion would be the improvement in both SAPHO symptoms and IgAN after amalgam removal. However, even if the temporal sequence may suggest a possible association, it is not sufficient to infer causality and especially if we examine the case closely. There was a significant delay between the placement of the amalgam fillings and the onset of clinical symptoms: approximately two years for palmoplantar pustulosis, and four years for IgA nephropathy. Such a long latency weakens the argument for a direct causal relationship, and this important point is not discussed in the article. Other potential confounders, such as cessation of medications, natural remission of SAPHO, or dietary/lifestyle changes, are neither controlled for nor thoroughly discussed. Moreover, the improvement could be coincidental or multifactorial.

Conclusions

Finally, while the concomitant occurrence of SAPHO syndrome and IgAN is rare and intriguing, the report would benefit from a more comprehensive and critical appraisal of the existing literature on metal hypersensitivity, including cases where allergy was confirmed, and the role of systemic inflammation in IgAN pathophysiology. We strongly support case-based discussions that explore underrecognized associations, however, we caution against drawing definitive causal inferences in the absence of comprehensive clinical, radiographic, and immunologic investigations. Further well-documented cases and prospective studies are essential before dental amalgam can be implicated in the pathogenesis of SAPHO syndrome.
