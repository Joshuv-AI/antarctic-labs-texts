A Compositional Kernel Model for Feature Learning
Feng Ruan, Keli Liu, Michael Jordan

Abstract
….. We study a compositional variant of kernel ridge regression in which the predictor is applied to a coordinate-wise reweighting of the inputs. Formulated as a variational problem, this model provides a simple testbed for feature learning in compositional architectures. From the perspective of variable selection, we show how relevant variables are recovered while noise variables are eliminated. We establish guarantees showing that both global minimizers and stationary points discard noise coordinates when the noise variables are Gaussian distributed. A central finding is that ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -type kernels, such as the Laplace kernel, succeed in recovering features contributing to nonlinear effects at stationary points, whereas Gaussian kernels recover only linear ones.



1
Introduction

Deep learning has achieved remarkable success across domains such as vision, language, and science. A widely believed explanation for this success is representation learning — also called feature learning — the empirically observed ability of deep models to automatically extract task-relevant features from raw data,
without manual engineering
, to support downstream prediction
Bengio et al. 2013
.

This ability is generally attributed to two fundamental ingredients of deep models: (i) their
compositional architecture
and (ii) the use of
optimization
. The
compositionality
of the architecture endows the model with the ability to form intermediate representations of the data via composition of simple transformations. These representations are not manually defined but are learned from data by
optimizing
a loss function designed to minimize prediction error.

However, despite the empirical success of this paradigm, our theoretical understanding of how and why such representations emerge remains fundamentally limited. In particular, it remains unclear how the interplay between
compositional structure
and
optimization
gives rise to task-aligned features — and under what conditions this mechanism succeeds or fails. To address this gap, we study a stylized compositional model that preserves these two core ingredients of feature learning — while remaining simple enough to enable analysis of how features are learnt during training.

Our starting point is the classical kernel ridge regression (KRR) framework, which aims to learn a nonlinear function
f
that predicts the response
Y\in\mathbb{R}
from the covariate
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
(X,Y)\sim\mathbb{P}

Cucker and Smale 2002
. The function
f
is chosen from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H
(e.g., Sobelev space). In KRR, the learning problem is formulated as the solution to the variational problem

\min_{f}\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Here,
\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}
denotes the RKHS norm. The expectation term measures the accuracy of approximation, while the regularization term
\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}
imposes a certain amount of smoothness, which helps to achieve good
generalization to unseen test data.

To incorporate feature learning into this framework, we introduce a
compositional variant
of KRR, in which the function
f\in H
is applied to a learned coordinate-wise reweighting of the input

\min_{\beta}\min_{f}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-f(\beta\circ X))^{2}\right]+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}.

(1.1)

Here,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
defines a learnable input transformation (coordinate-wise reweighting), where
\beta\circ X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the Hadamard product, defined by
(\beta\circ X)_{j}=\beta_{j}X_{j}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,d
. In this setup,
feature learning
corresponds to learning
\beta
: the model adaptively up-weights and down-weights coordinates before applying the predictor
f
.

Building on this formulation, we use the model as a simple testbed to study feature learning. In this paper, we formalize this question through the perspective of
variable selection
:

If
Y
depends only on a few coordinates of
X
, can the compositional model (
1.1
) recover these relevant features and eliminate irrelevant ones through joint optimization of
f
and
\beta
?

Formally, we assume
Y
depends on
X
only through a subset of coordinates

X_{S_{*}}:=(X_{j})_{j\in{S_{*}}}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]

(1.2)

for some unknown index subset
S_{*}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
. For identifiability,
we define
S_{*}
as the
minimal
subset of coordinates of
X
that suffices to predict
Y
(a precise definition is given in Definition
3
).
From the perspective of
variable selection
, a central goal of feature learning is to discard as
many noise variables as possible—and a more ambitious one—recovering the exact
support, so that
\operatorname{supp}(\beta)
matches the true relevant features.

1.1
Overview of Main Results

Feature learning in this model is governed by the double minimization in (
1.1
).
Since for each fixed
\beta
, the inner minimization reduces to a standard KRR solution with weighted
input
\beta\circ X
, this leads us to the reduced objective

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda):=\min_{f\in H}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(Y-f(\beta\circ X))^{2}\right]+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}.

(1.3)

The learning problem thus reduces to minimizing
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
over
\beta
.
From the perspective of
variable selection
, two fundamental questions drive our analysis:

\mathsf{(P1)}

Under what conditions does minimizing
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
yield a sparse
\beta
that discards all the noise variables?

\mathsf{(P2)}

More stringently, when does
\beta
exactly recover all relevant features, i.e.,
\operatorname{supp}(\beta)=S_{*}
?

Answering these questions is far from straightforward: the objective
\mathcal{J}
is nonconvex in
\beta
and depends on it nonlinearly. This motivates our study of the
landscape
of
\mathcal{J}
, including its first variation, global minimizers, and stationary points. We analyze these objects under various data distributions
(X,Y)\sim\mathbb{P}
and translation-invariant RKHS
H
, with particular focus on when the landscape promotes variable selection as a form of feature learning. Methodologically, we use tools from probability, Fourier analysis, and the calculus of variations to uncover how the geometry of
\mathcal{J}
reflects the interplay between
\mathbb{P}
and
H
, and when this variational objective identifies predictive features.

Our results are purely
static
in nature: we characterize the landscape of
\mathcal{J}
and the statistical properties of its global minimizers and critical points, which serves as a foundation for studying feature learning in
\mathcal{J}
. Questions about the
dynamics
of optimization algorithms, or about
finite sample effects
arising from replacing
\mathbb{E}
by its empirical counterpart
\mathbb{E}_{{\rm emp}}
, are clearly important in practice. However, these investigations should build on the present foundation and are best pursued as separate lines of work beyond the scope of this paper.

With the goal of characterizing the landscape of
\mathcal{J}
in mind, we now summarize our main results, showing how
\mathcal{J}
induces variable selection at its stationary points and global minimizers.

•

(
Global Minimizer Recovers Features Perfectly
, Section
3
)
Assuming the relevant and irrelevant features are statistically independent, for small
\lambda
the global minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
exactly recovers the true features. The independence assumption is not merely technical: in many applications, truly predictive features in a system—such as object shape in an image—should vary independently of background clutter or sensor noise.

However, the objective
\mathcal{J}
is nonconvex, and global minimization is rarely tractable in nonconvex settings. This shifts
our focus to understanding the behavior of local minima and stationary points.

•

(
Stationary Points Eliminate Noise Variables
, Section
4
)
A striking feature of the objective
\mathcal{J}
is its ability to filter out irrelevant variables—even at
stationary points
, not just global minima. Suppose the irrelevant features follow a Gaussian distribution, a canonical distribution for modeling noise. Then, we show that any stationary point
\beta_{\dagger}
with nontrivial predictive power—formally,
\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
—must assign zero weight to all irrelevant coordinates. This denoising effect holds regardless of the distribution of relevant features and holds for all
\lambda>0
.

These results ensure that stationary points with nontrivial prediction is automatically noise-free—but can they also recover the relevant features? We provide some answers to this in Section
5
.

•

(
Advantage of Laplace over Gaussian Kernels
)
A key theme in our analysis is that recovery of relevant features at stationary points depends critically on the choice of
H
. We say a feature
X_{i}
has a main effect if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y]
. Among common kernels, those based on the
\ell_{1}
norm—such as the Laplace kernel—enjoy a distinctive advantage: they are sensitive to main effects of any form. As an example, the point
\beta=0
can never be stationary if any feature exhibits a main effect. In contrast, Gaussian kernels respond only to linear effects: if the features are nonlinearly predictive but linearly uncorrelated with
Y
, then
\beta=0
is stationary despite
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y]
.

•

(
Laplace Kernel Detects All Main Effects
)
This advantage of Laplace kernels extends beyond the origin
\beta=0
. Assuming mutual independence between the relevant features, we show that within any bounded region, every stationary point
\beta_{\dagger}
must assign nonzero weights to features that carry a main effect for small enough
\lambda
. The independence assumption ensures that variation in
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]
can be attributed to individual features
X_{i}
in an unambiguous way. In short,
\ell_{1}
-type kernels enable local optima—not just global optima—to recover all relevant features that exhibit main effects.

•

(
Recovering Interactions Requires Coordination
)
In contrast, recovering features involved only in interaction effects requires coordination among variables. Assume mutual independence between the relevant features. We say that a feature
X_{i}
participates in an interaction if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
for some index set
S\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
where
i\in S
. In words,
X_{i}
contributes information about
Y
beyond what is already contained in the other variables of
S
. We further assume that
X_{i}
has no main effect, meaning
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[Y]
.

If all interaction partners are inactive (i.e.,
\beta_{j}=0
for all
j\in S\backslash\{i\}
), then the partial derivative with respect to
\beta_{i}
can vanish—implying
X_{i}
is undetectable in isolation. This occurs, for example, at
\beta=0
, which is a stationary point under such conditions. However, once the partner features
(X_{j})_{j\in S\backslash\{i\}}
become active—that is, their coefficients
\beta_{j}
are nonzero—the partial derivative with respect to
\beta_{i}
becomes nonzero. As a result, any stationary point with sufficiently active partners (meaning that
(\beta_{j})_{j\in S\backslash\{i\}}
are bounded away from
0
) must also activate
X_{i}
.

We conclude with a brief discussion of the algorithmic implications from our main results above. These are not formal guarantees, but qualitative insights that follow naturally from our study.

•

(
Automatic Elimination of Irrelevant Features
)
If an iterative algorithm is initialized at a point
\beta^{0}
with
\mathcal{J}(\beta^{0},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
and the objective decreases monotonically, then every iterate
\beta^{t}
maintains
\mathcal{J}(\beta^{t},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
, and its limiting stationary point will automatically exclude irrelevant features. In this sense,
\mathcal{J}
embeds a denoising principle: once predictive power is achieved, irrelevant features vanish under any descent algorithm.

•

(
The Interaction Barrier
)
Features with main effects generate descent directions in the objective (a first-order variation), so they are readily discovered by local search. By contrast, interaction-only features produce no such first-order signal and remain hidden until their partner variables are active. Thus, our analysis suggests that either higher-order variations are needed to detect them, or
their recovery relies on the numerical algorithm’s trajectory. In the later case, initialization becomes important—algorithms starting too close to the origin may fail to escape flat regions where interaction signals are suppressed.

1.2
Related Work

This paper contributes to the growing line of work on understanding
feature learning
, broadly defined as the process by which compositional models automatically extract predictive representations from raw inputs by minimizing data-driven objectives
Bengio et al. 2013
. A key conceptual distinction of
feature learning
from classical variable selection is that feature learning is inherently
continuous
and
optimization-driven
: instead of making discrete keep-or-discard decisions over coordinates, the representation is parametrized by real-valued weights or transformations that are optimized jointly with the predictor, with the goal of achieving better generalization on unseen data.

Our focus in this paper is on the structure of the solutions—specifically, the support of the global minimizer and stationary points. By studying these supports, we view feature learning through the lens of variable selection, thereby providing a theoretical foundation that connects the continuous landscape of feature learning with the discrete problem of selecting relevant variables.

That said, there are two lines of related work for this piece of manuscript.

•

(
Variable selection in kernel methods
)
Early work by Vapnik and collaborators considered variable selection in kernel-based models (e.g., support vector machines), using a variational formulation similar to (
1.1
) but with a binary restriction
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{d}

Weston et al. 2000
;
Guyon et al. 2002
. Subsequent studies introduced continuous relaxations—conceptually close to our proposed model (
1.1
)—mainly for applications in bioinformatics and related areas
Grandvalet and Canu 2002
;
Cao et al. 2007
;
Allen 2013
;
Chen et al. 2017
. Our paper can be viewed as a continuation of this line: we treat
\beta
as continuous and learn it through optimization. The crucial difference is that previous work did not analyze the statistical variable selection properties of the resulting solutions of
\beta
in any continuous relaxations—not even for global minimizers, let alone stationary points. As a result, our work provides the missing statistical foundation for this line of research, with guarantees for both global minimizers and stationary points, under various distributional assumptions
\mathbb{P}
and choices of RKHS
H
.

•

(
Feature learning in kernel methods
)
A recent line of work—motivated by the empirical success of deep learning—has studied feature learning in the context of kernel methods. The key idea is to extend predictors from the form
f(X)
to two-layer such as
f(\beta\circ X)
, as studied in this paper, or more generally
f(UX)
with a learnable matrix
U

Fukumizu et al. 2009
;
Jordan et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Radhakrishnan et al. 2024
;
Follain and Bach 2024
;
Li and Ruan 2025a
;
Li and Ruan 2025b
;
Zhu et al. 2025
. These formulations are compositional in nature and aim to endow kernel methods with the ability to adaptively discover low-dimensional, task-relevant representations. Empirically, these two parameterizations often offer complementary advantages in practice: the diagonal form
f(\beta\circ X)
acts directly on raw features, making it suited to datasets where individual features carry interpretable meaning (e.g., tabular datasets), while the full matrix form
f(UX)
allows learning linear combinations of features and is conceptually closer to two-layer neural networks
Chen et al. 2023
;
Radhakrishnan et al. 2024
;
Beaglehole et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Danhofer et al. 2025
.

The main contribution of this work is to provide a statistical and variational foundation for learning
f(\beta\circ X)
: we characterize when the continuous, optimization-driven formulation of feature learning in kernel methods (
1.1
) yields statistically valid variable selection. This perspective is crucial to feature learning because, in regimes where the coordinates of
X
are independent, correct variable selection in
\beta
is a necessary prerequisite for generalization. A major theoretical result in this paper reveals that the choice of kernel—particularly the Laplace kernel—plays a decisive role in enabling such feature recovery. At the same time, we establish a strong denoising property of
\mathcal{J}
, showing how irrelevant features can be removed through continuous optimization. Taken together, these results clarify when optimization in compositional kernel methods can be relied upon to achieve genuine feature learning rather than spurious fits.

Another recent line of work centers on the so-called AGOP (Average Gradient Outer Product) method
Radhakrishnan et al. 2024
. This line of work also adopts the parameterization
f(\beta\circ X)
(and
f(UX)
) within kernel ridge regression and can be viewed as operating on the same objective
\mathcal{J}
. AGOP relies on an iterative update scheme
\beta^{(t+1)}={\rm AGOP}(\beta^{(t)})
(and
U^{(t+1)}={\rm AGOP}(U^{(t)})
resp.) designed to extract a low-dimensional set of relevant features, and has been studied both theoretically—with generalization error bounds that outperform that of standard kernel methods—and empirically, with remarkable success on tasks such as tabular dataset predictions
Radhakrishnan et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2025
. Our study takes a complementary perspective. Instead of analyzing a specific iterative scheme, we focus on the optimization landscape of
\mathcal{J}
itself and establish population-level guarantees on feature recovery for its global minimizers and stationary points. These results hold under more relaxed distributional assumptions and highlight how the geometry of
\mathcal{J}
shapes variable selection independently of any algorithmic choices. A central insight of this analysis is that the choice of kernel is crucial: whereas existing guarantees primary focus on smooth kernels, we show that nonsmooth
\ell_{1}
-type kernels such as the Laplace kernel enables the recovery of nonlinear main effects that Gaussian kernels miss. Interestingly, a recent algorithmic development on AGOP—namely xRFM
Beaglehole et al. 2025
, a state-of-the-art method for tabular datasets—demonstrates empirically that adopting the Laplace kernel improves generalization over other kernels (e.g., Gaussian), aligning with the theoretical insights developed in this paper.

1.3
Technical Contributions

From a technical standpoint, the most directly related work is a recent foundational study of compositional models of the form
f(UX)
, which shares one author with the present paper
Li and Ruan 2025a
;
Li and Ruan 2025b
. In that work, both the matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and the function
f\in H
are learned jointly. While some arguments in this paper are inspired by that line of work, the present study departs from it in two crucial ways. First, we restrict attention to the diagonal case
U=\operatorname{diag}(\beta)
, focusing on variable selection behavior, so a coordinate system is fixed in advance. Second—and more significantly—we relax the key assumptions on the kernel: earlier work assumes smooth, rotationally invariant kernels (e.g., Gaussian), whereas here we work with the Laplace kernel and other
\ell_{1}
-type kernels, which are nonsmooth and anisotropic.

This relaxation is essential to the main conceptual insight developed in Section
5
. Our analysis shows that the choice of kernel fundamentally shapes the feature recovery behavior of stationary points. The Laplace kernel, induced by the
\ell_{1}
norm, enjoys a distinct advantage: it can detect main effects of any form—linear or nonlinear—whereas Gaussian kernels are sensitive only to linear main effects. At a fundamental level, the Laplace kernel effectively involves the map
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto|x-x^{\prime}|
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in its derivative, which is
conditionally negative definite
, while the Gaussian kernel involves
|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}
, which is not. Fourier analysis makes this distinction precise and shows that this conditional negative definiteness underlies the contrasting feature recovery behaviors.

To accommodate these broader assumptions, Section
2
revisits the variational foundations, establishing continuity, directional differentiability, and stationarity conditions. In particular, we derive first variation of
\mathcal{J}
that underpins later analysis, with special care devoted to the nonsmooth behavior of the Laplace kernel. Sections
3
and
4
then present initial variable selection results for global minimizers and stationary points. Remarkably, we show that any stationary point of
\mathcal{J}
with nontrivial prediction power eliminates noise features when they are Gaussian distributed. Finally, Section
5
builds on these foundations to develop the central conceptual message new to the literature: nonsmooth, anisotropic
\ell_{1}
-type kernels such as the Laplace kernel give rise to qualitatively different stationary-point structures that enable stronger relevant feature recovery guarantees. There, we also explain why
\ell_{1}
-type kernels are indispensable to this phenomenon. Taken together, Sections
2
–
5
provide the complete story for this paper. We list important open questions in Section
6
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Translation Invariant RKHS

We now give a quick review of translation invariant reproducing kernel
Hilbert spaces (RKHS); more general references on RKHS can be found in
Aronszajn 1950
;
Cucker and Smale 2002
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space of real-valued continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated to a
translation-invariant kernel, and
C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of continuous functions equipped with

L_{\infty}
norm. The space
H
is defined in terms of the Fourier transform by

H:=\left\{f:f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\hat{f}|^{2}(\omega)}{k(\omega)}d\omega<\infty\right\}

where we require
k\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to be even, strictly positive satisfying the normalizing condition:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(\omega)d\omega=1.

Lemma 1

(Continuous embedding)
.

For any
f\in H
,
\left\|{f}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}
, and
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0
.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwartz, we have

\left\|{f}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}^{2}\leq\|\hat{f}\|_{L_{1}}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\hat{f}|^{2}(\omega)}{k(\omega)}d\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(\omega)d\omega=\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

By the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, we get
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0
.
∎

Given that
H
is continuously embedded into
C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this means that
H
is a reproducing kernel
Hilbert space. We then define the kernel function

K(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(\omega)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\omega\rangle}d\omega.

Note that
K
is real-valued as
k
is even, and continuous as
k\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
K(x,x^{\prime})=K(x-x^{\prime})
.
The continuous function
K(x,\cdot)
lies in
H
, and have Fourier transform

k(\omega)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,x\rangle}
. These functions
K(x,\cdot)

are known as
reproducing kernel
. Each
f\in H
can be represented as

f(x)=\langle f,K(x,\cdot)\rangle_{H},~~~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Lemma 2

(Denseness of
H
in
L_{2}(\mu)
)
.

For any probability measure
\mu
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, the Hilbert space
H
is dense in
L_{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

Suppose this is not true. There is nonzero
f\in L_{2}(\mu)
such that
\int fhd\mu=0

for all
h\in H
. In particular, taking
h(\cdot)=K(\cdot-z)\in H
, we obtain

\int f(x)K(x-z)d\mu(x)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Using the Fourier inversion formula,

K(x-z)=\int k(\omega)e^{2\pi i\langle x-z,\omega\rangle}d\omega
, we get

\begin{split}\iint f(x)k(\omega)e^{2\pi i\langle x-z,\omega\rangle}d\omega d\mu(x)=0~~~\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{split}

Define the Fourier transform of
f
under
\mu
as
F(\omega):=\int f(x)e^{2\pi i\langle x,\omega\rangle}d\mu(x)
. Then the above equation becomes

\int F(\omega)k(\omega)e^{-2\pi i\langle z,\omega\rangle}d\omega=0~~~\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

That is, the inverse Fourier transform of
F(\omega)k(\omega)
is identically zero.
Since
k(\omega)>0
, this implies
F(\omega)=0
almost everywhere
\omega
, so
f=0
in
L_{2}(\mu)
—a contradiction.
∎

2.1.1
Examples

Two classical examples that readers should bear in minds are:

Example 1

(Gaussian RKHS)
.

The Gaussian RKHS corresponds to the case where

K(x)=e^{-\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}},\quad~\text{and}~\quad k(\omega)=\pi^{d/2}e^{-\pi^{2}\left\|{\omega}\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

The reproducing kernel is
K(x,x^{\prime})=e^{-\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}
, which defines the Gaussian RKHS on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\clubsuit

Example 2

(Laplace RKHS)
.

The Laplace RKHS corresponds to the case where

K(x)=e^{-\left\|{x}\right\|_{1}},\quad~\text{and}~\quad k(\omega)=\frac{2^{d}}{\Pi_{i=1}^{d}(1+4\pi^{2}\omega_{i}^{2})}.

The reproducing kernel is
K(x,x^{\prime})=e^{-\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}}
, which defines the Laplace RKHS on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
\clubsuit

The above examples are canonical members of a broader family of translation-invariant RKHSs. A classical result of Schoenberg
Schoenberg 1938
states that
K(x)=\psi(\|x\|_{2}^{2})
defines a translation-invariant RKHS on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
d
if and only if
\psi
is non-constant and admits the representation

\psi(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\mu(dt)

for some finite measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
. Under our normalization,
K(0)=\int k(\omega)d\omega=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(dt)=1
, so in what follows we assume that
\mu
is a probability measure.
This perspective leads to the following class of RKHSs, with the Gaussian RKHS arising as a simple special case.

Example 3

(Radial Kernel RKHS)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with compact support in
(0,\infty)
. Define

\begin{split}K(x)=\psi(\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}^{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\mu(dt),\quad\quad k(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\pi t^{-1})^{d/2}e^{-\pi^{2}t^{-1}\left\|{\omega}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\mu(dt).\end{split}

Then
K
and
k
form a Fourier transform pair, and define a translation-invariant RKHS as above.

We can also use such functions
\psi
to define analogous
\ell_{1}
-type kernels where the
Laplace kernel serves as a special instance.

Example 4

(
\ell_{1}
type RKHS)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with compact support in
(0,\infty)
. Define

\begin{split}K(x)=\psi(\left\|{x}\right\|_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\left\|{x}\right\|_{1}}\mu(dt),\quad\quad k(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(2t)^{d}}{\Pi_{i=1}^{d}(t^{2}+4\pi^{2}\omega_{i}^{2})}\mu(dt).\end{split}

Then
K
and
k
are a Fourier transform pair, and define a translation-invariant RKHS as above.

2.2
Kernel Ridge Regression

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a random vector, and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
be a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty
.
We define

\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}

and consider the following minimization problem, which we will refer to as
kernel ridge regression
:

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda).

The existence of a unique minimizer to this problem is known
Cucker and Smale 2002
. We denote this minimizer by

f_{\beta,\lambda}
.

Lemma 3
.

We always have
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Proof.

Plug in the test function
f=0
, we get
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]=\mathcal{I}(0,\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

The minimizer
f_{\beta,\lambda}
to this problem satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(Y-f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X))g(\beta\circ X)]=\lambda\langle f_{\beta,\lambda},g\rangle_{H},~~~\forall g\in H.

Proof.

This follows by setting the first variation of
f\mapsto\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)
equal to zero.
∎

We denote

r_{\beta,\lambda}(x,y):=y-f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ x)

which we will refer to as the
residual function
at
\beta
.
This residual
r_{\beta,\lambda}
captures the part of
y
that is not explained by the regression model

f_{\beta,\lambda}
evaluated on the transformed input
\beta\circ x
.

Lemma 5
.

We always have
\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
and
\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Proof.

We note
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\geq\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

We have

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)K(\beta\circ X,z)]=\lambda f_{\beta,\lambda}(z),~~~\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

For every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define
g_{z}:=K(\cdot,z)\in H
. Applying Lemma
4
with
g_{z}
, we get

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)g_{z}(\beta\circ X)]=\lambda\langle f_{\beta,\lambda},g_{z}\rangle_{H}.

By the reproducing property of the RKHS
H
,
\langle f_{\beta,\lambda},g_{z}\rangle_{H}=\langle f_{\beta,\lambda},K(\cdot,z)\rangle_{H}=f_{\beta,\lambda}(z)
. Substituting back completes the proof.
∎

We establish an identity that connects the RKHS norm of
f_{\beta,\lambda}
to the residual
r_{\beta,\lambda}
and the kernel
K
.
Throughout the rest, we always use
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
to denote an independent copy of
(X,Y)
.

Lemma 7
.

We always have

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})]=\lambda^{2}\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)K(\beta\circ X,z)]=\lambda f_{\beta,\lambda}(z).

Setting
z=\beta\circ X^{\prime}
for an independent copy
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
in Lemma
6
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})|X^{\prime}]=\lambda f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X^{\prime}).

Multiplying both sides by
r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
and taking expectation gives

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})\right]=\lambda\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X^{\prime})].\end{split}

Finally, applying Lemma
4
with
g=f_{\beta,\lambda}
gives

\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X)]=\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Combining the last two identities establishes the desired relation.
∎

2.3
Continuity in Parameter

A fundamental property is that the minimizer
f_{\beta}
and the objective
\mathcal{J}
depend
continuously on the parameter
\beta
.
This fact essentially follows from the reinterpretation of the
f(UX)
model in related work
Li and Ruan 2025a
;
for completeness, we include a self-contained proof in this manuscript.

First, we prove

Lemma 8
.

For every
\lambda>0
, the mapping
\beta\mapsto f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X)
is continuous under the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
norm, i.e.,

\lim_{\beta^{\prime}\to\beta}\mathbb{E}[(f_{\beta^{\prime},\lambda}(\beta^{\prime}\circ X)-f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X))^{2}]=0.

Also, the function
\beta\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
is continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The proof of Lemma
8
is given in Appendix
A.1
.
As a consequence, we get

Lemma 9
.

For every
\lambda>0
, the minimizer
f_{\beta,\lambda}
depends continuously on
\beta
in the Hilbert space
H
-norm:

\lim_{\beta^{\prime}\to\beta}\left\|{f_{\beta^{\prime},\lambda}-f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}=0.

Proof.

The functional
\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)
is quardratic in
f
. Therefore, we have

\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)=\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)+\mathcal{Q}(f-f_{\beta,\lambda})

where
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)
is a quadratic term that is lower bounded by
\lambda\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{H}^{2}
. The
same identity holds when replacing
\beta
with
\beta^{\prime}
. This shows that

\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}-f_{\beta^{\prime},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}+\mathcal{J}(\beta^{\prime},\lambda)\leq\mathcal{I}(f_{\beta,\lambda},\beta^{\prime},\lambda).

Since
f_{\beta,\lambda}\in H
is continuous and uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we get

\mathcal{I}(f_{\beta,\lambda},\beta^{\prime},\lambda)\to\mathcal{I}(f_{\beta,\lambda},\beta,\lambda)=\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
as
\beta^{\prime}\to\beta
. Also, by Lemma
8
,
we have
\mathcal{J}(\beta^{\prime},\lambda)\to\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
. This implies

\limsup_{\beta^{\prime}\to\beta}\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}-f_{\beta^{\prime},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq\limsup_{\beta^{\prime}\to\beta}(\mathcal{I}(f_{\beta,\lambda},\beta^{\prime},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(\beta^{\prime},\lambda))=0.

The proof is thus complete.
∎

2.4
First Variation

We study the first variation of
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
with respect to the parameter
\beta
.
To this end, we define for every
\beta,x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime}):=K(\beta\circ x,\beta\circ x^{\prime})=K(\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})).

We use the notation

\mathrm{D}K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})[v]:=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(K_{\beta+sv}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime}))

to refer to the directional derivative (also called
Gâteaux differential
) of the map
\beta\mapsto K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})
evaluated at
\beta
in the direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whenever the limit exists.

Theorem
1
provides an explicit expression for the directional derivative
of the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
with respect to the parameter
\beta
. Given any direction
v
, we write

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]:=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}\left(\mathcal{J}(\beta+sv,\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\right)

if the limit exists.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that for every
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the directional derivative

\mathrm{D}K_{\beta+tv}(x,x^{\prime})[v]

exists for all
t\in[0,\epsilon)
and is continuous in
t
. In addition, assume the uniform integrability condition:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\epsilon)}(\mathrm{D}K_{\beta+tv}(X,X^{\prime})[v])^{2}\right]<\infty.

Then the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
exists and is given by

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\mathrm{D}K_{\beta}(X,X^{\prime})[v]].

In the above,
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
denote an independent copy of
(X,Y)
.

Related formulas have appeared in the author’s earlier work on
f(UX)
model
Li and Ruan 2025a
, though under stronger smoothness assumptions, i.e., full Fréchet differentiability on the map
\beta\mapsto K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})
for fixed
x,x^{\prime}
, and in more specialized settings where
K
is assumed to be a radial basis kernel.

In contrast, our analysis establishes the directional differentiability of
\mathcal{J}
under a minimal assumption: that the map
\beta\mapsto K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})
admits directional derivatives that satisfy a uniform integrability condition. This relaxation is crucial for handling kernels such as the Laplace kernel, where the map
\beta\mapsto K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})
is not Fréchet differentiable, yet directional derivatives (i.e., Gâteaux differentials) remain well-defined. Our proof is inspired by prior work but departs from it in details. See Appendix
A.2
for the full argument.

2.4.1
Examples

We illustrate Theorem
1
for two representative RKHSs introduced earlier:
one induced by a radial kernel and one by an
\ell_{1}
-type kernel. Both are defined through a function
\psi
of the form

\psi(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\mu(dt),~~~\text{for}~~~z\geq 0

where
\mu
is a probability measure with compact support that is contained in
(0,\infty)
. The function
\psi
is
differentiable on
[0,\infty)
with

\psi^{\prime}(z)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{-tz}\mu(dt),~~~\text{for}~~~z\geq 0

where
\psi^{\prime}(0)
is understood as the right-hand derivative of
\psi
at
0
.

Example 5

(First Variation for Radial Kernel RKHS)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with support in a compact subset of
(0,\infty)
, and define

\psi
as above. In example
3
, we showed that

K(x)=\psi(\|x\|_{2}^{2})

defines a radial kernel and induces an RKHS
H
. For such kernels,
K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2})
is continuously differentiable in
\beta
, with directional derivative

\mathrm{D}K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})[v]=2\psi^{\prime}(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2})\cdot\sum_{i}\beta_{i}v_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}.

Since
\psi^{\prime}
is bounded by
|\psi^{\prime}(0)|=\int t\mu(dt)
, the uniform integrability condition in Theorem
1
holds whenever
X
has finite fourth moment. In this case, the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
exists for all
\beta
and
v
, and is given by

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]&=-\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cdot\psi^{\prime}(\|\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2})\cdot\sum_{i}\beta_{i}v_{i}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})^{2}].\end{split}

This gives the first variation formula to radial kernel RKHSs. In particular, the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
is linear in the direction
v
.

\clubsuit

We can analogously compute the directional derivative for
\ell_{1}
type kernel RKHS.

Example 6

(First Variation for
\ell_{1}
-type RKHS)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with support in a compact subset of
(0,\infty)
, and define

\psi
as above. In example
4
, we showed that

K(x)=\psi(\|x\|_{1})

defines an
\ell_{1}
type kernel and induces an RKHS
H
. For such kernels,
K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{1})
is directionally differentiable for every
\beta
and direction
v
, with directional derivative

\mathrm{D}K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})[v]=\psi^{\prime}(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{1})\cdot\sum_{i}w(\beta_{i};v_{i})|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|,

where

w(\beta_{i};v_{i}):=\begin{cases}{\rm sgn}(\beta_{i})v_{i}~~~&\beta_{i}\neq 0\\
|v_{i}|~~~&\beta_{i}=0\end{cases}.

Since
\psi^{\prime}
is bounded, the uniform integrability condition in Theorem
1
holds whenever
X
has finite second moment. In this case, the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
exists for all
\beta
and
v
, with

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]&=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cdot\psi^{\prime}(\|\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})\|_{1})\cdot\sum_{i}w(\beta_{i};v_{i})|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|].\end{split}

Unlike the radial kernel RKHSs, the directional derivative

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
is
not
linear in
v
.
Thus, for
\ell_{1}
type kernels, the objective
\mathcal{J}
is not differentiable in the classical (Fréchet) sense.

\clubsuit

Theorem
1
and the above examples illustrate that, in many scenarios of interest,
the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
exists for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all directions
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Motivated by these results, throughout the paper we will be only interested in scenarios where

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]
exists for all
\beta
and directions
v
.

This directional regularity is fundamental—it enables a principled notion of stationarity, which play a central role in understanding the landscape of
\mathcal{J}
.

2.5
Directional Stationarity and Local Optimality

This paper is concerned with the minimization problem of
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
over
\beta
:

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}~\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda).

In general, the objective
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
is nonconvex in the parameter
\beta
, so global minimization is typically intractable for numerical algorithms. It is therefore more natural to study local notions of optimality, which better reflect the types of solutions produced by numerical optimization methods.

Definition 1
.

We say that
\beta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
global minimizer
of
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
if

\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
.
We say that
\beta_{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
local minimizer
of
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)\leq\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\quad\text{for all }\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ with }\|\beta-\beta_{\dagger}\|\leq\delta.

When the objective
\mathcal{J}
admits directional derivatives at every
\beta
, a basic necessary condition for local optimality is the nonnegativity of all directional derivatives.

Lemma 10
.

If
\beta_{\dagger}
is a local minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
, then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)[v]\geq 0
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\beta_{\dagger}
is a local minimizer, and
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)[v]
exists. Then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)[v]=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}\left(\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger}+sv,\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)\right)\geq 0.

This completes the proof.
∎

This motivates the notion of
directional stationary points
(also known as
D-stationary points
in the variational analysis literature
Rockafellar and
Wets 1998
).

Definition 2
.

We say that
\beta_{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a directional stationary point of
\mathcal{J}
if

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)[v]\geq 0,~~~\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Remark 1
.

We restrict attention to directional stationarity, which captures the minimal first-order condition associated with local minimizers. Stronger notions, such as Clarke stationarity, are often more convenient for algorithmic purposes but require establishing additional regularity
Rockafellar and
Wets 1998
. A detailed discussion will be deferred to a future, separate paper focused on algorithms.

In the rest of the paper, we study the statistical properties of global minimizers
\beta_{*}
, local minimizers, and directional stationary points
\beta_{\dagger}
. While global minimizers provide a natural benchmark, our main interest lies in whether local minimizers and stationary points—typically reached by iterative algorithms—can still support feature learning, that is, whether they recover low-dimensional structure in the data that is relevant for prediction.

3
Global Minimizers

Towards understanding the landscape of the
\mathcal{J}
, we begin by analyzing the global minimizers of
\mathcal{J}
, and focus on two foundational questions.

\mathsf{Q1}

(Existence).

Does
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
attain its minimum at some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
?

This question ensures the global minimizers are well-defined and excludes degenerate cases where minimizers “escape to infinity”. On a positive side, we will show that a global minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
always exists whenever
X
is a continuous random variable.

\mathsf{Q2}

(Feature Recovery).

Does the global minimizer of
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
select the coordinates of
X
that are predictive of
Y
?

This raises a fundamental statistical question: can the compositional model
f(\beta\circ X)
uncover the relevant sparse structure in the data at its global minimizer?

3.1
Existence

In this section, we address the question
(\mathsf{Q1})
. We show that when
X
has a continuous distribution,
\mathcal{J}
admits a global minimizer in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
X
has continuous density on its support. Then, for every
\lambda>0
,

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

As a corollary, the function
\mathcal{J}
admits a global minimizer
\beta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
.

Proof.

First, by plugging a test function of all zero, we see that

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\leq\mathcal{I}(0,\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}],~~~\forall\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Next, we expand the objective:

\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}[Yf(\beta\circ X)]+\mathbb{E}[f(\beta\circ X)^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Applying the reproducing property and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[Yf(\beta\circ X)]&=\mathbb{E}[Y\langle K(\beta\circ X,\cdot),f\rangle_{H}]\\
&=\langle\mathbb{E}[YK(\beta\circ X,\cdot)],f\rangle_{H}\leq\left\|{\mathbb{E}[YK(\beta\circ X,\cdot)]}\right\|_{H}\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}.\end{split}

The squared RKHS norm admits an explicit expression:

\left\|{\mathbb{E}[YK(\beta\circ X,\cdot)]}\right\|_{H}^{2}=\langle\mathbb{E}[YK(\beta\circ X,\cdot)],\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}K(\beta\circ X^{\prime},\cdot)]\rangle_{H}=\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})]

where
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}
is an independent copy of
(X,Y)
.
Combining the above gives a lower bound

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)&\geq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})]}\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.\end{split}

Minimizing the right-hand side over
\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}\in[0,\infty)
gives

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\min_{f}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-\lambda^{-1}\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})].

Note that
K(\beta\circ x,\beta\circ x^{\prime})=K(\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime}))\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
when
x\neq x^{\prime}
.
Additionally,
\mathbb{P}(X\neq X^{\prime})=1
since
X
is assumed to have continuous density. As
K
is uniformly bounded, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem then implies
\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}K(\beta\circ X,\beta\circ X^{\prime})]\to 0
as
\beta\to\infty
. This leads to

\liminf_{\beta\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

Since
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
for all
\beta
, this implies the limit

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
exists and equals
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Finally, since
\mathcal{J}
is continuous in
\beta
(by Lemma
8
) and bounded above by
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
, it must attain its infimum over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That is, there exists
\beta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda).

This completes the proof.
∎

Continuity of
X
serves as a sufficient condition for
\mathcal{J}
to admit a finite minimizer. In the presence of discrete variables, it may happen that
\limsup_{\beta\to\infty}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
(see
(
Li and Ruan 2025a
, Section 5)
). However, we are not aware of any situation where there is a global minimizer at infinity—a question we leave open.

3.2
Feature Recovery

From a variable selection perspective, we are interested in whether the global minimizer
\beta_{*}
of the population objective
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
captures relevant features of the input
X
—those that are predictive of the response
Y
. This leads to three guiding questions.

\mathsf{(a)}

Characterization of Relevant Features:

How should we formally define the minimal set of features in
X
sufficient for predicting
Y
?

\mathsf{(b)}

Irrelevant Feature Elimination:

Does the minimizer
\beta_{*}
assign zero weight to features that are unrelated to the prediction of
Y
?

\mathsf{(c)}

Recovery of Predictive Structure:

Does the minimizer
\beta_{*}
capture at least some—or all—of the features that contribute to predicting
Y
?

We address these three questions in the subsections that follow.

3.2.1
Core Sufficient Feature Set

To formalize the notion of task-relevant features, we introduce the concept of a core sufficient feature set: the smallest subset of the input
X
that retains all information needed to predict the response
Y
.

Definition 3

(Core Sufficient Feature Set)
.

Let

S_{*}:=\bigcap\{S\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}:\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]\}.

(3.1)

If
S_{*}
additionally satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]
, then it is called the
core sufficient feature set
.

Remark 2
.

This notion parallels the classical concept of the
central mean subspace
for sufficient dimension reduction. The central mean subspace is the smallest linear subspace
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|P_{U}X]
, where
P_{U}
is the orthogonal projection onto
U

Cook and Li 2002
;
Cook 1998
. In contrast, the core sufficient feature set seeks a minimal subset of coordinate indices
S
such that
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
. Both aim to identify minimal representations of the input
X
that preserve the full predictive information about
Y
, though in different structural forms.

Remark 3
.

Note that a core sufficient feature set may not always exist. For instance, if
X_{1}=X_{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{2}]
, then both
\{1\}
and
\{2\}
satisfy the condition
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
but their intersection
\{1\}\cap\{2\}=\emptyset
does not satisfy
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]
in general. Consequently, the set
S_{*}
defined in (
3.1
) fails to satisfy
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]
, and no core sufficient feature set exists.

Despite the ill-conditioned case noted in the preceding remark, Proposition
3
shows that, under the mild assumption that the feature vector
X
is continuous, the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
exists.

Proposition 3
.

Assume
X
has continuous density with support
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
exists.

Proof.

We call a subset
S\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
a
sufficient feature set
if

\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}].

Among all such sets, we consider those with the smallest cardinality. Let
S^{\prime}
denote one such set.

Below we show that
S^{\prime}\subset S
for any sufficient feature set
S
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that this is not true for some sufficient feature set
S
. Since
S^{\prime}\not\subset S
and, by definition,
S^{\prime}
has cardinality less than or equal to that of
S
, it must be that
S^{\prime}\setminus S\neq\emptyset
and
S\setminus S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. We can thus write

S=A\cup B~~~\text{and}~~~S^{\prime}=A\cup C,

where
A=S\cap S^{\prime}
, and
B,C\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
are nonempty, disjoint from each other and from
A
.
By the definition of sufficiency of
S,S^{\prime}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A},X_{B}]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A},X_{C}].

Since
X
has a continuous density with support
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the conditional distribution
(X_{B},X_{C})|X_{A}=x_{A}
has full support in
\mathbb{R}^{|B|+|C|}
for any
x_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{|A|}
. Therefore, we know that conditioning on
(X_{A},X_{B})
or on
(X_{A},X_{C})
must give the same value as conditioning only on
X_{A}
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A},X_{B}]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A},X_{C}].

Thus,
A
is itself a sufficient feature set. But
A\subsetneq S^{\prime}
by construction, which contradicts the minimality of
S^{\prime}
. This proves that
S^{\prime}
is a subset of every sufficient feature set.

With this property, we know that

S^{\prime}\subset S\quad\text{for every sufficient feature set }S,

so the intersection over all sufficient sets equals
S^{\prime}
. Since
S^{\prime}
is itself sufficient by construction, it satisfies the definition of the core sufficient feature set. This proves existence.
∎

Beyond Proposition
3
, we believe that
the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
exists in a wide range of practical scenarios, though a full characterization is beyond the scope of this paper. For instance, when
X
is uniformly distributed on the hypercube, the set
S_{*}
exists as a consequence of the Fourier expansion applied to
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]

O’Donnell 2014
. This belief is also supported by a large body of work in sufficient dimension reduction, where analogous constructs—such as the central mean subspace—are known to exist under remarkably mild assumptions
Cook 1998
.

To enable a rigorous study of feature learning, we need to focus on settings where a well-defined
target structure exists. Therefore, we assume the existence of core sufficient feature set
throughout:

Assumption 3.1
.

The core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
exists for the distribution
(X,Y)\sim\mathbb{P}
.

Given Assumption
3.1
, there is a meaningful statistical target—the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
, which captures the minimal collection of input features required to predict the response
Y
. The problem of variable selection concerns whether minimizing
\mathcal{J}
successfully recovers
S_{*}
. Understanding when and why this recovery occurs is central to unraveling how compositional model
f(\beta\circ X)
learns structure from data.

3.2.2
Irrelevant Feature Elimination

We first study one aspect of feature learning: when does the global minimizer
\beta_{*}
eliminate irrelevant features by setting coordinates outside the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
to zero? Such sparsity shows that the model can suppress irrelevant inputs automatically.

Assumption 3.2
.

Assume that
X_{S_{*}}
and
X_{S_{*}^{c}}
are probabilistically independent. Here,
S_{*}
denotes the
core sufficient feature set of the distribution
(X,Y)\sim\mathbb{P}
, and
S_{*}^{c}=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}\backslash S_{*}
denotes its complement.

This assumption states that the relevant features in
X
for predicting
Y
—namely, those in the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
—are independent of the remaining features
X_{S_{*}^{c}}
. Such structural independence avoids potential statistical confounding, making it clear in principle to distinguish relevant features based solely on their predictive contribution.

Assumption
3.2
is satisfied in synthetic settings where the data generation process is at least partially controlled—for example, in applications such as computer experiments—where we can intentionally design
X
to follow a specific distribution, such as isotropic Gaussian
Santner et al. 2003
.

For a subset
S\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
,
\Pi_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the coordinate projection onto
S
, defined by

(\Pi_{S}\beta)_{j}=\begin{cases}\beta_{j}~~\text{for $j\in S$}\\
0~~~\text{for $j\in S^{c}$}\end{cases}.

Theorem 4
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
. For every
\lambda>0

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda),~~~\forall\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The inequality is strict when
{\rm Cov}(\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta\circ X)\neq 0
and
J(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Proof.

For every
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the translated function
\tau_{\xi}f
by

(\tau_{\xi}f)(x):=f(x+\xi),~~~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By the translation-invariance of the RKHS
H
, we have
\left\|{\tau_{\xi}f}\right\|_{H}=\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}
. By the minimizing property of
\mathcal{J}
, we deduce for every vector
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
f\in H
that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\xi))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-(\tau_{\xi}f)(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{\tau_{\xi}f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-(\tau_{\xi}f)(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{\tau_{\xi}f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda).\end{split}

Define
c:=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}]
. Since
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|\Pi_{S_{*}}X]
, we can decompose the squared error as

\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\xi))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[Y|\Pi_{S_{*}}X]-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\xi))^{2}\right]+c.

Substituting into the previous inequality gives

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[Y|\Pi_{S_{*}}X]-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\xi))^{2}\right]+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}+c\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda).

By Assumption
3.2
,
\Pi_{S_{*}}X
and
\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X
are independent. Therefore, setting
\xi:=\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X
, we may condition on
\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X
to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[Y|\Pi_{S_{*}}X]-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X))^{2}\mid\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X\right]+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}+c\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda).

Taking expectation on both sides, and using
X=\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X
, we get

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbb{E}[Y|\Pi_{S_{*}}X]-f(\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}}X+\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X))^{2}\right]+\lambda\|f\|_{H}^{2}+c\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda).\end{split}

By minimizing over
f\in H
, we get

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\min_{f}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda).

This proves the desired inequality.

Our derivation shows equality holds in

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta,\lambda)

only if the minimizer
f_{\beta,\lambda}
obeys

(\tau_{\xi})f_{\beta,\lambda}=(\tau_{\xi^{\prime}})f_{\beta,\lambda}~~\text{for almost all}~\xi=\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X,\xi^{\prime}=\beta\circ\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}X^{\prime}

where
X,X^{\prime}
are independent copies from
\mathbb{P}
.
If
\operatorname{Cov}(\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta\circ X)\neq 0
, then with positive probability we have
\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}
.
Fix such a realization with
\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}
. Then, for the
the deterministic vector
z=\xi-\xi^{\prime}\neq 0
,

f_{\beta,\lambda}(x+z)=f_{\beta,\lambda}(x)~~~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

However, since
f_{\beta,\lambda}\in H
, it satisfies
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{\beta,\lambda}(x)=0

according to Lemma
1
. In turn, this implies that
f_{\beta,\lambda}
must be identically zero. As a consequence, it must follow that
J(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.
∎

We provide sufficient and necessary conditions for which the minimum value
\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Assumption 3.3
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\neq 0
under
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

Lemma 11
.

Assumption
3.3
holds if and only if

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

Proof.

Assume
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\neq 0
. Let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the all-ones vector. We claim that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{1},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
. Suppose instead that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{1},\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
. Then the minimizer
f_{\mathbf{1},\lambda}
must be identically zero, which by Lemma
4
implies
\mathbb{E}[Yg(X)]=0
for all
g\in H
. Since
H
is dense in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
by Lemma
2
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}[Yg(X)]=0
for all
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
, and hence
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=0
almost surely—contradicting our assumption
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\neq 0
.
We conclude that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{1},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
, and thus

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

Conversely, assume
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=0
. Then the minimizer
f_{\beta,\lambda}
is the zero function for every
\beta
.
As a result,
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
for all
\beta
, and hence

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.
∎

We are now ready to state our main result, which combines
Theorem
4
and Lemma
11
.

Assumption 3.4
.

Assume the irrelevant features
(X_{i})_{i\in S_{*}^{c}}
has nondegenerate covariance.

Theorem 5
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
,
3.4
.
For every regularization parameter
\lambda>0
,

\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta_{*})\subset S_{*},~~~\forall~\beta_{*}~\text{minimizing}~\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\lambda).

Remark 4
.

If
\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\lambda)
does not attain its infimum in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we interpret the set of global minimizers as empty. In this case, all conclusions involving global minimizers should be understood as vacuously true. The same remark applies to later statements.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Fix
\lambda>0
and let
\beta_{*}
be a global minimizer. Suppose, for contradiction, that

\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta_{*})\not\subset S_{*}
. Then
\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta_{*}\neq 0
.
Under Assumption
3.3
, we have

\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
by Lemma
11
.
Moreover, Assumption
3.4
guarantees that

{\rm Cov}(\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta_{*}\circ X)\neq 0
.
Finally, combining Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
,
Theorem
4
implies the strict inequality
\mathcal{J}(\beta_{*},\lambda)>\mathcal{J}(\Pi_{S_{*}}\beta_{*},\lambda)
, contradicting the optimality of
\beta_{*}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.3
Relevant Feature Recovery

In contrast to the previous subsection on eliminating irrelevant features, we now ask the complementary question: when does the global minimizer
\beta_{*}
retain features that are truly predictive of
Y
? That is, does
\beta_{*}
assigns nonzero weights to the components of the core sufficient feature set
S_{*}
? This question is critical for understanding whether feature learning preserves all information relevant for prediction—not just filters out the noise.

Theorem 6
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
.
There exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for every
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
:

\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta_{*})\supset S_{*},~~~\forall~\beta_{*}~\text{minimizing}~\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\lambda).

Proof.

For any
f\in H
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(f,\beta,\lambda)&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|\beta\circ X])^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])^{2}]\end{split}

where the inequality follows from the optimality of conditional expectation.
Taking the infimum over
f
yields, for any
\beta
and any
\lambda>0
,

\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\geq\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])^{2}].

Since
S_{*}
is the core sufficient feature set, any set
S
with
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
must obey

S_{*}\subset S
. Thus,

\inf\{\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}])^{2}|S_{*}\not\subset S\}>\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}],

which implies the existence of
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
\lambda>0
,

\inf\{\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\mid S_{*}\not\subset\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\}\geq\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}]+\epsilon.

On the other hand, since
H
is dense in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
by Lemma
2
, where
\mathbb{P}_{X}
denotes the distribution of
X
, the regression function
x\mapsto\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
can be approximated arbitrarily well in
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
by functions in
H
. In particular, there exists
f_{\varepsilon}\in H
such that

\mathbb{E}[(Y-f_{\varepsilon}(X))]^{2}<\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}]+\epsilon/2.

Let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the all-ones vector. Then for sufficiently small
\lambda>0
,

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{1},\lambda)\leq\mathcal{I}(f_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{1},\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f_{\varepsilon}(X))]^{2}+\lambda\left\|{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{H}^{2}<\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}]+\epsilon,

so we have

\inf\{\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\mid S_{*}\not\subset\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\}>J(\mathbf{1},\lambda)\geq\inf\{\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\mid\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

It follows that, for these small
\lambda>0
, any global minimizer
\beta_{*}
of
\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\lambda)
must satisfy
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta_{*})\supset S_{*}
, since otherwise its objective would exceed
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{1},\lambda)
.
∎

4
Stationary Points and Irrelevant Feature Elimination

The preceding results characterize the statistical behavior of global minimizers, providing conditions under which they eliminate irrelevant features and retain those that are predictive. However, in practice, such global minimizers may be computationally intractable—due to the nonconvexity of the objective, iterative algorithms are typically guaranteed to converge only to stationary points. This raises a fundamental question for feature learning: do stationary points preserve similar statistical structure, or are such guarantees unique to global optima?

Unlike global minimizers, stationary points are more difficult to characterize, and analyzing them requires more delicate analytical techniques. To proceed, we divide the analysis into two parts. This section focuses on the following question:

(
\mathsf{Q3}
)

Do stationary points eliminate irrelevant features?

We provide a positive result: if the irrelevant features are Gaussian distributed, then every stationary point necessarily suppresses them—that is, any nonzero coordinate of a stationary point must correspond to a relevant feature for predicting the response.

Assumption 4.1
.

The irrelevant features
(X_{i})_{i\in S_{*}^{c}}
are jointly Gaussian distributed with nondegenerate covariance.

Theorem 7
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
4.1
. Then, for every
\lambda>0
,

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]\leq 0,~~~\text{where}~~v=-\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta.

The inequality is strict if
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
and
\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta\neq 0
.

Remark 5
.

Compared to Theorem
4
, Theorem
7
provides a sharper conclusion: it identifies a descent direction
v=-\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta
toward the relevant feature subsets. This direction has a natural interpretation—it removes weight from irrelevant coordinates while leaving the relevant ones unchanged. This refinement relies on the additional Gaussian assumption on the irrelevant features.

Proof.

Fix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the problem is translation-invariant, we can without loss of generality assume

\mathbb{E}[X]=0
.
Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Gaussian random vector independent of
(X,Y)
with

\xi=(0,\xi^{\prime}),\quad\xi^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{S_{*}^{c}}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,{\rm Cov}(X_{S_{*}^{c}})).

Let
\pi_{s}
denote the probability measure of the random vector
\sqrt{s}\,\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}\circ X_{S_{*}^{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S_{*}^{c}|}
.
For every function
f\in H
and
s>0
, we define
P_{s}
as the convolution type operator:

\begin{split}(P_{s}f)(x)&=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[f(x+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi)\big]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\xi^{\prime}}\big[f(x_{S_{*}},x_{S_{*}^{c}}+\sqrt{s}\,\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}\circ\xi^{\prime})\big]\\
&=\int f((x_{S_{*}},x_{S_{*}^{c}}+y))\pi_{s}(dy).\end{split}

For every such function
f
, we use the independence of
\xi^{\prime}
and
(X,Y)
, along with the tower property and Jensen’s inequality to get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[(Y-(P_{s}f)(\beta\circ X))^{2}]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[f(\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi)|X])^{2}]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi))^{2}].\end{split}

(4.1)

Let
\beta^{(s)}:=\beta-sv
with
v=-\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta
. Then
\beta^{(s)}=\beta+s\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta
. We expand vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by grouping coordinates in
S_{*}
and
S_{*}^{c}
:

\begin{split}\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi&=(\beta_{S_{*}}\circ X_{S_{*}},\;\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}\circ X_{S_{*}^{c}}+\sqrt{s}\,\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}\circ\xi^{\prime})\\
\beta^{(s)}\circ X&=(\beta_{S_{*}}\circ X_{S_{*}},\;(1+s)\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}\circ X_{S_{*}^{c}})\end{split}.

Since
\xi^{\prime}
has the same Gaussian distribution as
X_{S_{*}^{c}}
and since
\xi^{\prime}
is independent of
X
whereas
X_{S_{*}}
and
X_{S_{*}^{c}}
are independent, we conclude the following equality in distribution:

\begin{split}(X_{S_{*}},\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(X_{S_{*}},\beta^{(s)}\circ X).\end{split}

As a result,

\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]-f(\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]-f(\beta^{(s)}\circ X))^{2}].

Using that
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]
, and adding
\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])^{2}]
on both sides, we get for all
f
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X+\sqrt{s}\,\beta\circ\xi))^{2}]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta^{(s)}\circ X))^{2}].\end{split}

(4.2)

Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we have established for every
f\in H
,

\mathbb{E}[(Y-(P_{s}f)(\beta\circ X))^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta^{(s)}\circ X))^{2}].

(4.3)

Additionally, since
P_{s}f
is a convolution type operator, we can compute its Fourier transform as follows.
We define the Fourier transform of the measure
\pi_{s}
as

\widehat{\pi}_{s}(\eta):=\int e^{-2\pi i\langle\eta,y\rangle}\pi_{s}(dy).

Then,

\begin{split}\widehat{P_{s}f}(\omega)&=\iint f((x_{S_{*}},x_{S_{*}^{c}}+y))e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\omega\rangle}dx\,\pi_{s}(dy)\\
&=\int\pi_{s}(dy)\iint f((x_{S_{*}},x_{S_{*}^{c}}+y))e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\omega\rangle}dx_{S_{*}}dx_{S_{*}^{c}}\\
&=\int e^{2\pi i\langle\omega_{S_{*}^{c}},y\rangle}\pi_{s}(dy)\cdot\widehat{f}(\omega)=\widehat{f}(\omega)\cdot\widehat{\pi}_{s}(-\omega_{S_{*}^{c}}).\end{split}

Because
\|\widehat{\pi}_{s}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\int\pi_{s}(dy)=1
, this immediately implies

|\widehat{P_{s}f}|(\omega)\leq|\widehat{f}|(\omega)
for all
\omega
. Thus

\left\|{P_{s}f}\right\|_{H}^{2}=\int\frac{|\widehat{P_{s}f}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}d\omega\leq\int\frac{|\widehat{f}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}d\omega=\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

(4.4)

Combining (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we have shown for every
f\in H
,

\mathbb{E}[(Y-(P_{s}f)(\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{P_{s}f}\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta^{(s)}\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Thus, taking infimum over
f\in H
on both sides yields
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\leq\mathcal{J}(\beta^{(s)},\lambda)
. This yields

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]=-\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(\mathcal{J}(\beta^{(s)},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda))\leq 0

as desired.

We show the above inequality is strict when
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)<\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
and
\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta\neq 0
. Let
\Sigma:={\rm Cov}(X_{S_{*}^{c}})
. We compute the Fourier transform of the Gaussian measure
\pi_{s}
and get

\widehat{\pi}_{s}(\omega_{S_{*}^{c}})=e^{-2\pi^{2}s\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}}\,\,~~~\text{where}~~~\|\eta\|^{2}_{\Sigma}:=\eta^{\top}\Sigma\eta.

Following the above derivation, and using Fatou’s lemma, we get for all
f\in H
,

\begin{split}\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}-\left\|{P_{s}f}\right\|_{H}^{2})&\geq\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\frac{|\widehat{f}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}(1-|\widehat{\pi}_{s}(-\omega_{S_{*}^{c}})|^{2})d\omega\\
&\geq 2\pi^{2}\cdot\int\frac{|\widehat{f}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}~d\omega\geq 0.\end{split}

The quantity is strictly positive unless
f
is zero. Since

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(\beta^{(s)},\lambda)=\mathcal{I}(\beta^{(s)},f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda},\lambda)&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}(\beta^{(s)}\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}\\
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)\leq\mathcal{I}(\beta,P_{s}f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda},\lambda)&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-(P_{s}f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda})(\beta\circ X))^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{P_{s}f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}\end{split}

we get

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]&=-\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(\mathcal{J}(\beta^{(s)},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda))\\
&\leq-\lambda\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(\left\|{P_{s}f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}-\left\|{f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2})\\
&\leq-2\pi^{2}\lambda\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\frac{|\widehat{f}_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}d\omega.\end{split}

Since
\beta^{(s)}\to\beta
by Lemma
9
, we get
\left\|{f_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}-f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}\to 0
, as
s\to 0^{+}
. Therefore, for every
R>0
, we apply Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to get

\begin{split}\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int\frac{|\widehat{f}_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}d\omega&\geq\liminf_{s\to 0^{+}}\int_{\left\|{\omega}\right\|\leq R}\frac{|\widehat{f}_{\beta^{(s)},\lambda}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}d\omega\\
&=\int_{\left\|{\omega}\right\|\leq R}\frac{|\widehat{f}_{\beta,\lambda}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}d\omega.\end{split}

Since the result holds for every
R>0
, we deduce

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[v]\leq-2\pi^{2}\lambda\int\frac{|\widehat{f}_{\beta,\lambda}|(\omega)^{2}}{k(\omega)}\cdot\|(\beta\circ\omega)_{S_{*}^{c}}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}d\omega.

The right-hand side equals zero only in one of two cases: either
f_{\beta,\lambda}
is identically zero, which implies
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
, or
\beta_{S_{*}^{c}}=0
, meaning
\beta
has no support outside the relevant coordinates.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
4.1
.
Let
\lambda>0
.
If
\beta_{\dagger}
is a directional stationary point of
\mathcal{J}(\cdot,\lambda)
, then one of the following must hold:

(i)~~\Pi_{S_{*}^{c}}\beta_{\dagger}=0~~\qquad\text{or}\qquad(ii)~~\mathcal{J}(\beta_{\dagger},\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

Remark 6
.

Compared to Theorem
5
, which concerns global minimizers of
\mathcal{J}
, Corollary
1
shows that a similar feature elimination property holds more broadly at directional stationary points—under an additional Gaussian assumption on the irrelevant features
X_{S_{*}^{c}}
. In particular, any stationary point that achieves nontrivial prediction error must lie entirely within the relevant feature subspace. This extends the statistical interpretation of feature sparsity beyond the global minimum.

5
Stationary Points and Relevant Feature Recovery

In this section, we continue our analysis of stationary points of
\mathcal{J}
, turning to the feature recovery question complementary to
(\mathsf{Q3})
:

(
\mathsf{Q4}
)

Can stationary points capture at least some—or all features—in
S_{*}
?

The answer to
(\mathsf{Q4})
is subtle and depends not only on the data distribution but, more crucially, on the choice of kernel
K
. The kernel defines the RKHS
H
and shapes the nonconvex landscape of
\mathcal{J}
, thereby influencing which features can be identified at stationary points.

Our analysis shows that
\ell_{1}
-type RKHS (Example
4
) are particularly effective for this purpose, enabling recovery of relevant features under milder distributional assumptions than commonly used radial kernel RKHS (Example
3
) such as the Gaussian RKHS. We outline the underlying intuition in Section
5.1
, and briefly recall the kernel class here.

Specifically, we focus on the following class of
\ell_{1}
-type
translation-invariant kernels:

K(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(\left\|{x-x^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}),~~~\text{where}~~~\psi(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\mu(dt)

(5.1)

where
\mu
is a probability measure whose support is compact, and contained in
(0,\infty)
. The Laplace kernel arises from the Dirac measure at
z=1
, i.e.
\mu=\delta_{1}
, in which case
\psi(t)=e^{-t}
. This will serve as our canonical example.
In contrast, the Gaussian kernel does not belong to this class.

All main results in this section focus on the
\ell_{1}
-type kernel class, addressing
(\mathsf{Q4})
under various distributional assumptions. We assume
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
throughout this section.

Remark 7
.

The assumption
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
is without loss of generality. Otherwise, one may subtract the mean from
Y
before regression or, equivalently, reformulate the problem with an intercept term
\gamma
:

\min_{\beta}\min_{f}\min_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(\beta\circ X)-\gamma)^{2}]+\lambda\left\|{f}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

The objective
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
can then be defined accordingly, and all subsequent results continue to hold.

5.1
Why the Laplace Kernel? A First Indication

We aim to understand when stationary points of
\mathcal{J}
recover the relevant features—this is the central question posed in
(\mathsf{Q4})
. As we will see, the answer depends not only on the data distribution but also on the choice of kernel
K
, which fundamentally shapes the optimization landscape.

To build intuition for this kernel dependence, we consider a simpler diagnostic question:

\mathsf{(Q5)}

Suppose the true feature set
S_{*}\neq\emptyset
. (i) When is
\beta=0
not stationary? (ii) When does every stationary point
\beta
of
\mathcal{J}
satisfy
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\cap S_{*}\neq\emptyset
?

This question
(\mathsf{Q5})
serves as a basic test of whether the kernel supports signal detection. Question (i) is the most basic: it asks whether the trivial solution
\beta=0
can be ruled out a stationary point. Question (ii) is stronger—it asks whether all stationary points capture some relevant feature. The two questions in
(\mathsf{Q5})
are in fact equivalent under the additional assumptions of Theorem
7
, since Theorem
7
ensures that every stationary point satisfies
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\subseteq S_{*}
.

Perhaps surprisingly, the Laplace kernel—and more broadly, the class of
\ell_{1}
-type kernels—passes this test under much weaker assumptions than the commonly used Gaussian kernel.
We start by presenting the positive guarantees shared by all the
\ell_{1}
type kernels.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose the
\ell_{1}
-type kernel is used in the objective
\mathcal{J}
. Then the directional derivative
at
\beta=0
along the direction
e_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq p
satisfies

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[Ye^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta.

As a result,
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]<0
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq 0
.

The key to Theorem
8
lies in the structure of the
\ell_{1}
-type kernel, which induces a bivariate map
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto|x-x^{\prime}|
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. This map is classically known to be
conditionally negative definite
, a fact we make precise in Lemma
12
via a Fourier representation. The general definition of conditional negative definite kernel can be found in the monograph
Wendland 2004
, though this definition is not needed for our purpose; the lemma provides all that is required for our analysis.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, and let
f\in L_{2}(\nu)
be complex-valued, satisfying the mean-zero condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,d\nu(x)=0.

Suppose
\int x^{2}\nu(dx)<\infty
. Then

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)\overline{f(x^{\prime})}\,|x-x^{\prime}|\,d\nu(x)\,d\nu(x^{\prime})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\zeta x}\,d\nu(x)\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}\,d\zeta.

As a result, the left-hand side is always nonpositive, and is strictly negative whenever
f\not\equiv 0

\nu
-a.e.

Proof.

For each
s>0
, the function
z\mapsto e^{-s|z|}
has the Fourier inversion representation

e^{-s|z|}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i\zeta z}m_{s}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,\quad m_{s}(\zeta):=\frac{2s}{4\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}+s^{2}}.

Applying this to the kernel
e^{-s|x-x^{\prime}|}
and using Fubini’s theorem, we get

\begin{split}\iint f(x)\overline{f(x^{\prime})}e^{-s|x-x^{\prime}|}\,d\nu(x)d\nu(x^{\prime})&=\int\left|\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i\zeta x}\,d\nu(x)\right|^{2}m_{s}(\zeta)\,d\zeta.\end{split}

Since
|x-x^{\prime}|=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(1-e^{-s|x-x^{\prime}|})
, with the uniform bound
|\frac{1}{s}(1-e^{-s|x-x^{\prime}|})|\leq|x-x^{\prime}|

for all
s>0
, the Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem applies:

\begin{split}\iint f(x)\overline{f(x^{\prime})}|x-x^{\prime}|\,d\nu(x)d\nu(x^{\prime})&=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}\iint f(x)\overline{f(x^{\prime})}\big(1-e^{-s|x-x^{\prime}|}\big)\,d\nu(x)d\nu(x^{\prime}).\end{split}

Since
\int f\,d\nu=0
, the integral of the constant term vanishes, so we have

\begin{split}\iint f(x)\overline{f(x^{\prime})}|x-x^{\prime}|\,d\nu(x)d\nu(x^{\prime})&=-\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}\int\left|\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i\zeta x}\,d\nu(x)\right|^{2}m_{s}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\\
&=-\int\left|\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i\zeta x}\,d\nu(x)\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}\,d\zeta\end{split}

where the last equality follows from monotone convergence, since
m_{s}(\zeta)/s\uparrow\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}
as
s\to 0^{+}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We apply the first variation formula of
\mathcal{J}
. By definition

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}K_{0}(x,x^{\prime})[e_{i}]&=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(K_{se_{i}}-K_{0})(x,x^{\prime})\\
&=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(\psi(s|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|)-\psi(0))=\psi^{\prime}(0)|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|.\end{split}

Since
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
,
f_{0,\lambda}
is the zero function. Thus,
r_{0,\lambda}(x,y)=y-f_{0,\lambda}(0)=y
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]&=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[r_{0,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{0,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\mathrm{D}K_{0}(X,X^{\prime})[e_{i}]]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|].\end{split}

Let
f_{i}(x_{i})=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}=x_{i}]
. Then
\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(X_{i})]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
. Applying Lemma
12
, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]&=\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(X_{i})f_{i}(X_{i}^{\prime})|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(X_{i})e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[Ye^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\end{split}

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]<0
since
f_{i}\neq 0
under
\mathbb{P}
. Finally, we note
\psi^{\prime}(0)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}t\mu(dt)
.
Since
\mu
is not concentrated at
0
, we get
\psi^{\prime}(0)<0
. As a result, we obtain

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[Ye^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta<0.

This completes the proof.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
8
is the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

For
\ell_{1}
type kernel,
\beta=0
is stationary for
\mathcal{J}
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]=0
for all
i
.

Our analysis shows that
\ell_{1}
-type kernels are sensitive to marginal signals of
any kind
: at
\beta=0
, the objective is not stationary whenever some feature has a nonzero
main effect
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq 0
. By contrast, radial basis kernels (e.g., Gaussian kernel) are far more restrictive. As we will see in Section
5.1.1
, they only respond to marginal signals of
linear type
, i.e., when
\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]\neq 0
.

On the downside, when the signal is nontrivial but lacks main effects—meaning
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]=0
for all
i
but
\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\neq 0
—the objective
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
has vanishing directional derivatives at
\beta=0
, and thus provides no first-order information for identifying relevant features. In such cases, even
\ell_{1}
-type kernels are powerless at
\beta=0
. This highlights a limitation of first-order analysis: detecting such higher-order signals likely requires exploiting higher-order variation in
\mathcal{J}
, a problem we leave open.

5.1.1
Comparison to Radial Basis Kernels

Our analysis focuses on
\ell_{1}
-type kernels because they are sensitive to marginal signals of all type. To place this in context, we contrast them with radial basis kernels (e.g., Gaussian), which exhibit more selective behavior at
\beta=0
: they respond only to marginal signals of
linear type
, as we show below.

Consider

K(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(\left\|{x-x^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}),~~~\text{where}~~~\psi(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\mu(dt)

for some nonnegative probability measure
\mu
with compact support in
(0,\infty)
. This class includes the Gaussian kernel as a canonical example.

We assume
X
has finite fourth moments in this subsection
5.1.1
.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose the radial basis kernel is used in the objective
\mathcal{J}
. Then the directional derivative
at
\beta=0
along the direction
e_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq p
is zero, i.e.,
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]=0
for all
i
. Moreover,

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left(\mathcal{J}(se_{i},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot(\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}])^{2}\leq 0.

The right-hand side is strictly negative if and only if
\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]\neq 0
.

Proof.

Fix
i
and define

G_{i}(s):=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}[r_{se_{i},\lambda}(X,Y)r_{se_{i},\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\psi^{\prime}(s^{2}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|^{2})|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|^{2}].

By Theorem
1
, for every
s
, the directional derivative at
se_{i}
along
e_{i}
exists and satisfies

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(se_{i},\lambda)[e_{i}]=sG_{i}(s).

In particular,
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]=0
for all
1\leq i\leq p
.

By Lemma
8
, the map
s\mapsto r_{se_{i},\lambda}(X,Y)
is continuous in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
. Since
\psi^{\prime}
is continuous
and bounded, and
X
has finite fourth moments, it follows that
G_{i}
is continuous by the dominated convergence theorem. Therefore, we have

\mathcal{J}(se_{i},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(te_{i},\lambda)[e_{i}]dt=\int_{0}^{s}tG_{i}(t)dt=\frac{s^{2}}{2}G_{i}(0)+o(s^{2})~~(s\to 0).

This yields

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left(\mathcal{J}(se_{i},\lambda)-\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)\right)=\frac{1}{2}G_{i}(0).

It remains to compute
G_{i}(0)
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
, the minimizing function at
\beta=0
is
f_{0,\lambda}\equiv 0
, the zero function, and hence
r_{0,\lambda}(X,Y)=Y
.
Substituting into the definition of
G_{i}(t)
gives

G_{i}(0)=-\frac{2}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot\mathbb{E}[YY^{\prime}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|^{2}]=\frac{2}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\cdot(\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}])^{2}.

The claim follows by combining the last two identities.
∎

Radial basis kernels suffer from a fundamental limitation: at
\beta=0
, they detect only marginal signals of
linear type
(i.e., when
\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]\neq 0
) and ignore other nonlinear main effects. The lemma formalizes this:
\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)
is second-order flat at
\beta=0
, with all first-order directional derivatives vanishing, and the leading second-order term depending only on
\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]
. This restriction persists under reparameterization. For example, setting
\beta_{i}=\sqrt{|\theta_{i}|}
and defining
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta,\lambda):=\mathcal{J}(\sqrt{|\theta|},\lambda)
yields

\tilde{J}(se_{i},\lambda)-\tilde{J}(0,\lambda)=-\frac{s}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}(0)\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]^{2}+o(|s|)~~~(s\to 0^{+})

so the objective varies at first order but still only through
\mathbb{E}[YX_{i}]
.

Thus the dependence on linear correlations is intrinsic, and radial kernels cannot reveal nonlinear main effects through its leading-order behavior near zero. This motivates our focus on
\ell_{1}
-type kernels.

5.2
A Decomposition of Directional Derivative

In this subsection, we analyze the directional derivative

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]

at a general
\beta
along the coordinate directions
e_{i}
. We restrict attention to
\ell_{1}
-type kernels and assume
X
has finite second moment, which guarantees the existence of the derivative (see Example
6
).

5.2.1
An Integral Representation

Lemma 14
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\beta_{i}\geq 0
. Then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\psi^{\prime}(\left\|{\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})}\right\|_{1})|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|].

Proof.

With
\beta_{i}\geq 0
, we get

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})[e_{i}]&=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{s}(K_{\beta+se_{i}}(x,x^{\prime})-K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime}))=\psi^{\prime}(\left\|{\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})}\right\|_{1})|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|.\end{split}

Substituting this into the first variation formula from Theorem
1
gives the result.
∎

We recall the definition of
\psi
from (
5.1
).
The function
\psi
and its derivative
\psi^{\prime}
satisfy

\psi(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tz}\,\mu(dt),~~~\quad~~~\psi^{\prime}(z)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,e^{-tz}\,\mu(dt).

For each
t>0
, using the Fourier inversion formula, we get

e^{-t\|x\|_{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,x\rangle}\,q_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega,\quad\text{where}\quad q_{t}(\omega):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{2t}{4\pi^{2}\omega_{j}^{2}+t^{2}}.

Combining the two, we obtain for every
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\begin{split}\psi\big(\|x\|_{1}\big)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,x\rangle}\,q_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt)\\
\psi^{\prime}\big(\|x\|_{1}\big)&=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,x\rangle}\,tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt).\end{split}

(5.2)

These formulas yield an integral representation for the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\cdot)[e_{i}]
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\beta_{i}\geq 0
. Then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\,e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})\rangle}\,|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

Proof.

By Lemma
14
,
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
is expressed in terms of
\psi^{\prime}(\left\|{\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})}\right\|_{1})
. By (
5.2
), we have

\psi^{\prime}\big(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{1}\big)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\rangle}\,tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt).

Substituting this representation into the expression for
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
in Lemma
14
and applying Fubini’s theorem to exchange the order of expectation and integration yields the desired formula.
∎

5.2.2
A Proxy for Directional Derivative

In Section
5.1
, we show that
\ell_{1}
-type kernels prevent
\beta=0
from being stationary whenever there is a nontrivial main effect feature. This conclusion relies on a key fact: the bivariate function
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto|x-x^{\prime}|
is
conditionally negative definite
, which forces the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]
to be strictly negative whenever
Y
depends marginally on some coordinate
X_{i}
. This is made precise via the Fourier representation of
|x-x^{\prime}|
in Lemma
12
.

This raises a natural question: does the same mechanism extend to general
\beta\neq 0
?
To pursue this, we aim to expand the conditionally negative definite kernel
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto|x-x^{\prime}|

within the directional derivative formula. Let us define

R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y):=r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}.

Our earlier formula of directional derivative (Lemma
15
) shows

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}\,\,|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).\end{split}

To pursue a Fourier-based representation, we are led to consider the quadratic form

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]

with the aim of applying Lemma
12
. However, a key difficulty
arises: the lemma crucially relies on the mean-zero condition, which does not hold in
our setting—
\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]\neq 0
in general.

To overcome this obstacle, we work with a centered integrand:

S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(x,y)=R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)].

This leads to the following quantity, which we view as a
proxy
for the directional derivative:

\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\overline{S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}\,|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

After centering, this proxy quantity admits a Fourier-type representation.

Lemma 16
.

\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

Proof.

Let us define

T_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(x_{i}):=\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\mid X_{i}=x_{i}]
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X_{i})]=\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]=0.

We now compute

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\overline{S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}\,|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X_{i})\overline{T_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X_{i}^{\prime})}\,|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\right]\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[T_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X_{i})e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta.\end{split}

The first and third equality follow from the tower property of conditional expectation, and the second from Lemma
12
, noting that
T
satisfies the mean-zero condition.
∎

Finally, using the identity

\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]={\rm Cov}(r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}})

we arrive at the following formula, which will be useful for future reference.

Lemma 17
.

\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\rm Cov}(r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}})\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

5.2.3
Error Control

We now quantify the error between the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
and
its proxy
\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
. The difference between the two arises from the lack of centering
in the original integrand, specifically the presence of the nonzero mean term
\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]
.

The following lemmas provide bounds to control this discrepancy. Let

M_{\mu}:=\sup\{t:t\in\mathop{\rm supp}(\mu)\}

denote the upper endpoint of the support of the measure
\mu
.

Lemma 18
.

For every
\beta,\lambda
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]|^{2}tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\leq\lambda M_{\mu}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

Proof.

We begin by observing that
t\leq M_{\mu}
for all
t
in the support of
\mu
, and so

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]|^{2}tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\leq M_{\mu}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]|^{2}q_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt).

To evaluate the right-hand side, we expand:

\begin{split}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]|^{2}&=\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})\rangle}].\end{split}

where
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is an independent copy of
(X,Y)
.
By (
5.2
), we have

K_{\beta}(x,x^{\prime})=\psi(\|\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\|_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ(x-x^{\prime})\rangle}q_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt).

Applying Fubini’s theorem, and using the representation above, we obtain:

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]|^{2}q_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ(X-X^{\prime})\rangle}q_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\,\mu(dt)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)r_{\beta,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cdot K_{\beta}(X,X^{\prime})\right].\end{split}

By Lemma
7
, this expectation equals
\lambda^{2}\left\|{f_{\beta,\lambda}}\right\|_{H}^{2}
, and
is at most
\lambda\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
by Lemma
5
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 19
.

For every
\beta,i,\lambda
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]|^{2}tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\leq 2M_{\mu}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{2}].

Proof.

By definition
|R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}|(x,y)\leq|r_{\beta,\lambda}|(x,y)
for all
\beta,\omega,x,y
and thus

\mathbb{E}[|R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, this implies

|\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[|R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)|^{2}]\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|^{2}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|^{2}].

The estimate then follows from
\iint tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)=\int t\mu(dt)\leq M_{\mu}\int\mu(dt)=M_{\mu}
.
∎

With these lemmas in preparation, we reach the final conclusion. Let

M_{X}:=\max_{i}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}],~~~\text{and}~~~M_{Y}:=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

Lemma 20
.

For every
\beta,i,\lambda
,

|\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]-\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)|\leq C(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+1).

The constant
C<\infty
depends only on
M_{X}
,
M_{Y}
and
M_{\mu}
.

Proof.

Fix
\beta,i,\lambda
. Note

\lambda\cdot|\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]-\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)|=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}

where

\begin{split}\Delta_{1}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]\mathbb{E}[\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]~tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\\
\Delta_{2}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)}]\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|]~tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\\
\Delta_{3}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X,Y)]\mathbb{E}[\overline{R_{\beta,\omega,\lambda}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}]~tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\end{split}

The measure
tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)
is finite with
\int tq_{t}(\omega)d\omega\mu(dt)\leq M_{\mu}
.
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and invoking Lemmas
18
and
19
, we obtain that the error terms
|\Delta_{1}|,|\Delta_{2}|
are bounded by
C\sqrt{\lambda}
and
|\Delta_{3}|
is bounded by
C\lambda
, where
C<\infty
depends only on
M_{X},M_{Y},M_{\mu}
.

∎

Remark 8
.

While both Sections
5.2.1
and
5.2.3
involve Fourier analysis—one exploits the forward transform of
\psi^{\prime}
(e.g., Lemma
15
), the other through the inverse transform of
\psi
(e.g., Lemma
18
) —this repetition is necessary. The key is that error control requires expansion in the frequency domain, where bounds involving
M_{\mu}
emerge (Lemma
18
). We do not know how to carry out error control without working in the frequency domain.

5.2.4
Conclusion

We conclude by presenting the decomposition of the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
.
For clarity of presentation, we define
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda):=-\lambda\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\beta_{i}\geq 0
, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)

where
|\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)|\leq C

for some constant
C<\infty
depending only on
M_{X}
,
M_{Y}
, and
M_{\mu}
, and where

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)=\iiint\left|{\rm Cov}(r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}})\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt)

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
17
and
20
, and
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)=-\lambda\mathcal{S}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)

∎

Theorem
9
decomposes the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
into two terms:
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
scaled by
1/\lambda
, and a remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
scaled by
1/\sqrt{\lambda}
. Crucially, the remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
is uniformly bounded.
The term
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
aggregates the squared covariance between the modulated residual

r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)\,e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}
and the coordinate function
e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}
evaluated at the current
\beta
, so intuitively it quantifies the extent to which feature
X_{i}
explains what remains in the residual
r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)
. This decomposition holds for all
\beta
and any data distribution with finite second moments.

We note
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
depends on
\lambda
since the residual
r_{\beta,\lambda}
depends on
\lambda
.
As a result, we are unsure whether it is the leading term.
In the next subsection, we will evaluate
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
under the functional ANOVA model and identify regimes where it is
uniformly bounded away from zero
. In these regimes,
-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)/\lambda
is the dominating term in
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
for small
\lambda
, and the interpretation is that the feature
X_{i}
explains the residual and are detectable through the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
.

5.3
Functional ANOVA Model

By definition of the core feature set
S_{*}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S_{*}}]

so all predictive information in
X
is contained in
X_{S_{*}}
.
To quantify how each coordinate
X_{j}
within
S_{*}
contributes—either individually
or through interactions—we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 5.1
.

The variables
(X_{j})_{j\in S_{*}}
in
S_{*}
are mutually independent.

Under this independence assumption
5.1
, the regression function
f(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
admits a unique functional ANOVA decomposition
Stone 1994
:

f(x)=f_{0}+\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\subseteq S_{*}}f_{S}(x_{S}),

where
x_{S}=(x_{j})_{j\in S}
and each
f_{S}
depends only on the coordinates in
S
,
and the component functions
f_{S}
satisfy the orthogonality
conditions:

\mathbb{E}[f_{S}(X_{S})|X_{T}]=0,~~~\forall T\neq S.

In this decomposition, the constant term is
f_{0}=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
, the first-order component is
f_{i}(X_{i})=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]
,
and the second-order componet is
f_{i,j}(X_{i},X_{j})=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i},X_{j}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{j}]
, and so on for higher-order terms.
Each
f_{S}
captures a distinct contribution to the regression function—whether a main effect
from individual features (
|S|=1
) or from interactions among multiple features (
|S|>1
). The decomposition makes explicit the contributions of individual features and their interactions.

In what follows, we use the functional ANOVA model as a stylized framework to understand feature learning.
Although some of the results can be extended to slightly weaker conditions—such as replacing mutual independence with pairwise or approximate independence—we do not pursue that direction here, as it would be a distraction from the main ideas.

5.3.1
Decomposition of Directional Derivative under Functional ANOVA

Under the functional ANOVA model, the directional derivative at those
\beta
with
\beta_{i}=0

D\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]

admits a particularly simple decomposition. The perhaps surprise fact is that the term
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)
in the decomposition
(Theorem
9
) becomes independent of
\lambda
. Lemma
21
offers the key insight.

Lemma 21
.

Suppose
X_{i}
is independent of
(X_{j})_{j\neq i}
. Then, at every
\beta
with
\beta_{i}=0
,

{\rm Cov}\big(r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}\big)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}].

In particular, the right-hand side—and hence the covariance term—is independent of
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\beta_{i}=0
. By definition,

\begin{split}&r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}\\
&=(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}+(\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}]-f_{\beta,\lambda}(\beta\circ X))e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}.\end{split}

Since
X_{i}
is independent of
X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}
, the second term above is independent of
e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}
. Therefore,

{\rm Cov}\big(r_{\beta,\lambda}(X,Y)e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},\,e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}\big)={\rm Cov}\big((Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle},\,e^{2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}\big).

Since the first factor in the covariance is mean-zero, the conclusion follows.
∎

Theorem 10
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\beta_{i}=0
, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
5.1
. Let

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta):=\iiint\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}\right]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

Then

\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)

where
|\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)|\leq C

for some constant
C<\infty
depending only on
M_{X}
,
M_{Y}
, and
M_{\mu}
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Theorem
9
and Lemma
21
.
∎

Theorem
10
refines the earlier decomposition in Theorem
9
by showing that
the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
splits into a dominant term

-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)/\lambda
, and a smaller term of order
1/\sqrt{\lambda}
, which is negligible when

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0
. Indeed, Theorem
10
not only shows that for any
\beta
with
\beta_{i}=0
,

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\lambda\,\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]=-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta),

but also, via the uniform bound
|\mathcal{R}_{i}(\beta,\lambda)|\leq C
, yields the stronger statement

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,\beta_{i}=0}\left|\lambda\,\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]+\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)\right|=0.

It is therefore important to understand when the leading term
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)
is strictly postive.

Lemma 22
.

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)\geq 0
with equality holds if and only if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\cup\{i\}}]=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}]
.

Proof.

We investigate when equality
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=0
holds. Let us denote

Q(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}tq_{t}(\omega)\mu(dt)
.
Since
q_{t}
is
strictly positive and
\mu
is not concentrated at zero, we get
Q(\omega)>0
for all
\omega
. Thus,

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=\iint\left|\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{Q(\omega)}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\,d\omega.

The weights
Q(\omega)/(2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2})
is strictly positive. Thus,
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=0
if and only if

\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]=0~~a.e.~\omega,\zeta.

This implies the characteristic function of the random variable

\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\cup\{i\}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}]

is almost everywhere zero, so the random variable must vanish almost surely.
∎

Therefore, the leading term
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)\cup\{i\}}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}]
; that is, if
X_{i}
helps explain the target
Y
beyond what is already captured by
X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}
.

Remark 9
.

Theorem
10
and Lemma
22
together provide intuitions how the objective
\mathcal{J}
learns relevant features. They show that when
X_{i}
helps explain the target
Y
beyond what is already captured by
X_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}
, then the term
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)
is strictly positive, making the derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
strictly negative for small
\lambda
, thereby pushing the model to activate that coordinate
i
.

5.4
Relevant Feature Recovery

In this section, we provide answers to (
\mathsf{Q4}
)—namely, understand when stationary points of

\mathcal{J}
recover the relevant features. To this end, we assume independence of the feature variables
(Assumption
5.1
), and work under the functional ANOVA model
(section
5.3
)

\mathbb{E}[Y|X]=\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\subseteq S_{*}}f_{S}(X_{S}).

A feature
i\in S_{*}
may contribute to prediction through one of two types of signals:
(i) a
main effect
, meaning the univariate component
f_{{i}}
in the ANOVA expansion is nonzero, i.e.,

f_{i}(X_{i})\not\equiv 0
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
; or (ii) an
interaction
, meaning
i
appears in some higher-order
component
f_{S}
with
|S|\geq 2
and
f_{S}\not\equiv 0
for some
S\ni i
.
In the subsections that follow, we investigate regimes under which stationary points of
\mathcal{J}
recover the corresponding feature.

5.4.1
Recovery of Main Effects

We begin with the case where a relevant feature
i\in S_{*}
contributes through a main effect—that is,
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq 0
. In this case, we show that the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
is uniformly negative over a neighborhood of
\beta
where
\beta_{i}=0
, ensuring the model is driven to activate coordinate
i
.

Theorem 11
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
5.1
. Suppose
i\in S_{*}
has a main effect:
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq 0
. Then, for every compact set
\mathsf{C}\subset\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\beta_{i}=0\}
, we have

\inf_{\beta\in\mathsf{C}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0.

Consequently, there exist constants
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
and all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
,

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\varepsilon.

Remark 10
.

Theorem
11
shows that any feature
i
with a nontrivial main effect induces a uniformly negative directional derivative across compact sets where it is inactive (i.e,
\beta_{i}=0
). This guarantees a descent direction toward activating
X_{i}
, reinforcing the idea that features of main effects are reliably detectable by the objective when
\lambda
is small.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

We note
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)
is not continuous in
\beta
due to the discontinuity of
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
.
To remedy this issue, we define for every
i
, subset
A\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)=\iiint\left|\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

By definition,
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)
when
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)=A
.

We claim that
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)
is lower semicontinuous. Fix any sequence
\beta^{(n)}\to\beta
. For each fixed
(\omega,\zeta,t)
, the inner expectation in the integrand is continuous in
\beta
, so the integrand converges pointwise. Moreover, the integrand is nonnegative. By Fatou’s lemma, we conclude

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta^{(n)})\geq\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta),

so
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}
is lower semicontinuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Next, we show
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)>0
when
i\not\in A
. To see this,
suppose
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)=0
. Then the integrand vanishes almost everywhere,

\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]=0~~a.e.~\omega,\zeta.

This implies that the characteristic function of the random variable

\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])|X_{B\cup\{i\}}]~~~~\text{where}~~~B:=\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)

vanishes almost everywhere, so the random variable must be zero almost surely. However, consider its
inner product with the main effect component
f_{{i}}(X_{i})
. If
i\not\in A
, then

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]|X_{B\cup\{i\}}]\cdot f_{{i}}(X_{i})\right]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])\cdot f_{{i}}(X_{i})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[Y\cdot f_{{i}}(X_{i})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]\cdot f_{{i}}(X_{i})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[f_{{i}}^{2}(X_{i})],\end{split}

where the last equality uses that
\mathbb{E}[f_{i}(X_{i})]=0
and
X_{i}
is independent of
X_{A}
whenever
i\notin A
. Since
f_{i}(X_{i})=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\not\equiv 0
by assumption, we have
\mathbb{E}[f_{i}^{2}(X_{i})]>0
, contradicting the claim that the random variable is zero. Thus,
we have
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)>0
when
i\not\in A
.

We now return to the proof of Theorem
11
. Fix any subset

A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{i\}
. The function
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)
is lower semicontinuous
and strictly positive on the compact set
\mathsf{C}
. By the extreme value theorem, it attains its minimum
on
\mathsf{C}
. Thus, there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{A}>0
such that

\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)\geq\varepsilon_{A}\quad\forall\beta\in\mathsf{C}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be the minimum of all such
\varepsilon_{A}
over subsets
A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{i\}
.

For any
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
, we have
i\notin\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
by assumption, so

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{i,\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}(\beta)\geq\epsilon_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}\geq\epsilon>0.

This shows
\inf_{\beta\in\mathsf{C}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0
, as desired.

Finally, by Theorem
10
, there exists
C<\infty
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
and for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)+C\sqrt{\lambda}.

It follows that for sufficiently small
\lambda>0
, we have

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\epsilon
must hold for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
5.1
. Suppose
i\in S_{*}
has a main effect:
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{i}]\neq 0
.

For every compact subset
\mathsf{C}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for all

, if

Corollary
3
strengthens Corollary
2
by extending the signal-extraction property from the origin
\beta=0
to arbitrary compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Once a main effect exists for a feature
X_{i}
, any directional stationary point that remains within a bounded region must include
X_{i}
for small
\lambda
.

5.4.2
Recovery of Interactions

Next, we consider features
i\in S_{*}
that contribute only through interaction terms—that is,
f_{{i}}\equiv 0
but
f_{S}\not\equiv 0
for some
S\ni i
with
|S|\geq 2
. This implies in particular
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]
. At the origin
\beta=0
, the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(0,\lambda)[e_{i}]
vanishes (Corollary
2
), reflecting that
X_{i}
does not influence the objective at first order when considered independently of other variables. Consequently, learning such features requires moving away from the origin to parameter configurations of
\beta
that capture their joint contribution.

We show that once the other variables in an interaction set
S
are sufficiently active, the objective induces a descent direction along coordinate
i
. Specifically, we show that the directional derivative
\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]
is uniformly negative over a neighborhood of
\beta
where
\beta_{i}=0
, and
\beta_{j}
remains bounded away from zero for all
j\in S\setminus\{i\}
. The interpretation is that the model is driven to activate coordinate
i
once its interaction partners are already represented in the current parameter configuration.

Theorem 12
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
5.1
. Let
i\in S\subset S_{*}
where
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathsf{C}\subset\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\beta_{i}=0\text{ and }\beta_{j}>0\ \text{for all }j\in S\setminus\{i\}\right\}
. Then,

\inf_{\beta\in\mathsf{C}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0.

Consequently, there exist constants
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
and all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
,

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\varepsilon.

Remark 11
.

Theorem
12
shows that a feature
i
involved interactions with other variables
X_{S\backslash\{i\}}
induces a uniformly negative directional derivative over compact sets where
\beta_{i}=0
and the other coordinates in
S\setminus\{i\}
are active. This ensures a descent direction along
e_{i}
, and such features can be learned through coordinated activation of their interaction partners when
\lambda
is small.

Mathematically, Theorem
12
generalizes Theorem
11
: when
S=\{i\}
, the interaction reduces to a main effect and the condition on
S\setminus\{i\}
is vacuous. Conceptually, the key distinction is that main effects are identifiable from first-order variation already at
\beta=0
, whereas interaction effects become identifiable once the other variables in
S
are sufficiently active.

Proof of Theorem
12
.

We define
for every
i
, subset
A\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)=\iiint\left|\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta\cdot tq_{t}(\omega)\,d\omega\mu(dt).

By definition,
\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)
when
\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)=A
. Also,

\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)
is lower semicontinuous in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We show
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)>0
when
i\not\in A
and
S\backslash\{i\}\subset\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
. To see this,
suppose
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)=0
. Then

\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])e^{-2\pi i\langle\omega,\beta\circ X\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\zeta X_{i}}]=0~~a.e.~\omega,\zeta.

This implies that the characteristic function of the random variable

\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])|X_{B\cup\{i\}}]~~~~\text{where}~~~B:=\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)

vanishes almost everywhere, so the random variable must be zero almost surely. However, consider its
inner product with
g(X_{S}):=\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]
. If
i\not\in A
and
S\backslash\{i\}\subset B
, then

S\subset B\cup\{i\}
, and

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]|X_{B\cup\{i\}}]\cdot g(X_{S})\right]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}])\cdot g(X_{S})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[Y\cdot g(X_{S})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]\cdot g(X_{S})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(X_{S})],\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the orthogonality of ANOVA components:

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{A}]\cdot g(X_{S})]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{T\subset A}f_{T}(X_{T})\cdot\sum_{i\in W,W\subset S}f_{W}(X_{W})]=0.

This yields a contradiction, since
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(X_{S})]>0
by assumption. Therefore,
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)>0
when
i\not\in A
and
S\backslash\{i\}\subset\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
.

We now return to the proof of Theorem
12
. Fix any subset
A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{i\}
. By construction of
\mathsf{C}
, we have
\beta_{i}=0
and
S\setminus\{i\}\subset\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
. Therefore, the preceding argument apply, and we conclude that
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)>0
for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{N}_{i,A}
is lower semicontinuous, and
\mathsf{C}
is compact, it attains its minimum on
\mathsf{C}
. Thus, there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{A}>0
such that

\mathcal{N}_{i,A}(\beta)\geq\varepsilon_{A}\quad\forall\beta\in\mathsf{C}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be the minimum of all such
\varepsilon_{A}
over subsets
A\subset\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\{i\}
.

For any
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
, we have
i\notin\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)
by assumption, so

\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)=\mathcal{N}_{i,\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}(\beta)\geq\epsilon_{\mathop{\rm supp}(\beta)}\geq\epsilon>0.

This shows
\inf_{\beta\in\mathsf{C}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)>0
, as desired.

Finally, by Theorem
10
, there exists
C<\infty
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
and for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\mathcal{M}_{i}(\beta)+C\sqrt{\lambda}.

It follows that for sufficiently small
\lambda>0
, we have

\lambda\cdot\mathrm{D}\mathcal{J}(\beta,\lambda)[e_{i}]\leq-\epsilon
must hold for all
\beta\in\mathsf{C}
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
5.1
.
Let
i\in S\subset S_{*}
with
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]
, and let
\mathsf{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact set such that

\beta_{j}>0\quad\text{for all }j\in S\setminus\{i\},\quad\text{for all }\beta\in\mathsf{C}.

Then there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
, if
\beta_{\dagger}

4
complements Corollary

by extending the signal-extraction guarantee to nonlinear interaction effects. Specifically, once an interaction involving
X_{i}
is present—that is,
\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S}]\neq\mathbb{E}[Y|X_{S\backslash\{i\}}]

6
Open Problems

We list some open questions which are important for theoretical or numerical development of
the compositional kernel model.

•

(Dynamical problem)
This paper has focused on the statics of the variational problem, but in practice one needs iterative schemes to minimize the nonconvex objective
\mathcal{J}
. Important questions concern the design of initialization and the choice of update dynamics. For example, our analysis shows that when predictive features contribute only through interactions,
\beta=0
is always a stationary point. This indicates the importance of carefully chosen initializations to ensure recovery of the true signals.

•

(Finite sample rates)
Our analysis is at the population level; in practice we only have access to finite sample data. How many samples suffice to guarantee that the same variable selection results hold with high probability?

•

(Generalization error)
In the regimes where the coordinates of
X
are independent, selecting the right features is a necessary prerequisite for good generalization. How do our population-level
variable selection
results translate into guarantees on finite sample
generalization error
? Does an
\ell_{1}
norm penalization on
\beta
help reduce sample complexity and improve generalization?

•

(Dependence)
In the context of predictive feature recovery, our results assume independence between covariates. As explained earlier, this independence is mainly used for attributing prediction signals to individual features and feature subsets. Extending the analysis to dependent designs is particularly interesting.

•

(Scale)
This paper discusses the problem of
feature learning
through the lens of
variable selection
. However, we do not analyze what the magnitude of
\beta
means. Scale plays a crucial role in trading-off approximation and regularization, and the real problem is to learn all the intrinsic scales of the data, a point being raised in the related work on
f(UX)
model
Li and Ruan 2025a
. Can the
\beta
-parameterized model learn such scales automatically?

•

(Nonsmooth kernels and the
f(UX)
context) This paper has demonstrated the benefit of using coordinate-wise
\ell_{1}
-type kernels, such as the Laplace kernel
e^{-\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}}
, for sparse feature recovery. A natural next question is whether nonsmooth but rotationally invariant kernels, such as the rotational version of the Laplace kernel
e^{-\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}}
, can aid recovery of low-dimensional subspaces in the related
f(UX)
context.
