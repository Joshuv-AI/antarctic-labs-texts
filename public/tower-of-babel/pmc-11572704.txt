Photon Upconversion of Defect-Bound Excitons in hBN-Encapsulated MoS2 Monolayer
Authors: Ewa Żuberek, Justyna Olejnik, Joerg Debus, Ching-Hwa Ho, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Leszek Bryja, Joanna Jadczak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtomic defects associated with vacancies in two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenide monolayers efficiently trap charged carriers and strongly localize excitons. Defects in semiconducting monolayers are seldomly utilized for enhancing optical phenomena, although they may provide resonant intermediate states within the energy band gap for applications with multiphoton excitations, like highly efficient and thermally robust photon upconversion. In an MoS2 monolayer encapsulated by hBN with high defect and resident electron densities, we observe an upconversion of localized exciton (XL) emission with a huge energy gain of up to 290 meV. The upconverted XL emission is robust up to temperatures of about 120 K and exhibits a sublinear or a nearly linear laser power dependence for the energy gain of about 100 meV and above 200 meV, respectively. The upconversion mechanism is explained by a cooperative energy transfer process between the photocreated and resident electrons, in which hybridized pairs of single sulfur vacancies likely act as real intermediate states. Additionally, we find a weak upconversion of the neutral exciton photoluminescence with an energy gain of about 350 meV for quasi-resonant excitation of the XL exciton. It is attributed to a two-step, two-photon absorption.

Introduction

Atomically thin transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) possess direct band gaps in the visible and near-infrared spectral range at the K– and K+ points of the Brillouin zone.1 Their two-dimensional (2D) nature combined with a reduced dielectric screening of the Coulomb interaction allows for the formation of both tightly bound excitons with binding energies of hundreds of meV as well as higher-order excitonic complexes with binding energies of tens of meV.2−12 Interlayer van der Waals interactions realize the combination of these semiconducting monolayers with other 2D materials and vertical heterostructures with tunable optoelectronic properties, making them interesting for electronic devices or nanophotonics.13 Also, recent advances in assembling high-quality TMDC monolayers surrounded by hBN provided thorough insights into optoelectronic properties of excitonic complexes as well as exciton–exciton and exciton–phonon interactions revealed by optical spectroscopy.14−17 However, TMDC monolayers prepared by mechanical exfoliation may have a native defect density of about 1013 cm–2, which usually reduces the performance of their optoelectronic properties. A reduction in the defect density requires effective passivation and control methods.18 These atomic defects mainly comprise chalcogen vacancies,18−20 which form gap states and alter the concentration of residual carriers. Hence, in most TMDC monolayers a broad subgap emission appears at low temperatures about several hundred meV below the neutral exciton.21,22 This subgap luminescence is often called L-band whose intensity typically grows with increasing defect density and is observed over a wide range of temperatures, even at room temperature,23 exhibiting long lifetimes.22,24

Currently, atomic defects in TMDC monolayers can be deliberately generated and controlled by in vacuo annealing,19 electron,25 focused X-ray,26 and ion beam irradiation. Furthermore, recent studies combining controlled defect engineering with optical spectroscopy as well as atomic imaging and ab initio theory identified the optical signature of pristine chalcogen vacancies in hBN/MoS2/hBN.19,20 Hitherto, defects in TMDC monolayers have seldom been utilized for enhancing optical phenomena. Because of their inherent states within the band gap of the host material they may provide resonant intermediate states in, for example, two-photon absorption processes27 and photon upconversion (UPC).12 In particular, they may improve the efficiency of photon UPC remarkably while optically exciting the TMDC monolayer within its nominal transparent window. This resonant defect-state enhancement of the UPC may be highly promising also for low-excitation-intensity bioimaging applications28,29 with negligible tissue damaging and for highly efficient energy harvesting devices.30,31

Here, we demonstrate the upconversion of photoluminescence (PL) of a defect-bound (localized) exciton (XL) in an MoS2 monolayer encapsulated by hBN. The PL upconversion possesses a huge energy gain of about 292 meV and is robust up to temperatures of 120 K. It is observed only for hBN/MoS2/hBN heterostructures in which the XL PL dominates the neutral exciton emission and the concentrations of both defect states and resident electrons are high. The evaluation of the UPC PL as a function of the excitation energy, incident laser power, and temperature reveals that the upconversion mechanism is given by a cooperative energy transfer process between the photocreated and a resident electron, whereby hybridized pairs of single sulfur vacancies act as real intermediate states. This spin and momentum conserving energy transfer involving resonantly shallow and deep defect states in the MoS2 monolayer represents a highly efficient and thermally robust type of photon upconversion.

Methods

MoS2 crystals were grown by a chemical vapor transport technique. Prior to the crystal growth, the powdered compounds were prepared from the elements Mo (purity: 99.99%) and S (99.999%) by reaction at 1000 °C for 10 days in quartz ampules. The mixture was slowly heated to 1000 °C. The chemical transport was achieved with I2 as the transport agent having a concentration of about 5 mg/cm3. The growth temperature was gradually changed from 1030 to 980 °C, with a temperature gradient of 3 °C/cm and a growth time of 20 days. The crystals had the shape of thin-layered plates with thicknesses and surface areas ranging from 20 to 1000 μm and from 20 to 100 mm2, respectively.

The van der Waals hBN-encapsulated MoS2 heterostructures were prepared by using high-purity hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) and Si substrates (300 nm SiO2). The monolayers were mechanically exfoliated from the MoS2 bulk crystals using the deterministic all-dry stamping method, similar to the technique reported by Castellanos-Gomez et al.32 An MoS2 monolayer and hBN crystals were first exfoliated on a flexible PDMS gel-film stamp rigidly attached to a glass slide. In the hBN-encapsulated structures s1 and s2 the thicknesses of the hBN flakes were about 120 nm for the bottom layer and about 5 nm for the top layer, respectively. In the hBN-encapsulated structures s3 and s4 the thicknesses of the hBN flakes were about 15 nm for the bottom layer and about 5 nm for the top layer, respectively. During the transfer process, the substrate and the stamp were placed below an optical microscope equipped with an xyz-positioning stage. A long-working-distance microscope objective enabled us to locate and deterministically transfer selected flakes to the substrate. After each transfer step the sample was heated to about 180 °C for 30 min in air. After adding the last layer to the sample, a final thermal annealing was performed in air for 30 min at 180 °C (structure s1). Then, after at least 60 min, the structures s2, s3, and s4 were annealed at 220, 420, and 550 °C, respectively. The characterization of an exemplary hBN/MoS2/hBN structure by Raman spectroscopy and room temperature PL measurements at each stage of preparation is presented in the Supporting Information.

For the PL and UPC PLE experiments, the samples were mounted on the coldfinger of a nonvibrating closed-cycle helium cryostat, in which the temperature could be varied from 7 to 350 K. The regular PL was excited by the second harmonic 532 nm (2.33 eV) of a continuous-wave single-mode Nd:YAG laser. The UPC PLE was performed by a continuous-wave Ti:sapphire laser whose emission wavelength was tuned in the range from 740 to 850 nm. The laser beam was focused on the sample under normal incidence using a high-resolution, long-working distance (10 mm) 50× microscope objective (NA = 0.65). The diameter of the excitation spot was about 1 μm. The emission from the sample was collected by the same microscope objective and was analyzed with a 0.5 m focal length spectrometer equipped with a 600 lines/mm grating and a Peltier-cooled charged-coupled-device Si camera. The RC spectrum was measured at the same setup using a filament lamp as a light source. To eliminate the scattered laser light, a set of short- and long-pass edged filters was used.

Results and Discussion

MoS2 monolayers encapsulated in hBN were annealed at four different temperatures for 30 min in order to observe the photoluminescence of the defect-bound exciton XL. A detailed description of the structures and their characterization can be found in the Methods section and in the Supporting Information, respectively. In Figure 1a, intensity normalized PL spectra are shown for the four hBN-encapsulated MoS2 monolayers s1 (180 °C), s2 (220 °C), s3 (420 °C), and s4 (550 °C). The spectra were measured at room temperature and were excited nonresonantly by laser light having an energy of 2.33 eV. For all structures, we observe PL lines assigned to the neutral exciton states XA and XB.1,5 Since the energy position of the XA PL slightly changes from flake to flake between 1.862 and 1.880 eV at 295 K, the neutral exciton energy EXA is chosen as the reference value. Accordingly, the energy difference E – EXA is depicted as the energy scale in each spectrum. For sample s4 annealed at 550 °C, the PL spectrum additionally contains a prominent low-energy emission peak. It likely originates from defect-induced states within the MoS2 band gap and is labeled by XL. Figure 1b presents the corresponding PL spectra of the heterostructures recorded at low temperature T = 7 K. As clearly seen for the sample s4, the PL intensity of XL significantly exceeds that of XA. It manifests itself in the PL intensity ratio of both lines given by IXL/IXA = 36.7. For the sample s3 this ratio (13.5) is also significant, whereas for structures s1 and s2 it is less than 1. The low values are primarily due to the dominant intensity of the X emission, which is characteristic for a low concentration of resident electrons in the MoS2 monolayer. In recent studies, it was shown that the significant electron concentration in TMDC monolayers is mainly traced back to a charge transfer from the SiO2/Si substrate5,7,16,17 as well as to defects acting as donors or acceptors33,34 leading to significant n- or p-doping, respectively. The bottom hBN layers used in hBN-encapsulated structures act as barriers for charged carriers, and the effectiveness of such a barrier depends on hBN layer thickness.35 Hence, our structures are divided qualitatively into two groups: The first group consists of the structures s1 and s2 having a low defect density and low electron concentration (the thicknesses of the bottom hBN flakes are about 120 nm; see the Methods section). The second group includes the structures s3 and s4 with a high defect density and a relatively high electron concentration (the thicknesses of the bottom hBN flakes are about 15 nm; see the Methods section). The level of the n-doping is also reflected in the complementary reflectance contrast (RC) spectra, which are shown in Figure 1c for the structures s2 and s4. At a relatively high 2D electron concentration (s4, green line) the negative trion (T) resonance is well resolved, while for a low electron density (sample s2) the X feature dominates over the negligible T resonance. Furthermore, for comparing the PL spectra of the structures studied at room temperature with recent results on defect-induced PL in annealed MoS2 monolayers reported in ref (23), we estimate that the concentration of defects in our structures (from s1 to s4) increases from the order of 1013 to 1014 cm–2.

Let us now thoroughly characterize the XL emission of structure s4 which demonstrates the highest defect and resident electron concentrations among the structures studied. Figure 2a compares the low-temperature PL and RC spectra. The exciton and negative trion resonances are clearly distinguishable in the RC spectrum at about 1.94 and 1.91 eV, respectively, whereas the XL resonance is not observed likely due to its localized nature and, correspondingly, small density of states. Instead, the PL of XL is highly pronounced at about 1.74 eV.

(c) Laser power dependence of the XL PL for Eexc = 2.33 eV (green points) and Eexc = 1.80 eV (red points). (d) Temperature evolution of normalized PL spectra of sample s4. Furthermore, Figure 2b presents variations in the XL line emission intensity for scanning the incident photon energy Eexc through the XA resonance. The excitation energy range is indicated by the red arrows in the PL spectrum shown in panel (a). The intensity of the XL emission is maximum when the XA exciton is resonantly excited at about 1.93 eV. It underlines that the XL PL is closely linked to the neutral intravalley exciton since it diffuses within the MoS2 monolayer and becomes bound to a defect after optical excitation. The evolution of the integrated XL PL as a function of the laser power P is given in panel (c) using a double-logarithmic presentation. For an excitation energy below (red points) and above (green points) the XA resonance, the integrated PL intensity demonstrates sublinear dependence on the laser power: The respective slopes α are evaluated to be 0.69 and 0.78 for the excitation energy of 2.33 and 1.8 eV, respectively. Remarkably, for high excitation powers, we observe a saturating behavior, which is expected when the exciton generation rate exceeds the recombination rate of the states responsible for the XL. Because of the typically long lifetime of these energetically deep-lying localized excitons their emission intensity goes in saturation for reaching high laser powers.

Figure 2d depicts the temperature evolution of normalized PL spectra from 7 to 260 K. The XL band dominates the XA peak over the entire temperature range. Besides the thermally induced broadening and red-shift of the PL lines, the XL PL intensity as a function of the inverse temperature is presented in Figure 2e in terms of an Arrhenius plot. The XL PL intensity is enhanced starting from 7 K and going to about 40 K (from right to left in the plot). On the contrary, the intensity of the XA PL solely decreases with rising temperature. Above 40 K, the XL PL intensity gradually decreases. We use a modified biexponential model36 for describing the temperature dependence of the XL intensity: I(T) = I0(1 + A1e–E1/kBT)/(1 + A2e–E2/kBT) with proportionality constants A1 and A2. We obtain the activation energy E1 of about (3 ± 1) meV, while the deactivation energy E2 amounts to (51.5 ± 3.8) meV.

Let us consider different scenarios to explain the origins of energies E1 and E2 and, in turn, the trapping of carriers and thermal dissociation of the XL complex. The energetic position of the XL-related defect states within the band gap of the MoS2 monolayer or below the conduction band (CB) edge plays an important role in identifying essential defects. Considering a binding energy of the neutral A exciton of about 220 meV37,38 in combination with the XL PL energy difference of about 200 meV (and 270 meV for the weak low-energy peak) with respect to the XA resonance, the defect states relevant for XL lie about 420 meV (490 meV) below the CB. On the basis of ref (39) and especially ref (20) a single sulfur vacancy (VS), double sulfur vacancy, nearest-neighboring single sulfur vacancy pairs (VSVS) in the same and different planes, and a single hydrogen atom filling a single sulfur vacancy (HS) have states at this energy split by spin–orbit coupling. As will be discussed later, the nearest-neighboring single sulfur vacancy pairs play the major role in the XL emission; hence, we propose here tentatively that the XL emission is caused by a recombination of an exciton bound to such a hybridized pair of single sulfur vacancies. The initial intensity increase of XL (with an activation energy of 3 meV) is likely related either to a dissociation of very shallow bound carriers originating likely from the antisite defect S2Mo or to a thermally activated transfer from carriers at energetically close lying defects to VSVS so that, in both cases, the XL PL is enhanced subsequently. By comparison, the deactivation energy E2 is most probably attributed to a dissociation of XL into a highly long-living localized state related to sulfur or hydrogen defects.

Theoretical predictions show that the binding energy of a small localized exciton complex D+X (i.e., D+ + e– + h+) defined with respect to dissociation into the most energetically favorable products such as neutral donor D0 and a free hole h+ is only 7.2 meV.3 However, the energy required to separate a single exciton from the D+X is significantly higher ranging from 60 to 100 meV.3,40 It is worth noting that an ionized (bare) donor D+ represents a positive point charge, which acts as a strong positive Coulomb center. In an n-type structure, it can bind additional surrounding electrons to become a D– = D+ + 2e–. This optically inactive object can further capture a photoexcited electron–hole pair to become a radiative bound state DX– = (D–)X = D+ + 3e– + h+. According to theoretical modeling in ref (3), the binding energy of such a complex dissociating into an exciton and a negative donor ion (D–) is 84.4 meV, which is about 30 meV higher than the evaluated value of E2. However, the dissociation of the DX– into the neutral donor and trion likely requires a smaller energy of about 50 meV. For a smaller excitonic complex D0X = D+ + 2e– + h+, the dissociation into the exciton and neutral donor requires 32.4 meV,3 which is smaller than the evaluated E2. Nevertheless, in the case of the complex D+XX = D+ + 2e– + 2h+, the binding energy defined with respect to the dissociation into the most energetically favorable products such as D0X and a free h+ is 51.8 meV,3 which resembles the evaluated deactivation energy of E2.

We now focus on the upconversion of the XL emission in the hBN encapsulated MoS2 monolayer demonstrating its dependence on variations in the excitation energy within the transparency window of MoS2, laser power, and temperature. We will present the data obtained for the sample s4, while for the other investigated heterostructures we did not observe the upconverted XL emission. Figure 3a illustrates the regular PL spectrum (left scale, black curve only) and UPC PL spectra (right scale) measured for Eexc = 1.586, 1.514, and 1.462 eV. Additionally, in Figure 3b the PL spectrum and UPC PL spectra are compared with each other for the excitation energies of 1.725, 1.683, and 1.583 eV over a wide spectral range with an enlargement of the detection region of the neutral exciton (inset). Interestingly, we also observe a relatively weak upconversion of the XA emission. The UPC PL spectra shown in Figure 3b were acquired by a factor of 10 longer than that depicted in Figure 3a. The intensity of the upconverted XA PL is highest for tuning the laser excitation energy to the XL resonance. The power-dependent evolution of the integrated XA UPC intensity is described by a nearly linear slope of α = 1.20 ± 0.04 (see Figure S4). Figure. 3c displays the integrated XL UPC PL as a function of the excitation energy detuned from −300 to −100 meV below the XL PL peak position. The upconverted PL intensity of XL gradually rises from −300 to −219 meV; then its energy dependence is almost constant with characteristic steps in the range from −219 to −150 meV. Finally, the upconverted PL intensity of XL rises from −150 to −100 meV. The absolute UPC energy gain is given by the absolute energy difference |Eexc – EXL|. As seen from Figure 3c, it reaches a maximum value of 290 meV. It is worthwhile to mention that the energy gain for the upconverted XA emission takes values of about 350 meV; however, the upconverted intensity is negligibly low.

We further study the dependence of the upconverted XL PL on the incident laser power P for the excitation energies of 1.641, 1.540, and 1.499 eV, which correspond to energy gains of about 104, 205, and 246 meV, respectively. Typical UPC PL spectra recorded for different P and for Eexc = 1.641 eV are depicted in Figure 3d, while in Figure 3e the integrated UPC PL for all the for excitation energies is shown as a function of P using a double-logarithmic presentation. The power-dependent evolution of the integrated XL UPC intensity for Eexc = 1.641 eV is described by a sublinear slope of α = 0.76 ± 0.02, which resembles the slope of the regular PL intensity excited at 1.8 eV (see Figure 2c). Interestingly, the upconverted XL emission also slightly tends to saturate, as indicated by the data point taken at the highest laser power. On the other hand, the power-dependent evolution of the integrated XL UPC intensity for Eexc = 1.549 eV and Eexc = 1.499 eV is described by nearly linear slopes of α = 1.12 ± 0.03 and α = 1.18 ± 0.05, respectively. It is noteworthy that a nearly linear dependence on the excitation power corresponds to the energy gain for which the UPC intensity dependence reveals characteristic steps (see Figure 3c).

In order to gain further insight into the nature of the XL upconversion mechanism, we perform temperature-dependent UPC PL measurements. Figure 4a–d provides an overview of the integrated intensity of the upconverted XL emission for different temperatures ranging from 7 to 120 K obtained at two excitation energies. Figures 4a and 4c present typical UPC PL spectra recorded for low (10 mW) and high (40 mW) laser power excited at the energies 1.626 and 1.550 eV, respectively. Changes in the integrated XL UPC emission are depicted by Arrhenius plots in Figures 4b and 4d. The intensity of the upconverted XL emission decreases with a rising temperature. Its thermal behavior is described by a biexponential function similar to that discussed previously. The corresponding Arrhenius-like modeling (red dashed curves) yields each two deactivation energies: for Eexc = 1.626 eV we obtain E1* = 1.5 meV and E2* = 42 meV, while for Eexc = 1.550 eV the energy values slightly change to E1* = 3.9 meV and E2* = 37 meV; the absolute errors amount to ΔE1* ≈ 0.5 meV and ΔE2* ≈ 3 meV. The first deactivation energies characteristic for T < 40 K resemble the values E1 obtained for the temperature dependence of the regular XL PL intensity. However, the UPC PL intensity decreases at low temperatures in contrast to the PL. The second deactivation energy lies about 10 meV below E2 but is comparable to the dissociation energy of the complex D0X.3

Let us summarize the experimental observations for the upconverted XL emission: (a) Only the hBN-encapsulated MoS2 monolayer with high defect and resident electron densities exhibits a pronounced PL and UPC emission of the defect-bound exciton XL. (b) In addition to the UPC PL of XL the emission of the neutral exciton XA is weakly upconverted. (c) The absolute maximum energy gain of the XL UPC PL amounts to approximately 290 meV. (d) The PL intensity is scaled sublinearly with increasing incident laser power. (e) The UPC PL intensities are scaled sublinearly or nearly linearly with increasing incident laser power for the energy gain of about 100 meV and above 200 meV, respectively. (f) The XL UPC PL intensity reduces with rising temperature up to 120 K rather independently of the laser power and excitation energy.

On the basis of these experimental results, we now discuss a possible mechanism leading to the upconversion of the XL PL. The high UPC energy gain of 290 meV and the weakening of the UPC PL intensity with increasing temperature rule out both a single-phonon-assisted as well as a multiple-phonon-mediated UPC process. The latter high-order process would be highly inefficient in lifting the electron from, e.g., a deep defect state within the band gap to the XL-related state, while the former would require a vibrational mode with an energy of about 2300 cm–1 (0.29 eV). In both cases one would expect an enhancement in the UPC PL intensity with rising temperature, which is related to the increasing phonon population,14,17,41 leading to a high rate of phonon-mediated electron scattering. Alternatively, a two-photon absorption, which transfers the photocreated electron to the conduction band of MoS2, with a subsequent relaxation of the electron to a energetically low-lying defect state would demonstrate a superlinear or quadratic dependence on the incident laser power.

The sublinear laser power behavior of the normal and upconverted XL PL (for energy gain of about 100 meV) suggests a single-photon mechanism in which the emissive state becomes efficiently saturated. We suggest as the UPC mechanism an efficient cooperative UPC energy transfer by a resident electron bound to a defect, likely originating from a sulfur vacancy (see Supplementary Notes 1 and 2). The previously discussed sulfur-related vacancy states probably possess long lifetimes so that they are easily saturated through optical excitation. The energy transfer depends significantly on the distance between the vacancies and their spectral overlap.42−44 Since only in sample s4 the defect and resident electron densities are high, the sulfur vacancies are sufficiently close, and in turn, the XL PL becomes upconverted. Increasing the temperature leads to an enlargement of the distances between them and a reduction in the UPC XL PL intensity. Moreover, with increasing temperature, the resident electron diffuses and becomes captured to another spatially and energetically close-lying defect state; hence, this nonradiative thermally driven channel decreases the efficiency of the XL UPC process.

In the context of the temperature evolution of the regular XL PL, we figured out that the defect state relevant for XL lies approximately 420 meV below the edge of the conduction band ECB. According to the measurements and calculations performed for an MoS2 monolayer in ref (20), we assign the XL emission to the recombination of an exciton bound to a hybridized pair of single sulfur vacancies which are positioned at different planes (VSVS)DP. The trap energy levels corresponding to the (VSVS)DP lie 372 or 499 meV below the conduction band.20 For the observed UPC of the XL emission, the excitation energy Eexc is varied between 290 and 100 meV below the XL PL peak. Accordingly, states from about 710 to 520 meV below ECB are optically addressed. Here, the two defect states of nearest-neighboring single sulfur vacancy pairs in the same plane (VSVS)SP located at 612 and 686 meV below the ECB come into play,20 for high UPC energy gains. For low energy gains, also, the excitation of single sulfur vacancies is possible. The energies of the hybridized pair states depend on the distance between the neighboring sulfur vacancies and an increasing distance between the (VSVS) pairs leads to a convergence of the defect levels into levels resembling the states of VS. These corresponding states are energetically closer (about 600 to 480 meV) to ECB. This thermally driven energy shift in the vacancy states may also affect the UPC efficiency of XL emission.

Following this arrangement of sulfur vacancy states, the UPC mechanism is initiated by the excitation of an electron from the valence band (VB) to the sulfur vacancy pair state (VSVS)SP, as shown in Figure 5. This electron interacts with a neighboring resident electron in terms of a cooperative energy transfer; under spin, momentum, and energy conservation, the photocreated (resident) electron is lifted to a higher (lower) lying (VSVS)DP state. Finally, the photoelectron recombines with the hole in the VB to provide the XL emission. The overall upconversion efficiency is determined by the excitation cross sections, both from the ground state to the intermediate state given by sulfur vacancy pairs and from the intermediate state to the final state, as well as by the (VSVS) density and lifetime. Depending on its lifetime, the UPC PL intensity may exhibit a power dependence with a slope varying between 1 and 2.45 The sublinear dependence observed here for the smaller energy gain of 100 meV is due to the fact that the involved pair states are long-lived, leading to an efficient saturation by less than one incident photon.

Scheme of the UPC mechanism for the XL emission based on a cooperative energy transfer process involving sulfur vacancy pair states and a resident electron.

The observation of the weak upconverted XA PL for laser excitation energies nearly resonant with XL may be explained by a two-step two-photon absorption process,45 where the intermediate state is real and involves a sulfur vacancy state. Accordingly, the first photon excites a sulfur vacancy state, and the second photon lifts the electron to the conduction band. After a nonradiative energy relaxation, the electron becomes Coulomb-bound with the initially photogenerated hole resulting in the XA exciton.

Conclusions

We demonstrate the photonic upconversion of an exciton XL bound to a hybridized pair of single sulfur vacancies positioned at different atomic planes. The UPC energy gain takes large values from 100 to 292 meV, indicating that intermediate states are resonantly involved in the UPC process, which lie between 520 and 710 meV below the conduction band edge. We propose that the UPC mechanism is initiated by an electron excited from the valence band to a state of paired sulfur vacancies lying in the same plane. The photoelectron and a neighboring resident electron interact with each other in terms of a cooperative energy transfer involving states of paired sulfur vacancies lying in different planes. The final recombination of the energetically raised photoelectron with the valence band photohole yields the XL emission. The UPC emission depends sublinearly on the power of the incident laser light which underlines the longevity of the intermediate UPC states; thus, the pairs of sulfur vacancies become efficiently saturated. Moreover, the UPC of the XL emission is observed only in hBN/MoS2/hBN heterostructures in which the concentrations of both defect states as well as resident electrons are relatively high. It highlights that both resident electrons as well as the cooperative energy transfer which is particularly effective at short distances between the sulfur vacancies play the major role in the UPC process. Furthermore, the upconverted XL emission exhibits biexponential temperature behavior with two characteristic deactivation energies of about 2.5 and 40 meV. These energies are assigned to the thermally activated diffusion and capture of the resident and photocreated electrons to spatially and energetically close-lying vacancy states yielding a degradation of the UPC emission. Our results provide further insights into the nature of localized excitons in TMDC monolayers and notably extend the current discussion about atomic defects and their benefits in highly efficient and thermally robust photon upconversion.
