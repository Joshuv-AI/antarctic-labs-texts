Optometrists’ practice of falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults: A mixed methods systematic review
Authors: Si Ye Lee, Khyber Alam, Jason Charng, Hamed Niyazmand, Jingyi Chen, Anne-Marie Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity optometrists are primary eye care professionals who are well positioned to provide assessment and treatment for vision-related falls prevention. The aim of this mixed methods review was to synthesise the best available evidence for community optometrists’ practice of falls prevention assessment and treatment for community dwelling older adults.MethodsA mixed methods systematic review utilised the convergent integrated approach in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines and was conducted following a published protocol. Four databases, Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus and CINAHL Complete, and grey literature were searched from January 1980 to November 2025. Quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods studies that investigated community optometrists’ practice of falls prevention screening, assessments, treatment or providing falls prevention advice to community dwelling older adults were eligible for inclusion. Two independent reviewers utilised standardised methods to search, screen, and code the studies included. Methodological quality of studies was assessed by two independent reviewers using JBI critical appraisal tools. Data synthesis followed the JBI convergent integrated approach.ResultsFrom 3853 articles screened, 11 articles (seven quantitative, one qualitative and three mixed methods studies) and four reports met the inclusion criteria. Two analytical themes were identified: (i) there was limited evidence that optometrists practiced falls risk screening and assessment, although they were aware and knowledgeable about the association between vision impairments and falls; (ii) there was sparse evidence for optometrists providing falls prevention treatment. There was limited evidence that optometrists were conducting key ocular assessments relevant for identifying falls risk such as depth perception or contrast sensitivity, and scant evidence of falls screening. There was some evidence for the practice of tailored spectacle prescription, but scant evidence for providing falls prevention advice. There was evidence for optometrists providing effective falls prevention treatment within a multidisciplinary team, but no evidence that identified if optometrists referred patients to other health professionals for falls prevention care.ConclusionsFurther research that investigates how community optometrists as primary care health professionals can systematically practice falls prevention care is urgently required.PROSPERO registration numberCRD 42024539668.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07760-9.

Background

The increasing global ageing of the population means that falls among older adults are a socioeconomic problem that demands urgent attention. Globally, around 30% of community dwelling older adults (aged ≥ 65 years old) experience a fall annually [1], and 10% of older adults experience two or more falls annually [2]. These falls contribute to over 38 million disability adjusted life years (DALYs) lost [3, 4]. Recently, international collaborators developed the World Guidelines for Falls Prevention (WFG). The WFG provide falls prevention recommendations to guide health professionals in incorporating evidence-based strategies for falls prevention with the strength of recommendations following a modified Grading of Recommendations, Assessments, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [3, 5]. The GRADE system is a systematic framework to assess the quality of the evidence ranging from high, moderate, low and very low [5]. The WFG recommend routine falls screening of older adults by healthcare providers and that healthcare professionals take a multidisciplinary approach to targeted falls prevention assessment and treatment [3].

Vision interventions recommended by the WFG identify eye care professionals as pivotal healthcare professionals who can effectively incorporate falls prevention assessment and treatment to manage falls risk in older adults [3]. The WFG recommend that vision assessment and treatment should be a component of multifactorial falls risk treatment [3]. Impaired vision is an independent risk factor for falls in community dwelling older adults [6, 7]. Older adults who have less than 6/12 visual acuity (reported as 68.7% in a large multi-national study), have significantly increased falls and recurrent falls compared to older adults with normal vision [8]. The guidelines further recommend that all health professionals routinely ask older adults about falls during consultations (GRADE: 1 A evidence) [3]. Multiprofessional, multifactorial assessment is recommended for community dwelling older adults identified to be at high risk of falling (GRADE: 1B evidence).

The WFG recommend including vision management as a component of falls prevention assessment and treatment, although the evidence for specific vision related assessments and interventions remains limited, and recommendations rely on expert consensus (GRADE: E evidence) [3]. These recommendations for vision comprise of referrals for early cataract surgery [9], prescribing single vision spectacles for outdoor activities [10] and counselling older adults about the short term increased falls risk associated with new spectacle prescriptions [3, 11]. However, some vision interventions have been found to show efficacy in large randomised trials [10–12] and recent Australian falls prevention guidelines for community dwelling older adults [13] recommend timely referral for cataract surgery as Level 1 A, indicating a strong recommendation with a high level of evidence and tailoring of spectacle prescription by limiting the changes to the prescription where possible as Level 2B, indicating a weak or conditional recommendation with an intermediate level of evidence.

To enact practice in keeping with these recent guidelines, community optometrists are well positioned to potentially play an important public health role in falls prevention because multiple national guidelines for vision health recommend that adults aged ≥ 65 years have an annual vision assessment to detect incipient eye disease and refractive error [14–17]. Over 90% of older adults require vision correction and management of chronic eye conditions [18, 19]. Common causes of reversible vision impairment include cataracts [6] and refractive error [20] and irreversible causes include glaucoma [21] and age-related macular degeneration [20–24]. The WFG recommend that eye care professionals, such as optometrists, assess visual acuity, screen for hemianopia and neglect, contrast sensitivity and depth perception while promoting regular eye examinations to prevent and treat visual impairment (GRADE: E) [3]. A targeted vision assessment for falls should include taking a falls history, providing tailored prevention advice, referring to other health professionals for home modifications or exercise, and discussing older adults’ preferences and needs regarding falls prevention [3].

National optometry guidelines for falls prevention provide recommendations for optometrists regarding spectacle prescription and providing older adults with advice regarding appropriate spectacle use, such as cautious step navigation when using multifocal spectacles [24–26]. These guidelines include recommendations that are consistent with the WFG namely; adapting case history to include questions about falls history, intrinsic risk factors and safe and appropriate glasses use; encouraging early referral for cataract surgery; and advising older adults to consult other allied health professionals for relevant falls prevention assessment and treatment [24–26]. In contrast with the WFG, optometry guidelines do not provide recommendations for incorporating evidence-based falls prevention assessment, such as systematic screening and assessment for falls risk and providing specific falls related advice. It has been suggested that practice of falls prevention care may rely on optometrists’ knowledge and awareness about falls prevention and identified barriers to incorporating recommendations into clinical practice [27, 28].

However, despite the evidence for effective visual interventions for falls prevention [20, 22–24], studies investigating optometrists’ practice of falls prevention are sparse [29] compared to extensive evidence for falls prevention practices by other allied health professionals [30–37]. An initial scoping review was conducted to map existing evidence of eye care practitioners’ awareness and behaviour about falls prevention care for older adults [38]. Findings suggested that community eye care practitioners (ophthalmologists or optometrists) had low levels of awareness about falls and may not be routinely implementing falls prevention care [38]. However, the scoping review did not directly explore practices of falls prevention care, included a range of eye care professionals who were not all primary eye care providers, such as ophthalmologists, sources of evidence were not critically appraised, secondary sources, such as reviews were included and resultant data were not synthesised [38]. These findings highlighted the need for a systematic investigation that focused directly on what practices of falls prevention screening, assessment, treatment and advice were being provided by optometrists as primary eye care providers. Therefore, the next step was to conduct a systematic review with the aim of synthesising current evidence for community optometrists’ practices in falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults. Understanding optometrists’ current practices around falls prevention is important to guide how optometrists can strengthen their practice of evidence-based falls prevention care, resulting in improved health outcomes for community dwelling older adults [39–42]. No reviews have yet synthesised the evidence for optometrists’ practice of falls prevention care for older adults.

The objective of this systematic review was to synthesise the best available evidence for community optometrists’ practice of falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults.

Methods

Design

Findings from the review were reported using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines (see Supplementary File 1, S. Table 1) [43]. The review followed a published protocol [44] and was registered with PROSPERO CRD 42024539668 [45]. A mixed methods design that followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines for mixed methods reviews was conducted [46]. A mixed methods approach to the review was chosen to integrate multiple types of evidence, allowing for a more nuanced and more holistic understanding of the phenomenon of interest, in this case, optometrists’ practice of falls prevention care [46–48]. Guided by JBI methodology recommendations for mixed methods reviews, a convergent integrated approach was followed [47]. This approach allows the data from diverse types of evidence to be seamlessly integrated and analysed, providing a unified and comprehensive evidence synthesis [46–48].

Search strategy

A senior librarian from the University of Western Australia guided the creation and execution of the source selection and full search strategy. The search strategy followed the JBI 3-step method (see published protocol for full description) [44] and aimed to identify both published and unpublished studies [49]. Databases searched were Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL Complete, with a search strategy tailored to each database. A detailed search strategy for the Ovid MEDLINE database can be found in Supplementary File 1, S. Table 2. Grey literature was searched through Web of Science, ProQuest and Google Scholar to identify relevant reports not captured by the database searches.

Quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods studies and reports published in English were eligible for inclusion. Conference abstracts, review articles and dissertations were excluded. The search was undertaken from January 1980 to November 2025, reflecting a rise in the identification of the problem of falls and investigations focused on falls prevention within this time [50].

Selection criteria

Following JBI guidelines for conducting mixed methods systematic reviews, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined by “population,” “phenomenon of interest” and “context” [51].

Population

This review focused on optometrists' practice in the community. Studies were included if registered optometrists engaged in practices of falls prevention care for older adults in a community setting. Studies where optometrists were working in residential aged-care facilities were excluded. Older adults were defined as adults aged 65 years and older [52, 53]. Studies were also included where the optometrists described their practices or perspectives about providing falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults. Registered optometrists were defined as qualified eye care practitioners registered with a national health practitioner regulation agency [54], such as the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) or comparable institutions in other countries where optometry is a regulated profession (e.g. registration as an optometrist with the General Optical Council in the United Kingdom [UK]). No exclusion criteria related to characteristics of the optometrists were applied. Studies that described the practices of other eye care professionals such as ophthalmologists, refractionists and opticians were excluded. Studies where other healthcare professionals, such as nurses or doctors, provided eye care were also excluded.

Phenomenon of interest

The phenomenon of interest was any type of practices by the optometrist that were directly related to falls prevention care. “Practice” was defined as optometrists undertaking any form of falls prevention care, either falls related assessment (such as screening or ocular health assessment) or falls related treatment (including glasses prescription adaptation modification to reduce falls risk or providing falls prevention advice) for their patients.

Context

Studies conducted in community settings in older adult populations were included. Studies conducted in residential aged care facilities and hospitals were excluded, as older adults in these settings receive specialised care from health professional teams. There was no exclusion based on geographical location.

Data extraction

The search results were uploaded onto COVIDENCE (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), and duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers (SYL and JaC) screened the titles and abstracts, and any studies assessed as eligible were retrieved in full. Subsequently, these studies were evaluated against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (SYL and HN). Reviewer discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third senior reviewer (AMH) during the screening process. Using the standardised JBI data extraction tool [51, 55, 56], data were subsequently extracted by the two reviewers (SYL and JiC). Data extracted included authors, year of study, setting, methodology, population, community optometrists’ practice, phenomenon of interest (falls assessment, treatment, or both assessment and treatment), study outcomes and a summary of the implications for optometrists’ practices in falls prevention care [51].

Assessment of methodological quality

Standardised JBI critical appraisal tools appropriate for each trial design were used [55–57]. Two reviewers (SYL and HN) independently conducted the critical appraisal and disagreements were resolved with a third reviewer (AMH). A summary score of the appraisals was created for each study and report (see Supplementary File 1, S. Tables 3, 4, 5 and 6). Following the JBI guidelines for mixed methods reviews, structured certainty grading such as GRADE or ConQual was not conducted. JBI guidelines explain that structured grading cannot be accommodated in mixed methods reviews, due to complexities introduced from data transformation and integration [47].

Data analysis and synthesis

Analysis followed the JBI convergent integrated approach [51]. Data were extracted from qualitative studies and assigned a level of credibility according to the JBI guidelines [51, 58]. Quantitative data extracted were transformed into ‘qualitised data’, by converting numerical results into textual descriptions following the recommended approach [47, 51]. These ‘qualitised data’ were then pooled with the qualitative data and the pooled data were analysed using thematic synthesis [59]. The analysis used three stages and took a back and forth approach previously described [59]. First, data were repeatedly scrutinised to undertake line-by-line free coding with both results and discussion in each study scrutinised [59]. Second, the coded data were examined, and similar codes were grouped according to the phenomenon of interest (optometrists’ practice in falls prevention care - either assessment and/or treatment) to form categories (see Supplementary File 1, S. Table 7, and 8). Since the phenomenon of interest was optometrists’ practice of evidence-based falls prevention, the coded data were examined in the context of the WFG [3]. The WFG groups evidence recommendations according to; (i) falls risk stratification, (ii) assessment, (iii) management and interventions, (iv) assessment and treatment algorithm. Categories were re-examined and subsequently organised to create descriptive themes. Finally, these descriptive themes were scrutinised to generate synthesised findings via analytical themes, which interpreted how the descriptive themes related to each other and assisted to answer the review question [59]. The team continually conferred and re-examined the dataset at each stage to confirm the final integrated findings [59].

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The study selection with the final included studies is presented in the PRISMA flow chart (see Fig. 1). The search identified 4620 articles. After removing 767 duplicates, 3853 articles were screened by title and abstract for eligibility and 3821 articles were excluded. Subsequently, 27 full text articles and five reports were assessed for eligibility, and 11 articles and 4 reports were included in the review. Excluded studies (from 8 countries) with full reasons for exclusion are listed in Supplementary File 1, S. Table 9.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of included studies (Australia n = 9, United States n = 2) and reports (Australia n = 2, European countries n = 1, United Kingdom n = 1) are presented in Table 1. There were four randomised controlled trials (RCT) [10, 11, 60, 61], three cohort studies [62–64], one qualitative study [28], three mixed methods studies [65–67], and four reports [24, 26, 27, 68]. All four RCT evaluated optometric practice for its efficacy for reducing falls and injuries in community dwelling older adults. In the first RCT, community optometrists’ practice was to provide a vision assessment followed by appropriate spectacle prescription as part of a multifactorial intervention [60]. In the second RCT, community optometrists provided an assessment and appropriate spectacle prescription as part of a multifactorial intervention for older adults who had received an ambulance call-out for falls [61]. In the third RCT, community optometrists’ practice was to prescribe single vision spectacles as a single intervention, for older adults with refraction problems [10]. In the fourth RCT, community optometrists conducted a visual assessment and provided new spectacles as a single intervention [11]. Of the three cohort studies, all described optometrists’ practice of vision assessments to identify falls risk. In the first study, an optometrist found that greater visual impairment, particularly in the inferior field, in older adults diagnosed with glaucoma was associated with increased falls [64]. In the other two studies, optometrists found that greater contrast sensitivity decline in older adults diagnosed with age-related macular degeneration was associated with increased falls and patients’ concerns about falling [62, 63]. The qualitative study utilised a Delphi design that convened a panel of 12 experienced Australian community optometrists to reach consensus on recommended optometry practice of falls prevention assessment and treatment and explore the feasibility of implementing the recommendations into optometric practice [28]. In the three mixed methods studies, the first study surveyed community optometrists in Texas to investigate their practices of falls prevention assessment and treatment of older adults [67]. In the second study, an optometrist practiced comprehensive vision assessments for older adults with visual impairments and examined the association between vision impairments and falls [65]. In the third study, an optometrist provided a vision assessment and follow-up falls care recommendations for older adults as part of a multidisciplinary team [66]. Two reports were optometry specific professional guidelines (one developed for Optometry Australia, the second developed for the European Council of Optometry and Optics) that provided recommendations for optometrists for falls prevention practices (screening, assessment and treatment) for community dwelling older adults [24, 26]. The third report was completed by an Australian public health institute and also provided falls prevention recommendations for optometrists’ practice [27]. The fourth report surveyed optometrists in the UK to understand if they were confident in their practice of assessing falls risk and undertaking falls risk assessments [68].

Analytical theme 1: There was scant evidence that optometrists practiced falls risk screening or assessment although they were knowledgeable and aware of the association between vision impairments and falls

Four descriptive themes (referenced data [10, 11, 24, 26, 28, 62–68]) contributed to Analytical Theme 1 (see Fig. 2). These descriptive themes identified that there was scant evidence that falls screening (to identify history of falls and level of risk) was being undertaken, although optometrists were aware of the need to assess older adults’ general health status during history taking and to screen for additional relevant falls information. Visual acuity and depth perception were considered essential tests to assess falls risk and conducting additional clinical tests particularly for contrast sensitivity, and visual fields were described.

Practices related to falls screening

Multiple studies highlighted the importance of identifying falls risk factors and the patients’ history of falls while history taking, though evidence on how this falls screening should be conducted in practice was limited [28, 64, 66, 67]. One study reported that 81% of practicing optometrists did not conduct any screening, but nearly half were willing to incorporate it into history taking [67].

General health status as a component of history taking

Two studies identified that practicing comprehensive screening for overall health was important since management of falls risk was based on appropriate prescribing and advice for the patient’s levels of activity and mobility [10, 65]. There was no additional evidence that screening was being conducted in practice. Three studies identified that there was a need for additional screening to identify frailty and poor health among older adult patients [11, 63, 65].

Visual acuity and depth perception as components of falls risk assessment

Eye assessments were identified by optometrists as a key practice for evaluating older adults’ falls risk. Visual acuity assessments by optometrists identified that visual acuity problems were associated with falls risk [62, 63, 65]. One study identified that deficits were associated with increased injury rates and a 21-fold increased odds of bump accidents [65]. Additionally, optometrists identified that falls risk increased with diminished depth perception [60, 65], highlighting the importance of it being an essential component of falls assessment. No studies identified if these practices were being conducted systematically by optometrists when assessing older patients.

Additional clinical tests as part of falls risk assessment

In four controlled studies, optometrists specifically measured the association between falls and reduced contrast sensitivity and visual field deficits [62–65]. One study identified that a one unit decrease in contrast sensitivity was associated with a 45% increased odds of falling [65]. Assessing peripheral vision was found to be particularly important for older adults with glaucoma, who experienced a 57% increased falls rate [64], and older adults with age-related macular degeneration, who experienced a 9.2% increased falls rate compared to older adults without visual impairment [62]. There was no evidence that these additional targeted clinical tests were being practiced by optometrists when completing vision assessments for older adults.

Analytical theme 2: There was sparse evidence that optometrists were providing falls prevention treatment

Five descriptive themes (see Table 3) (referenced data [10, 11, 24, 26–28, 60, 61, 65–68]) were synthesised to contribute to analytical theme 2 (see Fig. 2). These descriptive themes identified that optometrists had low levels of knowledge about practicing falls prevention care and there was limited evidence of tailoring spectacle prescriptions to manage falls risk or providing patients with advice about falls prevention. There was sparse evidence that optometrists practiced falls prevention care within a multidisciplinary team or referred to other allied health professionals for falls prevention. Limited evidence identified that optometric practice of falls prevention care could be aided by supportive organisational changes in optometric clinics.

Optometrists’ knowledge about falls prevention care

Synthesised data identified that optometrists had limited knowledge about falls prevention care [24, 65, 67, 68]. One study identified that 90% of practicing optometrists recognised the association between vision and falls [67]. One study and one report identified that knowledge gaps existed, such as having knowledge about how to conduct falls risk assessments [67, 68]. Optometrists described that their practice was constrained by lack of training, limited understanding of comprehensive falls prevention, and not knowing how to take a multidisciplinary approach [61, 65, 67]. In one report, 75.9% of 154 optometrists surveyed reported that they would benefit from more information about falls [68].

Tailoring spectacle prescription to address falls risk

Evidence for optometrists’ practice of tailored spectacle prescription to reduce falls risk was only found in the four RCTs that evaluated spectacle prescriptions for their efficacy in reducing falls [10, 11, 60, 61]. Two reports recommended this practice should be undertaken [24, 27]. Patient specific factors were identified as needing consideration when prescribing either separate spectacles for distance and near vision or multifocal spectacles, which were evaluated as being appropriate under certain patient circumstances [10, 28]. Prescribing single vision spectacles to reduce falls risk based on levels of patient activity and mobility was supported by one study and recommended to be practiced by two reports [10, 24, 26]. Tailoring prescribing practices to reduce falls risk by specifically addressing appropriate spectacle design (use of multifocal vs. single vision lenses), was evidenced by one study and recommended by three reports [10, 24, 26, 28].

Falls prevention advice as a component of treatment

There was only evidence from one RCT that optometrists’ practice included providing falls prevention advice regarding spectacle prescription to older patients [10]. Two RCTs recommended that optometrists’ practice should include providing appropriate spectacle advice for older adults [10, 11], because older adults are known to require extended adaptation periods when updating spectacle prescriptions, resulting in increased falls risk [10, 11]. One study and two reports recommended that optometrists advise older patients to undertake home modifications [24, 26, 28]. There was no evidence for optometrists’ practice of this recommendation.

Collaboration within a multidisciplinary team

Two RCTs included optometrists within a multidisciplinary team that provided an effective falls prevention intervention for older adults [60, 61]. Three studies identified that optometrists needed to refer older adults to other relevant health professionals if they were to facilitate delivery of multifactorial falls prevention interventions [28, 66, 67]. One report noted a lack of standardisation of optometry referrals to multidisciplinary falls prevention teams [27]. There was no evidence for referrals to general practitioners for falls care, although one study and one report identified that this practice could potentially facilitate access to other relevant allied health professionals, such as occupational therapists and physiotherapists [24, 28].

Organisation-wide changes as support for falls preventive care

No evidence for optometrists practicing falls prevention by using organisational changes were identified. Organisational level changes that could support optometrists to practice falls prevention treatment were suggested by two studies and one report [28, 67, 68]. Three studies identified that optometrists could provide resources, such as patient brochures and referrals to support their falls prevention practices [66–68]. One study and one report recommended that optometrists make organisational changes, by incorporating practice staff. For example, dispensers who are trained in interpreting prescriptions and advising patients on appropriate eyewear and optical devices [69], could be part of falls prevention messaging to support optometric practice of falls prevention assessment and treatment [28, 68]. There was strong recognition that organisational change could support optometrists to develop individualised falls prevention plans through shared decision making with the patient [26, 28, 66, 67]. Trusting relationships between optometrists and patients were seen as facilitators for creating these individualised treatment plans [68].

Discussion

Synthesised findings revealed limited evidence that community optometrists practiced falls prevention screening, assessment and treatment for older adults. Optometrists identified the strong association between vision impairment and falls risk but there was scant evidence that they practiced falls prevention screening and assessment. Findings also identified sparse evidence that optometrists’ practice of falls prevention treatment systematically incorporated evidence based strategies, such as tailoring spectacle prescription to address falls risk and collaborating with other health professionals to provide multidisciplinary care. These findings are supported by an earlier scoping review of vision health practitioners’ practices of falls prevention care [38], which reported that community eye care practitioners had low levels of awareness about falls and may not be routinely incorporating falls evidence into practice. Overall, the review findings suggest that optometrists’ practices in falls prevention care differ from the recommendations provided in the WFG, which highlight that all health professionals should screen, assess and deliver multifactorial interventions to reduce older adults’ fall risk [3]. The limited evidence that was available to include in the review suggests that optometric policy and practices in the area of falls prevention need further investigation. Falls are an increasing global problem for older adults and healthcare systems and primary care practitioners are recognised as having an important public health role in providing services aimed at reducing falls in older adult populations [70, 71].

Review findings highlighted that there was scant evidence that community optometrists were practicing falls history taking, in contrast to the WFG and community guidelines for falls prevention, which emphasise the importance of enquiring about previous falls in older adults [3, 13]. The absence of structured screening would likely restrict opportunities to identify older adults’ general status and daily activity levels, which is critical in subsequent tailored management to prevent falls and injuries [72, 73]. There was limited evidence that key falls prevention assessments, such as visual acuity and depth perception, were being conducted. A multi centred European study found that visual acuity below 6/6 increased falls incidence by 22% and injurious falls by 20% [8]. Decreased visual acuity level also affect balance and spatial awareness resulting in an increased risk of trips, slips, or missteps notably in complex or hazardous environments for navigation [74]. A prevailing theory suggests, appropriate visual inputs are critical for maintaining the accuracy of the vestibular-ocular reflex. A large cross-sectional study found that those with visual acuity decline or uncorrected refractive error performed worse in the balance test, translating to a rate of falling of 1.6 times greater per unit decrease of visual acuity levels [75]. Impaired stereoacuity further contributes to visual risk factors for falls risk in older adults, because stereoacuity is vital for judging distances to safely navigate the environment [76]. Stereoacuity worse than 85 arc seconds has been associated with a 3.5 times increased risk of falls [77]. A multi centred cohort study found those with stereoacuity worse than 85 arc seconds performed worse at physical activities and balance [78]. Only two observational studies provided evidence that optometrists’ practice included additional clinical tests, for example contrast sensitivity. Contrast sensitivity is arguably the most important visual risk factor for falls demonstrating a stronger predictor for falls compared to visual acuity alone [77, 79]. Reduced contrast sensitivity is associated with a 30% and 40% increased prevalence of recurrent and recent falls, respectively, with every 0.1 logCS being associated with a 5% increase in falls [79]. Similar findings were found by Mehta and colleagues, where every 0.15 log unit reduction in high spatial frequency contrast senstivity (18 cycles per degree) increased falls by 40% [77]. Decreased contrast sensitivity also impacts hazard detection in low-illumination environments, which may further elevate falls risk in older adults [73]. Visual acuity and contrast sensitivity reduction also results in increased body sway especially in more challenging environments [80]. Lastly, visual field is crucial because it can detect presence of blind spots and the extent of peripheral vision for safe navigation which may impair obstacle detection [21, 81]. A prospective longitudinal study, identified that those with severe visual field defects (mean deviation index < -12.01 dB) significantly increased the rate of falls requiring hospitalisation by 14% [82]. A systematic review highlighted peripheral field losses were significantly associated with reduced walking speed, increased collisions and altered walking patterns [83]. Further evidence suggests it is crucial to investigate the location of any visual field defects because inferior field defects were specific risk factors for falls resulting in injuries [84]. These key vision assessments have been recommended for optometrists from a prior narrative review [85].

There was also some evidence that while optometrists were aware of the association between vision impairment and falls, they had limited knowledge about falls prevention care which could be a barrier to practice. The COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation) of behaviour change offers a plausible explanation for this practice barrier as an individual needs capability (knowledge and awareness), motivation and opportunity (access and support) to enact behaviours, in this case practicing falls prevention care [86]. Evidence that optometrists were prescribing single-vision distance spectacles for older adults was limited to controlled conditions in two RCTs [10, 11]. Multifocal spectacles, while convenient, are known to cause blurred and distorted peripheral vision, especially in the lower visual field [87, 88]. Single vision spectacles reduce visual distortions and improve depth perception, therefore reducing the risk of missteps and falls [88]. Overall, there was consistent evidence from multiple sources [27, 28, 60, 61, 66–68] that optometrists recognised the importance of collaborating within a multidisciplinary team for falls prevention care, but only two RCTs and one mixed methods study provided evidence that collaboration was being practiced [60, 61, 66]. Improving opportunity by providing access to multidisciplinary teams could enable optometrists to increase their practice of falls prevention [86]. Multidisciplinary management is consistently recommended in falls prevention guidelines [3, 13], which would require system level changes if optometrists are to be included as collaborators in multidisciplinary teams. An implementation review in the area of falls prevention found that there are gaps in strategies that promote effective falls prevention implementation at the level of the organisation, community, and policy/society, such as actively involving stakeholders and developing collaborations [89].

Implications for practice and research

Evidence from this review suggests that optometrists undertaking ocular assessments and screening for falls prevention during consultations with older adults is scarce, despite strong evidence supporting their importance [3, 13, 38, 85]. Vision treatments for falls prevention, such as tailored spectacle prescriptions, are also underutilised. Effective translation of evidence for falls prevention into practice by optometrists could enhance early detection of vision related falls risk and delivery of timely vision interventions, including multidisciplinary referrals. Education in falls prevention practice may be required. Education and training are known to be primary enablers for health professionals to enact positive behaviour changes in their practice [86]. Implementation research is also important to investigate optometrists’ practice. Implementation frameworks [41, 42] explain that exploring barriers and enablers to community optometrists implementing falls prevention recommendations into the ‘real world’ setting of clinical practice could improve translation of evidence in optometric settings [41]. Visual interventions are an established effective component of multifactorial trials [90–93]. A large network meta-analysis concluded that vision interventions when provided with other interventions, such as exercise, are effective for reducing falls and falls related injuries [94]. Evidence suggests that optometrists have limited utilisation of key vision assessments for falls risk due to unclear guidance on which visual function to assess and uncertain professional roles to incorporate these assessments in relation to falls prevention [27, 78]. Therefore, goals for research should further investigate how optometrists can feasibly implement the key visual related falls prevention assessments and what support is required to optimise this in clinical practice. Specific goals for practice should task optometrists to incorporate falls screening, key visual assessments for falls, falls prevention treatment and advice into clinical practice when they consult with older adults. In the context of this evidence, the global ageing population and increasing falls rates [1, 95], the scarcity of available evidence regarding optometrists’ practices in falls prevention care suggests further implementation studies that examine optometrists’ implementation of falls prevention evidence into practice are urgently required.

Implications for guidelines and policy

Current optometry professional standards [96, 97] do not identify optometrists’ role in falls prevention, despite strong evidence and national recommendations for the efficacy of including vision interventions as part of multifactorial interventions to reduce falls [3, 13, 90, 91]. Optometry curriculum emphasises ocular health assessment and disease management but does not include falls prevention as a core competency [96]. Findings from this review suggest opportunities to strengthen optometry guidelines and curriculum by aligning with the WFG and national falls guidelines and explicitly describing the role and standards for ocular assessment and treatment for falls prevention. This could increase optometrists’ knowledge and motivation and address profession specific barriers to incorporating falls prevention care [86]. Scope-of-practice regulations further shape the extent to which optometrists can engage in falls prevention. In low and middle income countries, optometrists are often viewed as ‘refractionists’ with limited clinical scope, and regulatory constraints restrict the role of optometrists in managing and diagnosing ocular diseases [98, 99]. In high income countries, optometrists are able to independently assess ocular health and manage ocular diseases, but are not formally recognised as contributors to preventative public health [96]. Furthermore, in many countries where optometrists have extended scope of practice, reimbursement for optometrists remains a key barrier [100]. Limitations to compensation for optometrists incorporating additional patient care such as falls prevention may result in less engagement. Community engagement also influences perceptions of eye care and public awareness of optometrists’ scope [101]. Where optometrists are integrated into community health centres and primary care, there is a greater opportunity to deliver falls prevention [102].

Strengths and limitations

The review has several limitations. A key limitation was the small number of studies included, with the review identifying that there was sparse evidence about optometrists’ practices in falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults. Taking a mixed methods approach allowed diverse sources of evidence to be included but meant the authors could not definitively assess the quality of the findings through frameworks like GRADE or ConQual [46]. Hence, the authors could not identify with certainty the level of evidence for optometrists’ practices for falls prevention care. The mixed methods approach of ‘qualitising’ data may introduce potential interpretive biases and carries the risk of oversimplification; however, the authors used a transparent and systematic approach to data transformation, as recommended by JBI guidelines, to maintain the contextual meaning of the original quantitative data [46]. The review sought to clearly focus on optometrists’ practice of falls prevention to provide highly relevant results for this health profession. However, this meant the review did not include ophthalmologists or opticians, potentially missing complementary insights from vision related professionals. The included studies did not identify any low vision optometrists as a distinct subgroup, which limited the ability to examine their falls prevention practices in this review. The predefined search strategy, developed with expert guidance from a senior librarian, may not have captured all variations in terminology used internationally. As an example, search terms such as ‘mobility’, which may be used in some countries where optometrists provide mobility clinic services, were not included and may have identified additional literature. The absence of studies from low income countries may limit the generalisability of the review findings because eye problems in these countries differ from countries with developed health systems, with optometric practice in low income countries being more focused on diagnosing and managing preventable and treatable causes of blindness [19, 103]. Lastly, none of the included studies were conducted in falls clinics, which may have limited the ability to examine supporting perspectives from optometrists providing falls prevention care in those settings.

A key strength of the review was its focus on an underexplored area of falls prevention care for older community dwelling adults, namely, optometrists’ practice. The integration of findings from a broad range of study designs provided a diverse set of data for synthesis, resulting in holistic findings that are relevant for community optometrists and other stakeholders. The findings of the review suggest there are gaps in optometrists’ practices of falls prevention care for older community dwelling adults and identify that scarce studies have investigated these practices.

Conclusion

Falls-related injuries among older adults are a leading cause of injury related hospitalisations and therefore preventing falls is an urgent public health priority [1, 95]. Community optometrists are primary health care practitioners who treat large numbers of community dwelling older adults annually and are experts in managing vision impairments, which are known to be strongly associated with falls. Therefore, community optometrists are well positioned to potentially contribute to public health initiatives for falls prevention care. However, the available evidence provided limited insights into community optometrists’ practice of falls prevention screening, assessment and treatment for older adults. Few studies reported optometric practice that included conducting key ocular assessments relevant to falls risk, providing falls preventive advice, tailoring spectacle prescription for mobility levels or referrals to other health professionals for multidisciplinary falls care. Future systematic reviews could broaden the search strategy to capture complementary terminology used internationally, such as mobility, and to explore the perspectives on low vision optometrists or those working in mobility clinic settings. Further studies that evaluate optometrists’ practices of falls prevention care for community dwelling older adults and the effectiveness of these practices for reducing falls are required. Implementation studies that identify barriers and enablers to optometrists delivering falls prevention care in primary eye care settings are recommended.
