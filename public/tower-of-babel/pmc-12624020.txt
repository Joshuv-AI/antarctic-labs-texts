Cost-effectiveness of finotonlimab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone for recurrent or metastatic head and neck cancer in China
Authors: Yu Zeng, Aixin Li, Jing Zhou, Hao Wen, Keqin Zhang, Qi Wu, Su Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrent or metastatic head and neck cancer (R/M HNSCC) is highly prevalent in Southern China and Southeast Asia. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors such as finotonlimab combined with chemotherapy have shown promising survival benefits, their high costs pose challenges for healthcare systems in resource-constrained settings. This study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of finotonlimab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone as the first-line treatment for R/M HNSCC from the perspective of the Chinese healthcare payer.MethodsA 3-health-state Markov model was developed to simulate long-term clinical and economic outcomes for patients receiving either finotonlimab with cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil (finotonlimab + C5F) or placebo with C5F (Placebo + C5F). Survival data were reconstructed from published clinical trials and extrapolated using parametric models. Direct medical costs, health state utilities, and adverse event disutilities were sourced from the literature. The primary outcome was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), with a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $39,835.62 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained. Sensitivity and scenario analyses were conducted to explore uncertainty.ResultsThe finotonlimab + C5F group yielded 1.048 QALYs at a cost of $51,453.4, while the Placebo + C5F group yielded 0.788 QALYs at $22,020.2. The ICER was $113,051.4 per QALY gained, exceeding the WTP threshold. Scenario analysis revealed persistently high ICERs in finotonlimab + C5F group across different time horizons (2, 8, 10 years). One-way sensitivity analysis identified drug cost and PFS utility as the most influential parameters. Probabilistic analysis showed 0% probability of cost-effectiveness at the WTP threshold.ConclusionAlthough finotonlimab combined with chemotherapy provides clinical benefits for patients with R/M HNSCC, it is not cost-effective at current pricing in China. Substantial price reductions, biomarker-guided patient selection, and inclusion in national reimbursement programs may improve its economic viability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23864-5.

Introduction

Recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (R/M HNSCC) remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where access to innovative therapies is limited1. Despite advancements in surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies, the prognosis for patients with R/M HNSCC remains poor, with median overall survival typically less than one year. Platinum-based chemotherapy regimens, often incorporating 5-fluorouracil and a taxane, have long been considered standard first-line treatments. However, these approaches rarely yield durable responses, and most patients experience disease progression ultimately2,3. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) pathway has changed the therapeutic landscape for R/M HNSCC4. Agents such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab have demonstrated survival benefits and are now integrated into standard-of-care regimens, especially in patients with PD-L1–positive tumors (The 5-year survival rate has increased from 5.0% to 15.4–23.9%)5.

Finotonlimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting PD-1, has shown efficacy in combination with docetaxel in advanced squamous non-small cell lung cancer6. Recent studies have proved finotonlimab combined with C5F prolonged both OS and PFS7,8. However, its high acquisition cost raises concerns about its economic viability, particularly within the constraints of China’s healthcare reimbursement system.

No prior studies have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of finotonlimab plus C5F for R/M HNSCC. We developed a partitioned survival model comparing this regimen with placebo plus C5F from the perspective of the Chinese healthcare system. The model estimated QALYs, costs, and ICERs using survival projections, medical costs, utility values, and adverse event data. Sensitivity and scenario analyses were conducted to assess uncertainty. This study presents the first pharmacoeconomic evaluation of finotonlimab in R/M HNSCC, with implications for reimbursement and clinical decision-making.

Methods

We developed a Markov model survival model with three mutually exclusive health states—PFS, progressive disease (PD), and death—to simulate the disease course and economic outcomes of patients with R/M HNSCC by “heemod” package. Death is the absorbing state, and the switching OS and PFS curves of the three health states (PFS to PD is determined by the PFS curve and PD to death is determined by the OS curve). A 3-week cycle length with a 5-year time horizon was adopted to capture long-term clinical and economic outcomes (Fig. 1)9.Fig. 1Markov model structure with three health states: PFS, PD, and death.

Markov model structure with three health states: PFS, PD, and death.

Clinical efficacy data were obtained from a phase III randomized controlled trial evaluating finotonlimab plus C5F versus placebo plus C5F (NCT04146402). This study enrolled 370 patients, who were assigned to two groups in a 2:1 ratio (Finotonlimab + C5F group: 247 people; Placebo + C5F group: 123 people). Treatment was given in 3-week cycles for a total of 35 cycles and maintained for 2 years. Finotonlimab (200 mg) was administered on the first day of each cycle. Chemotherapy was given in cycles 1–6, with cisplatin (75 mg/m2) given on the first day of treatment and 5-fluorouracil (750 mg/m2) given on days 1–5 of treatment. In the 7th treatment cycle, finotonlimab maintenance treatment was started for up to 2 years if no PD or intolerable toxicity occurred. Adverse events and discontinuation rates were obtained from published literature. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves for OS and PFS were digitized using GetData Graph Digitizer (version 2.26), and individual patient data were reconstructed using the “IPDfromKM” package10. Parametric survival models including Weibull, exponential, log-logistic, lognormal, gamma, generalized gamma, Gompertz, and Royston-Parmar spline models were fitted to the reconstructed data, and model selection was based on Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC and BIC) by “flexsurv” package. The best-fitting distributions were used to extrapolate survival beyond the observed trial period.

Costs included direct medical expenditures: drug acquisition, administration, laboratory monitoring, imaging, hospitalization, management of grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs), follow-up, supportive care, and end-of-life care. Unit costs were obtained from published literature. Utility values for health states (PFS = 0.65; PD = 0.52)11 and disutilities from adverse events were derived from previously validated utility studies in oncology settings. The primary outcome was the ICER, calculated as the additional cost per additional QALY gained. Secondary outcomes included total costs, QALYs, and the incremental net monetary benefit (INMB), with a WTP threshold set at $39,835.62 per QALY (three times China’s 2024 per capita GDP)13,14.

In deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses, we examined parameter uncertainty by varying key model inputs across established ranges: costs were adjusted by ± 25%, utility or disutility values by ± 10%, and discount rates were varied from 0% to 8%. For parameters with baseline values of 0%, the analysis employed a range of 0% to 1%. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) using 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations was performed, drawing input parameters from appropriate distributions (gamma for costs, beta for utilities and probabilities) to estimate uncertainty around the ICER. Scenario analyses were also conducted by varying the model’s time horizon (2, 8 and10 years).

Results

Base-case results

In the base-case analysis, treatment with finotonlimab plus C5F resulted in an average of 1.048 QALYs per patient at a total cost of $51,453.4. In contrast, the placebo plus C5F group yielded 0.788 QALYs at a total cost of $22,020.2. This led to an incremental QALY gain of 0.260 and an incremental cost of $29,433.2, resulting in an ICER of $113,051.4 per QALY gained. Given the predefined WTP threshold of $39,835.62 per QALY, the finotonlimab regimen was not cost-effective. Sensitivity analyses

One-way sensitivity analysis identified that the model outcomes were most sensitive to the cost of finotonlimab, utility values in the PFS state, and the discount rate (Fig. 2). As the cost of finotonlimab varied between its lower boundary ($507.85) and upper boundary ($846.41), the ICER fluctuated from $88,838.88 to $138,163.06 per QALY gained. The ICER was also sensitive to utility of PFS (ICER ranging from $127,922.97 to $102,001.37 per QALY), discount rate (ICER ranging from $109,145.23 to $116,141.43 per QALY), cost of laboratory tests and scans per cycle (ICER ranging from $111,022.29 to $115,979.65 per QALY), and utility of survival with disease progression (ICER ranging from $112,159.87 to $114,874.53 per QALY). Probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that at a WTP threshold of $39,835.62/QALY, the probability of finotonlimab plus C5F being cost-effective was 0% (Fig. 3). Notably, we conducted a threshold analysis by varying the cost of finotonlimab from $0 to $1500 per 100 mg to evaluate the INMB of finotonlimab plus C5F versus placebo plus C5F at a WTP threshold of $39,835.62 per QALY gained. Scenario analysis

Scenario analyses demonstrated that shortening the model horizon to 2 years further worsened cost-effectiveness (ICER = $179,525.6/QALY), while extending the horizon to 8 years or 10 years improved the ICER to $99,094.4/QALY, $94,591.5/QALY respectively, which still exceeded the WTP threshold (Table S2).

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive cost-effectiveness evaluation of finotonlimab combined with C5F versus C5F alone as first-line treatment of recurrent or metastatic R/M HNSCC from the perspective of the Chinese healthcare system. Although the addition of finotonlimab to C5F regimen has demonstrated meaningful clinical efficacy in prolonging PFS and OS, our findings indicate that such benefits are accompanied by substantially increased costs that are not justified by the incremental health gains, under current pricing conditions.

The base-case ICER of $113,051.4 per QALY gained far exceeds China’s accepted WTP threshold, which is set at three times the national GDP per capita ($39,835.62)15–18. This is consistent with findings from other PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor-based regimens across various tumor types—including lung, liver, and R/M HNSCC or nasopharyngeal carcinoma—where high drug prices have consistently limited cost-effectiveness despite demonstrated survival benefits, rendering them potentially cost-effective only in specific subpopulations such as patients with PD-L1 expression ≥ 50%15,19–21. The SCT-I10A study also mentioned that the PD-L1 CPS ≥ 20 subgroup showed more significant survival benefits. However, since specific survival data from this study were not publicly available, we were unable to perform further analysis on this potentially cost-effective population. We performed the first analysis of finotonlimab, and the ICER was higher compared with other ICIs. These results underscore that high clinical efficacy does not inherently translate into acceptable economic value, especially when drug costs dominate the treatment budget.

From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that efforts to improve the economic value of finotonlimab in R/M HNSCC should focus on maximizing clinical benefit per treated patient. This could involve identifying biomarkers that predict durable response or resistance, such as PD-L1 expression or immune gene signatures, thereby enabling treatment personalization22. In addition, optimizing treatment duration or integrating finotonlimab with other cost-efficient modalities (e.g., radiotherapy or metronomic chemotherapy) may enhance therapeutic efficiency.

Although this is the first study to model the cost-effectiveness of finotonlimab in R/M HNSCC, several limitations merit discussion. First, long-term survival beyond the observed trial follow-up was estimated through parametric extrapolation, which introduces uncertainty. While model validation was performed, longer follow-up and real-world data are needed to confirm projections. Second, utility inputs were drawn from published sources not specific to R/M HNSCC, potentially limiting their ability to reflect the nuances of quality-of-life impacts in this disease. Third, the analysis excludes indirect costs such as productivity loss and caregiver burden, which may underestimate the broader societal benefits or drawbacks23. Finally, it is difficult to accurately stratify patients and conduct subgroup analyses23,24.

Nevertheless, the robustness of our model is supported by consistency with clinical trial outcomes, validated survival projections, and comprehensive sensitivity analysis. These findings offer timely and policy-relevant evidence for clinicians, hospital administrators, and reimbursement decision-makers tasked with evaluating the value of high-cost oncology therapies.

Conclusion

Finotonlimab plus C5F prolongs survival in R/M HNSCC but is not cost-effective at current pricing in China. Price negotiations, policy support, or refined treatment strategies are needed to improve its economic viability.
