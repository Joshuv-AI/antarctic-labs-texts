High shear dry extruded corn in feedlot receiving diets
Authors: Emma L Rients, Dathan T Smerchek, Stephanie L Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo hundred twenty-six Angus crossbred steers (initial BW = 230 ± 19 kg) from a single ranch in South Dakota were enrolled in a 38-d study to evaluate the effects of a high shear dry extruded corn product on growth performance, total tract starch digestibility, and serum glucose and insulin of receiving feedlot steers. Steers were randomly allocated to pens of 6 or 7 steers per pen, with 3.66 m of linear bunk space. Dietary treatments (n = 12 pens/treatment) were initiated on d 0, including a diet with dry rolled corn (ROLL), steam flaked corn (FLAKE; 0.29 to 0.31 kg/L) or high shear dry extruded corn (EXT; Hi-Gel; Insta-Pro International; Grimes, IA). Bodyweights were measured prior to feeding at study initiation (d −1 and 0), d 17, and on d 37 and 38. On d 37, blood and fecal grab samples were collected from one steer per pen. Estimated total tract starch digestibility was calculated using the equation proposed by Zinn et al. Data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) with pen as experimental unit. Body weights were assessed using a 4% shrink to account for gut fill. Initial weights were used as a covariate in the analysis of growth performance data. Corn processing method did not influence BW, ADG, or DMI throughout the study (P ≥ 0.15). There was however, an increase in feed efficiency (G:F) in EXT compared to ROLL with FLAKE being intermediate (P = 0.007). Corn processing method influenced estimated total tract starch digestibility where EXT and FLAKE were greater than ROLL (P = 0.0002). Serum glucose and insulin were not influenced by dietary treatment (P ≥ 0.19). Weekly TMR samples were evaluated using a Penn State shaker box. There were greater particle size and more variability in the EXT TMR between week compared to ROLL and FLAKE (TRT x week P ≤ 0.04). Our results indicate the improvements in feed efficiency and estimated total tract starch digestibility offered by high shear dry extrusion are comparable to that of steam flaked corn (SFC) in diets containing 25 to 30% corn. Overall, results from this study indicate high shear dry extruded corn is a potential alternative for feedlot operators to increase starch digestion and feeding value of corn grain in feedlot receiving diets.

Introduction

Corn is the most common grain fed in the feedlot industry, and most corn undergoes some sort of processing prior to feeding (Samuelson et al. 2016). Grain processing allows starch molecules in corn grain to become more available in the rumen environment and lower gastrointestinal tract. Starch is a significant energy source in livestock feeding, providing the necessary carbohydrates for growth and other productive functions. The influence of corn grain processing on feed efficiency and total tract starch digestibility have been documented extensively (Zinn and Owens 2008; Zinn et al. 2011). By increasing starch availability through more intensive processing methods, the relative feeding value of corn grain can be enhanced. However, while the increased proportion of rapidly fermentable carbohydrates (RFC) can enhance growth performance and feed efficiency, greater risks exist related to ruminal fermentation dysfunction. Increased RFC presentation to the rumen results in greater production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) and a resulting decrease in rumen pH, both of which contribute to metabolic diseases such as acidosis (Nagaraja and Lechtenberg 2007).

Dry-rolling and steam flaking represent two of the most common corn processing methods used in feedlot diets (Samuelson et al. 2016). Dry rolling uses pressure to crack the corn grain, increasing its surface area and allowing rumen microbes to have greater access to the starch molecules (McAllister et al. 1994). Steam flaking uses moisture and heat to soften the grain before passing through rollers. In addition to mechanically altering the corn grain kernel, this process also gelatinizes the starch, making it more soluble and available to rumen microbes (Zinn 1990). The comparison of dry rolled and steam flaked corn in the diets of feedlot cattle has been researched thoroughly, and it is well accepted that steam flaked corn has greater digestibility which can lead to increased growth and performance of feedlot cattle (Owens et al. 1997). The novel process of high shear dry extruding corn (Hi-Gel Insta-Pro International, Grimes IA) cracks the corn grain kernel and applies temperature and pressure that results in a highly gelatinized final product. Dry extruded corn has been shown to improve growth performance when used in poultry diets (Meyer and Bobeck 2021) but this processing method has not been evaluated in feedlot diets.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of corn processing method on the growth performance, total tract starch digestibility and serum glucose and insulin of receiving feedlot steers. The hypothesis was that cattle fed steam flaked and high shear dry extruded corn would have better performance and starch digestibility compared to cattle fed dry rolled corn.

Materials and methods

Two hundred twenty-six Angus crossbred steers (initial BW = 230 ± 19 kg) purchased from a single ranch in South Dakota were enrolled in a 38-d study. Upon arrival to the Iowa State University Ruminant Nutrition Farm (Ames, IA), steers were randomly placed into pens of 6 or 7 steers per pen, with 3.66 linear meters of bunk space per pen. Steers receiving chopped grass hay and a common TMR containing corn silage, sweet bran, DDGS, and dry rolled corn (DRC). Two days after arrival (d 0), steers were vaccinated with Vision 7 and Vista 5 (Merck Animal Health, Rahway, NJ), administered Dectomax pour-on (Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ), weighed and 8 bulls were excluded. Dietary treatments (n = 12 pens/treatment) were initiated on d 0, including a diet with dry rolled corn (ROLL), steam flaked corn (FLAKE; 0.29 to 0.31 kg/L) or high shear dry extruded corn (EXT; Hi-Gel).

Analyzed nutrient composition and starch characteristics of dry-rolled, steam-flaked, and high-shear dry-extruded corn used in this trial are shown in Table 1. Dry-rolled corn was processed at Key Cooperative (Gilbert, IA) using a roller mill (Roskamp roller mill with dual 12 rollers; ∼5000 µm). Consistent with minimal thermal processing, the dry-rolled corn had a starch gelatinization of 4.6% and a level of cook of 7.5%. Steam-flaked corn was produced by La Crosse Milling Co. (Cochrane, WI). Cleaned whole corn was tempered in warm water and steam-conditioned in a vertical tower for 45 to 60 min to raise kernel moisture and soften the protein matrix, then rolled through corrugated rolls to a flake density of approximately 0.29 to 0.31 kg/L (22.5 to 24 lb/bu). Flakes were cooled and dried before storage. The resulting product had a starch gelatinization of 38.9% and a level of cook of 61.8%, ∼ particle size of 7000 µm. High-shear dry-extruded corn was produced by Insta-Pro International (Grimes, IA). Cleaned whole corn was hammermilled to a geometric mean particle diameter of 390 µm and gravity-fed into a dry extruder, where mechanical shear, friction, and pressure (without added steam) generated high-temperature, short-time cooking. The cooked material was forced through a die and cut into dense, expanded discs, which were cooled by forced air in a rotary drum before storage. The extrudate had a starch gelatinization of 61.9% and a level of cook of 89.7%.

Initial diets had corn treatments included at 30% of the diet on a dry matter (DM) basis, full diet composition is in Table 2. Steers were evaluated for signs of illness including showing lethargy, depression, eye or nasal discharge, coughing, breathing rapidly or any combination of these signs. On d 4, greater than 50% of animals across all pens and treatments began to indicate clinical signs of illness, including coughing, labored breathing, lethargy, and increased rectal temperature when assessed. Due to these illness concerns across all pens and treatments, diets were altered (Table 3) to reduce corn inclusion to 25% and increase SweetBran inclusion by 5% in the diet on d 5 and in feed antibiotic (350 mg chlortetracycline/steer, Penchlor 50, Pharmgate Animal Health, Wilmington, NC) was added from d 5 to 9. After in-feed antibiotic treatment, animal health was still a concern with animals continuing to display clinical signs of illness suggesting prolonged respiratory infection, so all steers were administered Draxxin (1.1 mL/cwt; Zoetis Animal Health) on d 17. Morbidity was widespread across all pens and treatments.

Steers were fed once daily using a wagon mixer at approximately 0800 h. Daily pen feed delivery was recorded using the Performance Beef Analytics system (Performance Livestock Analytics, Ames, IA). Bunks were managed according to a slick bunk management approach, increasing feed delivery by 0.27 kg/steer on a DM basis after two consecutive days of slick bunks. Final individual bodyweights were collected on d 37 and 38 prior to feeding. Leftover feed on d 38, as well as any soiled or accumulated feed throughout the study, was weighed, sampled and discarded. Feed intake was calculated by subtracting the amount of discarded feed on a dry matter basis from the amount of delivered feed on a dry matter basis. Weekly total mixed ration (TMR) and ingredient samples were collected and dry matter was determined by drying samples in a 70°C oven for 48 h. Dried samples were ground and sent to a commercial laboratory for proximate analysis (Dairyland Laboratories, Arcadia, WI). Weekly TMR samples were also tested for particle size determination using a shaker box (Penn State Particle Separator). Each weekly TMR was analyzed in triplicate. Frozen TMR samples were thawed and mixed well. Approximately 300 g of sample was added to the particle separator. To separate the particle size, the box was shaken back and forth five times on each side, going around the box twice. Sample in each pan was weighed and the percent in each pan was calculated.

Representative samples of each corn source (dry-rolled, steam-flaked, and high-shear dry-extruded [Insta-Pro International, Grimes, IA]) were submitted to commercial laboratories to characterize nutrient composition and starch. Dry-rolled and steam-flaked corn were each analyzed as a single composited sample, whereas the extruded product was sampled from 10 production totes and is reported as the mean ± SD. Moisture (forced-draft oven; AOAC 930.15), crude protein (combustion; AOAC International 2019, 990.03), crude fat (ether extract; AOAC International 2019, 920.39), crude fiber (AOAC International 2019, 978.10), and ash (AOAC International 2019, 942.05) were determined at Eurofins Nutrition Analysis Center (Des Moines, IA). Geometric mean particle diameter and geometric standard deviation of the ground corn used to manufacture the extruded product were determined by dry sieving (ANSI/ASAE S319; Baker and Herrman 2002). Total starch and gelatinized starch were determined enzymatically at the Wenger Technical Center (Sabetha, KS), and level of cook was calculated as gelatinized starch divided by total starch. Starch concentration (enzymatic; Hall 2009), 7-h in vitro ruminal starch digestibility (4-mm grind), and the calculated starch digestion rate (Kd) were determined at Cumberland Valley Analytical Services (Waynesboro, PA).

On d 37, blood and fecal grab samples were collected from one steer per pen. Blood was collected via jugular venipuncture into a serum vacutainer. Samples were allowed to clot for at least 20 min prior to being centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min. Serum was aliquoted and samples were stored at −80°C until analyzed. Fecal samples were frozen until dried before being sent to Dairyland Laboratories (Arcadia, WI) for the analysis of fecal starch and nitrogen via NIR.

Total tract starch digestibility was determined using the equations from (Zinn et al. 2007): TTSD=100(1-((0.938-0.497×FN+0.0853×FN2)×FS ÷DS). Where FN is fecal nitrogen concentration (% DM), FS is fecal starch concentration (% DM) and DS is dietary starch concentration (% DM).

Serum glucose was analyzed using a commercially available kit (FUJIFILM Wako Diagnostics; intra-assay CV ≤ 7.14%; inter-assay CV = 8.0%). Serum insulin concentrations were analyzed via ELISA (Mercodia Bovine Insulin ELISA, Mercodia, Inc., Winston Salem, NC; intra-assay CV = 2.9%).

Data were analyzed using the Mixed procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) with the experimental unit of pen and fixed effect of corn processing treatment. All body weights were shrunk 4% to account for gut fill. Initial body weights were evaluated to be used as a covariate in the analysis of growth performance data and were found to be significant. Using a criteria of being greater than three standard deviations from the treatment mean, only one outlier was identified and removed (ROLL, for d 37 fecal starch, N and TTSD calculation). Statistical significance was considered at P ≤ 0.05 and tendency, 0.1 ≥ P > 0.05.

Results and discussion

For maximum starch digestion, corn grain must be processed. Corn processing methods and the resulting influence on starch availability and feedlot cattle growth performance have been studied extensively. In general, increasing the degree of corn processing improves starch availability and ultimately the feeding value of the corn grain resulting in improved gains and feed efficiency (Zinn and Owens 2008; Zinn et al. 2011). Dry rolling, steam flaking, or fermentation (high moisture corn storage) are the most common methods for ruminant diets to increase the extent of starch digestion (Zinn and Owens 2008). However, other processing methods, such as dry-extrusion may represent another viable processing method to increase starch digestibility of corn in feedlot diets.

As is common in newly received cattle, health was a challenge during this experiment, though it was not different due to treatment. Cattle across all pens and treatments showed clinical signs of illness suggesting respiratory infections, including increased respiratory effort, coughing and lethargy. In feed antibiotics were administered and dietary corn inclusions were decreased (from 30% to 25% of diet DM) for all treatments on d 5 to further assist the recovery of the cattle. After secondary treatment with injectable antibiotics, steers recovered, and in the second half of this short trial overall performance was satisfactory. Yet it is important to note, in this study, cattle did not achieve ad libitum feed intakes by the conclusion of the trial. This likely contributed to the lack of differences observed for overall DMI (P = 0.12) between corn processing treatments. Others have noted various dry corn processing methods do not alter feed intake in finishing cattle (Turgeon et al. 1983; Secrist et al. 1995). Corona et al. (2005) investigated the effects of various corn processing methods in feedlot finishing diets containing 75% corn grain on a DM-basis fed to cattle weighing approximately 311 kg and reported no influence on DMI for dry corn processing methods (dry-rolled corn, ground corn, or whole corn). However, cattle given ad libitum access to diets containing SFC to cattle can result in mild suppression of feed intake when compared to other dry processing methods (Zinn 1987; Corona et al. 2005). However, due to the novelty of dry extruded corn, further work, including longer duration studies and increased dietary inclusion, is needed to determine if it’s inclusion in feedlot cattle diets may influence DMI.

In this 38-d receiving trial corn processing method did not influence final BW or overall ADG (P ≥ 0.15; Table 4). In a previous trial, cattle fed SFC had greater ADG, feed efficiency (G:F), and carcass yield compared to cattle fed either dry-rolled corn, ground corn, or whole corn (Corona et al. 2005). A majority of prior experiments investigating corn processing methods in feedlot cattle were conducted in finishing cattle rations where corn grain comprises a much greater proportion of the total diet. In the present study, calves weighed ∼230 kg at the initiation of the trial and corn grain (either DRC, SFC, or high shear dry extruded corn) comprised only 25% of the diet (DM-basis).

Corn processing method influenced feed efficiency in the present study. There was an increase in G:F in EXT compared to ROLL with FLAKE intermediate (P = 0.007). Similar to results reported here, in prior work, greater degree of grain processing (SFC replacing DRC in a finishing diet) improved only G:F without affecting overall growth rate (Barajas and Zinn 1998). Yet, in other experiments using cattle similar in size (251 ± 2 kg) to those described here, both ADG and G:F were improved when SFC replaced DRC in the diet (González-Vizcarra et al. 2017). Here the observed improvement in G:F in this study was likely driven by the increased estimated starch digestibility (P = 0.01; Table 4) in EXT (96.3%) compared to ROLL (90.7%) with FLAKE (94.9%) intermediate. Similar effects were observed in an experiment comparing SFC and DRC corn with either ad libitum feed and limit feeding strategies (González-Vizcarra et al. 2017). When cattle were limit fed, and DMI was similar between treatment groups, steam flaking increased feed efficiency compared to dry-rolled corn (González-Vizcarra et al. 2017). This is relevant to interpretation of present results given that in this relatively short trial cattle were continuing to increase on feed and did not achieve ad libitum intakes. Feed efficiency results from the present study agree with a plurality of prior research that suggests as degree of corn processing increases, so too does feed efficiency (Barajas and Zinn 1998; Zinn and Owens 2008; Zinn et al. 2011). In this study, EXT, offered the greatest improvement in feed efficiency. Improved feed efficiency caused by starch digestibility comparable to SFC makes high shear dry extrusion corn a potentially viable alternative for feedlot operators. More work is needed, however, to establish equivalency of high shear dry extruded corn to SFC across production systems and diet types.

This study also evaluated whether greater starch availability would increase circulating glucose and insulin concentrations. Ruminants still have a high requirement for glucose in growing and highly metabolically active tissues, however, compared to non-ruminants, a relatively small proportion of that demand is met by glucose that is absorbed directly by the gastrointestinal tract (Yost et al. 1977). Instead ruminants rely on hepatic gluconeogenesis to supply energy to glucose dependent tissues. Propionate is the primary gluconeogenic precursor to hepatic glucose production. The proportion of propionate to acetate generally increases as dietary grain inclusion, or starch digestibility in the rumen increases (Yost et al. 1977; Young 1977; Hatew et al. 2015). Contrary to these potential outcomes, d 37 serum glucose was unaffected by corn processing treatment (P = 0.19; Table 5). This agrees with findings reported by Freitas et al. (2020) when comparing whole shell to dry rolled corn (at 70% DM) in a finishing diet where serum glucose was unaffected by corn processing. Additionally, Lozano et al. (1995) reported portal flux of glucose appears to be unaffected by increased degree of processing of steam-flaked sorghum.

Insulin is an endocrine peptide hormone that mediates an integrated anabolic response to nutrient availability. Insulin is primarily responsible for regulating metabolic flux and is the primary mediator that signals for the uptake of glucose along with other energetic metabolites to supply energy to various tissue depots for growth or storage (Sasaki 2002; Petersen and Shulman 2018). Through grain processing, site of digestion and digestibility of a feedstuff is altered (Owens et al. 1986). Thus, availability of glucose for absorption at the small intestine is altered as well as the volatile fatty acids available as a precursor for gluconeogenesis. In the present study, corn processing method did not influence circulating insulin concentration (P = 0.85; Table 5). However, other studies have reported contrasting results, albeit in cattle in the feedlot finishing phase. In finishing steers fed high grain diets with three different degrees of corn processing (24.5, 56.4, or 81.1% enzymatically available starch), serum insulin was lower in the low (24.5%) and high (81.1%) degree of processing after 28 and 84 days on study (Brown et al. 2000). There was also a quadratic effect on feed efficiency, where the middle treatment resulted in the best feed efficiency (Brown et al. 2000). While these results conflict with observations reported herein, this does suggest starch availability can influence circulating insulin concentration. Starch digestibility ranged from 90.7% to 96.3% in the present study. It appears the combination of absolute difference in starch digestibility between treatments and total corn grain inclusion in the diet may not have been great enough to elicit biologically meaningful differences in either glucose or insulin.

Variability of TMR mix was assessed throughout this experiment (Fig. 1). Total mixed ration particle size distribution was assessed on a weekly basis using a Penn State Particle Separator and methods described by Kononoff et al. (2003). This was completed to evaluate if processing methods impacted mix uniformity and subsequent diet consistency. The EXT TMR had a greater proportion of particles ranging from 0.79 to 1.91 cm and was more variable from week to week compared to ROLL and FLAKE (TRT × week P ≤ 0.04; Fig. 1). The unique bulk density and physical properties of the extruded feedstuff may have led to segregation or mixing errors during feeding. Variation in TMR particle consistency from pen to pen can have implications on both growth performance and animal health. Although not measured in this study, feedstuffs or total mixed rations with variable particle size can lead to increased sorting and selection of high starch or high fiber diet components (DeVries et al. 2008). Additionally, finishing cattle growth performance and efficiency as well as the location and degree of starch digestion can be influenced by corn particle size (Galyean et al. 1979; Hironaka et al. 1979). Future work with high shear dry extruded corn should consider TMR mix consistency and potential consequences, such as feeding behavior and sorting as these changes may result in decreased growth performance and feed efficiency.

Conclusion

Improving the nutrient availability via a novel corn processing method represents an opportunity for the feedlot producer. In this experiment, compared to dry rolled corn, high shear dry extrusion corn processing resulted in improvements in estimated starch digestibility and feed efficiency during this feedlot receiving trial comparable to that of SFC. Future work should investigate the efficacy of this novel corn processing method in feedlot finishing diets as the sole concentrate source or as a blend with corn grain processed through various methods. Given the high degree of starch digestibility, identifying optimal inclusion rates in both growing and finishing diets would be valuable. Overall, results from this study indicate high shear dry extruded corn is a potential alternative for feedlot operators to increase starch digestion and feeding value of corn grain in feedlot receiving diets.
