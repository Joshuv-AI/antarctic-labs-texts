Therapeutic effect of Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook for lateral meniscus tear repair
Authors: Shuang Yang, Li Yin, Dongfa Liao, Jinbiao Liu, Qingyun Xie, Wei Wang, Weijie Yang, Bing Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the therapeutic effect of using Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook technique by arthroscopy to all-inside suture the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area.MethodSeventy-two patients with the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area were selected and divided into three groups: a Combined Group (n = 24) treated with Fast Fix and suture hook, a Fast-Fix Group (n = 24), and a Suture Hook Group (n = 24). All patients had longitudinal tears located in the red and red-white zone. All patients received arthroscopic all-inside suture in the red and red-white zones of the meniscus with no complex tears or defects. Knee function was evaluated preoperatively and postoperatively using the Lysholm score and knee flexion-extension range of motion (ROM). MRI images were collected at least six months post-operation to assess meniscus healing. Follow-up was conducted at 3, 6, and 12 months, with no major complications such as infection or secondary trauma, and good patient compliance.ResultAt 6 months and 12 months post-operation, the patients in the Combined Group showed significant improvements in both Lysholm scores and ROM (P < 0.05), demonstrating the positive therapeutic effects of the combined treatment. The Lysholm score increased from 53.71 ± 11.37 points preoperatively to 70.63 ± 8.47 points at 6 months (P < 0.05) and 81.04 ± 13.08 points at 12 months (P < 0.05). The ROM improved from 103.58° ± 10.75° preoperatively to 123° ± 9.15° at 6 months (P < 0.05) and 125.13° ± 7.49° at 12 months (P < 0.05). MRI results confirmed good healing of the meniscus after more than six months, further supporting the effectiveness of the procedure. Compared with the other two surgical technique, the combined technique yielded significantly better functional recovery and structural healing than either technique alone (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe combination of the Fast Fix meniscus suturing device and suture hook technology provides a safe, stable, and effective method for the arthroscopic repair of lateral meniscus tears. This technique demonstrates significant clinical benefits in terms of knee function recovery, meniscus healing, and patient satisfaction. We recommend wider clinical adoption of this combined technique for improved outcomes in meniscus repair.Keywords: Fast fix suture device, Suture hook, Arthroscopy, Lateral meniscus, Popliteal hiatus

Introduction

As an important part of the knee joint, the meniscus has the functions of buffering gravity, participating in movement, maintaining joint stability, lubrication, dispersing stress, etc. Its unique “half moon” structure can scientifically and effectively disperse the longitudinal stress to the surrounding areas, so that the body can produce efficient, coordinated and stable movement [1]. Meniscal injury is a disaster for sports practitioners such as athletes and soldiers. While it brings negative effects on sports and subjective feelings, it also produces a “butterfly effect”, leading to persistent and malignant consequences on other normal structures in the joint cavity. Therefore, the treatment of meniscal injuries is one of the main tasks of sports medicine. In recent years, with the vigorous development of arthroscopy technology, various kinds of treatment techniques for meniscus injury emerge in an endless stream. There are classic Fast-Fix suture, the “inside-out” suture, the “outside-in” suture, the “all-inside” suture with the suture hook, and the latest” modified single-needle arthroscopic repair technique” in recent years [2]. Among these, the lateral meniscus tear in the popliteal hiatus area has been a challenging issue in surgery, and it remains the focus of current research in this field. This region is a natural, roughly 2 cm long opening in the posterolateral joint capsule through which the popliteus tendon passes. The meniscus in this area is unique because it lacks firm capsular attachments, rendering it more mobile and less stabilized than other meniscal regions. This inherent mobility, combined with the stress concentration from popliteus tendon motion and the region’s relative hypovascularity, makes it particularly susceptible to injury and complicates surgical repair. The traditional treatment for the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area has mainly been the Fast-Fix suture technique, but clinical outcomes are mixed due to its disadvantage of potentially injuring the posterior structures. Although using the suture hook technique can help avoid this risk, it presents challenges due to the difficulty of the procedure, long operation times, and the precision required for needle placement. As a result, this technique is currently only routinely used in a few high-level hospitals, depending on the surgeon’s experience and skill level.

Given these challenges, we hypothesize that the combination of the Fast-Fix suture technique with the suture hook technique may offer complementary advantages and improve surgical outcomes by addressing the weaknesses of each method. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to explore the therapeutic potential of these two techniques when used together, which may offer a more effective solution, especially for medical institutions with developing arthroscopic capabilities. This study aims to demonstrate that the joint use of these two techniques provides better clinical outcomes and greater efficiency.

Materials and methods

General materials

In a retrospective study, 72 patients with the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area who underwent surgical treatment at our hospital between January 2020 and September 2025 were selected and divided into three groups. The Combined Group (n = 24) received arthroscopic treatment using the Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook technique. The Fast-Fix Group (n = 24) underwent repair using only the Fast Fix suture device. The Suture Hook Group (n = 24) underwent repair using only the suture hook technique. The three groups were matched for age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and preoperative Lysholm score and range of motion (ROM) (all P > 0.05, see Table 1).

Inclusion criteria: ①In good health condition; ②Isolated lateral meniscus injury caused by a specific traumatic event, with mechanisms including sports-related twisting (n = 42), falls (n = 18), and direct lateral impact to the knee (n = 12); ③Typical Ⅲ° injury signal in the posterior corner of the lateral meniscus on preoperative MRI; ④The tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area was confirmed by operation to be longitudinal in morphology, located in the red and red-white zone; ⑤All the patients have received treatment using one of the three defined techniques by arthroscopy; ⑥Good living habits, no smoking and alcohol consumption; ⑦Follow up on time.

Exclusion criteria: ①History of osteoarthritis or secondary traumatic osteoarthritis; ②Previous history of knee surgery; ③Has been diagnosed with rheumatism, rheumatoid arthritis, gouty arthritis or other immune system diseases; ④History of knee joint puncture treatment(such as PRP, sodium vitonate, etc.); ⑤There is a rupture of the cruciate ligament confirmed by arthroscopy, or significant joint instability just before the surgery; ⑥History of tuberculosis, tumor, bacterial or viral infection before or after surgery; ⑦Did not perform rehabilitation exercise follow the guidance of a specialist. All patients had isolated lateral meniscal pathology, with no additional meniscal lesions or supplementary surgical procedures. All subjects volunteered to join the study and signed the Surgical Informed Consent Form before surgery.

Arthroscopic exploration confirmed that all enrolled patients had isolated lateral meniscal pathology, with no concomitant ligament injuries, cartilage defects, or other meniscal lesions that required supplementary surgical procedures. The mean body mass index (BMI) of the three groups were 23.4 ± 2.1 kg/m², 23.8 ± 1.9 kg/m² and 23.1 ± 2.3 kg/m², respectively. A post hoc power analysis was conducted, which confirmed that the sample size of 24 provided over 90% power to detect significant differences in the primary outcomes (Lysholm scores and ROM), given the observed effect sizes.

Surgical technique

All patients received prophylactic intravenous cefazolin 0.5–2 h preoperatively. Under general anesthesia, patients were positioned supine. A thigh tourniquet was inflated to 300 mmHg after limb exsanguination. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial arthroscopic portals were established for diagnostic arthroscopy. The arthroscopy proved that the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area was longitudinal and located in the red and red-white zones, and the overall tension of the posterior structure was poor. The meniscal tissue at the tear edges was freshened and debrided using an arthroscopic planer to stimulate healing.

Suture Hook Technique: The suture hook technique was performed first. An appropriately curved suture hook device, loaded with a #2 PDS(II) suture, was introduced. The needle was passed in a vertical or oblique manner from the inferior to the superior surface of the meniscus, traversing the tear. The suture limb was retrieved, creating a loop. A #2 − 0 non-absorbable braided suture was then shuttled through this loop and pulled across the tear. An arthroscopic knot pusher was used to secure multiple half-hitches, creating a stable knot inside the capsule. This process was repeated to place multiple sutures as needed for stable fixation.

Fast-Fix Suture Technique: Subsequently, the Fast-Fix 360 meniscal repair device was employed. The device was positioned perpendicular to the tear line. The first anchor was deployed on one side of the tear by advancing the needle to its depth stop and activating the mechanism. The device was then withdrawn, the slider was advanced to prepare the second anchor, and the second needle was inserted approximately 5 mm from the first anchor, on the opposite side of the tear. The device was removed, and the suture was tensioned using a dedicated knot-cinching tool, which slides a pre-tied knot to the meniscal surface, securing the repair.

The two techniques were used in a complementary fashion to achieve a stable, watertight closure of the tear. The combined suture construct was assessed for stability and tension. Finally, the joint was irrigated, instruments were removed, and the portals were closed. The tourniquet was deflated, and a compressive dressing was applied. Post-operative management

To reduce blood accumulation in the joint, an elastic bandage was routinely applied for 3 days post-surgery, covering the upper joint capsule with moderate tightness. If any blood infiltration occurred during this period, the dressing should be changed promptly. From the first postoperative day, the patient wore a knee hinge brace locked at 0° (extended position) and performed straight leg elevation exercises. Gradually, sandbags were applied to the ankle within the patient’s tolerance to strengthen the quadriceps and perform ankle pump exercises to prevent thrombosis. The patient was instructed to perform moderate ground movements with the assistance of crutches, but the affected limb was not allowed to bear weight. During the first three weeks post-surgery, the brace remained locked at 0° and was gradually adjusted to support angles of 30°, 60°, 90°, and 120°. Weight-bearing on the affected leg was strictly prohibited for six weeks. After six weeks, partial weight-bearing with the assistance of crutches was permitted. This conservative protocol was implemented to protect the suture repair in the highly mobile and vulnerable popliteal hiatus region during the early healing phase, minimizing tensile and shear forces despite the longitudinal (non-radial) nature of the tears. All patients were followed up at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months after operation.

Data acquisition

All the patients completed the Lysholm scoring form before surgery, and measured and recorded the range of motion (ROM) of the knee joint (Fig. 2). Simultaneously underwent MRI examination (within 3 months prior to surgery) (Fig. 3). Afterwards, the Lysholm scoring form and knee joint flexion and extension range of motion (ROM) record form were filled out in all outpatient follow-up visits after surgery. All patients were reviewed with MRI of the affected knee joint at least six months after surgery, and meniscus injury signals were recorded (Fig. 4). Functional outcomes and MRI healing grades were compared among the three groups.

Data analysis

All data were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS 22.0 software and Excel software. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to quantify the magnitude of differences. For comparisons among the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Tukey test was used for continuous variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for ordinal data (MRI grades). The Lysholm score (in points) and ROM (in degrees) are used as quantitative data, expressed as (mean ± SD). In the Combined Group, three queues of postoperative sample data were compared with preoperative data using independent sample t-test (Table 2). The postoperative Lysholm score and ROM of the three groups were compared (Tables 3 and 4). To ensure the accuracy of statistical results, normality and homogeneity of variance tests were conducted on the above sample data. Result

Comparison of Lysholm scores of 24 patients in the combined group before and after operation

The Lysholm scores of 24 patients (Table 2) were 53.71 ± 11.37 points before operation and 58 ± 10.34 points at 3 months after operation, with no statistically significant difference compared to before operation (P > 0.05). At 6 months after operation, the score was 70.63 ± 8.47 points, which showed a statistically significant difference compared to preoperative values (P < 0.05). At 12 months after operation, the score was 81.04 ± 13.08 points, which showed a statistically significant difference compared to preoperative values (P < 0.05). It is believed that the recovery of knee joint function is not significant in the short term (3 months), but shows significant improvement in the long term (more than 6 months), and the Lysholm score gradually increases with the increase of postoperative time (Fig. 5).

Comparison of ROM of 24 patients in the combined group before and after operation

ROM of 24 patients (Table 2): Preoperative 103.58 °± 10.75 °, postoperative 108.83 °± 12.35 °, with no statistically significant difference compared to preoperative levels (P > 0.05). At 6 months after operation, there was a statistically significant difference of 123 °± 9.15 ° compared to before operation (P < 0.05). At 12 months after operation, there was a statistically significant difference of 125.13 °± 7.49 ° compared to before operation (P < 0.05). It is believed that the knee joint range of motion does not improve significantly in the short term (3 months), but shows a significant increase in the long term (more than 6 months), and with the increase of postoperative time, ROM gradually increases (Fig. 5).

Comparison of MRI grades of 24 patients in the combined group before and after operation

The MRI re-examination results of 24 patients more than six months after operation showed a significant difference in the degree of meniscus injury compared to preoperative levels (P < 0.05) (Table 5), and the mean degree of injury was significantly reduced compared to preoperative levels (Fig. 6), indicating significant healing compared to preoperative levels.

Conclusion

The using of Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook technique by arthroscopy to all-inside suture the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area has a good therapeutic effect. The advantages of the two methods complement each other, with advantages such as safety, stability, convenience, efficiency, and economic practicality. It is recommended to strengthen clinical popularization.

Discussion

The complexity and importance of surgical treatment for the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area

The posterior corner of the lateral meniscus has an anatomical structure that is completely different from other parts of the meniscus. There is a hole about 2 cm long in this area, and the hamstring tendon runs diagonally through it, producing contraction movement [3]. The meniscus here is in a free state, and due to the lack of coronal ligament or joint capsule connection behind it, it has great flexibility. Moreover, its blood supply is poor compared to other parts, and tearing is more common and the treatment is relatively complex [4]. The popliteal hiatus area of the lateral meniscus poses distinct surgical challenges due to its unique anatomy. The passage of the popliteal tendon through a 2 cm hiatus increases meniscal mobility, making stabilization difficult. Coupled with poor vascularity that hinders healing and a confined posterior compartment that restricts arthroscopic access, this region complicates effective tear repair. Our study addresses these issues by employing a combined Fast Fix suture device and suture hook technique, designed to provide robust fixation and precise suture placement tailored to this challenging anatomical zone [5, 6]. While resection offers short-term pain relief, suturing is preferred for its long-term benefits in preserving joint stability and preventing osteoarthritis, particularly in young or active patients [7]. However, the anatomical constraints of the popliteal hiatus area often result in high suture failure rates [8].

Our study demonstrates that combining the Fast Fix device with a suture hook overcomes these challenges, as evidenced by significant improvements in Lysholm scores (from 53.71 ± 11.37 preoperatively to 81.04 ± 13.08 at 12 months, P < 0.05) and ROM (from 103.58° ± 10.75° to 125.13° ± 7.49° at 12 months, P < 0.05), alongside MRI-confirmed healing at 6 months post-operation (P < 0.05). These outcomes highlight the clinical efficacy of our approach in achieving durable repairs in this complex region.

The lack of standardized definitions and treatment protocols for the popliteal hiatus area of the lateral meniscus has historically hindered progress in clinical management. Leveraging the classification framework established by Zheng Jiapeng’s team [9], our study applied the combined suture technique to specific tear subtypes, yielding consistent healing and functional recovery. These results not only reinforce the relevance of precise anatomical categorization but also position our hybrid approach as a practical solution, offering surgeons a reliable method to enhance patient outcomes in this anatomically demanding area.

The diversity of clinical selection and application status for the meniscus suture technique in the current stage

At present, meniscus suturing techniques can be divided into external to internal suturing, internal to external suturing, and full internal suturing [10]. The technique of internal to external suturing can achieve precise and firm suturing of the meniscus under arthroscopy, commonly used for suturing the body and posterior part of the meniscus [11]. The technique of internal to external suture leads through the torn meniscus inside the joint and knots outside the joint capsule, reducing the impact of the knot to the meniscus. According to literature statistics, the failure rate of this suture method is about 20% [12], and there are reports that the rate of hidden nerve injury is about 1.52% −9% [13]. The technique of external to internal suturing does not require additional incisions, and its advantages lie in its speed, economy, and repeatability [4]. The process involves inserting a needle from outside the body, passing through the torn meniscus to form a closed loop lasso, and then tying a knot outside the joint capsule. However, when this technique is used to suture the posterior corner of the meniscus, especially in the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area, the operating space and needle insertion angle are significantly limited, and it is only suitable for about one-third of the area from the anterior corner of the meniscus to the body [14, 15]. The Fast Fix suture method is currently the most commonly used method in clinical practice, which enters through the anterior medial and lateral approaches of the knee joint, from front to back directly to the posterior corner of the meniscus, reducing the difficulty of posterior compartment operation of the knee joint. However, the biggest drawback of this method is that it is prone to damage the posterior hamstring tendon, hamstring nerve, popliteal artery and other structures [16, 17], requiring a high level of depth control for needle insertion. At the same time, the structure of the puncture needle at the tip of the Fast Fix suture device is relatively fragile, and during operation, it often breaks, fails to activate the fixation block, or the fixation block falls off due to narrow gaps and compression deformation by the femoral condyle [8, 18]. In addition, the economy of suturing devices is poor, and patients often have to bear high costs. The application of suture hooks has effectively solved these shortcomings of suturers, and is currently widely used for injuries in different parts of the lateral meniscus, especially in the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area [19]. Its approach is the same as that of the Fast Fix suture device, entering from the anterior inner or anterior outer side until the posterior meniscus. Compared with the Fast Fix suture device, the suture hook is more controllable during operation, and the control of the needle entry and exit points is more precise, reducing the possibility of damaging the posterior structure [19]. In addition, the cost of suture hook technology is low, and the revision rate of secondary surgery is significantly lower than that of Fast Fix suture device [20]. However, due to the limited space in the posterior compartment, it is more difficult to operate compared to the Fast Fix suture device. For the tear of the posterior root of the meniscus, the current mainstream approach is to fix and repair it with tibial tunnel and anchor suture [21, 22].

This study combines Fast Fix and suture hook techniques to optimize the repair of the lateral meniscus tear in the popliteal hiatus area. The results showed that the Lysholm score significantly increased from 53.71 ± 11.37 preoperatively to 81.04 ± 13.08 at 12 months postoperatively (P < 0.05), and the range of motion (ROM) improved from 103.58° ± 10.75° to 125.13° ± 7.49° (P < 0.05). MRI at 6 months postoperatively confirmed healing (P < 0.05). This method combines the accessibility of Fast Fix and the precision of the suture hook, reducing the risk of neurovascular injury and device failure, and significantly addressing the high failure rate of this region as mentioned in Sect. 6.1. While tibial tunnel fixation is the mainstream choice for posterior root meniscus tears, the combined technique presented in this study provides a more suitable solution for the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area, particularly for young, active patients, balancing functional recovery and joint stability.

The feasibility of using Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook technique to treat the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area

The combined use of Fast Fix suture device and suture hook technique reflects an advanced approach to managing complex meniscus injuries. The two suturing techniques involved in this study are currently very mature technologies. In a retrospective analysis of 37 patients who underwent Fast Fix suture device combined with suture hook technique by arthroscopy to all-inside suture the tear of the lateral meniscus within the popliteal hiatus area, Fang Chaohua et al. showed that both techniques achieved good results in suturing horizontal tears [23]. There was no significant difference in ROM, Lysholm score, and VAS score between the two techniques at the same time point. In comparison, the suture hook operation took significantly longer, but the cost was significantly lower. Based on the comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of the two methods, we believe that the combined application of these two methods can complement each other’s strengths and weaknesses, and provide better operational experience for surgeons while ensuring patient safety and efficacy. When using suture hooks for suturing, our team generally tends to use 2.0 tendon thread, and the PDSII thread used in this process is only used as a guide thread. The difference between these two types of sutures is that the tendon thread is soft, the suture is firm, durable, not easy to fall off, but not absorbable. Long term retention in the body may cause some degree of wear on the meniscus and cartilage. PDS II thread is tough and composed of polydioxanone, which can be absorbed in the human body. However, its disadvantages include easy breakage, smooth surface, weak knotting, and easy detachment. Through academic exchanges and literature review, it has been found that the most commonly used method currently is to directly sew with PDSII thread. If it is simply sutured with hooks, based on the above drawbacks, we still do not advocate the direct use of PDSII suture. However, in combination with Fast Fix suture device, PDSII suture can compensate for the non absorbable suture of Fast Fix suture device, minimizing the amount of suture retained in the body. At the same time, Fast Fix suture device can compensate for the shortcomings of PDSII suture that is easy to break and fall off. With the support of Fast Fix suture device, the PDSII suture will be more firm and stable. Therefore, it is feasible to combine these two suturing methods, and our team is also interested in adopting this concept in the future.

The selection of evaluation indicators for postoperative efficacy of meniscus operation and its clinical guidance significance

Except for MRI examination, which can quantify and grade the degree of meniscus injury based on imaging signal intensity and morphology, there is currently no specific evaluation index for meniscus injury and postoperative efficacy. The commonly used evaluation methods in this field are basically universal methods for knee joint evaluation, such as Lysholm score, IKDC score, knee joint flexion extension range of motion (ROM), and VAS score. A good evaluation indicator is the specific manifestation of patients’ subjective feelings such as pain, discomfort, and functional impairment, and it is also an important tool for developing diagnosis and treatment plans and conducting academic exchanges [24, 25]. The Lysholm score includes 8 items, including limping, swelling, support, climbing, strangulation, squatting, instability, and pain [26, 27], and has been proven effective and reliable in evaluating the effectiveness of knee joint surgery [28, 29]. However, some studies have questioned the effectiveness of the Lysholm score [30]. Blonna reported that the ceiling effect of Lysholm score is high, ranging from 64% to 70% [25]. The IKDC subjective scoring system was proposed in 1987 as a standardized reference system for the health status of the knee joint [31]. In the 1990 s, the IKDC scoring system was updated to assess patients’ symptoms, function, and participation in physical activities [32]. The IKDC subjective score consists of 18 items, covering symptoms, physical activity, and functional performance, comprehensively covering an individual’s health status. The effectiveness and reliability of this system as a knee joint function evaluation indicator in various knee joint diseases have been verified. Studies have shown that although both Lysholm score and IKDC subjective score exhibit ceiling effects, they are still considered as the best indicators for knee joint function assessment within an acceptable range [33]. In contrast, the Lysholm score showed a higher ceiling effect in the evaluation of ACL reconstruction, which should be given attention when assessing the postoperative functional status of patients. This article chooses Lysholm score as the evaluation index because meniscus operation is less difficult compared to ACL reconstruction operation, and patients have less subjective feelings in all aspects. Compared to IKDC score, Lysholm score filling is simpler, time-saving, and patients have stronger compliance. Therefore, in clinical practice, the selection of evaluation indicators should not be arbitrary. It should be comprehensively grasped based on the location of the patient’s injury, the length of the disease course, the severity of symptoms, and the difficulty of operation. Accurate and personalized selection is necessary in order to better formulate diagnosis and treatment plans. Our study supports this choice, showing Lysholm scores improved from 53.71 ± 11.37 preoperatively to 81.04 ± 13.08 at 12 months post-meniscus surgery (P < 0.05), alongside ROM gains from 103.58° ± 10.75° to 125.13° ± 7.49° (P < 0.05), and MRI-confirmed healing at 6 months (P < 0.05). These results validate Lysholm’s suitability for assessing meniscus repair efficacy, guiding personalized treatment strategies effectively.

Comparative efficacy of the combined technique

The introduction of control groups in this study provides robust evidence for the superiority of the combined technique. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, the combined approach yielded significantly better functional recovery and structural healing than either technique alone. This synergy likely stems from the Fast-Fix device providing strong, stable fixation while the suture hook allows for meticulous suture placement in the challenging popliteal hiatus anatomy, resulting in a more biomechanically sound and biologically conducive environment for healing.

From an economic perspective, the combined technique may initially appear costly due to the use of the Fast-Fix device; however, its synergy with the low-cost suture hook may reduce overall expense by minimizing revision rates and improving healing efficiency, potentially offering better long-term cost-effectiveness compared to using either device alone.

Limitations and shortcomings of this study

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the absence of second-look arthroscopy prevents direct visualization of meniscal healing, although MRI serves as a reliable and non-invasive alternative for assessment. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up. The longest follow-up time in this study is 12 months, but whether the recovery of 12 months after operation can represent the final treatment effect is unknown, and a longer follow-up is needed to further verify the conclusion. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and, where ethically feasible, direct arthroscopic assessment are needed to corroborate our results.

Although no major complications (e.g., infection, neurovascular injury) occurred in our cohort, potential risks include suture loosening, joint stiffness, or device failure. Long-term follow-up beyond 12 months is planned to monitor these outcomes and the progression of osteoarthritis.

Future research directions

Future studies should include multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods to validate the superiority of this combined technique. Additionally, biomechanical studies comparing the load-to-failure and cyclic loading performance of this hybrid repair against standard techniques are warranted. The development of next-generation devices that integrate the advantages of both the Fast-Fix and suture hook techniques could further simplify the procedure and improve outcomes.
