Modeling growth performance of heterogeneous rabbits in a pastured system using nonlinear, spline and random regression models
Authors: Hameed Akande Bashiru, Saidu Oyarekhua Oseni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Nonlinear models were more biologically interpretable than spline models.•The von Bertalanffy model best described growth in heterogeneous pastured rabbits.•All spline regression models overestimated body weight of rabbit at all ages.•The unstructured model was the best fit random regression model.

Introduction

In many low-input production systems, particularly in the tropics, rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) are increasingly valued for their contribution to meat production and household food security. This is due to their high reproductive rates and efficient feed conversion (Lebas et al., 1997; Oseni & Lukefahr, 2014). They are also adapted to diverse production conditions including low-input systems widely practiced in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In particular, most of the rabbit stocks in LMICs are heterogeneous or multi-colored (Lukefahr, 1998; Oseni, 2008), on account of random, non-directional and non-specific mating between and within local and exotic rabbit breeds over several decades (Oseni & Lukefahr, 2014). This genetic diversity introduces considerable variability in growth trajectories, making accurate modeling both challenging and essential.

Despite the adaptability of heterogeneous rabbits to tropical systems, their performance metrics in terms of growth such as average daily weight gain, weight at weaning and final weight is lower compared with other breeds, especially in temperate regions. Due to increasing global concerns about animal welfare, alternative production systems such as the pastured rabbit system have been proposed for raising rabbits (Gidenne et al., 2024; Nielsen et al., 2020). A pastured rabbit system refers to a low-input production environment where rabbits are housed in mobile enclosures on sown or natural forage plots, allowing direct grazing while providing protection from predators. Pastured systems, as opposed to conventional cage-based systems, offer numerous advantages including improved animal welfare and reduced feed costs (Joly et al., 2018; Trocino & Xiccato, 2010). The system also differs from conventional cage-based systems by exposing animals to natural forages, variable environmental conditions and greater behavioral freedom, which can influence growth variability. However, despite the abundance of productivity metrics of rabbits under this system in Europe (Gidenne et al., 2020; Legendre et al., 2019; Martin et al., 2016), there are limited studies under tropical conditions, especially in Africa. In particular, no study has comprehensively compared the performance of nonlinear, spline and random regression growth models in such systems.

In livestock production and management, growth modeling is a crucial aspect as it provides valuable information on the growth patterns, maturity rates and growth model parameters. Growth models also enable the prediction of weight gain over time in order to optimize feeding strategies and make informed breeding and management decisions (Aggrey, 2002). Several statistical approaches have been employed to model animal growth, including nonlinear models, spline regression models and random regression models. Classical nonlinear growth models such as the Gompertz, Logistic, von Bertalanffy, and Richards models are widely used to describe sigmoidal growth patterns in livestock species (Hossein‐Zadeh, 2025; Teleken et al., 2017). These nonlinear models can be used to describe the growth trajectory of rabbits by relating their age to their weight, and also provide insights into the biological growth process by estimating important growth parameters. These models vary in their ability to capture parameters such as inflection point, asymptotic weight, and the maturity rate, thus making model selection a critical aspect of growth analysis. On the other hand, spline regression models allow for greater flexibility in capturing local variations in growth by fitting piecewise polynomial functions to data points at predefined knots (Keith & Allison, 2014; Perperoglou et al., 2019). Additionally, random regression models (RRMs) provide an alternative approach by incorporating random effects to account for individual variability in growth trajectories (Meyer, 2004). According to Schaeffer (2004), repeated measures on individuals over time or longitudinal data such as weekly body weight records are best analyzed using RRMs. Spline and random regression models offer potential biological advantages for modeling heterogeneous populations. Splines can capture abrupt changes or growth plateaus, while RRMs account for individual variability and repeated measures over time, potentially revealing biologically meaningful differences in growth trajectories. Despite the extensive application of these models in livestock research, there is limited information on their comparative performance in describing the growth of heterogeneous rabbits. In addition, previous studies on rabbit growth modeling have primarily focused on rabbit populations raised under conventional cage-based systems.

Consequently, there is a need for evaluation of different growth models to determine their suitability for predicting body weight changes in rabbits. Understanding which growth models best describe the performance of heterogeneous rabbits raised under low-input, welfare-friendly systems has broad implications for rabbit production globally, especially in light of the increasing interest in sustainable and alternative production systems in both high- and low-income countries. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to model the growth performance of heterogeneous rabbits in a pastured system using nonlinear, spline, and random regression models, and compare the goodness-of-fit of these models in order to select the best model for accurate growth prediction to guide breeding programs and management decisions under pastured systems.

Materials and methods

Study location

This study was conducted at the Pastured Rabbit Unit of Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Osun State, Nigeria. The study site is located at latitude 7°31′06″N and longitude 4°31′22″E, within the hot and humid tropical forest ecological zone of southwestern Nigeria. The area experiences a mean annual rainfall of 1000–1250 mm, typically occurring between March and October, and a mean relative humidity ranging from 75 % to 100 %.

Ethical clearance

This study was conducted in strict accordance with ethical guidelines for the use and care of animals in research. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Health and Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Public Health, Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Nigeria, with approval number IPH/OAU/12/2772.

Experimental rabbit population

Highly diverse base population of heterogeneous rabbits (Oryctolagus cunniculus) were used for this study. The rabbits were obtained from composite heterogeneous rabbit populations reared in Ede and Ile-Ife, Osun State and Ibadan, Oyo State in Southwestern Nigeria. In this study, heterogeneous rabbits refer to multi-colored breeds of rabbits most commonly reared under low-input systems in Africa (Oseni & Lukefahr, 2014). In total, base population of heterogeneous rabbit populations consisted of a total of 32 nulliparous females and 8 males of 6 to 7 months of age and weighing between 1.8 and 2.8 Kg.

Management practices

Description of the pastured rabbit plot

The Pastured Rabbit Plot is a rectangular area of 80 m × 45 m in dimension. The fence was made of bamboo sticks and reinforced with galvanized fencing wire to prevent invasion of reptiles and rodents. Drained (used) engine oil was used regularly to spray the bamboo sticks and applied around the plot to prevent termites and soldier ants’ infestation which are perennial problems in the experimental site. The base of the fence was concretized about one-foot deep into the ground to prevent rabbits from digging out. Pasture species such as Tridax species (Tridax pucumbens), Guinea grasss (Panicum maximum), Morning glory (Ipomoea involucrate), Samoan clover, (Desmodium scorpuirus), and elephant grass (Pennisetum purpureum) was planted in the unit and available for the rabbits. Furthermore, sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) and Moringa shrubs (Moringa oleifera) were also planted in the plot due to their reported benefits and adequacy for feeding rabbits (Klinger et al., 2018). In addition, from weaning until the end of the experiment, rabbits were supplemented with 75 g of commercial pellets per day, containing 2574 kcal/kg metabolizable energy (ME), 16.64 % crude protein (CP) and 12.1 % crude fibre (CF).

Rabbit housing

Each rabbit was housed individually in a spacious rabbit tractor measuring 100 × 30 × 45 cm. The tractors are movable and are made of galvanized wire mesh at the sides and bottom, and covered with aluminium-based roofing sheet. The tractors were placed directly on the pastured floor so as to allow rabbits to forage on the pasture grasses and legumes directly. The tractors were rotated every two days across the plot to minimize overgrazing and ensure constant access to fresh forage.

Rabbit health management

Before the commencement of the experiment, all the animals were injected with Ivomec® (active ingredient: ivermectin) to treat both internal and external parasites at a recommended dosage of 0.2 ml per animal. Further, the tractors were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected with Izal® two weeks prior to commencement of the experiment. All experimental procedures and guidelines relating to biosecurity, including quarantine and stock separation and adaptation was followed strictly.

Rabbit identification and mating

Each rabbit was identified by an ear tattoo and a cage number. Mating was routinely performed in the morning before the weather became hot, with does being taken to the bucks' cages for mating. Pregnancy tests were conducted on each doe 12 to 14 days after mating through abdominal palpation. Does that were not gravid were immediately re-mated. For all pregnant does, kindling boxes were placed in their cages on the 25th day of gestation, and the boxes were checked daily to determine the date of kindling. After kindling, the boxes were inspected daily for dead kits, which were promptly removed if found. Kits were sexed at weaning in the 6th week of age, and their sex was recorded thereafter.

Data collection

Three hundred and forty-three (343) one-day-old heterogeneous rabbits were obtained from the base population described above across four parities. Body weight records of each rabbit were taken immediately after kindling and weekly with the aid of a sensitive digital scale with sensitivity of 1 g and maximum capacity of 5 kg, for 20 weeks. The 20-week study period approximates the age of sexual maturity for medium-sized rabbit breeds under tropical conditions.

Data analysis

Generalized linear model

The General Linear Model (GLM) procedure of SAS® (2009) software was used to fit a linear model with fixed effects of sex, parity, and their interaction to evaluate their influence on body weight. Sex and the interaction between sex and parity were not significant at any age and were therefore excluded from the final analysis. Assumptions of normality and variance homogeneity were evaluated with residual plots, Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. The analyses showed no meaningful violations. Means were separated using Duncan’s Multiple Range test of the same software.

Nonlinear growth models

Four classical nonlinear growth models: von Bertalanffy, Richards, Gompertz and Logistic Models were also fitted to the body weight records. The four nonlinear growth models were selected because they are the most biologically interpretable sigmoidal models in livestock growth studies (Teleken et al., 2017). They allow estimation of key parameters such as asymptotic weight, inflection point and maturity index, which are essential for comparing growth patterns across diverse populations. These nonlinear models were fitted using the NLIN procedure of SAS® (2009) according to the equations presented in Table 1 using Marquardt iterative option (Marquardt, 1963). Standard errors and 95 % confidence intervals for derived quantities were obtained by the delta method using the estimated variances and covariances from the NLIN output. Further, fitness of the models was compared using selected goodness-of-fit criteria including Coefficient of determination (R2), Akaike Information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). In addition, we calculated ΔAIC (difference from the best model) and Akaike weights (wᵢ) to assess the relative support for each model. wᵢ were calculated following the information-theoretic approach of Burnham and Anderson (2004), which converts ΔAIC values into normalized relative likelihoods. The weights represent the probability of a model being the best among the candidate set, given the data. Residual diagnostics were also performed to assess model fit. Spline and modified spline models

The spline models (SP3–SP6) were chosen to evaluate the effect of progressively increasing knot numbers on model fit, following approaches used in previous livestock growth modeling studies (Aggrey, 2002; Bashiru et al., 2020). This enabled direct assessment of how knot reduction or expansion influences prediction accuracy. The raw data were initially plotted as a scatter plot to determine the most appropriate knot locations for the spline model. Based on this, six potential knots were identified and used to fit the full spline model which was designated as SP6. The knot locations for the full SP6 model were determined through visual inspection of scatter plots of age against body weight. Subsequently, modified spline models, designated ass SP5, SP4, and SP3 with five, four and 3 knots, respectively, were developed by progressively reducing the number of knots. This was done to evaluate the impact of knot reduction and model simplification on the overall fit and performance of the spline models. Random regression models

Four random regression models (both under the assumption of homogeneous and heterogeneous variances and co-variances) were used to analyze the growth performance of heterogeneous rabbits across four parities. Under the assumption of homogeneous variances and covariances, the Compound Symmetry (CS) model was employed. However, under the assumption of heterogeneous variances and co-variances, the Unstructured Model (UN), Autoregressive Model (AR(1)) and the Toeplitz Model (TOEP) were used. The CS structure assumes equal variances across ages and constant correlations among repeated measures, which may be plausible if rabbits show relatively uniform growth variability across ages and body weights are equally correlated over time. The UN model, being the most flexible, allows each variance and covariance to differ and is appropriate when biological variability changes substantially with age, as rabbits may exhibit higher variability during rapid growth phases compared to later stages. The [AR(1)] structure assumes that correlations decay exponentially with increasing time lag, which aligns with the expectation that body weights measured closer in time in growing rabbits are more similar than those measured farther apart. The TOEP structure, by contrast, assumes correlations depend only on the lag between measurements, which may reflect situations where growth variability follows lag-specific patterns but without the strict exponential decay assumed by AR(1), while still requiring fewer parameters than the UN model. These random regression models were fitted using the MIXED procedure of SAS software. Nonlinear growth modelling of heterogeneous rabbits raised under a pastured rabbit system

Growth model parameters

Table 4 presents the estimated model parameters and standard errors of nonlinear growth models used to describe the growth patterns of heterogeneous rabbits raised under a pastured system. The parameters reported include were parameter A (Asymptotic weight) which is the theoretical mature weight, parameter B (Scaling parameter) which affects the curve's inflection point or shape, parameter K (Maturity index) which is a measure of growth rate or how quickly the animal approaches maturity and parameter D (Shape parameter) which is specific to the Richards model and allows for further flexibility in curve shape. Across all models and parities, the asymptotic weight (A) generally increased with parity, except in the case of the Richards model. The highest asymptotic weight was predicted by the Richards model in Parity 1 (2836.5 g), though with extremely large standard errors which indicated poor model fit. Large standard errors indicate parameter instability which may limit its biological interpretability. Lower asymptotic weights were estimated by other models with the Logistic models consistently estimating lowest asymptotic weight across all parities. The scaling parameter (parameter B) ranged from 0.0140 to 7.3295, with the Richards model estimating the lowest value and the Logistic model estimating the highest. The values also increased with parity in Gompertz, von Bertalanffy and Logistic models. The increasing values with parity suggest shifts in the growth curve's inflection point toward later stages in higher parities. Across all models, Parameter K also increased with parity which suggests faster growth and earlier maturity in later parities. Furthermore, across all parities, parameter K was lowest in the Richards model and highest in the Logistic model. The parameter D makes the Richards model to be flexible by altering the sigmoidal shape of the curve. Correlation matrix among model parameters

The correlation matrix among growth model parameters for the Gompertz, Logistic, von Bertalanffy, and Richards models showed an inverse relationship between the asymptotic weight and both the scaling parameter and the maturity index (Supplementary File: Table S1). Across all models and parities, the correlation between asymptotic weight (A) and the maturity index (K) was consistently strong and negative (r > -0.75). This suggests that rabbits predicted to reach higher mature weights tend to have slower growth rates or reach maturity more gradually. Furthermore, across all models, there was moderate to strong negative correlation between parameters A and B. This indicates that higher asymptotic weights are associated with lower values of the scaling parameter. This implies a possible trade-off between the final size and the steepness of the growth trajectory. However, positive correlation was observed between parameters B and K across models. This suggests that rabbits with steeper initial growth curve also tend to grow faster towards maturity. For Richards model, strong negative correlations (r > -0.90) were observed between parameters A and D across parities which denote that as mature weight increases, the shape of the curve predicted by Richards model becomes flatter. However, strong positive correlations were observed between parameters B and D, as well as parameters D and K.

Ease of computation of nonlinear growth models

In terms of comparative ease of computation for the four nonlinear growth models, all models successfully converged across all parities, except the Richards model at first and fourth parities, where convergence was not achieved within the maximum of 100 iterations (Supplementary File: Table S2). At first parity, computation times ranged from 0.26 to 0.34 s, with 7 to 100 iterations. At second parity, computation times ranged from 0.23 to 0.49 s, with 5 to 39 iterations. At third parity, times ranged from 0.29 to 0.48 s, with 5 to 81 iterations. At fourth parity, computation times ranged from 0.29 to 0.37 s, and iterations ranged from 5 to 100. Overall, the Gompertz model recorded the shortest computation time at the first, third, and fourth parities, while the Richards model had the shortest time at second parity. Conversely, the Richards model had the longest computation time at the first (tied with Logistic) and fourth parities. The von Bertalanffy and Logistic models had the longest computation times at the second and third parities, respectively. In terms of iteration count, the Gompertz model consistently required the fewest iterations across all parities, while the Richards model required the most which could be likely due to its additional shape parameter.

Selection of best-fit nonlinear and spline regression

Table 7 presents the goodness-of-fit tests for the nonlinear and spline regression models used to evaluate the body weight of heterogeneous rabbits. The metrics used include the coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², residual standard deviation (RSD), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), ΔAIC, Akaike weights (wᵢ) and the sum of residuals (the difference between observed and predicted values). A negative residual indicates overestimation while a positive residual indicates underestimation and a zero value indicates perfect prediction. Higher values of R² and adjusted R² suggest a better model fit, while lower values of RSD, AIC, ΔAIC and BIC indicate better performance. All models showed high R² and adjusted R² values (R² > 0.89), ranging from 0.907 to 0.993 and 0.890 to 0.992, respectively, suggesting that the models adequately accounted for the variability in body weight. However, due to the small differences in R² and adjusted R² among models and the fact that models had varying numbers of parameters, selection based solely on these metrics may be misleading. Therefore, more robust criteria such as AIC and BIC, which penalize over-parameterized models were preferred. Across all parities, the von Bertalanffy model consistently has the lowest AIC (ΔAIC = 0), making it the best-supported model. This was also supported by their relatively low sum of residuals. Its Akaike weights are the highest (ranging from ∼55 % to 80 %), showing that it has the strongest probability of being the best among the compared models. Gompertz and Richards models are competitive alternatives in some cases. For example, at Parities 1–3, they have moderate ΔAIC (1–4) with corresponding moderate Akaike weights (8–32 %). The Logistic model has higher ΔAIC (≥4) across parities and very low weights (<7 %), showing weak support relative to the top models. Spline (SP3–SP6) models consistently have very high ΔAIC (≥10 up to >30) and Akaike weights approaching zero, indicating clear inferior fits compared to the nonlinear growth models. Overall, the sum of residuals showed that the nonlinear models were better predictors of body weight than spline models. For instance, the von Bertalanffy, Gompertz, Logistic, and Richards models overestimated total body weight by only 69.34 g, 111.96 g, 48.08 g, and 1053.57 g, respectively. Random regression models

Table 8 presents the co-variance parameter estimate and residuals of random regression models (RRM). The unstructured models had the lowest residuals while the Toeplitz model had the highest. The residual is the difference between the observed values and the values predicted by the model. Generally, lower residual values indicate a better fit of the model to the data since residuals represent unexplained variance. The unstructured model had the lowest residual (0.9625) making it the best-performing model. Both the Toeplitz and autoregressive models had very high residuals (5380.76 and 5256.61, respectively), indicating relatively poor fit to the data compared with the unstructured model. While these models (Toeplitz and autoregressive models) are simpler and easier to interpret, their assumptions about covariance structure (based on time lag and exponential decay, respectively) seem too restrictive to capture the rabbit growth data’s true complexity. The autoregressive model assumes that the variance-covariance structure decays exponentially as time or distance between observations increases. However, the Toeplitz model follow a more rigid structure where the relationship between time points decays at a constant exponential rate. The residual of the compound symmetry (CS) model (2069.20) was lower than those estimated by the autoregressive and Toeplitz models. However, it is higher than that of the unstructured model. The CS model assumes a very simple structure where all time points have the same covariance. Comparison of random regression models

Table 9 presents the model selection criteria for the random regression models. The random regression models that employed heterogeneous variance and co-variance structure (Unstructured, Autoregressive and Toeplitz models) had lower AIC, AICC, BIC and –2 Res LogL values indicating that heterogeneous variance and co-variance structure was the more appropriate. Further, the unstructured model had the lowest AIC, AICC, BIC and –2 Res LogL values and was selected as the best-fit random regression models. The unstructured model consistently outperforms the other models in all criteria: AIC, AICC, -2 Res LogL, and BIC. This makes it the best choice for this data set, as it captures the variability and complexity more effectively, with the least error. The unstructured variance-covariance structure allows each variance and covariance to be estimated freely, providing a highly flexible fit. The Toeplitz model, which assumes that covariances between time points depend on the time lag, performs better than the compound symmetry and autoregressive models but worse than the unstructured model. The autoregressive model assumes that correlations decrease exponentially with increasing time intervals. Its performance is slightly worse than the Toeplitz model and much worse than the unstructured model. This model likely fails to capture the full range of variability in the data, as the exponential decay assumption may be too rigid. The Compound Symmetry Model assumes equal variances and covariances across all time points, which is an oversimplification of the data's true structure. Discussion

Growth patterns of heterogeneous rabbits

In the present study, there were parity-specific growth patterns implying that parity significantly influenced growth performance of rabbits. This indicates that as the mothers progress through more parities, their offspring tend to perform better in terms of growth. Significant parity effects on growth performance traits were also reported by Apori et al. (2015), Oseni et al. (2021) and Oseni and Bashiru (2022) for different breeds of rabbits raised in Ghana and Nigeria, respectively. The improved performance at later parities could be due to several maternal factors such as experience (Castellini et al., 2010), physiological condition (Belabbas et al., 2023; Pinto-Pinho et al., 2023), and possibly better lactation performance in later parities which could influence early growth (El-Gindy et al., 2022; Ludwiczak et al., 2021). This suggests that first-time mothers might require more stringent management procedures. This could also guide resource allocations in a pastured rabbit system by facilitating improved planning of resources such as feed and space allocation to maximize growth performance and productivity. Furthermore, the results suggest that both genetic factors (related to parity) and environmental factors (such as maternal care and nutrition) play a significant role in the growth performance of rabbits.

Nonlinear growth model parameters

The range of Parameter A (asymptotic weight) estimated by nonlinear models in the present study is lower than values estimated by nonlinear models (>3500 g) reported by Wojnarowska et al. (2022) for Popielno white and New Zealand white rabbits raised in Poland. Furthermore, the range of Parameter A reported by Gupta et al. (2022) for Gompertz (2987–3179 g), Logistic (2322–2343 g) and von Bertalanffy (3800–4350 g) are higher than values obtained in the present study. However, values obtained in the present study for Parameter A are higher than range of 558.21–1249.77 reported by Setiaji et al. (2013) for Rex, Local and Flemish giant breeds of rabbits raised in Indonesia. The variation in asymptotic weight (A) observed between the present study and previous reports can be attributed to several factors. First, genetic differences among breeds play a major role, as larger breeds such as New Zealand White rabbits are expected to reach higher mature weights than the breeds examined in this study. Environmental and management conditions also contribute, since rabbits raised under temperate conditions with optimized housing and feeding may achieve greater growth potential compared to those raised under tropical systems with more variable resources. The range of values for Parameter B are in line with values reported by Wojnarowska et al. (2022) but lower than scaling parameters reported by Gupta et al. (2022) and higher than values reported by Setiaji et al. (2013). The Richards model produced negative estimates of the scaling parameter (B) for parities 2, 3, and 4. Since this parameter determines the relationship between the initial size and the asymptotic size, negative values are biologically implausible because they imply invalid or undefined growth at the starting point. Such estimates likely reflect model misfit or convergence issues rather than true biological patterns. The values obtained for the maturity index (Parameter K) in the present study are within the range of maturity index reported by Wojnarowska et al. (2022) and Gupta et al. (2022) and but higher than values reported by Setiaji et al. (2013). For the shape parameter (Parameter D) in Richards Model, the shape parameter is positive only for the first parity and negative for the subsequent parities. The shape parameter determines the point of inflection and curve flexibility. It makes the Richards model a general model that nests Gompertz, Logistic, and other forms as special cases (Tjørve & Tjørve, 2017). In the Richards model, the shape parameter (D) was positive for first-parity animals, indicating a typical sigmoid curve with a symmetric growth trajectory and a well-defined inflection point. However, in subsequent parities (2–4), the parameter became negative, suggesting a shift in curve shape. This indicates that animals in higher parities tend to reach their peak earlier, with less symmetry around the inflection point. The significant differences between models highlight the importance of selecting appropriate growth models based on specific study objectives. The Logistic model might be more suitable for estimating the early and rapid growth phases, given its higher estimates for the maturity index (K) and scaling parameter (B). On the other hand, the Richards model, with its higher asymptotic weight (A) and the inclusion of the shape parameter (D), might be more flexible and suitable for capturing the complexities of growth dynamics, especially in later parities. However, the Richards model consistently produced biologically implausible parameter estimates in our data and failed to converge in some parities. This is likely due to its higher complexity and greater number of parameters, which can cause over-parameterization and instability, particularly in heterogeneous populations. This should be considered when applying highly flexible nonlinear models to similar datasets. For Gompertz, Logistic, and von Bertalanffy models, there is a consistent pattern where the asymptotic weight (A) is negatively correlated with both the scaling parameter (B) and the maturity index (K) across all parities. Conversely, there is a positive correlation between B and K. This suggests an inverse relationship between asymptotic weight and growth rate, implying that higher asymptotic weight is associated with a slower growth rate (Ali et al., 2020). This might imply a trade-off between reaching a higher maximum weight and the speed of growth. Understanding the inverse relationship between asymptotic weight and growth rate can inform breeding strategies for rabbits. Selection programs might need to balance the goal of achieving higher final weights with maintaining acceptable growth rates to optimize overall productivity of rabbits. Furthermore, these insights can be used to optimize feeding and management practices. For instance, understanding that higher asymptotic weights might come at the cost of slower growth rates can help in planning nutrition and growth monitoring schedules to achieve desired production outcomes.

Age and body weight at inflection

The inflection point marks the transition from accelerating to decelerating growth. Models predicting earlier inflection points indicate rabbits reach their peak growth rate sooner. Models predicting higher weights at the inflection point suggest a more significant growth momentum, potentially leading to better overall growth performance. Both body weight and age at the inflection point generally increased with parity across all models, a pattern most consistent for the Gompertz and Logistic models. This trend suggests that as does mature and undergo successive reproductive cycles, their offspring may reach peak growth later and at heavier body weights, possibly reflecting improved maternal adaptation, milk yield, and survival of kits with increasing parity. From a practical standpoint, the earlier inflection ages estimated by the von Bertalanffy model indicate that maximum daily weight gain occurs relatively early, which aligns with the weaning period when kits transition to solid feed and forage in pastured systems. This highlights a critical window for nutritional support to sustain rapid post-weaning growth. By contrast, the consistently higher body weights and later inflection ages predicted by the Logistic model (8–9 weeks) imply a longer period of rapid growth before deceleration, suggesting a broader management window. The Richards model produced the most variable and biologically implausible estimates for heterogeneous rabbits (e.g., extremely high body weight of 1045.82 g and late inflection age of 13.97 weeks at first parity), which likely reflects overparameterization and model instability rather than true biological differences. There is variability in the computational efficiency of the models depending on the parity.

Selection of best-fit nonlinear and spline models

Based on goodness-of-Fit metrics, all models showed high R² and adjusted R² values (>0.90), indicating that they explained a large portion of the variability in body weight. R² and adjusted R² values were close across all models, making them less useful for differentiation. Further, R2 values could be misleading (Li et al., 2017) and other selection criteria such as AIC and BIC which considers model complexity including number of parameters fitted by penalizing over-parameterization were used for evaluating model performance. The von Bertalanffy model had the lowest AIC and BIC values and highest Akaike weights across all parities, indicating it as the best-fit model. In addition, model selection based on ΔAIC and Akaike weights clearly identified the von Bertalanffy model as the most reliable growth function across all parities, with the lowest AIC (ΔAIC = 0) and the highest model support. This indicates a strong probability that it best describes growth patterns relative to other models. In contrast, spline models generally had higher AIC, BIC, ΔAIC values and negligible Akaike weights, indicating poor performance. In the present study, coefficient of determination (R2) and adjusted coefficient of determination (Adj. R2) were high (>0.90). These values are consistent with the range of R2 and Adj R2 reported by Fernandes et al. (2019) and de Sousa et al. (2022), Gupta et al. (2022), Obioma et al. (2020), Orheruata et al. (2010), Setiaji et al. (2013), Teleken et al. (2017) for nonlinear models like Gompertz, Logistic and von Bertalanffy applied to model the growth performance of different breeds of rabbits. However, the range of R2 for all nonlinear models in the present study is higher than 0.54 reported by Mallam et al. (2023) for Gompertz and Logistic models applied to modelling the body weight of mixed breeds of rabbits raised under the tropical conditions in Nigeria. In the present study, von Bertalanffy model was selected as the best-fit nonlinear model. This agrees with the report of Jacob et al. (2015) that selected von Bertalanffy model as the best-fit over Gompertz and Logistic models for modeling the body weight of Soviet Chinchilla rabbit. In addition, Gupta et al. (2022) selected von Bertalanffy model as the best-fit model for Soviet Chinchilla rabbits and Gompertz model as the best-fit for White Giant rabbits. Furthermore, von Bertalanffy model was also selected as the best-fit model by de Sousa et al. (2022) for describing the growth trajectory of New Zealand white rabbits raised in India. Based on AIC and BIC values, Gompertz model was selected as the best-fit model by Wojnarowska et al. (2022) for Popielno White rabbits while von Bertalanffy model was selected as the best-fit model for New Zealand white rabbits. However, it contradicts that of Chen et al. (2021) that selected Logistic model as the best-fit over Gompertz, Brody, Von Bertalanffy, and Richards models for a crossbred population of meat rabbits in China. Differences in the best-fit model across studies can be attributed to several factors. Breed-specific growth trajectories play an important role, as larger or faster-growing breeds may align better with particular models. Environmental and management conditions, including tropical versus temperate climates, housing, and feeding regimes, also influence growth patterns and model performance. Variation may further arise from the choice of selection criteria, since AIC, BIC, or R² emphasize different aspects of model adequacy. The superior performance of the von Bertalanffy model and the Unstructured RRM has practical implications for rabbit production. These models provide more accurate growth parameter estimates, enabling better-informed decisions about slaughter age, feeding schedules, and selection of breeding stock for desired growth trajectories compared to less accurate alternatives.

All spline models (SP3, SP4, SP5, SP6) consistently overestimated the average weight at kindling across all parities. Residual distributions (Supplementary File: Figures S1–S4) showed that nonlinear models generated narrower and more symmetric residuals compared with spline models. The consistent overestimation of body weight by all spline models indicates a potential issue with model accuracy for predicting growth performance of rabbits. Several factors might explain why spline models overestimated body weights in this study. This may reflect the inherently piecewise nature of splines, which may not align well with the smooth, sigmoidal growth patterns. Splines are local, piecewise fits whose basis functions can overreact to sparse or noisy segments of data leading to biased extrapolation at domain boundaries (Perperoglou et al., 2019). The high heterogeneity of our rabbit population further amplifies overfitting, as the model averages diverse trajectories into a single flexible curve rather than capturing the underlying biological signal. Knot selection also critically shapes model behavior. Generally, fewer knots (SP3) predicted higher growth rates, whereas more knots (SP6) predicted lower growth rates. The knot placement strategy, although systematic, may not have optimally captured the growth phases of these rabbits, thereby amplifying prediction bias. These combined factors likely underlie the poorer performance of the spline models compared with biologically based nonlinear models and random regressions. The consistent overestimation of body weight suggests a need for refinement, possibly through incorporating additional data points or adjusting the model framework to better capture growth trajectory in heterogeneous rabbits.

Limitations

This study was conducted using a heterogeneous rabbit population raised under tropical pastured conditions. While these conditions reflect an increasingly relevant welfare-focused production system, the observed growth patterns and identified best-fit models may be specific to this genetically diverse population and may not generalize to purebred or commercial strains without further validation. Likewise, environmental and management factors unique to low-input tropical pastured systems, including climate and forage availability, may influence growth and model performance differently than in intensive cage-based or temperate production systems. Finally, the consistently poor performance of the spline regression models suggests that the applied linear spline approach may not be inherently well-suited to the continuous, curvilinear nature of rabbit growth. Future work might examine alternative spline types such as cubic or penalized splines, adaptive knot-placement strategies or mixed-effects splines to better model heterogeneous populations.

Conclusion

Among the nonlinear models evaluated, the von Bertalanffy model consistently provided the most reliable and biologically realistic description of rabbit growth under a pastured, low-input tropical system, whereas the Richards model often yielded unstable and biologically implausible estimates due to overparameterization. These models identified the critical period of maximum growth (inflection point) between 6 and 9 weeks of age, which coincides with the weaning age. This finding offers practical guidance for optimizing feeding strategies at this age, such as timely supplemental pellet provision and pasture rotation schedules, to improve feed efficiency and maximize growth rate before market age. By contrast, all spline regression models performed poorly and consistently overestimated body weight at all ages. Among the covariance structures tested, the Unstructured RRM gave the best fit, with the lowest residuals and information-criteria values. Collectively, these findings provide evidence-based recommendations to guide farmers, producers, and policymakers in developing feeding and breeding strategies for heterogeneous rabbits raised under tropical pastured systems.
