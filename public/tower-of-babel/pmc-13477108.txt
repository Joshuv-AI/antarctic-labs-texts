The influence of a longitudinal virtual research mentorship program on medical students’ research skills
Authors: Gianna Dafflisio, Nina Uzoigwe, John A. Treffalls, Samantha Xu, Benjamin L. Shou, Lena E. Trager, Sharon Ben-Or, Tyler Wallen, Roman V. Petrov, Charles Bakhos, Sai Yendamuri, Doraid Jarrar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveResearch experience is critical for medical students pursuing cardiothoracic surgery, yet many lack access to mentorship and research opportunities. To address this gap, the Eastern Cardiothoracic Surgical Society launched a virtual, longitudinal research mentorship program for students at institutions with limited cardiothoracic surgery opportunities.MethodsThe Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association distributed an anonymous survey to all program applicants (N = 94), assessing demographics, career goals, and research self-efficacy. Five selected students participated in a 24-week curriculum that covered research fundamentals and practical skills application. Following program completion, participants were surveyed and interviewed.ResultsSeventy-five of 94 applicants completed the initial survey (79.8% response rate). The majority of applicants (48 out of 75 [64.0%]) had presented research but not published an article (41 out of 75 [54.7%]), and more than half (44 out of 75 [58.7%]) reported insufficient mentorship. After program completion for the 5 chosen participants, majority agreed/strongly agreed they were comfortable preparing a manuscript (4 out of 5 [80.0%]); giving poster/oral presentations (5 out of 5 [100%]); and using statistical skills (3 out of 5 [60.0%]), writing skills (4 out of 5 [80.0%]), and communication skills (5 out of 5 [100%]). Common interview themes included limited exposure to cardiothoracic surgery at home institutions, research skills improvement, improved exposure to the field, and the benefit of performing research in a collaborative setting. All participants believed this program would benefit other students.ConclusionsThere are gaps in research exposure and mentorship among students interested in cardiothoracic surgery. This inaugural program effectively enhanced participants’ research skills and provided significant exposure to cardiothoracic surgery mentors. Similar initiatives may foster further opportunities for student growth and early engagement within the specialty.

Perspective

This study highlights how structured virtual research-mentorship can bridge longstanding gaps in cardiothoracic surgery exposure and research training, offering a scalable model to strengthen the specialty's pipeline and broaden access for motivated students at underresourced institutions.

Cardiothoracic surgery residency is an increasingly competitive field to match into.1, 2, 3 Integrated cardiothoracic surgery residency (CT I-6) specifically had a notably low match rate of around 31.7% among all applicants in 2020 and 41.0% among US senior applicants, making it among the most competitive surgical subspecialties.3 Successful applicants have often completed several cardiothoracic surgery rotations, have authored multiple articles and conference presentations, and have more than 1 cardiothoracic surgery mentor guiding them through the process. However, clinical exposure, research experience, and cardiothoracic surgery mentorship are not easily accessible for many medical students nationwide. Many students attend medical schools that lack an in-house cardiothoracic surgery residency program and some attend institutions without an affiliated cardiothoracic surgery department at all. These students have a difficult time finding outside rotations and mentors.

Research experience is critical for medical students pursuing cardiothoracic surgery.4 Previous studies have demonstrated that more than 70% of successful CT I-6 applications had more than 5 articles.5 In addition, all current CT I-6 residency programs are at academic hospitals, and several CT-I6 and general surgery programs require dedicated research time as part of the residency curriculum. Although some medical schools have built research into their curriculum,6 there is still a large discrepancy in academic opportunities within different types and locations of medical schools.7,8 And for students who are given the opportunity to contribute to a project, dedicated research mentors are not always available to guide students through the process.

To address these hurdles, the Eastern Cardiothoracic Surgical Society (ECTSS) created a virtual, longitudinal research mentorship program to engage students at institutions with limited cardiothoracic surgery mentorship opportunities remotely. Although previous studies by the Thoracic Surgery Medical Student Association (TSMA) have highlighted the need for mentorship and networking opportunities in students pursuing cardiothoracic surgery,9 there is no current literature highlighting the research-specific needs of this population. Methods

Applicant Survey

The TSMA distributed applications for the inaugural ECTSS longitudinal research mentorship program. The call for applications was distributed via the TSMA email listserv and on X (formerly Twitter). After submitting application materials, all applicants were asked to complete an anonymous survey. It was explicitly stated that the survey was separate from and would not affect the application process. This was an institutional review board-approved study (IRB-P00046240; 08/2023). The 27-item survey included questions on demographics, career goals, and perceptions of current research abilities before program completion. Survey questions were given as a Likert scale from 1 to 5, where 1 = “Strongly Disagree” and 5 = “Strongly Agree.”

Program Selection and Curriculum

Eligibility criteria included current enrollment as a medical student, and applicants were required to submit their curriculum vitae and a personal statement. Applications were then scored with significant weight given to students without prior abstracts, presentations, or articles. Significant weight was also given to students without mentorship opportunities as demonstrated by a lack of home departments or training programs in cardiothoracic surgery. Lastly, consideration was given to student interest in learning research skills. The final cohort size was kept small at 5 students to facilitate open conversation and room for individualized conversation.

The ECTSS Research Mentorship Program was a 24-week program based on a senior author's previous experience teaching a Research Scholars elective class for high school seniors.10 The curriculum was divided into 2 main components: The Foundations of Research and The Application of Research Skills. The breakdown of lectures within each component is demonstrated in Table 1. The curriculum was taught weekly via Zoom, with 3 volunteer cardiothoracic surgery faculty moderators. Postprogram Survey and Interview

The TSMA conducted a 12-item anonymous survey and an 18-item 1-on-1 interview with all program participants. These included questions on career goals, perceptions of research abilities after program completion, and feedback on program curriculum. Survey questions were given as a Likert scale from 1 to 5, where 1= “Strongly Disagree” and 5 = “Strongly Agree.” The 1-on-1 interview was conducted via Zoom and recorded after obtaining consent for publication from each participant. The interview recordings were then transcribed by Reduct.Video software (Reduct Inc). Direct comparison of the recordings and transcriptions was then done to ensure accuracy. Public data on current research productivity was collected by searching the PubMed database for any manuscript publication by program participants during 2024 or 2025.

Thematic Analysis

The interview transcriptions were evaluated using qualitative coding to search for common themes. All interview transcripts were read in full, making note of significant and recurring concepts. Once themes were established from the list of concepts, transcripts were re-reviewed to search for subthemes and encompassing quotes.

Results

Applicant Demographics

Seventy-five of 94 applicants completed the survey (79.8% response rate). Twenty-eight (37.3%) respondents were women. Respondents resided in a total of 20 countries and included 47 (62.7%) international medical graduates. The majority (44 out of 75 [59.5%]) grew up in a middle-income household, with 21 (28.4%) and 4 (5.4%) growing up low income or below the poverty line, respectively. Most (54 out of 75 [72.0%]) planned to apply to CT I-6 programs, combined general surgery/thoracic programs (52 out of 75 [69.3%]), or general surgery programs (48 out of 75 [64.0%]). Further applicant characteristics are described in Table 1.

Previous Research Experience of Applicants

Before program inception, the majority of applicants had already presented research (48 out of 75 [64.0%]). Of the applicants who had previously presented research, 17 had presented 1 or more posters (17 out of 48 [35.4%]), 19 had presented 1 or more oral presentations (19 out 48 [39.6%]), and 12 had given both a poster and an oral presentation (12 out of 48 [25.0%]). In contrast, the majority of applicants had not yet published an article (41 out of 75 [54.7%]). Of the 34 applicants who had previously published an article, 18 published as a coauthor (18 out of 34 [52.9%]), 5 published as a first author (5 out of 34 [14.7%]), and 11 published as both a coauthor and first author (11 out of 34 [32.4%]).

Applicant Self-perceptions of Research Ability

The majority of applicants had a clear idea of their long-term career goals (69 out of 75 [92.0%]) and research focus (50 out of 75 [66.7%]). Most believed they lacked sufficient mentorship (44 out of 75 [58.7%]) and were either neutral or not aware of research funding opportunities that medical students can apply for (51 out of 75 [68.0%]). In terms of research skills, the majority of applicants were comfortable preparing a manuscript (51 out of 75 [68.0%]) and giving a poster or oral presentation (57 out of 75 [76%]). The majority of applicants also believed they had sufficient writing skills (53 out of 75 [70.7%]) and sufficient authorship negotiating skills (50 out of 75 [66.7%]). Applicant's confidence was mixed on statistical skills, with 37.3% (28 out of 75) believing they had sufficient skills, 28.0% (21 out of 75) were neutral, and 34.7% (26 out of 75) believing they had insufficient skills. Applicants were also divided on preparing applications for research-related scholarships, with 36.0% (27 out of 75) feeling proficient, 28.0% (21 out of 75) feeling neutral, and 36.0% (27 out of 75) feeling nonproficient. Program Participant Survey Responses

All 5 program participants completed the anonymous postprogram survey (100% response rate). After program completion, 3 participants were interested in applying to either the CT I-6 or combined general surgery/thoracic programs residency programs, and all 5 participants were interested in applying to general surgery residency programs. The majority of participants had a clear idea of their long-term career goals (4 out of 5) but were neutral on having a clear idea of long-term research focus (3 out of 5). Most felt fully supported by their mentorship team (4 out of 5) and were neutral (3 out of 5) on research funding opportunities that medical students can apply for. The majority of participants were confident in their proficiency in content knowledge (4 out of 5). In terms of research skills, the majority of participants were comfortable preparing a manuscript (4 out of 5) and giving a poster or oral presentation (5 out of 5). The majority of participants also believed they had sufficient writing skills (4 out of 5), statistical skills (3 out of 5), and authorship negotiating skills (5 out of 5). Participants were divided between proficient (2 out of 5) and neutral (3 out of 5) on preparing applications for research-related scholarships. Limited Prior Exposure to Cardiothoracic Surgery at Home Institution

All 5 participants stated there were barriers to gaining exposure to cardiothoracic surgery at their current institution, and 4 said the exposure was very limited. Barriers to gaining exposure included a lack of a home cardiothoracic surgery department, limited female faculty, no clinical rotation opportunities, no relevant research projects, and no student cardiothoracic surgery interest groups.

Representative Participant Quote:“We don't have any interest groups. We do have an option to do an elective with cardiothoracic surgery. It's very selective. [Our town] does not have a major academic hospital here. We're more of a community-based program… we don't have a lot of research going on.”

“We don't have any interest groups. We do have an option to do an elective with cardiothoracic surgery. It's very selective. [Our town] does not have a major academic hospital here. We're more of a community-based program… we don't have a lot of research going on.”

When asked what would be most helpful to address these barriers, students suggested establishing more programs like the ECTSS longitudinal research mentorship program, hosting specific events to facilitate female and minority involvement, programs to connect with faculty at outside institutions, and social media. All 5 participants went into the ECTSS Research Mentorship Program hoping to increase their general research experience. Students also hoped to build statistical skills, make connections with mentors, and gain exposure to academic medicine:“[I was looking for] a general understanding of research methodology, manuscripts, construction, what it takes to get something submitted and accepted to a journal… And really having an understanding of the more academic side of medicine. And so the ECTSS was a fantastic opportunity to come across.”“I wanted somebody whom I could maybe call a mentor, because I don't have any at my home institution, so just having somebody who could take me under their wing was what I was hoping for.”

Improvement of Research Skills

All 5 participants stated that the ECTSS Research Mentorship Program improved their research skills. Specifically, students quoted improved understanding and ability in literature reviews, statistics, public speaking and presentations, writing a manuscript from start to finish, and coordinating deadlines:“I had done some very minimal literature review, looked up some YouTube videos, and watched how to construct a manuscript and write a paper. But now, since doing this, I feel much more comfortable with the entire process, and I've helped so far on one more paper…I wouldn't have been able to contribute and help out as much as I did, had I not gone through this program.”“Dr Yandamori really walked us through how to run the software and use our numbers and run certain tests, whether it's a Kaplan-Meier or a Cox regression, to really understand our data. So those are tests that I have actually never run myself. And then by the couple of weeks…I was much more comfortable doing that myself and…being able to interpret that.”

Improved Exposure to Cardiothoracic Surgery

All program participants stated that this program improved their exposure to cardiothoracic surgery. Students also said it increased their exposure to new treatments and surgical outcomes, excellent cardiothoracic surgery mentors, and the realm of academic medicine:“[This program] has benefited me in so many ways. Being able to do a bigger project on a national level and getting connected with all these different mentors is awesome. We have 2 cardiothoracic surgeons near us and that's it. It's hard reaching out to other cardiothoracic surgeons. And even if you do reach out to other institutions, they're already busy with the students at their school. So, it's harder to connect.”

Performing Research in a Collaborative Setting

Despite the 18-item interview survey having no questions directed at collaboration in research, all 5 program participants listed group collaboration among the program's benefits:“…it would be the importance of research as it relates to clinical patient care, and the impact that you can have when you really work together in a team to come up with an idea, research that idea, get a paper out there and then talk to people in the field and experts in the field and really get their opinion on the topic.”

“…it would be the importance of research as it relates to clinical patient care, and the impact that you can have when you really work together in a team to come up with an idea, research that idea, get a paper out there and then talk to people in the field and experts in the field and really get their opinion on the topic.”

The value of collaboration in research was also evident for the students who were able to attend the ECTSS Annual Meeting following the completion of the program:“I would say the biggest factor as far as learning more about cardiothoracic surgery and exposure was actually attending the meeting and bringing that full circle, going to their conference where you meet a bunch of fantastic people, listen to a bunch of lectures on different topics. I wish that had been emphasized for me when I was a first-, second-, third-year medical student, as opposed to a fourth year, how important and how much you can learn from really attending conferences and participating in research.”

Discussion

In this study, we identified significant gaps in research exposure and mentorship among medical students who are interested in cardiothoracic surgery but lack a cardiothoracic surgery department and/or local mentors. Our study demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of a structured, virtual research mentorship program hosted by a national cardiothoracic surgical society. Participants reported substantial improvements in their research skills, manuscript preparation, statistical knowledge, and professional communication, highlighting the potential for targeted interventions to bridge existing disparities in academic development.

Access to cardiothoracic surgery mentorship and research opportunities is historically concentrated in major academic centers, leaving students from less-resourced institutions at a distinct disadvantage.4,7,8 This disparity may contribute to the ongoing underrepresentation of certain demographic and educational backgrounds in cardiothoracic surgery training programs.12,13 Our group of applicants highlighted this gap, featuring a significant number of international graduates, individuals from lower socioeconomic status, and those lacking prior research experience. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that students from nonacademic institutions often face barriers to early specialty exposure and scholarly engagement.9,14 Significant work has been done in this area, with groups like the TSMA, The American Association for Thoracic Surgery, The Society of Thoracic Surgeons, The Association of Black Cardiovascular and Thoracic Surgeons, Women in Thoracic Surgery, and Latinos in Cardiothoracic Surgery. These groups all aim to create free programming, resources, and scholarships geared toward medical students interested in cardiothoracic surgery, with explicit missions to reach students from all backgrounds. The TSMA, in particular, is a highly accessible resource that may help to fill the gap when there is no medical school-based cardiothoracic surgery interest group, which some program participants highlighted as a limitation at their school. Resources such as these are essential when thinking about recruiting and engaging the next generation of cardiothoracic surgeons.

All 5 students who participated in the program reported significant improvements in research-related competencies throughout the experience. Longstanding research fellowships for medical students have consistently demonstrated increased student interest, understanding, and pursuit of careers in academic medicine.15,16 Previous studies have also suggested that longitudinal mentorship relationships are beneficial for academic development in surgical residents and medical students.17, 18, 19 The ECTSS Research Mentorship Program's 24-week curriculum emphasized foundational skills and practical application through a collaborative project, which appears to have provided a useful framework for the continued development of these competencies. The success of this model highlights the potential value of sustained, structured research mentorship, especially for students outside traditional academic hubs. In fact, all 5 students stated they believed this program would benefit other students like themselves.

Although the ECTSS 24-week curriculum gave participants a research education with both breadth and depth, this longitudinal format may be outside the bandwidth for many organizations and individual researchers. It may therefore be advantageous to adopt this curriculum into a shorter format. Potential areas for integration of a research crash course include a boot camp for surgical interns or a half-day medical student research symposium at ECTSS or similar conferences. Medical schools themselves may have the ideal infrastructure to host longitudinal research programs, and many schools have done so successfully.20,21

Thematic analysis of participant interviews further highlighted the program's multifaceted impact. Beyond skill acquisition, participants cited increased exposure to the broader academic cardiothoracic surgery community and a deeper understanding of the academic surgeon's career trajectory. Attendance at the ECTSS Annual Meeting and presentation of research findings provided tangible opportunities for professional integration. These findings align with prior work showing that early participation in academic societies and engagement with research promote career advancement and well-being among medical students and surgical residents.22, 23, 24, 25 Our findings also support the growing recognition that research experiences promote essential nontechnical skills such as teamwork, critical thinking, and resilience, attributes increasingly emphasized in modern surgical training paradigms.26,27 The collaborative nature of the program, wherein students developed and completed a project as a team, reinforced these skills within an authentic academic context.

Limitations

The findings of our study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the small sample size of program participants limits the generalizability of our results. Although consistent themes emerged, the experiences of a larger and more diverse group could vary. Additionally, a majority of program applicants were at institutions outside of the United States. It is unclear how this may influence generalizability of the applicant survey results to US medical students; however, the distribution and proportion of scores from US students alone was extremely similar to that of the entire cohort. Second, the study relied on self-reported perceptions of improvement in research skills, which are subject to response and social desirability biases. Because the applicant survey was collected anonymously, we have no way of directly comparing survey responses of the program participants before and after program completion. This precludes our analysis from comparing the results from Figures 2 and 3. Using objective evaluations, such as pre- and postprogram assessments of research output, would yield a more reliable measure of program effectiveness. Third, participants were selected from students who actively sought additional research opportunities, which may introduce selection bias favoring highly motivated individuals. Fourth, the absence of a control group hinders our ability to conclusively attribute skill enhancements solely to the program because students may have engaged in other educational activities simultaneously. Fifth, the distribution of the program application through online social media platforms excludes students who do not use these platforms. Finally, the postprogram interview was conducted less than a year after program completion, making it difficult to assess whether students continued to use their newly gained research skills, and if they have since contributed any other conference abstracts or presentations. Future research should include longitudinal tracking of academic productivity, residency match outcomes, and career trajectories to more thoroughly evaluate the lasting influence of early mentorship programs. Beyond the statistical validity of the study, the significant commitment required of faculty to maintain the longitudinal mentorship program may limit expansion of the program.

Conclusions

There are significant gaps in research exposure and mentorship among many medical students interested in cardiothoracic surgery. The inaugural ECTSS Research Mentorship Program successfully enhanced research skills, academic exposure, and professional development among 5 medical students from underrepresented institutions. Structured, longitudinal mentorship initiatives may serve as a critical tool for promoting equity in access to cardiothoracic surgery careers and academic success. Broader adoption of similar programs by other surgical societies and training organizations is warranted and may facilitate an excellent opportunity to engage the next generation of cardiothoracic surgeons.
