Neddylation related gene signature for prognostic biomarkers in colorectal cancer with integrated bioinformatics and experimental analysis
Authors: Li Zhou, Lujuan Pan, Haisheng Lan, Houji Guo, Xusen Huang, Hua Li, Qianli Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of neddylation in colorectal cancer (CRC) is increasingly recognized as a significant factor. Our study employed bioinformatics analysis to investigate the functions of neddylation-related genes in CRC.MethodsTranscriptome data from multiple public databases were integrated and analyzed using a comprehensive bioinformatics approach, including differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and machine learning tactics to identify prognostic genes. These genes were then implemented to establish a risk prediction model. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was utilized to reveal the distribution and interactions of various cell types. The characteristics of immune cell infiltration were assessed by immune infiltration analysis. Finally, the functional roles of the prognostic gene were validated through the wound healing assay, cell counting kit-8, and transwell assay.ResultsThree genes—CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7—were identified as prognostic markers. The risk prediction model built on these genes demonstrated modest predictive performance, with the area under the curve values exceeding 0.6 at 3, 5, and 7 years. Immune infiltration analysis showed that the infiltration levels of 15 immune cells, including plasmacytoid dendritic cells and eosinophil, were markedly higher in the high-risk cohort. Furthermore, scRNA-seq data indicated that M1 macrophages exhibited higher scores for the neddylation gene set in CRC. Experimental validation demonstrated that knockdown of ANKRD13D inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionThis study established a preliminary prognostic risk model based on CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7, which exhibits exploratory predictive value for CRC prognosis. These results identified CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7 as potential candidate biomarkers for prognostic assessment in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04075-w.

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the second most common and lethal malignancy, contributing to approximately 10% of all cancer-related deaths annually [1]. Projections indicate that by 2040, the number of new CRC cases will soar to 3.2 million, with an estimated 1.6 million deaths [2]. Although early diagnosis and intervention have led to some improvements in the overall survival (OS) of CRC patients, the incidence remains alarmingly high [3]. Furthermore, first-line treatments such as chemotherapy and targeted therapy, are often limited by patient-specific dose-limiting toxicities and cancer cell resistance [4]. Treatment for advanced CRC remains particularly challenging, with only modest improvements in survival outcomes. Only about 14% of patients with metastatic CRC survive beyond five years [5]. Given these challenges, a deeper understanding of the molecular features, potential biomarkers, and therapeutic targets of CRC is essential to improve diagnostic and treatment strategies [6].

Neddylation is a ubiquitin-like modification that conjugates NEDD8 to target proteins, regulating their stability, functionality, and interactions [7], and is thereby closely linked to numerous biological processes such as DNA damage repair, cellular survival, and development [8]. Dysregulated neddylation contributes to tumor growth and drug resistance in multiple cancers [9–11]. In CRC tissues, NEDD8-mediated neddylation process enhances proliferation, migration, and survival while inhibiting apoptosis [12, 13] and drives drug-resistant recurrence [14, 15]. Importantly, targeting neddylation has been demonstrated to sensitizes CRC to topoisomerase inhibitors [16], providing a therapeutic approach to overcome drug resistance. Mechanistically, the ubiquitin-like E3 ligase Smurf1-mediated RRP9 neddylation drives CRC cell proliferation, inducing pre-rRNA processing [17]. Hence, inhibition of the overactivation of neddylation pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC.

Against this backdrop, our research focuses on CRC’s molecular characteristics and aims to clarify the roles of neddylation-related genes (NRGs)—a gene set that has rarely been investigated in previous CRC studies, and their potential as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. We integrated transcriptomic data from multiple databases and bioinformatics tools to construct an NRG-specific prognostic risk model. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was applied to resolve tumor transcriptomes at single-cell resolution [18], enabling not only cell type identification but also the exploration of cell–cell interaction networks centered on NRG expression across subsets. We further correlated immune infiltration patterns and drug sensitivity with NRG-based risk stratification. These analyses are expected to provide novel theoretical foundations and potential targets for the earlier detection and prognostic evaluation of CRC.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The gene expression data and clinical information of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-CRC [514 colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and 177 rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) cases] were retrieved from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), encompassing 595 CRC cases with survival information and 49 controls. The validation set (GSE39582) and scRNA-seq dataset (GSE166555) were gained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE39582 consisted of 573 CRC tissue samples with survival data. GSE166555 included tissue samples of 13 CRC and 12 controls. Moreover, the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) yielded 491 NRGs (scoring ≥ 1). Mitochondrial function genes were sourced via the MitoCarta3.0database, with the top 20 genes selected based on scores.

Differential expression analysis

The DESeq2 package was implemented with the objective to retrieve differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CRC and controls in the TCGA-CRC, with thresholds of |log2fold change| >0.5 and P < 0.05. Volcano and heatmap plots of DEGs were derived using the ggplot2 and pheatmap packages, respectively.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The WGCNA software constructed a gene co-expression network for TCGA-CRC. Outlier samples were removed (height cutoff = 170). A soft threshold ensuring scale-free topology (R2 = 0.8) was applied, and genes were clustered into modules using dynamic tree cutting, setting the tree-cutting merge height at 0.4 and requiring a minimum of 30 genes per module. Module–trait relationships were evaluated by Pearson correlation, and the most correlated module was selected as the key module, where the genes within key module were assigned as key module genes.

Identification and enrichment analysis of intersection genes

Overlapping genes among DEGs, NRGs, and key module genes were defined as intersection genes. To elucidate the biological significance and pathway involvement, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were implemented via the clusterProfiler package with P < 0.05.

Identification and validation of prognostic genes

In the TCGA-CRC, univariate (P < 0.1) followed by proportional hazards assumption testing (P > 0.05) was applied to intersection genes. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis was carried out via the glmnet package, and selected genes were subjected to multivariate Cox analysis for prognostic genes [hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1 and P < 0.05]. Based on the ideal cutoff values of prognostic gene expression levels, CRC patients in the TCGA-CRC were split into high- and low-expression groups via the survminer package’s surv_cutpoint function. Moreover, Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival and log-rank tests were used for survival comparison. Expression differences between CRC and normal tissues were visualized via boxplots. Spearman correlation on prognostic genes and mitochondrial function genes was assessed using the psych package. Moreover, the GeneMANIA platform (http://genemania.org/) was used to construct a gene-gene interaction network.

Construction and validation of risk model

A risk model was constructed based on the expression levels of prognostic genes and OS survival information in the TCGA-CRC. The following is the formula implemented to figure out the riskscore: "\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ risk\,score\, = \,\sum\limits_{{i = 0}}^{n} {\beta i * xi} $$\end{document}".

βi represented risk coefficient, and xi denoted gene expression. Patients were divided into high-risk group (HRG) and low-risk group (LRG) using the surv_cutpoint function (minprop = 0.5). Risk curve was plotted, and heatmap of prognostic gene expression levels in HRG and LRG was displayed. KM survival curves were drawn via the survminer package to compare survival differences between HRG and LRG. To gauge the capacity of the risk model, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were set up via the timeROC software. Additionally, the GSE39582 dataset was implemented for external validation of the risk model.

Construction of nomogram

Univariate (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.05), PH test (P > 0.05), along with multivariate (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.2) Cox regression analyses were used to identify independent prognostic factors among clinical features (age, stage, M/N/T stage, and gender) and risk score. A nomogram composed of independent predictors was ascertained via the rms package. Predictive performance was evaluated using ROC curves, decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves.

Immune microenvironment analysis

The ssGSEA algorithm from the GSVA package was applied to estimate the enrichment scores of 28 types of immune cells in CRC microenvironments. Differences in immune infiltration between LRG and HRG were displayed using boxplot with the ggplot2 package. Expression of 38 immune checkpoint molecules was analyzed. Correlation analysis was applied on differentially immune cells and immune checkpoints with mitochondrial function genes. ESTIMATE algorithm was used to compute immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores. Tumor mutation burden in TCGA-CRC was calculated via maftools.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 198 chemotherapy drugs were predicted using oncoPredict based on GDSC. Differences were assessed by Wilcoxon test with Bonferroni correction (adj.P < 0.05). Additionally, spearman correlation between risk score and drug sensitivity was evaluated.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using GSVA with the gene set “c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt” in the TCGA-CRC. Differential activity between risk groups was identified using limma with |t| >2 and P < 0.05.

ScRNA-seq analysis

Quality control was performed with Seurat: Genes ascertained in fewer than 200 cells were removed, as were cells with nFeature_RNA ≥ 5,000, nCount_RNA ≥ 25,000, and mitochondrial content ≥ 5%. After log normalization, the vst method was implemented to pick the top 2,000 highly variable genes. The dataset’s dimensionality was decreased through principal component analysis (PCA), followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization and unsupervised clustering (resolution = 0.4 for overall, 0.3 for macrophages) using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Cell clusters were annotated with relevant cell markers from Table S1 to identify different cell types and examine their distribution and proportions. For the macrophage clustering results, annotation was conducted with reference to literature [19–22] and CellMarker2.0 database Cell–cell communication was analyzed with CellChat.

Gene set and enrichment analysis at cell level

The neddylation gene set (491 NRGs) was scored per cell using AUCell and the AddModuleScore functions. Scores were visualized on UMAP and violin plots. Moreover, based on the Hallmark gene set via the MSigDB database, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was implemented using the ssGSEA algorithm in the irGSEA package [23].

Cell culture

Human CRC cells (HCT116) (Catalog No.: YC-C004) and the ANKRD13D knockout cell line (HCT116-KO) (Catalog No.: YKO-XN85350) were purchased from Ubigene Biosciences Co., Ltd., China. These cells were cultured in a medium composed of 90% McCoy’s 5 A and 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using Trizol reagent (Beyotime, China), and reverse transcription was performed using BeyoRT™ M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (Beyotime, China). RT-qPCR was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions using BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The relative mRNA expression levels were assessed using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to the internal reference gene GAPDH. The primers for ANKRD13D (ANKRD13D qPCR Primer Pair, QH72569S) were purchased from Beyotime.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate and cultured overnight. At 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Wound healing assay

To evaluate cell migration, the cell lines were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate and incubated for 24 h. A monolayer was mechanically disrupted by creating a scratch with a 200 µL pipette tip. After scratching, the wells were washed three times with PBS and incubated in fresh medium. Images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h using an optical microscope, and the wound closure was assessed using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

For the migration assay, the lower chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate was filled with DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, while the upper chamber received a cell suspension at a density of 2 × 105 cells in serum-free medium. The cells were then transferred to the incubator and incubated for 48 h. The chambers were removed, washed with PBS, and stained with crystal violet solution. For the invasion assay, the membrane of the Transwell chamber was pre-coated with a layer of Matrigel, and the subsequent steps were consistent with those of the migration assay. Finally, the migrated and invaded cells were photographed using a microscope and counted using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out via R version 4.2.1, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05. The Wilcoxon test or Student’s t test was implemented to figure out group differences. The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Result

Identification of 46 intersection genes and their functional enrichment

In the TCGA-CRC, differential analysis was conducted between CRC and control samples, identifying 11,684 DEGs. Among them, 6,362 genes were upregulated and 5,322 were downregulated (Fig. 1A-B). WGCNA identified modules associated with CRC traits after removing one outlier sample (Fig. 1C). To safeguard the setup of a network without scale, the ideal soft threshold power β was defined at 20 (Fig. 1D). Then, genes with comparable patterns of expression were grouped into 11 modules, according to the clustering dendrogram (Fig. 1E). Among these modules, MEgrey60 (cor = -0.7, P < 0.001) and MEdarkolivegreen (cor = 0.58, P < 0.001) had the strongest correlation with CRC traits and were picked as key modules, yielding 2,070 key module genes (Fig. 1F). A total 46 intersecting genes were produced by the intersection of DEGs, NRGs, and key module genes (Fig. 1G). Enrichment analysis was implemented to uncover the functions of these intersection genes. GO enrichment revealed a total of 237 entries. “Ubiquitin ligase complex”, “DNA conformation change”, “histone mRNA catabolic process”, and “programmed necrotic cell death” were enriched (Fig. 1H). KEGG analysis highlighted 16 significant pathways, such as “ubiquitin mediated proteolysis”, “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” and “NF-kappa B signaling pathway” (Fig. 1I). The enrichment analysis of intersection genes further revealed their potential roles in ubiquitination pathways and related signaling pathways.

Identification of CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7 as prognostic genes for CRC

To screen for genes related to CRC prognosis, univariate Cox regression analysis was implemented on the 46 intersection genes, revealing 13 survival-related genes (P < 0.1) (Figure S1A). Subsequently, these genes were subjected to the PH assumption test (P > 0.05) (Table S2) and LASSO analysis, filtering down to 10 genes (Figure S1B). Finally, multivariate Cox regression analysis ascertained CCNF (P < 0.01, hazard ratio (HR) = 0.574, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.395–0.835), ANKRD13D (P < 0.01, HR = 2.572 (1.698–3.894), and PSMA7 (P < 0.05, HR = 0.714 (0.51–0.999) as prognostic genes (Figure S1C). Based on the optimal thresholds of the prognostic genes [CCNF: 3.71794974, ANKRD13D: 4.171278189, and PSMA7: 9.527298989], CRC patients were categorized into high- and low-expression groups. KM curves confirmed significant survival differences between groups for all three genes (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). All genes were significantly upregulated in CRC tissues (Fig. 2B). GeneMANIA revealed that the prognostic genes primarily interact through physical interactions and shared protein domains, with functional associations including “proteasome complex” and “cellular response to hypoxia” (Fig. 2C). Given that the neddylation process may affect mitochondrial function, correlation analysis between prognostic genes and mitochondrial function genes showed that the ANKRD13D had negative association with SDHD (cor < -0.37, P < 0.01), while CCNF and PSMA7 had positive association with CYC1 (cor > 0.39, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). These results support the prognostic relevance of CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7 in CRC.

Development of a risk model for CRC

To more accurately forecast the prognosis of CRC patients, a risk model was constructed via the three prognostic genes. The risk model calculated risk scores and categorized patients into HRG and LRG via the minprop = 0.5. Risk curve unveiled that the risk scores and death samples were higher in the HRG than in the LRG (Fig. 3A). Additionally, Expression patterns of prognostic genes differed between risk groups (Figure S1D). KM curve unveiled that HRG had significantly worse survival outcomes compared to LRG (P = 0.012, Fig. 3B). Time-dependent ROC analysis for CRC showed the area under the curve (AUC) values greater than 0.6 at 3, 5, and 7 years, indicating moderate discriminative ability (Fig. 3C). External validation in GSE39582 yielded AUC values between 0.563 and 0.616, confirming the model’ consistent, though modest, predictive performance (Fig. 3D-F, Figure S1E).

Construction of a nomogram for predicting CRC patient survival

To better guide clinical decision-making, univariate Cox regression was conducted, alongside the PH assumption tes (Table S3). Age (P < 0.01, HR = 1.044 (1.025–1.063), stage (P < 0.01, HR = 2.265 (1.756 − 2.922), T stage (P < 0.01, HR = 2.019 (1.275 − 3.197), and risk score (P < 0.01, HR = 2.088 (1.446 − 3.016) were identified as independent prognostic factors through multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 4A-B). Subsequently, these factors were incorporated into the construction of a nomogram for predicting CRC survival (Fig. 4C). Additionally, calibration plot showed good agreement between predicted and actual survival rates (Fig. 4D). The nomogram’s dependability and applicability were illustrated by ROC curve, which unveiled that the AUC values at 3, 5, and 7 years all surpassed 0.79 (Fig. 4E). DCA indicated that the model had good predictive ability and could benefit patients (Fig. 4F), supporting its potential for prognostic assessment and treatment guidance in clinical practice.

Characterization of immune cell infiltration patterns across CRC risk stratifications

To learn about the link among risk groups and immune cell infiltration, the ssGSEA revealed significant differences in infiltration of 17 immune cell types between risk groups (Figure S2). Except for activated CD4 T cell and memory B cell, which had lower infiltration in the HRG, the infiltration of the remaining immune cells was significantly higher, including eosinophil, immature B cell, macrophage, etc. (Fig. 5A). Plasmacytoid dendritic cells correlated most positively with risk score (cor = 0.277, P < 0.001), while memory B cells correlated most negatively (cor = -0.243, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Additional examination using the ESTIMATE algorithm revealed that the EstimateScore, StromalScore, and ImmuneScore were higher in the HRG, indicating increased overall immune infiltration (Fig. 5C). Among 38 immune checkpoint genes, 26 were differentially expressed between groups (with 2 genes showing no detectable expression) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). Specifically, HHLA2 downregulated and ADORA2A, CD70, and LAG3 upregulated in the HRG. Moreover, the correlation between differentially infiltrated immune cells, immune checkpoints, and mitochondrial function genes was explored. SDHD was observed to have the greatest negative association with CD56dim natural killer (NK) cells (cor = -0.604, P < 0.001) and the highest positive association with activated CD4 T cells (cor = 0.430, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). Moreover, MRPL12 and NRP1 had the largest negative correlation (cor=-0.457, P < 0.001), whereas ATP5F1D and TNFRSF14 had the strongest positive correlation (cor = 0.411, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). To further investigate the genetic basis of these observations, the mutational landscape of CRC patients was analyzed. Mutation profiling showed APC, TP53, and TTN were the most frequently mutated genes in both risk groups (Fig. 6C). These results provided a fresh viewpoint for understanding the immune microenvironment of CRC.

Chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity and biological pathways activity

To predict the expected treatment responses of the HRG and LRG to 198 commonly used chemotherapeutic drugs, a drug sensitivity analysis was conducted. It was found that there were significant differences in 32 drugs among the groups (Fig. 7A). The HRG showed higher sensitivity to 13 drugs, including staurosporine, dasatinib, and JQ1. Subsequently, the correlation between risk scores and drugs was examined, revealing that ML323 and Bortezomib were positively connected with risk scores, while JQ1 and AZD8186 were negatively connected (Fig. 7B). To elucidate the biological pathways affected by different risk groups in CRC, GSVA identified 136 differentially activated pathways (Fig. 7C, Table S4). The “notch signaling pathway”, “ECM receptor interaction”, and “VEGF signaling pathway” were activated in the HRG, while the “cell cycle”, “nucleotide excision repair”, and “ubiquitin mediated proteolysis” were inhibited, revealing significant differences in biological pathways between different risk groups of CRC.

ScRNA-seq reveals cellular heterogeneity, functional profiles, and neddylation-related features in CRC

Our discoveries on the role of neddylation in CRC necessitated additional single-cell resolution analysis to clarify its roles at the cellular level. After data filtering, 67,397 cells and 23,684 genes were obtained (Figure S3A). PCA was performed on the top 2,000 variable genes for dimensionality reduction (Figure S3B-C). Unsupervised clustering identified 19 clusters, annotated as six major cell types, namely epithelial cell, fibroblast, endothelial cells, T cell, B cell, and macrophage (Fig. 8A). Further test of the proportion of different cell types in CRC and control samples revealed that the proportion of T cells was increased in the CRC group, while the proportion of epithelial cells was relatively decreased (Fig. 8B). Additionally, the PSMA7 gene was expressed in all 6 cell types (Fig. 8C). To delve into the functional characteristics of different cell types, GSEA was performed (Fig. 8D). The results unveiled that multiple pathways were activated in macrophages, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, including “P53 pathway” and “notch signaling”. These results provide light on the many and complex functions that different cell types play in the cellular microenvironment. Cell communication allowed for the inference of potential cell-cell interactions. It was observed that the frequency and intensity of T cell communication were significantly enhanced in CRC (Fig. 8E). Moreover, neddylation gene set scoring showed higher activity in macrophages, indicating correlation between macrophages and neddylation (Fig. 8F). To further elucidate how neddylation influences the immunophenotype, subclustering of macrophages further yielded 5 cell subpopulations (Figure S3E), and annotated using marker genes (Table S5) in Fig. 9A. M1 macrophages consistently exhibited higher neddylation scores, indicating a correlation between M1 macrophages and neddylation (Fig. 9B).

Knockdown of ANKRD13D inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells

Given the limited research on ANKRD13D in CRC, we further explored its role in CRC through functional experiments. RT-qPCR confirmed the effective knockdown of ANKRD13D in HCT116 cells (Fig. 10A). CCK-8 assay showed reduced proliferation (Fig. 10B). The wound healing assay revealed that the migration ability was reduced at 24- and 48-hours post-knockdown (Fig. 10C). The Transwell assay demonstrated impaired migration and invasion in knockdown cells (Fig. 10D), suggesting a functional role for ANKRD13D in CRC progression.

Discussion

Previous studies have highlighted the potential targeting the neddylation pathway as a promising therapeutic strategy in cancers [12]. In CRC, neddylation has been shown to promote tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and survival, contributing to poorer patient outcomes [12]. Against this backdrop, the present study has conducted a series of systematic analyses to systematically explore potential neddylation-related biomarkers in CRC and has successfully identified three genes with prognostic value (CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7). A risk model based on these genes demonstrated moderate discriminative ability, laying a preliminary foundation for their potential clinical translation in CRC management.

CCNF (Cyclin F) mediates intracellular protein degradation to preserve genomic integrity. Multiple analyses have unveiled that a better prognosis is linked to higher levels of CCNF expression in CRC tissue samples [24], a finding that aligns with the results of our study. Similarly, PSMA7 (proteasome subunit alpha type 7) is not only overexpressed in colorectal tumor tissues—a finding consistent with our present study—but also in a variety of other tumor tissues, suggesting it may hold potential as a therapeutic target across multiple cancers [25]. Previous studies have linked PSMA7 overexpression to liver metastasis in CRC [26], while its depletion can suppress the tumorigenic behavior of CRC cells [27]. Notably, in our research, we further observed that CRC patients with high PSMA7 expression had longer OS, indicating that the role of PSMA7 in CRC may be context-dependent and warrants further exploration of its underlying mechanisms. The ubiquitin-interacting motif (UIM) found in the protein ANKRD13D (ankyrin repeat domain 13D) can bind to lys63-linked ubiquitin chains on the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and interact with other ANKRD13 family members to regulate ligand-activated EGFR internalization [28]. Although its role in CRC remains largely unexplored, ANKRD13D upregulation has been associated with poor prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [29]. In abdominal aortic aneurysm, ANKRD13D upregulation may inhibit ubiquitinated EGFR on the cell surface, attenuating proliferative signaling of internalized EGFR [30]. To further investigate the role of ANKRD13D in CRC, we performed a series of functional assays and demonstrated that ANKRD13D knockdown significantly suppresses CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in CRC progression. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial function is regulated by neddylation and cullin-RING ligases (CRLs) [31], and our correlation analysis extended this finding by linking the three identified prognostic neddylation-related genes: specifically, ANKRD13D showed a negative association with SDHD, while CCNF and PSMA7 exhibited positive associations with CYC1. These associations suggested that these NRGs may influence CRC progression by modulating mitochondrial processes, offering a potential mechanistic basis for their roles in CRC biology.

The application of single biomarkers in diagnosis and prognostic assessment often faces limitations. In contrast, multi-biomarker signatures, by integrating multiple relevant indicators, offer enhanced value in diagnosis and prognosis [32]. Building on this concept, a risk prediction model was successfully constructed incorporating CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7, which achieved AUC values exceeding 0.6 at 3, 5, and 7 years, indicating consistent moderate discriminative ability across different cohorts for CRC prognosis. As an advanced predictive tool, the core advantage of the nomogram lies in its ability to conduct highly personalized risk assessments based on specific patient characteristics and unique disease attributes [33]. We therefore developed a nomogram that integrates risk scores with relevant clinical parameters (age, stage, and T stage). This individualized assessment approach enables clinicians to more accurately predict individual CRC prognosis, thereby providing robust support for formulating more targeted treatment plans.

Our multi-omics investigation reveals a cohesive biological narrative centered on NEDD8-mediated neddylation in HRG. GSVA revealed significant differences in biological pathways between groups: including Notch [34–36] and VEGF signaling pathways [37, 38], were activated in the HRG. These pathways are not only pivotal for CRC progression but are also known to be regulated by neddylation. For instance, neddylation can stabilizes key Notch effectors such as Hairy and enhancer of split 1 (HES1), and is simultaneously required for VEGF-driven angiogenesis. The successful combination of NOTCH and neddylation inhibition in other cancers, along with the broad anti-angiogenic efficacy of MLN4924, suggests that targeting this neddylation-mediated oncogenic network holds significant promise for CRC therapy [39, 40].

This neddylation-driven network appears to extend into the tumor immune microenvironment. We observed significant infiltration of various immune cells in HRG, including NK cells, which are generally associated with favorable prognosis [41]. However, this potentially favorable immune landscape may be counteracted by neddylation-mediated mechanisms, as which has been documented to influence the M1/M2 polarization balance of macrophages and their secretory profiles (which may serve as targets for cancer therapy), ultimately worsening the prognosis [42–45]. Our scRNA-seq analysis pinpointed macrophages, particularly the M1 subset, as a primary site of neddylation pathway activity (exhibited higher scores for the neddylation gene set). This is supported by established biology: recent studies have identified cGAS (which is involved in chemoresistance and tumor immune microenvironment regulation in CRC [46]) as a neddylation substrate in macrophages [47]. Collectively, these insights suggest that neddylation activity within macrophages may subvert anti-tumor immunity and contribute to the aggressive phenotype of the HRG. Regarding immune checkpoints, TIGIT showed significant differences between different risk groups, consistent with its documented overexpression in the CRC tumor microenvironment [48]. Blocking TIGIT can effectively boost tumor-specific T cell immunity and prevent NK cell depletion in a colon cancer rat model in a way that is dependent on NK cells [49]. Given that the function and stability of numerous immune checkpoints are regulated by post-translational modifications [50], it is plausible that neddylation may contribute to this phenomenon in HRG. Future studies should investigate whether TIGIT is a substrate for neddylation-regulated ubiquitination.

These collective findings highlight the potential of NRGs as prognostic biomarkers for CRC therapy, though the current conclusions are tempered by several study limitations. First, constrained by funding and sample availability, our analysis primarily relied on transcriptome data from public sources. Although the prognostic model demonstrated moderate predictive ability (as indicated by the AUC values), its validation was limited to a single external dataset (GSE39582). Future evaluations using multi-center, large-scale clinical cohorts or additional independent datasets would strengthen confidence in the model’s robustness. Furthermore, comparative analyses with existing CRC prognostic models or random gene sets are needed to better ascertain its added clinical value. Second, among the key prognostic genes identified, only ANKRD13D has been experimentally validated in this study. The roles of the other two genes, CCNF and PSMA7, remain unverified in CRC tissues or cell lines at either the mRNA or protein levels, which limits the assessment of their potential as therapeutic targets. Moreover, the current functional validation of ANKRD13D focused solely on its influence on CRC cell proliferation and migration. Further mechanistic investigations, such as exploring its interaction with EGFR, conducting ubiquitination assays, or performing rescue experiments—coupled with more stringent screening criteria (e.g., multiple testing correction for comparative analyses of immune features) are essential to identify its direct downstream targets, core signaling pathways. Finally, the scRNA-seq analysis was based on static RNA sequencing data from a relatively small sample size, which may not fully capture the complete spectrum of tumor heterogeneity. This limitation, along with the inherent inability of such data to reflect real-time transcriptional states and cellular behaviors in a dynamic microenvironment, constrains the interpretation of cell–cell interaction mechanisms. Future investigations could overcome these constraints by leveraging multi-omics integration to infer regulatory dynamics, and by employing spatial transcriptomics to directly contextualize cell-cell interactions within the tissue architecture.

Conclusion

Through the integration of multiple datasets, this study has systematically illuminated the crucial role of NRGs in CRC. We identified three key prognostic genes—CCNF, ANKRD13D, and PSMA7—and developed a risk prediction model based on these genes. In vitro experiments preliminarily validated the function of ANKRD13D in CRC cells, suggesting that it may play an important role in the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells. Furthermore, scRNA-seq analyses revealed the elevated expression of the neddylation gene set in M1 macrophages within the CRC tumor microenvironment. This study exhibits exploratory predictive value for clinical practice in CRC management.
