Injuries at elite field hockey tournaments: results of a new injury assessment protocol
Authors: Till-Martin Theilen, Yannick Braun, Bibhu Nayak, Benita Kiat-Tee Tan, Leigh Gordon, Udo Rolle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAccurate assessment of injury severity is crucial for effective injury risk management in elite sports. To standardise the documentation of injury severity, the International Hockey Federation (FIH) introduced a new injury assessment protocol (IAP), completed by FIH medical officials. This study aimed to evaluate the application of the IAP, with a specific focus on capturing injury severity.MethodsInjury data, including injury site, affected body part, cause and type of injury, were collected during the Olympic Games (2021, 2024), Commonwealth Games (2022) and Women’s FIH World Cup (2022). Medical officials classified injury severity based on estimated absence from competition: mild (1–7 days), moderate (8–28 days) and severe (>28 days).ResultsThe overall injury incidence was 39.71 per 1000 player match hours in men and 29.99 in women. Overall, 22 injuries were classified as moderate, representing 8.9% of male and 14.4% of female injuries. Four severe injuries were recorded (3.3% in men, 1.0% in women). The remaining cases were no time-loss injuries and mild injuries (87.8% in men, 84.6% in women). Among female athletes, 64.3% (9/14) of moderate injuries involved the head. Among men, 75.0% (6/8) of moderate injuries affected the upper and lower extremities. Severe injuries included two contusions, one ACL tear and one temporal bone fracture.ConclusionThe IAP delivered comprehensive injury data, including severity. The introduction of the IAP will contribute to setting a new standard of injury surveillance in (elite) field hockey. Special attention will need to be paid to head injuries in future trials.Keywords: Injuries, Prevention, Field hockey, Olympics, Sporting injuries

Introduction

Modifying the ruleset to ensure a safe, fair and yet enjoyable sporting experience for athletes and spectators is a central role of every sports governing body. Injury prevention plays a key role in achieving this goal. The prevalence and severity of injuries not only form the public image of a sport but may also affect the number of new young athletes and the careers of amateur and professional players. Therefore, reliable injury data is essential to improve player safety to preserve the sport’s attractiveness and success.1 2

Field hockey is an Olympic team sport of great athleticism and speed. Due to its high-velocity play, the use of sticks and a solid ball that exceeds speeds of up to 100 km/hour, field hockey entails a possible risk for severe injuries. To address the injury risk, the International Hockey Federation (Fédération Internationale de Hockey; FIH) implemented a basic injury surveillance system at elite tournaments over 20 years ago. Currently, injuries that led to time stoppage by the umpire are recorded on a single-page match injury report (MIR) by FIH technical officers at the scoring table.

The current system has provided consistent data on injury epidemiology in elite field hockey tournaments.3 4 However, the data do not include information on injuries during play without time stoppage. The number of injuries may therefore be underestimated, as shown in a previous study of the 2016 EuroHockey U18 Championships, in which additional pitch-side observers documented injuries.5 Furthermore, the traditional FIH injury surveillance system has not captured injury severity, a crucial factor in evaluating safety measures and rule changes.6,8 Assessing injury severity is hardly assessable by the traditional system, since the technical FIH officials are not medically trained to diagnose whether an injury is mild or serious. To address these shortcomings, the FIH Health and Safety Committee has introduced a new injury assessment protocol (IAP) at the 2021 Olympic Games in Tokyo. The IAP involves FIH medical officials at the pitch side recording all injuries, including those not leading to time stoppage and assessing injury severity.

The presented study evaluated the application of the new IAP in elite men and women field hockey tournaments held between 2021 and 2024.

Methods

Data acquisition

The data were prospectively collected at elite field hockey tournaments between 2021 and 2024. The 2021 Olympic Games (Japan), the 2022 Commonwealth Games (UK), the 2022 Women’s FIH World Cup (Netherlands and Spain) and the 2024 Olympic Games (France) were included in the analysis. Only injuries sustained during the official playing time were recorded.

The IAP was designed by the FIH Health and Safety Committee based on previously published injury data and on general recommendations for injury recording.3 4 6 8 The IAP form is shown in online supplemental figure 1. IAPs were designed to be completed by FIH medical officials attending the matches. Based on previous publications, any new musculoskeletal complaint during play, which resulted in the stoppage of play by the umpire or affected the player’s ability to fully engage in the match for up to 20 s without time stoppage (no-time-loss injury), was counted as an injury.3 5

The IAP form included a total of 10 categories regarding the injury: player gender, player position (striker, midfielder, defender, goalkeeper), site (midfield; 25 yard; circle), penalty-related injury, time of play, injured body part (lower arm (including hand, forearm); upper arm (including elbow, shoulder); lower leg (including foot, ankle); upper leg (including knee, hip); head/face; trunk), cause (player-to-player; player-to-equipment; player-to-surface; hit-by-stick; hit-by-ball; non-contact-injury), type (sprain; contusion; laceration; bruise; concussion; other), the severity grade of an injury (defined as mild; moderate; severe; fatal) and return to play during the same match.

Injury severity was graded based on time-loss from play and practice: mild (absence of 1–7 days), moderate (absence of 8–28 days) and severe (absence greater than 28 days) according to the IOC consensus statement.6 Because long-term follow-up of each injury was not possible, FIH medical officials estimated the length of time-loss at the pitch-side or after further diagnostics had defined the type of injury (eg, fracture). Estimating the player’s likely absence by physicians to determine injury severity has previously been done during the Olympic Games in 2004 and 2008.9 10

In addition to the IAP, all medical injury reports (MIR), officially completed by non-medical FIH officials at the scoring table, were obtained. To place the newly assessed injury epidemiology data from the IAP in the context of the established MIR system, injury counts from both recording methods were compared across each match and tournament. The MIR recorded only injuries that required a time stoppage by the umpire. Injury severity was not assessed by the MIR. The MIR form is shown in online supplemental figure 2.

Study conduct

This study followed STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.6 11 In accordance with the FIH regulations, all players registering for international FIH events consent to the collection and statistical evaluation of injury data (article 11.1 and 11.2 in General Tournament Regulations Outdoor Competitions, FIH, May 2022). However, players retain the right to opt out of any medical injury survey should they choose not to do so. In the presented study, none of the players refused injury assessment. To protect player confidentiality and ensure the integrity of personal data, all injury-related information was anonymised prior to analysis.

Equity, diversity and inclusion

Sex and ethnicity exclusions were not used in this study. No specific effort was made to include or exclude minorities. The author group consisted of one woman and five men of different ethnicities, from four institutions across four countries, with different career stages and clinical disciplines.

Statistical analyses

To ensure comparability of injury incidences, the average number of injuries per match and the number of injuries per 1000 player match hours (PMH) including the 95% CIs were calculated.8 The total number of player-hours was defined as number of matches × minutes per game (60 min) × players per game (22 players) / 60 min. The Welch-Anova test was used to compare continuous variables between groups in table 1. The χ2 test was used to determine significant correlations between categorical variables in table 3. Statistical significance was assumed if p<0.05. The data were processed and analysed using Excel and R.

Results

Injury incidence of IAP and MIR recording methods

During the 7 tournaments (3 men’s tournaments, 4 women’s tournaments), a total of 250 matches (103 men, 147 women) were played in which 5500 PMH (2266 men, 3234 women) accumulated (table 1).

The number of injuries recorded by the IAP was compared with those captured by the MIR for the same tournaments to assess the impact of the new IAP on injury epidemiology. IAP and MIR report forms were available for all matches/injuries. Missing data in at least one injury-related category occurred in 9 IAP (4.8%) and in 15 MIR (9.8%) forms, which was not significantly different between the two recording methods (p=0.0738).

A significant difference in the number of injuries between IAP and MIR was seen only during the 2024 Olympic Games (table 1). For all other tournaments, injury counts did not differ between the recording methods. The higher number of injuries during the 2024 Olympic Games resulted in an overall greater number of injuries documented by the IAP in men, but not in women (men: 90 vs 70 injuries, p=0.0013; table 1).

Injury severity

The IAP captured injury severity. In 79 (87.8%)/90 injuries in men and 82 (84.6%)/97 injuries in women, athletes could continue to play immediately (no-time-loss injury) or could resume play after 8 days of recovery (mild injuries). In men’s tournaments, 8 (8.9%)/90 injuries were classified as moderate and 3 (3.3%)/90 injuries as severe. Among women, 14 (14.4%)/97 injuries were moderate and 1 (1.0%)/97 was severe (table 2). Online supplemental table 1 lists all moderate and severe injuries. This corresponded to one moderate injury every 12–13 matches or 3.5 moderate injuries per 1000 PMH in men, and one moderate injury every 10–11 matches, or 4.3 moderate injuries per 1000 PMH in women. Severe injuries were less frequent, occurring at a rate of 1.3 and 0.3 severe injuries per 1000 PMH among men and women, respectively (table 2). There were no career-ending or fatal injuries in the cohort.

The injured body part did not differ significantly among injury severity grades (table 3). In comparison between men and women athletes, women showed more injuries to the head/face with 45 (46.4%)/97 injuries compared with 24 (26.7%)/90 injuries in men (p=0.033). The lower leg was the most commonly affected body part in men (25/90 injuries; 27.8%; table 3).

The injury cause was significantly different among the three injury severity grades, both in men and women (p=0.0194 and p<0.0008, respectively). In men, the injury cause shifted from ball-related mild injuries (46.8%) to stick-related moderate injuries (50.0%). In women, stick-related injuries were more common among mild (31.7%) than among moderate injuries (14.3%; table 3).

The injury type showed a significant difference among the severity grades in men and women (p<0.0001 and p<0.0001; table 3). As contusions were the most frequent injury type among mild injuries (68.4% in men and 61.0% in women), injuries such as fractures, skin lacerations and concussions were mainly part of moderate and severe injuries. Among moderate injuries, there were six bone fractures (two in men and four in women) and six lacerations (two in men and four in women). There were four fractures to a digital, one to a metacarpal and one to the nasal bone. One fracture to the temporal bone in a male athlete was graded as severe. All lacerations were rated as moderate injuries. Five lacerations occurred to the head/face (four in women, one in men) and one to a finger of a male player (table 3 and online supplemental table 1). All bone fractures and lacerations resulted from a hit by the ball or the stick. The category ‘Other injuries’ included one spontaneous and one traumatic shoulder subluxation among moderate injuries. One severe injury occurred due to a non-contact injury in a female athlete, resulting in an ACL tear (online supplemental table 1).

Injury patterns of moderate and severe injuries

Table 4 summarises descriptive data on selected categories associated with moderate and severe injuries. All severe injuries occurred in the circle. However, no further consistent pattern of injury cause or type could be identified. Head/face injuries were more commonly seen among female athletes (9/12 injuries); most were moderate (11/12 injuries) and primarily caused by ball contact (6/12 injuries), mainly leading to lacerations (5/12 injuries) and concussions (4/12 injuries). No specific pattern could be found for concussions (table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the application of a new IAP in field hockey tournaments of the highest playing level. Number of injuries, injury characteristics and injury severity were analysed.

Evaluation of the new IAP

The new IAP produced an injury dataset within the incidence range of previous reports on injury epidemiology in field hockey. Across all tournaments in this study, there was an average of 39.71 and 29.99 injuries per 1000 PMH in men and women using the IAP. Previous data in professional field hockey ranged from 36.2 to 57.9 injuries per 1000 PMH in men and 14.0 to 34.2 injuries per 1000 PMH in women, although with different recording methods.312,14

During the women’s Commonwealth Games 2022 and FIH World Cup 2022 less injuries were recorded by the IAP in comparison to the MIR. The discrepancy is likely due to the independent nature of the recording methods. Table officials may have interpreted a time stoppage as an injury, while medical officers may not have observed an injury. Overall, however, the IAP collected a higher number of injuries compared with the traditional MIR. This was expected since the MIR only listed injuries with time stoppage, while the IAP also included injuries during play without interruption. In addition, the pitch-side FIH medical officers were specifically assigned to record injuries, whereas table officers have multiple other recording duties. It can be assumed that an extra assignment of additional personnel with special medical training will produce a more complete (and accurate) data capture. For example, a higher injury count was recorded at the elite 2016 EuroHockey U18 Championships, where additional (non-medical) personnel recorded injuries with and without time stoppage.5

Injury severity assessment

Over the last two decades, new standards of injury recording have been elaborated and manifested in consensus statements such as in football and rugby.15 16 These standards not only include the collection of simple injury epidemiology data but also the assessment of injury severity. Assessing injury severity is essential for implementing an effective injury risk management in team sports.8 17

In the current study, the FIH adhered to the injury severity definition recently published by the International Olympic Committee, defining severity by time-loss.6 One of the main challenges of injury severity assessment has been incomplete data due to low follow-up rates. Therefore, to be independent of follow-up drop-outs, injury severity in the present study was estimated by FIH medical officials following the approach first used by Junge et al in the 2004 Olympic Games.9

In the past, many different definitions (eg, time-loss injuries, injuries leading to medical attention), severity metrics (eg, average days of time-loss, diverse end-points of time-loss) and data collection methods (eg, report forms, questionnaires, interviews, team physician/physiotherapist reports) of injury severity have been used in field hockey.913 18,21 The systematic review by Barboza et al, for example, summarised that the majority of injuries in elite field hockey are classified as mild.22 In Olympic tournaments, Engebretsen et al reported that 15.2% of injuries at the 2012 Olympic Games led to more than 7 days of absence, and Junge et al found that 2.3% of injuries at the 2004 Olympic Games resulted in a time-loss of greater than 1 month.9 13 However, these studies combined data from male and female athletes. A sex-specific dataset in elite field hockey has been published on female athletes in Canada. Based on reports from athletic therapists, 17% of injuries resulted in a time-loss of 8–21 days, while 2% led to more than 21 days of time-loss.19 All of these reports show a low incidence of severe injuries in field hockey in line with the findings of the IAP.

The introduction of the IAP at four major tournaments has yet to produce the largest dataset on injury severity in elite field hockey to date. However, injury severity was estimated rather than based on real-time follow-up. Further development of recording methods will be needed to further refine injury severity assessment in the future.

Identification of (moderate and severe risk) injury patterns

The IAP confirmed the high incidence of head injuries in women (46.4% in women vs 26.7% in men). This finding is in line with other studies showing that women sustained twice as many injuries to the head than men in Olympic, professional and U18 field hockey tournaments.3 5 9 In the literature, concussions are the most frequently reported head injuries in women’s field hockey, followed by contusions and lacerations to the head and face caused by player-to-player contact and the ball.9 23 In this study, the majority of head/face injuries were no-time-loss and mild injuries, but 9 (64.3%)/14 of moderate injuries were injuries to the head/face in women. These consisted of skin lacerations, concussions, a nasal contusion and a nasal fracture. Contrarily, 6 (75.0%)/8 of moderate injuries in men affected the upper and lower extremities with fractures and skin lacerations to fingers and the hand, a shoulder subluxation and an ankle sprain.

The circle remained the primary ‘hot spot’ for injuries, accounting for nearly half of all cases. This is consistent with a previous report.24 Notably, all four severe injuries recorded by the IAP occurred within the circle. These injuries happened without any specific injury pattern. However, two of the four injuries occurred during the play of a penalty corner and involved leg injuries (one tibial bone bruise and one knee bone abrasion). The tibial injury resulted from a high-speed ball striking a defending player. The risk of severe injuries to defenders during play of a penalty corner, particularly by high-speed or drag-flick balls, has been discussed before.24 25 Although only one severe ball-related injury during a penalty corner was recorded by the IAP, the risk, while seemingly low, cannot be ruled out entirely.

The other two severe injuries occurred during regular play. One male athlete sustained a temporal bone fracture from a hit by the ball during defence play unrelated to a penalty corner. Protective headwear was not mandatory in this situation. The other severe injury was a tear of the ACL in a female athlete. This injury occurred without any interference. Specific ACL injury rates in field hockey are lacking. In other team sports, women have shown a higher risk of ACL injuries compared with men.26 27

Limitations

Defining injury severity based on time-loss until return to play is considered the gold standard.6 However, tracking recovery of injured athletes is a complex challenge for injury research teams, especially during large-scale international events such as the Olympic Games. In this study, time loss was estimated, therefore introducing a measurement bias. The injury evaluation at the field site depends largely on the subjective judgement of the examiner. Additionally, only injuries during active play were recorded. Players who could continue to play but missed the following match due to an injury were not captured, adding a selection bias. Caution should also be exercised when interpreting the results of this study. The low number of moderate and severe injuries limits the ability to make broad decisions on potential rule changes. Further studies, with a larger accumulation of injury data, are needed to better identify injury mechanisms of concern over time.

Clinical implications

This study found that moderate and severe injuries account for 13.2% in male and 15.4% in female field hockey players. Head injuries made up close to 50.0% of the cases warranting close attention in future trials. Especially, it remains to be determined why female athletes appear to be more susceptible to head injuries and whether protective gear or rule changes are necessary.

Currently, the IAP relies on the ‘pen and paper’ documentation. Other sports have already developed electronic injury surveillance systems, as recently outlined by the United States Olympic and Paralympic Committee (USOPC).28 Developing app-based or web-based injury recording systems in field hockey may contribute to a modernised up-to-date injury surveillance, eventually enhancing data quantity and quality.

Conclusion

In the last decades, multiple datasets on field hockey injuries have been applied, presenting a heterogeneous picture of injury epidemiology. The introduction of the IAP will contribute to establishing a new standard of injury surveillance in (elite) field hockey in the future. Continuous injury severity surveillance will define playing situations prone to severe injuries. It will be of interest if the effort of employing extra staff, for example, physicians, will contribute to a more complete and accurate injury dataset for effective evidence-based injury prevention in the future.
