Scaling Laws for Gradient Descent and Sign Descent for Linear Bigram Models under Zipf’s Law
Frederik Kunstner, Francis Bach

Abstract
Recent works have highlighted optimization difficulties faced by gradient descent in training the first and last layers of transformer-based language models, which are overcome by optimizers such as Adam. These works suggest that the difficulty is linked to the heavy-tailed distribution of words in text data, where the frequency of the k k th most frequent word π k \pi_{k} is proportional to 1 / k 1/k , following Zipf’s law. To better understand the impact of the data distribution on training performance, we study a linear bigram model for next-token prediction when the tokens follow a power law π k ∝ 1 / k α \pi_{k}\propto 1/k^{\alpha} parameterized by the exponent α > 0 \alpha>0 . We derive optimization scaling laws for deterministic gradient descent and sign descent as a proxy for Adam as a function of the exponent α \alpha . Existing theoretical investigations in scaling laws assume that the eigenvalues of the data decay as a power law with exponent α > 1 \alpha>1 . This assumption effectively makes the problem “finite dimensional” as most of the loss comes from a few of the largest eigencomponents. In comparison, we show that the problem is more difficult when the data have heavier tails. The case α = 1 \alpha=1 as found in text data is “worst-case” for gradient descent, in that the number of iterations required to reach a small relative error scales almost linearly with dimension. While the performance of sign descent also depends on the dimension, for Zipf-distributed data the number of iterations scales only with the square-root of the dimension, leading to a large improvement for large vocabularies.



1
Introduction

Recent works have shown that one of the primary benefits of Adam
(
Kingma & Ba, 2015
)

in training transformed-based language models
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)

lies in how it handles the first and last layers
(
Zhang et al., 2025
;
Zhao et al., 2025
)
.
For language models, the input and output dimensions correspond to distinct words in the vocabulary,
where the
k
th most frequent word has frequency
\pi_{k}\propto 1/k
following Zipf’s law
(
Piantadosi, 2014
)
.

Kunstner et al. (2024)
provide evidence that this heavy-tailed distribution
leads to optimization difficulties for gradient descent that Adam is able to overcome.
They argue that Zipf’s law is “worst-case” in that it combines a large imbalance in frequencies,
while decaying slowly enough that most samples come from the tail.

Our objective is to formalize this empirical observation,
and to describe the impact
of the heavy-tailedness of the data distribution
on the convergence of gradient descent (GD) and sign descent (SD)
as a proxy for Adam
(
Tieleman & Hinton, 2012
;
Bernstein et al., 2018
;
Balles et al., 2020
;
Chen et al., 2023
)
.
We consider a linear bigram model for next-token prediction trained with the square loss,
where the token frequencies
\pi_{k}
follow a power law
\pi_{k}\propto 1/k^{\alpha}
with exponent
\alpha>0
.
While this problem would be solved directly rather than with iterative methods,
it is a good starting point for the theoretical investigation of optimization dynamics.
Despite its apparent simplicity, this model already reproduces the observation
that GD performs poorly on Zipf-distributed data (see
Fig.

1
).
The behavior of gradient and sign descent are also not well described by current results,
see
Section

1.3
.

Our approach is inspired by the line of work on theoretical scaling laws,
or asymptotic convergence as the dimensionality grows
(e.g.,
Caponnetto & Vito, 2007
;
Advani et al., 2020
;
Berthier et al., 2020
;
Bahri et al., 2021
;
Cui et al., 2021
;
Maloney et al., 2022
;
Paquette et al., 2024
)
.
Instead of analyzing the generalization error of online gradient descent
as the dimension of the model and sample size grow,
we study the convergence rate of GD as the dimension and the number of iterations grow.
Spectral assumptions on the eigenvalues of the Hessian following a power-law are common in the literature,
as they correspond to assumptions on the covariance of the features.
But these works focus on power-laws that are not “too” heavy-tailed,
1/k^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>1
,
which lead to sublinear convergence rates independent of dimension.
In contrast, we focus on the case
\alpha\leq 1
where it becomes impossible to make progress with a fixed number of steps as the dimensionality grows.
We show that it is possible to make progress by finding the right scaling of the number of iterations with the dimension.

1.1
Contributions

1.

We propose a simplified model of the data frequencies
that allows for tractable closed-form dynamics on the linear bigram problem
for both GD and SD.
We show experimentally that the scaling under
that model matches empirical performance (
Fig.

2
).

2.

We derive scaling laws for GD and SD
under this simplified model as a function of
\alpha>0
.
These results cover the challenging case of
power-laws decreasing as slow or slower than Zipf’s law (
\alpha\leq 1
)
that is often ignored in asymptotic analyses.
This setting leads to a qualitatively different result,
requiring the number of iterations to grow with
d
.

3.

We show that sign descent with a well-selected step-size scales better with dimension
than GD for Zipf-distributed data.
This result confirms the benefits of SD
and preconditioning-like interventions
to mitigate the poor performance of GD.
However, this result is specific to the regime
\alpha\leq 1
,
as sign descent exhibits worse scaling otherwise,
showing that which algorithm performs better depends on properties of the data.

1.3
Related work

Convergence of Adam and sign descent.

Sign descent is a common proxy for Adam
as it captures the main property, that the updates are uniform across coordinates

(
Bernstein et al., 2018
;
Balles et al., 2020
;
Chen et al., 2023
)
.
This “scale-freeness” has been shown to reduce the dependence on the condition number of the problem
(
Zhuang et al., 2022
)
.
However, this result does not imply SD outperforms GD,
as known results for sign-like methods depend on the dimension
d
instead of the condition number
(e.g.,
Safaryan & Richtárik, 2021
;
Das et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
.
In the bigram problem with Zipf-distributed data, the dimension grows faster than the condition number, leading to worse guarantees for sign descent.
Instead of worst-case bounds, we rely on more fine-grained assumptions on the data and asymptotic equivalence to obtain the results of
Theorem

1.1
.
We compare our results to existing rates in
Appendix

B
.

SDE approximations of sign methods.

Scaling laws have been derived for online sign-like algorithms through
stochastic differential equations
(
Ma et al., 2021
;
Malladi et al., 2022
;
Xiao et al., 2024
;
Compagnoni et al., 2025
)
.
The focus of these works is on the scaling of the step-size with batch size
and the asymptotic stationary distribution of the algorithm which controls the generalization error.
As noise is not necessary to reproduce the performance gap between GD and Adam
(
Kunstner et al., 2023
)
,
we instead focus on the impact of heavy-tailed data on the deterministic dynamics.

Scaling laws and asymptotic results.

Empirical scaling laws have been developed to extrapolate the performance of deep networks at scale
and how to balance compute across model and data sizes
(
Rosenfeld et al., 2020
;
Kaplan et al., 2020
;
Hoffmann et al., 2022
)
.
Many works have contributed to the theoretical understanding of this scaling behavior
through high dimensional analyses and random matrix theory
(
Advani et al., 2020
;
Bahri et al., 2021
;
Maloney et al., 2022
;
Bordelon et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2024
;
Paquette et al., 2024
)
,
or using classical source/capacity conditions from learning theory
(
Caponnetto & Vito, 2007
;
Berthier et al., 2020
;
Cui et al., 2021
)
, see
Velikanov & Yarotsky (2024)
for the use of source/capacity conditions
in the context of optimization.
These works assume the eigenvalues decay as a power law with exponent
\alpha>1
.
The resulting scalings are consistent
with the observation that training dynamics converge to a well-defined limit
as width or depth increases
(
Yang et al., 2021
;
Bordelon et al., 2024a
;
Noci et al., 2024
)
,
but does not describe the regime
\alpha\leq 1
, which includes Zipf’s law.
This regime might be more relevant when considering scaling the vocabulary size,
as in the work of
Gowda & May (2020)
;
Tao et al. (2024)
.
While they hypothesize that larger vocabularies might lead to worse performance due to overfitting,
as larger vocabularies implies fewer examples per word,
we show that larger vocabulary size introduces difficulties
in getting the
training error
down.
Closest to our work is perhaps the blog post of
Bulatov (2023)
,
who show that the loss under GD
should approximately behave as
-\log(t/d)

on a problem matching our setting with
\alpha=1
.
Our work provides a formal justification for this scaling.

Problem setup

In this section, we present the problem setting,
the modeling assumptions we introduce to make the problem tractable,
and the approach we use to derive our results.
We start from a convex quadratic in reduced form,

f({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})
,
where the eigenvalues/vectors pairs of
{\mathbf{A}}
are
(\lambda_{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}{}^{d}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
.
The loss can be expressed in terms
of the distance to the solution along each eigenvector,
\delta_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}}\right\rangle
,
as
f({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum\!{}_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}({\mathbf{x}}){}^{2}
.
The dynamics of GD with step-size
\eta
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}-\eta{\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})
,
are also available in closed-form,

\displaystyle\!\!f({\mathbf{x}}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\eta{\mathbf{A}})^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathbf{x}}_{0}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}}\right)}}\right)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!{\mathbf{A}}{(1-\eta{\mathbf{A}})^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathbf{x}}_{0}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{{d}}\lambda_{i}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{2t}\delta_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})^{2}\!.

(1)

The specific quadratic problem we focus on is the
linear bigram model with the square loss.

Problem 2.1

(Linear bigram model with square loss)
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be
n
samples
representing one-hot encodings from
d
classes (or tokens),
with their concatenation
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{Y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times d}
.
We fit a linear model with weights
{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
using the square loss,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}})=\frac{1}{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}-{\mathbf{Y}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

We define
\pi_{k}
and
\pi_{k\,|\,j}

as the frequencies and conditional frequency statistics of the data,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\smash{i=1}}^{n}\mathds{1}_{x_{i}=k},

\displaystyle\pi_{k\,|\,j}\coloneqq\frac{\sum\!{}_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{y_{i}=k}\mathds{1}_{x_{i}=j}}{\sum\!{}_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{x_{i}=j}},\quad(\text{with the convention }\nicefrac{{0}}{{0}}=0)\quad\forall j,k\in[d].

The eigenvalues and distances to the solution
are directly related to the frequency statistics.

Proposition 2.2
.

The eigenvalues and distances to the solution
of
Problem

2.1
initialized at
{\mathbf{W}}\,{=}\,0
are

\displaystyle\smash{\lambda_{ij}=\pi_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\delta_{ij}(0)=\pi_{j\,|\,i}\quad\text{ for }i,j\in[d].}

Proof.

The optimization problem separates into
d
independent
d
-dimensional subproblems,

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}})=\frac{1}{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}-{\mathbf{Y}}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{w}}^{j}-{\mathbf{y}}^{j}}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
{\mathbf{w}}^{j},{\mathbf{y}}^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the
j
th columns of
{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{Y}}
.
Each subproblem has the same Hessian given by
{\mathbf{X}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\top}}{\mathbf{X}}/n=\Diag([\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d}])
,
so the eigenvalues are the frequencies, each with multiplicity
d
.
From
{\mathbf{w}}^{j}=0
, the distance to the solution is the magnitude of the solution
{\mathbf{s}}^{j}

of the normal equations,

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathbf{s}}^{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{X}}}}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{y}}^{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\pi_{1\,|\,j},\ldots,\pi_{d\,|\,j}\end{bmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top},

as
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{j}
are one-hot,

({\mathbf{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{y}}^{j})_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{x_{i}=k}\mathds{1}_{y_{i}=j}

and
({\mathbf{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{X}})_{kk}=\sum\!{}_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{x_{i}=k}
.

2.1
Modeling assumptions

Getting an interpretable form of the convergence of
Eq.

1

requires assumptions on the values of
\lambda_{i}
and
\delta_{i}
.
Assuming
\mu\leq\lambda_{i}\leq L

leads to the typical rates in smooth (strongly-)convex optimization,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})-\mathcal{L}^{*}\leq\frac{L\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}({\mathbf{w}}_{0})^{2}}{t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})-\mathcal{L}^{*}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L}}}\right)^{t}(\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{w}}_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{*}),

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the minimum loss
(see, e.g.,
Nesterov, 2018
)
.
While valid, these worst-case bounds are too coarse to capture the richness of the behavior of GD
and become vacuous in high-dimensions.
We compare our results to classical rates in
Appendix

B
.
To obtain more fine-grained results, we assume that the distributions
of the frequencies
\pi_{k}
and conditional frequencies
\pi_{k\,|\,j}
follow power laws.

Assumption 2.3

(Heavy-tailed data)
.

We assume that the frequencies and conditional frequencies
follow a frequency-rank power law with exponent
\alpha>0
.
That is, assuming the frequencies are sorted (
\pi_{k}\geq\pi_{k+1}
)
and defining the sorting permutations
\rho_{j}

such that
\pi_{\rho_{j}(k+1)\,|\,j}\geq\pi_{\rho_{j}(k)\,|\,j}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\propto\frac{1}{k^{\alpha}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi_{\rho_{j}(k)\,|\,j}\propto\frac{1}{k^{\alpha}},\quad\text{ for all }j,k,

where by
\pi_{k}\propto\nicefrac{{1}}{{k^{\alpha}}}
we mean the the distribution is normalized,

\pi_{k}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{zk^{\alpha}}}
where
z=\sum\!{}_{k=1}^{d}\nicefrac{{1}}{{k^{\alpha}}}
.

This assumption may appear strong,
as it would be satisfied for example if the words were sampled i.i.d.
with frequencies
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d}

as
\pi_{k\,|\,j}=\pi_{k}
.
But it does not require that all conditional distributions be the same,
and the most likely next-token after word
j
can depend on
j
.
This assumption merely asks that, once sorted,
the next-word frequencies also follow a power law with the same exponent.
Some distributions might deviate from this trend
if a token can only logically be followed by specific tokens,
or if the word being conditioned on is rare
and our dataset is relatively small.

2.2
Strategy for the analysis

Our goal is to derive scaling laws for the loss of
Problem

2.1
in
d
dimensions
after
t
steps,
\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)
, as
d\to\infty
.
Such scaling laws can be interpreted as approximating the convergence rate for large
d
,
or serve as a guide on how to scale the hyperparameters of the optimizer as we increase the vocabulary size.
Formally, we compute the asymptotic limit of the rate
r(t)

at which the relative loss decreases,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}r(t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right),\quad\text{ where $\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}$ is notation for }\quad\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}=r(t),

Works on scaling laws typically model the absolute value of the loss.
This approach degenerates when the loss at initialization vanishes or diverges as
d\to\infty

which happens when
\alpha\leq 1
.
Considering the relative decrease circumvents the issue,
as also noted by
Bulatov (2023)
;
Tao et al. (2024)
.

Another potential degeneracy is the scaling of time.
If the problem becomes more difficult as
d
grows, it might be impossible to make progress in finite time.
To take a concrete example, suppose that
\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}=0

and
\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)=r_{d}(t)\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)
with
r_{d}(t)=(1-1/d)^{t}
.
If we take the limit as
d\to\infty
for a fixed
t
,
we obtain
\lim_{d\to\infty}(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})^{t}=1
.
The rate no longer depends on
t
, and we cannot make progress unless
t
grows with
d
.
If we instead introduce a rescaled time variable
\tau
and scale
t_{d}(\tau)=\tau d
,
we recover a linear rate in the rescaled time
\tau

as
(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})^{\tau d}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}e^{-\tau}
.
This is the same issue encountered in random matrix theory,
where the dimensions of the matrix are taken to grow jointly with a fixed ratio
to avoid degenerate solutions
(
Potters & Bouchaud, 2020
)
.
It can be verified that
t_{d}(\tau)=\tau d
is the “right” scaling,
as the limit
r_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))
degenerates otherwise.
Using
f(x)\ll g(x)
for
\lim_{x\to\infty}\nicefrac{{f(x)}}{{g(x)}}=0
,
we have
r_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}1
if
t_{d}(\tau)\ll d

and
r_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}0
if
t_{d}(\tau)\gg d
;
we either make no progress or solve the problem instantly.
Our results are derived by taking
the finite dimensional rate
r_{d}(t)

with a scaling
t_{d}
such that the asymptotic rate
r(\tau)

is well defined in terms of the rescaled time
\tau
,

\displaystyle r(\tau)\coloneqq\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))=\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}.

5
Conclusion

We have presented scaling laws for gradient descent (GD) and sign descent (SD)
on the linear bigram model as a function of the power law exponent
\alpha
of the word frequencies.
Rather than hide the dimension dependence in problem specific constants,
we consider the scaling of running time and dimension as the problem grows in size
to get precise estimates of the scaling.
Our results highlight the benefit of SD
and the need to address ill-conditioning to improve the performance of GD.

Our results show that the typical neural scaling law
(1/\epsilon)^{p}
for some
p
is specific to the regime
\alpha>1
.
This regime may accurately describe the dynamics as we increase width or depth and the training dynamics converge,
but it might miss a large dimension dependence as we scale the vocabulary size.
The scaling we obtain for
\alpha<1
and
\alpha=1
have a different functional form and highlight the dependency on dimension.
For GD on Zipf-distributed data, the scaling of
d^{1-\varepsilon}

shows a non-trivial interplay between the desired error
\varepsilon
and the dimension.
Our results suggests that longer training might be required when scaling the vocabulary size.
Algorithms that target this dimension dependence, for example by estimating word frequencies
(
Li et al., 2022
)
, would be an interesting next step.

Our approach however has limitations.
We do not cover the online case, for which the analysis should be extendable using existings tools.
The addition of momentum for sign descent would be more complex but particulary interesting to dampen oscillations.
Handling more complex models such as bilinear models
(
Mikolov et al., 2013
)
or the cross-entropy loss would be interesting,
but it is not obvious how to extend the analysis without a closed-form solution for the training dynamics.
Finally, getting finite-dimensional results by tracking a correction term for finite
d
would be enlightening,
as the convergence to the asymptotic regime can sometimes be slow, especially in the case
\alpha=1
.

Appendix B
Comparison with worst-case rates

In this section, we compare our rates against results obtained using classical analyses
to highlight the benefit of the asymptotic analysis in capturing the dependence on dimension.
Our goal is not to imply those bounds are poor;
each of the work cited below studied a specific problem
and the assumptions were selected to highlight the impact of the condition number, non-convexity, variance, or other issue.
However, due to their worst-case generality,
existing results do not capture the dimension dependence on the problem of the linear bigram problem (
Problem

2.1
)
with Zipf-distributed frequencies (
Assumption

2.3
)
and predict worse behavior than actually observed.

In this section, we focus on Zipf-distributed data (
\alpha=1
)
as it is the most relevant to text data. To simplify notation,
we assume that the conditional frequencies directly
follow a power-law
\pi_{k\,|\,i}\propto 1/k
,
instead of assuming that there exists a reordering
\rho_{i}
such that
\pi_{\rho_{i}(k)\,|\,i}\propto 1/k

as in
Assumption

2.3
.
This reordering does not affect the dynamics of the loss and can be ignored without loss of generality.

B.1
Standard smooth, (strongly-)convex rates.

Classical results in smooth, convex optimization are derived
under the assumption that the objective function
\mathcal{L}_{d}
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu\geq 0
.
We write the function rates in matrix form for the loss
\mathcal{L}_{d}
defined in
Problem

2.1
,
but this could equivalently be transformed to a vector form using and
\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}\|^{2}_{2}=\|{\mathbf{W}}-{\mathbf{W}}_{*}\|^{2}
if

{\mathbf{x}}=\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{W}})
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{*}=\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{W}}_{*})
where
\mathrm{vec}
stacks the columns of
{\mathbf{W}}
as a single vector.
For a twice-differentiable function,
this is equivalent to assuming that the eigenvalues of the Hessian
are bounded by
\mu\leq\lambda_{ij}\leq L
for all
i,j\in[d]
at every possible input.
We compare against simple forms available in this setting
(
Nesterov (2018, Cor. 2.1.2)

Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004, Eq. 9.18)
).
While is possible to slightly improve the constants in these bounds,
these constants do not meaningfully affect the asymptotic behavior as
d
grows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}\leq\frac{2L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{W}}_{0}-{\mathbf{W}}_{*}}}\right\|^{2}_{F}}{t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L}}}\right)^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right).

To better compare these rates with our results, we normalize them by
\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}\leq\frac{L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{W}}_{0}-{\mathbf{W}}_{*}}}\right\|^{2}_{F}}{t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right)}\eqcolon r^{\mathrm{sub}}_{d}(t),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L}}}\right)^{t}\eqcolon r^{\mathrm{lin}}_{d}(t).

Proposition B.1

(Values of the constants)
.

On

\alpha=1
(

)

\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{sub}}_{d}(t)\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}2\frac{d}{\log(d)}\frac{1}{t},

\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{lin}}_{d}(t)\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{d}}}\right)^{t}.

The proof follow from substituting the constants with the values

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{dz},

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{z},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{W}}_{0}-{\mathbf{W}}^{*}}}\right\|^{2}_{F}=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}}_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right).

where

The eigenvalues are

Using that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}}_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}\delta_{ij}(0)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\pi_{j\,|\,i}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{zj}}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{W}}_{0}-{\mathbf{W}}_{*}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\delta_{ij}(0)^{2}=d\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{zj}}}\right)^{2}=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}}_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right).

∎

Both rates struggle to predict the progress in “early” iterations, when

d
.
The sublinear rate requires a scaling

Neither captures the progress that can be made by running

\varepsilon=1/2
.
Instead, both rates predict no progress.
We visualize the given rates in

The linear and sublinear rates are not converging to

(

B.2
Rates for sign descent

Analyses on sign-like methods in the literature
typically target more complex algorithms such as RMSProp
(
Tieleman & Hinton, 2012
)
or AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al., 2011
)
for
Das et al. (2024)
;
Liu et al. (2025)
,
or consider more general problems including non-convex functions for
Bernstein et al. (2018)
;
Safaryan & Richtárik (2021)
.
We are not aware of existing analyses that specifically target sign descent on diagonal quadratic problems such as
Problem

2.1
.
This makes a direct comparison difficult.
It might be that the rates described in those papers for the chosen problem setting or algorithm are tight.
However, our message is that the resulting rates are too pessimistic
even for a problem as simple as
Problem

2.1
and suggest runtimes for sign descent
that are off by a factor depending on the dimension.

The main difficulty in studying sign descent and sign-like methods more generally
is the strong dependence on the coordinate system used.
For
Problem

2.1
the dynamics perfectly separate along coordinates
which makes it possible to derive a closed form for the dynamics.
Other works typically rely on assumptions on the Hessian
that quantify how close to diagonal it is.
For example, bound the Hessian with a diagonal matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
,

{\mathbf{H}}\preceq{\mathbf{L}}
in Loewner ordering,
and obtain rates that depend on the trace of
{\mathbf{L}}

(e.g.,
Bernstein et al., 2018
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
.
For
Problem

2.1
, the Hessian is diagonal and made of
d

of
{\mathbf{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\mathbf{X}}/n=\Diag([\pi_{1},...,\pi_{d}])

\Tr({\mathbf{L}})=\Tr(\nabla^{2}\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{d}({\mathbf{W}}))=d
.

Anisotropic smoothness and AdaGrad.

Using this assumption,
Liu et al. (2020, Theorem 4.1)
show the following convergence rate for AdaGrad.
To simplify their results and show the rate in its best light,
we assume there is no noise in the gradient (
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\bm{\sigma}}}}\right\|_{1}=0
in their notation),
that AdaGrad is run with the parameter
\epsilon=0
,
that the algorithm is run with projections onto the constrained set
{\mathcal{W}}=\{{\mathbf{W}}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\mathbf{W}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\pi_{1}\}

and that we initialize at
{\mathbf{W}}=0
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}\leq\frac{\Tr({\mathbf{L}})\pi_{1}}{T}.

Normalizing the loss
and simplifying the constants
using the same approach as in

gives the following asymptotic upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}\leq r^{\mathrm{Adagrad}}_{d}(t)\coloneq\frac{\Tr({\mathbf{L}})\pi_{1}}{T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}}\right)}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\frac{d\log(d)}{T}\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}.

Although we might expect Adagrad to outperform sign descent as it uses decreasing step-sizes to avoid the oscillations,
this rate estimate that the number of iterations should scale with
d\log(d)

instead of the scaling of

we find for sign descent.

Preconditioning effect of Adam.

Das et al. (2024)

study RMSProp, or Adam without momentum (

They use high-probability arguments to handle the dynamics of the preconditioner and random initialization.
Their rate shows that Adam can perform better on diagonal quadratics

by replacing the condition number

\kappa_{\mathrm{Adam}}=\min\{d_{{\mathbf{W}}}+1,\kappa\}

Assuming that their bound holds with probability

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle t\geq\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{Adam}}}}\right).

Unfortunately, on

.

B.3
Source and capacity assumptions

The classical source/capacity condition have typically been used to describe
risk bounds in learning theory
for infinite dimensional kernel methods, random feature models
or regression models when the dimension
d
grows
(e.g.,
Caponnetto & Vito, 2007
;
Advani et al., 2020
;
Berthier et al., 2020
;
Bahri et al., 2021
;
Cui et al., 2021
;
Maloney et al., 2022
;
Paquette et al., 2024
)
.
Using the notation of
Cui et al. (2021)

where
{\bm{\Sigma}}
is the covariance of the data
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{*}
is the solution,
the typical assumption is that for parameters
\alpha>1,r\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{\Sigma}}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}})<\infty,

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{*}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1-2r}{\bm{\theta}}_{*}<\infty.

For finite dimensionals system,
\Tr({\bm{\Sigma}}^{1}{\alpha})<\infty

should be interpreted as
\lim_{d\to\infty}\sum\!{}_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{1/\alpha}<\infty
,
where
\lambda_{k}
are the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
.
This implies that the data is “effectively” low-dimensional, even as
d\to\infty
.

Cui et al. (2021)
further assume that the eigenvalues of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and the components of
{\bm{\theta}}_{*}
follow power laws,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=k^{-\alpha},

\displaystyle[{\bm{\theta}}_{*}]_{k}^{2}=k^{-1-\alpha(2r-1)}.

Our setting follows a similar idea.
The parameter
\alpha
corresponds to our power law parameter
\alpha
governing the conditional frequencies
and the distance to the solution corresponds to the marginal probabilities.
In terms of assumptions, the main difference is
that we normalize the eigenvalues and the distance to the solutions
as those represent frequencies, and that we allow for
\alpha\leq 1

to describe Zipf-distributed data.
As we have
d^{2}
eigenvalues, where each distinct value is repeated
c
times,
we could collapse to a
d
-dimensional system with distances to the solution summed to obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\pi_{k}=\frac{1}{z}k^{-\alpha},

\displaystyle[{\bm{\theta}}_{*}]_{k}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\pi_{j\,|\,k}^{2}=\frac{1}{z^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}j^{-2\alpha}.

Up to the normalization constant
z
,
we recover the source/capacity condition with the same
\alpha
and
r=1/2

as the distance to the solution
[{\bm{\theta}}_{*}]_{k}^{2}
is independent of
k
.
The main conceptual difference is that we use this setting to investigate
the performance of deterministic optimization algorithms, as
Velikanov & Yarotsky (2024)
,
instead of investigating risk bounds.

Appendix C
Proofs for gradient descent

This section gives the proof of
Theorem

3.1
for the scaling of gradient descent.

C.1
Standard results

We start with standard results that are used in the subsequent proofs.
The following classical relationships between sums and integrals of monotone functions
will be used to bound the approximation error
induced by analyzing the asymptotics of the integral
instead of the sum.

Lemma C.1

(Sum-Integral)
.

For a function
f
that is monotone on
[a,b]
,

if
f
is increasing on
[a,b]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}f(k)\leq\int_{a}^{b}f(k)\,\mathrm{d}k\leq\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}f(k),

if
f
is decreasing on
[a,b]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a+1}^{b}f(k)\leq\int_{a}^{b}f(k)\,\mathrm{d}k\leq\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}f(k).

To apply these sum-integral relationships to the dynamics of gradient descent in
Theorem

3.1
,
we need to describe when they are increasing or decreasing.

Lemma C.2

(Unimodal sequence)
.

The sequence
s(k)=k^{-\alpha}(1-k^{-\alpha})^{t}
is non-negative on
k\geq 1
and unimodal.
It monotonically increases until
k_{*}=(1+t)^{1/\alpha}
,
then monotonically decreases.

Proof.

As
s(k)
is non-negative, we can instead look at its logarithm,

\displaystyle\log s(k)

\displaystyle=\log(N)-\alpha\log(k)+t\log(1-k^{-\alpha}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{k}}\log s(k)

\displaystyle=\alpha t\frac{k^{-\alpha-1}}{1-k^{-\alpha}}-\frac{\alpha}{k}=\frac{\alpha t}{k(k^{\alpha}-1)}-\frac{\alpha}{k}=\frac{\alpha(t-1)(k^{\alpha}-1)}{k(k^{\alpha}-1)}.

The denominator is positive on
k\geq 1
,
and the numerator is positive for small
k
until
the derivative changes sign at
\alpha t-\alpha(k^{\alpha}-1)=0
, or

k_{*}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}
.
∎

At she partial sum
H_{d,\alpha}=\sum\!{}_{k=1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}
,
appears in the proof of gradient and sign descent,
we give its asymptotic behavior independently.

Lemma C.3

(Normalizer Asymptotics)
.

As
d
grows, the partial sum
H_{d,\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}
behaves as

\displaystyle H_{d,\alpha}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}d^{1-\alpha}\,\text{ if }\,\alpha<1,

\displaystyle H_{d,1}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\log(d)

\displaystyle H_{d,\alpha}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\zeta(\alpha)\,\text{ if }\,\alpha>1,

where
\zeta
is the zeta function,
defined as the limit of
H_{d,\alpha}
,

\zeta(\alpha)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-\alpha}<\infty
for
\alpha>1
.

Proof.

For
\alpha>1
, the sum converges to
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-\alpha}=\zeta(\alpha)
.
For
\alpha\leq 1
, the sum diverges as
d
grows.
As the sequence
k^{-\alpha}
is decreasing in
k
,
we can use the
Sum-Integral Lemma
(
C.1
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{d+1}k^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}k\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}k.

If
\alpha<1
, the integrals evaluate to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((d+1)^{1-\alpha}-1}}\right)}{1-\alpha}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d^{1-\alpha}-1}}\right)+1}{1-\alpha},

and both terms are asymptotically equivalent to
d^{1-\alpha}/(1-\alpha)
as
d\to\infty
.
If
\alpha=1
, this gives

\displaystyle\log(d+1)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}k^{-\alpha}\leq\log(d)+1.

Both terms are asymptotically equivalent to
\log(d)
.
∎

The main purpose of the
Sum-Integral Lemma
(
C.1
) and the
Unimodal Lemma
(
C.2
)
is to bound on the error incurred
by approximating the sum with the integral form of the loss.

Lemma C.4

(Approximating error)
.

The approximation error between the following sum and integral,

\displaystyle S_{d}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}s(k)

\displaystyle I_{d}(t)=\int_{1}^{d}s(k)\mathrm{d}k

where

\displaystyle s(k)=k^{-\alpha}(1-k^{-\alpha})^{t}

can be bounded by the following error term,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S_{d}(t)-I_{d}(t)}}\right|\leq\delta_{d}(t)\quad\text{ where }\quad\delta_{d}(t)\coloneq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{1+t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{1+t}}}\right)^{t}&\text{ if }1+t\leq d^{\alpha},\\
\frac{1}{d^{\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{d^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{t}&\text{ if }1+t\geq d^{\alpha}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By the
Unimodal Lemma
(
C.2
), the sequence
s(k)
is increasing until
k_{*}=(1+t)^{1/\alpha}
then decreasing,
which lets us use the
Sum-Integral Lemma
(
C.1
).

For large
t
.

Suppose that
t
is sufficiently large such that
k_{*}\geq d
and
1+t\geq d^{\alpha}
,
meaning that the sequence
s(k)
is increasing on
[1,d]
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+s(1)\leq\sum_{1=1}^{d}s(k)\leq\int_{1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+s(d).

(6)

Using that
s(1)=0
gives
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|I_{d}(t)-S_{d}(t)}}\right|\leq s(d)
when
t
is large.

For small
t
.

If
t
is small and
k_{*}<d

the sequence flips from increasing to decreasing on
[1,d]
.
We still use the same idea, but bound the increasing and the decreasing subsequences separately.

Upper bound.

As the sequences
s(k)
in increasing on
[1,k_{*}]
and decreasing on
[k_{*},d]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor-1}s(k)\leq\int_{1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+2}^{d}s(k)\leq\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k.

Summing both bounds and adding the remaining terms

s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor),s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}s(k)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}\!\!\!\!s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}^{d}\!\!\!\!s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor)+s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1)\leq\int_{1}^{d}\!\!s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+s(k_{*}),

where the last inequality uses the following simplifications,

\displaystyle\min\{s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor),s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1)\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}\min\{s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor),s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1)\}\mathrm{d}k\leq\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}s(k)\mathrm{d}k,

\displaystyle\max\{s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor),s(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1)\}

\displaystyle\leq s(k_{*}).

Lower bound.

Now using the lower bound,

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}s(k),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k\leq\sum_{k=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}^{d-1}s(k).

Summing both bounds, we can complete the integral
by adding and subtracting
\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k

and adding the remaining terms
s(1)
and
s(d)
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}s(k)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k-\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k_{*}}}\right\rfloor+1}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k+s(1)+s(d)\geq\int_{1}^{d}s(k)\,\mathrm{d}k-s(k_{*})+s(d),

where the last inequality uses that
s(1)=0
,
s(k)\leq s(k_{*})
.

Combining the results
for the small
t
regime gives

\displaystyle I_{d}(t)+s(k_{*})\geq S_{d}(t)\geq I_{d}(t)-s(k_{*})+s(d),\quad\text{ so }\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|I_{d}(t)-S_{d}(t)}}\right|\leq s(k_{*}).

The final bound
in
Lemma

C.4
expands
s(x)=x^{-\alpha}(1-x^{-\alpha})^{t}

and replaces
k_{*}
by
(1+t)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
.
∎

Lemma C.5

(Asymptotics of the integrals)
.

Let

be the integral form given in

The following limits hold.

\displaystyle\text{If }\alpha<1,

\displaystyle t_{d}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau d^{\alpha},

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{I_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))}{H_{d,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}E_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\tau)\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\tau$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\tau+1},

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}d^{\tau},

\displaystyle\text{if }\alpha>1,

\displaystyle t_{d}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau,

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{I_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))}{H_{d,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},1+2t}}\right)}{\alpha\zeta(\alpha)}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\tau$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}C\frac{1}{\tau^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\mathcal{L}_{d}(0),

Proof.

For
\alpha>1

We use the change of variable

\displaystyle I_{d}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{d^{-\alpha}}^{1}z^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-z)^{t}\mathrm{d}z

As

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\alpha I_{d}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}z^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-z)^{t}\mathrm{d}z\eqcolon B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},1+t}}\right).

As

As it is not easy to intuit the rate from the Beta function,

Using Stirling’s formula, the Beta function behaves as

The integral can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{\tau d^{\alpha}}\!\!\!\!z^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{z}{\tau d^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{\tau d^{\alpha}}\!\!\!\!\!=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\!\!a(z,d)\mathrm{d}z\,\,\text{ where }\,\,a(z,d)\coloneq\mathds{1}_{\{z\leq\tau d^{\alpha}\}}z^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{z}{\tau d^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{\tau d^{\alpha}}\!\!\!.

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{I_{d}(\tau d^{\alpha})}{H_{d,\alpha}}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}E_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\tau).

To simplify for large

we use the fact that the generalized exponential integral

in decreasing in

\nicefrac{{e^{-\tau}}}{{\tau+n}}\leq E_{n}(\tau)\leq\nicefrac{{e^{-\tau}}}{{\tau+n-1}}

(
DLMF, 2025

Â§8.19(ix)

Both bounds are asymptotically equivalent to
e^{-\tau}/(\tau+1)
.

For
\alpha=1

we use the change of variable
k=d^{z}
or
z=\log_{d}(k)
to get

\displaystyle I_{d}(t)=\log(d)\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-d^{-z}}}\right)^{t}\mathrm{d}z.

The normalizer scales as
H_{d,\alpha}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\log(d)
(

C.3
) so only the integral remains.
To make meaningful progress, we introduce the scaling
t_{d}(\tau)=d^{\tau}
for
\tau\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\frac{I_{d}(d^{\tau})}{\log(d)}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{d^{\tau-z}}{d^{\tau}}}}\right)^{d^{\tau}}\mathrm{d}z.

As

, the integrand converges to

if

and to

if

,
and is dominated by

so by the DCT we can swap the limit and integral to get

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{I_{d}(d^{\tau})}{H_{d,\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{d^{\tau-z}}{d^{\tau}}}}\right)^{d^{\tau}}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{\tau}0\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\tau}^{1}1\mathrm{d}z=1-\tau.

∎

Lemma C.6

(Approximation error is negligible)
.

Let
\delta_{d}(t)
be the upper bound on the approximation error

(

).
We have that

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\delta_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))}{I_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))}=0\text{ if }\alpha\leq 1,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\delta_{d}(t)}{I_{d}(t)}=0\text{ if }\alpha>1.

Recall that the bound approximation error

(

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S_{d}(t)-I_{d}(t)}}\right|\leq\delta_{d}(t)\quad\text{ where }\quad\delta_{d}(t)\coloneq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{1+t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{1+t}}}\right)^{t}&\text{ if }1+t\leq d^{\alpha},\\

,

t
does not scale with

In this regime,

\displaystyle\delta_{d}(t)=\frac{1}{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{t+1}}}\right)^{t}\leq\frac{1}{t+1}.

\displaystyle x_{*}(\tau)=\arg\min_{0<x\leq 1}\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(T_{d}(\tau),dx).

These choices result in the scaling

\displaystyle r(\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\alpha c_{2}&\text{ if }\tau\leq\sqrt{\frac{1-c_{1}}{4c_{2}}}\\
\frac{(c_{1}+c_{2}4\tau^{2})^{2\alpha}}{4\tau^{2}}&\text{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.76662pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.6333pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\tau$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}c_{2}^{2\alpha}\frac{1}{(2\tau)^{2-4\alpha}}.

Proof.

Substituting
\phi=dx
, taking the limit as
d\to\infty
,
and using that
H_{d,p}\sim\frac{d^{1-p}}{1-p}
for
p<1
,
define
f_{\tau}(x)
as the limit of
r_{d}(\tau,dx)
as
d
grows,

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(x)=\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(\tau,dx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{H_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor dx}}\right\rfloor,2\alpha}-2H_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor dx}}\right\rfloor,\alpha}(dx)^{-\alpha}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor dx}}\right\rfloor(dx)^{-2\alpha}+\frac{d-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor dx}}\right\rfloor}{4\tau^{2}}(dx)^{-2\alpha}}{H_{d,2\alpha}},

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}x^{1-2\alpha}-2\frac{1}{1-\alpha}x^{1-2\alpha}+x^{1-2\alpha}+\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}x^{-2\alpha}-\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}x^{1-2\alpha}}{\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}}.

We will show that our choice of step-size
corresponds to taking
r(\tau)=\min_{0<x\leq 1}f_{\tau}(x)
.
Gathering terms,
f_{\tau}(x)
is proportional to the following polynomial

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(x)\propto x^{1-2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}-2\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}x^{-2\alpha},

which has a unique stationary point at

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha}{4\tau^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-2\alpha)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}-2\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}}}\right)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}4\tau^{2}}}\right)^{-1}.

If
x_{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)\not\in[0,1]
,

f_{\tau}(x)
must be decreasing over
[0,1]

as
\lim_{x\to 0}f_{\tau}(x)=\infty
and
r_{\tau}(1)
is finite,
and the minimum must be at
1
.
If the stationary point is in
(0,1]
, it must be the minimum.
This gives

\displaystyle x_{*}=\arg\min_{0<x\leq 1}f_{\tau}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}x_{\mathrm{stat}}&\text{ if }0<x_{\mathrm{stat}}\leq 1,\\
1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

and
0<x_{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)\leq 1
is equivalent to
\tau\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha^{2}}}
.
If
\tau\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha^{2}}}

and
x_{*}(\tau)=1
, we get

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(x_{*}(\tau))=1-2\frac{1-2\alpha}{1-\alpha}+(1-2\alpha)=2\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha}.

If
\tau<\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha^{2}}}

and
x_{*}(\tau)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}4\tau^{2}}}\right)^{-1}

we get

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(x_{*}(\tau))

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2\alpha}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{1-2\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{1-2\alpha}-2\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}x^{-2\alpha}}}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}4\tau^{2}}}\right)^{2\alpha}}{4\tau^{2}},

which can be simplified for large
\tau
as

f_{\tau}(x_{*}(\tau))\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\tau$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}\right)^{2\alpha}\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\tau}}\right)^{2-4\alpha}}
.
∎

D.3
Scaling of sign descent for
\alpha>1/2

For
\alpha>1/2
,
the expression for the loss does not simply as
d\to\infty
.
The conditional frequencies decay fast,
meaning that most of the loss comes from the few high-frequency words.
As a result, we cannot define the scaling of the step-size
as the minimization problem for the optimal scaling in the limit
d\to\infty
.
Instead, we use the fact that the (normalized) loss can not converge to 0
unless all components enter the oscillatory regime,
at which point we can compute an optimal step-size.

Proposition D.4
.

For the relative loss defined in
Definition

D.1
,
if
\alpha>1/2

and
4T^{2}\geq\frac{d-1}{2^{\alpha}-1}
, the optimal-step size is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{*}(d,T)=\arg\min_{\phi}r_{d}(T,\phi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{d-1}{4T^{2}}}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

This gives the following scaling
for
\tau^{2}>\nicefrac{{1}}{{(2^{\alpha}-1)}}

\displaystyle T_{d}(\tau)=\tau\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{d},

\displaystyle\phi(\tau)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{1/\alpha},

\displaystyle r(\tau)=\frac{1}{\zeta(2\alpha)}\frac{1}{1+\tau^{2}}.

Proof.

If
\phi\geq 2
,
the normalized loss is lower-bounded by the error on the first two components,

\displaystyle r_{d}(T,\eta(T,\phi))

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\phi}}\right\rfloor}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k^{-\alpha}-\phi^{-\alpha}}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{k=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\phi}}\right\rfloor+1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2T\phi^{\alpha}}}}\right)^{2}}{H_{d,2\alpha}}.

This is lower-bounded by a constant
C>0
independently of
T
,
and implies that we cannot make progress by running longer unless
\phi<2
.
If only the first component is oscillating, the optimal
\phi
is

\displaystyle\phi_{*}(d,T)=\argmin_{\phi}r_{d}(T,\eta(T,\phi))=\argmin_{\phi}(1-\phi^{-\alpha})^{2}+\frac{d-1}{4T^{2}}\phi^{-2\alpha}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{d-1}{4T^{2}}}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

To be consistent with only having two components oscillating, this requires

\phi_{*}(d,T)\leq 2
,
giving the constraint that this only holds when

(1+\frac{d-1}{4T^{2}})^{1/\alpha}\leq 2
or

4T^{2}\geq\frac{d-1}{2^{\alpha}-1}
.
Taking the scaling
4T_{d}(\tau)^{2}=\tau^{2}d
gives the limit

\displaystyle\phi(\tau)=\lim_{d\to\infty}\phi_{*}(d,T_{d}(\tau))=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\quad\text{ if }\quad\tau^{2}>\frac{1}{2^{\alpha}-1},

and the asymptotic loss

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(T_{d}(\tau,d),\phi(\tau))

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\phi(\tau)^{-\alpha}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\phi(\tau)^{-2\alpha}}{\zeta(2\alpha)},

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-1}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}}}\right)^{-2}}{\zeta(2\alpha)}=\frac{1}{1+\tau^{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta(2\alpha)},

where
H_{d,2\alpha}\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle d$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\zeta(2\alpha)
, the Riemann zeta function.
∎

Proposition E.1

(Formal version of
Theorem

1.1
for gradient descent)
.

To reach a relative loss of
\varepsilon

3.1

t
needs to scale with

\displaystyle\text{ If }\alpha<1,

\displaystyle t_{d}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=d^{\alpha}\tilde{\Theta}(\log(1/\varepsilon)),

\displaystyle\text{ if }\alpha=1,

\displaystyle t_{d}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=d^{1-\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\text{ if }\alpha>1,

\displaystyle t_{d}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C/\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\text{ where }C=\tfrac{\Gamma(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}{\alpha\zeta(2\alpha)}.

Proof.

For

3.1

\displaystyle r(\tau)\coloneq\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(t_{d}(\tau))-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}=1-\tau\text{ with }t_{d}(\tau)=d^{\tau}\quad\implies\quad\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}_{d}(d^{1-\varepsilon})-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}{\mathcal{L}_{d}(0)-\mathcal{L}_{d}^{*}}=\varepsilon.

For
\alpha>1
,

we know that

r(\tau)\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\tau$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\nicefrac{{C}}{{\tau^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}}
.
Letting
\tau(\varepsilon)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C/\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}

gives that
r(1/\varepsilon)\mathrel{\raisebox{-0.74997pt}{\smash{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-0.59789pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1/\varepsilon$}}}{{\sim}}$}}}\varepsilon
as

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(\tau)\frac{1}{C}\tau^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}=1\implies\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(1/\varepsilon)\frac{1}{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C/\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(1/\varepsilon)\varepsilon=1.

For
\alpha<1

We have that
\lim_{d\to\infty}r_{d}(\frac{1}{2}\tau d^{\alpha})=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}E_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\tau)

The exact solution is

\displaystyle\tau(\varepsilon)=E_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\varepsilon}}\right),

where

To get an idea of its growth, we show

\varepsilon

\displaystyle\tau_{-}(\epsilon)\leq\tau(\varepsilon)\leq\tau_{+}(\varepsilon)\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\varepsilon\leq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e^{2-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rfloor},\frac{W(6)}{6}}}\right\}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha},

\displaystyle\text{ where }\tau_{+}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right),

\displaystyle\tau_{-}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right)-\!\log\!\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rceil.

To show that
\tau(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle E_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rceil}(\tau)\leq E_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\tau)\leq E_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rfloor}(\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\tau+n}\leq E_{n}(\tau)\leq\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\tau+n-1}\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

\displaystyle a(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\tau+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rceil}\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{e^{-\tau}}{\tau+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rfloor-1}\eqcolon b(\tau).

\tau_{+}
excludes
\varepsilon

,
and that
\tau_{-}
excludes
\varepsilon
from the left,
\varepsilon<a(\tau_{-}(\varepsilon))
,

\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.
As both bounds decrease with
\tau

\tau(\varepsilon)
must increase
grow faster than
\tau_{-}(\varepsilon)
but slower than
\tau_{+}(\varepsilon)
.

For the upper bound,
we show that

is
\varepsilon

\displaystyle b(\tau_{+}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{e^{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\varepsilon}}\right)}}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rfloor-1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right\rfloor-1}.

\varepsilon
