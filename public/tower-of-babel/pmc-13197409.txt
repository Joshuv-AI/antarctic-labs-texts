Extending the boundaries of ultraviolet-visible meta-optics via direct imprinting of tantalum pentoxide composite
Authors: Eunji Lee, Hyunjung Kang, Heechang Yun, Junsuk Rho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Broadband metasurfaces capable of precise electromagnetic wave control from the ultraviolet (UV) to the visible range enable applications such as multispectral imaging, secure multi-band optical encryption, and multi-spectral microscopy. However, realizing scalable UV-visible broadband metasurfaces remains challenging due to the subwavelength critical dimensions and high aspect ratios required for UV operation, which are difficult to fabricate reliably using conventional lithography. These difficulties are further compounded by the intrinsic trade-off between refractive index and absorption in dielectric materials such as TiO2 and SiC, which exhibit strong UV absorption. Here, we introduce a scalable nanoimprint lithography (NIL) platform based on tantalum pentoxide (Ta2O5) particle-embedded resin (PER) that overcomes these material and fabrication limitations. The Ta2O5 PER exhibits a refractive index of 1.9 at 300 nm and maintains a relatively high refractive index with negligible absorption across the visible range. This material is also compatible with diverse meta-atom geometries. Using this approach, we fabricated a broadband metahologram and a hyperbolic phase metalens. Electromagnetic simulations predict conversion and focusing efficiencies approaching 80% at 320 nm, and experimental validation yielded 64% conversion efficiency for the metahologram and 61.3% focusing efficiency for the metalens. The metasurfaces consistently reconstruct designed images across 320–635 nm, confirming broadband operation. This one-step direct patterning enabled high aspect ratio without additional deposition or etching processes. This provides a practical route toward scalable, high-performance metasurfaces spanning the UV-visible spectrum.

Introduction

Metasurfaces have recently attracted considerable attention as advanced platforms capable of precisely controlling the amplitude, phase, polarization, and wavefront of electromagnetic waves at subwavelength scales1–4. They have demonstrated immense potential across a broad spectral range, from ultraviolet (UV) to near-infrared (NIR), enabling innovative applications such as imaging5, sensing6, and holographic displays7,8. Accordingly, a variety of material platforms have been widely explored, given their central role in determining the operational bandwidth and optical performance of metasurfaces across different spectral ranges. Silicon-based materials are widely used for NIR applications9,10, while titanium dioxide (TiO₂)11,12 and gallium nitride (GaN)13–15 are preferred for the visible range. Wide-bandgap materials such as zirconium dioxide (ZrO2)16, hafnium oxide (HfO2)17, and aluminum nitride (AlN)18 are extensively employed for UV operation.

Despite these advances, realizing metasurfaces that operate across both the UV and visible ranges remains challenging. Achieving broadband operation further requires materials that provide both sufficiently high refractive index and negligible optical loss across the relevant spectral span. Broadband-compatible deposited dielectrics, such as chemically vapor-deposited silicon nitride (Si₃N₄)19–21 and sputtered tantalum pentoxide (Ta2O5)22, can satisfy these optical requirements. However, their fabrication typically requires thick-film deposition followed by high-resolution electron beam lithography (EBL) and multi-step etching. These processes are costly, time-intensive, and difficult to scale for mass production23. In addition, while photolithography is highly scalable, achieving UV subwavelength features below 200 nm is difficult. Thus, scalable fabrication of broadband UV-visible metasurfaces has remained elusive. Overcoming both the material and fabrication barriers is therefore essential to enable practical metasurfaces for UV-visible applications.

Nanoimprint lithography (NIL) with particle-embedded resin (PER) offers a scalable alternative by enabling single-step replication of nanoscale patterns from a reusable master mold without additional deposition or etching24,25. Embedding high-refractive-index nanoparticles (NPs) can increase the refractive index of PER from ~1.5 to above 1.8, while maintaining compatibility with high-throughput fabrication26, flexible substrates27–30, and large-area manufacturing31,32. However, broadband UV–visible metasurfaces have not yet been demonstrated using NIL, primarily because existing PER materials suffer from a trade-off between refractive index and optical absorption. TiO₂ PER, for example, provides high refractive index for visible metasurfaces but exhibits significant absorption in the UV due to its relatively small bandgap (Eg) of ~3.2 eV33–38. Similar trade-offs appear in other PER materials39, leaving the development of NIL-compatible, high-index, low-loss material a significant challenge. Moreover, imprint conditions such as UV dose, pressure, and curing kinetics are highly material dependent, requiring careful optimization to achieve high pattern fidelity and consistent optical performance.

In this study, we demonstrate UV-visible metasurfaces realized through a single-step NIL enabled by Ta2O5 PER. Dispersing Ta2O5 NPs into a UV-curable matrix yielded a PER that has the refractive index of 1.9 at 300 nm and maintains a relatively high refractive index with negligible absorption across the visible. The NIL process faithfully replicated rectangular nanoposts and cylindrical nanopillars with high aspect ratio (AR). Accurate pattern transfer was secured through systematic control of imprint pressure, UV exposure, and NP weight ratio. This versatility was validated with a UV-visible metahologram and a hyperbolic-phase UV metalens, for which electromagnetic simulations predicted efficiencies approaching 80% at 320 nm. Experimental measurements yielded 64% conversion efficiency for the metahologram and 61.3% focusing efficiency for the metalens alongside diffraction-limited imaging. The hologram consistently reconstructs images across 320–635 nm, confirming broadband functional operation despite wavelength-dependent efficiency variations. By integrating materials engineering with optimized NIL processing, this work overcomes barriers in scalable broadband metasurface fabrication. This advancement opens a practical route toward compact, high-performance photonic systems operating from the UV to the visible.

Results

Properties of Ta2O5 PER

Ta2O5 NPs were characterized to establish their optical and structural properties for advanced photonic applications. Laser-diffraction particle size analysis confirmed that more than 90% of the Ta2O5 NPs employed have diameters below 40 nm (Supplementary Note 1), ensuring compatibility with effective medium theory assumptions. Uniform thin films were fabricated by dispersing the NPs in methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK) followed by spin-coating and thermal solvent removal. Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed a densely packed morphology with randomly distributed NPs throughout the film matrix (Fig. 1a). We assessed the optical properties of the films using UV-visible spectroscopy. The Tauc plot analysis yielded an optical bandgap of 4.08 eV (Fig. 1b). By exploiting these NP characteristics, we fabricated PER by dispersing Ta2O5 NPs in a UV-curable polymer matrix. This formulation comprises Ta2O5 NPs as the high-index filler, a monomer as the structural binder, a photo-initiator for radical-mediated crosslinking, and MIBK as the processing solvent. The composition enabled uniform dispersion and stabilization of the NPs within the cross-linked polymer network, thereby preserving their intrinsic optical properties under applied processing conditions.

The optical homogeneity of PER was validated via complementary theoretical and experimental approaches. Two-dimensional finite-element analysis (FEM) simulations of the Gaussian beam propagation demonstrated beam directionality preservation with minimal scattering (Fig. 1c), confirming that the subwavelength particle dimensions support effective medium behavior. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements further revealed a smooth film surface with root-mean-square roughness below 1.2 nm, indicating negligible topographical irregularities that could induce scattering (Supplementary Note 2). Haze and transmittance measurements (Supplementary Note 3) further verified that the PER films exhibited negligible light scattering, in good agreement with the simulation results.

The refractive index was analyzed using ellipsometry (Fig. 1d). The measured amplitude ratio and phase difference of light reflected from the PER film surface were fitted using the Tauc-Lorentz model and an effective medium approximation (EMA) framework incorporating the intrinsic optical constant of Ta2O5 (Supplementary Note 4)42,43. The measured refractive index reached 1.9 at 300 nm for the 80 wt% Ta2O5 films, in good agreement with the Bruggeman mixing model prediction at a volume fraction of 36%.

Comparative X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on the NP and PER films to verify the preservation of structural integrity throughout the fabrication process. Both samples exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline orthorhombic Ta2O5 (Fig. 1e). The analysis confirmed that the embedding and UV-curing processes do not disrupt the crystalline lattice of the NPs. The preservation of crystallinity ensured that the intrinsic optical properties were maintained, thus enabling the PER to be used in high-performance photonic applications.

Fabrication of Ta2O5 PER-based Metasurface

Building on the established material platform, we developed a PER-based NIL process to fabricate Ta2O5 metasurfaces with high transfer fidelity. Figure 2a shows a schematic overview of the entire fabrication process. The master mold shown in Fig. 2b was fabricated on a silicon substrate using EBL, with detailed procedures described in the Methods section and Supplementary Note 5. The master mold was treated to be hydrophobic by the vapor-phase deposition of a fluorosilane self-assembled monolayer (SAM) to facilitate demolding. Hard-polydimethylsiloxane (h-PDMS) and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) were sequentially coated to replicate a bilayer soft mold. h-PDMS was selected because of its high stiffness and deformation resistance with low viscosity, which makes it ideal for precise nanostructure replication. However, owing to the inherent brittleness of h-PDMS, a PDMS buffer layer was added to enhance durability while preventing crack formation. The soft mold was coated with a SAM, and Ta2O5 PER was drop-cast onto it. After solvent evaporation and complete relaxation of solvent-induced mold swelling16, UV curing under mild pressure enabled the high-fidelity transfer of nanostructures onto the adhesion-promoted glass substrate. The resulting patterns replicated nanostructures with high accuracy, highlighting the versatility of the NIL process (Fig. 2d). Two representative structures, including rectangular nanoposts and cylindrical nanopillars, were selected because they correspond to commonly used meta-atom configurations in dielectric metasurfaces. The rectangular structures were designed based on the Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase44,45 for holographic metasurfaces, whereas the circular pillars were implemented based on the propagation-phase for metalens applications. These results suggest that the proposed process can be effectively scaled up to fabricate multiplexed metasurfaces encompassing diverse nanostructure libraries for multifunctional optical applications. Low-magnification SEM images (Supplementary Note 6) further confirm the uniform pattern transfer across large areas. In addition, the tilted-view SEM images of the master mold and imprinted metasurfaces (Supplementary Note 7) reveal vertically aligned sidewalls, demonstrating high structural fidelity, with high ARs above 7.5.

A series of experiments was conducted under various conditions to optimize the NIL process parameters for the introduced Ta2O5 PER. Propagation-phase structures with spatially diverse geometries and sensitive to local fabrication conditions served as a representative configuration for deriving generalized conclusions. Pattern transfer accuracy depended primarily on the complex interplay between the UV curing time, applied pressure, and weight ratio (Supplementary Note 8). The UV curing time proved critical for achieving adequate mechanical strength. Insufficient curing, less than 20 min resulted in structural collapse during demolding owing to inadequate mechanical integrity. Pressure above 4 bar caused non-uniform load distribution across the soft mold, resulting in deformation of the soft mold in certain regions. The weight ratio significantly affected structural stability, particularly for high-AR features. While low ARs around 5 show low sensitivity to NP content, PER with insufficient loading below 50 wt% led to poor stability of higher-AR structures. Notably, stable nanostructures were fabricated at an NP concentration of 80 wt%, a loading ratio selected to provide the high refractive index necessary for high-performance metasurface operation. This also provides a robust path to diverse architectures with consistent accuracy and reproducibility.

The resulting phase map determined the rotational orientation of each meta-atom (Supplementary Note 10), creating a spatially encoded wavefront that reconstructs the target image. Figure 3d presents the experimental validation spanning UV to visible, with the detailed optical setup described in the Methods section and Supplementary Note 11. The reconstructed holographic images demonstrated fidelity to their simulated counterparts, with consistent image quality maintained across the entire spectral region. Quantitative analysis revealed polarization conversion efficiencies of 64%, 28%, 18%, 7%, and 3% at 320, 405, 450, 532, and 635 nm, respectively. The conversion efficiencies were determined by measuring the power ratio between the incident circularly polarized light and the converted cross-polarized component. Individual wavelength-specific optimizations could further enhance the performance at the targeted wavelengths.

Discussion

In this study, we present a high-refractive index Ta2O5 PER integrated with single-step NIL, overcoming both material and fabrication barriers in broadband metasurface design. Conventional UV and visible metasurface materials such as ZrO2 and TiO2 face a compromise between refractive index and extinction coefficient, restricting broadband phase control. By tuning NP weight ratio and keeping particle sizes below the wavelength scale, Ta2O5 PER reaches refractive indices of 1.9 at 300 nm and maintains a considerably high refractive index with negligible absorption across the visible range. NPs maintain their intrinsic optical and crystalline properties when embedded in the resin matrix. The NIL process enables direct patterning of functional meta-atoms, removing the need for sequential deposition or etching. The high AR was achieved in both PB phase metahologram and propagation-phase metalens, providing full 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}π phase coverage at short wavelength. This capability demonstrates high-fidelity replication of complex metasurface architectures across different phase-control mechanisms and spectral ranges. Thin residual layers, nanoscale sidewall irregularities, and subtle height reduction introduce small phase deviations and slight index deficits, providing an explanation for the reduced efficiency relative to idealized simulations. Despite these minor imperfections, the metasurface maintains high-quality hologram reconstruction and diffraction-limited imaging, confirming that the fabrication tolerances remain well within the operational margin of the design.

Concurrent optimization of material properties and NIL parameters offers a scalable route to broadband metasurfaces. This study provides high-refractive index and mechanically stable structures with precise phase control, suitable for compact imaging systems, multi-band encryption and holographic displays. Compatibility with diverse meta-atom geometries also supports integration with inverse design and active tuning for adaptive photonic functions.

Future work could include wavelength-specific design optimization to improve performance. Hybridization with other wide-bandgap nanoparticles could extend operation deeper into the UV range. Mixed-particle packing and atomic layer deposition (ALD)-based densification offer a potential route to further increase the effective refractive index with UV-transparent materials32. Mechanical reliability under repeated thermal cycling or prolonged UV exposure should be tested for outdoor and space uses. Extending the process to roll-to-roll imprinting and flexible substrates could accelerate the transition from laboratory prototypes to large-scale manufacturing, strengthen the case for industrial adoption of Ta2O5 PER and NIL in broadband meta-optics.

Materials and methods

Formulation of Ta2O5 PER

The Ta2O5 PER was prepared from four components: a mixture of 30% Ta2O5 NPs dispersed in MIBK (DT-TAO-30MIBK, Ditto Technology), 7 wt% monomer (dipentaerythritol penta/hexa-acrylate, Sigma-Aldrich) in MIBK, 3 wt% photo-initiator (1-hydroxycyclohexyl phenyl ketone, Sigma-Aldrich) in MIBK, and MIBK itself (Duksan General Science). The optical properties of the PERs were determined by the post-evaporation weight ratio of Ta2O5 NPs, monomer, and photo-initiator after MIBK solvent evaporation.

Fabrication of the master mold

Master molds for the metahologram and metalens were fabricated on Si substrates. First, the substrates were spin-coated with a positive tone photoresist (PR) (ZEP520A, ZEON) at 5000 rpm for 60 s. Subsequently, patterns were drawn using high-speed EBL (ELS-BODEN, ELIONIX; acceleration voltage: 50 kV, beam current: 10 nA). The exposed resist was developed in a ZEP developer (ZED-N50, ZEON) for 2 min for the metahologram and for 3 min 30 s for the metalens. A 65 nm-thick chromium (Cr) layer was then deposited using electron beam evaporation (KVE-ENS4004, KVT). The lift-off process to remove the remaining PR was conducted by immersing the sample in N,N-Dimethylacetamide (DAEJUNG) at 50 °C for 5 min followed by sample sonication in an ultrasonic cleaner (Crest Ultrasonics) for approximately 10 min. The remaining Cr mask served as an etching mask during deep-reactive-ion etching. Finally, the Cr etching mask was removed via wet etching using a CR remover (CR-7, Microchem).

Fabrication of the soft mold

The master mold was vapor treated with a fluorosurfactant (trichloro(1H, 1H, 2H, 2H-perfluorooctyl)silane, JSI silicone) at 130 °C for 5 min. This process, which was repeated twice, reduces the surface energy and facilitates the release of the soft mold. h-PDMS was prepared by mixing 1.7 g vinylmethyl copolymers (VDT-731, Gelest), 9 μL platinum-catalyst (SIP6831.2, Gelest), 0.05 g modulator (2,4,6,8-tetramethyl-2,4,6,8-tetravinylcyclotetrasiloxane, Sigma-Aldrich), 1 g toluene (Duksan General Science), and 0.5 g siloxane-based silane reducing agent (HMS-301, Gelest). The mixture was mixed using vortex mixer (Barnstead Thermolyn Maxi Mix II) and then spin-coated onto the master mold at 4000 rpm for 60 s, then baked at 70 °C for 30 min. The PDMS was prepared by mixing the PDMS base (Sylgard 184 A, Dow Corning) and its curing agent (Sylgard 184 B, Dow Corning) at a 10:1 weight ratio, followed by vacuum degassing. The degassed PDMS was poured over the h-PDMS layer and cured at 70 °C for 2 h. Finally, the resulting soft mold (h-PDMS/PDMS) was gently peeled from the master mold and used to replicate the Ta2O5 PER structure.

Nanoimprint lithography process

The receiver glass substrate was spin-coated with PMMA (495 A8, Microchem) at 5000 rpm for 60 s and baked at 180 °C for 5 min to promote nanopattern adhesion. The soft mold was vapor-deposited with fluorosurfactant at 130 °C for 5 min. This process was repeated twice to lower the surface energy. The Ta2O5 PER was drop-cast onto the treated soft mold and left at room temperature for ~2 min to allow spontaneous evaporation of the MIBK solvent. The soft mold was aligned and brought into conformal contact with the PMMA-coated glass substrate. The mold was then laminated under 2 bar pressure and cured under UV light for 30 min using imprinting equipment (NANOTOOL-C800, HUNETPLUS; UV intensity: 0.8 mW/cm2 at 365 nm). Finally, the mold was meticulously removed, yielding a high fidelity PER nanopattern on the substrate.

Optical measurement

A laser with six discrete wavelengths served as the illumination source: 320 nm (UV-FN-320-5 mW, CNI Laser), 375 nm (375 laser diode, Thorlabs), 405 nm (violet-blue laser, CrystaLaser), 450 nm (450 laser diode, Thorlabs), 532 nm (532 laser diode, Thorlabs), and 635 nm (635 laser diode, Thorlabs). Each beam was collimated and directed onto the sample using free-space optics. An adjustable iris diaphragm (ID25, Thorlabs) was used to reduce the beam size and match it to the sample size. In UV holography, circularly polarized light was produced by cascading a linear polarizer with an achromatic quarter-wave plate. The same combination acted as an analyzer for the zeroth-order beam. In the visible range, an RCP polarizer (CP1R532, Thorlabs) and an LCP polarizer (CP1L532, Thorlabs) were used as the converter and analyzer, respectively. The hologram image and the PSF were recorded with a scientific CMOS camera (Panda 4.2, PCO). The PSFs were magnified using an objective lens (LMU-15X-UVB; 15x; NA = 0.3, Thorlabs) coupled to a tube lens. This magnification assembly was mounted on a motorized stage (Z825B, Thorlabs) to align them with the focal plane of the metalens. The overall magnification was determined by replacing the metalens with a negative USAF 1951 resolution target, and the image was captured under identical imaging-system conditions.
