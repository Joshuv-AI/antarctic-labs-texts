Progression-free Survival Following Primary Staging with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT Versus Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT in Prostate Cancer: Results from the PRISMA-PET Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Karen M. Buch-Olsen, Steinbjørn Hansen, Mie H. Vilstrup, Mads H. Poulsen, Paw C. Holdgaard, Karsten E.A. Zieger, Jon T. Asmussen, Jorun Holm, Kasper T. Pedersen, Søren Hess, Søren S. Madsen, Matthias Eiber, Oke Gerke, Malene G. Hildebrandt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageStaging of prostate cancer with prostate-specific membrane antigen 1007 ([18F]PSMA-1007)–positron emission tomography/contrast-enhanced computed tomography (PET/ceCT) compared to sodium fluoride (Na[18F]F)–PET/ceCT showed a visual advantage toward longer progression-free survival beyond 2 yr from diagnosis. However, this difference was statistically insignificant. A longer follow-up is needed to determine whether such a difference manifests itself.

ADVANCING PRACTICE What does this study add? This study shows that staging with [18F]PSMA-1007–positron emission tomography/contrast-enhanced computed tomography (PET/ceCT) affected disease stage and level of progression compared with sodium fluoride (Na[18F]F)-PET/ceCT in newly diagnosed prostate cancer. Progression-free survival did not differ significantly, though a visual difference suggested a potential long-term benefit for patients staged with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT. Clinical Relevance Differences in diagnostic sensitivity between staging modalities can lead to varying degrees of stage migration, thereby influencing patient classification, treatment allocation, and the composition of prognostic risk groups. The greater sensitivity of [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT resulted in fewer patients being categorized as nonmetastatic, illustrating how the imaging modality itself may affect case selection and subsequent oncological outcomes. Consequently, comparisons of progression-free survival across studies and treatment strategies should be interpreted in light of the diagnostic tools used, as differences in staging performance may systematically alter the apparent disease burden and prognosis of included patients. Patient Summary

In this study, we compared two types of scans that are used to assess whether and how much prostate cancer (PCa) has spread at the time of diagnosis. We found no clear difference in how quickly the disease progressed between the two groups. However, patients staged with the newer prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA-PET/CT) scan tended to remain stable for longer. These findings suggest that PSMA-PET/CT may offer a potential benefit for patients with newly diagnosed PCa, but later follow-up is warranted.

Introduction

Alongside rapid technological advancements over the past few decades, medical imaging has seen significant improvements. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) has emerged as an imaging modality in prostate cancer (PCa), and it has proven to be more sensitive than conventional imaging for primary staging [1], [2], [3], [4], [5].

Contrast-enhanced CT (ceCT) and bone scintigraphy (BS) constitute conventional imaging and were previously used to stage primary unfavorable intermediate and high-risk PCa. An alternative is sodium fluoride (Na[18F]F)–PET/ceCT, which assesses bone metabolism similar to BS, but it is more sensitive than BS for the detection of bone metastases [6]. Switching imaging methods for staging may introduce stage migration and alter patient management, as shown for PSMA-PET/CT in the Prostate-specific membrane antigen PET-CT in patients with high-risk prostate cancer before curative-intent surgery or radiotherapy (proPSMA) and PRISMA-PET trials [5], [7], although the knowledge of the impact on patient benefit remains unclear. This uncertainty contributed to earlier hesitancy in recommending PSMA-PET/CT in clinical guidelines [8]. When the European Association of Urology (EAU) updated its PCa staging guidelines in 2024, PSMA-PET/CT was recommended for staging high-risk PCa. Despite this, the current guidelines still call for evidence on patient outcomes from PSMA-PET/CT–based staging [9].

This trial aimed to provide level 1 evidence for diagnostic imaging in the primary staging of patients with high-risk or unfavorable intermediate-risk PCa. Our primary objective was to determine whether staging with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT compared with Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT resulted in longer progression-free survival (PFS). Secondary objectives included comparing stage allocation for pelvic lymph node and other metastases, residual disease, and treatment allocation.

Patients and methods

The PRISMA-PET trial was a multicenter, superiority randomized controlled clinical trial (Fig. 1). The study was registered with European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database (EudraCT) (2021-000123-12), ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05123300; full protocol available; registered on August 31, 2021), and the Regional Committees on Health Research for Southern Denmark (S-20190161) [11]. The trial adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice standards. All participants provided written informed consent. Data were handled in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation, and a Research Electronic Data Capture database was used for data registration, computer-generated and web-based central randomization in block sizes of 4 and 6 without blinding, and secure storage.Fig. 1CONSORT 2025 flow diagram—PRISMA-PET. The trial was conducted at three hospitals in the Region of Southern Denmark, enrolling patients from three departments of urology, with imaging performed in corresponding departments of nuclear medicine. Two patient representatives were involved in planning the study design. The study was initiated as a pharmaceutical clinical trial in October 2021. Seven patients were enrolled in a pilot phase in 2020; four of them renewed their consent after October 2021 and were included in this analysis. The trial was terminated in January 2025, following changes in the EAU and subsequently Danish guidelines, which shifted from recommending BS and ceCT to recommending PSMA-PET/CT for staging primary PCa [9], [12]. In addition, although the planned inclusion period was 3 yr, the inclusion had already been extended to reach the target sample size.

Patients and randomization

Eligible patients had newly diagnosed biopsy-proven PCa with unfavorable intermediate- or high-risk disease or clinical suspicion of metastatic spread warranting imaging. A maximum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level of 200 ng/ml was permitted. Participants were referred by the participating departments of urology. Because of this recruitment structure, prescreening data for patients who were assessed but not referred are unavailable.

Consenting patients were randomized 1:1 to either Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT or [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/ceCT. Both imaging modalities were performed in conjunction with a ceCT, if kidney function allowed, or otherwise with a non–contrast-enhanced diagnostic CT (Fig. 1). Randomization was stratified by risk group and conducted through a Research Electronic Data Capture database designed for this purpose [11]. The intention-to-treat principle was followed, and the patients were analyzed according to their randomized scan group.

The scanning procedures

Patients were scanned from the cranial vertex to below the knees in both groups. Na[18F]F was administered intravenously at a targeted dose of 200 MBq, and patients were scanned 30 min postinjection (p.i.). [18F]PSMA-1007 was administered intravenously at an aimed dose of 2 MBq/kg (minimum 150 MBq and maximum 300 MBq), and patients were scanned 60 min p.i. Imaging was conducted on GE Discovery 710 (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, United States) and GE Discovery MI (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, United States). Siemens Biograph mCT 64-4R PET/CT system (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA) Siemens Biograph Vision 600 Edge PET/CT system (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA) (710 and MI) and Siemens Biograph (mCT Flow 64 and Vision 600 Edge) PET/CT scanners. PET and CT acquisition parameters were adopted from clinical settings [7], [11].

Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT and [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT interpretation

Interpretations of PET/CT scans were performed visually, as in routine clinical practice. The PET scans were reported by physicians specialized in nuclear medicine. Radiologists read the CT scans in accordance with the EAU guidelines. A dichotomous conclusion was derived from the reports to reflect the clinical need to determine whether metastases were present.

Treatment decision

Treatment decisions were made in accordance with current clinical guidelines. The treatment plans were determined pragmatically, reflecting routine clinical practice. Although the randomized imaging result contributed to the treatment strategy, treatment decisions also incorporated tumor characteristics, PSA level, pathology, prostate multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging findings (if clinically performed), age, performance status, comorbidity, and patient preferences. Additional imaging or biopsy was performed when clinically indicated.

Follow-up and data collection

The follow-up was strictly clinical with no additional trial-specific follow-up procedures. The registered start of follow-up was the day of inclusion and randomization. The follow-up data were collected from January 3, 2026, to January 21, 2026, by examining patients’ medical records, including laboratory test results and death registrations. A PFS event was defined as death from any cause for all patients, biochemical relapse for patients treated with curative intent, and a change in treatment regimen for patients with primary disseminated disease. Biochemical relapse was deemed after prostatectomy, with or without salvage radiation therapy initiated because of residual disease, if unmeasurable PSA levels increased to 0.2 µg/l or above, or after curative radiotherapy with an increase of 2.0 µg/l above nadir [9]. Upon death, the date was recorded. If none of these were registered, they were deemed PFS event free.

Quality-of-life questionnaires were distributed to a subset of patients who provided consent, but the outcomes are not analyzed here.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics comprised median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and frequency tables for categorical variables. We used the Fisher exact test for exploratory bivariate testing of clinical categorical variables.

The prespecified primary analysis of PFS in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group versus the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group used Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusted for risk status (intermediate- vs high-risk PCa) as a covariate [13]. Graphical displays included Kaplan-Meier plots, risk tables, and the corresponding log-rank test results [11].

We investigated the proportional hazards assumption by comparing observed and predicted survival curves, log-log plots, goodness-of-fit tests, and Schoenfeld residuals [14].

We supplemented post hoc with a restricted mean survival time analysis, with risk status as covariate, as a robust complementary analysis to provide a clinically interpretable absolute difference between the groups for illustrative purposes. Further, we supplemented post hoc exploratory Kaplan-Meier plots stratified by risk status and initial treatment. Finally, we revisited the primary analysis and investigated a possible interaction of group and risk status.

No data were missing. The significance level was 5% (two sided), and only the primary analysis of PFS was performed with a confirmatory intent. All remaining statistical tests were of an exploratory nature without adjustment for multiple testing. All analyses were performed using Stata/MP 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Sample size calculation

A total of 402 patients (201 in each arm) was deemed sufficient to detect a statistically significant difference in PFS, assuming hazard rates of 0.35 and 0.5 for the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group and the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group, respectively, yielding a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.7. Estimates are based on a 4-yr study, comprising 3 yr of accrual and a minimum 1 yr of follow-up period (two-sample comparison of survivor functions; exponential test; power: 80%; significance level: 5% [two sided]). The estimated expected number of events was 251 under the alternative hypothesis (113 in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group and 138 in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group, respectively) and 254 under the null hypothesis (127 in each group). Accounting for a 10% loss to follow-up, 448 patients (224 in each arm) would provide sufficient statistical power for the analyses [11].

Interim analysis

We assumed the distribution of patients across stages N0M0, N0M1a, N0M1b, N1M0, N1M1a, and N1M1b to differ between Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT and [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT. More specifically, we assumed the sum of absolute differences in the proportions of patients in the different stages to differ by at least 20% [15], [16]. A difference of at least 20% is necessary, but not sufficient to demonstrate an advantage of [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT over Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT, as both treatment choice and treatment effect will influence PFS in both study arms.

One interim analysis was planned for the first 270 included patients, to reach 60% of the overall recruitment target of 448 patients. If the difference in the distribution of patients across stages fell short of 15%, stopping the study early because of futility would have been considered by the study core team (KMB-O, SH, OG, and MGH). Potential early stopping because of early success was not considered. The primary hypothesis was not tested in the interim analysis, and adjustment for multiple testing was not necessary [11].

Results

We included 385 men, of whom 186 were in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group and 188 in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group, after excluding 11 who did not meet the inclusion criteria (n = 2) or withdrew informed consent (n = 9; Fig. 1) [10]. One patient in each group was included because of suspicion of metastases; both were favorable intermediate risk. The trial termination resulted in fewer patients for follow-up than our power calculation indicated was necessary (374 rather than 402; Table 1). No serious adverse events were reported. Median follow-up time for patients without event (n = 297) was 32 mo (interquartile range: 25–39 mo). Stage distribution and treatment decision

More patients were classified as non-metastatic when staged with Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT (n = 143 [77%]) than with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT (n = 130 [69%]). A slightly higher proportion of patients in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group were diagnosed with pelvic lymph node metastases without distant metastases (n = 22 [12%] vs n = 18 [10%]). Likewise, more patients were classified with metastatic disease in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group (n = 36 [19%] vs n = 25 [13%]; Supplementary Table 2).

There was no statistically significant difference in the distribution of treatments between groups (p = 0.44), and these aligned with differences in staging (Supplementary Table 3), with more patients in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group receiving curative intent treatment (n = 124 [67%] vs n = 117 [62%]). Furthermore, more patients staged with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT were treated according to lymph node metastatic disease in the pelvis (N1M0) with bicalutamide (n = 16 [8.5%] vs n = 8 [4.3%]) or outside the pelvis (NxM1a) with lifelong androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) and radiotherapy toward the prostate gland (n = 23 [12%] vs n = 9 [4.8%]; Fig. 2).Fig. 2Alluvial diagrams of patient flow from risk stratification over staging to treatment allocation for the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT (n = 186, top) and the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT (n = 188, bottom) groups, respectively. Frequencies and reasons for PFS events

Relapse or progression was observed for 37 patients in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group and 25 patients in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group (p = 0.096), with more patients in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group experiencing biochemical relapse after prostatectomy (n = 15 [41%] vs n = 8 [32%]; p = 0.60) and residual disease after prostatectomy (n = 5 [14%] vs n = 2 [8.0%]; p = 0.69; Supplementary Table 4).

PFS events include relapse, progression, and death. In the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group, 28 experienced PFS events and were alive at the end of follow-up, 9 experienced PFS events before death, and 6 died without a previous PFS event, resulting in a total of 43 events. In the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group, 22 experienced PFS events and were alive at the end of follow-up, 3 experienced PFS events before death, and 9 died without a previous PFS event, resulting in 34 events. The annual cumulative number of PFS events (including death for any reason) is shown in Supplementary Table 5.

In both groups 3 patients experienced residual disease after prostatectomy and received salvage radiotherapy with subsequent unmeasurable PSA level, hence not regarded as having PFS events.

In the primary analysis, the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis of PFS was adjusted for risk status and indicated a statistically insignificant advantage for the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group (HR: 0.74, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.47–1.16; p = 0.19; Supplementary Table 6). The proportional hazards assumption was borderline (global test: p = 0.0495), potentially violated for risk status (p = 0.0232; Supplementary Fig. 7). The Kaplan-Meier plot showed a minor visual advantage for the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group (Fig. 3), with a log-rank test p = 0.23. Patients in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group were, on average, 1.6 mo (95% CI: –8.8–4 mo; p = 0.21) longer event-free survival than those in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group over a 3.63-yr period of follow-up (Supplemental Table 8). Discussion

In the comparison between the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group and the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group, we did not observe a significant difference in PFS. The Kaplan-Meier curves deviated beyond 2 yr of follow-up, suggesting a possible effect on PFS in favor of the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group. In stratified Kaplan-Meier plots, this pattern was evident in the high-risk group but not in the intermediate-risk group. Differences in stage distribution were observed between the imaging groups, with more patients in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group diagnosed with both pelvic lymph node metastases and other metastatic diseases. Consequently, treatment allocation differed between the groups, with a higher proportion of patients receiving curative-intent treatment in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group. Fewer patients in the [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT group experienced PFS events than those in the Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT group, suggesting that these patients were more appropriately managed.

To our knowledge, there is currently no published evidence on patient outcomes from staging patients with newly diagnosed PCa with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT. However, previous results from a subgroup of the PRISMA-PET trial showed that introducing this imaging modality led to stage migration and changes in treatment strategy, with altered treatment plans in 14% of the patients [7]. Similar results were reported by Madsen et al [17], who enrolled patients with high-risk disease and analyzed them using a comparable design, and observed changes in treatment plans in 13% of the patients. In the randomized ProPSMA trial, treatment plans changed in 27% of the patients in a more selected population of patients with high-risk PCa eligible for prostatectomy. In the study, patients were randomized to staging with either BS and ceCT or PSMA-PET/CT. If two or fewer metastases were detected on the initial imaging, the alternative imaging modality was performed to allow direct comparison with the initial scan [5].

These data suggest that changes in treatment plans based on PSMA-PET/CT results do not translate into differences in PFS in the first 2 yr after diagnosis. A nonsignificant divergence in PFS indicates a potential advantage for patients staged with PSMA-PET/CT. This pattern of late-emerging PFS events may reflect disease suppression from systemic treatment strategies. Many patients with high-risk disease received radiotherapy to the prostate gland and pelvic lymph nodes combined with 3 yr of ADT, with some patients additionally receiving Abiraterone for 2 yr, and patients with low-volume metastatic disease were treated with lifelong ADT and radiotherapy to the prostate gland. If metastases are overlooked at staging, they will likely be masked by the systemic therapy. These patients typically do not undergo imaging if their PSA levels are satisfactory and their performance status is preserved; therefore, undetected metastases may not be uncovered during short-term follow-up.

The trial’s strength lies in its multicenter, randomized controlled design, which minimizes bias and provides high-quality evidence. The pragmatic multicenter approach was closely aligned with routine clinical practice in hospitals across many countries, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the results. Because of the randomized design, we have two similar groups, and any observed difference is not attributable to the Wills-Rogers phenomenon, which arises when stages migrate within a nonrandomized cohort [18].

The use of Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT as a comparator limits the generalizability of the trial because BS rather than Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT was previously recommended in clinical guidelines. Another limitation is the variation in PFS event definitions across treatment regimens. Because stage and treatment distributions differed between the two groups, the criteria for PFS events also differed, resulting in a heterogeneous composite end point that was not fully comparable between groups, but was attributable to the intervention. Following early termination of the trial, it is slightly underpowered, given an estimated HR of 0.74 and an assumed value of 0.70. Nonetheless, 374 of the 402 patients needed according to the power calculation (93%) were available for analysis. The low number of events in this population with a high long-term survival rate [19] because of a highly effective treatment for both localized, locally advanced, and primarily disseminated disease suggests that the planned evaluation of the primary end point 1 yr after the last enrolled patient may have been too early and impedes further assessments of overall survival, which is pending.

Our results suggest that, in the short term, it has no measurable impact on PFS whether patients are staged with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT or Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT. Visually, we observed a potential benefit primarily for the patients with high-risk disease staging with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT, but its confirmation warrants longer exploratory follow-up data. We plan to assess overall survival once the expected median follow-up of 5 yr has been reached [11]. PFS follow-up will be extended to allow exploratory reevaluation of the long-term potential of the signals observed in this analysis.

Conclusions

Staging with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT altered disease stage and the number of patients experiencing PFS event compared with Na[18F]F-PET/ceCT in patients with newly diagnosed PCa. We found no statistically significant difference in PFS following staging with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT, although we observed some uncertain signs of improved PFS for patients with high-risk disease staged with [18F]PSMA-1007-PET/ceCT. Exploratory analyses after longer follow-up are pending.
