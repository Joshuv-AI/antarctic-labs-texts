Outline Rectangles, Allocations, and Latin Young Diagrams
Jack Allsop, Dani Kotlar, Ian M. Wanless

Abstract
A Young diagram is Latin if there is an assignment of integers to its cells so that each row i i of length l i l_{i} is populated by the numbers 1 , … , l i 1,\ldots,l_{i} , and the numbers in each column are distinct. A Young diagram is called wide if any subdiagram, formed by a subset of its rows, dominates its conjugate. Chow et al. [Advances in Applied Mathematics, 31, 2003] conjectured that any wide Young diagram is Latin. We introduce a notion of an allocation which can be thought of as a coarse attempt at finding a Latin filling for a Young diagram. Using a theorem of Hilton we prove that a Young diagram has an allocation if and only if it is Latin. This enables us to prove Chow et al.’s conjecture for Young diagrams with three distinct row lengths.



1
Introduction

Young diagrams are a way of representing partitions of natural numbers. Given a partition
n=l_{1}+l_{2}+\cdots+l_{m}
of a number
n
, where
0\leqslant l_{1}\leqslant l_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant l_{m}
, the corresponding Young diagram
Y
is formed by
m
rows of squares that are left-aligned, where the
i
-th row from the bottom consists of
l_{i}
squares.
Chow, Fan, Goemans, and Vondrak posed the following question
[
2
]
:

Suppose the squares of a Young diagram are filled, each with an element of the ground set of a given matroid
M
, so that the rows are independent sets in
M
. Can the elements in each row be rearranged so that the columns are independent?

Their conjecture, named the Wide Partition Conjecture, is that the answer is positive if and only if the Young diagram is so-called
wide
, where wideness is defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

A Young diagram is wide if any subdiagram formed by a subset of its rows dominates its conjugate.

The ‘only if’ part of their conjecture can easily be shown to hold, so the conjecture is really about the ‘if’ part.
Handling this problem for general matroids is hard, since even the ‘easy’ case in which all rows of the given Young diagram have the same length is a notorious open problem known as Rota’s basis conjecture
[
5
]
. So, as in
[
2
]
, we focus on the simple case of a free matroid.
A Young diagram is
Latin
if there is an assignment of integers to its cells so that each row
i
of length
l_{i}
is populated by the numbers
1,\ldots,l_{i}
, and the assignment is injective in each column. We call such an assignment a
Latin filling
.
Chow et al. showed that in the case of a free matroid, the Wide Partition Conjecture can be reduced to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 2
.

Every wide Young diagram is Latin.

In
[
2
]
, Conjecture
2
was proved for Young diagrams with two distinct row lengths and for Young diagrams with three distinct row lengths that are self-conjugate. It was also shown there that proving Conjecture
2
for all self-conjugate Young diagrams will imply the general case. However, these results do not imply that Conjecture
2
holds for all Young diagrams with three distinct row lengths.
One of the main results of this work is a proof of Conjecture
2
for such Young diagrams:

Theorem 3
.

A wide Young diagram with three distinct row lengths is Latin.

To prove Theorem
3
, we introduce the notion of an
allocation
, which is a coarse filling of a Young diagram adhering to certain constraints. Instead of specifying which symbol goes in each cell, we subdivide the Young diagram into sub-rectangles and partition the symbol set into smaller subsets. An allocation specifies how many elements of each symbol subset should be in each sub-rectangle. We defer the formal definition of an allocation to §
3
. Our approach is inspired by the notion of an
outline rectangle
, introduced by Hilton
[
4
]
for rectangular arrays. We rely on Hilton’s result, which states that any outline rectangle can be traced back to a Latin square, to demonstrate that if a Young diagram has an allocation, then it has a Latin filling. Thus, another of our main results is that we show that to prove Conjecture
2
it suffices to prove the following equivalent conjecture:

Conjecture 4
.

Any wide Young diagram has an allocation.

We prove Conjecture
4
for Young diagrams with three distinct row lengths.
The method of proof suggests that it might be possible to prove Conjecture
4
in the general case by induction on the number of distinct row-lengths.

Two recent works applied other approaches to the problem of Latin fillings of Young diagrams. Chow and Tiefenbruck
[
3
]
made some progress on the so-called Latin Tableau conjecture of Chow et al.
[
2
]
, which is a generalisation of Conjecture
2
.
Aharoni et al.
[
1
]
took a hypergraph approach and showed that Conjecture
2
would follow if equality holds between a matching parameter of a 3-partite 3-hypergraph related to a given Young diagram, and its covering dual.

This paper is arranged as follows: In Section
2
, we show that the complexity of determining wideness of a Young diagram is at most quadratic in the number of distinct row lengths.
In Section
3
, we introduce allocations and use a theorem of Hilton to show that Conjecture
2
is equivalent to Conjecture
4
.
In Section
4
, we prove that any wide Young diagram with three distinct row lengths has an allocation.
In Section
5
, we discuss two approaches to proving Conjecture
4
in general.

2
Testing wideness

Throughout this section, let
Y
be a Young diagram with
p
distinct row lengths
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{p}
and for
i=1,\ldots,p
, let the number of rows of
Y
of length
a_{i}
be
e_{i}
. Also set
a_{0}=0
.

Definition 5
.

A row block in
Y
is a maximal set of rows of the same length.
A column block is a maximal set of columns of the same length.
The symbol blocks are
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{p}
, where
S_{i}
is the set of symbols
\{a_{i-1}+1,a_{i-1}+2,\ldots,a_{i}\}
.
When row/column/symbol is not specified, we shall assume that ‘block’ refers to a row block.

The row blocks of
Y
are numbered from bottom to top (from short to long), while the column blocks are numbered from left to right (from long to short). As defined, the symbol blocks are numbered according to the ascending order of symbols.

Considering Definition
1
, which subdiagrams do we need to check to determine whether
Y
is wide? Chow et al.
[
2
]
showed that it suffices to consider ‘tail’ subdiagrams (i.e., subdiagrams consisting of all rows from some row
r
downwards), and check whether the top
w
rows of each such subdiagram dominates the leftmost
w
columns. However, it seems we do not need to consider all values of
r
and
w
.

Notation 1
.

Denote by
Y[r]
the tail subdiagram of
Y
consisting of all the rows from
r
downwards.

Throughout this paper, when
a
and
b
are integers with
a\leqslant b
we will use the interval notation
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
. We will use
[a]
as shorthand for
[1,a]
.

Theorem 6
.

To check whether
Y
is wide it suffices to check whether the top
w
rows of the subdiagram
Y[r]
dominates its leftmost
w
columns, whenever
r
is the top row of a block
k
and
w\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
.

Proof.

First fix
r
in some block
k
and consider varying
w
within some interval
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
, where
1\leqslant i<k
(there are only
a_{k}
columns in
Y[k]
so we do not need to consider
w>a_{k}
),
or within the interval
[1,a_{1}]
. Let
D
be the sum of the lengths of the top
w
rows of
Y[r]
minus the sum of the lengths of the leftmost
w
columns of
Y[r]
. Wideness dictates that
D
must be non-negative. As we increase
w
by
1
, we add to
D
the length of one new row and subtract the length of one new column. If this reduces
D
then each subsequent increase in
w
will also decrease
D
because the new rows get shorter but the new columns have the same length (since we are within the interval
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
). So
D
will be minimized by taking
w=a_{i+1}
, which is the only value of
w
that we need to test.

On the other hand, if
D
increases when we add 1 to
w
, then
D
will decrease each time we reduce
w
(the rows we are discarding are getting bigger, and the columns stay the same), so
D
will be minimized by taking
w=a_{i}
or, in the case where
w\in[a_{1}]
,
D
will be minimized by taking
w=1
.
Either way, we only need to test
w
at one end of the interval.

Next, we consider fixing
w
and moving
r
within the interval of
e_{k}
rows of the same length as
r
.
The argument is similar.
As we move
r
down the diagram, we add a row of some length
\ell
, subtract a row of length
a_{k}
and subtract
w
from the column total (meaning
D
increases by
\ell-a_{k}+w
).
If this reduces
D
, then each subsequent time we move
r
downwards will also decrease
D
, because
\ell
decreases and
w-a_{k}
is constant. So we minimize
D
by moving
r
to the top row of the
(k-1)
-st block.
On the other hand, if
\ell-a_{k}+w\geqslant 0
, we should move
r
to the top of the
k
-th block.

We claim that for a given
r
, the top row of row block
k
, we do not need to check whether the top
w
rows of
Y[r]
dominates its leftmost
w
columns for both
w=1
and
w=a_{k}
, as the domination conditions with
w=1
and
w=a_{k}
are equivalent.
First, note that for
w=1
, the condition is
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}\leqslant a_{k}
, which means that in
Y[r]
, there are at most
a_{k}
rows.
Also note that there are
a_{k}
columns in
Y[r]
, so the sum of the lengths of the leftmost
a_{k}
columns of
Y[r]
is
|Y[r]|
.
Suppose
Y[r]
passes the domination test with
w=1
.
That is,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}\leqslant a_{k}
. Then,
Y[r]
has at most
a_{k}
rows. So, the sum of the lengths of the top
a_{k}
rows and the sum of the lengths of the leftmost
a_{k}
columns of
Y[r]
are both equal to
|Y[r]|
.
Thus,
Y[r]
passes the domination test with
w=a_{k}
.
Now, assume
Y[r]
does not satisfy the domination condition with
w=1
.
That is,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}>a_{k}
.
This means that there are more than
a_{k}
rows in
Y[r]
.
So, the sum of the lengths of the top
a_{k}
rows in
Y[r]
is less than
|Y[r]|
.
Thus,
Y[r]
does not satisfy the domination condition with
w=a_{k}
.
∎

The next observation follows from the last part of the proof of Theorem
6
.

Observation 2
.

If
r
is the top row of a block
k
, the wideness condition for
Y[r]
with
w=a_{k}
is equivalent to
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}\leqslant a_{k}
.

For a given row
r
, the top row in block
k
, and
w=a_{j}
where
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
, what is the condition to be verified if we want to establish wideness?

Notation 3
.

For
k\leqslant p
, denote by
Y_{k}
the subdiagram
Y[r]
where
r
is the top row in block
k
.
For
j\leqslant k
we denote by
\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
(resp.
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}
) the sum of the lengths of the leftmost
a_{j}
columns (resp. top
a_{j}
rows) in
Y_{k}
.

Claim 4
.

We have:

\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}=\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t}e_{t}+a_{j}\sum_{t=j}^{k}e_{t}

(1)

and also

\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}=\sum_{u=1}^{j}\left((a_{u}-a_{u-1})\sum_{v=u}^{\smash{k}}e_{v}\right).

(2)

Proof.

The first identity is obtained by partitioning the area covered by the leftmost
a_{j}
columns of
Y_{k}
into horizontal blocks of length at most
a_{j}
.
The second identity is obtained by partitioning the same area into vertical blocks of widths
a_{1},a_{2}-a_{1},\ldots,a_{j}-a_{j-1}
.
∎

The value of
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}
depends on the relation between

a_{j}
and the
e_{i}
’s.

Notation 5
.

For a diagram with
k
row blocks and
m\in[1,k]
let
\textnormal{se}_{m,k}:=\sum_{t=m}^{k}e_{t}
, the number of rows in the upper
k-m+1
row blocks.

Claim 6
.

We have

\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}=\begin{cases}a_{j}a_{k}&\text{if }a_{j}\leqslant e_{k},\\
\sum_{t=i}^{k}e_{t}a_{t}+(a_{j}-\textnormal{se}_{i,k})a_{i-1}&\text{if }\textnormal{se}_{i,k}<a_{j}\leqslant\textnormal{se}_{i-1,k}\text{ for some }i\in[2,k],\\
|Y_{k}|&\text{if }\textnormal{se}_{1,k}\leqslant a_{j}.\end{cases}

(3)

Proof.

The first and third cases are obvious. For the second case, note that since
a_{j}>\textnormal{se}_{i,k}
, the subdiagram consisting of the top
a_{j}
rows contains the blocks
i,\ldots,k
entirely, and
a_{j}-\textnormal{se}_{i,k}
rows in block
i-1
.
∎

Theorem 7
.

To check whether
Y
is wide, we need to perform no more than the following tests:

a_{k}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i},\;\;\;\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,p,

(4)

and for
k=1,\ldots,p
, and
j=1,\ldots,k-1
,

\sum_{t=i}^{k}e_{t}a_{t}+(a_{j}-\textnormal{se}_{i,k})a_{i-1}\geqslant\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}a_{t}e_{t}+a_{j}\sum_{t=j}^{k}e_{t}\quad\text{if}\quad\textnormal{se}_{i,k}<a_{j}\leqslant\textnormal{se}_{i-1,k}\quad\text{for}\quad i\in[2,k].

(5)

Proof.

By Theorem
6
, to establish wideness, we need to show that
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}\geqslant\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant k\leqslant p
.
By Observation
2
, the inequality (
4
) is the case where
w=a_{k}
.
When
w\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\}
and
\textnormal{se}_{i,k}\leqslant a_{j}\leqslant\textnormal{se}_{i-1,k}
for some
i\in[2,k]
, then applying (
1
) and (
3
) to
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}\geqslant\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
yields (
5
). Now suppose that
a_{j}\leqslant e_{k}
. We prove that
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}\geqslant\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
is implied by (
4
).
To see this, note that by (
2
) when
a_{j}\leqslant e_{k}
the inequality
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}\geqslant\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
is equivalent to

a_{j}a_{k}\geqslant\sum_{u=1}^{j}\left((a_{u}-a_{u-1})\sum_{v=u}^{k}e_{v}\right).

(6)

But

\sum_{u=1}^{j}\left((a_{u}-a_{u-1})\sum_{v=u}^{k}e_{v}\right)\leqslant\sum_{u=1}^{j}\left((a_{u}-a_{u-1})\sum_{v=1}^{k}e_{v}\right)=a_{j}\sum_{v=1}^{k}e_{v}.

So, if (
4
) holds, then
a_{j}a_{k}\geqslant a_{j}\sum_{v=1}^{k}e_{v}
holds, and this is stronger than (
6
).
If
\textnormal{se}_{1,k}\leqslant a_{j}
, then
\textnormal{sr}_{j,k}\geqslant\textnormal{sc}_{j,k}
gives a trivial domination inequality.
∎

Corollary 8
.

The number of domination checks required to test whether a Young diagram is wide is at most a quadratic function of the number of distinct row lengths.

3
Outline rectangles and allocations

In
[
4
]
Hilton introduced the notion of an
outline rectangle
, related to Latin squares:

Definition 9
.

Let
C
be an
m\times m
matrix of multisets of symbols from
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{m}\}
. For
i\in[m]
let
\rho_{i}
be the sum of the cardinalities of the multisets in row
i
, let
c_{i}
be the sum of the cardinalities of the multisets in column
i
, and let
\sigma_{i}
be the number of occurrences of symbol
\tau_{i}
among all the multisets. Then
C
is an outline rectangle if there is some integer
n
such that the following is satisfied:

(i)

n
divides each of
\rho_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
\sigma_{i}
,

(ii)

cell
(i,j)
contains
\rho_{i}c_{j}/n^{2}
symbols,

(iii)

the number of occurrences of
\tau_{k}
in row
i
is
\rho_{i}\sigma_{k}/n^{2}
, and

(iv)

the number of occurrences of
\tau_{k}
in column
j
is
c_{j}\sigma_{k}/n^{2}
.

(The definition in
[
4
]
is more general, as the numbers of rows, columns, and symbols in
C
may not be equal, but here we shall only need this special version).

Hilton showed in
[
4
]
that an outline rectangle can be obtained from any
n\times n
Latin square
L
as follows. Let
P=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
,
Q=(q_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, and
S=(s_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be sequences of positive integers satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}=n
. Construct the outline rectangle
C
from
L
by amalgamating rows

p_{0}+\cdots+p_{i-1}+1,\ldots,p_{0}+\cdots+p_{i}
, columns
q_{0}+\cdots+q_{i-1}+1,\ldots,q_{0}+\cdots+q_{i}
, and symbols
s_{0}+\cdots+s_{i-1}+1,\ldots,s_{0}+\cdots+s_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,m
, where we interpret
p_{0}=q_{0}=s_{0}=0
.
The array
C
thus obtained is called
the reduction modulo
(P,Q,S)
of
L
.
Hilton
[
4
]
also showed that this works both ways, that is, we can obtain a Latin square from any outline rectangle:

Theorem 10
.

Each outline rectangle
C
is the reduction modulo
(P,Q,S)
of some Latin square
L
, for some sequences
P
,
Q
, and
S
.

Inspired by this idea, we introduce the notion of an
allocation
:

Figure 1:
Rectangular regions for an allocation.

Definition 11
.

Let
Y
be a Young diagram consisting of
e_{i}
rows of size
a_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
, where
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{p}
, as shown in Figure
1
. Define
a_{0}=0
and
b_{i}=a_{i}-a_{i-1}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
. An
allocation
for
Y
is a collection of nonnegative integers
z_{ijk}
for
1\leqslant j,k\leqslant i\leqslant p
, satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{i}z_{ijk}=e_{i}b_{j}

\displaystyle\text{for }1\leqslant j\leqslant i\leqslant p,

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i}z_{ijk}=e_{i}b_{k}

\displaystyle\text{for }1\leqslant k\leqslant i\leqslant p,

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{p}z_{ijk}\leqslant b_{j}b_{k}

\displaystyle\text{for }1\leqslant j,k\leqslant p.

(9)

Our first major result regarding allocations states that wideness is a necessary condition for the existence of an allocation.

Theorem 12
.

If a Young diagram
Y
has an allocation, then it is wide.

Proof.

Assuming that
Y
has an allocation, we will use Theorem
6
to
establish that
Y
is wide. Let
1\leqslant j_{0}\leqslant i_{0}\leqslant p
and consider the subdiagram
Y^{\prime}=Y_{i_{0}}
. The
total number of cells in the leftmost
a_{j_{0}}
columns of
Y^{\prime}
is

\sum_{j=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=j}^{i_{0}}b_{j}e_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=k}^{i_{0}}b_{k}e_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=k}^{i_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}z_{ijk}=\sum_{j=1}^{i_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{i_{0}}z_{ijk},

(10)

by
(\ref{e:sumj})
. Now, by
(\ref{e:sumi})
,

\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{i_{0}}z_{ijk}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}b_{j}b_{k}=b_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}b_{k}=b_{j}a_{j_{0}}.

(11)

On the other hand,

\sum_{k=1}^{j_{0}}\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{i_{0}}z_{ijk}\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{i_{0}}\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{i_{0}}z_{ijk}=\sum_{i=j}^{i_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{i}z_{ijk}=\sum_{i=j}^{i_{0}}b_{j}e_{i},

(12)

by
(\ref{e:sumk})
. Combining
(\ref{e:zijkapp1})
and
(\ref{e:zijkapp2})
we find
that
(\ref{e:zijkact})
is no more than

\sum_{j=1}^{i_{0}}b_{j}\min\left(a_{j_{0}},\sum_{i=\smash{j}}^{i_{0}}e_{i}\right),

which is the number of cells in the top
a_{j_{0}}
rows of
Y^{\prime}
.
It follows that
Y
is wide.
∎

If Conjecture
4
is true, then the converse of Theorem
12
also holds.

Our next major result on allocations is an analogue of Theorem
10
for Young diagrams.

Theorem 13
.

A Young diagram
Y
has an allocation if and only if
Y
is Latin.

Proof.

If
Y
is Latin, then it has an allocation; specifically, given a Latin filling of
Y
let
z_{ijk}
, for
1\leqslant j,k\leqslant i\leqslant p
, be the number of symbols from symbol block
k
that appear in the rectangular region in
Y
where row block
i
and column block
j
intersect (as illustrated in Figure
1
).

Suppose that
Y
consists of
e_{i}
rows of size
a_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant p
, where
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{p}
. Henceforth, we may assume that
Y
has an allocation
Z^{\prime}=(z^{\prime}_{ijk})_{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant i\leqslant p}
. We shall embed the underlying array of
Z^{\prime}
in a
(p+1)\times(p+1)
array and complete this array to an outline rectangle, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Embedding an allocation in an outline rectangle. The size of each cell is indicative of the size of the multiset that is assigned to it.

Let
n=2a_{p}
, let
b_{p+1}=a_{p}
, and let
e_{p+1}=n-(e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{p})
.
Define

\begin{split}z_{ijk}=\begin{cases}z^{\prime}_{ijk}&\text{if }\max(j,k)\leqslant i\leqslant p,\\
0&\text{if }i<\max(j,k)\leqslant p,\text{ or }j\leqslant i\leqslant p<k=p+1,\\
e_{i}b_{j}&\text{if }i<j\leqslant p<k=p+1,\\
e_{i}b_{k}-(z_{i1k}+z_{i2k}+\cdots+z_{ipk})&\text{if }i<j=p+1,\\
b_{j}b_{k}-(z_{1jk}+z_{2jk}+\cdots+z_{pjk})&\text{if }i=p+1.\end{cases}\end{split}

(13)

We verify that each
z_{ijk}
is nonnegative.
The first three cases in (
13
) are obvious, since
(z^{\prime}_{ijk})
is an allocation.
For the fourth case, suppose
k\leqslant i\leqslant p
. Then

e_{i}b_{k}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}z_{i\ell k}=e_{i}b_{k}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{i}z^{\prime}_{i\ell k}=e_{i}b_{k}-e_{i}b_{k}=0,

from
(\ref{e:sumj})
. If
p\geqslant k>i
then

e_{i}b_{k}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}z_{i\ell k}=e_{i}b_{k}\geqslant 0.

Finally,

e_{i}b_{p+1}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}z_{i\ell(p+1)}=e_{i}b_{p+1}-\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{p}e_{i}b_{\ell}=e_{i}\left(b_{p+1}-\sum_{\smash{\ell}=i+1}^{p}b_{\ell}\right)\geqslant 0.

The last inequality holds since
b_{p+1}=a_{p}
.
We conclude that
z_{i(p+1)k}\geqslant 0
whenever

.

Discussion: towards the general case

It is natural to try to generalise the construction in Section 4 to Young diagrams with any number of row lengths.
Let
Y
be a wide Young diagram with
p
row blocks.
We assume the subdiagram
Y^{\prime}
consisting of the first
p-1
blocks has an allocation
(z^{\prime}_{ijk})_{1\leqslant k,j\leqslant i\leqslant p-1}
adhering to certain, yet unknown, assumptions

generalizing (
30

z^{\prime}_{ijk}=z^{\prime}_{ikj}
for all
1\leqslant k,j\leqslant i\leqslant p-1
.
We aim to find an allocation
(z_{ijk})_{1\leqslant k,j\leqslant i\leqslant p}
of
Y
that is an extension of
(z^{\prime}_{ijk})
(i.e.
z_{ijk}=z^{\prime}_{ijk}
for
1\leqslant k,j\leqslant i\leqslant p-1
) and that satisfies the symmetry property
z_{pjk}=z_{pkj}
for all
1\leqslant k,j\leqslant p
.

If we choose values
z_{pjk}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant k\leqslant p-1
satisfying (
X
), then the rest of the allocation will be determined by (
7
) and (
8
):

z_{pjp}=b_{j}e_{p}-\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}\quad j=1,\ldots,p-1,

(79)

z_{ppk}=b_{k}e_{p}-\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}\quad k=1,\ldots,p-1,

(80)

and

\begin{split}z_{ppp}&=b_{p}e_{p}-\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}z_{ppk}=b_{p}e_{p}-\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\left(b_{k}e_{p}-\sum_{\smash{j}=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}\right)\\
&=b_{p}e_{p}-e_{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}b_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}=(b_{p}-a_{p-1})e_{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}.\end{split}

(81)

If we impose the constraints on the sums in (

), (
80
), and (
81
) arising from the non-negativity constraints of each variable and from (
9
), then we obtain upper and lower bounds for each of the variables

\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}

\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}z_{pjk}

We can show that wideness implies that each lower bound is indeed smaller than or equal to the corresponding upper bound. However, so far we have not been able to show that the system of inequalities is consistent, that is, that we can actually choose non-negative values

such that all our constraints are satisfied.

in Section

suggests that new ideas should be considered.

, where the possible entries of a Young diagram are viewed as edges in a 3-partite 3-hypergraph
