Mechanisms of yield and quality enhancement in Panax Vietnamensis: A comparison of three artificial cultivation models
Authors: Yifan Cheng, Ha Thanh Tung Pham, Pengfei Tu, Luqi Huang, Xiuming Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPanax vietnamensis is a medicinally valuable species whose wild populations are threatened by overexploitation, necessitating the development of sustainable, high-yielding cultivation systems.MethodA six-year systematic assessment was conducted on three artificial cultivation models: standard field (SF), artificial forest (AF), and wild forest (WF). Growth parameters, biomass production, and saponin content (via HPLC-ELSD) were measured. Furthermore, non-targeted metabolomic analysis (LC-MS/MS) was employed to elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying yield and quality in SF-grown plants.ResultsThe SF model produced the highest biomass at year five, significantly outperforming the AF and WF systems, while achieving competitive total saponin content.Metabolomic profiling identified the fifth year as a pivotal developmental transition, characterized by the coordinated upregulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis and stress adaptation pathways, alongside a significant enrichment of the key saponin precursor dimethylallyl diphosphate in taproots. Moreover, a functional division of labor was observed: rhizomes primarily synthesized precursors, whereas taproots functioned as the primary sink for saponin storage, indicating an efficient resource allocation strategy.ConclusionThis study establishes the five-year SF model as a scientifically grounded and sustainable cultivation protocol for P. vietnamensis. These findings advance our understanding of how cultivation-driven metabolic shifts mediate quality enhancement, providing a framework that integrates agricultural productivity with species conservation.

Introduction

The cultivation of agricultural crops has a long and evolving technological history; however, the systematic domestication of medicinal plants remains an area requiring continuous refinement. Wild medicinal species, such as Panax ginseng Meyer (P. ginseng) are traditionally highly prized due to the perceived superior quality often attributed to environmental stress-induced synthesis of bioactive compounds [1]. Nevertheless, wild populations face severe challenges, including slow growth rates, resource scarcity, and inconsistent quality caused by habitat instability [2]. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of overharvesting, habitat degradation, and escalating market demand have been associated with a precipitous decline in wild medicinal plant populations, potentially accelerating the risk of local extinction for vulnerable species. Recent studies have demonstrated that certain domesticated medicinal plants can exhibit a superior capacity for bioactive compound accumulation relative to their wild counterparts. For instance, cultivated Rehmannia glutinosa (Gaertn.) Libosch. ex Fisch. & C. A. Mey. develops significantly higher levels of active constituents with more consistent pharmacological efficacy than wild-grown plants [3]. These findings underscore the critical necessity for direct quality assessment assessments comparing wild and cultivated medicinal materials.

Panax vietnamensis Ha et Grushv. (P. vietnamensis), a highly valued member of the genus Panax L., is renowned for its unique saponin profile, characterized by an abundance of ocotillol-type triterpenoids [[4], [5], [6]]. Its considerable economic importance is reflected in a stark market prices dichotomy: artificially cultivated roots are valued at approximately 170/kg, whereas wild specimens can command premium prices up to 40,000/kg. In 2010, Vietnam formally incorporated this species into its national pharmacopoeia, authorizing its medicinal application. Currently, it is widely processed into health wines, cosmetics, and various functional products distributed domestically and exported to international markets such as Japan and South Korea.

P. vietnamensis is primarily cultivated in Southeast Asia, notably in Vietnam and China's Yunnan Province. Escalating market demand has intensified the exploitation of wild populations, resulting in significant biological resource depletion across their indigenous habitats [7]. Consequently, artificial cultivation offers an imperative strategy for both ecological conservation and sustainable utilization. Similar to related species such as P. ginseng [8], Panax quinquefolius L. [9], and Panax notoginseng (Burkill) F. H. Chen ex C. H. Chow (P. notoginseng) [10], P. vietnamensis is highly sensitive to micro-ecological conditions. Despite early propagation efforts initiated in Vietnam since 1979, successful large-scale commercial production has not been fully realized, indicating that systematic elucidating its domestication mechanisms remains severely limited [7,11].

To bridge this crucial research gap, we implemented and systematically evaluated three artificial cultivation models, including standard field (SF), artificial forest (AF), and wild forest (WF), within the natural distribution area of P. vietnamensis in China. Building upon prior preliminary studies [12,13], hypothesizes that optimized cultivation environments can modulate secondary metabolism to achieve an equilibrium between biomass accumulation and phytochemical quality. Specifically, this study was designed to identify the optimal cultivation model and harvest timing through a comparative evaluation of three distinct systems, and to elucidate the spatiotemporal metabolic shifts driving secondary metabolite biosynthesis within the prioritized SF system across its six-year developmental trajectory.

By integrating morphological evaluation, biomass quantification, and targeted saponin analysis across three models, this work establishes the first comprehensive growth-phytochemical correlation model for P. vietnamensis. Building upon the identification of the five-year SF model as the optimal cultivation system, we subsequently utilized non-targeted metabolomics to reveal the specific metabolic basis governing its harvest timing and quality formation. These findings provide the first scientifically grounded cultivation framework for Chinese production regions and offer novel insights into the metabolic mechanisms driving yield and efficacy enhancement in optimized artificial cultivation systems.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

Field experiments were conducted at elevations of 1673–1900 m near the Tropic of Cancer in Yunnan Province, China (Table 1). A randomized complete block design was implemented, comprising three replicates per treatment with each replicate containing ten plants. This study evaluated three distinct artificial cultivation models, including the SF model utilizing standard agricultural practices such as shade net installation (10–15% light transmittance), soil tillage, manual fertilization, and drip irrigation. The AF model involved cultivation under semi-natural forest canopies that provided 60–70% shade, supplemented with shade nets of 25–30% transmittance alongside standard farming and irrigation practices. Growth conditions and agronomic practices

Seeds were sourced from healthy four-year-old mother plants. Seedlings were raised under the three cultivation models for two years (sowing spacing: 5 × 5 cm) and transplanted in the third year. In the SF and AF models, seedlings were transplanted at 25 × 25 cm (approx. 180,000 plants/ha, plot area: 12 m2 per replicate). Field management for SF and AF included manual weeding, organic fertilizer application (37,500 kg/ha, twice annually), and pest control (e.g., polyoxin). Conversely, the WF model (transplanting spacing: 25 × 30 cm, 150,000 plants/ha) received no artificial pollination, fertilization, or pesticide application.

Sample collection and agronomic trait evaluation

At harvest, ten plants from each age group (1 to 6-year-old) within each experimental plot were randomly sampled. To calculate plot yield, taproots and rhizomes were separated, cleaned, sliced, and air-dried in the shade. Agronomic traits, including plant height, rhizome/taproot length, fresh/dry weights, and fibrous root number, were measured systematically.

Additionally, majonoside R2 (MR2) and vina-ginsenoside R2 (VR2) were provided by the Kunming Institute of Botany (CAS) and the Saigon Pharma Technology Center (Vietnam), respectively. To ensure representative sampling and mitigate individual biological heterogeneity, tissues (taproots and rhizomes) from ten independent plants within each replicate plot were pooled and homogenized to form a single composite sample. Consequently, three biological replicates (n = 3) were established for each cultivation model at each growth year. Approximately 0.5 g of the resulting dried powder (passed through an 80-mesh sieve) was accurately weighed and subjected to ultrasonic extraction (250 W, 40 kHz) in 25 mL of methanol for 30 min. After cooling, the weight loss was compensated with methanol, and the extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) analysis.

Analytical method and validation

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Shimadzu LC-20AD system equipped with an evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD), using a Pntulips RSZG-C18 Plus column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase comprised acetonitrile (A) and water (B) using a gradient elution (Supplementary Table S1) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Injection volume was 20 μL.

The reference standards were prepared and diluted into a series of concentrations to establish calibration curves (Supplementary Table 2–3). Method sensitivity was evaluated by determining the LOD and LOQ. The LODs for the six major saponins ranged from 0.0244 to 0.0844 mg/mL, while the LOQs ranged from 0.0813 to 0.2813 mg/mL (detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 4). All analytes exhibited excellent linearity (R2 ≥ 0.993) within the tested concentration ranges. These parameters demonstrate the high sensitivity of the analytical system for the quantification of major saponins in P. vietnamensis. The analytical method was rigorously validated. Methodological repeatability and 24 h stability tests yielded Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) values below 1.0% for all analytes (Supplementary Table 5–6). The mean recoveries ranged from 95.7% to 104.4%, with RSD values ≤ 3.7%, confirming high accuracy (Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, the method demonstrated robust performance across minor variations in column temperature, flow rate, and column batches (RSD <3.0%; Supplementary Table 8–10).

Metabolomic profiling of SF model

Sample pooling strategy and LC-MS/MS acquisition

To rigorously mitigate individual biological heterogeneity and ensure that the analyzed data accurately represent the population mean, a pooling strategy was employed. For the metabolomics analysis, three biological replicates (n = 3) were employed for each developmental year. To minimize individual biological variation and enhance sample representativeness, a pooling strategy was adopted: each replicate was a composite sample formulated by homogenizing tissues from ten independent plants (totaling n = 30 plants per group). This sample size and pooling approach follow standard practices in plant metabolomics research, particularly for long-cycle perennial medicinal herbs cultivated under controlled environments [14]. Profiling was executed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system coupled to an AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer equipped with an Electrospray Ionization source. Scans were performed in positive and negative ion modes. The m/z scan range was 60–1000 Da for TOF-MS and 25–1000 Da for product ions, with a mass resolving power of 35,000 FWHM. Collision energy (CE) was optimized to 35 ± 15 eV to maximize fragment ion coverage.

Data processing and metabolite annotation

Raw data (mzXML) were processed using the XCMS package in R for peak detection and alignment. Metabolite annotation was achieved by matching accurate mass (mass error <5 ppm) and MS/MS fragmentation patterns against established databases. All reported annotations strictly meet the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level 2 confidence criteria (putative identification). Functional pathway mapping was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (BR08001).

Multivariate statistics and model validation

Positive and negative ion mode datasets were integrated and subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with 95% confidence ellipses to evaluate intra-group consistency. For biomarker selection, a Random Forest (RF) algorithm was employed. To ensure optimal predictive accuracy and prevent overfitting, the model utilized a bootstrapping approach (stabilizing at an OOB error rate of 0.05). Model robustness was comprehensively validated: the taproot and rhizome models achieved AUC-ROC values of 0.889 and 0.778, with specificities of 0.91 and 0.84, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1). Feature selection was strictly governed by the Mean Decrease Gini metric, retaining only the top 15 most informative variables (Gini >2). Furthermore, differential metabolites were rigorously filtered using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) method, with the significance threshold set at an adjusted P-value (q-value) < 0.05 to strictly control for false positives in high-dimensional multiple testing.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Before statistical analysis, the normality of data distribution and the homogeneity of variance were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Differences among cultivation models and developmental years were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test using SPSS 29.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.0. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Effects of cultivation models on morphological traits

Artificial cultivation represents a strategic approach for conservation and sustainable utilization of rare medicinal plants. P. vietnamensis, although nationally protected in both Vietnam and China, remains critically threatened by illegal wild harvesting [15]. The successful domestication of such forest-dwelling species involves bridging complex interdisciplinary gaps between ecology, and agronomy [16]. Our systematic evaluation reveals that the successful domestication of this forest-dwelling species involves significant morphological plasticity in response to distinct cultivation environments.

Aboveground morphological plasticity

Plant height is a critical architectural trait directly correlated with photosynthetic capacity and underground biomass accumulation [17]. Our results demonstrated that cultivation models significantly modulated growth dynamics (Fig. 3A). SF P. vietnamensis (SFPV) exhibited a robust, staged growth pattern, with height increasing from 24.9 cm (year 1) to a substantial 92.5 cm (year 6). Interestingly, WF P. vietnamensis (WFPV), despite initially stunted growth in the first two years, showed a dramatic “compensatory growth” phase after year three, eventually surpassing all models with a maximum height of 106.3 cm in the sixth year (Supplementary Table 11). Stem diameter and leaf area, which govern mechanical support and light interception efficiency [18], also showed system-specific variations (Fig. 3B and C, Supplementary Table 12–13). SFPV maintained the largest stem diameter from the fourth year onwards, reaching 7.6 mm, providing the structural integrity required to support its substantial biomass. In contrast, AF P. vietnamensis (AFPV) growth appeared constrained, with both stem thickness and leaf area declining in the sixth year, likely due to excessive shading or suboptimal nutrient cycling in the semi-natural forest plots. Notably, SFPV achieved its maximum leaf area (87.5 cm2) in year six, facilitating a significant increase in aboveground fresh weight (63.1 g), which was 6.3 times higher than that of AFPV (Fig. 3D–Supplementary Table 14).

Development of underground storage organs

The underground system, comprising the rhizome and taproot, is the primary reservoir for bioactive saponins in P. vietnamensis [5]. Morphological analysis (Fig. 4) revealed that artificial cultivation induced pronounced “swelling” of these organs—a typical domestication response [10]. Rhizome development, characterized by annual stem scars, reflected the chronological age and vigor of the plants. SFPV rhizomes showed superior growth in length, diameter, and biomass compared to AFPV and WFPV (Fig. 3F–H). By the sixth year, the dry matter accumulation in SFPV rhizomes reached its peak (Fig. 3L), reinforcing the SF model's efficiency in producing high-yield medicinal parts.

Taproot development, however, showed a distinct temporal pattern. While elongation in SFPV stabilized after the fourth year (Fig. 3I), biomass (fresh and dry weight) continued to increase, reaching a maximum in year five (Fig. 3J and M). Five-year-old SFPV taproots were approximately 4.6 times heavier than those of AFPV, suggesting that intensive management in the SF model significantly accelerates the “thickening” phase of the storage organs.

WFPV maintained a morphology most similar to the wild reference (WWPV), with more slender rhizomes and lower overall biomass, reflecting the resource-limited conditions of its natural habitat. The divergent underground phenotypes between SFPV and WFPV underscore the high morphological plasticity of P. vietnamensis. Intensive cultivation (SF) prioritizes biomass partitioning toward storage organs, whereas the forest models (AF/WF) balance growth with environmental stressors.

In summary, the SFPV model outperformed AF and WF in both aboveground vigor and underground yield. However, the similarity of WFPV to wild specimens suggests that while SF is optimal for rapid biomass production, WF might be preferable for maintaining “wild-like” morphological characteristics. These modifications align with domestication responses in other Panax species, where intensive management prioritizes carbon partitioning toward storage tissues [19,20].

Dry matter accumulation and yield performance

As a perennial herb, P. vietnamensis can survive for decades in the wild. However, artificial cultivation significantly accelerates its life cycle.Dry matter accumulation showed distinct periodicity across three developmental stages (Fig. 3K–M).

Growth and resource accumulation stage (Years 1–4)

During the initial seedling phase (Years 1–2), biomass accumulation was constrained by limited seed reserves and developing photosynthetic capacity. AFPV growth was significantly lower than other models during this period (P < 0.01). As plants entered the rapid growth phase (Years 3–4), they exhibited vigorous vegetative expansion. By the fourth year, the dry weight of taproots in SFPV reached 18.20 g, significantly outperforming AFPV (5.05 g) and WFPV (15.69 g). This four-year trajectory represents the critical window for establishing the structural framework and primary storage capacity of the medicinal organs.

Harvest maturity and yield quantification (Years 5–6)

The harvest stage (Years 5–6) marked the peak of biomass accumulation across all systems. Individual plant dry weight in the SF model peaked at year 6 (74.60 g), while WF and AF models reached their maximums at year 5.

At the production scale (Table 2), all three cultivation models attained their maximum dried yield per hectare during the fifth year. The SFPV yield reached 2835 kg/ha, significantly exceeding WFPV (2280 kg/ha) and AFPV (1890 kg/ha) (P < 0.05). Notably, all systems experienced a yield decline in the sixth year, accompanied by an increase in data variance (RSD up to 0.08 in SFPV). This decline is likely associated with increased susceptibility to soil-borne pathogens and pests as the cultivated populations age. Spatiotemporal dynamics of total saponins

Total saponin content was initially low in year-1 taproots (4.52%–5.40%), with the highest accumulation observed in the SF and lowest in the WF. Following rhizome formation in year 2, saponin accumulation accelerated significantly across all models, with SFPV consistently exhibiting the highest levels.

In terms of specific tissues, SFPV rhizomes (SFPVR) exhibited continuous accumulation, attaining a maximum total saponin content of 18.77% in the sixth year. Conversely, SFPVT reached 15.88% in the fifth year and plateaued at 15.97% in the sixth year. Interestingly, both taproots and rhizomes of AFPV and WFPV attained their absolute maximum saponin levels in the fifth year, followed by a noticeable decline in the sixth year. A cross-model comparison of peak total saponin content confirmed that SFPVR achieved the highest overall concentration, whereas WFPV rhizomes (WFPVR) and WFPVT showed the lowest values (Fig. S2I).

Saponin composition profiles in underground tissues

Monomeric saponin accumulation patterns showed strong dependence on both compound type and cultivation model (Fig. S2A–H). The two predominant ocotillol-type (OCT) saponins—MR2 and VR2—followed trends parallel to total saponin accumulation under SF cultivation. In AF and WF systems, however, these saponins exhibited distinct accumulation dynamics. For instance, VR2 in AFPV rhizomes (AFPVR) reached maximum levels in the fourth year, declined during the fifth year, then recovered in the sixth year. Protopanaxadiol (PPD)-type saponins (GRb1, GRd) and protopanaxatriol (PPT)-type saponins (GRg1, NR1) displayed model-specific accumulation patterns, frequently showing multiphasic “M"- or “W"-shaped curves. These findings demonstrate type-specific responses of saponins to different cultivation conditions, indicating complex regulation of biosynthetic pathways.

Accumulation patterns of monomeric saponins

Based on combined yield (Table 2) and quality (Fig. 5) assessments, the SF model exhibited the highest overall performance. The SF approach adopted facility-based management practices, including optimized planting density, rigorous environmental control, and systematic disease management [9,21]. These controlled conditions promoted both vigorous expansion and high saponin accumulation. However, because taproot saponin levels plateaued after year 5 and disease incidence (which degrades yield) increased in year 6, an integrated analysis firmly supports harvesting SFPV during the fifth year. This corresponds to a standard two-year seedling stage followed by a three-year transplant growth cycle.

The AF model, utilizes artificial forests with shading structures, enabling efficient land use. However, suboptimal light distribution and elevated humidity in this system restricted growth rates and delayed dry matter accumulation. The observed decline in saponin content in year 6 is likely attributable to root senescence and resource depletion caused by increased pest susceptibility in aging plants. Thus, a five-year harvest cycle is also optimal for AF systems.

Finally, the WF model relies on natural forest shade with minimal human intervention. Although growth rates and absolute saponin contents were lower than in SF model, the chemical profiles and morphological traits of WF-grown materials most closely resembled those of WWPV. This model is highly suitable for producing premium, eco-friendly medicinal materials. Given the decline in secondary metabolites and yield after year 5, harvesting in the fifth year remains the recommended strategy for the WF model as well.

Metabolomic basis for optimal harvest timing in SFPV

Dynamic shift between primary and secondary metabolism

Based on previous studies, SFPV has been identified as a highly suitable species for large-scale cultivation. To decipher the metabolic reprogramming governing the growth and quality of SFPV, untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted across six developmental years. A total of 2272 metabolites were identified. In SFPVT, a clear metabolic transition was observed: carbohydrates and glycosides remained stable during the initial three years but exhibited a sharp increase from the fourth year, peaking in the fifth year (Fig. 6A). Conversely, primary metabolites such as organic acids, peptides, and lipids showed a consistent decline. This “metabolic flip” suggests a strategic resource reallocation from vegetative growth to secondary metabolite biosynthesis and stress adaptation—a phenomenon corroborated by the surge in saponin accumulation (Fig. 5C). Growth-stage specific reprogramming and biomarker identification

Unsupervised PCA revealed distinct clustering of SFPVT samples by year, with the fifth-year taproots (SFPVT5) occupying a unique metabolic space (Fig. 6C). This suggests that year 5 represents a critical physiological turning point. While SFPVR also showed year-to-year divergence, the clustering was more compact (Fig. 6D), implying higher metabolic stability in the rhizome compared to the taproot.

To identify specific chemical signatures, a Random Forest (RF) model was employed, as the RF-derived biomarkers (Fig. 6E and F) and their corresponding group-clustered heatmaps (Fig. 6G and H) provide a high-resolution view of the maturation process. In the SFPVT (Fig. 6G), markers for early growth (Years 1–3) included 4-Hydroxy-3-methoxycinnamaldehyde and Spermine, which are linked to cell wall lignification and rapid division. In contrast, L-Malic acid, Glucose 6-phosphate, and Xanthohumol showed peak intensities in the 5th year, suggesting heightened energy metabolism and specialized metabolite flux at this stage. Regarding the SFPVR (Fig. 6H), the 6th-year rhizomes were characterized by the accumulation of Salicylic acid and Wedelolactone, indicating that older rhizomes may prioritize defense-related signaling pathways. Notably, L-Malic acid and Cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate (cAMP) also peaked in the later years (Years 5–6), likely acting as secondary messengers and carbon sources for sustained saponin synthesis.

Pathway integration and saponin biosynthesis flux

Integration of metabolomic data into the triterpenoid biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 7) revealed a coordinated upregulation of precursors and intermediates in the 5th year. This bioenergetic support is essential for the high-cost synthesis of complex OCT and PPT saponins. Metabolic pathway analysis and growth-defense trade-offs in SFPV

A dedicated KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites revealed distinct metabolic activation patterns during SFPV development. In the five-year-old SFPVT, key pathways including flavonoid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis were significantly activated (Fig. 8G, Fig. S5A). The fifth growth year represented a critical developmental phase distinct from other stages, as coordinated upregulation occurred in pathways associated with dry matter accumulation and bioactive compound synthesis. A group-clustered heatmap of hub metabolites in the SFPVT demonstrated that compounds such as 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, gallic acid, 20(R)-notoginsenoside R2, geniposidic acid, (R)-2,3-dihydroxy-3-methylbutanoate, and trehalose showed gradual accumulation during the first four years, peaked in the fifth year, and declined thereafter. Furthermore, metabolites associated with carbon metabolism, such as glucose 6-phosphate and sucrose, increased from Years 1 to 4 before decreasing in Years 5 to 6 (Fig. S5B).

A similar pattern was observed in the SFPVR, with five-year-old samples showing enhanced activity in cofactor biosynthesis, anthocyanin biosynthesis, amino acid and flavonoid pathways, and secondary metabolite synthesis (Fig. 8H, Fig. S5C). The group-clustered analysis of the rhizome confirmed that key regulatory metabolites including propanoic acid, 3-methylbutanoate, and 20(R)-notoginsenoside R2 also peaked in Year 5, confirming synchronized metabolic activation between taproots and rhizomes at this specific stage (Fig. S5D).

In terms of specific biosynthetic flux, metabolic analysis revealed that DMAPP, a central precursor in saponin biosynthesis, was highly enriched in five-year-old taproots. As DMAPP and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) serve as universal building blocks for all terpenoid compounds in the isoprenoid pathway, the accumulation of DMAPP directly underpins the peak saponin accumulation observed at this stage [22]. Additionally, glucose 6-phosphate serves as a key precursor for saponin biosynthesis because it supplies essential substrates including erythrose-4-phosphate and phosphoenolpyruvate through the pentose phosphate pathway. These compounds represent the initial substrates for the Methylerythritol phosphate pathway, ultimately leading to IPP and DMAPP synthesis [23]. Malic acid, though indirectly linked to saponin production, contributes carbon skeletons and energy that support the formation of IPP and DMAPP. The gradual increase in both glucose 6-phosphate and malic acid during the first three years reflects an extended preparatory phase for carbon skeleton assembly required for subsequent high-intensity saponin biosynthesis.

Furthermore, other indirect metabolites including gallic acid, carnosol, umbelliferone, and trans-piceid are primarily associated with plant defense and may indirectly influence saponin accumulation through resource competition or signal crosstalk. These results collectively suggest a functional trade-off between primary growth and secondary metabolism under resource-limited conditions, perfectly aligning with known mechanisms of plant stress adaptation and further validating the fifth year as the physiological turning point for optimal harvesting.

Metabolic differentiation and functional specialization between tissues

Volcano plot analysis provided a detailed overview of the chemical divergence between the two tissues at their physiological peak, identifying 102 upregulated and 94 downregulated metabolites in five-year-old taproots compared to rhizomes (Fig. S4K). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these differential metabolites were primarily associated with flavone and flavonol biosynthesis as well as the broader biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. 8I). Distinct tissue-specific accumulation patterns were evident, where rhizomes exhibited a higher abundance of L-malic acid along with compounds such as (2R)-2,3-dihydroxypropanoic acid, indole-3-acetaldehyde, 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, maleic acid, 4-hydroxy-3-methoxycinnamaldehyde, daphnetin, and 2-hydroxyphenylacetic acid. In contrast, taproots were characterized by the predominant accumulation of 5′-deoxy-5′-methylthioadenosine, erythromycin, citraconic acid, and phenethylamine. These metabolic signatures reflect a sophisticated functional specialization between the two tissues regarding secondary metabolite synthesis and precursor allocation.

A particularly notable finding was the significant enrichment of L-malic acid, a key TCA cycle intermediate, within the rhizomes during the fifth year. In model systems like Arabidopsis thaliana, root-secreted L-malic acid has been demonstrated to recruit beneficial Bacillus subtilis to the rhizosphere [24]. The high concentration of L-malic acid in SFPVR5 presents a plausible biological mechanism by which the rhizome might modulate the recruitment of beneficial microbiota to support plant health. While the translation of this metabolic enrichment to specific rhizosphere signaling in Panax species remains a hypothesis, it suggests that integrated microbiome and root exudate analyses could be a promising direction for future research [25].

Beyond potential signaling roles, the tissues appear to exhibit a “source-sink” relationship in secondary metabolism where the rhizome primarily supplies essential precursors while the taproot functions as the principal accumulation site for specialized metabolites. The predominant presence of compounds such as 5′-deoxy-5′-methylthioadenosine and phenethylamine in taproots supports their role in storing end products including saponins and flavonoids. This metabolic division of labor represents an efficient resource allocation strategy. By spatially separating biosynthetic pathways from storage functions, the plant may enhance its stress resilience through reduced metabolic interference and the specialized safeguarding of active compounds. Furthermore, the elevated saponin accumulation observed in the SF model aligns with the theory of stress-induced secondary metabolism. The shift in metabolic flux toward saponins and phenolics in five-year-old plants likely reflects an adaptive response to prolonged cultivation, reinforcing the role of these compounds in chemical defense and osmotic adjustment. The synchronous activation of flavonoid and cofactor biosynthesis further supports a complex interplay between environmental stress signaling and medicinal quality formation in P. vietnamensis.

Limitations and future perspectives

While this study provides a multi-dimensional evaluation of P. vietnamensis under different cultivation regimes, several limitations should be acknowledged to guide future research. A primary constraint is that the comprehensive untargeted metabolomic profiling was strategically focused on the SFPV model rather than encompassing the AFPV and WFPV systems. This decision was based on the fact that SFPV represents the dominant commercial cultivation mode requiring urgent standardization of harvest timing and quality control protocols. Furthermore, the SFPV environment provides a more controlled setting to isolate the intrinsic metabolic reprogramming associated with plant age, whereas the high environmental stochasticity of AFPV and WFPV sites might introduce confounding variables that mask core developmental signals. Nevertheless, future studies should employ comparative metabolomics across all three models to identify specific “wild-simulated” chemical markers that differentiate forest-grown plants from farm-grown ones.

Another limitation involves the depth of our mechanistic exploration regarding the rhizosphere. Although our data highlighted a significant enrichment of L-malic acid in five-year-old rhizomes and suggested a potential role in microbial recruitment, this remains a chemical-based hypothesis. Integrating the current metabolomic findings with 16S rRNA sequencing of the rhizosphere microbiome and conducting targeted root exudate collection would be essential to confirm whether this “metabolic peak” at Year 5 indeed triggers a beneficial shift in the soil microbial community. Such integrated “multi-omics” approaches would provide a more holistic understanding of how different cultivation practices influence the interplay between plant physiology and ecological adaptation.

Finally, while the five-year harvest window identified in this study appears optimal for SFPV based on both biomass and metabolite accumulation, the potentially slower growth rates and higher environmental stress in AFPV and WFPV systems might shift this physiological turning point. Long-term monitoring of these semi-wild models is necessary to determine if their “peak medicinal value” aligns with the farm-grown counterparts. Expanding the research to include transcriptomic analysis could further reveal the underlying gene regulatory networks that drive the tissue-specific division of labor and stress-induced saponin biosynthesis observed in this study. Despite these limitations, the current findings offer a robust scientific foundation for the sustainable cultivation and quality evaluation of P. vietnamensis.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanisms governing yield and efficacy enhancement in P. vietnamensis across three distinct artificial cultivation models. The SF model emerged as the superior production system for balancing biomass expansion and saponin accumulation, with peak productivity being consistently reached in the fifth growth year. Metabolic analysis revealed that this stage represents a critical physiological turning point characterized by the coordinated activation of specialized metabolite pathways and stress adaptation mechanisms, a process specifically underpinned by the significant enrichment of key precursors such as DMAPP in the taproots.

Our findings further identify the fifth growth year as a pivotal metabolic transition stage defined by the synchronized upregulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis and precursor flux. A significant contribution of this work is the characterization of a clear functional specialization between the rhizomes and taproots, where the former serves as the site for precursor synthesis while the latter functions as the primary reservoir for saponins and flavonoids. This sophisticated metabolic division of labor represents an efficient resource allocation strategy that enhances both environmental resilience and medicinal potency, offering the first empirical evidence of such tissue-specific coordination in P. vietnamensis.

These results establish a rigorous and scientifically-grounded protocol for the sustainable cultivation of this endangered species. Specifically, the SF model with a five-year cycle is recommended as the optimal production standard, incorporating a two-year seedling phase followed by a three-year field transplantation period. This framework effectively integrates industrial production capacity with ecological sustainability, ensuring high medicinal quality while supporting the conservation of wild genetic resources. Furthermore, the identified metabolic principles and cultivation strategies provide a scalable blueprint for optimizing the production of other high-value medicinal plants with similar ecological requirements, ultimately advancing our fundamental understanding of how cultivation practices modulate the intricate balance between vegetative growth and quality-determining metabolic processes.
