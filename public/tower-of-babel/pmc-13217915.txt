Myco-foods and the gut microbiome: impacts of mycelial extracts, biomass, and mold-fermented foods
Authors: Johanna I Keigler, Juliana Maria Leite Nobrega de Moura Bell, Maria L Marco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEdible filamentous fungi include mushrooms and molds, which are consumed as extracts, mycelial biomass, and fermented foods. These fungal foods are often high in protein and fiber and are generally regarded as nutritious. This narrative review examines current knowledge on how mycelia from molds, including edible strains of Aspergillus, Rhizopus, Neurospora, Fusarium, Mucor, and Paecilomyces, affect the gut microbiome. Allfour human trials on these foods (two extracts, one biomass, and one fermented food) reported a measurable effect on the gut microbiome. These studies, plus the additional eight animal and eight in vitro studies performed, frequently found increases in the proportions of intestinal Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, and lactobacilli. Bacteroides, Roseburia, and Eubacterium, which are recognized for their roles in fiber metabolism, were also frequently enriched, and numerous studies reported increases in fecal short-chain fatty acids. Notably, effects on the gut microbiome may be fungal species and food format-dependent. Although β-glucans and chitin are likely key determinants of gut microbiome responses to dietary mycelium, future studies should investigate how these and other potentially bioactive components of mycelia and fungal metabolites are metabolized by intestinal microorganisms. Such studies will result in an improved understanding of how myco-foods could support human health.Keywords: Fungi, gut microbiota, fermented foods, mycelium, mold, myco-foods

Introduction

The human gastrointestinal tract houses trillions of microorganisms, which are increasingly linked to health and well-being. 1 While there are various genetic and environmental factors that determine the composition and function of the human gut microbiome, diet is a major, plausibly modifiable determinant. 2 , 3 Habitual diets, individual foods, and even single dietary compounds can alter the activity of the gut microbiome. 4-6 Thus, understanding the impacts of different dietary components on the gut microbiome can lead to improved dietary recommendations and food formulations.

Edible filamentous fungi include mushrooms (the fruiting body of a filamentous fungus), and mycelium (the vegetative form of mushrooms or molds), while excluding yeast. 7 According to the archeological record, filamentous fungi in the form of mushrooms were likely part of human diets more than 10,000 y ago. 8 Beyond mushroom-forming species, filamentous fungi include edible molds, which do not form macroscopic fruiting bodies. 9 These fungi can be consumed in mycelial extract, biomass, and fermented food formats. Many compounds with medicinal importance (such as antibiotics, immunosuppressants, and statins) have been extracted or derived from molds, including Aspergillus and Fusarium species. 10 However, mycelial extracts from edible molds are also of interest because of their high fiber and other bioactive contents. Several species of molds, such as Fusarium venenatum, Aspergillus oryzae, and Neurospora crassa, are also cultivated as mycelial biomass foods or “mycofoods”. 7 Mycelial biomass has been promoted as a resource-efficient food containing up to 60% protein and 21%–56% fiber by dry weight. 11 , 12 Lastly, molds have been used in traditional food fermentations for millennia. 13 They are necessary to make certain cheeses, fermented meats, soy sauce, and alcoholic beverages. Fermented foods in which the mycelia from those fungi are dominant typically use legumes and grains as substrates, wherein the mycelium is enmeshed with its substrate. Examples include tempeh (legumes fermented by Rhizopus spp.), koji (rice fermented by Aspergillus spp.), and oncom (food byproducts fermented by Neurospora spp.). 14-16

This narrative review examines studies that measured how mycelial extracts and foods, specifically from mold species in the Ascomycetes and Mucoromycota phyla, alter the composition and functional activities of the gut microbiome. Other reviews have synthesized current knowledge on how mushroom polysaccharides 17-19 and culinary or medicinal mushrooms can affect the gut microbiome; 20 , 21 therefore, those foods are not discussed here. Instead, we focused on extracts, mycelial biomass, and fermented foods mainly composed of or derived from mold species of filamentous fungi (Figure 1). These foods are of interest because mycelial biomass and mold-fermented foods are increasingly recognized as sustainable sources of protein 22 , 23 and dietary fibers 24 that differ compositionally from plant fibers. 25 This review encompasses publications from the past 10 y (2016–2026) available in Google Scholar and PubMed and identified using search terms such as fungal biomass, mycoprotein, fungal polysaccharides, fungal species names, fermented foods, as well as the terms gut microbiome, gut microbiota, and intestinal bacteria. The safety and allergenicity of fungi-based foods are also important considerations, but beyond the scope of this review. Owing to the limited number of human and mechanistic studies, we recommend a multidisciplinary approach integrating expertise in food processing, analytical chemistry, and microbiology to further elucidate their potential to improve health through gut microbiome modulation.

Depiction of three types of mycelial foods. These types include extracts, which primarily contain fibers such as β-glucans and/or chitin from the cell walls of A. niger or other edible mycelia; mycelial biomass, most commonly “mycoprotein” from F. venenatum; and whole fermented foods, which are primarily grain-based (e.g., rice) or legume-based (e.g., soybeans) and contain both fungal mycelium and plant substrate.

Effects of mycelial extracts on the gut microbiome

The components extracted from three species of mold fungi have been examined for their capacity to alter the intestinal microbiota of humans, animals, and in vitro models (Table 1). Five studies focused on extracts primarily comprised of the dietary fibers β-glucan and chitin polysaccharides, which function as structural cell wall polysaccharides in all fungi. 26 , 27 β-glucans are a structurally diverse group of soluble, linear or branched polysaccharides consisting of glucose monomers linked by β-glycosidic bonds. 28 The levels of β-glucan polysaccharides ranged from 1% to 24% in the dry mycelia across 12 fungal species. 24 Fungal glucan structures vary in β-(1–6), and β-(1–3) linkages 29 (Figure 2). β-glucans from yeast and filamentous fungi have β-(1,6) linkages that cereal β-glucans do not contain, and filamentous fungi have β-(1,6) side chains that differ from the structures of yeast β-glucans. 25 , 28 Species of filamentous fungi differ in the structure, molecular weight, and side-chain substitutions in their β-glucans. 30 Chitin is a nondigestible polysaccharide primarily composed of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) linked by β-(1–4)-glycosidic bonds. 31 Mycelia of filamentous fungi contain about 5%–20% chitin by dry mass. 32

Two human studies have evaluated the effects of fibers extracted from molds on the gut microbiome (Table 1). These studies tested chitin-glucan (CG), an insoluble fiber containing both chitin and glucans extracted from the cell wall of Aspergillus niger. Specifically, a longitudinal study examined for changes to the gut microbiota and metabolites of 15 healthy subjects who consumed 4.5 g CG daily for three weeks. 33 The intake of CG was associated with changes in the relative abundance of nine microbial genera, including significant increases in Roseburia, Eubacterium, and Ruminococcacae and decreases in Blautia and Dorea. 33 Increases in the fecal levels of the SCFAs butyric, caproic, and isovaleric acids were also reported. Quantities of the long-chain fatty acid (LCFA) vaccenic acid increased, while levels of fecal arachidonic acid decreased. 33 In the second study, a cross-over randomized-control trial (RCT) was performed with 16 subjects with cardiometabolic risk who consumed 4.5 g A. niger-derived CG per day for three weeks. 34 Compared to individuals provided a maltodextrin control, CG intake was associated with significantly higher levels of Erysipelotrichaceae, Ruminococcaceae, and bacteria in the Eubacterium ventriosum group. 34 This study also measured fecal metabolites (SCFAs, LCFAs, and bile acids) but did not detect changes in these compounds compared to baseline. 34 Notably, however, evidence of increased colonic fermentation was indicated by higher breath hydrogen levels. 34 Supplementation with CG also improved lipid metabolism and postprandial glycemia, which was correlated with increased breath hydrogens. Supplementation with H2 (via water) was associated with improved glucose and lipid metabolism in a clinical trial. 39 Thus, hydrogen production may be one mechanism through which fungal fiber fermentation by the gut microbiota influences host metabolism.

In pre-clinical mouse models, two studies assessed the capacity of edible mold extracts to alter the gut microbiome (Table 1). A study with healthy rats found that A. niger-derived CG supplementation at 10% of the diet with Bifidobacterium breve 2 L (at 109 CFU/day) increased the number of B. breve 2 L in stool compared to bacterial inoculation alone. 35 This finding indicates that A. niger-derived CG may confer prebiotic effects by supporting the colonization and persistence of Bifidobacterium in the intestine. A study with healthy mice also evaluated how glycosylceramide purified from A. oryzae-fermented koji may affect the gut microbiome (Table 1). Ingestion of the glycosylceramide (at 1% of dietary intake) was associated with an increased abundance of B. coccoides, 36 a putatively beneficial gut commensal. 40

The microbial metabolism of CG was also examined in vitro (Table 1). When incubated in human stools, the CG was associated with increased proportions of Roseburia spp. and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae and higher SCFA (propionate, acetate, and butyrate) levels. 37 Another study found that 100 Bifidobacterium strains, including the probiotic Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 were able to grow on CG as a sole carbon source. 35 In contrast, β-glucan purified from F. venenatum supported the growth of Bacteroides (11 of 23 gut-associated strains) but not Bifidobacterium (three strains) 25 (Table 1). Notably, this study showed that Bifidobacterium and Lactiplantibacillus strains could grow on the oligosaccharides released by Bacteroides. 25 The finding indicates that fungal β-glucan consumption may result in increases in beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium due to nutritional cross-feeding. 25

Effects of mycelial biomass on the gut microbiome

Mycelial biomass is a complex matrix consisting of proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and other components. Thus far, one human study has reported the effects of mycelial biomass consumption on the gut microbiome (Table 2). Mycoprotein from F. venenatum is one of the most well-established examples of edible mycelial biomass and is the main ingredient used by the food company Quorn™ in their processed mycoprotein product, which contains 45% protein, 13% fat, 25% fiber, 10% non-fiber carbohydrates, and 3.4% ash by dry weight. 41 In a crossover RCT, 20 healthy adult males consumed 240 g/day of either processed F. venenatum “mycoprotein” (Quorn™) or processed red meat for two weeks. 42 F. venenatum mycoprotein consumption was associated with increased proportions of fecal lactobacilli, Roseburia, and Akkermansia and no changes in fecal SCFA compared to red meat intake. 42 Dietary mycoprotein also significantly decreased the quantities of fecal genotoxicity and nitroso compounds, biomarkers of colorectal cancer risk, while red meat slightly increased the levels of these markers compared to baseline values. 42 Lactobacilli have been associated with antigenotoxicity effects, likely due to interactions with nitroso compounds, 43 although these potential interactions require further study. In that trial, self-reported protein intake did not change significantly from baseline for either diet. 42 However, the consumption of mycoprotein was associated with a significant increase in fiber compared to either processed red meat intake or baseline levels. 42 The increased intake of F. venenatum biomass fiber, which contains β-glucans, 25 may have contributed to the observed shift in the intestinal bacterial proportions. While fungal β-glucans vary in structure and differ from cereal β-glucans, the consumption of cereal β-glucans was associated with significantly increased proportions of fecal Roseburia and lactobacilli 44 , 45 and yeast β-glucans and A. bisporus mushrooms were associated with increases in Akkermansia levels in rodents. 46 , 47

Four in vitro studies examined the effects of mycelial biomass from F. venenatum (Quorn™) on the fecal microbiome composition and SCFA levels (Table 2). The first study compared F. venenatum biomass, chicken meat, and oats, each of which were passed through INFOGEST-simulated gastric and small intestine conditions. 48 When subsequently incubated in human stool samples, the F. venenatum mycoprotein was associated with significantly increased proportions of Bacteroides compared to chicken meat, along with greater total SCFA production, but lower levels of the BCFAs isovalerate and isobutyrate. 48 In this regard, mushrooms have also been associated with increased Bacteroides proportions in stools in vitro. 51 However, another study that used a different pre-digestion method followed by incubation to compare F. venenatum mycoprotein to chicken meat and soy, no significant changes in fecal SCFAs or bacterial alpha- and beta-diversity were found. 49 A third study reported that the total SCFAs quantities produced in fecal slurries treated with whole mycoprotein (6% fiber by wet weight) were significantly lower than those elicited by purified mycoprotein fibers (75% fiber by wet weight). 50 SCFA levels resulting from incubation with purified mycoprotein fibers were similar to those generated by oligofructose, rhamnose, and laminarin. 50 Finally, the incubation of biomass from Rhizopus oligosporus (42.8% total fiber, 36.4% insoluble fiber), Paecilomyces variotii (31.1% fiber, 23.5% insoluble fiber), and F. venenatum mycoprotein (21.6% fiber, 14.9% insoluble fiber, all dry basis) in a model of human fecal microbiota resulted in increased production of SCFA by all three species, with P. variotii and F. venenatum resulting in higher levels similar to wheat bran. 12 While R. oligosporus and F. venenatum mycoprotein are established as human foods, P. variotii biomass has been used in animal feed 52 , 53 but was more recently introduced as PEKILO® by Enifer™ for both human and animal consumption. These in vitro studies collectively indicate that mycelial biomass from several mold species tends to stimulate SCFA production by intestinal microorganisms. The fiber component of this biomass is likely important to this effect, as demonstrated by the similar trend observed for in vitro studies on extracted fungal fibers (Table 1).

Effects of mycelia-fermented foods on the gut microbiome

While many food fermentations are led by bacteria and yeast, including some that involve molds, other food fermentations are primarily driven by the growth of mold mycelium. One human study assessed the effects of a mycelium-fermented food on the gut microbiome (Table 3). A crossover-design RCT compared the consumption of okara biscuits fermented by R. oligosporus on the gut microbiomes of 15 healthy adults. 54 Okara is the fiber-rich residue from soymilk production and can be fermented with mycelial fungi into different foods. 54 , 55 For this trial, okara was incubated with R. oligosporus at 30 °C for 24 h, and biscuits (100 g) containing either 20% of the fermented or unfermented (control) okara were consumed daily for three weeks. 54 Fermented okara biscuit intake was associated with significantly higher levels of Bifidobacterium in the stools compared to the okara biscuit controls. 54 This effect may have been related to the significant increase in soluble dietary fiber provided by the fermented okara as opposed to the unfermented okara control, which slightly decreased soluble dietary fiber and increased insoluble fiber intake. 54 Okara that was enzymatically treated to increase soluble fiber was a more effective substrate for Bifidobacterium in vitro than untreated okara, 56 thus indicating that soluble fiber is a potential metabolic link between mold-fermented foods and a bifidogenic effect. For both fermented and unfermented okara interventions, the levels of serum acetic acid and total SCFAs were higher than baseline levels, possibly due to increased total dietary fiber intake. 54 However, only the ingestion of fermented okara biscuits was associated with increased serum propionic acid and fecal valeric acid. 54

A total of six animal studies reported on the capacity of mycelia-fermented foods to alter the gut microbiome (Table 3). Two of these studies examined the effects of foods fermented with Neurospora crassa, a fungus used to ferment soymilk and soybean oil byproducts into the traditional Indonesian food Oncom. 62 Fractionated components of the whole fermented foods, which contained transformed substrate and fungal material, were evaluated. In one study, carbohydrate fractions from okara fermented with N. crassa, prepared via hot-water extraction and separated into crude sugar, polysaccharide, and oligosaccharide fractions, were fed to healthy rats at a level of 2 g/kg body weight for 3 weeks. 55 A sugar fraction extracted from unfermented okara was used as a control. 55 The relative abundance of Ruminococcaceae was elevated in rats fed fermented polysaccharides, whereas Prevotellaceae was significantly higher in stools of the controls. 55 This study also evaluated whole fermented okara in human fecal slurries and reported higher production of propionic and butyric acid associated with fermented okara. 55 Notably, N. crassa fermentation more than doubled the soluble polysaccharide and oligosaccharide contents of the okara, a property attributed to the ability of N. crassa to hydrolyze cellulose. 55 This increase in soluble polysaccharides may have promoted the production of those SCFAs.

In another study, N. crassa fermented black rice was examined in hypercholesterolemic mice. 57 The mice were fed either N. crassa fermented black rice (FBR) fiber or unfermented rice fiber control at 2.5 g/kg (low-dose group) or 5 g/kg (high-dose group) for 6 weeks. FBR resulted in taxonomic changes at the phylum level, and the mice fed the high dose of FBR also had elevated levels of fecal Akkermansia and Bacteroides. 57 Stools from FBR-treated mice (both doses) had increased acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, and isovaleric acid levels compared to the controls. 57 FBR consumption was associated with significantly lower body and organ weights, improved liver morphology, lower LDL cholesterol, and reduced total bile acids in the liver, the latter of which was possibly due to the bile acid-binding ability of the fermented fiber. 57 Notably, fermentation of the rice bran may have led to these findings because the FBR had increased soluble dietary fiber (30% vs. 17%) and reduced insoluble fiber (52% vs. 62%), specifically within the cellulose (7% vs. 9%) and hemicellulose (38% vs. 46%) fractions compared to the rice fiber controls. 57

Amazake, a traditional Japanese rice beverage typically fermented with A. oryzae, was also examined in healthy mice. 58 In this study, the composition of amazake was modified by adding a second saccharification step to produce higher levels of isomaltose, a product of starch hydrolysis by A. oryzae. 58 Dietary supplementation of the isomaltose-rich amazake at a level of 200 µL per day for 2 weeks was associated with increased proportions of fecal Muribaculum intestinale and Parabacteroides merdae compared to the control diet, in which no amazake was consumed. 58 In other mouse studies, M. intestinale proportions were positively associated with polysaccharide-rich, low-fat chow diet intake 63 and gut barrier integrity, 64 inhibition of brain tumor growth 65 and reduced cholesterol. 66 In humans, P. merdae was linked to improved gut barrier integrity 67 and reduced atherosclerosis, 68 but also to positive associations with polycystic ovary syndrome and hypertension. 69

The remaining three animal studies compared mycelia-fermented foods made with different fungal species (Table 3). Mice fed a Western diet were given 10% w/w amazake, fermented with either Aspergillus luchuensis or A. oryzae for 2 months. 59 The two products were compositionally different: A. luchuensis amazake contained more isomaltooligosaccharides, citric acid, and ferulic acid (measured via high-performance liquid chromatography) than the one made with A. oryzae. 59 In contrast to A. oryzae amazake, the consumption of A. luchuensis amazake was associated with significantly increased proportions of Lachnospiraceae. 59 This effect was positively and significantly correlated with increased fecal secretory Immunoglobulin A (IgA) antibodies and occludin. 59 Lachnospiraceae were previously shown to stimulate IgA production in mice, conferring an immunomodulatory effect. 70 A. oryzae amazake was associated with increased proportions of fecal A. muciniphila. 59 These results indicate that the gut modulatory effects of amazake may vary between the fungal species used to make this food.

Rats fed a high-fat diet and given 20% w/w soybean tempeh made by either Rhizopus stolonifer, Rhizopus microsporus, or Rhizopus oryzae for three weeks had lower numbers of Enterobacteriaceae (15-, 19-, and 6-fold, respectively) and A. muciniphila (20-, 181-, and 16-fold, respectively) in their cecal contents compared to the controls according to qPCR. 60 Tempeh made using R. microsporus and R. oryzae was also associated with significantly decreased Clostridium coccoides numbers in the cecum, while R. stolonifer tempeh intake was associated with significantly higher quantities of bifidobacteria and lactobacilli. 60 This effect of R. stolonifer tempeh may be associated with its significantly higher levels of isoflavone aglycones compared to both the R. microsporus and R. oryzae tempehs, as well as unfermented soybeans. 71 Isoflavone aglycones are polyphenolic compounds and phytoestrogens that have previously been shown to support the growth of Bifidobacterium. 72 While isoflavones are present in soybeans, most are in the less bioavailable form of isoflavone glycosides, the latter of which are hydrolyzed by fungal enzymes into the more bioavailable isoflavone aglycones. 60 Finally, fecal mucin and IgA intestinal biomarkers were measured in this study. Fecal mucin concentrations were higher among rats fed the tempehs relative to the controls. Rats fed the R. oryzae tempeh had higher quantities of fecal IgA (mg/g dry feces). 60

Finally, a rat study compared five fermented soybean foods, three of which were mold-fermented, namely, douchi and doujiang, which were made using A. oryzae and furu made using Mucor racemosus; the other two were natto (made using Bacillus subtilis) and soy yogurt (made using lactic acid bacteria). 61 Rats were fed unfermented (steamed) soybeans or one of the soybean fermented foods for 2 weeks. 61 Compared to the diet and unfermented soybean controls, doujiang and furu intake increased levels of bifidobacteria and lactobacilli, while douchi was associated with increased Staphylococcus in cecal contents. 61 While polysaccharides in the fungal material and soybeans were not discussed, fungal fermentation significantly decreased concentrations of sucrose and the legume oligosaccharides raffinose and stachyose. 61 Correlations were drawn between changes in protein secondary structure in the food matrices after fermentation and changes in rat gut microbiota, but a mechanistic connection and delineation of the contributions of carbohydrates versus proteins to the observed responses remain unclear.

Outlook for health benefits from mycelial extracts and foods via modulation of the gut microbiome

Of the human, animal, and in vitro studies on mycelial extracts (six studies, Table 1), biomass (five studies, Table 2), fermented food (seven studies, Table 3) currently available, most reported that these foods conferred a significant change to the proportions of several genera in the gut microbiome. Changes to the gut microbiome were variable across the food type and experimental approach used. However, despite this variation, bacteria recognized for their roles in fiber metabolism and potential health-promoting effects 73 were enriched, including Bacteroides, 48 , 57 Roseburia, 33 , 37 , 42 and Eubacterium. 33 , 34 Studies reported increases in the levels of bacteria regarded to be beneficial in the digestive tract, including Akkermansia, 35 , 42 , 57 , 59 Bifidobacterium 35 , 54 , 60 , 61 and lactobacilli. 42 , 55 , 60 , 61 Thus, mycelial foods may provide health-promoting properties by modulation of the gut microbiome.

The enrichment of fiber-degrading intestinal bacteria by the mycelial extracts, biomass, and fermented foods is consistent with their elevated fiber content. High dietary fiber contents of mycelial biomass have been reported for several species, 12 , 25 , 74 and fermentation of plant substrates by mold species, such as N. crassa and R. oligosporus, was shown to increase the soluble fiber content of the food. 54 , 55 , 57 Thus, these features position mycelial foods as potentially valuable dietary interventions to increase fiber intake.

Mycelial fiber is mainly in the form of chitin and β-glucans contained in filamentous fungal cell walls. Chitin-degrading bacteria have been identified in the human gut microbiome but remain underexplored. 75 β-glucan linkages can also be split by certain bacterial enzymes; thus, these compounds also function as fermentable dietary fibers. 76 , 77 The ability of cereal β-glucans to modulate the gut microbiome has been reported in RCTs, along with potentially related benefits to metabolic health. 78-80 Underlying mechanisms linking cereal β-glucans to gut and metabolic health outcomes have been proposed, including the impacts of increased viscosity, production of propionate, and cross-feeding interactions between gut microbiota. 28 , 44 While fungal cell wall carbohydrates are comparatively understudied, mycelial foods and mushrooms have been associated with similar metabolic health benefits to those reported for oat fiber. 34 , 57 , 79 , 81 The most studied fiber extract contains chitin and β-glucans (CG) from A. niger and was shown to alter the gut microbiota composition in human, animal, and in vitro studies. 33-35 , 37 Notably, because chitin and β-glucans are not accessible to human digestive enzymes, evidence may show that fungal chitin and β-glucans, along with other β-glucans, act as prebiotics, 82 meeting the criteria for prebiotic classification. 83

Beyond polysaccharides and one study on a glycosylceramide, 36 the effects on the gut microbiome of other bioactive components from mold mycelia and mycelia-fermented foods, including proteins, remain relatively unknown. The potential for those other components to alter microbial activity in the intestine was shown for proteins and flavonoids from mushrooms, both of which were reported to modulate the gut microbiome and confer gut health benefits. 20

Changes to the gut microbiota observed with mycelial food intake and exposures did not always result in changes to fecal or serum SCFAs. This was particularly notable among the human studies. 34 , 42 , 49 Thus far, other human studies of dietary fiber intake and fecal SCFA production have also shown mixed results and inconsistent effects on total or individual fecal SCFA levels. 84 Measurements of fecal SCFAs may not reliably correlate to SCFA levels in the gut or serum, and thus do not necessarily correlate with the abundance of SCFA-producing taxa. 85 However, the animal model and in vitro studies examined here typically reported increased levels in one or more SCFAs following mycelial extract and food exposure, and this indicates that the fungal matrix was fermented.

More information is needed on the differences between mycelial foods made using different species, strains, and food substrates. The genetic diversity of these fungi, along with consideration of the evolution of their cell wall composition, should be considered in the context of dietary fungi‒gut interactions. It is not clear whether gut microbiota responses will follow general trends across fungal species or preparations. Similarly, it is unclear how the type of mycelial extract or food or their dosage affects the gut microbiome. When measured in animal and in vitro studies, fungal species-dependent effects on microbial taxa or SCFAs were found. 59 , 60 , 86 Understanding these differences can be facilitated by characterizing the compositions of many types of fungal foods, especially fungal glycan composition. Efforts such as the Davis Food Glycopedia that utilize advanced analytical chemistry to describe the carbohydrate composition of foods can help build a biochemical understanding of mycelial foods. 87 Processing strategies must also be considered; the properties of filamentous fungi and fungal extracts are affected by cooking and purification methods. 17 , 88

Currently, there are too few studies to draw conclusions on whether mycelial foods can benefit health via modulation of the gut microbiome. Among the reports examined here, only six investigated host‒microbe interactions, and each of these reported on different health-related endpoints. One human study reported a reduction in colorectal cancer biomarkers in humans consuming fungal biomass instead of red meat. 42 In one in vitro and two animal studies, mycelial fiber extract and mold-fermented foods were associated with immunomodulatory effects. 37 , 59 , 60 A total of three animal studies reported gut barrier improvements. 37 , 59 , 60 More clinical and mechanistic research is needed to better understand the functionality of these mycelial foods and extracts in the gastrointestinal tract.

Additionally, prior to performing human studies, the mycelial constituents should be thoroughly characterized. Examples of well-characterized ingredients include oats and their β-glucans, both of which have been compositionally described, 87 , 89 and dose-dependent effects on the gut microbiome and health outcomes, including cholesterol-lowering effects have been reported in meta-analyses of clinical trials. 90-92 Forthcoming human studies on mycelial fungal foods should also give specific attention to habitual diets and the capacity of those diets to influence study outcomes. For example, variable responses to prebiotics may be due to differences in the gut microbiome stemming from baseline levels of dietary fiber intake. 93 , 94 Moreover, comparing myco-foods to relevant controls, matched for fiber and protein intake, will help to clarify the specific contribution of the mycelial foods to other dietary interventions. Future research should apply metagenomics methods as well as examine for other intestinal microorganisms, including fungi, which can also be impacted by dietary interventions. 95 Other, more comprehensive methods like metabolomics are similarly valuable and are useful for determining the mechanistic connections between dietary components, the gut microbiota, and host phenotypes. 96 , 97 Finally, human studies should not just focus on the gut microbiome response but also include other metrics of gut health 98 and assessments of short- and long-term benefits for systemic health.

In summary, while studies thus far indicate that mycelial foods alter the gut microbiome, these effects appear to be nuanced and variable. Most notably, several mycelial foods appear to exert gut microbiome-modulating capacities that resemble dietary fiber. Missing links between food composition, mechanisms of food‒gut microbiota interactions, and health outcomes remain. The diverse and complex category of mycelial foods offers many opportunities to identify forms of edible filamentous fungi that could promote a healthier gut microbiome. Mycelial foods are an important area of research as we continue to examine the relationships between diet, the gut microbiome, and human health.
