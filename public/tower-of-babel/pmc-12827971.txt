Enhanced adsorption of ranitidine onto activated carbon‑modified zeolite composite and investigation of the removal mechanism
Authors: Fatemeh shirvani, Majid Hashemi, Maryam Dolatabadi, Saeid Ahmadzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ranitidine is an H2-receptor antagonist that inhibits histamine activity, thereby decreasing gastric acid secretion. The presence of ranitidine in water resources poses environmental risks, potentially leading to disrupting aquatic ecosystems, and affecting human health through contaminated drinking water sources. Therefore, its removal from aquatic environments is essential. The maximum removal rate of 99.8% was achieved under best operating condition of a solution pH of 8.5, ranitidine concentration of 20 mg L⁻¹, activated carbon (AC) /modified zeolite (AC/MZ) composite mass of 250 mg L⁻¹, and a contact time of 35 min, closely aligning with the predicted removal rate of 100% from the model. The proposed model accuracy evaluated by analysis of variance. Kinetic model studies indicate that the adsorption of ranitidine onto the AC/MZ composite follows a second-order model, with a R2 of 0.9998, suggesting a chemisorption mechanism. Equilibrium studies demonstrate a strong alignment with the Langmuir isotherm, exhibiting a correlation coefficient of 0.9993, which suggests monolayer and homogeneous adsorption characteristics. The maximum adsorption capacity (qₘ) and RL values were calculated as 463.0 mg g⁻¹ and 0.0162, respectively.

Introduction

Emerging pollutants encompass a wide range of substances, which may not have been previously recognized as environmental contaminants1. Pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, hormones, illicit drugs, personal care products, microplastics, additives, plasticizers, pesticides, and flame retardants rare earth elements are among the chemicals classified as emerging pollutants. National and international agencies regulate and oversee the use of these substances in response to growing awareness of their environmental impacts and toxic effects on human health2,3.

Ranitidine is a strong H2-receptor blocker that effectively lowers gastric acid production. It has been used to treat peptic ulcers and gastroesophageal reflux disease, but safety concerns, particularly possible carcinogenicity have prompted its removal from numerous markets4,5. Following metabolism through demethylation and N- and S-oxidation, approximately 30% to 70% of ranitidine is excreted unchanged in urine within one day after oral administration6. Research indicates that photoderivatives of ranitidine, produced through solar irradiation in aquatic environments, are detrimental to all forms of life7. Ranitidine has been detected in water at ng L− 1 to µg L− 1 levels. Its widespread presence across diverse aquatic systems raises significant environmental concern8. Nitrosamine precursors known carcinogens, can pollute environmental media. Ranitidine has a substantial propensity to form N-nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA), a toxic and carcinogenic compound8. Due to ranitidine severe effects on public health, different treatment efforts are being implemented, such as oxidation9, adsorption10, ozonation11, Fenton12 and photo-Fenton process. Adsorption is a standard method for removing organic contaminants, including pharmaceuticals compondes, dyes, heavy metals, pesticides etc. from contaminated waters13,14. This can be attributed to its distinctive characteristics, which encompass a straightforward design, ease of construction and operation, high removal rate without the production of by-products, and low installation and operational costs15.

Activated carbon (AC) is an effective, non-toxic adsorbent for wastewater treatment due to its high surface area, porous structure, chemical stability, and ability to adsorb pollutants via physical and chemical interactions. Its regenerability enables reuse, lowers disposal costs, and makes it a preferred option for industrial wastewater management16,17. Alternative raw materials, including biomass and agricultural wastes, can be employed to produce activated carbon in order to reduce production costs, despite the commercial availability of activated carbon. The advantages of this technology include the ready availability, abundance, and low cost of the feedstock, alongside the valorization of waste through conversion into a valuable product18. Among various environmental waste materials that can be used to produce activated carbonssuch as corn straw, palm shell, bamboo, grape stalk, rice husk, sawdust, and bagasse, walnut shells stand out as one of the most abundant byproducts. Walnut shells predominantly consist of hemicellulose, lignin, and cellulose, with high carbon content, along with minor amounts of volatile compounds and ash. Owing to their sustainable availability, robustness, and high rigidity, walnut shells are regarded as a suitable raw material for activated carbon production19. Zeolites are among the most commonly utilized adsorbents in water treatment, owing to their rate and cost-effectiveness. Recent studies have demonstrated the development of composite materials composed of carbon structures and zeolites to improve sorption rate20.

The present study uses a composite of AC and iron-modified zeolite (MZ) (AC/MZ) composite as a adsorbent to effectively remove ranitidine from an aqueous solution. The developed method has multiple significant advantages, such as cost-effectiveness, rapid adsorption kinetics, and exceptional high adsorption capacity. The one-factor method is restricted to assessing the impact of a single variable on the target value. In contrast, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) enables the evaluation of combined effects and interactions among multiple factors. RSM also clarifies the functional relationships between these factors and the target value, identifying optimal parameter settings, which makes it a preferred approach for optimizing experimental design. The study employs a central composite design (CCD) methodology to systematically optimize adsorption conditions for enhanced rate. Additionally, the study conducts a thorough evaluation of various adsorption isotherms and kinetic models to characterize and elucidate the performance of AC/MZ composite in the removal of ranitidine.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ranitidine (C16H15N5O7S2, ≥ 99%) was obtained from Poursina Pharmaceutical Company (Iran). Zeolite was obtained from Negin Powder Company (Iran). Ferric chloride, sodium chloride, ferrous sulfate, sodium hydroxide, phosphoric acid, hydrochloric acid were purchased from Merck.

Activated carbon Preparation

A total of 10 g of walnut shell powder was systematically combined with a 37% solution of phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) within a controlled reactor environment. The mixture was then subjected to heating in an Aven (Pars Azma, model S.R 81) apparatus at a consistent of 80 ± 5 °C for a time of 10 h. This thermal treatment was conducted to facilitate the complete evaporation of water, ensuring that the walnut shell powder was adequately processed for subsequent analysis or application.

Subsequently, the combination was placed in a furnace (Furnace, KSL1700X-H5) for a one-step carbonization-activation treatment. The furnace was heated to 900 ± 5 °C at a rate of 5 °C per minute in a nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere and maintained at this temperature for one hour. At the same time, the nitrogen atmosphere was replaced with carbon dioxide (CO₂) and maintained for one hour. The solid products underwent repeated soaking and washing with double distilled water (DDW) until they reached neutrality, after which they were dried in an oven at 80 ± 5 °C21.

Preparation of modified zeolite

The modified zeolite (MZ) was synthesized through the co-precipitation method. Specifically, 2.32 g of FeCl₃0.6 H₂O and 1.12 g of FeSO₄0.7 H₂O were dissolved in 100 mL of DDW while being mixed. The mentioned solution was stirred continuously with 4.0 g of natural zeolite added. To achieve an anaerobic condition, nitrogen was added to the solution that was poured into a Pyrex reactor. After that, the Pyrex reactor was subjected to sonication for two hours at 20 ± 2 °C. The pH of the suspension was adjusted to approximately 10.0 through the dropwise addition of NaOH after sonication. The contents of the reactor were transferred to a shaking incubator, where they were agitated at a speed of 200 revolutions per min (rpm) for a duration of 12 h. Following this mixing period, the supernatant was carefully removed and discarded to isolate the sediment. The remaining sediments were subsequently washed with DDW, continuing this process until a neutral pH was achieved. Once the washing was complete, the synthesized modified zeolite (MZ) was dried to a constant weight to ensure the removal of any remaining moisture. The dried material was then subjected to sieving to obtain particle sizes ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 mm, thereby preparing the MZ for subsequent applications and analyses22.

Preparation of activated carbon (AC) – modified zeolite (AC/MZ) composite

AC/MZ composite was synthesized utilizing a hybrid methodology. This approach was adapted with modifications from the methodologies established by Wongcharee et al. (2019) and Sengupta et al. (2020). The integration of these techniques was designed to enhance the interaction between the activated carbon and modified zeolite, thereby optimizing the composite’s structural and functional properties for improved adsorption performance. The procedural adjustments made in this study aimed to refine the synthesis process, ensuring a more homogeneous distribution of components and enhancing the overall efficacy of the resultant composite material. A thorough mixing of 1.0 g of activated carbon (AC) and 1.0 g modified zeolite (MZ) (1:1%w/w) ensured homogeneous distribution. The powdered mixture was combined with a 0.4 M NaOH solution in a 1:1 mass/mass ratio. A magnetic stirrer (Heidolph) was used to stir the mixture for 24 h at 20 ± 2 °C. The NaOH step was combined to ensure that zeolite was absorbed into the carbon carrier and that the two materials were impregnated effectively with strong bonds. Subsequently, the impregnated mixture was subjected to thermal treatment in a drying oven for 8 h at 80 ± 5 °C, followed by cooling to 20 ± 2 °C. DDW was used to wash the synthesized AC/MZ composite until its pH was neutral. Subsequently washing, the composite was dried in an oven at 105 ± 5 °C for one day23.

Adsorption experiments

All experiments were performed using an Erlenmeyer flask with a useful volume of 100 mL as a reactor. Various parameters were studied using the batch process, including the solution pH4–10, ranitidine concentration (10–50 mg L− 1), AC/MZ composite mass (100–500 g L− 1), and reaction time (5–45 min) at room temperature (20 ± 2 °C). The solution was agitated at 200 rpm (rpm) in a rotary shaker. Following the designated reaction time, the filtrates were extracted with syringes and subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The UV-Vis spectrophotometer (OPTIZEN 3220 model) at 315 nm was used to determine the concentration of ranitidine both before and after the adsorption process24.

Experimental design

Optimizing the ranitidine adsorption system using the AC/MZ composite through CCD-RSM is an efficient approach that enables the control of multiple variables with minimal experimental effort. The experiment was planned and executed by Design Expert 13.0. X1, X2, X3, and X4 are determined by the selected variables, which include the solution pH, ranitidine concentration, adsorbent mass (AC/MZ composite), and reaction time. This study utilized the CCD to evaluate how the ranitidine concentration, solution pH, AC/MZ composite mass, and reaction time impact the removal of ranitidine. The adequacy of these models was assessed using several error metrics, including Adjusted R², the sum of squared errors (SSE), and the Chi-squared statistic (χ²). The models were ultimately evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

Results and discussion

Structural characterization of AC/MZ composite

The FE-SEM micrographs in Fig. 1-(a) were used to analyze the surface morphology of the AC/MZ composite. As illustrated in Fig. 1-(a), the AC/MZ composite exhibits a smooth surface, though it features cavities of varying sizes distributed across its surface. This suggests that low-cost agricultural biomass can be transformed into adsorbent materials with high surface area through economical activation methods.

Fig. 1(a)- FE-SEM images of AC/MZ and (b)- N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of AC/MZ.

(a)- FE-SEM images of AC/MZ and (b)- N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of AC/MZ.

As depicted in Fig. 2-(b), the nitrogen adsorption isotherm aligns with a type IV curve according to IUPAC classification, displaying a closed hysteresis loop within a P/P₀ range of 0.41 to 0.96. This implies that the samples possess a significant number of mesopores, which aligns with the pore size distributions. The pore sizes of the AC/MZ composite, measuring 18 nm, further confirm their classification as mesoporous materials. At low concentrations, the isotherm curve is steep, suggesting monolayer adsorption. The AC/MZ composite exhibits a high BET specific surface area of 831.6 m² g⁻¹, total pore volume of 0.95 cm3 g− 1, and mean pore diameter of 7.2 nm the AC/MZ composite. Specifically, the size, distribution, and connectivity of the pores within the adsorbent material directly affect the accessibility of the adsorbate molecules to the active sites on the adsorbent surface. A well-developed pore network facilitates efficient transport of the adsorbate, thereby enhancing the kinetics of the adsorption process and leading to higher equilibrium capacities. Conversely, limitations in pore structure can restrict the diffusion of molecules, resulting in slower removal rates and reduced overall effectiveness of the adsorbent.

XRD patterns of the (a) natural zeolite (NZ), (b) modified zeolite (MZ), (c) activated carbon (AC) and (d) AC/MZ composite.

The crystal phase of the natural zeolite (NZ), MZ, AC, and AC/MZ composite were analyzed by X-ray diffraction analysis (D/max-2500, Rigaku, Japan). Figure 2-a shows the XRD patterns of the NZ. The diffraction peaks at 9.1, 11.5°, 13.0°, 17.4°, 19.1°, 22.6°, 24.8°, 28.5°, and 30.2° were appereant, that the XRD analysis of the JCPDS card (01–079–1460). The pattern peaks indicate that clinoptilolite is the primary component of this natural zeolite. The literature reported the same pattern of findings27. Modified zeolite using iron (MZ) almost keep on NZ crystalline structure. Although iron crystals are too small to be visible by XRD, Fig. 2-b, presented 4 weak diffraction peaks at 48.5, 50.2°, 58.3°, and 67.8°, which correspond to the major reflections of Fe2O328. The peak positions remained essentially unchanged following modification, although a slight reduction in peak intensities was observed, which may be ascribed to distortions of the mesoporous channels resulting from the collapse of the pore structure during the modification process29. The XRD pattern obtained for AC is presented in Fig. 2-c. Broad diffraction features near 23.5° indicate the presence of amorphous carbon within the sample30. As anticipated, the amorphous AC exhibits no pronounced diffraction peaks. During the formation of AC/MZ composite, the zeolite retains its crystallinity (see Fig. 2-d). However, the observed peak intensities decline as the proportion of amorphous activated carbon increases, due to dilution and enhanced scattering from the carbon phase. The higher amorphous content generally lowers overall diffraction intensities and can cause slight peak broadening, attributable to micro-strain or reduced zeolite crystallite size. A substantial amorphous carbon fraction tends to reduce the overall crystallinity index. Minor shifts in the 2θ positions of zeolite reflections can arise from lattice distortions in the zeolite framework caused by confinement or interfacial interactions with carbon. Additionally, interfacial strain or microstructural features at the AC/MZ composite boundary can modify peak shapes, leading to subtle asymmetry or broadening near the principal reflections.

Effect of parameter

Solution pH strongly influences adsorption by changing the ionization states and surface charge of both adsorbate and adsorbent. pH shifts can therefore modify electrostatic interactions and affinity: for example, below a weak base’s pKa the adsorbate is largely protonated (which can increase repulsion with a positively charged adsorbent), while above the pKa the adsorbate may be neutral, promoting interaction with negatively charged adsorbents31,32. Thus, optimizing solution pH is essential for maximizing adsorption rate and ensuring effective removal of contaminants or delivery of target molecules in various environmental and industrial applications.

The ionization of ranitidine in water is critically determined by its pKa of approximately 8.2, influencing its behavior across varying pH levels. Ranitidine can be represented in two forms: the protonated form (Ranitidine⁺) and the unprotonated form (Ranitidine0). When the pH is less than the pKa, specifically at pH values below 8.2, the concentration of Ranitidine⁺ increases, as the acidic environment promotes protonation; for instance, at pH 4.0, the ratio of Ranitidine⁺ to Ranitidine0 can be calculated using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, resulting in a significantly higher proportion of the positively charged species31,32. At pH 8.2, the concentrations of both forms are equal, indicating a balanced state of ionization (50% Ranitidine⁺ and 50% Ranitidine0). As the pH rises above 8.2, moving towards more basic conditions, such as at pH 7.0 or pH 10.0, the equilibrium shifts towards the Ranitidine− form. For example, at pH 7.5, the proportion of Ranitidine⁺ decreases dramatically, resulting in a predominance of the neutral form.

The optimal pH for the adsorption of ranitidine onto an adsorbent (AC/MZ composite) with a pHZPC of 7.8 (see Fig. 5) can be determined by considering the charge characteristics of both the Ranitidine and AC/MZ composite. Ranitidine has a pKa of 8.2, meaning that at pH values below 8.2, it predominantly exists in its protonated form (Ranitidine⁺), while at pH values above 8.2, it tends to exist as the neutral form. When the pH is below the pHZPC of 7.8, the surface of the adsorbent will carry a positive charge. For instance, at a pH of 5.0, which is below the pKa of ranitidine, the majority of ranitidine will be positively charged. As a result, both the adsorbent and the adsorbate will have a net positive charge, leading to electrostatic repulsion and reduced adsorption rate.

Conversely, as the pH approaches and exceeds the pHZPC, the adsorbent’s surface becomes negatively charged. For example, at a pH of 8.5, the adsorbent will predominantly carry a negative charge, while ranitidine, being above its pKa, will mostly exist in its neutral form. Therefore, the best adsorption of ranitidine onto the adsorbent is likely to occur at a pH slightly above the pKa of 8.2, particularly around pH 8.5 to 9.5, where the adsorbent is negatively charged, providing optimal conditions for the adsorption process. This pH range enhances the interactions between the adsorbent and the neutral form of ranitidine, facilitating effective adsorption.

Figure 6-(a) illustrates the impact of the ranitidine concentration and the pH of the solution on the removal rate of ranitidine at a mass of 200 mg L⁻¹ and an contact time of 25 min, presented as a contour plot. As the concentration of ranitidine increased from 10 to 50 mg L⁻¹, the removal rate of ranitidine decreased correspondingly, from 92.9% to 58.2%. This finding suggests that the removal rate is significantly impacted by higher concentrations of ranitidine. Consequently, understanding the relationship between pollutant concentration and adsorption dynamics is essential for optimizing adsorption systems and improving their rate in removing contaminants from aqueous environments. The removal rate decreased significantly with the increase in ranitidine concentrations, which was caused by saturation and occupation of the available sites on the adsorbent. At higher ranitidine concentrations, the adsorption process will become slower due to increased competition for low reactive sites. The adsorbent’s mass gradient decreases, causing the transfer of ranitidine molecules from the solution to the particle surface. Concentration is a key factor in adsorption. Higher pollutant concentrations increase the concentration gradient between the solution and the adsorbent surface, which strengthens the driving force for mass transfer. This accelerates adsorption kinetics by enhancing diffusion of pollutant molecules toward the adsorbent and increasing collisions with active sites, leading to higher adsorption rates and capacity33,34.

Adsorbent mass is a key factor in adsorption because it determines the number of active sites available for pollutant binding and thus affects removal rate and capacity: an appropriate mass provides sufficient surface area for effective mass transfer and high adsorption efficiency, while too little leads to underutilization and low removal and too much can cause site saturation, particle aggregation, and reduced mass-transfer efficiency. Therefore, optimizing adsorbent mass is necessary to balance cost-effectiveness and adsorption performance and to avoid diminishing returns in environmental and industrial treatment systems35,36. A gradual increase in the percentage of ranitidine removal was observed with a progressive increase in the AC/MZ composite mass (adsorbent dose) until equilibrium was attained, as shown in Fig. 6-(c). The saturation level was observed after a specific dose because all active sites were gradually occupied35.

Understanding the relationship between contact time and adsorption rate is essential for optimizing operational conditions in treatment systems, as it enables the design of processes that can enhance pollutant removal while minimizing costs and resource usage in environmental and industrial applications. Contact time is a vital parameter in the adsorption method, as it influences the extent and rate of pollutant removal from a solution. Sufficient contact time allows the adsorbate in the solution and the adsorbent surface to attain equilibrium by promoting efficient mass transfer and interfacial interactions. Adsorption proceeds rapidly at early stages, but the rate commonly declines as the adsorbent’s active sites become progressively occupied and saturation is approached7,31.

Conclusion

The current work deals with employing RSM to investigate the main and concurrent effects of solution pH, ranitidine concentration, adsorbent mass, and contact time on the removal rate of ranitidine from polluted water. The developed quadratic polynomial model demonstrated significance, evidenced by high F-values of 1052.6 and p-values < 0.0001. The values of coefficients of determination (R2), predicted R2 and adjusted R2 were found to be 0. 9964, 0.9954, and 0.9935, respectively.This high removal rate underscores the potential of walnut shells as a sustainable and effective adsorbent for the removal of ranitidine from aqueous solutions. Under optimal parameters, a best ranitidine removal rate of 98.6% was attained. The kinetic analysis results demonstrate that the second-order kinetic model is the most suitable representation for this study, implying that the adsorption process is primarily governed by a chemisorption mechanism. This suggests that the interaction between ranitidine and the AC/MZ composite involves the formation of chemical bonds. Furthermore, the adsorption behavior of ranitidine is most accurately described by the Langmuir isotherm model. This finding indicates that the adsorption occurs in a monolayer configuration, reflecting a homogeneous distribution of adsorption sites on the surface of the AC/MZ composite.
