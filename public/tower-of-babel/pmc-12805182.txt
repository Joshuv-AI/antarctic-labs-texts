Cumulative Burden of Geriatric Conditions and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Older Adults
Authors: Kehinde Tom-Ayegunle, Yasser Jamil, Justin B Echouffo-Tcheugui, Oshozimhede E Iyalomhe, Ariela R Orkaby, Min Ji Kwak, Parag Goyal, Ajar Kochar, Michael G Nanna, Abdulla A Damluji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults face a high burden of cardiovascular disease that is exacerbated by geriatric conditions. The collective influence of geriatric conditions on outcomes remains unclear.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to determine whether the coexistence of multiple geriatric conditions confers a graded increase in risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsThe authors used data from the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk In Communities) study to examine the association between different numbers of geriatric conditions (0-1 vs 2 vs 3-4 vs ≥ 5) and cardiovascular events. Primary outcome was MACE, defined as all-cause death, acute myocardial infarction, or stroke. Logistic regression was used to assess the unadjusted and adjusted association between number of geriatric conditions and MACE.ResultsAmong participants, 18% had 0 to 1, 19% had 2, 31% had 3 to 4, and 32% had ≥5 geriatric conditions. Those with ≥5 conditions were older, more often female, and more frequently hospitalized. Frailty, sarcopenia, and cognitive impairment were most prevalent. Multimorbidity contributed the highest odds for MACE. Compared with 0 to 1 conditions, ≥5 conditions conferred significantly greater risk of myocardial infarction (OR: 4.62; 95% CI: 2.92-7.72), stroke (OR: 6.03; 95% CI: 3.04-13.75), death (OR: 3.12; 95% CI: 2.12-4.74), and MACE overall (OR: 4.26; 95% CI: 3.17-5.83). Results were consistent in weighted analyses.ConclusionsIn a community-based cohort, we observed a graded and synergistic association between the number of geriatric conditions and MACE. These findings highlight the need to integrate systematic geriatric assessment into cardiovascular risk models to improve stratification and personalize care in older adults.Key words: aged, cardiovascular diseases, cognitive impairment, comorbidity, frailty, polypharmacy

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a global public health problem, particularly as the world's population ages. According to the U.S Census Bureau, 16.5% of the U.S. population is over 65 years, with this proportion expected to increase to 20.3% by 2030.1 Older adults are at an increased risk of developing geriatric conditions, which are associated with an increased risk of major cardiovascular events (MACE).2 Geriatric conditions include multimorbidity, polypharmacy, cognitive decline, vision impairment, hearing impairment, urinary incontinence, falls, physical impairment, and frailty.3 Understanding how these conditions impact cardiovascular outcomes can inform effective strategies to mitigate adverse outcomes in older populations.

Most evidence to date has evaluated the effect of individual geriatric syndromes on cardiovascular outcomes. In a prospective cohort, frailty independently predicted incident MACE and other adverse outcomes.4 Likewise, low energy and slow gait speed have been linked to higher cardiovascular risk.5 These impairments alter care patterns. For instance, in patients with coronary disease, frail individuals undergo percutaneous coronary intervention less often than nonfrail individuals (15% vs 33%; P < 0.001) and are substantially less likely to receive coronary aArtery bypass graft (1% vs 9%; P < 0.001).6 There remains a paucity of studies that comprehensively explore how these conditions collectively influence cardiovascular risk.7 We aimed to examine the impact of geriatric conditions and their combined effect on cardiovascular risk in older adults using data from the community-based ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk In Communities) Study.

Methods

Study design and population

The study design and procedures of the ARIC study have been previously described in detail.8 ARIC is an ongoing, prospective observational study that originally enrolled 15,792 participants aged 45 to 64 years recruited between 1987 and 1989 (visit 1) from 4 communities in the United States: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Washington County, Maryland. After the initial baseline visit, participants were followed up by telephone (annually before 2012; twice yearly after). The present analysis included participants who attended ARIC visit 5 (2011-2013). Survivors of the original cohort underwent comprehensive geriatric assessments at visit 5 and were then followed every 6 months to ascertain cardiovascular events and vital status. Participants with incomplete data on any geriatric domain at visit 5 or with missing outcome information were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of 5,134 (Supplemental Figure 1). The Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Demographic characteristics, medical conditions, and health care utilization

Demographic characteristics (eg, age, education, and marital status), health history, body mass index (BMI), and results of fasting laboratory assays were obtained from the baseline visit. Hospitalization information for the past 5 years was ascertained through hospitalization reports.

Geriatric conditions

During visit 5, participants were assessed for the presence of geriatric conditions, which included physical frailty, multimorbidity, cognitive decline, polypharmacy, physical impairment, hearing impairment, vision impairment, and urinary incontinence. Participants were then divided into 4 groups according to the number of geriatric conditions they had at visit 5: 1) 0 to 1 condition; 2) 2 conditions; 3) 3 to 4 conditions; and 4) 5 or more conditions. All 9 geriatric domains were assessed concurrently at ARIC visit 5 in a cross-sectional analysis, with subsequent capture of MACE and mortality events. Geriatric conditions were defined as following.

Physical frailty

Frailty in each patient in the ARIC study was assessed using the 5 domains defined by the Fried frailty phenotype.9 These domains include the following: 1) exhaustion, defined as present if the participant responded “some of the time” or “most of the time” to either of the following questions: “I felt everything I did was an effort” or “I could not get going;” 2) low physical activity: lowest 20th percentile of the level of sports activity in leisure time based on the Baecke physical activity questionnaire; 3) weakness: lowest 20th percentile in sex- and BMI-adjusted grip strength; 4) slowness: lowest 20th percentile in sex- and height-adjusted walking speed during a 4-m walk test; and 5) weight loss: >10% weight loss compared with the prior visit or a BMI of <18.5 kg/m2 at the time of assessment. If 3 or more of the 5 criteria were present,10 the individual was categorized as frail, and those with 1 or 2 of the 5 were categorized as “prefrail.”

Multimorbidity

Multimorbidity was defined as the prevalence of 2 or more chronic conditions in an individual. Specifically, the chronic conditions included in this study were cancer, diabetes, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, chronic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, dementia or Alzheimer disease, heart failure, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, as they are available data at baseline visits.

Cognitive decline

Participants were classified as having cognitive decline if they had at least one of the following: 1) impairment in either 1 of 2 cognitive domains: executive function or memory; 2) if participant or proxy reported a diagnosis of cognitive impairment or dementia by a physician; or 3) a score ≥2 on the AD8 instrument. The AD8 Dementia Screening Interview11 is an 8-item battery of queries regarding an individual’s cognitive status, assessing memory, temporal orientation, judgment, and functioning.

Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy was defined as taking ≥5 medications concurrently,12 including prescription and nonprescription drugs. Recent medication use (≤4 weeks) was collected during visit 5. Prescriptions and over-the-counter medicines were identified by direct visual inspection of medication containers during visit 5 and by participants bringing their up-to-date medication list. Medications included in this study are antihypertensive, statin, anticoagulant, aspirin, anxiolytics, antipsychotics, sedatives, anticonvulsants, antidementia, central nervous system altering medications, antidiabetics, beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, aldosterone antagonists, loop diuretic agents, digoxin, antiasthmatic medications, antiarrhythmic drugs, heart failure medications, and blood thinners.

Physical impairment

The short physical performance battery evaluation consisted of a chair stand test, a standing balance test, and a gait speed test.13 For the chair stand test, study participants were instructed to rise from a seated position 5 times consecutively, with their arms folded across their chest. Scores were assigned based on the time required to complete the task, ranging from 0 (inability to finish within 60 seconds) to 4 (<11.2 seconds). The standing balance test comprised 3-foot positions: semitandem, tandem, and side-by-side. Participants were first assessed in the semitandem position, progressing to the tandem stance if they successfully maintained the semitandem. The side-by-side position was evaluated only if the participant could not complete the semitandem stance. For the semitandem and side-by-side stances, a score of 1 was given if the participant held the position for 10 seconds, and 0 if they could not. The tandem stance was scored based on the duration maintained: 0 (≤3 seconds), 1 (>3 to <10 seconds), and 2 (≥10 seconds). The total score ranged from 0 to 4. In the gait speed test, participants were instructed to walk a distance of 4 m at their usual pace, and the time taken to complete the faster of 2 trials was recorded. Scores were assigned based on the time required to walk the 4-m distance, ranging from 0 (inability to walk 4 m) to 4 (<4.82 seconds). The overall short physical performance battery score was calculated as the sum of the scores from all 3 components. It ranged from 0 to 12, and higher scores indicated better physical function.

Hearing impairment

The evaluation of hearing ability was conducted using pure tone air conduction audiometry.14,15 The assessment involved calculating each ear's pure tone average (PTA) by measuring the hearing thresholds at 4 distinct frequencies: 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 kHz (kHz). Higher PTA values corresponded to poorer hearing acuity. Furthermore, the researchers categorized the PTA into high (4.0 and 8.0 kHz), medium (1.0 and 2.0 kHz), and low (0.25 and 0.50 kHz) frequency ranges. These frequency-specific PTA values were calculated for the better hearing ear and the left and right ears separately, allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of hearing ability across different frequency spectrums. Both hearing impairment with (237/705 [34%]) and without (468/705 [66%]) aids were included under the hearing impairment variable during our analysis.

Vision impairment

Vision assessment was performed at a single Eye Determinants of Cognition Study visit with measurement of distance visual acuity.16 For distance visual acuity, each increment of 0.1 logMAR corresponds to a change equivalent to 5 letters or 1 line on the ETDRS (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study) chart. Elevated logMAR values are indicative of more pronounced impairment. Both vision impairment with (186/234 [79%]) and without (48/234 [21%]) aids were included under the vision impairment variable during our analysis.

Urinary incontinence

Urinary incontinence was assessed using a questionnaire and performed examination tests such as a stress test, pad test, and Q-tip test. During the cough test, patients undergo an examination with a full bladder; the release of urine from the urethra during coughing indicates stress incontinence.

Cardiovascular outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of a 3-point MACE. A MACE was defined as all-cause death, acute myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke. Death was classified as any death occurring by visit 5 and subsequent mortality events occurring between visit 5 and visit 6 were captured, whereas stroke was defined as prevalent cases identified by the end of visit 5. MI was confirmed by physician diagnosis, which included secondary cardiovascular endpoints identified from medical records during visit 5.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and as median for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were dichotomized and reported as the number of participants and proportions. Clinical and demographic characteristics differences between geriatric and nongeriatric syndrome groups were assessed using unpaired t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the unadjusted associations between having 2, 3 to 4, or 5 or more geriatric conditions (compared to 0-1 conditions) and MACE in older adults at visit 5 (model 1). A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for potential confounders, including age, biological sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, smoking status, and hospitalization history (model 2).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing baseline characteristics between participants with and without MI outcomes using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Missing MI outcome data (n = 393) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. The imputation model included age, sex, race, BMI, current smoking status, education level, and history of hospitalization. A predictor matrix was specified so that MI was imputed using all other variables, and the imputation method was logistic regression. Lastly, we ranked the relative influence of individual geriatric conditions on the likelihood of experiencing a MACE using separate logistic regression models, followed by a weighted analysis based on the ORs from these models. Results with a P value<0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.3.2), Posit Software, PBC.

Results

Baseline demographic characteristics

Our final study sample included 5,134 participants; of those, 910 (18%) had 0 to 1 geriatric conditions, 985 (19%) had 2 geriatric conditions, 1,603 (31%) had 3 to 4 geriatric conditions, and 1,636 (32%) had ≥5 geriatric conditions. Table 1 presents the baseline demographic data and functional characteristics of the study population stratified according to the burden of geriatric conditions. The mean age was 75.6 years, with 37% of the population aged between 70 and 74 years. Female participants constituted 57% of the cohort. Most of the enrolled participants (79.7%) were non-Hispanic Whites. Of the study population, 5.7% were overweight, 5.7% smoked at least 1 cigarette per day, and 2.9% had a history of hospitalization within 5 years.

The most common geriatric conditions were sarcopenia (70.7%), frailty (66.2%), and cognitive decline (56.4%), followed by multimorbidity (42.0%), vision impairment (38.4%), functional decline (32.7%), polypharmacy (24.8%), hearing impairment (15.8%), and urinary incontinence (5.5%). Those with 2 conditions (n = 985) had similar demographics but slightly higher BMI (28.5 kg/m2) and lower rates of advanced education (46.5% vs 56.6%). Participants with 3 to 4 conditions (n = 1,603) were more often older, with higher BMI (29.0 kg/m2) and lower proportions of non-Hispanic Whites (73.1%). The ≥5 conditions group (n = 1,636) had the highest mean BMI (29.2 kg/m2), hospitalization rates (5.2%), and the lowest prevalence of advanced education (38.1%). Smoking rates were low across all groups (Table 1). The prevalence of geriatric conditions according to biological sex is presented in Figure 1.

In separate logistic regression models predicting MACE, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and frailty showed the strongest influence among geriatric conditions, with ORs of 4.04, 3.01 and 1.94, respectively (Supplemental Table 1). The results from this model were then used to perform a proportional logistic regression exploring the association between weighted counts of geriatric conditions and MACE. In unadjusted analyses (model 1), higher weighted counts of geriatric conditions were associated with progressively greater odds of MACE, all-cause death, MI, and stroke (all P < 0.01). After adjustment for age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, smoking, education, and prior hospitalization (model 2), these associations remained robust, although attenuated. For MACE, adjusted ORs were 1.64 (95% CI: 1.17-2.34), 3.23 (95% CI: 2.34-4.531), and 7.57 (95% CI: 5.55-10.54) across the 3 to 5, 6 to 8, and 9 condition categories, respectively. Corresponding adjusted ORs for death were 1.57 (95% CI: 1.02-2.50), 2.60 (95% CI: 1.71-4.06), and 4.21 (95% CI: 2.81-6.52); for MI, 1.55 (95% CI: 0.85-2.95), 4.74 (95% CI: 2.79-8.62), and 11.19 (95% CI: 6.72-20.08); and for stroke, 2.22 (95% CI: 1.00-5.61), 3.20 (95% CI: 1.48-7.98), and 9.90 (95% CI: 4.83-23.90) (Supplemental Table 2).

Lastly, to address missing MI outcomes, baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without reported MI events. Characteristics were generally similar; however, those with missing MI outcomes were slightly younger (74.83 vs 75.60 years) and less often White (64.1% vs 79.7%). The prevalence of most geriatric conditions was comparable, although cognitive decline (48.9% vs 56.4%), vision impairment (23.9% vs 38.4%), hearing impairment (9.4% vs 15.8%), and functional decline (22.6% vs 32.7%) were less frequent in the missing MI group. The distribution of cumulative geriatric conditions did not differ materially between groups (Supplemental Table 3). Multiple imputation was applied for the 393 missing MI values. In unadjusted models, the odds of MI increased stepwise with 2, 3 to 4, and ≥5 geriatric conditions (OR: 1.88 [95% CI: 1.11-3.31]; 4.23 [95% CI: 2.69-7.00]; and 6.37 [95% CI: 4.10-10.48], respectively), with similar trends for MACE (1.90 [95% CI: 1.36-2.67], 3.33 [95% CI: 2.49-4.53], and 6.50 [95% CI: 4.91-8.78]). These associations remained significant after multivariable adjustment; for ≥5 conditions, the adjusted ORs were 4.62 (95% CI: 2.92–7.72) for MI and 4.26 (95% CI: 3.17–5.83) for MACE (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis examined the cumulative effect of multiple geriatric conditions on cardiovascular risk among older adults in the ARIC community study at visit 5. Major findings indicate that: 1) over half (63%) of older adult participants had ≥3 geriatric conditions present; and 2) a dose-response and synergistic relationship between the number of geriatric conditions and CVD risk exists, with the presence of ≥5 geriatric conditions being associated with significantly higher odds of MACE, including MI, stroke, and death compared to those with 0 to 1 geriatric condition, even after adjusting for demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, and hospitalization history (Central Illustration).

As individuals age, the incidence of 1 or more geriatric conditions significantly rises. Multiple studies assessing the prevalence of geriatric conditions observed increasing prevalence with aging, with rates as high as 68.8% among those aged ≥80 years.17,18 These include frailty, multimorbidity, mild cognitive impairment, dementia, falls, incontinence, and sensory deficits.19 Our current findings extend prior work by showing a high number of overlapping geriatric conditions in older patients, with 69% of participants aged ≥65 years having ≥2 geriatric conditions. This is likely related to the accumulation of age-related pathologic changes leading to accumulation of deficits including reduced bone density, muscle mass, higher burden of atherosclerotic disease, reduced cerebral flow, and other impairments at the cellular level (ie, impaired cellular repair mechanisms, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation).20, 21, 22 Lastly, although sarcopenia, frailty, and cognitive decline were the most prevalent geriatric conditions, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and frailty conferred the highest odds of MACE highlighting these domains as priority targets for future research.

Our findings reveal a graded, dose-dependent, and synergistic association between geriatric condition burden and cardiovascular risk. Although most prior studies have examined the impact of individual geriatric syndromes on cardiovascular outcomes.23, 24, 25, 26 A few have assessed their combined effects. In one cohort of 2,189 community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years, the coexistence of frailty and cognitive impairment was linked to a greater incidence of MACE including death, MI, and stroke than either syndrome alone.7 The analysis of Hanlon et al27 on 20,566 U.K. Biobank participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus demonstrated a similar pattern of dose dependent association. The current analysis is unique since it combined impact of multiple geriatric conditions on CVD events, whereby in a proportional logistic regression, the combination of geriatric syndromes led to higher odds of MACE compared to a single geriatric condition. This association remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders. This association is expected, since the geriatric condition apart from the underlying biological changes often hinder self-care abilities, adherence to treatment regimens, diminishes resilience and physiologic reserve, and reduce functional independence, and therefore, having cognitive impairment with polypharmacy will have higher rates of MACE. Moreover, with increasing geriatric conditions, patients will often be treated conservatively due to the perceived risk of adverse events.20,28, 29, 30, 31 These results highlight the imperative for holistic, multidisciplinary care models that integrate geriatric and cardiology expertise to enhance risk prediction, support complex decision-making, and deliver targeted prevention and management strategies beyond conventional cardiovascular approaches.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has both several strengths and limitations. First, this is a large, community-based cohort that includes a biracial population (Black and White adults), which allows for greater generalizability of the findings to diverse populations. Second, the study includes a comprehensive assessment of geriatric conditions, offering a holistic evaluation of age-related risks. Lastly, the study included different cardiovascular outcomes to help understand relationships between geriatric conditions and cardiovascular health. Our findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. Although derived from a large observational cohort, these findings reflect associations rather than causal effects. This limitation stems in part from the definition of MACE, in which most components were assessed cross-sectionally at visit 5, whereas death was ascertained at the end of follow-up, resulting in outcomes that were not strictly concurrent. Prospective studies incorporating serial geriatric assessments are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between multimorbidity and CVD. Furthermore, missing data in key variables, whether from nonresponse or limited availability, may have introduced selection bias. On analyzing the participants with missing data, it was noted that they have similar age category and the number of geriatric conditions, which may reflect nondifferential selection bias between those with or without missing data. Moreover, the dichotomization of certain continuous variables for geriatric condition assessment may have introduced misclassification bias; because of its consistent application to the study population, it may be nondifferential.32 Although multiple imputation yielded estimates consistent with the complete-case analysis, nonrandom missingness of MI could still bias the MACE composite, and these results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Current conventional risk models lack the integration of synergistic coexisting geriatric syndromes, which may underestimate true risk in this population. In this observational cohort, both unweighted and weighted counts of geriatric conditions were independently associated with higher odds of MACE. Prospective internal and external validation studies are needed to evaluate the feasibility, incremental benefit, and reproducibility of integrating a geriatric condition score into routine CVD risk models. Furthermore, future studies should examine specific patterns or clusters of co-occurring conditions to better capture potential differences in risk.
