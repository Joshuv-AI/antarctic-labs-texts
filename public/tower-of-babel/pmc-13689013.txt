Antibiotic de-escalation in severe intra-abdominal infections: a single-center retrospective cohort study on safety and prognostic factors
Authors: Liqin Zhang, Huilin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotic de-escalation is a key antimicrobial stewardship strategy aimed at reducing the ecological and adverse effects of broad-spectrum therapy without compromising efficacy. However, its real-world safety and impact on clinical outcomes in patients with severe intra-abdominal infections (IAIs) remain underexplored.MethodsA single-center, retrospective cohort study was conducted from June 2022 to June 2025. Adults with severe IAIs who received initial broad-spectrum antibiotics and achieved source control within 48 h were included. Patients were stratified into de-escalation and non-de-escalation groups on the basis of the antibiotic management strategy. Propensity score matching (1:1) was used to balance baseline characteristics. The primary outcome was clinical cure at 30 days. Secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality, lengths of ICU and hospital stay, antibiotic duration, and the incidence of secondary infections and Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI).ResultsAmong 405 eligible patients, 212 were matched (106 per group). The de-escalation group had a significantly higher clinical cure rate (79.8% vs. 63.5%; OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.29–3.89; P = 0.004) and lower 28-day mortality (9.6% vs. 21.2%; OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.18–0.88; P = 0.02). De-escalation was also associated with shorter ICU and hospital stays, reduced antibiotic duration, and fewer secondary infections. Multivariable analysis confirmed de-escalation (aOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.48–4.97) and adequate source control (aOR 3.52, 95% CI 1.81–6.85) as independent predictors of clinical cure.ConclusionAntibiotic de-escalation following adequate source control is associated with improved clinical outcomes and reduced mortality in severe IAIs without increasing the risk of complications. These findings support its integration into antimicrobial stewardship programs for this high-risk population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14080-1.Keywords: Antibiotic de-escalation, Severe intra-abdominal infection, Clinical outcomes, Antimicrobial stewardship, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Severe intra-abdominal infection (IAI) represents a leading cause of sepsis and septic shock in intensive care units (ICUs) [1] and is characterized by profound inflammatory responses and organ dysfunction [2]. This condition is associated with a substantial mortality burden, frequently exceeding 20% in critically ill patients [3]. Successful management fundamentally relies on timely source control and prompt appropriate antimicrobial therapy [4].

While international guidelines endorse empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics as crucial for initial survival [5], prolonged use drives the global crisis of antimicrobial resistance, particularly the alarming prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) such as carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales [6, 7]. This creates a vicious cycle of increasing resistance and selection pressure [8]. Furthermore, extended broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure may increase treatment-related ecological harm, particularly intestinal microbiome disruption and Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), whereas other adverse events, such as nephrotoxicity, are drug- and context-dependent rather than inherent to all broad-spectrum regimens [9].

To address this therapeutic dilemma, antimicrobial stewardship programs recommend antibiotic de-escalation, which involves transitioning to targeted, narrow-spectrum therapy on the basis of microbiological data and clinical improvement [10]. The objective is to mitigate ecological and iatrogenic harm without compromising efficacy. Recent observational studies have suggested that antimicrobial stewardship interventions in IAI can reduce unnecessary postoperative antibiotic exposure and promote guideline-concordant short-course therapy; however, most available studies have focused on antibiotic duration or empirical prescribing practices rather than the prognostic effect of a clearly defined de-escalation strategy in severe IAI [11]. In a dedicated study of healthcare-associated IAI, Montravers et al. reported that anti-infective de-escalation was feasible, but its implementation was limited by multidrug-resistant organisms and nonfermenting gram-negative bacilli, and the observational design left residual uncertainty regarding confounding by early clinical response and source-control adequacy [12]. Therefore, despite guideline support, the safety and clinical impact of de-escalation in severe IAI remain insufficiently defined by primary clinical evidence.

Consequently, a distinct evidence gap exists regarding the effectiveness and safety of a dedicated de-escalation strategy in severe IAI, particularly in high-risk subgroups. Clinicians often harbor reservations about narrowing antimicrobial coverage in critically ill patients because of fears of treatment failure. To address this, our single-center retrospective cohort study aimed to explore the impact of an antibiotic de-escalation strategy under relatively homogeneous clinical practices and local microbial ecology while recognizing that such a design primarily provides institution-specific real-world evidence rather than directly generalizable national or global estimates. Specifically, our objectives were (1) to compare the primary outcome of clinical cure and key secondary outcomes (including 28-day mortality, length of stay, and incidence of MDRO acquisition and CDI) between patients managed with and without an antibiotic de-escalation strategy; (2) to identify independent predictors of clinical cure; and (3) to assess the robustness of the findings through subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Ultimately, this study seeks to add to the limited real-world evidence base to help inform antimicrobial prescribing practices for this critically ill population.

Methods

Study design

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, adult patients with severe intra-abdominal infections (IAIs) were identified via institutional electronic medical records (EMRs) and clinical data warehouses (June 2022–June 2025). The protocol followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongjiang County People’s Hospital (No: JLSY-2025-072). Informed consent was waived because of the study’s retrospective, anonymized design. Strict data confidentiality was maintained throughout the research.

Study population

The study cohort was identified through a systematic query of the institution’s EMR system. We initially screened all adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the general surgery, gastrointestinal surgery, and ICU between June 2022 and June 2025 with a primary or secondary discharge diagnosis related to intra-abdominal infection. This initial electronic search was followed by a detailed manual chart review conducted independently by two trained clinical researchers to confirm diagnoses and verify eligibility against the predefined criteria. Any discrepancies in the eligibility assessment were resolved through discussion with a third senior physician investigator to reach a consensus.

Patients were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a definitive diagnosis of severe IAI, defined as the presence of sepsis or septic shock according to the Sepsis-3 international consensus definitions [13] or an Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score of ≥ 10 upon admission or diagnosis [14]; (3) a confirmed intra-abdominal source of infection, such as gastrointestinal perforation, anastomotic leakage, complicated appendicitis or diverticulitis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, or intra-abdominal abscess; (4) underwent an initial source control intervention (surgical or percutaneous drainage) within 48 h of diagnosis, regardless of whether complete adequacy was ultimately achieved; and (5) initiation of broad-spectrum empirical antibiotic therapy for at least 48 h following the index diagnosis.

The exclusion criteria included any of the following: (1) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (2) survival, transfer to another institution, or death within 72 h after the index IAI diagnosis; (3) a primary diagnosis of mild-to-moderate acute pancreatitis without documented infected necrosis; (4) a severe immunocompromised state, defined as the receipt of solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation within the past year, active chemotherapy or radiotherapy for malignancy, treatment with high-dose corticosteroids (equivalent to > 20 mg/day of prednisone for ≥ 14 days), or advanced HIV infection (CD4 count < 200 cells/µL); (5) IAI primarily caused by nonbacterial pathogens or instances where all intraoperative cultures yielded only contaminants without clinical correlation; (6) the absence of intraoperative or drained fluid microbiological culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results; and (7) patients transferred from an external institution after receiving > 48 h of antibiotic treatment for the current IAI episode.

Patient grouping and treatment definitions

Patients were retrospectively stratified into two mutually exclusive cohorts—the de-escalation group and the non-de-escalation group—based on the management strategy implemented after the initial phase of empirical therapy. Classification was performed by two independent infectious disease specialists who reviewed the complete electronic medical records, with any disagreements resolved through consultation with a third senior physician.

The de-escalation group included patients who underwent a planned modification of their empirical broad-spectrum regimen to pathogen-directed therapy with a net reduction in unnecessary antimicrobial coverage. This action was justified by microbiological findings or, when no definitive pathogen was identified, by predefined clinical response criteria indicating improvement after empirical therapy, including hemodynamic stability without escalating vasopressor support, defervescence or a clear downward temperature trend, improving abdominal signs, decreasing the leukocyte count or procalcitonin level, and no radiological or operative evidence of uncontrolled infection. Specific criteria, consistent with established antimicrobial stewardship principles [15], included the following: (1) switching from an empirical regimen with unnecessary anti-pseudomonal, anti-MRSA, anti-enterococcal, or antifungal coverage to a culture- and susceptibility-confirmed regimen with a net reduction in antimicrobial spectrum; for example, ertapenem was considered de-escalation only when it replaced an anti-pseudomonal carbapenem after Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter spp., and other nonfermenting gram-negative bacilli requiring anti-pseudomonal coverage were not isolated; cephalosporin-based regimens were considered de-escalation only when the selected agent provided narrower effective coverage than the empirical regimen; (2) discontinuing empirical MRSA or antifungal agents after such infections were ruled out; or (3) transitioning from an intravenous combination regimen to effective oral or intravenous monotherapy when redundant antimicrobial coverage was removed. These adjustments were required to be implemented within 96 h following empirical treatment initiation.

The nondeescalation group comprised all other patients, categorized as follows: (1) Continuation: Maintaining the initial broad-spectrum regimen throughout the treatment course; (2) Escalation: Broadening therapy within the same 96-hour window due to clinical deterioration or identification of resistant pathogens (e.g., adding colistin for carbapenem-resistant organisms); or (3) Inappropriate Deescalation: narrowing to an agent that was inactive against the identified pathogen or narrowing in culture-negative cases without predefined clinical response criteria, adequate source control, or documentation excluding ongoing uncontrolled infection. A 72-hour landmark design was applied before exposure classification. Patients who remained hospitalized and clinically evaluable 72 h after the index IAI diagnosis were classified according to subsequent antibiotic management within the 96-hour window.

To address selection bias and confounding by indication—where the decision to de-escalate was influenced by baseline severity and clinical trajectory—a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was performed. Patients were matched 1:1 using a nearest-neighbor algorithm. Propensity scores were calculated via a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating baseline demographics, infection characteristics, and a critical dynamic variable reflecting clinical stability at 72 h. Covariate balance was rigorously assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with all specific parameters detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Variables and data collection

The primary endpoint was clinical cure, assessed at a test-of-cure (TOC) visit 30 days after the initiation of antibiotic therapy. To ensure comprehensive capture and prevent information censoring related to hospital stay duration, follow-up was not restricted to the inpatient setting but was completed via electronic records and structured telephone interviews. In accordance with IDSA guidelines [16], clinical cure was defined as the complete resolution of signs and symptoms attributable to the IAI without further intervention. The strict four-point composite criteria, which incorporate procalcitonin-guided parameters [17], are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Secondary outcomes included 28-day all-cause mortality, lengths of stay in both the ICU and hospital, and the total duration of systemic antibiotic therapy. The incidences of secondary infections (including new MDRO acquisition) and Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) were recorded throughout the same 30-day follow-up window to ensure temporal consistency with the primary outcome. Additionally, time to clinical cure was evaluated as a time-to-event secondary outcome, defined as the number of days from empirical therapy initiation to the date all cure criteria were met. Diagnostic standards for these complications [18, 19] are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Covariates for multivariable adjustment and PSM were categorized into three domains. First, the demographics and comorbidities included age, sex, BMI, the Charlson Comorbidity Index [20], and serum albumin levels at admission. Second, illness severity and infection characteristics included APACHE II score [21], SOFA score [22], admission lactate level, baseline procalcitonin level, infection origin, anatomical source of IAI, and microbiological data. The microbiological variables included culture positivity, monomicrobial or polymicrobial infection, isolated bacterial or fungal species, MDRO status, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles for clinically relevant isolates. We also included a dynamic variable, clinical stability at 72 h, to account for the patient’s early physiological response to empirical treatment prior to any de-escalation decisions. Third, therapeutic variables covered the initial empirical regimen and an objective assessment of the adequacy of the initial source control procedure.

Adequate source control was defined as the macroscopic elimination of the infectious source, successful control of anatomical derangements, and complete drainage of localized fluid collections during the initial intervention, without the clinical requirement for an unplanned surgical or percutaneous reintervention within 7 days. Clinical stability at 72 h was defined as the successful weaning of all vasopressor support combined with an unequivocal trend of clinical improvement, characterized by defervescence to < 38.0 °C or a > 10% decrease in serum lactate from admission, documented at the 72-hour mark.

Sample size estimation and data integrity

The sample size was calculated for the primary endpoint of clinical cure on the basis of a superiority design. To ensure relevance, calculations were powered by institutional audit data of severe IAI patients (2020–2021), which demonstrated clinical cure rates of approximately 74% in those undergoing de-escalation versus 52% in the non-de-escalation group. Consequently, the anticipated cure rates for the power calculation were conservatively set at 75% and 50% for the respective groups. The sample size was computed using a two-sided test for comparing independent proportions, with a statistical power of 80% and a type I error rate (alpha) of 0.05. This indicated that a minimum of 55 patients per group was needed. To account for potential data attrition common in retrospective research, target enrollment was inflated by 15% to 65 patients per group, yielding a total required sample size of 130 patients.

To ensure the fidelity of the retrospective data, all the information was extracted from the institutional EMR system by two clinical researchers trained on a standardized form and who were blinded to the study objectives and patient outcomes. The accuracy was verified by a senior critical care physician who independently reabstracted and validated a random 15% sample of all patient records. Interrater reliability for key variables was formally assessed on this validation sample using Cohen’s kappa for categorical variables (target > 0.85) and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement. This analysis yielded excellent reliability, with an ICC for the APACHE II score of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.91 to 0.98) and for the SOFA score of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88 to 0.96). Any discrepancies identified during dual abstraction were resolved by consensus discussion, with the senior physician acting as the final adjudicator.

Missing data patterns and proportions were thoroughly examined for all covariates. Missingness was addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), operating under the assumption that the data were missing at random. The comprehensive methodology for the imputation process and the pooling of subsequent statistical estimates is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance. Baseline continuous and categorical variables were compared using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests on the basis of data distribution. Following the 1:1 PSM procedure, covariate balance was confirmed with an SMD < 0.1 threshold. In the matched cohort, paired tests (McNemar’s test, paired t test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank test) were employed to analyze outcomes. To further adjust for any residual confounding and identify independent predictors of clinical cure, a conditional logistic regression model was applied to the matched data. LASSO regression was used to perform objective variable selection and prevent model overfitting among the candidate predictors, including the anatomical source of IAI, infection origin, monomicrobial or polymicrobial infection, MDRO infection, and adequacy of empirical antimicrobial coverage. Individual bacterial species and detailed susceptibility results were summarized descriptively because of sparse cell counts and overlap with MDRO status. The most robust variables identified were then entered into a conditional logistic regression for the final multivariable analysis. To ensure robustness, subgroup analyses were performed on the primary outcome across different strata. Finally, sensitivity analyses, including the use of alternative matching algorithms and an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach on the full cohort, were conducted to validate the stability of the results. For the time-to-event outcome (time to clinical cure), Kaplan‒Meier curves were constructed, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).

Results

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

As shown in Figs. 1 and 948 patients were screened, and 405 were eligible for the unmatched cohort (147 de-escalation and 258 non-de-escalation). Exclusions were due to age < 18 years (n = 45), nonbacterial infection (n = 102), severe immunosuppression (n = 89), mild-to-moderate pancreatitis without documented infected necrosis (n = 115), discharge, transfer, or death within 72 h after the index IAI diagnosis (n = 78), and missing key eligibility or outcome data (n = 114).

Flowchart of patient selection

Patients excluded by the 72-hour landmark criterion had greater early illness severity than the eligible cohort did, with higher APACHE II scores (21.6 ± 5.8 vs. 17.9 ± 4.9), higher SOFA scores (10 [8, 13] vs. 8 [6, 10]), more frequent septic shock at diagnosis (56.4% vs. 42.0%), and a higher incidence of MDRO infection (34.6% vs. 22.5%). Their main infection sources were bowel perforation (38.5%), postoperative intra-abdominal infection or anastomotic leakage (25.6%), biliary infection (20.5%), and appendicitis or diverticulitis (15.4%). Within this excluded group, 36 patients died, 24 were transferred to another institution, and 18 were discharged within 72 h.

PSM yielded 106 matched pairs (n = 212). In the unmatched cohort, the non-de-escalation group had significantly greater severity (APACHE II, SOFA, lactate, and procalcitonin) and a higher incidence of MDRO infections and vasopressor use (all P < 0.05; Table 1). Clinical stability at 72 h was also significantly lower in the non-de-escalation group (41.1% vs. 82.3%, P < 0.001). Following matching, all 20 covariates, including the 72-hour clinical stability metric, were well balanced between groups with all SMDs < 0.1.

The microbiological etiology and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of the matched cohort are summarized in Table 2. Culture-positive infections were documented in 176 of 212 patients (83.0%), including 88 monomicrobial infections (41.5%) and 88 polymicrobial infections (41.5%). Culture-negative infection was observed in 36 patients (17.0%). The most common anatomical sources were appendicitis or diverticulitis (29.2%), bowel perforation (28.3%), biliary infection (21.7%), and postoperative intra-abdominal infection or anastomotic leakage (20.8%). The most frequently isolated organisms were Escherichia coli (37.7%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.8%), Enterococcus spp. (14.2%), Bacteroides fragilis (13.2%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (8.0%), Acinetobacter baumannii (6.1%), and Candida spp. (5.2%). MDRO infection was identified in 65 patients (30.7%), mainly extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (15.1%), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (6.6%), carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (4.2%), and multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa (3.3%). Among the Enterobacterales isolates, the susceptibility to carbapenems was greatest (89.6%), and the susceptibility to ceftriaxone or cefotaxime (64.2%) and fluoroquinolones (55.2%) was lower. Among the P. aeruginosa isolates, 58.8% remained susceptible to meropenem, and 70.6% remained susceptible to piperacillin-tazobactam. No relevant imbalance in microbiological etiology or MDRO distribution was observed between the matched groups.

Comparison of clinical outcomes

Primary outcome data were available for 104 matched pairs (n = 208); two pairs were excluded because of facility transfer before assessment. The de-escalation group achieved a significantly higher clinical cure rate (79.8% vs. 63.5%; OR 2.24; 95% CI 1.29–3.89; P = 0.004). Factors associated with clinical cure

Multivariate analysis revealed five independent predictors of clinical cure (Table 3) on the basis of LASSO variable selection (Fig. 4). Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence that an antimicrobial de-escalation strategy, following adequate source control, is associated with significantly improved clinical outcomes in severe IAIs. The primary findings were a substantially higher clinical cure rate at 30 days and significantly accelerated recovery kinetics, as evidenced by the shorter time to clinical cure in the de-escalation group. Although the 30-day assessment window might appear to be prolonged for acute infections, confirming the durability of the clinical resolution and capturing potential late treatment failures, such as recurrent abscesses or anastomotic leaks, were essential. Beyond these primary results, de-escalation also conferred critical secondary benefits, including a marked reduction in 28-day mortality and a significant curtailed emergence of MDROs and CDI. These combined improvements likely stem from the mitigation of microbiome disruption and iatrogenic toxicity associated with sustained broad-spectrum agents [9, 13, 21], reinforcing the principle that optimizing antibiotic therapy is a pivotal component of modern antimicrobial stewardship that directly enhances patient survival.

The observed benefits of a shorter antibiotic course in our cohort are consistent with a paradigm shift in modern IAI management, as reflected in recent international guidelines. The 2024 Surgical Infection Society guidelines strongly recommend a fixed, short course of antibiotics following adequate source control [23], a position supported by landmark trials such as STOP IT, which established the noninferiority of this approach [23, 24]. Similarly, a 2024 Italian multidisciplinary consensus supports this shorter-duration strategy, emphasizing that once the source of infection is controlled, prolonged antibiotic therapy offers no additional benefit [25]. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that within this short course framework, the intentional narrowing of the antibiotic spectrum, or de escalation, is a key driver of both efficacy and safety. The significant reduction in total antibiotic duration in our de-escalation group (median 10 vs. 15 days) is noteworthy. Although recent guidelines and the STOP-IT trial suggest durations as short as 5 days for stable IAI with adequate source control [23, 24], our cohort’s 10-day median reflects the clinical reality of managing severe sepsis and high APACHE II scores (mean 18), where clinicians exercise greater caution. Importantly, our data demonstrate that the 5-day reduction achieved via de-escalation was a primary driver of improved outcomes, as prolonged therapy was independently linked to microbiome disruption and secondary MDRO acquisition. It is crucial to distinguish between treatment duration and the timing of outcome assessment. While a 5-day course may suffice for bacterial suppression, a 30-day test-of-cure (TOC) window is essential to confirm the durability of that response. This observation period is required to capture late-onset treatment failures, such as recurrent abscesses or anastomotic leaks, which often manifest only after the cessation of antimicrobial therapy. Therefore, evaluating the clinical cure rate at 30 days provides more rigorous validation of the safety of our de-escalation and short-term strategies.

The cornerstone of any successful antibiotic strategy for severe IAIs, including de escalation, is unequivocally timely and adequate source control. This principle is extensively supported in the literature as the most critical determinant of patient prognosis [26, 27]. Effective source control involves a multidisciplinary approach to eliminate the infection focus and restore physiological function. Our multivariate analysis quantitatively confirmed its paramount importance, identifying adequate source control as the strongest independent predictor of clinical cure. These findings underscore that de escalation is not a standalone intervention but a safe and effective subsequent step once the foundational principle of source control has been fulfilled.

The rationale for adopting de-escalation extends beyond achieving clinical efficacy to minimizing antibiotic-related harm. In this context, the term metabolic burden should not be interpreted as a directly measured host metabolic endpoint in our study but rather as the cumulative ecological and microbiome-mediated metabolic disturbance imposed by unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure. Prolonged exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics has been associated with increased risks of hospital-acquired diarrhea and Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) [28, 29]. This risk is not determined by treatment duration alone but also varies by antibiotic class. Later-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems have been identified as high-risk antibiotic classes for hospital-acquired CDI, and surgical cohort data have specifically linked carbapenem and third-generation cephalosporin exposure with CDI among surgical inpatients [30, 31]. This is particularly relevant in severe IAI because gastrointestinal surgery, source-control procedures, critical illness, and intestinal barrier disruption may compound antibiotic-related CDI risk. Mechanistically, these high-risk exposures can disrupt the diversity and functional capacity of the intestinal microbiota, thereby altering microbial metabolic pathways involved in bile acid transformation, short-chain fatty acid production, nutrient competition, mucosal immune homeostasis, and colonization resistance [32]. These disturbances may create a permissive intestinal environment for Clostridioides difficile overgrowth, MDRO colonization, and secondary infections. Our observation of reduced MDRO acquisition in the de-escalation group is also consistent with evidence that prolonged antibiotic therapy and broad-spectrum agents, including meropenem, are independent risk factors for MDRO development [33]. Therefore, de-escalation may reduce this burden by narrowing antimicrobial selection pressure and shortening exposure to broad-spectrum agents once source control, microbiological data, and clinical stability support a safer targeted regimen. Our findings are consistent with this biological rationale: the de-escalation group had a shorter antibiotic duration and lower rates of secondary infections and MDRO acquisition, whereas the CDI tended to decrease but not significantly. Nevertheless, because microbiome or metabolomic profiles were not directly measured, this mechanism should be interpreted as a plausible explanation rather than a proven causal pathway in the present cohort.

The clinical significance of preventing MDRO infections cannot be overlooked, as they are independently associated with dramatically worse patient outcomes. A recent meta-analysis revealed that MDRO infections are associated with an almost twofold increase in mortality [34]. This finding is corroborated by studies in ICU populations identifying MDRO infection as an independent predictor of death [35], specifically in the context of IAIs, where MDRO isolation is a known predictor of 28-day mortality [36]. Our own multivariate analysis further confirmed this finding, identifying MDRO infection as an independent negative predictor of clinical cure. Therefore, by implementing a de escalation strategy that reduces the selection pressure for MDROs, we are directly addressing a key modifiable risk factor for mortality.

To strengthen the validity of our findings, we employed robust methodological approaches, including PSM, to mitigate the inherent confounding of an observational study. We specifically addressed the possibility raised by peer review that the greater clinical cure in the de escalation group was biased toward patients with more rapid clinical improvement [15]. A classic pitfall in retrospective antimicrobial stewardship research is confounding by early clinical response, wherein patients who are clinically deteriorating are naturally less likely to undergo de-escalation, which falsely inflates the apparent mortality of the non-de-escalation cohort. By explicitly defining and forcefully matching our cohorts on the basis of their clinical stability at 72 h, we effectively neutralized this critical bias. This ensured that patients in both matched groups were on a demonstrably similar clinical trajectory during the precise timeframe when the decision to de-escalate was formulated. The consistency of our primary result across multiple sensitivity analyses, including different matching algorithms and model-based adjustments on the full cohort, provides a high degree of confidence that the observed benefits are attributable to the de-escalation strategy itself rather than baseline or early prognostic discrepancies. Furthermore, the use of LASSO regression for variable selection identified a clinically coherent set of independent predictors, reinforcing the robustness of our prognostic model.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings, and our findings remain susceptible to unmeasured confounding. Because antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, MDRO prevalence, source-control practices, and stewardship protocols vary substantially across institutions and regions, our results should be interpreted as institution-specific real-world evidence reflecting local microbial ecology rather than estimates that can be directly extrapolated to national or global populations. While our methodology minimized confounding by indication through matching for 72-hour stability, residual unmeasured factors or a higher intrinsic likelihood of recovery in the de-escalation group may still partially contribute to the observed differences in clinical cure rates. Second, although we refined the definition of adequate source control to include objective criteria such as the absence of unplanned reinterventions within 7 days, its initial intraoperative assessment inherently retains some degree of clinical subjectivity. Third, the sample size, while sufficient for the primary analysis, limited the statistical power for certain subgroup analyses, such as the effect of de escalation in the MDRO population, where a positive but nonsignificant trend was observed. These specific findings warrant further investigation in larger, dedicated studies.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials to provide definitive evidence for de escalation strategies in severe IAIs. Such studies could also explore the integration of novel rapid diagnostic technologies to facilitate earlier and more precise de escalation. Additionally, developing standardized, objective criteria for defining adequate source control would enhance consistency in both clinical practice and future research.

Conclusion

This single-center retrospective cohort study suggests that antimicrobial de-escalation, guided by adequate source control and early clinical reassessment, is associated with favorable outcomes in selected patients with severe IAIs who remained clinically evaluable during the de-escalation decision window. This approach was associated with higher clinical cure rates and lower mortality while reducing the emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms. These findings support the integration of de escalation as a core component of antimicrobial stewardship programs in the management of this complex patient population.
