Formulation and evaluation of taste-masked curcumin nanoparticles for improved antioxidant and anticancer efficacy
Authors: Mulham Alfatama, Zalilawati Mat Rashid, Omar A Harasstani, Hamzeh Alkhatib, Asif Nawaz, Hazem Choukaife
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound derived from turmeric, is well-documented for its therapeutic properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities. However, its clinical and nutraceutical applications are limited by its poor aqueous solubility, low oral bioavailability, and unpleasant taste. This study aimed to encapsulate curcumin in alginate-based nanoparticles, with and without chitosan coating, to improve its palatability while preserving its therapeutic efficacy. Two optimized formulations uncoated curcumin-loaded alginate nanoparticles (CANPs) and chitosan-coated nanoparticles (CANPs-Cs) were developed, exhibiting average particle sizes of 199.51 ± 9.40 nm and 312.90 ± 10.30 nm, polydispersity indices of 0.26 ± 0.090 and 0.27 ± 0.09, zeta potentials of − 23.10 ± 1.10 mV and + 28.50 ± 1.00 mV, encapsulation efficiencies of 93.6 ± 3.5% and 90.8 ± 0.1%, and drug loading of 1.32 ± 0.15% and 1.25 ± 0.19%, respectively. FTIR analysis confirmed the compatibility and successful encapsulation of curcumin within the nanoparticle matrix. The chitosan-coated formulation (CANPs-Cs) exhibited enhanced antioxidant activity and superior taste-masking ability, as evidenced by a significant reduction (~ 50%) in curcumin release within artificial saliva compared to CANPs. Additionally, both formulations demonstrated potent cytotoxic effects against the CT26 colon cancer cell line, with CANPs-Cs maintaining therapeutic efficacy while offering improved sensory attributes. These findings suggest that CANPs-Cs is a promising delivery system for curcumin, offering enhanced bioactivity and palatability suitable for food and pharmaceutical applications.Keywords: Curcumin, Nanoparticles, Taste masking, Anticancer, PalatabilitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Curcumin is a yellow crystalline bioactive compound present in the orange-yellow color of turmeric. It is commonly used as an ingredient in Asian cuisine. Recent studies have demonstrated that curcumin possesses a broad spectrum of therapeutic properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-ischemic, anticancer, antimicrobial, and wound healing effects, as well as potential benefits in preventing neurodegenerative diseases1,2. Despite these benefits, curcumin’s therapeutic application is limited due to its poor chemical stability, low solubility and poor oral bioavailability1,3. These limitations are mainly attributed to its chemical structure, which makes it prone to degradation, oxidation, and hydrolysis. Moreover, the clinical and traditional oral administration of curcumin is hindered by its characteristic earthy and bitter taste, which adversely affects patient compliance and acceptance3. Several approaches have been proposed to improve the limitations of curcumin, including the use of adjuvants, chemical modifications or encapsulation in liposomes4, nanoemulsion5, cyclodextrins6, polymeric carriers7,8, and lipid nanoparticles9.

Recent studies have focused on encapsulating curcumin within nanoparticles composed of various food-grade polymers such as alginate, casein, chitosan, sodium caseinate, and zein, aiming to enhance its stability, solubility, and bioavailability10,11. Alginate is a naturally occuring anionic polysaccharide derived from brown seaweed. It has been widely studied for its potential as a drug delivery system due to its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxicity. Alginate is a hydrophilic polymer that forms a gel-like matrix when it comes into contact with divalent cations such as calcium ions. This property makes it suitable for use in oral drug delivery systems as it can protect drugs from the harsh acidic environment of the stomach, and provide controlled release of drugs in the intestine12,13. Alginate-based nanoparticles have emerged as a promising delivery platform for curcumin, effectively enhancing its aqueous solubility and chemical stability, thereby overcoming critical limitations and improving its therapeutic potential14. Additionally, alginate nanoparticles can be engineered to mask the earthy and bitter taste of curcumin, thereby improving its palatability and patient acceptance15.

Chitosan (Cs), a widely studied biopolymer, exhibits strong mucoadhesive properties that enhance the penetration of macromolecules across intestinal and nasal mucosal barriers by facilitating adhesion and transiently opening tight junctions16,17. It has been thoroughly studied for the delivery of pharmacological agents and food due to favorable properties, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, non-immunogenicity, and low-toxicity18. The combination of alginate and chitosan can provide a versatile and effective drug delivery platform with improved stability, mucoadhesion and palatability.

This study designed two variants of alginate nanocarriers for curcumin loading, with and without a chitosan coating. Nanoparticles were prepared via ionic gelation and optimized by adjusting formulation and processing parameters. The resulting nanoparticles were evaluated in vitro for antioxidant and anticancer activities. Palatability was assessed through in vitro curcumin release using artificial saliva and confirmed by a human sensory panel for taste evaluation. The objective was to identify promising nanocarriers to improve oral delivery of curcumin.

Material and methods

Materials

Curcumin (purity, > 98%, Mw = 368.36 g/moL, C12H20O6) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA). Guluronic acid-rich sodium alginate (molecular weight = 4.8 × 106 ± 1.8 × 105 Da, nominal viscosity = 300 mPa.s, mannuronate/guluronate ratio = 0.59; Manugel® DMB, ISP, USA) was used as the matrix of nanoparticles. Chitosan (molecular weight = 1.5 × 106 Da, degree of deacetylation 10 = 86%; Zhejiang Aoxing Co. Ltd., China) was used as a coating agent. Phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma Aldrich, USA), RPMI 1640 (with glucose, Glutamine, and phenol red) (Fisher Scientific, UK), fetal bovine serum (FBS), Trypsine-EDTA (Sigma Aldrich, USA) and penicillin–streptomycin mixed (Nacalai Tesque, Japan) were used as cell culture reagents. 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) was used for the MTT assay. 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (USA) for testing antioxidant activity.

Ethical statement

Experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines and regulations of the Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin Human Research Ethics Committee (UHREC), and ethical approval was obtained (Approval No. UniSZA/UHREC/2021/329).

Preparation of uncoated and coated nanoparticles

Curcumin alginate nanoparticles (CANPs) were fabricated by ionotropic gelation method, as described earlier with some modifications14. Briefly, 1 mL of curcumin (0.25 mg/ml) in ethanol containing Span 80 (2%) was added dropwise into 20 mL of alginate solution (0.1% w/v, pH 4.7) under continuous magnetic stirring at 1,000 rpm for 10 min, followed by sonication for 1 min at amplitude 50% using probe sonicator (Qsonica Q500 Sonicator, Newtown, CT 06,470, USA) to form an (o/w) emulsion. Thereafter, 3 mL of CaCl₂ (0.1% w/v) was added dropwise, followed by homogenization at 15 000 rpm for 5 min using high-speed homogenizer (Wiggen Hauser D-500, Berlin, Germany).

Curcumin–alginate nanoparticles coated with chitosan (CANPs-Cs) were prepared using the same procedure, wherein the CANPs were suspended in 3 mL of chitosan solution (0.1% w/v) dissolved in 1% (v/v) acetic acid (pH 4.6). The resulting mixture was homogenized at 15,000 rpm for an additional 5 min. After homogenization, all samples were subjected to centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected for further analyses, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The resulting nanoparticles were resuspended in deionized water for physicochemical characterization. For analyses requiring dry samples, the nanoparticles were subsequently lyophilized using a freeze-dryer (ALPHA 1–2 LD plus, Germany).

Preparation process of curcumin alginate nanoparticles (CANPs) and coated with chitosan nanoparticles (CANPs-Cs).

Optimization and characterization

The optimization of both uncoated and coated nanoparticles involved a comprehensive exploration of various formulation and processing parameters. The chosen factors encompassed alginate concentration, CaCl2 concentration, curcumin concentration, chitosan concentration, and homogenization time. In order to determine the optimal formulation, the impacts of all five factors were systematically assessed at three different levels (low, medium, and high), considering several response variables such as particle size (nm), polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency (EE).

Particle size, polydispersity index and zeta potential

Particle size and PDI were assessed through dynamic light scattering (DLS), while zeta potential was determined using electrophoretic light scattering (ELS). The measurements were conducted employing a Particle Size Analyzer (Litesizer 500, Anton Paar, Austria).

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL)

EE was determined using an indirect method, adapted from previous studies with minor modifications19,20. Fresh samples were subjected to ultrafiltration technique with an ultrafiltration tube (cutoff molecular weight 30 kDa, Millipore) for 10 min at 2500 rpm to separate the nanoparticles from the unloaded curcumin. The amount of free curcumin in the filtered supernatant was quantified using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1280, 220–240 (CE), Kyoto, Japan) at a wavelength of 427 nm. The EE percentage (EE%) was calculated in triplicate using Eq. (1).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The FTIR spectra were acquired by accumulating 28 scans per spectrum within the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1, employing an FTIR spectrophotometer IR Prestige-21 (Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan). The experimental procedure included running all samples in triplicate, and each spectrum was subjected to analysis using IR solution software (version 1.40).

Surface morphology

The morphological structures of the dried samples were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL, JSM-6360 LA), operating at a voltage of 10 kV. Prior to imaging, each sample underwent a coating process with a thin layer of gold. Additionally, freshly prepared nanoparticles were analysed using transmission electron microscope (TEM). The nanoparticles were suspended in ethanol, and a drop of the suspension was deposited onto 300-mesh copper grids, air-dried at room temperature, and subsequently examined by TEM at a magnification of 98,000 × .

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH technique (2,2-diphenyl-1-picryl hydrazyl) relies on antioxidants capturing the DPPH free radical, resulting in a decrease in absorbance at 517 nm21. To assess the antioxidant activity of blank and loaded CANPs and CANPs-Cs, the DPPH assay was employed, following established methodologies with certain modifications from previous works19,20. The assay involved preparing various concentrations (1000–0 µg/ml) of each formulation. Specifically, 200 µL of DPPH solution was combined with 20 µL of each sample solution in a 96-well microplate. The mixture was then incubated in darkness at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, absorbance readings were taken at 517 nm wavelength using a UV-1800 Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). Quercetin solution served as the standard, and ethanol was utilized as a negative control.

Cell culture

The CT26 colon cell line was obtained from the cell culture laboratory at UniSZA (Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin). These cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 µg/mL, respectively). The cell culture was maintained in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Subculturing was carried out twice a week using 0.05% trypsin to detach the cells. For the MTT cytotoxicity assay, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well.

Cell viability assay

The MTT assay was employed to assess the cytotoxic impact of curcumin, CANPs, and CANPs-Cs on the CT26 cell line. CT26 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, as previously described. After aspirating the culture medium, the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently treated with varying concentrations of curcumin, CANPs, and CANPs-Cs (0, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL). Following a 24-h incubation period, the media were discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, MTT solution (20 μL, 5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and allowed to incubate for 4 h.

Following the incubation with MTT solution, the medium was aspirated, and 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plate containing DMSO was shaken on an orbital shaker for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, absorbance measurements were taken using a plate reader (Biotek Synergy 2, Winooski, VT, USA) at wavelengths of 570 nm. The IC50 values, representing the concentrations of free curcumin and curcumin nanoparticles that inhibit 50% of cell growth, were calculated according to Eq. (4). All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Palatability

In vitro curcumin release

In accordance with established protocols outlined in prior studies22,23, artificial saliva was meticulously prepared. This involved dissolving 200 mg of methyl-p-hydroxybenzoate in 80 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 5.9 mg of MgCl2.6H2O, 16.6 mg of CaCl2.2H2O, 62.5 mg of KCl, and 1130 mg of KH2PO4. Simultaneously, 1000 mg of sodium carboxymethyl cellulose was dissolved in 20 mL of distilled water under heat until complete dissolution. Subsequently, the two solutions were amalgamated and adjusted to a pH of 6.75 using 1 M NaOH.

The prepared artificial saliva (10 mL) was placed in a shaker bath, subjected to 50 strokes per minute, and maintained at a temperature of 37 ± 0.2 °C for 15 min to ensure temperature equilibrium prior to testing. Subsequently, a specific amount of CANPs, and CANPs-Cs samples were immersed into artificial saliva for 30 s and 1 min, and the amount of released curcumin was determined using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1280, 220–240 (CE), Kyoto, Japan) at a wavelength of 427 nm.

Human panel

Palatability evaluation was conducted following the method outlined by a previous report with slight modifications24. Healthy volunteers from Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin, Besut Campus, participated as panelists after providing informed consent, in accordance with ethical guidelines. The inclusion criteria required participants to be healthy students aged 18–35 years, who provided written informed consent following approval by the Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin Human Research Ethics Committee (UHREC) under protocol code UniSZA/UHREC/2021/329. Exclusion criteria comprised individuals who smoke, suffer from cough and flu, use prescribed medications, pregnant mothers, and those with allergies to any component of the formulations. All six participants evaluated both CANPs and CANPs-Cs formulations based on attributes such as smell, taste, taste-masking, texture, aftertaste, and overall acceptability. The assessment of palatability and taste-masking employed a 5-point hedonic scale, ranging from 1 (very unpleasant), 2 (slightly unpleasant), 3 (acceptable), 4 (slightly pleasant), to 5 (very pleasant), in accordance with the method outlined by Tomoko and Beglar15.

Initially, volunteers were instructed to abstain from consuming food or beverages for 1 h before the test. Subsequently, all test materials were presented to the panelists in small plastic cups. Each panelist was required to evaluate five samples, including the positive control. They were instructed to place the tested samples at the center of their tongue, hold them there for 30 s while moving their tongue to spread the samples, and then spit out the samples. After one minute, volunteers provided feedback by responding to questions in a set of questionnaires, following the methodology described in a previous study25. To neutralize the taste, volunteers consumed unsalted crackers and thoroughly rinsed their mouths with plenty of water. The tested products were presented in the following order: curcumin solution as a positive control, blank NPs, blank CANPs-Cs, CANPs and CANPs-Cs.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate as independent studies, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Group differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, while two-way ANOVA was applied where appropriate to evaluate the combined effects of different factors. Graphs and additional statistical analyses were generated using Prism version 10.4.0 (Boston, MA, USA). Non-significant results are indicated as ‘ns’ (p > 0.05), and significance levels are denoted as * p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, *** p ≤ 0.001, and **** p ≤ 0.0001.

Results and discussions

Optimization of CANPs and CANPs-Cs

Figure 2 illustrates the influence of varying alginate concentrations (0.05%, 0.10%, and 0.20% w/v) on the physicochemical characteristics of CANPs and CANPs-Cs. As shown in Fig. 2(A), particle size increased proportionally with alginate content. For CANPs, the mean size rose from 212.5 ± 10.1 nm at 0.05% to 421.4 ± 18.5 nm at 0.20%. CANPs-Cs exhibited an even greater size increase, from 270.6 ± 20.7 nm to 673.7 ± 31.0 nm, which can be attributed to the enhanced matrix density resulting from polyelectrolyte interactions between chitosan and alginate.

The effect of alginate concentration on (A) particle size, (B) PDI, (C) zeta potential and (D) EE for CANPs and CANPs-Cs.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) also showed a non-linear trend with alginate content Fig. 2 (D). For CANPs, EE% rose from 59.8 ± 0.1% at 0.05% to a peak of 81.8 ± 0.2% at 0.10%, then dropped to 53.7 ± 0.2% at 0.20%. A similar trend was observed in CANPs-Cs, with EE% increasing from 79.8 ± 0.7% to 90.8 ± 0.1%.

The initial increase in EE% is attributed to the enhanced matrix formation at moderate alginate concentrations, which restricts drug diffusion. However, at higher concentrations, increased internal viscosity and compactness lead to a densified polymer network that decreases the free volume within the matrix26. This reduced free volume limits the space available for drug molecules to be entrapped, thereby hindering efficient drug encapsulation27. Additionally, the tighter matrix structure can reduce the mobility of drug molecules during formulation, further decreasing entrapment efficiency. These observations are consistent with earlier findings, which noted that excessive alginate impedes efficient drug encapsulation due to densified matrix formation28.

As shown in Fig. 2 (B), the PDI value ranged from 0.2 to 0.3, indicating moderate particle size heterogeneity. CANPs consistently showed lower PDI values than CANPs-Cs, reflecting better size uniformity. Figure 2 (C) shows zeta potential trends, with CANPs exhibiting negatively charged surfaces from − 18 to − 33 mV, suggesting good colloidal stability. In contrast, CANPs-Cs displayed positive surface charges due to chitosan coating, which further increased with alginate concentration, reflecting surface rearrangement and polyelectrolyte interfacial interactions. Overall, the optimal formulation was achieved at 0.10% alginate, yielding the most favorable particle size and highest EE%: 245.0 ± 9.9 nm and 81.8 ± 0.2% for CANPs, and 320.5 ± 15.9 nm and 90.8 ± 0.1% for CANPs-Cs.

Fig. 3 demonstrates the effect of calcium chloride (CaCl₂) concentration on the physicochemical characteristics of CANPs and CANPs-Cs, using a fixed alginate concentration (0.1% w/v), curcumin concentration (0.25% w/v), and homogenization time (5 min). As seen in Fig. 3 (D), the EE% peaked at 0.10% CaCl₂ for both CANPs (78.3 ± 0.5%) and CANPs-Cs (89.5 ± 0.4%), suggesting an optimal crosslinking density that forms a stable network capable of effectively entrapping curcumin molecules. At lower CaCl₂ concentrations (0.05%), EE% decreased, accompanied by particle aggregation Fig. 3 (A). This decline may result from an imbalance in the Ca2⁺:alginate ratio, leading to inadequate crosslinking and excessive protonated amine groups (NH₃⁺) from chitosan. The presence of these unneutralized positive charges can induce electrostatic repulsion, promoting nanoparticle aggregation, consistent with previous studies26.

The effect of CaCl₂ concentration on (A) particle size, (B) PDI, (C) zeta potential and (D) EE for CANPs and CANPs-Cs.

Conversely, increasing the CaCl₂ concentration to 0.20% resulted in a marked drop in EE%. This is likely due to excessive ionic crosslinking compressing the alginate matrix, reducing internal pore size and limiting the available space for curcumin encapsulation. The imbalance created by a high Ca2⁺ to alginate ratio leads to an overly dense and compressed polymer network. This densification restricts the flexibility and expansion of the alginate chains, thereby hindering efficient drug entrapment29. In contrast, inadequate CaCl₂ concentration causes insufficient crosslinking, resulting in a loose matrix with weaker structural integrity and increased particle aggregation due to unneutralized electrostatic repulsion between alginate chains30.

PDI values shown in Fig. 3 (B) remained below 0.3 for all formulations, indicating an acceptable size distribution. The lowest PDI was observed at 0.10% CaCl₂, further confirming this concentration as optimal for achieving uniform particle formation.

Zeta potential analysis showed that CANPs had a negative surface charge ranging from − 28.0 ± 2.9 mV, while CANPs-Cs exhibited positive values of 28.7 ± 0.6 mV (Fig. 3 (C)). These surface charges suggest strong colloidal stability and confirm the successful electrostatic interaction between negatively charged alginate and positively charged chitosan. The zeta potential remained relatively stable across increasing CaCl₂ concentrations for both CANPs and CANPs-Cs, indicating that surface charge was largely maintained under these formulation conditions.

As shown in Fig. 4 (A), curcumin concentration had a minimal effect on particle size in both formulations. The sizes remained relatively stable, ranging between 200 and 220 nm for CANPs and 300–330 nm for CANPs-Cs, with no significant trend observed. This suggests that the curcumin loading levels did not substantially disrupt the nanoparticle matrix structure during formation.

The effect of curcumin concentration on (A) particle size, (B) PDI, (C) zeta potential and (D) EE for CANPs and CANPs-Cs.

In contrast, Fig. 4 (D) demonstrates a clear inverse relationship between curcumin concentration and EE%. At the lowest concentration (0.25% w/v), maximum EE% was observed, with values of 93.6 ± 3.5% for CANPs and 91.4 ± 0.7% for CANPs-Cs. However, EE% declined progressively with increasing drug content, reaching approximately 41% at 2.0% curcumin for both systems. This drop can be attributed to the saturation of the encapsulation matrix; when the drug exceeds the carrier’s entrapment capacity, unencapsulated curcumin is likely lost to the external phase. A similar observation was reported by Shukla et al., where higher drug loading reduced EE% due to overflow beyond the matrix’s loading threshold31.

The PDI values were consistently below 0.3 for the 0.25% curcumin concentration, indicating acceptable size distribution and colloidal stability Fig. 4 (B). Slight fluctuations at higher concentrations may reflect increased particle heterogeneity due to excess drug presence during formation. Zeta potential analysis confirmed the stability of both nanoparticle systems Fig. 4 (C). CANPs maintained a negative charge around − 20 to − 25 mV, while CANPs-Cs exhibited positive values (~ + 28 mV), reflecting the chitosan coating. These values fall within the range considered acceptable for stable colloidal dispersions.

Figure 5 presents the effect of varying chitosan concentrations on the particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and EE% of CANPs-Cs. Chitosan was selected as a coating material due to its well-documented compatibility with alginate and its ability to form stable polyelectrolyte complexes, thereby enhancing nanoparticle stability and curcumin palatability32.

The effect of chitosan concentration on (A) particle size, (B) PDI, (C) zeta potential and (D) EE for CANPs-Cs.

As expected, increasing the chitosan concentration resulted in a progressive increase in particle size (Fig. 5 (A)), in line with previous studies reporting that additional chitosan deposition forms a thicker coating layer around the nanoparticle core, thereby increasing its hydrodynamic diameter33. Similarly, the PDI (Fig. 5 (B)) and zeta potential (Fig. 5 (C)) both rose with increasing chitosan concentration. This suggests a broader size distribution and enhanced surface charge, likely due to the accumulation of positively charged amino groups (NH₃⁺) on the nanoparticle surface, further confirming successful chitosan deposition.

However, a notable observation was the significant reduction in EE% at a chitosan concentration of 0.05% w/v (Fig. 5 (D)), decreasing from 85.63 ± 4.70% at optimal conditions. This reduction may be attributed to the increased flexibility and extended molecular conformation of oligomeric chitosan chains at lower concentrations, which may interfere with the entrapment of curcumin by disrupting the compactness of the matrix. As these chitosan chains are more loosely arranged at low concentrations, they might not efficiently seal the drug within the core, leading to reduced retention34. Additionally, the encapsulation system has a loading threshold, beyond which the matrix becomes saturated and cannot accommodate more curcumin molecules. When the curcumin concentration exceeds this threshold, excess curcumin remains unencapsulated in the external phase, which may lead to precipitation or aggregation, further lowering apparent entrapment efficiency. This phenomenon highlights the importance of optimizing both chitosan and drug concentrations to maximize encapsulation efficiency and maintain nanoparticle stability.

Collectively, the results indicate that while increasing chitosan concentration enhances surface properties and colloidal stability, it also introduces trade-offs in terms of encapsulation efficiency at suboptimal levels. Therefore, careful optimization of chitosan concentration is crucial to balance coating integrity, stability, and drug loading performance in CANPs-Cs formulations.

Figure 6 demonstrates the impact of homogenization time on the particle size of chitosan-coated alginate nanoparticles (CANPs-Cs). As shown in Fig. 6 (A), increasing the homogenization time from 1 to 5 min resulted in a significant reduction in particle size, from 426.10 ± 41.20 nm to 312.90 ± 10.30 nm. This reduction can be attributed to the enhanced shear force and energy input provided by increased homogenization, which facilitates more effective size reduction and droplet breakup during nanoparticle formation.

The effect of homogenization time on (A) particle size, (B) PDI, (C) zeta potential and (D) EE for CANPs-Cs.

However, when the homogenization time was extended to 15 min, the particle size increased to 364.90 ± 18.50 nm. This reversal may be explained by the onset of colloidal instability caused by excessive mechanical energy input. Prolonged homogenization can induce particle coalescence and structural disruption, ultimately leading to aggregation and increased size. This trend is consistent with previous reports, which observed that extended homogenization produced smaller but unstable microcapsules prone to aggregation and subsequent size increase35. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing homogenization time to balance shear energy input and nanoparticle stability. Based on the observed trends, a homogenization time of 5 min appears to be optimal for producing uniformly sized, stable CANPs-Cs with minimal aggregation.

Based on the optimization studies, the optimal formulation of CANPs exhibited an average particle size of 199.51 ± 9.40 nm, PDI value of 0.26 ± 0.09, zeta potential of − 23.10 ± 1.10 mV, EE% of 93.6 ± 3.5%, and DL% of 1.32 ± 0.15% (Fig. 7 (A and B)). In contrast, the optimal CANPs-Cs formulation was achieved using 0.1% w/v alginate, 0.1% w/v CaCl₂, 0.25% w/v curcumin, and 0.1% w/v chitosan, with a homogenization time of 5 min. These conditions resulted in nanoparticles with a particle size of approximately 312.90 ± 10.30 nm, narrow size distribution (PDI 0.26 ± 0.09), favorable zeta potential (28.50 ± 1 mV), and high EE% of 90.8 ± 0.1% with DL% of 1.25 ± 0.19% (Fig. 7 (C and D)). Both formulations exhibited excellent colloidal stability and drug entrapment, indicating effective coating and matrix interaction between alginate and chitosan. These optimized parameters reflect an effective compromise between mechanical processing intensity and polymer–drug interactions, thereby ensuring structural integrity, particle uniformity, and efficient curcumin encapsulation in the nanoparticle system.

Particle size distribution (A, C) and zeta potential profiles (B, D) of CANPs (A, B) and CANPs-Cs (C, D) under optimized formulation conditions.

FTIR analysis

The curcumin spectrum exhibited its characteristic absorption bands, including a prominent peak at 3506.09 cm⁻1, corresponding to phenolic O–H stretching (Fig. 8 (A)). Other notable peaks were observed at 1625.99 cm⁻1 for aromatic C = C stretching and 1504 cm⁻1 for C–O and C–C vibrations. These findings are consistent with previous reports, thereby confirming the intact molecular structure of curcumin36.

FTIR spectra of (A) curcumin, (B) alginate, (C) chitosan, (D) CANPs and (E) CANPs-Cs.

The sodium alginate spectrum showed typical polysaccharide signals, with a distinct C–O–C stretching vibration at 1026.13 cm⁻1, and strong carboxylate group peaks at 1596.13 cm⁻1 (asymmetric stretch) and 1408.04 cm⁻1 (symmetric stretch), indicating the presence of guluronic and mannuronic acid residues (Fig. 8(B))37. In Spectrum (C), chitosan displayed characteristic functional group peaks at 3360 cm⁻1 (O–H stretching), 1593.20 cm⁻1 (C = O bending), and 1379.1 cm⁻1, corresponding to amide III and NH bending vibrations38.

Following the formation of nanoparticles, the CANPs and CANPs-Cs spectra (D and E) showed notable shifts and broadening of peaks. In particular, a wide band near 3388.93 cm⁻1 was observed, attributed to overlapping O–H stretching from alginate, curcumin, and chitosan. Additionally, in the region between 1614.42 cm⁻1 and 1732.10 cm⁻1, pronounced peaks were evident, corresponding to carboxyl and carbonyl stretching. These features indicate intermolecular interactions involving the carboxylate groups of alginates, amine groups of chitosan, and the aromatic structures of curcumin within the nanoparticle matrix.

In CANPs-Cs, the appearance of a broad, intense peak within the 3000–3650 cm⁻1 range provides further evidence of the integration of hydroxyl groups following chitosan coating. The intensification and shifting of peaks in the 1614.42–1730.80 cm⁻1 range suggest strong electrostatic interactions and possible hydrogen bonding among the carboxyl groups of alginates, amino groups of chitosan, and phenolic hydroxyl groups of curcumin2.

These spectral changes provide strong evidence for the successful formation of a polyelectrolyte complex and the efficient encapsulation of curcumin within the nanoparticle matrix. Importantly, no significant peak disappearance or distortion was observed in the curcumin spectrum, indicating that the chemical integrity of curcumin was preserved throughout the encapsulation process. These observations are consistent with previous findings, further confirming the compatibility and synergistic interactions among alginate, chitosan, and curcumin in the nanoparticle system32,39.

Surface morphology study

SEM and TEM analysis confirmed that both formulations exhibited nanoscale dimensions, as presented in Fig. 9. The particle sizes determined by SEM were consistently smaller than those measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS), which is likely due to dehydration and partial nanostructure collapse during SEM sample preparation. Morphological assessment in Fig. 9 (A) and (B) revealed that the nanoparticles were predominantly spherical with a relatively narrow size distribution, indicative of a uniform synthesis process. The application of a chitosan coating (CANPs-Cs) resulted in a visibly smoother and more continuous surface compared to uncoated CANPs, supporting successful surface modification (Fig. 9B). However, SEM images also revealed some degree of particle aggregation, potentially due to the freeze-drying process. TEM analysis (Figs. 9C and D) provided further insight into nanoparticle morphology, showing particles with minimal aggregation and greater dispersion compared to the SEM images, attributable to the TEM sample preparation technique, which reduces clustering. TEM micrographs of uncoated CANPs (Fig. 9C) displayed nearly spherical nanoparticles, whereas the chitosan-coated CANPs (Fig. 9D) exhibited enhanced sphericity and a smoother, more uniform coating layer, consistent with effective chitosan deposition.

Drug release of CANPs and CANPs-Cs

The initial release of curcumin from both CANPs and chitosan-coated CANPs (CANPs-Cs) was evaluated within the first minute in artificial saliva. Results demonstrated that the chitosan coating significantly improved curcumin retention, reducing its release from 1.25% in uncoated CANPs to 0.4% in CANPs-Cs within this short time frame. This reduction in early curcumin release is crucial for effective taste masking, thereby potentially enhancing patient compliance40. These findings corroborate earlier studies that reported strong electrostatic interactions between alginate and chitosan, forming a robust polyelectrolyte complex41. This electrostatic bonding, coupled with the inherently slow diffusion rate of curcumin within the nanoparticle matrix, likely impedes the rapid release of curcumin from the chitosan-coated nanoparticles. In addition, a previous study demonstrated that the cumulative release of curcumin from alginate microspheres was approximately 2.72% within the first 24 h in PBS. This notably low initial release indicates that the formulation successfully minimized burst release, which is generally attributed to drug molecules loosely associated with the microsphere matrix42.

Antioxidant activities of CANPs and CANPs-Cs.

The antioxidant activity of curcumin was assessed by measuring the reduction in the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical’s characteristic color intensity using UV–visible spectrophotometry21. Figure 10 presents the comparative antioxidant capacity of blank nanoparticles, CANPs, and CANPs-Cs across a concentration range of 15.625 to 1000 µg/mL. Statistical analysis demonstrated a significant increase (p < 0.05) in radical scavenging activity with increasing concentrations of both CANPs and CANPs-Cs. Notably, at the highest concentration (1000 µg/mL), the percentage inhibition improved from 19.14% in uncoated CANPs to 23.42% in chitosan-coated CANPs. This enhancement is likely attributable to the inherent antioxidant properties of curcumin, primarily mediated by its phenolic hydroxyl groups43. Importantly, curcumin retained its free radical scavenging capability post-encapsulation, as the reactive keto-enol moiety was effectively protected from premature degradation by the nanoparticle matrix44. Mechanistically, curcumin donates a hydrogen atom to neutralize the DPPH radical, converting it into a stable, yellow-colored diphenyl-picrylhydrazine diamagnetic compound45. Consequently, the chitosan coating appears to provide additional stabilization to curcumin within the nanoparticles, thereby preserving and possibly enhancing its antioxidant efficacy. In line with our results, a previous study reported that the antioxidant activity of encapsulated curcumin was slightly lower than that of free curcumin, with the DPPH radical scavenging assay showing values of 3.33 ± 0.02 μM Trolox/mg for the nanoemulsion compared to 3.53 ± 0.11 μM Trolox/mg for native curcumin46.

Cytotoxicity study

The cytotoxic activity of free curcumin, CANPs and CANPs-CS was assessed against the colon cancer CT26 cell line (Fig. 11). A colorimetric analysis of cell viability was conducted after 24 h of treatment. Cell viability decreased progressively with increasing curcumin concentrations for all formulations. The IC50 value for CANPs was determined to be 25.5 ± 0.51 µg/mL, while the IC50 value for CANPs-CS was significantly higher at 45.8 ± 1.34 µg/mL, likely due to the chitosan coating delaying curcumin release. In contrast, free curcumin exhibited an IC50 of 108.3 ± 1.52 µg/mL. These results also aligned with the results obtained for DPPH inhibition, in which the sustained release imparted by the chitosan coating influenced the IC₅₀ values of CANPs-Cs compared to CANPs.

(A) Cytotoxic effects of blank nanoparticles, free curcumin, CANPs, CANPs-Cs and with different concentrations and (B) the IC50 values on the CT26 colon cancer cell line.

Controlled drug delivery systems are designed to maintain stable plasma drug concentrations while minimizing the risk of burst release. By preventing the initial surge in drug levels, these systems reduce the likelihood of negative therapeutic effects and promote a more consistent pharmacological response. In addition, they effectively lower both the frequency and duration of drug administration, which not only improves patient adherence but also enhances the overall quality of the treatment experience47. Consistent with previous reports, numerous formulations have demonstrated improved bioavailability relative to pure curcumin, primarily attributed to enhanced membrane permeability and increased cellular uptake48. For example, a study reported that treatment of HT-29 with encapsulated curcumin alginate-arabic gum nanoparticles and free curcumin for 24 h showed IC50 of 49.06 ± 1.26 and 78.04 ± 1.22 respectively. This aligns with the present study, highlighting how nanoparticle encapsulation improves the anticancer efficacy of curcumin49.

It is worth noting that blank nanoparticles displayed a degree of cytotoxicity at higher concentrations (between 400 and 100 µg/mL) potentially due to the disruptive effects of small particle size on cellular membranes, leading to compromised cell viability. The cytotoxicity assay further demonstrated that curcumin-loaded nanoparticles were statistically more effective than free curcumin in suppressing cancer cell proliferation.

Palatability of CANPs and CANPS-Cs

A 5-point hedonic scale was employed to evaluate the panelists’ preferences for CANPs and CANPs-Cs focusing on sensory attributes such as smell, taste, taste masking, texture, aftertaste, and overall acceptability. A higher score reflected greater acceptability, while a lower score indicated panelists’ continued perception of undesirable sensory attributes, particularly the aftertaste (Fig. 12). Among the tested attributes, CANPs-Cs received the highest scores for smell (3.33), taste (2.67), taste masking (4.00), texture (2.83), and overall acceptability (2.67). However, it recorded a relatively low score for aftertaste (1.82), suggesting that the sour aftertaste remained a concern despite successful taste masking.

Palatability score of free curcuminm, blank nanoparticles, CANPs, blank NPs-Cs and CANPs-Cs.

Although none of the differences in sensory scores were statistically significant, 80% of panelists rated CANPs-Cs highly in terms of taste masking, highlighting the formulation’s effectiveness in masking the natural bitterness of curcumin. The slightly sour taste and odor noted in CANPs-Cs may be attributed to the use of a low concentration (0.1%) of chitosan, which could contribute to the sensory perception of acidity. Panelists also commented that while the curcumin flavor was effectively masked, the sour aftertaste was particularly pronounced and undesirable. These findings suggest that future work should focus on optimizing the chitosan concentration or incorporating flavor-modifying agents to mitigate the sour aftertaste and further improve consumer acceptability. Aligning with our results, a study reported that both wet and dry chitosan-coated alginate beads containing paracetamol provided a marked enhancement in aftertaste scores when compared with paracetamol powder50. Another study observed that encapsulating pure black seed oil (BSO) within alginate beads restricted the release of its odor into the surrounding environment, unlike the strong smell emitted by unencapsulated BSO. In addition, the alginate bead matrix formed a protective barrier that reduced the direct interaction of BSO with taste receptors, thereby diminishing its inherent taste15.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed curcumin-loaded calcium alginate and chitosan nanoparticles (CANPs and CANPs-Cs), demonstrating favorable physicochemical characteristics such as nanoscale particle size, high encapsulation efficiency, and clear evidence of molecular interactions. The chitosan-coated formulation (CANPs-Cs) exhibited superior performance in several aspects, including enhanced antioxidant activity, significantly reduced initial drug release, and effective taste masking. In addition, cytotoxicity assays confirmed the potential of the formulations—particularly CANPs-Cs to induce cell death in colon cancer (CT26) cells, supporting their therapeutic relevance. Despite these promising outcomes, the sour aftertaste associated with CANPs-Cs remains a challenge and warrants further formulation optimization. Although this study provides valuable preliminary evidence regarding palatability, the limited number of human panel participants represents a constraint. Future research should involve larger-scale clinical evaluations to confirm these findings and to establish a comprehensive assessment of consumer acceptance and product applicability. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that these nanoparticle systems are promising candidates for curcumin delivery in both pharmaceutical and functional food applications, provided those sensory limitations, particularly aftertaste, are addressed through further refinement.
