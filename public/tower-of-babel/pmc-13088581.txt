Comparative analysis of transformer, CNN, and YOLO architectures for mandibular condyle segmentation on panoramic radiographs: a deep learning benchmark
Authors: Serkan Yilmaz, Hilal Peker Ozturk, Hatice Seda Ozgedik, Ismail Hakan Avsever, Bugra Senel, Murat Tasyurek, Mehmet Hakan Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to perform the first multi-architecture comparison of pixel-level mandibular condyle segmentation on panoramic radiographs using transformer-based (RT-DETR), CNN-based (EfficientNet, Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt), and YOLO-based (YOLOv9-Seg, YOLOv11-Seg) deep learning models.MethodsA dataset of 1,300 panoramic radiographs (2,600 condyles) was retrospectively curated. Ground-truth masks were annotated by a primary radiologist and reviewed by a senior radiologist; inter-observer agreement was quantified on a blinded 10% subset (Dice: 0.92 ± 0.03). Six state-of-the-art architectures were trained and evaluated on a fixed test set. Performance was assessed using Intersection over Union (IoU), Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), precision, recall, and F1-score.ResultsAll models achieved high segmentation accuracy, with DSC values ranging from 0.819 to 0.866. The transformer-based RT-DETR model showed the highest numerical DSC (0.866), IoU (0.764), and F1-score (0.866), indicating a balanced overall segmentation profile. Among the one-stage detectors, YOLOv9-Seg provided competitive results (DSC: 0.862) with high recall (0.902), outperforming CNN-based alternatives. YOLOv11-Seg showed high sensitivity but lower precision compared to other architectures.ConclusionsDeep learning enables accurate and automated condylar segmentation on panoramic radiographs. While RT-DETR showed favorable anatomical fidelity for quantitative morphometry, YOLOv9-Seg presented a viable real-time alternative. This study establishes a benchmark for selecting segmentation architectures tailored to specific clinical needs in TMJ analysis.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a uniquely complex articulation responsible for mastication, speech and mandibular functional movements, and the mandibular condyle constitutes its principal osseous component. The condyle transmits occlusal loads to the cranial base and guides mandibular motion within the glenoid fossa; consequently, its cortical outline and trabecular structure reflect lifelong adaptive remodelling influenced by functional loading, development, trauma and degenerative disease. Previous anatomical and radiographic studies have documented substantial inter-individual variation in condylar morphology, with oval or round forms reported as the most prevalent, followed by pointed/bird-beak, angled/diamond and irregular “crooked finger” configurations [1, 2]. Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) -based studies further confirm significant morphological diversity and highlight the relationship between condylar form, age, sex and occlusal patterns [3].

Precise radiographic evaluation of the condyle serves as a cornerstone not only for diagnosing pathologies ranging from TMJ osteoarthritis to developmental anomalies, but also for guiding complex orthodontic and prosthodontic interventions [4]. While CBCT is indisputably the gold standard for high-resolution three-dimensional visualization, its routine application is often precluded by concerns regarding radiation burden and limited accessibility [5]. Conversely, panoramic radiography remains the ubiquitous workhorse of general dental practice; yet, this accessibility comes at a cost. The modality is plagued by inherent projection artefacts—specifically magnification, geometric distortion, and the superimposition of craniofacial structures—which severely compromise manual interpretation and inflate observer variability [2, 3]. Arayapisit et al. [3] notably showed that some panoramic “morphological types,” especially angled condyles, may not be anatomically present on CBCT. This highlights the difficulties of using morphology-based assessment on two-dimensional projections.

While several studies have examined condylar shape classification on panoramic radiographs [1, 2], most rely on expert visual inspection and categorical labelling, limiting reproducibility and scalability. Manual delineation of condylar boundaries is time-consuming, subject to inter-examiner variability and often hindered by partial visibility or blurred cortical margins. These limitations highlight the need for automated, standardised methods capable of accurate boundary localisation and morphological representation [6, 7].

Deep learning has brought transformative progress to dental radiology, especially in detection and classification tasks involving implants, teeth, restorations and anatomical landmarks. YOLO-based detectors, Faster/Mask R-CNN, and contemporary convolutional backbones like EfficientNet and ConvNeXt have demonstrated superior performance across diverse dental imaging challenges [8, 9]. Nonetheless, investigations concerning the mandibular condyle have primarily concentrated on the categorization of shape types, often subsequent to the manual cropping of a region of interest or the utilization of a distinct detection model [7]. These pipelines provide categorical information but do not capture pixel-level morphology, which is essential for quantitative morphometry, asymmetry assessment, and longitudinal structural monitoring.

Semantic segmentation offers significant advantages over classification or bounding-box detection, as it assigns a label to every pixel and generates masks closely approximating the true anatomical contour [6, 10]. Pixel-level delineation is particularly valuable for condylar morphology, given its irregular boundaries, variation in cortical sharpness and frequent superimposition with adjacent structures [2, 11]. Yet, to date, studies have predominantly focused on shape classification or simple detection [7], and no study has systematically compared multiple state-of-the-art segmentation architectures (CNN, Transformer, and YOLO-based) for condylar analysis on panoramic radiographs.

Moreover, several cutting-edge vision architectures have not previously been applied to any dental task. Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) is a recent attention-based model capable of efficient global reasoning and has only recently been adapted for segmentation tasks [12]. Such global context modelling is particularly relevant for panoramic imaging, where local texture cues are often unreliable. Likewise, YOLOv9-Seg and YOLOv11-Seg introduce major architectural refinements, such as Programmable Gradient Information and new backbone/neck designs, but have not yet been evaluated in dental radiology [13, 14]. Consequently, the literature lacks a comprehensive benchmark comparing transformer-based, CNN-based and YOLO-based segmentation frameworks on a unified condyle dataset.

The present study addresses this gap by performing the first multi-architecture comparison of pixel-level mandibular condyle segmentation on panoramic radiographs. Six state-of-the-art models—EfficientNet, Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt, RT-DETR, YOLOv9-Seg and YOLOv11-Seg—were trained and evaluated under identical conditions using a fully annotated condyle dataset. Beyond overlap-based metrics, we provide a detailed analysis incorporating IoU, Dice, ROC–AUC, confusion matrices and precision–recall trade-offs to characterise architectural advantages and clinically relevant error patterns.

Our primary aim is to identify which deep learning paradigm offers the most reliable and clinically meaningful segmentation performance. We hypothesised that: (i) all architectures would achieve high accuracy despite severe class imbalance; (ii) transformer-based RT-DETR would yield the most balanced combination of precision, recall and overlap metrics due to its global attention mechanisms; and (iii) YOLOv9-Seg, as a lightweight one-stage model, would provide competitive segmentation quality with superior computational efficiency, supporting real-time or resource-constrained deployment.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective deep learning study was performed using anonymized panoramic radiographs obtained from institutional clinical archives. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Health Science, Gulhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval ID: 2025/480). All procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with institutional data protection policies. No patient-identifiable information was used, and all radiographs were de-identified prior to analysis. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) guidelines to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting of the deep learning model performance.

Image acquisition and dataset curation

All panoramic radiographs were acquired using the same digital panoramic unit (Planmeca ProMax, Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) at 66 kVp, 8 mA and 15.8 s exposure settings. All examinations were performed by the same experienced radiology technician following a standardized clinical protocol, ensuring consistency in patient positioning and exposure conditions. The maximum tube potential of the system was 66 kVp and the tube current was fixed at 8 mA for all exposures.

All images were exported in jpeg format, ensuring full visibility of the bilateral mandibular condyles. Image evaluation and annotation were performed on the same diagnostic monitor under controlled ambient lighting conditions to minimise display-related variability.

Radiographs showing motion artefacts, truncated anatomy, metallic overlap obscuring the TMJ region, or pathologies visibly altering condylar morphology were excluded. The final dataset consisted of 1300 panoramic radiographs (2600 condyles). Splitting was performed at the radiograph level to prevent information leakage. Both condyles were annotated and evaluated separately, as commonly adopted in comparable dental imaging studies. The dataset was randomly partitioned into 70% training, 15% validation and 15% test subsets, and this split was kept fixed across all experiments to ensure strict comparability of model performance.

Annotation protocol

Annotation platform and guidelines

Pixel-level segmentation masks were created using Makesense.ai, an open-access annotation tool that supports polygon-based mask generation and direct export to COCO and YOLO formats, facilitating seamless integration with the deep learning frameworks used in this study (Mask R-CNN, RT-DETR, and YOLO-Seg). The annotation process adhered to a standardized protocol adapted from established radiological segmentation workflows [7]. Each mandibular condyle was delineated using fine polygonal contours that traced the superior cortical margin, posterior curvature, anterior slope, and medial–lateral extensions. Trabecular regions were included only when cortical boundaries were indistinct. Anatomical structures adjacent to the condyle, such as the articular eminence, glenoid fossa, and mandibular ramus, as well as image artefacts, were strictly excluded from the region of interest (ROI).

All annotations were performed by two experienced dentomaxillofacial radiologists (with > 8 years and > 15 years of experience, respectively). To evaluate the reliability of the manual annotations prior to labeling the entire dataset, an inter-observer variability analysis was conducted. A random selection of 130 panoramic radiographs, constituting about 10% of the entire dataset, was independently segmented by both radiologists under conditions of blindness. The Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) was used to measure how similar the two sets of manual masks were. The analysis produced a mean inter-observer DSC of 0.92 ± 0.03, demonstrating significant consistency in boundary delineation. Following this validation step, the remaining images were annotated by the primary radiologist, and all masks were subsequently reviewed by the second senior radiologist. Any discrepancies in the final dataset were resolved through joint consultation and consensus to establish the definitive ground truth.

Deep learning architectures

Semantic segmentation

Semantic segmentation refers to the process of assigning a meaningful class label to every pixel within an image, aiming to delineate object regions with high precision. Unlike object detection, which approximates location via bounding boxes, segmentation captures the exact morphological contours. This approach is particularly valuable in dental imaging for resolving irregular boundaries and distinguishing anatomical structures from surrounding tissues based on learned spatial and textural patterns [15].

Mask R-CNN

Mask R-CNN is a two-part architecture that can find objects and make pixel-by-pixel mask predictions at the same time. It builds on the Faster R-CNN framework by adding a second branch that is only for making masks. A Region Proposal Network (RPN) creates candidate regions in the first stage. RoIAlign then refines these regions to keep their spatial consistency. In the second stage, it guesses the bounding boxes, class labels, and a binary segmentation mask for each area of interest. Mask R-CNN is a good starting point for pixel-level anatomical segmentation tasks because it is modular and easy to connect to different backbones [16].

ConvNeXt

This version of Mask R-CNN has a ConvNeXt backbone and a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) as its base. ConvNeXt brings a new convolutional design that is based on transformer-era architecture ideas. It uses bigger kernels, simpler residual blocks, and better normalization layers. These changes improve the representation of features while keeping the speed of the calculations. Multi-scale features work well with FPN to combine them, which lets the network capture both fine-grained contours and high-level structural patterns in the targeted anatomical areas. This synergy makes things more stable in situations where shapes change a lot, like condyle morphology [17].

EfficientNet

This setup uses EfficientNet as the feature extractor and compound scaling to find the right balance between network depth, width, and resolution. EfficientNet’s squeeze-and-excitation blocks and optimized convolutions give it a lot of representational power without using a lot of processing power. The architecture makes multi-scale feature maps that are good for Mask R-CNN’s detection and segmentation branches when it is combined with an FPN module. Its efficiency makes it useful for medical imaging datasets where high-resolution inputs are common but computing power may be limited [18].

RT-DETR

RT-DETR-Seg adapts the Real-Time DETR architecture to pixel-wise segmentation tasks. Unlike conventional CNN-based detectors, RT-DETR is built around an attention-driven design that removes the need for handcrafted anchors and non-maximum suppression. The model uses a hybrid encoder to capture long-range dependencies and a decoupled head to improve convergence speed. In the segmentation version, mask prediction is attached to the detection outputs, enabling the network to produce region-level masks with transformer-based contextual reasoning. This approach benefits structures that exhibit irregular geometry or overlap with surrounding tissues, as attention mechanisms allow the model to maintain global coherence [12].

YOLOv9

YOLOv9-Seg is a new version of the YOLO family that adds a segmentation branch to the YOLOv9 detection architecture so that anatomical structures can be defined at the pixel level. The model includes a number of new architectural features, the most important of which are Programmable Gradient Information (PGI) and the Generalized Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (GELAN). Together, these improve gradient flow, feature fusion, and learning at multiple scales. These improvements make YOLOv9-Seg more accurate while still being efficient with computer resources, which means it can be used in real time on systems with limited resources. The lightweight segmentation head makes it possible to get high-quality class-specific masks without slowing down the inference speed. This makes YOLOv9-Seg a good choice for biomedical image segmentation tasks where accuracy and speed are very important [13].

YOLOv11

YOLOv11-Seg is an extension of the YOLOv11 detection framework that adds a segmentation output to make masks for each class. YOLOv11 adds architectural improvements like better backbone blocks, dynamic spatial feature alignment, and better neck structures that are made for fast inference. The segmentation head uses lightweight convolutional modules to keep performance up to date while making masks that are as good as the best. This means that YOLOv11-Seg is good for situations where you need fast and reliable segmentation, especially when you want to use models on edge or resource-limited devices [14].

Training environment and model optimization

All models were trained on the same dedicated workstation equipped with an Intel Core i9-12900 K (12th Gen) CPU, 64 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3080 Ti GPU (12 GB). To ensure strict comparability, all architectures were trained using identical input resolution, batch size, preprocessing pipeline and regularization settings.

EfficientNet was trained for 300 epochs and reached a minimum validation loss of 0.504 at epoch 11, after which the loss plateaued without signs of overfitting. For Mask R-CNN, model convergence was monitored through regional proposal and mask branch losses separately, and the final weights were selected based on the highest validation mAP. ConvNeXt was trained within a detection-oriented framework that reports precision, recall and average precision (AP) rather than scalar loss values.

For detection-based architectures (RT-DETR, YOLOv9 and YOLOv11), performance was reported using COCO-style AP₅₀:₉₅ metrics instead of validation loss. To enable unified cross-model comparison, a validation-error proxy (1 − AP₅₀:₉₅) was calculated, yielding values of 0.604 for RT-DETR, 0.574 for YOLOv9, and 0.565 for YOLOv11. These proxy errors reflect the increased complexity of simultaneous localization and segmentation relative to classification-based models.

Evaluation metrics

Segmentation performance was evaluated using standard pixel-wise binary classification metrics widely adopted in the semantic segmentation literature. Confusion matrices were constructed based on true-positive (TP), false-positive (FP), false-negative (FN), and true-negative (TN) pixel counts, from which accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity, and F1-score were derived. To specifically assess boundary agreement between predicted masks and ground truth, Intersection over Union (IoU) and the Dice coefficient were computed, as these metrics directly quantify spatial overlap and are particularly relevant for medical image segmentation [19]. In this study, binary prediction masks were generated using a predefined threshold, and these metrics were calculated separately for each test image; the per-image results (Mean ± SD) were reported to provide a more detailed evaluation of segmentation performance across the dataset. In addition, pixel-level Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were calculated to evaluate threshold-independent discriminative performance. For each test image, the segmentation models produced instance-wise mask probability maps. These probabilities were multiplied by the corresponding detection confidence scores, and the maximum value across overlapping instances was assigned to each pixel to form a continuous-valued prediction score map. Ground-truth masks were binarized as background (0) and target region (1). Both the ground-truth masks and predicted score maps were flattened over all pixels in the test set to obtain the final pixel-wise ROC-AUC values. This multi-metric evaluation strategy enabled a comprehensive assessment of segmentation accuracy, overlap quality, and robustness to class imbalance.

Comparative evaluation framework

To ensure fair comparison across architectures, all models were evaluated using identical training, validation and test partitions. Confusion matrices were generated for both the condyle and background classes. IoU, Dice and F1 scores were used to characterise boundary localisation accuracy, while ROC–AUC assessed robustness across varying decision thresholds. Analysis of false-positive and false-negative patterns provided clinically relevant insight into over- and under-segmentation tendencies, enabling a rigorous head-to-head comparison of CNN-, transformer- and YOLO-based architectures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v28.0. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Since the metric distributions deviated from normality, non-parametric tests were employed. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to determine whether the differences in Dice, IoU, Precision and Recall scores between the best-performing model and the other architectures were statistically significant. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pixel-wise confusion matrices were generated for all six deep learning architectures—EfficientNet, Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt, RT-DETR, YOLOv9 and YOLOv11—using the same test dataset and a unified preprocessing protocol. Given the strong class imbalance inherent to condylar segmentation, confusion matrices provide more informative insight than accuracy alone, allowing detailed assessment of false-positive and false-negative patterns and boundary localisation behaviour. Despite the overwhelming prevalence of background (BG) pixels, the models display substantial differences in the distribution of false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN), which represent the most diagnostically relevant errors. EfficientNet showed a recall-oriented behaviour, capturing a substantial proportion of condyle pixels while maintaining relatively low FP rates, indicating good sensitivity to condylar morphology. Mask R-CNN yielded the most conservative segmentation boundaries, reflected by minimal FP values but increased FN counts, suggesting under-segmentation in regions with reduced contrast. ConvNeXt achieved high condyle sensitivity with stable FP–FN balance, consistent with its large-kernel, context-aware convolutional design.

RT-DETR exhibited a well-balanced confusion matrix, with moderate FP and FN values and consistent condyle localisation. As a transformer-based approach, it effectively reduced fragmented predictions and preserved boundary coherence. YOLOv9 showed a more recall-oriented pattern, identifying a broader condylar region but generating higher FP, whereas YOLOv11 produced cleaner, more conservative masks with reduced FP and slightly lower recall.

Across all architectures, FN regions were primarily located near superior and posterior cortical boundaries, whereas FP activations commonly occurred in trabecular regions with condyle-like textures.

Although accuracy values are uniformly high for all models (0.996–0.997), this metric is largely dominated by the overwhelming proportion of background pixels. Consequently, more informative insights are obtained from precision, recall and their derived measures (F1, IoU, Dice), which better reflect how each architecture handles the much rarer condyle class.

A review of the overall metrics in Table 1 shows that all models operate within a consistently high-performance range, with precision values spanning 0.754–0.865, recall values between 0.801 and 0.905 and F1 scores clustering around 0.819–0.866. This indicates that even the comparatively weaker architecture performs well in condyle segmentation, while the differences between models mainly reflect architectural tendencies rather than fundamental capability gaps. Among them, RT-DETR yielded the highest numerical F1, IoU, and Dice values, suggesting a favorable balance between precision and recall. YOLOv9 follows closely as a strong one-stage alternative, whereas Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt and EfficientNet form a central group with similar harmonic performance but differing in how they trade off false positives against false negatives. YOLOv11 yields slightly lower F1 and IoU scores yet remains within a level that is still suitable for clinical interpretation. Across all architectures, specificity remains exceptionally high (0.997–0.999), which is consistent with the heavy dominance of background pixels and reflects the models’ reliable suppression of erroneous condyle predictions.

Precision and recall provide complementary insights into how each model approaches the condyle segmentation task. Precision describes how reliably predicted condyle pixels correspond to the true anatomy, whereas recall reflects how comprehensively the model captures all true condyle pixels. Based on Table 1, Mask R-CNN (0.865), EfficientNet (0.864) and RT-DETR (0.859) emerge as the most precision-oriented architectures, producing fewer false-positive predictions and therefore yielding cleaner, more conservative segmentation boundaries. This outcome aligns well with their architectural principles: Mask R-CNN relies on a two-stage mechanism that prioritizes high-confidence region proposals; EfficientNet benefits from stable hierarchical convolutional features; and RT-DETR suppresses noisy activations through its transformer-based global attention structure. In contrast, ConvNeXt (recall = 0.905) and YOLOv9 (0.902) clearly behave as recall-oriented models, successfully identifying a larger portion of true condyle pixels—an advantage in applications where missing even small anatomical regions is unacceptable. Their moderately lower precision values (0.771 and 0.825, respectively) suggest a slight tendency toward over-segmentation in ambiguous texture regions. YOLOv11 exhibits a similar trend with high recall (0.897) but the lowest precision (0.754), reflecting a more liberal activation of the positive class. From a practical standpoint, the choice between precision- and recall-oriented models must be guided by the clinical context: recall-dominant models such as ConvNeXt or YOLOv9 may be preferred for screening tasks to avoid missing pathology, while precision-oriented models like Mask R-CNN, EfficientNet or RT-DETR are better suited for quantitative assessments where over-segmentation could distort volumetric or shape-based measurements.

The combined behaviour of precision and recall is best captured through aggregated measures such as the F1 score, IoU and Dice coefficient, which summarize overall segmentation quality. RT-DETR achieved the highest numerical values across all three metrics (F1 = 0.866, IoU = 0.764, Dice = 0.866), indicating a favorable overall balance between precision and recall in this comparison. YOLOv9 closely follows with F1 = 0.862, IoU = 0.757 and Dice = 0.862, demonstrating that a well-optimized one-stage detector can rival transformer-based approaches in pixel-level accuracy. Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt and EfficientNet cluster tightly together with F1 scores between 0.831 and 0.834 and IoU values between 0.711 and 0.714, showing that despite substantial architectural differences, these models converge to a similar level of segmentation fidelity. YOLOv11 performs slightly below this cluster (F1 = 0.819, IoU = 0.694), yet still maintains a robust Dice score above 0.81, indicating reliable performance even if slightly less precise or complete than the higher-ranking models. The near-equivalence of F1 and Dice values across all architectures reflects the binary nature of the evaluation and confirms the internal consistency of the segmentation metrics.

The performance differences observed across Table 1 can be understood more clearly when viewed through the architectural philosophies underlying each model family. CNN-based classifiers such as EfficientNet and ConvNeXt exhibit distinct tendencies: EfficientNet emphasizes precision with moderate recall, resulting in stable but slightly conservative segmentation outcomes, whereas ConvNeXt—with its large kernels and hierarchical design—leans toward maximizing recall, enabling the detection of subtle anatomical structures at the expense of minimal over-segmentation. Mask R-CNN, as a two-stage detector, displays a high-confidence decision-making process; its precision-dominant behaviour stems from its selective activation of positive regions and inherently stricter segmentation thresholds. RT-DETR, the transformer-based architecture, demonstrates the most balanced profile, maintaining both high precision (0.859) and high recall (0.873). Its superior F1, IoU and Dice values highlight its ability to leverage global attention mechanisms to maintain anatomical coherence and reduce fragmented predictions. YOLOv9 and YOLOv11, representing one-stage detection paradigms, show complementary strengths: YOLOv9 combines strong recall with solid precision, making it suitable for real-time segmentation tasks requiring both sensitivity and efficiency, while YOLOv11 displays competitive recall but somewhat reduced precision, suggesting potential benefits from additional tuning of thresholds or loss weights.

Overall, the segmentation metrics presented in Table 1 illustrate that although all models achieve high performance, they differ meaningfully in how they balance precision and recall. RT-DETR showed a favorable overall compromise and may be considered a strong candidate for automated condyle analysis workflows. ConvNeXt and YOLOv9 are particularly suitable when maximal sensitivity is desired, such as in early detection or exploratory analyses where missing anatomical details may be detrimental. In contrast, Mask R-CNN and EfficientNet are advantageous in tasks where minimizing false positives is critical, including semi-automatic segmentation pipelines where radiologists refine model-generated contours. Although YOLOv11 performs slightly behind other architectures numerically, its high recall and comparatively simple deployment make it an attractive option for resource-constrained or real-time environments. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that no single architecture outperforms all others across every metric; instead, each model offers a distinct and clinically relevant balance of strengths. This reinforces the importance of aligning model selection with the specific diagnostic or operational requirements of the condylar segmentation task.

ROC curve and AUC analysis of deep learning models

The ROC curves in Fig. 2 provide a comparative view of how each model balances sensitivity and false-positive tolerance across different decision thresholds. Although all six architectures produce curves that lie well above the diagonal reference line—indicating performance substantially better than random classification—their shapes and AUC values highlight meaningful distinctions in how they respond to varying levels of classification strictness. Models such as Mask R-CNN and ConvNeXt exhibit sharply rising ROC curves with minimal horizontal spread, reflecting strong separation between true condyle pixels and background predictions. Their AUC scores (0.978 and 0.985, respectively) confirm a highly reliable discrimination capability, consistent with their high precision values observed in earlier metric tables.

RT-DETR and YOLOv11 demonstrate similarly stable ROC profiles, showing steep initial rise and gradual saturation toward the upper right of the graph. EfficientNet presents the lowest AUC value (0.810), with a noticeably flatter ROC shape compared to transformer- and detection-based architectures. Across all models, the high AUC values (0.810–0.985) collectively demonstrate that each architecture possesses strong discriminatory power for the condyle segmentation task. The differences observed are consistent with the architectural philosophies of the models: two-stage detectors and large-kernel CNNs deliver the cleanest decision boundaries; transformer-based methods provide balanced, context-aware separation; and one-stage YOLO variants achieve competitive results while maintaining computational efficiency. These findings reinforce the notion that while all evaluated models are suitable for condyle segmentation, the optimal choice depends on whether the intended application prioritizes stability across thresholds, maximal sensitivity, or operational simplicity. It should be noted that ROC-AUC reflects threshold-independent pixel-level discrimination performance and should not be interpreted as a direct substitute for anatomical overlap measures such as Dice or IoU.

Discussion

This study presents the first multi-architecture comparison of state-of-the-art deep learning models—including EfficientNet, Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt, RT-DETR, YOLOv9-Seg and YOLOv11-Seg—for pixel-level segmentation of the mandibular condyle on panoramic radiographs. Across all evaluated architectures, high segmentation performance was achieved, confirming that deep learning can reliably delineate condylar anatomy on panoramic images despite inherent projection-related limitations. More importantly, the results reveal meaningful architectural differences with direct implications for clinical and research-oriented TMJ applications.

Previous deep learning studies on the mandibular condyle have predominantly focused on shape classification rather than direct boundary delineation [7]. Abd Elaziz et al. [7] employed YOLOv8-based region extraction followed by MobileViT classification, achieving high categorical accuracy but without generating pixel-level anatomical masks. Similarly, large-scale panoramic studies targeting dental implants or other landmarks [8, 9], such as the work of Leblebicioğlu Kurtuluş et al. [9], relied on detection or classification paradigms and did not address condylar morphology. Accordingly, no prior study has systematically compared multiple segmentation architectures for direct condylar delineation on panoramic radiographs, nor evaluated transformer-based or next-generation YOLO segmentation frameworks in dental imaging. The present study fills this gap by establishing the first benchmark for condyle-level semantic segmentation across CNN-, transformer- and YOLO-based paradigms.

RT-DETR yielded the highest numerical overlap metrics and showed a balanced segmentation profile, achieving the highest F1, IoU, and Dice values. This behavior is consistent with transformer architectures’ ability to integrate global contextual information, which is particularly advantageous in the TMJ region where cortical boundaries are often indistinct due to radiographic blur or overlapping anatomy. The smooth and continuous masks produced by RT-DETR support its suitability for quantitative morphometric analysis and longitudinal monitoring, where boundary coherence is critical. Our statistical analysis further supported these findings; the Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed that RT-DETR achieved significantly higher Dice and IoU values than EfficientNet, ConvNeXt, and YOLOv11-Seg, whereas no significant difference was observed relative to YOLOv9-Seg or Mask R-CNN for these overlap metrics. However, the lack of statistical significance compared to YOLOv9-Seg (p = 0.178) and Mask R-CNN (p = 0.734) in Dice scores suggests a potential performance ceiling for segmentation tasks on 2D panoramic radiographs. In such instances, model selection should be guided by specific clinical needs rather than overlap metrics alone. For example, the significantly higher precision of RT-DETR compared to YOLO variants (p < 0.001) underscores its utility in minimizing over-segmentation, a crucial requirement for reliable condylar morphometry and asymmetry assessment.

YOLOv9-Seg closely followed RT-DETR, outperforming all CNN-based models while maintaining a one-stage, real-time architecture. Its recall-oriented behaviour enabled more comprehensive capture of condylar regions, albeit with moderately increased false-positive activation. Such a trade-off may be acceptable in screening or AI-assisted triage scenarios, where sensitivity is prioritised over conservative boundary definition.

Mask R-CNN, ConvNeXt and EfficientNet formed a stable mid-range performance group with comparable harmonic metrics but distinct error tendencies. Mask R-CNN exhibited the highest precision, producing conservative segmentation boundaries with minimal false positives, consistent with its two-stage region proposal mechanism. EfficientNet demonstrated similarly high precision with moderate recall, whereas ConvNeXt maximised recall through its large-kernel, context-aware convolutional design. Collectively, these models represent reliable alternatives when interpretability or architectural simplicity is prioritised.

YOLOv11 achieved slightly lower IoU and Dice scores, primarily due to reduced precision and mild over-segmentation. Nevertheless, its recall remained high and qualitative outputs preserved anatomically plausible contours. Given its compact architecture and inference efficiency, YOLOv11 remains a promising option for edge-device deployment, chairside applications or integration into imaging hardware.

Across all architectures, segmentation errors were consistently concentrated along the posterior–superior border of the condyle, where panoramic projection effects tend to obscure or distort cortical bone. Similar discrepancies have been reported in CBCT–panoramic comparison studies, which demonstrated that certain panoramic morphology patterns do not correspond to true anatomical configurations [2, 3]. These findings indicate that segmentation errors predominantly reflect intrinsic imaging limitations rather than algorithmic deficiencies. Notably, enhanced segmentation architectures cannot fully compensate for areas of poor radiographic clarity, underscoring the importance of modality-specific training rather than transferring assumptions from CBCT-based morphology. ROC-AUC reflects pixel-level discrimination and should not be interpreted as a direct substitute for Dice or IoU, which more directly quantify anatomical boundary agreement between predicted and ground-truth segmentation masks.

Accurate pixel-level condylar segmentation enables applications that extend beyond categorical shape classification. Automated extraction of morphometric parameters facilitates growth analysis and asymmetry assessment, supporting early detection of condylar hyperplasia, hypoplasia or functional remodeling. In addition, segmentation outputs may serve as a foundation for TMJ screening tools, orthodontic and orthognathic planning, and large-scale epidemiological studies where manual annotation is impractical. With real-time inference capability—particularly in YOLO-based architectures—automated condyle segmentation could be integrated into PACS systems, orthodontic software or AI-assisted diagnostic workflows.

This study provides several novel contributions to dental artificial intelligence research. First, it represents the first head-to-head comparison of multiple deep learning architectures for pixel-level condyle segmentation. Second, it introduces RT-DETR, YOLOv9-Seg and YOLOv11-Seg to dental radiology for the first time. Third, the use of a standardised dataset with identical train–validation–test splits ensured a rigorous and unbiased comparison. Finally, the combined quantitative and qualitative evaluation offers practical guidance for selecting architectures tailored to specific clinical and operational requirements.

Despite the strong performance observed, several limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset was derived from a single institution and imaging system, which may limit generalisability. Inherent panoramic projection artefacts constrain segmentation accuracy in regions with poor cortical definition. Furthermore, the models were trained primarily on clinically normal condyles, and performance in pathological cases remains to be explored. Although statistical comparisons were performed for key overlap and classification metrics, the analysis was limited to pairwise comparisons with RT-DETR and did not include external validation on an independent multi-center dataset. In addition, ROC-AUC values reflect pixel-level discrimination performance and should not be interpreted as a direct substitute for boundary agreement metrics such as Dice or IoU. Finally, the absence of CBCT-based anatomical ground truth limits direct validation of projection-related discrepancies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that deep learning enables accurate and fully automated segmentation of the mandibular condyle on panoramic radiographs. Among the evaluated architectures, the transformer-based RT-DETR showed a numerically favorable and anatomically coherent performance profile, while one-stage YOLO models—particularly YOLOv9-Seg—provided competitive accuracy with real-time feasibility. By providing the first multi-architecture benchmark for pixel-level condylar segmentation, this work establishes a reference framework for selecting deep learning models tailored to specific clinical and operational requirements. These findings support the integration of automated condyle segmentation into workflows for TMJ screening, morphometric analysis, asymmetry assessment and large-scale epidemiological research. Overall, this study lays the foundation for robust, scalable and clinically applicable AI-driven condylar analysis in both research and routine dental practice.
