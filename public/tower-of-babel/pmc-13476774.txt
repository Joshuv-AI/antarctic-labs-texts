Mosaic Loss of Y Chromosome and Risks of Peripheral Arterial and Aortic Diseases
Authors: Zhiwei Zeng, Yuanming Li, Zhongchen Li, Hanning Liu, Bowang Chen, Hao Wang, Qinglong Meng, Runze Chen, Ling Jin, Chunyu Yu, Xueying Wang, Jinhua Chen, Xi Li, Zhe Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMosaic loss of Y chromosome (mLOY) is the most prevalent somatic mutation in males associated with aging.ObjectivesThis study aimed to test the association of mLOY with new-onset peripheral arterial and aortic diseases (PAAD).MethodsMales from the UK Biobank with available genotyping data were analyzed. The Mosaic Chromosomal Alterations pipeline was used to detect mLOY. The multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were fitted to investigate the association of mLOY with incident peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD). Multiplicative and additive interaction between mLOY and smoking status on the risk of incident PAD and AAD was further evaluated.ResultsThis study included 216,086 participants (mLOY: n = 43,458 [20.1%]). Over a median follow-up period of 13.7 years (IQR: 12.9-14.5), individuals with mLOY exhibited a significantly higher risk of both PAD (adjusted HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.10-1.24) and abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) (adjusted HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.14-1.38). Notably, although mLOY was not significantly associated with increased risks of either PAD or AAA among never-smokers, these associations were most pronounced in former smokers (P for multiplicative interaction <0.001). Furthermore, smoking status and mLOY synergistically increased the risk of both PAD and AAA (P for additive interaction <0.05).ConclusionsmLOY is a novel PAAD-related risk factor. Individuals with mLOY are strongly advised to avoid or quit smoking to reduce the risk of PAAD.

Peripheral arterial and aortic diseases (PAAD) are major contributors to morbidity and mortality, imposing substantial health care and socioeconomic burdens.1 Globally, peripheral arterial disease (PAD) affects over 113 million individuals, whereas aortic diseases, particularly aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD), occur in 1% to 3% of the population, with aneurysm rupture carrying a mortality rate exceeding 50%.2,3 For both conditions, surgical intervention remains the primary treatment for advanced stages; however, it is associated with high costs and significant perioperative risks.1 Therefore, identifying novel risk factors is crucial for early intervention and prevention in high-risk populations. Emerging evidence highlights that genetic predisposition contributes to PAAD, underscoring the need for further research.4,5

Mosaic loss of Y chromosome (mLOY), defined as the loss of the entire Y chromosome in blood cells, is the most frequent somatic mutation in men, with a prevalence of >20% in those over 60 years of age.6, 7, 8 The biological significance of mLOY was historically underappreciated due to the old paradigm of the Y chromosome as a “genetic wasteland”, with its perceived role limited to testicular development.9,10 However, emerging evidence has challenged this misconception, revealing the broader implications of mLOY in hematological and multiple solid cancers, and Alzheimer disease.8,11,12 In the field of cardiovascular diseases, mLOY has been associated with heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and adverse outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease, or in those undergoing coronary angiography or transcatheter aortic valve replacement.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Nevertheless, its relationship with PAAD, which is also strongly associated with aging, remains unclear.5,18 Notably, smoking is recognized not only as the second major risk factor for mLOY, but also as a well-established contributor to the development and progression of PAAD.1,19,20 Previous studies have demonstrated that smoking behaviors can modify the impact of mLOY on lung diseases.21 Building on this precedent, the dual association of smoking with both mLOY and PAAD raises important questions regarding their potential synergistic interactions in driving PAAD development.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive investigation leveraging large-scale prospective cohort data from the UK Biobank (UKB). Our study aimed to examine the associations of mLOY with incident PAD and AAD, and to evaluate the potential modifying effect of smoking on these associations.

Methods

Study participants

The UKB is a large prospective population-based cohort study recruiting over 500,000 adults aged 40 to 69 years from 22 centers across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. It has collected comprehensive information, including physical examinations, sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and biological samples (Supplemental Methods 1).22

Of the 488,377 participants with available baseline array genotyping data, we restricted our analysis to individuals whose self-reported sex matched their genetic sex as male (n = 223,193). Participants were further excluded if they met any of the following criteria: 1) prevalent PAD or AAD at baseline (n = 2,599); 2) substandard genotyping quality (missingness >1.5%, abnormal heterozygosity, or autosomal phase B allele frequency autocorrelation >0.03; n = 2,198); 3) sex chromosome aneuploidy (n = 188); 4) history of hematological malignancies, to prevent blood-derived tumor clones from confounding the accurate detection of mCAs (n = 999); 5) ever received chemotherapy or radiotherapy (n = 550); or 6) lack of complete follow-up data (n = 573). A total of 216,086 male participants were finally included in following analysis. Detection of mLOY

Raw genotyping array intensity data were used to calculate B-allele frequencies (BAFs) and log2 R ratios, which were then analyzed using ukb2txt and mosaic chromosomal alterations (MoChA) pipeline to detect mLOY.12,23,24 MoChA was pseudoautosomal region 1–based mLOY screening pipeline,8 which employed Illumina Gencall algorithm for raw intensity normalization and genotype calling, followed by the conversion of Illumina genotyping data to the Variant Call Format (VCF) using bcftools. SHAPEIT4 software was used for phasing to infer haplotypes.25 To ensure high sample quality, those with genotyping call rate <0.97 or baf_auto >0.03, and genotype-phenotype sex discordance were excluded. We identified male participants harboring mLOY based on the established criteria, including a minimum event size >2 MB and a relative copy number <2.5 in pseudoautosomal region 1. The proportion of cells with mLOY, namely mosaic cell fraction (mCF), was estimated using BAF values and BAF deviation.

Covariates assessment

All covariates were collected at baseline. Age was determined based on the year and month of birth of each participant and the recruitment time of cohorts. Body mass index (BMI) was measured by weight and height. Sociodemographic information included ethnicity (White, Asian, Black, mixed, and others), Townsend deprivation index, annual household income (< or ≥£31,000), and education background (having a college/university degree or not). Lifestyle factors included smoking status, smoking intensity, alcohol consumption (gram/day), physical activity, and diet score.

Smoking intensity was quantified as the average lifetime smoking exposure, calculated by dividing the cumulative pack-years by the number of years elapsed between age 16 and the age at recruitment. Alcohol consumption was calculated using the consumption frequency and the standard alcohol content across different beverage types.27 Participants were identified as having ideal physical activity if they reported ≥150 min/week of moderate activity, ≥75 min/week of vigorous activity, or 150 min/week of combined activity.28 The diet score was evaluated by the consumption of fruits, vegetables, fish, red meat, and processed meat.27 Information on medications (lipid-lowering, antidiabetic, and antihypertensive medication) was collected through standardized touchscreen questionnaires or verbal interviews. We also assessed clinical comorbidities and medical history, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, and cancer. Blood pressure was collected by physical measurements. Air pollution, including PM2.5 and PM10 was estimated according to residential address. All laboratory biomarkers were determined at enrollment. Detailed information for covariates is provided in Supplemental Methods and Supplemental Tables 1 to 3.

Ascertainment of outcomes

Incident PAD and AAD cases were identified through multiple data sources, including inpatient and outpatient records, death registries, and linkages to local medical systems, which were defined using International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) codes, ICD-9 codes, and Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures version 4 codes (Supplemental Table 4).29 AAD was further stratified into thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA), abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), and aortic dissection (AD). Follow-up time was calculated from baseline assessment until PAD or AAD diagnosis, death, or the censoring date (December 12, 2022), whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

The proportion of missing data for each covariate is detailed in Supplemental Table 5. Missing values were imputed using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations approach with a random forest algorithm.30 The imputation models incorporated all covariates and the outcome variables. To minimize imputation-related uncertainty, we generated 25 independent data sets and pooled the subsequent model estimates according to Rubin rules. Baseline characteristics of the pooled imputed cohort were highly comparable to those of the complete-case sample (Supplemental Table 6). Baseline characteristics were expressed as median (IQR: 25th–75th percentiles) for continuous data and as numbers (percentages) for categorical data. Variables in 2 groups were compared by Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate.

The associations of mLOY with the risk of incident PAD and AAD were determined by Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed for all outcomes, with individuals without mLOY serving as the reference group. The results were presented as HRs and 95% CIs. Two multivariable adjusted models were fitted. Model 1 fitted with only age and age2; whereas model 2 further incorporated ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, household income, education level, smoking status, smoking intensity, drinking gram, physical activity, diet score, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, cancer, antihypertension medication, cholesterol-lowering medication, antidiabetic medication, C-reactive protein, PM2.5, PM10, and the first 10 primary components of ancestry. The proportional hazards assumption was verified by visual inspection of scaled Schoenfeld residuals plotted against follow-up time, revealing no significant violations for any of the evaluated outcomes. Furthermore, for outcomes that demonstrated statistically significant positive associations in the primary Cox analyses (specifically, incident PAD and AAA), we constructed restricted cubic spline models to investigate potential nonlinear relationships with mCF. These models were adjusted for the same covariates included in model 2. To flexibly model these relationships while minimizing the risk of overfitting, we prespecified 3 knots (located at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the mCF distribution) for both incident PAD and AAA.

We tested whether smoking status modified the associations of mLOY and risk of incident PAAD using a log-likelihood ratio test to compare models with and without cross-product interaction terms. To further explore the additive interaction between smoking status and mLOY, individuals who never smoked and did not harbor mLOY were set as reference. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion (AP) were assessed by interaction R package.

To validate the robustness of our results, sensitivity analyses were conducted by: 1) utilizing the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for the potential impact of mortality as a competing event; 2) excluding participants with baseline cancer; 3) excluding participants with PAD or AAD events occurring within the first year of follow-up to mitigate any potential effect of reverse causality; 4) imputing missing continuous and categorical data using median values and a “missing” indicator, respectively, following the previous study30; 5) restricting the follow-up duration to the first 5 years to minimize potential exposure misclassification arising from the dynamic nature of mLOY over the long-term follow-up. Subgroup analyses were further performed by stratifying age (<60 vs ≥60), overweight/obesity (BMI: <25 kg/m2 vs ≥ 25 kg/m2), hypertension (no vs yes), and diabetes (no vs yes).

To investigate the potential causal relationships between mLOY and the risks of PAD and AAA, we performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using the TwoSampleMR R package.31 We utilized 156 independent mLOY-associated variants as genetic instruments. For the clinical outcomes, summary-level statistics were obtained from the latest data release (R10) of the FinnGen study, comprising 2,489 cases for PAD and 3,869 cases for AAA, both compared against 381,977 controls. The inverse variance-weighted method was employed as the primary analytical approach. Furthermore, to rigorously account for potential horizontal pleiotropy and instrumental outliers under varying assumptions, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted utilizing the weighted median, MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO methods.

Statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline participant characteristics

The final study comprised 216,086 males, with a median age of 58.6 (IQR: 50.6-64.0) years. A total of 43,458 (20.1%) were identified as mLOY carriers. The distribution of mLOY by age groups was shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Compared with the non-mLOY group, individuals in the mLOY group were significantly older (median age, 63.5 [IQR: 59.4-66.8] vs 56.6 [IQR: 49.1-62.7] years). They were also more likely to be White and less deprived yet had lower household income and education levels. Furthermore, the mLOY group exhibited higher proportions of both former and current smokers. In the unadjusted analysis, mLOY was robustly associated with increased risks of both PAD (HR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.92-2.17) and AAD (HR: 2.19; 95% CI: 2.03-2.35). When evaluating AAD subtypes, significant crude associations were similarly observed for TAA (HR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.11-1.55), AAA (HR: 2.78; 95% CI: 2.54-3.04), and AD (HR: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.16-2.04). After initial adjustment for age and age2 (model 1), the effect sizes were substantially attenuated; although PAD, AAD, and AAA remained highly significant, the associations for TAA and AD lost statistical significance. In the fully multivariable-adjusted model (model 2), mLOY maintained significant independent associations with incident PAD (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.10-1.24) and overall AAD (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.07-1.25). Notably, subtype analysis revealed that the association with AAD was predominantly driven by AAA (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.14-1.38), whereas no significant associations were observed for TAA (HR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.79-1.11) or AD (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.77-1.39) (Table 2).

To further elucidate the dose-response relationship between mCF and disease risks, we categorized the study population into five groups based on the mCF distribution. Compared to non-mLOY carriers, individuals with low-level mLOY (mCF <5%) did not exhibit significantly increased risks for either PAD (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.92-1.16) or AAA (HR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.84-1.19) in the fully adjusted models. However, the risks became statistically significant when mCF reached 5% and generally escalated with higher mCF burdens. For incident PAD, the HRs were 1.14 (95% CI: 1.04-1.25), 1.22 (95% CI: 1.09-1.36), and 1.41 (95% CI: 1.25-1.60) for mCF ranges of 5% to 10%, 10% to 20%, and ≥20%, respectively (Figure 4A). A similar elevated risk pattern was observed for AAA across the corresponding higher mCF strata (Figure 4B). Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analyses revealed significant nonlinear associations of continuous mCF with both PAD (P for nonlinearity = 0.007) (Figure 4C) and AAA (P for nonlinearity <0.001) (Figure 4D) risks. No significant associations were observed across any of the stratified mCF groups for TAA and AD (Supplemental Figure 2). The robustness of results was also confirmed through multiple sensitivity analyses (Supplemental Tables 7 to 11). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale prospective study to demonstrate robust associations between mLOY and the incident risk of PAD and AAD. Subtype analysis further revealed that the elevated AAD risk is predominantly driven by AAA. Furthermore, we observed a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship, where the risks of PAD and AAA became evident only with the mCF exceeding a 5% threshold. Notably, smoking history and mLOY exhibited a synergistic joint association with disease development. The Y chromosome has long been considered a "genetic wasteland" due to its primary role in sex determination. Consequently, mLOY, the most common age-related somatic mutation, was traditionally viewed as a benign byproduct of aging.10 However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that mLOY serves as an independent risk factor for multiple conditions, including atrial fibrillation, heart failure, carotid plaque, Alzheimer disease, and Parkinson disease.11,15,16,32,33 In addition, mLOY predicts long-term mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement or angiographic procedures, as well as in those with chronic kidney disease.13,14,17 The present study extends mLOY research into PAAD, revealing it as a novel genetic risk factor. Importantly, we identified a significant dose-response relationship between mCF and the risks of both PAD and AAA. This association remained robust independent of major established risk factors such as age and smoking, suggesting that mLOY is an independent risk factor for both PAD and AAA.

Our findings offer a crucial methodological clarification regarding previous phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS). Although Lin et al comprehensively analyzed over 1,140 ICD-10-coded phenotypes and reported associations between mLOY and prevalent PAD and AAD, they failed to identify significant longitudinal associations with incident cases. This discrepancy regarding incident risk is likely attributable to methodological differences in control group definitions. Specifically, to streamline their high-throughput PheWAS pipeline, Lin et al included individuals with pre-existing target diseases in their control pool. This approach likely inflated the mLOY frequency within the control group, thereby attenuating the relative hazard estimates for incident events.5,18 Furthermore, when we replicated the case-control definitions by Lin et al within our pipeline, the reanalysis actually yielded nominally significant associations for both incident PAD and AAD (data not shown). The apparent discrepancy fundamentally stems from the statistical thresholding inherent to PheWAS designs. Because Lin et al tested 1,140 diseases simultaneously, they necessitated a highly stringent Bonferroni correction. Consequently, these nominal P values did not satisfy the adjusted threshold, causing true cardiovascular signals to be statistically masked by the severe multiple-testing penalty. In contrast, our hypothesis-driven study focused specifically on targeted vascular outcomes. Moreover, our in-depth analyses captured critical clinical nuances often obscured in broad PheWAS approaches. First, when stratifying by mCF burden, the association with incident PAD in the highest mCF stratum (mCF ≥20%) was sufficiently robust to withstand even stringent multiple-testing corrections. Second, although Lin et al analyzed AAD as a single composite endpoint, our subclassification demonstrated that the risk of AAD is predominantly driven by AAA, rather than TAA or AD. Combining these etiologically distinct subtypes into a single broad phenotype inevitably dilutes the specific pathophysiological signal of AAA.18

The specific impact of mLOY across the broader cardiovascular disease spectrum remains complex and somewhat inconsistent in current literature. For instance, although myocardial infarction and ischemic heart disease share common atherosclerotic mechanisms with PAD and AAA, previous findings are conflicting. Lim et al16 reported no significant associations between mLOY and coronary artery diseases in the UKB, whereas Hussain et al26 observed a 68% higher risk of incident myocardial infarction among older men with high-burden mLOY in the ASPREE cohort, alongside null findings for ischemic stroke. These discrepancies across coronary and cerebral arterial beds highlight the need to determine whether mLOY selectively associates with specific vascular territories. In this study, we demonstrated that the association between mLOY and overall AAD was almost entirely attributable to AAA. Clinically, although TAA and AD are primarily linked to hypertension or genetic connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan and Ehlers-Danlos syndromes), atherosclerosis represents the core etiology for AAA.1,34 Together with our robust findings for PAD, this site-specific aortic association strongly suggests a potential mechanistic link between mLOY and the progression of atherosclerosis in peripheral and abdominal arteries.

Furthermore, our study revealed a significant modifying effect of smoking status on the association between mLOY and PAD/AAA risk. This aligns with findings by Sun et al35, who demonstrated that the predictive value of mosaic chromosomal alterations for cardiovascular mortality was predominantly observed in former smokers. Our subsequent additive interaction analysis indicated a pronounced synergistic effect between smoking and mLOY concerning the risk of incident PAD and AAA. Mechanistically, tobacco smoke is rich in mutagens that directly escalate the DNA damage burden and chromosomal breakage frequency in hematopoietic stem cells, thereby inducing and accelerating the emergence and clonal expansion of mLOY.19,36,37 Moreover, smoking leaves a lasting imprint on the immune system. Even after smoking cessation, the remodeling of the adaptive immune system induced by a history of smoking can persist long-term through epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation.38 We hypothesize that several interrelated biological and epidemiological factors may explain this counterintuitive pattern. First, the absence of the overwhelming “masking effect” of active smoking in former smokers might allow the intrinsic pathogenic impact of mLOY to become more apparent against the backdrop of persistent immune remodeling. Conversely, because smoking is the most critical traditional risk factor for PAD and AAA, current smokers inherently carry a profoundly high baseline risk driven by acute vascular damage from ongoing tobacco exposure. This excessively high baseline risk may create a ceiling effect, thereby compressing the relative effect of mLOY in the active-smoking population. Second, current smokers generally exhibit poorer overall health status and may succumb to competing mortality risks (eg, other fatal smoking-related diseases) before the clinical onset of PAD or AAA.39 This differential survival can distort the estimates in former smokers through healthy survivor bias. Finally, reverse confounding cannot be ruled out; some former smokers may have initiated smoking cessation precisely due to the manifestation of early vascular symptoms or related comorbidities, which could artificially inflate the observed association in this specific stratum. Corroborating our concept of an additive interaction, Weng et al21 previously reported a similar interaction between smoking history and mLOY concerning chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in the UKB; however, this phenomenon was not replicated in the China Kadoorie Biobank cohort. Therefore, our current findings regarding the synergistic cardiovascular risks necessitate further validation across diverse ethnic populations and distinct cohorts. Collectively, this “double-hit” phenomenon through the synergistic effects of smoking-related damage and mLOY emphasizes the paramount importance of tobacco avoidance in PAAD high-risk participants, which is consistent with current PAAD guidelines.1

The precise biological mechanisms underlying the association between mLOY and cardiovascular disease pathogenesis remain incompletely understood. Previous experimental evidence indicates that monocyte-macrophages with LOY exhibit elevated paracrine secretion of transforming growth factor-β1, thereby promoting cardiac fibrosis.15 Furthermore, population-based proteomic analyses have linked mLOY to key systemic pathophysiological processes, including inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation.40 In addition, mLOY may be involved in the accumulation of tissue senescent cells and accelerating biological aging at a broader systemic level.41 Despite these insights, the exact molecular pathways through which mLOY participates in the onset of PAD and AAA warrant further experimental investigation.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, potential unmeasured confounders might not have been included in our analyses. Second, this study is exclusively based on the UKB cohort and lacks validation in an independent external population. The generalizability of our findings is limited by the predominantly European ancestry of the UKB population, making it challenging to extrapolate these results to other ethnic groups due to significant difference of mLOY prevalence across ethnic groups.42 External validation in independent, multiethnic cohorts is warranted to verify the robustness, reproducibility, and generalizability of our core findings. Third, our analysis relied on a single baseline mLOY assessment treated as a fixed exposure across the entire 13.7-year follow-up period. mLOY is a dynamic clonal hematopoiesis that exhibits within-individual fluctuations over time. Failing to account for changes in mLOY status during the long-term follow-up could result in exposure misclassification, potentially biasing our hazard estimates toward the null, particularly in late follow-up.43 Thus, the reported point estimates are likely conservative, which should be further validated by repeated mLOY assessment during the follow-up period. Finally, given the observational design of our study, the present data establish epidemiological associations rather than causal relationships. Although we discussed the potential pathogenic activities of mLOY-bearing cells based on emerging biological evidence, experimental studies are required to robustly elucidate whether mLOY causally drives the development of PAD and AAA.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates significant associations between mLOY and increased risks of PAD and AAA. The growing accessibility of sequencing technologies and the development of mLOY detection methods, coupled with increasing recognition of mLOY’s role in disease progression and adverse outcomes, position mLOY as a promising next-generation biomarker for many diseases. Our findings emphasize the critical importance of smoking cessation and prevention, particularly for individuals with mLOY who are at risk for PAD and AAA. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying pathways of mLOY-mediated pathogenesis.
