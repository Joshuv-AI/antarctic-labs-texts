Neural regulation of lung cancer: from mechanisms to clinical implications
Authors: Lijia Xu, Xiaokui Sun, Yundan Yao, Hao Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer exhibits extremely high incidence and mortality rates among all malignancies, and its behavior is closely associated with the nervous system. Researchers have found that the nervous system influences the hallmark capabilities of lung cancer, such as proliferation, stemness, invasion, metastasis, treatment resistance, metabolic reprogramming, immune surveillance evasion, and angiogenesis. Conversely, lung cancer and anti-tumor therapies also affect the nervous system. The emergence of this knowledge has in turn provided new opportunities for lung cancer treatment and the innovation of therapeutic concepts. This review comprehensively summarizes the interaction between the nervous system and lung cancer based on the logical framework of cancer hallmarks. What’s more, we also conclude several novel anti-tumor strategies based on cancer neuroscience to facilitate further implication and clinical transition.

Introduction

With the development of cancer biology and neuroscience, researchers no longer only regarded the nervous system as the communicational network across organs to organs. They also confirmed its vital role in tumor initiation and progression. Cancer neuroscience, a novel discipline exploring the interaction between cancer and the neural components, emerged soon after. In conclusion, neural components could directly influence cancer behaviors via neural derived substances, such as neurotransmitters, neurohormones, and growth factors. Tumors could also attract nerves and establish high innervated environment via the secretion of axon guidance proteins and neurotrophic factors. The emergence of cancer neuroscience provides us with novel insights into cancer initiation and development and, more importantly, reveal potential anti-tumor strategies, helping address severe challenges in cancer therapy.

Lung cancer has the highest mortality rate among all the cancer types, indicating further investigation is urgently needed [1]. Depending on pathology, lung cancer can be classified into small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [2]. NSCLC accounts 85% of lung cancer cases, more importantly, the diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC patients faces drastic challenges [3]. A large portion of NSCLC patients are first diagnosed with advanced and metastatic state, increasing therapy burden for them [4]. Although conventional therapy such as cytotoxic chemotherapy has shown modest improvements in survival of lung cancer patients, limitations such as drug resistance and serious side effects are still barrier for further success [3]. As for SCLC, it is mainly derived from neuroendocrine cells and is regarded as the most intractable cancer, of which overall 5-year survival rate is less than 10% [5]. Previous treatment for SCLC mainly concentrates on early radiotherapy and combination of multiple chemotherapeutic agents such as cis-platinum and etoposide, which only receive confined efficacy [6]. We are eager to evolute our cognition for lung cancer and develop corresponding anti-tumor strategy. Several evidence supports the important role and potential implication of cancer neuroscience in lung cancer therapy. For example, although there is no evidence supporting that NSCLC also originates from neuroendocrine cell, it can transform into SCLC type under the treatment of chemotherapy or radiotherapy and further gains enhanced abilities of chemotherapy resistance and aggressiveness, which is termed as “NE transformation” [7]. Researchers also indicated the intimate interaction between lung cancer and nervous system during tumor proliferation, invasions, and metastasis [2, 8].

In this review, we systemically summarize the influence of neural-derived substances on tumor initiation and progression in lung cancer based on the framework of cancer hallmarks and how lung cancer hijacks these signals and reprograms neural activities in turn. We also propose several anti-tumor strategies based on cancer neuroscience and summarize corresponding challenges in clinical transition at the same time.

Interaction between lung cancer and the nervous system: based on cancer hallmarks

Cancer hallmark, proposed by Douglas Hanahan and Robert A Weinberg, is an advanced theoretical framework for oncologists to better understand cancer biology [9]. Tumors are thought to acquire several hallmarked abilities during the multistep development, such as sustaining proliferation, resisting cell death, inducing angiogenesis, and activating invasion and metastasis [9–11]. Proliferation and stemness

Recently, research has confirmed the active role of neural signaling in lung cancer proliferation. For example, epinephrine increased DNA synthesis and thereby increased proliferation of NSCLC cells, which could be reversed by β2-AR antagonist, propranolol [12]. This process was regarded to be associated with ERK/CREB pathway, what’s more, blocking the pathway with U0126 hindered CREB phosphorylation and suppressed cell proliferation [13]. While in another SCLC cell line, NCI-H69, activation of adrenergic signaling and its downstream pathway showed no significant effect on cancer proliferation, implying the differences in different pathologic types [14]. In vivo studies identified treatment inducing chronic stress and aberrant adrenergic signaling also facilitated lung cancer proliferation via the regulation of circMBOTA2, a kind of circular RNA [15]. Chronic stress also promoted tumorigenesis in lung epithelial cells. Researchers found that chronic stress led to excessive secretion of norepinephrine, which further induced the activation of L-type voltage-dependent calcium channels, triggered calcium mobilization, and finally upregulated insulin-like growth factor 2 [16]. Insulin like growth factor in turn activated its corresponding receptor IGF-1R on epithelial cells and promoted lung tumor formation [16]. A further experiment also indicated that chronic stress also enhanced lung tumorigenesis in both KrasLSL−C12D/WT lung cancer model and LLC1 syngeneic tumor model, associated with ADRB2/CREB/CLOCK axis [17]. More importantly, researchers found that Olanzapine, a kind of anti-depression agent, helped with reduction of NE releasing and constrained lung cancer proliferation [17]. Adrenergic signaling also contributed to cancer stemness. Banerjee et al. demonstrated that epinephrine increased number of cancer stem cells in three NSCLC cell lines including NCI-H322, NCI-H441, and NCI-H1299 via the evaluation of spheroid formation assay. At the same time the markers of stem cells such as hedgehog and aldehyde dehydrogenase-1 were also upregulated [18]. Except for adrenergic signaling, researchers also confirmed other neural-derived substances that also promoted cancer proliferation. For example, substance P and its corresponding receptor, neurokinin-1 receptor, had been confirmed to promote proliferation of NSCLC both in vitro and in vivo [19]. While a certain antagonist targeting neurokinin-1 receptor, aprepitant inhibited the proliferative effects of substance P and could lead to cancer apoptosis, indicating a potential anti-tumor therapy for NSCLC [19]. A recent study also indicated that glutamatergic nerves could establish synapse like with SCLC, and the continual neural signal input sustained the high speed of proliferation [20]. What’s more, hindering the glutamatergic signaling reversed the situation [20].

However, other neural signals may exert anti-proliferative effects in lung cancer cells. For example, in NCI-H69, activation of dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) with bromocriptine led to interpretation of cAMP and PLD associated pathway and thereby inhibited cancer proliferation. What’s more, overexpression of DRD2, the gene who codes dopamine receptor D2, blocked lipopolysaccharide-induced A549 and SK-MES-1 cell proliferation and inhibited tumorigenesis in xenograft models [21]. Grant et al. also identified activation of dopamine receptor D1 also constrain cellular proliferation via the inhibition of ERK1/2 pathway [22]. Another important neural-derived substance which drastically influences lung cancer proliferation is somatostatin (SST). Plenty of studies have identified its suppressive role on proliferation, exerting via its corresponding receptor, SST receptors (SSTRs). Researchers found that activation of SSTRs inhibited cellular proliferation and induced apoptosis [23]. Several anti-tumor therapy targeting SSTRs emerged and gradually became mature. For example, due to the specific expression of SSTR on neuroendocrine tumors, various SST analogs have been designed for precise delivery of chemotherapy agents or radionuclides [23].

Death resistance and cancer therapy resistance

Several preclinical studies have indicated the important role of adrenergic signaling in chemotherapy radiotherapy, or target therapy resistance. For instance, in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, activation of β2-AR could upregulate the expressions of several drug efflux-related genes such as ABCG2 [24]. A further research confirmed that adrenergic signaling also upregulated the expression of ABCB1 gene, another important drug efflux-related gene. Increased ABCB1 expression and upregulated P-glycoprotein functions further induced 5-fluorouracil resistance [25]; similar situation could also be observed in breast cancer [26]. Researchers found that restraint stress led to aberrant adrenergic signal and increased MDR1 expression in vivo cancer model, finally enhancing pump functions of P-glycoprotein and conferring resistance to doxorubicin and paclitaxel [26]. Except the regulation on drug efflux pump, adrenergic signaling also directly impeded cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy agents. For example, norepinephrine also helped with increasing cellular proliferation and decreasing cisplatin-induced apoptosis in HT29 cells via the overexpression of miR-155, an important regulator for apoptosis.[27] In several cell lines of head and neck squamous carcinoma such as SCC-9, SCC-25, Cal27 and FaDu, pretreatment of NE decreased the cleavage of caspase-3 and upregulated the expression of Bcl-2, which further interrupted anti-tumor effects of cisplatin [28]. Norepinephrine and epinephrine also mediated drug resistance to paclitaxel in breast cancer cells through the regulation of G2/M transition, especially including cyclin-dependent kinase-1 [29].

Recently, a clinical study also indicated that β2-AR is a possible biomarker for chemosensitivity and prognosis in advanced colorectal cancer [30]. Although the effect of adrenergic signaling on chemotherapy has been evaluated in several cancer types, direct evidence to support that the same situation occurs in lung cancer patients is still lacking and remains necessary. Adrenergic signaling also contributed to radiation resistance. Researchers confirmed that adrenergic signaling led exerted inhibitory effects on radiotherapy both in vitro and in vivo [31]. While cotreatment of radiotherapy and propranolol enhanced the anti-tumor efficiency via the improvement of anti-tumor immunity [31]. Another study indicated that NSCLC cells treated with propranolol in combination with radiation had elevated levels of phosphorylated γH2AX, a marker of double-stranded DNA breaks.[32]

In addition to promoting chemotherapy and radiotherapy resistance, adrenergic signaling also contributes to resistance in novel targeted therapy. Researchers found activation of β2-AR cooperatively induced downstream signaling pathway of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). It finally resulted in the inhibition of liver kinase B1, a classic tumor suppressor, and expression interleukin-6 (IL-6), a cytokine contributing to immunosuppression [33]. IL-6 also serves as an important factor for EGFR mutation (EGFR T790M), which is considered as the key point for EGFR inhibitor resistance [34]. What’s more, these effects could be abrogated by β blockers [33]. Park et al. also confirmed that duloxetine, a kind of serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, also helped with enhancement of the sensitivity of NSCLC to EGFR inhibitors such as lapatinib, gefitinib, and erlotinib via ATF4/REDD1/mTORC1/S6K1 signaling axis [35]. Another research also confirmed that aberrant adrenergic signaling induced resistance of anti-programmed cell death protein 1 monoclonal antibody. Mechanistically, norepinephrine promoted the secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 and adenosine from tumor cells, further impeding chemotaxis of CD8+ T cells [36].

Other neural signaling is also associated with anti-tumor therapy resistance in lung cancer and other cancer types. Hu et al. found that acetylcholine (Ach), an important neurotransmitter, was accumulated in drug-resistant cancer cells in NSCLC [37]. Mechanically, upregulation and paracrine secretion of Ach induced WNT pathway via the activation of Ach muscarinic receptor 3, and finally increased drug tolerance [37]. With deeper exploration, researchers confirmed that EGFR inhibitors upregulation the expression of choline acetyltransferase, who took part in Ach biosynthesis. More importantly, genetic editing of Ach biosynthesis or pharmacological intervention in Ach signaling via darifenacin reversed this situation, highlighting the potential of improving EGFR inhibitor resistance by targeting Ach-associated pathways. As for breast cancer, activation of Ach nicotinic receptor contributes to several chemotherapeutic agents resistance such as paclitaxel, mainly through the downregulation of ABCG2 and inactivation of NFκB signaling pathway [38]. While opposite results have been observed in gastric cancer. Knockdown of Ach nicotinic receptor in gastric cancer cells made them more resistant to 5-FU, which was associated with overactivation of antiapoptotic signaling pathway [39]. It is have been reported that lung cancer could attract nerves and hijack neural signals to increase treatment resistance. For example, NSCLC could release nerve growth factors to increase cancer innervation [40]. Furthermore, 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) released by infiltrated nerves activated PI3K/AKT/mTOR in NSCLC cells, enhanced metabolism of cancer cells, and indirectly induced an immunosuppressive environment, hindering the efficacy of PD-1 monoclonal antibody [40]. Another study also indicated that 5-HT also drove resistance to aumolertinib via the attenuation of ferroptosis, while palonosetron, an FDA approved HTR3 antagonist, could reverse the situation [41]. Except for promoting cancer innervation, neurotrophic factor such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor also directly contributed to cisplatin resistance in several NSCLC cell lines such as A549 and H1299 [42]. As for SCLC cells, previous study identified a subpopulation of stem-like cells in SCLC which were marked by CD133 and exhibited multiple drug resistance [43]. Researchers further observed that this kind of cells upregulated the expression of several neuropeptide receptors after receiving treatment of chemotherapy [43]. More importantly, a broad-spectrum neuropeptide antagonist named peptide-1 increased etoposide sensitivity in these cells [43]. In other types of cancer such as breast cancer, neuropeptide Y (NPY) and its corresponding receptor NPY1R have been confirmed to contribute to chemotherapy resistance [44]. And more importantly, researchers developed a kind of peptide–drug conjugate to overcome this situation and achieve the desired result [45].

Invasion and metastasis

Plenty of studies demonstrated the nervous system contributed to cancer metastasis [46]. As for lung cancer, brain metastasis is quite common and constitutes one of the primary causes of death among lung cancer patients [47]. The interaction between the nervous system and lung cancer occurs at several stages of brain metastasis, especially in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), local invasion, survival in the circulatory system, invasion of blood brain barrier, and colonization in the brain (Fig. 1).

EMT and local invasion are regarded as the first step of distant metastasis that tumor cells change their morphological characteristics to increase their mobility and break through the basement membrane. Neural-derived substances and neural signaling contributed a lot to EMT. For example, treatment of NE in A549 cells, a kind of NSCLC cell line, stimulated the expression and secretion of TGF-β, further promoting morphological alterations and increasing the expression of mesenchymal markers [48]. Researchers confirmed chronic stress induced by foot shocks led to aberrant adrenergic signaling in lung cancer-bearing mouse model, increasing the expression of vimentin, an important kind of mesenchymal marker [49]. This situation could be reversed β2-AR antagonists. Researchers also identified Chaihu Longgu Muli Decoction, a traditional Chinese herbal formula for depression treatment, could also inhibit chronic-stress induced lung cancer EMT via the suppression of Rap1/ERK signal pathway [50]. Neuroglial also contribute to EMT of lung cancer. For example, research confirmed that Schwann cells, the primary neuroglial in peripheral nervous system, could regulate the expression of Snail and Twist, both of which are important transcriptional factors for EMT [51]. Mechanically, Schwann cell-derived CXCL5 could activate CXCR2 on lung cancer cells and further promoted their EMT via PI3K/AKT/GSK-3β pathway [51]. Neural signaling also increased the secretion of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family, which is regarded tightly associated with extracellular matrix degradation and cancer invasion. For example, activation of β2-AR via isoproterenol, a kind of NE analog, upregulated the expression of MMP-2 and MMP9 in A459 cells via ERK/CREB pathway [13]. Neuron-specific enolase (NSE) is a soluble cerebral protein derived from neuroendocrine cells and peripheral neurons. But recently, several studies have also identified its high expression in neuroendocrine tumors such as SCLC. More importantly, NSE signaling drastically influences local invasion. For instance, NSE regulated fibrinolytic activity and activated invasion-related pathways such as NGFR-dependent signaling pathway to promote local invasion [52].

After losing attachment of extracellular matrix, cancer cells face another programmed cell death, anoikis [53]. Previously, researchers confirmed chronic stress and overactivated adrenergic signaling contributed to anoikis resistance via YAP1 associated pathway. However, there is no other direct evidence indicating that a similar situation occurs in lung cancer. After the entry of blood circulation, lung cancer cells appear to have a stronger ability to survive in it [54]. Shear stress is another potential threat to cancer survival [55]. However, no direct evidence supports neural signaling helps circulating cancer cells to enhance adaptability to shear stress. While circulating cancer cells may evade neuro-immune attack via special mechanism during their circulation. An interesting phenomenon is that astrocytes secrete tissue plasminogen activator to activate the tumor-killing effects of plasmin. However, circulating lung cancer cells upregulated the expression of anti-PA serpins such as neuroserpin to impede plasmin generation thereby constrained the astrocyte-killing signals [56].

The dysfunction and disruption of blood brain barrier is the next critical stage in brain metastasis. Recent studies have demonstrated that the interactions between lung cancer cells and various kinds of cells such as neurons, endothelial cells, microglia and astrocytes are tightly associated with invasion of brain blood barrier. For example, α-synuclein released by neurons served as an important signal molecule to trigger activation of astrocytes, resulting production of vast proinflammatory factors and finally destroying the blood brain barrier [57]. Microglia could also promote astrocytes to secrete tumor necrosis factor, leading to inflammatory response and thereby disrupting blood brain barrier [2]. Several neural signaling also profoundly influence the function of tight junction. For example, substance P could downregulate the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and claudi-5 in brain microvascular endothelial and compromise the integrity of blood brain barrier [58]. Researchers have already confirmed the autocrine means of substance P, gastrin-releasing peptide and other neural-derived substances in SCLC [59]. Whether tumor-derived substance P facilitates invasion of blood brain barrier is valuable question.

Cellular components in the nervous system also facilitated survival and colonization of lung cancer cells in the brain. For example, astrocytes could be educated by brain metastatic cancer cells to serve as an “accomplice” to create an immunosuppressive environment [60]. A similar study also hinted that SCLC cells could recruit astrocytes via the secretion of Reelin, who also exerted the same recruitment function in brain development [61]. In turn, infiltrative astrocytes sustainably secreted neuronal pro-survival factors such as SERPINE1 [61]. It is believed that neural characteristics of SCLC helped them a lot to survive in the brain. NSCLC also shared the ability to educate microglia via IL-6 associated pathway and transform them into pro-tumorigenic subtypes [62]. Another study also indicated that NSCLC-derived exosomes induced a secretion of Dkk-1 from brain endothelium, which finally promote transformation of microglia into anti-inflammatory phenotypes [63]. After colonization in the brain, lung cancer cells gradually acquire several neuronal features such as axon-like protrusions, which helps them to engage into the giant neural network and hijack neural activity for their development [20, 64].

Immunosuppression and other hallmarks

The nervous system and immune system are tightly connected, and their bidirectional effects influence cancer initiation and progression. Mechanically, immune cells express vast corresponding receptors of neural-derived substances at the same time. Take catecholamines and stress hormones as an example. Catecholamines could act on innate immune cells to facilitate cancer development. For instance, a recent study indicated chronic stress induced M2 macrophage polarization via HF1α associated pathway [65]. M2 macrophages thereby helped with establishment of immunosuppressive environment and promoted lung cancer progression [65]. Catecholamines also reduced antigen presentation of dendritic cells while this kind of effect could be blocked by propranolol [66]. Researchers also confirmed overactivated adrenergic signaling decreased NK cell cytotoxicity, leading to uncontrollable growth of lung cancer [67]. Adrenergic signaling also affects the adaptive immune system, such as inhibit T cell activation and induce exhaustion. Researchers found acute restraint stress drastically upregulated concentration of corticosterone and further led to T cell exhaustion in NSCLC model mouse [68]. However, treatment of glucocorticoid receptor inhibitor, RU486, recovered the functions of T cell [68].

Moreover, catecholamines also acted on hematopoietic stem cells or progenitor cells in bone marrow, regulating myelopoiesis and maturity [69]. These populations of inflammatory cells in turn modulated TME and further facilitated tumor progression.

Other neural signaling also contributes to immune suppression. Level of 5-HT was increased when model mouse bore depression [70]. Augmented 5-HT activated its corresponding receptor 5-HTaR on infiltrating lymphocytes and further promoted an immunosuppressive environment, reflecting in increasing numbers of CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Treg cells and decreasing the ratio of Th1/Th2 cells. Another study indicated that the expression of Alpha5 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α5-nAChR) tightly associated with PD-L1 expression on T cells via STAT3/Jab1 axis [70]. Inhibition of α5-nAChR on T cells recovered IL-2 secretion and enhanced T cell-mediating killing of lung cancer cells [70]. Another study identified that expression of transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1 (TRPV1), which is an important sensor for various chemical or physical triggers, was significantly augmented in NSCLC and its high expression linked to worse immune response [71]. On the contrary, TRPV1 knockdown increased infiltration of activated T cells in TME, decreased number of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and rebalanced the ratio of M1/M2 macrophages, finally inhibiting tumor growth and promoting anti-tumor immune response [71]. These experiment results hint nerve–immune axis plays an essential role in cancer progression and influences the efficiency of immunotherapy.

Angiogenesis is essential for supply of nutrients cancer survival, gradually becoming a potential target for cancer therapy [72]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and other factors are considered as the most important regulators for cancer angiogenesis. Various neural signals have been proven to regulate the expression of VEGF. In oral squamous cell carcinoma, activation of β2-AR in cancer cells promoted the expression and secretion of VEGF-c, while propranolol could reverse the situation [73]. Another study demonstrated norepinephrine upregulated VEGF in melanoma tumor cell lines [74]. What’s more, these signal molecules could also regulate expression and function of VEGF receptor. Dopamine could act on D2 receptor to induce endocytosis of VEGF receptor 2 and prevent bind of VEGF and its receptors [75]. Several neural signaling could directly work on endothelial cells to promote angiogenesis. For example, in pancreatic cancer, norepinephrine released by intratumor nerves activated β2-AR on endothelial cell to enhance their aerobic glycolysis, which was critical for angiogenesis [76]. Another investigation indicated treatment of cabergoline, an FDA-approved D2R agonist, induced apoptosis of endothelial cells and decreased tumor angiogenesis in SCLC [77].

Neural signaling also influences metabolism of lung cancer. Researchers confirmed activation of Y5 receptor via NPY promoted metabolic reprogram in lung cancer cells, reflexed as upregulation in fatty acid metabolism [78]. Metabolically active tumor cells progressively enhance their invasive and metastatic potential, ultimately contributing to the development of brain metastasis [78]. Another recent study found that GABA was significantly accumulated in NSCLC cells with a high tendency to brain metastasis [79]. Further exploration indicated that 4-aminobutyrate aminotransferase, which transformed GABA into succinaldehydic acid, was inhibited [79]. Accumulated GABA further provided energy via the tricarboxylic acid cycle, resulting in deterioration of NSCLC [79].

In conclusion, the nervous system plays important role in the acquirement and sustainability of hallmark abilities in lung cancer (Table 1). Further investigation may provide novel insights into the understanding of cancer imitation and deterioration. The impact of lung cancer on the nervous system

Beyond the influence of the nervous system on tumors, lung cancer can conversely affect and even reshape the nervous system. For instance, Delta-Like Ligand 3, which was highly expressed in SCLC cells, could promote neurogenesis through related pathways [80, 81]. Lung cancer could also attract innervation via the secretion of nerve growth factor, brain-derived neurotrophic factor and other neurotrophic factors. Lung cancer cells could also recruit astrocytes and hijack proliferative signaling of astrocytes to support their own survival [82]. What’s more, lung cancer cells could also form synapse-like structure with nerves, thereby influencing neural electrical activity. In other cancer types, some tumor-derived substances triggered overactivation of innervated nociceptor neurons, led to aberrant secretion of neuropeptides associated with immunity, finally promoting T cell exhaustion [83]. However, similar results were not observed in the interaction between lung cancer and the nervous system. Furthermore, in head and neck cancer, tumor cells can drive neuronal reprogramming. Specifically, neurons that originally secrete tumor growth-inhibiting neurotransmitters are transformed into those that secrete tumor growth-promoting neurotransmitters under the influence of cancer cells [84]. However, the question of whether lung cancer cells harbor a similar capacity remains unanswered.

In conclusion, the nervous system and lung cancer are tightly connected and decoding their interactions provides us with a more comprehensive perspective to understand lung cancer initiation and development. More importantly, several anti-tumor strategies can be developed with the guidance of cancer neuroscience.

Clinical implications based on cancer neuroscience: novel strategies and renewal conception

Considering the potent effects of the nervous system on cancer progression, several anti-tumor strategies have been developed based on the results of these preclinical experiments and some of them have been evaluated in high quality clinical trials. Drug repurposing and combination therapy

To date, a variety of antagonists have been developed to inhibit the activation of neural receptors. With the rapid advancement of cancer neuroscience, researchers have gradually recognized that these commercially mature antagonists also exert promising anti-tumor effects [85–87]. This process is named by “drug repurposing” and has already been an essential part of the development of anti-tumor agents. The corresponding antagonists could serve as cytotoxic agent to directly kill lung cancer. Because lung cancer relied on these signals for their survival and development. These antagonists could also act as sensitizers for other anti-tumor therapies such as radiotherapy and immunotherapy. For example, adrenergic signals influence DNA damage repair, thereby inducing radiotherapy resistance in several cancer cell lines [88]. However, interrupting corresponding signals with antagonists reversed the situation [88]. Several neuropeptides are associated with construction of immunosuppressive environment, and more importantly, blocking these signaling helped with improvement of treatment response for immunotherapy [83, 89–91]. Propranolol, a kind of non-selective antagonist for β-AR family, previously are implicated in treatment of high pressure, myocardial infarction, and pheochromocytoma [92]. Recently, propranolol has been identified as a potential agent to treat lung cancer. Several studies confirmed the anti-proliferative effects of propranolol in lung cancer lines such as A549 and H1299 [93, 94]. Mechanically, propranolol arrested cell cycle of cancer cells via CDKN1A and induced apoptosis via caspase 3 pathway at the same time [94]. Except for direct lethal effect, propranolol also helped with inhibition of EMT, thereby handled severe issues such as drug resistance and metastasis [48]. Propranolol also helped with the improvement of anti-tumor immunity. For example, propranolol inhibited adrenergic signaling in TME and promoted the activation and functions of NK cells, which enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy [95]. Propranolol could also serve as a chemosensitizer in chemotherapy. Researchers found that combination of propranolol and 5-fluorouracil exerted promising anti-tumor effects in resistant tumor cells derived from colorectal carcinoma [96]. Mechanically, propranolol disrupted the hypoxic adaption, a major mechanism for 5-fluorouracil resistance, and helped with activation of apoptosis [96]. Several clinical analysis also supported the value of the treatment of propranolol or other β-blockers in lung cancer patients. For example, a univariate analysis of NSCLC patients in a study indicated that β-blocker use was associated with improved disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to no β-blocker treatment [97]. An additional multivariate analysis showed that β-blocker receipt was associated with improved DFS and OS in cancer patients with lung cancer [98]. More high-quality clinical trials should be carried out to evaluate the real anti-tumor efficacy.

Aprepitant is an FDA-approved antagonist for neurokinin-1 receptor, whose scope is mainly focused on emesis induced by chemotherapy [99]. Recently, several preclinical experiments also proved that inhibiting signaling associated with substance P via aprepitant exerted potent anti-tumor effects. Researchers confirmed crosstalk between neurokinin-1 receptor and EGFR led to uncontrollable proliferation of lung cancer cells [100]. While aprepitant could repress cancer proliferation and migration of lung cancer cells via the interruption of EGFR associated signaling pathway [100]. Aprepitant also improved gefitinib resistance, and the combination of the two agents increased the efficiency of targeted therapy [100]. Rolapitant, another antagonist for neurokinin-1 receptor, also exerted ideal cytotoxicity to lung cancer cells [101]. However, anti-tumor efficiency of them has been only evaluated in preclinical experiments and mouse models. Considering the important role of neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists in managing chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, as well as their potential to act as chemosensitizers, more clinical trials should be designed to explore application potential of corresponding combination therapy.

Although the advancement of drug repurposing has shed new light on cancer therapy, several critical challenges persist that hinder its widespread application. First, many repurposed antagonists, which are often originally developed for non-oncological indications, carry inherent side effects that pose risks to neural function. For instance, drugs targeting neuroregulatory pathways may inadvertently disrupt normal neural signaling, leading to complications like cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction, or peripheral neuropathy. These off-target effects not only compromise patient quality of life, but also limit therapeutic tolerability, potentially forcing dose reductions or treatment discontinuation. Second, the field suffers from a scarcity of high-quality clinical trials to validate the efficacy and safety of repurposed drugs in oncological settings. Many existing studies remain small-scale, retrospective, or limited to preclinical models, lacking the rigorous randomized controlled designs needed to establish causal relationships between repurposed agents and improved cancer outcomes. This gap in evidence slows regulatory approval and clinical adoption, as clinicians remain cautious about integrating unproven therapies into standard protocols. Finally, the suboptimal potency of many repurposed drugs in cancer contexts cannot be overlooked. These agents are often not optimized for oncological targets, for example, they may exhibit low affinity for cancer-specific isoforms of receptors or fail to accumulate sufficiently in tumor microenvironments. Such limitations can reduce therapeutic efficacy, requiring higher doses that exacerbate side effects or rendering the drugs ineffective against aggressive or treatment-resistant malignancies.

Targeted therapy and other therapies

Targeted therapy represents a pivotal advancement in modern oncology, distinguished by its precision in addressing cancer cells while minimizing harm to healthy tissues. Based on the aberrant expression of neural-associated receptors on tumor cells, they gradually are regarded as ideal targets. As we previously discussed, SSTRs are widely expressed in lung cancer cells and SSTR2 shares the most significant correlation with clinical outcomes in lung cancer patients [102]. Right now, SSTR2 has been already recognized as a significant therapy target. High expressed SSTR2 has been implicated to deliver radioactive or cytotoxic payloads to TME of lung cancer to exert cytotoxic effects or image tumors [103]. SST analogs have also been designed to overcome several problems such as low blood half-life and weak combining ability. Previously, researchers found that RAYZ101, which comprised α-emitting radioisotope 225AC, chemical chelator DOTA, and a somatostatin analog octreotide, showed promising anti-tumor effects either alone or conjunction with chemotherapy [104]. In another preclinical study, targeting agent, 211At armed with octreotide, was sufficient to suppress SCLC growth [105]. Furthermore, this category of targeted therapy has been demonstrated to upregulate the expression of major histocompatibility complex class I on the surface of tumor cells [105]. This observation not only indicates an enhancement in the anti-tumor immune response but also underscores the therapeutic potential of combining this targeted approach with immunotherapeutic strategies. In another experiment, the combination of PEN-221, a miniaturized peptide-drug conjugate to target SSTR2, and mertansine, a potent cytotoxic payload, eliminated tumors in SSTR-positive SCLC xenograft mouse models with deep tumor penetration and limited plasma exposure, resulting in effective anti-tumor effect and durable tumor control [106]. Furthermore, several SSTR2-targeted imaging agents have also been designed for use in positron emission tomography [107]. In the future, researchers should investigate additional aberrantly expressed receptors and other neural-associated targets on tumor cells to further promote the development of targeted therapies.

Considering the importance of neural activities, especially chronic stress on lung cancer initiation, mindfulness therapy, cognitive therapy and other mental treatments may also be taken into lung cancer treatment. Several clinical studies indicated that Mindfulness-based interventions significantly decreased anxiety, depression, and fatigue in lung cancer patients, furthermore, improved the quality of their life [108, 109]. In other cancer types such as breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and prostate cancer, mindfulness based therapy also showed ideal effects [110–112]. As research continues to unravel the mechanistic links between psychological states, neural activity, and cancer biology, these therapies stand to become vital allies in the fight against lung cancer, offering a way to treat not just the tumor, but the person navigating it.

Side effects on the nervous system of anti-tumor therapy

With the conceptional development, medical personnel no longer regarded cancer elimination as the final goal for cancer therapy. Oncologists have also emphasized that improving patients' quality of life and enhancing end-of-life care for patients should be regarded as key priorities in cancer treatment [113]. With this in mind, although anti-tumor therapies help lung cancer patients by reducing tumor burden, the side effects they cause can affect other systems such as the nervous system. The inconveniences arising from this are often overlooked. In this section, we summarize the status of relevant research, hoping to help associated practitioners gain a better understanding of this issue.

Radiotherapy is widely used in lung cancer patients due to its accuracy; however, it still causes numerous injuries associated with the nervous system. For example, radiotherapy could straightly influence peripheral nerves or nerve plexuses and lead to pain and numbness in the innervated part of the irradiated area, which is termed as radiation-induced neuritis [114]. Radiotherapy is also abundantly used in lung cancer patients with brain metastasis, impacting the nervous system drastically. Researchers confirmed radiotherapy induced decline in neurogenesis and neuronal damage directly. More importantly, it causes hippocampal damage, disrupts neuroendocrine pathway, and induced cognitive dysfunction [115].

Surgical treatment constitutes a crucial component in the management of lung cancer. During the surgical procedure, numerous nerves such as the intercostal nerves may sustain direct damage. Studies have demonstrated that intercostal nerve injury can lead to postoperative pain syndrome and exert a significant impact on patients' prognosis [116]. Surgery-induced damage of recurrent laryngeal nerve led to hoarseness, dysphagia, or aspiration [117]. As for surgical treatment of brain-metastatic lung cancer, resection of brain metastases may destroy the surrounding brain tissues, causing postoperative epilepsy and cognitive impairment [2].

Chemotherapy remains a core component in the treatment of lung cancer; however, due to the toxicity of chemotherapeutic agents, it causes numerous neurological complications. Chemotherapy-induced neuropathy is the most common and well-known one, which is characterized by dysesthesias, tingling, and numbness [118, 119]. Mechanically, chemotherapeutic agents induced neurotoxicity by breaking down the peripheral nervous system via interruption of axonal transport mechanisms, damage to the myelin sheath, or provoking apoptosis in neuronal cell bodies of the dorsal root ganglia [120].

Other anti-tumor therapies, such as immunotherapy and targeted therapy also induced neural damage, and corresponding mechanisms have been well summarized by Jorg Dietrich in a recent review [121]. In summary, the neuro-related side effects of anti-tumor therapies also severely impact patients' quality of life, which constitutes one of the key areas that researchers need to focus on.

Conclusion and future directions

With the development of cancer neuroscience, the concept that the nervous system exerts a significant impact on tumors has gradually gained widespread acceptance [122–124]. Several recent outstanding studies have also demonstrated that lung cancer, regardless of its pathological type, is closely associated with the nervous system [125–127]. Based on the theoretical framework of cancer hallmarks, we have discussed the influence of neuro-tumor interactions on both entities. The nervous system significantly affects tumor growth, stemness, invasion and metastasis, resistance to cancer treatments, and remodeling of the immune microenvironment. Conversely, lung cancer also influences neural axon formation and its electrophysiological activities. Even in other tumor types, tumor cells can promote neuronal reprogramming to facilitate their own survival. Therefore, based on the aforementioned theories regarding the regulatory role of neural signaling in lung cancer progression, we propose three targeted anti-tumor strategies with detailed mechanistic support. First, drug repurposing, focused on clinically approved neural signaling antagonists, is a cost-effective approach: these agents interfere with downstream oncogenic pathways activated by neural transmitters to inhibit tumor progression, and their long-established long-term safety profiles significantly reduce the time and financial costs of developing novel anti-tumor agents. Second, combination therapy, which integrates neural signaling interruption with conventional treatments like chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy, enhances efficacy by addressing complementary oncogenic vulnerabilities; it can also mitigate therapy-related side effects, such as by inhibiting neural transmitter-induced sensory neuron hyperactivation to reduce treatment-associated adverse events. Third, targeted therapy targets the aberrantly expressed receptors for neural-derived substances in lung cancer cells. These receptors act as oncogenic drivers by promoting cell migration and angiogenesis, so targeting them enables precision anti-tumor effects, with ongoing efforts to develop specific inhibitors or antibody–drug conjugates against these receptors for lung cancer treatment. We also emphasize that the impact of anti-tumor treatments on the nervous system should not be overlooked, as it is closely related to the quality of life of cancer patients. Finally, although significant progress has been made in tumor neurobiology, there are still many pressing issues to be addressed and difficult predicaments to overcome. Through further integration with other disciplines, the establishment of cross-disciplinary thinking, and the in-depth application of multidisciplinary technologies, we will be able to unravel the mysteries of lung cancer.
