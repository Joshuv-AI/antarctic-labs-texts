How hyperoxia affects systemic redox state: insights from PULSE-Ox, a randomised double-blind mechanistic feasibility trial
Authors: Andrew F Cumpstey, Anna D Clark, Magdalena Minnion, Siobhan F Crosby, Renato C Nogueira, Helen Moyses, Daniel Martin, José E Tanus-Santos, Alan A Jackson, Mark Edwards, Michael PW Grocott, Martin Feelisch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxygen therapy is ubiquitous in medical practise, but recommendations for administration during surgery are conflicting, and mechanisms of harm are ill-defined. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that all anaesthetised patients receive 80% oxygen intraoperatively to reduce surgical site infections. Whether such high oxygen concentrations cause oxidative stress remains uncertain.MethodsAdults undergoing major cancer surgery were randomly allocated to conservative (FiO2=0.3, n=8), typical (FiO2=0.55, n=10) or recommended (FiO2=0.8, n=10) oxygen throughout anaesthesia. The aims were to determine whether i) intraoperative oxygen at or below WHO-recommended levels affects whole-body redox state, and ii) redox profiling could distinguish between different oxygen concentrations. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) and oxygen reserve index were monitored (co)oximetrically. Paired arterial/central venous blood samples/gases were collected 2-hourly. Lipid peroxides, protein carbonyls, free thiols, antioxidants and nitroso species were quantified using Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry, Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), colourimetry and gas-phase chemiluminescence.ResultsDouble-blinded recruitment of 28 patients (median age 65.8 yr) to three different intraoperative oxygen concentrations was feasible and successful during the study period from September, 2018 to May 2021. While SpO2 remained unchanged, systemic oxygen utilisation paradoxically decreased with higher oxygen concentrations (P=0.0006). While free thiol levels rose moderately, nitrosation increased significantly (P=0.039/0.002/0.039 for FiO2=0.3/0.55/0.8). Administration of 80% oxygen was associated with diminished reductive power (P=0.012) and significant oxidative stress (P=0.004).ConclusionsCross-sectional redox profiling may allow early identification of patients at risk of postoperative complications, while longitudinal profiling may distinguish between oxygen intervention groups. High oxygen concentrations challenge the body’s ability to maintain redox balance, potentially increasing vulnerability to other stressors. Our results lay the foundation for large clinical effectiveness studies evaluating oxygen therapy targets and suggest that ‘redox scoring’ could guide individualised oxygen administration.Trial registration numberNCT03552627 (Clinicaltrials.gov)Keywords: cancer, hyperoxia, oxidative stress, personalised medicine, redox balance, surgical stress

Oxygen is the principal oxidant of all aerobic life and the most commonly used drug in medicine.1, 2, 3 Yet considerable uncertainty exists about its optimal administration during general anaesthesia.4 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that all anaesthetised patients receive FiO2=0.8 to reduce surgical site infections.5 However, evidence to support this approach is insufficient and administering FiO2>0.6 might increase mortality.6 In 2019, an updated revision of the WHO recommendation concluded there was no definite beneficial effect of administering FiO2=0.8 during surgery, but also no definite signal of harm.7,8

Results from the largest available randomised controlled trial of intraoperative oxygen (PeRioperative OXygen FractIon - Effect on Surgical Site Infection and Pulmonary Complications After Abdominal Surgery; PROXI), showing no difference in surgical site infections rates and worse outcomes with FiO2=0.8 in post-hoc analyses),9, 10, 11, 12 would caution against the WHO recommendation, but were surprisingly considered ‘mechanistically implausible’ by the WHO’s initial guideline development group.4 Why many anaesthetists do not follow the WHO recommendations (most administer ≈50-55% oxygen intraoperatively13, 14, 15) is unclear but may relate to the presumed ‘U-shaped’ relationship between oxygen concentration and harm.4,16,17 There is growing concern about the harmful effects of oxidative stress caused by higher oxygen concentrations, particularly in critical care and other acute clinical settings.17 However, this harm was not evident in all reviews,18 and whether this concern also applies to anaesthesia is unclear.19 Few perioperative oxygen studies have been representative of typical (FiO2=0.5-0.55) intraoperative oxygen administration, as well as both WHO-recommended (FiO2=0.8) and conservative oxygen administration (e.g. FiO2=0.3). No previous trial has yet included the preoxygenation and emergence periods within the double-blinded randomisation.

Humans need a continuous oxygen supply since the body’s buffering capacity is limited. Oxygen uptake, delivery and utilisation are therefore tightly controlled, and the latter is associated with the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). During surgery, supplemental oxygen is often delivered in excess under the assumption that this provides a ‘safety net’ should complications arise, although these assumptions have been challenged.20,21

The way the body senses environmental or metabolic challenges/stressors and adapts to enhanced ROS production involves dynamic interactions between short-lived sulfur and nitrogen-containing reactive species. The interaction of these molecules with their biological targets has been conceptualised in the ‘reactive species interactome’ framework.22,23 Both lower (hypoxia) and higher-than-normal (hyperoxia) oxygen levels are associated with enhanced ROS production and oxidative stress.24,25 While oxygen sensing, acclimatisation and adaptation to hypoxia are well-studied processes,26 how humans cope with hyperoxia is incompletely understood. The objective of the current investigation was two-fold: 1. to determine how supplementary oxygen alters ROS production; and 2. to test whether an enhanced oxidative burden affects systemic redox state. Long-duration major abdominal cancer surgeries were chosen to enable studying these processes for as long as possible. We hypothesised that exposure to hyperoxia would elicit adaptive metabolic changes to maintain redox balance. If the body’s redox-homeostatic capacity became overwhelmed, this might predispose patients to adverse clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

A single-centre, prospective, interventional, double-blinded randomised trial (Perioperative UtiLisation of SupplEmental Oxygen; PULSE-Ox) was conducted at University Hospital Southampton, a large teaching hospital in the UK and a national referral centre for complex surgical procedures. The trial sponsor was University Hospital Southampton NHS Foundation Trust. The trial was approved by the South Central – Hampshire A Research and Ethics Committee (18/SC/0018) and prospectively registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03552627) in March 2018. Eligible participants were aged ≥16 yr and were undergoing major elective abdominal surgery with both an arterial and central venous catheter being placed as part of their anaesthetic management. Patients were excluded if they were pregnant; were undergoing neurosurgery or cardiac surgery; needed one-lung ventilation at any point during their procedure; had baseline SpO2 <90%; severe COPD (GOLD stage 3 or 4), interstitial lung disease or other inflammatory lung condition (including being on home-oxygen); had a BMI >35; had any documented concerns about a potentially difficult airway; or had sickle cell disease or thalassemia. Unsuitability for total intravenous anaesthesia or for fixed positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) administration was added as an additional exclusion criterion in an amendment (notified 12 December 2019; approved 20 December 2019). All participants provided written informed consent.

Interventions

Participants were randomised to receive either an FiO2=0.8, FiO2=0.55 or FiO2=0.3 (the WHO recommendation, typical practice, or more conservative administration, respectively) for the duration of their anaesthesia, from the first oxygen administration through to when the patient left the operating theatre. Anaesthesia was conducted using a total intravenous technique with target-controlled infusions of propofol and remifentanil, with rocuronium for neuromuscular blockade. All participants were ventilated using a pressure control mode to achieve a tidal volume of 6–8 ml/kg ideal body weight with a PEEP of 5 cmH2O. Ondansetron was used for antiemesis, and no patients received dexamethasone or other steroids. Other aspects of anaesthetic management were left to the discretion of the treating anaesthetist.

A baseline blood sample was collected immediately after insertion of the initial peripheral intravenous cannula, prior to any supplementary oxygen administration. Immediately after anaesthesia commenced and after intubation, an arterial catheter was placed in the radial artery for blood pressure monitoring, followed by a central venous catheter placed in the right internal jugular vein for administration of medication. Paired blood samples (to allow calculation of tissue oxygen extraction) were then collected 2-hourly, from the radial arterial line and the right internal jugular central venous catheter, until the end of surgery. A further peripheral venous blood sample was collected 7 days after surgery (see Supplementary Fig. 1.

Outcome measures

To assess oxygenation status, we continuously monitored peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) by standard pulse oximetry as well as the difference between arterial and venous oxygen saturation by using multispectral co-oximetry. This captures the supra-physiological oxygenation range after arterial haemoglobin (Hb) is fully saturated using a device marketed as providing a dimensionless ‘oxygen reserve index’ (ORi).27 Mean fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), SpO2 and ORi were calculated using measurements recorded every 10s by iCollect software (iCollect GE Healthcare, Chicago, Illinois, USA) for FiO2 and SpO2, or a Masimo Radical-7 co-oximeter (Masimo Corporation, Irvine, California, USA) for ORi throughout the duration of oxygen administration. FiO2 recording failed for one participant in the 30% group; ORi recording failed for one participant in each of the 30% and 55% groups. Arterial and venous partial pressures of oxygen (PaO2, PvO2), arterial and venous Hb concentration, and arterial and venous oxygen saturation (SaO2, SvO2) were determined in paired, 2-hourly blood samples using a Radiometer ABL90 Flex Plus blood gas analyser.

A targeted panel of four biochemical readouts in blood plasma/serum were measured that provide complementary information on redox processes and represent a summary statement of ‘whole-body redox state’ at any moment in time: total free thiols (a readout of overall thiol/disulfide balance), protein carbonyls (a marker of oxidative stress), total antioxidants (indicative of reductive power), and circulating nitroso species (a marker of nitrosative stress secondary to the reaction of nitric oxide with oxygen or superoxide).23 The concentration of serum-free thiols, along with nitroso species, antioxidants, and protein oxidation products, was measured in plasma/serum samples collected 2-hourly to provide an assessment of whole-body redox balance. Lipid (per)oxidation products were also quantified to ensure comparability of the degree of oxidative stress between surgical procedures.

Adverse events were defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant who had received a study intervention. Serious Adverse Events (SAEs) were defined as any untoward medical occurrence that resulted in death, was life-threatening, prolonged inpatient hospitalisation, or resulted in significant disability.

Redox-metabolomic analyses and calculation of oxygen content and extraction

All blood samples were centrifuged (Capricorn CEP 2000, Capricorn Laboratory Equipment Ltd, Ringwood, Hampshire, UK) at 4,000 x g for 3 min straight after collection and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen in the anaesthetic room. EDTA samples were centrifuged immediately; serum samples were centrifuged after standing for 30 min at room temperature. After storage at -80 oC, samples were analysed in batches. Total free thiol (TFT) concentrations were determined using a Konelab 20 clinical chemistry analyser (Thermo Scientific),28 and normalised to protein determined by the Coomassie (Bradford) Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific). Total nitroso species (RXNO) concentrations were measured in N-ethylmaleimide-treated EDTA plasma samples by reductive gas-phase chemiluminescence after nitrite removal.29 Total antioxidants were measured by the ferric reducing ability of plasma (FRAP) assay.30 Oxidative stress was measured by quantifying protein carbonyls (PCs) using a commercial ELISA (Abcam).31 Serum concentrations of the lipid peroxidation products malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) were quantified by mass spectrometry after derivatisation with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (see Supplementary Methods and References for details).

Oxygen extraction is dependent on delivery and cell/tissue utilisation. Oxygen delivery depends on total arterial and venous oxygen content (CaO2 and CvO2, calculated using the Hb concentration, Hb-bound and dissolved oxygen, as well as Hüfner’s constant: 1.34, and the solubility constant for oxygen: 0.023)32 and cardiac output. Oxygen extraction ratio was calculated by dividing the difference between CaO2 and CvO2 by the CaO2.

Randomisation and masking

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 allocation ratio using pre-determined sealed envelopes generated by an independent third-party. All participants, research team members and the sponsor’s study team remained masked to the allocated intervention throughout the trial. The treating clinical anaesthetist remained unblinded and delivered the allocated FiO2 intervention. All monitors of oxygen administration on the anaesthetic machine were covered during surgery, keeping research team members blinded to the allocation. All samples were analysed using anonymised subject codes before the data were unblinded at the end of the study using electronic recordings of the anaesthetic delivery collected contemporaneously during each individual procedure. These recordings were also used at the end of the trial to assess the clinical anaesthetists’ compliance in successfully delivering the intervention.

Sample size and statistical analyses

The main purpose of this study was to assess the feasibility of the intervention and to estimate parameters in order to inform a future fully powered study. A sample size of 10 in each group would produce two-sided 95% confidence intervals with a distance from the mean to the limits that is equal to 0.7 standard deviations. Using data from Koksal and colleagues,33 if a similar effect size was observed for total thiols (one of our primary redox endpoints), a sample size of 13 in each group (39 in total) would achieve 90% power to detect a difference of 1.1 nmol/mg protein at a 0.05 significance level, assuming a common standard deviation within a group of 0.5. This difference was considered to be biologically significant as it represented the between-group difference measured in the only available similar intraoperative trial,33 and a similar difference was associated with a significant increase in all-cause mortality in the general population.34 Statistical analysis was performed on an intention-to-treat basis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline patient characteristics and physiological observations. After normality testing (Shapiro-Wilk), means for each intervention were compared using analysis of variance or equivalent non-parametric tests, as appropriate. Linear mixed repeated measures models were used to investigate differences (between treatment groups) in variables measured at multiple timepoints. For each variable, the initial model included time (as a continuous variable), randomised treatment group and baseline value. All statistics were calculated using GraphPad Prism or STATA. No interim analyses were performed. Statistical significance was assumed at P=0.05.

Results

Twenty-eight patients were enrolled, randomly allocated to one of three intervention groups, and completed the study between 27 September 2018 and 8 May 2021, when the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted most elective major surgery in the UK (Fig. 1). The data from one patient in the FiO2=0.8 group were excluded from final statistical analyses as their anaesthesia and surgery were much shorter than those of every other participant, although the patient returned for the day-7 follow-up (see Supplementary Fig.2 for comparison of redox data of this particular individual against other study participants in the same intervention group). Double-blinded patient allocation to different oxygen concentrations is feasible

Adult patients were successfully recruited in a double-blinded fashion to three different intraoperative oxygen concentrations during a mechanistic clinical trial. Clear separation in the mean FiO2 was achieved (mean [95% CI] O2%: 33.2 [29.2–37.2]% / 55.1 [52.6–57.7]% / 79.8 [77.4–82.2]%, for the FiO2=0.3 / 0.55 / 0.8 groups respectively, P<0.001). While SpO2 readings did not differ between groups (98.0 [96.9–99.0]% / 98.8 [98.0–99.6]% / 99.4 [99.1–99.7]% for FiO2=0.3 / 0.55 / 0.8 respectively, P=0.22; Fig. 2c), both arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) and the ORi increased in a dose-dependent manner with higher FiO2, revealing direct associations across levels of hyperoxemia (Fig. 2b and e–h). PaO2 increases translated into progressively larger amounts of oxygen dissolved in blood (Fig. 2d).

Systemic oxygen utilisation decreases in an oxygen-dependent manner

Although mean arterial oxygen content (CaO2) remained unchanged (P=0.709), there was a significant increase in mean venous oxygen content (CvO2) at higher FiO2, resulting in progressively lower arteriovenous differences in blood oxygen content with higher-level oxygen administration. Consequently, mean systemic oxygen extraction ratios progressively decreased at higher intraoperative FiO2 (0.28 [0.25 – 0.31] / 0.23 [0.20 – 0.26] / 0.20 [0.18 – 0.22] for FiO2=0.3 / 0.55 / 0.8, respectively [P=0.0006]; Fig. 2i–l).

The intraoperative oxygen concentrations studied did not appear to contribute to serious adverse events

Two patients in the FiO2=0.3 group and two patients in the FiO2=0.55 group experienced SAEs (arrhythmias, abdominal collections requiring drainage, pulmonary embolism and stoma ischaemia; all considered unrelated to the oxygen intervention). Two other patients in the FiO2=0.3 group and one patient in the FiO2=0.55 group experienced adverse events (pneumonia). These incidents were considered to be within the expected rate of events for surgical procedures of this length/magnitude by the Data Monitoring Committee. Adverse events are summarised in Supplementary Table 2.

Oxidative stress is similar between major abdominal surgeries

Quantification of serum-free MDA and 4-HNE levels revealed considerable variability during surgery with FiO2=0.55 compared with baseline. Notwithstanding trajectory differences in these oxidative stress markers between patients, median values were in the same order of magnitude irrespective of the nature of the procedure (hepatobiliary or colorectal; Fig. 3). This indicated that the degree of surgical stress was similar across the main procedures included in our study, an important prerequisite for investigating what effect different levels of oxygen have on whole-body redox state using a more comprehensive panel of biomarkers.

Supplementary oxygen elicits adaptive redox metabolic responses

While circulating TFT levels tended to increase during surgery in the majority of patients, overall steady-state values did not change between the start and end of surgery with FiO2=0.3 (P=0.109), FiO2=0.55 (P=0.106) or FiO2=0.8 (P=0.250) (Fig. 4a). Although PCs did not change during surgery with FiO2=0.3 (P=0.641) or FiO2=0.55 (P=0.084), oxidative stress increased significantly in patients receiving FiO2=0.8 (P=0.004) (Fig. 4b). This was mirrored by a significant decrease in antioxidants only in the FiO2=0.8 group (P=0.012) (Fig. 4c). RXNO concentrations significantly increased during surgery in all three intervention groups (P=0.039 for FiO2=0.3, P=0.002 for FiO2=0.55 and P=0.039 for FiO2=0.8) (Fig. 4d).

Redox imbalance is associated with higher risk of surgical complications

Despite not appearing different in any other measure performed during standard clinical preassessment (including physiological observations, clinical laboratory-based haematology and biochemical investigations routinely performed preoperatively), redox analyses revealed that three patients (2 from the FiO2=0.3 group and one from the FiO2=0.55 group) started surgery with higher concentrations of select redox markers (either both RXNO and FRAP, or PCs) compared with the rest of their group (see highlighted traces in Fig. 4b–d). The same three patients went on to experience infectious adverse or serious adverse events after their surgery (one abdominal collection needing drainage and two pneumonias). One of these patients (highlighted in yellow) also displayed an exaggerated oxidative stress response during the surgical procedure.

Cross-sectional comparison of redox biomarker concentrations between the start and end of surgery allowed a subset of patients at risk of complications to be identified, but did not offer any insight into how and when redox state changes throughout surgery. We therefore went on to conduct higher-resolution redox profiling in arterial samples to assess commonalities and differences in temporal biomarker profiles at different stages during surgery; importantly, this included the effects of oxygen and anaesthesia themselves before any surgical incision began. Although central venous samples were also analysed for TFT, FRAP and RXNO, only moderate arteriovenous concentration differences were found (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Temporal high-resolution redox profiling offers insights into trajectory and magnitude of metabolic responses to oxygen

TFT moderately increased during surgery in all intervention groups (P=0.26, linear mixed modelling) (Fig. 5a). Median PC concentrations in patients were all above levels in healthy control31 (dotted line in Fig. 5b) and unexpectedly decreased at the end of surgery in the FiO2=0.3 and FiO2=0.8 groups. FRAP gradually increased during surgery with FiO2=0.3, remained relatively stable with FiO2=0.55 and progressively decreased in the group receiving FiO2=0.8. Moreover, the absolute change in magnitude and the rate of increase in RXNO concentrations also appeared to depend on administered oxygen concentration. The greatest change (and fastest rise after surgical incision) was seen in the FiO2=0.8 group, and the smallest change (and slowest rise) was seen in the FiO2=0.3 group (P=0.05, linear mixed modelling). Taken together, these results unequivocally demonstrate that trajectories and magnitude of changes in whole-body redox state during surgery depend on the concentration of intraoperative oxygen administered, and may even suggest that FiO2=0.55 (“typical” administration) is tending towards an injudicious choice, with FiO2=0.8 being a potentially injurious level. The dynamic nature of the changes observed, sometimes in opposite directions, indicates that measured levels at any given point in time reflect alterations in transport and/or metabolic fluxes of an adaptive system.

Individual redox profiling unveils discrete adaptive patterns to graded hyperoxia

We reasoned that the redox state of individual patients may be more readily compared if all four readouts were displayed side-by-side. Since no pertinent reference data on extracellular redox state in healthy individuals exists in the literature, we calculated the arithmetic mean for each biomarker from patients’ plasma/serum samples taken at baseline (i.e. prior to their assignment to a specific intervention group and well before administration of any premedication, anaesthetic or oxygen); these values served as reference for conversion of absolute concentrations into heatmaps (see Fig. 6a). The combinatorial pattern of biomarker concentrations at the beginning of surgery differed from the mean values of all patients at baseline, with unique profiles for each. Although not immediately obvious, there were commonalities in the direction and extent of changes in redox biomarker patterns in response to anaesthetic and surgical stress within each group (e.g. P19, P20 and P27 all portray parallel changes (Δ) in redox profile, despite presenting with dissimilar redox states at the start of surgery). When physiological oxygenation and biochemical redox states were combined (Fig. 6b), each interventional group displayed a discrete pattern of response.

Discussion

This double-blind randomised mechanistic trial demonstrates successful administration of three different intraoperative FiO2s (0.3, 0.55 and 0.8) throughout anaesthesia in adults undergoing major non-cardiac surgery. Previous perioperative oxygen trials have typically only randomised to a ‘high’ (e.g. FiO2=0.8-1.0) or ‘low’ (FiO2=0.3-0.4) FiO2 and thus have not been representative of current practice internationally. A particular strength is the inclusion of both preoxygenation and emergence within the intervention, avoiding any effects that administering 100% oxygen prior to intubation might elicit.35 We also demonstrate the feasibility of cross-sectional redox profiling to identify patients at greater risk of complications and temporal high-resolution profiling to distinguish between responses to different oxygen concentrations.

The dangers of administering supplementary oxygen are believed to be mainly due to oxidative stress.14 This reduces the bioavailability of NO, affecting redox regulation, cardiovascular function and intermediary metabolism. We observed dose-dependent increases in PaO2 and ORi without changes in SpO2 (Fig. 2b and c). PaO2 and ORi have been reported to correlate,36 but this has not been demonstrated across surgeries of this duration. ORi appears to track PaO2 well (Fig. 2f–h), and its non-invasive nature holds promise for monitoring hyperoxemia-related risk.

Oxygen administration was accompanied by complex alterations in concentration of lipid and protein (per)oxidation products, mechanistically linked to oxidative stress and cancer.37,38 PCs appear better suited to mark enhanced ROS production than reactive aldehydes, which rapidly react with other biomolecules, including thiols. Yet, even these supposedly stable compounds revealed an unexpected dynamic in vivo.

Under normal physiological conditions, dissolved oxygen is often ignored as almost all oxygen transport occurs bound to haemoglobin. Yet, with supraphysiological concentrations, oxygen transport is no longer controlled by the Bohr-Haldane relationship, which matches oxygen (and CO2) transport to tissue demand.39 Since greater PaO2 directly translates into higher dissolved oxygen concentrations (see Fig. 2d), venous oxygen saturation increases (as evidenced by higher ORi values). This means venous erythrocytes can no longer take up and buffer all the oxygen being delivered but not consumed, leading to progressive oxygen accumulation as long as anaesthesia continues. The only way to deal with this excess is chemical reduction. Since mitochondrial respiration is a demand-led process and forced delivery during anaesthesia markedly exceeds the physiological demand, this results in increased oxidative stress. Our observations strongly caution against the liberal use of oxygen during surgery.

Distinct patterns of compensatory redox-metabolomic changes emerged in response to different oxygen concentrations. Patients who later developed complications had redox profiles at the start of surgery distinct from those of other patients within the same group. Administering FiO2=0.8 intraoperatively appears to overwhelm the body’s capacity to maintain redox balance. These changes were accompanied by reduced oxygen utilisation, potentially marking alterations in mitochondrial function. Changes in redox state may lead to metabolic reprogramming,40 affect metabolic flexibility, and compromise the body’s ability to accommodate other stressors.

To the best of our knowledge, the use of redox markers for risk scoring in the clinical setting has not been demonstrated before. We chose a combination of four analytes that mark electron exchange processes at different levels of regulation. In combination, these readouts provide greater insight into the metabolic processes serving to maintain redox balance than any single marker. Using high-resolution readouts of redox regulation allows a more refined characterisation of the body’s redox state than that currently provided by routine clinical laboratory services. Cross-sectional redox phenotyping (i.e. the measurement of multiple RSI readouts to capture an individual’s ‘redox state’) allows markers of response and resilience to be compared between people of different age, sex and ancestry in the absence/presence of other metabolic perturbations.

Earlier trials have explored redox changes to two different oxygen levels. Koksal and colleagues33 reported higher TFT levels with an FiO2=0.8 compared with an FiO2=0.4 during shorter hemicolectomy procedures. However, all patients were preoxygenated with an FiO2=0.8. Two other small studies (n=30 and 24) reported lower oxidative stress during elective colonic resection with an FiO2=0.8 versus 0.3.41,42 Both trials used volatile maintenance and volume-controlled ventilation modes, which are more pro-inflammatory than propofol and pressure-controlled ventilation,43,44 and only started the interventional oxygen concentration after anaesthetic induction. Another study in 56 patients undergoing major cancer surgery reported increased nitroso species postsurgery,45 but oxygenation levels were not controlled. In another trial of 258 patients at risk of cardiovascular complications, oxidative stress increased during surgery, but no differences were seen between FiO2=0.3 and 0.8.46,47 The authors argued this was because goal-directed fluid therapy was used to maintain intraoperative cardiac output (CO), suggesting hyperoxia only induced oxidative stress indirectly through vasoconstriction and tissue ischaemia. We did not protocolise CO monitoring, but CO monitor use, intravenous fluid administration and estimated blood losses were very similar in all three groups (see Supplementary Table 1), and changes in flow would not explain why some markers decreased at higher FiO2, whilst others increased or stayed constant. If oxidative stress were due to flow changes, all markers should alter proportionately.

Our observation that whole-body oxygen extraction is reduced at higher levels of oxygen administration may be a result of haemodynamic alterations affecting delivery, flow redistribution or (sub)cellular changes affecting oxygen consumption. However, we saw no changes in blood pressure, heart rate or lactate, and major surgery would not increase CO. In fact, CO either decreases in children breathing FiO2>0.8548 or does not change after 30 min of exposure to FiO2 =1 in invasively ventilated patients.49

We measured mixed venous oxygen levels (SvO2) rather than central mixed venous oxygen saturations (ScvO2); however, the two correlate closely (ScvO2 tends to be lower than SvO2 as blood returning from the kidneys tends to be better-oxygenated). Whilst we cannot exclude that microcirculatory flow redistribution could have resulted in functional oxygen shunting,49 the greater rise in RXNO seen with higher intraoperative FiO2s suggests that mitochondrial changes directly affect oxygen consumption. Hyperoxia, per se, is known to inhibit cellular respiration.50 Mechanistically, this may be a consequence of S-nitrosylation of mitochondrial complex 1 secondary to enhanced nitrosative stress,51 as observed with higher FiO2 in some but not all patients in the present study. Excess oxygen has also been associated with decreasing glycolytic capacity in isolated lung mitochondria.52

Two participants with outlying values in the FiO2=0.3 group and one in the FiO2=0.55 oxygen group (highlighted in Fig 4, Fig 5, Fig 6) displayed a pattern of whole-body redox state distinct from all other participants. With a greater understanding of redox signatures, these patients could have been identified preoperatively as being at higher risk of postoperative complications. If adopted for future presurgical screening, larger patient numbers may reveal biomarker signatures that highlight endotypes of risk and resilience to particular levels of oxygen exposure (‘personalised oxygen therapy’).

Changing the FiO2 could offer a novel way of modulating the surgical stress response. Multicentre effectiveness studies are urgently needed to determine whether these distinct redox-metabolomic responses to oxygen translate into different clinical outcomes. Even a very small reduction in mortality could translate into large numbers of individual lives saved, considering the millions of surgical procedures performed annually. The inter-individual heterogeneity in patients’ tolerance to perioperative oxygen administration also merits further investigation. Characterising different ‘oxygen phenotypes’ could allow better perioperative risk stratification and enable the development of new therapeutic interventions to mitigate undesirable effects.

Our study is not without limitations. PULSE-Ox was a small, single-centre mechanistic feasibility study, and as such, these results may not be applicable to other patient cohorts. Many eligible patients were unable to participate because of logistical constraints (primarily staffing, particularly after redeployment during the pandemic). It was considered unethical to reopen recruitment after the pandemic ended, given that the feasibility for larger clinical and cost-effectiveness trials had already been demonstrated by then. All but one (whose surgery was aborted because of unexpected disease progression) underwent long surgical procedures for cancer resections, and ethnic diversity was limited, albeit representative of the regional population. This meant that not all aspects of anaesthetic management (e.g. fluid management) could be standardised across procedures. However, the balanced perioperative characteristics between groups suggest that the randomisation process controlled for this effectively.

In conclusion, we report that randomising to three different intraoperative FiO2s in adult patients undergoing abdominal cancer resections is feasible and unmasked distinct patterns of adaptive redox-metabolomic changes between oxygen groups with considerable inter-individual differences. Administering higher FiO2 did not change SpO2 but resulted in dose-dependent increases in PaO2 and ORi, indicative of enhanced levels of dissolved oxygen in blood and increased tissue oxygen saturation, accompanied by reduced oxygen extraction. Temporal profiling of redox markers revealed that different oxygen concentrations elicit discernible patterns of adaptive change, and individual redox scoring may allow identifying patients at greater risk of future postoperative complications. Overall, it appears that administering FiO2=0.8 intraoperatively overwhelms the body’s redox regulatory capacity, which is associated with reduced oxygen consumption at the whole-body level. Further prospective work is needed to identify the optimum concentration of oxygen to administer intraoperatively during general anaesthesia and to investigate the possibility that hyperoxia leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and unfavourable metabolic reprogramming.
