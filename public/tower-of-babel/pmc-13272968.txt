Neurophysiological effects of partial gravity on bimanual control: a parabolic flight study
Authors: Deanna M. Kennedy, Osmar P. Neto, Madison Weinrich, Renee Abbott, Nathan Keller, Rashika Rao, Traver Wright, Bonnie J. Dunbar, Ana Diaz-Artiles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bimanual coordination is essential for many spaceflight tasks, yet the effects of altered gravity on its behavioral and neural underpinnings remain unclear. This study examined the influence of microgravity (0 g) and partial gravity (0.25–0.75 g) on isometric bimanual coordination during parabolic flight. Participants performed rhythmic force tasks while force and electromyography (EMG) data were collected. Results indicated that mean force and force smoothness differed across gravity conditions, with the most pronounced behavioral impairments occurring in microgravity. Performance under partial gravity (0.25–0.75 g) generally trended toward the 1 g condition. At the neural level, exploratory EMG-EMG cross-wavelet analysis indicated reduced beta band (13–30 Hz) interlimb coordination in 0 g compared with 1 g, whereas traditional coherence measures did not show differences related to gravity. These neural findings should be interpreted cautiously and as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The findings suggest that the absence of gravitational loading is associated with the greatest challenges for coordinated motor output, while partial loading may help preserve performance. These results have implications for astronaut training and countermeasure development for future lunar and Martian missions.

Introduction

Bimanual coordination, the ability to synchronize actions between both limbs, is critical for spaceflight tasks such as operating spacecraft controls, assembling equipment, and conducting experiments. These activities become especially challenging in altered gravity, where the absence or reduction of gravitational forces disrupt motor control strategies1–3. For example, research on manual control has shown slower movement velocities, reduced accuracy, increased variability, less smooth trajectories, and impaired force control in altered gravity1,4–8. These decrements likely reflect challenges in multisensory integration and predictive motor planning rather than deficits in a single modality6,9–14. In particular, impairments may arise both from reduced afferent load related cues (vestibular, proprioceptive) and from feedforward motor commands that fail to anticipate the new gravitational load during early adaptation12,15.

Most prior research investigating manual control has focused on unimanual movements1,5,7,11,16–20, leaving open the question of how bimanual coordination is affected under different gravitational levels. Bimanual coordination requires complex neural integration, such as interhemispheric communication and dynamic interactions between homologous muscles, processes that may be sensitive to changes in gravitational load2,8,21–23. Understanding how both microgravity and partial gravity shape these processes is therefore essential for designing countermeasures to support astronaut performance during space missions and on the Moon and Mars.

Neural crosstalk, which involves the interaction between ipsilateral and contralateral motor commands, has been emphasized as a critical factor in bimanual coordination tasks21,24,25. Neural crosstalk can limit the ability to produce independent limb patterns, particularly under challenging conditions24,26–30. Spaceflight analogs such as head down tilt (HDT), centrifugation, water immersion, and parabolic flight have been widely used to probe these constraints and examine how gravitational load influences motor control1,2,5,7,18,31. Yet the specific neural processes associated with reduced performance in altered gravity remain unclear.

Electromyography (EMG) offers a powerful method for probing neural interactions associated with motor coordination32–34. EMG-EMG cross-wavelet normalized power captures the time-frequency dynamics of intermuscular coupling, while coherence quantifies the strength of frequency-specific synchronization but may be less sensitive under brief or dynamic conditions35–38. Because direct neural recordings are not practical in flight, EMG provides a noninvasive window into the shared neural inputs underlying coordination, making it particularly valuable for studying motor control in altered gravity. Despite this potential, applications of EMG-based intermuscular coupling analyses in altered gravity remain limited.

The present study addresses this need by examining the effects of microgravity and partial gravity on bimanual coordination and intermuscular coupling during parabolic flight. Participants performed isometric force coordination tasks across five gravity levels (0 g, 0.25 g, 0.5 g, 0.75 g, 1 g), while force and EMG measures were used to assess force production stability, harmonicity, and intermuscular synchronization. By combining cross-wavelet normalized power and coherence analyses, we evaluated both the dynamic and frequency-specific aspects of intermuscular coupling across gravitational contexts. Given the brief and novel nature of parabolic flight exposures, neural measures are interpreted as exploratory indices of coupling related to coordination. These results advance our understanding of neuromuscular control in altered gravity and inform countermeasure development for future lunar and Martian missions.

Results

We investigated the effects of varying gravitational levels on bimanual coordination and neural interactions, using parabolic flight to generate gravity conditions of 0 g, 0.25 g, 0.5 g, 0.75 g, and 1 g. Participants performed two bimanual coordination tasks: a 1:1 pattern, where both limbs produced rhythmic force pulses synchronously, and a 1:2 pattern, where the right limb produced two pulses for every pulse of the left limb. Task performance was evaluated using unimanual measures: force harmonicity (H, an index of cycle-by-cycle smoothness), mean force, force variability, inter-peak interval (IPI) and IPI variability, as well as the bimanual measure of IPI ratio. Neural coupling between homologous muscles was assessed with EMG-EMG cross-wavelet normalized power and coherence. Effects primarily reflecting task demands or limb differences are summarized under Task compliance, while the main gravitational effects are presented in the following sections.

Task Compliance and Expected Effects

The task compliance check analyses confirmed the expected task and limb effects. Harmonicity was lower in the 1:2 task compared to the 1:1 task, particularly in the left limb, reflecting the greater difficulty of maintaining complex coordination with the non-dominant limb. Force variability (Standard Deviation, SD; Coefficient of Variation, CV) was consistently higher in the left limb, consistent with known asymmetries in bimanual control39–42 (Fig. 1). Inter-peak intervals (IPI) were shorter in the 1:1 task and longer in the 1:2 task, with the left limb showing greater differences between tasks and higher overall values than the right limb. Similarly, IPI variability (SD IPI) was higher in the 1:2 task than the 1:1 task and was amplified in the left limb (Fig. 2). Finally, IPI ratios clustered around 1.0 in the 1:1 task and 2.0 in the 1:2 task (Fig. 3), demonstrating that participants reliably performed the instructed coordination patterns. Discussion

The study investigated the impact of microgravity and partial gravity on bimanual coordination and intermuscular coupling during parabolic flight. Our primary findings revealed that participants maintained effective timing of bimanual force production across gravity conditions. However, force production was less harmonic in microgravity (0 g) than at higher gravity levels, particularly during the more complex (1:2) task. Mean force production was also lower in 0 g compared to 1 g. These findings indicate that the absence of gravitational loading is associated with reduced force smoothness and output during coordinated tasks.

Exploratory EMG-EMG cross-wavelet analyses revealed reduced beta-band (13–30 Hz) power in 0 g compared to 1 g. Given the post-hoc nature of the frequency-specific comparisons and the dependence on transformation, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory and interpreted with caution. Traditional coherence analysis did not reveal significant gravity-related effects.

Behavioral Effects of Gravity

Behavioral impairments were most pronounced at 0 g, particularly during the complex 1:2 task, where force production became less smooth and more distorted, especially in the left (non-dominant) limb. These findings align with prior work showing that microgravity disrupts the multisensory feedback (e.g., vestibular, proprioceptive, load-related cues) critical for precise movement coordination45,46 and resonate with astronauts reports of difficulty maintaining fine motor control in microgravity47. A possible explanation is that the absence of continuous gravitational input may disrupt the integration of sensory cues related to load, contributing to the observed reductions in force smoothness. In contrast, partial gravity conditions (0.25–0.75 g) generally supported smoother and more harmonic force production, with values at 0.75 g approaching those observed at 1 g. This pattern mirrors evidence that sub-Earth gravitational loads can enhance sensorimotor integration and support motor control7,48,49 and suggests that the greatest challenges occur in the complete absence of load, while partial gravity provides meaningful support for coordinated performance.

Harmonicity results extend prior work by showing that smoothness of force production is degraded in microgravity, particularly for the left limb during the 1:2 complex task. While harmonicity is less commonly reported in the altered-gravity literature than global smoothness indices such as spectral arc length (SPARC) or dimensionless jerk, it is well suited to our rhythmic isometric force paradigm because it captures distortions within each contraction cycle that arise from contralateral interference in bimanual tasks29,50–52. In this respect, our findings align with other reports of reduced movement smoothness in microgravity, such as increased jerk and irregular trajectories during reaching and manipulation tasks18,53,54.

These distortions may reflect heightened susceptibility to neural crosstalk, whereby contralateral motor commands interfere with each other and are harder to suppress in the absence of stabilizing load related feedback21,29,55,56. In microgravity, increased reliance on predictive control strategies may contribute to greater sensitivity to interlimb interference making it more difficult to sustain smooth, independent limb output6,9,21. Transitions to altered gravity may therefore disrupt both feedforward predictions and the weighting of available sensory inputs, consistent with multisensory reweighting models11–13 and updates to internal representation6,9,14. In contrast, partial gravity conditions (e.g., 0.75 g) may provide sufficient gravitational cues to partially restore sensorimotor integration and support smoother output45,57,58. Mean force differed across gravity conditions, with lower values observed in 0 g and 0.25 g relative to 1 g. This finding is consistent with prior reports of reduced force output under unloading and extends these observations to rhythmic bimanual coordination tasks59–61.

Unlike prior studies of dynamic reaching or free-limb movements1,4,7,18,54,62,63, our visually guided isometric task with forearm support produced minimal gravity-related differences in force variability. The Lissajous display may have stabilized performance41,50,64,65, brief trial durations limited the accumulation of variability66–68, and the highly skilled participant sample further dampened variability69,70. These design features likely account for the discrepancy with earlier work reporting stronger variability effects.

EMG Findings and Frequency-Specific Interpretations

Exploratory cross-wavelet analysis revealed reduced beta-band (13–30 Hz) intermuscular coupling in 0 g relative to 1 g. Beta band activity has been linked in prior work to corticospinal contributions to steady intermuscular coordination34,37,38,71,72. The observed reduction may therefore reflect sensitivity of coordination related neural processes to altered gravitational loading. However, given the transformation dependence of the statistical interaction and the absence of supporting coherence effects, this band specific finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Across behavioral and EMG outcomes, performance decrements were most pronounced in 0 g, whereas partial gravity levels (0.25–0.75 g) generally supported performance closer to 1 g. This pattern suggests that the complete absence of gravitational loading poses the greatest challenge for coordinated motor output, while partial loading may provide sufficient sensory input to stabilize performance7,49. It remains possible that reduced proprioceptive and vestibular cues in microgravity contribute to changes in neural coupling6,9–14, but the present findings do not establish a direct mechanistic pathway.

Traditional coherence analysis did not detect significant gravity-related effects. The absence of coherence finding may reflect differences in sensitivity between methods. Coherence captures long-range, sustained coupling and may require prolonged exposure to altered gravity to detect adaptations, whereas cross-wavelet power is more sensitive to transient, task-dependent changes36,37. Additionally, methodological factors such as the high-pass filtering inherent in wireless EMG systems could have attenuated low-frequency components critical for detecting subtle coherence differences in the alpha (8–13 Hz) and lower beta bands (13–20 Hz)73–75. Further discussion of these considerations is provided in the Limitations. Future studies with longer-duration microgravity exposure or alternative environments (e.g., space missions, bed rest studies) may provide further insights into whether these effects reflect transient adaptation or more persistent shifts in corticospinal coupling.

While novelty and brief exposure likely contributed to early performance variability during parabolic flight, the consistent impairment observed in 0 g across multiple behavioral measures supports a gravity related contribution. Because analyses focused on steady portions of each parabola, immediate transition dynamics were not directly examined. Adaptation frameworks predict that early disruptions may be followed by recalibration with prolonged exposure12,13. Thus, the current results likely capture an early adaptation phase rather than steady state performance under sustained microgravity. Future work will be needed to characterize transition specific effects and the time course of adaptation under partial and microgravity.

Our previous ground-based tilt paradigm experiments2,8, provide a useful comparison. Parabolic flight provides true 0 g and partial-g plateaus (0.25–0.75 g), whereas head-up/down tilt modulates axial load and is best interpreted as a partial-g-like unloading analog. Despite these methodological differences, some consistent trends emerge across studies. Both paradigms show reduced force magnitude under unloading (tilt: lower peak force at 0 g compared to 1 g; flight: lower mean force at 0 g compared to 1 g). However, differences were also noted. While tilt studies reported greater force variability at 1 g, our in-flight data showed no significant gravity effect on SD of force/CV of force, although a similar trend was observed, likely reflecting the shorter exposure duration, stabilization of the Lissajous pattern, and our relatively skilled subject sample. Timing measures also diverged. The tilt studies suggested greater IPI variability at 1 g, whereas in flight we observed IPI variability peaking at 0 g and decreasing with increasing g (0 g > 1 g). Coherence results also differed. In the tilt paradigm, force-force coherence was reduced in simulated Martian gravity relative to Earth, especially in the 8–12 Hz band, highlighting impaired interlimb synchronization under partial unloading. In contrast, during parabolic flight, EMG-EMG coherence did not vary across gravity levels, although exploratory cross-wavelet analyses indicated reduced beta-band power in 0 g relative to 1 g. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in measurement domain (force vs. EMG), exposure duration (sustained vs. transient) and analysis approaches (time-resolved vs. averaged coherence). Thus, while both paradigms suggest reduced coupling under unloading, the effect reached significance for coherence in tilt, whereas in flight a difference was observed only in exploratory cross-wavelet analyses. Together, the two paradigms provide complementary perspectives on gravitational effects on bimanual coordination, underscoring both the robustness of timing under unloading and the sensitivity of force control to context and methodology.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

These findings contribute to our understanding of coordination dynamics under altered gravity76,77,78. In line with prior evidence that microgravity disrupts multisensory integration and predictive motor planning13,79, our results demonstrate that altered-gravity affects aspects of bimanual coordination and is associated with differences in frequency-dependent intermuscular coupling. Exploratory cross-wavelet analyses identified a beta-band difference between 0 g and 1 g, whereas coherence measures did not reveal gravity-related effects. Partial gravity conditions (0.25–0.75 g) generally supported performance closer to 1 g48,49,80. By applying cross-wavelet analysis alongside coherence, the present study extends prior approaches and illustrates how time-frequency methods may provide complementary insight into neuromuscular coordination under altered gravity37,38,81.

At the same time, these theoretical insights carry practical implications for astronaut training and mission design. Microgravity has been shown to impair manual control47, and our findings indicate that bimanual coordination is particularly vulnerable in 0 g, with partial gravity mitigating some behavioral effects49,82. Training protocols incorporating partial-gravity simulations (e.g., centrifugation, bed-rest analogs) may help astronauts maintain stable and coordinated force outputs31,82–84. Equipment and tasks design may also benefit from strategies that enhance sensory feedback or stabilize rhythmic force production under unloading conditions8,20,85. For example, biofeedback approaches that reinforce smooth, single-lobed force cycles (high harmonicity) and flags corrective sub-movements could support precision during bimanual tasks in microgravity. Because the neural findings reported here are exploratory, further work is needed before targeting specific frequency bands as training objectives.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the participant sample consisted primarily of highly skilled individuals (e.g., fighter pilots and participants with prior parabolic flight experience). While this minimizes variability in motor performance, it limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, particularly individuals with less motor training or experience in altered gravity environments69,86,87. Caution is advised when extrapolating these results to astronauts with varying training backgrounds or to entirely untrained individuals.

Second, parabolic flights provide only short-duration exposures to each gravity level, which may not capture the full range of physiological adaptations that occur during extended space missions3,19. This temporal limitation restricts our understanding of long term neuromuscular adjustments to sustained microgravity or partial gravity environments.

Third, the use of wireless EMG systems introduces inherent technical constraints. Built-in hardware filters (typically ~20 Hz) can attenuate low-frequency signals in the alpha (5–13 Hz) and lower beta (13–20 Hz) bands, potentially reducing sensitivity to subtle frequency-specific effects36,88. Wireless transmission also carries risks of packet loss or latency fluctuations that may introduce minor artifacts, especially at lower frequencies73,89,90. Although these factors were mitigated through preprocessing, they remain potential sources of error.

Fourth, while EMG-EMG cross-wavelet power provides a time-frequency representation of intermuscular coupling, it is sensitive to signal quality and preprocessing choices. Factors such as electrode shifts, variations in skin impedance, and transient signal fluctuations may influence frequency-domain estimates91–93. The observed beta-band difference depended on variance stabilizing transformation and post-hoc frequency comparisons and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence of gravity dependent neural modulation.

Finally, the absence of significant gravity related effects in EMG coherence warrants careful interpretation. Coherence values are bounded between 0 and 1 and may be reduced by non-coherent intervals or noise. Exposure duration, signal processing characteristics, and the transient nature of parabolic flight may also influence coherence estimates18,19,94–96. Band specific interpretations should therefore be made cautiously. Although no gravity related modulation was detected outside the beta range, the present study may have been underpowered to detect smaller effects in other frequency bands. Accordingly, the current findings should not be taken as evidence of exclusive beta band modulation but rather as an initial indication that frequency-dependent coupling under altered gravity merits further investigation.

Future directions

Further investigation is needed to better characterize how partial gravity environments, such as those on the Moon ( ~ 0.16 g) or Mars ( ~ 0.38 g), affect fine motor tasks and EMG coupling. Exploring a broader range of bimanual tasks (e.g., tool use, maintenance activities, or emergency procedures) and combining cross-wavelet analyses with complementary neural methods (EEG, fNIRS) could clarify how the CNS responds to partial gravity. Additionally, research on countermeasures, including novel training regimens and feedback devices, may support astronauts in maintaining effective bimanual coordination under unloading conditions97. Such work would not only strengthen mission readiness but also offer translational benefits for rehabilitation contexts on Earth, where reduced load or altered sensory environments (e.g., virtual reality) similarly challenge motor coordination65.

Summary

This study provides insights into the effects of altered gravity on bimanual coordination during parabolic flight. Coordination timing remained largely stable across gravity levels. However, force production was less smooth and mean force was lower in microgravity (0 g), with partial gravity conditions (0.25–0.75 g) generally supporting performance closer to 1 g. These findings indicate that the complete absence of gravitational loading poses the greatest challenge for coordinated motor output, whereas partial loading may help preserve more Earth-like performance. Exploring EMG-EMG cross-wavelet analyses identified reduced beta band (13–30 Hz) intermuscular coupling in 0 g relative to 1 g, whereas traditional coherence measures did not reveal gravity-related differences. Given the post-hoc nature of the frequency-specific comparisons and their dependence on transformation, these neural findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory98. These results refine our understanding of motor coordination under altered gravity and underscore the importance of considering both behavioral performance and frequency-dependent neural coupling when evaluating sensorimotor adaptation in spaceflight contexts. While limited by short exposure duration and a highly skilled participant sample, this work provides a foundation for future studies examining longer duration adaptations, partial-gravity environments, and complementary neural measures. The findings have implications for astronaut training, task design, and broader applications in neuromechanics and rehabilitation.

Methods

Participants

Twelve healthy adults (6 males, 6 females; mean age = 40.2 years, SD = 8.7 years) participated in this study. All participants were confirmed to be right limb dominant using the Edinburgh Handedness Survey99 and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Individuals with high susceptibility to motion sickness were excluded. Most participants (10 out of 12) had previous parabolic flight experience. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the NASA Johnson Space Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) (STUDY00000329), the Texas A&M University IRB (STUDY2024-0425), and the Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord Ouest II (Avis no. 22.04602.000171), adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects.

Parabolic Flight

This study was conducted during the 82nd European Space Agency parabolic flight (PFC82) campaign in June 2023. The study was conducted over four parabolic flight days aboard Novespace’s Airbus A-310 Zero G aircraft. The first flight focused on microgravity (0 g) and included 30 parabolas, during which all 12 participants completed 10 trials (one per parabola) for our experiment. Each subject participated in other experiments during the remaining 20 parabolas. The subsequent three flights (4 participants per flight) covered partial gravity conditions (0.25 g, 0.5 g, and 0.75 g) with 10 parabolas per gravity level. The duration of altered gravity was approximately ~20 s (0 g), ~30 s (0.25 g), ~40 seconds (0.5 g), and ~50 s (0.75 g), with pull-up and pull-out phases at 1.8 g (~20 seconds each). 1 g data were collected pre- and post-flight on the ground, but only pre-flight data from the partial gravity day were analyzed to minimize potential training or fatigue effects.

Apparatus

Participants were seated in modified commercial airline seats secured with a lap belt to minimize trunk movement during parabolas. The seats were fitted with custom-mounted aluminum instrument trays affixed to the armrests, which positioned participants’ elbows at 90° angle in contact with the trays to provide stability, minimize proximal motion, and isolate the task to the forearm (see Fig. 5). The forearms were held in a neutral (mid-prone) orientation and stabilized with 3D-printed arm cuffs mounted on the armrests, ensuring proper alignment with the instrument trays. Each arm was outfitted with an adjustable static force measurement system, comprising a force transducer and an amplifier that converted the applied force into a voltage signal. The force transducers were adjusted so that each participant’s wrist contacted the load cells in a position optimal for producing isometric force using the left and right triceps brachii muscles. The position of each participant was continuously monitored to ensure that proper alignment was maintained throughout the experiment. Force signals were recorded using an AD converter (NI USB-6210 Board, National Instruments Corp, Austin, TX, USA) connected to a computer programmed to sample at 200 Hz. Muscle activity was recorded using a wireless EMG) system (Delsys Inc., Boston, MA, USA) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Procedure

Data from four participants were collected simultaneously, with individual force sensors and visual feedback monitors connected to a dedicated laptop per subject. EMG signals from all four subjects were synchronized to the same EMG recording base connected to an additional EMG dedicated laptop. All data were collected and stored using custom LabVIEW software installed on all five laptops. This setup ensured continuous data recording during microgravity and partial gravity periods and an additional 15 seconds during hypergravity ( ~ 1.8 g) phases. Participants received real-time feedback on their force production via 3D mobile theater goggles (Bigeyes H3; Vision Electronics Co.), which displayed a cursor representing their output to facilitate performance monitoring and adjustments during tasks.

Bimanual Coordination Tasks

Participants were required to perform two bimanual coordination tasks: 1:1 in-phase and 1:2 multi-frequency tasks. The 1:1 task required participants to generate synchronized isometric force pulses with both arms simultaneously while the 1:2 task required participants to produce two force pulses with the right arm for every force pulse generated with the left arm (force coordination patterns shown in Fig. 6A, C). While performing the tasks, participants wore a head-mounted display showing a Lissajous plot to guide performance, consisting of a goal template and a cursor representing the forces produced by each arm (Fig. 6B, D). The cursor moved horizontally (left to right) as force was exerted by the right arm and vertically (bottom to top) as force was exerted by the left arm. The goal template outlined the specific force patterns needed for each coordination task. The size of the template was adjusted individually so that the maximum force required corresponded to 20% of each participant’s maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVC). MVC was determined separately for each arm from three 5 s maximal isometric efforts against the transducers, with the highest 5 s average taken as the MVC. Data Collection Procedure

Participants were trained on the coordination tasks one day before the first flight to familiarize themselves with the experimental procedures. Training consisted of 14 practice trials for each coordination task (1:1 and 1:2), with each trial lasting 30 s. Training began with the 1:1 task, followed by the 1:2 task. After training, baseline data were collected with two 30-second trials for each task in the same sequence as the training.

During flight data collection, participants completed two trials of the 1:1 task and three trials of the 1:2 task for each gravitational condition included in the experiment (0 g, 0.25 g, 0.50 g, 0.75 g). Trials began at the onset of the microgravity or partial gravity period and continued throughout their duration, extending an additional 15 seconds into the subsequent hypergravity phase. However, for the present analysis, only data collected during the microgravity or partial gravity period were included. For each participant, performance metrics (described below) for each task and gravity level were averaged across trials (two trials for the 1:1 task and three trials for the 1:2 task). To minimize transient effects at trial onset and offset, the first and last 2 s of each trial were removed prior to averaging.

Measures and data reduction

All data collection and analysis were performed using MATLAB (v2020a, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). Force signals from each arm were sampled at 200 Hz and low-pass at 10 Hz (dual-pass Butterworth) to remove high-frequency noise. EMG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz and bandpass filtered between 20 and 450 Hz using a zero-phase Butterworth filter to remove movement artifacts and high frequency noise. A notch filter was applied to suppress line interference.

Force velocity and acceleration were derived with a 3-point difference algorithm. For each trial, the force signals were detrended to remove slow drifts and rescaled to a normalized range (−1 to 1) before being segmented into single force cycles for analysis. To reduce onset and offset artifacts, the first and last 2 s of each trial were excluded. Performance outcomes were then calculated for both unimanual and bimanual measures. Unimanual metrics included force harmonicity (smoothness of force cycles), mean force, force variability, inter-peak interval (IPI), and inter-peak interval variability (SD of the IPI). Bimanual metrics included IPI ratio, EMG-EMG cross-wavelet power, and EMG-EMG coherence. Together, these measures capture participants’ ability to generate and time isometric force pulses across gravity conditions.

Unimanual Measures

Force Harmonicity (H)

Force harmonicity (H) quantifies the smoothness of rhythmic force production by identifying distortions in the acceleration trace for each limb’s force-time series27,28,30. To compute H, the force trace was segmented into non-overlapping half-cycles defined by consecutive zero-crossings100. Within each half-cycle, extrema of the acceleration trace were extracted and used to classify the cycle. When the acceleration trace contained a single peak with a sinusoidal profile, H was set to 1, indicating smooth, harmonic force production (Fig. 7A). When distortions occurred without a sign change (did not cross the zero-crossing), H was calculated as the ratio of the minimum-to-maximum acceleration ratio extrema, resulting in values between 0 and 1 (Fig. 7B). When the acceleration trace changed signs (crossed from positive to negative or vice versa) within a half-cycle, H was set to 0, indicating disrupted force output (Fig. 7C). Mean H per trial was obtained by averaging across all half-cycles, yielding a continuous measure of harmonicity ranging from 0 (fully inharmonic) to 1 (highly harmonic).Fig. 7Illustration of harmonicity (H) computation from the force-time series.Each panel shows a single half-cycle of force (black line) with its corresponding acceleration trace (green line). Harmonicity was selected because the present task involved rhythmic, pulse-like isometric contractions, where distortions in one limb often arise from contralateral interference during bimanual coordination30,101. Unlike global smoothness such as spectral arc length (SPARC) or dimensionless jerk, which are optimized for continuous kinematic trajectories, harmonicity is cycle-resolved, event-locked to force zero-crossings, robust to baseline shifts and amplitude scaling, and selectively penalizes intra-cycle corrective sub-movements. Prior work has successfully applied harmonicity to detect distortions linked to neural crosstalk in bimanual coordination27–29,40, making it directly relevant to the theoretical mechanisms examined here.

Mean force

Mean force was calculated to determine the control of force for each arm. It was determined by averaging the absolute force produced during each trial.

Force Variability

Force variability was calculated to assess the consistency of force production for each limb within each trial. It was determined by calculating the standard deviation (SD) of the absolute force produced during each trial, with higher variability indicating less consistent force control. Additionally, the coefficient of variation (CV) of force was calculated as the ratio of the SD to mean force for each trial. This measure normalizes the variability relative to the average force output, providing a dimensionless metric to compare relative force consistency across conditions and tasks. Higher CV values indicate greater relative inconsistency in force production.

Interpeak interval (IPI)

IPI represents the average time between two consecutive force peaks during each trial. IPIs were computed for each limb cycle-by-cycle with each cycle representing every other zero crossing of the force signal.

IPI variability (SD IPI)

SD IPI was computed as the standard deviation of IPI within each trial, indicating the stability of timing between peaks.

Bimanual Measures

Inter-Peak Interval Ratio (IPI Ratio)

The IPI ratio was calculated as a measure of coordination between the two limbs based on the timing of force peaks. This metric provides a temporal measure of goal attainment, independent of coordination tendencies and actual force trajectories. To determine IPI ratio, the inter-peak intervals for each arm during a trial were calculated, and the ratio of the right limb’s cycle duration to the left limb’s cycle duration was computed. An IPI ratio of 1.0 indicates that the intervals for the right and left arms are equal, as required in the 1:1 task. An IPI ratio of 2.0 indicates that the interval for the left arm is twice that of the right arm, as required in the 1:2 task.

The decomposition covered frequencies from 5 to 150 Hz, with logarithmic spacing to ensure finer resolution at lower frequencies.

EMG-EMG Coherence

For EMG-EMG coherence analysis, we utilized a wavelet-based coherence approach consistent with the methodology applied to force signals37,38,102. Coherence was computed for both interference EMG signals and high-pass filtered rectified EMG signals (filtered at 250 Hz and rectified) as the mean across the scales of interest and time. High-pass filtering at 250 Hz was employed to isolate high-frequency features analogous to amplitude modulation, enhancing the clarity of oscillatory components in the 5–100 Hz range. This technique reduces amplitude cancellation effects, sharpens the resolution of motor unit action potentials, and minimizes movement-related artifacts56,71. High-pass filtering at 250 Hz before full-wave rectification isolates the motor unit action potential (MUAP) carrier signal and removes low-frequency noise. Rectification then acts as a simple envelope detector, converting the amplitude fluctuations of MUAP trains into the 5–100 Hz range. As a result, coherence of the rectified signal reflects the timing and common drive of MUAP activity rather than low-frequency artifacts, while zero-phase filtering preserves the true phase relationships. This approach is equivalent to computing coherence on the Hilbert envelope of a high-frequency EMG band.

Given that the wireless EMG sensors employed in this study incorporate a built-in high-pass filter around 20 Hz, interference EMG signals may not accurately capture low-frequency content below this threshold73,89,90. This limitation could result in false negatives when analyzing the alpha (5–13 Hz) and low-beta (13–30 Hz) bands, as these signals may lack sufficient low-frequency information. To address this, the high-pass filtered rectified EMG signals preserved the critical envelope and oscillatory characteristics, enhancing the detection of coherence in these bands.

Statistics

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were employed to analyze the effects of Gravity (0 g, 0.25 g, 0.50 g, 0.75 g, 1 g), Task (1:1, 1:2), Limb (right, left), and Frequency Bands (5–13 Hz, 13–30 Hz, 30–60 Hz, 60–100 Hz and 100–150 Hz for EMG data) on various performance and neuromuscular metrics. Random intercepts for each participant were included to account for repeated measures and within-subject variability. The models were implemented using the lmer function in the lme4 package in R (v. 4.4.1). Assumptions were checked using residual diagnostics. When normality or homoscedasticity was violated, variance-stabilizing transformations (e.g., square root) were applied prior to hypothesis testing to satisfy model assumptions. Pairwise comparisons were derived from estimated marginal means (emmeans) with Tukey’s adjustment for comprehensive pairwise sets (p.tukey). For EMG analyses, gravity effects were evaluated across the five predefined frequency bands. For within-slice pairwise inference we report multiplicity-adjusted p-values using both Tukey and Westfall single-step (multivariate t; mvt) adjustments. To address multiplicity across the broader set of EMG analytic choices (frequency bands, post-hoc contrasts, and model variants), we additionally defined an EMG family comprising all EMG post-hoc pairwise tests across the analyzed frequency bands and conditions, including both the Raw and outlier-filtered analyses and (when applicable) the raw-scale and square-root–transformed outcomes; we controlled the false discovery rate across this EMG family using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (reported as q values). Consistent with the breadth of EMG analytic degrees of freedom, EMG findings, particularly those emphasizing a specific band, are described as exploratory/hypothesis-generating. These contrasts were interpreted cautiously and, where applicable, as exploratory.

Our main conclusions are based on statistical models that used the complete dataset. In addition, we ran a sensitivity analysis to ensure our findings weren’t skewed by a few extreme data points (outliers). For this check, outliers were identified globally (across all data for that measure) and within each experimental cell (e.g., 0 g, Task 0, Left Hand), using a criterion in which the distance from the median exceeded 3.5 times the median absolute deviation (MAD). An alternative method using 1.5 x the interquartile range (IQR) fences was applied in rare cases where the MAD was zero. The models were then reran on a temporary dataset with these outliers removed to evaluate robustness.

Statistical results are reported with F-values, p-values, effect sizes (partial η² or ω²) for omnibus tests, along with post-hoc pairwise comparisons including estimates, standard errors (SE), degrees of freedom, t-ratios, and standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g) for pairwise contrasts. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all tests. All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.4.1) within the Visual Studio Code (v1.92.1) environment. Figures and visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism (v10.2.3) for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA) and BioRender.com.
