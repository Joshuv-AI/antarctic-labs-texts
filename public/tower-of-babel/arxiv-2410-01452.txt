Ensembles provably learn equivariance through data augmentation
Oskar Nordenfors, Axel Flinth

Abstract
Recent work has shown that group equivariance emerges in ensembles of neural networks as the result of full augmentation in the limit of infinitely wide neural networks (neural tangent kernel limit). In this paper, we extend this result significantly. We provide a proof that this emergence does not depend on the neural tangent kernel limit at all. We also consider stochastic settings, and furthermore general architectures. For the latter, we provide a simple sufficient condition on the relation between the architecture and the action of the group for our results to hold. We validate our findings through simple numeric experiments.



keywords
Neural networks, ensembles, equivariance

1
Introduction

Consider a learning task with an inherent symmetry. As an illustrative example, we can think of classifying images of, say, apples and pears. The classification should, of course, not change when the image is rotated, which means that the learning task is
invariant
to rotations. If we segment the image, the apple segmentation mask should instead rotate with the image – such tasks are called
equivariant
. An a priori known symmetry like this is a strong inductive bias, which should be possible to use to improve performance of the model.

There are essentially two ’meta-approaches’ to do the latter. The first is to, through data augmentation, ensure that the data respect the symmetry. In our example, one would add rotated versions of the training images to the dataset. This is versatile, simple, and effective. However, there are of course no guarantees that the model after training exactly obeys the symmetry, in particular on out-of-distribution data. To solve this problem, one can use the second meta-approach, namely, to use a model that inherently respects the symmetry. During the last half decade, an entire framework concerning so-called equivariant neural networks has emerged under the name of
Geometric Deep Learning
(GDL)
Bronstein et al. (2021)
.

The relations between these two approaches are, from a theoretical point of view, still unclear: there is yet to emerge a definitive answer to the question of if and when the augmentation strategy is guaranteed to yield an equivariant model after training. Recently, an interesting discovery in this regard was made in
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
. In this paper, it was shown that
ensembles
of
infinitely wide
neural networks (in the neural tangent kernel limit
Jacot et al. (2018)
) are equivariant
in mean
, even though individual members of them are not. See Figure
1
for an illustration. Although this asymptotic result is already interesting, it has some drawbacks. The proofs rely heavily on the simplified dynamics of the NTKs, and therefore do not work at all for network of finite width. Furthermore, the proof only works for finite groups, and is only formulated for the somewhat unrealistic gradient flow training. The purpose of this article is to show a similar result for more realistic scenarios.

1.1
Literature review

The question of the effect of symmetries in the data on the training of neural networks has often been understood as a comparison question: Is it better to train a non-equivariant architecture on augmented (that is, artificially symmetric) data, or to use a manifestly equivariant architecture as, e.g.
Cohen et al. (2019)
;
Maron et al. (2019)
;
Kondor and Trivedi (2018)
;
Weiler and Cesa (2019)
;
Finzi et al. (2021)
;
Fuchs et al. (2020)
? Empirical comparisons between the two approaches as part of experimental evaluations of manifestly equivariant architectures have been made more or less since the invention of equivariant networks – more systematic investigations include
Gandikota et al. (2021)
;
Gerken et al. (2022)
.

In
Chen et al. (2020)
, a group theoretical framework for data augmentation was developed, which has inspired many treatises of the question, including ours. Linear (i.e. kernel) models are studied in
Elesedy and Zaidi (2021)
;
Mei et al. (2021)
;
Dao et al. (2019)
.
Lyle et al. (2019)
;
Lyle et al. (2020)
study so-called
feature-averaged
networks. So-called
linear neural networks
– i.e., neural networks with a linear activation function – are studied in
Chen and Zhu (2024)
and
Duan and Montúfar (2025)
. In these settings, equivalence between augmenting the data and restricting the networks can be proven. The same has not been established for bona fide neural networks with non-linear activation functions – they are studied in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, but there, only local guarantees are derived. Similar results appear in
Simsek et al. (2021)
where the invariance of certain symmetric subspaces of permutations of the weights under gradient flow was demonstrated.

The work most closely related to ours is
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
. There, it was realized that equivariance emerges from augmentation in ensembles, and not in individual networks. They provide a formal explanation of this only in the so-called
neural tangent kernel (NTK) limit

Jacot et al. (2018)
. Importantly, the optimization dynamics of neural networks in the NTK-limit turn linear
Lee et al. (2019)
, so that that setting is considerably simpler than the one we consider here. We should also mention the concurrent
Maass and Fontbona (2024)
, which concerns the behavior of machine learning models trained on augmented data in the
mean-field limit
, which include ensembles. This work is also indirect in that it technically concerns so-called Wasserstein gradient flows of certain functionals, which are limits of the ensemble dynamics. In neither work are architectures other than fully connected ones treated.

Note that this work is
not
about
actively
adapting the training of a priori non-equivariant models to make them (approximately equivariant), such as in e.g.
Pertigkiozoglou et al. (2024)
;
van der Ouderaa et al. (2023)
. Instead, this work is about showing that equivariance emerges
without regularization
in ensembles.

One important line of work, which we will rely on for the presentation of our results, is the study of equivariant training algorithms from
Li et al. (2021)
and
Brugiapaglia et al. (2022)
. In an idealized setting, this, for example, refers to gradient flow on symmetric data. This flow will then be an equivariant flow.

Equivariant flows have been studied before in the context of generative models
Köhler et al. (2020)
;
Katsman et al. (2021)
;
Satorras et al. (2021)
. In this paper, we use results from
Köhler et al. (2020)
in a different context, namely, to study the flow of the
parameters of the network
under (stochastic) gradient flow (descent).

1.2
Contribution

The main message of this paper is that the phenomenon of neural network ensembles being equivariant in mean is
much more general
than
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
suggests. In fact, the emerging equivariance has very little to do with the tangent kernel limit. What instead matter is the equivariance of the
training algorithm
(which applies to gradient descent methods), and symmetric initialization of the weights. We are here heavily inspired by the works
Li et al. (2021)
and
Brugiapaglia et al. (2022)
, but we apply them in a more general setting than before. For example, instead of making an assumption of symmetric data as in
Brugiapaglia et al. (2022)
, we also let the data symmetry be a property of the training algorithm.

More involved architectures

Where previous works have been confined to MLPs, i.e. fully connected neural networks, we derive results in a framework (that the authors developed themselves in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
) that is capable to handle most modern architectures (including transformers). The idea here is to model a neural network architecture as a fully connected architecture with weights confined to an affine subspace
\mathcal{L}
of the parameter space
\mathcal{H}
. We call
\mathcal{L}
the
architecture space
. Our results reveal that the
geometry
of
\mathcal{L}
is key – only when
\mathcal{L}
is
invariant to an action
of the symmetry group are we able to show that ensembles become equivariant. Although we not prove theoretically that the latter condition is necessary, we do numerical experiments that show that when it is not satisfied, the ensembles become less equivariant.

Random augmentations

Another contribution is that we treat the case of mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with randomly drawn data augmentations. This is in contrast to e.g.
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
, where the data usually is assumed to contain the entire orbits under the group action of every element of the data set. Circumventing the latter allows us to treat infinite and finite groups in a unified manner.

Finite ensembles

The main results of the paper only apply to the true mean of the ensemble, which is only realized in a theoretical infinite-ensemble-member limit. In practice, one of course need to resort to sample approximations of this mean. We therefore derive basic results on the sample complexity needed to achieve approximate equivariance. This contribution is minor and not the focus of the work – the bounds are simple, but easy to interpret: We show that for a finite group,
O(\log(|G|))
ensemble members are enough to realize the equivariance approximately for a given element
x
. For a connected Lie group, we show that
O(\mathrm{dim}(G))
sample suffice.

Limitations

We make the same global assumptions as in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
; The symmetry group needs to be compact (excluding e.g. the group of rigid motions
\mathrm{SE}(d)
), the augmentations are done according to the Haar measure on the group (meaning that they are uniform), and also that all non-linearities are equivariant with respect to group actions on the intermediate spaces of the network. The latter requirement is however satisfied in many natural architecture.

Paper outline

In Section
2
, we define our setting, and in particular introduce equivariant training algorithms. We also show the abstract result Lemma : An ensemble of neural networks trained with an equivariant training algorithm becomes equivariant. In Section
3
, we present the architecture space formalism, and prove some important lemmas. In
4
, we then prove the main results: Gradient flow with fully augmented data, and SGD with random augmentations, yield equivariant ensemble as long as the architecture space of the neural network is compatible with the group symmetry. We also show our concentration results for finite ensembles. In
5
, we validate our findings with some simple numerical experiments.

A graphical illustration of our results. The symmetry group here is
C_{2}
, acting on the parameter space through reflection in the
x
-axis, so that the
x
-axis correspond to equivariant models. Snapshots of the parameters of ensemble members as they are trained on symmetric data are shown. At all times, most individual ensemble members do not lie on the line of symmetric models. However, their distribution always is symmetric about the

their mean

2
Preliminaries

Let
X
and
Y
be finite-dimensional vector spaces. We are concerned with neural networks
\Phi_{A}:X\to Y
, that are defined as function of the form

\displaystyle x_{0}\in X,\quad x_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}(A_{i}(x_{i})),\,\forall i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\},\,\Phi_{A}(x_{0})=x_{N}\in Y.

(1)

Here,
A_{i}
are trainable linear maps, i.e. elements of
\Homm(X_{i},X_{i+1})
, and
\sigma_{i}
are fixed non-linearities. We write
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{N-1}\Homm(X_{i},X_{i+1})
for the ambient space for the parameter
A=(A_{i})_{i=0}^{N-1}
.

Let us proceed as in the GDL framework
Bronstein et al. (2021)
and define equivariance with respect to representations of a group. We assume that a group
G
is acting on the spaces
X_{i}
through some
unitary representations

\rho_{i}

Fulton and Harris (2004)
– i.e. maps
\rho_{i}
from the group
G
to the set of unitary matrices that respect the group action:
\rho_{i}(gh)=\rho_{i}(g)\rho_{i}(h)
. Importantly, we can lift these representations to the space of weights.

Example 2.1

(Trivial representation)
.

The simplest possible representation on any space is the
trivial
one, that maps every element
g\in G
to the identity,
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}(g)=\mathrm{id}
. In other words,
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}(g)x=x
for all
g\in G
.

Example 2.2

(Discrete rotations of images)
.

Let
G=C_{4}
act on the space of
n\times n
pixel images by representation
\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}
, which takes the input image and rotates it a multiple of 90 degrees. In other words, if
x:[n]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a grayscale image, then
\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}
acts by

\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}(g)x[i]=x[\pi_{g}^{-1}i],

where
\pi_{g}
is the permutation of the pixels corresponding to the rotation
g
. If we have a tuple of images, then
\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}
acts by rotating each entry in the tuple in the same way.

Definition 1

(Representation on
\Homm
)
.

Given unitary representations
\rho_{i}
on
X_{i}
, we define representations
\widehat{\rho}_{i}
on
\Homm(X_{i},X_{i+1})
through

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{i}(g)(A_{i})=\rho_{i+1}(g)\circ A_{i}\circ\rho_{i}(g)^{-1}

and a representation
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}
as the direct sum of these. We will refer to these representations as the
lifted representations
.

Example 2.3
.

If
G
acts trivially on all spaces, it is not hard to see that the lifted representation
\rho
is also trivial.

Remark 1
.

While the representations on the input and output space are given by the symmetries of the problem and hence are fixed, the representations on the intermediate spaces are a priori free to choose. This point has subtle but important consequences for the meaning of our main results – we will discuss them when we present it.

We call a map
f:X\to Y

\rho
-
equivariant
if
f\circ\rho_{0}(g)=\rho_{N}(g)\circ f
for all
g
. The space of
\rho
-equivariant linear maps between
X_{i}
and
X_{i+1}
is denoted
\Homm_{G}(X_{i},X_{i+1})
. We write
\mathcal{H}_{G}=\bigoplus\Homm_{G}(X_{i},X_{i+1})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{G}=\{A\in\mathcal{H}\vcentcolon\rho(g)A=A\}
.

We will note two assumptions we will make throughout the paper. These assumptions also appear in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
.

Assumption 1
.

The non-linearities
\sigma_{i}
are all
\rho
-equivariant.

Note that without this assumption the strategy of making a neural network equivariant by restricting the linear layers to be equivariant fails.

Assumption 2
.

The group
G
is compact.

This assumption is needed to ensure that the Haar measure of the group
Krantz and Parks (2008)
is (normalizeable to) a probability measure. It should also be noted that when
G
is compact, it is no true restriction to assume that the representation is unitary – one can always ensure this by redefining the inner products on
\mathcal{H}
. The assumption is satisfied by all finite groups, but also groups like
\SO(n)
and
\mathrm{O}(n)
(albeit not the group of all rigid motions
\mathrm{SE}(n)
.)

2.1
Training algorithms

We will assume that we are given training data
(x,y)
distributed according to some distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
X\times Y
. To produce a model from the training data we apply a training algorithm. We will use the definition of training algorithms and iterative training algorithms from
Li et al. (2021)
.

Definition 2

(Training algorithm)
.

A
training algorithm

\mathcal{A}
is a mapping from a sample
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
\mathcal{D}
to a distribution
p
on the space of neural networks
\mathcal{M}=\{\Phi_{A}:X\to Y\}
. For a deterministic training algorithm, the output is instead simply a neural network.

Most training algorithms used in practice are iterative, in the sense that they are defined by repeatedly applying some fixed update rule
U
, that may have random components. In other words, they are Markov chains dependent on the network architecture and data:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)\in S\,|\,A_{t})=\int_{M}u_{\Phi,D}(A^{t},a)\mathrm{d}a,

where the transition function
u_{\Phi,D}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
is dependent on the data and the neural network architecture.

Definition 3

(Iterative training algorithm)
.

Given the following data:

•

A sample
D
from
\mathcal{D}

•

An initial weight distribution
p_{0}
;

•

A number of iterations
T
;

•

A neural network architecture
\Phi_{\cdot}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{M}
.

•

An update rule
U
.

we define an
iterative training algorithm

\mathcal{A}
by the procedure:

1.

Initialize
A^{0}\sim p_{0}
.

2.

For
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
, calculate recursively

A^{t+1}=U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)

3.

Return
\Phi_{A^{T}}
.

Remark 2
.

Denoting by
\mathcal{U}^{t}(D)
the map applying the first
t
iterations of the update rule, we have that if
A^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{T}(D)
, then
\Phi_{A^{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(D)
.

To achieve an equivariant model, it is not enough to merely consider training algorithms; we must consider
equivariant
training algorithms. For a training sample
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, let, for every
g\in G
,
gD\vcentcolon=\{(\rho_{0}(g)x_{i},\rho_{N}(g)y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the transformed training sample.

Definition 4

(Equivariant training algorithm)
.

We say that a training algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
equivariant
if

\rho_{N}(g)^{-1}\circ\mathcal{A}(gD)\circ\rho_{0}(g)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(D),\quad\text{for all }g\in G,\,D\in(X\times Y)^{n},

It is possible to show that iterative training algorithms are equivariant if the update is equivariant. To show this, we need the following Lemma from
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
.

Lemma 2.1

(Equivariance to joint transformation)
.

Let
\Phi_{A}
be a neural network, then, under Assumption (1) that all non-linearities are equivariant, we have that,

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{N}(g)\Phi_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x),

(2)

for every
g\in G
and
x\in X
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the number of layers
N
. To this end, denote by
\Phi^{N}_{A}
the network given by the first
N
layers of
\Phi_{A}
. For
N=1
we have

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(g)\Phi^{1}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}(g)\sigma_{0}(\rho_{1}(g^{-1})A_{0}\rho_{0}(g^{-1})^{-1}x)=\sigma_{0}(\rho_{1}(g)\rho_{1}(g^{-1})A_{0}\rho_{0}(g^{-1})^{-1}x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}(\id A_{0}\rho_{0}(g)x)=\Phi^{1}_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x),\quad\forall g\in G,

where the first equality is by definition of
\rho(g)
, the second equality is by
\rho
-equivariance of
\sigma_{0}
, and the third equality follows since
\rho_{i}(g)
is a representation,
\forall i\in\{0,1\}
,
\forall g\in G
.

Now, assume that
\Phi^{K}_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{K}(g)\Phi^{K}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)
,
\forall g\in G
. We want to show that
\Phi^{K+1}_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{K+1}(g)\Phi^{K+1}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)
,
\forall g\in G
. We have

\displaystyle\rho_{K+1}(g)\Phi^{K+1}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho_{K+1}(g)\sigma_{K}(\widehat{\rho}_{K}(g^{-1})A_{K}\Phi^{K}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x))

\displaystyle=\rho_{K+1}(g)\sigma_{K}(\rho_{K+1}(g^{-1})A_{K}\rho_{K}(g^{-1})^{-1}\Phi^{K}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{K}(\rho_{K+1}(g)\rho_{K+1}(g^{-1})A_{K}\rho_{K}(g^{-1})^{-1}\Phi^{K}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{K}(\id A_{K}\rho_{K}(g)\Phi^{K}_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{K}(A_{K}\Phi^{K}_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x))=\Phi^{K+1}_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x),\quad\forall g\in G,

where the first equality is by the definition of
\Phi_{A}
, the second equality is by the definition of
\widehat{\rho}_{K}(g)
, the third equality follows by
\rho
-equivariance of
\sigma_{K}
, the fourth equality follows since
\rho_{i}(g)
is a representation,
\forall i\in\{K,K+1\}
,
\forall g\in G
, the fifth equality follows by the inductive assumption, and the final equality is just the definition of
\Phi^{K+1}
.

By induction
\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{N}(g)\Phi_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)
,
\forall g\in G
.
∎

This lemma says that if the non-linearities of our network are equivariant, then the entire network is equivariant with respect to the group representation
\rho_{\mathcal{J}}
on
\mathcal{H}\oplus X
defined by
\Phi(\rho_{\mathcal{J}}(g)(A,x))=\Phi_{\rho(g)A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)
and the representation
\rho_{N}
on the output space. This can be seen as a relaxed kind of equivariance. Note that if
\rho(g)A=A
,
\Phi_{A}
becomes equivariant – this is, somewhat streamlined, the core principle behind GDL. Now, note that this holds ’in distribution’ as well: If
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A
for every
g\in G
, then

\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{N}(g)\Phi_{\rho(g^{-1})A}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho_{N}(g)\Phi_{A}(x),\quad\forall g\in G.

We can use this idea to prove the stochastic version of Theorem C.1 from
Li et al. (2021)
.

Lemma 2.2

(Equivariance of update step implies equivariance of training algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an iterative training algorithm. If the update rule satisfies

\displaystyle\rho(g)U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(\rho(g)A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},gD),

(3)

and the initial weight distribution satisfies
p_{0}=p_{0}\circ\rho(g)^{-1}
, for every
g\in G
, then
\mathcal{A}
is an equivariant training algorithm.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. Let
\mathcal{U}^{t}
denote the first
t
iterations of the iterative update rule
U
, so that if
A^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{T}(D)
, then
\Phi_{A^{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(D)
. The base case is just the invariance assumption:
\rho(g)A^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}A^{0}
.

For the inductive step, assume that
\rho(g)A^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{t}(gD)
, then we have

\displaystyle\rho(g)A^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\rho(g)U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(\rho(g)A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},gD)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{t+1}(gD),

where the last equality is by inductive assumption. By induction,
\Phi_{\rho(g)A^{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(gD)
. It follows by Lemma
2.1
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(D)\circ\rho_{0}(g)^{-1}=\Phi_{A^{T}}\circ\rho_{0}(g)^{-1}=\rho_{N}(g)^{-1}\circ\Phi_{\rho(g)A^{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho_{N}(g)^{-1}\circ\mathcal{A}(gD),

which is what was to be shown.
∎

2.2
Ensembles

Ensembling refers to the practice of training a number of independent base models, and then averaging them. In our setting, we may define them as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{A^{t}}](x),\quad\Phi_{A^{t}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(D).

(4)

When the training algorithm is gradient flow, this definition is the same as in
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
.

One should note that this is an idealization. In practice, one needs to resort to a sample mean of the distribution
\mathcal{A}(D)
via initializing
M
neural networks (according to the initialization defined by
\mathcal{A}
), training them independently, and then averaging them after
t
steps. This is the strategy we will follow in the experimental section. The bulk of our claims will be about the the true mean, but will get back to the effects of finite sampling in Section
4.3
.

If an ensemble is trained with an equivariant algorithm on symmetric data, its mean will be equivariant. More specifically, we have the following.

Theorem 2.3

(Equivariant training leads to equivariant ensembles)
.

Assume that an ensemble model is trained with an equivariant training algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on a dataset
D
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(gD),

(5)

for every
g\in G
. Then, the ensemble model is equivariant.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an equivariant training algorithm and let
\Phi_{A^{t}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
. Then, by Equation
5
, we have,

\displaystyle\Phi_{A^{t}}\circ\rho_{0}(g)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho_{N}(g)\circ\Phi_{A^{t}}.

Taking the expectation of both sides yields

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{A^{t}}]\circ\rho_{0}(g))(x)=(\rho_{N}(g)\circ\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{A^{t}}])(x),

for every
g\in G
and
x\in X
. This is the same as,

\displaystyle\overline{\Phi}_{t}(\rho_{0}(g)x)=\rho_{N}(g)\overline{\Phi}_{t}(x).

∎

Remark 3
.

If the dataset is symmetric, that is if

D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}gD,

then the condition

\mathcal{A}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(gD)

holds trivially. This observation was made in
Brugiapaglia et al. (2022)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

(Data symmetric update yields data symmetric algorithm)
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an iterative training algorithm with update rule
U
. If
U
satisfies

\displaystyle U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},gD),

(6)

for every
g\in G
and every
D
, then
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mathcal{A}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(gD)
for every
g\in G
and every
D
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an iterative training algorithm with an update rule
U
satisfying equation
6
. Letting
\mathcal{U}^{t}
denote the first
t
iterations of the iterative update rule
U
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{1}(D)=U(A^{0},\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(A^{0},\Phi_{\cdot},gD)=\mathcal{U}^{1}(gD),

by Equation
6
. For the inductive step, if
\mathcal{U}^{t}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{t}(gD)
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{t+1}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(\mathcal{U}^{t}(D),\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(\mathcal{U}^{t}(gD),\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U(\mathcal{U}^{t}(gD),\Phi_{\cdot},gD)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\mathcal{U}^{t+1}(gD),

where the first and last equalities are by definition, the second equality by the inductive assumption, and the third equality is by Equation
6
. The statement now follows by induction.
∎

Thus, we see that symmetric data is not a necessity for Equation
5
to hold – it suffices that the update rule does not change if we transform the data. In Section
4
, we will show that mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with data batches that are augmented ’on the fly’.

3
The architecture space formalism

The model defined in Section
2
is really only a faithful model for fully-connected feedforward neural networks without bias. As was realized in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, we may conveniently include other architectures in the framework (biases, CNN:s, RNN:s, transformers, …) by confining the tuple of linear maps to an
affine subspace

\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
which we will refer to as the
architecture space
. Let recall and motivate this definition by giving some examples of architecture spaces, including one that we will use as a running example in the following.

Example 3.1

(Trivial architecture space)
.

In a fully connected network without bias, all layers are linear, and the weights are not constrained at all. Hence, MLP:s without bias have architecture space
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}
. We refer to this as the
trivial architecture space
.

Example 3.2

(Architectures with bias)
.

Biases can be included by the standard ’homogenization’ trick – add a ’dummy feature’ to all intermediate spaces
\widetilde{X}_{i}=X_{i}\oplus\mathbb{R}
, then restrict the maps between
\widetilde{X}_{i}
and
\widetilde{X}_{i+1}
to the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}=\bigg\{H_{A,b}=\begin{bmatrix}A&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\,\big|\,A\in\Homm(X_{i},X_{i+1}),b\in X_{i+1}\bigg\}.

The linear maps in
\mathcal{B}_{i}
then emulate the action of affine maps:
H_{A,b}(x,1)=(Ax+b,1)
. The architecture space of the net as a whole is then a direct sum:
\mathcal{L}=\oplus_{i\in[L]}\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Note that it is always possible to construct an architecture space ’layer-wise’ in this manner.

Figure 2:
Symmetric (left) and asymmetric (right) filter. Indices in the support are grey.

Example 3.3

(Running example: Convolutional layers)
.

For some integers
d_{i}
, let
X_{i}=(\mathbb{R}^{n,n})^{d_{i}}
denote spaces of tuples of images. We may define a
convolutional operator
between two such spaces as operators
C
of the form

\displaystyle(Cx)_{k}=\sum_{\ell\in[d_{i}]}\psi_{k\ell}*x_{\ell},

where
\psi_{k\ell}
,
k\in[d_{i+1}],\ell\in[d_{i}]
are filters. Let us denote this operator by
\langle\psi\rangle
.
The space of all operators of this form, where the filters supports are all contained in a fixed set
\Omega
, form a linear (and therefore affine) subspace
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{H}
. The corresponding architecture is a CNN.

Common choices for
\Omega
are
3\times 3
or
5\times 5
squares. Just as was done in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, we will additionally here consider the two non-canonical supports in Figure
2
, since they illustrate important aspects of our results. We call the left support the
symmetric
support, and the right the
asymmetric
support, and denote the corresponding architecture spaces
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sym}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{as}}
.

As is carefully presented in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, many more architectures can be thought of as an MLP with weights confined in an architecture space
\mathcal{L}
, including e.g. RNNs and transformers. Importantly, the non-linearities
\sigma
often need to be chosen in a non-standard way (in for instance transformers,
\sigma
is responsible for the attention mechanism). We refer to the appendix of said article for detailed arguments.

Let us now state an assumption we will make throughout the text.

Assumption 3
.

The space
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{H}_{G}
is non-empty.

This assumption ensures that there exists an admissible and
\rho
-equivariant architecture. It also has a technical consequence that we will need: it allows us to write the architecture space
\mathcal{L}=A_{\mathcal{L}}+\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
, for an
A_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{H}_{G}\cap\mathcal{L}
, and
\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
the tangent space of
\mathcal{L}
.

3.1
The invariance criterion

In
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, neural network training with augmented data was compared with the ’layerwise equivarization strategy’ from geometric deep learning. The latter, in our language, simply means confining the layers
A_{i}
to be equivariant with respect to the intermediate representations, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{G}
. It turned out that the sets
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{H}_{G}
are invariant under augmented training dynamics
under some additional geometric assumptions on
\mathcal{L}
. The story in this paper will be the same: In order for ensembling to yield equivariance,
\mathcal{L}
needs to be compatible with the lifted representation, in the following manner.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. The space
\mathcal{L}
is
\rho
-invariant if
\rho(g)\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
for all
g\in G
.

Intuitively,
\rho
-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
can be interpreted as the corresponding architecture not being
inherently biased
against being
\rho
-equivariant.

The condition of
\rho
-invariance was again already used in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, to derive the so-called
compatibility condition
(meaning that
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
commutes with the orthogonal projection
\Pi_{G}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{G}
) needed to establish the results of that paper. Many examples of
\rho
-invariant architecture spaces corresponding to commonly used architectures and reasonable group representations were given. Examples included fully architectures connected ones with and without bias, RNNs, residual connections, etc. Let us here discuss a few important examples.

Example 3.4

(Convolutions)
.

Consider the
\mathcal{L}
corresponding to a convolutional architecture, and let
C_{4}
act through
\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}
(see Example
2.2
) on all intermediate spaces. It is not hard to show that the the corresponding lifted representation acts by rotating each filter,
\rho(g)\langle\psi\rangle=\langle\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}(g)\psi\rangle
. (For a formal proof, see for example
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
). Consequently, a
\langle\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sym}}
will be mapped by
\rho
onto a tuple of other symmetric filters, so that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sym}}
is
\rho
-invariant. By the same argument,
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{as}}
is not.
As we previously remarked (Remark
1
), there is however no need to choose the
\rho^{\mathrm{rot}}
-representations in the intermediate layers. However, it turns out that representations on the intermediate spaces making
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{as}}

\rho
-invariant do not exist. The somewhat involved proof for this is given in Appendix
6
.

Example 3.5

(Trivial architecture spaces and representations)
.

The trivial architecture space is invariant independently of
G
and
\rho
, since we of course always have
\rho(g)\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, by the virtue of
\rho
being a representation. On the other hand, if the lifted representation is trivial, any architecture space
\mathcal{L}
will be
\rho
-invariant, since
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}(g)\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{id}(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}
for all
g\in G
in that case.

Example 3.6

(Pre- and post MLP:s)
.

In fact, the observations in the previous examples one can be used to argue that any architecture in a certain sense can be modified into an invariant one. Let us assume that an architecture with architecture space
\mathcal{L}
for parametrizing functions
X\to Y
is given. The representations of the group on
X
and
Y
,
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{N}
, are fixed, but as we remarked in
1
, the ones on the intermediate spaces are not. Now let us modify our architecture as follows: Instead of directly inputting the data in
X
into the network, we pre-compose it with a fully connected MLP
X\to X
. In the same manner, we do not use the output of the network directly, but post-compose it with a fully connected MLP
Y\to Y
. The architecture space of the resulting architecture gets the form
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{extended}}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{pre}}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{post}}
.

Now, we choose the intermediate representations
\rho_{i}
on all intermediate spaces to be trivial. It is then not hard to see that the lifted representation
\rho
on
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{extended}}
decomposes as
\rho^{\mathrm{pre}}\oplus\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}\oplus\rho^{\mathrm{post}}
, where
\rho^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
\rho^{\mathrm{post}}
are some possibly non-trivial representations. Now, since
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{post}}
are trivial architecture spaces, they will be invariant under
\rho^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
\rho^{\mathrm{post}}
, respectively. On the other hand,
\mathcal{L}
will of course be invariant under
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}
. Hence,
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{extended}}
will
always
be
\rho
-invariant. We record this in Proposition
3.1
below.

As for our global assumptions, note that all non-linearities in the resulting structures map between spaces on which
G
acts trivially, so that
1
is satisfied. As for the assumption
3
of
\mathcal{H}_{G}
being non-empty, note the zero map is always equivariant with respect to
\rho^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}
, and
\rho^{\mathrm{triv}}
and
\rho^{\mathrm{post}}
, respectively. It is also clear that the zero map is contained in the non-restricted
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{pre}}
- and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{post}}
-spaces, so that
\mathcal{H}_{G}\cap\mathcal{L}
is still non-empty after composing with an MLP.

Using pre-and post MLP:s like this may be detrimental for performance – all spatial structure of the data is destroyed, for instance. The construction is still interesting from a theoretical point of view, as it works for any group and representation, even a priori unknown ones.

Proposition 3.1

(Composing with MLPs yields invariance)
.

Any architecture can be turned into an invariant one by pre-and post composing it with MLP:s, and ( re - ) choosing all intermediate representations to be trivial.

Before moving on, let us prove one crucial lemma for what follows:
\rho
-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
is equivalent to
\rho
-equivariance of the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
. Note that this a well-known result from linear algebra. We include a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.2

(Orthogonal projection onto invariant subspace is equivariant)
.

\mathcal{L}
is
\rho
-invariant if and only if
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
is
\rho
-equivariant.

Proof.

Let us begin by proving the
only if
part. Assume that
\rho(g)\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\forall g\in G
. Clearly, if
\exists B\in\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
,
\exists g\in G
, such that
\rho(g)B\notin\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
, then
A=A_{\mathcal{L}}+B\in\mathcal{L}
, but
\rho(g)A=\rho(g)A_{\mathcal{L}}+\rho(g)B=A_{\mathcal{L}}+\rho(g)B\notin\mathcal{L}
. By contraposition, we must have
\rho(g)\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
,
\forall g\in G
, since we have assumed that
\rho(g)\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\forall g\in G
. Now, suppose that
x\in\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
and
y\in\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\langle\rho(g)y,x\rangle=\langle y,\rho(g)^{*}x\rangle=\langle y,\rho(g)^{-1}x\rangle=\langle y,\rho(g^{-1})x\rangle=0,\quad\forall g\in G,

where the second equality is by unitarity, the third equality is by definition of representations, and the final equality follows from
\rho(g)\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
,
\forall g\in G
. Thus, it holds that
\rho(g)\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
,
\forall g\in G
. We can decompose any
z\in\mathcal{H}
as
z=z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}+z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}
, where
z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}z
and
z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}z
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)z=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)(z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}+z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}})=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}+\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}=\rho(g)z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}=\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}z,

\forall g\in G
, where the second equality is by linearity, the third equality follows by
\rho(g)\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
and
\rho(g)\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
,
\forall g\in G
, and the last equality is by definition of
z_{\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}}
. This concludes the
only if
part of the proof.

Let us now prove the
if
part of the proof. Assume that
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
is
\rho
-equivariant. Then we have for any
B\in\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
that

\displaystyle\rho(g)B=\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}B=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)B\in\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L},\quad\forall g\in G,

since
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a
\rho
-equivariant projection onto
\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
. Thus, we have for any
A\in\mathcal{L}
that

\displaystyle\rho(g)A=\rho(g)(A_{\mathcal{L}}+B)=\rho(g)A_{\mathcal{L}}+\rho(g)B=A_{\mathcal{L}}+\rho(g)B\in\mathcal{L},\quad\forall g\in G,

since
\mathcal{L}=A_{\mathcal{L}}+\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
with
\rho(g)A_{\mathcal{L}}=A_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\rho(g)
is linear.
∎

4
Equivariance of augmented neural network training

This section will be devoted to proving our main theorem: training a neural network with a
\rho
-invariant architecture space
\mathcal{L}
with augmentation will yield equivariant ensembles. We will consider two algorithms: gradient flow with ’full augmentation’ (i.e., training using all transformed versions of each data points with infinitesimal learning rate) and stochastic gradient descent with random augmentations.
The strategy is clear: each of the algorithms, we will show that they are equivariant in the sense of Definition
4
and fulfill Equation
5
, and then apply Theorem
2.3
.

Before going into the details of each algorithm, let us give the general setting: We consider a fixed neural network architecture
\Psi_{A}
, with weights
A
confined to an architecture space
\mathcal{L}
. Given a set
D
of training data from
X\times Y
and a loss function
\ell:Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
, we define a
nominal risk function

\displaystyle R_{D}(A)=\frac{1}{|{D}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\ell(\Psi_{A}(x),y)).

In order to prove our results, we will need to make one more global assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The loss function
\ell
is
\rho
-invariant, that is
\ell(\rho_{N}(g)y,\rho_{N}(g)y^{\prime})=\ell(y,y^{\prime}).

This assumption can be interpreted as saying that the loss should not be biased towards non-equivariant functions. This assumption was also used in
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, and it was there argued that it is often satisfied. As an example, if
G
acts trivially on
Y
(i.e., we are striving for an invariant model),
\ell
is trivially
\rho
-invariant.

We are going to train the neural network by minimizing modified versions of
R
over
\mathcal{L}
using modified versions of gradient flow/descent algorithms. To do so practically, we use a parametrization of
\mathcal{L}

c\mapsto A_{\mathcal{L}}+Lc
, where
A_{\mathcal{L}}
is a ’basepoint’ in
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{H}_{G}
, and
L:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
an isometric embedding, meaning that
L^{*}L=\mathrm{id}
, and then optimize the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(c)=R(A_{\mathcal{L}}+Lc).

Notice that in the examples we have discussed, such an
L
is easy to describe – for example in the convolutional case,
L
takes a vector of pixel values and inserts them to the pixels of the filter.

Importantly, calculating gradients of
\mathrm{R}(c)
and then using them to update
A
through the parametrization is the same as calculating gradients of
R
and then projecting them to
\mathrm{T}\mathcal{L}
, since

\displaystyle L\nabla\mathrm{R}(c)=LL^{*}\nabla R(A_{\mathcal{L}}+Lc)=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla R(A),

where
LL^{*}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
follows from the fact that
L
is an isometric embedding.

To satisfy the conditions of Lemma
2.2
, we need to have
\rho
-invariantly initialized weights. Note that a Gaussian initialization of the coefficients
c
will, as soon as
\mathcal{L}
is
\rho
-invariant, yield a
\rho
-invariant distributions of the parameters
A
:

Lemma 4.1

(Invariant distribution of coefficients implies invariant distribution of weights)
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathcal{L}
be a parametrization of
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{L}=A_{\mathcal{L}}+L\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
A_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{H}_{G}
. Then, if
c
is a standard Gaussian vector (with i.i.d. entries), and
\mathcal{L}
is
\rho
-invariant,
A=A_{\mathcal{L}}+Lc
will have a
\rho
-invariant distribution.

Proof.

Because
c
is standard Gaussian,
A
will also have a Gaussian distribution, with mean
A_{\mathcal{L}}
and covariance matrix
LL^{*}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
, where the latter follows from the fact that
L
is unitary. Now, by the same argument,
\rho(g)A=\rho(g)A_{\mathcal{L}}+\rho(g)Lc
will be Gaussian with mean
\rho(g)A_{\mathcal{L}}=A_{\mathcal{L}}
– remember that
A_{\mathcal{L}}\in\mathcal{H}_{G}
– and covariance
\rho(g)L(\rho(g)L)^{*}
. We may now argue

\displaystyle\rho(g)L(\rho(g)L)^{*}=\rho(g)LL^{*}\rho(g)^{*}=\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)^{*}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)\rho(g)^{*}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}.

The penultimate step follows from the
\rho
-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
, and the final one from the unitarity of
\rho(g)
. This shows that
A
and
\rho(g)A
both are Gaussians with mean
A_{\mathcal{L}}
and covariance
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
, and we are done.
∎

Remark 4
.

Note that there is of course no necessity to use a Gaussian initialization in general. For instance, if the intermediate representations are all trivial (which we know we can always use in the MLP setting, see Example
3.5
),
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A
is actually a vacuous condition for all but the first layer. Note that Li, Zhang and Arora in
Li et al. (2021)
remark that they only need to assume that the first layer is invariantly initialized to prove their results – since they are using MLPs, this is in perfect resonance with our argument here.

4.1
Gradient flow

Let us first consider the strategy of applying gradient flow to a fully augmented risk. Gradient flow hereby refers to letting the parameters
A
flow according to the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{A}=-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla R_{D}(A)

for some fixed time
t
, from some initialization
A_{0}
. Equivalently, we can phrase this as applying the
flow map

\mathcal{F}^{t}_{D}
of
R_{D}
once. That is, apply the update map

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{GF}}(A^{0},\Phi_{\cdot},D)=\mathcal{F}_{D}^{t}(A^{0})

once to a randomly initialized weight
A^{0}
.

By Lemma
2.2
, this yields an equivariant training algorithm if the initialization is
\rho
-invariant and the update is
U^{\mathrm{GF}}
is equivariant.

Lemma 4.2

(Equivariance of
U^{GF}
)
.

In addition to Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
, assume that
\mathcal{L}
is
\rho
-invariant. Assume further that
R_{D}
continuously differentiable in
A
. Then,
U^{\mathrm{GF}}
is equivariant.

Proof.

The smoothness assumption shows that the flow map is well-defined (by Picard-Lindelöf).

Let us first show that under our assumption, we have
R_{gD}(\rho(g)A)=R_{D}(A)
.

\displaystyle R_{gD}(\rho(g)A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|{D}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\ell(\Psi_{\rho(g)A}(\rho_{0}(g)x),\rho_{N}(g)y))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem.}\ref{lem:jointequi}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|{D}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\ell(\rho_{N}(g)\Psi_{A}(x),\rho_{N}(g)y))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ell\text{ inv.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|{D}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\ell(\Psi_{A}(x),y)=R_{D}(A).

Differentiating the equality
R_{gD}(\rho(g)A)=R_{D}(A)
yields
\nabla R_{D}(A)=\rho(g)^{*}\nabla R_{gD}(\rho(g)A)
, which together with the unitarity of
\rho
yields

\displaystyle\nabla R_{gD}(\rho(g)A)=\rho(g)\nabla_{D}R(A).

(7)

Equipped with (
7
), the equivariance of
U^{\mathrm{GF}}
now follows by some standard ODE theory arguments: By definition, what is to be proven is
\mathcal{F}_{gD}(\rho(g)A^{0})=\rho(g)\mathcal{F}_{D}(A^{0})
. That is:

\displaystyle\text{ If $\gamma$ solves}\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma}(s)&=-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla_{D}R(\gamma(s))\\
\gamma(0)&=A^{0}\end{cases},\text{ $\gamma^{g}:=\rho(g)\gamma$ solves }\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma^{g}}(s)&=-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla_{D}R(\gamma^{g}(s))\\
\gamma^{g}(0)&=\rho(g)A^{0}\end{cases}

It is clear that
\gamma^{g}(0)=\rho(g)\gamma(0)=\rho(g)A^{0}
. As for the differential equation, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma^{g}}(s)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho(g)\text{ linear}}}{{=}}\rho(g)\dot{\gamma}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma\text{ solves ODE}}}{{=}}-\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla_{D}R(\gamma(s))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:eqviproj}}}{{=}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)\nabla_{D}R(\gamma(s))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{eq:gradient_transformation}}}}{{=}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla R_{gD}(\rho(g)\gamma(s))=-\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla R_{gD}(\gamma^{g}(s)).

∎

One way to view this result is that it is essentially a consequence of the fact that an equivariant vector field yields an equivariant flow map, which is proved in
Köhler et al. (2020)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Ensembles of i.i.d invariant initialized neural networks with a
\rho
-invariant architecture space trained with gradient flow on fully augmentated datasets are equivariant.

Proof.

Combine Lemmata
2.2
,
4.2
, Theorem
2.3
and
3
.
∎

Remark 5
.

As we have previously noted, full group augmentation is practically impossible when dealing with infinite groups. Theorem
4.10
is however still relevant for it. That is, we could approximate the full augmentation by augmenting with a finite subset
H
of
G
. Then, the risk we should optimize is
R^{H}(A)=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}R(\rho(h)A).

If
H
is a subgroup of
G
, this directly corresponds to performing full augmentation with the smaller set
H
. For instance, one could approximate
\mathrm{SO}(3)
by the subgroup of rotational symmetries of an icosahedron. Our results then immediately imply that the ensemble models will be equivariant with respect to that subgroup.

4.2
Stochastic gradient descent

We now move on to
mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with random augmentation
. This is again an iterative algorithm, with steps defined as follows: At each step, we uniformly draw a batch
B_{t}\subseteq D
of
s
data-examples from the training set
D
together with
s
i.i.d Haar-distributed group elements
g_{k}
. We apply
(\rho_{0}\oplus\rho_{N})(g_{k})
to
(x_{k},y_{k})
, for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, then calculate the average gradient of the estimated risk over the batch. Let us denote

\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})y_{k})

then, given some sequence of step-sizes
\{\gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, we can define the update rule

\displaystyle A^{t+1}=U^{SGD}(A^{t},\Phi_{\cdot},D)\vcentcolon=A^{t}-\gamma_{t+1}\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A^{t}).

(8)

We will assume that the draw of data points and group elements for each batch is independent of each other for every time step and of the weights at every previous time step.

Remark 6
.

Independence of the draw of batches and the weights is a faithful model for SGD where the batches are drawn with replacement, which also includes the deterministic GD setting. The assumption of independence of the batches
B_{t}
and weights
\{A^{k}\}_{k=0}^{t-1}
is necessary for our proofs, but is an idealization of the way SGD really works. In practice, the batches in mini-batch SGD are
dependent
since they are sampled without replacement.

Note that the projected descent emerges in the same way as above by updating the parametrization coefficients
c^{t}
. Each realization of the process
A^{t}
corresponds to one run of the SGD.
We want to show that mini-batch SGD with random augmentation yields an equivariant ensemble, when applied to invariantly initialized weights. Let us begin by proving the following very intuitive lemma about random functions: If two random functions, that almost surely are differentiable, have the property that their pointwise distributions are equal, the same is true for their gradients. For the proof, we will invoke the Cramér-Wold Theorem
Cramér and Wold (1936)
.

Theorem 4.4

(Cramér-Wold)
.

A probability distribution
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is uniquely determined by the set of distributions
p\circ\pi_{v}^{-1}
on
\mathbb{R}
, indexed by the unit vector
v
, where
\pi_{v}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto\langle x,v\rangle
.

We now formulate and prove the lemma.

Lemma 4.5

(Equidistributed functions have equidistributed gradients)
.

Let
\varphi
and
\psi
be random functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that almost surely are
C^{1}
. If for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\psi(x)
, then also
\nabla\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\nabla\psi(x)
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\psi(x)
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle X_{n}\vcentcolon=\frac{\varphi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\varphi(x)}{\frac{1}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\frac{\psi(x+\frac{1}{n}v)-\psi(x)}{\frac{1}{n}}=\vcentcolon Y_{n},\quad\forall x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Now, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
v\in S^{d-1}
,
X_{n}\to\langle\nabla\varphi(x),v\rangle
and
Y_{n}\to\langle\nabla\psi(x),v\rangle
a.s. as
n\to\infty
. Thus, the distributions of the limit random variables (i.e., the distributions of the directional derivative in direction
v
) also agree. So that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\varphi(x),v\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\langle\nabla\psi(x),v\rangle,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\forall v\in S^{d-1}.

Since this holds for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
v\in S^{d-1}
, we can apply the Cramér-Wold Theorem to conclude that

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\nabla\psi(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

∎

From the above it follows that an invariant function has an equivariant gradient.

Lemma 4.6

(Invariant function has equivariant gradient)
.

If
f
is a random function
\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
that almost surely is
C^{1}
and satisfies
f(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}(f\circ\rho(g))(A)
, for every
g\in G
and every
A\in\mathcal{H}
, then
\nabla f
satisfies
\nabla f(\rho(g)A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)\nabla f(A)
, for every
g\in G
and every
A\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\nabla f(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\nabla(f\circ\rho(g))(A)=\rho(g)^{*}\nabla f(\rho(g)A),\quad\forall g\in G,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{H},

where the first equality in distribution follows from Lemma
4.5
with
\varphi=f
and
\psi=f\circ\rho(g)
, and the second is simply the chain rule. The result follows by applying
\rho(g)
to both sides of the equality.
∎

To apply the above result to our risk, we need to demonstrate its invariance. Let us do this next.

Lemma 4.7

(Invariance of
\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}
)
.

The risk
\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}
satisfies

(i)

\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{gD}}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)
,

(ii)

\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(\rho(g)A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)
,

for every
g\in G
,
A\in\mathcal{H}
, and
D
.

Proof.

We will begin by proving (i). We have for
h\in G
arbitrary, due to
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{N}
being distributions,

\displaystyle\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{hD}}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k})\rho_{0}(h)x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})\rho_{N}(h)y_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k}h)x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k}h)y_{k}).

Now, by the invariance property of the Haar measure, the tuple of group elements
g_{1},\dots g_{s}
are equidistributed with the tuple
g_{1}h,\dots g_{s}h
. Since the data points
(x_{k},y_{k})
are independent of the draw of the
g_{k}
, we can conclude that the above is equidistributed with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})y_{k})=\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A),

which is what we wanted to show.
Next, we will prove (ii). Applying Lemma
2.1
, we get that for every
A\in\mathcal{H}
that

\displaystyle\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(\rho(h)A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{\rho(h)A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})y_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\rho_{N}(h)\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(h)^{-1}\rho_{0}(g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})y_{k}).

Invoking the
\rho
-invariance of
\ell
and then that
\rho
is a representation, the above can be rewritten to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(h^{-1}g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(h^{-1}g_{k})y_{k})

Now, by the invariance property of the Haar measure, the tuple of group elements
g_{1},\dots g_{s}
are equidistributed with the tuple
h^{-1}g_{1},\dots h^{-1}g_{s}
. Since the data points
(x_{k},y_{k})
are independent of the draw of the
g_{k}
, we can conclude that the above is equidistributed with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\ell(\Phi_{A}(\rho_{0}(g_{k})x_{k}),\rho_{N}(g_{k})y_{k})=\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A),

which is what we wanted to show.
∎

We now show that the gradient of
\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}
, in a distributional sense, is equivariant.

Lemma 4.8

(Equivariance of
\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}
)
.

The gradient of
\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}
satisfies

(i)

\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{gD}}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)

(ii)

\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(\rho(g)A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)
,

for every
g\in G
,
A\in\mathcal{H}
, and
D
.

Proof.

(i) follows by Lemma
4.5
and (ii) by Lemmata
4.6
and
4.7
.
∎

Lemma 4.9

(Equivariance and data symmetry of
U^{\mathrm{SGD}}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be
\rho
-invariant, then the update rule
U^{\mathrm{SGD}}
satisfies

(i)

U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},gD)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},D)
,

(ii)

\rho(g)U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(\rho(g)A,\Phi_{\cdot},gD)
,

for every
g\in G
, and
D
.

Proof.

Let us first prove (i). By Lemma
4.8
(i)., we have that

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},gD)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{gD}}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},D),

which proves (i). For the second part, we will again use Lemma
4.8
, which yields

\displaystyle\rho(g)U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(A,\Phi_{\cdot},D)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)\big(A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A-\rho(g)\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{D}}(\rho(g)A)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\rho(g)A-\gamma\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\nabla\widehat{R^{\mathrm{aug}}_{gD}}(\rho(g)A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}U^{\mathrm{SGD}}(\rho(g)A,\Phi_{\cdot},gD),

where the third equality follows by
\rho
-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
and Lemma
3.2
, the fourth equality follows from Lemma
4.8
(ii), and the fifth equality follows from Lemma
4.8
(i). ∎

With this, we are now equipped to prove the main result of the paper.

Theorem 4.10

(Main theorem: Equivariance of ensembles trained by mini-batch SGD with random augmentations)
.

Ensembles of i.i.d invariant initialized neural networks with a
\rho
-invariant architecture space trained with mini-batch SGD with random augmentations are equivariant.

Proof.

By Lemmata
4.9
,
2.2
, and
2.4
, mini-batch SGD with random augmentations satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, by which it follows that ensembles trained by mini-batch SGD with random augmentations are equivariant.
∎

4.3
Finite ensembles

All of the results up until now are concerned with the properties of
\overline{\Phi}_{t}
, i.e. the true mean of the ensemble over the random draw of the initialization and the randomness of the training algorithm. In practice, one of course needs to resort to approximate this mean via sampling: That is, draw
M
independent initializations
A^{0}_{k}
, train each model separately to weights
A_{k}^{t}
, and then evaluate the sample mean

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)=\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\Phi_{A_{k}^{t}}(x).

(9)

Appealing to the law of large numbers, this sample mean of course get close to
\overline{\Phi}_{t}(x)
as
M
grows large, and the speed will essentially depend on the variance of the random variable
\Phi_{A_{k}^{t}}(x)
. To estimate the latter is in general hard – in the NTK-limit it is easy, since a formula for the variance can be obtained. Gerken and Kessel used the latter to derive a simple sample bound in
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
. To obtain general bounds for finite-width neural networks is hard and well beyond the scope of this paper.

Let us here only formalize the intuitive picture we gave above: Any bound on the variance of
\Phi_{A_{k}^{t}}
(over different independent training runs) will give us a sample bound for achieving approximate equivariance.

Proposition 4.11

(Approximate equivariance of finite ensembles)
.

Let
Y=\mathbb{R}^{d}
be equipped with the standard Euclidean norm. Assume that the distribution
\Phi_{A^{t}}(x)
of the neural network outputs for an element
x\in X
is

(i)

almost surely bounded,
||\Phi_{A^{t}}(x)||_{2}\leq\xi_{t}

(ii)

has a bounded variance
\mathbb{E}(||\Phi_{A^{t}}(x)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x)||_{2}^{2})=V_{t}<\infty
.

Case 1:
The orbit
O_{x}=\{\rho_{0}(g)x\,|\,g\in G\}
is finite.
Then, for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
if

\displaystyle M\geq C\cdot\max\bigg(\frac{V_{t}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{\xi_{t}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\log\bigg(\frac{(d+1)\cdot|{O_{x}}|}{\delta}\bigg)

the sampled ensemble
\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{N}(x)
will obey

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in G}||\rho_{N}(g)\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)-\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(\rho_{0}(g)x)||_{2}\leq\varepsilon

with a probability higher than
1-\delta
. Here,
C>0
is a constant independent of
\Phi,x,G
.

Case 2:
The orbit
O_{x}
is infinite. Then, under the additional assumption that

(iii)

\Phi_{A^{t}}
almost surely has a Lipschitz-constant smaller than
K
,

we have for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
that if

\displaystyle M\geq C\cdot\max\bigg(\frac{V_{t}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{\xi_{t}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\log\bigg(\frac{(d+1)\cdot\mathcal{N}(O_{x},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3\max(K,1)})}{\delta}\bigg)

the sampled ensemble
\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)
will obey

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in G}||\rho_{N}(g)\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)-\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(\rho_{0}(g)x)||_{2}\leq\varepsilon

with a probability higher than
1-\delta
. Here,
C>0
is a constant independent of
\Phi,x,G
, and
\mathcal{N}(O_{x},\cdot)
denotes the covering numbers for
O_{x}
.

Proof.

Note that by the triangle inequality and Theorem
4.10
we have

\displaystyle||\rho_{N}(g)\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)-\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(\rho_{0}(g)x)||_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq||\rho_{N}(g)\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)-\rho_{N}(g)\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x)||_{2}+||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(\rho_{0}(g)x)-\rho_{N}(g)\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x)||_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=||\rho_{N}(g)\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(x)-\rho_{N}(g)\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x)||_{2}+||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(\rho_{0}(g)x)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(\rho_{0}(g)x)||_{2}.

(10)

Hence, if

\displaystyle||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)||_{2}\leq r

(11)

for
r=\varepsilon/2
and every
y\in O_{x}
, we obtain the stated equivariance bound. For a fixed
y
, bounding the probability of failure of (
11
) is simple: applying the Matrix Bernstein inequality
Tropp and others (2015)
to
(\Phi_{A^{t}}(x)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(x))/M
, we get that for every
y\in O_{x}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)||_{2}\geq r\Big)\leq(d+1)\cdot\exp{\Bigg(\frac{-r^{2}/2}{\frac{V_{t}}{M}+\frac{2r\cdot\xi_{t}}{3M}}\Bigg)}.

(12)

In case 1, we can now take a union bound over
O_{x}
for the Inequality
12
. We get the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists y\in O_{x},\,||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)||_{2}\geq r\Big)\leq|O_{x}|\cdot(d+1)\cdot\exp{\Bigg(\frac{-r^{2}/2}{\frac{V_{t}}{M}+\frac{2r\cdot\xi_{t}}{3M}}\Bigg)}.

Thus, by choosing
M\geq\ln{\Big(\frac{|O_{x}|\cdot(d+1)}{\delta}\Big)}\cdot\Big(\frac{2V_{t}}{r^{2}}+\frac{4\xi_{t}/3}{r}\Big)=C\cdot\max\Big(\frac{V_{t}}{r^{2}},\frac{\xi_{t}}{r}\Big)\log\Big(\frac{d|{O_{x}}|}{\delta}\Big)
, we guarantee that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)||_{2}\leq r,\,\forall y\in O_{x}\Big)\geq 1-\delta.

As for case 2, let us WLOG assume that
K\geq 1
– the other case is exactly the same. Let
Q
be an
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3K}
- net for
O_{x}
. By the above argument, we get that (
11
) for
r=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3K}
and every
y\in Q
with a failure probability smaller than stated. This is however enough to get the inequality with
r=\varepsilon
on the entire orbit: First, notice that both
\widehat{\Phi}_{M}^{t}
and
\overline{\Phi}^{t}
also are
K
-Lipschitz continuous as averages of
K
-Lipschitz continuous functions. Now, if
z\in O_{x}
is arbitrary, we can argue that there exists a point
y\in Q
with
||z-y||\leq\tfrac{\epsilon}{3K}
, and consequently

\displaystyle||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(z)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(z)||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(z)-\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)||_{2}+||\widehat{\Phi}^{t}_{M}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)||_{2}\ +||\overline{\Phi}^{t}(y)-\overline{\Phi}^{t}(z)||_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot\tfrac{\epsilon}{3K}+K\cdot\tfrac{\epsilon}{3K}+K\cdot\tfrac{\epsilon}{3K}\leq\varepsilon,

where we in the final estimate used
K\geq 1
. The claim follows.
∎

The above bounds of course do not say much without an estimate of
|{O_{x}}|
and
\mathcal{N}(O_{x},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3K})
. As for the former, an obvious upper bound is
|O_{x}|\leq|G|
, yielding the statement that an ensemble with
O(\log(|{G}|))
members is enough to obtain approximate equivariance for a given element
x
, which in terms of the group size is a relatively mild growth. In special cases,
|O_{x}|
can be much smaller than
|G|
. An example is the standard action of the permutation group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
: Then,
|{G}|=n!
, but
|O_{x}|\leq n
.

A natural use case for the infinite orbits is the case of
G
being a connected Lie group. In that case, the following proposition shows that the logarithm of the covering number does not grow faster than the dimension of
G
.

Proposition 4.12

(Bound on the covering number of the orbit)
.

Let
G
be a connected lie Group. Then, we can bound

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{N}\left(O_{x},\frac{\varepsilon}{3\max(K,1)}\right)\leq\dim(G)\cdot\log\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\max(K,1)\cdot C(\mathrm{diam}(G),\rho_{0})\cdot||x||_{2}\right),

where
C(\mathrm{diam}(G),\rho)
is a constant dependent on the diameter of the group and the representation
\rho_{0}
.

Proof.

Let us begin by recalling some basic facts about compact, connected Lie groups (see e.g.
Fulton and Harris (2004)
). Let
\mathfrak{g}
denote the Lie algebra of
G
and
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
the exponential map. Since
G
is a connected, compact Lie group, we know that

\displaystyle G=\{\exp(H)\,|\,||H||\leq\mathrm{diam}(G)\}.

(13)

Furthermore, since
\rho_{0}
is a representation, we have
\underline{\exp}(\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(H))=\rho_{0}(\exp(H))
, where
\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(X)
is the differential of
\rho_{0}:G\to\mathrm{GL}(X)
at
e
, and
\underline{\exp}
on the left is the exponential map
\mathfrak{gl}(X)\to GL(X)
:

\displaystyle\underline{\exp}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{k!}A^{k}

It is well known that
\underline{\exp}
is differentiable, with a derivative that fulfills

\displaystyle||\underline{\exp}^{\prime}(A)B||_{2\to 2}\leq\exp(||A||_{2\to 2})||B||_{2\to 2}.

Hence, for
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}
with
||H||,||H^{\prime}||\leq\mathrm{diam}(G)
, we get

\displaystyle||\rho_{0}(\exp(H))x-\rho_{0}(\exp(H^{\prime}))x||_{2}\leq||\underline{\exp}(\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(H))-\underline{\exp}(\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(H^{\prime})||_{2\to 2}||x||_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sup_{||\widetilde{H}||\leq\mathrm{diam}(G)}\exp(||\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\widetilde{H})||_{2\to 2})\cdot||\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(H))-\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(H^{\prime})||_{2\to 2}\cdot||x||_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sup_{||\widetilde{H}||\leq\mathrm{diam}(G)}\exp(||\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\widetilde{H})||_{2\to 2})\cdot||\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}||\cdot||H-H^{\prime}||\cdot||x||_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=:C(\mathrm{diam}(G),\rho_{0})\cdot||H-H^{\prime}||\cdot||x||_{2}

From this inequality, we quickly derive that if
P
is a net of width
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3\cdot\max(K,1)}~\cdot~{C(\mathrm{diam}(G),\rho_{0})||x||_{2})}
of the ball of radius
\mathrm{diam}(G)
in
\mathfrak{g}
, the set
\{\rho_{0}(\exp(H))x\,|H\in P\}
is a net of width
\varepsilon/(3\max(K,1))
of the orbit. It is well known that such a net
P
can be constructed of cardinality less than

\displaystyle\left(1+\tfrac{2\cdot\mathrm{diam}(G)\cdot 3\max(K,1)\cdot C(\mathrm{diam}(G),\rho_{0})\cdot||x||_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathrm{\dim}(G)}.

(see e.g. the appendix of
Foucart and Rauhut (2013)
). We here used that
\dim\mathfrak{g}=\dim G
. The claim follows.
∎

The moral of the above result is that when
G
is a Lie-group,
O(\dim(G))
ensemble members are needed to realise equivariance approximately (where the implicit constant depends on
\rho_{0}
and
\mathrm{diam}(G)
).

5
Numerical experiments

We perform two small numerical experiments. Their purpose is to test the robustness of our theorems to using the sample mean instead of the true ensemble mean, the necessity of the
\rho
-invariance assumption of
\mathcal{L}
, and the
\rho
-invariance of the initial distribution of parameters. The code, that relies heavily of the code made available from
Nordenfors et al. (2025)
, can be found
at
https://github.com/onordenfors/ensemble_experiment

and additional technical details (such as hardware specifications, licenses for the datasets, etc.) in Appendix
7
.

We use two metrics to evaluate the invariance of the trained models. First, similarly to
Gerken and Kessel (2024)
,
we calculate the average
orbit same prediction
(OSP). That is, for each test image, we test how many of the transformed versions of it are given the same prediction as the untransformed one by the ensemble model. OSP lies between
1
and
|G|
, where
a value equal to the order of the group
|G|
indicates perfect invariance. The second metric we calculate is the average symmetric KL-divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
between the class probabilities predicted on
an
example with each of the
transformed
versions of it. Note that the class probabilities can vary quite a lot but still yield the same prediction, whence this is a finer measure of invariance than the OSP.

and

10
epochs on the MNIST dataset
LeCun et al. (1998)
, using SGD with a constant learning rate of
0.01
, a batch size of 32, and cross entropy loss. We apply random augmentation, and test the ensembles on both MNIST and CIFAR-10
Krizhevsky (2009)
.

,

Case 1:
C_{4}

We first choose
C_{4}
, i.e. rotations of multiples of
90^{\circ}
, as our symmetry group. For this
G
,
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sym}}
is
\rho
-invariant, but
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{as}}
is not (this is actually true for all possible choices of the intermediate representations – see Appendix
6
). Hence, we expect that the former ensembles become equivariant, whereas the latter do not. We initialize the ensembles by drawing the coefficients
c
from a standard normal distribution (see Lemma
4.1
). Since
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{as}}
is not
\rho
-invariant, this does not produce an invariant initial distribution. We can mitigate this by setting the ’corner pieces’ of all asymmetric filters equal to zero. We hence get three ensembles: One symmetric, one asymmetric initialized
\rho
-invariantly, and one asymmetric initialized ”naïvely”.

In Figure
4
(top), we plot the values of our two metrics on the two datasets at the end of epoch 10, for different number of ensemble members. We see that for all models, the ensembles become more equivariant as the number of members grows, showcasing the inherent ’equivariazing’ effect of ensembling well. On MNIST, the larger ensembles come close to a perfect OSP of
4
. However, it can also be observed that ensembles of a size as small as 50 have an OSP above 3.5
on out-of-distribution data
. We also see that the symmetric ensembles outperform the asymmetric ones initialized invariantly, which in themselves outperform the naïvely initialized asymmetric ones. This suggests that
\rho
-invariance of the architecture also plays an important role empirically. The differences are more prominent on the OOD data, which is to be expected – the networks have actually ’seen’ rotated nines in the MNIST data and can hence have learned to predict them correctly even if the network is not inherently equivariant – the same is not true for rotated cars in the CIFAR10 set.

We perform a second set of experiments with
C_{16}
, i.e., rotations of multiples of 22.5
∘

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sym}}
nor
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{assym}}
are
\rho

3\times 3
square, and so on. We therefore include a fourth model in these experiments, namely a standard, full
3\times 3
-support CNN. Note that due to interpolation effects, the corresponding space
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{cnn}}
is also not perfectly
C_{16}

. The experimental setup is exactly as above (except a batch size of
128

The performance with respect to the different metrics for the four models at epoch
10
is presented in Figure
4
(right) (more details again in Appendix
7
). Compared to the
C_{4}
experiment, all models fare much worse – no model comes close to the optimal OSP of
16
. Note that is what to be expected from our theory – the compatibility assumption is not fulfilled for any model. We also see that the standard CNN:s outperform the other models vastly – which is also what could be expected, given that the
3\times 3
-square is closer to being an invariant support than the symmetric and asymmetric supports are.

The necessity of
\rho
-invariance.

In our theoretical results,
\rho
-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
is shown to be
sufficient
to achieve equivariant ensembles. What does our experiments say about the
necessity
of it? This is a subtle point, that requires a nuanced response. As already discussed, the results indicate the
relevance
of the condition. In both of our experiments, the cases with (more) invariant
\mathcal{L}
-spaces outperformed the other ones. On the other hand, the ensembles with non-invariant
\mathcal{L}
are also surprisingly equivariant. In the
C_{16}
experiments, the
CNN
-models achieve an OSP of over
13
on out-of-distribution data, and the different restricted architectures around
9
, although neither
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{cnn}}
, nor the other spaces, are perfectly invariant. This is not close to the perfect
16
, but also far from the value of
2.5
one expects from a completely random model.

We can at this point only speculate on why ensembles with non-invariant
\mathcal{L}
become as equivariant as they do. A possible explanation is revealed by our proofs: There we need
\rho
-invariance to guarantee
\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)
for all
g
. In all of our cases,
\rho(g)\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}
is still ”almost” equal to
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)
for many
g
. For instance, in the
C_{4}
-case,
\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)=\Pi_{\mathcal{L}}\rho(g)
only fails due to one non-zero corner in the
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{asym}}
-filters. We investigate this point further in Appendix
9.2
, where we perform
C_{4}
-experiments with
5\times 5
-filters, which contain more energy in the asymmetric part. Our results there support this hypothesis somewhat, but more work is needed to draw definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that ensembles of neural networks become equivariant when trained with mini-batch SGD on randomly augmented data. This result generalizes previous works in that they apply to finite-width neural networks, and more importantly, to more general architecture than just MLPs. In regards to the latter, our analysis revealed that the geometry of the architecture plays a role. When their architecture spaces fulfill a geometric invariance condition, the equivariance of the ensembles follow.
