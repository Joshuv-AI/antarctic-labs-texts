Abstract
. Let 𝒮 \mathscr{S} be the class of all structures whose growth rate on orbits of subsets of size n n is not faster than 2 n p ⁡ ( n ) \frac{2^{n}}{p(n)} for any polynomial p p . In this article we give a complete classification of all structures in 𝒮 \mathscr{S} in terms of their automorphism groups. As a consequence of our classification we show that 𝒮 \mathscr{S} has only countably many structures up to bidefinability, all these structures are first-order interpretable in ( ℚ , < ) (\mathbb{Q};<) and they are interdefinable with a finitely bounded homogeneous structure. Furthermore, we also show that all structures in 𝒮 \mathscr{S} have finitely many first-order reduct up to interdefinability, thereby confirming Thomas’ conjecture for the class 𝒮 \mathscr{S} .



1.
Introduction

There are three natural sequences of counting orbits attached to a permutation group
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
.

We write

•

o_{n}(G)
for the
number of
n
-orbits of
G
, i.e., the number of orbits of the coordinate-wise action of
G
on
X^{n}

•

\ell_{n}(G)
for the
number of injective
n
-orbits of
G
, i.e., the number of those
n
-orbits of
G
which contain tuples with pairwise different entries,

•

u_{n}(G)
for the
number of orbits of
n
-subsets of
G
, i.e., the number of orbits of the natural action of
G
on
n
-element subsets of
X
.

We call the sequences
(\ell_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
or
(u_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
, the
labelled
and
unlabelled growth
of
G
, respectively.

In this paper we always assume that the group
G
is
oligomorphic
, i.e., any (or equivalently all) of the sequences defined above have finite values. In this case we can assume without loss of generality that the domain set
G
is closed in the topology of pointwise convergence, since switching to the closure of the group does not change its orbit growth functions. By a Theorem of Engeler, Ryll-Nardzeski, and Svenonius we know that this is equivalent to
G
being an automorphism group of an
\omega
-categorical structures
[
Hod93
]
. We also know that in this case every orbit of
G
is a realization of a complete type in
\mathfrak{A}

[
Hod93
]
. By the Downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem we can (and we will) also assume that the underlying set
X
is countable (see also the discussion in
[
Cam90
]
, Section 1). We use the notation
o_{n},\ell_{n},u_{n}
and the corresponding terminology for the group and the corresponding structure
\mathfrak{A}
interchangeably. The study of the behaviour of the sequences above for oligomorphic groups was initiated by Cameron and Macpherson
[
Cam00
,
Cam90
]
, and it has been a subject of active research since.

There are many structural results available about
G
, or the corresponding structure
\mathfrak{A}
as above, when we put more restrictive upper bounds on the sequences
(\ell_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
or
(u_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
. Clearly, a structure is finite if and only if any of these sequences are eventually 0. For this reason one can argue that the slower the labelled or the unlabelled growth of a structure is, the closer it is a finite structure. This intuition is also confirmed by some the concrete known results we mention below.

As for
(\ell_{n}(G))
we know that it has an exponential upper bound if and only if
G
is a closed supergroup of a finite direct product of symmetric groups (with the action on the disjoint union)
[
BB21b
]
. In fact, it is shown in the same paper that
\ell_{n}(G)<cn^{dn}
for some
d<1
if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
is
cellular
, i.e., it is a first-order reduct of a finite cover of a unary structure with finitely many predicates (see more details in
[
Bra22
,
Bod24
]
or in Section
2.7
).

Compared to the labelled growth the situation tends to be much more complicated if we put similar upper bounds for the unlabelled growth of a group. Already the case when
u_{n}(G)=1
for all
n
, these groups are called
highly homogeneous
, is far from obvious, and these groups were classified by Cameron: they are exactly the automorphism groups of the 5 reducts of
(\mathbb{Q},<)
(see Section
2.9
). Those closed groups for which
u_{n}(G)
has a polynomial upper bound are classified in
[
FT20
]
(or in
[
Fal19
]
). It turns out, as it is shown in the same paper, that in this case the sequence
(u_{n}(G))
is in fact always
quasi-polynomial
, i.e., it can be written as

u_{n}(G)=a_{k}(n)n^{k}+\dots+a_{0}(n)

where all the coefficients
a_{i}(n)
are periodic functions.

In the case when the structure
\mathfrak{A}
is stable an interesting dichotomy holds.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Bra22
]
, Theorem 3.10)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a stable
\omega
-categorical structure. Then exactly one of the following holds.

(1)

u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})<c^{n}
for all
c>1
if
n
is large enough.

(2)

u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})>c^{n}
for all
c
if
n
is large enough.

This means that for automorphism groups of stable structures there is no difference between ‘slower than exponential’ and ‘at most exponential’ unlabelled growth. We also know a structure description of those structures which satisfy item (i) in the above theorem. A structure is called
hereditarily cellular
iff can be built up from finite structures by taking finite disjoint unions, wreath products with the pure set, and first-order reducts (see more details in Section
2.7
). Hereditarily cellular structures are introduced, under a different terminology in
[
Lac92
]
, and their automorphism groups are discussed in detail in
[
Bod24
]
. By the results of
[
Bra22
]
we also know that a stable
\omega
-categorical structure has slower than exponential unlabelled growth (item (1) in the theorem above) if and only if it is hereditarily cellular. From this we can conclude the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a countable structure. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathfrak{A}
is hereditarily cellular.

(2)

\mathfrak{A}
is stable and
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})<c^{n}
for all
c>1
if
n
is large enough.

(3)

\mathfrak{A}
is stable and
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})<c^{n}
for some
c
if
n
is large enough.

If we remove the stability assumption then the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
no longer holds. The simplest counterexample is the wreath product
Z_{2}\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q})
whose unlabelled growth is the Fibonacci sequence. In general, it seems that the larger
c
is in an upper bound
c^{n}
for the unlabelled growth, the more complicated the situation gets.

In this paper we focus our attention to the case when
c<2
. We define
\mathscr{S}
to be the class of those countable structures
\mathfrak{A}
such that for no polynomial
p
do we have
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})\geq\frac{2^{n}}{p(n)}
. The class
\mathscr{S}
was introduced and studied in a recent paper by Simon
[
Sim18b
]
(where these were called structures with “few finite substructures”). The following is shown in the aforementioned paper.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathscr{S}
be a structure with a primitive automorphism group. Then
u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})=1
for all
n
, (and thus
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
is one of the 5 groups in Cameron’s classification).

We note that in the original formulation of Theorem
1.3
in
[
Sim18b
]
also allows
\mathfrak{A}
to be strictly stable (stable but not
\omega
-stable) in the conclusion, however this case can be excluded by an argument presented in
[
Bra22
]
. Theorem
1.3
confirms a long-standing conjecture by Macpherson. We also note that the number 2, hidden in the definition of
\mathcal{S}
, in the assumption of Theorem
1.3
is optimal. This is witnessed by the structure
S(2)
, called the
local order
, whose automorphism is primitive and its unlabelled growth is approximately
2^{n}/n
.

Finally, we mention a recent structural result for general exponential upper bound. A first-order
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
formula is said to have the
independence property
if we can find an infinite set
A
of
m
-tuples and a family
\bar{b}_{I}:I\subset A
of
n
-tuples such that
\phi(\bar{a},\bar{b}_{I})
holds if and only if
\bar{a}\in I
. A structure
\mathfrak{A}
is called
NIP
if there is no formula in any elementary extension of
\mathfrak{A}
that has the independence property. A structure is called
monadically NIP
if all of its unary expansions are NIP.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
BL21
]
, Theorem 5.3)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a structure such that
\mathfrak{A}<c^{n}
for some
c
. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is monadically NIP.

In fact, it is conjectured that the converse of Theorem
1.4
also holds for
\omega
-categorical structures (
[
Bra22
]
, Conjecture 2).

1.1.
The main result

In the present paper we give a complete classification of the class
\mathscr{S}
in terms of their automorphism groups. This is a common generalization of the classification results given in
[
FT20
]
and
[
Bod24
]
, and in particular contains the classification of all closed permutation groups with subexponential unlabelled growth. Our classification results are based on the description given
[
Sim18b
]
and the description of hereditarily cellular structures/groups as given in
[
Bod24
]
. We also obtain a description of structures in
\mathscr{S}
similar to that of hereditarily cellular structures we mentioned earlier: a structure is contained in
\mathscr{S}
if and only if it can be built up from certain finite covers of
\mathbb{Q}
and finite structures by taking finite disjoint unions, wreath products with the pure set, and first-order reducts. The full details are provided in Section
7
.

As a consequence of our classification we verify two conjectures made in
[
Bra22
]
: one about a description of structures with subexponential orbit growth (Conjecture 1, see Theorem
7.12
), and one about gaps in unlabelled growths in the class
\mathscr{S}
(Conjecture 3). The latter can be formulated as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}\in\mathscr{S}
. Then there exists some
d\in\omega,d\geq 1
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(u_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))^{1/n}=\gamma_{d}
where
\gamma_{d}
denotes the largest real root of the polynomial
x^{d}-x^{d-1}-\dots-1
.

We also show that
\mathscr{S}
contains only countably many structures up to bidefinability.

Our classification also gives us the following the characterization of structures with at most polynomial unlabelled growth.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a countable structure. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

u_{n}(\mathfrak{A})
is at most polynomial.

(2)

There exists an expansion of
\mathfrak{A}
by finitely many constants which is bidefinable with a disjoint union of finitely many copies of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
and some cellular structure.

Theorem
1.6
is especially relevant in the context of CSPs; see our discussion below.

1.2.
Model theoretical consequences

Finite homogeneity and finite boundedness

We say that a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
homogeneous
if every partial isomorphism between substructures of
\mathfrak{A}
extends to an automorphism of
\mathfrak{A}
. It follows from an easy counting argument that if
\mathfrak{A}
is homogeneous and has a finite relational signature then
o_{n}(\mathfrak{A})\leq 2^{cn^{k}}
for some
c>0
where
k
is the maximum of 2 and the maximal arity of
\mathfrak{A}
, in particular
\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-categorical. We say that a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
finitely homogenizable
if
\mathfrak{A}
is first-order interdefinable with a homogeneous structure in a finite relational signature. Note that the upper bound above does not guarantee finite homogenizability: for instance the infinite dimensional vector space
\mathfrak{V}
over a finite field
K
we have
o_{n}(\mathfrak{V})\leq{|K|^{n}\choose n}<|K|^{n^{2}}
but
\mathfrak{V}
is not finitely homogenizable
[
BCS18
,
Mac91
]
. In the present paper, however, we show that every structure in
\mathscr{S}
is finitely homogenizable. In fact, we show that every structures in
\mathscr{S}
is interdefinable with a homogeneous relational structure
\mathfrak{A}
which is also
finitely bounded
, meaning that the class of finite structures that embed into
\mathfrak{A}
can be described by finitely many forbidden substructures.

Interpretability in
(\mathbb{Q};<)

If
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are structures then we say that
\mathfrak{B}
is (first-order) interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
if there exist a
d\in\omega
and a partial surjective map
I\colon A^{d}\rightarrow B
such that for all relations defined by atomic formulas in
\mathfrak{B}
, including
x=y
and
x=x
, are definable in
\mathfrak{A}
. Our classification shows that every structure in
\mathcal{S}
is interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. We also note that for this result the number 2 in the definition
\mathcal{S}
is also tight since, as will be shown in an upcoming paper of the author,
S(2)
is not interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
.

1.3.
Thomas’ conjecture

In
[
Tho91
]
Thomas made the conjecture that every countable homogeneous structure with a finite relational signature has finitely many reducts up to first-order interdefinability. An equivalent formulation of the conjecture states that automorphism groups of all aforementioned structures have finitely many closed supergroups contained in the symmetric group acting on the domain set of the structure. The conjecture has been verified for many well-known homogeneous structures. The list includes the rationals with the usual ordering
[
Cam76
]
, the countably infinite random graph
[
Tho91
]
, the homogeneous universal
K_{n}
-free graphs
[
Tho96
]
, the expansion of
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
by a single constant
[
JZ08
]
, the universal homogeneous partial order
[
PPP+14
]
, the random ordered graph
[
BPP15
]
, and many more
[
Aga16
,
AK18
,
BJP16
,
BBPP18
]
. Note that if we drop the assumption that the signature of the homogeneous structure
\mathfrak{A}
is relational, then Thomas’ conjecture is false even if we keep the assumption that
\mathfrak{A}
is
\omega
-categorical: already the countable atomless Boolean algebra has infinitely many first-order reducts
[
BCS18
]
.

Note that all aforementioned results only solve Thomas’ conjecture for specific structures mostly using some general techniques from Ramsey theory, and they all provide the exact number of reducts of such structures. Another way to approach this conjecture is to prove (or potentially disprove) it for more restrictive classes of structures, but potentially without specifying the exact number of reducts. We mention two results in this direction. In
[
Bod24
]
the conjecture has been verified for hereditarily cellular structures, and in
[
Sim18a
]
it is verified for
\omega
-categorical primitive NIP structures with thorn rank 1.

In this paper we show that Thomas’ conjecture also holds for every structure in the class
\mathscr{S}
. Since this class contains all hereditarily cellular structures and all expansion of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
with finitely many constants, our result generalizes the results of both
[
Bod24
]
and
[
JZ08
]
with regard to Thomas’ conjecture.

1.4.
Connection to CSPs

Homogeneous and in general
\omega
-categorical structures also make an important in the study of
Constraint Satisfaction Problems
, or
CSPs
for short. These are usually formulated as follows. The CSP over a structure
\mathfrak{B}
with a finite relational signature, denoted by
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
, is the decision problem where the input is a finite structure
\mathfrak{A}
with the same signature as
\mathfrak{B}
, and we need to decide whether there exists a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
. By the famous result of Bulatov of Zhuk, we know that if
\mathfrak{B}
is finite then the computational complexity of
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
satisfies a dichotomy: it is either in
\mathbf{P}
or it is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete (
[
Bul17
,
Zhu20
]
). Both proofs use various techniques and results from universal algebra many of which can also be generalized to
\omega
-categorical structures, as well. In order to formulate a complexity dichotomy we need further model-theoretical restriction since in general even homogeneous structures with a finite relational signature can have undecidable CSPs (see for instance
[
BN06
]
). One way to guarantee that
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
is at least in
\mathbf{NP}
is to assume that
\mathfrak{B}
is homogeneous and finitely bounded, or a reduct of such a structure. This motivates the following complexity dichotomy conjecture, originally presented in
[
BPP21
]
.

Conjecture 1.7

(Infinite-domain CSP dichotomy conjecture)
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a first-order reduct of finite bounded homogeneous relational signature. Then
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
is either in
\mathbf{P}
or it is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete.

For a general introduction and more details on infinite domain CSPs we refer the reader to
[
Bod21
,
Pin22
]
.

A natural subclass of structures for which the above conjecture is sometimes considered is the class of structures which are interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
(see for instance
[
Bod04
]
, partial results in this direction have been obtained in
[
BK08
,
BK09
,
BM18
,
Bod22
,
BJM+24
,
BB25
]
. The conjecture has also been verified for various other classes of structures, see
[
BP15
,
KP18
,
BB21a
,
BJP17
,
BMM18
,
Bod22
,
MNP23
,
MP24
]
.

As we mentioned in Section
1.2
we know that all structures in
\mathscr{S}
are interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. In
[
Bod22
]
Conjecture
1.7
has been verified for hereditarily cellular structure which are exactly the stable structures in
\mathscr{S}
by Theorem
1.2
.

A more approachable goal would be to verify Conjecture
1.7
for structures with at most polynomial unlabelled growth. By using Theorem
1.6
one can argue that this is enough to show it in the case when the structure
\mathfrak{B}
is bidefinable with a disjoint union of finitely many copies of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
and some cellular structure (see the discussion at the end of Section
8
). This looks promising since the infinite-domain CSP dichotomy conjecture has already been solved for all reducts of
(\mathbb{Q};<)

[
BK09
]
and all hereditarily cellular structures
[
Bod22
]
. We want to emphasize, however, that in order to recover the complexity of a CSP of structure usually a much finer distinction than just bidefinability is necessary, namely we need to identify relations which are
primitively positively
definable. Therefore, even in the simple-looking case outlined above the solution to Conjecture
1.7
poses some serious challenges.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Permutation group notation

For a set
X
, we write
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
for the group of all permutations of
X
. We say that a group
G
is a
permutation group

G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
for some set
X
. Note that in this case the set
X
is unique, and we call it the
domain
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
. The cardinality of
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
is called the
degree
of
G
. We say that two permutation groups
G
and
H
are
isomorphic as permutation groups
if there is bijection
e\colon\operatorname{Dom}(X)\rightarrow\operatorname{Dom}(H)
such that
H=eGe^{-1}=\{e^{-1}ge:g\in G\}
. All groups appearing in this paper will be permutation groups, and when we say that two permutation groups are isomorphic we always mean that they are isomorphic
as permutation groups
.

Assume that
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is a permutation group, and
Y\subset X
. We introduce the following notation.

•

G_{Y}
denotes the
pointwise stabilizer
of
Y
, that is,

G_{Y}=\{g\in G\colon\forall y\in Y(g(y)=y)\}.

•

G_{\{Y\}}
denotes the
setwise stabilizer
of
Y
, that is,

G_{\{Y\}}=\{g\in G\colon\forall y\in Y(g(y)\in Y)\}.

•

G|_{Y}
denotes the
restriction of
G
to
Y
, that is,

G|_{Y}=\{h|_{Y}\colon h\in G\},

provided that
Y
is invariant under
G
.

•

G_{(Y)}:=G_{\{Y\}}|_{Y}=G_{\{X\setminus Y\}}|_{Y}
.

•

G_{((Y))}:=G_{\operatorname{Dom}(G)\setminus Y}|_{Y}
.

If
Y
is finite, say
Y=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, then we simply
G_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}
for
G_{Y}
.

Every permutation group
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is equipped with a topology called the
topology of pointwise convergence
. This topology is defined to be the subspace topology of the product space
X^{X}
where
X
is equipped with the discrete topology. We say that a permutation group is
closed
if it is closed in the topology of pointwise convergence. Equivalently, a group
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is closed if the following holds: for all
g\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
, if for every finite
F\subset X
there exists
g^{\prime}\in G
such that
g^{\prime}|F=g|F
then
g\in G
.

Let us assume that
E
is an equivalence relation on some set
X
. Then for an element
x\in X
we write
[x]_{E}
for the unique
E
class that contains
E
. Note that in this case
[x]_{E}=[y]_{E}
holds if and only if
(x,y)\in E
. We denote by
X/E
the set of
E
classes on
X
, i.e.,
X/E=\{[x]_{E}:x\in X\}
. If
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is a group then we say that
E
is a
congruence
of
G
if
E
is preserved by every element of
G
, that is, for all
x,y\in X
and
g\in G
we have
(x,y)\in E
if and only if
(g(x),g(y))\in E
. If
E
is a congruence of
G
then there is a natural homomorphism
G\mapsto\operatorname{Sym}(X/E)
defined by
g\mapsto g/E
where
g/E:[x]_{E}\mapsto[g(x)]_{E}
. We write
G/E
for the image of this homomorphism, i.e.,
G/E=\{g/E:g\in G\}
.

2.2.
Orbit growth functions

Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be a permutation group, and let
n\in\omega
. Then we introduce the following notation.

•

\mathcal{O}_{n}(G)
denotes the
set of
n
-orbits of
G
, i.e., the set of orbits of the coordinate-wise action of
G
on
X^{n}
,

•

for
a\in X^{n}
we denote by
o_{G}(a)
the orbit of the tuple
a
, i.e, the unique
n
-orbit of
G
containing
a
,

•

for
a,b\in X^{n}
we write
a\approx_{G}b
if
o_{G}(a)=o_{G}(b)
,

•

o_{n}(G)
denotes the
number of
n
-orbits of
G
, i.e.,
o_{n}(G)=|\mathcal{O}_{n}(G)|
,

•

\ell_{n}(G)
denotes the
number of injective
n
-orbits of
G
, i.e., the number of those
n
-orbits of
G
which contain tuples with pairwise different entries,

•

u_{n}(G)
denotes the
number of orbits of
n
-subsets of
G
, i.e., the number of orbits of the natural action
G\curvearrowright{X\choose n}=\{Y\subset X\colon|Y|=n\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

It follows easily from the definitions that for any permutation group
G
we have
u_{n}(G)\leq\ell_{n}(G)\leq n!u_{n}(G)
and
o_{n}(G)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{n\brace k}\ell_{k}(G)
where
{n\brace k}
denotes the Stirling number of the second kind.

A permutation group is called
oligomorphic
if and only if
o_{n}(G)
is finite for all
n\in\omega
. By Remark
2.1
it follows that the oligomorphicity of a group
G
is also equivalent to
u_{n}(G)
(or
\ell_{n}(G)
) being finite for all
n\in\omega
. We call the sequence
(\ell_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
the
l
abelled growth profile
of
G
, and
(u_{n}(G))_{n\in\omega}
the
u
nlabelled growth profile
of
G
. The orbit growth functions
\ell_{n}(\cdot)
and
u_{n}(\cdot)
are introduced and discussed in general in
[
Cam00
,
Cam90
]
. Note that at some places in the literature the sequences
o_{n},\ell_{n}
and
u_{n}
are denoted by
F_{n}^{*},F_{n}
and
f_{n}
, respectively. In this paper we are mostly concerned with the unlabelled growth profile of permutation groups.

Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be a permutation group, and let
S\subset X
. Then we write
\operatorname{acl}_{G}(X)

2.3.
Automorphisms of structures

In this paper we denote structures by Fraktur letters

(sometimes with subscripts or other diacritics), and we write

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
for the automorphism group of a structure

In this paper every structure is assumed to be countable, unless otherwise mentioned. We also assume for convenience that all structures in our discussions have a purely relational signature.

2

Constants can be considered to be unary relations represented by a single element.

We are considering the following two notions of equivalence on first-order structures.

Definition 2.2

Let

•

Remark 2.3

Note that bidefinability is a symmetric condition. Indeed, if

We say that a countable structure

Let

(1)

(2)

(3)

(4)

up to interdefinability and the closed supergroups of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})

(5)

2.4.
Direct products and disjoint unions

Let
\{X_{i}\colon i\in I\}
be a family of pairwise disjoint sets, and for each
i\in I
let
G_{i}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X_{i})
be a permutation group. Then we define the
direct product
of the permutation groups
G_{i}
, denoted by
\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
, to be the set of all permutations of
X:=\bigcup\{X_{i}\colon i\in I\}
which can be written as
\bigcup\{f(i)\colon i\in I\}
, for some
f\colon I\to\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
that satisfies
f(i)\in G_{i}
for all
i\in I
. In this case
\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
as a group is also the usual direct product of the groups
G_{i}\colon i\in I
. It is clear from the definition that direct products of closed groups are closed.

Direct products of groups correspond to disjoint unions of structures in the following sense.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
be family of relational structures with pairwise disjoint domains.

Then we define the
disjoint union

\mathfrak{B}
of the structures
\mathfrak{A}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, denoted by
\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{A}_{i}
such that

•

the domain set of
\mathfrak{B}
is
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
,

•

its signature of
\mathfrak{B}
is the union of the signatures of
\mathfrak{A}_{i}:i\in I
together with some (distinct) unary symbols
U_{i}\in I\not\in\bigcup_{i\in I}\Sigma(\mathfrak{A}_{i})
,

•

U_{i}^{\mathfrak{B}}=A_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and,

•

R^{\mathfrak{B}}=R^{\mathfrak{A}_{i}}
for all
R\in\Sigma(\mathfrak{A}_{i})
.

It is easy to see that if
(\mathfrak{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
are as above then
\operatorname{Aut}(\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{A}_{i})=\prod_{i\in I}(\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A}_{i}))
. It is also clear that finite unions of
\omega
-categorical structures are
\omega
-categorical which in turn implies that oligomorphic groups are closed under taking finite direct products.

2.5.
Wreath products

If
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is a permutation group, and
S
is a set then we define the power
G^{S}
to be
\prod_{s\in S}G_{s}
where
G_{s}:=\{(g,s)\colon g\in G\}
and
(g,s)(g^{\prime},s)=(gg^{\prime},s)
. This makes sense since the sets
\operatorname{Dom}(G_{i})=X\times\{i\}
are pairwise disjoint. Then
\operatorname{Dom}(G^{S})=X\times S
, and since all
G_{i}
are isomorphic to
G
it follows that
G^{S}
is isomorphic to the power
G^{|S|}
in the usual group theoretical sense.

Now let
H\leq\operatorname{Sym}(Y)
be an another permutation group. Then we define
wreath product
of the groups
G
and
H
, denoted by
G\wr H
, as follows. The domain set of
G\wr H
is
X\times Y
and a permutation
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(X\times Y)
is contained in
G\wr H
if and only if there exist
\beta\in H
and
\alpha_{y}\in G
such that
\sigma(x,y)=(\alpha_{y}(x),\beta(y))
. Note that in this case we have a natural surjective homomorphism
\phi
from
G\wr H
to
H
by mapping every
\sigma
to its action of the second coordinates. The kernel of this homomorphism is exactly the power
G^{Y}
, as defined above. Moreover, the map
\iota\colon\beta\mapsto((x,y)\mapsto(x,\beta(y))
splits the homomorphism
\phi
, that is,
\phi\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}(Y)
. This implies that
G\wr H
can be written as a semidirect product
G^{Y}\rtimes e(H)
.

We can also define wreath products of structures as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be structures with disjoint relational signatures. Then we define the structure
\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}
, called the
wreath product of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
as follows.

•

The domain set of
\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}
is
A\times B
,

•

the signature of
\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}
is
\Sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cup\Sigma(\mathfrak{B})\cup\{E\}
where
E
is a distinguished binary symbol not contained in
\Sigma(\mathfrak{A})\cup\Sigma(\mathfrak{B})
,

•

E^{\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}}=\{(a_{1},b),(a_{2},b)\colon a_{1},a_{2}\in A,b\in B\}
,

•

R^{\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}}=\{(a_{1},b),\dots,(a_{k},b)\colon(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in R^{\mathfrak{A}},b\in B\}
for all
R\in\Sigma(\mathfrak{A})
,

•

S^{\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}}=\{(a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{k},b_{1})\colon(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in S^{\mathfrak{B}}\}
for all
S\in\Sigma(\mathfrak{B})
.

The correspondence between wreath products of groups and wreath products of structures is as follows. Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be a structure as in Definition
2.6
. Then we have
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B})=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{B})
. This implies immediately that if
G
and
H
are closed then so is
G\wr H
. Moreover, if
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are
\omega
-categorical then so is
\mathfrak{A}\wr\mathfrak{B}
which implies that if
G
and
H
are oligomorphic groups then
G\wr H
is also oligomorphic.

2.6.
Covers of permutation groups

In this section we introduce the notion of covers of permutation groups which plays a crucial role in this article. This construction will allow us to decompose certain oligomorphic permutation groups into simpler parts from which the original group can be recovered. Our definition of covers is largely based on the ones introduced in
[
Iva99
,
EMI95
,
Pas11
,
BB21b
]
. In some cases covers are considered for structures and in some other cases it is only considered for
closed
permutation groups (these are called
permutation structures
in
[
EMI95
]
and
[
Pas11
]
). One important difference in our definition compared to the earlier ones is that we also define everything for general permutation groups without any closedness assumption. Although all permutation groups showing up during our constructions will be closed, this will sometimes not follow immediately from the definition, so we prefer this more general treatment.

Notation 2.7
.

Let
\pi\coloneqq X\mapsto Y
be a map. Then we write
\sim_{\pi}
for the equivalence relation
\{(a_{1},a_{2}):\pi(a_{1})=\pi(a_{2})\}
. If
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
and
\sim_{\pi}
is a congruence of
G
then
\pi
gives rise to a homomorphism

defined by
\mu_{\pi}(g)(a)=\pi(g(\pi^{-1}(a)))
. We that mapping
\pi
is clear from the context we simply write
\mu
for
\mu_{\pi}
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
G
and
G^{*}
be permutation groups. Then a triple
\Phi=(G,\rightarrow G^{*},\pi)
is called a

of
G^{*}
if

(1)

\pi
is a surjective map from
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
to
\operatorname{Dom}(G^{*})
,

(2)

the equivalence relation
\sim_{\pi}
is preserved by
G
,

(3)

the image of
G
under
\mu_{\pi}
equals
G^{*}
.

The sets
\pi^{-1}(w)\colon w\in\operatorname{Dom}(G^{*})
are called the
fibers
of
\Phi
.

Let

, and let
A:=\pi^{-1}=a
. Then we call the groups

•

G_{(A)}
,

•

\mu_{\pi}^{-1}(G_{a})
,

•

G_{((A))}

the
fiber group
, the
binding group
and the
pointwise binding group
of
\Phi
at
a
, respectively. The
kernel of
\Phi
is the kernel of the homomorphism
\mu_{\pi}
.

If all fibers of
\Phi
are finite then
\Phi
is called a

of

. More generally, for any class
\mathcal{C}
of groups

is called a
\mathcal{C}

of

if all pointwise binding groups of

Note that the binding group and the pointwise binding group at a given point are always normal subgroups of the corresponding fiber group. We say that
\Phi

if all factor groups

Note that if
G
is closed then the direct product of the pointwise binding groups of

is a normal subgroup of

is

if all of its pointwise binding groups are trivial.

We say that two covers

We say that a group

is a
cover
of
G^{*}
if there exists a
\pi\colon G\rightarrow G^{*}
such that
\Phi=(G,G^{*},\pi)
is a cover of
G^{*}
. A structure
\mathfrak{A}
is called a cover of
\mathfrak{B}
is
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{A})
is a cover of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{B})
. Finite covers and
\mathcal{C}
-covers of structures are defined analogously.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be an arbitrary permutation group, and let
\sim
be a congruence of

. Let us define
\pi\colon a\mapsto[a]_{\sim}
. Then it is easy to see that

and

is a cover. In fact every cover a permutation group arises from the construction above. Indeed, let
(G,G^{*},\pi)
be a cover. Let

. Then there is natural bijection between

and

defined by

.

Notation 2.10
.

For a tuple

we write
\pi_{i}(a)=a_{i}
.

Observation 2.11
.

Let
(G,G^{*},\pi)
be a cover. Then we can relabel the elements of the domain of
G
such that the following hold.

(1)

every element of
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
is a pair

(2)

\pi=\pi_{2}

(3)

if
a\approx_{G^{*}}b
then
\pi_{1}(\pi_{2}^{-1}(a))=\pi_{1}(\pi_{2}^{-1}(b))
, and the map
(f,a)\mapsto(f,b)
extends to a permutation in

Proof.

Let us first relabel each element

Now let

be an orbit of

, and let us fix an element

2.7.
Hereditarily cellular groups and structures

In this section we briefly recall the classification of hereditarily cellular structures mostly taken from
[
Bod24
]

We first need to introduce a couple of notations.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
N_{i}\leq\operatorname{Sym}(Y_{i})
be permutation groups for some pairwise disjoint sets
Y_{0},\dots,Y_{k}
, and let
H
be a permutation group which normalizes
N\coloneqq\prod_{i=0}^{k}N_{i}
. Then we write
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
for the permutation group with domain
(Y_{0}\times\{0\})\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(Y_{i}\times\omega)
generated by
(N_{0}\wr(\operatorname{id}(\{0\}))\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}(N_{i}\wr\omega)
and the permutations
(a,n)\mapsto(h(a),n)\colon h\in H
.

Note that the definition of the group
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
differs from the one given in
[
Bod24
]
, however the equivalence of the two definitions follows from the discussion in Section 3 in the aforementioned paper.

It is clear from the definition that

\mathcal{G}(N;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})=(N_{0}\wr(\operatorname{id}(\{0\}))\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}(N_{i}\wr\omega).

Now we are ready to give the definition of hereditarily cellular groups/structures.

Definition 2.16
.

We define the classes of closed permutation groups
\mathcal{H}_{i}\colon i\in\{-1\}\cup\omega

•

\mathcal{H}_{-1}=\emptyset
,

•

For
n\geq 0

G
is contained in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
if and only if
G
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
for some
N_{0},N_{1},\dots,N_{k},H\in\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
where
Y_{0}=\operatorname{Dom}(N_{0})
is finite, and
N_{0}=\operatorname{id}(Y_{0})
.

We write
\mathcal{H}
for the union of all classes
\mathcal{H}_{n}\colon n\in\omega
. A group
G
is called

if
G\in\mathcal{H}
, and a structure is called
hereditarily cellular
if its automorphism group is hereditarily cellular. Following our notational convention we write
\mathscr{H}
for the class of hereditarily cellular structures.

The
rank
of a hereditarily cellular group
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{rk}(G)
is the smallest
n
such that
G\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
. The rank of a hereditarily cellular structure is the rank of its automorphism group.

Hereditarily cellular groups/structures with rank at most 1 are called
cellular
.

Remark 2.17
.

It is clear from the definition that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is exactly the class of permutation groups with finite degree.

Note that in
[
Bod24
]

[

.

Let

(1)

(2)

(3)

(4)

(5)

. In this case we can modify the elements

by first permuting the elements of

stabilizes eventually. By doing this modification for each fiber we can obtain a sequence

in

. Let

Next we show that in fact every closed cover of a permutation group with finite fiber factors arises from the construction above.

.

Let

and

Proof.

Let

be a cover with finite fiber factors. We assume as usual that

are as in conditions (a)-(c). For

we write

and

for the fiber and pointwise binding groups of

at

are finite, there are isomorphic to some permutation group with finite degree. Let us pick some permutation groups
P_{a}:a\in X^{*}
with finite degree, and some surjective homomorphism

with
\ker(\phi_{a})=B_{a}

then
(P_{a},\phi_{a})=(P_{b},\phi_{b})

.

We define a map

we write
\eta_{a}\colon u\mapsto\pi_{1}(g((u,a)))
. By using condition (c) we can conclude that

\displaystyle\psi(gh)((d,a))=

\displaystyle(\phi(\eta_{a}(gh))(d),\mu(gh)(a))=(\phi(\eta_{\mu(h)(a)}(g)\eta_{a}(h))(d),\mu(g)(\mu(h)(a)))=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\phi(\eta_{\mu(h)}(g))(\phi(\eta_{a}(h))(d)),\mu(g)(\mu(h))(a))=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(g)(\phi(\eta_{a}(h)),\mu(h)(a))=\psi(g)(\psi(h)((d,a)).

Let
\tilde{G}=\psi(G)
. Then

is a finite cover of

.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

be a finite cover. Then if

.

Before showing Lemma

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

a sequence of permutations of some countable set

has a convergent subsequence.

Proof.

Let

be an enumeration of

. Then by induction on

we can define subsequences
(g_{i}^{j})_{i\in\omega}
such that

if

and
|\{g_{i}^{j}(x_{j})\colon i>j\}|=1
. Then the sequence

is a convergent subsequence of

.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Let
(g_{i}^{*})_{i\in\omega}
a convergent sequence in

with limit

. We have to show that

. Let us choose

with

. Clearly, the set

, in particular, it is finite. Thus, by using Lemma

we can take a subsequence

of

which is convergent. Then we have

which finishes the proof of the lemma.

4.
Groups with not too fast unlabelled growth

We denote by
\mathcal{S}
the class of closed groups
G
such that for no polynomials

do we have
u_{n}(G)\geq\frac{2^{n}}{p(n)}
. As usual, the corresponding class of structures is denoted by
\mathscr{S}
. The class

was introduced and discussed in
[
Sim18b
]
, and it is the main topic of this article. In this paper we refer to the elements of
\mathcal{S}
(resp.
\mathscr{S}
) as groups (resp. structures) with
not too fast
unlabelled growth.
4
4

4

The word ‘slow’ is a bit overloaded and usually indicates something slower than what we want to have, for instance polynomial or slower than exponential. Our upper bound is slightly faster than ‘slower than exponential’, this is why we picked the term ‘not too fast’.

Next we recall one of the main results of
[
Sim18b
]
rephrased in a way that it is more suitable for our discussion.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{S}
. Then there exists a permutation group
G^{*}
with
\overline{G^{*}}\in\mathcal{H}
, and a cover
(G,G^{*},\pi)
such that for all
a\in\operatorname{Dom}(G^{*})

•

either
|\pi^{-1}(a)|=1
or

•

for every finite subset
B
of
\operatorname{Dom}(G)\setminus\pi^{-1}(a)
the group
(G_{B})|_{\pi^{-1}(a)}
is a finite cover of a highly set-transitive, but not highly transitive group.

Moreover, for a given

satisfying item (2) the fibers of the finite cover do not depend on our choice of

.

Proof.

We follow the proof of Theorem 5.1. in
[
Sim18b
]
. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a structure with

. We define

to be the equivalence relation of equialgebraicity on elements of order type (i.e.

iff

and

) extended by the equality relation on the rest of the structure.

Then let us consider the equivalence relations

on

, and

as defined in the proof of Lemma 5.1 in
[
Sim18b
]
. The exact details of the definition of

(*)

•

has finite classes,

•

is the automorphism group of a stable structure, and

•

for all

(1)

|C|=1
.

(2)

for any finite subset

The unlabelled growth of the group of

Now let us define

Let

•

|C^{\prime}|=|\bigcup_{C\in C^{\prime}}C|=1
.

•

for any finite subset

of

Then by defining

∎

Note that it does not follow directly from the argument presented in

]
that any of the groups

)
.

There is no infinite strictly descending sequence of groups in

.

Let

5.2.
Normal subgroups

In this section we examine all those pairs

(1)

(2)

, and

(3)

and

So let us consider some pair

as above which we will fix throughout this subsection. By renaming the elements of

We start with a few observations.

Lemma 5.7
.

N^{*}\triangleleft G^{*}
.

Proof.

Let

and

∎

By Lemma
2.46

G^{*}
and

are as in the conclusion of Lemma
2.46
. By Theorem

we know that
\operatorname{Ker}(\mu|_{G})=\mathcal{K}^{L}_{H}

Proposition 5.8
.

H=H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\ker(\mu|_{N})=\ker(\mu|_{G})\cap N

. Let

and

\hat{\alpha}\in N

∎

By Lemma
2.46
we know that
\circlearrowleft\in N^{*}
if and only if
\circlearrowleft\in G^{*}

\circlearrowleft_{\sigma}\in N^{*}
for some

Let

It follows from Theorem

Lemma 5.9
.

\tilde{M}

(1)

(2)

The fact that

5.3.
Finite covers with not too fast unlabelled growth

In this section we classify those closed finite coverings of highly set-transitive groups which are also contained in the class
\mathcal{S}
, and we describe when we have a normal subgroups relation between two such groups with the same fibers. We will see that a group
G\in\mathcal{F}
is contained in
\mathcal{S}
if and only if its pointwise binding group is highly transitive.

We start by introducing a sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}:d\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
of subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
by imposing some stronger, but still exponential, upper bounds on the unlabelled profile. Within the class
\mathcal{F}
these classes correspond exactly to those groups whose fiber size is at most
d
.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we denote by
\gamma_{d}
the largest real root of the polynomial
f_{d}(x):=x^{d}-x^{d-1}-\dots-x-1
, and we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{d}
the class of those closed groups
G
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
we have
u_{n}(G)<(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}
if
n
is large enough.

Example 5.12
.

\gamma_{1}=1
and
\gamma_{2}=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.618
, the golden ratio. The next values of this sequence are approximately
\gamma_{3}\approx 1.839,\gamma_{4}\approx 1.928,\gamma_{5}\approx 1.966.

Lemma 5.13
.

The sequence
(\gamma_{d})_{d}
is strictly increasing and
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{d}=2
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\gamma_{d}>0
since
f_{d}(0)=-1<0
. Since
f_{d}(\gamma_{d})=0
we have
f_{d+1}(\gamma_{d})=\gamma_{n}f_{n}(\gamma_{n})-1=-1<0
. Therefore,
\gamma_{n+1}
must be greater than
\gamma_{n}
.

Put
\gamma=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{d}
. If
t\geq 2
then we can easily show by induction that
f_{n}(t)\geq 1
. Indeed,
f_{1}=1
and
f_{n+1}(t)=tf_{n}(t)-1>2-1=1
. Therefore,
\gamma_{d}<2
, and thus
\gamma\leq 2
. On the other hand, if
p<2
then

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl({\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)^{k}=\frac{1}{p(1-\frac{1}{p})}=\frac{1}{p-1}>1,

and thus
\sum_{k=1}^{d}({\frac{1}{p}})^{k}>1
for some
d\in\omega
. In this case we have

f_{d}(p)=p^{n}\Bigl(1-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Bigl({\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)^{k}\Bigr)<0,

and hence
\gamma_{d}>p
. This proves that
\gamma=2
.
∎

It is clear from the definition that
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subset\mathcal{S}_{2}\subset\dots
, and by Lemma
5.13
we have
\bigcup_{d\in\omega}\mathcal{S}_{d}\subset\mathcal{S}
. Later we will see that in fact the equality holds.

For convenience, we sometimes write
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
for
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 5.14
.

The classes
\mathcal{S}_{d}\colon d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
are closed under taking stabilizers of finite sets.

Proof.

For
d=\infty
this is exactly Lemma 2.2 in
[
Sim18b
]
, and for
d<\infty
essentially the same proof works.
∎

Our next goal is to give a description of all groups in
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{S}_{d}
for
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Let us fix a group
G\in\mathcal{F}
. Then we can rename the elements of
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
such that there exists a finite cover
(G^{*},G,\pi)
and groups
H\triangleleft L\leq\operatorname{Sym}(F)
as in the conclusion of Theorem
5.2
. We also use the notation from Subsection
5.1
. Next we show that
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
if and only if
H
is highly set-transitive and
|F|\leq d
. We know that all finite coverings of
\operatorname{Sym}(\omega)
have a polynomial orbit growth (see
[
FT20
]
). In particular all these groups are contained in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
. This handles the case when
G^{*}=\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{Q})
. Next we examine what happens in the other cases.

We first need the following general observation about the unlabelled growth of finite-index subgroups.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
H\leq G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Then
u_{n}(H)\leq|G:H|u_{n}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{i}:i\in I
be such that
G=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}\gamma_{i}H
. If the
A_{j}\in{X\choose n}:j\in J
represent all orbits of the action
G\curvearrowright{X\choose n}
then the sets
\gamma_{i}(A_{j})
represent all orbits of the action
H\curvearrowright{X\choose n}
. Therefore, the inequality
u_{n}(H)\leq|G:H|u_{n}(G)
follows.
∎

The following is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
5.15
.

Corollary 5.16
.

Let
G
be a permutation group, and let
H
be a finite-index subgroup of
G
. Then
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
if and only if
H\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
.

Lemma 5.17
.

Let us assume that
G^{*}\neq\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{Q})
, and let
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}
. Then
G_{F\times\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}}
fixes the set
F\times(s_{1},s_{2})
, and the restriction

K:=(G_{F\times\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}})|_{F\times(s_{1},s_{2})}

is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}^{H}_{H}(\operatorname{id}(F)\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<))=H\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.42
we know that
G^{*}
preserves the relation
\operatorname{Sep}
. It is easy to check that
(t,s_{2},s_{3},s_{1})\in\operatorname{Sep}
if and only if
s_{1}<t<s_{2}
. Moreover,

K^{*}\coloneqq(G^{*}_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}})|_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}=\operatorname{Aut}((s_{1},s_{2});<)\simeq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<).

This implies that
G_{F\times\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}}\leq\mu_{\pi}^{-1}(G^{*}_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}})
preserves the set

and
\mu(K)|_{(s_{1},s_{2})}\leq K^{*}
. We know that

contains the group
\mathcal{K}^{H}_{H}(\operatorname{id}(F)\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<))
from which we can easily conclude that

contains both
\mathcal{K}^{H}_{H}|_{F\times(s_{1},s_{2})}
and
(\operatorname{id}(F)\wr\operatorname{Aut}((s_{1},s_{2});<))
. Therefore,

is a finite cover of

. The only thing we have to check that the kernel of this cover is
\mathcal{K}^{H}_{H}|_{F\times(s_{1},s_{2})}
, that is,
\delta_{a}(\alpha)\in H
for all

and

. By our definition

extends to and element of

fixing

pointwise. But then
\delta_{s_{1}}(\alpha)=\operatorname{id}
which implies
\delta_{a}(\alpha)\in H
.
∎

Corollary 5.18
.

Let us assume that
G^{*}\neq\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{Q})
, and let

G_{0}\coloneqq H\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<)).

Then for all

we have
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
if and only if
G_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
.

Proof.

The “if” direction is obvious since
G_{0}\leq G
. The other direction follows form Lemmas
5.14
and
5.17
.
∎

Now let us fix the group
G_{0}
as in Corollary
5.18
. Then we can write the following recursion formula for the unlabelled growth of
G_{0}
.

Lemma 5.19
.

u_{n}(G_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{|F|}{u_{i}(H)u_{n-i}(G_{0})}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be an
n
-element subset of
F\times\mathbb{Q}
. Let
m
the maximum of those numbers which appear as the second coordinate of an element of
A
, and let
A_{\max}\coloneqq\{a\in A\colon\pi(a)=m\}
. Then the
G_{0}
-orbits of
A_{\max}
and
A\setminus A_{\max}
can be recovered from the orbit of
A
. Conversely, by the construction of
G_{0}
it follows that the orbit of
A
is uniquely determined by the orbits of
A_{\max}
and
A\setminus A_{\max}
. Whenever
|A_{\max}|=i
then we have
u_{i}(H)
many choices for the orbit of
A_{\max}
and
u_{n-i}(G_{0})
many choices for the orbit of
A\setminus A_{\max}
. Hence, the recursion formula follows.
∎

The following statement follows from the resolution of linear recursion. For the sake of completeness, we also give a direct proof.

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of positive numbers satisfying the recursion
a_{n+k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}a_{n+i}
for some
c_{i}>0
. Let
q
be the largest real root of
f(x):=x^{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_{i}x_{i}
. Then there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
C_{1}q^{n}<a_{n}<C_{2}q^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We can clearly find
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
C_{1}q^{n}<a_{n}<C_{2}q^{n}
holds for
n\leq k
. We show by induction that this choice suffices.

Let us suppose that we have
C_{1}q^{m}<a_{m}<C_{2}q^{m}
holds for
m<n
. Then we have

a_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}a_{n-k+i}\geq C_{1}q^{n-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}q^{i}=C_{1}q^{n-k}(q^{k}-f(q))=C_{1}q^{n}.

The other inequality follows similarly.
∎

Lemma 5.21
.

Let us assume that
G^{*}\neq\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{Q})
, and let
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
if and only if

is highly set-transitive and
|F|\leq d
. Moreover, if

and

then
u_{n}(G)\geq C\gamma_{d}^{n}
for some

.

Proof.

G_{0}
be as in Corollary

Lemma 5.28

Let

(*)_{m}

The forward direction is clear. Now let us assume that

and

\displaystyle u=

\displaystyle(u_{11},\dots,u_{1\ell_{1}},u_{21},\dots,u_{2\ell_{2}},\dots,u_{n^{\prime}\ell_{n^{*}}})\text{, and}

\displaystyle v=

\displaystyle(v_{11},\dots,v_{1\ell_{1}},v_{21},\dots,v_{2\ell_{2}},\dots,v_{n^{\prime}\ell_{n^{*}}})

where
\pi_{2}(u_{ij})=u_{i}^{*}
and

for all

. We show that the tuples

.

In this section we describe all pairs

Lemma 6.1
.

The classes
\mathcal{H}_{n}
are closed under taking closed finite covers.

Proof.

We show the statement by induction on
n
. For
n=0
the statement is trivial. For the induction step let
G^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, and let
(G,G^{*},\pi)
be a finite cover. In this proof we use the recursive description of the classes
\mathcal{H}_{n}
given in Theorem
2.19
.

So let
P^{*}:=(K^{*},\nabla^{*},\Delta^{*})
be an
\omega
-partition of
G^{*}
with components contained in
\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
. Let

K=\pi^{-1}(K^{*}),\nabla:=\pi^{-1}(\nabla^{*}),\Delta:=\pi^{-1}(\Delta^{*}),P:=(K,\nabla,\Delta).

Then it is clear from the definition that
P
is an
\omega
-partition of
G
. It remains to prove that the components of
P
are contained in
\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
. We put
X\coloneqq\operatorname{Dom}(G)
and
X^{*}\coloneqq\operatorname{Dom}(G^{*})
. Let
Y
be a
\Delta
-class, and let
Y^{*}:=\pi(Y)
. Let

N^{*}:=(G^{*})_{((Y^{*}))},N:=\mu_{\pi}^{-1}(N^{*}).

Then
N
is a finite cover of
N^{*}
witnessed by the map
\pi|_{Y}
. Since
N^{*}
is a component of
P^{*}
we know that
N^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis
N\in\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
.

By an abuse of notation we write
N_{F}
for the group

\{g|_{Y}\colon g\in G_{F}\wedge\mu_{\phi}(g)\in N^{*}\}

where
F
is any subset of
X
. We claim that for every finite set
F\subset X\setminus Y
the group
N_{F}
is a finite-index closed subgroup of
N
. The fact that
N_{F}
is closed follows from Lemma
3.5
(see the proof of Lemma
3.4
). Now let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
be an enumeration of
F
. Then the orbit of
a_{i}
with respect to the group
\mu_{\pi}^{-1}((G^{*})_{X^{*}\setminus Y^{*}})
is contained in the
\sim_{\pi}
class of

, in particular it is finite. Therefore,

|(\mu_{\pi}^{-1}((G^{*})_{X^{*}\setminus Y^{*}}))|_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i}}\colon(\mu_{\pi}^{-1}((G^{*})_{X^{*}\setminus Y^{*}}))|_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i+1}}|<\infty

for all

. This in turn implies that

|(\mu_{\pi}^{-1}((G^{*})_{X^{*}\setminus Y^{*}})):(\mu_{\pi}^{-1}((G^{*})_{X^{*}\setminus Y^{*}}))|_{F}|<\infty,

and thus
|N:N_{F}|
is finite.

We obtained that for every finite subset
F
of

N_{F}\supset N_{\operatorname{fin}}
. On the other hand, by definition we have

G_{((Y))}=\bigcap\{N_{F}\colon F\subset X\setminus Y,|F|<\infty\}.

Therefore,

. Then by Lemma

(2)

, and thus the cover
(G,G^{*},\pi)
is isomorphic to some cover in
\mathbf{A}
.
∎

Lemma
6.3
has the following consequence which we will need later.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(G,G^{*},\pi)
a finite cover with
G\in\mathcal{H}
. Then there exist
G_{0}\leq G
and
G_{0}^{*}\leq G^{*}
with
G_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
(G_{0},G_{0}^{*},\pi)
is strongly trivial.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.3
we can assume that
(G,G^{*},\pi)\in\mathbf{A}
. We show the statement by induction of
\operatorname{rk}(G)
. If
\operatorname{rk}(G)=0
, that is
\operatorname{Dom}(G)
is finite then we can take both
G_{0}
and
G_{0}^{*}
to be the identity group. Otherwise, let us assume that
H,H^{*},\rho,N_{i},N_{i}^{*}:i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
are as in Definition
6.2
. We can assume without loss of generality that
H=N=\prod_{i=0}^{k}N_{i}
and
H^{*}=N^{*}=\prod_{i=0}^{k}N_{i}
. By the induction hypothesis, we know that there exist some
H_{0}\leq H,H_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
H_{0}^{*}\leq H^{*}
such that
(H_{0},H_{0}^{*},\rho)
is a strongly trivial cover. Let

\displaystyle G_{0}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(H_{0};(H_{0})|_{\operatorname{Dom}(N_{0})},\dots,(H_{0})|_{\operatorname{Dom}(N_{k})}),

\displaystyle G_{0}^{*}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(H_{0}^{*};(H_{0}^{*})|_{\operatorname{Dom}(N_{0}^{*})},\dots,(H_{0}^{*})|_{\operatorname{Dom}(N_{k}^{*})}).

Then it is easy to check that the groups
G_{0}
and
G_{0}^{*}
are as stated in the lemma.
∎

7.
Finishing the classification

In this section we put together the pieces from the previous sections and present a classification of groups in all the classes
\mathcal{S}_{d}\colon d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. We start with a necessary condition which follows directly from the results of the previous sections.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then every permutation group in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is isomorphic to some group
G=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
where

(1)

the triple
(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
is as in Definition
3.1
,

(2)

(\tilde{G},G^{*},\pi_{2})
is isomorphic to some finite cover in
\mathbf{A}
(see Definition
6.2
),

(3)

for all
a\in X^{*}
we have
D(a)=(B_{a},F_{a},\phi_{a})
where either
|\operatorname{Dom}(F_{a})|=1
or

(a)

B_{a},F_{a}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{S}_{d}

(b)

B_{a}
and
F_{a}
have the same fibers, and

(c)

B_{a}\triangleleft F_{a}
.

Proof.

The statement of the lemma essentially follows from Theorem
4.4
and the discussion in Section
3
. The only thing we have to check is that if
G=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
and
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
then for the groups
B_{a}
and
F_{a}
as above we have
B_{a},F_{a}\in\mathcal{S}
. Since
|F_{a}:B_{a}|<\infty
it is enough to check that
B_{a}\in\mathcal{S}
. The finiteness of
|F_{a}:B_{a}|
also implies that there exists some finite subset
S
of
\operatorname{Dom}(G)\setminus\pi_{2}^{-1}(a)
such that
G_{S}|_{\pi_{2}^{-1}(a)}
is isomorphic to
B_{a}
(see for instance the proof of Lemma
4.5
). On the other hand, clearly
u_{n}(G_{S}|_{\pi_{2}^{-1}(a)})
is at most
u_{n}(G)
. Therefore,
B_{a}\in\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

Note that in the construction of the group
\tilde{G}
(see the proof of Lemma
3.3
) the fibers
F
of
\tilde{G}
can be chosen arbitrarily as long as
F_{a}/B_{a}
embeds into
\operatorname{Sym}(F)
. On the other hand, we know by Corollary
5.26
that all these quotient groups are isomorphic to
Z_{k}
for
k\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
or
Z_{2}\times Z_{2}
. Since all these groups embed into
S_{5}
, this means that we can always choose the group
\tilde{G}
above so that all of its fibers are of size at most 5.

Next we show that the converse of Lemma
7.1
is also true: every group
G=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
as described above is contained in the class
\mathcal{S}_{d}
. We will show this via a third equivalent description of the classes
\mathcal{S}_{d}
analogous to Theorem
2.21
. Namely, we show that
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is exactly the smallest class of permutation groups which contains
(\mathcal{S}_{d}\cap\mathcal{F})\cup\operatorname{id}(\{\emptyset\})
and is closed under taking finite direct products, wreath products with
\operatorname{Sym}(\omega)
, finite-index supergroups, and isomorphisms. We present a slightly stronger formulation of this statement in Theorem
7.10
below.

We start by showing that the classes
\mathcal{S}_{d}
are in fact closed under the aforementioned constructions. By definition, this is clear for taking supergroups, and isomorphisms.

Lemma 7.3
.

The classes
\mathcal{S}_{d}\colon d\in\mathbb{N}
are closed under taking finite direct products and wreath product with
\operatorname{Sym}(\omega)
.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is closed under taking finite direct products. For this, it is enough to show that if
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
then
G_{1}\times G_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Let
X_{i}\coloneqq\operatorname{Dom}(G_{i}),X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}
. Let
A\in{X\choose n}
. Then the orbit of
A
is uniquely determined by
|A\cap X_{1}|
and the
G_{i}
-orbit of
A\cap X_{i}
for
i=1,2
. For
|A\cap X_{1}|
we have
n+1
choices, and if
k\coloneqq|A\cap X_{1}|
is given then we have
u_{k}(G_{1})
choices for the
G_{1}
-orbit of
A\cap X_{1}
and
u_{n-k}(G_{2})
choices for
A\cap X_{2}
. Therefore,
u_{n}(G)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}u_{k}(G_{1})u_{n-k}(G_{2})
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then by our assumption we have
u_{n}(G_{i})<c(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
(which depends on
\varepsilon
). By the formula above we obtain

\displaystyle u_{n}(G)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}u_{k}(G_{1})u_{n-k}(G_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}u_{k}(G_{1})u_{n-k}(G_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c^{2}(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}

which is clearly smaller than
(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}
if
n
is large enough.

For the second part of the lemma let us assume that
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
. We show that
G\wr\operatorname{Sym}(\omega)\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Let
X=\operatorname{Dom}(G)
, and let
A\in{{X\times\omega}\choose n}
. We can assume without changing the orbit of
A
that

|A\cap(X\times\{0\})|\geq|A\cap(X\times\{1\})|\geq\dots\geq|A\cap(X\times\{n\})|=0.

We put
k_{i}\coloneqq|A\cap(X\times\{i\})|
. Then
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}k_{i}=n
. Observe that the
(G\wr\operatorname{Sym}(\omega))
-orbit of
A
is uniquely determined by the sequence
(k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1})
and the
G
-orbits of the sets
A_{i}:=\{a\coloneqq(a,i)\in A\}
. The number of choices for the sequence
(k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1})
is
p(n)
, that is the number of partitions of
n
. The asymptotic behaviour of the partition function
p(n)
is well-known:
p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}\exp\bigl(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\bigr)
(see for instance
[
Nat03
]
, Section 16.4). In particular, for all
c>0
we have
p(n)<c^{n}
if
n
is large enough. By our assumption, we know that
u_{n}(G)\leq c(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then based on our argument above we give the following upper bound on
u_{n}(G\wr\operatorname{Sym}(\omega))
:

\displaystyle u_{n}(G\wr\operatorname{Sym}(\omega))=

\displaystyle\sum\Bigl\{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}u_{k_{i}}(G):k_{0}\geq\dots\geq k_{n-1}\wedge\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}k_{i}=n\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\Bigl\{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}c(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{k_{i}}:k_{0}\geq\dots\geq k_{n-1}\wedge\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}k_{i}=n\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{n}p(n)(\gamma_{d}+\varepsilon)^{n}

which is smaller than
(\gamma_{d}+2\varepsilon)^{n}
if
n
is large enough.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

Note that for
d=\infty
our proof above does not work. However, as we will see,
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{S}_{d}
, so the statement of the lemma is still true in this case.

Now let us assume that
(\tilde{G},G^{*},\pi)
where
\tilde{G}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then by Lemma

we can find a finite cover
(\tilde{H},H^{*},\rho)\in\mathbf{A}
and groups
\tilde{N_{0}},\dots,\tilde{N_{k}},N_{0}^{*},\dots,N_{k}^{*}

,

•

,

•

\tilde{N}_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
,

•

,

•

\tilde{G}=\mathcal{G}(\tilde{H};\tilde{N_{0}},\dots,\tilde{N_{k}})
,

•

,

\pi((a,n))=(\rho(a),n)
.

Now let

. Note that in this case

3.1
. Let

we write

and we also use the analogous notation with tildes and asterisks.

Lemma 7.5
.

With the above notation
N\triangleleft H
and the group
G
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
.

Proof.

Let
n\in N
and
h\in H
, and let
\tilde{n}\in\tilde{N}
and
\tilde{h}\in\tilde{H}
witnessing this fact as in Definition
3.1

\tilde{N}\triangleleft\tilde{H}
we have
\tilde{h}^{-1}\tilde{n}\tilde{h}\in\tilde{N}
which witnesses the fact that

. Therefore,
N\triangleleft H
.

The isomorphism from right to left is induced by the bijection
\iota\colon((u,a),n)\mapsto(u,(a,n))
. Let
G^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
.

We introduce the following notation:

•

T\coloneqq\operatorname{id}(Y_{0}\times\{0\})\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\operatorname{id}(Y_{i})\wr\omega))

•

N_{n,i}\coloneqq\{\kappa(\sigma,n):\sigma\in N_{i}\}

•

K\coloneqq\{\kappa(\sigma,\omega):\sigma\in H\}
,

and we also use the analogous notation with tildes and asterisks. Then we know that
G^{\prime}
is locally generated by the groups
T,N_{n,i}
and
K
, and
\tilde{G}
is locally generated by
\tilde{T},\tilde{N_{n,i}}
and
\tilde{K}
.

Let us fix for all

a map
\psi_{a}
which is a right inverse of
\phi_{a}
such that

•

\phi_{a}(\operatorname{id})=\operatorname{id}
, and

•

\phi_{a}
only depends on the orbit of

Then we define a map
\gamma
by the formula
\gamma(g)\coloneqq(u,a)\mapsto(\phi_{a}(\eta_{a})(u),\mu_{\pi_{2}}(a))
. This definition works on all groups
\tilde{H},\tilde{N_{i}},\tilde{G}
, and for all

in any of these groups we have
\gamma(\tilde{g})=g
.

Let
B
be the kernel of the homomorphism

is equal to the direct product of the groups

Using the description of the group

•

\mu_{\iota}(T)=\gamma(\tilde{T})

•

\gamma(N_{n,i})\subset\mu_{\iota}(\widetilde{N_{n,i}})\subset\gamma(N_{n,i})B
,

•

\gamma(K)\subset\mu_{\iota}(\tilde{K})\subset\gamma(K)B

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\iota}(G^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\mu_{\iota}(T),\mu_{\iota}(N_{n,i}):(n,i)\in\omega\times\{0,\dots,k\},\mu_{\iota}(K)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\gamma(\tilde{T}),\gamma(\widetilde{N_{n,i}}):(n,i)\in\omega\times\{0,\dots,k\},\gamma\tilde{K}\rangle}B=\gamma(\tilde{G})B=G.\qed

Lemma 7.6
.

With the above notation
|\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k}):\mathcal{G}(N;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})|
is finite.

Proof.

Let
G=\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
and
G_{0}=\mathcal{G}(N;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
. Then the map homomorphism

defined on

and

have the same kernel and they are equal where both of them are defined. Therefore,

which is finite by our argument in Lemma

.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and let
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Then
G
can be constructed from the following groups by taking isomorphism, finite-index supergroups, finite direct products and wreath products with

.

(1)

\operatorname{id}(\{\emptyset\})
.

(2)

G=H\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<)
where
H
is a finite-degree highly set-transitive group with degree at most
d
.

(3)

G=\langle H\wr\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q};<),\circlearrowleft_{\sigma}\rangle
where
H
is a highly set-transitive group with degree at most
d
and
H\triangleleft H\sigma
.

Remark 7.8
.

Clearly, the “finite-degree” condition is only needed if

.

Proof.

We can assume that
G=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
as in Lemma
7.1
. We prove the statement by induction on
\operatorname{rk}(G^{*})
.

We first show the induction step. By defining the groups
H,H^{*},N_{0},\dots,N_{k},
as above we know by Lemma
7.5
that
G
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
where
H,N_{0},\dots,N_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\operatorname{rk}(H^{*})<\operatorname{rk}(G^{*})
. By Lemma
7.6
and Corollary

we can conclude that
\mathcal{G}(N;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Clearly,

. Therefore, all the groups
N_{0},\dots,N_{k},N,H
are contained in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Then the induction step follows from Lemma
7.6
and the definition of the group
\mathcal{G}(H;N_{0},\dots,N_{k})
.

By our argument above it is enough to show the lemma in the case when
\tilde{G}
and
G^{*}
have finite domains. Let
B
be the kernel of the natural homomorphism

. Then

is finite and

is isomorphic to the direct product of the groups

Let

(2)

(b)

.

\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{d\in\omega}\mathcal{S}_{d}

The following is a straightforward consequence of Theorem

Theorem 7.12
.

Let

9.
Counting structures with not too fast unlabelled growth

In this section we show that Thomas’ conjecture holds for structures with not too fast unlabelled growth, that is, every structure in
\mathscr{S}
has finitely many reducts up to interdefinability; or equivalently every group in
\mathcal{S}
has finitely closed supergroups. The idea of our proof is similar to the one given given in Section 5 of
[
Bod24
]
where the statement is shown for the special case of hereditarily cellular structures: we define some parameters such that they never increase when taking a reduct, and such that up to bidefinability there are only finitely many structures in
\mathcal{S}
with any given finite upper bound on these parameters. As a byproduct of our argument we also obtain that
\mathscr{S}
contains countably many structures up to bidefinability; or equivalently
\mathcal{S}
contains countably many groups up to isomorphism.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
G
be a permutation group. Then we write

•

\epsilon(G)
for the number of congruences of
G
, and

•

w(G)
for the supremum of the set

\{|\operatorname{acl}_{G_{x}/E}(\emptyset)|:x\in\operatorname{Dom}(G),\text{$E$ is a congruence of $G_{x}$}\}.

Observation 9.2
.

The following are clear from the definition.

•

If
G
is oligomorphic then
\epsilon(G)
and
w(G)
are finite.

•

If
E
is a congruence of
G
then
\epsilon(G/E)\leq\epsilon(G)
and
w(G/E)\leq w(G)
.

•

If
G\leq H
then
\epsilon(H)\leq\epsilon(G)
.

We know the following from
[
Bod24
]
.

Theorem 9.3
.

For all
n,m\in\omega
there exist finitely many groups
G\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
with
w(G)\leq m
up to isomorphism.

Our plan in this section to show an analogous statement for the class the whole class
\mathcal{S}
, and thereby show that every structure in
\mathcal{S}
has finitely many closed supergroups. By using the following observation it is enough to check the finiteness “up to isomorphism”.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
G
be a closed oligomorphic group. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

has finitely many closed supergroups.

(2)

G
has finitely many closed supergroups up to isomorphism.

Proof.

We know that
G
is the automorphism group of some
\omega
-categorical structure
\mathfrak{A}
. Then item

is equivalent to

having finitely many reducts up to

and item
2
is equivalent to

having finitely many reducts up to

]
.

We start by a couple of easy observations about the functions

Lemma 9.5

Let

\epsilon(G^{*})\leq\epsilon(G)

The first claim is clear since for every congruence

, and thus

with
\epsilon(G)\leq n
and
w(G)\leq m
up to isomorphism.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
satisfies the conditions of the lemma. Let
(\tilde{G},G^{*},D)
be as in item
2
of Theorem
7.10
. Since
G
is a cover of
G^{*}
with transitive binding groups we have
\epsilon(G^{*})\leq\epsilon(G)\leq n
and
w(G^{*})\leq w(G)\leq m
. Combining this with Proposition
9.6
and Theorem
9.3
we obtain that there are finitely many possibilities for the group
G^{*}
up to isomorphism. Then the statement of the lemma follows from Corollary
9.8
.
∎

The following is a direct consequence of Lemma
9.9
.

Corollary 9.10
.

\mathcal{S}
contain countable many groups up to isomorphism. Equivalently
\mathscr{S}
contains countably many structures up bidefinability.

Now we are ready to prove Thomas’ conjecture for the class
\mathscr{S}
.

Theorem 9.11
.

Every group in
\mathcal{S}
has finitely many closed supergroups. Equivalently, every structure in
\mathscr{S}
has finitely many reducts up to interdefinability.

Proof.

Let
G\in\mathcal{S}
. Then by Corollary
7.11
we know that
G\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Now let
G^{+}
be a closed supergroup of
G
. Then
G^{+}
is also contained in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
. Moreover, we have
\epsilon(G^{+})\leq\epsilon(G)
and
w(G^{+})\leq w(G)
. Therefore, by Lemma
9.9
there are finitely many possibilities for the group
G^{+}
up to isomorphism. The statement of the lemma then follows from Lemma
9.4
.
∎

Note that since every structure in
\mathscr{S}
is finitely homogenizable (see Corollary
7.14
), it follows that all these structures indeed fall into the scope of Thomas’ conjecture.

10.
Interpretation of structures with not too fast unlabelled growth

In this section we show that all structures in
\mathscr{S}
are interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Our strategy is to use Theorem
7.12
by showing that all constructions listed in the theorem preserve interpretability in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
.

Definition 10.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be structures. We say that
I
is an
interpretation
of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
if there exists some
d\in\omega
and such that
I
is a partial surjective map from
A^{d}
to
B
such that for every atomic formula
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
in the signature of
\mathfrak{B}
the relation

I^{-1}(R)\coloneqq\{(a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{1}^{d},\dots,a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{d}):\mathfrak{B}\models\varphi(I(a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{1}^{d}),\dots,I(a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{d}))\}

is definable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

The number
d
above is called the
dimension
of the interpretation.

We say that
\mathfrak{A}

interprets

\mathfrak{B}
, or
\mathfrak{B}
is
interpretable
in
\mathfrak{A}
, if there exists an interpretation of
\mathfrak{B}
in
\mathfrak{A}
. For a fixed structure
\mathfrak{A}
we write
\operatorname{I}(\mathfrak{A})
for the class of structures which are interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Let us consider some interpretation
I
as in the definition above. By applying the definition for the formula
(x=x)
we see that the domain set of
I
needs to be definable. Moreover, by applying the definition for the formula
(x=y)
we obtain that the kernel of the map
I:\operatorname{Dom}(I)\rightarrow B
also needs to be definable in
\mathfrak{A}
. This means essentially that the domain set of
\mathfrak{B}
can be thought of as a definable quotient of some definable substructure of some power of
\mathfrak{A}
and every relations of
\mathfrak{B}
needs to be represented by some set of tuples which are also definable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

We start with some easy observations about interpretability in a given structure.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C}
be relational structures such that
\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C}\in\operatorname{I}(\mathfrak{A})
and
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{C}
have disjoint signatures. Then
\mathfrak{B}\uplus\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{B}\wr\mathfrak{C}
are interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

Let
I:A^{d}\rightarrow B
and
J:A^{e}\rightarrow C
be interpretations of
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{C}
, respectively. We can assume without loss of generality that
d=e
and that
B
and
C
are disjoint.

Let us define the partial map

I+J:A^{d+2}\rightarrow B\cup C,(a_{1},\dots,a_{d+2})\mapsto\begin{cases}I(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\text{ if $a_{d+1}=a_{d+2}$}\\
I(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\text{ if $a_{d+1}\neq a_{d+2}$}\end{cases}

wherever it is defined. Then it is easy to see that
I+J
is an interpretation of
\mathfrak{B}\uplus\mathfrak{C}
in
\mathfrak{A}
.

For the other claim we simply consider the partial map

I\times J:A^{2d}\rightarrow B\times C,(a_{1},\dots,a_{2d})\mapsto(I(a_{1},\dots,a_{d}),J(a_{d+1},\dots,a_{2d}))

wherever it is defined. Then it can be checked easily that
I\times J
is an interpretation of
\mathfrak{B}\wr\mathfrak{C}
in
\mathfrak{A}
.
∎

Corollary 10.3
.

Let us assume that
\mathfrak{B}\in\operatorname{I}(\mathfrak{A})
. Then every strongly split finite cover of
\mathfrak{B}
is interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

Clearly, every structure interprets the 1-element pure set as witnessed by the constant map. It is also clear that
\operatorname{I}(\mathfrak{A})
is closed under taking reducts, therefore it is enough to show that every strongly trivial finite cover of
\mathfrak{B}
is interpretable in
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e., for all finite subsets
(F;=)\wr\mathfrak{B}\in\operatorname{I}(\mathfrak{A})
. This follows directly from Lemma
10.2
.
∎

Theorem 10.4
.

\mathscr{S}\subset\operatorname{I}(\mathbb{Q};<)
and
\mathscr{H}\subset\operatorname{I}(\omega;=)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2
we know that all finite covers of reducts of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
are reducts of some strongly split finite cover of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Therefore, by Corollary
10.3
all these structures are interpretable in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Then the statement of the theorem follows from Theorems
7.12
,
2.22
and Lemma
10.2
.
∎
