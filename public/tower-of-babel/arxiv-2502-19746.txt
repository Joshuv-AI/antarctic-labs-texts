An Isometric Embedding of the ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} product space of two bounded subspaces of the Gromov-Hausdorff Space into the Gromov-Hausdorff Space
Takuma Byakuno

Abstract
In this paper, we prove the ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} product space of two bounded subspaces of the Gromov-Hausdorff space can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

1
Introduction

In 1909, Fréchet
[
3
, pp.161–162]
showed that an arbitrary separable metric space can be isometrically embedded into the metric space of all real-valued and bounded sequences
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)
, which is usually denoted by
\ell^{\infty}
and equipped with the supremum distance:

\mathrm{the\ distance\ between}\ (x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\ \mathrm{and}\ (y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}:\sup_{n=1,2,\ldots}|x_{n}-y_{n}|.

Indeed, we can define the isometric embedding of a separable metric space
(X,d)
into
\ell^{\infty}
by
x\mapsto(d(x,x_{n})-d(x_{n},x_{1}))_{n=1}^{\infty}
for
x\in X
, where
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
is a dense subset of
X
.

In 1927, Urysohn
[
6
]
constructed the separable complete metric space
U
satisfying the following:

•

For an arbitrary separable metric space
X
, there exists a suitable subspace
M
of
U
such that
M
is isometric to
X
,

•

An arbitrary isometry between finite subsets of
U
can be extended to an isometry on
U
.

Additionally,
U
is unique up to an isomorphism and called
the
Urysohn universal space
today.

In 1932, Banach
[
1
, p.187]
showed that an arbitrary separable metric space can be isometrically embedded into the metric space of all continuous functions on the closed interval
[0,1]
.
It is a Banach space usually denoted by
C([0,1])
and equipped with the supremum distance:

\mathrm{the\ distance\ between}\ f\ \mathrm{and}\ g:\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|f(t)-g(t)|.

This fact was proved as an application of Fréchet’s isometric embedding and Banach-Mazur theorem
[
1
, p.185]
, which states that every Banach space can be isometrically embedded into
C([0,1])
.
This space
C([0,1])
is not isometric to the Urysohn universal space according to Mazur-Ulam theorem, which states that all surjective isometries between normed spaces are affine.
However, in 2008, Dutrieux and Lancien
[
2
]
showed that a Banach space into which all compact metric spaces can be isometrically embedded has a linearly isometric copy of
C([0,1])
.

Generally, a metric space
X
is
universal
for a class
C
consisting of metric spaces if all
Y\in C
can be isometrically embedded into
X
.
The above works focus on the universality for a class of all separable metric spaces.

On the other hand, in 2017, Iliadis, Ivanov, and Tuzhilin
[
4
]
showed that all finite metric spaces can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space, which means this space is universal for a class of all finite metric spaces.
The
Gromov-Hausdorff space
is a metric space consisting of a certain set
\mathcal{M}
and Gromov-Hausdorff distance
d_{\mathrm{GH}}
defined later.
The set
\mathcal{M}
is usually defined as the set of all isometry classes of compact metric spaces, but we now consider
\mathcal{M}
as a set characterized by

•

For every
X\in\mathcal{M}
,
X
is a (nonempty) compact metric space,

•

For an arbitrary compact metric space
X
,
we can find a suitable
Y\in\mathcal{M}
such that
Y
is isometric to
X
,

•

For every
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
, we obtain
X=Y
if
X
is isometric to
Y
.

The Gromov-Hausdorff space
\mathcal{M}
has a dense countable set consisting of isometric copies of all finite metric spaces endowed with rational valued distance functions and therefore
\mathcal{M}
is separable.
In addition, we can show that
\mathcal{M}
is complete, but
\mathcal{M}
is not isometric to the Urysohn universal space.
See
[
4
, Section 3]
and
[
5
, Main Theorem]
.

According to
[
4
]
, there are many open problems related to the geometrical properties of
\mathcal{M}
.
For instance, in this paper, we focus on

Problem 1.1
.

Can we isometrically embed all compact metric spaces into the Gromov-Hausdorff space
\mathcal{M}
?

Iliadis, Ivanov, and Tuzhilin’s result can be considered as a partial answer to this problem, and this partial answer can be generalized to the following as an easy application of
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

All bounded metric subspaces of an
\ell^{\infty}
normed space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

In this paper, we will prove the next theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\leq r},\mathcal{B}(r)

(r>0)
be the following metric subspaces of the Gromov-Hausdorff space
\mathcal{M}
\rm{:}

\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}=\left\{Z\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Z)\leq r\right\}\,,\,\mathcal{B}(r)=\left\{Z\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Z)=r\right\}\,,

where
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Z)
is the diameter of a metric space
Z
.
Then the
n
th cartesian power
(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}
equipped with
\ell^{\infty}
distance can be embedded into
\mathcal{B}(5rn)
.
Namely, we can construct a map
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}:(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}\to\mathcal{B}(5rn)
satisfying

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))=\max_{k=1,2,\ldots,n}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{k},Y_{k})

for all
X=(X_{k})_{k=1}^{n},Y=(Y_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\in(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}
.

This theorem implies

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be bounded metric subspaces of the Gromov-Hausdorff space.
Then the product space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
equipped with
\ell^{\infty}
distance can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
X,Y
be bounded metric spaces which can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.
Then the product space
X\times Y
equipped with
\ell^{\infty}
distance can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

In particular, we can also show Proposition
1.2
by using this corollary since a bounded subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{1}
can be isometrically embedded into
\mathcal{M}
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X,Y
be sets, and
R
a subset of
X\times Y
.
For
A\subset X
, we define
R[A]
as an
image
of
A
:

R[A]=\left\{y\in Y\,\middle|\,\mathrm{there\ exists}\ a\in A\ \mathrm{such\ that}\ (a,y)\in R\right\},

and we define
R^{-1}
as an
inverse
of
R
:

R^{-1}=\left\{(y,x)\,\middle|\,(x,y)\in R\right\}\subset Y\times X.

Thus, an
inverse image

R^{-1}[B]
is also defined similarly.

Using this notation, we write
\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
for the system
of all subsets
R
of
X\times Y
satisfy
R[\{x\}]\neq\emptyset
and
R^{-1}[\{y\}]\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.
In addition, we define
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)
as the
distortion
of
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
if
X,Y
are metric spaces endowed with distance functions
d_{X},d_{Y}
respectively:

\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)=\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(X,Y;R)=\sup\left\{|d_{X}(x,\xi)-d_{Y}(y,\eta)|\,\middle|\,(x,y),(\xi,\eta)\in R\right\}.

As described above, the Gromov-Hausdorff space is a metric space consisting of a certain set
\mathcal{M}
and Gromov-Hausdorff distance
d_{\mathrm{GH}}
.
The Gromov-Hausdorff distance is typically defined by using the “Hausdorff distance”,
but we can alternatively define the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
between
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
as

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)\,.

We define
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)
as the
diameter
of a metric space
(X,d)
:

\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)=\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X,d)=\sup_{x_{0},x_{1}\in X}d(x_{0},x_{1}).

It is straightforward to see
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)\leq\max\{\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X),\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y)\}
and

|\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)-\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y)|\leq 2d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)

for
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
, which means a map
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is 2-lipschitz continuous.
Therefore, if
\mathcal{X}
is a bounded set of
\mathcal{M}
, we can obtain
\sup_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)<\infty
.

By the definition of
\mathcal{M}
, for an arbitrary compact metric space
X
, there exists uniquely
Y\in\mathcal{M}
such that
X
is isometric to
Y
.
Then we may consider
[X]=Y
.
On the other hand, we can similarly define the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between two compact metric spaces
X,Y
which do not necessarily satisfy
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
.
However, we can see easily
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=d_{\mathrm{GH}}([X],[Y])
.
Generally speaking, we can see
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=d_{\mathrm{GH}}(Z,Y)
if
X
is isometric to a metric space
Z
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We shall prove Theorem
1.3
and show some corollaries.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

First of all, we define a compact metric space
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)
for an arbitrary point
X=(X_{k})_{k=1}^{n}\in(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}
.

we fix a two-point set
X_{0}=\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
equipped with distance function
d_{0}
satisfying
d_{0}(p^{+},p^{-})=3r
and we may assume
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are disjoint by replacing isometric copies of them.
For simplicity, we consider
p^{1}=-p^{-1}
and
p^{-1}=-p^{1}
.

Then we put
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}X_{k}
as a set and we can define the distance function
d^{r,n}_{X}
of
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)
satisfying the following if the distance function of
X_{k}
is denoted by
d_{k}
:

d^{r,n}_{X}(x_{k},x_{l})=\begin{cases}d_{k}(x_{k},x_{l})&\mathrm{if:}k=l,\\
5r|l-k|&\mathrm{if:}k\neq l\end{cases}

for
x_{k}\in X_{k}
,
x_{l}\in X_{l}
,
k,l\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

Figure 1:
The definition of
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)

Now we consider
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)=[\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)]\in\mathcal{M}
for simplicity.
In particular, by mean of
d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},x_{n})=5rn
for some point
x_{n}\in X_{n}
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X))=5rn
, which means
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)\in\mathcal{B}(5rn)
.
Thus, we can define a map
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}:(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}\to\mathcal{B}(5rn)
.

Let
X=(X_{k})_{k=1}^{n},Y=(Y_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be arbitrary fixed points of
(\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})^{n}
.
we put
m=\max_{k=1,2,\ldots,n}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{k},Y_{k})\leq r
for short.
We shall prove

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))=m=\max_{k=1,2,\ldots,n}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{k},Y_{k}).

Firstly, we shall show
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))\leq m
.
For any positive number
\varepsilon
with
m<\varepsilon
,
we can obtain
R_{k}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X_{k},Y_{k})

(k=1,2,\ldots,n)
satisfying
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{k})<2\varepsilon
by the definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff distance.
Then the following set
R
belongs to
\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))
:

R=R_{0}\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}R_{k}\subset\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X)\times\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y)\,,

where
R_{0}=\{(p^{1},p^{1}),(p^{-1},p^{-1})\}\subset X_{0}\times Y_{0}
(we put
Y_{0}=\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
).
It is straightforward to see
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2\varepsilon
, which means
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))<\varepsilon
.
Indeed, for any points
(x,y)\in R_{k}
and
(\xi,\eta)\in R_{l}
, we obtain

|d^{r,n}_{X}(x,\xi)-d^{r,n}_{Y}(y,\eta)|\leq\begin{cases}0&\mathrm{if:}k\neq l,\\
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{k})&\mathrm{if:}k=l\end{cases}\ (\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{0})=0)\,.

As a consequence, we have
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))\leq m
.

Secondary, we shall show
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))\geq m
by contradiction.
Thus, we suppose
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))<m
, which means there exists
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2m\leq 2r
.
Now we divide the argument among
4
steps to obtain a contradiction.

Step 1
.

In this step, we shall show that
4
sets
R[\{p^{1}\}]
,

R[\{p^{-1}\}]
,
R^{-1}[\{p^{1}\}]
,
R^{-1}[\{p^{-1}\}]
are one-point sets including in
X_{0}=Y_{0}=\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
, which means we can define
R^{s}(p^{t})\in\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
(
s,t\in\{1,-1\}
,
R^{1}=R
) as the point satisfying
R^{s}[\{p^{t}\}]=\{R^{s}(p^{t})\}
.
In addition, we shall show that they satisfy

R(p^{1})=R^{-1}(p^{1})\,,\,R(p^{-1})=R^{-1}(p^{-1})\,,\,R(p^{1})=-R(p^{-1})\,.

Firstly, suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
R[\{p^{1}\}]\not\subset\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
.
It means there exist a number
k=1,2,\ldots,n
and a point
y_{k}\in Y_{k}
such that
(p^{1},y_{k})\in R
.
On the other hand, we can take some point
z\in\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y)
which satisfying
(p^{-1},z)\in R
.
Since
|d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},p^{-1})-d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},z)|\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)
,
we have

d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},z)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},p^{-1})<2r+3r=5r\,,

thus
z\in Y_{k}
; otherwise we obtain
d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},z)\geq 5r
by the definition of
d^{r,n}_{Y}
.
Then we have
d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},z)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y_{k})\leq r
, so we obtain a contradiction:

3r=d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},p^{-1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},z)<2r+r=3r\,.

Therefore, we have
R[\{p^{1}\}]\subset\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
.

Secondary, we shall show that
R[\{p^{1}\}]
is a one point set, which means there exists
R(p^{1})\in\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
such that
R[\{p^{1}\}]=\{R(p^{1})\}
.
Let
z_{0},z_{1}
be points of
R[\{p^{1}\}]
.
Suppose
z_{0}\neq z_{1}
for the sake of contradiction.
Then we have
\{z_{0},z_{1}\}=\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
by
z_{0},z_{1}\in R[\{p^{1}\}]\subset\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
, so we have

3r=d^{r,n}_{Y}(p^{1},p^{-1})=d^{r,n}_{Y}(z_{0},z_{1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},p^{1})<2r\,,

which is a contradiction to
r>0
.
As a consequence, we can define
R(p^{1})\in\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
as an above point.
In addition, we can show that
R[\{p^{-1}\}]
is a one-point set contained in
\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
similarly, therefore we can also define
R(p^{-1})\in\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
satisfying
R[\{p^{-1}\}]=\{R(p^{-1})\}
.

In particular, if
R(p^{1})=R(p^{-1})
, we have

3r=d^{r,n}_{X}(p^{1},p^{-1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{Y}(R(p^{1}),R(p^{-1}))<2r\,,

which is a contradiction.
It means
R(p^{1})=-R(p^{-1})
.
Additionally, the above argument can also be applied to
R^{-1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X))
.
Hence, we can also define
R^{-1}(p^{1})
,
R^{-1}(p^{-1})
and they satisfy
R^{-1}(p^{1})=-R^{-1}(p^{-1})
.

We only need to verify
R(p^{1})=R^{-1}(p^{1})
.
By the argument above, we can consider
R
,
R^{-1}
as two maps
R,R^{-1}:\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}\to\{p^{1},p^{-1}\}
, and see two maps
R
and
R^{-1}
are inverses of each other by the definition of
R
,
R^{-1}
.
It means the maps are equal to each other, therefore we have
R(p^{1})=R^{-1}(p^{1})
.

Step 2
.

In this step, we shall show that there exists a permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

such that

R[X_{k}]\subset Y_{\sigma(k)}\,,\,R^{-1}[Y_{k}]\subset X_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}

for an arbitrary number
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

For an arbitrary fixed point
x_{k}\in X_{k}
, we can show that all points
y\in R[\{x_{k}\}]
do not belong to
Y_{0}
.
Indeed, we have
d^{r,n}_{Y}(y,R(p^{s}))\neq 0
for any
s\in\{1,-1\}
by calculation:

0<3r=5r-2r\leq 5rk-2r<d^{r,n}_{X}(x_{k},p^{s})-\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)\leq d^{r,n}_{Y}(y,R(p^{s}))\,.

It means
R[X_{k}]\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}
.

Now, we defined a map
\sigma:\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
by the graph

\left\{(k,l)\,\middle|\,\mathrm{there\ exist}\ x\in X_{k},\ y\in Y_{l}\ \mathrm{such\ that}\ (x,y)\in R\right\}\,.

To show the well-definedness of
\sigma
,
It suffices to show
l_{0}=l_{1}
for arbitrary
4
points
x,\xi\in X_{k}
,
y\in Y_{l_{0}},

\eta\in Y_{l_{1}}
satisfying
(x,y),(\xi,\eta)\in R
.
Since we have

d^{r,n}_{Y}(y,\eta)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{X}(x,\xi)<2r+r=3r\,,

we can obtain
|l_{0}-l_{1}|<1
if
l_{0}\neq l_{1}
.
However
|l_{0}-l_{1}|<1
means
l_{0}=l_{1}
, and it contradics to
l_{0}\neq l_{1}
.

The above argument can also be applied to
R^{-1}
, therefore we can define a map
\tau:\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
similarly.
Moreover, we have

R[X_{k}]\subset Y_{\sigma(k)}\,,\,R^{-1}[Y_{k}]\subset X_{\tau(k)}

for any
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
by the definition of
\sigma,\,\tau
.

To show the claim of this step, we shall show that
\sigma
and
\tau
are inverses of each other.
For any
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, it is straightforward to see:

X_{k}\subset R^{-1}[R[X_{k}]]\subset R^{-1}[Y_{\sigma(k)}]\subset X_{\tau(\sigma(k))}.

It means
\tau(\sigma(k))=k
by the assumption of disjointness.
Reversing the roles of
R
and
R^{-1}
, we can also obtain
\sigma(\tau(k))=k
.

Step 3
.

In this step, we shall show that
\sigma
is the identity map.

Firstly, we can obtain
\sigma(1)=1
.
Indeed, we have

5r\sigma(1)=d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{1},R(p^{+}))\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)+d^{r,n}_{X}(x_{1},p^{+})<2r+5r=7r

for some points
x_{1}\in X_{1}
and
y_{1}\in R[\{x_{1}\}]
, therefore
\sigma(1)\leq 1
.

Secondary, we shall show
\sigma(k)=k
for
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,n\}
.
Since
\sigma(k)>1
, we have

5r|\sigma(k)-k|=5r||\sigma(k)-1|-|k-1||=d^{r,n}_{Y}(y_{k},y_{1})-d^{r,n}_{X}(x_{k},x_{1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2r

for some points
x_{k}\in X_{k}
and
y_{k}\in R[\{x_{k}\}]
.
Then we obtain
|\sigma(k)-k|<1
, that is
\sigma(k)=k
.

Step 4
.

In this step, we shall show a contradiction.
As a result of step
2
, we can define
R_{k}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X_{k},Y_{k})
by
R_{k}=R\cap(X_{k}\times Y_{k})
for any
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
In particular, since
R_{k}\subset R
, we have

2d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{k},Y_{k})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{k})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2m\,,

that is
2m<2m
.
It is a contradiction.

As a consequence, we have
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r,n}(Y))=m
.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Since
\sup_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)<\infty
,
\sup_{Y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y)<\infty
, there exists
r>0
such taht
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}
, which means
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}\times\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}
