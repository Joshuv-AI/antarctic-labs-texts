Volumes of Nullhomotopies in Nilpotent Spaces
Kyle Hansen

Abstract
. The Shadowing Principle of Manin has proved a valuable tool for addressing questions of quantitative topology raised by Gromov in the late 1900s. The principle informally provides a way for bounded algebraic maps between differential graded algebras to be translated into nearby genuine maps between their geometric realizations. We extend this principle to finite towers of principal K ⁡ ( G , n ) K(G,n) fibrations, and in particular apply this construction to nilpotent spaces. As a specific application of the extended principle, we provide upper bounds on the asymptotic behavior of volumes of nullhomotopies of Lipschitz maps into nilpotent spaces. We further refine these bounds in the case when c = 1 c=1 to nearly meet those of the simply connected setting. We similarly refine these bounds in the event the target space is coformal, and demonstrate that the bounds in this setting are nearly sharp.



1.
Introduction

In
[
Gro78
]
, Gromov demonstrates for reasonable spaces that there is a polynomial in
L
bounding the number elements of a homotopy class that admit
L
-Lipschitz representatives.
That is, he suggested that topological invariants be studied not only algebraically, but quantitatively as well.
Whether two manifolds are cobordant, or two maps are homotopic, are questions which in theory carry some geometric data along with them.
Historically such equivalences have been dealt with in mostly algebraic ways, ignoring issues of computability or the potential sizes of their geometric realizations.
However, as Gromov has said in
[
Gro99
]
,

…we can organize an innocuous enough metric on
\mathbf{S}^{3}
so that it takes more than
10^{30}
years to contract certain loops in the sphere and in the course of contraction we need to stretch the loop to something like
10^{30}
light years in size. So if
10^{30}
years is all the time you have, you conclude that the loop is not contractible and whether or not
\pi_{1}(\mathbf{S}^{3})=0
becomes a matter of opinion.

Together with Gromov, the work of Nabutovsky and Weinberger (such as
[
NW00
,
NR03
]
) paved the way for further quantitative study of topological spaces.
The past decade of work (e.g.,
[
Man15
,
Man16
,
Gut17b
,
CDMW18
,
CMW18
,
MW20
]
) by Chambers, Guth, Manin, and Weinberger, among others have developed Gromov’s initial inquiries into a fruitful field of study, turning quantitative topology from an idea into a practical theory.

Sullivan’s localization methods in homotopy theory have repeatedly proved to be an invaluable tool for understanding algebraic invariants of topological spaces, and proves useful in Gromov’s suggested program as well.
As long as we only care about the rational homotopy type of a space (i.e., if we start by rationalizing our space, or localizing our space at
\left\{0\right\}
), standard algebraic invariants for a large class of spaces can be completely encoded by Sullivan’s model.
This includes simply connected spaces, but extends more generally to
nilpotent spaces
, a class of spaces which show up naturally in the study of almost nonnegatively curved manifolds (see
[
KPT10
]
).
Since Sullivan models completely encode the rational homotopy invariants of nilpotent spaces, such spaces make ideal objects of study in the story of quantitative topology.

One may naturally view quantitative topology as a cousin of the more familiar field of geometric group theory.
For example, recall that the
Dehn function

\delta_{G}
of a finitely presented group
G
measures the size of a disk needed to fill a loop of length
\ell
(i.e., the geometric realization of a word
w
of wordlength
\ell
) in the Cayley graph of
G
.
If
G
is additionally nilpotent then Malcev’s correspondence places
G
inside a Lie group
\mathbb{G}
as a cocompact lattice.
In particular,
G
is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{G}
, and one may study the asymptotics of
\delta_{G}
in
\mathbb{G}
instead.
Alternatively, viewing
\mathbb{G}
as the universal cover of
K(G,1)
, we view
\delta_{G}
as an indicator of topological complexity.
The additional geometric data in this setting allows one to define various high-dimensional analogues of Dehn functions, all of which are isoperimetric in spirit.
That is, we ask how large an
n
-dimensional filling must be among all
(n-1)
-dimensional objects of a given size which admit a filling, where a “filling” may be defined (for example, and not exclusively) by the existence of a nullhomotopy.
See
[
You11
,
You13
]
as well as
[
BBFS08
,
Gro83
,
Whi84
]
for various notions of (not necessarily equivalent) higher order Dehn functions.

In this paper, we are primarily interested in a question of quantitative topology which can also be seen as an investigation of a certain higher order Dehn function.

{question}

Let
Y
be a closed Riemannian manifold.
If
f:\mathbf{S}^{n}\to Y
is a nullhomotopic,
L
-Lipschitz map, what is the volume of an efficient nullhomotopy in terms of
L
? How does this depend on the topology of
Y
?

In the case
Y=K(G,1)
with
G
a finitely generated nilpotent group, this question can be seen as asking for the volume of an optimal (homotopic) filling of an
L
-Lipschitz map
F:\mathbf{S}^{n}\to\widetilde{Y}=\mathbb{G}
, which in turn can be seen as a higher order Dehn function of
G
.
The current paper seeks to address this question for
Y
a nilpotent space more generally.

In
[
CDMW18
]
the answer to the above question is shown to depend in general on the rational homotopy type of the target space
Y
.
In deeper conversation with a conjecture of Gromov’s, Manin has made a study of this question when
Y
is simply connected, and shows the following:

{theorem}
[
[
Man19
, Theorem A(ii)]
]
If

is simply connected and

is
L
-Lipschitz, then one can always find a nullhomotopy whose volume is at most
O(L^{2n})
.

In fact, the full result is a strengthening of the relevant conjecture of Gromov’s; see
[
Man19
, Conjecture C; Theorem A]
.
A family of examples shows that this bound is, in some sense, sharp:

{theorem}
[
[
CMW18
, §7]
]
Let

. There is a simply connected space

and an

such that any nullhomotopy of

has volume

.

The proof of
Section

1
utilizes a powerful tool, developed by Manin for simply connected spaces in
[
Man19
]
, called the
Shadowing Principle
.
One consequence of the principle is that it provides a method for homotoping a map which is algebraically bounded (i.e., whose pullback is bounded on the level of DGAs) into a bounded geometric map.
Somewhat more concretely, suppose a genuine map
f:X\to Y
into a simply connected space has an algebraic model
f^{*}m_{Y}:\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y}\to\Omega^{*}(X)
which lies in the same homotopy class as a map whose dilatation is bounded by
L
.
(One may think of the dilatation as an algebraic analogue of the Lipschitz constant.)
The principle then demonstrates that
f
itself lies in the homotopy class of a genuine map whose Lipschitz constant is bounded (asymptotically) by
L
.

The guiding heuristic of the principle is that it is often easier to create bounded maps on the level of algebraic information, and that this is nearly enough to produce bounded geometric maps;
if you have an (unbounded) geometric map which has a bounded algebraic counterpart, then there is a bounded geometric map homotopic to the original one.
Moreover, the algebraic counterpart to this new map is not too far away from the bounded algebraic counterpart of the original.
We encourage the reader to refer to the original paper
[
Man19
]
for further exposition and intuition for this principle, as well as its applications and the idea behind the principle’s name.

One limitation of this principle is that one must manage to find bounded algebraic maps in the first place, to which one may then apply the principle.
This task may not be any easier than finding their geometric counterparts.
For instance, in
[
Man19
, §5.3]
, a naive application of the principle produces nullhomotopies of volume
O(L^{n+2})
for maps
\mathbf{S}^{m}\to\mathbf{S}^{2n}
as long as
m\geq 2n-1
.
However,
[
Ber22
]
shows that it is possible to find linear nullhomotopies for maps
\mathbf{S}^{3}\to\mathbf{S}^{2}
, and
[
BM22
, Theorem A(iii)]
implies that this linear bound is always possible for maps into spheres.

1.1.
Summary of Results

The Shadowing Principle in
[
Man19
]
requires the target spaces to be simply connected.
The main contribution of this paper is the extension of the Shadowing Principle to certain (towers of) principal fibrations (
Section

3.2
).
Concretely, this results in a generalization of the principle to nilpotent targets (
Section

3.2
).
As an application, we produce upper bounds on the volumes of nullhomotopies in general nilpotent spaces (
Section

4
).
The upper bounds we attain by this method are not yet known to be sharp, and we expect they can be refined.
A more careful analysis on simple spaces, for instance, reduces the expected upper bound somewhat significantly (
Section

4
).
Thus far we have been unable to find a family of example spaces which come close to the general upper bounds.
However, restricting our attention to spaces whose differential is quadratic (i.e., coformal spaces), we are able to futher refine the upper bounds (
Section

4
) and demonstrate a family of examples which nearly meet these bounds (
Section

5
).

To be more concrete, we briefly introduce some notation.

[

]

between compact (piecewise) smooth Riemannian manifolds with boundary.

2.
Background and Preliminaries

We provide here our notation and conventions for nilpotent spaces, as well as Sullivan DGAs.
All spaces throughout the paper, unless otherwise stated, are assumed to be compact Riemannian manifolds or finite simplicial complexes.
Let
Y
be such a space, and recall that there is an action
\pi_{1}(Y)\curvearrowright\pi_{n}(Y)
for all
n\geq 1
induced by the Whitehead bracket.
Namely, we have
\gamma\bullet\beta\coloneqq[\gamma,\beta]+\beta
where
[\cdot,\cdot]:\pi_{1}(Y)\otimes\pi_{n}(Y)\to\pi_{n}(Y)
is the Whitehead product.

Before providing all relevant definitions, we note here that we are primarily interested in spaces up to
rational equivalence
which we define a little later on.
Suffice to say, we only focus on the non-torsion aspects of algebraic invariants when possible.
For instance we might identify
\mathbf{S}^{2n+1}
and
K(\mathbb{Z},2n+1)
.
We will be primarily interested in spaces which admit triangulations, and the algebra of their differential forms, conflating the notation in this setting.
We let
\Omega^{*}(Y)
denote the smooth differential forms when
Y
is a Riemannian manifold, as well as the algebra of piecewise smooth forms when
Y
is a simplicial complex.

2.1.
Nilpotent Spaces

Let
G
be a nilpotent group acting on
M
, an abelian group.
Recall that an action
G\curvearrowright M
is
nilpotent
if the lower central series of the action terminates.
That is,
M_{c+1}\cong\left\{0\right\}
and
M_{c}\not\cong\left\{0\right\}
for some
c
, where

M_{j}=M^{G}_{j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}M&j=1,\\
\left\langle g\bullet m-m\;\middle|\;g\in G,m\in M^{G}_{j-1}\right\rangle&j>1.\end{cases}

2.1
.

If
\pi_{1}(Y)
is nilpotent and acts nilpotently on
\pi_{n}(Y)
for all
n
we say that
Y
is
nilpotent
.
If
\pi_{1}(Y)
is
c
-step nilpotent and the action is
c
-step nilpotent for all
n

Y
is
c
-step nilpotent

If

Y
is
simple
or

.
If one of the above properties holds for all
n\leq N
for some
N
we say that the property holds
up through degree
N

Clearly, simply connected spaces are simple, and simple spaces are nilpotent.
Note that nilpotent spaces don’t always enjoy the same properties as simply connected spaces.
This is especially relevant for questions of how to create new nilpotent spaces out of old ones.
For instance, nilpotent spaces are closed under direct products; however, nilpotent spaces are
not
closed under wedge sums (a property which
is
enjoyed by simply connected spaces).
One can see nearly immediately that
\mathbf{S}^{1}\vee\mathbf{S}^{1}
is not nilpotent, even though both factors are.

Rather than constructing examples of nilpotent spaces using products or wedges, we instead build nilpotent spaces up inductively. This is, nilpotent spaces may be defined as those spaces whose Postnikov tower admits a principal refinement; in particular, a nilpotent space may be constructed as the inverse limit of principal
K(G,n)
fibrations.

2.2.
Sullivan Models

We now turn to producing algebraic models of the above ideas.
This will allow use to produce a more broad collection of examples of nilpotent spaces fairly easily, without needing to describe the Postnikov tower of a space quite so tediously.

Much of this chapter is a conglomeration of definitions and results from
[
FHT12
,
FHT15
,
GM13
]
.
We again start by providing the relevant definitions and notation for Sullivan models, and then describe when it is possible to build up such models as elementary extensions.
We conclude with a few examples of such models.

For our purposes, a

(a

(\mathcal{A},d)

We define

The alternating algebra
\wedge Z
on a graded vector space
Z=\left\{{Z}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is a graded algebra, which can be made into a DGA with an appropriate choice of differential (a trivial example being
d=0
).
We define

\wedge^{k}Z=\operatorname{span}\left\{z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}\;\middle|\;z_{i}\in Z\right\}

and define
\wedge^{\geq k}Z
and
\wedge^{\leq k}Z
and
\wedge^{>k}Z
and
\wedge^{<k}Z
in the obvious ways.

When
\mathcal{A}=\wedge Z
the
space of indecomposables
is the graded vector space

Q{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{+}}{{\mathcal{A}}^{+}\wedge{\mathcal{A}}^{+}}\cong Z

and in degree
n
the map
Z\to\wedge Z
inducdes an identification

{(Q{\mathcal{A}})}^{n}\cong{Z}_{n}.

Define a commutative cochain algebra morphism
(\mathcal{A},d)\to(\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z,d)
by
{a\mapsto a\otimes 1}
.
We identify
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\otimes 1
and
Z=1\otimes Z
.
This morphism is a
Sullivan extension
if

(1)

H^{0}(\mathcal{A},d)=\mathbb{Q}
(that is,
\mathcal{A}
is connected),

(2)

Z=\left\{{Z}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is concentrated in positive degrees, and

(3)

Z
satisfies the
nilpotence condition
.
That is,
Z
is the union of an increasing family of subspaces

0={Z}(0)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq{Z}(r)\subseteq\cdots

where the differential satisfies

\im{d}|_{{Z}(r)}\subseteq\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}&r=1\\
\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge{Z}(r-1)&r>1.\end{cases}

The extension is
minimal
if in addition

(4)

\im{d}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{+}\otimes\wedge Z+\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge^{\geq 2}Z
.

The
fiber
of the extension is the DGA
(\wedge Z,\overline{d})
where
\overline{d}
is induced by the quotient

\wedge Z\cong\frac{\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z}{{\mathcal{A}}^{+}\otimes\wedge Z}.

We will drop the adjective “Sullivan” from here on out, except if the extension in question is not minimal.
Abusing terminology, we may call
\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z
itself a minimal extension of
\mathcal{A}
.
We may write
\mathcal{A}\left\langle Z\right\rangle
for
\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z
in the event that
\im{d|_{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
(i.e., under the nilpotence condition
Z=Z(1)
), and we call such a minimal extension
elementary
.

We are primarily interested in studying connected DGAs of the form
\mathcal{A}=\wedge V
for some graded vector space
V=\left\{V_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that can be built by minimal extensions of the form
\wedge V_{\leq n}\otimes\wedge V_{n+1}
.
Here,
{V}_{\leq n}
denotes
\left\{{V}_{k}\right\}_{k\leq n}
.

Suppose for instance that the extension
\wedge V_{\leq n}\otimes\wedge{V}_{n+1}
is minimal.
If
{V}_{n+1}={{V}_{n+1}}(c)
, then this extension decomposes into a sequence of
c
elementary extensions.
If this is true for every
n
, we say that
\wedge V
is
c
-step nilpotent
.
If
c=1
then every extension is elementary; hence for every
n
the differential satisfies the more strict requirement

(2.6)

\im{d|_{{V}_{n}}}\subseteq\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}

and we call
\wedge V

simple
or
abelian
.
In the event
{V}_{1}=0
the above expression is automatically satisfied, and we call
\wedge V

simply connected
.

Recall that a map of DGAs is a
quasi-isomorphism
if it induces an isomorphism on cohomogology.
A
minimal (Sullivan) model
of a DGA morphism
\varphi\colon(\mathcal{A},d)\to(\mathcal{B},d)
is a quasi-isomorphism
m_{\varphi}\colon(\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z,d)\to(\mathcal{B},d)
where
\mathcal{A}\otimes\wedge Z
is a minimal extension of
\mathcal{A}
, and where
m_{\varphi}(a\otimes 1)=\varphi(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Again, we will drop the adjective “Sullivan” and simply call such models
minimal
.

Note that our terminology disagrees slightly with that in
[
FHT15
]
. We require that
Z=\left\{{Z}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
, but they relax this condition to allow for nontrivial subspaces of degree
n=0
in the fiber.
In this way, their definition of a minimal Sullivan model is slightly more broad; however, we will soon restrict our attention to a context where these definitions agree.
(See
[
FHT15
, §3.7]
.)

The existence of minimal models of fibrations is a well-established fact:

{theorem}

[
[
FHT12
, Thm. 14.12]
]
Let
\varphi\colon(\mathcal{A},d)\to(\mathcal{B},d)
be a morphism of connected DGAs for which
H^{1}(\varphi)
is an injection.
Then
\varphi
has a (unique) minimal model.

In particular, the minimal model of a connected DGA always exists:

{proposition}

[
[
FHT15
, Prop. 1.8]
]
If
(\mathcal{B},d)
is a connected DGA, there is a minimal Sullivan algebra
\wedge V
and a quasi-isomorphism
\varphi:(\wedge V,d)\to(\mathcal{B},d)
.

Abusing terminology, we call
\wedge V
itself the
minimal Sullivan model
of
\mathcal{B}
in this situation.
In particular, we can now define the minimal model of a topological space.
As before, we will frequently drop the adjective “Sullivan.”

2.7
.

If
Y
is a Riemannian manifold or simplicial complex, the
minimal model of

is
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y}
, the minimal model of
\Omega^{*}(Y)
.
If

is a principal

fibration for

is the minimal model of

It is worth noting that the differential

of a DGA decomposes into

where

so that

maps an indecomposable to a linear combination of products of

Of particular note is the

component

.
It is straightforward to see that if

is a DGA, restricting to the quadratic component

The remaining components

of the differential are a

[

]
.
We will eventually be interested in studying the setting when the differential itself

When

we will call

(see
[

]
).

2.3.
Realization and Rationalization

In
[
HMR16
]
it is shown that if

(equivalently,

-localization) of

in which

is a bijection for all

.
Moreover, the rationalization functor

, thought of as the Malcev completion of

, is exact in the category of (
c
-step) nilpotent groups.
When
M
is abelian, this rationalization

agrees with the standard tensor product with
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.8
.

Let

and

be nilpotent spaces.
Suppose

is an isomorphism for all

.
In this case we call

a
rational (homotopy) equivalence
.
Two spaces
X
and
X^{\prime}
are

if there is a chain of rational equivalences connecting them:

X\xrightarrow{}Y_{0}\xleftarrow{}Y_{1}\xrightarrow{}\cdots\xleftarrow{}Y_{N}\xrightarrow{}X^{\prime}

Minimal models provide a functor from topological spaces to differential graded algebras
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\square}:\mathbf{Top}\to\mathbf{DGA}
.
Dually there is a “realization” functor
\langle\;\cdot\;\rangle:\mathbf{DGA}\to\mathbf{Top}
for producing a topological space out of the given algebraic data.

2.9
.

Given a space
X
the
rationalization of
X
is
X_{\mathbb{Q}}=\langle\mathcal{M}^{*}_{X}\rangle
.

A central power of rational homotopy theory is that for nilpotent spaces, these functors are inverses up to rational equivalence.
That is

{theorem}
[
[
FHT15
, Proposition 8.2]
]

induces a rational equivalence

X\to\langle\Omega^{*}(X)\rangle\xrightarrow{\langle m_{X}\rangle}\langle\mathcal{M}^{*}_{X}\rangle=X_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Since we are interested in nilpotent spaces up to rationalization, we will always assume that we are working with any rationally equivalent space when possible.

2.4.
Algebraic Obstruction Theory

Following now
[
GM13
, §11]
, we present some homotopy theory for DGAs, with a quantitative focus as in
[
Man19
, §3.2]
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be DGAs with morphisms
\varphi,\psi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
.
An
(algebraic) homotopy
from
\varphi
to
\psi
is then a morphism

\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle

for which
\Phi|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
dt=0\end{subarray}}=\varphi
and
\Phi|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
dt=0\end{subarray}}=\psi
.
For such an algebraic homotopy
\Phi
, we will write
\Phi^{i}_{j}
for the
t^{i}(dt)^{j}
term of
\Phi
.
Moreover, we think of
\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\left\langle t^{(0)},dt^{(1)}\right\rangle
as an algebraic model for the unit interval
I\coloneqq[0,1]
.
In the event that
\mathcal{B}=\Omega^{*}(X)
for a sufficiently nice space
X
, we may realize this algebraic model as a genuine interval by a morphism

\rho:\Omega^{*}(X)\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle\to\Omega^{*}(X\times I).

We may further utilize this algebraic model of the unit interval as a tool for integration of homotopies.
That is, we define a linear operator
\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}:\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle\to\mathcal{B}
by

\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}b\otimes t^{i}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}b\otimes t^{i}dt=(-1)^{\deg{b}}\frac{1}{i+1}b,

as well as an operator
\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}:\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle\to\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle
by

\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}b\otimes t^{i}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}b\otimes t^{i}dt=(-1)^{\deg{b}}\frac{1}{i+1}b\otimes t^{i+1}.

These operators will allow us to discuss obstructions to extending homotopies, and serve as formal analogues of fiberwise integration (see
[
BT13
]
).
Before this discussion, however, we state the following facts which are easily proved from the given definitions.

{proposition}

These operators satisfy the conditions

(2.11)

\displaystyle d\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}u\right)+\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}du

\displaystyle=u|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
dt=0\end{subarray}}-u|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
dt=0\end{subarray}}

(2.12)

\displaystyle d\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}u\right)+\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}du

\displaystyle=u-u|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
dt=0\end{subarray}}\otimes 1.

{corollary}

If
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathbb{R}\left\langle t,dt\right\rangle
is a homotopy from
\varphi
to
\psi
then

d\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(a)\right)+\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}d\Phi(a)=\psi(a)-\varphi(a).

These tools allow us to develop algebraic obstruction theory, which provides algorithmic processes for computing algebraic obstructions. These obstructions are used to determine whether a partial algebraic homotopy (a map which is a homotopy when restricted to a sub-DGA) may be extended to the entire DGA.

Let

Having fixed a definition for

\operatorname{Dil}\left(\varphi\right)\coloneqq\max\left\{\operatorname{Dil}\left(\varphi|_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{B}}\right),\|\varphi|_{V}\|_{op}^{1/n}\right\}.

For example, suppose that

Then

Then under an appropriate definition we have

Suppose further that

for which

Since

and the norms of

and

.
More specifically if

is itself

\operatorname{Dil}\left(f^{*}m_{Y}\right)\leq C^{\prime}L
.

Given a DGA homotopy

For each

we define a realization

by

as a heuristic for the “formal length” of the formal interval.
With this, we may define a family of dilatations for

\operatorname{Dil}_{T}\left(\Phi\right)\coloneqq\operatorname{Dil}\left(\rho_{T}\Phi\right).

3.
The Shadowing Principles

In this, the heart of the paper, we generalize the Shadowing Principle of
[
Man19
]
to targets which appear as principal
K(G,n)
fibrations, and hence to nilpotent spaces.
We would ultimately like to get an upper bound on the volume of nullhomotopies in nilpotent spaces, and the original principle provided a useful tool for such analysis in the simply connected setting (see
[
Man19
, Theorem 5.5]
).
By the help of
Section

3.2
, we attain our goal in
Section

4
.

Before turning to our versions of the Shadowing Principle, we provide some intuition for interpreting the principle outside of the fibered setting.
Given a target
Y
and an
n
-dimensional space
X
, we assume that we have an algebraic map
\varphi:\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y}\to\Omega^{*}(X)
which has dilatation bounded by a quantity
L
.
We might wonder how far away we must look to find a genuine map which has similar bounds.
That is, we put some reasonable metric on
\Hom(\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y},\Omega^{*}(X))
, such as

d(\varphi,\psi)=\inf\left\{\operatorname{length}(\Phi)\;\middle|\;\varphi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\simeq}}\psi\right\}

where the
formal length
of such a homotopy
\Phi
is

\operatorname{length}(\Phi)\coloneqq\operatorname{Dil}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}\Phi\right).

This gives constant homotopies trivial length, is nondegenerate according to applications of (
2.12
), and satisfies the triangle inequality by
[
Man19
, Prop. 3.11]
.
With this metric in mind, the Shadowing Principle states that if
\varphi
is itself in the homotopy class of some genuine map
f:X\to Y
and
\operatorname{Dil}\left(\varphi\right)\leq L
, then there is
O(L)
-Lipschitz map
g\simeq f
whose pullback
g^{*}m_{Y}
is at most
O(L)
far from
\varphi
.
If it is too hard to construct a bounded geometric map with certain properties, it may be easier to construct one whose pullback is bounded.
The principle then yields a genuine map homotopic to the original map, but with bouded Lipschitz constant.

In
Section

3.1
we introduce establish necessary quantitative results in simplicial geometry, which are crucial to the proof of the fibered and nilpotent versions of the principle.
In
Section

3.2
we prove the relevant principles.

3.1.
Simplicial Geometry

We work primarily in the simplicial category as our model for homotopy theory.
See
[
GJ09
]
for details on this setting, and its equivalence to the model category of CW complexes.
In particular, fibrations will all be (geometric realizations of) fibrations of finite simplicial sets.
A key ingredient in this work (as in
[
CDMW18
,
CMW18
,
Man19
]
) is that of simplicial approximation.
Given a simplicial complex
X
with standard metric, one of the most common subdivision schemes of
X
is barycentric subdivision.
This scheme has certain geometric disadvantages though; simplices may become arbitrarily skinny, causing massive distortion in comparison to the standard simplex.

We say that a simplicial subdivision of

is
L
-regular
if there is a constant

(depending on the dimension of

) for which simplices are
r

\frac{1}{L}
.
Although barycentric subdivision is not regular, there are a handful of schemes (see
[
FW13
,
EG99
]
for examples) which are.
We assume from here on out that all subdivisions will be regular.

Lipschitz maps between simplicial complexes can be approximated by simplicial maps without increasing the Lipschitz constant too much.
First, recall the following definitions:

3.1
.

Let

be a Lipschitz homotopy.
After embedding

in a (piecewise) smooth manner, Rademacher’s Theorem implies that

is differentiable almost everywhere (since

is Lipschitz), and hence one may define the

of

by

\vol{H}\coloneqq\int_{X\times I}|\operatorname{Jac}(H)|\dvol{}.

The
length
of

is

\operatorname{length}(H)\coloneqq\max\left\{\Lip(H|_{\left\{x\right\}\times I})\;\middle|\;x\in X\right\}.

The

of

is

\operatorname{thickness}(H)\coloneqq\max\left\{\Lip(H|_{X\times\left\{t\right\}})\;\middle|\;t\in I\right\}.

Observe that for

-dimensional space,
\vol(H)=O(\operatorname{length}(H))\cdot O(\operatorname{thickness}(H)^{n})
.

{proposition}

[Quantitative Simplicial Approximation Theorem
[

In particular,

Sketch of proof.

Paying quantitative attention to the proof of the (non-quantiative) simplicial approximation theorem from
[

]

and uses the Lebesgue covering lemma to carefully produce nearly-linear homotopies between the uncontrolled map and its simplicial approximation.
By induction up the skeleta of

4.1.
Filtrations

The main quantity we analyze is the weight of indecomposables of a minimal model.
This will give us a measure of how the differential interacts with certain indecomposables.

4.1
.

Let
\omega\in{V}_{n}
be indecomposable of degree
n
for a minimal Sullivan DGA
(\wedge V,d)
.
Fix a basis
\left\{\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\right\}
on
V
.
The
weight
of
\omega
is defined by
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\coloneqq|\omega|=n
if
d\omega=0
, and otherwise, writing

d\omega=\sum_{i}r_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{i_{1}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{i_{R_{i}}})

as a linear combination of products of indecomposables, we inductively define

\mathrm{wt}(\omega)=\max_{i}\left\{\sum_{k}\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{i_{k}})\right\}.

We may further define the weight of a product of indecomposables to be the maximal sum of weights of the individual factors.
Note that though we started with a basis for
V
, the weight is well-defined independent of the specific basis we started with.
Later we will set particular bases and conventions of notation.

In order to perform analysis on the weights of indecomposables, we present some helpful filtrations on the Sullivan DGAs in terms of the differential
d
.
The indexing of
[
FHT15
, §2]
(from whom we borrow our notation) begins at
J=0
.
Unfortunately, this conflicts with the convention of initializing the indices of the central series at
1
, to which we adhere.
This collision of conventions is somewhat inevitable.
We stick with convention—at the cost of this collision—and index starting at
J=1
.

4.2
.

The
cautious filtration
on
{V}_{n}
is
W_{n}^{J}\subseteq W_{n}^{J+1}
defined by

W_{n}^{J}\coloneqq\begin{cases}{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n})&J=1\\
{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left(W_{1}^{J-1}\wedge W_{n}^{J-1}\right)\right)&J>1.\end{cases}

The
naive filtration
on
{V}_{n}
is
\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}\subseteq\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J+1}
defined by

\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}\coloneqq\begin{cases}{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n})&J=1\\
{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left({V}_{1}\wedge\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J-1}\right)\right)&J>1.\end{cases}

That is, one may always pick
{V}_{n}^{\leq J}=\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}
, but it may be possible to find a more refined definition of
{V}_{n}^{\leq J}
.
Moreover, having fixed a norm on
{V}_{n}
we define the
M_{n}^{J}
(resp.
\widetilde{M}_{n}^{J}
) the
J
th cautious step
of the cautious filtration by

M_{n}^{J}\coloneqq\begin{cases}W_{n}^{1}&J=1\\
\left(W_{n}^{J-1}\right)^{\perp}\cap W_{n}^{J}&J>1.\end{cases}

and the
J
th naive step
of the naive filtration by

\widetilde{M}_{n}^{J}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\widetilde{W}_{n}^{1}&J=1\\
\left(\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J-1}\right)^{\perp}\cap\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}&J>1.\end{cases}

In particular observe that

M_{n}^{J+1}\oplus W_{n}^{J}=W_{n}^{J+1}

and

\widetilde{M}_{n}^{J+1}\oplus\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}=\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J+1}.

{note}

The distinction in the naive and cautious filtrations is subtle, but important.
In the naive filtration, we essentially say that “we know
\pi_{1}
acts on our space, and we don’t care
which
elements are doing the action”, and so we take all of the action all at once.
In the cautious filtration, we move more slowly, considering only those pieces in the
J
th step of
\pi_{1}
itself, when building the
(J+1)
st stage of the filtration.
Because of this, the cautious filtration might take twice as long as the naive one to terminate.
The main result of this section is that in fact the two filtrations agree.

We begin with the easy direction:

{proposition}

{V}_{n}^{\leq J}\subseteq\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}
and
W_{n}^{J}\subseteq\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}
for all
n
and all
J
.

Proof.

For
J=1
, by definition we have
\im{d|_{{V}_{n}^{\leq 1}}}\subseteq\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}
, and so obviously

{V}_{n}^{\leq 1}\subseteq{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\right)=W_{n}^{1}.

Moreover, clearly by definition we have
W_{n}^{1}=\widetilde{W}_{n}^{1}

Inductively then we have

\im{d|_{{V}_{n}^{\leq J}}}\subseteq\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left({V}_{1}\wedge{V}_{n}^{\leq J-1}\right)\subseteq\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left({V}_{1}\wedge\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J-1}\right)

{V}_{n}^{\leq J}\subseteq{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left({V}_{1}\wedge\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J-1}\right)\right)=\widetilde{W}_{n}^{J}.

\displaystyle W_{n}^{J}

\displaystyle={V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left(W_{1}^{J-1}\wedge W_{n}^{J-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq{V}_{n}\cap d^{-1}\left(\wedge^{\geq 2}{V}_{<n}\oplus\left({V}_{1}\wedge W_{n}^{J-1}\right)\right)

{corollary}

\widetilde{W}_{n}^{c}={V}_{n}

By the previous proposition

∎

4.3

For

When

.

Note that

To this end, write

A priori,

is unbounded.
Let

Moreover, fix

In particular,

Write

{lemma}

\left\langle d\Omega,\xi\right\rangle<0

Write

.
Since

Since

Since

\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
is a quadratic summand of
d\omega
for which
d(\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})
contains a cancelling summand of
\xi\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
.
Suppose without loss of generality that
|\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}|\leq|\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}|
.
If
(d\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
cancels with
\xi\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
, then by consideration of degrees we must have
\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\overline{\mathbf{y}}
.
On the other hand, suppose
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge d\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
cancels with
\xi\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
.
If
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
is overweight, then either
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
agrees with
\overline{\mathbf{y}}
(in which case, we were actually considering internal cancellation), or else
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
is an overweight factor of
\xi
which contradicts the minimality assumptions on
|\overline{\mathbf{x}}|
.
On the other hand, if
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
is not overweight, then
\xi=\xi_{2}
must have
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
as a factor.
That is,
\xi_{2}=\overline{\mathbf{p}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{q}}=\overline{\mathbf{p}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
.
But then
\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge d\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge d\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime},\overline{\mathbf{p}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle\neq 0
implies that
\overline{\mathbf{p}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
is a nonzero summand of
d\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
.
Since
\overline{\mathbf{y}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{p}}
are both overweight, this contradicts the inductive assumption.

Finally, suppose there is a cubic summand
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}
in
d\omega
whose differential contains (without loss of generality) a cancelling summand
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
which was produced by
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\wedge d\overline{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}
.
Inductively, since this term came from a quadratic differential on
\overline{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}
, we may assume that
\overline{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
is not overweight.
If
\xi=\xi_{2}
, then
\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle=0
by consideration of word lengths, and so it is enough to consider cancellation when
\xi=\xi_{3}
.
But in this case, note that each factor of
\overline{\mathbf{a}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{b}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{c}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
is overweight by assumption.
This implies that each factor of
\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
is overweight as well, contradicting the weight of
\overline{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
.

Therefore, if the weight of
\omega
is maximized by a summand of the form
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
, at most one factor is overweight, and so without loss of generality
\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})\leq 2|\overline{\mathbf{x}}|-2
and
\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})\leq 2|\overline{\mathbf{y}}|-1
. In particular,

\mathrm{wt}(\omega)=\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})\leq 2(|\overline{\mathbf{x}}|+|\overline{\mathbf{y}}|)-3=2(|\omega|+1)-3=2|\omega|-1.

On the other hand, if the weight of
\omega
is maximized by a summand of the form
\overline{\mathbf{a}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{b}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{c}}
, each factor may be overweight, which inductively implies that

\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq(2|\overline{\mathbf{a}}|-1)+(2|\overline{\mathbf{b}}|-1)+(2|\overline{\mathbf{c}}|-1)=2(|\omega|+1)-3=2|\omega|-1,

and
\omega
is overweight when
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)
is maximized in one of these ways.
∎

We record this improvement as a separate result:

{corollary}

If
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y}=\wedge V
is simple, then
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq 2n-1
for all
\omega\in{V}_{n}
.

In particular the bounds in
Section

4
hold, and thus so does
Section

4
.

4.4.
Bounds on Coformal Spaces

We conclude this section by reducing the upper bounds for coformal spaces.
By
[
NM78
, Prop. 3.3]
, we may take as our definition that a space
Y
is
coformal
if
(\mathcal{M}^{*}_{Y},d)
is such that
d=d_{1}:V\to V\wedge V
is quadratic.

{lemma}

If
d
is quadratic and
\omega\in M_{n}^{J}
, then

d\omega\in\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leq n_{2}\\
n_{1}+n_{2}=n+1\end{subarray}}\left(\bigoplus_{i_{1}+i_{2}\leq J+c}M_{n_{1}}^{i_{1}}\wedge M_{n_{2}}^{i_{2}}\right).

Proof.

Note that
a priori
, we know only that
i_{1},i_{2}\leq J
and
i_{1},i_{2}\leq c
in the above expression.
For instance, since
d
is quadratic, we can write

d\omega=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leq n_{2}\\
n_{1}+n_{2}=n+1\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\leq c}\Omega_{(i_{1},i_{2},n_{1},n_{2})}\right)\in\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leq n_{2}\\
n_{1}+n_{2}=n+1\end{subarray}}\left(\bigoplus_{i_{1},i_{2}\leq c}M_{n_{1}}^{i_{1}}\wedge M_{n_{2}}^{i_{2}}\right).

The result is obvious for
n\leq 2
.
Inductively assume the result is true for all
k<n
for some
n>2
.
By
Section

4.1
, if
\omega\in M_{n}^{J}
, then
d_{nil}\omega\in\bigoplus_{i+j\leq J}M_{1}^{i}\wedge M_{n}^{j}
so that it is enough to consider terms of
d_{sim}\omega
.
To this end, let
(N_{1},N_{2})
be such that
\Omega_{(i_{1},i_{2},N_{1},N_{2})}\neq 0
for some
(i_{1},i_{2})
.
In particular, assume
1<N_{1}\leq N_{2}<n
.
Moreover, define

\ell\coloneqq\max\left\{i_{1}+i_{2}\;\middle|\;\Omega_{(i_{1},i_{2},N_{1},N_{2})}\neq 0\right\}

and fix

(I_{1},I_{2})\coloneqq\max_{\leq_{2}}\left\{(i_{1},i_{2})\;\middle|\;i_{1}+i_{2}=\ell,\;\Omega_{(i_{1},i_{2},N_{1},N_{2})}\neq 0\right\}.

Let
\Omega=\overline{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\in M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}}
be a nonzero basic summand of
d\omega
, and define
\xi\coloneqq(\delta\overline{\mathbf{x}})\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\in M_{1}^{1}\wedge M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}-1}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}}
.

By
Section

4.1
, either
\xi=0
or
\xi
is cancelled externally.
However, injectivity of
\delta
and the fact that
(I_{1}-1,N_{1})<_{2}(I_{2},N_{2})
implies nearly immediately that
\xi\neq 0
.
Hence, there is some external summand
\Omega^{\prime}=\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\in M_{N_{1}^{\prime}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}\wedge M_{N_{2}^{\prime}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}}
of
d\omega
for which
d\Omega^{\prime}
has a summand
\xi^{\prime}
that cancels with
\xi
.
Here as before we order degrees so that
N_{1}^{\prime}\leq N_{2}^{\prime}
.

Since
\xi^{\prime}
is a summand of
d\Omega^{\prime}
and since
\xi^{\prime}
cancels with
\xi
we must have

\xi^{\prime}\in\left[\left(\im{d|_{M_{N_{1}^{\prime}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}}}\wedge M_{N_{2}^{\prime}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}}\right)+\left(M_{N_{1}^{\prime}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}\wedge\im{d|_{M_{N_{2}^{\prime}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}}}}\right)\right]\cap\left[M_{1}^{1}\wedge M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}-1}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}}\right].

In particular there are
a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}
for which

\xi^{\prime}\in\left(M_{n_{1,1}}^{a_{1}}\wedge M_{n_{1,2}}^{b_{1}}\wedge M_{N_{2}^{\prime}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}}\right)+\left(M_{N_{1}^{\prime}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}\wedge M_{n_{2,1}}^{a_{2}}\wedge M_{n_{2,2}}^{b_{2}}\right)

where
n_{1,1}+n_{1,2}=N_{1}^{\prime}+1
and
n_{2,1}+n_{2,2}=N_{2}^{\prime}+1
.
Suppose first that

(A1)

\xi\in\left(M_{n_{1,1}}^{a_{1}}\wedge M_{n_{1,2}}^{b_{1}}\wedge M_{N_{2}^{\prime}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}}\right)\cap\left(M_{1}^{1}\wedge M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}-1}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}}\right).

4.7.1
.

Assuming (
A1
), then
\Omega^{\prime}\in(I_{1},I_{2},N_{1},N_{2})
.

Proof of Claim 4
:

By considering degrees, and the assumption that
N_{1}^{\prime}\leq N_{2}^{\prime}
, we see that we must have
N_{2}^{\prime}=N_{2}
.
Moreover, without loss of generality we have
n_{1,1}=1
and
N_{1}^{\prime}=n_{1,2}=N_{1}
, since
1<N_{1}
.
In particular, this implies that
a_{1}=1
.
Furthermore, since

\xi^{\prime}\in\im{d|_{M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}}}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}^{\prime}},

this implies that in fact
\xi^{\prime}
came from the nilpotent component of the differential.
By definition we must then have
b_{1}\leq I_{1}^{\prime}-1
.

Suppose first that
N_{1}<N_{2}
.
Then
I_{2}^{\prime}=I_{2}
and
b_{1}=I_{1}-1
.
But then
I_{1}=1+b_{1}\leq I_{1}^{\prime}
.
Thus since
(N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime})=(N_{1},N_{2})
, the maximality of
(I_{1},I_{2})
implies that in fact
(I_{1}^{\prime},I_{2}^{\prime})=(I_{1},I_{2})
.

If
N_{1}=N_{2}
, there are two possibilities.
If
b_{1}=I_{1}-1
, then the above proof shows
(I_{1}^{\prime},I_{2}^{\prime},N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime})=(I_{1},I_{2},N_{1},N_{2})
.
On the other hand, if
b_{1}=I_{2}
then
I_{1}^{\prime}\geq b_{1}+1>I_{2}
, which contradicts the maximality of
I_{2}
.

By injectivity of
\delta
this implies that
\Omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{span}\left\{\Omega\right\}
, which means that cancellation was internal, a contradiction.

Suppose next that

(A2)

\xi^{\prime}\in\left(M_{N_{1}^{\prime}}^{I_{1}^{\prime}}\wedge M_{n_{2,1}}^{a_{2}}\wedge M_{n_{2,2}}^{b_{2}}\right)\cap\left(M_{1}^{1}\wedge M_{N_{1}}^{I_{1}-1}\wedge M_{N_{2}}^{I_{2}}\right).

Note that by induction we have
a_{2}+b_{2}\leq I_{2}^{\prime}+c
.
In the previous case, we could essentially ignore the situation
N_{1}^{\prime}=1
.
Now however we have either
N_{1}^{\prime}=1
or (without loss of generality)
n_{2,1}=1
.
We consider these in the reverse order:

4.7.2
.

Assuming (
A2
), if
n_{2,1}=1
then
(I_{2}^{\prime},I_{1}^{\prime},N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime})=(I_{1},I_{2},N_{1},N_{2})
.

Proof of Claim 5
:

In this situation, we see by the same arguments as in the previous claim, that
N_{1}^{\prime}=N_{1}
and
N_{2}^{\prime}=n_{2,2}=N_{2}
, and that furthermore
a_{2}=1
so that
b_{2}\leq I_{2}^{\prime}-1
.

In the event that
N_{1}<N_{2}
we have therefore that
b_{2}=I_{2}
which means
I_{2}^{\prime}\geq 1+b_{2}>I_{2}
, contradicting the maximality of
I_{2}
.

If
N_{1}=N_{2}
there are two possibilities. If
b_{2}=I_{2}
then as above we contradict the maximailty of
I_{2}
.
On the other hand, if
b_{2}=I_{1}-1
then
I_{2}^{\prime}\geq b_{2}+1=I_{1}
.
Hence (noting the switch of position of
I_{2}^{\prime}
and
I_{1}^{\prime}
in the lefthand term) we must have
(I_{2}^{\prime},I_{1}^{\prime},N_{1}^{\prime},N_{2}^{\prime})=(I_{1},I_{2},N_{1},N_{2})
.

As before, in the above setting injectivity of
\delta
implies then that
\Omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{span}\left\{\Omega\right\}
.

4.7.3
.

Assuming (
A2
), if
N_{1}^{\prime}=1
, then
I_{1}+I_{2}\leq J+c
.

Proof of Claim 6
:

In this case, because
1<N_{1}
, we know that
N_{1}^{\prime}\neq N_{1}
.
Therefore, without loss of generality, we have
n_{2,1}=N_{1}
and
n_{2,2}=N_{2}
,
which implies that
a_{2}=I_{1}-1
and
b_{2}=I_{2}
.
Moreover we must have
I_{1}^{\prime}=1
, and thus

\Omega^{\prime}
is a term in
d_{nil}\omega
.
In particular,
I_{2}^{\prime}=J-1
.
By induction, therefore, we see that
(I_{1}-1)+I_{2}=a_{2}+b_{2}\leq(J-1)+c
.

Hence
\ell=I_{1}+I_{2}\leq J+c
as required.
∎

The above result indicates that we might reasonably hope to refine the bounds on the weights of indecomposables in the event that
d
is quadratic.
Indeed, we can.

{lemma}

Let
\omega\in M_{n}^{J}
for some
c
-step nilpotent DGA
(\wedge V,d)
with quadratic differential.
Then for
n\geq 2
we have

\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq(c+1)(n-1)+J-c.

In particular any
\omega\in{V}_{n}
has
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq(c+1)(n-1)
.

Proof.

For
n=2
and
J=1
the result is trivial because
d|_{M_{2}^{1}}=0
.
Note that for
\overline{\mathbf{t}}\in M_{1}^{J}
we have
\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{t}})=J
.
Since
d
is quadratic we have
d|_{M_{2}^{J}}=d_{nil}|_{M_{2}^{J}}
, so that
Section

4.1
implies inductively that if
\omega\in M_{2}^{J}
then
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq J+1
which is the desired form when
n=2
.
In particular,
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq c+1
for all
\omega\in{V}_{2}
.

Inductively assume the result holds for all
(j,k)<_{2}(J,n)
for some
(J,n)
.
Suppose that
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}\in M_{n_{1}}^{i_{1}}\wedge M_{n_{2}}^{i_{2}}
is a summand of
d\omega
.
By
Section

4.4
we have
i_{1}+i_{2}\leq J+c
.
By induction, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle\leq\Big((c+1)(n_{1}-1)+i_{1}-c\Big)+\Big((c+1)(n_{2}-1)+i_{2}-c\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(c+1)(n-1)+J-c.

Since
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\wedge\overline{\mathbf{y}}
was an arbitrary summand of
d\omega
we have
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq(c+1)(n-1)+J-c
as desired.
∎

{corollary}

If
Y
is coformal then
\mathrm{wt}(\omega)\leq(c+1)(n-1)
for all
\omega\in{V}_{n}
.

In particular the bounds in
Section

4
hold, and thus so does
Section

4
.

5.
High Volume Nullhomotopies in Coformal Spaces

We conclude by demonstrating the (near) sharpness of the bounds in
Section

4
.
This section may be viewed as motivation for further study of the general bounds of
Section

4
.

We take as inspiration a family of maps into simply connected examples which must necessarily have high-volume nullhomotopies, as constructed in
[
CMW18
, §7]
.
There, one constructs a space
X_{n}
by attaching
(n+1)
-cells to
\mathbf{S}^{2}\vee\mathbf{S}^{2}
in order to kill
\pi_{n}(\mathbf{S}^{2}\vee\mathbf{S}^{2})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. By taking iterated Whitehead brackets of
L
-Lipschitz maps one can find a nullhomotopic
L
-Lipschitz map
S^{n}\to X_{n}
for which every nullhomotopy has degree
\Omega(L^{2n-2})
over some
(n+1)
-cell.
We seek a generalization of this example to nilpotent spaces, where the volume fundamentally relies on the nilpotence class of the space in question.

Section

4
shows that the family we construct in
Section

5
is (nearly) optimal among coformal spaces.

By
[
NM78
, Proposition 3.3]
, we may view a coformal space as one whose (rational) higher order Whitehead brackets contain zero.
(See
[
AA78
]
for more on the connection between higher order Whitehead brackets and minimal models.)
In this subsection we build
L
-Lipschitz maps into a
c
-step space, and maps into that space through (standard) Whitehead brackets.
In
Section

5
we show that the resulting map has sufficiently high degree to require nullhomotopies of volume
\Omega(L^{(c+1)(n-1)})
.

Define
X_{c}\coloneqq\bigvee_{i=1}^{c}\mathbf{S}^{2}_{i}
to be a boquet of
c
-many
2
-spheres. Note that
H_{2}(X_{c})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{c}
.
Define
\mathcal{T}\colon X_{c}\to X_{c}
to be any self-map inducing the
(c\times c)
-matrix

(5.1)

\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
1&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&1&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&1\end{bmatrix}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}^{c})\cong\operatorname{Aut}(H_{2}(X_{c})).

For each
q\in\left\{A,B\right\}
let
\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{q}
be a copy of the mapping torus of
\mathcal{T}
:

\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{q}\coloneqq\frac{\left(X_{c}\times I\right)\cup_{\mathcal{T}}\left(X_{c}\times\left\{1\right\}\right)}{(x,0)\sim(x,1)}.

Given
*\in X_{c}
common to each copy of
\mathbf{S}^{2}
define
\gamma=\left\{*\right\}\times\mathbf{S}^{1}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{q}
to be the circle traced out by the basepoint.
Denote
X_{2c}=(X_{c})_{A}\vee(X_{c})_{B}=(\bigvee_{i=1}^{c}\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,i})\vee(\bigvee_{i=1}^{c}\mathbf{S}^{2}_{B,i})
.
Define
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
to be the mapping torus of
{\mathcal{T}_{A}}\vee{\mathcal{T}_{B}}:X_{2c}\to X_{2c}
under the obvious choices of domain and codomain for each copy of
\mathcal{T}
.
Note that
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}=\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{A}\cup_{\gamma}\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{B}
.
It is straightforward to see that
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}})\cong\mathbb{Z}
is generated by the natural inclusion
t\colon\mathbf{S}^{1}\to\gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
.

{lemma}

Let
\alpha_{j}\in\pi_{2}(\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{A})
be the homotopy class of the map
\mathbf{S}^{2}\to\mathbf{S}^{2}_{j}\hookrightarrow\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{A}
.
Then
t\bullet\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j+1}
, and
[t,\alpha_{j}]=\alpha_{j+1}
for
j<c
while
t\bullet\alpha_{c}=\alpha_{c}
and
[t,\alpha_{c}]=0
.
Identical results hold for
\beta_{j}
generators of
\pi_{2}(\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{B})
.

Proof.

By definition the generator
t\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}})
acts on
\pi_{2}(\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)_{A})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\pi_{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
under the basis
\left\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{c}\right\}
by the
(c\times c)
matrix in (
5.1
) above.
∎

Inductively use Whitehead brackets to define

\zeta_{k,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\beta_{1}&k=2,j=1,\\
[\alpha_{1},\zeta_{k-1,c}]&k>2,j=1,\\
[t,\zeta_{k,j-1}]&j>1.\end{cases}

Conflating maps with the homotopy elements they represent, observe that
\zeta_{k,j}\in\pi_{k}(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}})
.

{lemma}

For all
k>2
and
j<c
we have
[t,[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]]=[\alpha_{j+1},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
and for all
k\geq 2
we have
[t,\zeta_{k,c}]=0
.

Proof.

The fact
[t,\zeta_{2,c}]=0
is true by
Section

5
(applied, of course, to
\beta_{j}
instead of
\alpha_{j}
).
Let
k>2
and assume for induction that the result holds for all lower indices.
By the Jacobi identity we have

\displaystyle[t,[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]]

\displaystyle=-[\alpha_{j},[\zeta_{k-1,c},t]]-[\zeta_{k-1,c},[t,\alpha_{j}]]

(induction)

\displaystyle=-[\alpha_{j},0]-[\zeta_{k-1,c},[t,\alpha_{j}]]

(antisymmetry)

\displaystyle=[[t,\alpha_{j}],\zeta_{k-1,c}].

In the event
j<c
,
Section

5
implies then that
[t,[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]]=[\alpha_{j+1},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
while if
j=c
, the same result indicates that
[t,[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]]=[0,\zeta_{k-1,c}]=0
as required.
∎

{corollary}

\zeta_{k,j}=[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
for all
k
and
j
.
In particular
\zeta_{k,c}=[\alpha_{c},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
.

Proof.

Inductively by the previous lemma,
\zeta_{k,j}=[t,\zeta_{k,j-1}]=[t,[\alpha_{j-1},\zeta_{k-1,c}]]=[\alpha_{j},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
, where the base case
j=1
is true by definition.
∎

{lemma}

For all
k
, one has
\zeta_{k,c}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
denote the mapping cylinder of
\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}
.
The universal cover
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}}
of
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
may be viewed as
\mathbb{Z}
copies of
\mathscr{M}
identified in the obvious ways.
Let
\mathscr{M}_{p}
denote the copies of
\mathscr{M}
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}}
over the interval
[-p,p]
so that
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}=\bigcup_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{M}_{p}
.

Let
\widetilde{\alpha_{c}}:\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,c}\hookrightarrow X_{2c}\times\left\{0\right\}\hookrightarrow\widetilde{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}}
lift
\alpha_{c}:\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,c}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
in the obvious way.
Similarly denote
\widetilde{\beta_{c}}
and
\widetilde{\zeta_{k,c}}
.
It is not hard to see that
\mathscr{M}_{p}
deformation retracts to
X_{2c}\times\left\{p\right\}
through standard deformation retractions of each copy of
\mathscr{M}
so that
\pi_{n}(\mathscr{M}_{p})\cong\pi_{n}(X_{2c})
for all
p
.
Moreover, because
t\bullet\alpha_{c}=\alpha_{c}
(and similarly for
\beta_{c}
) we see that this retract sends
(\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,c}\vee\mathbf{S}^{2}_{B,c})\times\left\{0\right\}
identically onto
(\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,c}\vee\mathbf{S}^{2}_{B,c})\times\left\{p\right\}
.
Then the retract identifies the class of
\widetilde{\alpha_{c}}
with that of
\mathbbm{1}_{A,c}:\mathbf{S}^{2}_{A,c}\hookrightarrow X_{2c}
.
The same is true for
\widetilde{\beta_{c}}
under the obvious changes.
That is to say, the isomorphism
\pi_{2}(\mathscr{M}_{p})\cong\pi_{2}(X_{2c})
induced by the retraction will identify
\widetilde{\alpha_{c}}
and
\widetilde{\beta_{c}}
with
\mathbbm{1}_{A,c}
and
\mathbbm{1}_{B,c}
respectively.
By induction, since
\zeta_{k,c}=[\alpha_{c},\zeta_{k-1,c}]
and since
t\bullet\zeta_{k,c}=\zeta_{k,c}
, we see that the retraction of
\mathscr{M}_{p}
identifies the class of
\widetilde{\zeta_{k,c}}
in
\pi_{k}(\mathscr{M}_{p})
with the nonzero class
[\mathbbm{1}_{A,c},[\mathbbm{1}_{A,c},\cdots[\mathbbm{1}_{A,c},\mathbbm{1}_{B,c}]\cdots]]\in\pi_{k}(X_{2c})
.
Hence for any
p
, the map
\widetilde{\zeta_{k,c}}
is not nullhomotopic in
\mathscr{M}_{p}
.

If to the contrary
\zeta_{k,c}
were nullhomotopic by some
H:\mathbf{S}^{k}\times I\to\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
, there would be a lift
\widetilde{H}:\mathbf{S}^{k}\times I\to\widetilde{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}}
nullhomotopoing
\zeta_{k,c}
inside
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}}
.
By compactness there is some
p
for which this nullhomotopy is contained entirely within
\mathscr{M}_{p}
, a contradiction.
∎

{lemma}

Let

be a space for which

and
g\colon\mathbf{S}^{n_{2}}\to Y

-Lipschitz, and the second is

{theorem}

There is a space
Y
which is
c
-step nilpotent and coformal up through degree
n
, together with a nullhomotopic,
L
-Lipschitz map
f:\mathbf{S}^{n}\to Y
such that any nullhomotopy of
f
has volume
\Omega(L^{(c+1)(n-1)})
.

Proof.

The argument here is identical to that of
[
CMW18
, §7]
.
Let
f=\zeta_{n,c}^{(L)}
, and let
Y
be the space formed by attaching
(n+1)
-cells to
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
, whose attaching maps form a basis for
\pi_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
.
Ensure that
Y
is coformal through degree
n
by attaching higher cells to kill any nontrivial higher order Whitehead products.
In particular, fix a standard nullhomotopy
N_{n,c}
of
\zeta_{n,c}
in
Y
.
Such a nullhomotopy has degree
C\neq 0
(in the sense of relative homology) over some
(n+1)
cell
q
of
Y
because (by
Section

5
),
\zeta_{n,c}
is not nullhomotopic in
\mathbb{T}_{\mathcal{T}_{A}\vee\mathcal{T}_{B}}
.
This yields a closed
(n+1)
-form
\omega
on
Y
such that
\int_{\mathbf{S}^{n}\times I}N_{n,c}^{*}\omega=C\neq 0
.
Let
f^{\prime}=\zeta_{n,c}\circ\varphi_{n,L}
where
\mathbf{S}^{n}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}
is a self-map of degree
L^{(c+1)(n-1)}
.
Note that
f^{\prime}\simeq f
as both are representatives of
L^{(c+1)(n-1)}\zeta_{n,c}
by definition.
Hence a nullhomotopy
N_{n,c,L}
of
f
may be defined by first homotoping
f
to
f^{\prime}
, followed by the standard nullhomotopy
N_{n,c}
.
This nullhomotopy has volume bounded below by
\int_{\mathbf{S}^{n}\times I}N_{n,c,L}^{*}\omega=L^{(c+1)(n-1)}C=\Omega(L^{(c+1)(n-1)})
.

Now suppose that
N^{\prime}
is some other nullhomotopy of
f
. Let
\widetilde{N}
be the map
\mathbf{S}^{n+1}\to Y
formed by performing
N_{n,c,L}
along the northern hemisphere and
N^{\prime}
along the southern hemisphere, connected along the
\mathbf{S}^{n}
equator along the map
f
itself.
The (rational) Heurewicz map sends
\pi_{n+1}(Y)
to
0
and so the total degree of
\widetilde{N}
must be
0
, and hence
\widetilde{N}
has zero degree on any
(n+1)
-cell.
In particular, this means that
\int_{\mathbf{S}^{n}\times I}(N^{\prime})^{*}\omega=\int_{\mathbf{S}^{n}\times I}N_{n,c,L}^{*}\omega=\Omega(L^{(c+1)(n-1)})
.
∎
