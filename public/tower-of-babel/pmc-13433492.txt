Outcomes of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with blast phase myelofibrosis: molecular signature and intensive chemotherapy matter
Authors: Francesca Valsecchi, Maria Chiara Finazzi, Silvia Salmoiraghi, Chiara Pavoni, Anna Grassi, Alessandra Algarotti, Marta Bellini, Marco Frigeni, Annalisa Condorelli, Benedetta Rambaldi, Giuliana Rizzuto, Gianluca Cavallaro, Clara Belotti, Martina Milani, Orietta Spinelli, Federico Lussana, Alessandro Rambaldi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with blast-phase (BP) myeloproliferative neoplasms have dismal outcomes, but allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) may offer a potential cure. However, the optimal pre-transplant blast-reduction therapy remains to be determined. We retrospectively analyzed outcomes in a molecularly annotated cohort of 24 patients with BP myelofibrosis (MF) who underwent alloHSCT between 2008 and 2023. Before conditioning, 20 patients received intensive induction chemotherapy, 1 received decitabine plus venetoclax, and 3 proceeded directly to transplant. At transplantation, 13 patients (54%) were in complete remission (CR), defined as bone marrow blast < 5%, or CR with incomplete count recovery (CRi), while 11 (46%) had active disease. The median follow-up was 2.9 years (range 0.02–15.5). The 3-year overall survival (OS) was 62%, progression-free survival (PFS) was 49%, relapse incidence was 38%, and non-relapse mortality was 12%. There was a trend toward better OS and PFS in patients transplanted in CR/CRi compared with those with active disease (OS 68% vs. 55%, HR 0.42, p = 0.16; PFS 61% vs. 36%, HR 0.49, p = 0.21). Mutations in TP53 and EZH2 were associated with significantly inferior PFS (HR 6.28, p = 0.008 for TP53 and HR 3.8, p = 0.04 for EZH2).Compared with a historical cohort of 50 patients transplanted during the same period for chronic-phase MF, outcomes were similar (3-year OS: 62% vs. 58%; p = 0.6181). In conclusion, our results suggest that achieving remission before alloHSCT in BP-MF is associated with favorable outcomes. The adverse impact of TP53 or EZH2 mutations supports early post-transplant strategies to prevent relapse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02191-7.

Introduction

Myelofibrosis (MF) is a Philadelphia-chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by bone marrow reticulin fibrosis, cytopenias, leukoerythroblastosis, extramedullary hematopoiesis resulting in splenomegaly, and constitutional symptoms. MF is classified into primary MF (PMF) and secondary MF (SMF), including post-essential thrombocythemia (ET) and post-polycythemia vera (PV) [1]. Patients with PMF and post-ET MF harbor driver mutations in JAK2 V617F in 50–60%, CALR in 25–30%, and MPL in 5–10%, while 10% are triple-negative. In post-PV MF, almost all patients display the JAK2 V617F mutation [2]. Clinical presentation and outcome are highly heterogeneous. The clinical course of MPN is characterized by an increased risk of leukemic transformation with rates of 1–4% for ET, 3–7% for PV, and 9–13% for PMF [3]. Blast phase (BP) MPN is defined by the presence of ≥ 20% of blasts in bone marrow (BM) and/or peripheral blood (PB), while in accelerated phase (AP) MPN, BM and/or PB blasts range from 10% to 19% [4, 5]. The prognosis of patients experiencing leukemic transformation is extremely poor [6–9]. AlloHSCT represents the only potentially curative treatment option for BP-MPN [7, 10–14]. To reduce relapse risk, blast reduction is recommended before transplantation [3]. However, due to the lack of randomized studies comparing intensive chemotherapy with less intensive approaches, the optimal treatment strategy before alloHSCT remains a matter of debate. In the literature, few studies specifically addressed the outcomes of patients with BP-MF undergoing alloHSCT [15–17].

In this study, we aimed to assess the outcomes of patients who underwent alloHSCT for BP-MF at our Institution and to identify clinical and molecular factors predicting post-transplant survival.

Materials and methods

Study population and definitions

Twenty-four consecutive patients with either PMF or SMF in BP, who underwent alloHSCT at ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital, Bergamo (Italy), between 2008 and 2023, were included in this analysis.

Diagnosis of MF was based on the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) [18] and the 2022 International Consensus Classification (ICC) [5] criteria. Diagnosis of BP required the presence of ≥ 20% of circulating or BM blasts [4, 5]. Patients with AP, defined by 10% to 19% blasts, were excluded. DNA samples were analyzed in 22 out of 24 patients before transplantation using a commercial Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel, which targeted gene regions associated with myeloid neoplasms (Sophia Myeloid Solution or Custom Myeloid Solution by SOPHiA GENETICS, SA, CH). The libraries were sequenced and demultiplexed on an Illumina MiniSeq instrument (San Diego, CA). The aligned sequences were analyzed to identify differences compared to the reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), with a detection limit/sensitivity (LOD) of 2.5% variant allele frequency (VAF). Variant calling and assessment of the functional effects of mutations were performed using SOPHiA DDM™ software, which references multiple online biological databases. Frameshift and nonsense variants were always considered to have a functional impact. Single-nucleotide variants and in-frame indels were included if they were not described as polymorphisms in public human variation databases (GnomAD and ddSNP). For alterations affecting splicing sites and regions, we used the Human Splicing Finder (http://www.umd.be/HSF3) to predict their effects on splicing.

Multi-hit TP53 mutations were defined according to the 2022 ICC criteria [5]. Cytogenetic information was available for 22 patients at the diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and for 23 patients at the time of transplantation. Patients were stratified into three categories according to the 2022 ELN risk classification by genetics at diagnosis of AML [19].

Treatment and response assessment

Patients treated with intensive chemotherapy received induction chemotherapy with either the “7 + 3” (cytarabine + anthracycline, such as idarubicin or daunorubicin) regimen (n = 13), the ICE (idarubicin-cytarabine-etoposide) scheme (n = 6), or CPX-351 (n = 1), followed by 1 or 2 consolidation cycles at the clinician’s discretion. One patient was treated with decitabine and venetoclax.

Response was assessed according to the 2022 ELN response criteria for AML [19] after the induction chemotherapy or 1 cycle of decitabine plus venetoclax, and at the time of transplantation. Complete remission (CR) was defined as BM blasts < 5%, absence of circulating blasts and extramedullary disease, with complete hematologic recovery (neutrophils ≥ 1 × 109/L and platelets ≥ 100 × 109/L). CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) was defined as CR above, except for neutrophils < 1 × 109/L and/or platelets < 100 × 109/L. Partial response (PR) was defined as a reduction of BM blasts by at least 50% or to a blast percentage between 5% and 25%, in the presence of complete hematologic recovery [19].

Spleen size was measured by ultrasound. Spleen data were available before induction therapy for BP in 15 patients (3 patients had undergone splenectomy) and before conditioning to transplant in 19 patients (5 patients had undergone splenectomy). Spleen response, evaluated after blast reduction therapy, was defined as any reduction in the maximum spleen diameter.

Conditioning intensity was defined according to previously published criteria [20]. Reduced intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens included thiotepa-busulfan-fludarabine (thiotepa 5 mg/kg, busulfan 8 mg/kg, and fludarabine 150 mg/m2), busulfan-fludarabine (busulfan 8 mg/kg and fludarabine 150 mg/m2), fludarabine-melphalan (fludarabine 150 mg/m2 and melphalan 140 mg/m2), and FLAMSA-TBI (fludarabine 120 mg/kg, cytosine arabinoside 8 g/m2, amsacrine 400 mg/m2, total body irradiation 4 Gy, and cyclophosphamide 120 mg/kg). Myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimens encompassed thiotepa-busulfan-fludarabine (thiotepa 10 mg/kg, busulfan 9.6 mg/kg, and fludarabine 150 mg/m2) and busulfan-fludarabine (busulfan 12.8 mg/kg and fludarabine 160 mg/m2). Graft versus host disease (GvHD) prophylaxis was chosen according to local institutional practice. The Mount Sinai Acute GvHD International Consortium (MAGIC) criteria were used to grade acute GvHD (aGvHD) [21]. Chronic GvHD (cGvHD) was classified according to the National Institute of Health (NIH) criteria published in 2015 [22]. Only patients alive at 3 months after transplantation were evaluated for cGvHD. Neutrophil recovery was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count ≥ 500/uL. Platelet recovery was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with a platelet count ≥ 20,000/uL, in the absence of platelet transfusions in the previous 7 days. Primary graft failure was defined as failure to achieve neutrophil recovery by day + 30, with associated pancytopenia. Poor bone marrow function was defined as frequent transfusion dependence (blood and/or platelets) and/or requirement of growth factor support, in the absence of other possible causes. Full donor chimerism was defined as > 95% of cells being of donor origin [23].

Study endpoints and statistical analysis

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS), defined as the time from transplantation to death from any cause, and progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from transplantation to relapse or death. Secondary endpoints included the cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) and non-relapse mortality (NRM), measured from transplantation, with death and relapse treated as competing events, respectively. Baseline continuous variables were summarized using medians with ranges, while categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. OS and PFS were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. CIR and NRM were estimated using cumulative incidence functions that account for competing risks, with group comparisons made using Gray’s test. Univariate analyses employed Cox proportional hazards models for OS and PFS, and Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models for CIR and NRM. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0).

Results

Patient, disease, and transplant characteristics

Patients, disease, and transplant characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Among the 24 patients, 5 (20.8%) had a progression from a previous PMF, 10 (41.7%) from a post-ET MF, and 9 (37.5%) from a post-PV MF. Driver mutations for MF were JAK2 V617F in 50% (n = 12), CALR in 33.3% (n = 8; type 1, n = 5 and type 2, n = 3), and MPL in 8.3% (n = 2), while 2 patients (8.3%) were triple negative. The median time between MF diagnosis and leukemic transformation was 1.3 years (range, 0-11.3 years).

At BP diagnosis, the median BM blast count was 30% (range 15–90%). According to the 2022 ELN risk classification of AML [19], 7 patients (29%) had an intermediate risk and 17 (71%) had an adverse risk-disease. Four (18%) of 22 patients had a complex karyotype. The median time from BP-MF diagnosis to alloHSCT was 4.5 months (range, 0.3–6.4 months), and the median age at transplant was 59 years (range, 50–66 years). Before transplantation, 5 patients had undergone splenectomy (4 for splenomegaly, 1 for abdominal trauma). At least one high-molecular-risk (HMR) mutation (ASXL1, EZH2, SRSF2, IDH1/2, or U2AF1) was detected in 45.5% (n = 10) of patients. Six (27%) patients had ≥ 2 HMR mutations (median of 2). The most frequent additional mutations were TET2 (31.8%), SETBP1 (31.8%), EZH2 (22.7%), RUNX1 (22.7%), and TP53 (18.2%). Two (9.1%) patients had multi-hit TP53 mutations and 2 single-hit mutations. The donor type was matched unrelated (MUD, n = 9, 38%) or mismatched unrelated (MMUD, n = 8, 33%) in most patients. Reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) was selected for 66.7% of patients (n = 16).

Treatment before transplant and response

Before alloHSCT, 20 patients (83.3%) received intensive chemotherapy, 1 patient received decitabine and venetoclax, and 3 patients received supportive therapy only (Fig. 1). Among the 20 patients treated with intensive chemotherapy, 13 underwent induction with the 7 + 3 regimen, 6 with the ICE scheme, and 1 with CPX-351. Following induction chemotherapy, 17 of 20 (85%) patients achieved CR/CRi, 1 achieved PR, and 2 had no response (NR). Subsequently, 7 patients received one or two additional cycles of intermediate-dose cytarabine or cytarabine plus idarubicin (5 for consolidation and 2 for reinduction). The patient treated with CPX-351 received one additional reinduction cycle with CPX-351. The patient treated with decitabine and venetoclax received three cycles and achieved CR after the first cycle. Among the 18 patients in CR/CRi after the first cycle of chemotherapy, 5 progressed before transplant conditioning. At transplant, disease status was CR/CRi in 13 patients (54.2%; 12 treated with intensive chemotherapy and 1 treated with decitabine plus venetoclax) and active disease in 11 (45.8%). The median bone marrow blast count at transplantation was 4%. The median spleen size was 21 cm (range 14–28 cm) at the time of blastic phase evolution and 19.5 cm (range 13–30 cm) before conditioning (17 cm in patients in CR/CRi and 22 cm in those with active disease). A reduction in spleen size was observed in 6 (50%) of 12 evaluable patients treated with intensive chemotherapy, with a median decrease of 2 cm (range, 1–8 cm).

Engraftment, relapse, and graft versus host disease

Neutrophil engraftment occurred after a median time of 16 days, and platelet recovery after 21 days. Two patients (8.3%) developed poor bone marrow function: one received donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI), and the other was successfully treated with post-transplant splenectomy and eltrombopag. Three patients (12.5%) experienced primary graft failure, of whom one died early, while the remaining two were successfully managed with an autologous CD34 + boost (n = 1), or a second alloHSCT (n = 1). Among the 3 patients with primary graft failure, 2 had mismatched donors, 2 had received BM grafts, and 2 had severe splenomegaly (≥ 20 cm).

Nine patients (37.5%) relapsed after alloHSCT: 4 with BM relapse, 2 with isolated extramedullary (EM) relapse, and 3 with both BM and EM relapse. Despite salvage lines of treatment (including DLI, azacytidine and venetoclax, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells infusions [24], and second alloHSCT), 8 of 9 patients failed to achieve a subsequent remission and died of relapse. Four (16.7%) patients underwent a second transplantation, 2 for hematological relapse of AML, 1 for molecular relapse of MF, and 1 for graft failure. Three (75%) of 4 died, all due to relapse. Of 21 evaluable patients, 13 (61.9%) achieved full donor chimerism by day + 30; at the last chimerism evaluation on day + 365, 12 maintained full donor chimerism. Overall, 9 patients (37.5%) received DLI for mixed chimerism and/or hematological relapse.

The only patient who was bridged to transplant with decitabine and venetoclax is alive and disease-free at last follow-up.

Acute GvHD was documented in 12 (50%) patients, limited to grade I (n = 9) or grade II (n = 3). Chronic GvHD occurred in 9 of 20 evaluable patients (45%), with 3 (15%) classified as moderate and 4 (20%) as severe. The cumulative incidence of acute GvHD grade > I at + 100 days was 8%, while that of moderate to severe chronic GvHD was 25% at 1 year and 30% at 3 years. The total number of deaths was 11, with 8 (72.7%) due to relapse and 3 (27.3%) due to NRM. At the most recent follow-up, 13 patients (54%) were alive.

Outcomes

With a median follow-up after transplant of 2.9 years (range 0.02–15.5 years), the estimated 3-year OS, PFS, CIR, and NRM were 62%, 49%, 38% and 12%, respectively. Patients transplanted in CR/CRi showed a non-significant trend towards improved OS (68% vs. 55%, HR 0.42, 95% CI 0.12–1.44; p = 0.166) and PFS (61% vs. 36%, HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.15–1.53; p = 0.218) compared with those with active disease (Fig. 2A-B).

By univariate analysis, myeloid donor chimerism < 95% at day + 90 was significantly associated with inferior OS (HR 19.6, 95% CI 1.73-222.27; p = 0.016), PFS (HR 25.29, 95% CI 2.24-285.71; p = 0.009), and with an increased CIR (HR 25.29, 95% CI 3.55-180.04; p = 0.001) (Table 2). Regarding the impact of additional mutations on outcome, TP53 mutations were associated with inferior PFS (HR 6.28, 95% CI 1.61–24.55; p = 0.008) and increased CIR (HR 4.95, 95% CI 1.15–21.19; p = 0.031), while EZH2 mutations correlated with decreased PFS (HR 3.8, 95% CI 1.05–13.76; p = 0.042). Patients harboring either TP53 or EZH2 mutations (n = 8) had markedly inferior outcomes, with 3-year OS, PFS, and CIR of 38%, 0%, and 100%, respectively, compared with 79%, 71%, and 21% in patients without these mutations (Fig. 3A-C). Notably, all these 8 patients achieved CR/CRi after one cycle of induction therapy, but 4 patients had relapsed at the time of transplantation.

No significant associations with transplant outcomes were observed for driver mutations, Karnofsky performance status, HTC-CI score, spleen response, donor type, or conditioning intensity and time from blastic evolution to transplant. To better assess the impact of the time from diagnosis to transplant on outcome, we stratified patients by the median time from blastic evolution to transplant (4.5 months). The 3-year OS for patients transplanted < 4.5 months after diagnosis of blastic phase was 67%, compared with 57% for those transplanted > 4.5 months after blastic evolution (p = 0.29). Similarly, the 3-year PFS was 58% and 39% for patients transplanted within < 4.5 months and > 4.5 months, respectively (p = 0.45).

Comparison with patients transplanted for chronic phase MF

Lastly, we compared these 24 patients with BP-MF with a historical cohort of 50 consecutive patients who underwent HSCT for chronic-phase (CP) MF at our institution during the same timeframe. Patient and transplant characteristics of the latter have already been reported [25]. A comparison of baseline characteristics and transplant variables between the two cohorts is reported in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S1). The two populations were largely comparable, except for the intensity of the conditioning regimen, with a higher proportion of patients receiving a MAC regimen in the BP-MF cohort. Pre-transplant mutational status analysis showed that SETBP1 (31.8% vs. 0%, p = 0.0001) and RUNX1 (22.7% vs. 2%, p = 0.009) mutations were significantly more prevalent in BP-MF. In particular, SETBP1 mutations were identified in 7 patients in the BP-MF cohort. Of these, 3 harbored concomitant RUNX1 mutations, and 2 of the 3 also carried EZH2 mutations. In this subgroup (n = 3), no disease relapses occurred, and one patient died from transplant-related mortality. Among the 4 patients with isolated SETBP1 mutations, all experienced disease relapse, and three subsequently died due to relapse. Notably, two of these patients also harbored TP53 mutations, whereas the remaining two carried EZH2 mutations. In contrast, both patients with isolated RUNX1 mutations were alive and relapse-free at last follow-up, and none exhibited additional unfavorable mutations (TP53 or EZH2). Given the low number of patients with these mutations in our cohort, we cannot draw conclusions about the biological and clinical role of this association, and further studies are needed to address this topic.

TP53 mutations (18.2% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.19) were also more frequent in patients with BP-MF, though the difference was not statistically significant. Regarding driver mutations, no differences were observed in either frequency or median VAF between the two groups. Despite the more advanced disease phase, the outcomes of patients transplanted in blast versus chronic phase MF were similar (3-year OS 62% vs. 58%, p = 0.6181; 3-year PFS 49% vs. 50%, p = 0.7702; 3-year CIR 38% vs. 27%, p = 0.31; 3-year NRM 12% vs. 24%, p = 0.3616, respectively) (Fig. 4A-B).

Discussion

In this retrospective, molecularly annotated cohort of patients undergoing alloHSCT for BP-MF, we confirm the potential curative role of alloHSCT with a 3-year OS of 62% and a low NRM of 12%. Although leukemic transformation of MF is historically associated with a poor prognosis [1, 2], our findings strongly support the role of transplantation even in this advanced phase of the disease and are against a nihilistic attitude some physicians can have for these patients. Notably, a trend toward improved survival was observed among patients undergoing transplantation in CR/CRi, compared with those with active disease, consistent with the concept that optimizing disease control before starting the conditioning regimen is associated with long-term disease control after transplantation. Even more importantly, our results confirm the prognostic value of TP53 or EZH2 mutations.

Data on the outcomes of BP-MF primarily come from retrospective studies that often include patients with BP-MPN. In these studies, median survival without transplantation is typically less than 1 year [6–9]while alloHSCT remains the only treatment strategy improving outcomes, with survival rates reaching 30% at 3 years [7, 10–13]. Few studies have evaluated the role of alloHSCT, focusing specifically in patients with BP-MF [15–17]. In the retrospective study by Alchalby and colleagues, which included 46 BP-MF patients, the 3-year OS and PFS were 33% and 26%, respectively [16]. Keyzner et al. performed a subgroup analysis of BP-MF, reporting 2-year OS and PFS of 42% and 42% [17]. Similarly, in the study by Ciurea et al., 2-year OS and PFS were 49% and 26%, respectively [15].

In our cohort, both OS and PFS were comparable if not better than those reported in several previous studies. This result may reflect the careful selection of patients eligible for transplantation in a center with consolidated experience in this setting, as well as advances in supportive care and transplant platforms over time. However, no difference in clinical outcome was observed among patients who received a conditioning regimen that included a double alkylator (TBF), which may enhance antileukemic activity, albeit with increased toxicity [26].

Given the high risk of relapse, a blast reduction therapy is recommended before alloHSCT [27]. However, the optimal bridging therapy before transplantation remains controversial, and in daily clinical practice, it should be selected primarily based on the patient’s performance status. Historically, patients with BP-MPN were treated with AML-like chemotherapy regimens. The Mayo-AGIMM study, a retrospective analysis of 410 patients with BP-MPN, reported a higher CR/CRi rate with AML-like induction chemotherapy than with hypometilating agents (HMA) or other agents (59% vs. 5%). Patients who received alloHSCT (n = 24) had superior 3-year OS (32%) compared with patients in CR/CRi who did not undergo transplantation (19%) and with patients neither transplanted nor in CR/CRi (1%) [7]. More recently, data on less-intensive schemes based on HMA and venetoclax in BP-MPN have increased. One of the most relevant retrospective studies is that of Gangat and colleagues, who recently described 47 patients with BP-MPN (32 treatment-naïve, 15 relapsed/refractory) treated with HMA and venetoclax. In univariate analysis, achievement of CR/CRi at alloHSCT was significantly associated with higher OS [28]. Recently, the GIMEMA group presented the results of the multicenter, phase 2 ENABLE study, which evaluated the combination of decitabine and venetoclax in BP-MPN patients unfit for intensive chemotherapy. At 12 months, EFS was 32.8% and OS 45.7%. After one cycle, the rate of CR and CR + PR were 38% and 60%, respectively. Early response appears to be associated with improved outcomes and successful transition to transplant [29]. In our study, intensive induction chemotherapy was the predominant bridging approach, with remission rates comparable to those reported in previously published studies [7, 12–14]. Notably, of 18 patients in CR/CRi after induction chemotherapy, 5 progressed before transplant conditioning, emphasizing the importance of minimizing time to transplant when complete remission is achieved.

Since only one patient was bridged to transplant with decitabine and venetoclax, we cannot draw any conclusions about which pre-transplant treatment is preferable.

Regarding the impact of pre-transplant response, we observed a trend toward improved survival in patients who underwent alloHSCT in remission. The difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the small sample size. Consistent with our observation, Alchalby et al. reported that pre-transplant disease remission (CR vs. no CR) was the only significant factor for post-transplant survival (3-year OS 69% vs. 22%, respectively, p = 0.008) [16]. The importance of remission status at transplant has also recently been highlighted in a retrospective EBMT study of 663 patients with BP-MPN, in which active disease at transplant predicted poorer outcomes (inferior OS and PFS, and higher CIR and NRM) [13].

Full donor chimerism at day + 30 was shown to predict long-term disease control in MF [30]. In our experience, mixed donor chimerism was associated with a higher risk of relapse or death. These results underscore the importance of strict monitoring of donor chimerism after transplant and consideration of preemptive interventions.

Additionally, TP53 and EZH2 mutations were associated with extremely poor prognosis. Specifically, TP53-mutated patients had the worst outcome, with no one alive and relapse-free at 3 years. This aligns with the findings published by Gagelmann and colleagues, which showed that TP53 mutations, particularly in a multi-hit configuration, are associated with worse outcomes [31]. Due to the small sample size, we could not assess the impact of multi- vs. single-hit TP53 mutations. We also observed that EZH2 mutations were clinically significant and, along with TP53, delineated a subgroup with near-universal relapse at 3 years. Notably, all patients with TP53 or EZH2 mutations in our cohort had a very poor prognosis, even if they all achieved CR/CRi status after one cycle of chemotherapy, underscoring the need for new therapeutic strategies for this particularly demanding group.

Finally, we compared the study population with a contemporaneous cohort of 50 consecutive patients transplanted for chronic-phase MF at our center during the same timeframe. Molecular comparisons between the two cohorts showed that SETBP1 and RUNX1 mutations were significantly more common in blast phase-MF, with SETBP1 mutations found exclusively in BP-MF. A recent study of 36 triple-negative PMF patients linked SETBP1 mutations to more aggressive disease [32]. These data suggest that these mutations may contribute to leukemic transformation, but further studies are needed to confirm this. Most importantly, we found that post-transplant outcomes were similar between blast- and chronic-phase patients, consistent with Keyzner et al.’s study [17]. This finding reinforces the idea that, in appropriately selected candidates who can undergo alloHSCT, blast-phase transformation does not necessarily preclude excellent outcomes.

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged, including its retrospective design and small sample size, which may reduce statistical power, especially in subgroup analyses. Additionally, because the study focused on transplant-eligible patients, we did not analyze BP-MF patients who were treated with chemotherapy or less intensive regimens and did not proceed to transplant. Larger, multicenter, prospective studies are needed to confirm our findings. Subgroup analyses and comparative studies of CP-MF cohorts may provide deeper insights into the mutational landscape driving progression from MF to acute myeloid leukemia.

The blast phase of myelofibrosis was once believed to have such a poor prognosis that curative treatment was not even considered an option. However, newer data, including those presented here, demonstrate that alloHSCT is a viable and potentially curative option for BP-MF, with outcomes comparable to those of CP-MF transplantation. Achieving remission before transplantation is advantageous and should be actively pursued. Mutations in TP53 or EZH2 identify patients at very high risk of relapse and underscore the need for new strategies to prevent post-transplant relapse.
