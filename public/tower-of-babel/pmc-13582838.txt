Non-coding regulatory variants in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis risk and pathogenesis
Authors: Darius Ramkhalawan, Paola Parrales, Nadja Makki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is a common pediatric musculoskeletal disease that has significant impacts on childhood quality of life. Recent genome-wide association studies have identified dozens of genetic risk loci and hundreds of risk variants that primarily reside in non-coding regions of the genome. Follow up studies suggest a complex genetic architecture in which several tissues may be affected, however few causal disease variants have been identified. Here we review the functional AIS-associated variants that have been identified, how they may contribute to disease etiology, and necessary advancements that will link the effects of non-coding variants to disease mechanisms.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common pediatric musculoskeletal disorder worldwide and is characterized by a lateral curvature of the spine that develops during the adolescent growth spurt in otherwise healthy children1 (Fig. 1A). AIS affects approximately 2-3% of children worldwide, with females facing a substantially higher risk of curve progression2. As the child grows, the curvature can progress and lead to chronic pain, physical deformity, impaired cardiopulmonary function, and psychological stress. Current treatment options for AIS remain limited to bracing for mild or moderate curves (Cobb angle <40°) and surgical correction of the curve in severe cases (Cobb angle >40°)5. Bracing is most effective if diagnosis occurs prior to skeletal maturity, but it is not corrective and requires prolonged adherence, resulting in a substantial fraction of patients having to undergo surgery6,7. The only corrective treatment for AIS is spinal fusion surgery, where metal rods are inserted into the vertebrae to derotate the spine. The physical tethering of vertebrae during spinal fusion results in mobility loss, and the invasive nature of the surgery carries the risk of serious post-surgical complications, such as neurological injury8. Early detection and intervention are essential to reduce the risk of curve progression, but it is currently not possible to identify patients at risk of developing AIS before the onset of symptoms. There is conflicting evidence to support that preventative screening of children for AIS improves outcomes9, therefore a reliable prognostic test could significantly improve clinical decision-making.

A genetic component to AIS has long been established through twin and family studies10–14; however, the unclear inheritance pattern suggests a multifactorial etiology with a complex genetic architecture. Over the past decade, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in diverse populations have identified more than two dozen genetic loci that are associated with AIS risk15–28. These large, population-scale studies are a powerful tool to identify possible biomarkers and disease mechanisms.

The lead variants identified by GWAS, however, are not necessarily the causal variants. GWAS variants are identified by genotyping case and control populations for common variants using genotyping arrays, and then variants are associated with the disease phenotype through statistical analysis29. GWAS variants are therefore only highly correlated with true causal variants unless further validation studies are performed. Thus, when identifying causal variants, those in the same linkage disequilibrium block as the lead variant must also be considered. At many AIS loci, the association interval contains tens to hundreds of linked variants with uncertain consequences. Furthermore, most of these variants lie in noncoding regions of the genome30. Given that GWAS variants largely do not affect protein structure, their contribution to the disease is likely due to their perturbation of gene regulatory elements. GWAS variants tend to be enriched in areas that coincide with markers of enhancers30, which are short cis-regulatory elements that regulate gene expression spatially and temporally.

Tightly controlled enhancer activity is essential in developmental processes and is implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases31,32. Enhancer location and orientation relative to the gene promoter can be variable, with some enhancers acting over large genomic distances, and in some cases, a single enhancer can co-regulate multiple genes33–37. It is predicted that there are hundreds of thousands of enhancers in the genome that are precisely regulated by transcription factor binding at specific binding sites, which can be significantly affected even by single-nucleotide changes38–40. The complexities of enhancer biology make it difficult to decipher how they, and the GWAS variants they harbor, contribute to disease phenotypes. In AIS, this complexity is compounded by the fact that the disease likely reflects contributions from several relevant tissues, including cartilage, intervertebral disc, bone, muscle, and possibly neural lineages involved in postural control (Fig. 1B).

Loci with experimentally supported noncoding regulatory mechanisms

GWAS and meta-analyses have now identified at least 26 lead AIS susceptibility loci15–27,41–45. Despite this growing list, only a small subset of AIS loci have been interrogated experimentally at the level of individual noncoding elements or candidate causal variants. Nonetheless, these studies provide experimental evidence that noncoding regulatory variation contributes to AIS susceptibility. At the same time, the small number of functionally explored loci highlights how incomplete the present map still is. Most AIS-associated regions remain mechanistically unresolved, and even at the best-studied loci, variant-to-element-to-gene relationships are only partially defined.

The sections below summarize the loci for which functional data are currently available and highlight both what has been learned and what remains unclear.

PAX1: sex-biased gene regulation affects extracellular matrix pathways

The Paired Box 1 (PAX1) locus provides the clearest example of a noncoding AIS association being followed into in vivo enhancer biology. PAX1 encodes a transcription factor required for sclerotome specification and vertebral column development46. A female-specific AIS susceptibility locus located on chromosome 20p11.22, downstream of PAX1, was discovered based on GWAS findings, establishing one of the first strong indications that sex-biased disease risk in AIS could be encoded, at least in part, at the level of distal regulatory sequence42,44,47.

Initial functional work at this locus focused on enhancer discovery. In zebrafish assays, several candidate elements were tested, but only two, Xe1 and PEC7, reproducibly drove reporter expression in developing somatic or spinal tissues. PEC7 is particularly notable because it harbors rs169311, a variant in strong linkage disequilibrium with the lead SNP rs6137473. Introduction of the AIS-associated allele into PEC7 abolished enhancer activity in zebrafish, providing direct evidence that sequence-level variation at this locus can disrupt enhancer output42. This was an early and influential example of a common AIS-associated variant altering the function of a developmental enhancer rather than a coding sequence.

More recently, Ushiki and colleagues extended this work in mouse models and moved the field beyond reporter assays alone47. They first showed in mouse transgenic enhancer assays that PEC7 is active in the neonatal tail tip and adult intervertebral disc. They then generated CRISPR knockout mice for PEC7, Xe1, and the combined Xe1 + PEC7 interval. Deletion of Xe1 alone or Xe1 together with PEC7 produced a kinky tail phenotype, and the combined deletion showed a female-biased penetrance that was attenuated by tamoxifen treatment, implicating estrogen-responsive pathways in the sex dimorphism of the phenotype. This is particularly compelling considering the role of estrogen signaling in muscle progenitor cells contributing to the progression of the scoliotic curve in mice48. RNA-seq from Xe1 + PEC7 mutant tails further pointed to altered extracellular matrix programs, including collagens and aggrecan-related genes. This series of experiments makes the PAX1 locus the strongest current example of an AIS-associated regulatory region progressing from association to enhancer characterization to in vivo perturbation.

Despite these advances, key mechanistic questions remain. The precise contribution of the human risk haplotype to endogenous PAX1 expression remains incompletely resolved, and the phenotypic readouts observed in model organisms are still surrogates for the human disease. Interestingly, the Xe1 and PEC7 enhancers are in a “gene desert,” ~200 kb away from the nearest gene (PAX1). Enhancer–promoter interactions can occur over long distances and, in both humans and mice, the genes encoding the transcription factors NK2 Homeobox 4 (NKX2-4), NK2 Homeobox 2 (NKX2-2), and Forkhead Box A2 (FOXA2) are located within 1 Mb of the enhancers and could potentially also be regulated, though this does not preclude the possibility of trans interactions. Nonetheless, the PAX1 locus demonstrates that a distal, noncoding AIS association can alter enhancer function in vivo, interact with sex-biased biology, and converge on matrix-related developmental pathways.

ADGRG6/GPR126: a regulator of chondrocyte development maintains proper spinal alignment

Adhesion G Protein-Coupled Receptor G6 (ADGRG6, also known as GPR126) is among the most consistently replicated AIS susceptibility loci and has strong biological support from animal models. The original GWAS signal centered on rs6570507, and follow-up studies in Japanese, Chinese, and multi-ethnic cohorts reinforced the locus as a reproducible contributor to AIS susceptibility20,23,45. Fine-mapping and replication also identified additional linked variants, including rs7774095 and rs7755109, strengthening the view that the associated interval contains one or more regulatory alleles rather than a single obvious coding change23,45.

The biological relevance of this locus lies in the fact that its gene-level biology is highly relevant to spine development, as demonstrated in animal models. In mice, cartilage-specific loss of Adgrg6 in osteochondral progenitor and committed chondrocyte lineages leads to a postnatal scoliosis-like spinal curvature with defects in the annulus fibrosis of the intervertebral disc and defects in the rib cage cartilage modeling pectus excavatum49,50, while knockdown in zebrafish causes delayed spinal ossification and impaired axial development23. These animal studies support the idea that altered ADGRG6 dosage or signaling in cartilage and related connective tissues could influence AIS pathogenesis.

The specific noncoding elements at the human ADGRG6 locus that might mediate this effect have remained unclear. Integration of transcriptomic and H3K27ac datasets from AIS-relevant tissues with AIS-associated haplotypes identified candidate regulatory elements at this locus51,52. Of six sequences tested in human chondrocytes, two showed enhancer activity in luciferase assays: one in intron 4 (ADGRG6_4) and one in the 3′UTR (ADGRG6_6)51. Chromatin interaction data also suggested that at least one of these elements may contact the ADGRG6 promoter. A follow-up study identified a 200 bp sequence encompassing the SNP rs1040525, which is located in intron 5 of the ADGRG6 gene and is predicted to target the ADGRG6 promoter, where the AIS risk allele resulted in greater enhancer activity in human chondrocytes by luciferase assays52.

A recent study extends the mechanistic framework at this locus by linking Adgrg6 to another established AIS gene, SRY-Box Transcription Factor 9 (Sox9)41,53,54. Using spatial transcriptomic profiling of the cartilage-specific Adgrg6 conditional mouse model, Aceves and colleagues found reduced Sox9 expression together with broader downregulation of extracellular matrix programs in the intervertebral disc55. They further showed that Sox9 occupies accessible chromatin within the Adgrg6 locus in Sox9-positive skeletal cells, including a conserved intronic region, consistent with a direct regulatory relationship. Most notably, reduction of Sox9 dosage increased both the penetrance and severity of scoliosis in the Adgrg6 mutant background, supporting a feedforward circuit in which Adgrg6 and Sox9 help maintain postnatal spinal homeostasis. This work strengthens the case that variation at the ADGRG6 locus may act within a broader regulatory network rather than through an isolated enhancer-gene effect.

The ADGRG6 locus therefore illustrates both the progress and the remaining gaps in the field. We now have strong evidence that ADGRG6 is functionally relevant to spine development and that the human locus contains active regulatory elements in chondrocytes. What is still missing is a complete accounting of which linked variants are functional, how they alter enhancer activity in endogenous chromatin, and whether they converge specifically on ADGRG6 or on a wider local regulatory network.

BNC2: enhancer gain-of-function points to increased gene expression

BNC2 encodes Basonuclin Zinc Finger Protein 2, a transcription factor implicated in embryonic development and mesenchymal lineage biology56, making it a plausible effector gene in skeletal patterning. The BNC2 locus was one of the earliest AIS regions in which a common noncoding variant was directly linked to allele-dependent enhancer activity. In an extended GWAS with Japanese discovery and Japanese and Chinese replication cohorts, the strongest associated SNPs mapped to intron 3 of BNC2, and eQTL analyses suggested that the susceptibility haplotype increased BNC2 expression, raising the possibility that increased, rather than reduced, regulatory output is pathogenic at this locus25.

Functional follow-up identified rs10738445 as a likely causal regulatory variant. The susceptibility allele showed stronger YY1 Transcription Factor 1 (YY1) binding and higher enhancer activity than the non-susceptibility allele, as demonstrated by luciferase reporter assays carried out in HeLa cells, providing a clear example in which risk is associated with increased transcription factor occupancy and enhancer output. This distinguishes BNC2 from loci in which the risk allele appears to weaken enhancer activity and underscores that AIS-associated regulatory variation can act through either gain or loss of cis-regulatory function depending on the locus and developmental context.

The in vivo relevance of the locus was further supported by zebrafish experiments showing that BNC2 overexpression induces body curvature in a dose-dependent manner, linking altered gene dosage to axial phenotypes. Later work identified an active chondrocyte enhancer in intron 3 of BNC2 (BNC2_2) that overlaps AIS-associated SNPs and is predicted to contact the BNC2 promoter51. Together, these findings support BNC2 as an important AIS locus in which noncoding variation has been linked to allele-specific enhancer activity, while gene overexpression studies point to dosage sensitivity as a plausible pathogenic mechanism. Even so, functional dissection of the locus remains incomplete. The current literature has prioritized two candidate regulatory features at the BNC2 locus, but the broader associated interval has not been systematically interrogated, leaving open the possibility that additional linked functional alleles remain unresolved.

UNCX: allele-dependent, transcription factor-mediated regulation of a developmental gene

The UNC Homeobox (UNCX) locus adds an important conceptual dimension to AIS genetics by pointing toward developmental patterning and transcription factor-mediated enhancer activation outside the more familiar cartilage-centered framework. Yonezawa and colleagues interrogated the AIS-associated 7p22.3 locus using a combination of in silico prioritization, reporter assays, and in vivo experiments. Their study identified rs78148157 as a functional variant located in an enhancer of UNCX and showed that the risk allele increased enhancer-driven expression57.

Mechanistically, this locus is notable because the enhancer appears to be regulated by Early Growth Response 1 (EGR1). The rs78148157-containing enhancer was transactivated by EGR1, and the risk allele showed stronger EGR1 binding, providing a plausible explanation for the increased transcriptional output. This is one of the clearest examples in AIS of a specific transcription factor being linked to the allelic behavior of a candidate enhancer.

The in vivo arm of the study further supported pathogenic relevance: overexpression of UNCX mRNA in zebrafish larvae caused body curvature and abnormal branching of caudal primary motor neurons, implicating defective neurogenesis. The severity of the body curvature increased in a dose-dependent manner when the larvae were injected with increasingly higher doses of UNCX mRNA. The authors note that, in humans, EGR1 is upregulated in response to estrogen and suggest that increased estrogen levels during female puberty would increase UNCX expression via increased EGR1 expression and stronger EGR1 binding to the risk allele of rs78148157. These findings are important because they widen the biological scope of AIS beyond extracellular matrix and cartilage homeostasis. The UNCX locus suggests that altered enhancer activity may also impinge on neural development, somitic patterning, or other axial programs that contribute to spine alignment.

LBX1: dysregulated expression implicates neural and muscle pathways in AIS

Ladybird Homeobox (LBX1) is among the most consistently replicated AIS susceptibility loci and encodes a homeobox transcription factor with roles in muscle precursor migration and dorsal spinal cord interneuron specification58–60. Functional studies of the original rs11190870-associated region showed physical interaction with the LBX1/FLJ41350 (LBX1-AS1) promoter region and greater reporter activity of the AIS risk allele in HEK293T cells61. In zebrafish, overexpression of human LBX1 or zebrafish lbx genes, but not FLJ41350, induced body-axis curvature, supporting LBX1 as the more likely effector gene at the locus61. Surprisingly, conditional knockout of Lbx1 in sensory neurons also resulted in a scoliosis-like phenotype in mice62. These findings are interesting in that they suggest LBX1 expression may be tied to downstream morphogenic factors, and its careful regulation is essential for normal vertebral axis development. A later fine-mapping study identified a second candidate regulatory variant, rs1322330, in the LBX1 promoter; the risk allele was associated with lower LBX1 expression in paraspinal muscle, reduced promoter activity, and weaker protein binding, while LBX1 knockdown impaired myotube formation63. Taken together, these findings support a regulatory role for the LBX1 locus in AIS and suggest that multiple regulatory variants and cellular contexts may contribute to its effects on disease risk.

MIR4300HG: a noncoding locus associated with scoliotic curve progression

The MIR4300 host gene, MIR4300HG, remains the best-studied example of a noncoding regulatory locus associated with AIS progression rather than disease occurrence. In a GWAS comparing patients with and without curve progression, Ogura and colleagues identified a significant locus on chromosome 11q14.124. Fine-mapping followed by in silico and in vitro analyses identified rs35333564 in MIR4300HG as the functional variant most likely to explain the signal. This distinction between susceptibility and progression is especially important in AIS, where the clinical problem is often not only who develops a curve, but which curves will worsen enough to require intervention.

The mechanistic data at this locus come primarily from electrophoretic mobility shift assays and luciferase reporter assays. Among several linked candidates, only rs35333564 showed allele-dependent protein binding and differential enhancer activity, with the risk allele driving lower reporter output than the non-risk allele in both HeLa and A172 cells. The topologically associating domain (TAD) contained only MIR4300HG, strengthening the case that the enhancer acts on this transcript or its embedded microRNA rather than on a distant neighboring gene. Intriguingly, MIR4300HG and MIR4300 were reported to be expressed in the spinal cord, bone, vertebral disc, and cartilage24.

MIR4300HG also illustrates the limits of current approaches. The functional assays at this locus were performed in HeLa cells, rather than in an AIS-relevant musculoskeletal cell type, and the downstream effector pathway remains relatively indirect because the locus acts through a noncoding host transcript and its embedded microRNA, rather than through a well-characterized structural or signaling gene. The biological interpretation is therefore less resolved than for ADGRG6, BNC2, or PAX1. Still, MIR4300HG is an important reminder that regulatory variation in AIS may influence not only disease initiation, but also curve progression, and that the relevant outputs may include post-transcriptional regulatory networks.

Methodological bottleneck

The loci discussed above establish an important proof of principle: common AIS-associated noncoding variants can alter enhancer activity, transcription factor binding, and downstream gene expression (Table 1). They also reveal a shared limitation in how the field has progressed. Most functional studies have relied on locus-by-locus interrogation of a small number of candidate elements selected on the basis of linkage disequilibrium, chromatin features, motif predictions, or local gene biology. This strategy has been highly informative, but it leaves much of the associated noncoding landscape unexplored.

Biochemical approaches used to nominate enhancers, such as H3K27ac ChIP-seq, have provided an important catalog of regulatory elements across AIS-relevant tissues and cell types51. However, such methods are correlative and therefore require functional validation. Individual reporter assays, including luciferase, lacZ, and in vivo transgenic enhancer assays, can define quantitative, spatial, or temporal regulatory activity, but they also have important limitations. They require prior selection of candidate sequences, often depend on external normalization, and are inherently difficult to scale. These constraints become especially limiting in AIS, where each associated region can contain tens to hundreds of linked noncoding variants and where the relevant biology may differ across cartilage, intervertebral disc, bone, muscle, and neural lineages. High-throughput sequencing-based approaches now offer a way to address this bottleneck more directly. Massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) introduce hundreds to thousands of candidate regulatory sequences into a cell, whereby active enhancers drive transcription of an RNA barcode that is normalized to its corresponding DNA barcodes, providing a direct, quantitative readout of enhancer activity64,65. By designing MPRA libraries that contain both risk and non-risk variants, MPRAs make it possible to test the differential regulatory effects of GWAS variants systematically across many loci in disease-relevant cell types. CRISPR-based screening methods, such as PERTURB-seq66, can extend this framework by interrogating the downstream transcriptional consequences of enhancer perturbation, although these approaches often require single-cell readouts and remain comparatively costly. Together, these methods offer a path beyond single-locus proof-of-principle studies toward scalable identification of causal regulatory variants and the gene regulatory networks they disrupt in AIS.

Conclusions and perspectives

Collectively, the loci studied to date support a coherent, if still incomplete, model of how noncoding variants contribute to AIS. Common associated alleles can alter enhancer activity, change transcription factor binding, and shift the expression of genes involved in sclerotome specification, extracellular matrix biology, cartilage homeostasis, and axial patterning. The direction of effect is not uniform: at some loci the risk allele weakens enhancer output, whereas at others it strengthens enhancer activity or increases transcription factor occupancy. Likewise, the relevant target genes span transcription factors, signaling receptors, and noncoding transcripts. This heterogeneity argues against a single unifying molecular mechanism for AIS, but it strongly supports the broader framework that dysregulated gene expression is a central part of disease biology.

What the field still lacks is scale. The current literature is built on a small number of carefully executed, low-throughput studies at a handful of loci. Most AIS GWAS regions have never been subjected to systematic variant-level testing, and even at well-studied loci, only a fraction of linked variants have been functionally assayed. In addition, many studies rely on proxy systems, such as heterologous reporter cell lines, developmental tissues that are suggestive rather than definitive, or animal phenotypes that model only part of the human condition. The relevant cell type is also likely to vary by locus. Chondrocytes may be the most tractable and best-supported system for some genes, but other loci may act in muscle, bone, or neural populations involved in proprioception and postural control. A major unresolved problem is therefore not just identifying functional variants but identifying the correct biological context in which to study them. Several priorities follow from this: generating high-resolution epigenomic maps in healthy and diseased tissues, applying high-throughput functional screens, and integrating allele-specific binding and perturbation assays in relevant cell types (Box 1). Together, these approaches will be necessary to connect causal variants to their target genes and the gene regulatory networks they disrupt in AIS.

In that sense, the field is now at an inflection point. The first generation of AIS post-GWAS studies has established that regulatory variants can be functional, biologically meaningful, and experimentally tractable. The next step is to determine, in a systematic way, which of the many linked noncoding variants across AIS loci truly perturb cis-regulatory logic and how those perturbations are wired into disease-relevant cell states. Answering that question will be essential if AIS genetics is to move from association toward mechanism, biomarkers, and ultimately intervention.
