Correspondence on “The Environmental Microbiology Minimum Information (EMMI) Guidelines: qPCR and dPCR Quality and Reporting for Environmental Microbiology”
Authors: Katherine Crank, Katerina Papp, Casey Barber, Phillip Wang, Aaron Bivins, Daniel Gerrity
Published: 2023

The recently proposed Environmental Microbiology Minimum Information (EMMI) Guidelines published by Borchardt et al.1 come at a consequential time in the field of environmental microbiology. The significance of these guidelines has been magnified by the unprecedented use of environmental microbiology in informing public health decisions during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly through wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) initiatives. These efforts have generated a wealth of invaluable data from across the globe. The challenge of comparing these data, which have been collected through various methods, in different locations, and by different laboratories, underscores the critical importance of establishing data standards. The purpose of this Correspondence is to provide further clarity and support regarding the distinction between concentration factors (CFs) and equivalent sample volumes (ESVs) as described in Borchardt et al.1

ESV, in the context of molecular methods such as quantitative PCR (qPCR) or digital PCR (dPCR), represents the volume of original sampled medium that undergoes analysis in each PCR reaction, accounting for all processing steps involved. These steps include concentration and/or filtration, nucleic acid extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis (when applicable), and the quantification step itself. The instrumental results from molecular analyses are typically reported in units of gene copies (gc) per reaction or gene copies per volume of reaction (e.g., gc/μL). While these results hold internal validity within the experiment and may be interpretable by microbiologists and laboratory scientists, they lack context for end users of the data, such as utilities, public health officials, and other stakeholders. Hence, it becomes imperative to translate these instrument-specific outcomes into externally valid units that appropriately reflect the original sample.

In environmental applications, ESV can be applied to various sample types, including liquids (e.g., gc/L), solids or pellets (e.g., gc/g), air (e.g., gc/m3), or surfaces (e.g., gc/cm2). Theoretically, ESV can even be used in a wastewater context to extrapolate solids-based concentrations to the original volume of influent wastewater from which the solids were settled. This would aid in assessing partitioning of targets and estimating total influent wastewater loading, accounting for both liquid and solid fractions. The approach for calculating ESV will vary in each of these applications and based on the methods employed by a given study, but the key is to ensure transparency in communicating this nontrivial parameter so that data can be verified and interpreted accurately by secondary users. For digital platforms, achieving an accurate representation of ESV requires consideration of any dead volume that is not reflected in the instrumental result. In other words, the calculated ESV should account only for the volume of DNA template included in valid partitions (dPCR) or droplets (ddPCR).

The authors of the EMMI Guidelines have appropriately emphasized the significance of ESV, particularly in the context of striving for harmonization in environmental microbiology: “A key example is the concept of the equivalent sample volume or mass analyzed; that is, after processing steps the fraction of the original sample amount collected that is ultimately added to the PCR reaction and analyzed. When this parameter is not reported it is very difficult to compare results across laboratories and achieve harmonization.”1 The EMMI Guidelines similarly emphasize CFs, under the “Sample Reduction” and “Nucleic Acid Extraction” reporting elements. The CF should represent the degree of concentration achieved throughout sample processing and can also be used to convert instrument-specific concentrations (e.g., gc/μL) to sample concentrations (e.g., gc/L). However, CF is not synonymous with ESV, and reported CFs may sometimes focus solely on sample concentration steps while inadvertently omitting important unit conversions and volume fractions associated with downstream sample processing. CFs may also lack the context necessary to demonstrate the representativeness of a given sample. Consider a sample processed with small-volume direct extraction of nucleic acid. This approach could achieve a 10-fold or greater CF, but the ESV could still be on the order of several μL. The small ESV would more readily highlight method sensitivity concerns and the potential for pipetting bias, which would not be apparent if CF were the sole metric reported.

Therefore, if one chooses between the two parameters, ESVs are more comprehensive and offer greater practical value than CFs. Specifically, ESVs achieve the same objective of converting instrument-specific results into sample concentrations but are inherently more intuitive to use and communicate. ESVs also offer a more robust interpretation of the methods used, as they unambiguously reflect the amount of sample subjected to analysis. By reporting ESVs, researchers incorporate many of the elements suggested by the EMMI Guidelines and essential details recommended by the MIQE guidelines, as well.2 Therefore, implementing ESVs into a data analysis workflow requires minimal additional effort and aligns seamlessly with best practices in the field of environmental microbiology. In conclusion, we appreciate the inclusion of ESV within the EMMI Guidelines, and we strongly encourage researchers to adopt, define, and report ESVs rather than, or as a complement to, CFs. This recommendation not only aligns with the priorities outlined in the EMMI Guidelines but also enhances the clarity, comparability, and robustness of data generated in environmental microbiology studies.
