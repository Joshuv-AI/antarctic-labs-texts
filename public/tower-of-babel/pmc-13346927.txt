Compressive strength of mortar incorporating recycled nonmetallic printed circuit board waste: experimental and interpretable machine learning analysis
Authors: Asif Shahzad, Sardar Kashif-ur-rehman, Muhammad Faisal Javed, Kashif Mehmood, Hisham Alabduljabbar, Furqan Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen disposed of in landfills, printed circuit boards (PCBs) release hazardous substances. Furthermore, the global sand crisis has gained considerable attention among environmentalists over the last few years, and the United Nations has proposed some initiatives to reduce the use of river sand. Despite the existence of several promising sustainable alternatives to alluvial sand, there has been little effort to implement those initiatives in the construction industry. This study explores the use of recycled non-metallic PCB (NM-PCB) as a partial replacement for fine aggregate in mortar, combined with silica fume and marble powder. No data on their combined effects on mortar properties has been discovered yet which restricted large-scale application of NM-PCB in the construction industry. Therefore, this study investigates the potential of machine learning (ML) to predict the compressive strength (CS) of mortar containing NM-PCB, silica fume, and marble powder, as CS is the most critical property of concrete. A comprehensive experimental dataset of 270 samples was developed with eight (8) input variables, with compressive strength as the output. Due to the significant pozzolanic activity of silica fume and the micro filler effect of marble powder, their optimal dosage in mortar was determined using machine learning. The highest Compressive Strength (CS) achieved was 17.6 MPa in a mix containing 5% SF, 5% MP and 3% NM-PCB. Linear Regression, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting models were applied to predict compressive strength, with RF and XGB optimized via grid search and validated using k-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The Random Forest model was the most accurate, achieving an R² of 0.96, while XGBoost also performed well with R² = 0.90. SHAP analysis showed that silica fume (7–12 kg/m³) and NM-PCB (13–22 kg/m³) enhance compressive strength when combined with over 360 kg/m³ of cement. ICE and PDP analyses highlighted curing age as the most influential factor, with silica fume, water–cement ratio, and superplasticizer dosage also significantly affecting strength. A graphical user interface was developed as a decision-support tool for researchers and preliminary mix design optimization for practitioners and externally validated with experimental results demonstrating that recycling NM-PCB in concrete promotes sustainable construction.Keywords: Eco-efficient cement-based materials, Sustainable mortar, E-waste, NM-PCB powder, Compressive strength, Machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The rapid expansion of global electronic consumption has led to an unprecedented rise in electronic waste (E-waste), making it one of the fastest-growing solid waste with annual rate of 20–25% each year because new products are coming in the market and old are replaced quickly and many electronic devices become outdated in a short time1.Around 50 million tons (MT) of E-waste were made in 2018, Half of it originated from personal devices including smartphones, tablets, monitors, computers, and chargers, and the other half came from big appliances, Despite the hazardous nature of this waste, only about 20% is recycled globally each year, leaving nearly 40 MT to be burned, dumped, or improperly managed, contributing toxic elements such as lead, cadmium, and mercury into the environment2. An estimate says that the average manufacturing rate of PCBs has gone up by 8.7% every year over the world, and by 14.5% and 11% in China and Southeast Asia, respectively3. The amount of electronic garbage stored in dump yards has grown quickly, around five times faster than the pace of population growth4,5. Also, PCB trash is a material that does not break down in the environment and might stay there for a long period. Still, using this material in the building industry might reduce the need for natural resources and the damage to the environment that comes from getting rid of it, which would promote sustainable practices6. NM-PCB also contain appreciable amounts of silica and glass fibers, making them structurally rigid. The accumulation of PCB waste therefore represents both an environmental liability and a potential secondary resource. The construction sector, particularly cement-based materials, offers a promising pathway for valorization due to its high material consumption capacity and the ability of cementitious matrices to encapsulate and immobilize waste particles. Consequently, investigating the feasibility of incorporating NM-PCB into mortar systems aligns sustainable construction practices. Recycling offers many benefits, such as cutting down on pollution, waste, and the use of natural resources7.

Recent research suggests the feasibility of using electronic trash into concrete and mortar as a sustainable substitute for natural aggregates. PCBs are different from other types of E-waste, including plastics or glass, because they may make concrete stronger. Because PCB is a composite material, it has sufficient amount of silica in it, which makes PCB as good candidate to be used in concrete8. Research conducted by9 examined the partial replacement of Printed Circuit Board Waste as a substitute for sand at volumes of 10%, 20%, and 30% over periods of 7 and 28 days. The findings indicated that PCB replacement resulted in a decrease in concrete compressive strength at both 7 and 28 days for the 10% and 30% substitutions when compared to the control mix due to NM-PCB irregular size and shape which increase voids and create stress concentration points where the cracks can initiate more easily. Some researchers made geopolymer concrete with crushed NM-PCB, which is a type of nonmetallic printed circuit board (NM-PCB). Even when fly ash and GGBS were added, it lost of its compressive strength when the replacement level was more than 30%, this decrease may be due to insufficient alkali medium for its activation and the strength gets decrease, tensile strength, and flexural strength were improved up to 30% and 40% of the fine aggregates were replaced with NM-PCB waste10. In another study, a decrease in compressive, tensile, and flexural strength was observed when the substitution exceeded 20%,The strength degradation was accredited to the mismatch in particle size distribution between manufactured sand and NM-PCB sand, which resulted in lower strength and a less compact microstructure11,12., utilized crushed NM-PCB as a fine aggregate substitute, replacing 3%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% by weight in concrete. They tested both the fresh (i.e., slump) and hardened qualities of concrete (i.e., compressive, flexural, and tensile splitting strength). The results revealed that as the percentage of PCB went up, the concrete slump went down due to bonding nature of the cement - NM-PCB particles. However, the concrete’s compressive strength went down as the percentage of PCB went up, except for the 15% replacement, which exhibited better performance than the control mixes the decrease is due to addition of higher percentage of NM-PCB decreases the self-weight of concrete and thus reduces the compressive strength of concrete. To minimize the strength reduction associated with NM-PCB incorporation, silica fume and marble powder will be introduced as supplementary materials to increase particle packing density and enhance the interfacial transition zone. Their pozzolanic and filler effects are expected to reduce voids, densify the microstructure, and thereby restore or improve the mechanical performance of the modified concrete.

In addition to NM-PCB waste, Marble powder, a by-product of cutting and polishing poses disposal concerns due to its high alkalinity and fine particle size, which can contaminate soil and waterways. However, when finely ground, marble powder primarily acts through filler and nucleation effects, with limited secondary reactions in alkaline environments. At present, numerous scholar13–15 have undertaken extensive experimental investigations on the utilization of waste marble powder and waste Glass as a partial substitute for cement in the production of mortar and concrete. Ashish et al.14 made cement net mortar and cement mortar by replacing some of the cement and sand with waste marble powder. They then examined the mechanical properties of the new materials. The findings indicated that the optimal mechanical characteristics of the composite cementitious material were achieved. When metakaolin replaced 10% of the cement and waste powder replaced the sand, it met the requirements for sustainable development.

Similarly, silica fume a composite material and by-product of silicon production, is characterized by its extremely small particle size and a surface area of approximately 22,000 m²/kg. Because it is not crystalline in nature, it is especially good for pozzolanic reactions16,17. Silica fume is made up mostly of SiO2 (about 90%), although it also has small amounts of Fe2O3, CaO, MgO, Na2O, and K2O16,17. It interacts quickly and in large amounts with pozzolanic materials18.The inclusion of silica fumes improved the performance of concrete while simultaneously reducing their costs.

Recent studies indicate that most of the NM-PCB research is concentrated on experimental investigations, with minimal exploration in the domain of effective and accurate computational scientific modeling. Also, laboratory testing is costly, time-consuming, and inadequate for capturing the material’s heterogeneity and sensitivity to many factors. Hence an AI-driven computational framework is vital for efficient, accurate and sustainable strength prediction. In another study research examines the mechanical properties of waste-printed circuit board-reinforced concrete incorporating silica fume, alongside predictive modeling utilizing artificial neural networks (ANN). The ANN model’s predicted values yielded R2 values between 0.94 and 0.99, demonstrating the model’s effectiveness19,20. Use Linear Regression (LR) and Random Forest (RF) machine learning techniques with high coefficient correlation (R2) values to guess how NM-PCB coarse aggregate concrete will behave mechanically. The Linear Regression (LR) model was better at predicting mechanical property outcomes than the Random Forest (RF) model. Moreover, many studies are conducted regarding models, particularly XGBoost, Random Forest, and Linear Regression, forecast the mechanical properties of sustainable concrete incorporating various industrial, agricultural, and construction wastes21–29. study employs five interpretable machine learning (ML) techniques: M5P model tree, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Random Forest (RF), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Also studies by30–32 aims were to predict the compressive strength of recycled aggregate self-compacting concrete (RA-SCC). These studies consistently demonstrate that tree-based ensemble algorithms surpass simpler regressors, with XGBoost achieving the highest accuracy. LR, RF, and XGBoost were selected in this study to represent linear, bagging, and boosting approaches, respectively. For the moderate-sized dataset, three-based ensembles present stable performance with lower overfitting consequence, supporting with the study’s comparative objective rather than extensive benchmarking.

The novelty of this study lies in the simultaneous incorporation of all three industrial by-products (i.e. NM-PCB, silica fume and marble powder) within a unified mixed design and the application of machine learning techniques to develop interpretable, high-accuracy predictive models. This paper’s principal contributions were as follows: The creation of an experimental dataset for the compressive strength (CS) of mortar utilizing electronic trash and cementitious materials through thirty distinct mix formulations. An extensive examination of the mechanisms affecting the performance of mortar base materials using NM-PCB revealed critical parameters of the solid waste materials. The following factors were utilized as input variables.to establish the mortar compressive strength database: Cement (C), Sand (S), Marble powder (MP), Silica fume (S.F), Water- Cement ratio (W/C), Superplasticizer (S.P), Curing age (CUA), and NM-PCB. Multiple machine learning (ML) methods were employed to create projecting models for the CS of mortar containing NM-PCB. Various evaluation metrics were employed to assess the predicted accuracy of distinct models and identify the optimal model. A sensitivity study of input factors about their impact on CS was performed utilizing Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). A user-friendly Python-based graphical user interface (GUI) was developed to facilitate practical implementation of the machine learning model for compressive strength prediction.

Significancy of study

Recent investigations underscore the intricate aspects influencing the compressive strength of cementitious material including discarded materials. The interplay of these elements is frequently nonlinear, complicating the prediction of their cumulative impact on compressive strength by conventional experimental techniques. Consequently, it is essential to provide a comprehensive computational framework that incorporates a diverse array of crucial variables to predict the compressive strength of mortar containing NM-PCB and supplemental cementitious materials (SCMs).This approach not only reduces environmental burdens by diverting waste from landfills and lowering cement and sand consumption but also offers the construction industry a computationally efficient, AI-driven tool for sustainable materials design and strength. Also, NM-PCB mortar is highly heterogeneous and extremely sensitive to several variables, such as the percentage of NM-PCB fine aggregate (PCB), water-cement ratio (w/c), age of concrete (days), Silica fumes (SF), Marble powder (MP). To achieve this objective, experimental datasets were generated from laboratory experiment, and accurate mathematical models with significant generalization and predictive capability were constructed using AI techniques (XGBoost, Random Forest, and Linear Regression). The execution of the generated models was subsequently validated by several statistical measures and performance indicators.

NM-PCB was inspected with a Scanning Electron Microscope (Fig. 2a) which reveals that it had both fibrous and particle components. When looked at under a microscope, the NM-PCB shows that it is largely made up of loosely tangled glass fibers and fiber-particulate aggregates of thermosetting resin. The finer parts are mostly made up of single glass fibers and resin powder. The NM-PCB was noted to acquire a claviform morphology, whilst the other particles had irregular shapes. Separating the thermosetting resin from other constituents that constitute large bundles of fiber-particulate is confronting. The differences in shape and size result from the physical techniques exercised in recycling and crushing. The resin surface exhibits a rough, irregular morphology that facilitates both retention and growth of C-S-H gel, promoting intimate physical contact at the microscale. The resin acts as an effective filler within the ITZ, occupying pores to create denser microstructures with reduced porosity, thereby enhancing the interfacial properties of the cement mortar33. Glass fibers, owing to their high strength and modulus, alleviate stress concentration in the cement matrix by redistributing loads and bridging cracks, thereby mitigating the brittleness and crack propagation typical of cement-based materials34,35. Compared to the relatively smooth surface of pure glass fibers, the residue resin coating provides a textured surface that increases the contact area with the cement matrix. This enhanced interfacial roughness promotes stronger mechanical interlocking, thereby improving the energy absorption efficiency during fiber fracture or pullout36,37.The SEM-EDX pattern shows the presence of silica (SiO2), CaO, Al2O3, and MgO which is also reported by38 as shown in Fig. 2b diffraction pattern show. The survival of amorphous (SiO2) suggests that the NM-PCB powder keeps pozzolanic activity, assisting the hydration of cement. PCBs mainly due to the reaction of silica in the NM-PCB powder with the available calcium in the cement and marble powder as shown in Table 1 results in the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) gel, which facilitates the binding and strength development in mortar alongside filler effect.

Testing methodology for compressive strength

All the constituents were mixed using a mechanical mixer as per ASTM C 305. Superplasticizer was combined with water. Cement, Silica fume, marble powder, fine aggregate, and NM-PCB were all added to the mixer pan as shown in Fig. 3a, then added half of the water and a two-minute rotation. The remaining half of the water was then added to the mixer pan in two steps as shown in Fig. 3b, while the mixer was rotated for another two minutes. The overall mixing time was four minutes. To test the Compressive Strength (CS), specimens measuring 50.8 mm cubes were cast as shown in Fig. 3c, curing as shown in Fig. 3d period was kept 7,14 and 28 days and CS was done on 3 cubes of each mix after completion of curing period as shown in Fig. 3e–f until fracture as shown in Fig. 3g. The compressive strength test was done using a load-controlled compression testing machine that could handle 2000 kN, following ASTM C109/C109M-20. Data description utilized in models construction

Comprehensive research was accompanied by assessing the CS of sustainable mortar (SM), emphasizing supplemental cementitious materials (SCMs) and the recycled NM-PCB. This entailed the establishment of a database of 270 test entries derived from experimental research. Verifying data reliability is crucial for ML algorithms, since it facilitates them to fully comprehend the dataset’s structure and its allied variables. Consequently, the selection of appropriate algorithms is essential for precise analysis. The database categorizes Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCMs), such as silica fume (SF) and marble powder, inside the cementation material (CM) classification, which comprises conventional cement as well. The use of SCM’s such as silica fume and marble powder in SM with NM-PCB can influence compressive strength owing to their distinct physical and chemical characteristics.

Silica fume, due to its pozzolanic properties and small particle size, enhances long-term strength development and workability. Marble powder possesses latent hydraulic and micro-filler properties that enhance the strength and durability of cementitious composites at both early and later stages. SF, because it is very small and has a greater silica concentration, packs the matrix and makes it stronger at an early age. When put together, these SCMs can work together to change the speed of hydration, the morphology of the pores, and the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between NM-PCB and paste. The data set contained in this investigation consists of NM-PCB mixtures with SCMs (SF and MP), which are frequently utilized in combined forms; thus, their effects have been combined modeled. ML modeling techniques used in study

The CS of SM mortar was measured through experimental methodologies. Eight variables were encompassed in the procedures, yielding a singular output (CS). Ensemble machine learning techniques, such as RF and XGB, were used to predict the CS of SM mortar. It is customary to apply machine learning algorithms to analyze outcomes based on input variables. Only 30% of the dataset was employed for testing, while the remaining 70% was used for training machine learning algorithms. Also, 80/20 and 70/30 data split ensures that the models are trained on sufficient data while providing an unbiased assessment of their generalization capability44.The model’s performance is indicated by a strong R2 value between the expected and experimental results.

A minimal gap value indicates a substantial difference, as denoted by a low R2 value. Statistical testing and error evaluations were employed to validate the model’s accuracy. Extreme gradient boosting

XGB and GB are decision tree-based ensemble learning methods46,47, Their fundamental principle entailed developing a sequence of low learners, where each iteration focused on training with the residuals from the preceding model to progressively diminish prediction errors and improve model efficacy. GB employed gradient descent to lower the loss function and often used information from the first-order derivative to locate the optimal spots to split the data. While XGB enhances GB by incorporating regularization terms such as Lasso regularization (L1-alpha) and Ridge regularization (L2-lambda) to regulate the complexity of weak learners and reduce the risk of overfitting.

Random forest regression

RF is an ensemble learning strategy that tries to make regression problems more accurate by constructing a lot of decision tree models and pooling their expected results48,49. RF used the predictions of multiple decision trees to make the final regression prediction.

Partial dependence plot analysis (PDP)

Partial dependence plots (PDPs) help you see the average partial link between the predicted response and one or more characteristics. When there are strong interaction effects, the partial response relationship might not be the same for everyone. So, a regular curve, like the PDP, can hide the details of the relationship that is being tested. So, ICE charts make the PDP better by showing how the projected response and the feature are related for certain observations56. ICE plots specifically illustrate the variability in fitted values over the spectrum of a covariate, indicating the locations and degrees of potential heterogeneities.

Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) plots serve as a tool for visualizing supervised learning models generated by “black box” algorithms. These algorithms use training data (where xi = (xi, 1, …, xi, p) is a vector of predictors and yi is the response) to construct a model that maps the features x to fitted values .

ICE plots expand upon Friedman’s (2001) partial dependence plot (PDP), which emphasizes the average partial link between a collection of predictors and the projected response. ICE plots decompose this average by illustrating the estimated functional relationship for each individual observation. Plotting a curve for each observation helps identify interactions in as well as extrapolations in predictor space.

ICE and PDP analysis

(ICE) and (PDPs) are tools utilized for interpreting ML models, focusing on correlations between predictions and input variables. ICE charts are used to show how the forecast for each data point changes when one variable is changed and the others stay the same. This makes it possible to see how changing features affect the predictions for different instances of the dataset.

Figure 16 shows the ICE plot for the mortar, which includes NM-PCB and SCMs (S.F. and M.P). The curing age has a significant effect on the compressive strength of mortar composite. The main reason for the rise is that cement particles keep getting wet, which make extra hydration products (Calcium Silicate Hydrate-CSH gel) that fill the holes and spaces in the concrete matrix. This process makes concrete that is denser, less porous, and stronger in the end. The second influencing factor is the quantity of superplasticizer, specifically polycarboxylate-ether (PCE), which improves the workability of mortar and facilitates substantial reductions in mixing water. The reduced water content is the primary factor contributing to the normal enhancement in compressive strength. Multiple studies point to significant 28-day strength enhancement, typically ranging from 5% to 40%, depending upon the mixture and dosage, when the superplasticizer is utilized correctly and is compatible with cement. Nonetheless, specific superplasticizer formulations or excessive dosages may impede cement hydration and diminish early-age strength. Consequently, the overall effect is contingent upon the superplasticizer’s chemistry, dose level, water-to-cement ratio, and curing circumstances67.The initial enhancement in strength, coupled with a reduced replacement level of SCMs, results from decreased water consumption, improved particle packing (attributable to M.P.), and improved pozzolanic reactions (due to S.F). However, an elevated level of replacement might dilute the cement matrix, hinder strength development, and negatively impact workability when the proportion of supplementary cementitious materials exceeds the ideal level. Consequently, this leads to a decrease in CS. Therefore, it is critical to perform thorough mixed design and testing to determine the appropriate amount of SCMs necessary to attain the desired concrete qualities (Fig. 16). The w/c ratio demonstrates both a growing and declining tendency. A higher w/c ratio means that more water is mixed into the mixture. Capillary pores stay open as extra water that wasn’t absorbed during hydration evaporates, which makes the concrete more porous and weaker. Due to this, the density and the CS go down. The average CS is slightly influenced by NM-PCB. As a result, their impact on CS is less. The decreasing trend in compressive strength with the increasing amount of NM-PCB is attributable to several causes, comprising a weakened aggregate-matrix bond, heightened porosity and microcracking, quality variations, and reduced mechanical characteristics of the mortar.

This study initiated the development of a mortar compressive strength database through experimental testing involving NM-PCB and SCMs. Then, three machine learning algorithms were used to construct predictive models for the compressive strength of SM mortar. These models were then compared to see how accurate they were. After that, the SHAP analytic method was used to look at how input parameters affected the compressive strength of SM mortar. This was followed by PDP and ICE studies. A graphical user interface was created to provide swift and intuitive estimations of the compressive strength of SM mortar. The subsequent findings were derived.

The addition of NM-PCB, silica fume, and marble powder at reduced replacement levels improves the 28-day compressive strength of sustainable mortar, with the optimal mix (M-4) attaining 17.6 MPa, in contrast to 14.9 MPa for the control mix. Excessive replacement levels, however, diminish compressive strength due to the dilution of cementitious chemicals and the detrimental effects of superplasticizers on hydration.
