Successful balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection for common femoral artery pseudoaneurysm after venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation decannulation
Authors: Shinichi Ijuin, Naoki Matsunaga, Sayaka Goto, Taiki Moriyama, Yuta Tanaka, Akihiko Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemoral artery pseudoaneurysm is a serious vascular complication after venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) decannulation, and optimal management remains challenging. We report the case of a 74-year-old man with acute pulmonary embolism who developed a common femoral artery pseudoaneurysm after VA-ECMO removal. Ultrasound-guided compression was unsuccessful, and surgical repair was considered but deferred because of the patient’s clinical condition and operating room unavailability. Balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection achieved complete thrombosis without distal embolization or other complications. This technique may represent a feasible, minimally invasive alternative to surgical repair for femoral artery pseudoaneurysms following VA-ECMO decannulation, particularly in high-risk surgical patients.Keywords: Balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection, Pseudoaneurysm, Common femoral artery, VA-ECMO, Decannulation

Femoral artery pseudoaneurysm is a well-recognized vascular complication of large-bore arterial cannulation, such as after venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO).1,2 Owing to the large diameter of ECMO cannulae and the need for routine anticoagulation therapy, it is critical to achieve reliable hemostasis after decannulation. Vascular complications after peripheral VA-ECMO decannulation are typically managed with surgical intervention,3,4 but this may not always be immediately feasible, particularly in patients with clinical conditions or situations complicated by competing emergencies and limited institutional resources. Under such circumstances, less invasive treatment strategies that can be performed promptly are more desirable.

Interventional radiology-based treatments, including balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection, have been reported for pseudoaneurysms that develop after the removal of other mechanical circulatory support devices.5,6 These techniques are less invasive compared with surgical repair. Nevertheless, because of the large-bore nature of ECMO cannulae, the effectiveness and safety of balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection for pseudoaneurysms following ECMO decannulation have not been well established.

This report describes a case of femoral artery pseudoaneurysm that developed after VA-ECMO decannulation, which was successfully treated with balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection as a less invasive treatment option.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man was transferred to our emergency department with circulatory shock. He had a significant smoking history and a medical history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed an acute pulmonary embolism. Despite anticoagulation therapy, the hemodynamic instability persisted, and VA-ECMO was initiated via the right common femoral artery (17F cannula) and left common femoral vein (21F cannula). The patient’s circulatory status gradually stabilized upon admission, and VA-ECMO was discontinued on hospital day 5. At our institution, bedside manual compression is the first-line approach for arterial decannulation after peripheral VA-ECMO, whereas surgical repair is considered according to patient-specific factors. Vascular closure devices are not used. In the present case, arterial decannulation was performed with manual compression for 50 minutes, which achieved hemostasis without apparent complications. Systemic anticoagulation therapy was continued, and the postdecannulation course was uneventful.

On hospital day 7, the patient developed acute pain in the right groin at the arterial cannulation site during mobilization, whereas his vital signs remained stable. Physical examination revealed localized tenderness without signs of infection. Duplex ultrasound study and contrast-enhanced imaging demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm arising from the common femoral artery at the previous cannulation site. On contrast-enhanced computed tomography, the pseudoaneurysm had a neck diameter of 3.1 mm, a neck length of 5.6 mm, and a cavity measuring 20.4 × 31.2 × 34.8 mm (Fig 1). There was no evidence of overlying skin compromise. Ultrasound-guided compression was attempted, but this failed to achieve adequate flow control. Surgical repair was initially planned, but the operating room was temporarily unavailable because of an emergency operation for another critically ill patient. Furthermore, the patient’s history of COPD increased the risks associated with general anesthesia. Considering the patient’s stable condition, lack of immediate surgical access, and desire to avoid general anesthesia, balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection was performed as an alternative treatment. Vascular access was obtained via the left common femoral artery, through which a 5.2F Selecon MP Catheter II (Terumo; maximum balloon diameter, 9 mm) was advanced and positioned across the neck of the right common femoral artery pseudoaneurysm. Under real-time fluoroscopic guidance, the balloon was inflated with the minimum volume required to temporarily occlude flow into the pseudoaneurysm, with adequate neck occlusion confirmed by angiography through the guiding catheter. A mixture of thrombin (5000 units) and contrast agent was carefully injected into the pseudoaneurysm sac via a catheter needle under real-time ultrasound guidance, resulting in immediate thrombosis and complete cessation of flow. The balloon was maintained in the inflated position for 15 minutes after thrombin injection and subsequently deflated after confirmation of complete thrombosis. The access site was managed with manual compression. The total procedure time was 52 minutes (Fig 2). Follow-up ultrasound study confirmed complete resolution of the pseudoaneurysm without evidence of distal embolization or arterial occlusion. Anticoagulant therapy was continued throughout the postoperative course. Thereafter, the patient had an uneventful clinical course without recurrence, and he was transferred to another hospital 10 days after the procedure. The patient and his family provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Discussion

Femoral artery pseudoaneurysm is a potentially serious complication after VA-ECMO decannulation, particularly in patients receiving ongoing antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy.4,7 In such cases, surgical repair is often considered the standard treatment, as it enables direct visualization and definitive vascular repair,4 but this requires specialized expertise and an operating room.

In the present case, no preclose technique or vascular closure device was used, and the 17F arterial cannula was removed with manual compression. Although preclose strategies using suture-mediated closure devices have been reported for percutaneous VA-ECMO decannulation,8 whether such an approach would have prevented pseudoaneurysm formation in this case remains uncertain.

Balloon-assisted thrombin injection is an established treatment for iatrogenic femoral artery pseudoaneurysms after diagnostic or interventional catheterization, with a high reported success rate.9,10 However, its application after VA-ECMO decannulation remains less well established because of large-bore arterial access, ongoing anticoagulation, and concerns regarding distal embolization. Consequently, reports of its use in ECMO-related pseudoaneurysms remain limited. Although some reports have described balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection after ECMO decannulation, these were limited to pseudoaneurysms of the superficial femoral artery related to distal perfusion cannulas or lacked detailed procedural descriptions.4

In the present case, balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection was performed because immediate surgical repair was not feasible. The patient’s significant smoking history and underlying COPD further supported the decision to avoid general anesthesia. The potential value of balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection in selected patients after VA-ECMO decannulation may extend beyond avoidance of general anesthesia. Large-bore arterial cannulation, ongoing anticoagulation, and critical illness may complicate surgical groin management and increase the risk of wound-related morbidity. Avoiding surgical groin exposure may therefore be particularly advantageous in the selected patients at increased risk of wound complications or when immediate surgical repair is not feasible. However, this approach should be considered an alternative for carefully selected patients rather than a replacement for definitive surgical repair. Although the procedure required approximately 52 minutes, it was completed successfully without the need for general anesthesia or surgical exposure and achieved complete thrombosis of the common femoral pseudoaneurysm without complications. These findings highlight the feasibility and effectiveness of this minimally invasive approach even after large-bore ECMO cannulation. This case underscores the importance of individualized treatment strategies based on patient background and institutional resources.

Nevertheless, this technique should be performed with caution, particularly because of the risks of distal arterial thrombosis and embolization.11 Although absolute anatomical cutoff values have not been established, a wide or very short pseudoaneurysm neck may increase the risk of thrombin leakage into the parent artery. A known allergy to thrombin, active local infection, compromised overlying skin, or limb ischemia are contraindications to thrombin injection.12,13 Adequate temporary occlusion of the parent artery with the available balloon catheter is also an important technical requirement for this balloon-assisted technique. A shorter balloon may permit focal occlusion but requires precise positioning, whereas a longer balloon may provide more stable coverage but exposes a longer arterial segment to inflation. Thus, adequate neck coverage, avoidance of excessive balloon oversizing, and minimal inflation to achieve temporary occlusion may be more important than balloon length alone. However, evidence regarding the optimal balloon configuration remains limited, and balloon selection should be individualized according to vascular anatomy and pseudoaneurysm morphology. Careful patient selection, real-time ultrasound guidance, and close postprocedural monitoring are essential.8

The detailed procedural technique and imaging findings presented in this report may facilitate the safe application of balloon-assisted thrombin injection in carefully selected patients with pseudoaneurysm after VA-ECMO decannulation. The optimal indications and safety profile of balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection for ECMO-related vascular complications also require further clarification.

Conclusions

Balloon-assisted percutaneous thrombin injection may represent a feasible and minimally invasive alternative for selected patients with common femoral artery pseudoaneurysm after VA-ECMO decannulation when surgical repair is high risk or not immediately feasible.
