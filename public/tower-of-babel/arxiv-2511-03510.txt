Inertial Repulsion from Quantum Geometry
Maike Fahrensohn, R. Matthias Geilhufe

Abstract
We derive a repulsive, charge-dipole-like interaction for a Dirac particle in a rotating frame, arising from a geometric U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) gauge symmetry associated with the Berry phase. The Lagrangian of this system includes a non-inertial correction due to centrifugal field coupling. By imposing gauge symmetry and treating it as a full gauge theory, the Lagrangian is extended to include Berry connection and curvature terms. Upon integrating out the geometric gauge field, the effective action is obtained. This leads to the emergence of a repulsive, long-range effective interaction in the Lagrangian. Explicitly, in the non-inertial frame of the observer, the geometric gauge invariance effectively leads to a repulsive Coulomb-interaction in momentum space. In real space, the inertial repulsion manifests in a 1 / | r | 2 1/|r|^{2} potential, which is symmetric about the origin of rotation and mirrors charge-dipole interaction.

In this paper, we focus on the effect of the centrifugal force on quantum particles in a rotating frame and show that its connection to quantum geometry induces a repulsive effective force. As the centrifugal force is odd under spatial inversion, it couples linearly to the position operator of the electron

The use of gauge fields plays an important role in modern physics, permeating a wide range of fields from high energy and nuclear physics to condensed matter

In the context of the dipole coupling to the centrifugal force, we treat the Berry curvature as a dynamical degree of freedom of the theory that, upon integration, can mediate effective forces and leads to a charge-dipole-like repulsive effective interaction, emerging from the geometry of quantum phase space.

In the following, we develop the theory for a Dirac particle in a rotating frame. However, it should be noted, that the effect is more general and can be extended to Dirac particles in accelerated systems.

While the derivation of the inertial repulsion was motivated from rotation-induced inertial effects and is applicable to a wide range of rotating physical systems, the underlying mechanism extends far beyond purely rotational systems. We derived the emergent, repulsive, long-ranged interaction for a fermion in a rotating frame. This effective interaction arises after integrating out the geometric gauge potential (Berry potential) from the Lagrangian containing a centrifugal field coupling correction term due to the non-inertial frame of reference. The resulting effective interaction in Eq.
8
shows a Coulomb-like structure in momentum space. In real space, this leads to a symmetric interaction term that decays as
1/|\bm{r}|^{2}
and corresponds to a force
F\propto 1/|\bm{r}|^{3}
, mirroring a charge-dipole interaction. The necessary prerequisite for this interaction is the presence of an interaction term in the Hamiltonian that couples linearly to the position operator
\bm{r}
. Such terms can be Fourier transformed into momentum space, leading to derivatives in the momentum which can minimally couple to a geometric gauge field and, consequently, yield the repulsive effective interaction term derived in the previous section. This framework is therefore not restricted to pure rotation but can be applied to general accelerated systems with acceleration
\bm{a}
, where an inertial potential
\sim m\bm{a}\cdot\bm{r}
linear in
\bm{r}
appears. In fact, the derivation of the centrifugal coupling term in the Hamiltonian conducted by Hehl and Ni
Hehl and Ni 1990
does not rely only on rotation but instead derives the term for a general 4-acceleration of the observer. We expect to see the emergence of a similar inertial repulsion term, depending on the four-acceleration
a
of the observer’s frame. Further discussion of such a term lies beyond the scope of this paper and will be covered separately. The derivation presented in the supplementary material considers the special case
\bm{a}=-\omega^{2}\bm{d}
of a purely rotational system with angular velocity
\omega
and radial position
\bm{d}
.

More generally, the formalism presented here can be extended to further fields coupling linearly to the position operator, leading to a repulsive effective geometric potential. A prominent example is the coupling of an electric field to a charged particle
Talin et al. 2008
;
Griffiths 2020
, where the potential energy takes the role of the centrifugal field coupling, or infrared-active phonons
Decius 1968
;
Zan et al. 2024
, which involve electrons coupling to dipolar displacements.

The inertial repulsion derived in this work, therefore, does not only constitute a peculiarity of the specific rotational system, but rather has wide-ranging potential applications as a geometric mechanism and can contribute to previously neglected corrections to fermionic energy levels in rotating frames.

M
receives a correction stemming from the Berry curvature
\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q})
that takes the form:

\bm{M}(r)=\frac{e}{2\hbar}\int\limits_{BZ}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\omega(\bm{q},\bm{r})\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q}),

(30)

where
\omega(\bm{q},\bm{r})
is a weight that can depend on the Fermi energy.

In momentum space, this becomes:

\bm{M}(\bm{k})=\frac{e}{2\hbar}\int\limits_{BZ}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q})\underbrace{\int\mathrm{d}^{3}re^{-i\bm{kr}}\omega(\bm{q},\bm{r})}_{\eqqcolon\omega(\bm{q},\bm{k})}.

(31)

The square of the magnetization term in the Lagrangian can then be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\bm{M}(\bm{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bm{M}(\bm{k})\bm{M}(-\bm{k})

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\left(\frac{e}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q\mathrm{d}^{3}q^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{6}}\omega(\bm{q},\bm{k})\omega(\bm{q^{\prime}},-\bm{k})\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q})\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q^{\prime}}).

(33)

In the long-wavelength limit,
\omega(\bm{q},\bm{r})
varies slowly in
\bm{r}
such that:

\omega(\bm{q},\bm{k})\approx(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(k)\omega(\bm{q}),\quad\omega(\bm{q})=\omega(\bm{q},\bm{k}=\bm{0}).

(34)

This approximation yields:

\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\bm{M}(\bm{r})^{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\left(\frac{e}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q\mathrm{d}^{3}q^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\omega(\bm{q})\omega(\bm{q^{\prime}})\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q})\bm{\Omega}(\bm{q^{\prime}}).

(35)

Assuming diagonal kernel approximation, this reduces to:

\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\bm{M}(\bm{r})^{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\left(\frac{e}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\omega^{2}(\bm{q})\Omega_{\mu\nu}\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(36)

The Lagrangian then acquires the additional geometric term:

\mathcal{L}\supset-\frac{\mu_{0}}{\chi}\left(\frac{e}{2\hbar}\right)^{2}\omega^{2}(\bm{q})\Omega_{\mu\nu}\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(37)

To lowest order,
\omega
may be regarded as constant, leading to a Lagrangian distribution mirroring the form of the field-strength tensor term
-\frac{1}{4g^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
in gauge theory. This reinforces the interpretation of the Berry curvature as a geometric field strength with the Berry connection as the corresponding gauge field.

Effective Action Calculation

We begin with the full Lagrangian in Eq.
24
. To isolate the effects of the geometric gauge field, we consider its contributions to the action. It suffices to study the explicitly gauge potential-dependent terms in the action:

S_{\text{gauge}}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left\{\beta\gamma^{2}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{centr}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}\psi^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\psi_{\bm{k}}-\frac{1}{4g^{2}}\Omega_{\mu\nu}\Omega^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{gf}}\right\}.

(38)

To obtain the quadratic structure of this gauge sector, we first rewrite the square of the Berry curvature through integration by parts:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Omega_{\mu\nu}\Omega^{\mu\nu}

(39)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left(\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{A}^{\nu}-\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}\partial^{\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\left(\partial_{\nu}\partial^{\nu}\mathcal{A}^{\mu}-\partial_{\nu}\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{A}^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\left(\Box_{\bm{k}}\eta^{\mu\nu}-\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\right)\mathcal{A}_{\nu}.

The last term can be cancelled by using Coulomb gauge
Magpantay 1994
as the gauge fixing term in the Lagrangian, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{gf}}=g^{-2}\left(\bm{\nabla}_{k}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}\right)^{2}
. The gauge-dependent part of the action then simplifies to:

S_{\text{gauge}}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}k^{3}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left\{\beta\bm{J}_{\bm{k}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}K\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{k}\right\},

(40)

where we have introduced the effective current operator
\bm{J}_{\bm{k}}=\gamma^{2}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{centr}}\psi^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\psi_{\bm{k}}
and the kinetic operator
K=-g^{-2}\eta\Box_{\bm{k}}
of the gauge field, where
\eta=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)
is the Minkowski metric tensor and
\Box_{\bm{k}}
the momentum-space d’Alembertian.

We now formally integrate out the gauge field over the exponential of the gauge-dependent action.
Since the gauge sector is quadratic, this is a Gaussian integral
Zee 2010
:

\displaystyle e^{iS_{\text{gauge}}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{A}\exp\left(i\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\left\{\beta\bm{J}_{\bm{k}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}K\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{k}}\right\}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{4}}{\det(K)}}\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\mathrm{d}^{3}k\mathrm{d}^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\bm{J}_{\bm{k}}D(\bm{k}-\bm{k^{\prime}})\bm{J}_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}\right),

where we use
\beta^{2}=\mathbf{1}
and introduce the propagator
D(\bm{k})
, defined as the inverse of
K
, i.e.,
KD(\bm{k})=\delta^{(3)}(\bm{k})
. This can be calculated to be:

\displaystyle KD(k)

\displaystyle=-\eta g^{-2}\Box_{\bm{k}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}re^{-i\bm{k}\bm{r}}\tilde{D}(\bm{r})

(42)

\displaystyle=\eta g^{-2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}rr^{2}\tilde{D}(\bm{r})e^{-i\bm{k}\bm{r}}

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{k})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}r}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-i\bm{k}\bm{r}},

and, therefore,

D(\bm{k})=\eta g^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}r}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{-i\bm{k}\bm{r}}}{r^{2}}=\eta\frac{g^{2}}{4\pi k}.

(43)

Fourier transformation of the propagator to position space then yields:

D(\bm{r})=\eta\frac{g^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}r^{2}},

(44)

which decays with
\frac{1}{r^{2}}
.
