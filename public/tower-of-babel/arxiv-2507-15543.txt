A new construction for Melnikov chaos in piecewise-smooth planar systems
A. Calamai, M. Franca, M. Pospíšil

Abstract
In this paper we consider a piecewise smooth 2 2 -dimensional system x → ˙ = f → ​ ( x → ) + ε ​ g → ​ ( t , x → , ε ) \dot{\vec{x}}=\vec{f}(\vec{x})+\varepsilon\vec{g}(t,\vec{x},\varepsilon) where ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 is a small parameter and f → \vec{f} is discontinuous along a curve Ω 0 \Omega^{0} . We assume that 0 → \vec{0} is a critical point for any ε ≥ 0 \varepsilon\geq 0 , and that for ε = 0 \varepsilon=0 the system admits a trajectory γ → ​ ( t ) \vec{\gamma}(t) homoclinic to 0 → \vec{0} and crossing transversely Ω 0 \Omega^{0} in γ → ​ ( 0 ) \vec{\gamma}(0) . In a previous paper we have shown that, also in an n n -dimensional setting, the classical Melnikov condition is enough to guarantee the persistence of the homoclinic to perturbations, but more recently we have found an open condition, a geometric obstruction which is not possible in the smooth case, which prevents chaos for 2 2 -dimensional systems when g → \vec{g} is periodic in t t . The results are illustrated by examples.

Introduction

In this paper we deal with planar, piecewise-smooth systems and we prove, by Melnikov theory, the
occurrence of a chaotic behavior when the fixed point of the unperturbed system lies on the discontinuity level.

More in detail, we consider the piecewise-smooth system

\dot{\vec{x}}=\vec{f}^{\pm}(\vec{x})+\varepsilon\vec{g}(t,\vec{x},\varepsilon),\quad\vec{x}\in\Omega^{\pm},

(PS)

where
\Omega^{\pm}=\{\vec{x}\in\Omega\mid\pm G(\vec{x})>0\}
,
\Omega^{0}=\{\vec{x}\in\Omega\mid G(\vec{x})=0\}
,
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is an open set,
G
is a
C^{r}
-function on
\Omega
with
r>1

such that
0
is a regular value of

G
. Next,
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a small parameter, and
\vec{f}^{\pm}\in C^{r}_{b}(\Omega^{\pm}\cup\Omega^{0},{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
,
\vec{g}\in C_{b}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and

G\in C_{b}^{r}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
, i.e., the
derivatives of
\vec{f}^{\pm}
,
\vec{g}
and
G
are uniformly continuous and bounded up to the
r
-th order, respectively,
if
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and up to
r_{0}
if
r=r_{0}+r_{1}
, with
r_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
0<r_{1}<1
and the
r_{0}
-th derivatives are
r_{1}

Hölder continuous.

Here and in the sequel, we
use the shorthand notation

to

We assume that both the systems

\vec{0}

Moreover, the

transversal to
\Omega^{0}
at

Further, we assume that for
\varepsilon=0

\vec{\gamma}(t)
, crossing transversely the discontinuity level
\Omega^{0}
at some point
\vec{\gamma}(0)\neq\vec{0}

That is, the homoclinic trajectory
{\vec{\gamma}}(t)

\vec{\gamma}(t)\in\begin{cases}\Omega^{-},&t<0,\\
\Omega^{0},&t=0,\\

So

Our aim is to obtain a sufficient condition for the occurrence of a chaotic pattern, when we turn on a small perturbation
\varepsilon\neq 0
in
(

).

In the smooth case, the problem of detecting the occurrence of chaotic solutions for non-autonomous dynamical systems is now classical.
In particular, it is

More in detail, let us consider the smooth planar system

\dot{\vec{x}}=\vec{f}(\vec{x})+\varepsilon\vec{g}(t,\vec{x},\varepsilon),\quad\quad\vec{x}\in\Omega,

(S)

having a homoclinic trajectory, where both
\vec{f}

In this framework, after the pioneering work of Melnikov

Let us assume for illustrative purposes that
\vec{g}

Roughly speaking, in order to prove the existence of the chaotic pattern
in a discontinuous setting, Battelli and Fečkan glued together infinitely many
solutions of the perturbed problem,
which mimic
different time-translation of the unperturbed homoclinic.
For this purpose, they had to solve infinitely many equations via implicit function theorem, using
a Lijapunov-Schimdt reduction (needed in the

A key point of their contribution, and one of the main technical and theoretical issues,
was to understand how the discontinuity of the flow affects the jumps in the projections
of the exponential dichotomy, and how the non-degeneracy condition required in the

This way they managed to prove the persistence of the homoclinic
[
4

[

If, instead, we assume that the critical point
lies on the discontinuity hypersurface
\Omega^{0}
,
the problem of detecting a chaotic behavior becomes even more challenging. Why shall one insist on studying this case?
In many real applications this is what really happens, e.g. in systems with dry friction.
In this setting,
as a preliminary step, in
[
12
]
it was shown that the Melnikov condition found by Battelli and Fečkan together with a further

(always satisfied in two dimensions) are enough to prove the persistence of the homoclinic trajectory.

Subsequently, in
[
15

we considered the case of a “one-sided” homoclinic.
In other words, in the discontinuous setting like (
PS
) we assumed that

{\vec{\gamma}}(t)\in\Omega^{+}
for any

.
In this case, with suitable assumptions we proved that the chaotic trajectories are still located in
\Omega^{+}
.
We point out that, even if the main motivation and applications are set in the discontinuous case,
a crucial idea in both the papers
[
12
]
and
[
15
]
was that the problem addressed could be
equivalently stated for continuous systems and reduced, in some sense, to a problem of location of trajectories.
Moreover, we stress that in
[

]
and
[

, like in the paper by Battelli and Fečkan
[

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Hence, in the case in which the origin lies on the discontinuity hypersurface

splits in two branches, the problem of the occurrence of chaotic patterns was still open.
The purpose of the paper is to fill this gap, at least in the two-dimensional case.

We think it is worth mentioning the recent papers
[
24
,
25
]
, still concerning the
n
-dimensional case,

n\geq 2
, and in which
the origin lies on the discontinuity hypersurface

As pointed out, here we focus on the two-dimensional case. In this setting, in the recent paper
[

which ensures the persistence of the homoclinic

In fact, anytime we have sliding phenomena

phenomena take place, scenarios which may exist just in a discontinuous context.

In this paper we complete the picture
of the two-dimensional, discontinuous case showing
that, when close to the origin no sliding phenomena occur,
system (

as in the smooth setting; while according to
[
12

, if there is sliding close to the origin, even if

), so we deduce the existence of a sequence
of zeros of
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)
, say

\bar{{\mathcal{T}}}=(\bar{T}_{j})
where
\bar{T}_{j}\in[\Theta j,\Theta j+1[
, which satisfies (
2.12
) and (
2.13
),
so that we can apply Theorem
2.8
.

Hence for any
{\mathrm{e}}\in{\mathcal{E}}
we find (a not uniquely defined)
\vec{\xi}({\mathrm{e}})
and a sequence
\alpha_{j}^{{\mathrm{e}}}=\alpha_{j}^{{\mathrm{e}}}(\varepsilon)
such that the
trajectory

\vec{x}(t,T_{0}+\alpha_{0}^{{\mathrm{e}}};\vec{\xi}({\mathrm{e}}))

has property
\mathbf{C_{e}}
, but we just know that
|\alpha_{j}^{{\mathrm{e}}}(\varepsilon)|\leq 1
.

Finally notice that if we happen to know that
R(t)
is such that
M_{r}(0)=M^{\prime}_{r}(t)\equiv 0
in an arbitrarily small
neighborhood of the origin, then we can apply Remark
5.8
and we find that
\alpha_{0}^{{\mathrm{e}}}=O(\varepsilon)
.

Example 7.4
.

Let us consider system (
7.1
) with
g_{1}(t,x,y)=xh(t)
,
h(t)=\sign(t)\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{|t|}
,
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
,
r\geq 2
,
where
\sign(t)=1
if
t>0
,
\sign(t)=-1
if
t<0
, and
\sign(t)=0
if
t=0
.

It can be shown that
h\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}})
so
\vec{g}\in C^{r}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}\times\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.
Notice that
h
is odd, and consequently
\mathcal{M}
is odd. So, in what follows, we assume that
\theta>0
.

Once again
plugging the given perturbation into (
7.5
), we get

\displaystyle A(k,\theta)=-\int_{-\infty}^{-\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{-(t+\theta)}\,dt+\int_{-\theta}^{0}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t+\theta}\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t+\theta}\,dt.

Now, we estimate these three integrals:

\displaystyle I_{1}=-\int_{-\infty}^{-\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{-(t+\theta)}\,dt=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t+\theta)}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t}\,dt,

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}\,dt\to 0

as
\theta\to\infty
.
Next, integrating by parts, we derive

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int_{-\theta}^{0}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t+\theta}\,dt=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}t{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r+1}t\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{\theta}-\frac{2r+1}{k}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt.

Similarly,

\displaystyle I_{3}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t+\theta}\,dt=2\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}t{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r+1}t\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{\theta}+\frac{2r+1}{k}\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt.

So we have

\displaystyle A(k,\theta)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t+\theta)}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{t}\,dt+\frac{2}{k}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{\theta}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{2r+1}{k}\left[\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt-\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt\right].

For the integrals in the bracket, we have the following estimates

\left|\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt\right|\leq{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt^{2}}\,dt,

where

\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{\int_{\sqrt{\theta}}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt^{2}}\,dt}{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}=\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}{2k\sqrt{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}=\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{1}{2k\sqrt{\theta}}=0

by de l’Hopital’s rule, and analogously

\left|\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k(t^{2}-\theta)}\sin^{2r}t\cos t\,dt\right|\leq{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt^{2}}\,dt,

where

\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\theta}}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt^{2}}\,dt}{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}}=\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}}{2k\sqrt{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}}=\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\frac{1}{2k\sqrt{\theta}}=0.

In conclusion, for
\theta>0
we find

\mathcal{M}(\theta)=\frac{1}{3}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{\theta}+R_{1}(\theta),

where
R_{1}(\theta)\to 0
as
\theta\to+\infty
.

Since
\mathcal{M}
is odd, it follows

\mathcal{M}(\theta)=\sign(\theta)\frac{1}{3}\sin^{2r+1}\sqrt{|\theta|}+\sign(\theta)R_{1}(|\theta|).

This means that the Melnikov function has a doubly infinite sequence of negative minima
{\mathcal{M}}(b_{2i})

and positive maxima
{\mathcal{M}}(b_{2i+1})
such that

\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathcal{M}}(b_{2i})<-\frac{1}{6}<0<\frac{1}{6}<{\mathcal{M}}(b_{2i+1}),&i\in{\mathbb{Z}},\\
b_{j}=\sign(2j-1)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(2j-1)\right)^{2},&j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\end{array}

(7.6)

so assumption
P1
holds with
\bar{c}=1/6
(see also Remark

2.15
).
Moreover, note that the zeros are getting further and further away from each other as
\theta
tends to
+\infty
or

-\infty
.

In fact
{\mathcal{M}}(0)=0
since
{\mathcal{M}}
is odd. Further we find

{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(\theta)=2A_{\theta}(3,\theta)-A_{\theta}(2,\theta),

A_{\theta}(k,\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k|t|}h^{\prime}(t+\theta)dt

for
A_{\theta}(k,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}A(k,\theta)
,
where we used the exponential behaviour of the integrand and the Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. So when

\theta=0
we find

A_{\theta}(k,0)=2(2r+1)\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-ks^{2}}\sin^{2r}s\cos s\,ds,

{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)=2A_{\theta}(3,0)-A_{\theta}(2,0)

(7.7)

taking into account that the integrand is even and we set
t=s^{2}
.

One can compute numerically
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)
for some specific values of
r
; for instance, we find the following:

\textrm{ for }r=2:{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)\cong 0.023;

\textrm{ for }r=3:{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)\cong-0.020.

Note that in both cases
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.
However in this case, (
2.11
) and even (
2.8
) are not satisfied because we do not have a uniform
control of the slope of
{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)
in its zeroes.
So we can just apply Theorem
2.14
, and we
obtain a
chaotic pattern
similar to the one of Example
7.1
. That is, for any sequence of zeros of
{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot)
, say
{\mathcal{T}}=(T_{j})
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, such that
T_{0}=0
,

T_{j+1}-T_{j}\geq\Theta
, we obtain a set
\Sigma({\mathcal{T}})
such that for any
{\mathrm{e}}\in{\mathcal{E}}
there is a

\vec{\xi}({\mathrm{e}})\in\Sigma({\mathcal{T}})
, and a sequence
(\alpha_{j}^{\mathrm{e}}(\varepsilon))
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\vec{x}(t,T_{0}+\alpha_{0}^{\mathrm{e}}(\varepsilon);\vec{\xi}({\mathrm{e}}))
has property
\mathbf{C_{e}}
.

Further

{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(T_{0})\neq 0
, so we can apply Remark
5.8
to find
|\alpha_{0}^{{\mathrm{e}}}(\varepsilon)|\leq C_{\alpha}\varepsilon
.
However for
k\neq 0
we just have the a priori
estimate

|\alpha_{k}^{{\mathrm{e}}}(\varepsilon)|\leq B_{2k}=b_{n_{2k}+1}-b_{n_{2k}}=\begin{cases}2\pi^{2}|n_{2k}|,&n_{2k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\},\\
\frac{\pi^{2}}{2},&n_{2k}=0.\end{cases}

(7.8)

As far as we are aware, even in the smooth case, it was not possible to apply
the results available in literature to this type of Melnikov functions (with distance between consecutive zeros going to infinity).

We can go even further with a more general aperiodic perturbation satisfying
P1
:

Example 7.5
.

Let us consider system (
7.1
) with
g_{1}(t,x,y)=xh(t)
and odd function
h\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
|h(t)|\leq H_{0}
,

|h^{\prime}(t)|\leq H_{1}
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and some constants
H_{0},H_{1}>0
; further we assume that there is a sequence
(\theta_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
\theta_{j}>0
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\theta_{j}\to+\infty
as

j\to+\infty

such that
h(\theta_{2j})<-c
,
h(\theta_{2j+1})>c
for some
c>0
and
h^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
.

As before we find that

\mathcal{M}(\theta)=2A(3,\theta)-A(2,\theta),

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\theta)=2A_{\theta}(3,\theta)-A_{\theta}(2,\theta).

(7.9)

We assume firstly
\theta>0
and we investigate the integrals:

\left|\int_{-\infty}^{-\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h(-(t+\theta))\,dt\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t+\theta)}|h(t)|\,dt\leq H_{0}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}\,dt

which tends to
0
as
\theta\to\infty
. Integrating by parts we get

\displaystyle\int_{-\theta}^{0}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h(t+\theta)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k(t-\theta)}h(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{h(\theta)}{k}-\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}h(0)}{k}-\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}{k}\int_{0}^{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h^{\prime}(t)\,dt.

Here if
{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h^{\prime}(t)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, we can find
K>0
such that

\left|\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}h(0)}{k}+\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}{k}\int_{0}^{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right|\leq\frac{K}{k}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta};

otherwise, if
{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h^{\prime}(t)\not\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, using de l’Hopital’s rule, as
\theta\to\infty
we find

\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}h(0)}{k}+\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}{k}\int_{0}^{\theta}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{kt}h^{\prime}(t)\,dt\sim\frac{h(0)}{k}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}+\frac{h^{\prime}(\theta)}{k^{2}}.

Finally similarly,

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}h(t+\theta)\,dt=\int_{\theta}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k(t-\theta)}h(t)\,dt=\frac{h(\theta)}{k}+\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}}{k}\int_{\theta}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}h^{\prime}(t)\,dt

with, for
\theta
large enough,

\left|\frac{{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{k\theta}}{k}\int_{\theta}^{\infty}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-kt}h^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right|\leq 2\frac{|h^{\prime}(\theta)|}{k^{2}}.

In conclusion,

A(k,\theta)=\frac{2h(\theta)}{k}+R(\theta,k),\quad|R(\theta,k)|\leq\frac{\bar{K}{\,\textrm{\rm e}}^{-k\theta}}{k}+\frac{3|h^{\prime}(\theta)|}{k^{2}}

for
\theta>0
large and a suitable
\bar{K}>0
.
Hence, using the fact that
\mathcal{M}
is odd,
and since by assumption
h^{\prime}(\theta)\to 0
as
\theta\to+\infty
,
we obtain

\mathcal{M}(\theta)=\frac{h(\theta)}{3}+R(\theta),\quad|R(\theta)|\to 0\text{ as $\theta\to+\infty$}.

This means that
\mathcal{M}(\theta_{2j})<-\tfrac{c}{6}<0
,
\mathcal{M}(\theta_{2j+1})>\tfrac{c}{6}>0
and

\mathcal{M}(-\theta_{2j})>\tfrac{c}{6}>0
,
\mathcal{M}(-\theta_{2j+1})<-\tfrac{c}{6}<0
if
|j|
is large enough, so
assumption
P1
is fulfilled. Further from the assumptions on
h
it follows that the distance between consecutive zeros of
{\mathcal{M}}
is
unbounded.

Hence we find a sequence of zeros of
{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot)
, say
{\mathcal{T}}=(T_{j})
, such that
T_{0}=0
,
T_{j+1}-T_{j}\geq\Theta
,
but we do not have control on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(T_{k})
so we can just apply Theorem
2.14

and we obtain results analogous to the ones
of Example
7.4
, in particular we just find
|\alpha_{k}|\leq B_{2k}
and
(B_{2k})
may become unbounded.
Notice that in this result the function
h
can again be regarded also as some kind of noise on which we have no information but the one given in
the statement.

However, if we assume
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
(which is in fact an assumption on
h
, see (
7.9
) and (
7.7
)) we can apply
Remark
5.8
and we find
\alpha_{0}(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon)
.
