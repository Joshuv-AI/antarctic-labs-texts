Comparative prognostic value of TyG index and TyG-BMI for 90-day all-cause mortality in critically ill older adults with ASCVD: a machine learning analysis of the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Shuangmei Zhao, Qianqian Wang, Xingyu Li, Shiyin Ma, Chucheng Jiao, Guangdong Wang, Huihui Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) are emerging surrogate markers of insulin resistance and metabolic risk. However, their comparative prognostic value in critically ill older adults with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains unclear. We aimed to compare the prognostic performance of the TyG index and TyG-BMI for 90-day all-cause mortality in this high-risk population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients aged ≥ 65 years with ASCVD from the MIMIC-IV database. The TyG index and TyG-BMI were measured at intensive care unit admission, and 90-day all-cause mortality was the primary outcome. Kaplan–Meier analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to assess associations with mortality. Five machine learning algorithms, including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), were adopted to construct mortality risk prediction models, with discrimination assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Additionally, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) approach was used to identify the key predictors of mortality.ResultsA total of 2,368 critically ill older adults with ASCVD were included, and 18.5% died during follow-up. In multivariable Cox analysis, the TyG index was independently associated with higher 90-day mortality risk (HR 1.114, 95% CI 1.014–1.225; P = 0.025), whereas TyG-BMI was not significantly associated with mortality (P = 0.883). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed progressively worse survival across increasing TyG index quartiles. In machine learning models, the TyG index consistently outperformed TyG-BMI. XGBoost showed the best discrimination (AUPRC = 0.455; specificity = 0.912). Adding the TyG index improved AUC from 0.763 to 0.794 (P = 0.005), whereas adding TyG-BMI did not significantly improve AUC (0.783; P = 0.101). SHAP analysis identified the TyG index as the most important metabolic predictor.ConclusionIn elderly critically ill patients with ASCVD, the TyG index showed better prognostic performance than TyG-BMI and was an independent predictor of 90-day mortality. These findings suggest that the TyG index may be a useful and cost-effective biomarker for risk stratification in this high-risk population.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03212-1.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Triglyceride glucose index (TyG), Triglyceride glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI), MIMIC-IV database, Machine learning

Research insight

What is currently known about this topic?

The Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index and TyG-BMI are established surrogates for insulin resistance and are closely linked to chronic cardiometabolic risk. However, their prognostic utility remains poorly defined in the high-acuity Intensive Care Unit (ICU) environment, particularly among elderly patients with Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD). This specific population is characterized by a unique intersection of physiological frailty and acute metabolic derangement, where conventional risk stratification tools may fail to capture the complexity of critical illness.

What is the key research question?

Do the TyG index and TyG-BMI index exhibit comparable prognostic value for predicting 90-day all-cause mortality in elderly critically ill patients with ASCVD?

What is new?

This study demonstrates that the TyG index is a superior and independent predictor of 90-day mortality in elderly ASCVD patients, whereas TyG-BMI lacks independent prognostic significance in this high-acuity context. By leveraging an integrated XGBoost-SHAP framework to capture non-linear metabolic interactions, we identified the TyG index as a core mortality driver. It offers significant incremental value and more accurate risk reclassification than TyG-BMI, effectively neutralizing the "obesity paradox" through precise metabolic profiling.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Standalone TyG signals provide superior risk stratification in geriatric ICUs. Replacing BMI-integrated metrics enables earlier identification of high-mortality risk, facilitating personalized metabolic management to improve survival in elderly ASCVD patients.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), primarily encompassing ischemic heart disease and ischemic stroke, remains a predominant global health burden, affecting over 244 million individuals worldwide [1–3]. This burden is disproportionately borne by metabolically vulnerable populations, whose clinical trajectory is often complicated by pre-existing metabolic derangements. While traditional intensive care research has focused predominantly on acute hemodynamic fluctuations, the role of chronic metabolic dysregulation in determining outcomes for critically ill patients has been relatively under-characterized. However, metabolic dysfunction serves as a cornerstone pathophysiological mechanism in ASCVD, intimately linked to insulin resistance (IR), impaired glucose-lipid homeostasis, and systemic endothelial dysfunction [4–6]. In the geriatric cohort, the clinical landscape of ASCVD is uniquely shaped by the aging-metabolism axis, where chronic low-grade inflammation and cellular senescence synergistically exacerbate vascular vulnerability. Consequently, traditional risk models may fail to capture the complex and dynamic metabolic shifts occurring during acute critical illness in this susceptible population. Existing literature has largely focused on general adult populations, leaving a critical knowledge gap regarding how the “obesity paradox” in the elderly alters the prognostic fidelity of combined metabolic-anthropometric indices.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a robust surrogate for IR and cardiovascular risk stratification [7–9]. However, as a biochemical measure, the TyG index overlooks critical anthropometric dimensions. Consequently, the TyG-BMI index was developed to integrate metabolic and obesity-related risks [10–12]. A pivotal consideration in this context is the “obesity paradox” frequently observed in elderly critically ill cohorts, where a higher BMI appears to confer a paradoxical survival advantage. Since BMI alone is a crude measure that fails to distinguish between lean muscle mass and metabolically active adipose tissue, the integration of TyG with BMI into a single index may potentially resolve this paradox. This integration helps identify individuals who harbor severe insulin resistance that drives adverse outcomes despite a seemingly “protective” BMI. However, it remains unclear whether the inclusion of BMI provides incremental value or introduces noise when assessing acute metabolic stress in older patients with ASCVD. Despite these potential biomarkers, significant gaps remain in our understanding of their comparative prognostic value among vulnerable elderly populations [13–15]. Specifically, in the high-stakes environment of the ICU, metabolic indicators are inherently modulated by acute interventions, including exogenous insulin therapy, nutritional support, and vasoactive agents. Traditional linear frameworks are often insufficient to disentangle these multi-dimensional confounders or to identify potential threshold effects of insulin resistance. To address these limitations, our study leverages advanced machine learning (ML) algorithms coupled with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretability analysis to move beyond “black-box” predictions and provide a mechanistic visualization of how these indices contribute to mortality risk [16, 17]. Accordingly, the present study specifically focuses on an elderly ASCVD cohort to evaluate and compare the prognostic utility of the TyG versus TyG-BMI index. We aim to validate biomarker strategies that optimize clinical management for complex geriatric ASCVD patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective observational cohort study that extracted research data from the publicly accessible Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV v2.2) database. Developed by the Computational Physiology Laboratory of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), MIMIC-IV contains comprehensive de-identified clinical records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center covering 2008 to 2019 [18]. Access was granted after completion of the mandatory training (ID: 65512045). Given that all patient health-related information in MIMIC-IV has been fully de-identified, additional informed consent from patients was not required for this study. More comprehensive details regarding this public database are available on its official website: https://mimic.mit.edu/. We enrolled adult patients diagnosed with ASCVD, defined as a composite of coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, and peripheral arterial disease identified via ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes (Table S1). To refine the phenotype, we prioritized codes associated with atherosclerotic manifestations while excluding non-atherosclerotic etiologies, such as cardioembolic stroke or inflammatory vasculitis, to focus on metabolic-driven vascular damage. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) aged under 65 years; (2) having an ICU stay of less than 24 h; (3) lacking complete data on body weight, height, triglycerides (TG), and first blood glucose measurement upon ICU admission; (4) with multiple hospital admissions; (5) suffering from severe comorbidities including end-stage renal failure, liver cirrhosis, or malignant tumors; and (6) having incomplete survival follow-up or being censored prior to the 90-day landmark.

Data extraction

PostgreSQL (version 13.7.2) was used to extract data from the database, and Structured Query Language (SQL) was applied to simplify the data extraction procedure. Data were collected across five main categories: (1) Demographic characteristics, including age, gender, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI); (2) Clinical severity scores, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS)-II, Oxford Acute Illness Severity Score (OASIS), and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APS)-III; (3) Physiological parameters, including mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, and respiratory rate; (4) Hematological and biochemical indicators, including hemoglobin (Hb), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count, white blood cell count (WBC), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), blood glucose, serum sodium, serum chloride, glycated hemoglobin, blood urea nitrogen, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol, and serum creatinine; (5) Pre-existing comorbidities such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetes, as well as treatments including mechanical ventilation, vasoactive agents, aspirin, and statins. All clinical and laboratory data were retrieved from the initial 24 h of ICU admission. To capture intrinsic metabolic stress, only the earliest laboratory measurements obtained prior to exogenous glucose or insulin administration were utilized.

Calculation of TyG and TyG-BMI

The TyG index was calculated as ln [TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2]. BMI was calculated as body weight (Kg)/height2 (m). TyG-BMI index was determined based on the combination of TyG index and BMI. TyG-BMI index was computed according the equation: TyG index × BMI [19].

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was 90-day all-cause mortality, with 28-day and 180-day mortality as secondary outcomes. For methodological consistency between survival analysis and ML, 90-day mortality was defined as a fixed-window binary endpoint. The cohort was limited to patients with confirmed survival status within this period to mitigate censoring bias and support the use of discriminative metrics, including AUROC and AUPRC, alongside Cox proportional hazards models.

Missing data handling

To ensure findings’ robustness, we implemented a structured missing data protocol. Variables with a missingness rate > 30% were excluded to maintain model stability (Table S2). For the remaining variables (missingness < 5%, Table S3), Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was employed under the Missing at Random (MAR) assumption. Crucially, the imputation process was conducted independently for the training and testing sets to prevent data leakage. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis comparing the imputed dataset with complete-case analysis was performed to validate the results’ consistency (Table S4).

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on baseline TyG and TyG-BMI levels. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Inter-group comparisons utilized t-tests, ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U, or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous data, and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables.

The cumulative incidence of the primary endpoint was estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To facilitate direct comparison of effect sizes between indices, both were standardized and rescaled per SD increment. The robust stability of these models was ensured through a multi-step validation process: the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was rigorously verified using Schoenfeld residuals (Global P > 0.05, Tables S5–S8), influential observations were checked using Dfbeta values, and the functional forms of continuous predictors were justified via restricted cubic splines (RCS) to explore potential nonlinear relationships. To minimize confounding bias beyond univariate screening, the final adjustment set was pre-specified via a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) (Fig. S1) and clinical expertise. To ensure model parsimony, we prioritized variables with established prognostic relevance in ASCVD, excluding clinically redundant markers based on correlation screening. Model I was adjusted for age and gender. Model II was further adjusted for age, gender, MAP, hypertension, diabetes, RBC, WBC, calcium, chloride, creatinine, ventilation, SOFA, and SAPS-II scores, alongside aspirin and statin use. Comprehensive severity-of-illness scores were included to account for systemic physiological derangements and organ dysfunction. Subgroup analyses were conducted across clinically relevant strata, with interaction P-values adjusted via the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure to mitigate Type I errors. Finally, the predictive performance of the two indices was compared using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

It was considered that the two-tailed P value of < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2) alongside Python (3.9.12.).

Machine learning framework and interpretability

To prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned into training (80%) and independent testing (20%) sets prior to feature selection. To address the inherent class imbalance (18.5% mortality), the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied during the training phase. Feature engineering, including LASSO-based selection and 10-fold cross-validation grid search, was conducted exclusively within the training set to optimize hyperparameters for maximizing the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC). The predictive performance of five distinct ML algorithms including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) was evaluated to identify the optimal framework. Model assessment relied on a comprehensive suite of metrics, including ROC-AUC, AUPRC, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), with 95% CIs calculated using 1000-fold bootstrap resampling. To ensure model stability, candidate variables were rigorously screened for mathematical independence, which involved excluding redundant indicators like triglycerides prior to model construction. Beyond statistical discrimination, clinical utility was quantified through Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibration curves using the Brier score. Specifically, a sensitivity analysis compared the incremental predictive value across four configurations: (1) Baseline (conventional variables), (2) Base + TyG, (3) Base + TyG-BMI, and (4) Combined Model. Improvements in AUPRC and net clinical benefit were evaluated to determine the prognostic contribution of each metabolic index for geriatric ASCVD.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to the best-performing model (Base + TyG) to quantify the direction and magnitude of each predictor’s contribution to 90-day mortality risk. This framework ensures the clinical interpretability of the metabolic signals within the decision-making process. Furthermore, the optimized XGBoost framework was integrated into a publicly accessible web-based calculator (available at: https://prediction-tool.streamlit.app/) to facilitate real-world implementation. This tool translates complex ensemble learning outputs into immediate risk estimates for bedside stratification and precision decision-making.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the enrollment of 2,368 eligible geriatric ASCVD patients. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the prognostic consistency between the imputed and complete-case datasets (Table S4). Among the participants, 439 (18.5%) reached the primary endpoint of 90-day mortality. Baseline characteristics stratified by survival status are summarized in Table 1. Compared to survivors, non-survivors were significantly older and presented with a higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease. They also exhibited elevated heart and respiratory rates, reflecting greater physiological derangement and clinical severity upon admission. Intriguingly, survivors demonstrated a higher prevalence of hypertension and hyperlipidemia, while the prevalence of type 2 diabetes did not differ significantly between the two groups. Notably, the baseline TyG index was significantly higher in non-survivors than in survivors (P < 0.05); however, the TyG-BMI index showed only a marginal, non-significant elevation (P = 0.099). Regarding therapeutic interventions, non-survivors required higher utilization of vasopressors but received significantly lower rates of aspirin and statins within the initial 24 h of admission. This divergence likely reflects a combination of greater hemodynamic instability in the non-survivor group and potential disparities in the administration of guideline-directed antiplatelet and lipid-lowering therapies at clinical presentation.

The association between TyG and 90-day all-cause mortality

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a graded decline in survival probability across increasing TyG quartiles (Fig. 2A). This trend remained consistent across 28-day, 90-day, and 180-day follow-ups (Figs. S2A–S3A). The validity of subsequent regression estimates was confirmed as the PH assumption was fully satisfied for both continuous and categorical TyG models (Global P > 0.05; Tables S5–S6). In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, the TyG index emerged as a robust independent predictor of 90-day all-cause mortality (Table 2). When analyzed as a standardized continuous variable, each 1-SD increment in the TyG index was associated with a significantly heightened risk of death in the unadjusted model (HR: 1.377; 95% CI: 1.263–1.502; P < 0.001). This association remained resilient even after exhaustive adjustment for confounders, including disease severity and protective therapies, in the fully saturated Model 2 (HR: 1.114; 95% CI: 1.014–1.225; P = 0.025). Further evaluation via quartile stratification including Q1 (≤ 8.57), Q2 (8.57–8.99), Q3 (8.99–9.47), and Q4 (> 9.47) demonstrated that patients in the highest quartile (Q4) faced the greatest mortality risk. Compared with Q1, the unadjusted HR for Q4 was 2.094 (95% CI: 1.602–2.737), which slightly increased after adjusting for age and gender (Model 1: HR: 2.291; 95% CI: 1.748–3.003). Upon full adjustment in Model 2, the HR for Q4 versus Q1 remained elevated at 1.331 (95% CI: 0.993–1.785). Although the linear trend across quartiles was marginally significant (P for trend = 0.064), the consistent hazard elevation in the highest metabolic stress group underscores the prognostic relevance of the TyG index in high-acuity geriatric ASCVD patients.

Multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis was performed to characterize the dose-response relationship between the TyG index and 90-day mortality. To ensure model stability, four knots were positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the TyG distribution, with the median value serving as the reference point (HR = 1). As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the RCS curves demonstrated a consistent linear association between the TyG index and 90-day mortality, with no evidence of significant non-linear deviation (P for non-linearity = 0.275). The risk of 90-day mortality increased progressively with elevating TyG levels, a trend that remained robust across the 28-day and 180-day survival windows (Figs. S4A–S5A). This sustained linear pattern justifies the modeling of the TyG index as a continuous linear predictor in our primary survival analyses, reinforcing its reliability as a stable prognostic marker across various clinical timeframes. Subgroup analysis (Fig. 4A) confirmed the TyG index as a consistent predictor of 90-day mortality across most clinical strata. Notably, its prognostic value was significantly modulated by aspirin use (P-interaction = 0.002; FDR-adjusted P = 0.022), with a more pronounced association observed in patients receiving aspirin (HR: 1.454; 95% CI: 1.213–1.743) and those without diabetes (HR: 1.299; 95% CI: 1.135–1.487). While interaction trends were noted for hyperlipidemia (P = 0.096) and mechanical ventilation (P = 0.074), these did not survive FDR correction. These findings indicate that the TyG index’s prognostic utility in geriatric ASCVD is context-specific, exhibiting particular sensitivity to baseline glucose metabolism and antiplatelet therapy.

The association between TyG-BMI and 90-day all-cause mortality

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in 90-day survival across TyG-BMI quartiles (Log-rank P = 0.073; Fig. 2B). Although marginal visual separation appeared in 28-day and 180-day exploratory analyses (Figs. S2B–S3B), TyG-BMI failed to establish a definitive stepwise decline in survival probability. Statistical validity was confirmed via Schoenfeld residuals, with all models adhering to the PH assumption (Global P > 0.05; Tables S7–S8). In multivariable Cox analysis (Table 2), each 1-SD increase in the standardized TyG-BMI index showed a modest association with 90-day mortality in the unadjusted model (HR: 1.104; 95% CI: 1.009–1.208; P = 0.032). The emergence of significance in the age- and gender-adjusted Model 1 (HR: 1.404 for Q4 vs. Q1, P = 0.012) suggests a “masking effect” (negative confounding), where demographic factors and baseline nutritional status likely suppressed the crude association. However, this relationship was markedly attenuated and lost independent significance upon full adjustment in the saturated Model 2 (HR: 1.007; 95% CI: 0.918–1.104; P = 0.883). Consistent results were observed in quartile stratification analysis, where the elevated risk in Q4 (> 297.06) vanished after accounting for acute illness severity and comprehensive metabolic confounders (Model 2: HR: 1.051; 95% CI: 0.796–1.387; P = 0.726; P for trend = 0.506). Collectively, these findings indicate that while TyG-BMI captures a component of metabolic risk, its incremental prognostic utility is limited compared to the standalone TyG index in this high-acuity geriatric cohort.

Multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis (Fig. 3B) characterized the dose-response relationship between the TyG-BMI index and 90-day mortality. Consistent with the non-linearity test (P for non-linearity = 0.161), the TyG-BMI index exhibited a linear mortality trajectory. As illustrated by the spline curve, the risk of 90-day all-cause mortality increased progressively with elevating TyG-BMI levels once exceeding the reference threshold. This linear association remained stable across the 28-day and 180-day exploratory follow-ups (Figs. S4B–S5B), justifying the treatment of TyG-BMI as a continuous linear predictor in the primary survival models. Unlike the TyG index, TyG-BMI showed broad prognostic consistency across all clinical strata without significant interaction effects, including hypertension (P = 0.217), diabetes (P = 0.943), and aspirin use (P = 0.965; Fig. 4B). The mechanical ventilation interaction similarly failed FDR correction (P adjusted ​= 0.240). This uniform attenuation in fully adjusted models across sub-cohorts underscores the limited incremental utility of TyG-BMI compared to the standalone TyG index in geriatric ASCVD.

Machine Learning: TyG, TyG-BMI and 90-day mortality

Following LASSO-based feature selection (Fig. 5), 20 robust predictors were identified from the training cohort to capture the high-dimensional complexity of geriatric ASCVD. To address the 18.5% mortality imbalance, we optimized the training phase using SMOTE and hyperparameter tuning. Among the five algorithms (Table 3; Figure S6), XGBoost was identified as the optimal framework due to its superior precision-recall balance and clinical utility. While SVM showed a slightly higher AUC, XGBoost achieved the highest AUPRC (0.455), specificity (0.912), and accuracy (0.810). This performance profile is particularly advantageous for high-stakes clinical decision-making, where minimizing false positives is critical. Consequently, XGBoost was selected for subsequent clinical benchmarking and interpretability analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 6A and B, the XGBoost framework significantly outperformed conventional clinical scoring systems (SOFA, APS-III, SAPS-II, and OASIS) in both ROC-AUC and AUPRC. To rigorously quantify the incremental value of the TyG index, we employed a complementary evaluation framework encompassing both ML discrimination and traditional clinical reclassification metrics. As summarized in Table S9, the integration of TyG into the XGBoost framework led to a significant enhancement in discriminative power. Specifically, the AUC increased from 0.763 to 0.794 (P = 0.005). Notably, while the TyG-BMI index offered a modest gain (AUC = 0.783), it failed to reach statistical significance compared to the baseline model (P = 0.101), further underscoring the superior predictive sensitivity of the standalone TyG index within the ML architecture. To further evaluate the clinical magnitude of this prognostic gain, reclassification metrics were calculated within the multivariable Cox proportional hazards framework (Table 4). The addition of the TyG index significantly enhanced the model’s risk stratification capacity, yielding a continuous NRI of 0.220 (95% CI: 0.142–0.264, P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.042 (95% CI: 0.024–0.064, P < 0.001). In contrast, the reclassification gain provided by the TyG-BMI index was substantially lower (NRI: 0.136; IDI: 0.028). These results provide robust, multi-methodological evidence that the TyG index offers an irreplaceable and statistically superior contribution to mortality risk assessment in elderly patients with ASCVD.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive investigation to compare the prognostic capacity of the TyG and TyG-BMI indices specifically among elderly critically ill patients with ASCVD. Our findings suggest that while both indices are associated with 90-day mortality, the standalone TyG index offers more robust and consistent prognostic value relative to the composite TyG-BMI index within this high-acuity geriatric population. The TyG index maintained a significant independent association in multivariable Cox analysis, and its incremental value was confirmed by significant improvements in NRI (0.220, P < 0.001) and IDI (0.042, P < 0.001). This indicates enhanced risk reclassification by capturing metabolic patterns often overlooked by traditional scoring systems. Furthermore, our integrated ML framework provided complementary insights. XGBoost was selected as the optimal model for its superior AUPRC (0.455), high specificity (0.912), and stability, effectively addressing the 18.5% mortality class imbalance while minimizing clinical false-alarm rates. By isolating feature selection within the training cohort, we mitigated data leakage and ensured generalizability. This methodological rigor, supported by SHAP interpretability, underscores the TyG index as a critical metabolic signal providing a unique prognostic contribution beyond conventional clinical risk factors.

Beyond statistical discrimination, the pragmatic value of our framework was rigorously evaluated through calibration and DCA. While AUC quantifies risk-ranking capacity, DCA provides a more nuanced assessment of clinical utility in real-world scenarios. Our results demonstrated a high degree of alignment between predicted and observed 90-day mortality, which was underscored by a low Brier Score. This robust calibration indicates that the model is a dependable decision-support tool providing a stable foundation for risk assessment in the high-acuity ICU environment. As illustrated in Fig. 7B, the Base + TyG model achieved a superior net benefit over both intervene-all and intervene-none strategies across a wide and clinically relevant threshold range of 0% to 38%. Notably, the standalone TyG-based framework consistently offered higher clinical net gain compared to the TyG-BMI or combined configurations. This suggests that for clinicians establishing intervention thresholds, prioritizing the TyG index over composite adiposity-metabolic indices may yield more efficient risk stratification. By quantifying this benefit, we demonstrate that the TyG index processed through an optimized ML framework functions as a precise instrument for personalizing care rather than merely serving as a statistical correlate. These findings prove that our framework is not only methodologically robust but also a practical tool for identifying high-risk geriatric patients who may benefit from intensified metabolic interventions or specialized hemodynamic monitoring.

The underlying reason for this enhanced performance lies in the algorithm’s capacity to integrate non-linear interactions and high-dimensional synergies between metabolic markers and clinical covariates. While the model utilizes 20 variables to ensure stability, SHAP analysis revealed that predictive weight is highly concentrated. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, clinical interventions (aspirin and vasopressors) and renal function (BUN) were primary drivers, yet the TyG index emerged as the most influential metabolic predictor. Critically, the XGBoost algorithm uncovers “tipping points” in mortality risk that traditional linear models overlook, providing a more granular threshold for intervention. SHAP dependence plots further elucidate that the prognostic contribution of the TyG index is not a static linear effect but is dynamically modulated by the broader clinical context. Unlike traditional Cox regression, the ML algorithm autonomously captures the complex interplay between the TyG index and other critical variables. This dynamic interaction reflects the high biochemical sensitivity of the TyG index, which captures acute metabolic stress more effectively than anthropometric markers. Conversely, the addition of BMI in the TyG-BMI index appears to introduce a “buffering” effect, where the relative stability of body mass across different strata masks the granular metabolic fluctuations detected by the standalone TyG signal. This leads to the observed superiority of the standalone TyG index, whose signal remains robust across subgroups, whereas the TyG-BMI signal is consistently attenuated. This finding provides a novel perspective on the “obesity paradox” in geriatric medicine, suggesting that for risk stratification in the acute phase, direct biochemical markers of insulin resistance are superior to those adjusted for body mass. Crucially, the higher feature importance of the standalone TyG index when evaluated alongside TyG-BMI in a combined model (Figure S10) confirms that its dominance is not an artifact of shared information but reflects a genuinely stronger biological link to adverse outcomes.

Within the field of critical care medicine, the association between BMI and clinical outcomes has been extensively scrutinized [20–22]. Prior investigations have demonstrated an L-shaped relationship between BMI and in-hospital mortality, suggesting that both underweight and obesity harbor distinct risks [23–25]. While numerous cohort studies highlighted the predictive strength of the TyG-BMI index in general populations [26–28], its efficacy shifts significantly in the high-acuity geriatric context [29–32]. Our findings align with observations in chronic heart failure cohorts where the standalone TyG index outperformed TyG-BMI in predicting long-term mortality [33, 34]. By focusing specifically on 90-day mortality, our study clarifies that in the immediate period following ICU admission, biochemical surrogates of insulin resistance such as the TyG index [35–37] offer a more sensitive prognostic signal than anthropometric-integrated indices in geriatric patients with ASCVD.

The relative superiority of the TyG index may be explained by the unique physiological shift characteristic of the elderly, namely the “obesity paradox” and the high prevalence of sarcopenic obesity. In geriatric patients with ASCVD, BMI is a notoriously crude measure that fails to distinguish between protective muscle mass, which serves as a nutritional reserve, and deleterious visceral adiposity, which acts as a primary driver of systemic inflammation [38, 39]. Furthermore, during critical illness, ICU-based BMI measurements are frequently confounded by acute fluid shifts, aggressive volume resuscitation, and generalized tissue edema. These factors collectively lead to anthropometric distortion, rendering weight-based metrics less reliable. By decoupling the metabolic signal from these unstable physical fluctuations, the standalone TyG index provides a more direct reflection of acute metabolic stress. We propose that the TyG index acts as a “metabolic quality filter” by identifying metabolically vulnerable individuals whose systemic toxicity of insulin resistance overrides the purported structural or energy-reserve benefits of a higher body mass [40–42]. Mechanistically, elevated TyG levels in the acute phase of ASCVD may reflect a state of metabolic exhaustion. In this state, excessive lipid accumulation and hyperglycemia synergistically trigger oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, which subsequently exacerbate plaque instability and myocardial strain [43–45]. Consequently, our findings suggest that when a high BMI is accompanied by significant lipid and glucose dysregulation, the pro-inflammatory sequelae of insulin resistance may neutralize the protective effects associated with the “obesity paradox”. This renders patients high-risk despite an apparently protective body weight. Our sensitivity analysis further validates this prioritization, as the standalone TyG index model consistently outperformed both the TyG-BMI and combined configurations in precision-recall evaluations. This reinforces the clinical observation that in high-acuity geriatric settings, integrating BMI into the metabolic signal may introduce predictive noise rather than clarity, likely due to the highly unstable nature of body mass measurements during acute catabolism. Therefore, the TyG index represents a sophisticated addition to current predictive models, facilitating individualized risk stratification in geriatric critical care.

However, several limitations of this study merit consideration. Firstly, the single-center and retrospective nature of the MIMIC-IV database implies that while we employed rigorous multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses to mitigate bias, the geographic and institutional generalizability of our findings warrants further multi-center prospective validation. Regarding laboratory measurements, while strict ‘fasting’ status is unrecorded in the ICU, we used early admission measurements prior to insulin or glucose interventions to minimize bias. In this context, the TyG index reflects stress-induced insulin resistance and acute metabolic response. This “real-world” snapshot provides a pragmatic and time-sensitive baseline for risk stratification at the bedside. In addition, although we adjusted for comprehensive severity scores like SOFA and SAPS-II that integrate complex physiological derangements and circulatory support requirements, some granular treatment details such as specific revascularization procedures or precise nutritional protocols remained unmeasured. Importantly, the strong independent association maintained by the TyG index, even after adjusting for these major clinical drivers, underscores its unique prognostic contribution. Finally, while our 90-day follow-up focuses on the critical short-term window, it establishes a foundation for future research to evaluate the long-term predictive stability of these metabolic indices.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the standalone TyG index outperforms the TyG-BMI index as a superior independent determinant of 90-day mortality in elderly critically ill patients with ASCVD. Our findings demonstrate that while the TyG index maintains a robust and consistent association with adverse outcomes, the prognostic independence of the TyG-BMI index is fundamentally attenuated in this specific high-acuity cohort. This suggests that biochemical metabolic stress, rather than anthropometric adiposity, serves as the primary driver of mortality in the geriatric ICU population. Consequently, the TyG index represents a precise and streamlined biomarker that facilitates the advancement of individualized metabolic risk stratification, providing a pragmatic tool for precision medicine in geriatric critical care.
