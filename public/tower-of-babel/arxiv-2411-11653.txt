Wall laws for viscous flows in 3D randomly rough pipes: optimal convergence rates and stochastic integrability
Mitsuo Higaki, Yulong Lu, Jinping Zhuge

Abstract




Abstract.

This paper is concerned with effective approximations and wall laws of viscous laminar flows in 3D pipes with randomly rough boundaries. The random roughness is characterized by the boundary oscillation scale
\varepsilon\ll 1
and a probability space with ergodicity quantified by functional inequalities. The results in this paper generalize the previous work for 2D channel flows with random Lipschitz boundaries to 3D pipe flows with random boundaries of John type. Moreover, we establish the optimal convergence rates and substantially improve the stochastic integrability obtained in the previous studies. Additionally, we prove a refined version of the Poiseuille’s law in 3D pipes with random boundaries, which seems unaddressed in the literature. Our systematic approach combines several classical and recent ideas (particularly from homogenization theory), including the Saint-Venant’s principle for pipe flows, the large-scale regularity theory over rough boundaries, and applications of functional inequalities.

Keywords.
Stationary Navier-Stokes system, Navier’s wall laws, randomly rough pipes, functional inequalities, large-scale regularity

Contents

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

This paper is devoted to the effective approximation of viscous flows, governed by the stationary Navier-Stokes system, in 3D pipes with randomly rough boundaries. Understanding the effect on fluid induced by boundary roughness remains a core challenge in fluid dynamics even in the laminar regime when the Reynolds number of the flow is sufficiently small. Indeed, laminar flows with rough boundaries discussed in this paper naturally appear in biomechanics (e.g., blood flow in vessels) and engineering (e.g., corroded or corrugated pipes). If the roughness is relatively mild, one may address such problems by finding analytic solutions
[
CS72
,
SJZ01
,
Wan06
,
MJ16
]
or by numerical computations
[
FN77
,
DS79
,
NOK84
,
MF13
,
LLS19
]
, albeit not exhaustively.

These direct approaches are unavailable or require extensive computational resources if the boundaries are more complicated and wildly oscillating at small scales. In the presence of such irregularities, it becomes impractical to record the fluid dynamics meticulously; instead, one should focus on understanding the average fluid configuration. Wall laws or effective boundary conditions serve this purpose. Here we quote a definition from the pioneering work by Jäger-Mikelić
[
JM01
]
: “In general, the approach of replacing the no-slip condition at rough boundaries with the non-penetration condition plus a relation between the tangential velocity and the shear stress, is called the
wall laws
.” Namely, the rough boundary is replaced by an artificially smoothed one, and an effective boundary condition is imposed to represent the averaged effect of the roughness. Typically, the effective boundary condition for laminar flows is known to be Navier-type slip, and thus called Navier’s wall law.

As a representative work from the engineering side, Nikuradse
[
Nik33
]
empirically studied the wall laws by detailed experiments for turbulent flows; see
[
KSCT05
,
FS14
,
CHSF21
]
for recent progress in this direction. Achdou-Pironneau-Valentin
[
APV98
]
verified by numerical simulation the Navier’s wall laws for laminar flows, derived by two-scale asymptotic expansion, for a periodic boundary with roughness in scales
\varepsilon\ll 1
; see also
[
AP95
,
AMPV98
]
. Then the mathematically rigorous justification of the Navier’s wall laws is obtained by Jäger-Mikelić for 2D periodic channels in
[
JM01
]
and for 3D slabs between periodic plates in
[
JM03
]
, with the optimal
O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
convergence rates, i.e., the error bounds in
L^{2}
space between the real solution and the effective approximation. In most physically relevant scenarios, however, the boundaries are self-similar with non-periodic or random structures. In a series of papers
[
BGV08
,
GV09
,
GVM10
]
, Gérard-Varet with collaborators studied the Navier’s wall laws in 2D channels with randomly oscillating boundaries. The seminal work by Basson–Gérard-Varet
[
BGV08
]
established the Navier’s wall law for rough boundaries generated by stationary random processes and obtained suboptimal convergence rates
o(\varepsilon)
. The near-optimal convergence rate
O(\varepsilon^{3/2}|\log\varepsilon|^{1/2})
was obtained by
[
GV09
]
under the assumption of finite range of dependence on the random boundaries. Finally, Gérard-Varet–Masmoudi
[
GVM10
]
proved the optimal convergence rates
O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
for quasi-periodic boundaries satisfying a Diophantine condition.

In summary, the previous mathematical study of wall laws mainly targets laminar flows in 2D channels or 3D slabs, which are less applicable compared to those in 3D pipes. Additionally, all of these studies assume that the boundaries are at least locally Lipschitz. The objective of this paper is to develop a systematic approach to study wall laws and quantitative convergence rates for laminar flows in 3D pipes with random roughness. We will also relax the boundary regularity from local Lipschitz boundaries to more generalized boundaries of John type, which are not necessarily local graphs of functions and allow for fractals and cusps. Moreover, we will substantially improve the stochastic integrability under certain ergodicity conditions.

1.2
Main results

We first define the infinite pipes with rough boundaries.
Let
D_{r}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~|~x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}<r^{2}\}
denote an infinite cylinder with radius
r>0
. Then we consider a rough infinite cylinder (connected open set)
D^{\varepsilon}
satisfying the thickness condition

D_{1}\subset D^{\varepsilon}\subset D_{1+\varepsilon}.

(1.1)

Here
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
is the thickness of the rough boundary of
D^{\varepsilon}
, as well as the wavelength of the oscillation. Due to this set-up, we often write
D_{1}
as
D^{0}
without confusion. In view of the geometry, it is convenient to use the cylindrical coordinates
(x_{1},r,\theta)
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
r\cos\theta\\
r\sin\theta\end{bmatrix},\quad(x_{1},r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\times[0,2\pi).

(1.2)

For example, the cylinder
D^{0}
can be simply expressed as
D^{0}=\{r<1\}
. For a specific point
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, we use
(x_{1},r_{x},\theta_{x})
to denote its cylindrical coordinates. Moreover, given
a\geq\varepsilon
and
x\in\partial D^{0}
, let
S_{a}(x)
be the cylindrical cube defined by

\displaystyle S_{a}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~|~|y_{1}-x_{1}|<a,\mkern 9.0mu|\theta_{y}-\theta_{x}|<a,\mkern 9.0mu|r_{y}-r_{x}|<a\}.

(1.3)

Define
S_{a,+}(x)=D^{\varepsilon}\cap S_{a}(x)
.

The mathematical study of fluids occupying
D^{\varepsilon}
requires some minimal regularity on the boundary known as John domains
[
Joh61
]
for which the divergence equation is solvable in
L^{2}
space
[
ADM06
]
. We first recall the definition of the usual bounded John domains.

Definition 1.1

Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open bounded set and
\tilde{x}\in\omega
. We say that
\omega
is a John domain
(or a domain with boundary of John type)
with respect to the center
\tilde{x}
and with constant
L>0
if for any
y\in\omega
, there exists a Lipschitz mapping
\rho:[0,|y-\tilde{x}|]\to\omega
with Lipschitz constant
L
, such that
\rho(0)=y,\rho(|y-\tilde{x}|)=\tilde{x}
and
{\rm dist}(\rho(t),\partial\omega)\geq t/L
for all
t\in[0,|y-\tilde{x}|]
.

Examples of John domains are: Lipschitz domains, one-sided NTA domains, domains with inward cusps or certain fractals such as Koch’s snowflake. Notice that domains with outward cusps are not John domains.

The following definition is a variant of the unbounded John domains in
[
HPZ24
,
HZ23
]
.

Definition 1.2

Let
D^{\varepsilon}
be an unbounded domain satisfying (
1.1
).
We say that
D^{\varepsilon}
is a
John domain
above
\varepsilon
-scale with constants
(L,K)
if for any
x\in\partial D^{0}
and any
R\in[\varepsilon,1]
, there exists a bounded John domain
S^{*}_{R}(x)
with respect to the center
x_{R}\in D^{0}
and with constant
L>0

according to Definition
1.1

such that

S_{R,+}(x)\subset S^{*}_{R}(x)\subset S_{KR,+}(x),

(1.4)

where
S_{R,+}(x)=D^{\varepsilon}\cap S_{R}(x)
.

The constants
(L,K)
do not play roles in our proof (except for the dependence of constant
C
) and we may assume
K=2
without loss of generality:
(L,K)=(L,2)
.

Next we define the flux in John domains
D^{\varepsilon}
. Let
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}=\partial D^{\varepsilon}
. Let
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
. Let
D^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}=D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{a<x_{1}<b\}
and
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}=\Gamma^{\varepsilon}\cap\{a<x_{1}<b\}
. Assume that
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in H^{1}(D^{\varepsilon}_{a,b})^{3}
satisfies
\nabla\cdot u=0
in
D^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}
and
u=0
on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}
(in the sense of trace). We say that the
flux of
u
in
D^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}
is
\phi
if it holds that, for any
\varphi(x_{1})\in C_{0}^{\infty}([a,b])
,

\int_{D^{\varepsilon}_{a,b}}u_{1}(x)\varphi(x_{1})\,{\rm d}x=\phi\int_{a}^{b}\varphi(x_{1})\,{\rm d}x_{1}.

(1.5)

If this is the case, we write
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u]=\phi
.
Alternatively, one can extend
u
by zero to a slightly larger cylinder (e.g. to
D_{1+\varepsilon}
) so that the flux can be define in the usual way in terms of the trace on
\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{x_{1}=t\}
. In this extension, the divergence-free condition is preserved. Indeed, we can show that, for any
t\in[a,b]
,

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u]=\int_{\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}}e_{1}\cdot u\,{\rm d}\sigma=\int_{\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}}u_{1}\,{\rm d}\sigma,

(1.6)

where
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
and
\,{\rm d}\sigma
is the surface measure. Similarly, for
u\in H^{1}(D^{0}_{a,b})^{3}
with
\nabla\cdot u=0
in
D^{0}_{a,b}
and
u\cdot\nu=0
on
\Gamma^{0}_{a,b}
(where
\nu
is the inward normal vector on
\Gamma^{0}_{a,b}
), we say that the flux of
u
in
D^{0}_{a,b}
is
\phi
if it holds that, for any
t\in[a,b]
,

\Phi^{0}[u]=\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{0}}e_{1}\cdot u\,{\rm d}\sigma=\int_{\Sigma_{t}^{0}}u_{1}\,{\rm d}\sigma.

(1.7)

Now we introduce the stationary Navier-Stokes system in a rough cylinder
D^{\varepsilon}
with the no-slip boundary condition on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}=\partial D^{\varepsilon}
.
Consider

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}+\nabla p^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot u^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}]=\phi,\\
u^{\varepsilon}=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(NS)

Since we are interested in the laminar regime, we have assumed that the viscosity is equal to
1
and assume that the flux
\phi
is small. The existence and uniqueness of solutions for (
NS
), known as the Leray’s problem, can be established for small flux and remains to be an open question without smallness; see Section
2.1
. In view of the formal limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, it is naturally expected that the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of (
NS
) is approximated well by the solution of the Navier-Stokes system in the smooth pipe
D^{0}
with the no-slip condition on
\Gamma^{0}:=\partial D^{0}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u^{0}+u^{0}\cdot\nabla u^{0}+\nabla p^{0}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{0},\\
\nabla\cdot u^{0}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{0},\\
\Phi^{0}[u^{0}]=\phi,\\
u^{0}=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{0}.\end{array}\right.

(1.8)

This approach to find approximations is called the Dirichlet’s wall law. The unique bounded solution of (
1.8
) is, under the smallness on
\phi
, the famous Hagen-Poiseuille flow in a pipe

u^{0}=\frac{2\phi}{\pi}(1-r^{2})e_{1},\qquad p^{0}=-\frac{8\phi}{\pi}x_{1}.

(1.9)

The Hagen-Poiseuille flow
u^{0}
does not decay along the
x_{1}
-axis. Since we are considering the solutions of (
NS
) that are near
u^{0}
in appropriate topologies, we introduce

L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon}):=\Big\{f\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(D^{\varepsilon})~\Big|~\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})}:=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(D^{\varepsilon}_{k,k+1})}<\infty\Big\}.

(1.10)

It is not hard to define the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})
in a similar manner.

Without any additional self-similar structure on the rough boundary
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
, we have the following approximation estimate that verifies the Dirichlet’s wall law rigorously.

Theorem A

Assume that
D^{\varepsilon}
is a John domain satisfying (
1.1
).
There exists sufficiently small
\phi_{0}>0
such that for all
|\phi|<\phi_{0}
, the system (
NS
) has a unique solution
u^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
such that

\|u^{\varepsilon}-u^{0}\|_{L^{2}_{{\rm uloc}}(D^{\varepsilon})}\leq C|\phi|\varepsilon,\qquad\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-\nabla u^{0}\|_{L^{2}_{{\rm uloc}}(D^{\varepsilon})}\leq C|\phi|\varepsilon^{1/2},

(1.11)

where
u^{0}
is given by (
1.9
) and the constant
C
is independent of
\phi,\varepsilon
.

Observe that theorem
A
is established under the deterministic setting independent of the particular element
D^{\varepsilon}
with the exception of the John domain assumption. In order to get a higher-order approximation estimate or convergence rate, the self-similar structure of the boundaries of
D^{\varepsilon}
at small scales must be taken into account. In this paper, we are focusing on randomly rough pipes under certain stationary and ergodic assumptions.

Let
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
be the sample space of rough infinite cylinders
D^{\varepsilon}
satisfying the thickness condition (
1.1
) and the John domain condition in Definition
1.2
with uniform constants
(L,K)=(L,2)
. Two elements
D^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}
are regarded as the same element if and only if
D^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
be the maximal
\sigma
-algebra generated by the elements of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. We further assume that there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\to[0,1]
satisfying the following property:
\mathbb{P}
is stationary with respect to a group of transformations consisting of
\theta
-rotations and
x_{1}
-translations, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}
satisfies, for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\circ R_{\theta}=\mathbb{P}\circ T_{t}=\mathbb{P},

(1.12)

where

R_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
0&\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\qquad T_{t}D^{\varepsilon}=(t,0,0)+D^{\varepsilon}.

(1.13)

Under the above assumptions,
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space. Roughly, the random elements
D^{\varepsilon}
, or their boundaries
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
, can be viewed as homogeneous random fields on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
which are stationary with respect to
\theta
-rotations and
x_{1}
-translations. For the corresponding homogeneous random field for 2D channels, we refer to
[
BGV08
,
GV09
]
.

To establish a higher-order convergence rate that improves Theorem
A
, we need to assume certain quantitative ergodicity for random elements (fields) in
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, it is assumed in Gérard-Varet
[
GV09
]
that random rough boundaries have no correlation at large distances, interpreted as a version of finite range of dependence assumption, leading to almost sharp convergence rate under
L^{2}
stochastic integrability. In this paper, inspired by stochastic homogenization theory recently advanced by Gloria-Otto (e.g.
[
GO17
]
), we introduce the functional inequalities on random rough boundaries, rather than on coefficients as is typically done in homogenization theory. Such quantitative ergodicity eventually yields sharp convergence rates with exponential or Gaussian stochastic integrability.

We consider two functional inequalities as displayed in Definition
1.3
below, in the similar spirit as
[
GO17
, Definition 2.2]
for the first one and as
[
DG20b
, Definition 2.1]
for the second one. Let
X
be a scalar or vector-valued random variable defined on the probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
. Then we define the oscillation of a random variable
X
with respect to
D^{\varepsilon}
restricted to the cube
S_{a}(z)
with radius
a\geq\varepsilon
and center
z\in\Gamma:=\Gamma^{0}
by

\displaystyle\Big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{a}(z)}}{\osc}X\Big)(D^{\varepsilon})

(1.14)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{|X(\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon})-X(\widehat{D}^{\varepsilon})|~\bigg|~\begin{array}[]{l}\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon},\widehat{D}^{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{\varepsilon},\\
\partial\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}=\partial\widehat{D}^{\varepsilon}=\partial D^{\varepsilon}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus S_{a}(z)\end{array}\bigg\}.

Definition 1.3

Let
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
be the probability space defined above.

(1)

We say that
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
satisfies the spectral gap inequality if there exists
C>0
such that for any random variable
X
on
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
, we have

\var[X]\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X\big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg].

(SG)

(2)

We say that
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
satisfies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality if there exists
C>0
such that for any random variable
X
on
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
, we have

{\rm Ent}[X]\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X\big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg],

(LSI)

where

{\rm Ent}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}\log X^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\log\mathbb{E}[X^{2}].

(1.15)

Remark 1.4

The functional inequalities in probability spaces are widely used in various applications, not limited to homogenization theory. Concrete examples satisfying (
SG
) or (
LSI
) will be constructed by Poisson point process or Bernoulli process in Appendix
B
.

Our second main theorem concerns the Navier’s wall law, which provides a higher-order approximation for the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of (
NS
). In fact, by a formal calculation with the help of boundary layers (see Section
1.3
and Section
3
) and under the stationarity assumption, the higher-order approximation of
(u^{\varepsilon},p^{\varepsilon})
takes the form of

u^{\rm N}=u^{0}+\varepsilon\alpha u^{1},\qquad p^{\rm N}=p^{0}+\varepsilon\alpha p^{1},

(1.16)

where
u^{0}
is the Hagen-Poiseuille flow in (
1.9
) and

u^{1}=-\frac{2\phi}{\pi}(1-2r^{2})e_{1}\qquad p^{1}=\frac{16\phi}{\pi}x_{1}.

(1.17)

Here the parameter
\alpha
is determined by the expectation of the boundary layers and therefore depends only on the probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
with the stationarity assumption; see Section
3.3
for details. In particular, it reflects a physical property of the rough pipe and is independent of the flux
\phi
.

Then it is straightforward to verify that
(u^{\rm N},p^{\rm N})
in (
1.16
) satisfies the Navier-Stokes system in the smooth pipe
D^{0}
with a Navier’s slip condition on
\Gamma^{0}=\partial D^{0}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u^{\rm N}+u^{\rm N}\cdot\nabla u^{\rm N}+\nabla p^{\rm N}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{0},\\
\nabla\cdot u^{\rm N}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{0},\\
\Phi^{0}[u^{\rm N}]=\phi,&\\
(I-\nu\otimes\nu)u^{\rm N}=\lambda^{\varepsilon}(I-\nu\otimes\nu)\mathbb{D}(u^{\rm N})\nu&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{0},\\
u^{\rm N}\cdot\nu=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{0}.\end{array}\right.

(1.18)

Here
\nu
is the inward normal vector on
\Gamma^{0}
and
I-\nu\otimes\nu
is the tangential projection. Moreover,

\mathbb{D}(u)=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{T})

is the strain rate tensor. In (
1.18
), the relationship between
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
and
\alpha
is given by

\lambda^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{1-2\alpha\varepsilon}.

(1.19)

The system (
1.18
) will be called the
effective
Navier-Stokes system. The last two equations in (
1.18
) on the boundary
\Gamma^{0}
is called the Navier’s slip condition. The fourth equation in (
1.18
) says that the tangential velocity is proportional to the tangential stress on the boundary, where the constant
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
is called the slip length and
1/\lambda^{\varepsilon}
is called the friction factor. The last equation in (
1.18
) is the non-penetration condition. The Navier’s slip condition recovers the no-slip or full-slip condition for
\lambda^{\varepsilon}=0
or
\lambda^{\varepsilon}=\infty
, respectively. Observe that the parameters
\phi,\lambda^{\varepsilon}
uniquely determine the effective Navier-Stokes system. Moreover, the solution (
1.16
) is unique in
L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{0})^{3}
if one imposes the smallness of
\phi,\varepsilon
.

The theorem below verifies the Navier’s wall law rigorously under functional inequalities and establishes sharp convergence rates. Throughout this paper, we let, for any
x\in D^{0}
,

\delta=\delta(x):={\rm dist}(x,\Gamma^{0})=1-r_{x}.

Theorem B

Let the probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
satisfy the stationary assumption. There exists sufficiently small
\phi_{0}>0
such that for all
|\phi|<\phi_{0}
, the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of (
NS
) in
H^{1}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
and the solution
u^{\rm N}
of (
1.18
) given by (
1.16
) satisfy the following:

(1)

Under the spectral gap inequality (
SG
), we have

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c\|u^{\varepsilon}-u^{{\rm N}}\|_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}}{|\phi|\varepsilon^{3/2}}\Big)\Big]\leq C,

(1.20)

and

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c\|\delta(\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-\nabla u^{{\rm N}})\|_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}}{|\phi|\varepsilon^{3/2}}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

(1.21)

(2)

Under the logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
LSI
), we have

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c\|u^{\varepsilon}-u^{{\rm N}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}}{|\phi|^{2}\varepsilon^{3}}\Big)\Big]\leq C,

(1.22)

and

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c\|\delta(\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-\nabla u^{{\rm N}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}}{|\phi|^{2}\varepsilon^{3}}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

(1.23)

The constants
c,C>0
are independent of
\phi,\varepsilon
.

Remark 1.5

The solution
u^{\rm N}
of the effective Navier-Stokes system (
1.18
) is actually defined only by using the stationarity assumption. However, this “predicted” approximation of
u^{\varepsilon}
may not provide better convergence rates than the ones in Theorem
A
if no ergodicity assumptions are made on the probability space. The general validity of the Navier’s wall law cannot be expected due to the counterexamples in
[
GVM10
, Section 3]
.

Let us briefly discuss the novelty of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
. First, compared with the previous work
[
BGV08
,
GV09
]
, these two theorems generalize the results from 2D channels to 3D pipes with the John domain assumption and establish the Navier’s wall law on the cylinder. We point out that the approaches in
[
BGV08
,
GVM10
]
are not applicable to our domains with extreme roughness, which is explained more in detail in Section
1.3
.
Second, in Theorem
B
, we establish the optimal convergence rates
O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
(see Remark
3.7
) with exponential or Gaussian stochastic integrability under (
SG
) or (
LSI
) respectively, including the new convergence rate for gradient. While in
[
GV09
]
, the near-optimal convergence rate
O(\varepsilon^{3/2}|\log\varepsilon|^{1/2})
was obtained with
L^{2}
stochastic integrability under the assumption of finite range of dependence. It remains to be an interesting question whether one can improve the stochastic integrability with the assumption of finite range of dependence, just as in stochastic homogenization for elliptic equations
[
AS16
,
AKM19
,
AK22
]
.

Our last main theorem is an approximate Poiseuille’s law, illustrating the relationship between the flux, the pressure drop, and the slip length (or fraction factor) appearing in the effective system (
1.18
). Let us recall from e.g.
[
SS93
,
MOHR12
]
that the well-known Poiseuille’s law in a circular pipe for laminar flows under no-slip condition is

\Delta_{\ell}p:=p(0)-p(\ell e_{1})=\frac{8\mu\ell\phi}{\pi R^{4}},

(1.24)

where
\Delta_{\ell}p
is the so-called pressure drop,
\ell
is the distance at which the pressure drop is measured,
\mu
is the dynamic viscosity,
\phi
is the flux and

is the radius of the pipe. Even in rough domains, the above Poiseuille’s law remains accurate up to an error of
O(\varepsilon)
thanks to Theorem

\lambda^{\varepsilon}
in the effective system (

\lambda^{\varepsilon}
, determined by the expectation of the boundary layers (see Section

), plays the same role as the homogenized coefficients in homogenization theory
[

]
, whose computation in practice from a realization of random coefficients is a classical challenge; see e.g.
[
Mou19

Fis19
,
CJOX24
]
for some recent developments. As far as the authors are aware, a robust algorithm for computing the slip length in the presence of random boundaries has not been developed in the previous mathematical studies.

The following theorem answers the above two questions.

Theorem C

(Poiseuille’s law for rough pipes)

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
B
, for any
\ell\in(0,1]
, we have:

(1)

Under the spectral gap inequality (

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c|\Delta_{\ell}p^{\varepsilon}-(8\phi\ell/\pi)(1-2\varepsilon\alpha)|}{|\phi|\varepsilon^{3/2}}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

(1.25)

(2)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{c|\Delta_{\ell}p^{\varepsilon}-(8\phi\ell/\pi)(1-2\varepsilon\alpha)|^{2}}{|\phi|^{2}\varepsilon^{3}}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

The constants

The Poiseuille’s law for a rough pipe in Theorem

1.3
Outline of proofs

A novel and systematic approach is developed in this paper to study the wall laws, combining several concepts originating from the recent progress in stochastic homogenization theory. Our proofs fundamentally depend on the quantitative analysis of boundary layers which are understood as correctors in the context of homogenization theory. Precisely, the boundary layers are the solutions of linear Stokes system with an oscillating boundary data

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}+\nabla\pi^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=(1-r^{2})e_{1}&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(1.27)

Notice that the solution to this system is not unique as the flux is allowed to be arbitrary. For our application, we “normalize” the boundary layers through

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[(1-r^{2})e_{1}-v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=\frac{\pi}{2}.

(1.28)

The normalized boundary layer is carefully constructed so that the approximation
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}
in (
1.29
) below has favorable properties in the proofs. It is emphasized that our boundary layers are still defined in the whole domain
D^{\varepsilon}
without any rescaling due to the geometry of
D^{\varepsilon}
. This is quite different from the boundary layers in 2D channels considered by
[
BGV08
,
GV09
,
GVM10
]
, which are usually defined in upper half-planes after rescaling.

Boundary layers are commonly employed as correctors used to eliminate discrepancies caused by irregular boundaries. In the system (
1.27
), the boundary data is of
O(\varepsilon)
on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
due to the condition (
1.1
) and does not decay along the
x_{1}
-axis. Thus the solutions of (
1.27
) are supposed to have size of
O(\varepsilon)
locally and do not decay as
x_{1}
goes to infinity.
Under such constraints, obtaining the solutions of (
1.27
) is a challenging problem due to the unboundedness and the extreme roughness of the domain
D^{\varepsilon}
.

Actually, the existence of boundary layers is one of the major questions that has been addressed in
[
GVM10
]
where the well-posedness of boundary layer systems over bumpy half-space was established for arbitrary Lipschitz boundaries. This Lipschitz regularity assumption was recently relaxed to the John domains in
[
HPZ24
]
by using the large-scale Lipschitz estimate (also see
[
KP15
,
KP18
,
HP20
,
Zhu21
,
HZ23
]
for closely related developments), which is a key idea originating from homogenization theory. This paper adopts the approach developed in
[
HPZ24
]
to construct and estimate the boundary layers in rough pipes of John type.

The essential tool to tackle the difficulties in solving (
1.27
) is the Green’s function for the Stokes system. The Green’s function method is advantageous due to its broad applicability; however, it requires detailed and precise analysis when one deals with quantitative estimates. We thus combine the Saint-Venant’s principle and the large-scale Lipschitz estimate near the rough boundary at a scale no less than
\varepsilon
. This combination enables us obtain both exponential decay of the Green’s function in the direction of axis and the sharp large-scale estimates. These two results are crucial for constructing the solution of (
1.27
) and for deriving its deterministic size estimates in Proposition
3.1
, as well as for showing decay of correlation for locally perturbed boundaries in Proposition
3.5
. Let us reiterate that our approach of construction and estimates is established for the boundaries of John type which could be fractals, and can be readily adapted to rough domains with different geometry.

Both in the proofs of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B

u^{\varepsilon}

1.28

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}]=\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}]=\phi,\qquad u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}=u^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\mbox{on}\mkern 9.0mu\Gamma^{\varepsilon}.

Hence, given the size estimates of the boundary layers,

2.1
Saint-Venant’s principle

The Saint-Venant’s principle, as applied to our current situation, states that in the case of vanishing flux, the Stokes flow exhibiting mild growth at infinity must exponentially decay. This classical property has been well-understood in straight cylinders and more general smooth unbounded domains with outlets at infinity, as documented in the monograph
[
Gal11
]
. In this section, we will extend the Saint-Venant’s principle to the bumpy John domain
D^{\varepsilon}
and present the proofs that highlights how to effectively handle the boundary roughness of John type. It is crucial for the uniqueness of solutions for the Stokes system in
D^{\varepsilon}
in suitable function spaces, as well as the exponential decay of the Green’s function in Section
2.3
. We also prove the Saint-Venant’s principle to the Navier-Stokes system in

Recall from the introduction that for

Corollary 2.6

u_{0}\in H^{1}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}

(2.17)

(2.18)

Proof.

\tau_{0}
to be the same constant as in Theorem

2.2
Large-scale boundary regularity

The regularity estimates play important roles in constructing and estimating the Green’s function and boundary layers. In
[
GV09
]
, to obtain the quantitative sensitivity estimate of the boundary layers with respect to the boundary perturbation, Gérard-Varet established the uniform Hölder estimate for the Green’s function over bumpy
C^{1}
boundaries essentially by the compactness method originating from Avellaneda-Lin
[
AL87
]
. The uniform Lipschitz estimate via compactness method over bumpy boundaries were developed in
[
KP15
,
KP18
]
for elliptic equations in homogenization setting and
[
HP20
]
for steady Navier-Stokes system. In a seires of papers
[
Zhu21
,
GZ22
,
HZ23
,
HPZ24
]
, a quantitative method originating from
[
AS16
]
in elliptic homogenization was generalized to obtain the large-scale Lipschitz estimate and even higher order regularity over oscillating boundaries with minimal regularity assumption on the boundaries (which allows for fractals and cusps). Particularly in
[
HPZ24
]
, the large-scale boundary Lipschitz estimate was established for the Navier-Stokes system (including Stokes system) over a rough hyperplane of John type. The large-scale Lipschitz estimate in
[
HPZ24
]
can be generalized to the case of bumpy cylinders considered in this paper in the same spirit of
[
Zhu21
,
GZ22
]
for curved boundaries. Recall that we do not need the boundary layers in the proof of large-scale Lipschitz estimate.

In principle, the regularity of solutions exhibits different behaviors distinguished between interior points and points close to the rough boundary. Recall that
r_{x}=(x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2})^{1/2}
is the distance from
x
to the
x_{1}
-axis. Recall that
D_{1}\subset D^{\varepsilon}\subset D_{1+\varepsilon}
and assume
B_{r,+}(x)=D^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{r}(x)
. Suppose that
(u,p)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ B_{1,+}(x),\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ B_{1,+}(x),\\
u=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{1}(x).\end{array}\right.

(2.39)

Then we describe two cases:

•

For
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-\varepsilon
, since the point
x
is very close to the boundary, we can only show a large-scale regularity for the solutions of the Stokes system. That is, for any
\varepsilon<r<1/2
, we have

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}|\nabla u|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}+\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}|p-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1/2,+}(x)}p|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1,+}(x)}|\nabla u|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

(2.40)

where

is independent of
\varepsilon
and

.

•

For

with

, we can apply a pointwise interior estimate followed by a large-scale estimate in the first case if the region intersects with the boundary to get an estimate like

(2.41)

In the above estimates, it does not matter how

intersects the boundary. We refer to

, Theorem A]
for a proof of (

). Some straightforward modifications are needed as the system (

2.3
Green’s function of Stokes system

One of the main ingredients in this paper is the Green’s function.
Formally, the Green’s function (matrix-valued) is the solution of, denoting by ,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta G(\cdot,y)+\nabla\Pi(\cdot,y)=\delta_{y}I&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot G(\cdot,y)=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[G(\cdot,y)]=0,&\\
G(\cdot,y)=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}\end{array}\right.

(2.42)

for each
y\in D^{\varepsilon}
, where
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac delta supported at
y
. The flux condition

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[G(\cdot,y)]=\int_{\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}}e_{1}\cdot G(\cdot,y)=0

ensures the uniqueness. Otherwise, even in the case of
D^{0}
, one can add arbitrary Hagen-Poiseuille flow (
1.9
) to the Green’s function. Due to the flux condition, the Saint-Venant’s principle applies to the Green’s function.

In terms of continuity with respect to
y
variable, we need the following condition: suppose that
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
and let
(u,p)
be the weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=f&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u]=0,&\\
u=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(2.43)

Then we have

u(x)=\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}G(x,y)f(y)\,{\rm d}y,\qquad p(x)=\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}\Pi(x,y)\cdot f(y)\,{\rm d}y.

(2.44)

Moreover, it can be shown that
G(x,y)
satisfies the symmetry property
G(x,y)=G(y,x)^{T}
for any
x,y\in D^{\varepsilon}
.

Applying the large-scale estimates to the Green’s function, we have the following theorem. Recall that
\delta(x)=1-r_{x}
for
x\in D^{0}=\{r<1\}
. We use
\nabla_{x}G(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\nabla_{y}G(\cdot,\cdot)
to denote the derivatives with respect to the first and second variables of
G(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively.

Theorem 2.13

For
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}\leq 1-\varepsilon
, the following hold.

(1)

For
y\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{y}\leq 1-\varepsilon
, we have

|G(x,y)|\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\min\bigg\{\frac{1}{|x-y|},\frac{\delta(y)}{|x-y|^{2}},\frac{\delta(x)}{|x-y|^{2}},\frac{\delta(x)\delta(y)}{|x-y|^{3}}\bigg\}

(2.45)

and

|\nabla_{x}G(x,y)|\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\min\bigg\{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}},\frac{\delta(y)}{|x-y|^{3}}\bigg\}.

(2.46)

(2)

For
y\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{y}>1-\varepsilon
and
|x-y|>10\varepsilon
, we have

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(y)}|G(x,\cdot)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\min\bigg\{\frac{\varepsilon}{|x-y|^{2}},\frac{\delta(x)\varepsilon}{|x-y|^{3}}\bigg\}

(2.47)

and

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(y)}|\nabla_{x}G(x,\cdot)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\frac{\varepsilon}{|x-y|^{3}}.

(2.48)

Moreover, we have

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(y)}|\nabla_{y}G(x,\cdot)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\frac{\delta(x)}{|x-y|^{3}}.

(2.49)

Proof.

First consider the case
|x_{1}-y_{1}|\leq 2
. For this case, the proof of the desired estimates are almost the same as
[
HPZ24
, Appendix B]
and therefore we skip the detailed proof. For the case
|x_{1}-y_{1}|>2
, we need to extract the exponential decay of the Green’s function from the Saint-Venant’s principle. Observe that in the latter case
|x-y|
in the denominator can be ignored since
e^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\lesssim|x-y|^{-3}
.

Let us start with an estimate of the mixed derivatives of the Green’s function. Actually, by the duality argument as in the proof of
[
HPZ24
, Proposition B.3]
, for fixed
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
x_{1}=0
, we have
\|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D^{\varepsilon}_{1,2})}\leq C
where

is a universal constant depending only on the John domain condition. Due to the symmetry
G(x,y)=G(y,x)^{T}
, we have

\|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{2}(D^{\varepsilon}_{1,2})}\leq C

(2.50)

for a fixed
y\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
y_{1}=0
.

In view of the
x_{1}
-translation invariance of the estimates to be proved, without loss of generality, we may assume
x_{1}>2
and
y_{1}=0
. Note that
(\nabla_{y}G(x,y),\nabla_{y}\Pi(x,y))
still satisfies the system (
2.1
) in
x
variable. It follows from Theorem
2.1
, Remark
2.2
and (

) that there exists a universal constant

such that, for any

,

\|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{2}(D_{t}^{\varepsilon})}\leq Ce^{-ct}.

(2.51)

Hence, the large-scale Lipscthiz estimates in Section
2.2
gives, if
x_{1}>2
and
r_{x}\leq 1-\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(x,y)|+|\nabla_{y}\Pi(x,y)-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1/2,+}(y)}\nabla_{y}\Pi(z,y)\,{\rm d}z|

(2.52)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1,+}(x)}|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(z,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-cx_{1}}

and, if
x_{1}>2
and
r_{x}>1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(z,y)|^{2}dz\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}|\nabla_{y}\Pi(z,y)-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1/2,+}(x)}\nabla_{y}\Pi(\cdot,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1,+}(x)}|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(z,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-cx_{1}}.

Then, thanks to the fact that

regarded as a function of

with

,

|\nabla_{y}G(x,y)|\leq C\delta(x)e^{-cx_{1}}

(2.54)

and that, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with

,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla_{y}G(z,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}\leq C\varepsilon e^{-cx_{1}}.

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}|\nabla_{x}G(z,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{r,+}(x)}\bigg|\int_{1-\delta(y)}^{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}G(z,\frac{yt}{|y|})\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\,{\rm d}t\bigg|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}

(2.57)

and

, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(y)}|\Pi(x,\cdot)-\Pi_{\pm}(y)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}\frac{\varepsilon}{|x-y|^{3}}.

Here
\Pi_{\pm}(y)
satisfying

are two bounded functions that represent the limit of
\Pi(x,y)
as
x_{1}\to\pm\infty
. Moreover, we take
\Pi_{+}(x)
if
x_{1}>y_{1}
and
\Pi_{-}(x)
if
x_{1}<y_{1}
.

To obtain such estimates, we need an argument with the oscillation estimates of the pressure combined with the estimates of
G(x,y)
, similar to
[
HPZ24
, Proposition B.5]
. Roughly speaking, for each fixed
y\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{y}\leq 1-\varepsilon
, we can show the oscillation of
\Pi(x,y)
decays exponentially as
x_{1}-y_{1}\to\pm\infty
. This means
\Pi(x,y)\to\Pi_{+}(y)
as
x_{1}\to\infty
and
\Pi(x,y)\to\Pi_{-}(y)
exponentially as
x_{1}\to-\infty
. Obviously, by considering the total oscillation of
\Pi(x,y)
in
x\in D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{r\leq 1-\varepsilon\}
, we obtain
|\Pi_{+}(y)-\Pi_{-}(y)|
is bounded uniformly in
y\in D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{r\leq 1-\varepsilon\}
. If we assume
\Pi_{-}(y)=0
, then the solution given by (
2.44
) satisfies
p(x)\to 0
as
x_{1}\to-\infty
. Under this assumption, we see that
\Pi_{\pm}(y)
are both bounded. If
y\in D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{r>1-\varepsilon\}
,
\Pi_{\pm}(y)
is bounded in the

average sense as before.

To see
\nabla\cdot\Pi_{\pm}(y)=0
, it suffices to notice that
\nabla_{y}\cdot\Pi(x,y)=\delta_{x}(y)
. This can be seen by considering the system (
2.43
) with

for some scalar function

. Clearly,
(0,g)
is the solution of (
2.43
) with
f=\nabla g
. Thus by (
2.44
), we have

g(x)=\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}\Pi(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{y}g(y)\,{\rm d}y=-\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{y}\Pi(x,y)g(y)\,{\rm d}y.

(2.59)

Since the above identity holds for any
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D^{\varepsilon})
, we have
\nabla_{y}\Pi(x,y)=-\delta_{x}(y)
.
This implies
\nabla\cdot\Pi_{\pm}(y)=0
by letting
x\to\pm\infty
.

3
Boundary layers

Let us set

U^{0}=U^{0}(r)=(1-r^{2})e_{1}.

(3.1)

Then the Hagen-Poiseuille flow defined in (
1.9
) is represented as
u^{0}=(2\phi/\pi)U^{0}
. Our aim in this section is to construct boundary layers which correct the trace of
U^{0}
on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}=\partial D^{\varepsilon}
. A boundary layer
u
will be constructed as a solution in
H_{\rm uloc}^{1}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
of the Stokes system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
u=U^{0}&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

Notice the smallness
|U^{0}|\leq 2\varepsilon
on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
, which will be important when estimating
u
. Remark that there are no conditions on the flux in (
3.2
). This leads to the nonuniqueness of solutions. To avoid such indeterminacy, in Section
3.2
, we “normalize” a boundary layer
u
as

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[U^{0}-u]=\frac{\pi}{2}.

(3.3)

The process of this normalization is somewhat indirect as boundary layers do not satisfy the no-slip boundary condition and thus one cannot define the flux in
D^{\varepsilon}
directly by zero extension method. The boundary layer satisfying (
3.3
) will be called the
normalized boundary layer
and denoted by
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
. The normalized boundary layer
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
satisfies the flux condition
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u^{0}-(2\phi/\pi)v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=\phi
, which will be used when we prove the main theorems in Section
4
. Once the normalized boundary layer
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
is achieved, we investigate its relationship with the Navier’s wall law in Section
3.3
and estimate the decay of correlation in Section
3.4
. The results in Section
3.4
will be adopted in Section
3.5
to prove the concentration inequalities for
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
under the functional inequalities in Definition
1.3
; see Proposition
3.6
.

3.1
Existence and size estimates

In this section, we prove constructively the existence and estimates of the solution
(u,p)
of (
3.2
). Let
\eta
be a smooth cut-off function such that
\eta(r)=1
for

,
\eta(r)=0

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta w^{(1)}+\nabla q^{(1)}=F_{x}\mathbf{1}_{10\varepsilon}&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot w^{(1)}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
w^{(1)}=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon},\end{array}\right.

(3.15)

and
w^{(2)}
is expressed by

w^{(2)}(x)=\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}}G(x,y)F_{x}(y)(1-\mathbf{1}_{10\varepsilon}(y))\,{\rm d}y.

(3.16)

Then we can use the energy estimate to
w^{(1)}
and avoid the singularity of the Green’s function in (
3.16
). Indeed, integrating the first equation of (
3.15
) against
w^{(1)}
yields that

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}|\nabla w^{(1)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}F_{x}\cdot w^{(1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{D^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w^{(1)}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}|D^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)|^{5/6}\bigg(\int_{D^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w^{(1)}|^{6}\bigg)^{1/6}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{3/2}\bigg(\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}|\nabla w^{(1)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

where we have used (
3.13
) in the first inequality and the Sobolev-Poincaré inequality in the last inequality. This implies that, for any
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla w^{(1)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim 1.

(3.17)

Hence, by the fact
w^{(1)}=0
on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
and the Poincaré inequality, we obtain, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-4\varepsilon
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w^{(1)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\varepsilon.

Next we estimate
w^{(2)}
. By the boundary estimate of the Green’s function (
2.47
), we have

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w^{(2)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}\setminus B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|G(z,y)|^{2}|F_{z}(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}\setminus B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}\frac{e^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}(1+|x_{1}-y_{1}|)}{|x-y|^{3}}\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon.

Combining the estimates of
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
, we obtain, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-4\varepsilon
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\varepsilon.

(3.18)

Step 3: Gradient estimates of
w
and end of the proof.

We first estimate
\nabla w(x)
for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}\leq 1-4\varepsilon
. Note that
w
satisfies
-\Delta w+\nabla q=0
in
\{r<1-3\varepsilon\}
and that, for any
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}\leq 1-4\varepsilon
, we have
B_{1-r_{x}-3\varepsilon,+}(x)\subset\{r<1-3\varepsilon\}
. Thus, by the interior Caccioppoli inequality and Lipschitz estimate, we see that

|\nabla w(x)|\lesssim\frac{1}{1-r_{x}-3\varepsilon}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{1-r_{x}-3\varepsilon,+}(x)}|w|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1-r_{x}-3\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon}{1-r_{x}}.

(3.19)

Here we have used the estimate of
w
in Step 1 and Step 2, and
1-r_{x}-3\varepsilon\approx 1-r_{x}
.

Next, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-4\varepsilon
, we consider the split
w=w^{(1)}+w^{(2)}
as in Step 2. The estimate of
\nabla w^{(1)}
is contained in (
3.17
). The estimate for
\nabla w^{(2)}
is derived from

\nabla w^{(2)}(x)=\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}}\nabla_{x}G(x,y)F_{x}(y)(1-\chi_{10\varepsilon}(y))\,{\rm d}y.

Indeed, using (
2.49
) and the symmetry of
G(x,y)
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla w^{(2)}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}\setminus B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla_{x}G(z,y)|^{2}|F_{z}(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\bigg)^{1/2}\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\{1-3\varepsilon<r<1-\varepsilon\}\setminus B_{10\varepsilon,+}(x)}\frac{(1-r_{y})e^{-c|x_{1}-y_{1}|}(1+|x_{1}-y_{1}|)}{|x-y|^{3}}\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

By the estimates of
\nabla w^{(1)}
and
\nabla w^{(2)}
, we obtain for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-4\varepsilon
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla w|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim 1.

(3.20)

Summing up (
3.14
) and (
3.18
)–(
3.20
), we obtain the desired estimates (
3.9
)–(
3.10
).
∎

Remark 3.2

We point out that the large-scale Lipschitz estimate is the key to derive the size estimates in Proposition
3.1
which particularly including the large-scale size estimate (
3.10
) near the rough boundary. The crucial consequence of these estimates are the global
L^{2}
size estimates, i.e.,

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})}+\|\delta\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon

(3.21)

and

\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1/2}.

(3.22)

To prove these estimates, we decompose
D^{\varepsilon}
as
D_{1-4\varepsilon}=\{r<1-4\varepsilon\}
and
D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{1-4\varepsilon}
. Then (
3.9
) directly implies
\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\|_{L^{2}(D_{1-4\varepsilon}\cap\{k<x_{1}<k+1\})}\lesssim\varepsilon
. Moreover, by (
3.10
) and the Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{(D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{1-4\varepsilon})\cap\{k<x_{1}<k+1\}}|u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{(D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{1-4\varepsilon})\cap\{k<x_{1}<k+1\}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}|(D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{1-4\varepsilon})\cap\{k<x_{1}<k+1\}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{3}.

As a result, we have
\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon
. The proof for the estimates of
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
is similar.

3.2
Normalization

As we have pointed out, a boundary layer
(u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl},p^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl})
constructed above is not a unique solution of (
3.2
) and depends on the choice of the cut-off function
\eta
. However, one can “normalize” it in the following somewhat indirect manner. Fixing
\eta
and
(u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl},p^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl})
, we set

U^{\varepsilon}=U^{0}-u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl},\qquad P^{\varepsilon}=-4x_{1}-p^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}.

(3.23)

It is straightforward to verify that
(U^{\varepsilon},P^{\varepsilon})
is a nontrival solution in
H_{\rm uloc}^{1}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
u=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}\end{array}\right.

(3.24)

with nonzero flux
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[U^{\varepsilon}]\neq 0
. Therefore, by Remark
2.5
, the vector space
\mathcal{V}
spanned by the solutions of (
3.24
) is one dimensional and
U^{\varepsilon}
can be taken as a basis. Hence any boundary layer takes the form of
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=U^{0}-V^{\varepsilon}
for some
V^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{V}
, which excludes the dependence of
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
on the cut-off functions indirectly. Then, by choosing
V^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{V}
such that

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[V^{\varepsilon}]=\Phi^{0}[U^{0}]=\frac{\pi}{2},

(3.25)

we define

v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=U^{0}-V^{\varepsilon}

(3.26)

and call
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
the
normalized boundary layer
. By Remark
2.5
again,
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
is uniquely determined. Moreover,
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
satisfies the normalized condition (
3.3
) with
u=v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
.

Note that a boundary layer does not satisfy the no-slip boundary condition and thus its flux in
D^{\varepsilon}
cannot be defined directly. It is emphasized that fixing a special boundary layer enables us to analyze the fluctuation of the boundary layers by preventing arbitrarily change as the domain
D^{\varepsilon}
varies. In addition, we have the following.

Proposition 3.3

The unique normalized boundary layer
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
determined by (
3.25
)–(
3.26
) satisfies the size estimates in Proposition
3.1
.

Proof.

Let
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
be the boundary layer constructed as in Proposition
3.1
and let
U^{\varepsilon}
be given by (
3.23
). Since
U^{\varepsilon}
solves (
3.24
), by the large-scale regularity of Stokes system with no-slip boundary condition, we know that, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}\leq 1-4\varepsilon
,

|U^{\varepsilon}(x)|+|\nabla U^{\varepsilon}(x)|\lesssim 1,

(3.27)

and, for
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-4\varepsilon
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|U^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}+\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|\nabla U^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim 1.

(3.28)

Moreover, by the size estimates in Proposition
3.1
, it is not difficult to see that

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[U^{\varepsilon}]=\Phi^{\varepsilon}[U^{0}]+\Phi^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=\Phi^{0}[U^{0}]+O(\varepsilon).

(3.29)

Let
V^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
3.26
). Then, by (
3.25
), we have

\Phi^{\varepsilon}[V^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon}]=O(\varepsilon).

(3.30)

Since
V^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon}
solves (
3.24
), we have
V^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon_{1}U^{\varepsilon}
by Remark
2.5
, where
\varepsilon_{1}:=(2/\pi)\Phi^{\varepsilon}[V^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon}]
. On the other hand, observe that
V^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon}=-v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}+u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
. It follows that

v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}=u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\varepsilon_{1}U^{\varepsilon}.

Hence, the size estimates of
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
follows from those of
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
contained in Proposition
3.1
and the estimates of
\varepsilon_{1}U^{\varepsilon}
obtained by (
3.27
)–(
3.28
) and (
3.30
).
∎

3.3
Navier’s wall law

Let
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
be the normalized boundary layer uniquely determined by (
3.25
)–(
3.26
). We will investigate the connection between the boundary layer
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
and the Navier’s wall law. Specifically, we will determine the slip length
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
in the effective problem (
1.18
). The approach here is based on the asymptotic analysis when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Let
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
denote the expectation of
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
restricted to
D^{0}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
is a solution of the Stokes system in
D^{0}
with some boundary condition on
\Gamma^{0}=\partial D^{0}
. Recall that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is assumed to be stationary, i.e.,
x_{1}
-translation and
\theta
-rotation invariant. Hence
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
is invariant under
x_{1}
-translations and
\theta
-rotations. Then one can check that, solving the Stokes system in
D^{0}
explicitly,
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
takes the form of

\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=\varepsilon(\alpha+\beta r^{2})e_{1}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0muD^{0}

(3.31)

for some constants
\alpha,\beta
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq C
where
C
is independent of the element of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Thus
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
has a natural extension to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which will be denoted by
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
again. Hereafter, we will adopt this convention and use the following estimate:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]\|_{L^{\infty}(D^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon.\end{split}

(3.32)

To further reduce the free parameters and determine the structure of
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
, we take advantage of the flux condition on
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
to show the following property.

Proposition 3.4

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have

\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Proof.

Recall that
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
is defined in (
3.26
) and
V^{\varepsilon}
is chosen as the solution of the Stokes system (
3.24
) satisfying (
3.25
). We consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot V^{\varepsilon}=\int_{(D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D^{0})\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot V^{\varepsilon}+\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot V^{\varepsilon}.

(3.33)

Using the fact that
1-r^{2}=O(\varepsilon)
for
1-\varepsilon<r<1+\varepsilon
and the boundary size estimate of
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
(the first estimate in (
3.10
)), we see that
V^{\varepsilon}=U^{0}-v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
satisfies, for all
x\in D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D^{0}
,

\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon,+}(x)}|V^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\varepsilon.

Thus, by the Hölder inequality and a simple covering argument, we have

\bigg|\int_{(D^{\varepsilon}\setminus D^{0})\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot V^{\varepsilon}\bigg|\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

(3.34)

On the other hand, by
V^{\varepsilon}=U^{0}-v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
again, we have

\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot V^{\varepsilon}=\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot U^{0}-\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}.

(3.35)

Then obviously (
3.25
) yields

\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot U^{0}=\Phi^{0}[V^{0}]=\Phi^{\varepsilon}[V^{\varepsilon}].

(3.36)

Combining (
3.33
)–(
3.36
), we obtain

\bigg|\int_{D^{0}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}}e_{1}\cdot v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}\bigg|\lesssim\varepsilon^{2},

(3.37)

which implies the desired result.
∎

Now by (
3.31
) and Proposition
3.4
, we must have

\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=\varepsilon(\alpha-2\alpha r^{2})e_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(3.38)

as the leading term of order
O(\varepsilon)
has flux zero.
If we denote

U^{1}=U^{1}(r)=(1-2r^{2})e_{1},

(3.39)

then precisely we have

\|\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]-\varepsilon\alpha U^{1}\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{0})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}

(3.40)

and

\|\nabla\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]-\nabla(\varepsilon\alpha U^{1})\|_{L^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{0})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

(3.41)

We point out that the implicit parameter
\alpha
depends on the probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
.

The above asymptotic structure of
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
allows us to obtain the Navier’s wall law at the formal level; see also Remark
1.5
. Indeed, as is explained in the introduction,
u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}
defined by (
1.29
) will be our higher-order approximation in
D^{\varepsilon}
with no-slip boundary condition on
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}
. To get the effective Navier-Stokes system and Navier’s slip condition, we examine

\mathbb{E}[u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}]=u^{0}-\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]=u^{0}-\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\varepsilon\alpha U^{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0muL^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{0})^{3}.

(3.42)

Note that
U^{1}
satisfies the non-penetration condition on
\Gamma^{0}
and has zero flux in
D^{0}
. Define

u^{1}(r)=-\frac{2\phi}{\pi}U^{1}(r)=-\frac{2\phi}{\pi}(1-2r^{2})e_{1},

(3.43)

where the notation is aligned with the Hagen-Poiseuille flow in (
1.9
), and

u^{\rm N}(r)=u^{0}(r)+\varepsilon\alpha u^{1}(r)=\frac{2\phi}{\pi}\big((1-r^{2})-\varepsilon\alpha(1-2r^{2})\big)e_{1}.

(3.44)

Then it is easy to check

\mathbb{E}[u^{\varepsilon}_{\rm app}]=u^{\rm N}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0muL^{2}_{\rm uloc}(D^{0})^{3},

(3.45)

and that
u^{\rm N}
solves the effective problem (
1.18
) with some pressure
p^{{\rm N}}
. Using the explicit form (
3.44
), one can compute the slip lengths
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
as

\lambda^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{1-2\varepsilon\alpha}.

(3.46)

Consequently,
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
is a constant determined by the probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
and independent of the flux
\phi
. Moreover,
u^{\rm N}
is a unique bounded solution when
\phi,\varepsilon
are small.

3.4
Decay of correlation

In this section, we study the correlation between the normalized boundary layers, each of which corresponds to a rough domain
D^{\varepsilon}
. The mapping from a rough domain or its identified boundary to the corresponding boundary layer is highly nonlinear, determined implicitly by the system (
3.2
) and the uniqueness condition (
3.25
). Thus it is of great importance to understand how sensitive a boundary layer is to the perturbation of a boundary. In the following, we will show a sensitivity estimate that reveals the decay of correlation between the boundary layers. As a relevant result, we refer to
[
GV09
, Proposition 8]
for the decay of correlation between boundary layers in bumpy half-spaces with random roughness.

Let
D^{\varepsilon},\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}=\partial D^{\varepsilon},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}=\partial\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}
. Fix
z\in\Gamma^{0}=\partial D^{0}
. Assume
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}
outside of the cylindrical cube
S_{\varepsilon}(z)
defined in (
1.3
).
Let
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
and
\tilde{v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
be the boundary layers constructed by (
3.25
)–(
3.26
) corresponding to
D^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}
, respectively. From the construction, we see that, for
\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}=D^{\varepsilon}\cap\{x_{1}=t\}
not intersecting with
S_{\varepsilon}(z)
,

\int_{\Sigma^{\varepsilon}_{t}}e_{1}\cdot({v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\tilde{v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl})\,{\rm d}\sigma=0.

(3.47)

Proposition 3.5

For
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}<1-8\varepsilon
, we have

|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)-\tilde{v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}(1-r_{x})e^{-c|x_{1}-z_{1}|}}{|x-z|^{3}}

(3.48)

and

|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)-\nabla\tilde{v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}e^{-c|x_{1}-z_{1}|}}{|x-z|^{3}}.

(3.49)

The constant
C
is independent of
D^{\varepsilon},\widetilde{D}^{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

First we note that it suffices to prove the estimates for
|x-z|>20\varepsilon
since the size estimates in Proposition
3.1
directly gives the desired estimates for
|x-z|\leq 20\varepsilon
.

To simplify notation, we let
v=v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
and
\tilde{v}=\tilde{v}^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
with the associated pressures
q
and
\tilde{q}
, respectively.
Let
\zeta
be a smooth cut-off function such that
\zeta=0
in
S_{\varepsilon}(z)
,
\zeta=1
in
D^{\varepsilon}\setminus S_{2\varepsilon}(z)
and
|\nabla\zeta|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}
. Thus the support of
\nabla\zeta
is contained in
D^{\varepsilon}\cap S_{2\varepsilon}(z)
. Define

V=(v-\tilde{v})\zeta,\qquad Q=(q-\tilde{q}-q_{0})\zeta

(3.50)

with arbitrary
q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then it is easy to verify that
(V,Q)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta V+\nabla Q=-\nabla(v-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta+(q-\tilde{q}-q_{0})\nabla\zeta&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot V=(v-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
V=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(3.51)

Note that

V={v}-\tilde{v}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\{r<1-2\varepsilon\}.

(3.52)

To handle the nonvanishing divergence, we introduce a correction term
\hat{v}
which solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot\hat{v}=({v}-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta&\mbox{in}\ S_{2\varepsilon}^{*}(z),\\
\hat{v}=0&\mbox{on}\ \partial S_{2\varepsilon}^{*}(z).\end{array}\right.

(3.53)

Here
S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)
refers to a John domain such that, according to Definition
1.2
,

D^{\varepsilon}\cap S_{2\varepsilon}(z)\subset S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)\subset D^{\varepsilon}\cap S_{4\varepsilon}(z).

(3.54)

It follows from Lemma
A.1
that (
3.53
) has a solution
\hat{v}\in H_{0}^{1}(S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z))^{3}
satisfying

\|\nabla\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z))}\lesssim\|({v}-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta\|_{L^{2}(S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z))}\lesssim\varepsilon^{3/2}.

(3.55)

We extend
\hat{v}
by zero from
S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)
to the entire domain
D^{\varepsilon}
. Clearly, we have
\hat{v}\in H^{1}_{0}(D^{\varepsilon})^{3}
.

Define
(\widehat{V},\widehat{Q})=(V-\hat{v},Q)
. Observe that

\widehat{V}=V\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\{r<1-4\varepsilon\}

(3.56)

and that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\widehat{V}+\nabla\widehat{Q}=\Delta\hat{v}-\nabla(v-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta+(q-\tilde{q}-q_{0})\nabla\zeta&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\nabla\cdot\widehat{V}=0&\mbox{in}\ D^{\varepsilon},\\
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[\widehat{V}]=0,&\\
\widehat{V}=0&\mbox{on}\ \Gamma^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

(3.57)

We point out that the flux condition
\Phi^{\varepsilon}[\widehat{V}]=0
follows from
\nabla\cdot\widehat{V}=0
and (
3.47
).
Then we estimate
\widehat{V}
to prove (

). The Green’s representation gives

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}G(x,\cdot)\big(\Delta\hat{v}-\nabla({v}-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta+(q-\tilde{q}-q_{0})\nabla\zeta\big)

\displaystyle=:V^{(1)}+V^{(2)}+V^{(3)},

where
V^{(i)}
is the integral defined according to each of the three terms in the parenthesis. The above formula holds for any

since

.
Recall that
\hat{v}
is supported in the John domain

in (

). It follows from integration by parts and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle|V^{(1)}(x)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}G(x,\cdot)\Delta\hat{v}\bigg|=\bigg|\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}\nabla G(x,\cdot)\cdot\nabla\hat{v}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla G(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z))}\|\nabla\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z))}

where we have used (
2.49
) and (
3.55
) in the last inequality. Notice that (

) is actually applicable here by a simple covering argument.

Estimate of
V^{(2)}
.
Recall that
\nabla\zeta
is supported in
S_{2\varepsilon}(z)
and
|\nabla\zeta|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}
. It follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle|V^{(2)}(x)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}G(x,\cdot)\big(-\nabla({v}-\tilde{v})\cdot\nabla\zeta\big)\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|G(x,\cdot)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|\nabla({v}-\tilde{v})|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{3}(1-r_{x})e^{-c|x_{1}-z_{1}|}}{|x-z|^{3}},

where we have used (
2.47
) and (
3.10
) in the last inequality.

Estimate of
V^{(3)}
.
This estimate involves the pressure and thus we first need the connection between the pressure and velocity. In view of Lemma

, one has

\inf_{c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|q-c_{1}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|\nabla v|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

and

\inf_{c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\tilde{S}^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|\tilde{q}-c_{2}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\lesssim\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\tilde{S}^{*}_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

where

\displaystyle|V^{(3)}(x)|=\inf_{q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\int_{D^{\varepsilon}}G(x,\cdot)(q-\tilde{q}-q_{0})\nabla\zeta\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|G(x,\cdot)|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\inf_{q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{S_{2\varepsilon}(z)}|q-\tilde{q}-q_{0}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

where we have used (
3.58
)–(
3.59
) in the third inequality, and (
2.47
) and (
3.10
) in the last.

Combining the above estimates of

and

, we obtain the desired estimate (

). Indeed, from (
3.52
) and (
3.56
), we see that, for

with

and
|x-z|>20\varepsilon
,

|{v}(x)-\tilde{v}(x)|=|V(x)|=|\widehat{V}(x)|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{3}(1-r_{x})e^{-c|x_{1}-z_{1}|}}{|x-z|^{3}}.

(3.60)

Next we prove (
3.49
). Observe that, by (
3.52
), we have

\nabla{v}-\nabla\tilde{v}=\nabla V
in
\{r<1-4\varepsilon\}
.
We use the system (
3.51
) and the estimate of

obtained in (
3.60
). Since

and
\nabla\cdot V=0
in
\{r<1-4\varepsilon\}

which implies the desired derivative estimate (

3.5
Concentration of boundary layers

Combining the functional inequalities in Definition
1.3
with the deterministic estimate for the decay of correlation in the previous section, we can show the concentration inequalities of the normalized boundary layers. The resulting estimates are summarized in Proposition
3.6
below.

We first collect the estimates that hold independently of functional inequalities. Set

D_{0,1}^{\prime}=\{r<1-8\varepsilon\}\cap\{0<x_{1}<1\}.

Let
v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}
be the boundary layer constructed in Section
3.2
and consider the random variable
Y_{x}=v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)
. For
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}<1-8\varepsilon
, Proposition
3.5
implies that, for any

,

\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[Y_{x}]\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}(1-r_{x})e^{-c|x-z|}}{|x-z|^{3}}.

(3.61)

For
x\in D^{\varepsilon}
with
r_{x}>1-8\varepsilon
, on the other hand, we have the deterministic large-scale estimates in (
3.10
) for which the randomness does not play a role. Indeed, the estimates in (
3.10
) in Proposition
3.1
, combined with (
3.32
) and a covering argument (also see Remark
3.2
), give

\int_{D^{0}_{0,1}\setminus D_{0,1}^{\prime}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{D^{0}_{0,1}\setminus D_{0,1}^{\prime}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leq C\varepsilon^{3}.

(3.62)

We also consider
Y_{x}^{\prime}=\delta(x)\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)
. By the parallel argument as above, we have

\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[Y^{\prime}_{x}]\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}(1-r_{x})e^{-c|x-z|}}{|x-z|^{3}}

(3.63)

and

\int_{D_{0,1}\setminus D^{\prime}_{0,1}}\delta^{2}|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}|^{2}+\int_{D_{0,1}\setminus D^{\prime}_{0,1}}\delta^{2}|\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\nabla\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{3}.

(3.64)

Moreover, we consider the scalar random variable

X=\bigg(\int_{D^{\prime}_{0,1}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(3.65)

The triangle inequality and (
3.61
) give

\displaystyle\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[X]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{Q^{\prime}_{0,1}}\Big|\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}(x)]\Big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\bigg)^{1/2}

(3.66)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{Q^{\prime}_{0,1}}\frac{\varepsilon^{6}(1-r_{x})^{2}e^{-2c|x-z|}}{|x-z|^{6}}\,{\rm d}x\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{5/2}e^{-c|z|}.

The estimates (
3.61
)–(
3.66
) are independent of the functional inequalities in Definition
1.3
.

Proposition 3.6

The following hold.

(1)

Assume (
SG
). Then there exists a constant
\beta_{1}>0
such that we have

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\beta_{1}\varepsilon^{-3/2}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]\|_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}\Big)\Big]\leq C

(3.67)

and

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\beta_{1}\varepsilon^{-3/2}\|\delta(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\nabla\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}])\|_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

(3.68)

(2)

Assume (
LSI
). Then there exists a constant
\beta_{2}>0
such that we have

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\beta_{2}\varepsilon^{-3}\|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}\Big)\Big]\leq C

(3.69)

and

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\beta_{2}\varepsilon^{-3}\|\delta(\nabla v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\nabla\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}])\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}\Big)\Big]\leq C.

(3.70)

Proof.

(

)
Assume (
SG
). We first prove (
3.67
). In view of the stationarity and the boundary estimate (

\displaystyle\var[Y_{x}]

Integrating it in

\int_{D_{0,1}^{\prime}}\var[Y_{x}]\,{\rm d}x=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{D_{0,1}^{\prime}}|v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]|^{2}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\leq C\varepsilon^{3}.

(3.72)

Hence, by (

\mathbb{E}[X]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]^{1/2}\leq C\varepsilon^{3/2}.

(3.73)

With the bound (

Remark 3.7

We point out that the optimality of the order
O(\varepsilon^{3/2})
of the convergence rates is due to the deterministic
L^{2}
estimate near the boundary. Particularly, the estimates (
3.62
) and (
3.64
) cannot be improved in general. However, in view of the scaling of the 2D random boundary, the optimal convergence rates are expected to be
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. This is actually true for interior estimates, which can be seen from (
3.71
). In fact, by adding the weight
\delta^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>1/2
and following the same argument, we can show

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\beta\varepsilon^{-4}\|\delta^{\gamma}(v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}-\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}])\|_{L^{2}(D^{0}_{k,k+1})}^{2}\Big)\Big]\leq C,

provided (
LSI
) assumption (similar for (
SG
) assumption). The last estimate is optimal in terms of both the
O(\varepsilon^{2})
order of convergence rate and the Gaussian stochastic integrability. If
\gamma=1/2
, the convergence rate
O(\varepsilon^{2})
should be replaced by
O(\varepsilon^{2}|\log\varepsilon|^{1/2})
due to a technical reason.

U=U_{1}(r)e_{1}

\displaystyle U\cdot\nabla U=0

Appendix A
Pressure estimates

This appendix collects the estimates of pressure for the Stokes and Navier-Stokes systems.
We begin with the Bogovski’s lemma crucial for the study of the Navier-Stokes system.

Lemma A.1

(Bogovski)

Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded John domain and
f\in L^{2}(\omega)
. Then there exists a vector-valued function
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)^{d}
such that
\nabla\cdot u=f
in
\omega
and

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\omega)},

(A.1)

where
C
depends only on
d
and the constant of John domain.

The Bogovski’s lemma in John domains was proved in
[
ADM06
]
and it was shown that the John domain is a sufficient and almost necessary condition for the
L^{p}
solvability of the divergence equation. As a duality statement, we have the following lemma.

Lemma A.2

Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded John domain. Then, for
p\in L^{2}(\omega)
, we have

c\|\nabla p\|_{H^{-1}(\omega)}\leq\|p-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\omega}p\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq C\|\nabla p\|_{H^{-1}(\omega)},

(A.2)

where
H^{-1}(\omega)
is the dual space of
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
.

The above lemma can be used to estimate the pressure for Stokes or Navier-Stokes systems in bounded John domains.

Lemma A.3

Let
(u,p)
be a weak solution of
-\Delta u+\nabla p=\nabla\cdot F
in a bounded John domain
\omega
. Then we have

\|p-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\omega}p\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq C(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}).

(A.3)

Next we discuss the interior regularity of the Navier-Stokes systems. Let
\omega
be a bounded domain and
\omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\omega
. If
(u,p)
is a weak solution of the nonlinear system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+u_{0}\cdot\nabla u+u\cdot\nabla u_{0}+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ \omega,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ \omega,\end{array}\right.

(A.4)

with
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\omega)^{3}
, then the classical regularity theory of the stationary Navier-Stokes system implies that
u\in C^{\infty}(\omega^{\prime})^{3}
. Moreover, for any
k\geq 1
,

\|u\|_{C^{k}(\omega^{\prime})}+\|p-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\omega}p\|_{C^{k-1}(\omega^{\prime})}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{C^{k}(\omega)},\|u\|_{H^{1}(\omega)})\|u\|_{H^{1}(\omega)},

(A.5)

where the constnat
C(\|u_{0}\|_{C^{k}(\omega)},\|u\|_{H^{1}(\omega)})
, depending also on
\omega^{\prime}
and
\omega
, is an increasing function of
\|u_{0}\|_{C^{k}(\omega)}
and
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\omega)}
. In particular, we have

Lemma A.4

Let
(u,p)
be a weak solution of (
A.4
). Suppose that there exists some
\tau_{0}>0
such that
\|u_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\omega)}\leq\tau_{0}
and
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq\tau_{0}
. Then, for any
\omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\omega
, we have

\mathcal{O}_{\omega^{\prime}}[p]:=\sup_{\omega^{\prime}}p-\inf_{\omega^{\prime}}p\leq C(\tau_{0},\omega^{\prime},\omega)\|u\|_{H^{1}(\omega)}.

(A.6)

The last lemma is useful for the pointwise estimate of the pressure, particularly in unbounded domains.

Appendix B
Random cylinders satisfying functional inequalities

This appendix presents non-trivial examples of random cylinders that satisfy the function inequalities (
SG
) and (
LSI
). We remark that the aim is to demonstrate the relevance of assuming these function inequalities, rather than to offer techniques for the construction of general examples that satisfy (
SG
) or (
LSI
).

B.1
An infinite cylinder with Poisson boundary

In this section, we define a random infinite cylinder with the boundary specified by a Poisson point process. We will show that the law of the cylinder satisfies both (
SG
) and (
LSI
). To this end, recall for
0<\varepsilon<1
that
D_{1/\varepsilon}
denotes an infinite cylinder with radius
1/\varepsilon
and the boundary
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ |\ x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
. Let
\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
denote the surface measure of
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}=\partial D_{1/\varepsilon}
. Notice that
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}\simeq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Scaled cylinder.

Our construction of a random cylinder starts with a Poisson point process defined on the rescaled circular boundary
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
with radius
1/\varepsilon
and is inspired by the Poisson point process on a torus
[
GO15
, Definition 2]
and on the Euclidean space
[
GO17
, Definition 2.3]
. We remark that one can actually construct such a process, starting from a suitable stationary Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, using
[
LP18
, Theorem 5.1]
and the quotient mapping from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. To be more specific, given a realization
\tilde{Z}=\{\tilde{z}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
of the Poisson point process with a unit intensity, we define a transformation
A:\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by setting

A(\tilde{z}):=\begin{cases}(\tilde{z}_{1},0,0)+(1+\varepsilon/2)(0,\tilde{z}_{2},\tilde{z}_{3})&\text{ if }\tilde{z}\in S_{1}(\tilde{z}^{(n)})\cap\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon},\\
\tilde{z}&\text{ if }\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}\setminus\cup_{n}S_{1}(\tilde{z}^{(n)}),\end{cases}

where
S_{1}(\tilde{z}^{(n)})
denotes the 3D cylindrical cube with the center
\tilde{z}^{(n)}\in\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
; see (
1.3
) for the definition. Note that the transformation
A
admits jumps near the Poisson points
\tilde{z}^{(n)}
. Let
\tilde{D}^{1/\varepsilon}
be the cylindrical domain with the boundary defined by the range of
A
. By construction, the cylindrical domain
\tilde{D}^{1/\varepsilon}
is a John domain and is stationary with respect to
x_{1}
-translations and
\theta
-rotations. Let
\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon}
denote the set of all possible John cylindrical domains
\tilde{D}^{1/\varepsilon}

and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1/\varepsilon}
the corresponding
\sigma
-algebra. We denote by
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{1/\varepsilon}
the law of the random domain induced by the law of the Poisson point process on
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
. Given a random variable
X_{1/\varepsilon}
on the probability space
(\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon},\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{1/\varepsilon},\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{1/\varepsilon})
, we write the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{1/\varepsilon}[X]
of
X
by
\mathbb{E}_{1/\varepsilon}[X]=\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}[X\circ D]
, where
\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation taken against the law of random domain
D\in\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon}
. For clarity, we omit the dependence of the random domain
D
on
1/\varepsilon
when it is clear from the context.

Thanks to the log-Sobolev inequality for Poisson measure
[
Wu00
, Corollary 2.1]
(see also
[
AL00
]
), the induced law of the random domain
D\in\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with an
\varepsilon
-independent constant
C>0
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Ent}[X_{1/\varepsilon}]&\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}\Big[\big(X_{1/\varepsilon}\circ D(\tilde{Z}\cup\{\tilde{z}\})-X_{1/\varepsilon}\circ D(\tilde{Z})\big)^{2}\Big]\,{\rm d}(\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon})_{\tilde{z}}\\
&\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}\Big[\Big(\underset{D|_{S_{1}(\tilde{z})}}{\osc}X_{1/\varepsilon}\Big)^{2}\Big]\,{\rm d}(\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon})_{\tilde{z}}.\end{split}

(B.1)

The target cylinder.

Now we turn to the construction of the target random domain and show that its law satisfies the desired log-Sobolev inequality. Consider the scaling isomorphism
\Phi:\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}\mapsto\Gamma
defined by
\Phi(x)=\varepsilon x
for every
x\in\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
.
This isomorphism transforms the Poisson point process
\tilde{Z}=\{\tilde{z}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}
to the one
Z=\{z^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\{\varepsilon\tilde{z}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\Gamma
. This also allows us define the John domain

D^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\tilde{D}^{1/\varepsilon}

as in Definition
1.2
and the set
\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon}
. In addition, with the isomorphism
\Phi
, given every random variable
X
defined on
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
, we obtain a random variable
X_{1/\varepsilon}:=X\circ\Phi
defined on
\tilde{\Omega}_{1/\varepsilon}
. Moreover, thanks to (
B.1
), it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Ent}[X]={\rm Ent}[X\circ\Phi]&\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}\Big[\Big(\underset{D|_{S_{1}(\tilde{z})}}{\osc}X\circ\Phi\Big)^{2}\Big]\,{\rm d}(\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon})_{\tilde{z}}.\end{split}

(B.2)

An application of the change of variables
z=\varepsilon\tilde{z}
and the argument of the proof of
[
GO17
, Lemma 2.4]
, we have from above that

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Ent}[X]&\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}_{0,1/\varepsilon}\Big[\Big(\underset{D|_{S_{1}(\tilde{z})}}{\osc}X\circ\Phi\Big)^{2}\Big]\,{\rm d}(\Gamma_{1/\varepsilon})_{\tilde{z}}\\
&=C\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{\Gamma}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X\Big)^{2}\Big]\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}.\end{split}

(B.3)

Therefore, the law of
D^{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
LSI
), which in turn implies the inequality (
SG
).

B.2
An infinite cylinder with Bernoulli radii

In this section, we define the random infinite cylinders with boundaries specified by Bernoulli process. Assume for simplicity that
M=1/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
b:\mathbb{R}\times[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
be a random binary function defined by

b_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{1},\theta)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z},0\leq j\leq M-1}\bm{\xi}_{ij}\mathbf{1}_{[\varepsilon i,\varepsilon(i+1))}(x_{1})\mathbf{1}_{[\varepsilon j2\pi,\varepsilon(j+1)2\pi)}(\theta),

where
\bm{\xi}=\{\xi_{ij}\}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\xi}}:=\text{Ber}(1/2)^{\otimes\infty}
is a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables with equal probability, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{ij}=0)=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{ij}=1)=1/2
.

We define a random infinite cylinder with the binary radius function
1+\varepsilon b_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{1},\theta)
, namely we set

D^{\varepsilon}=D^{\varepsilon}(\bm{\xi}):=\{(x_{1},r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ ~|~x_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\mkern 9.0mu\theta\in[0,2\pi],\mkern 9.0mu0\leq r\leq 1+\varepsilon b_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{1},\theta)\}.

By the geometrical construction, it is not hard to see that
D^{\varepsilon}
is a John domain in Definition
1.2
with constants independent of
\bm{\xi}
, whose boundary is given by

\Gamma^{\varepsilon}:=\{(x_{1},r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ ~|~x_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\mkern 9.0mu\theta\in[0,2\pi],\mkern 9.0mur=1+\varepsilon b_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{1},\theta)\}.

Let
\Gamma=\{r=1\}
and
\Gamma_{ij}=\{(x_{1},r,\theta)~|~r=1,x_{1}\in[\varepsilon i,\varepsilon(i+1)),\theta\in[\varepsilon 2\pi j,\varepsilon 2\pi(j+1)\}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z},0\leq j\leq M-1
. Clearly,
\{\Gamma_{ij}\}
are mutually disjoint and
\Gamma=\cup_{ij}\Gamma_{ij}
.

Let
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
be the sample space of all the random infinite cylindrical domains
D^{\varepsilon}
and let
\mathbb{P}
be the induced probability measure on the sample space
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
by the Bernoulli measure
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\xi}}
. It is obvious that for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0\leq j\leq M-1
, the measure
\mathbb{P}
is stationary with respect to translation along
x_{1}
-direction by increments of
i\varepsilon
and rotation in
\theta
by angles of
2\pi j\varepsilon
. Further, any realization of
D^{\varepsilon}
is a John domain. By slightly abusing the terminology, we use
\mathbb{E}
to denote the expectation against both the law
\mathbb{P}
of the random cylinder and the Bernoulli measure
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\xi}}
.

Let
X
be a random variable defined on
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
. Define
F:\{0,1\}^{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by setting
F(\bm{\xi}):=X\circ D^{\varepsilon}(\bm{\xi})
. Then it follows from the log-Sobolev inequality
[
BL98
, Theorem 1]
of the Bernoulli measure
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\xi}}
that

{\rm Ent}[X]={\rm Ent}[F]\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\sum_{ij}|F(\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{e}_{ij})-F(\bm{\xi})|^{2},

(B.4)

where
\{\mathbf{e}_{ij}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and the addition inside
F
is modulo
2
. Moreover, by the definition of
F
, we see that

\displaystyle|F(\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{e}_{ij})-F(\bm{\xi})|

\displaystyle=|X\circ D^{\varepsilon}(\bm{\xi}+\mathbf{e}_{ij})-X\circ D^{\varepsilon}(\bm{\xi})|

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{z\in\Gamma_{ij}}\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{2\pi\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{ij}|}\int_{\Gamma_{ij}}\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{2\pi\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}.

Summing the inequality above over
i
and
j
and plugging the resulting estimate into (
B.4
) leads to

{\rm Ent}[X]\leq\frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{2\pi\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}X\Big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg],

(B.5)

where we have used the fact
|\Gamma_{ij}|=2\pi\varepsilon^{2}
. This is the desired log-Sobolev inequality, and the spectral gap inequality follows as a direct consequence.

Remark B.1

The probability space
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
constructed above by Bernoulli process is not stationary with respect to all the
x_{1}
-translations and
\theta
-rotations. Indeed, it is stationary only with respect to those
x_{1}
-translations and
\theta
-rotations in particular steps, namely,

\mathbb{P}\circ R_{\theta_{j}}=\mathbb{P}\circ T_{t_{i}}=\mathbb{P},

(B.6)

for
\theta_{j}=2\pi\varepsilon j
and
t_{i}=\varepsilon i
with
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Nevertheless, our main results still hold for this case. The only difference in the proof is that
\mathbb{E}[v^{\varepsilon}_{\rm bl}]
is not a simple laminar flow, but a periodic flow with period
2\pi\varepsilon
in
\theta
and
\varepsilon
in
x_{1}
. Then we only need to take another step to show the deterministic convergence rate of this periodic flow to a real laminar flow, which is much easier by using the Green’s representation in
D^{0}
. The details will be omitted.

Appendix C
Concentration bounds under functional inequalities

This appendix collects the inequalities utilized in Section
3.5
to establish the concentration of boundary layers based on functional inequalities.

C.1
Moment bound under the spectral gap inequality

The following proposition is used in the proof of Proposition
3.6
(
1
).

Proposition C.1

Assume that
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
satisfies (
SG
). Let
X
be a scalar random variable. Then we have, for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|^{2p}]

\displaystyle\leq(Cp)^{2p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\Big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[X]\Big)^{2p}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg]

(C.1)

\displaystyle+(Cp)^{2p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\varepsilon^{-1}\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[X]\Big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg)^{p}\bigg].

The constant
C
is independent of
p
and
X
.

Proof.

We follow an argument in
[
DG20a
, Section 3.1]
. For simplicity, we let
\osc[\,\cdot\,]
denote
\osc_{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}[\,\cdot\,]
in this proof. Without loss of generality, assume
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. Applying (
SG
) to the random variable
Y=|X|^{p}=|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|^{p}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2p}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]^{2}+C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\osc[|X|^{p}]^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg].

(C.2)

For
1\leq p\leq 2
, the Jensen’s inequality gives

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]^{p}\leq C^{p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon^{-1}\osc[X])^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg)^{p}\bigg],

(C.3)

where we have also used (
SG
) and the Hölder inequality in the second inequality. For
p>2
, the Young’s inequality gives, for any
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]^{2}\leq\frac{p-2}{p-1}\delta\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2p}]+\frac{1}{p-1}\delta^{2-p}\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}]^{p}.

(C.4)

By choosing
\delta
small (say,
\delta=1/2
), the first term of the right-hand side of the above inequality can be absorbed to the left-hand side of (
C.2
). It follows that, for any
p\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2p}]\leq C^{p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon^{-1}\osc[X])^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg)^{p}\bigg]+C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\osc[|X|^{p}]^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg].

(C.5)

Thus it suffices to estimate the last term in the above inequality.

Using the inequality
|a^{p}-b^{p}|\leq p|a-b|(|a|^{p-1}+|b|^{p-1})
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\osc[|X|^{p}]\leq 2p\osc[X]\sup|X|^{p-1},

(C.6)

where

\sup|X|:=\sup_{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}|X|:=\sup\{|X(\tilde{D}^{\varepsilon})~|~\tilde{D}^{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mkern 9.0mu\tilde{D}^{\varepsilon}=D^{\varepsilon}\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus S_{\varepsilon}(z)\}.

(C.7)

It follows that

\osc[|X|^{p}]\leq 2p\osc[X](\sup|X|)^{p-1}\leq 2p\osc[X](|X|+\osc[X])^{p-1}.

(C.8)

Then by using the inequality
(a+b)^{p-1}\leq 2a^{p-1}+(Cp)^{p}b^{p-1}
for all
a,b\geq 0
, we have

\osc[|X|^{p}]\leq 4p|X|^{p-1}\osc[X]+(Cp)^{p}\osc[X]^{p}.

(C.9)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\osc[|X|^{p}]^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}

\displaystyle\leq(8p)^{2}|X|^{2(p-1)}\int_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon^{-1}\osc[X])^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}

(C.10)

\displaystyle+2(Cp)^{2p}\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\osc[X]^{2p}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}.

Then the Hölder and Young’s inequalities give

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[(8p)^{2}|X|^{2(p-1)}\int_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon^{-1}\osc[X])^{2}\Gamma_{z}\bigg]

(C.11)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p-1}{p}\delta^{p/(p-1)}\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2p}]+\delta^{-p}(8p)^{2p}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon^{-1}\osc[X])^{2}\Gamma_{z}\bigg)^{p}\bigg].

Inserting (
C.10
)–(
C.11
) into (
C.5
), and by taking
\delta
small, we see that
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2p}]
on the right-hand side can be absorbed to the left-hand side, which gives the desired estimate.
∎

C.2
Exponential moment bound under the logarithmic Sobolev inequality

The following proposition is used in the proof of Proposition
3.6
(
2
).

Proposition C.2

Assume that
(\Omega^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon},\mathbb{P})
satisfies (
LSI
). Moreover, assume that a scalar random variable
X
has
L^{2}
-bounded local oscillation, i.e. there exists
L^{\prime}>0
such that

\sup_{D^{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma}\Big(\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[X]\Big)^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\leq(L^{\prime})^{2}

(C.12)

and that
X
has uniformly bounded local oscillation, i.e. there exists
L>0
such that

\sup_{D^{\varepsilon}\in\Omega^{\varepsilon}}\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\underset{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}{\osc}[X]\leq L.

(C.13)

Then we have, for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}[X]))\big]\leq\exp\Big(\frac{C_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}}{L}\lambda(e^{L\lambda}-1)\Big)

(C.14)

and, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq t]\leq 2\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp\Big(-\lambda t+\frac{C_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}}{L}\lambda(e^{L\lambda}-1)\Big).

(C.15)

Proof.

We follow an argument in
[
DG20a
, Section 3.2]
, which is essentially based on the Herbst’s argument. For simplicity, we let
\osc[\,\cdot\,]
denote
\osc_{D^{\varepsilon}|_{S_{\varepsilon}(z)}}[\,\cdot\,]
in this proof. First, using (
LSI
), we obtain for the random variable
e^{\lambda X/2}
:

{\rm Ent}[e^{\lambda X}]=\mathbb{E}[\lambda Xe^{\lambda X}]-\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\cdot\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}(\osc[e^{\lambda X/2}])^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\bigg].

(C.16)

By using the inequality
|e^{a}-e^{b}|\leq(e^{a}+e^{b})|a-b|
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle(\osc[e^{\lambda X/2}])^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda X}e^{\lambda{\osc}[X]}(\osc[X])^{2}.

Thanks to the assumptions (
C.12
)–(
C.13
), we then have

\int_{\Gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}(\osc[e^{\lambda X/2}])^{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{z}\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda L}.

Define
\phi(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]
for
\lambda\geq 0
. Then inserting the last display into (
C.16
) leads to

\lambda\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)-\phi(\lambda)\log\phi(\lambda)\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda L}.

Let
\psi(\lambda)=\log\phi(\lambda)
. The estimate above implies that

\Big(\frac{\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\Big)^{\prime}\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}e^{\lambda L}.

Integrating both sides on
[a,\lambda]
with
0<a\leq\lambda
shows that

\frac{\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}-\frac{\psi(a)-\psi(0)}{a}\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}}{L}(e^{\lambda L}-e^{aL}),

where
\psi(0)=0
is used. Since
\psi^{\prime}(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)/\phi(0)=\mathbb{E}[X]
, taking the limit
a\to 0
gives

\log\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}[X]))\big]=\psi(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}[\lambda X]\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}}{L}\lambda(e^{\lambda L}-1),

which proves the first assertion (
C.14
). Moreover, the Markov’s inequality leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X-\mathbb{E}[X]\geq t]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}[X]))\big]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp\Big(-\lambda t+\frac{C\varepsilon^{-2}(L^{\prime})^{2}}{L}\lambda(e^{\lambda L}-1)\Big).

Notice that the assumptions (
C.12
)–(
C.13
) are invariant under
X\mapsto-X
. Hence the same argument as above is valid for
-X
, which implies the second assertion (
C.15
).
∎
