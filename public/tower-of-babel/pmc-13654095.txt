TiLCA-Net: nuclei segmentation and classification in pathological images via task-specialized attention and efficient decoding
Authors: Liu Xiang, Xuejia Zheng, Huihui Tao, Bo Jin, Chi Jiang, Enqi Feng, Leping Wang, Xiaoni Zhong, Jie Song, Yong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNuclei segmentation and classification in whole-slide images (WSIs) are critical for tumor diagnosis and treatment. Existing methods often face multi-task feature conflict and computational redundancy. This study aimed to develop an efficient multi-task framework that balances segmentation accuracy and inference efficiency.MethodsTo seek an improved balance between segmentation accuracy and efficiency, this study proposes TiLCA-Net, a network that integrates multi-scale attention mechanisms with a lightweight decoding strategy to optimize comprehensive model performance. First, a Task-Specialized Attention Integrated Encoder (TSEncoder) is designed to decouple features by assigning spatial, global, and channel attention at multiple levels, thereby mitigating negative transfer. Second, we introduce an iterative cross-enhancement (ICE) module, which utilizes closed-loop iteration and median pooling to enhance global geometric consistency and robustness. Finally, a Lightweight Context Aggregation Decoder (LCA-Decoder) is introduced, replacing redundant structures with multi-scale aggregation to substantially reduce computational cost while preserving high-frequency details.ResultsExperiments on CoNSeP, MoNuSeg, and CervCell-Seg demonstrate that TiLCA-Net achieves a favorable balance between segmentation performance and computational efficiency. TiLCA-Net achieved mean panoptic quality (PQ) scores of 0.528, 0.578, and 0.592 on CoNSeP, MoNuSeg, and CervCell-Seg, respectively, compared with 0.504, 0.563, and 0.578 for HoVer-Net. These results indicate PQ improvements of 0.024, 0.015, and 0.014 on the three datasets, respectively. Patch-level benchmarking showed that TiLCA-Net required fewer floating point operations per second (FLOPs) and achieved higher batch throughput than HoVer-Net, but with higher single-patch latency and peak graphics processing unit (GPU) memory.ConclusionsThrough feature decoupling and efficient decoder reconstruction, TiLCA-Net provides a practical accuracy-efficiency trade-off for patch-level nuclei analysis in computational pathology.Keywords: Digital pathology, nuclei instance segmentation and classification, multi-task learning, attention mechanism, efficient network

Introduction

In modern clinical practice driven by precision medicine, histopathological examination based on tissue biopsy remains central to tumor diagnosis, grading, and prognostic evaluation (1,2). Although radiomics provides valuable macro-level tumor assessment, histopathology offers direct microscopic evidence of tissue morphology. With the advancement of computational pathology, whole-slide images (WSIs) have become an important medium for capturing the multidimensional information of the tumor microenvironment (TME) at gigapixel resolution (3). WSIs provide a data bridge for analyzing deep pathological features, ranging from tumor atypia to the spatial heterogeneity of stromal components (4).

Within this microscopic ecosystem, nuclei provide essential information for clinical decision-making. Nuclear morphology, mitotic activity, and spatial distribution are closely associated with pathological progression (5). For example, tumor epithelial atypia supports malignancy grading (6), normal epithelial cells reflect baseline tissue organization (7), spindle cells are important for soft-tissue tumor differentiation (8), and inflammatory cell infiltration can indicate the immune status of the TME (9). Therefore, accurate and high-throughput nuclei instance segmentation and classification are essential for building intelligent computer-aided diagnosis systems.

In recent years, nuclei segmentation and classification have been extensively investigated. Early methods mainly relied on thresholding and watershed-based image processing strategies (10,11). With the development of deep learning, convolutional neural network (CNN)-based models became the dominant solution. U-Net and nnU-Net enabled pixel-level nuclei segmentation (12,13), while HoVer-Net introduced horizontal and vertical distance maps to improve the separation of overlapping nuclei and became a widely used benchmark for simultaneous nuclei segmentation and classification (14). More recently, Transformer-based methods, such as CellViT and UN-SAM, have enhanced global modeling ability, although their computational complexity can limit high-throughput WSI analysis (15-17).

Traditional nuclei segmentation methods were further extended through marker-controlled watershed, active contours, and hand-crafted morphological features (18-22). However, these methods are sensitive to staining variation, stromal noise, and nuclear adhesion. Deep learning-based instance segmentation methods have improved robustness through detection-based, contour-aware, and geometry-guided designs. For example, Mask R-CNN and its histopathology-specific variants use a detect-then-segment paradigm (23,24), whereas StarDist, DCAN, and BESNet improve instance separation by modeling nuclear shape or boundary information (25-27). In addition to segmentation, nuclei classification is important for characterizing the TME and supporting pathological assessment (28,29). Recent studies have incorporated classification into end-to-end nuclei analysis frameworks to reduce error propagation from cascaded pipelines (30-34). Multi-task learning has therefore become a common strategy in computational pathology (35-37). However, hard parameter sharing may cause feature conflicts between tasks with different representation preferences. SMILE improves task isolation by using independent encoders and cost-sensitive learning, although the use of multiple encoders may increase computational cost (38).

Attention mechanisms have also been introduced to enhance discriminative feature representation in medical image analysis (39-43). These methods help suppress redundant background information and emphasize task-relevant structures. Nevertheless, a single uniform attention design may be insufficient for nuclei panoptic analysis because different subtasks depend on different types of information: segmentation requires fine boundary and texture cues, instance separation benefits from global geometric consistency, and classification relies more on high-level semantic representations. Despite these advances, practical nuclei analysis in WSIs still faces three major challenges. First, multi-task feature conflict may lead to negative transfer when segmentation, instance separation, and classification share the same representation. Second, complex tissue backgrounds, staining variation, red blood cells, and necrotic regions can weaken geometric consistency and interfere with instance separation. Third, dense decoder branches and redundant feature fusion increase computational cost, limiting deployment in resource-constrained computational pathology settings.

To address these issues, this study proposes TiLCA-Net, a computationally efficient multi-task network that improves nuclei segmentation and classification through feature decoupling and lightweight structural reconstruction. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

Proposal of a Task-Specialized Attention Integrated Encoder (TSEncoder): Addressing the issue of multi-task feature conflict, we adopt a multi-scale attention injection strategy. By deploying spatial, dynamic global, and channel attention at different encoder levels, this module improves the decoupling of features required for segmentation, regression, and classification tasks.

Methods

Overview of the model architecture

To address the challenges of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained pathological images, including dense nuclear stacking, morphological heterogeneity, and complex background noise, we propose TiLCA-Net for integrated nuclei instance segmentation and classification. As illustrated in Figure 1, TiLCA-Net follows a U-Net-like encoder-decoder paradigm and consists of a shared stem, three task-specialized encoder branches, the ICE Module, the LCA-Decoder, and a branch merging/post-processing module.

Overview of the TiLCA-Net architecture. CAB, channel attention block; CCA, Criss-Cross Attention; EULAM, Efficient Upsampling Large-Kernel Attention Module; GGCA, global guided context attention; ICE, iterative cross-enhancement; MedSCA, Median-based Spatial-Channel Attention; MS-CFE, Multi-Scale Context Feature Enhancement; NC, nuclear classification; NI, nuclear instance; NS, nuclear segmentation; SAB, spatial attention block.

The network contains three functionally complementary task branches. The nuclear segmentation (NS) branch generates binary semantic maps to distinguish nuclei from the background. The nuclear instantiation (NI) branch predicts horizontal and vertical distance maps for separating adjacent nuclei. To strengthen long-range geometric modeling in this branch, dynamic global guided context attention (GGCA) is embedded into its encoder pathway. The nuclear classification (NC) branch produces multi-class probability maps using high-level semantic features to distinguish different nuclear types.

After obtaining the outputs of the NS, NI, and NC branches, we used a two-stage marker-controlled watershed strategy to generate final nuclear instances (14,38). Initial markers were extracted from the gradient magnitude of the horizontal and vertical distance maps predicted by the NI branch and constrained by the foreground mask from the NS branch. A second watershed refinement based on distance transformation was then applied to reduce over-segmentation in large or fragmented nuclei. Finally, each nuclear instance was assigned a category label by majority voting over the NC branch predictions within the corresponding instance mask.

TSEncoder

ResNet-34 is adopted as the encoder backbone, and the proposed TSEncoder contains three parallel task-specialized branches (Figure 1). To align feature extraction with task-specific requirements, different attention modules are inserted at different encoder levels. In the NS branch, spatial attention blocks (SAB) are placed after shallow features (d0) to enhance boundary and texture cues. In the NI branch, GGCA is embedded at the mid-level feature (d2) to model long-range spatial dependencies and improve geometric consistency. In the NC branch, channel attention blocks (CAB) are applied before the high-level semantic feature (d3) to recalibrate channel responses for nuclear type classification.

The TSEncoder adopts a hybrid parameter-sharing strategy. The initial conv0 and max-pooling layers are shared to maintain consistent low-level inputs, whereas the residual blocks (d0–d3) are trained independently in each branch to reduce inter-task interference. Multi-Scale Context Feature Enhancement (MS-CFE)

MS-CFE refines features through channel and spatial attention, adjusts the channel dimension with a 1×1 convolution, and then applies three parallel depthwise convolution branches with 3×3, 5×5, and 7×7 kernels. The multi-scale outputs are fused through element-wise multiplication, channel shuffle, and pointwise projection, followed by a residual connection to preserve input information.

EULAM

EULAM restores spatial resolution using bilinear interpolation followed by a 3×3 depthwise convolution. It then uses LGAG to recalibrate skip features from the encoder. Loss function

To jointly optimize nuclei segmentation, instantiation, and classification, we define a multi-task loss composed of three branch-specific terms. For the NS branch, binary cross entropy loss and dice loss are used to balance pixel-wise classification and regional overlap. For the NI branch, mean squared error loss and gradient consistency loss are used to supervise the horizontal and vertical distance maps and preserve boundary geometry. The classification loss combines a cost matrix for minority-class misclassification with batch-wise class weighting to improve supervision for rare nuclear categories.

Datasets

Public benchmark: CoNSeP and MoNuSeg

We evaluated the model’s generalization performance on two public benchmarks: CoNSeP and MoNuSeg. The CoNSeP dataset (44) contains 41 H&E-stained colorectal cancer image tiles acquired at 40× magnification, each with a resolution of 1,000×1,000 pixels. While the original annotations encompass seven nuclear types, to maintain comparative consistency with state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods and align with the subsequent four-class configuration of our cervical cancer task, we followed the authors’ suggested category merging scheme: normal and malignant epithelial nuclei were merged into the Epithelial class; fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and muscle cells were consolidated into the Spindle class; and inflammatory cells alongside a small number of unclassified dead/miscellaneous cells were retained as Inflammatory and Miscellaneous, respectively. Consequently, the post-consolidation class distribution (illustrated in Figure 4A) comprises Epithelial (5,537, 36%), Spindle (5,706, 37%), Inflammatory (3,941, 25%), and Miscellaneous (371, 2%) nuclei. We also employed MoNuSeg (45), a public multi-organ H&E benchmark, to evaluate nuclei instance segmentation and cross-organ generalization. The experimental collection comprised 37 annotated training images and 14 evaluation images, each with a resolution of 1,000×1,000 pixels. Because MoNuSeg provides nuclear instance annotations without nuclear-type labels, it was used only for segmentation evaluation using Dice, Aggregated Jaccard Index (AJI), DQ, SQ, and PQ. Using a patch size of 540×540 pixels, a stride of 164 pixels, and mirror padding, we obtained 1,813 training patches and 686 evaluation patches.

Private clinical dataset: CervCell-Seg

To further evaluate TiLCA-Net in real-world cervical pathological scenarios, we constructed a private clinical dataset, CervCell-Seg, from cervical pathology cases collected at Shenzhen People’s Hospital. The dataset contained 47 H&E-stained image tiles from 20 independent female patients. According to the clinical records, patient age ranged from 34 to 78 years, with a mean age of 52.0±13.0 years and a median age of 50 years. The specimens were collected between May 10, 2022 and December 21, 2023. All private clinical data were de-identified before analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (approval No. LL-KY-2025300-01), and informed consent was waived because of the retrospective and de-identified nature of the study.

The diagnostic spectrum was dominated by squamous carcinoma and squamous epithelial lesion-related pathology, with one case of cervical adenocarcinoma. This composition reflects the dominant pathological patterns encountered in cervical cancer practice. The selected cases covered heterogeneous tumor morphology, inflammatory infiltration, stromal components, and variable nuclear appearances, providing clinically relevant morphological diversity for model evaluation. All selected slides were prepared using routine H&E-staining according to the standard clinical pathology workflow. WSIs were digitized using an SQS-600P digital pathology scanning system (TEKSQRAY, Shenzhen Shengqiang Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and viewed with DPVIEW software (version V2.0.11.20240801). The slides were scanned at 40× magnification, corresponding to a spatial resolution of 0.205915 μm/pixel. The scanner metadata recorded an objective lens model of UPlanXAPO20X and an instrument identifier of SQS600P-20220004. From these WSIs, representative image tiles of 1,000×1,000 pixels were selected for annotation and analysis.

Nuclear instance boundaries and category labels were annotated under the supervision of expert pathologists and stored in JSON format. To improve annotation reliability, all annotations were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists before final inclusion. Cases with inconsistent interpretations were re-evaluated and resolved through consensus discussion. By integrating pathological diagnostic criteria with computer-vision requirements, the annotated nuclei were categorized into four classes: tumor, normal, inflammatory, and spindle-shaped. The final dataset contained 12,279 annotated nuclear instances, including 1,690 tumor nuclei (13.76%), 6,094 normal nuclei (49.63%), 3,629 inflammatory nuclei (29.56%), and 866 Spindle-shaped nuclei (7.05%), as shown in Figure 4B. Dataset partition and quality control

For CoNSeP, we followed the official dataset partition, using 27 image tiles for training and 14 image tiles for testing. For MoNuSeg, the experimental collection was divided at the source-image level, with 37 annotated images used for training and 14 images used for evaluation. Patch extraction was performed after this division, and no source image appeared in both subsets. For CervCell-Seg, dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level to avoid potential data leakage and overestimation of model performance. Image tiles from the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training or testing subset. Specifically, 33 image tiles from 14 patients were used for training, while 14 image tiles from the remaining 6 patients were used for testing. No patient overlap existed between the two subsets. The same dataset-specific partitions were used for all comparative experiments to ensure fair model evaluation.

Before annotation and patch extraction, all candidate image regions were reviewed to ensure sufficient tissue content and recognizable nuclear morphology. Image tiles with severe out-of-focus blur, tissue folding, extensive background regions, or severe staining artifacts were excluded. The retained tiles were selected to cover representative cervical pathological structures, including tumor regions, inflammatory microenvironments, stromal components, and heterogeneous nuclear morphologies.

For patch extraction, CervCell-Seg, CoNSeP, and MoNuSeg followed the same sliding-window preprocessing strategy. Specifically, patches were extracted using a window size of 540×540 pixels and a stride of 164×164 pixels. Mirror padding was applied to edge regions to maintain a consistent patch size. This preprocessing protocol was kept identical for TiLCA-Net and all compared baseline models, including HoVer-Net, to ensure a fair comparison.

Implementation details

The proposed network was implemented using the PyTorch framework. The patch-level runtime benchmark reported in this study was measured on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti graphics processing unit (GPU) under a unified evaluation script. During training, data augmentation was applied, including random rotations, flipping, and cropping. The training procedure consisted of two stages: in the first stage (50 epochs), only the decoder parameters were updated; in the second stage (150 epochs), all layers were fine-tuned. For the reproduced HoVer-Net (14), SMILE (38), and TiLCA-Net experiments, the same dataset partitions and core training settings were used whenever applicable to ensure fair comparison. Specifically, the Adam optimizer was utilized with an initial learning rate of 10−4, which decayed by a factor of 0.1 every 25 epochs. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments employed fixed random seeds across PyTorch, NumPy, and CUDA backends, with deterministic convolution algorithms enabled.

Results

Experimental results and comparison

To comprehensively validate the performance of TiLCA-Net in nuclei segmentation and panoptic analysis tasks, we conducted comparative experiments against a selection of representative baseline models. The comparative methods encompass classic semantic segmentation architectures, such as U-Net (12), Mask-RCNN (23), DIST (48), and Micro-Net (49), as well as SOTA models in the field of pathological image analysis, including NC-Net (50), CDNet (51), Diffmix (52), and HoVer-Net (14). The experiments were conducted on the public CoNSeP and MoNuSeg datasets and the proprietary clinical CervCell-Seg dataset constructed in this study. For methods reproduced in this study, including HoVer-Net, SMILE, and TiLCA-Net, we used the same dataset partitions and preprocessing protocols described in Section 2.6.3. UN-SAM and HoVer-NeXt (53) were initialized from their official pretrained weights and then fine-tuned on the corresponding training sets. Results for previously published methods that were not reproduced in our environment were taken from the original publications and are reported as single values. Therefore, only the reproduced or newly evaluated methods are reported with mean ± standard deviation.

In particular, the improvement in PQ indicates that TiLCA-Net achieved a better balance between nuclear DQ and SQ. These results suggest that TiLCA-Net provides a more favorable balance between instance detection, boundary delineation, and computational efficiency among the reproduced methods.

Overall, the model achieved consistent improvements in both classification accuracy (NC-ACC) and weighted F1-score (NC-F1) across both datasets. Specifically, on CoNSeP, NC-ACC increased by 1.9 percentage points to 0.9128, with NC-F1 reaching 0.6561. On CervCell-Seg, NC-ACC and NC-F1 reached 0.9460 and 0.7434, respectively; these results indicate that the proposed TSEncoder played a positive role in mitigating multi-task feature conflicts and ameliorating negative transfer. Furthermore, at a fine-grained level, the model demonstrated a balanced classification capability: it outperformed HoVer-Net in specific categories on the CoNSeP dataset [such as Dice_0 (0.9551±0.0016) and Dice_2 (0.7313±0.0147)] and similarly surpassed the comparative baseline on the CervCell-Seg dataset, achieving 0.6115±0.0250 for Dice_1 and 0.4741±0.0114 for Dice_4 on CervCell-Seg. On the CoNSeP dataset, TiLCA-Net exhibits marginal decreases in Dice_3 and Dice_4, corresponding to the Inflammatory and Miscellaneous categories. These classes are characterized by high morphological heterogeneity and ambiguous boundaries, where the task-specialized attention strategy may allocate relatively less capacity compared to a shared encoder that implicitly averages features across all classes. Nevertheless, the overall classification accuracy and F1 score remain superior, indicating that the performance trade-off is localized to minority categories and does not compromise global discriminative ability.

Table 6 demonstrates the effects of progressively introducing the proposed components. M1 served as a lightweight shared-encoder baseline, whereas M2 represented a stronger hard-parameter-sharing baseline with higher encoder capacity. Compared with M2, introducing the task-specialized encoder branches in M3 improved Dice from 0.8429±0.0066 to 0.8450±0.0016, AJI from 0.5519±0.0011 to 0.5567±0.0055, DQ from 0.7605±0.0012 to 0.7627±0.0044, and PQ from 0.5880±0.0035 to 0.5899±0.0016. Further adding the ICE module in M4 increased DQ to 0.7641±0.0042 and PQ to 0.5922±0.0047, indicating that cross-task feature enhancement contributes to more reliable nuclear detection and instance-level quality. These results indicate that the LCA-Decoder improves the accuracy-complexity trade-off by reducing theoretical computation while maintaining competitive instance segmentation performance.

Table 8 evaluates the classification performance of different component configurations on CervCell-Seg. Compared with the hard-parameter-sharing baseline M2, introducing the task-specialized encoder in M3 increased Acc_NC from 0.9456±0.0090 to 0.9479±0.0023 and F1_NC from 0.7505±0.0012 to 0.7541±0.0042. Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that TiLCA-Net achieves competitive performance with lower theoretical computational cost. TiLCA-Net achieved PQ scores of 0.528, 0.578, and 0.592 on CoNSeP, MoNuSeg, and CervCell-Seg, respectively, outperforming HoVer-Net on all three datasets. These results validate the effectiveness of our proposed multi-scale attention injection strategy and efficient decoding architecture. The observed performance gains can be attributed to three synergistic mechanisms. First, the TSEncoder’s hierarchical attention deployment—SAB for shallow texture features, GGCA for mid-level geometric modeling, and CAB for deep semantic abstraction—aligns with the natural feature evolution in deep networks, effectively mitigating negative transfer (35,38). Second, the ICE module’s closed-loop iteration with median pooling demonstrates effectiveness in clinical WSIs containing staining artifacts and background interference. Third, the LCA-Decoder reduces the FLOPs of the complete TiLCA-Net configuration while maintaining competitive segmentation performance through depthwise separable convolutions and multi-scale feature aggregation.

It is worth noting that TiLCA-Net reduces FLOPs but has a larger parameter count than HoVer-Net. This increase primarily stems from the three task-specialized encoder branches, which are introduced to mitigate feature conflict among segmentation, instance separation, and classification tasks. In patch-level benchmarking on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti, TiLCA-Net achieved higher batch throughput than HoVer-Net, but it also showed higher single-patch latency and peak GPU memory consumption. Therefore, the efficiency advantage of TiLCA-Net should be interpreted as improved batch-processing throughput under the tested setting, rather than a proportional speed-up implied by FLOPs alone.

While TiLCA-Net demonstrates improvements over HoVer-Net, it is important to contextualize these results. Recent approaches such as CellViT (15) leverage Vision Transformers to capture long-range dependencies; however, transformer-based methods typically require substantially larger computational resources. Direct quantitative comparison on CoNSeP is currently infeasible, as CellViT was evaluated on PanNuke under substantially different training protocols (ViT-SAM backbone pre-trained on 104M histological patches). TiLCA-Net offers a complementary CNN-based solution that emphasizes the balance between segmentation performance and computational complexity, while further deployment-oriented optimization remains necessary before routine use on clinical workstations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged: (I) although the evaluation includes CoNSeP, MoNuSeg, and CervCell-Seg, further validation on larger and more diverse public datasets, such as PanNuke and MoNuSAC, is still required; (II) CervCell-Seg is a relatively small single-institution dataset, which limits broad clinical generalizability; validation on larger multi-center cohorts is therefore needed; (III) patch-level runtime and statistical results should be interpreted cautiously, as whole-slide runtime depends on tiling settings and overlapping patches do not represent independent patient-level evidence. Future work will address these through extended multi-tissue evaluation, multi-center clinical validation, and deployment optimization via quantization and pruning.

TiLCA-Net’s reduced FLOPs and higher batch throughput suggest potential utility for batch processing of WSI-derived patches. Nevertheless, practical deployment will require further optimization and validation under whole-slide inference workflows, including tiling strategy, post-processing time, memory scheduling, and hardware-specific acceleration. Accurate NC provides the foundation for deriving prognostic biomarkers such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density scores, nuclear pleomorphism indices, and spatial heterogeneity metrics for TME characterization.

Conclusions

To address feature alignment bias, limited long-range context modeling, and computational redundancy in H&E-stained pathological image analysis, this paper proposes TiLCA-Net, a multi-task network designed to balance segmentation accuracy and computational cost. Through a systematic architectural design, the proposed method strives to strike a better balance between accuracy and efficiency in nuclei instance segmentation and fine-grained classification tasks.

First, to mitigate the issues of feature conflict and negative transfer inherent in multi-task learning, we designed the TSEncoder mechanism. By embedding spatial, dynamic global, and channel attention at the feature extraction level, this module facilitates the differentiated representation of branch features, thereby improving the alignment between feature flows and specific task requirements. Second, targeting noise interference within complex TMEs, we introduced the ICE module. Leveraging a closed-loop iteration of CCA and a median robust enhancement mechanism, this module bolsters the model’s capacity to model global geometric consistency. Finally, we constructed an LCA-Decoder, which employs a hierarchical progressive feature aggregation strategy to minimize computational redundancy while preserving high-resolution details for fine-grained reconstruction.

Experimental evaluations on the public CoNSeP and MoNuSeg datasets and the proprietary clinical CervCell-Seg dataset indicate that TiLCA-Net achieves competitive performance across multiple evaluation metrics, demonstrating advantages over baseline models such as HoVer-Net. Notably, in cervical cancer nuclei classification tests derived from real-world clinical scenarios, the model demonstrates improved recognition capability for challenging samples—such as inflammatory cells with high morphological similarity—benefiting from the microenvironmental context modeling provided by the ICE module. Furthermore, ablation studies verified the effectiveness of individual components. While maintaining competitive PQ, TiLCA-Net reduces FLOPs relative to HoVer-Net and achieves higher batch throughput in patch-level benchmarking, with increased single-patch latency and memory usage.

In summary, TiLCA-Net highlights the performance potential of nuclei panoptic analysis under a computationally efficient design. Its performance across public benchmarks and the available private clinical cohort suggests potential utility for auxiliary computational pathology analysis, although larger multi-center validation, whole-slide-level evaluation, and deployment optimization are needed before clinical application.
