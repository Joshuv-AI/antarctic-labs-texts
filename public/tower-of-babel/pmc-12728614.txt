Nanoamplifier Agents Transiently Rise the Metabolism of β‑Amyloid Peptide in Urine for the Early Diagnosis of Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Yun Zhao, Enpeng Xi, Ziwei Wang, Nan Gao, Hanjun Sun, Guangshan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia without effective treatment. Therefore, early diagnosis for timely treatment and delayof the onset of AD are critical. At present, detecting β-amyloid (Aβ) in cerebrospinal fluid is still the most important clinical method. However, the invasive detection method is harmful and difficult to promote. Recent research has shown that Aβ was found not only in blood and cerebrospinal fluid, but also in urine, which could be used for noninvasive testing. Compared with blood/cerebrospinal fluid, the background proteins in urine are very low, but unfortunately the content of Aβ is even lower. Therefore, if the concentration of Aβ is increased with the background proteins maintained at the low level, urine could be an ideal noninvasive early detection target for AD. Gold nanoparticles (AuNP) with ultrasmall size (<6 nm) could be rapidly metabolized by the kidneys and excreted with urine, and easily regulated by the metabolic pathway between kidneys and liver by changing their size. After screening, we found 3 nm AuNP had the highest renal metabolic efficiency, and by modifying with kidney targeting peptides and Aβ antibody 6E10, the complex system (P6–Au) acted as a “Aβ-targeting renal metabolic carrier”, which both metabolized rapidly through the kidneys and increased the concentration of Aβ in urine. After tail vein injection of P6–Au, the Aβ content in the urine of 5×FAD transgenic mice increased by more than 20 times within the next 24 h, which resulted in the diagnosis time being advanced from the ninth month to the fifth month and provided a new approach for early detection of AD.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease with insidious onset and lacks effective treatment methods. The prolonged course of AD brings enormous nursing and economic pressure to society and families. At present, the number of deaths caused by stroke, heart disease and HIV has declined worldwide, but the number of deaths caused by AD has increased by more than 140%. − With the gradual increase in human life expectancy, AD may become the most prevalent disease in the world. − However, currently, there is no effective treatment for Alzheimer’s disease, and the only way to delay its progression is through collaborative intervention by both families and hospitals. The earlier intervention is carried out, the more ideal the delay effect for AD will be. Therefore, achieving early detection for AD is crucial for diagnosis. −

Up to now, detecting β-amyloid (Aβ) in cerebrospinal fluid is still the most important clinical method, which is very difficult to carry out in the population due to its invasive sampling method and high costs. − Detecting Aβ in the blood as a diagnostic basis for AD has been proposed as well, but the abundance of proteins and complex fluid conditions in the blood greatly affect its detection accuracy. Furthermore, it is difficult to continuously obtain cerebrospinal fluid or blood from elderly patients, not only due to physiological tolerance issues, but also due to inconvenience in daily life. − As a result, the early detection rate of AD in patients is currently less than 10%, and the detection rate in economically underdeveloped areas is even less than 1%. − Therefore, the method based on biological samples that are more easily obtainable, except cerebrospinal fluid and blood samples, could be valid for early AD diagnosis.

Due to the filtration effect of the glomerulus, only proteins below 40 kDa can enter the original urine through the normal glomerulus. − Therefore, Aβ of 4 kDa was found to exist not only in cerebrospinal fluid and blood but also in urine, where the background proteins are significantly low compared to those in blood and cerebrospinal fluid. But unfortunately, although Aβ can enter the original urine through the glomerulus, most Aβ will be reabsorbed back into the bloodstream through the renal tubules, resulting in low levels of Aβ in the final urine as well. , Thus, if the concentration of Aβ is increased with the background proteins maintained at the low level, urine could be an ideal noninvasive early detection target for AD. The Aβ-targeting renal metabolic carrier is urgently needed to achieve the transfer of Aβ from blood to urine and increase the Aβ concentration transiently in urine without causing significant changes of background proteins.

Gold nanoparticles (AuNP) with ultrasmall size (<6 nm) could be rapidly metabolized by the kidneys and excreted with urine and easily regulated by the metabolic pathway between the kidneys and liver by changing their size. Herein, a series of AuNP with different sizes and their metabolic pathways were investigated, and after screening, 3 nm AuNP displayed the best renal metabolic property. − The kidney targeting peptides (P) and Aβ antibody 6E10 (6) were subsequently modified on the surface of AuNP to realize the specific capture of Aβ. The experimental results show that this AuNP complex (named P6–Au) could capture Aβ in the original urine, prevent Aβ from being reabsorbed into blood, and increase the concentration of Aβ in urine. After the tail vein injection of P6–Au, the Aβ content in the urine of 5×FAD transgenic mice increased by more than 20 times within the next 24 h. Meanwhile, there was no significant change for the background proteins in the urine, which allowed for earlier diagnosis at the fifth month compared to the ninth month and provided a new approach for noninvasive early detection of AD in clinical practice (Scheme ).

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of AuNP of Different Sizes

AuNP, as an easily modifiable and renally excretable nanomaterial, can be used as a urine Aβ amplifier in this study. It is worth noting that when AuNP are metabolized by the kidneys, they needs to pass through the filtration of the glomerulus in order to enter the original urine, and the size of AuNP is closely related to the filtration of the glomerulus. Therefore, a series of 3–200 nm gold nanoparticles were synthesized using NaBH4 and sodium citrate reduction of the HAuCl4 method. The UV–visible absorption spectrum indicates that AuNPs ranging from 3 to 200 nm exhibit characteristic absorption peaks at 520 nm (Figure a). − As the size continues to increase, the plasmon resonance frequency of AuNP gradually decreases, and the characteristic absorption peak of AuNP shows a slight red shift. The hydration particle size indicates the synthesis of AuNP at 3, 5, 15, 50, 100, and 200 nm (Figure b). − The zeta potential indicates that these series of AuNP are all negatively charged, consistent with the potential of citric acid stabilized AuNP (Figure c). TEM and SEM images indicate that these series of AuNP are roughly spherical, and as the size increases, the formed spherical shape becomes more irregular (Figure d, e). The observed size is basically consistent with the hydrated particle size. HRTEM images show a clear lattice, which gradually increases with increasing AuNP size. The above experiments demonstrate the successful synthesis of AuNP within the range of 3–200 nm (Figure f).

Metabolism of AuNP with Different Sizes

In order to investigate the metabolism of AuNP of 3–200 nm in vivo, FITC was used to label AuNP of different sizes, and the sizes remained basically unchanged after labeling (Figure S1). AuNP have a quenching effect on FITC, and the smaller the size of AuNP, the stronger the quenching ability, but they can still maintain a certain fluorescence intensity. AuNP of 3 nm can still maintain a fluorescence intensity of 26.70 ± 2.96% (Figure S2). As shown in Figure a-c, the metabolism of AuNP in vivo was investigated by tail vein injection. The In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) showed that as the size of AuNP decreased, the fluorescence of renal metabolism significantly increased, and the peak of renal metabolism appeared 9–12 h after injection. Any size of AuNP will be metabolized by the liver, and as the size of AuNP gradually increases, the liver metabolism increases and the residual metabolism time in the body gradually increases. The above experiments indicate that small gold nanoparticles are more easily metabolized by the kidneys. In addition, the results of using the ICP method to investigate the metabolism of AuNPs with the same dose but different sizes injected through the tail vein (Figure d, e) in the liver and kidney are similar to the above conclusion, which once again proves that small-sized nanogold is more easily metabolized by the kidneys. Thus, 3 nm AuNP were selected as the basic material for synthesizing an urine Aβ amplifier.

Synthetic Nanoamplifier P6–Au

In order to further enhance the renal metabolism ability of 3 nm AuNPs and their specific binding ability to Aβ, we subsequently modified the surface of AuNPs (Figure a). First, the surface of AuNP was modified with 3-mercaptopropionic acid to synthesize Au-COOH. By titrating the free carboxyl groups on the surface of the Au-COOH, the results calculated according to the literature showed an average of 145.36 ± 20.56 free carboxyl groups per AuNP. , Then, side chain amino shielded kidney targeted peptide (P) (0.19 × 10–3 μmol/mL) was added to Au-COOH (0.38 × 10–3 μmol/mL) and connected to the surface free carboxyl groups via EDC/NHS coupling pathway to synthesize P–Au. Finally, excess 6E10 was added to P–Au and connected to the remaining surface free carboxyl groups via the EDC/NHS coupling pathway to synthesize P6–Au. At the same time, in order to investigate the influence of different modification conditions on the nanoamplifier, 6-Au of the same equivalent was synthesized as a control. Based on previous research, the number of P and 6E10 on each nanoamplifier was determined using the BCA method, and the results showed that on average there were 68.27 ± 15.34 P and 8 ± 2.93 6E10 on one nanoamplifier, proving the successful synthesis of the P6–Au nanoamplifier.

The various stages of the synthesis process for P6–Au were characterized. The hydration particle size results showed that the particle size of AuNP gradually increased under different modifications, and the particle size of P6–Au was 9.83 ± 0.65 nm (Figure S3). This may be due to the surface modification of AuNP by target peptide P and antibody 6E10, resulting in an increase in the size. FT-IR analysis confirmed the formation of corresponding chemical bonds at each step of the synthesis and modification process (Figure b). The stretching vibration signal at 1788 cm–1 corresponds to CO, while for AuNP it corresponds to the stabilizer citric acid on its surface. In Au-COOH, it corresponds to the free carboxyl groups on its surface. In P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au correspond to the peptides or proteins on its surface. The 1652 cm–1 signal corresponds to the stretching vibration signal of OC−NH, which appears after the modification of P and 6E10 on AuNP, indicating successful surface modification of P and 6E10 on AuNP. The 623 cm–1 signal is the stretching vibration signal of Au–S, which does not exist in AuNP. When using 3-mercaptopropionic acid to modify AuNP, this peak appears and exists in subsequent modified materials, indicating the stable existence of P and 6E10 connected by Au–S bonds throughout the synthesis process. The XPS results indicate that only two XPS peaks of Au 4f corresponding to Au (0) are displayed in AuNP (Figure c), indicating that gold ions have been reduced to AuNP. In addition to the two XPS peaks corresponding to Au (0), there are also two XPS peaks corresponding to Au (1) in Au-COOH, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au, which correspond to gold in the Au–S group. This indicates that Au–S is always present at all stages during the synthesis and modification process. AuNP and Au-COOH did not show any XPS peaks of N 1s (Figure d). P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au exhibit two N1S XPS peaks, attributed to nitrogen in CN or N–H groups at 399.8 eV and to nitrogen in OC−NH groups at 410.1 eV. − This result proves that the surfaces of the AuNP haves been successfully modified with P and 6E10. UV–visible absorption spectroscopy analysis (Figure S4) shows that with the surface modification process of AuNP, the peak undergoes a red shift and the peak intensity decreases, which is similar to previous reports. It also proves that P and 6E10 have been successfully modified on the surfaces of AuNP. The SEM and TEM images (Figure e, f) indicate that the materials synthesized at each stage are essentially spherical in shape and their size is consistent with the hydrated particle size. The zeta potential results (Figure S5) indicate that the zeta potential of AuNP increased from −28.25 to −0.19 mV after modification with P, then to 0.27 mV after modification with 6E10, and finally the potential of P6–Au was 0.51 mV. After modification, a potential change was generated, which once again proves the successful synthesis of P6–Au.

In Vivo Metabolism of Nanoamplifier P6–Au

First, the binding ability of P6–Au to Aβ in vitro was tested. AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au were added to the Aβ solution and mixed at room temperature for 1.5 h on a shaker. After centrifugation, the precipitate was taken for Western-blotting (WB). The results show (Figures a, S6) that only P6–Au and 6-Au can bind to Aβ. After detection of Aβ in the supernatant, Aβ decreased in the supernatant of the P6–Au and 6-Au groups, while there was no significant change in Aβ in the supernatant of the other groups (Figure S7). The above research indicates that the surface modified 6E10 with AuNP exhibits excellent ability to capture Aβ. Subsequently, the biological toxicity of P6–Au was investigated. Incubate P6–Au at different concentrations with Raw264.7 and 3T3 cells for 24 and 72 h, respectively. As shown in Figure S8, even with a P6–Au concentration of up to 100 μg/mL, the survival rates of both types of cells reached over 80%. The hemolysis experiment (Figure S9) showed that under the action of 100 μg/mL P6–Au, the hemolysis rate was less than 5%, indicating that P6–Au has strong biocompatibility. Cytotoxicity and hemolysis experiments showed that AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au all had low cytotoxicity (Figures S10, S11). According to previous studies, a concentration of 250 μg/kg (weight) was selected for in vivo injection. Following the schedule in Figure S12, AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au were injected into the tail vein of mice to verify their in vivo safety. The weight monitoring results showed that there was no significant decrease in the weight. Blood and major organs were collected according to the schedule, and there was no significant increase in the level of inflammation in the blood. There was no obvious damage to the liver, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys, which also proves that P6–Au has good biocompatibility (Figures S13–S15).

To investigate the metabolic distribution of P6–Au and other control materials, AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au were labeled with FITC. The changes in particle size and fluorescence intensity over time after FITC labeling are shown in Figures S16 and S17. After labeling with FITC, the size remained basically unchanged, and the fluorescence intensity decreased slightly but still retained a certain fluorescence intensity. P6–Au and other control materials were injected into mice via the tail vein according to the schedule shown in Figure b, and fluorescence imaging of major organs was performed at different times. The results show (Figure c-e) that compared to AuNP, P–Au undergoes increased renal metabolism, while 6-Au undergoes decreased renal metabolism. The fluorescence imaging results indicate that AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au and P6–Au are mainly metabolized by the liver and kidneys. Therefore, the gold content in the liver and kidneys at different time points was measured by ICP. The results showed that they were consistent with the fluorescence imaging results, reaching the peak of renal metabolism at 9–12 h after injection, and the gold content in the liver and kidney basically returned to the preinjection level within 24 h (Figure f, g). The results of tissue fluorescence analysis and kidney gold content (Tables S1 and S2) showed that the kidney fluorescence intensity of the 6-Au group was only 44.43 ± 6.31% of that of the AuNP group, and the kidney gold content was only 57.70 ± 11.57% of that of the AuNP group. This may be due to the lack of kidney targeting peptides and the increased size of 6-Au, which leads to a decrease in renal metabolism. The size of P6–Au slightly increased, but compared to AuNP, P6–Au still maintained a fluorescence intensity of 89.90 ± 9.11% and a renal gold content of 88.53 ± 5.90% at 9 h after injection. This indicates that P6–Au still maintains high renal metabolism, once again proving the excellent renal targeting effect of renal targeting peptide P. In summary, WB experiments and in vivo metabolic studies have shown that P6–Au achieves the capture and effective renal metabolism of Aβ, providing the possibility for urine Aβ amplification.

The Amplification Effect of Nanoamplifier P6–Au

Based on the encouraging amplification effect of P6–Au, we evaluated the in vivo efficacy of P6–Au in a 5×FAD mouse model. P6–Au, AuNP, P–Au and 6-Au were suspended in normal saline and injected into mice via the tail vein according to different groups. At 9 h after injection, mouse urine was collected. After centrifugation, the precipitate was taken for the WB experiment. The results showed (Figures a, S18) that bands appeared for the P6–Au group and 6-Au group of AD mice, and the 6-Au group band intensity was significantly lower than the P6–Au group. No bands were observed for wild type (WT) mice. The reason for this may be that Aβ of 4 kDa size can freely pass through the glomerulus into the renal tubule, where it forms a P6–Au-Aβ complex with P6–Au. Compared to free Aβ, the size of P6–Au-Aβ increases, causing the proximal tubules to be unable to reabsorb P6–Au-Aβ, and ultimately, P6–Au-Aβ will be excreted from the urine. Therefore, the concentration of Aβ in urine increased after the injection of P6–Au. Although 6-Au also accumulates Aβ, the lack of targeted peptide P resulted in significantly lower levels of Aβ in the urine of mice injected with 6-Au compared with the P6–Au group. The reason for the absence of bands in the WT group may be due to the low content of Aβ in normal mouse body fluids; therefore, even after amplification with P6–Au, no bands were observed. Then we collected urine samples from the P6–Au group of AD mice from 0 to 72 h for WB analysis. The results showed (Figures b, S19) that the metabolic peak was reached at 9 h and a clear band was observed. The band disappeared at 24 h, which is similar to the previous fluorescence experiment results. This indicates that P6–Au metabolism ends within 24 h in the body, and after the end of P6–Au metabolism, the amplification effect of urine Aβ disappears, proving that P6–Au is a transient urine Aβ nanoamplifier. To investigate the amplification effect of urine Aβ by P6–Au, urine samples were collected from AD mice 9 h after injection with P6–Au; and injection of saline was used as a control. We concentrated urine 0–40 times and analyzed it through the WB experiment. The results showed that significant bands could be observed when the urine of the Saline group was concentrated 20 times (Figure c). As shown in Figure S20, the quantitative results of WB indicate that the urine band of the Saline group concentrated 20 times is the same as that of the nonconcentrated P6–Au group, proving that P6–Au, as an urine Aβ amplifier, achieves approximately 20 times the release of urine Aβ. P6–Au effectively increased the content of Aβ in urine, which helps to achieve an early diagnosis of AD by detecting Aβ in urine.

In order to explore the effect of P6–Au on urine background protein, the increase of urine Aβ protein caused by the increase in urine background protein caused by kidney injury was excluded. BCA and proteomics were used to detect the urine background protein with or without the P6–Au injection. First, the urine protein content was detected by BCA. As shown in Figure d, there was no significant difference in the urine protein content between the Blank group and P6–Au group. Then the correlation between samples was analyzed. The Pearson correlation coefficient (R) was used as the evaluation index of biological repeatability correlation, and the correlation between the P6–Au group and Blank group was strong (Figure e). Finally, the differences of urine proteins between the Blank group and P6–Au group were analyzed by urine proteomics. The distribution analysis of urine protein abundance value showed that the box shape of biological duplicate samples was close to the violin shape, and the morphology of Blank group and P6–Au group were basically the same (Figure f). The above results showed that the injection of P6–Au did not cause significant changes in the content of the urine background protein. This indicates that the increase in Aβ concentration in urine after P6–Au injection is due to the specific capture of Aβ by P6–Au in the kidney, which leads to the increase in Aβ metabolism. It is not due to obvious kidney injury, which leads to an extensive increase of urine background protein and ultimately the increase of Aβ metabolism. The feasibility of P6–Au as a urine Aβ amplifier was proved.

In order to investigate the effects of using P6–Au on other urine detection methods. The ELISA method was used to detect Aβ (0.5 ng/mL) and 6E10 (5 ng/mL) coincubated systems in vitro. The results showed (Figure S21) that after 6E10 binding, the concentration of Aβ detected by the ELISA method decreased. This may be due to the spatial hindrance generated by the binding of 6E10 to Aβ, which blocks the ELISA antibody binding site on Aβ, resulting in a decrease in the detected Aβ content. We noticed that the free Aβ is a linear arrangement of 40–42 amino acids, lacking complex tertiary structures and only a small number of secondary structures (alpha helices). Therefore, Aβ has strong thermal stability. As a protein with special functions and tertiary structure, 6E10 has poor thermal stability, and thermal denaturation can effectively change the structure and function of the protein. In addition, the binding of antigens and antibodies is mainly achieved through small interaction forces such as van der Waals forces, resulting in poor thermal stability. , Therefore, thermal deformation is expected to re ionize Aβ bound to 6E10. To investigate the effect of the temperature on the binding force between 6E10 and Aβ, 6E10 bound to Aβ will be heated. As shown in Figure S22, the ELISA detection results gradually increase with the increase of temperature, reaching the highest level at 75 °C, and then gradually decrease. The reason may be that as the temperature increases, 6E10 gradually denatures, releasing bound Aβ.

After 75 °C, Aβ also underwent denaturation and could not restore its original structure, making it undetectable. Therefore, 75 °C may be the critical point for 6E10 denaturation and Aβ invariance. However, even after treatment at 75 °C, the detection results still did not reach the initial input amount of Aβ (0.5 ng/mL), possibly due to the retention of a portion of 6E10 and Aβ that cannot be thermally deformed and dissociated. Therefore, a small amount of SDS was added as a denaturing agent on the basis of heating. The results are shown in Figure S23. After the addition of 0.12% SDS, the ELISA detection results were consistent with the initial Aβ input. When too much SDS was added, the ELISA detection results decreased significantly, which may be due to the influence of SDS added to the system on the antibodies in ELISA, resulting in undetectability. The above experiment shows that after adding 0.12% SDS and heating at 75 °C for 10 min, the 6E10 bound Aβ can be completely dissociated without affecting the detection results of Aβ by ELISA. After sufficient interaction between P6–Au (50 μg/mL) and Aβ (500 ng) in vitro, 0.12% SDS was added and heated at 75 °C for 10 min. The ELISA detection results were basically consistent with the input amount (Figure S24). This indicates that the use of SDS and heating in P6–Au can also dissociate P6–Au from Aβ and re ionize Aβ, expanding the detection range of urine Aβ.

The concentration of Aβ in the urine of mice injected with AuNP, P–Au, 6-Au, and P6–Au was detected using the ELISA method (urine was treated as described above). The results showed that the Aβ in AD mouse urine was 23.13 pg/mL, and after injection of P6–Au, the Aβ in urine reached 488.36 pg/mL (Figure g). Using the ELISA method to monitor the temporal changes of Aβ in mouse urine again, it was once again demonstrated that the peak of renal metabolism was reached 9 h after injection (Figure h). Monitoring of urine Aβ in mice aged 2–12 months using P6–Au showed a gradual increase in Aβ levels in AD mice urine from 2 to 8 months (Figure i). By analyzing the urine Aβ content of mice injected or not injected with P6–Au, it was found that after injection of P6–Au, there was a significant difference in urine Aβ content between AD mice and WT mice at the age of 5 months (P < 0.001), and stable significant differences were observed between the ages of 5 to 12 months (P < 0.001). However, the difference in Aβ content in urine between AD mice and WT mice that were not injected with P6–Au was only observed at the age of 9 months (0.01 < P < 0.05). This difference is not stable and no significant difference was observed in the subsequent tests at the age of 11 and 12 months (P > 0.05). As shown above, P6–Au can detect the difference between AD mice and WT mice 4 months in advance by amplifying the content of Aβ in urine. And this stable and significant difference increases the credibility of the results (Figure j). Therefore, P6–Au is expected to achieve early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease by amplifying Aβ in urine, causing a brief increase in Aβ concentration in urine and increasing detection accuracy.

Conclusion

In summary, we report a method for transiently enhancing Aβ metabolism without increasing urinary background protein levels by developing AuNP modified with a kidney-targeting peptide and the Aβ antibody 6E10 (P6–Au), which can be utilized for early detection of AD. Through screening of different sizes of AuNP, we found that 3 nm AuNP had the best renal metabolic properties. After being modified with kidney targeting peptides and Aβ antibody 6E10, P6–Au was found to increase the concentration of Aβ more than 20 times, but not significantly change that for the background proteins in the urine in the 5×FAD transgenic mice model. Under the same ELISA detection method, significant differences could be observed in AD urines that were treated with P6–Au at the fifth month, which was four months earlier than the AD urines that were untreated with P6–Au. In this way, P6–Au exhibited excellent properties of a nanoamplifier agent and provided a new approach for the early detection of AD.
