Mechanisms of ultrasonic enhanced co-leaching of U and REEs from low-grade uranium resources

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Uranium, Tailings, Ultrasound, Alkali pretreatment, Acid leaching, Rare earths

Introduction

Uranium is a key strategic mineral resource and raw material essential for the development of clean, low-carbon, and economical nuclear power, with its strategic value becoming increasingly prominent in the global energy transition [1], [2]. As the global grade of uranium ore declines and investment in uranium exploration and mining continues to decrease, global uranium production fell by approximately one-third between 2016 and 2020 [3]. Against this backdrop, global demand for uranium resources continues to rise. According to the baseline scenario projections of the World Nuclear Association (WNA), under the most ambitious nuclear expansion plans, the global annual reactor-related uranium demand could exceed 100,000 tonnes by 2030 [4]. Taking China as an example, its proactive goal of achieving carbon neutrality by 2060 has accelerated the development of its nuclear power industry. It is projected that by the end of 2025, China's operational nuclear power installed capacity will reach 65 GW [4]. It is estimated that by 2030, China's uranium demand will exceed 20,000 tU, and its external dependence on uranium resources may be as high as 90% [5]. However, due to multiple factors including the depletion of uranium resources, environmental pressures, and geopolitical issues, the security situation of uranium resources globally is becoming increasingly severe [6].

In recent years, the recovery of uranium from unconventional secondary resources (e.g., industrial waste, tailings) has emerged as a sustainable alternative to traditional uranium ore exploitation, aligning with the goals of the circular economy [1]. Among these, uranium tailings, as solid waste generated during uranium mining and metallurgy, not only contain large amounts of refractory uranium but are also often associated with various REEs. REEs serve as core raw materials in fields such as new energy and high-end manufacturing and are regarded as “critical” and “irreplaceable” materials in modern industrial applications [7]. With global economic development and technological advancement, the demand for REEs continues to climb. The global annual production of rare earth oxides has increased from 60,000 tonnes in 1994 to 350,000 tonnes in 2023, while the annual demand is projected to possibly exceed 600,000 tonnes by 2030 [8]. Strategic stockpiling and resource competition for REEs among major economies are intensifying. Consequently, uranium tailings constitute a typical low-grade co-occurring resource reservoir of U and REEs. Their comprehensive recovery holds dual strategic value: alleviating the global uranium supply gap and ensuring the security of the REEs supply chain [9]. Currently, the global stockpile of uranium tailings has reached approximately 24 billion tons, from which nearly 9.6 million tons of U can be extracted, sufficient to meet the uranium demand of the nuclear industry for the next century [10], [11].

Notably, most uranium tailing ponds are located in water-collecting areas such as valleys. Under the long-term influence of rainwater leaching, leakage and migration of U and heavy metal elements are prone to occur. This not only causes severe radioactive contamination of surrounding soil and water bodies but also leads to the waste of secondary rare metals [12]. Therefore, the development of green and efficient metallurgy technologies to recover U and REEs from uranium tailings is not only an urgent need to alleviate the shortage of strategic resources in China but also a key path to eliminate environmental safety hazards of tailing ponds.

For complex low-grade uranium resources, it is difficult to leach U due to severe gangue inclusion and the complex occurrence state of U [13]. The gangue content in uranium tailings is very high, mainly including silicon dioxide, iron silicate salt and aluminum silicate salt [14]. The residual uranium in the tailings mostly embedded in the silicate lattice in the form of fine particles or coexisted with silicate minerals, making it difficult to be effectively leached [15]. It is worth noting that the proportion of residual U(IV) in the tailings is very high, which makes the efficient leaching dependent on strong oxidants. The ionic radius and chemical reactivity of REEs are similar to that of U, which causes REEs often undergo isomorphic substitution with U in the lattices of certain uranium-containing minerals [16]. Therefore, REEs often coexist with U in ores [17]. However, the current focus is mainly on the extraction of U, while the co-recovery of associated REEs is overlooked.

At present, intense reaction conditions are usually employed to leach low-grade uranium resources, such as the use of strong acids and oxidants (e.g., HF, HClO4, KMnO4), as well as high-temperature and high-pressure leaching [15]. Huang et al. [18] and Raileanu et al., [19] used HF, HClO4, MnO2 and H2O2, and potassium permanganate, respectively, to treat low-grade uranium ores. Studies have shown that in a strongly acidic environment, HF and HClO4 can effectively destroy the gangue structure of uranium-containing minerals and significantly promote the leaching of U. Demopoulos et al. [20] performed high-temperature and high-pressure acidic leaching on pyrochlore-containing uranium ores. Under conditions of 125 °C and an oxygen pressure of 345 kPa, the extraction efficiency of U exceeded 93%. It is worth noting that these traditional enhanced methods for uranium leaching have problems such as intense reaction conditions and significant reagent contamination. How to efficiently destroy gangue inclusions, improve leaching conditions, and weaken the re-adsorption phenomenon are the key issues for achieving efficient leaching of low-grade uranium resources [21].

Alkali leaching pretreatment has been used to disrupt the gangue structure and promote the release of U and REEs, and has become a research hotspot in the field of mineral processing in recent years [22]. Li et al. [15] and Fan et al. [23] employed the alkali pretreatment-acid leaching process to treat low-grade uranium tailings and uranium waste residue with different aluminosilicate structures, respectively. Both studies found that the alkali leaching pretreatment could selectively break down gangue components, dissolve silicate structures, and promote the formation of microporous channels, thereby substantially improving the subsequent acid leaching efficiency of uranium. Xing et al. [24] conducted alkali pre-desilication on high-alumina fly ash and found that after treatment with 150 g/L NaOH solution at 80 °C for 2 h, most of the amorphous aluminosilicate phase was dissolved. The released REEs formed rare earth hydroxide precipitates, providing highly active materials for subsequent mechanochemical extraction. Luo et al. [25] found that almost all the alunite in the tailings could be dissolved within 30 min using a high-concentration KOH solution (>13.5 mol/L). Therefore, how to further enhance the alkali pretreatment effect is of great significance for the subsequent acid leaching.

In recent years, external field-enhanced leaching technologies (e.g., ultrasonic wave, microwave, electric field) have become a research hotspot [26], [27], [28], [29]. Ultrasonic-assisted leaching technology exhibits cavitation, mechanical, and thermal effects, which can strengthen the disruption of gangue encapsulation layers and promote the leaching of target metals [30], [31]. [32] employed an ultrasound-assisted process to leach uranium-bearing residues. They found that the high-speed micro-jets, shock waves, and localized high temperature/pressure generated by ultrasonic cavitation could significantly fragment mineral particles and disrupt their encapsulated structure, thereby releasing occluded uranium and ultimately increasing the uranium recovery rate from 72% to 84%. Modiga et al. [33] used ultrasound-assisted alkali pretreatment combined with HCl to leach waste coal slag. They observed that longer ultrasonic duration during alkali pretreatment resulted in smaller particle sizes and, consequently, a higher extraction rate of REEs by subsequent HCl leaching. Xin et al. [34] found that the cavitation effect of ultrasound accelerated the decomposition of H2O2 in H2SO4 solution, generating hydroxyl radicals with stronger oxidizing power, thereby enhancing the overall oxidizing capacity of the solution. Hydrogen peroxide, as a strong oxidizing agent, is widely used in uranium ore leaching. However, under conventional conditions, its decomposition rate is slow and the conversion efficiency to the highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals is low, which limits its oxidative leaching performance [35]. When the cavitation bubbles generated by ultrasound violently collapse, a localized high-temperature and high-pressure environment is created [36]. This promotes the rupture of the oxygen–oxygen bond in H2O2 molecules, yielding hydroxyl radicals that subsequently oxidize insoluble U(IV) to soluble U(VI) [37], [38]. Therefore, the ultrasonic effect can both promote the dissolution of ore encapsulation layers and enhance the oxidizing efficacy of reagents like H2O2, thus facilitating the oxidative leaching of metals. However, the ultrasonic-assisted leaching mechanism of low-grade uranium resources has not been revealed fully. This study takes low-grade uranium resources—uranium tailings associated with rare earth elements—as the research subject and proposes a novel process of ultrasound-enhanced alkali pretreatment followed by acid leaching. The purposes of this study are: (1) to elucidate the leaching thermodynamics, kinetics, and ultrasound-enhanced mechanisms of U and REEs co-leaching in the HCl-H2O2 system, with emphasis on how ultrasound-coupled H2O2 generates hydroxyl radicals to promote the oxidative leaching of U(IV); (2) to systematically investigate the key factors affecting the co-leaching of U and REEs from uranium tailings and to identify the optimal conditions for synergistically enhanced leaching.

Experimental methods

Fig. 1 shows the process flow chart of the combined recovery of U and REEs by ultrasonic-enhanced alkali leaching pretreatment and acid leaching.

Flowchart of the ultrasonic-alkali leaching pretreatment − acid leaching experiment for uranium tailings for the recovery of U and REEs.

The experimental process is mainly divided into the following steps: (1) Alkaline leaching pretreatment: Uranium tailings samples were collected using the quartering method. After mixing the NaOH solution (50–200 g/L) with the uranium tailings at a specific liquid-to-solid (L/S) ratio (4:1–20:1), the mixture was placed into ultrasonic equipment with a frequency of 40 kHz to conduct the leaching experiment, with mechanical agitation maintained at 200 rpm. The leaching temperature was 40–80 °C, the duration was 1.0–3.0 h, and the ultrasonic power was 160–400 W. After the leaching experiment, the concentrations of silicon and aluminum in the filtrate were determined by ICP-OES. The alkali-leached residue was washed several times with distilled water and then dried for subsequent use. Through numerous experiments, the washing frequency and water dosage were determined to be 3 times and 10 mL/g, respectively. After water washing, the pH of the residue stabilized at approximately 7.0 ± 0.2 [23]. Mechanical agitation leaching at 200 rpm was selected as the unified baseline condition for all subsequent experiments, including both ultrasound-assisted leaching and non-ultrasonic blank control experiments. (2) Acid leaching of the pretreated residue: 15 g of the alkali-pretreated residue was placed in a 250 mL conical flask. A certain concentration of HCl solution was added at a liquid-to-solid (L/S) ratio of 5:1. Ultrasound-assisted leaching was then conducted at 50 °C with mechanical agitation at 200 rpm. The leaching time was 1.0–3.0 h, and the ultrasonic power was 160–400 W. After leaching, the concentrations of U and REEs in the filtrate were determined by ICP-OES. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are reported as the mean ± standard deviation.

For ease of description, uranium tailings, ultrasonic alkaline pretreated residue, conventional alkaline pretreated residue, ultrasonic acid leaching residue, and conventional acid leaching residue are abbreviated as UT, U-APR, C-APR, U-ALR, and C-ALR, respectively. Analysis and characterization techniques

The phase composition of the samples was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; λ = 1.54 Å; scan speed 5°/min; Rigaku Smartlab, Japan), with data processed using MDI Jade software [43]. The mass fractions of major elements (e.g., Si, Al, Fe) in the samples were determined by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF; using pressed pellet samples with a Rh target; Rigaku ZSX Primus III+, Japan) [44]. The chemical states of elements were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; C 1s calibration peak at 284.8 eV; Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi, USA), and the data were processed using Advantage software [23]. The micro-morphology and elemental distribution of solid samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS, ZEISS Geminisem 300, Germany) [45]. The pore size and specific surface area of the samples before and after leaching were measured using a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area and porosity analyzer (N2 adsorption atmosphere; Micromeritics ASAP 2460, USA), and the mesoporous (1–50 nm) pore size distribution was analyzed using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model [46].

The particle size distribution and average particle size of the samples were determined using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000; with deionized water as the dispersion medium; UK) [47]. The concentrations of silicon, aluminum, uranium, and REEs in both alkaline and acidic leachates were quantitatively determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5100, Australia). Prior to the analysis of U and REEs concentrations using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry, potential issues such as spectral overlap, matrix effects, and physical interferences were effectively mitigated by selecting discrete characteristic spectral lines, coupled with solvent extraction pre-concentration and a three-stage filtration procedure [48]. The occurrence forms of REEs in the ore sample were analyzed by the sequential extraction procedure proposed by the European Community Bureau of Reference, known as the BCR method, through sequential extraction to obtain the weak acid exchangeable, reducible, oxidizable, and residual fractions [49].

To achieve the co-leaching of U and REEs from UT, it is necessary to identify their respective advantageous leaching conditions. In this study, the leaching thermodynamics of U and four typical REEs (Ce, La, Y, Nd) with relatively high contents were calculated using the thermodynamic software Outotec HSC Chemistry. By retrieving fundamental thermodynamic parameters such as the standard Gibbs free energy of formation and reaction enthalpy for each element in H2SO4, HCl, and HNO3 systems from the software database, the corresponding Eh-pH diagrams in solution were constructed and plotted [50].

An electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR; 25 °C; Dark; Bruker A300, Germany) was used to detect the characteristic spectra of hydroxyl radical (·OH) in the leachate, which was used to analyze the variation law of hydroxyl radicals with different parameters during the ultrasonic enhanced leaching system with H2O2. Results and discussion

Leaching thermodynamics of uranium and rare earths elements

The Eh-pH diagrams for U, Ce, La, Nd, and Y in H2SO4, HNO3, and HCl systems at 25 °C and 70 °C are presented in Fig. 2 and Figs. S1-S4. These diagrams show that as the temperature increases from 25 to 70 °C, the Eh-pH curves for each element shift toward the lower left in all three acid systems. This shift leads to a narrowing of the pH range conducive to their oxidation and dissolution, which can be attributed to a decrease in the solubility of U and REEs with rising temperature. Since the pH of the acid leaching system adopted in this study is less than 1.0, the temperature does not have a significant impact on the leaching thermodynamics.

As can be seen from Fig. 2(a)–(c), the existence forms of U in different solutions (H2SO4, HNO3 and HCl) are UO2(SO4)22−, UO22+ and UCl3+, respectively [52]. In the H2SO4 system at 70 °C, uranium will form UO2(SO4)22− and UO22+ are pH < 6.3 and Eh > 0.29 V, respectively. The stability constant of the complex between uranyl ions and nitrate ions is relatively low, and it is a completely dissociated electrolyte [53]. Therefore, in the HNO3 system at 70 °C, U exists in the form of UO22+ under the conditions of pH < 3.7 and Eh > 0.38 V. In the HCl system at 70 °C, the existence forms of U are UCl3+ and UO22+ at pH < 3.7 and Eh > 0.40 V.

It can be seen from Fig. 2, Figs. S1–S4 and Table 2 that the leaching of La, Y, and Nd is independent of Eh. Since Ce has two valence states of + 3 and + 4, the leaching of Ce needs Eh < 1.04 V. Therefore, to achieve the co-leaching of U, Ce, La, Y, and Nd, the dominant intervals are as follows: For the H2SO4 system, the dominant intervals are pH < 1.9 and 0.29 V < Eh < 2.00 V, requiring a strongly acidic environment and a strong oxidation potential; The dominant intervals of the HNO3 system are pH < 2.2 and 0.38 V < Eh < 1.71 V. The dominant intervals of the HCl system are pH < 1.9 and 0.40 V < Eh < 1.04 V. A strongly acidic environment and moderate oxidizing conditions need to be maintained [56]. During the actual leaching process, Eh of HCl solution without H2O2 was 0.3–0.4 V, which increased to 0.6–0.8 V after adding H2O2. When ultrasonic waves are loaded into the leaching system of HCl − H2O2, Eh will increase to 0.8–1.0 V, which is thermodynamically conducive to the co-leaching of U and REEs.

Ultrasonic enhancement of the alkaline dissolution of gangue

Effects of alkali pretreatment conditions on the dissolution of gangue

Effects of the alkali leaching pretreatment parameters on the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al and the subsequent acid leaching efficiencies of U and REEs are shown in Fig. 4.

(a) NaOH concentration, (b) Alkali leaching temperature, (c) Leaching time, (d) L/S ratio, (f) Ultrasonic power. The subsequent acid leaching conditions are as follows: 1 mol/L HCl, leaching temperature 50 °C, reaction time 2 h, L/S ratio 5:1, ultrasonic power 400 W, and the addition amount of H2O2 5%. (Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments (n = 3)).

Fig. 4(a) shows the influence of NaOH concentration on the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al and the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs. Si and Al do not dissolve without NaOH pretreatment, and the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs are only 64.83% and 39.37% respectively. When the concentration of NaOH is 150 g/L, the dissolution efficiencies of Al and Si reach 5.90% and 3.66% respectively, and the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reach 82.20% and 56.47% respectively. This indicates that gangue such as silicoaluminates in UT greatly hinder the leaching of U and REEs. However, when the concentration of NaOH reaches 200 g/L, the dissolution efficiencies of Al and Si increase to 6.17% and 4.14% respectively, but the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs decrease instead.

Alkali can react with silica and silicate gangue, eroding minerals to form pores and exposing more encapsulated U and REEs, as shown in Eqs. (5), (6) [58]. However, excessive alkali leads to excessive dissolution of impurities, causing the generated aluminates and silicate ions to form sodium aluminosilicate colloid, which hinders the subsequent leaching of U and REEs, as shown in Eq. (7) [33]. Therefore, the optimal concentration of NaOH is selected as 150 g/L.

Fig. 4(b) shows the influence of alkali leaching temperature on the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al and the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs. With the increase in reaction temperature, the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al increase, promoting the leaching of U and REEs. The dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al reach the maximum values of 3.92% and 5.80% respectively at 80 °C, while the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reach the maximum values of 82.20% and 56.47% respectively at 70 °C. When the temperature rises from 40 °C to 60 °C, the dissolution efficiency of Si and Al changes little. However, the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al increase significantly from 60 to 70 °C. This is because temperature increase can reduce the viscosity of the alkaline solution, promoting ion diffusion and chemical reaction [59]. The optimal base alkali leaching temperature is selected as 70 °C.

Fig. 4(c) shows the influence of heating time on the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al and the subsequent leaching efficiencies of U and REEs. The dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al reach the maximum 3.58% and 6.05% respectively after leaching for 3 h. The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reach 82.20% and 56.47% respectively after leaching for 2 h, and then decrease slightly afterwards. The results show that the degree of erosion of gangue increases with the extension of alkali leaching time. However, an excessively long leaching time may lead to the formation of secondary precipitates, which is not conducive to leaching [23]. Therefore, the optimal alkali leaching pretreatment time is 2 h.

Fig. 4(d) shows the influence of the L/S ratio on the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al and the subsequent leaching efficiencies of U and REEs. The experimental results show that the larger the L/S ratio, the better the dissolution effect of aluminosilicate. When the L/S ratio is 20:1, Si and Al reach the maximum dissolution efficiencies of 4.31% and 6.37% respectively. The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs showed a rapid increase first and then a slow decrease. The maximum leaching efficiency was reached at L/S ratio 12:1, where the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs were 82.20% and 56.47% respectively. When the L/S ratio is greater than 12:1, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs begin to decrease. At L/S ratio of 20:1, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs decrease to 78.02% and 54.49% respectively. An increase in the L/S ratio can enhance the solid–liquid reaction effect and promote the dissolution of gangue. However, an excessive amount of alkaline solution will lead to excessive dissolution of impurities, forming secondary precipitates and colloids, which is unfavorable for the leaching of U and REEs [60]. Therefore, the optimal L/S ratio is selected as 12:1.

It can be seen from Fig. 4(e) that both the alkaline dissolution efficiencies of Al and Si and the acid leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increase with the increase of ultrasonic power. When the ultrasonic power reaches 400 W, the dissolution efficiencies of Si and Al reach 3.66% and 5.59% respectively. The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reach 82.2% and 56.47% respectively, which increased by 16.37% for U and 13.31% for REEs compared with the control group. The ultrasonic cavitation effect can enhance the contact and reaction between the alkaline solution and the gangue, promoting the erosion of the gangue. The greater the power, the more significant the ultrasonic enhancement effect [33]. Therefore, the ultrasonic power of the alkali pretreatment was selected as 400 W.

Comprehensively considering the effects of ore dissolution and the leaching of U and REEs, the optimized process conditions for the alkali pretreatment stage are determined as follows: NaOH concentration of 150 g/L, alkali leaching temperature of 70 °C, heating time of 2 h, liquid-to-solid ratio of 12:1, and ultrasonic power of 400 W. Under these conditions, triplicate experiments ensured the reproducibility of the results (SD, n = 3), and statistical analysis confirmed significant differences (U: P = 0.002 < 0.05; REEs: P = 0.004 < 0.05). Error bars in the figures represent the standard deviation of the three replicates. With ultrasonic assistance, the dissolution efficiency of silicon increased from 2.14% ± 0.28% to 3.66% ± 0.48%, with a growth ratio reaching 71%; the dissolution efficiency of aluminum increased from 3.82% ± 0.32% to 5.59% ± 0.61%, with a growth ratio reaching 46.3%.

Alkaline dissolution mechanism of gangue

The microscopic morphologies of UT, U-APR, and C-APR are shown in Fig. S5. After alkali leaching pretreatment, the surface of the particles in the UT is corroded by alkali to generate many holes. The samples pretreated by ultrasound have more severe erosion changes than those without ultrasound pretreatment. This indicates that the ultrasonic enhances the erosion effect of alkali on gangue [61], [62].

The XRD analysis of UT, U-APR, and C-APR is shown in Fig. 5(a), indicating that the main phases in the uranium ore are quartz, albite, potassium feldspar, and anorthite. After alkaline pretreatment, the characteristic peak attributed to potassium feldspar nearly disappears around 11°, and the peak intensity of SiO2 decreases. The diffraction peak intensity of gangue decreases more significantly under ultrasonic assistance than without ultrasound. This is likely because the alkaline solution disrupts the silicate and quartz phases in the ore and dissolves the gangue coating on the surface of the uranium tailings (refer to Eqs. (5)-(7)). The mechanical enhancement effect of ultrasound further intensifies the dissolution reaction of gangue by the alkaline solution, thereby exposing more of the encapsulated U and REEs [63], [64], [65].

The occurrence forms of REEs Ce, La, Nd, and Y in UT, U-APR and C-APR are shown in Fig. 5(b). The REEs in UT exist in four different forms: oxidizable, reducible, weak acid exchangeable and residual speciation [66]. The main occurrence state for Ce, La, Nd, and Y is the residual state and REEs are embedded in silicate crystals in the form of isomorphism, which is the most difficult form to leach [8]. After alkaline leaching, the proportion of residual state of REEs decreases. This indicates that ultrasound can enhance the dissolution of gangue silicates and silica, and promote the release of REEs in the lattice. The transformation of REEs from the stable residual state to other forms realized the activation of REEs in UT [67].

The particle size distribution of UT, U-APR, and C-APR is shown in Fig. 5(c). It can be seen that the average particle size of UT is 69.91 μm, and after conventional alkali leaching, the average particle size decreases to 59.82 μm. After ultrasonic alkali leaching, this value decreases to 53.73 μm, and the number of pores smaller than 10 μm increases significantly, while the number of pores larger than 100 μm significantly decreases.

The results of the microscopic pore structure of UT before and after alkali pretreatment are shown in Fig. 5(d) and Table 4. It can be seen that the BET specific surface area of the ultrasonic alkali leaching residue increased by 3.2766 m2/g, the average pore diameter decreased by 0.8604 nm, and the cumulative pore volume increased by 0.0259 m3/g. The number of micropores ranging from 1-50 nm increased significantly after ultrasonic alkali pretreatment. This indicates that ultrasound-assisted alkaline pretreatment intensifies the alteration of gangue particles, resulting in a reduction in particle size, an increase in pores, and an increase in the exposure area of minerals, which can provide more reaction sites for subsequent acid leaching [68].

This is mainly due to the intensification mechanism of ultrasound. On the one hand, the alkaline solution continuously impacts the gangue coating layer on the mineral surface through the mechanical shock effects generated by ultrasound, enhancing the mass transfer effect of the alkaline solution [69]. On the other hand, ultrasonic cavitation can generate local high temperature and pressure, which enhances the erosion efficiency of NaOH on gangue minerals [33]. Subsequent acid leaching data show that under ultrasonic assistance, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs from the uranium tailings reached 82.2% and 56.47%, respectively. Effects of acid leaching conditions on the leaching of U and REEs

Effects of different acid leaching parameters on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs in the alkali-pretreated residue is shown in Fig. 6.

Effects of acid leaching process parameters on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs in the alkali pretreatment residue. (a) Leaching agent types, (b) Leaching agent concentration, (c) Leaching temperature, (d) Leaching time, (e) L/S ratio, (f) H2O2 addition amount, (g) Ultrasonic power, (h) Comprehensive verification. (The initial acid leaching conditions are 2 mol/L HCl, leaching temperature of 50 °C, leaching time of 2 h, L/S ratio of 5:1, and H2O2 addition amount of 5%. The ultrasonic power in Figs. (a)-(f) is 400 W.).

Traditional uranium ore leaching processes generally use sulfuric acid as the leaching agent due to the low cost and weak corrosiveness [18], [70]. However, most of the accumulated uranium tailings in China are low-grade and high-gangue minerals, which have complex mineral properties and severe gangue encapsulation [11]. When sulfuric acid is used as the leaching agent, it will react with impurity ions to generate passivation layers such as sulfur films and jarosite residues, which cover the mineral surface to cause ore layer blockage, hindering the leaching of U and REEs [71]. Therefore, it is necessary to consider more suitable leaching acids.

The leaching effects of different acid with the same acidity were compared, and the results are shown in Fig. 6(a). The leaching behaviors of U and REEs are positively correlated, and the leaching efficiencies of different REEs (Ce, La, Nd, and Y) also show similar behaviors. The leaching efficiency in the HCl system is the highest with 82.20% for U and 56.47% for REEs, respectively. Compared with H2SO4, the leaching efficiency for U and REEs increased by 16.7% and 9.16%, respectively. Compared with HNO3, the leaching efficiency for U and REEs increased by 6.38% and 6.51%, respectively. Therefore, the leaching effects for U and REEs follow the order: HCl > HNO3 > H2SO4.

The better leaching effect of HCl is mainly due to the fact that Cl− can form stable complexes with U and REE, enhancing their solubility [72], [73], [74]. The complexing ability of SO42− and NO3− is relatively weak, and the generated uranyl sulfate and rare earth sulfates may form precipitates; in addition, SO42− tend to form precipitates [75]. Impurity ions (such as Ca2+, Ma2+, and Al3+) do not form precipitates with Cl−. Therefore, HCl was used as the acid leaching agent in this study.

Effect of HCl concentration on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs is shown in Fig. 6(b), indicating that with the increase of HCl concentration, both the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increase first and then decrease. An increase in acid concentration can provide more H+ and Cl−, promoting the leaching reactions [76]. The maximum leaching efficiency for U 85.12% and REEs 64.35% has been achieved with 2.5 mol/L HCl. Therefore, the HCl concentration is selected as 2.5 mol/L.

Effect of acid leaching temperature on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs is shown in Fig. 6(c). The leaching efficiencies of both U and REEs increase with the rise of leaching temperature. When the temperature rose from 30 to 50 °C, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increased rapidly from 74.95% and 54.62% to 85.12% and 64.35%, respectively. With temperature further increasing from 50 to 70 °C, and the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increased slowly, eventually reaching 88.41% and 67.95% respectively. An appropriate increase in temperature can promote reaction kinetics, but an excessively high reaction temperature will lead to the volatilization loss of HCl. Therefore, 70 °C is selected as the acid leaching temperature.

Effect of acid leaching time on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs is shown in Fig. 6(d). With the increase of acid leaching time, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs rose rapidly at first and then decreased slowly, reaching the maximum leaching efficiency at 2 h. When the leaching time was 1 h, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reached 77.23% and 54.45%, respectively. The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increased to 89.45% and 67.96% respectively after leaching for 2 h; while they slightly decreased with further increasing leaching time. The extension of acid leaching time enables H+ and Cl− to have sufficient time to enter the pores of mineral particles, promoting effective contact between solid and liquid phases and enhancing mass transfer and reactions [11]. However, an overlong leaching time may lead to re-adsorption of the leached ions and the formation of encapsulated precipitation. Therefore, the appropriate acid leaching time is 2 h.

Effect of L/S ratio on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs is shown in Fig. 6(e). With the increase of L/S ratio, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs gradually increase and reach the peaks of 90.43% and 72% respectively at L/S ratio of 10:1. Appropriate increase of the L/S ratio can provide more H+ and Cl− to participate in the reactions, and the mass transfer kinetics will be enhanced. An increase in the L/S ratio leads to a decrease in the concentration of generated metal ions, which will promote the reaction to proceed in the forward direction [77]. However, an excessively high liquid–solid ratio will lead to a large number of impurities being dissolved and a decrease in the concentration of metal ions in the leachate, which is not conducive to the subsequent recovery of metals. Therefore, the optimal L/S ratio for acid leaching is 10:1.

Effect of H2O2 addition amount on the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs is shown in Fig. 6(f). With the increase of H2O2 dosage, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs continuously increase and reach the maximum value 90.43% and 72% at H2O2 dosage of 5%, respectively. Compared with the control group, the leaching efficiency for U and REEs has increased by 21.77% and 12.80%, respectively. Therefore, the addition amount of H2O2 is selected as 5%. This is mainly because an increase in the amount of H2O2 added can increase the number of hydroxyl radicals used for uranium oxidation, and also promote the dissociation of gangue, thereby enhancing the leaching of U and REEs [78], [79], [80], [81].

It is worth noting that the change of the ultrasonic frequency has a very small influence on leaching. Modiga et al. [33] used ultrasound-assisted leaching for REEs from waste coal with different ultrasonic frequencies. The results showed that both ultrasound with frequency of 20 and 40 kHz could enhance the mass transfer and mineral decomposition processes, accelerate the reaction between NaOH and gangue minerals, and reduce the encapsulation of aluminosilicate gel. Zhang et al. [79], [80], [81] applied ultrasound-assisted leaching to extract germanium from zinc smelting slag and found that the ultrasonic frequencies at 20–40 kHz show a slight difference. Ultrasonic power is regarded as the most influential factor for leaching. The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs gradually rise with increasing ultrasonic power as shown in Fig. 6(g). The leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reach maximum values of 90.43% and 72% respectively at 400 W. Compared with the control group, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs increased by 16.76% and 12.33% respectively, indicating that ultrasound synergistically enhances the leaching of U and REEs. Specifically, the ultrasonic mechanical agitation can promote the release of encapsulated metals [79], [80], [81]. On the other hand, the local high-temperature and high-pressure environment generated by the cavitation effect of ultrasound significantly reduces the O-O bond breaking energy of H2O2, which can promote the decomposition of H2O2 to generate oxidizing groups such as hydroxyl radicals, facilitating the oxidative leaching of U(IV) to U(VI). The higher the power, the more significant the ultrasonic enhancement effect.

To sum up, the optimal process conditions for acid leaching are as follows: HCl concentration of 2.5 mol/L, reaction temperature of 70 °C, leaching time of 2 h, liquid-to-solid ratio of 10:1, H2O2 addition of 5%, and ultrasonic power of 400 W. Under these conditions, triplicate experiments ensured the reproducibility of the results (SD, n = 3), and statistical analysis confirmed significant differences (U: P = 0.003 < 0.05; REEs: P = 0.005 < 0.05). Error bars in the figures represent the standard deviation of the three replicates. A comprehensive verification experiment conducted using the ultrasonic-assisted alkali pretreatment-acid leaching method showed that the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs were 90.43% ± 2.86% and 72% ± 3.02%, respectively. In contrast, the direct acid leaching method without alkali pretreatment (as shown in Fig. 6(h)) yielded leaching efficiencies of only 67.55% ± 3.27% for U and 43.32% ± 3.11% for REEs. Therefore, compared with the direct acid leaching method, the leaching efficiencies increased by 22.83% for U and 29.68% for REEs.

The ultrasonic leaching method significantly enhances the conversion efficiency of U(IV) to U(VI). On one hand, the cavitation effect of ultrasound can induce the spin decomposition of water to generate ·OH radicals, thereby improving the oxidizing property of the solution [87], [88]. On the other hand, ultrasound can promote the decomposition of H2O2 to produce more ·OH, accelerating the oxidation of U(IV). The synergistic effect between ultrasound and H2O2 enables the ultrasonic to exhibit better performance in the oxidation of U(IV) compared with conventional leaching, thereby improving the leaching of uranium [44].

It can be seen from Fig. 8(d) that after acid leaching treatment, the characteristic peaks of gangue such as quartz in the UT become more prominent and stronger. This might be due to the fact that after alkaline pretreatment, the gangue structure of the UT is cracked, and crystalline structures such as silica become looser, thereby decomposing more impurity elements and purifying the UT during the acid leaching process [23]. Compared with conventional acid leaching, the characteristic peak of silica in the acid leaching residue extracted by ultrasonic leaching is stronger [79], [80], [81]. This might be due to the ultrasonic-enhanced dissolving of impurities and reactive passivation layer [63], [64].

With increasing HCl concentration, the characteristic peak of hydroxyl radicals gradually increases, and tends to a maximum at 2.5 mol/L HCl (Fig. 11). The above characterization has confirmed the advanced oxidation process involved in the ultrasonic-enhanced H2O2 system. Ultrasound promotes the decomposition of H2O2, generating more hydroxyl radicals. This is consistent with the experimental results of the uranium leaching rate. In fact, the leaching efficiency of U is only 68.66% without H2O2, which will increase to 90.32% when H2O2 is added (Fig. 6(f)). This indicates that the oxidative leaching of uranium depends on the synergistic strengthening effect of ultrasound and H2O2.

Migration and transformation rules of U and REEs

Fig. 12 summarizes the main migration and transformation mechanisms of U and REEs during the entire recycling process (ultrasonic-assisted alkali pretreatment and acid leaching). The gangue encapsulation and the insolubility of U(IV) in the tailings are the key factors restricting efficient leaching of U and REEs. The mechanical, cavitation, and thermal effects induced by ultrasound collectively promote the leaching process. During alkali leaching, the mechanical shock effects generated by ultrasound create more pores and microcracks on the particle surfaces, enhancing the solid–liquid mass transfer. This further disintegrates the gangue inclusions and exposes the active reactive surfaces of U and REEs, which facilitates the subsequent acid leaching. In the acid leaching stage, the excitation effect of ultrasound on H2O2 leads to the generation of more hydroxyl radicals, enabling the efficient oxidation of insoluble U(IV) into soluble U(VI). Since most REEs are associated with U, their leaching behavior follows a similar trend to that of U.

Schematic diagram of the leaching mechanisms for U and REEs from UT by ultrasonic-assisted alkali pretreatment and acid leaching.

The potential during the entire acid leaching process is maintained between 0.8–1.0 V, which can ensure the oxidative leaching potential for U. Apart from Ce, the potential has a relatively small impact on the leaching of REEs. In fact, the leaching potential is also below the reduction leaching potential of Ce, enabling the co-leaching of U and REEs. Therefore, ultrasonic-assisted two-stage leaching can enhance the co-recovery of U and REEs from UT.

Conclusions

In this study, an ultrasonic-assisted alkali pretreatment-acid leaching process was employed to achieve the synergistic co-extraction of U and REEs from low-grade uranium tailings. Under the optimized conditions, the leaching efficiencies of U and REEs reached 90.43% and 72%, respectively, which were 17.09% and 12.33% higher than those of conventional leaching, and 22.83% and 29.68% higher than those of direct acid leaching.

The mechanical shock and cavitation effects of ultrasound are the core mechanisms enhancing the leaching process. During the alkaline pretreatment stage, the mechanical shock of ultrasound promotes the dissolution of aluminosilicate gangue, reducing the average particle size from 69.91 μm to 53.73 μm, increasing the BET specific surface area from 8.07 to 11.35 m2/g, and increasing the number of 1–50 nm micropore channels. Meanwhile, the refractory residual species of REEs are transformed into soluble forms, breaking the gangue encapsulation and increasing active sites. During the acid leaching stage, the cavitation effect of ultrasound enhances the decomposition efficiency of H2O2, generating a large number of hydroxyl radicals, which accelerates the oxidative conversion of U(IV) to U(VI). The U(VI)/U(IV) ratio increases from 0.73:1 under conventional leaching to 1.56:1 under ultrasonic leaching.

Thermodynamic Eh-pH calculations confirm that the HCl-H2O2 system provides a favorable window for the synergistic leaching of U and REEs (pH < 1.9, 0.40 V < Eh < 1.04 V). Kinetic analysis shows that the leaching process follows the mixed-control model. Under ultrasonic action, the apparent rate constants of U and REEs increase from 0.0009 min−1 and 0.0004 min−1 to 0.0027 min−1 and 0.0006 min−1, respectively, demonstrating that ultrasound improves the leaching kinetics of U and REEs. These findings provide a theoretical basis and technical support for the synergistic extraction of U and REEs from low-grade uranium resources.

In future industrial applications, further consideration should be given to the safety and environmental protection issues of the hydrochloric acid system, as well as the development of industrial ultrasonic equipment. The alkaline leachate can be reused after impurity removal. U and REEs in the acid leachate can be recovered by extraction or ion exchange methods. The ultrasonic-enhanced leaching process developed in this study may also be extended to similar oxidative leaching processes of minerals containing gangue inclusions and low-valent metals.
