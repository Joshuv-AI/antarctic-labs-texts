Regulation of Type VI Secretion Systems of Burkholderia during transition to intracellular lifestyle
Authors: Hoi Ching Cheung, Daria Jacqueline Wüst, Miro Thorsten Wilhelm Plum, Patricia Reist Iscar, Marek Basler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic bacteria tightly regulate gene expression in response to environmental cues. In Burkholderia species, the Type VI Secretion Systems (T6SS) mediate interbacterial competition and host cell interactions. Here, we show that B. thailandensis switches from the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 to the anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 during phagosomal maturation. While T6SS-1 expression persists in the host cells, its assembly progressively declines with increasing T6SS-5 expression. This switch is controlled by the two-component system VirAG as deletion of virA blocks T6SS-5 expression and restores T6SS-1 assembly in host cells. Furthermore, we found that deletion of T6SS-5 membrane complex restores T6SS-1 assembly in host cells. Notably, T6SS-1 activity in host cells induces mitochondrial fragmentation and apoptosis. Our data suggest that Burkholderia has evolved a mechanism to silence T6SS-1 upon host entry to preserve its replicative niche.Subject terms: Autophagy & Cell Death; Microbiology, Virology & Host Pathogen Interaction; Organelles

Introduction

To adapt to various niches, pathogens employ different factors required for virulence and survival in the environment. The expression of these factors is often tightly controlled. The facultative intracellular pathogen Burkholderia pseudomallei is endemic in tropical and subtropical regions and is the causative agent of the disease Melioidosis (Sanchez-Villamil et al, 2020; Wiersinga et al, 2006; Meumann et al, 2023; Limmathurotsakul et al, 2010). Patients are commonly infected via inhalation, ingestion or skin abrasions (Barnes and Ketheesan, 2005). It is estimated to have 165,000 melioidosis cases leading to 89,000 deaths per year globally (Birnie et al, 2019; Limmathurotsakul et al, 2016), and the mortality rate ranges from 10 to 40% (Limmathurotsakul et al, 2010; Currie et al, 2021; Chantratita et al, 2023). Due to the disease’s high mortality rate and resistance to antibiotics, B. pseudomallei is listed as a tier 1 select agent by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Rhodes and Schweizer, 2016; Meumann et al, 2023). The closely related, less virulent Burkholderia thailandensis is often used as an attenuated model for studying Burkholderia virulence (Haraga et al, 2008).

Both B. pseudomallei and B. thailandensis are able to infect a range of phagocytic and non-phagocytic eukaryotic cells (Whiteley et al, 2017). Upon entry into host cells, B. thailandensis escapes the phagosome using effectors of the Type III Secretion System (T3SSBsa) (Stevens et al, 2002). B. thailandensis moves in the host cytosol by using an intracellular flagellum and by polymerising host cell actin with the bacterial protein BimA, which is a mimic of the eukaryotic Arp2/3 complex (Stevens et al, 2005). Actin-tails promote formation of membrane protrusions into the neighbouring host cell. In the protrusions, B. thailandensis assembles its T6SS-5 to fuse host cell membranes by VgrG-5, leading to a protrusion escape or fusion of host cells (Schwarz et al, 2010, 2014; Plum et al, 2024; Kostow and Welch, 2022; Toesca et al, 2014).

T6SS is structurally homologous to a contractile bacteriophage tail and functions by propelling a needle-like structure with effectors into a neighbouring target cell (Pukatzki et al, 2006; Hood et al, 2010; Ma et al, 2009; Pukatzki et al, 2007; Basler and Mekalanos, 2012). Its core structure consists of a membrane and baseplate complex formed around a VgrG-PAAR spike (Aschtgen et al, 2010; Durand et al, 2015; Brunet et al, 2015; Nazarov et al, 2018; Filloux and Freemont, 2016; Kudryashev et al, 2015; Felisberto-Rodrigues et al, 2011). The sheath is formed by the assembly of TssB-C around an inner tube of Hcp rings starting from the VgrG-PAAR base and extending into the cytoplasm. When the TssB-C sheath contracts, it drives the Hcp-VgrG-PAAR and associated effectors outside the cell and into the target cell (Basler et al, 2012; Szwedziak and Pilhofer, 2019; Shneider et al, 2013; Wood et al, 2019). The contracted sheath is disassembled by the ATPase ClpV (Basler and Mekalanos, 2012; Kapitein et al, 2013; Bönemann et al, 2009; Pietrosiuk et al, 2011).

B. pseudomallei and B. thailandensis have multiple T6SS with distinct roles and high homology between the two species (Schwarz et al, 2010; Angus et al, 2014). T6SS-5 was shown to be important for cell-to-cell spread (Schwarz et al, 2014; Toesca et al, 2014; Plum et al, 2024; Schell et al, 2007; Lennings et al, 2019); whereas T6SS-1 is required for bacterial killing (Schwarz et al, 2010; Lennings et al, 2019; Majerczyk et al, 2016). In previous work, functional chromosomal fusion of the sheath protein TssB of both T6SS-1 and T6SS-5 with fluorophores have been generated (Plum et al, 2024; Lin et al, 2022).

The expression and assembly B. thailandensis T6SS-5 are tightly regulated. The regulatory cascade starts with BsaN, a central regulator located in the T3SSBsa locus. It regulates T3SS effectors and two downstream T6SS-5 regulators VirAG and BprC (Chen et al, 2011, 2014). A two-component histidine kinase, VirAG, regulates T6SS-5 expression during infection; and BprC regulates T6SS-5 expression when grown in medium, except for the tssBC operon (Chen et al, 2011). The sensor VirA phosphorylates VirG upon sensing host cytosol when the bacterium escapes from the endosome and the phosphorylated VirG binds to the promoter regions of TssB-5 and Hcp-5 (Wong et al, 2015). However, the signals for BsaN and BprC activation are unknown and the redox signal sensed by VirAG is the only signal identified during infection so far. The regulation of the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 is poorly understood in B. thailandensis. It was shown that quorum sensing controls the expression of T6SS-1 effectors and VgrG-1 (Majerczyk et al, 2016). In terms of post-translational regulation of T6SS-1, it was shown that T6SS accessory protein TagM-1 is important for T6SS-1 assemblies (Lin et al, 2022).

In this study, we aim to understand how Burkholderia regulates its different T6SS in the environment and during infection. We investigated the expression and assembly of all five T6SS of B. thailandensis by live-cell imaging in medium and during infection. Here, the T6SS expression refers to the detection of uniformly distributed fluorescence signal in the bacterial cytosol, while the T6SS assembly refers to the detection of the formation and disassembly of bright foci within bacterial cytosol. We observed that the phagosomal environment is a transition state where there is a gradual increase in T6SS-5 expression and decrease of T6SS-1 assemblies, while deletion of T6SS-5 components restored T6SS-1 assembly. Furthermore, we identified VirAG as a regulator for both T6SS-5 expression and T6SS-1 assemblies. Deletion of virA abolished T6SS-5 expression and allowed T6SS-1 assemblies during infection, which led to apoptotic host cell death. This indicates that switch between the two T6SS allows the bacteria to adapt to their environment and to spread during infection.

Results

T6SS-2, T6SS-4 and T6SS-6 are not expressed during infection of A549 cells

B. thailandensis harbours five T6SS on different chromosomal loci and with different functions. Previous studies have mainly focused on the anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 as it alone was sufficient to cause virulence in mice (Schwarz et al, 2010). A transcriptomics study has hinted that T6SS-2, T6SS-4, and T6SS-6 have increased expression when inside of host cells (Heacock-Kang et al, 2021). However, it remains unclear if these T6SS are expressed intracellularly. To test this, we constructed strains of B. thailandensis with the T6SS sheath proteins TssB tagged with mNeonGreen expressed from the native chromosomal loci. We then infected a monolayer of A549 cells and performed live-cell imaging. Intracellular bacteria were identified by constitutive expression of cytoplasmic mCherry2 under a Burkholderia ribosomal promoter, or by expression of the TssB-5-mCherry2 fusion protein. Images were acquired between 11 and 12 h post-infection (hpi) to localize bacteria in the cytoplasm and in membrane protrusions. We failed to detect expression of T6SS-2, T6SS-4 and T6SS-6 during infection (Appendix Fig. S1A). In accordance with previous studies, the T6SS-5 was expressed in B. thailandensis at 12 hpi. Notably, the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 expression was detected during infection (Appendix Fig. S1B).

We next assessed T6SS expression when grown in liquid culture and imaged on LB (Luria–Bertani) agar pads. T6SS-2 and T6SS-4 were not expressed under such conditions as the fluorescently-labelled B. thailandensis strains exhibited background fluorescence levels comparable to those of the unlabelled strain. The T6SS-6-labelled strain exhibited heterogeneous expression, but no assemblies were detected (Appendix Fig. S1C).

T6SS-5 is repressed in vitro and induced within host cells before phagosomal escape

The anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 was shown to be expressed when B. thailandensis is in the host cell cytosol, however, T6SS-5 expression dynamics has never been analyzed (Wong et al, 2015). In accordance with previous studies (Chen et al, 2011), we detected no expression of T6SS-5 when B. thailandensis tssB-5-mScarlet-I was imaged in vitro on agar pads while its expression was induced by expressing VirAG from an arabinose-inducible plasmid (Appendix Fig. S1D).

To investigate the T6SS-5 expression dynamics during infection, we infected a monolayer of A549 cells with B. thailandensis tssB-5-mScarlet-I constitutively expressing cytosolic mCherry2 under Burkholderia ribosomal promoter pSC12 at a Tn7 insertion site. Host cell membranes were stained with CellMask deep red for 5 min and washed before imaging. Kanamycin was added to the medium to kill extracellular bacteria. Images were taken at 1-min intervals to identify the time point when TssB-5-msfGFP is expressed. During a 5-h imaging period, we tracked the bacteria and measured change in TssB-5-msfGFP fluorescence intensity in time. A region free of host cells and bacteria was selected at each timepoint to measure background green fluorescence. Using a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, we identified that T6SS-5 expression became significantly different from the background signal at approximately 3 h post-infection (Figs. 1B,C and EV1B,C; Movie EV1). Doing similar analysis on 116 bacteria across three biological replicates, we observed that most bacteria express T6SS-5 between 2 and 4 hpi (Fig. 1D).

Since previous studies focused on the expression of Hcp-5 but not TssB-5 (Chen et al, 2011; Wong et al, 2015), we investigated if Hcp-5 was also expressed within our identified time frame of TssB-5 expression. We performed immunofluorescence staining with an anti-Hcp-5 antibody on infected cells fixed at 3 hpi (Fig. EV1A). Hcp-5 was detected in a positive control, a B. thailandensis strain expressing VirAG from a pmlBAD plasmid, while no Hcp-5 expression was detected in the negative control ∆hcp-5 strain and the wild-type B. thailandensis expressed both Hcp-5 and TssB-5.

Interestingly, we noticed that the bacteria were in a membrane-bound compartment when expressing T6SS-5 (Figs. 1B and EV1B, yellow arrows). We therefore asked if T6SS-5 is expressed while the phagosomal membrane is still intact. To test this, we pre-loaded A549 cells with 0.25 mg/mL fluorescent dextran 100 kDa overnight, infected the cells with B. thailandensis in the absence of dextran, washed with OptimemTM with kanamycin (300 µg/mL) after 1 hpi and imaged immediately. We found that B. thailandensis expressed TssB-5-msfGFP when colocalized with dextran-labelled vacuoles (Figs. 1E and EV1D; Movie EV2). We then tracked the bacteria and measured the fluorescence intensity of TssB-5-msfGFP throughout the infection time course. Similar to Fig. 1C, fluorescence intensity of the dextran, T6SS-5 and bacterial cytosol (pSC12-mCherry2) were plotted (Figs. 1F and EV1E). The dextran signal remained between 500 and 2000 a.u. and only dropped to 200 a.u. after 2 h of increased T6SS-5 expression. By analyzing 76 bacteria in dextran-treated A549 cells across three biological replicates, we found that 87% of the bacteria expressed T6SS-5 when colocalized with fluorescent dextran. Furthermore, we imaged T6SS-5-expressing bacteria at 10 and 30 s intervals and found that B. thailandensis assembled T6SS-5 when inside of the phagosome (Figs. 1G and EV2; Movie EV3). Notably, the T6SS-5 assemblies did not disrupt the phagosomal membrane. This contrasts with the later stages of infection, when T6SS-5 assemblies are known to facilitate fusion of host cell membranes and cell-to-cell spread (Plum et al, 2024; Kostow and Welch, 2022).

Phagosomal maturation is important for T6SS-5 expression

To assess phagosomal maturation during infection with B. thailandensis expressing T6SS-5, we infected two HeLa cell lines stably expressing Rab5-mApple or Rab7-mApple and stained for LAMP1 (lysosome-associated membrane protein-1) by immunofluorescence. Rab5 is a GTPase associated with early phagosomes, Rab7 functions mainly on late phagosomes, and LAMP1 is a marker for acidified vacuoles (Kitano et al, 2008; Vieira et al, 2003; Hu et al, 2019; Chen et al, 1985a; Huynh et al, 2007; Chen et al, 1985b; Zhang et al, 2023). Figure 2A shows a schematic of the markers associated with the phagosomal pathway. We found that most bacteria express T6SS-5 in late phagosomal maturation stages, indicated by colocalization with Rab7-mApple, and that T6SS-5 expressing bacteria often colocalize with LAMP1-positive vacuoles between 2 and 3 hpi (Fig. 2B). We quantified over 400 T6SS-5 expressing bacteria in at least 3 biological replicates, and tested for colocalization with either Rab5, Rab7, and LAMP1. Only 2% of T6SS-5 expressing bacteria colocalize with Rab5-labelled phagosomes; whereas 16.5% of those colocalize with Rab7-labelled phagosomes and 29% with LAMP1-labelled phagosomes (Fig. 2C).

Since LAMP1 is a common marker for mature, acidified vacuoles (Chen et al, 1985a; Zhang et al, 2023; Huynh et al, 2007), we explored the role of phagosomal maturation as a signal for T6SS-5 expression. Acidification of vacuoles can be inhibited by bafilomycin A1 (Yamamoto et al, 1998; Matsumoto and Nakanishi-Matsui, 2019; Mauvezin and Neufeld, 2015). We pre-treated A549 cells with 100 nM bafilomycin A1 (BAF) 1 h before infection, infected the cells with B. thailandensis, washed with kanamycin at 1 hpi, and replenished the media with BAF. At the same time, we also pre-loaded the host cells with 0.25 mg/mL fluorescent dextran 100 kDa overnight to label phagosomes. To ensure BAF inhibits the acidification of vacuoles in our assays, we measured the fluorescence intensity of lysotracker and showed that BAF treatment significantly reduced acidification of vacuoles (Fig. 2F). Importantly, the addition of BAF largely abolished T6SS-5 expression (Fig. 2D; Movie EV4). We quantified the percentage of bacteria expressing T6SS-5 with or without BAF treatment (Fig. 2E). We analysed 69 bacteria in host cells treated with BAF, and 41 bacteria in host cells treated with DMSO across three biological replicates. Only 8 bacteria (11.5%) expressed T6SS-5 when the host cells were treated with BAF, compared to 85% of bacteria expressing T6SS-5 in the DMSO control. Among the eight T6SS-5-expressing bacteria residing in BAF-treated cells, one bacterial cluster escaped from the phagosome, whereas the rest of the bacteria remained confined within the phagosome (Appendix Fig. S2A,B).

To obtain a larger sample size, we performed flow cytometry on infected cells treated with BAF or DMSO. In line with our fluorescence imaging data, flow cytometry confirmed the lack of TssB-5-mScarlet-I expression in BAF-treated cells, which is indicated by the absence of mCherry signal (Figs. 2G and EV3C). This indicates that phagosomal maturation and acidification is an important signal for T6SS-5 expression.

Bacteria remain confined within dextran-labelled phagosomes in BAF-treated cells

To detect phagosomal rupture, we examined galectin-3 (Gal-3) colocalization to bacteria-containing compartments since Gal-3 binds to β-galactosides exposed upon membrane rupture (Jia et al, 2020; Paz et al, 2010; Hughes, 1999; Fig. 3C). We infected HeLa cells stably expressing cytosolic Gal-3-GFP, preincubated the cells with fluorescently labelled dextran as described above, and either treated the cells with BAF or DMSO. Images were acquired at two time points: 3–4 hpi (to assess phagosomal escape) and 7–8 hpi (to assess replication within phagosomes).

Representative images of B. thailandensis with or without Gal-3 colocalization in DMSO-treated cells are presented in Fig. 3A. In contrast, no Gal-3 colocalization was observed for bacteria residing within dextran-labelled phagosomes in BAF-treated cells at approximately 3 hpi or 8 hpi (Fig. 3B). We further quantified the proportion of bacteria colocalizing with Gal-3 in cells treated with BAF or DMSO. At 3:30 hpi, amongst 776 bacteria, 60 bacteria showed Gal-3 colocalization (8%) of bacteria colocalized with Gal-3 in DMSO-treated cells, whereas none of the 697 bacteria-containing vacuoles colocalized with Gal-3 at both 3:30 and 7:30 hpi in BAF-treated cells (Fig. 3D). In parallel, we quantified bacterial localization within dextran-labelled vacuoles and found that 100% of bacteria remained within dextran-labelled vacuoles at both 3:30 and 7:30 hpi, compared 12% in DMSO-treated cells at 3:30 hpi (Fig. 3E).

These observations indicate that dextran labelling of phagosomes was not affected by BAF treatment and suggest that bacteria remained confined within intact phagosomes throughout infection, consistent with the absence of phagosomal membrane damage and the retention of dextran within the vacuolar compartment.

In the host cell cytosol, T6SS-1 is expressed but not assembled

It was shown previously that B. thailandensis constitutively express and assemble T6SS-1 when imaged on agar pads (Lin et al, 2022). Little is known about how they regulate the T6SS-1 during infection. To test this, we infected A549 cells with a B. thailandensis strain that expressed the T6SS-1 sheath protein fused to a fluorophore, TssB-1-msfGFP, and performed live-cell imaging after 12 hpi. To our surprise, we observed that the T6SS-1 was expressed but not assembled, as indicated by uniform fluorescence throughout the bacterial cells without the appearance of dynamic foci typically associated with T6SS assembly events (Figs. 4A and EV4A; Movie EV5). To identify the point of T6SS-1 assembly inhibition, we infected A549 cells and acquired time-lapse images of at least 100 different bacteria in unique field of views across different time points. Images were captured every 30 s over a 5-min period to assess whether the bacteria assembled T6SS-1. At early infection time points when the B. thailandensis were in dextran-labelled vacuoles, all bacteria expressed T6SS-1, but not all bacteria assembled T6SS-1 (Figs. 4B,C and EV4B; Movie EV6). We quantified the percentage of bacteria assembling T6SS-1 at various time points, and found that 33% of intracellular bacteria assembled T6SS-1 between 1 and 2 hpi. This significantly decreased to 16% when imaged between 2 and 3 hpi. 11% of bacteria assembled T6SS-1 between 3 and 4 hpi and this further decreased to 1% after 4 hpi and 0% after 12 hpi (Fig. 4D). In all the time points, none of the cytosolic bacteria assembled T6SS-1. When we infected A549 cells with a B. thailandensis strain with both T6SS-1 and T6SS-5 fluorescently labelled, we found that amongst 93 bacteria, when the T6SS-5 was expressed, there were no T6SS-1 assemblies (Figs. 4E and EV4C). This data suggests that expression of T6SS-5 is negatively correlated with the assembly of T6SS-1.

Since VirAG is a two-component system that upregulates the T6SS-5 expression at the beginning of infection, we hypothesized that VirAG also plays a role in inhibiting T6SS-1 assembly. We expressed VirAG from an arabinose-inducible plasmid and imaged T6SS-1 assembly dynamics on agar pads in vitro (Fig. 5A; Movie EV7). The parental B. thailandensis strain expressing TssB-1-msfGFP assembled T6SS-1 constitutively. However, when VirAG was expressed, T6SS-1 assembly was completely abolished.

We then infected A549 cells with the ΔvirA mutant and imaged at 16 hpi. Deletion of virA allowed B. thailandensis to assemble T6SS-1 during infection (Fig. 5B; Movie EV8). The ΔvirA bacteria accumulated inside of single host cells and failed to spread. This is presumably because the protein required for actin tail motility, BimA, was reported to be not expressed in the absence of VirAG (Chen et al, 2011). We then explored if B. thailandensis ΔvirA assembles T6SS-1 at different time points during infection. We infected A549 cells and acquired time-lapse images of 1834 different bacteria in unique field of views across different time points in three biological replicates. Images were captured every 30 s over a 5-min period to assess whether the bacteria assembled T6SS-1. We found that bacteria assembled T6SS-1 both when inside of dextran-labelled phagosomes or in the cytoplasm (Fig. EV5A–D). Throughout the imaging period, 15–40% of B. thailandensis ΔvirA assembled T6SS-1 from 1 hpi to 6 hpi (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, we noticed a high variability in the percentage of bacteria assembling T6SS-1 at late infection time points (Fig. 5D). We imaged 25 infected cells after 12 hpi, with 1,406 bacteria over three biological replicates, and found that in each infected cell, the percentage of bacteria assembling T6SS-1 varied from 1 to 98%.

In addition, since the ΔvirA mutant lacks actin-tail motility and accumulated in a host cell at high bacterial cell density, we asked if the T6SS-1 assemblies observed in Fig. 4B could be caused by increased bacterial cell-cell contact rather than deletion of virA. To prevent bacterial spreading and induce accumulation in host cytoplasm, we infected A549 cells with a virA positive, bimA knock-out strain expressing TssB-1-msfGFP (Fig. EV5F). Importantly, in the presence of VirA, even when the bacteria were accumulated inside of one host cell and were in contact with one another, no T6SS-1 assemblies were observed suggesting that mere bacterial cell-cell contact or high number of bacteria in the cytoplasm are insufficient to trigger T6SS-1 assembly.

These results identify VirAG as an inhibitor of T6SS-1 activity during infection, as plasmid expression of VirAG suppresses T6SS-1 firing in vitro, whereas deletion of virA restores T6SS-1 assemblies during infection.

Deletion of T6SS-5 components partially restore T6SS-1 assembly

Since VirAG upregulates the expression of the anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 components, we investigated if T6SS-5 components may inhibit T6SS-1 assemblies, as recently observed for Bacteroides fragilis (Sheahan et al, 2024). We deleted tssM-5, encoding T6SS-5 membrane complex component TssM-5, or tssMJKL-5, encoding the complete T6SS-5 membrane complex and part of the baseplate, and examined T6SS-1 assembly on agar pads and during infection. During infection of A549 cells, we observed a subpopulation of bacteria assembling T6SS-1 (Figs. 6A and EV6A; Movie EV9). To quantify this, we imaged at least 9 fields of view per biological replicate, analysing at least 3000 bacteria per strain across three biological replicates. During infection, 0% of WT bacteria assembled T6SS-1 at 15 hpi, whereas 1.5% of the ΔtssM-5 mutant and 10% in ΔtssJLKM-5 mutant assembled T6SS-1 (Fig. 6C).

On agar pads, the expression of VirAG inhibits T6SS-1 assemblies. However, both the ΔtssM-5 and ΔtssJLKM-5 mutants assembled T6SS-1 in the presence of VirAG (Figs. 6B and EV6B; Movie EV10). T6SS-1 activity induces mitochondrial damage

The anti-bacterial T6SS-1 effector clusters contains multiple lipases. In which BTH_I2698 was identified as the homolog of Tle1 lipase (Appendix Fig. S3A) (Majerczyk et al, 2016). We hypothesize that the secretion of T6SS-1 effector lipases could have cross-kingdom effect on the host cells. This led us to examine mitochondrial integrity as a potential intracellular target of these effectors.

To test if B. thailandensis T6SS-1 activity causes mitochondrial damage, we infected A549 cells with the B. thailandensis ΔvirA strain that assembles T6SS-1 during infection. There was no difference in the number of bacteria (colony-forming units) in the infected cells between ΔvirA and ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant (Appendix Fig. S3B,C) suggesting that the bacterial burden was comparable. To visualize mitochondria, we stained the host cells with Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM), a dye that is fluorescent when sequestered by mitochondria with intact membrane potential. A549 cells were infected, stained and images were taken between 9 and 10 hpi. Host cells treated with 2 μM of carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) was used as a positive control for mitochondrial fragmentation, and untreated uninfected cells were included as a negative control (Fig. 7A). We classified three phenotypes of mitochondrial status—healthy, fragmented mitochondria, or mitochondria with no membrane potential (Fig. 7B). Healthy mitochondria are tubular, fragmented mitochondria appear as discrete dots, and no fluorescence was detected in mitochondria without membrane potential following TMRM staining. Over 100 infected cells were analyzed for the T6SS-1 assembling ΔvirA mutant, and the non-assembling ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant. For the ΔvirA mutant, 12 infected cells displayed a normal, filamentous mitochondrial phenotype, 98 cells showed fragmentation, and 9 cells had no mitochondrial potential (Fig. 7C). For the ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant, 47 infected cells displayed a normal, filamentous mitochondrial phenotype, 53 cells showed fragmentation, and 3 cells had no mitochondrial potential.

To quantitatively measure mitochondrial damage, we used the ImageJ plugin Mitochondria Analyzer and quantified the number of branches per mitochondria and the mean mitochondrial branch length (Hemel et al, 2021). Fragmented mitochondria appear rounder and as isolated puncta, and thus has less branches per mitochondria and shorter branch length. Cells infected with the ΔvirA mutant showed on average 1.5 branches per mitochondria and with a mean branch length of 1 µm (Fig. 7E). Whereas for cells infected with the ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant, they showed on average 2.3 branches per mitochondria and with a mean branch length of 1.4 µm. DMSO-treated uninfected host cells showed on average 3.3 branches per mitochondria and with a mean branch length of 1 µm; whereas CCCP-treated host cells showed on average 1.7 branches per mitochondria and with a mean branch length of 1.3 µm. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed and cells infected with the ΔvirA mutant had significantly less mitochondrial branching and shorter length, indicating a higher level of mitochondrial fragmentation.

Together, our data indicate that T6SS-1 assemblies damage host cell mitochondria, leading to a higher proportion of fragmented mitochondria and mitochondria lacking membrane potential.

T6SS-1 activity does not impact eukaryotic plasma membrane

To assess whether T6SS-1 activity affects the eukaryotic plasma membrane, we examined propidium iodide (PI) uptake in infected cells, as PI is only permeable to cells with compromised membrane integrity. A549 cells were infected with the ΔvirA mutant for 6-7 h and stained with Hoechst 33342 to label all nuclei, PI and TMRM to assess plasma and mitochondrial membrane integrity prior to imaging. Uninfected cells treated with ethanol were included as a positive control. In this condition, mitochondrial membrane potential is lost and nuclei were PI-positive (Appendix Fig. S3D). In untreated, uninfected cells (negative control), mitochondrial morphology appeared normal and nuclei were PI-negative. We then assessed 198 infected cells across three biological replicates, and observed mitochondrial fragmentation, while host cell nuclei were PI-negative (Green asterisks, Fig. 7F). Hence, this shows that T6SS-1 assemblies do not damage eukaryotic plasma membrane.

T6SS-1 activity triggers apoptosis

As mitochondrial fragmentation leads to apoptosis (Jagasia et al, 2005; Ellis, 1986), we asked if T6SS-1 activity could trigger apoptosis. We used a caspase-3/7 activity detection reagent containing a fluorophore-conjugated peptide substrate specific to caspase-3/7. Upon cleavage by active caspase-3/7, the released fluorophore intercalates with nuclear DNA and emits fluorescence. To optimize the staining conditions, a monolayer of A549 cells were either untreated or treated with staurosphorine, an apoptosis-inducing chemical, were stained with the caspase-3/7 activity detection reagent and apoptotic cells were identified (Fig. 8A).

Discussion

The T6SS-1 and T6SS-5 play different roles during B. thailandensis lifestyle. Our study showed that expression of T6SS-5 induced by the histidine kinase VirAG system in Burkholderia spp. inhibits T6SS-1 assemblies during infection and thus prevents T6SS-1 mediated increase in apoptosis via mitochondrial damage.

The lack of intracellular expression of T6SS-2, -4 and -6 is consistent with their unclear role in infection. In the study Heacock-Kang et al, 2021, the authors used a single-bacterium transcriptomics approach to compare the transcriptomes of individual intracellular B. pseudomallei with those of bacteria grown in liquid culture. Although they assessed the expression of T6SS-2, T6SS-4, and T6SS-6 under these conditions, only a limited subset of T6SS genes (1–3 components per system) was detected, indicating that no complete transcriptional activation of these T6SS clusters was observed. In addition, previous studies on T6SS-2 and T6SS-6 focused on mice infections and identified no role of the two T6SS in virulence (Schwarz et al, 2010). T6SS-4 is reported active under oxidative stress in vitro (Si et al, 2017; DeShazer, 2019). The anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5’s role in infection has been widely studied in literature (Schwarz et al, 2010, 2014; Toesca et al, 2014, 2014; Schell et al, 2007), however, the temporal dynamics of T6SS-5 expression in live cells remained uncharacterised. Moreover, the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 has primarily been studied in the context of bacterial competition assays (Schwarz et al, 2010).

Here, we identified VirAG as a switch between the T6SS-1 and T6SS-5. The VirAG dimer was reported to be reduced to its active monomer form upon exposure to host cytosolic glutathione (GSH), leading to activation of T6SS-5 expression (Wong et al, 2015). In our study, we observed T6SS-5 expression occurring also in late phagosomes before the bacteria appear in the host cytoplasm. Interestingly, we observed T6SS-5 assemblies inside late phagosomes. Although rare, this suggests that the other T6SS-5 components are also expressed at this stage, enabling the formation of functional assemblies. It is plausible that acidification and redox changes accompanying phagosome maturation are sufficient to initiate T6SS activation, which is then accelerated after bacterial escape to the cytosol. The discrepancy between previous work and out work is likely due to the fact that we focused on a different infection stage. Where previous work focused on cytosolic bacteria (6 hpi), we focused on bacteria within phagosomes (3 hpi). Moreover, in a related species B. mallei, T6SS expression was shown to occur prior to escape from late phagosomes, further supporting the findings of this study (Burtnick et al, 2010). Ultimately, both studies support the same overarching conclusion that VirAG is activated and T6SS-5 is expressed during infection.

We show that phagosomal maturation is important for T6SS-5 expression and is consistent with previous studies. Chua et al demonstrated that pH alkalinisation suppresses the T6SS-5 of B. pseudomallei, B. mallei, and B. thailandensis, as evidenced by the lack of MNGC formation (Chua et al, 2017). As MNGC formation is a hallmark for T6SS-5 activity in Burkholderia, these findings are consistent with our observation that inhibition of phagosomal acidification traps B. thailandensis inside vacuoles and greatly reduce T6SS-5 expression. The rare exceptions that we observed, in which B. thailandensis expressed T6SS-5 in bafilomycin-treated cells, may reflect incomplete inhibition of phagosomal maturation in individual host cells, or instances in which cytosolic signals leaked into the phagosome, thereby inducing T6SS-5 expression. Moreover, we concluded that B. thailandensis expresses T6SS-5 between 2 and 3 hpi, and the bacteria mostly are located in Rab7 and LAMP-1 positive late phagosomes. As our data represent single timepoint measurements, bacteria that do not colocalize with Rab5/7/LAMP1-positive phagosomes are presumed to be in the host cytosol, or in a phagosome not labelled by the markers mentioned above. Notably, a study by Hu et al reported that between 2 and 4 hpi, B. pseudomallei also co-localizes to Rab7 and LAMP-1 positive phagosomes (Hu et al, 2019). This suggests that B. thailandensis and B. pseudomallei likely share the similar intracellular trafficking dynamics.

Using Gal-3 recruitment as a marker for phagosomal rupture is common for studies of intracellular bacteria (Paz et al, 2010; Hong et al, 2021). Our observation that 8% of bacteria colocalizing with Gal-3 is consistent with a similar study by Szczesna et al (2024), which reported at comparable hpi, fewer than 10% of B. thailandensis colocalized with LC3, an autophagy marker recruited downstream of Gal-3 (Szczesna et al, 2024). The three exceptions of Gal-3 localization to dextran-labelled phagosomes may have resulted from transient or minor phagosomal membrane permeabilization insufficient for dextran diffusion out of the phagosome. Nevertheless, no such events were observed in BAF-treated cells, where bacteria remained confined to dextran-labelled phagosomes and showed no detectable colocalization with Gal-3.

The T6SS-1 has been shown to assemble in vitro, with several accessory proteins contributing to the regulation of T6SS assembly, whereas quorum sensing regulates the expression of T6SS effectors and the tip protein VgrG (Majerczyk et al, 2016; Lin et al, 2022). Surprisingly, we find that the T6SS-1 is expressed but not assembled during infection. The gradual decrease in T6SS-1 assemblies matching increase in expression of the anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 indicates a switch between the two T6SS. By deleting virA, we found that T6SS-1 assemblies during infection is restored. Since VirAG controls the expression of T6SS-5, we explored the possibility that expression of T6SS-5 inhibiting T6SS-1 assemblies. Expression of a T6SS inhibiting another T6SS assemblies was described in Bacteroides fragilis (Sheahan et al, 2024). There, deletion of T6SS membrane complex genes partially restored assemblies of the other T6SS. Interestingly, deletion of tssM-5, one of the T6SS-5 membrane complex components, partially restored firing of T6SS-1 during infection. Whereas deletion of the full membrane complex and part of the baseplate (ΔtssJLKM-5) resulted in a much higher proportion of bacteria firing T6SS-1 during infection. This suggests a similar mechanism of assembly inhibition as described B. fragilis (Sheahan et al, 2024).

It is unclear why T6SS-1 expression would be maintained during infection. One possible explanation is that B. thailandensis maintains a pool of T6SS-1 proteins ready to assemble once it exits the host and re-enters the environment. This suggests that the suppression of T6SS-1 inside host cells may be a temporary, reversible adaptation. Interestingly, B. mallei has undergone significant genome reduction, and is believed to be evolving towards a more specialized, intracellular lifestyle (Losada et al, 2010; Moore et al, 2004). Consistent with this shift, the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 of B. mallei is truncated and likely non-functional (Angus et al, 2014). This hints that T6SS-1 is selected against during the evolution of B. mallei in the intracellular environment. Hence, we can speculate that B. thailandensis inhibits T6SS-1 assemblies during infection as an adaptation to surviving an intracellular environment.

The VgrGs and effectors associated with the anti-bacterial T6SS-1 were identified in three gene clusters located outside the core T6SS locus, each situated at distinct chromosomal locations (Majerczyk et al, 2016). The T6SS-1 effectors remain uncharacterised. The presence of transposase genes in effector clusters II and III suggests that these loci arose through gene duplication events (Appendix Fig. S3A). Bioinformatic analysis identified several effectors with lipase domains (BTH_I2698, BTH_I2701), and with BTH_I2691 belonging to the VasX toxin family. Intriguingly, BTH_I2698 is a homolog of Tle1, a lipase in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Hu et al, 2014). Although T6SS-1 effectors are primarily considered antagonistic towards other bacteria, they may also exert transkingdom effects on eukaryotic cells. Moreover, we found that all T6SS-1 VgrGs contain the DUF2345 domain (Appendix Fig. S3A). This domain is shown to be present in the C-terminal of VgrGs from other bacteria such as VgrG2b from Pseudomonas aeruginosa, VgrGi of a clinical strain of Acinetobacter baumannii, and VgrG4 from Klebsiella pneumonia, all shown to contribute to virulence (Sá-Pessoa et al, 2023; Lopez et al, 2020; Berni et al, 2019).

We showed that the ΔvirA mutant that constitutively assembles T6SS-1 during infection causes more mitochondrial damage and apoptotic cell death than the ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant with inactivated T6SS-1. This was not due to a difference in bacterial load in cells infected with the ΔvirA mutant since image-based measurements and colony-forming unit analyses showed no significant differences (Appendix Fig. S3B,C). The fraction of mitochondria fragmenting in a T6SS-1 independent manner by the ΔvirA/Δhcp-1 mutant may be due to impaired intracellular survival upon deletion of the virulence regulator VirAG. It is possible that the bacterium expresses virulence factors regulated by VirAG that inhibit mitochondrial damage. In addition, because ΔvirA mutants is unable to spread from cell to cell, the increased intracellular bacterial burden may contribute to the observed phenotype. Nevertheless, by comparing a T6SS-1 assembling strain (ΔvirA) and the non-assembling strain (ΔvirA/Δhcp-1) mutant, we have elucidated that T6SS-1 assemblies induce mitochondrial damage, and thus triggering apoptosis. It is therefore possible that B. thailandensis evolved an ability to inhibit T6SS-1 to prevent triggering host cell death and thus preserve its replicative niche.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that VirAG acts as a switch between the anti-eukaryotic T6SS-5 and anti-bacterial T6SS-1 of B. thailandensis, enabling the bacterium to better adapt when transitioning from being in the environment to being intracellular. This highlights a regulatory mechanism that governs the utilization of distinct T6SS systems in response to environmental cues.

Bacterial strains, plasmids and culturing conditions

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Reagents and Tools table. Bacterial strains were grown in LB media overnight at 37 °C with aeration. When appropriate, antibiotics were added at the following concentrations: 50 μg/mL of trimethoprim, 30 μg/mL of gentamicin, or 30 μg/mL of chloramphenicol for E. coli; and 200 μg/mL of trimethoprim, 300 μg/mL of kanamycin, or 30 μg/mL of chloramphenicol for B. thailandensis.

Construction of genetic mutants and reporters

Genetic mutants were constructed via allelic exchange via the vector pDONRPEX-18Tp-SceI-pheS or pTOX (Fazli et al, 2015; Lazarus et al, 2019). The homologous flanking regions were at least 1000 bp. pDONRPEX-18Tp-SceI-pheS or pTOX derivatives were conjugated into B. thailandensis strains by E. coli SM10 λpir. For pDONRPEX-18Tp-SceI-pheS, positive conjugants were selected by trimethoprim resistance and counter selection were performed on M9 minimal medium agar plates supplemented with 0.4% glucose and 0.1% (w/v) 4-chloro-phenylalanine. For pTOX derivatives, all steps were performed in the presence of 2% glucose to prevent premature toxin induction. Conjugants were selected by chloramphenicol resistance and counter selection were performed on M9 minimal medium agar plates supplemented with 2% (w/v) rhamnose.

Bacterial strains constitutively expressing cytoplasmic fluorophores and reporter strains were constructed by chromosomal Tn7 transposase insertion (Choi et al, 2006). For constitutive expression, PS12 promoter (Burkholderia rpsL promoter) was employed. pUC18tminiTN7Tp and the helper plasmid pTNS2 were conjugated into B. thailandensis strains by E. coli SM10 λpir and conjugants were selected by trimethoprim resistance. Successful chromosomal insertion into attTn7 site downstream of the glmS gene were screened by PCR.

Mammalian cell lines and culturing conditions

The cell lines HeLa cells (CCL-2, ATCC), A549 lung cells (CCL-185, ATCC) were used in this study. HeLa cell lines stably expressing Rab 5 and Rab 7-mApple were provided by Prof. Dr. Anne Spang (Podinovskaia et al, 2021), and HeLa cells stably expressing Galectin-3-GFP were generated previous study from our group (Plum et al, 2024). The HeLa cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM with L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS, BioConcept AG); and A549 lung cells with Ham’s F-12 media with GlutaMAX™ (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All experiments were performed between passages 2 and 10 and the cell lines were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination by MycoStrip (InvivoGen).

Infection assays

Infection assays were performed as described previously (Plum et al, 2024). In brief, A549 and HeLa cells were seeded at 8 × 104 cells per well on μ-slide eight well glass bottom (Ibidi GmbH) 24 h prior to infection. Bacterial strains were grown overnight in low-salt LB at 37 °C with aeration. On the day of the infection assay, overnight bacterial cultures were subcultured in a 1:100 dilution and grown to mid-log phase in low-salt LB. Both the bacterial and mammalian cells were washed twice in Opti-MEM™ Reduced Serum Medium with GlutaMAX™ supplement (Sigma-Aldrich). Bacterial cells were added to the mammalian cells at the specified MOI and the μ-slide eight well glass bottom was spun down at 300 × g for 5 min. After 1 h post-infection, Opti-MEM™ supplemented with 300 μg/mL of kanamycin was added to kill extracellular bacteria.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were infected as described above. For labelling LAMP1, cells were fixed with ice cold 100% methanol for 15 min at specified timepoints. The fixed cells were then washed twice with PBS. For labelling Hcp-5, cells were fixed with ice cold 100% methanol for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and then treated with 1 mg/mL lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich) and 160 U/mL mutanolysin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at 37 °C with agitation. The fixed cells were then incubated with LAMP1 mouse monoclonal antibody (Abcam) or anti-Hcp-5 rabbit antibody (generated by GeneScript) were diluted to 1 μg/mL in 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 1 h. Cells were then washed twice with PBS. Anti-mouse or rabbit (Abcam) secondary antibodies were diluted 1:300 in 1% BSA and incubated with the cells overnight at 4 degrees. Cells were then washed twice with PBS and imaged.

Fluorescence microscopy

Infection imaging was performed using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 inverted microscope equipped with a CrestOptics X-Light V3 spinning disk confocal system and a Kinetix sCMOS camera. Images were acquired using a ×60 oil immersion objective (NA = 1.42; field of view: 302.28 × 302.28 µm) under live-cell conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Unless otherwise stated, eukaryotic cells were stained with CellMask deep red (5 µg/mL) and Hoechst 33342 (40 µM) for 5 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For imaging agar pads, images were acquired on a Nikon Ti-E inverted motorized microscope with Plan Apo 100x Oil Ph3 DM (NA = 1.4; field of view – 122.64 × 122.88 μm) objective lens.

Fluorescence intensity quantification

Time-lapse single plane images were acquired using the confocal microscopy setup described above. Bacteria were segmented with the Otsu thresholding algorithm in ImageJ. The segmented cells were saved as Regions Of Interest (ROIs), and the average fluorescence intensity within each ROI was measured for the relevant fluorescence channels. To account for background signals, fluorescence intensity was also measured from randomly selected ROIs in cell-free regions of the same channel.

Quantification of T6SS-1 assemblies

Time-lapse imaging of infected samples was performed using the confocal microscopy setup described above, with images acquired every 30 s over a 5-min period. At each timepoint, a z-stack of three optical sections was captured with a step size of 0.5 µm. Each bacterium was imaged only once in a unique field of view to avoid duplicate counts across timepoints. The images were subsequently processed by maximum intensity projection for analysis. The total number of intracellular bacteria per field was segmented with Cellpose (Stringer et al, 2021) on the first time point, and cells assembling T6SS-1 were identified and quantified manually across the remaining time-lapse images. At least 200 bacteria were counted in each of the three biological replicates.

To quantify the proportion of bacteria assembling T6SS on agar pads, a single z-slice image of bacteria on agar pads were segmented with Cellpose to determine the total number of bacteria in each field of view. Time-lapse images were taken every 10 s over a 5-min period. For the pmlBAD-VirAG, and pmlBAD-VirAG ΔtssM-5 strains, which exhibited lower firing frequencies and a greater tendency to form cellular aggregates, T6SS-1 assemblies were manually quantified. With the other strains with higher firing frequencies, T6SS-1 assemblies throughout the time-lapse series were identified using the Find Maxima function in ImageJ, and bacteria containing at least one maxima spot were scored as T6SS-1 active. The ImageJ macro script is provided in Appendix. Selected images were manually inspected for quality control. At least 5000 bacteria were quantified for each strain across three biological replicates.

Flow cytometry

On the day prior to infection, 5 × 105 cells were seeded on a six-well plate. Infection was performed as described above. At 15 hpi, cells were lysed with 0.1% Triton-X 100, filtered through a 35 μm mesh, and kept on ice until analyzed. Cell analysis was conducted using a BS LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer. A total of 100,000 events were analysed per condition. Flow cytometry data were analyzed and gated using FlowJo.

Mitochondrial damage quantification

Mitochondria were stained with Tetramethylrhodamine, Methyl Ester, Perchlorate (TMRM, Thermo Fischer) at 100 nM for 15 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For positive control, eukaryotic cells were treated with 2 µM of carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. Mitochondrial morphology was quantified using the ImageJ plugin Mitochondria Analyzer (https://github.com/AhsenChaudhry/Mitochondria-Analyzer). At least 100 images per experimental condition were acquired across three biological replicates. Infected cells were manually segmented to isolate individual cells for analysis. The mitochondria were thresholded and analyzed with Mitochondria Analyzer. The number of branches per mitochondria and mean branch length were plotted for comparative analysis.

Cell death assays

Apoptotic cell death were quantified by caspase-3/7 activity (ThermoFischer). Cells were first stained with CellMask deep red (5 µg/mL) and Hoechst 33342 (40 µM) for 5 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and washed twice with optimum containing 300 µg/mL kanamycin. CellEvent™ Caspase-3/7 green detection reagent were diluted 1:1000 from a stock concentration of 2 mM to a final working concentration of 2 µM, added directly to the same, and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were imaged without additional washing. Green fluorescence was measured with the 470 nm/510 nm filter. As a positive control for apoptosis, cells were treated with 1 µM of staurosporine for 18 h.

To quantify propidium iodide uptake in host cells, cells were stained with propidium iodide (5 μg/mL), TMRM (100 nM), CellMask deep red (5 µg/mL) and Hoechst 33342 (40 µM) for 5 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. They were then washed twice and imaged as single timepoint images. As a positive control, uninfected cells were permeabilized with 40% ethanol for 2 min prior to PI staining. In total, 198 infected cells were quantified across three biological replicates.

Bacterial competition assay

For quantitative killing assays, mid-log phase bacteria were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 5. Predator and prey species were mixed at 1:1 ratio, and 20 µL of the mixture was spotted on dry LB agar plates and incubated at 30 °C for 5 h. After incubation, the spots were harvested and serially diluted for plating on selective recovery plates (chloramphenicol 30 μg/mL for E. coli). CFU was counted after ~18 h of incubation at 37 °C. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed.

Image and statistical analysis

The images were analysed with ImageJ/Fiji and GraphPad Prism 9.1.0. were used for data analysis. Two-tailed t test was performed when comparing two means. Ordinary one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s, Bonferroni’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparison test were performed when comparing multiple means. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05 and the specific statistical testing for each experiment is listed in the figure legends.
