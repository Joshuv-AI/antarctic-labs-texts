Trends in initial primary treatment approach and biomarker testing across social determinants of health in early-stage non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Xiaohan Hu, Yu-Han Kao, Ashwini Arunachalam, Chijioke Okeke, Hina Mohammed, Ayman Samkari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the impact of a comprehensive set of social determinants of health (SDOH) on treatments, timing, and key biomarker testing for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsPatients with the first diagnosis of stage I–III NSCLC from 1 January 2015 to 15 October 2023 and treated at community health systems in the United States were eligible for this retrospective database study. We summarized initial primary treatment and time-to-treatment initiation (TTI) by Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), primary care provider (PCP) shortage areas, household income, and insurance type. Data cutoff was 15 October 2024.ResultsOf 8501 patients with stage I–III NSCLC, 32% underwent surgery-only and 14% also received neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant therapy. Greater percentages underwent surgery (with/without neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy) in counties with lowest SVI/vulnerability, highest median income, and no PCP shortage, and among those with private healthcare insurance (vs. Medicare/Medicaid). Median (range) TTI for any NSCLC-related treatment after diagnosis was 41 days (0–1846); TTI increased across treatment strategies by increasing SVI/vulnerability and decreasing household income. Annual rates of programmed death-ligand 1/EGFR mutation testing rose from 60%/51% in 2020 to 84%/82% in 2023, with greatest rates in counties with no PCP shortage.ConclusionsDisparities in early-stage NSCLC treatment by SDOH factors call for efforts to improve access to timely care for NSCLC.KEYWORDS: Biomarker testing, household income, resectable non-small cell lung cancer, social vulnerability index, time to treatment initiation, treatment disparities

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer in men and women and the leading cause of cancer death in both sexes in the United States (US) [1]. The incidence of lung cancer declined overall from 2012 to 2021 in the US, and advances in therapy together with earlier detection have improved survival of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the most common subtype [1–3]. Nonetheless, studies conducted in US real-world settings have documented disparities in cancer treatment strategies by socioeconomic and demographic factors such as household income, available healthcare insurance, race, and ethnicity [4–7]. Disparities linked to socioeconomic and demographic factors have been recorded in the NSCLC stage at presentation, time to treatment initiation (TTI), surgical resection rates, use of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, and engagement with remote symptom monitoring by electronic patient-reported outcomes [4–11]. These disparities, together with detriments in social determinants of health (SDOH), such as greater local social vulnerability, economic instability, and neighborhood-level deprivation, have been associated with lowered survival in different types of nonmetastatic cancers, including NSCLC [10–14].

The rapid changes in NSCLC therapy in recent years highlight the importance of understanding the current treatment landscape in order to identify areas of unmet need [15]. Systemic therapies for resectable, early-stage NSCLC now include the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) osimertinib for adjuvant therapy of EGFR-mutant NSCLC and ICIs for neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and perioperative therapy [16–18]. Thus, the standard of care for early-stage NSCLC now includes testing for EGFR genomic alterations as well as for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression.

Most studies of disparities in treatment of NSCLC were conducted before the expansion of treatment options beginning in late 2020 [5,10–13]. This retrospective study aimed to update our understanding of the potential impact of a comprehensive set of SDOH variables on treatments, timing, and key biomarker testing for early-stage NSCLC. We examined the primary treatment approach and TTI for patients with stages I–III NSCLC before 2020 and from 2020 onward, in addition to biomarker testing rates, across SDOH factors.

Methods

Data source

The Syapse Learning Health Network (LHN) is a database integrating multiple sources of longitudinal patient care information from large US community health systems, including structured and manually curated data from electronic health records; laboratory, pathology, and radiology reports; and facility cancer registries [19]. At the time of this study, the LHN included over 1.6 million records of patients with cancer from 460 hospitals and 1350 oncologists across 25 states (Syapse Holdings Inc. was acquired by N-Power Medicine on 30 December 2024 [19]).

This noninterventional study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was considered to be exempt from institutional review board approval as the data were deidentified before conducting retrospective analyses.

Patients

Patients eligible for this study were adults (≥18 years old) with a recorded diagnosis from 1 January 2015, to 15 October 2023, of stage I–III NSCLC that had not been previously treated. (The American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] edition used for staging was not prespecified.) Patients with any other primary cancer diagnosis preceding the NSCLC diagnosis were excluded. We defined two analytical cohorts according to date of initial NSCLC diagnosis: Cohort 1, with diagnosis from 2015 through 2019 and Cohort 2 with diagnosis from 1 January 2020, to 15 October 2023. Data cutoff was 15 October 2024.

Assessments and analyses

We used descriptive analyses to summarize patient characteristics, the initial primary treatment approach, and the TTI by study period (2015–2019 and 2020 to 15 October 2023) and according to SDOH factors, described below. The initial primary treatment approach after the NSCLC diagnosis was assigned to one of five categories: (1) surgery only, (2) surgery plus neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant therapy (hereafter referred to as perioperative therapy), including systemic anticancer therapy, postoperative radiation therapy (PORT), and chemoradiation therapy (CRT), (3) definitive CRT only, (4) only definitive radiation therapy or only systemic therapy without surgery (numbers combined), and (5) no NSCLC-specific treatment, which we defined as no surgery, systemic therapy, or radiation therapy identified before the earliest of recorded NSCLC recurrence, metastasis, death, or end of follow-up. (Supportive or palliative care was not considered in study definitions, because those data are not available in the LHN dataset.) The TTI was defined as the period (in days) between diagnosis and the start of the initial primary treatment.

For patients in Cohort 2 who underwent surgery with or without perioperative therapy (treatment categories 1 or 2 above), we summarized annual rates of PD-L1 and EGFR testing after the NSCLC diagnosis in total, within 90 days after diagnosis, and between diagnosis and surgery. Additionally, we summarized the percentages of patients tested within 90 days after the NSCLC diagnosis according to SDOH factors.

Factors used to describe social determinants of health

We evaluated four SDOH factors, namely, the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), shortage areas for primary care providers (PCPs), median annual household income, and healthcare insurance type. The SVI is an index that was developed by the US Centers for Disease Control Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry using 2020 census tract data to designate the vulnerability of communities to natural and anthropogenic hazards, and to disease outbreaks [12,20,21]. Each census tract is ranked on 16 US Census variables from the American Community Survey, overall and grouped into 4 themes (socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status/language, and housing type/transportation). Updated every 2 years, the SVI values are percentiles comparing each census tract with all other tracts, with values ranging from 0 (least vulnerable) to 1 (most vulnerable). Therefore, for example, an SVI of 0.82 signifies that the tract is more vulnerable than 82% of all other tracts and less vulnerable than 18% of all other tracts.

The income estimates from the 2020 American Community Survey data were used to determine the inflation-adjusted median household income for patients on a census tract level [22]. This information was summarized at a zip code level, using 2020 5-digit zip codes for each patient to derive this variable because the most recent version of the median income table is based on geocoded information from 2016 to 2020. We used the 2021–2022 Area Health Resource Files generated by the US Health Resources and Services Administration to determine whether patients dwelled in a county containing a PCP shortage area based on health professional shortage areas (HPSAs) data and on patients’ 2020 county-level Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) codes [23,24].

Healthcare insurance types were categorized as Medicare (including Medicare with Medicaid eligibility or with private supplement), Medicaid, private insurance, veteran/military insurance, self-insured, other, and unknown. Further details are provided in the Data Supplement.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R package (version 4.3.3, R Core Team, 2024).

Results

Patients

We included a total of 8501 patients with stage I–III NSCLC (see patient selection in Supplementary Figure S1): 4630 patients (54%) with diagnosis from 2015 through 2019 (Cohort 1) and 3871 (46%) with diagnosis from 2020 to 15 October 2023 (Cohort 2). The median age in both Cohorts 1 and 2 was 70 years; just over half of patients in each cohort were women; and 82% were White (Table 1). The majority of patients in each cohort (~84%) had ECOG performance status of 0 or 1, and most (92%) had a history of smoking (current or former). Initial primary treatment approach across time periods and SDOH factors

The initial primary treatment approach after the NSCLC diagnosis remained relatively unchanged between the first and second study periods examined for Cohorts 1 and 2, respectively (Table 2). Overall, 32% of patients underwent surgery only, 14% of patients received perioperative (neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant) therapy with surgery, 19% of patients received only definitive CRT, and 21% of patients received only definitive radiation therapy or only systemic therapy, while 14% of patients had no NSCLC-specific treatment (provision of supportive or palliative care was not assessed).

There was also no consistent change in the initial primary treatment approach by SDOH factors from the first to second study period. For initial primary treatment by SVI quartile, median annual household income, and PCP shortage area (yes/no), we observed only minor differences between Cohort 1 and Cohort 2 (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Among patients with “other” insurance type (i.e., types not assigned to the main insurance categories), 57 (28%) and 46 (41%) had surgery-only in the first (2015–2019) and second (2020–2023) study period, respectively, while 23 (11%) and 20 (18%) had surgery with perioperative therapy, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Among those who were self-insured in Cohort 1 and Cohort 2, 18 (39%) and 7 (18%), respectively, had no initial primary treatment. Because of the lack of consistent or other major differences in the initial primary treatment approach, we elected to present combined results for the two cohorts (Figure 1).

In the two combined cohorts, the percentage of patients who had surgery alone or surgery with perioperative therapy was 51% in the lowest SVI quartile (lowest vulnerability) and 43% in the highest SVI quartile (greatest vulnerability); the percentages with no initial primary treatment were 11% and 17%, respectively (Figure 1A). A similar pattern was seen for median household income: the percentage of patients who underwent surgery with or without perioperative therapy was 50% in the highest income quartile and 40% in the lowest income quartile; the percentages with no initial primary treatment were 12% and 19%, respectively (Figure 1B). Of patients in counties with no PCP shortage area, 54% underwent surgery with or without perioperative therapy, while this percentage was 45% in counties with one or more shortage areas; 10% and 15%, respectively, had no initial primary treatment (Figure 1C).

Differences in the initial primary treatment approach after NSCLC diagnosis were most evident for the different types of healthcare insurance (Figure 1D). Among patients in the three largest insurance categories, 60% of privately insured patients, 43% of those on Medicare, and 40% of those on Medicaid underwent surgery with or without perioperative therapy, including 22%, 12%, and 12%, respectively, who received perioperative therapy, while 8%, 15%, and 14%, respectively, had no NSCLC-specific treatment. We also observed that 27 of the 90 patients (30%) on veteran or military insurance received only definitive radiation or only systemic therapy, while 25 of the 86 self-insured patients (29%) had no NSCLC-specific treatment (Figure 1D).

Overall, the longest median TTI tended to be for surgery with perioperative therapy; and wide ranges in TTI were noted for all treatment categories, particularly for surgery with perioperative therapy and definitive radiation/systemic therapy.

For the SVI, the median overall TTI for any NSCLC-related treatment after the NSCLC diagnosis was 37 days for SVI quartile 1 (least vulnerable) and 47 days for SVI quartile 4 (most vulnerable). With regard to household income, median TTI for the greatest vs lowest income quartiles were 37 and 48 days, respectively. A similar pattern of TTI by treatment category as for any treatment was noted with regard to both SVI and income quartiles (Table 3). For PCP shortage areas (no vs yes), the difference in median TTI was ≤4 days except with regard to surgery with perioperative therapy, for which the median TTI was 63 vs 71 days for counties without vs with shortage area(s).

The median overall TTI for any treatment was 40, 41, and 45 days among patients who were privately insured, had Medicare, or had Medicaid, respectively, while the median TTI was 54 days for the 90 patients with veteran/military insurance and 36 days for the 86 self-insured patients (Table 3). The median TTI by treatment category mostly followed a similar pattern except for surgery with perioperative therapy, for which the median TTI was 67, 73, 67, and 59 days among patients who were privately insured, or had Medicare, Medicaid, or veteran/military insurance, respectively.

Biomarker testing trends over time and by SDOH factors

Among all 1764 patients in Cohort 2 who underwent surgery with or without perioperative therapy, a PD-L1 expression test was performed for 1214 patients (69%), including for 1122 within 90 days after the NSCLC diagnosis (64% overall, or 92% of those tested) and for 754 before surgery (43% overall, or 62% of those tested). A total of 1367 patients (77%) who underwent surgery had nonsquamous NSCLC or a histologic type other than squamous NSCLC. Among these 1367 patients, EGFR mutation testing was conducted for 918 patients (67%), including for 845 within 90 days after the NSCLC diagnosis (62% overall, or 92% of those tested) and for 513 before surgery (38% overall, or 56% of those tested).

A total of 179 patients (77%) in counties with no PCP shortage area and 792 (59%) in counties with one or more PCP shortage areas had a test for PD-L1 expression within 90 days after the NSCLC diagnosis. Those tested for EGFR mutation within 90 days after the diagnosis were 131 (72%) and 604 (58%) in no PCP shortage versus one or more PCP shortage areas, respectively (Table 4). Otherwise, we found no consistent testing patterns according to other SDOH factors or patient characteristics.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of 8501 patients seen at US community health systems, we observed no consistent trend of differences between the two study periods (2015–2019 vs 2020–2023) in choice of initial primary treatment approach for Cohort 1 vs Cohort 2 after a diagnosis of stage I–III NSCLC. For the two cohorts separately and when combined, general patterns in patient percentages indicated more frequent surgery, with or without perioperative therapy, in counties with lowest SVI/vulnerability (vs greatest SVI/vulnerability), in census tracts with highest median income (vs. lowest), in counties with no PCP shortage (vs one or more PCP shortage areas), and among patients with private healthcare insurance (vs. Medicare or Medicaid). The inverse pattern was observed for percentages of patients who had no initial primary treatment, although provision of supportive or palliative care was not assessable in the database. Moreover, the median TTI increased, both for any treatment and across treatment strategies, as SVI increased and as median household income decreased. We also observed that annual rates of PD-L1 testing rose from 60% in 2020 to 84% in 2023, while EGFR mutation testing rose from 51% to 82%; however, there were no consistent differences in rates by SDOH factors except for greater testing rates in counties with no PCP shortage area.

The findings of our study support and extend the findings of earlier studies examining treatment patterns for NSCLC by SDOH factors. In a study of 236,140 patients in the National Cancer Database (NCDB) with stage I NSCLC in 2010–2015, surgical resection was significantly less frequent in multivariable analyses among patients with lower income and educational levels, those living in rural areas, and those on Medicaid or who were uninsured, with a negative impact resulting in worse survival outcomes [11]. Using the NCDB to study 140,555 patients with stages I–III NSCLC in 2004–2013, Cushman et al. identified a TTI of ≤45 days as the optimal window for survival, and increased TTI was independently associated with poorer survival [10]. That study also noted disparities by race (highest TTI for African American and Hispanic patients) and insurance type (highest TTI for those on Medicaid, lowest TTI for those with private insurance) [10]. In a more recent study of 3595 patients with stages I–IIA NSCLC, the relative risk for radiation therapy rather than surgery was greater for those living in PCP shortage areas and non-metropolitan areas [9]. Moreover, the median TTI for surgery and that for radiation therapy increased with greater SVI, as in the present study.

Strengths of this study include a rich data source comprising a large study population treated in the community setting, the most common site of cancer therapy in the US [25]. We included a comprehensive set of SDOH factors, including SVI, median household income, and PCP shortage areas at the county level, together with healthcare insurance type, which was available for >95% of patients. The information gathered in this study addresses an important evidence gap in community oncology practice using recent longitudinal patient data with up-to-date follow-up until 15 October 2024. While we did not assess survival outcomes in this study, prior work has associated lowered survival with increased TTI and disparities in SDOH factors and socioeconomic conditions, as noted above [10–12,14].

We also acknowledge several limitations of this retrospective descriptive study. We were not able to determine specifics of NSCLC staging, as AJCC version information was not available in the database at the time of the study, and we included all stage III NSCLC without restricting to resectable stage III. The fourth category of initial primary treatments, i.e., only definitive radiation therapy or only systemic therapy without surgery (numbers combined), is the category that would include patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC. Our aim was to descriptively show trends across different SDOH categories within each specific primary treatment category using the same staging criteria (stages I to III) as the denominator. Therefore, we believe the internal validity of the study was not impacted by including nonresectable NSCLC. Moreover, we can speculate that the lower rates of surgery found among patients with less favorable SDOH factors (e.g., living in areas with highest vulnerability, lowest income, and shortages of primary care doctors, and/or with Medicare or Medicaid insurance) could be attributed to limited access to surgical care or, alternatively, to NSCLC diagnosis at an advanced stage when surgery is no longer an option. Had we been able to limit our analyses to patients with complete details of staging information, our findings may have been affected by selection bias favoring patients treated at practices that were better at adopting “guideline-concordant care.”

Another limitation is that the NSCLC diagnosis period was slightly shorter for Cohort 2. The uneven follow-up periods between cohorts, coupled with the COVID-19 pandemic onset in March 2020, could have had an impact on findings. However, our endpoints of initial primary treatment and biomarker testing are typically done within a fairly short time window after NSCLC diagnosis, and thus the longer follow-up for Cohort 1 should not have a substantial impact on the receipt of primary treatment or testing following a diagnosis.

Patients in this study were treated at community health systems in the Syapse Learning Health Network; therefore, our findings may not reflect findings for patients treated at academic centers or in rural care settings, and, as the study population comprised predominantly White patients, we were not able to evaluate potential disparities by race. Moreover, we were unable to assess whether supportive or palliative care was provided because that information was not available in the database. We note that, in the US, SVI data is provided at the census tract level, while median household income is provided at the zip code level. Finally, the analyses were entirely descriptive, with no adjustments made for potential confounding factors in evaluating study endpoints; therefore, no conclusions regarding causality should be drawn directly from this work.

Our finding of no consistent differences in primary treatment approach between the two study periods could be explained in part by the relatively recent approvals of ICIs for resectable NSCLC, with lags in adjusting practice patterns. Future research with updated datasets is needed to understand the shifting landscape of therapy and to further investigate the impact of SDOH factors on early-stage NSCLC treatment within specific patient clinical subgroups (e.g., stage-specific NSCLC). Our findings also support the need for efforts to understand barriers to timely initiation of treatment. Patients in this study were exclusively from community practices; therefore, targeted efforts are needed to understand whether such findings are also present at academic centers, and whether barriers or delays in treatment initiation differ by specific types of treatments, e.g., chemotherapy or ICI, to further shed light on relevant interventions. Understanding current TTI for early-stage NSCLC, especially across SDOH factors, can inform policy change and can help healthcare providers tailor their efforts to improve community health, ever more relevant in light of the expanding options for therapy of early-stage NSCLC [15–18]. Moreover, widening the geographic distribution of clinical trials and encouraging patient enrollment could help to provide more uniform care [26], as clinical trial participation is well-recognized as a means of accessing state-of-the-art therapy [25,27]. Recent recommendations for improving patient access to clinical trials have been published [25,27,28].

Conclusions

We observed variations in the initial primary treatment approach and TTI for stages I–III NSCLC across the SDOH factors explored in this descriptive, retrospective study that indicate disparities in NSCLC treatment by SVI, household income, insurance type, and PCP shortage areas. Changes from 2015–2019 to 2020–2023 in the primary treatment approach were unremarkable, with minimal differences in median TTI between the two study periods, both overall and by treatment category; however, biomarker testing rates improved each year for patients who underwent surgery for NSCLC. These findings are relevant in informing policy changes aimed at addressing disparities in treating NSCLC and call for efforts to improve access to timely care for NSCLC.
