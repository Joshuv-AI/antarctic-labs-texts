Effects of incentivising dialysis facilities on peripheral arterial disease care in patients undergoing haemodialysis: a claims-based cohort study
Authors: Yasunori Suzuki, Masao Iwagami, Sayuri Shimizu, Atsushi Goto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeripheral arterial disease (PAD) occurs frequently in patients undergoing dialysis, but early intervention for PAD may not be fully implemented. We evaluated the effects of financially incentivising dialysis facilities that provided early detection and management of PAD on outcomes of PAD care.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study identified patients aged 18–74 years who received maintenance haemodialysis between April 2016 and March 2021 from the JMDC Claims Database. The (time-dependent) exposure was claim for incentives for early detection and management of PAD. The outcomes were PAD screening tests (process indicator) and infections, revascularisation procedures, and amputations in the lower extremities (outcome indicators). We used Poisson regression models with generalised estimation equations for the number of screening tests and Cox proportional hazards models for the first incidence of the outcome indicator.ResultsOverall, 5850 patients on haemodialysis were identified: 5183 and 667 with and without claims for the incentive, respectively; the numbers of screening tests were 9070 and 776, respectively (adjusted ratio of the frequency, 1.89 [95% confidence interval 1.70–2.10]). Among patients with and without claims for the incentive, infections occurred in 479 and 109 (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 0.99 [0.80–1.23]), revascularisations were performed in 192 and 29 (adjusted HR, 1.11 [0.75–1.66]), and amputations were conducted in 72 and 9 patients, respectively (adjusted HR, 1.35 [0.66–2.75]).ConclusionThe financial incentive for early detection and management of PAD was associated with a higher frequency of PAD screening tests, but not with improved outcome indicators.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is highly prevalent (23%–42%) among patients with end-stage kidney disease who receive haemodialysis [1–4]. Patients on dialysis with PAD have a 9.12-fold higher risk of undergoing lower extremity amputation [5], which is associated with poor prognosis, with a 1-year survival rate of 52%–56% [6–8].

Preventive measures against the progression of PAD to amputation in patients undergoing dialysis include regular PAD screening and foot care, smoking cessation, exercise therapy, antithrombotic therapy, and revascularisation, similar to those in the general population. However, early prevention practices, including screening and foot care, are insufficiently performed in this population [9, 10].

In April 2016, Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) introduced a programme that financially incentivises dialysis facilities that engaged in early detection and management of PAD to attenuate the increasing incidence of limb amputation [11, 12]. The proportion of dialysis facilities claiming this incentive has increased steadily, reaching 78% as of July 2022 [13]. Additionally, by fiscal year (FY) 2020, the cost had exceeded 2.7 billion JPY [14]. However, the impact of this programme on process and outcome indicators of PAD has not been examined.

Using a large Japanese health insurance claims database, this study aimed to evaluate the association between the claims for financial incentives and the frequency of PAD screening tests (as a process indicator), as well as the incidence of lower extremity infection, revascularisation, or amputation (as outcome indicators) in patients on maintenance haemodialysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective cohort study that used the JMDC Inc. health insurance claims database. The source data comprised data from January 2005 to March 2021, and the database included 12 496 371 insured individuals. Since January 2005, JMDC Inc. has obtained claims data from multiple health insurance associations in Japan that cover employees of large companies and their dependents aged ≤74 years [15]. The JMDC database contains anonymized outpatients’ and inpatients’ information, including birth year and month, sex, medical care year and month, diagnosis, medical practice, prescribed drugs, medical institutions, and year and month of insurance enrolment and withdrawal. Diagnoses and medical practices are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and codes for the electronic claims processing system managed by the MHLW. Prescribed drugs are coded according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (ATC) codes managed by the World Health Organization. A unique ID is assigned to each patient, enabling longitudinal tracking even if the patient visits multiple medical institutions.

This study was conducted in accordance with the seventh revision of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yokohama City University (approval date: 10 November 2021; approval number: B200800024). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the anonymous nature of the database.

Financial incentive programme for early detection and management of PAD in Japan

Dialysis facilities can claim the financial incentive if the facility meets the following criteria: (i) evaluated the severity of lower extremity PAD and provided guidance management for all patients on maintenance haemodialysis in the facility; (ii) referred patients with an ankle-brachial index (ABI) ≤0.7 or skin perfusion pressure (SPP) ≤40 mmHg to medical institutions that have the following specialised treatment capabilities: cardiology, thoracic or vascular surgery, orthopaedic surgery, and dermatology or plastic surgery (no podiatry department in Japan); and (iii) notified the Regional Bureau of Health and Welfare on the medical institutions with which they collaborate.

Study participants

The inclusion period was set from April 2016 (the start of the programme) to March 2021 (the end of the study period). Patients undergoing haemodialysis for ≥3 months in outpatient dialysis facilities during this period were included, defined as when both practice codes for outpatient medical management fees for chronic maintenance dialysis and artificial kidneys were present in the same month and year [16] (Supplementary data, Table S1).

We excluded patients aged <18 years and individuals with a history of lower extremity amputation or kidney transplantation (Supplementary data, Tables S1 and S2). Patients on combined haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis or home haemodialysis (Supplementary data, Table S1) were also excluded because the frequency of foot care provided in dialysis facilities may be less than that provided for patients on haemodialysis. The time of cohort entry (start of follow-up) for eligible patients was defined as the month and year of the first haemodialysis session in the inclusion period. To allocate ≥6 months for background information collection, the cohort entry time for patients whose time between database and cohort entry was ˂6 months was set as 6 months after the time of database entry. Among the exclusion criteria, age, combined haemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis therapy, and home haemodialysis were evaluated at the cohort entry time. History of kidney transplantation and lower extremity amputation were evaluated from database registration to cohort entry (Supplementary data, Fig. S1).

Exposure

The exposure was claims for the incentive and was identified by the corresponding practice code (Supplementary data, Table S1). The comparison was defined as non-claims for the incentive.

Outcomes

The outcomes were the number of PAD screening tests (as a process indicator) and the outcome indicators, including infections, revascularisation procedures, and amputations in the lower extremities. Screening tests were identified using practice codes for ABI or SPP (Supplementary data, Table S3). Infections included cellulitis, skin abscess, erysipelas, necrotizing fasciitis, gas gangrene, osteomyelitis, or pyogenic arthritis. Their definitions were based on the records of disease names and ATC codes for systemic antibiotics or related practice codes in the same month (Supplementary data, Tables S4–S6; References S2 and S3). The definitions of revascularisation procedures, including endovascular treatment and surgical revascularisation, and lower extremity amputation were based on the definitions in previous studies [17–19] and the FY 2020 revision of the medical fee system in Japan (Supplementary data, Tables S7 and S8).

Potential confounders

We selected age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), antidiabetic medications, antihypertensive medications, antiplatelet agents, number of beds in the dialysis facility, and complication management fees for diabetes as potential confounders, using directed acyclic graphs [20]. These variables were identified based on ICD-10, disease name, ATC, and practice codes (Supplementary data, Tables S9 and S10). Previous studies showed that older age, male sex, diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral neuropathy, and PAD were associated with lower extremity amputations in patients receiving dialysis [5, 21–23]. We used the CCI to compile comorbidities [24]. Each comorbidity in the CCI was defined based on the algorithm proposed by Quan et al. [25]. Number of beds served as a proxy for level of care. Complication management fees for diabetes could be charged when physicians or nurses who meet the facility criteria provide outpatient guidance and management for patients with diabetes at high risk of diabetic foot lesions. Data at cohort entry were used for age, sex, and number of beds. The comorbidities and complication management fees for diabetes were assessed between 6 months before cohort entry and the cohort entry time (Supplementary data, Fig. S1).

Statistical analyses

We tabulated baseline characteristics of all eligible patients, including those with and without incentive claims during follow-up, and determined the number of screening tests per year and the incidence rate of each event per 100 person-years.

Follow-up began at the cohort entry time and ended at the earliest of the following events: first occurrence of a lower extremity infection, revascularisation, or amputation event; transition to peritoneal dialysis, combined haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis, home haemodialysis, or kidney transplantation; insurance withdrawal; or March 2021 (Supplementary data, Fig. S1).

To account for patients with initiated claims for the incentive during follow-up, we distinguished exposure periods from non-exposure periods by defining the exposure period as the time from the first claim for the incentive to the end of follow-up and the non-exposure period as the time from the start of follow-up to the time of the first claim for the incentive or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred earlier.

We calculated the ratios of the frequency of screening tests using a Poisson regression model including the logarithm of person-years in the offset term. Generalised estimating equations with an exchangeable correlation structure and a robust variance estimator were applied to address the correlation between observations within each patient. We also classified the screening tests into ABI and SPP in a sub-analysis. In addition, we estimated the hazard ratios (HRs) for each event using a Cox proportional hazards model. The aforementioned potential confounders at baseline were adjusted for in these analyses.

We performed a sensitivity analysis, defining infections based on the names of infectious diseases in the hospitalisation setting (sensitivity analysis #1). Next, in the analyses of infections and revascularisations, we excluded patients with a history of each event (sensitivity analysis #2). Moreover, to avoid prevalent-user bias [26], we conducted an analysis limited to patients with newly initiated claims for the incentive (sensitivity analysis #3). Finally, we examined the impact of possible confounding by dialysis vintage (sensitivity analysis #4). For this analysis, we restricted to patients who were not undergoing haemodialysis at the time of insurance enrolment and further adjusted for dialysis vintage, defined by the time period between the first year and month of haemodialysis in the JMDC database and the cohort entry time. Dialysis vintage was treated as a continuous variable.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

In outpatient dialysis facilities, 6244 patients underwent maintenance haemodialysis between April 2016 and March 2021 and had been on dialysis for ≥3 months. Among these patients, 394 were excluded based on the exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Consequently, 5850 patients were included in the analyses.

During a median (interquartile range) follow-up of 2.4 (1.2–2.9) years, 10 078 screening tests were performed, where 7866 (78.1%) and 2212 (21.9%) were for ABI and SPP, respectively. Screening tests were implemented in 62.9% (3236/5186) and 28.6% (190/664) of patients with and without incentive claims, respectively. During the same median follow-up period, 588 (10.1%) lower extremity infections, 221 (3.8%) revascularisations, and 81 (1.4%) lower extremity amputations occurred. The unadjusted amputation incidence rate (95% confidence interval [CI]) was 0.66/100 person-years (0.51–0.80).

After adjusting for potential confounders, the incentive claims were associated with a 1.89-fold (95% CI, 1.70–2.10) higher frequency of screening tests (Fig. 2). The incentive claims were associated with a 1.68-fold (95% CI, 1.50–1.88) and 3.41-fold (95% CI, 2.62–4.43) higher frequency of screening tests for ABI and SPP, respectively (Supplementary data, Fig. S2). Conversely, claims for the incentive were not significantly associated with the occurrence of infection (HR 0.99 [95% CI 0.80–1.23]), revascularisation, (HR 1.11 [95% CI 0.75–1.66]), and amputation (HR 1.35 [95% CI 0.66–2.75]) in the adjusted analysis (Fig. 3).

In sensitivity analyses #1–#3, which focused on infections in the hospitalisation setting, first-episode infections and revascularisations, and patients for whom claims for the incentive were newly started, the results were comparable with the main results (Supplementary data, Table S11). Sensitivity analysis #4, which incorporated dialysis vintage into the statistical model, comprised 4563 patients (78% of eligible patients) with a mean (SD) dialysis vintage of 5.4 (15.7) months. The incentive remained associated with a higher frequency of screening tests but not with the incidence of infections, revascularisations, or amputations (Supplementary data, Table S12).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that claims for the incentive were associated with a higher frequency of PAD screening tests. However, the incentive claims were not associated with the occurrence of lower extremity infections, revascularisations, or amputations.

The 2011 Japanese Society for Dialysis Therapy (JSDT) and the 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend regular foot examinations and PAD screening using ABI for patients receiving haemodialysis and patients aged >50 years with chronic kidney disease, respectively [11, 27]. It is plausible that compliance with the requirements for dialysis facilities to claim the incentive may increase the frequency of regular screening tests and foot care, early detection of PAD, referrals to collaborating hospitals, wound care, and revascularisation procedures; moreover, it may decrease the incidence of lower extremity infections and amputations. Although the effectiveness of PAD screening, foot care, patient education encompassing foot self-care, and hospital-clinic collaboration for lower extremity amputation or infection in patients receiving haemodialysis has not been fully investigated, a few studies have suggested the promising potential of these interventions.

An observational study of incident patients with diabetes undergoing haemodialysis in a national dialysis chain found that monthly foot assessments and patient education provided by dialysis nurses and collaboration of dialysis facilities and wound care physicians, podiatrists, and orthopaedics were associated with a 17% lower rate of lower extremity amputations [28]. In a small randomised controlled study of patients with diabetes undergoing dialysis at a dialysis centre, no amputations occurred in the group that received a similar intervention, whereas five lower extremity amputations occurred in the usual-care group [29].

This study's results do not support the hypothesis that claims for the incentive reduce the incidence of lower extremity amputations, with a point estimate of the HR for amputations close to one. Based on our analysis, possible reasons for this are that the incentive did not promote the prevention of infection causing amputations and the implementation of revascularisation for limb salvage.

Our finding that increased access to screening tests may not have increased access to revascularisations in patients with incentive claims suggests that barriers to the flow of the medical care process possibly existed at some phase of the diagnosis of PAD after screening, referral from the dialysis facility to the collaborating hospital, and follow-up after referral.

First, the inadequate implementation of screening tests may have hindered early PAD detection. Patients with incentive claims were screened significantly more frequently than those without incentive claims; however, the frequency was 0.86 per year, which is less than what was recommended by the 2011 JSDT guidelines (once a year) [11]. This frequency may reflect non-compliance with the conditions required for claiming the incentive by dialysis facilities, as indicated by the low proportion of screening tests performed in patients with incentive claims (63%).

Next, the predominant use of ABI in screening tests (78%) and low ABI and SPP thresholds for referrals may have led to the underdiagnosis of PAD and fewer referrals. The sensitivity of ABI ˂0.9, which is a common criterion for diagnosing lower extremity PAD, is reduced in patients on dialysis owing to vascular calcification [30]. A Japanese study involving patients on dialysis reported a higher cut-off value of 1.06, the optimal ABI for PAD diagnosis [31]. In this study, incentive claims were associated with a significant increase in the frequency of SPPs, which were not affected by vascular calcification but only accounted for 22% of the total number of screening tests during the follow-up period.

Finally, the inability of collaborating hospitals that provide specialised treatment for PAD to claim these financial incentives may have been a factor in the absence of increased revascularisation during follow-up after referral.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between the programme that financially incentivises dialysis facilities that provided early detection and management of PAD and process and outcome indicators of PAD, suggesting that this programme may not be producing the expected effects despite the substantial expenditure.

This study has some limitations. First, the small sample size or short follow-up period may have reduced the number of lower extremity amputations, making it difficult to estimate the HR for amputations with high precision or focus on major amputations. Second, misclassification of confounders or outcomes is possible because this study used administrative claims data. For example, we included digit amputations in the definition of lower extremity amputations. Because the digits include fingers and toes in the database, this could have led to an overestimation of amputation risk. However, as most digit amputation sites are assumed to be on the toes, the effect of this bias on the results is expected to be small and towards the null. Furthermore, to increase infection specificity, we defined infection diagnoses using a combination of the disease name and systemic antibiotics or related medical practices. We also performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to infectious disease names in the hospitalisation setting to confirm the consistency of our results. Third, this study's observational nature and use of an administrative claims database may have resulted in residual confounding. Because the JMDC database contains limited data on smoking and no data on dialysis vintage, potential risk factors for amputation [5, 21], we could not include these variables in the model for the main analyses. However, the results of the sensitivity analysis considering dialysis vintage were consistent with the main results. Fourth, patients included in the JMDC database were aged ≤74 years, mostly Asians, and may have had a higher socioeconomic status than the general population. Therefore, the generalisability of our results may be limited. Finally, although the foot care programme was not standardised, and variations may exist in its implementation across dialysis facilities, we could not examine the association between the incentive claims and process indicators, such as foot care, patient education, and referrals to collaborating hospitals because the JMDC database contained no direct information on them.

In conclusion, the financial incentive for early detection and management of PAD was associated with an improved process indicator (i.e. higher frequency of PAD screening tests); however, no association with the outcome indicators (infections, revascularisation procedures, and amputations in the lower extremities) was observed in patients undergoing haemodialysis. Further studies with larger populations, including more older adults, and longer observation periods are needed to accurately assess and suggest ways to improve the effects of this incentive programme on PAD outcomes.
