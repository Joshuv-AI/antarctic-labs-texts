Dietary taurine supplementation ameliorates growth performance, intestinal health, and environmental stress tolerance in summer-reared Megalobrama amblycephala
Authors: Kang Chen, Lu Chen, Zheng He, Peiyu Xie, Hong Liu, Zexia Gao, Huanling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rising frequencies of summer heatwaves driven by climate change impose severe physiological stress on aquaculture species. This study investigated the effects of dietary taurine supplementation (0.0, 0.8%, 1.2%, and 2.0%) on the growth, intestinal health, and environmental stress tolerance of Megalobrama amblycephala. Over an 8-week trial conducted under natural temperature fluctuations (26.5‒35.3 °C), 360 fish (initial body weight: 6.47 ± 0.04 g) were randomly allocated into 12 outdoor cages (three replicates per treatment). Taurine supplementation significantly enhanced growth performance, increasing final body weight (FBW), final body length (FBL), weight gain rate (WGR), and specific growth rate (SGR) compared to the control (P < 0.001), while survival rate was unaffected (P = 0.627). Polynomial regression identified 1.460% dietary taurine as the optimal level for maximizing SGR. Whole-body composition analysis revealed that taurine elevated crude protein and reduced ash contents (P < 0.05), with no significant difference in moisture or crude lipid (P > 0.05). Furthermore, taurine improved the muscle amino acid profile, significantly raising the levels of both essential and non-essential amino acids among the 17 amino acids detected (P < 0.05). Taurine supplementation enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity by increasing total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and the activities of the key serum antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase [SOD] and catalase [CAT]), reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap-1)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway (P < 0.05). It also exerted anti-inflammatory effects via suppressing the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (il-1β, tnf-α, and il-8) and increasing anti-inflammatory mediators (tgf-β and il-10) (P < 0.05). Intestinal health was improved, as evidenced by increased villus height and muscularis thickness, higher digestive enzyme activities (amylase, lipase, and trypsin), and upregulated mucin-related gene expression (P < 0.05). Finally, taurine supplementation improved the tolerance of M. amblycephala to environmental stress. Although 1.460% taurine maximized SGR, a lower supplementation level of 0.8% provided the best overall performance when considering feed conversion ratio (FCR) and stress tolerance. Therefore, a dietary taurine supplementation of 0.8% is recommended for optimal overall performance, effectively promoting growth, reducing FCR, strengthening antioxidant and anti-inflammatory defenses, supporting intestinal barrier function, and improving climate stress tolerance in M. amblycephala. These findings support the potential practical use of taurine as an ecologically sustainable feed additive that concurrently addresses production performance and climate adaptation in M. amblycephala aquaculture.

Introduction

Intensive aquaculture has significantly propelled the global aquaculture industry in recent years, yet this production model presents substantial challenges (Chen et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2020). Under high-density conditions, fish face increased risks of nutritional imbalance, metabolic disorders, and stress-induced disease (Chen et al., 2025). These issues are compounded by global climate change, which elevates water temperatures and directly stresses aquaculture species (Hu et al., 2023). The synergistic pressures from intensive farming and environmental warming hinder the sustainable development of the aquaculture industry and cause considerable economic losses (Huo et al., 2020, 2019; Sun et al., 2020). Megalobrama amblycephala, an important freshwater aquaculture species in China, is a warm-water fish with an optimal growth temperature range of 20‒28 °C. Increasingly frequent summer heatwaves, often accompanied by declines in dissolved oxygen (DO), critically compromise its reproduction and growth (Chen et al., 2025, 2024). Although fish can acclimate to moderate stress through physiological adjustments (Hu et al., 2023; Huo et al., 2019), excessive stress disrupts growth trajectories, metabolic homeostasis, disease resistance, manifesting as growth retardation, metabolic dysfunction, and immunosuppression (Chen et al., 2025; González-Ruiz et al., 2022). Consequently, developing effective nutritional strategies to mitigate these impacts is a critical research priority for both academia and industry.

Taurine (2-aminoethanesulfonic acid), a conditionally essential amino acid first discovered and isolated from bovine bile, exists predominantly in free form in aquatic species and is not incorporated into proteins (Salze et al., 2012; Salze and Davis, 2015). Despite this, it performs crucial physiological roles, including maintaining cellular osmotic homeostasis, scavenging oxygen-free radicals, modulating immune and endocrine functions, and regulating glucose and lipid metabolism (Salze and Davis, 2015; Seol et al., 2021). Its benefits extend to demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic capabilities (Ogun et al., 2025; Salze and Davis, 2015). Empirical studies also confirm its various roles, ranging from accelerating growth in Monopterus albus (Shi et al., 2022b) and Cyprinus carpio (Abdel-Tawwab and Monier, 2017), to enhancing antioxidant defenses and immune capacity in Clarias gariepinus (Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab, 2020). Taurine also improves osmotic homeostasis in Oncorhynchus mykiss (Huang et al., 2021), mitigates cadmium-induced oxidative stress across hepatic and renal systems of Clarias batrachus (Kumar et al., 2009), and alleviates intestinal oxidative damage while maintaining immune integrity of Ictalurus punctatus (Shi et al., 2021). These findings collectively establish taurine as a potent enhancer of both productivity and physiological resilience in aquaculture.

Intestinal immunity underpins immune homeostasis, with its integrity being critical for sustainable aquaculture (Ogun et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2022a). This physiological foundation relies on synergy between the physical barrier, redox defense systems, and immunomodulatory networks (Wang et al., 2025). A central regulator within this system is the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf 2)/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap-1) signaling pathway. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 stabilizes, translocates to the nucleus, and induces the expression of cytoprotective genes (such as ho-1, sod, and cat), thereby strengthening cellular antioxidant defenses (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022). Taurine contributes to intestinal health partly by supporting this mechanism; for example, in Trachinotus ovatus, dietary taurine improves growth, antioxidant status, and disease resistance via Nrf2/Keap-1/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway activation (Liu et al., 2022). Beyond modulating redox balance, taurine directly promotes structural integrity, increasing villus height and muscularis thickness in M. albus (Shi et al., 2022a), and acts as an immunoregulator, attenuating intestinal inflammation in species like I. punctatus (Shi et al., 2021), Ctenopharyngodon idella (Yan et al., 2019) and Dicentrarchus labrax (Martins et al., 2019), collectively demonstrating its role in promoting intestinal health.

Despite the broad functions of taurine, the actual efficacy and appropriate addition level of taurine in M. amblycephala in response to sustained high-temperature stress in summer remain to be elucidated. Building on our previous non-targeted metabolomics analysis that identified taurine as a promising mitigant against heat and hypoxia stress (Chen et al., 2025). This study investigated its dietary role under a scenario of persistent and more ecologically relevant summer high-temperature stress, seeking to bridge fundamental research with practical solutions for climate-resilient aquaculture. It was hypothesized that dietary taurine would improve growth performance, enhance antioxidant capacity via the Nrf2/Keap-1 pathway, strengthen intestinal barrier integrity, and increase tolerance to heat and hypoxia stress. Furthermore, by defining distinct optimal taurine levels for maximizing growth versus ensuring comprehensive stress adaptability, this study provides a practical feed formulation strategy to enhance the climate adaptability of M. amblycephala aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All these experiments conformed to the ethical guidelines of Huazhong Agriculture University for the use and care of animals and were approved by the University's Ethical Committee (approval No. 202510070006, date of approval: 1 August 2025).

Experimental diets

The basic diets were expanded compound feeds produced by Guangdong Evergreen Feed Industry Co., Ltd. (Zhanjiang, Guangdong, China). Table 1 lists the basic detailed composition of the basal diet, with moisture, crude protein, crude fat, and ash contents of 9.58%, 35.39%, 8.14%, and 8.06%, respectively. High-purity taurine (purity 99.99%) was purchased from Hubei Yuanda Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Huangshi, Hubei, China). Four graded taurine levels (0.0, 0.8%, 1.2%, and 2.0%) were selected based on previously reported optimal ranges for various teleost species, including C. carpio (Abdel-Tawwab and Monier, 2017), O. mykiss (Huang et al., 2021), and C. gariepinus (Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab, 2020). Precisely weighed taurine was dissolved in water to form solutions containing 0, 8, 12, or 20 g of taurine per kg of basal diet, corresponding to the T0.0 (control), T0.8, T1.2, and T2.0 groups, respectively. In order to enhance the attachment of taurine on the surface of feed particles and reduce its dissolution loss after water, an appropriate amount of feed-grade sodium carboxymethylcellulose (FH9, Changshu Wealthy Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China.) was added to each taurine solution as a binder (Ajay et al., 2024), and the components were evenly dispersed after full stirring. Each solution was uniformly sprayed onto the basal diet with concurrent manual stirring to ensure a consistent coating on all pellets. The treated feed was then spread in a thin layer and air-dried at room temperature until surface-dry before immediate use. Experimental design and culture management

The experiment was conducted at the experimental aquaculture facility of Hanchuan Evergreen Feed Co., Ltd. (Hanchuan, Hubei, China). Net cages (2 m × 2 m × 2 m) were suspended in outdoor earthen ponds averaging 2.5 m in depth. Juvenile M. amblycephala were bought from a local fish farm (Hanchuan Evergreen Feed Industry Co., Ltd., Xiaogan, Hubei, China) and domesticated for 2 weeks using a control diet. Then, 360 healthy fish with uniform size (initial body weight: 6.47 ± 0.04 g) were selected, fasted for 24 h, and randomly assigned to four experimental groups (T0.0, T0.8, T1.2, and T2.0 groups). Each experimental diet group was randomly assigned to three replicate cages, with 30 fish per cage. Fish were fed twice daily to apparent satiation by hand (07:30 and 17:30) over an 8-week period. Feed intake and mortality were recorded daily. The body weight of the fish in each cage was measured every 14 d. All cages remained under natural photoperiod conditions throughout the trial.

Water quality was maintained through a daily exchange of approximately 10% of the total pond volume. Each cage received continuous aeration from a dedicated air stone. A central impeller aerator was operated on sunny days in two 2 h intervals (04:00–06:00 and 13:00–15:00). During overcast or rainy periods, DO was monitored periodically, with supplemental aeration initiated when concentrations fell below 3 mg/L. Key water quality parameters were monitored weekly and maintained within the following ranges: pH 6.5‒9.0, DO > 6 mg/L, and total ammonia nitrogen < 0.3 mg/L.

Based on the species’ preference for middle and lower water layers, water temperature was recorded daily at a 1.5-m depth at 07:30 and 17:30 throughout the 56-d experimental period. Sample collection

Upon completion of the 8-week culture period, all fish were fasted for 24 h. After rapid anesthesia with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222; 100 mg/L, M14788, AbMole Bioscience Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and counting, three fish from each cage were randomly selected and stored at −20 °C for whole-body composition analysis. Four fish were randomly selected from each cage, and their body length and weight were measured to calculate growth performance and individual index. Blood samples were obtained from the tail vein using a 1 mL syringe, left at 4 °C for 2 h, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and the serum was separated and stored at −20 °C for the determination of serum biochemical indicators. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was quickly opened to separate the visceral mass, liver, intestine and other tissues, and then the visceral mass and liver were weighed. Then, a small segment of the hindgut was removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h for subsequent histological examination.

The remaining intestinal tissues were divided into two parts: one part was homogenized and stored at −20 °C for the quantification of digestive enzyme activities; the other part was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently archived at −80 °C for long-term storage. Finally, dorsal skeletal muscle samples were taken, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for muscle nutrient composition detection.

Growth performance analysis

Growth performance was evaluated using the following key metrics and their respective calculation formulas:Weight gain rate (WGR, %) = [Wt (g)/Nt − W0 (g)]/N0/[W0 (g)/N0] × 100;Specific growth rate (SGR, %/d) = [ln Wt (g)/Nt – ln W0 (g)/N0]/t × 100;Survival rate (SR, %) = Nt/N0 × 100;Hepatosomatic index (HSI, %) = Wh (g)/W (g) × 100;Viscerasomatic index (VSI, %) = Wv (g)/W (g) × 100;Condition factor (CF, g/cm3) = W (g)/L (cm) × 100;Feed intake (FI, g/fish) = Wf (g)/[(N0 + Nt)/2];Feed conversion ratio (FCR) = FI/[Wt (g) − W0 (g)];

where Wt is the final body weight; Nt is the final number of fish; W0 is the initial fish body weight; N0 is the initial number of fish; t is the experimental daysd; Wh is the liver weight; W is the fish body weight; Wv is the viscera weight; L is fish length; Wf is feed intake.

Proximate composition analysis

Whole-body composition and diet formulations were assessed using AOAC (2005) standard methods. In brief, moisture contents were determined by oven-drying at 105 °C to constant weight (method 935.29). Crude protein and crude lipid contents were analyzed by the Kjeldahl method (method 960.52) and the Soxhlet extraction method using petroleum ether (method 920.39), respectively. Ash content was determined by calcination at 550 °C to a constant weight (method 942.05). Muscle amino acid composition was analyzed in accordance with GB/T 18246–2019 (China National Standard, 2019), which typically involves acid hydrolysis followed by chromatographic separation and quantification.

Serum biomarker quantification

Serum concentrations of growth hormone (GH; YJ072221), insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1; YJ322003; IGF-2; YJ036383), lysozyme (LZM; YJ296520), reactive oxygen species (ROS; ml505698), and immunoglobulin M (IgM; YJ955010) were quantified using (enzyme linked immunosorbent assay) ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-2-1), catalase (CAT; A007-1-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-3-2) activities, and malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2) concentration were analyzed using corresponding kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). All serum samples were analyzed in duplicates (two technical replicates), and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Determination of intestinal digestive enzyme activities

Intestinal total protein (TP; E-BC-K318-M) concentration, trypsin (E-BC-K843-M), lipase (E-BC-K786-M), and amylase (E-BC-K006-M) activities were detected in strict accordance with the instruction manual of the kit from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Absorbance values were measured using a SpectraMax ABS full-wavelength microplate reader (Meigu Molecular Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Each intestinal homogenate sample was assayed in duplicate (two technical replicates) for each enzyme, and the average absorbance was used to calculate enzyme activity.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Intestinal RNA was extracted from individual fish using Trizol reagent from Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd. (DP424, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For each biological replicate (cage), intestinal tissues from two fish were processed independently. Subsequently, cDNA was then synthesized individually for each fish using a reverse transcription kit (R323–01, Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., NanJing, Jiangsu, China). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed in duplicate for each cDNA sample with Monad 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix Kit (160,734, Monad Biotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect platform (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Primers were designed according to the NCBI Primer-BLAST online tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) under the following parameters: amplicon size of 80‒250 bp, the primer length of 18‒22 bp, GC content of 40%‒60%, and a melting temperature of 58‒62 °C. To avoid genomic DNA amplification, primers were designed to span exon–exon junctions whenever possible. The stability of the candidate reference gene, β-actin, was evaluated by the Genorm/Normfinder algorithm; its expression stability (M value < 0.5) confirmed its suitability for normalization. The abbreviations and full names of all genes are provided in supplementary file abbreviations and full names of genes and proteins (Table S1). Relative gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin reference and calculated via 2−ΔΔCt analysis (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), with all primer sequences detailed in Table S2.

Histological analysis

Intestinal samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial 4 μm sections were cut using a microtome (UC7 RT, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). After sealing the neutral gum, intestinal morphological parameters were examined and images were captured by a motorized fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ni-E, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Intestinal morphological indices were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with three random regions sampled per experimental group.

Alcian blue–periodic acid–Schiff (AB-PAS) staining: the paraffin embedding process was the same as that for H&E staining. Serial 7 μm sections were poly-L-lysine-coated slide-mounted prior to AB-PAS staining. Staining was performed by preheating Alcian blue (AB) solution in a 60 °C oven for 10 min. After rinsing, the slides were sequentially treated with 1% periodate and Schiff's reagent, each applied with microwave heating for 5 min. After ethanol dehydration and xylene transparency, the sections were sealed with Canada balsam (C0174, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Stained sections were observed under a light microscope (Olympus CX41, Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and images were acquired using Motic Images Plus 2.0 ML software (Motic China Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, Fujian, China). For each intestinal segment, three individuals per treatment group were selected, and goblet cells were counted in representative areas using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Cryosections were warmed to 37 °C for 10 min and fixed in ice-cold acetone for 30 min. Antigen retrieval was achieved by boiling in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer 20 min, followed by naturally cooling. Sections underwent 30 min blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to saturate nonspecific sites, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit polyclonal anti-zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) primary antibody (GB15195, 1:300, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). After three PBS washes, samples were probed with a fluorophoreconjugated (FITC) secondary antibody (GB22401, 1:300, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) at 37 °C for 50 min, with intermittent PBS rinses. The 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was applied for 10 min for nuclear counterstaining, and then sections were subjected to autofluorescence quenching for 5 min. Images were captured using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ni–U, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blotting analysis

Approximately 500 mg of intestinal tissue from M. amblycephala was homogenized with 0.5 mL precooled radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (G2002, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was analyzed by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein content assay kit (AKPR017, Beijing Boxbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The protein samples were prepared by adding 5 × loading buffer (WB-0091, Dingguo Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and denaturing at 98 to 100 °C for 10 min. The samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 10% concentrated gel (P0012A, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 80 V for 30 min followed by 120 V for 60 min, followed by transferring to nitrocellulose (NC) membrane at a constant voltage of 100 V. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk powder for more than 1 h, then incubated with rabbit antibodies against HO-1 (A1346, 1:1000), NRF2 (A21176, 1:2000), P65 (A19653, 1:5000), and β-actin (AC026, 1:100,000) at 4 °C for 8 h. All antibodies were purchased from ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). After washing the membranes, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (bs-40295G-HRP, 1:10,000, Bioss Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 to 2 h. After another wash, protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey Clx infrared imaging system (9140, LI-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA), and their band intensities were quantified by ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Survival statistics after hypoxia or heat treatment

For the stress challenge, 27 fish were randomly selected from each dietary treatment (T0.0, T0.8, T1.2, and T2.0), comprising nine individuals from each of three replicate cages per treatment. These fish were then transferred to independent challenge tanks, designated for either heat or hypoxia stress experiments. Each stress assay thus involved a total of 108 fish (4 treatments × 27 fish) distributed among several challenge tanks. Heat stress: the water temperature in the tanks was precisely regulated using submerged heaters. The water temperature was initially maintained at 28 °C, and then increased stepwise by 0.5 °C every 30 min to 38 °C (maintained for 24 h), then to 39 °C (maintained for 24 h), and finally to 40 °C. Once stabilized at 40 °C (Fig. S1A), the fish were held at this temperature, and mortality was monitored every 30 min by removing and recording dead individuals until all fish in the group had died. Hypoxia stress: the level of DO was controlled by infusing the water with nitrogen or oxygen bubbled into the water. The DO level was reduced from 7.00 mg/L by 1.00 mg/L every 30 min until it reached approximately 0.48 mg/L (indicated by fish surfacing). Subsequently, the DO was maintained at 0.48 ± 0.20 mg/L (Fig. S1B), and the dead fish were recorded and cleared every 30 min until the whole group died. For both experiments, death was defined by the absence of response to external stimuli and the cessation of gill cover movement.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analyses, homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene's test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (SPSS 19.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) identified significant differences (P < 0.05) using Duncan's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Linear and quadratic effects of dietary taurine levels were evaluated. To estimate the optimal dietary taurine level, growth data (SGR and WGR) were subjected to polynomial regression analysis using the following model:Y=β0+β1X+β2X2,where Y is the growth parameter; X represents the dietary taurine levels; β0 is the intercept, and β1 and β2 are the linear and quadratic regression coefficients, respectively. The optimal level is calculated by setting the first derivative of the quadratic equation to zero (dY/dX = 0). Results are presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM).

The statistical model for the ANOVA was specified as follows:Yij=μ+αi+εij,where Yij is the observed value of the dependent variable; μ is the grand mean across all observations; αi is the effect of the i-th treatment (different dietary taurine levels); εij is to the random error associated with the j-th observation within the i-th treatment. Survival following hypoxia or heat stress was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences among dietary treatments were evaluated with the log-rank test.

Results

Growth performance and feed utilization

The results of growth performance and feed utilization are summarized in Table 2. Over the 8-week trial, no significant difference occurred in the SR of M. amblycephala (P > 0.05). The FBW, FBL, WGR, and SGR were significantly higher in all taurine-supplemented groups (T0.8, T1.2, and T2.0 groups) compared to the T0.0 group (P < 0.001). These growth parameters exhibited significant linear responses to increasing taurine levels (P < 0.001). Survival after hypoxia and heat challenge

To assess the contribution of taurine to environmental stress tolerance, M. amblycephala fed taurine-supplemented diets for 8 weeks were subjected to heat or hypoxia challenge tests. Under hypoxic stress, all experimental groups reached 100% mortality within 7.5 h. Under heat stress, a different survival pattern emerged. Fish in the T0.8 and T1.2 groups had significantly prolonged survival times. Discussion

Growth promotion by taurine and underlying mechanisms in M. amblycephala

Taurine, a widely distributed free non-protein amino acid in vertebrate tissues, plays pivotal physiological roles across diverse taxa (Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab, 2020). While some fish species can synthesize taurine endogenously, de novo production is often insufficient to meet metabolic demands during rapid growth or environmental stress, rendering dietary supplementation beneficial (Dong et al., 2018; Espe et al., 2012). In this study, dietary taurine supplementation significantly improved the growth performance of M. amblycephala, as evidenced by significant increases in the WGR and SGR. This finding corroborates earlier observations of taurine-mediated growth enhancement in various fish species, such as M. albus (Shi et al., 2022b), C. carpio (Abdel-Tawwab and Monier, 2017), Epinephelus fuscoguttatus (♀) × E. lanceolatus (♂) (Qian et al., 2021), C. idella (Pan et al., 2023), and Morone chrysops × M. saxatilis (Suehs and Gatlin, 2021), confirming its conserved biological function in growth regulation.

Quadratic regression analysis of SGR determined an optimal dietary taurine requirement at 1.460% for M. amblycephala. This value is comparable to the requirements reported for Paralichthys olivaceus (1.5%‒2.0%) (Park et al., 2002) and Seriola quinqueradiata (>1.0%) (Matsunari et al., 2010), but substantially surpasses the dependency level in D. labrax (0.2%) (Jose et al., 2010). Such interspecific variability may be related to species, divergent dietary compositions, rearing conditions, and fish size. Notably, taurine supplementation beyond the optimal level did not yield further growth benefits and even led to a decline in performance, highlighting the importance of precise dosage in aquafeed formulation. This may be a common phenomenon in aquatic nutrition, as evidenced by a dose-dependent growth promotion in juvenile T. ovatus, which peaked at 1.2% taurine supplementation and declined at 1.6% (Liu et al., 2022). Importantly, taurine showed stable growth-promoting effects in a variety of dietary formulations, including high-plant-protein (Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab, 2020), high-carbohydrate (Shi et al., 2022b), low-fishmeal diets (Liu et al., 2022), and standard commercial diets (Yan et al., 2019), highlighting its versatility as a feed additive.

In conjunction with its growth-promoting effects, dietary taurine supplementation also markedly influenced the body composition of M. amblycephala. A significant increase in whole-body crude protein was observed in fish fed diets containing 1.2% and 2.0% taurine supplementation, supporting the premise that taurine regulates protein anabolism to support growth (Li et al., 2008). This phenomenon is consistent with reports in other fish species, such as Salmo salar (Espe et al., 2012) and Scophthalmus maximus (Qi et al., 2012). Furthermore, taurine supplementation significantly enriched the contents of both EAA and NEAA in muscle. The elevated protein and amino acid levels may be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. Taurine facilitates the metabolic utilization of sulfur-containing amino acids such as methionine, thereby improving their efficiency for protein synthesis and deposition (Li et al., 2008; Salze and Davis, 2015). It may also protect the intestinal mucosa from anti-nutritional factors present in plant ingredients, such as saponins, thereby promoting amino acid absorption (Bavi et al., 2022). Another potential mechanism involves the upregulation of digestive protease activity, as observed in Rachycentron canadum (Salze et al., 2012), suggesting that taurine may stimulate protease secretion. A further noteworthy finding was that taurine supplementation significantly reduced whole-body ash content in juvenile M. amblycephala. Ash reflects total inorganic mineral content, and its decline reflects altered mineral metabolic balance. As a key substance to maintain osmotic pressure, taurine may enhance the ion transport activity of gills and kidneys under heat stress, promote the excretion of non-essential minerals, and thus optimize the ion balance in vivo (Salze and Davis, 2015; Shi et al., 2022b). This mechanism appears conserved among teleosts, with analogous ash reduction reported in M. albus, Acanthopagrus latus, and Oreochromis niloticus (Al-Feky et al., 2016; Dehghani et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2022a). The observed decline in whole-body ash content correlated with improved stress adaptability in fish, and no associated physiological dysfunctions were detected. These findings suggest that reduced ash levels reflect an adaptive metabolic adjustment rather than a pathological condition induced by mineral inadequacy. Nevertheless, the specific molecular mechanisms governing this metabolic shift require further clarification.

Fish growth is regulated by a complex network involving digestive efficiency, intestinal morphology, and endocrine signaling (Dehghani et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022). The GH promotes tissue development and protein biosynthesis by stimulating IGFs (Shi et al., 2020), which are crucial mediators in fish ontogeny (Nipkow et al., 2018). Taurine is known to induce GH release in mammals (Ikuyama et al., 1988). In the present study, dietary taurine elevated serum GH and IGF-2 in M. amblycephala, consistent with findings in T. ovatus (Wang et al., 2024). It is worth noting that IGF-1 exerts negative feedback on GH secretion. Hepatic IGF-1 biosynthesis is strictly GH-dependent, whereas IGF-2 production is less influenced by GH (Jasminka et al., 2007), which may explain the lack of significant IGF-1 elevation in the present study.

Dietary taurine modulates the Nrf2/keap 1/HO-1 pathway to enhance antioxidant capacity in M. amblycephala

The pathogenesis of fish enteritis fundamentally involves the accumulation of ROS and a consequent breakdown of redox homeostasis (Martins et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2021, 2022a). Given that antioxidant capacity is an important determinant of health and is strongly correlated with growth performance in aquatic animals (Dehghani et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2018), enhancing this defense system is of central importance. Taurine confers cytoprotective effects by directly scavenging various free radicals, such as hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anions, and by enhancing endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT (Salze and Davis, 2015). Studies have confirmed that dietary taurine supplementation enhances antioxidant enzyme activities and reduces ROS generation in multiple aquatic species (Dong et al., 2018). For example, in A. latus, dietary taurine improved the growth rate, antioxidant capacity, and digestive enzyme activities (Dehghani et al., 2020). Similarly, in Eriocheir sinensis, taurine supplementation (0.4%–0.8%) significantly increased the activities of T-AOC, SOD, and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), while diminishing MDA accumulation (Dong et al., 2018). The results obtained in M. amblycephala align with these established effects. In this study, dietary taurine supplementation elevated serum levels of T-AOC, CAT, and SOD, concurrently attenuating serum concentrations of MDA and ROS. At the molecular level, taurine-supplemented groups upregulated the intestinal expression of key antioxidant genes, including cu/zn sod, mn sod, cat, and gpx-1a. The observed antioxidant enhancement is mechanistically linked to the Nrf2-Keap 1 signaling axis, a master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis (Liu et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2020). Activation of this pathway involves the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, which transactivates target genes such as ho-1 by binding to antioxidant response elements, thereby rapidly inducing a cytoprotective response (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). Taurine chloramine (TauCl), a derivative of taurine, has been demonstrated to activate ho-1 transcription via the Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 pathway, fortifying the cellular antioxidant defense (Jang et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2022; Seol et al., 2021). This pathway is functionally conserved in teleosts, as illustrated by its role in mitigating ammonia toxicity and copper-induced oxidative damage in T. ovatus (Liu et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2020). In the present study, taurine-supplemented groups significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 while suppressing keap 1 expression in the intestine of M. amblycephala. These findings strongly support compelling evidence that the taurine-induced augmentation of antioxidant capacity is associated with the activation of the conserved Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 signaling pathway.

Dietary taurine augments systemic humoral immunity and attenuates intestinal inflammation via NF-κB suppression in M. amblycephala

Compromised innate immunity and tissue damage are common consequences of oxidative stress in teleosts (Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022). Evaluating fish immunocompetence often involves monitoring serum parameters: LZM and IgM are established primary biomarkers, while enzymes such as ACP and AKP contribute to defensive mechanisms, offering a composite view of systemic immune status (Chen et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2021). In M. amblycephala, dietary taurine supplementation resulted in elevated serum LZM activity alongside increased concentrations of IgM and AKP, signaling a reinforcement of both innate and adaptive immunological capacity. The immune-enhancing role of taurine observed herein is consistent with findings in other fish species, supporting its recognized benefits for fish health. These findings align with documented evidence, including increased IgM and complement components (C3/C4) alongside reduced lipid peroxidation in juvenile I. punctatus (Shi et al., 2021), and enhanced LZM activity, total immunoglobulins, and complement levels in Carassius auratus under ammonia stress (Ren et al., 2016). Furthermore, taurine's role in alleviating toxicity from pollutants like heavy metals underscores its function in supporting physiological robustness (Cheng et al., 2018; Li et al., 2016; Xing et al., 2016). The simultaneous upregulation of antioxidant defenses and immune parameters observed here implies that taurine assists in preserving immune cell efficacy by stabilizing the intracellular redox environment.

Taurine also demonstrates targeted modulatory effects on inflammatory processes. The cascade of inflammatory events, a critical defense against insults, is predominantly governed by the NF-κB signaling pathway, involving inhibitor kappa B (IkB) and IkB kinases (IKK) proteins (Liu et al., 2022). The present findings indicated that dietary taurine effectively attenuated the activation of this central pathway within the intestinal tissue of summer-reared M. amblycephala. This was evidenced by significant down-regulation of core NF-κB elements (P65, nf-κb, ikkβ, and ikkγ) at the transcriptional level, upregulation of the inhibitor ikbα, and a concomitant decrease in NF-κB P65 protein abundance. Together, these changes were indicative of attenuated NF-κB signaling pathway activation. Within the classical NF-κB signaling pathway, IKK-induced IkB degradation liberates NF-κB dimers, triggering transcriptional activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-8, and TNF-α (Liu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). Taurine demonstrates conserved immunoregulatory functions across vertebrates, promoting lymphocyte mitogenesis and suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators in mammals (Liu et al., 2022; Salze and Davis, 2015). In fish, inflammatory homeostasis depends on a balance between pro-inflammatory (IL-1β, IL-8, and TNF-α) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and TGF-β) (Liu et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2019). Supporting this, taurine has been shown to inhibit NF-κB expression, downregulate pro-inflammatory genes (il-1β, il-6, tnf-α, and il-8), and upregulate tgf-β in I. punctatus, thereby enhancing intestinal immune function and promoting intestinal health (Shi et al., 2021). Similarly, the present study showed that taurine supplementation significantly decreased intestinal mRNA levels of il-1β, il-8, and tnf-α, while increasing expression of the anti-inflammatory mediators il-10 and tgf-β in M. amblycephala. In summary, taurine modulated the immune response in M. amblycephala through a dual mechanism. It enhanced systemic immune readiness by elevating key humoral factors. Simultaneously, it selectively curbed excessive intestinal inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. This combined action, which fortifies overall immunocompetence while precisely attenuating dysregulated inflammatory responses, underscores the protective role of taurine in sustaining intestinal mucosal immunity and overall physiological homeostasis under conditions of thermal stress.

The preservation of intestinal integrity is attributable to dietary taurine supplementation in M. amblycephala

The fish mucosal immune system employs both specific (antibodies and lymphocytes) and nonspecific (macrophage activity, complement activation, and enzymatic defenses) components that collectively prevent pathogen invasion through cellular and humoral mechanisms (Dehghani et al., 2020). Structural preservation of intestinal architecture remains a prerequisite for efficient nutrient absorption, immune surveillance, and digestive physiology (Wang et al., 2025). Digestive enzymes are secreted by the digestive system and related glands to facilitate nutrient digestion and absorption, and are important indicators for assessing the digestive capacity, nutritional status and growth performance of fish (Fang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2016). Taurine supplementation has a pronounced protective effect on intestinal morphology. For example, it promotes villus elongation and goblet cell hyperplasia in C. carpio (Magouz et al., 2020), and enhances villus architecture in M. albus fed high-fat diets (Shi et al., 2022a). Consistent with these findings, the present results demonstrated that taurine-supplemented groups of M. amblycephala simultaneously increased villus height, muscularis thickness, and goblet cell number, thereby reinforcing the intestinal mucosal barrier. This structural fortification is crucial, as environmental stressors are known to compromise barrier function by transcriptionally suppressing TJ proteins, leading to increased intestinal permeability (Shang et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024). The present study revealed significantly diminished TJ proteins expression in the T0.0 group compared with taurine-supplemented groups. Notably, dietary taurine counteracted thermal stress-induced ZO-1 downregulation during summer conditions. The protective mechanism involves enhanced goblet cell differentiation and mucin secretion that preserve junctional complexes, ultimately normalizing intestinal permeability (Morales et al., 2022). The Notch signaling pathway regulates goblet cell development and functionality in fish (Morales et al., 2022). Recent evidence indicated that M. amblycephala enhanced chemical barrier competence through Notch pathway inhibition (downregulating notch 2 and jag1a), stimulating goblet cell proliferation and mucus production (Wang et al., 2025). The elevated goblet cell numbers observed in the taurine-supplemented groups may be associated with suppression of this pathway.

Furthermore, previous studies have established that dietary taurine significantly enhances the digestive enzyme activities during ontogeny in species such as R. canadum (Salze et al., 2012) and Cynoglossus semilaevis (Zheng et al., 2016), contributing to improve nutrient bioavailability and growth rates. The catalytic triad of lipase (triglyceride hydrolysis), amylase (carbohydrate catabolism), and trypsin (proteolytic activation) constitutes the core digestive enzyme system of the fish intestine (Cai et al., 2016; Sagiv, 2001). Studies in juvenile C. semilaevis have demonstrated significant positive correlations between growth performance and the activities of trypsin and lipase (Fang et al., 2017). The results showed that heat stress primarily inhibited the activities of amylase and lipase in M. amblycephala, which likely contributed to the observed growth impairment. Comparable inhibitory effects of stress on digestive enzymology have been reported in hybrid catfish (C. gariepinus ♂ × C. macrocephalus ♀) (Khieokhajonkhet et al., 2022).

Enhancement of M. amblycephala tolerance to environmental stress through dietary taurine supplementation

The present study evaluated the efficacy of dietary taurine supplementation in mitigating the adverse effects of heat or hypoxia stress in M. amblycephala. Taurine supplementation led to a significant extension in survival time when fish were subjected to these challenges. Under hypoxic conditions, the T0.8 and T2.0 groups exhibited delayed mortality, while fish in the T0.8 and T1.2 groups conferred a significant protective effect under heat stress. These findings align with established taurine effects in other species, such as enhanced hypoxia tolerance in C. idella (Yang et al., 2013) and delayed mortality under heat stress in P. olivaceus (Ogun et al., 2025). The improved tolerance of M. amblycephala to these stressors can be attributed to taurine's dual functions as an osmoregulatory and membrane-stabilizing compound, coupled with its antioxidant properties. Taurine can incorporate into cellular membranes, including those of erythrocytes and gill epithelia. This integration aids in maintaining membrane stability during hypoxia episodes, curtails excessive calcium ion influx, and consequently reduces risks associated with cell swelling and lysis (Ogun et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2013). In parallel, taurine's capacity to neutralize ROS and boost mitochondrial antioxidant defenses, such as SOD activity, helps limit oxidative damage to lipids, supports mitochondrial function, and retards apoptotic pathways (Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab, 2020; Cheng et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2013). Despite initial delays in mortality, cumulative survival ultimately converged across all groups under severe stress. This convergence suggests that the intensity of the environmental challenge eventually exceeded the compensatory capacity of taurine, surpassing its protective threshold.

Conclusion

In summary, dietary taurine supplementation significantly promoted the growth, nutritional quality, and overall health of juvenile M. amblycephala under summer high-temperature stress. It improved muscle amino acid composition, enhanced antioxidant capacity via the Nrf2/Keap-1/HO-1 signaling pathway, and strengthened anti-inflammatory response by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway. These combined effects reinforced intestinal barrier integrity and restored digestive enzymes activity, which collectively enhanced physiological adaptability. Under heat or hypoxia, taurine supplementation increased survival and stress adaptability of fish. The primary mechanistic evidence originated from transcriptional data, necessitating protein-level confirmation. Furthermore, taurine's ability to delay mortality exhibited a distinct threshold under extreme stress, indicating bounded efficacy. These summer-specific experimental conditions warrant further investigation across diverse culture environments and life stages. Although the present study identified 1.460% as the level that maximised growth performance (based on SGR), it is 0.8% that given the best overall trade-off between growth, FCR, and environmental stress tolerance. These findings establish taurine as a promising, sustainable feed additive that concurrently addresses productivity and climate adaptability. Future research should explore taurine's effects across environments and long-term application to optimize aquaculture strategies.
