Oral Health and Oral Health-related Quality of Life Among Geriatric Population- A Narrative Review
Authors: Jagreeti Kayasth, Vipul Yadav, Diksha Sharma, Geetanjali Yadav, B. C. Manjunath, Shreya Bhukal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe study aimed to assess how aging affects oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and determine how important dental care is for older people. The database of PubMed Central was electronically searched, and the search strategy was restricted to human studies (cross-sectional, case-control, and single- and double-blinded trials), and full-text English papers published from January 2010 through December 2022. The results showed an evident association between OHRQoL and elderly patients’ dental care, which necessitates preventive and early intervention along with prompt treatment for the elderly.

INTRODUCTION

The term “geriatric population” denotes individuals who are 65 years of age or older. People between the ages of 65 and 74 are referred to as “early elderly,” while those over the age of 75 are referred to as “late elderly.”[1] The World Health Organization estimates that the elderly population is expanding at a rate of 2.5% per year. The factors that are contributing to this demographic transition include advancements in healthcare and living conditions, which are resulting in increases in life expectancy.

The geriatric population continues to encounter substantial obstacles, despite these trends. Age-related declines in cognitive and physical functioning are common among older individuals, which can result in a higher risk of disability and a greater reliance on others for care.[2] Furthermore, chronic diseases, including cancer, diabetes, and heart disease, are more prevalent among older individuals.

In terms of healthcare, older individuals are frequently underserved, as they have restricted access to suitable medical care and rehabilitation services.[3] Social isolation is a common occurrence among older individuals, and it can have a detrimental effect on their mental and physical well-being, leading to a generalized poor quality of life in terms of oral health.[4]

The objective of this review is to evaluate the influence of aging on the oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and determine the importance of dental care for elderly individuals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The database of PubMed Central was electronically searched. The search technique was limited to full-text English papers published from January 2010 through December 2022, and it was limited to human studies (cross-sectional, case-control, and single- and double-blinded trials). Articles that were either irrelevant or lacked sufficient information were excluded. The data search and analysis employed MeSH terms/keywords such as “Geriatric Assessment,” “Oral Health,” “Older Adults,” and “Oral Health Related Quality of Life, India.” A total of 74 studies were found, out of which nine full-text and relevant studies were included.

The most frequently encountered dental diseases among individuals aged 65 and older are oral infections, periodontal disease, edentulism, dental caries, oral mucosal lesions, and temporomandibular pathology, according to the studies.[4] Hyposalivation is a common side effect of long-term medications for chronic diseases such as hypertension or dyslipidemia, which substantially increases the risk of caries and mucosal infections.[5]

According to the literature, edentulism is linked to nutritional deficiencies and has a detrimental effect on the quality of life of elderly individuals. The number of teeth has an impact on masticatory function, functional occlusion difficulties, maximal biting force, and the ability to wear dentures.[6] Oral cancer has a substantial impact on the elderly and adults in both high- and low-income countries.

According to the literature search, a recent study by Muhammad T et al., 2022[7] (India) reported tooth loss in 12.3% and one or more chronic conditions in about 64% of elderly individuals. The study also reported a higher likelihood of poor subjective well-being (32.6%), poor psychological health (27.4%), and poor SRH (80.1%) when smoking, chewing tobacco use, chronic maladies, and tooth loss were present. Another study by Nayan K et al., 2022[6] (India) stated a poor quality of life as assessed by GOHAI, and it was strongly correlated with old age, female gender, and edentulousness. In terms of their treatment requirements, 18.57% of the subjects necessitated emergent care. Intervention was necessary for 45.71% of the individuals, while 34.28% necessitated preventive care.

Mashalkar V et al., 2022[8] (India) reported that 37.7% of the study participants consistently experienced restrictions in their ability to consume a variety of foods, 31.6% experienced difficulty chewing food, and 89.4% reported that they were unable to ingest without experiencing discomfort. Around 69.8% of the participants in the study reported that they were never at ease dining in front of others. The low quality of life of senior patients in terms of their oral health was substantially correlated with the presence of a periodontal pocket, bleeding gums, tooth mobility, and loss of clinical attachment between 0 and 3 mm, as well as the existence of 20 or more absent teeth.

A study by Roma M et al., 2021[9] (India) reported that 70.82% of the participants required prosthetic rehabilitation, the majority of the participants had unmet oral health care needs, and their GOHAI scores were lower, which implies a reduced quality of life in relation to oral health.

Further, Bhat M et al., 2021[10] suggested that poor self-rated oral health is associated with poor socioeconomic status, smoking, dental visits, and severe periodontal disease.

Similarly, studies by Mehta A et al., 2020,[1] Chahar P et al., 2019,[11] Shivakumar KM et al., 2019,[2] Neelamana SK et al., 2020[3] found that older individuals aged 65 and older had higher prevalences of unmet dental treatment requirements and poor oral health status, both of which were associated with poor OHRQoL.

A decline in dental function results in a decline in self-esteem, psychosocial wellness, and nutritional requirements, which in turn impacts general health and ultimately leads to a substandard quality of life in the elderly.[9]

TREATMENT STRATEGIES

Education of both patients and carers regarding the significance of oral hygiene, and empowering elderly patients to maintain improved oral health through appropriate techniques and routines can be achieved by providing comprehensive oral health education, denture hygiene instructions emphasizing the correct cleansing and maintenance of dentures, and oral hygiene instructions covering care of natural teeth, flossing, and brushing.

Legislative measures, public health efforts, specialized dental care programs for the elderly, and integrating oral health care with primary and geriatric care have the potential to enhance awareness of the importance of oral health and encourage the regularity of dental visits. Tele-dentistry can offer remote consultations and follow-up care, thereby increasing the accessibility of dental services for individuals who reside in remote areas or have mobility issues. Support or insurance coverage can mitigate financial obstacles by reducing the costs of dental care.

CONCLUSION

Geriatric assessment was determined to be essential in the majority of studies to prevent comorbidities in these patients. The dental care of elderly patients and OHRQoL were determined to be interrelated. This demonstrates a clear concern for the health needs of the geriatric population by utilizing preventive and early intervention techniques in conjunction with treatment methods.
