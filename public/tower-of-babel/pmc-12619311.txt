Prevalence of ectopic eruption of permanent first molars in a group of children aged 5–8 years in Ankara, Türkiye: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Özlem Beren Satılmış, Nur Sena Önder, Akif Demirel, Şaziye Sarı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEctopic eruption (EE) of permanent first molars (PFMs) occurs due to the abnormal eruption path, which may result in the PFMs being impacted under the distal undercut of primary second molars (PSMs). This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of EE of PFMs and the findings associated with root resorption observed in PSMs.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study, panoramic radiographs of 4426 patients (17704 PFMs) aged 5 to 8 (≈ 7.92) years were investigated. The patients’ date of birth, date of panoramic radiography taken, age (year and month), sex, presence of EE of PFMs and associated findings (right-left, unilateral-bilateral, upper and lower jaw) and the degree of EE were recorded. EE was graded (I to IV) by using based on the resorption of the PSMs. Data was analyzed using Chi-Square and Binomial tests. The statistical significance level was set at 5%.ResultsEE was diagnosed in 67 cases (1.51%) and in 107 PFMs (0.60%). Of the cases diagnosed with EE, 44.78% (n = 30) were female and 55.22% (n = 37) were male and the sex distribution among EE cases did not deviate from the expected 1:1 ratio (p = 0.464). The mean age of the patients diagnosed with EE was 6.68 ± 0.76 years. Among 107 PFMs diagnosed with EE, 96 (89.72%) were in the upper jaw and 11 (10.28%) in the lower jaw. This distribution deviated markedly from an expected 1:1 ratio (p < 0.001). The difference in the bilateral/unilateral distribution between the upper and lower jaws was not statistically significant (p = 0.494). Also, no statistically significant difference was observed between PFM type and EE grades (p = 0.318).ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this study, the results showed EE occurred most commonly in the upper jaw and no statistically significant associations were observed between EE and sex or side (right and left). These findings emphasize the importance of early radiographic evaluation to guide timely interceptive treatment in pediatric patients. Also, further studies that more accurately predict risk indicators for PFMs affected by EE may enable more reliable assessment of cases in this regard.Keywords: Ectopic eruption, Dental anomaly, Permanent first molars, Prevalence

Background

Tooth eruption is a complex process involving the migration of the formed tooth from its developmental position within the bone to its functional position on the dental arch. The process may be subject to disruption for a variety of reasons, including genetic, cellular, molecular, or tissue-related factors. In the absence of harmonisation, the forces acting on the developing tooth structure may hinder the achievement of proper occlusion. A variety of issues may arise during the phase of tooth transition. One such example is ectopic eruption (EE) [1–3].

EE is defined as the eruption of a tooth in an abnormal position [4–6]. The abnormal eruption path is known to cause EE of permanent first molars (PFMs), which may result in the tooth becoming impacted under the distal undercut of the primary second molar (PSM) [5, 6]. The prevalence of EE of PFMs varies between 0.75% and 6% across different populations [7, 8]. It is thought that multifactorial factors, including local and genetic factors, play a role in the aetiology of EE. Extensive studies have identified the following aetiological factors: posterior positioning or a small upper jaw in relation to the skull base, inadequate anterior movement of primary teeth, an abnormal eruption path for permanent molars, differences in tooth and bone size, the growth of the tuber, delayed development and calcification of PFMs, crowding, malocclusion, small arches, familial predisposition, cleft lip and palate, primary teeth and large molars, are among these aetiological factors [5, 9, 10]. EE of the PFMs has been classified in various ways in the literature, based on factors such as the spontaneous correction of its position, its effect on PSMs and its severity [11].

Accurate diagnosis and timely management of EE are essential to safeguard the health of both the permanent molars and PSMs. In the majority of cases, diagnosis is made using radiographic imaging, typically in the form of panoramic or periapical views [5, 6, 12]. The selection of treatment is contingent upon the severity of the condition in question, as well as the probability of spontaneous resolution. In cases of a mild nature, close monitoring may suffice. However, in more advanced cases, the use of elastic separators, orthodontic appliances, or surgical intervention is often necessary [5, 6, 12]. In the absence of intervention, the consequences of EE can include root resorption, premature tooth loss and a reduction in arch length. These factors have the potential to contribute to malocclusion [13]. Consequently, the promotion of normal occlusal development and the minimisation of the necessity for future orthodontic treatment are contingent upon the early detection and implementation of appropriate interventions.

As EE constitutes a substantial dental anomaly, the execution of population-based prevalence studies is imperative to facilitate the prevention of the such outcomes mentioned above. However, there is a paucity of comprehensive epidemiological data on EE, especially with regard to variations across different age groups and between sexes [5, 6, 11–13]. Furthermore, although classification systems based on severity have been proposed, their practical relevance and influence on clinical decision-making are still being debated [12, 13]. In this context, the assessment of the frequency of EE in younger age groups provides valuable regional data that contributes to the existing body of research and supports the development of more effective preventive orthodontic strategies.

The objective of the current study was to determine the prevalence of EE of PFMs on the panoramic radiographs of children aged between 5 and 8 years. The null hypothesis (H0) was that there would be no statistically significant difference between the sexes of participants with EE and no statistical difference between PFM type and grades of EE.

Methods

Study design and followed checklists

The current work has an observational, cross-sectional and retrospective study design. In this retrospective cross-sectional study, the prevalence of EE was determined in panoramic radiographs previously taken from patients for various indications, and related factors were investigated. This observational study also followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist, which was developed to improve the quality of observational cross-sectional studies, as discussed in previous research [14].

Ethical approval and Informed Consent Forms

Ethical committee approval for the research protocol was obtained from the Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry Clinical Research Ethics Committee on 22.04.2024, with the decision number of 06/04. As the research design was a retrospective observational study, patients (or their parents/caregivers) who had previously given verbal and written informed consent for a panoramic radiographic examination were included in the study procedures. Also, the current study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association (WMA) Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical principles for medical research involving human participants or identifiable human material or data (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/) [15].

Sample size analysis

Assuming that the prevalence of EE was found to be 0.75% − 6% in previous literature [8], a minimum panoramic radiograph sample of 4037 individuals was found to be sufficient for the study procedures when the sample calculation was performed using the One Sample Proportion test with a power of 0.80 at a significance level of 0.05.

Study procedure

The study included panoramic radiographs with sufficient image quality for diagnostic evaluation. The inclusion of panoramic radiographs in the study procedures was carried out within the scope of the eligibility criteria listed below. To ensure standardization in terms of image quality, radiographic images obtained using the same radiography device and mode were included in the study procedures. In this context, panoramic radiographs were included which had previously taken with a digital panoramic x-ray device, Planmeca ProOne® (2016-02), using 62 kVp, 4 mA in pediatric mode. Additionally, digital panoramic radiographs and related patient information were obtained from the patient automation system of the Ankara University, Faculty of Dentistry Hospital (Synapse 7.2.000), filtered for each year and age group respectively.

Panoramic radiograph assessment and Inter-Examiner reliability

Two independent investigators recorded the following for each patient: date of birth; date of panoramic radiography taken; age in years and months; sex; presence or absence of EE; if present, the PFM (right or left; unilateral or bilateral; upper or lower jaw); and the degree of EE. The assessment of the first 550 panoramic radiograph were subjected to inter-examiner reliability (Kappa) test for both investigators and inter-examiner reliability was found to be approximately 0.91, demonstrating good reliability. Based on the good inter-examiner reliability, the remaining panoramic radiographs were assessed by only one investigator (either Ö.B.S. or N.S.Ö).

Grading the level of ectopic eruption

EE was classified according to resorption in PSMs, as described by Barberia-Leache et al. [13].

According to this classification:

Grade I (mild): Limited resorption of cementum or minimal penetration into dentin.

Grade II (moderate): Resorption of dentin without pulp exposure.

Grade III (severe): Distal root resorption with pulp exposure.

Grade IV (very severe): Resorption affecting the mesial root of PSMs.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were analyzed using SPSS v22 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as counts (n) and percentages (%), continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. In addition to frequency and percentage distributions of the data, the relationship between variables was examined by Chi-Square and Binomial tests. All binomial tests in the current study were performed as exact two-sided tests instead of using normal approximation, in order to provide more accurate and conservative p values. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Discussion

This study evaluated the prevalence of EE of PFMs in children aged 5–8 years. The results showed that the prevalence of EE (case based) was 1.51%, with the highest prevalence in the upper jaw. Also, according to obtained data, the null hypothesis was accepted.

EE of the PFMs refers to an abnormal deviation from its natural eruption pathway [11]. This condition is classified as a multifactorial pathological disorder that may be influenced by genetic and familial predispositions. Additional aetiological factors include aberrant angulation of PFMS, upper jaw hypoplasia, upper jaw retrognathism, macrodontia and anomalies in the calcification of PFMs [11, 16, 17]. EE is frequently overlooked during clinical and radiographic examinations. Consequently, paediatric dentists must be vigilant and possess the expertise to intervene early to prevent PFMs becoming irreversibly impacted. Failure to address this condition may result in the premature loss of PSMs, which can lead to further orthodontic complications such as loss of occlusal function, aesthetic impairments, pain, infection, resorption of adjacent tissues, crowding and space deficiencies [5, 11, 17, 18]. Investigating the prevalence of EE in PFMs on a community basis is therefore crucial for optimising individual treatment modalities. In line with this, the aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence of EE in our institution using demographic indicators.

A review of the extant literature revealed that the population included in EE studies was of different age ranges, as was summarised below. In this context, although the age ranges are almost similar, it can be posited that there are minor methodological differences in previous studies. In the current study, the intention was to include children within the 5–8 age range within the methodology. This age range corresponds to the early mixed dentition period, when PFMs typically begin to erupt in the oral cavity. The eruption of PFMs represents a critical juncture in the providing of acceptable occlusion. Dental anomalies, such as EE, during this phase can result in substantial complications, including the loss of space within the dental arch, the occurrence of root resorption of PSMs and the manifestation of varying degrees of malocclusion [1–6]. Consequently, the present study incorporated panoramic radiographic images of pediatric patients within the specified age range (5–8 years). In this particular context, it was considered that restricting the sample to this developmental period ensured homogeneity within the study population and strengthened the clinical validity of the obtained findings. In terms of the prevalence of EE of PFMs, Anoush et al. [19] found a prevalence rate of 2.1% in children aged 5–8 years; Shojaeipour et al. [20] found a rate of 2.8% in children aged 6–9 years and Salbach et al. [21] found a rate of 1.3% in children aged 5–9 years. Another study, conducted on a Turkish population of children aged 5–11 years, investigated the prevalence of EE of PFMs [8]. Güven [8] found EE in 2.65% of children, while Çalışkan et al. [22] found it in 0.83% of children aged 6–10 years and Kurkutan et al. [5] in 2% of children aged 5–12 years. The prevalence of EE of PFMs has been reported to range from 0.75 to 6% [8, 23]. The present study’s prevalence results are similar to those reported in the literature, with an EE prevalence of 1.51% (case-based) and 0.60% (tooth-based). By focusing on the 5–8 age range, the study aimed to highlight the importance of early diagnosis and prompt treatment. Additionally, this study found the highest prevalence of EE in children aged 6.68 years. Differences in EE rates may be due to various factors, including age group, number of patients, ethnicity, genetic factors, and caries status of the study population. Furthermore, this study aligns with other studies in the literature that found no sex difference in the presence of EEs [7, 13, 24].

The present study found that the prevalence of EE of PFMs in the upper jaw was significantly higher than in the lower jaw. This finding is consistent with those of studies by Güven et al. [8], Anoush et al. [19], Salbach et al. [21], Çalışkan et al. [22] and Mooney et al. [25]. However, it contradicts the findings of Chintakanon and Boonpinon [7], who showed that EEs were similar in the upper jaw and the lower jaw. Additionally, in a study conducted on permanent second molar, Bondemark et al. [24] found that the prevalence of EE was higher in the lower jaw than in the upper jaw, which contradicts the results of this study. We hypothesise that the higher prevalence of EE in the upper jaw is due to its posterior position relative to the cranial base and insufficient bone growth in the tuberosity region, which is consistent with previous literature. Additionally, in this study, no statistically significant differences were found between sexes in terms of EE, both on a case and tooth basis analyses. In this regard, it was thought that sex was not a decisive indicator or risk factor in terms of EE, but as authors, we recommend that this finding needs to be confirmed by further clinical studies.

Salbach et al. [21], Bondemark et al. [24] and Mooney et al. [25] found no difference between the right and left sides in their studies on EE of PFMs, and the results were similar. The present study corroborates the findings of previous studies. However, contrary to most studies in the literature, including the present study, Barberia-Leache et al. [13] reported that EE on the right side was more frequent and severe than on the left. These differing results may be due to incidental factors or differences in the parameters of the study design.

EE was classified according to resorption in PSMs in the current study. Accordingly, the clinical implications of the grading system (I to IV) should be emphasised. In mild cases (Grade I), the phenomenon is typically limited to cementum or superficial dentin resorption. These cases have been known to resolve spontaneously, provided that careful observation is undertaken. Conversely, moderate to severe cases (Grade II and III, respectively) entail dentin resorption and, in some cases (Grade III), exposure of the pulp. These cases generally require timely intervention to prevent premature tooth loss or infection. In cases of extreme severity, categorised as Grade IV, where resorption extends to the mesial root of the PSMs, there is a higher risk of early tooth loss, space loss on the dental arch, and subsequent malocclusion [13]. This underscores the necessity for prompt intervention. Therefore, the classification of EE based on the severity of resorption provides clinicians with valuable prognostic information for planning appropriate follow-up or treatment strategies. Accordingly, in the current study, no statistical difference was observed between EE grades in terms of PFM type, nor was any difference detected between sides (right and left) and the grades (I to IV). This result showed that the type of PFMs and the side on which the PFM is located did not significantly affect root resorption in PSMs. However, it is suggested that further cross-sectional studies with larger samples are needed to confirm this results.

The current study found that the rate of bilateral EE in the upper jaw was higher than the rate of unilateral EE. Furthermore, the prevalence of bilateral EE was higher than that of unilateral EE, regardless of the upper jaw or lower jaw. In the previous research assessing unilateral or bilateral occurrence of EE, some studies reported bilateral EE was detected more common than unilateral occurrence [13, 18], while some studies reported the contrary [20, 26, 27]. Additionally, Salbach et al. [21] found that the rate of bilateral EE in the upper jaw was higher than the rate of unilateral EE, consistent with the results of the current study. We hypothesise that the high rate of bilateral EE of PFMs, which is consistent with previous studies, may be due to the general structure of the upper jaw. Additionally, this situation may affect other teeth in the upper jaw or upper jaw development.

Although the present study had large sample regarding evaluating EE, it had some limitations. Initially, panoramic radiographs were used to detect EE. While panoramic radiographs provide valuable information, they also have limitations in terms of poor diagnostic quality and possible superposition. Second, the present study was conducted in a single centre. While a significant population was screened, multicentre studies are needed to increase the number of patients and examine the prevalence of EE and its relationship with dento-skeletal anomalies by including larger populations and racial variations. Despite its limitations, this study is expected to facilitate future research into EE.

Conclusions

This study highlights the importance of radiographic evaluation for detecting EE in children aged 5–8 years. Pediatric dentists should therefore pay particular attention during examinations, as early diagnosis is important for starting treatment at the most appropriate time. Early diagnosis and treatment can be advantageous for patients and parents in terms of saving time and money, and reducing the likelihood of complicated treatment. For this reason, it is extremely important for children to have regular dental check-ups. Future studies may contribute to the establishment of clinical guidelines for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up of prognosis. These findings should be confirmed by further comprehensive clinical observations or trials. Also, further studies that more accurately predict risk indicators for PFMs affected by EE may enable more reliable assessment of cases in this regard. In this context, future studies could investigate the development of AI- and machine learning-based tools, such as decision trees, to aid in the clinical diagnosis or prediction of EE.
