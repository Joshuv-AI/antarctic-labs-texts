Searching Axion-like Dark Matter by Amplifying Weak Magnetic Field with Quantum Zeno effect
Jing Dong, W. T. He, S.-D. Zou, D. L. Zhou, Qing Ai

Abstract
The enhancement of weak signals and the detection of hypothetical particles, facilitated by quantum amplification, are crucial for advancing fundamental physics and its practical applications. Recently, it was experimentally observed that magnetic field can be amplified by using nuclear spins under Markovian noise, [ H. Su, et al ., Phys. Rev. Lett. 133 , 191801 (2024) ]. Here, we theoretically propose amplifying the magnetic-field signal by using nuclear spins by the quantum Zeno effect (QZE). Under identical conditions, we demonstrate that compared to the Markovian case the amplification of the weak magnetic field can be enhanced by a factor about e 1 / 2 e^{1/2} under a Gaussian noise. Moreover, through numerical simulations we determine the optimal experimental parameters for amplification conditions. This work shows that the combination of the QZE and spin amplification effectively enhances the amplification of the weak magnetic field. Our findings may provide valuable guidance for the design of experiments on establishing new constraints of dark matter and exotic interactions in the near future.

II
model

According to Ref.
[
49
]
, there are two cells containing nuclear spins, i.e., the source and the sensor cells. In order to polarize the Rb spins and thus the
129
Xe nuclear spins, a static magnetic field with strength
B_{0}
is applied along
z
-axis. Due to the axion-mediated interaction between polarized neutrons,
the
129
Xe nuclear spins are also subject to a time-dependent transverse magnetic field. As a result,
the
129
Xe nuclear spin in a magnetic field is described by the following Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=-\gamma_{n}\mathbf{B}(t)\cdot{\hat{\mathbf{I}}},

(1)

where
\mathbf{B}(t)=B_{0}\hat{z}+B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)\hat{y}
is the total field experienced by
129
Xe nuclear spins with
\nu
being the Larmor frequency of the nuclear spins in the source cell,
\gamma_{n}=2\pi\times 11.78
Hz/
\mu
T
[
26
]
and
\hat{\mathbf{I}}=(\hat{I}_{x},\hat{I}_{y},\hat{I}_{z})
are the gyromagnetic ratio and the angular momentum operators of
129
Xe, respectively. Thus, the Larmor frequency of
129
Xe is

\displaystyle\nu_{0}=\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{0}}{2\pi},

(2)

where
\gamma_{n}
is the gyromagnetic ratio of
129
Xe. The spin Hamiltonian with the total field reads explicitly

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{n}B_{0}{\hat{I}_{z}}-\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t){\hat{I}_{y}}.

(3)

According to the Heisenberg equation of motion, we can obtain the dynamical equation for the nuclear spin angular momentum as

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{I}_{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{0}\hat{I}_{y}-\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)\hat{I}_{z},

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{I}_{y}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{n}B_{0}\hat{I}_{x},

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{I}_{z}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)\hat{I}_{x}.

(6)

The polarization of
129
Xe atoms can be defined as

\mathbf{P}=\frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{I}}\rangle}{v_{0}},

(7)

where
v_{0}^{-1}
is the atomic number density.
The dynamics of the three polarization components can be described by the Bloch equations as
[
50
]

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{y}-\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{z},

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{y}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{x},

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{z}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{x}.

(10)

Generally speaking, any quantum system inevitably suffers from the interaction with its environment. On account of a Markovian noise, the dynamics of
129
Xe spins can be described with the Bloch equations

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{y}-\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{z}-\frac{1}{T_{2}}P_{x},

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{y}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{x}-\frac{1}{T_{2}}P_{y},

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{z}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{x}-\frac{1}{T_{1}}P_{z},

(13)

where
T_{1}
(
T_{2}
) is the longitudinal (transverse) relaxation time of
129
Xe spin.
We consider the response of polarized
129
Xe spins to a transverse oscillating magnetic field. Considering a Gaussian noise, the dynamics of
129
Xe spins can be described by the Bloch equation as
[
51
]

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{y}-\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{z}-\frac{t}{T_{2}^{2}}P_{x},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{y}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{n}B_{0}P_{x}-\frac{t}{T_{2}^{2}}P_{y},

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{dP_{z}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}\cos(2\pi\nu t)P_{x}-\frac{t}{T_{1}^{2}}P_{z}.

(16)

To analyze the system more conveniently, we transform it to the rotating frame defined by

\hat{U}=e^{i2\pi\nu t{\hat{I}_{z}}}.

(17)

In the rotating frame, the effective Hamiltonian

\hat{\tilde{H}}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{H}\hat{U}-i\hat{U}^{\dagger}\frac{d}{dt}\hat{U}

(18)

can be simplified as

\hat{\tilde{H}}\approx\Delta{\hat{I}_{z}}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{n}B_{\text{ac}}{\hat{I}_{y}},

(19)

where
\Delta=2\pi(\nu-\nu_{0})
is the detuning, and we have dropped the fast-oscillating terms by the rotating-wave approximation
[
51
,
30
]
.
Thus, the Bloch equations of
129
Xe spins in the rotating frame read

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{x}=\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2}\tilde{P}_{z}+\Delta\tilde{P}_{y}-\frac{t}{T_{2}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{x},

(20)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{y}=-\Delta\tilde{P}_{x}-\frac{t}{T_{2}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{y},

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{z}=-\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2}\tilde{P}_{x}-\frac{t}{T_{1}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{z},

(22)

where
\tilde{P}_{\alpha}~(\alpha=x,y,z)
are the polarization of
129
Xe spins in the rotating frame.
According to Eq. (
2
), we can define the effective magnetic field in the rotating frame as
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=(B_{\mathrm{ac}}/2)\hat{y}-(\Delta/\gamma_{n})\hat{z}
.

When the Rb pump light is off, the
effective magnetic-field gradient induced by the Rb polarization is greatly suppressed, resulting in
T_{2}
being close to
T_{1}

[
49
]
. As a result, we assume
T_{2}\approx T_{1}=T
and thus we have

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2}\tilde{P}_{z}+\Delta\tilde{P}_{y}-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{x},

(23)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{y}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\tilde{P}_{x}-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{y},

(24)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2}\tilde{P}_{x}-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{z}.

(25)

III
Linear response

In practical applications, precise measurement of weak magnetic fields is often of great interest, such as those associated with precision medicine, deep-sea exploration and cardiac activity, etc
[
26
]
.
When
B_{\rm{ac}}
is weak enough, we can
ignore the first term in Eq. (
25
). In this case, the time evolution of
\tilde{P}_{z}

is decoupled with
\tilde{P}_{x}
,
\tilde{P}_{y}
and
B_{\rm{ac}}
. And
\tilde{P}_{x}
exhibits a linear dependence on
B_{\rm{ac}}
.
Under the weak-field approximation, Eqs. (
23
)-(
25
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{n}B_{\mathrm{ac}}}{2}\tilde{P}_{z}+\Delta\tilde{P}_{y}-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{x},

(26)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{y}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\tilde{P}_{x}-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{y},

(27)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{P}}_{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{t}{T^{2}}\tilde{P}_{z}.

(28)

When the polarization is initially along
z
-axis, i.e.,
\mathbf{P}(t=0)=(0,0,P_{0})
, the solutions to the above equations can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{2\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}\sin(\Delta t),

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{2\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}[\cos(\Delta t)-1],

(30)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{z}

\displaystyle=P_{0}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}.

(31)

Since the relation between the polarization in the rotating frame and the laboratory frame is

\displaystyle P_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{x}(t)\cos(\omega t)-\tilde{P}_{y}(t)\sin(\omega t),

(32)

\displaystyle P_{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{x}(t)\sin(\omega t)+\tilde{P}_{y}(t)\cos(\omega t),

(33)

\displaystyle P_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{z}(t),

(34)

we can obtain the polarization in the laboratory frame as

\displaystyle P_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{2\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}\left[\sin(\Delta t-\omega t)+\sin(\omega t)\right],

(35)

\displaystyle P_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{2\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}\left[\cos(\Delta t-\omega t)-\cos(\omega t)\right],

(36)

\displaystyle P_{z}

\displaystyle=P_{0}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}.

(37)

Thus, the magnitude of the transverse polarization can be expressed as

P_{\perp}=\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}\sin(\frac{\Delta t}{2}).

(38)

According to
B_{\mathrm{eff}}=8\pi\kappa_{0}M_{0}P_{\perp}/3
, where
\kappa_{0}=540
denotes the Fermi-contact enhancement factor between
129
Xe and
87
Rb
[
52
,
26
]
, the amplitude of the transverse effective field is

B_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\kappa_{0}M_{0}\frac{P_{0}B_{\rm{ac}}\gamma_{n}}{\Delta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}}{\sin(\frac{\Delta t}{2})}.

(39)

And thus the transverse field is amplified by a factor

\Pi\equiv\frac{|\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{eff}}|}{|\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{ac}}|}=\frac{2\lambda M_{n}\gamma_{n}}{\Delta}\sin\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2T^{2}}},

(40)

where
M_{n}=M_{0}P_{0}
is the nuclear magnetization of
129
Xe,
\lambda=4\pi\kappa_{0}/3
.
The optimal time
t_{\mathrm{opt}}
can be obtained by calculating
\partial\Pi/\partial t|_{t=t_{\mathrm{opt}}}=0
, and thus yields
\tan\left(\Delta t_{\mathrm{opt}}/2\right)=\Delta T^{2}/{2t_{\mathrm{opt}}}.

Now we expand
\tan(\Delta t_{\mathrm{opt}}/2)
to the third order of
\Delta t_{\mathrm{opt}}
and obtain the following expression
\left(\Delta t_{\mathrm{opt}}/2\right)+\left(\Delta t_{\mathrm{opt}}/2\right)^{3}/3=\Delta T^{2}/{2t_{\mathrm{opt}}}
.
The time required to reach the maximum transverse polarization is determined by

\displaystyle t_{\rm{opt}}=\sqrt{\frac{6}{\Delta^{2}}\left(-1+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{3}\Delta^{2}T^{2}}\right)}.

(41)

Again by Taylor expansion, we could obtain

\displaystyle t_{\rm{opt}}\simeq T\left(1-\frac{\Delta^{2}T^{2}}{24}\right).

(42)

Based on this finding, it is shown that the optimal time becomes longer as
T
increases and
\Delta
decreases,
which will be discussed in detail in the next section.

When the oscillation frequency of the external magnetic field matches the
129
Xe Larmor frequency, i.e.,
\Delta=0
, the time required to reach the maximum transverse polarization is
t_{\rm{opt}}=T
. It should be noted that the optimal time should be smaller than
T
in the non-resonance case, i.e.,
\Delta\neq 0
. The optimal amplification factors for the Markovian and QZE cases are
\Pi=2\lambda M_{n}\gamma_{n}T\rm{e}^{-1}

[
26
]
and
\Pi=2\lambda M_{n}\gamma_{n}T\rm{e}^{-1/2}
, respectively. Therefore, the QZE could enhance the amplification of the weak magnetic field by a factor
\sqrt{\rm{e}}
.

In this paper, we propose a noble-gas spin evolution in the dark and amplifying the measurement of the weak magnetic field by the QZE. In summary, we exploit the magnetic amplification considering the QZE through effective fields from collisions between alkali-metal atoms and noble-gas atoms, increasing the magnetic magnification by up to about 1.65-fold relative to the Markovian noise. Based on our analysis, we obtain the optimal time required to reach the maximum transverse polarization and thus the optimal response under different combinations of the parameters. Our results indicate that the amplification of measuring the weak fields can be further enhanced by the QZE as compared to the Markovian case.
