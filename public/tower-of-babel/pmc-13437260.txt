Comparison of Social Frailty and Oral Health Literacy Among Older Adults in Urban and Rural Areas: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Misaki Ichinomiya, Yasuhiko Shirayama, Shinji Fujiwara, Mio Kitamura, Shino Suma, Kosuke Kataoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSocial frailty and oral health literacy (OHL) may be influenced by the residential environment; however, evidence regarding regional differences among older adults remains limited. This study aimed to examine differences in social frailty and OHL between older adult study populations recruited from urban and rural areas.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 176 community-dwelling older adults (88 participants in the urban study population and 88 participants in the rural study population) enrolled between November 2024 and November 2025. Social frailty was assessed using a five-item assessment, and OHL was evaluated using a 16-item questionnaire. Oral health status was assessed using the Japanese version of the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT-J), and health-related quality of life (QOL) was measured using the Short Form-8 (SF-8). Ordinal logistic regression and multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the associations of study population (rural vs urban) with social frailty and OHL, respectively, adjusting for demographic and health-related factors.ResultsCompared with the urban study population, the rural study population showed a higher prevalence of social frailty (p = 0.004) and low OHL (p < 0.001). In contrast, mental health–related QOL and oral health status were more favorable in the rural study population (p < 0.001). Multivariable analyses showed that participants in the rural study population had higher levels of social frailty and lower OHL than those in the urban study population.ConclusionParticipants in the rural study population demonstrated favorable mental health–related QOL and oral health status; however, higher levels of social frailty and lower OHL were also observed. Given the cross-sectional design and differences in participant recruitment settings between the two study populations, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of causal relationships. Nevertheless, these findings may inform the development of region-specific, integrated approaches that combine support for social participation with improved access to health-related information.Keywords: social frailty, oral health literacy, regional differences, older adults

Introduction

As the global population continues to age, frailty has become a major public health concern among older adults. Frailty is widely recognized as a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, psychological, and social domains,1,2 and is associated with adverse health outcomes, including disability, falls, and hospitalization.3,4 Although previous research has primarily focused on physical frailty, increasing attention has been directed toward social frailty, which is characterized by declines in social participation, social roles, and interpersonal relationships among community-dwelling older adults.

Social frailty predicts future disability and mortality and may precede the onset of physical frailty.5–8 As an early indicator in the progression toward physical decline and activity limitations, social frailty is considered an important target for preventive interventions in older adults.

In addition to individual health status and physical function, previous studies suggest that social and environmental factors, including opportunities for social participation, access to community resources, and characteristics of the living environment, influence the development and progression of social frailty.9,10 Thus, social frailty may be closely associated with the local environment. In particular, regional differences between urban and rural areas may shape older adults’ daily lifestyles and social experiences, potentially contributing to differences in social frailty.11,12

Oral health is fundamental to overall health and is closely related to nutritional status, physical function, and quality of life (QOL).13–15 In older adults, daily self-management plays an important role in maintaining oral health. Therefore, the ability to obtain, understand, and appropriately use oral health information—that is, oral health literacy (OHL)—is an important factor related to health in this population.16–18 Access to health-related information and learning opportunities may also vary according to residential area, suggesting potential regional differences in OHL between urban and rural populations.19,20

Although social frailty and OHL may both be influenced by the regional environment, few studies have simultaneously examined regional differences in these factors among older adults. Consequently, regional patterns in social frailty and OHL across different residential settings remain poorly understood. This issue may be particularly relevant in rural areas experiencing population decline, where limited access to healthcare resources, fewer opportunities for social participation, and demographic changes may affect health behaviors and social connectedness among older adults. Comparing older adults living in such settings with those residing in urban communities may therefore help identify context-specific factors relevant to healthy aging. Given the limited evidence regarding regional disparities, exploratory analyses are warranted to generate hypotheses and inform future research and locally tailored support strategies.

The aim of this study was to examine regional differences in social frailty and OHL among older adults living in urban and rural areas. We further examined whether differences in social frailty and OHL between the rural and urban study populations remained after adjusting for age, sex, oral health status, and physical and mental quality of life.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study analyzed baseline data obtained from an ongoing cohort study of community-dwelling adults aged 65 years or older. Participants were enrolled between November 2024 and November 2025. Eligible participants were adults aged 65 years or older who were living independently in the community and were able to understand and complete the self-administered questionnaires. Individuals younger than 65 years and those who were unable to understand or complete the questionnaires were excluded.

In the urban area, participants were recruited through community-based activity programs, whereas in the rural area, participants were recruited from among older adults attending outpatient services at a local clinic. Despite the different recruitment settings, all participants were community-dwelling older adults living independently.

Participants completed self-administered questionnaires providing demographic and health-related information, and the completed questionnaires were collected by the research staff on the same day. Oral health assessments using the Japanese version of the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT-J) were conducted by a single dentist according to standardized procedures.

Overview of the Study Regions

This study was conducted in an urban area and a depopulated rural area within the same prefecture in Japan. The two areas were located approximately 50 km apart by road, corresponding to approximately 1.5–2 hours of travel by car. The urban area is the most populous city in the prefecture, with a population of approximately 250,000, of whom approximately 30% are aged 65 years or older. Approximately 200 medical institutions are located in this area. Public transportation services include approximately 110 train services and 1800 bus services per day, and there are approximately 40 supermarkets.

In contrast, the depopulated rural area has a population of approximately 450, of whom approximately 70% are aged 65 years or older. Only one medical facility, a clinic, is available. The area is mountainous and has a low population density. Public transportation is limited to an on-demand bus service operating three days per week, with two services per day, one in the morning and one in the afternoon. There are no supermarkets in the area; residents obtain daily necessities either by traveling to stores in a neighboring city approximately 30 km away or by using mobile retail services that visit the area several times per week.

Population size and age composition were obtained from the 2020 Census Small Area Statistics published by the Statistics Bureau of the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications.21

The clinic in the rural area serves as both the primary site for healthcare delivery and a venue for social interaction among local residents. Although the clinic differs structurally from community activity programs in the urban area, it may also facilitate interpersonal contact and social connectedness within the local community.22,23

Participants’ Demographic Characteristics

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, employment status, regular dental check-ups, and self-examination of the oral cavity using a mirror, were collected using a self-administered questionnaire.

Social Frailty

Social frailty was assessed using the criteria proposed by Makizako et al.5 The following five items were evaluated, with predefined cut-off responses shown in parentheses: “Do you go out less frequently compared with last year?” (yes); “Do you sometimes visit your friends?” (no); “Do you feel you are helpful to friends or family?” (no); “Do you live alone?” (yes); and “Do you talk with someone every day?” (no). Participants were categorized into three groups according to the number of criteria met: robust (0 items), social prefrailty (1 item), and social frailty (≥2 items).

Oral Health Literacy (OHL)

Oral health literacy (OHL) was assessed using the instrument developed by Ueno et al16 which consists of 14 dental-related terms and two statements. The 14 terms are dental plaque, interdental brush, dental floss, dental calculus, scaling, gingivitis, periodontal disease, fluoride mouth rinsing, fluoridated toothpaste, the 8020 Campaign, electric toothbrush, mouthwash, denture plaque, and denture cleanser. Participants were asked to indicate which terms they recognized.

The two statements, “Smoking is related to periodontal disease” and “Periodontal disease can be prevented by self-care”, were answered as either “true” or “false”. Each item was scored as 1 if the term was recognized or the statement was answered correctly, and as 0 otherwise. The total OHL score ranged from 0 to 16 and was calculated as the sum of all item scores. For analytical purposes, participants were categorized into three groups based on their total scores: low (0–5), medium (6–10), and high (11–16). The cut-off values were based on a previous study,16 and the same categorization method was adopted.

Oral Health Assessment Tool, Japanese Version (OHAT-J)

The Japanese version of the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT-J) includes eight items: lips, tongue, gums and oral tissues, saliva, natural teeth, dentures, oral cleanliness, and dental pain. Each item is scored as 0 (healthy), 1 (changes), or 2 (unhealthy), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 16. Higher scores indicate poorer oral health. The original OHAT and its Japanese version have demonstrated reliability and validity.24,25

Short Form-8 Health Survey (SF-8)

The Short Form-8 Health Survey (SF-8) includes eight items assessing physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role emotional, and mental health. Physical and mental health summary scores were calculated as the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS), respectively, using norm-based scoring methods. Higher scores indicated better health-related quality of life.26

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as number (%), unless otherwise specified. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between regions.

Complete-case analysis was performed separately for each analysis. Participants with missing data for variables required in a given analysis were excluded only from that analysis. The number of missing observations and valid denominators are reported in the relevant tables and table footnotes. Because the proportion of missing data was small, no imputation was performed.

Ordinal logistic regression analysis was used to compare levels of social frailty between the rural and urban study populations. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the test of parallel lines. Because this assumption was violated for OHL, multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to compare OHL categories between the rural and urban study populations. The primary models were adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and employment status) and health-related factors (Physical Component Summary [PCS], Mental Component Summary [MCS], and OHAT-J). Covariates were selected a priori based on previous studies and their clinical relevance as potential confounders. To assess the consistency of the findings using models with fewer covariates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using parsimonious models adjusted for age and sex.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The required sample size was estimated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany).27 Assuming the use of the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test, a medium effect size (d = 0.5) based on Cohen’s criteria,28 a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the estimated required sample size was 134. The final sample size of 176 exceeded the requirement for the planned between-group comparison.

The mean age did not differ significantly between the two regions (77.4 ± 5.8 years in the urban area and 79.2 ± 7.9 years in the rural area, p = 0.177). However, the proportion of women was significantly higher in the urban area than in the rural area (81.8% vs 59.1%, p < 0.001). No significant regional differences were observed in smoking status (p = 0.771), alcohol consumption (p = 0.659), or employment status (p = 0.408) (Table 1).

Oral health–related behaviors differed between regions. The frequency of regular dental check-ups was similar (p = 0.097), whereas self-examination of the oral cavity using a mirror differed significantly (p = 0.001), with participants in the rural study population being less likely to perform self-examination (Table 1).

Significant regional differences were observed for social frailty status (p = 0.004). Social frailty was more prevalent in the rural area, whereas robustness was more common in the urban area. The distribution of OHL also differed significantly between the two regions (p < 0.001), with low OHL being more prevalent in the rural area and high OHL being more prevalent in the urban area (Table 1).

SF-8 and OHAT-J Scores by Region

Among the eight SF-8 subscales, no significant regional differences were observed in physical functioning (PF), role physical (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), or the Physical Component Summary (PCS). In contrast, scores for social functioning (SF), role emotional (RE), and mental health (MH) were significantly higher in the rural area than in the urban area (p = 0.007, p = 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). Consistent with these findings, the Mental Component Summary (MCS) score was also significantly higher in the rural area (p < 0.001).

The mean OHAT-J score was significantly lower in the rural area than in the urban area (2.1 ± 1.6 vs 3.2 ± 2.0, p < 0.001), indicating better oral health in the rural area (Table 2).

Comparison of Social Frailty Between Study Populations

Ordinal logistic regression analysis showed that participants in the rural study population had higher odds of social frailty than those in the urban study population (odds ratio [OR] = 3.81, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.85–7.84) (Table 3). Age was positively associated with social frailty (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04–1.15), whereas higher PCS and MCS scores were associated with lower odds of social frailty (PCS: OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96; MCS: OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.99). Sensitivity analysis using an ordinal logistic regression model adjusted for age and sex yielded results broadly consistent with those of the primary analysis. In the sensitivity analysis, participants in the rural study population continued to show higher levels of social frailty than those in the urban study population (OR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.24–4.04; Supplementary Table 1).

Comparison of OHL Between Study Populations

Multinomial logistic regression analysis showed that OHL categories differed between the rural and urban study populations. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the rural study population had higher relative risk ratios for both low and medium OHL than those in the urban study population, with high OHL as the reference category (low vs high: relative risk ratio [RRR] = 4.48, 95% CI: 1.18–16.92; medium vs high: RRR = 3.49, 95% CI: 1.49–8.18) (Table 5). Sensitivity analysis using a multinomial logistic regression model adjusted for age and sex yielded results broadly consistent with those of the primary analysis. In the sensitivity analysis, participants in the rural study population continued to show higher relative risk ratios for both low OHL (RRR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.00–7.68) and medium OHL (RRR = 3.40, 95% CI: 1.64–7.07) than those in the urban study population, with high OHL as the reference category (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study identified significant regional differences in social frailty and oral health literacy (OHL) among older adults. Participants in the rural study population had higher levels of social frailty and lower OHL than those in the urban study population. In contrast, mental health–related quality of life and oral health status were more favorable in the rural study population. These findings indicate that the relationships among the social environment, health literacy, and health outcomes are complex and may vary according to regional characteristics. They also highlight the importance of considering both social and informational factors when developing strategies to maintain and promote health in aging populations, particularly in regions experiencing depopulation. Given the study design and recruitment procedures, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The higher prevalence of social frailty observed among participants in the rural study population may be partly explained by geographical and environmental conditions specific to the study setting. Social frailty is typically assessed using behavioral indicators such as frequency of going out and social interaction.5–7 In rural areas, longer travel distances and limited public transportation may restrict opportunities for mobility and face-to-face interaction.29,30 Therefore, rather than necessarily reflecting weak social ties, meeting the criteria for social frailty in these settings may partly reflect constrained opportunities for mobility and interaction. This interpretation suggests that commonly used indicators of social frailty may be context-dependent. When comparing regions, it is therefore important to consider whether these measures reflect social vulnerability itself or limitations in behavioral opportunities.

Despite the higher prevalence of social frailty in the rural study population, mental health–related quality of life remained more favorable. This finding highlights the limitations of evaluating social connections solely using quantitative indicators such as frequency of interaction. Previous studies have reported that social networks in rural areas may contract because of population decline and the outmigration of younger generations.31,32 Conversely, long-term residency and locally rooted ties may be maintained in these areas.33,34 Community ties grounded in local connections play an important role in the social networks of older adults.35 Even when the frequency of interaction is low, relationship qualities such as trust, mutual support, and a sense of belonging may provide a stable foundation for mental health. Therefore, the assumption that social frailty inevitably progresses in rural areas, particularly those experiencing depopulation, does not fully explain the results of this study. Social frailty and mental health do not necessarily reflect the same dimensions, and their relationship may vary according to the local social context.

These findings suggest that strategies to address social frailty may need to be tailored to local characteristics. Rather than focusing solely on increasing opportunities for social interaction, approaches may include strengthening mobility support and developing small-scale, community-based social hubs within residents’ immediate living environments. Previous studies of older adults in rural areas have identified access to nearby activity centers and transportation support as important factors related to social participation.36 Evidence also suggests that the physical environment of the community and the location of activity centers may influence older adults’ social participation.37 Accordingly, approaches to social frailty may benefit from addressing not only individual-level factors but also the surrounding physical and social environment.

Lower OHL was observed among participants in the rural study population, whereas oral health status was more favorable. Previous studies have reported associations between OHL and oral health status;16 however, the expected pattern was not observed in the present study. A recent scoping review of older adults found that OHL was generally associated with oral health behaviors and outcomes, although the strength and consistency of these associations varied across studies and settings.38 A recent cross-sectional study also reported that higher OHL was associated with more favorable oral health outcomes, including better oral health-related quality of life and fewer missing teeth among older adults.39 These findings, together with the results of the present study, suggest that the relationship between OHL and oral health may depend on contextual factors, including the local healthcare environment, living conditions, and opportunities for self-management.

One possible explanation is that dental services in the rural study setting were available only once a week on a fixed day, which may have limited opportunities to obtain oral health information and professional support. However, other unmeasured contextual factors may also have contributed to the observed findings. Studies conducted in rural populations have reported lower levels of OHL, partly attributed to reduced access to oral health information and educational resources.40 Conversely, environments in which professional dental care is not consistently accessible may encourage greater reliance on daily oral self-care. Research among older adults in rural areas of the United States has shown that limited access to dental care is associated with the use of various self-care practices, including over-the-counter products and home remedies for managing oral conditions.41 Such behaviors may partly compensate for limited access to professional care, although this possibility could not be directly evaluated in the present study.

Ensuring access to oral health information nevertheless remains essential. Integrated systems combining in-person support with digital tools may be useful in areas with limited healthcare resources. In rural and remote settings, telehealth involving healthcare professionals and community supporters has been reported to improve access to care while maintaining individualized support.42 Systematic reviews have also reported that mHealth and eHealth interventions may improve OHL among older adults.43 Region-specific information-delivery approaches that combine face-to-face support with appropriate digital technologies may therefore help maintain oral health and improve OHL, although their effectiveness should be evaluated in future intervention studies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, because this study was cross-sectional, causal relationships cannot be inferred, and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Second, because the study areas were geographically restricted, caution is required when generalizing the findings to other regions. Third, differences in participant recruitment methods may have introduced selection bias and limited the comparability of the two groups. In particular, participants in the rural study population were recruited from a clinical setting, whereas those in the urban study population were recruited through community-based activity programs. These different recruitment procedures may have resulted in differences in health characteristics, healthcare-seeking behaviors, or health awareness between the groups and should be carefully considered when interpreting the findings. Fourth, the relatively modest sample size and the inclusion of multiple covariates in the multivariable analyses may have reduced the precision of the estimates. In addition, the sex distribution differed substantially between the urban and rural groups, which may have influenced the observed associations despite statistical adjustment. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors such as educational attainment and income were not adjusted for. However, sensitivity analyses using parsimonious models adjusted for age and sex yielded results broadly consistent with those of the primary analyses, providing additional support for the consistency of the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides exploratory insights by comprehensively assessing social frailty, OHL, health-related quality of life, and oral health status in relation to regional characteristics. Future longitudinal research should incorporate regional factors, such as social capital and access to information, to clarify temporal relationships and potential causal pathways. Intervention studies integrating support for social participation with the provision of health information are also warranted to evaluate strategies tailored to regional characteristics.

Conclusion

This study identified significant regional differences in social frailty and OHL among older adults. Higher levels of social frailty and lower OHL were observed among participants in the rural study population, despite relatively favorable mental health-related quality of life and oral health status. These findings suggest that regional characteristics may need to be considered when assessing social frailty and OHL and when developing strategies to support healthy aging among older adults. Given the cross-sectional design, differences in participant recruitment settings between regions, and the geographically restricted study setting, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of causal relationships. Caution is also warranted when generalizing the findings beyond the two Japanese settings examined in this study. The findings may inform the development of region-specific approaches that integrate social and informational support through a combination of in-person and digital strategies.
