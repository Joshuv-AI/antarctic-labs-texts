An Empirical Analysis of LLMs for Countering Misinformation
Adiba Mahbub Proma, Neeley Pate, James Druckman, Gourab Ghoshal

Abstract
While Large Language Models (LLMs) can amplify online misinformation, they also show promise in tackling misinformation. In this paper, we empirically study the capabilities of three LLMs – ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude – in countering political misinformation. We implement a two-step, chain-of-thought prompting approach, where models first identify credible sources for a given claim and then generate persuasive responses. Our findings suggest that models struggle to ground their responses in real news sources, and tend to prefer citing left-leaning sources. We also observe varying degrees of response diversity among models. Our findings highlight concerns about using LLMs for fact-checking through only prompt-engineering, emphasizing the need for more robust guardrails. Our results have implications for both researchers and non-technical users.



1
Introduction

The widespread adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) presents both challenges and opportunities for online information integrity. Misinformation, defined as false or misleading information, has become increasingly prevalent online
Flynn et al. 2017
. While LLMs can potentially increase information pollution online by amplifying misinformation
Huang and Sun 2024
– producing fake content that is harder for humans to identify as false
Cao et al. 2024
– they also offer promising new ways for combating misinformation
Lucas et al. 2023
. For example, researchers have been able to leverage LLMs’ effectiveness in persuasion tasks to correct misinformed beliefs of individuals
Breum et al. 2024
;
Costello et al. 2024
, and have also designed LLM pipelines for fact verification
Pan et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
. Given these dual roles, it is essential to understand the mechanisms and preferences underpinning the LLMs. Understanding how different LLMs perform in finding supporting or debunking information given a claim is crucial for grounding current research in automated fact-checking. Moreover, since most non-technical users use LLMs through prompting
Knoth et al. 2024
, understanding how LLMs handle misinformation when prompted by the user would help us identify potential issues that may arise if people rely on LLMs for fact-checking.

In this paper, we conduct an empirical study to understand the capabilities of LLMs in countering misinformation, especially focusing on political misinformation and considering GPT 4o-mini, Gemini 1.5-flash, and Claude 3.5-Haiku as use-cases. We utilize a two-step chain-of-thought process where we prompt the models first to select credible sources for a given claim and corresponding factuality, and then ask the models to summarize the information in a persuasive manner (shown in Figure
1
). Analysis of the intermediate and final outputs show that the selected models find it challenging to draw from real, credible information from sources, and tend to prefer left-leaning sources. However, models differ in response diversity, shedding light on the limitations of current LLMs in combating misinformation through source attribution. Therefore, our main contributions are:

•

We design a two-step, prompt-engineered experimental setup to simulate real-world usage of LLMs for countering misinformation.

•

Our results show that models struggle to ground generated information in real sources.

•

We find that models tend to prefer left-leaning sources or deviate from sources provided during prompt engineering.

•

Models perform differently in output response similarity, which has implications in grounding efforts and reaching wide audiences.

2.2
Designing the LLM pipeline

LLM response generation is divided into two stages: source curation and rhetorical styling. In the first stage, the LLMs are asked to prove or disprove the statement provided, based on the corresponding factuality, by drawing from the 10 news sources listed, including headlines. This generates the core facts and sources for the output statement. In the second stage, the LLMs are asked to summarize the facts from the first stage to “effectively persuade an individual”, which provides the final output. The model-specific prompts and parameters are included in the Appendix
B
.

3
Experimental Settings

We analyze the following three areas: source generation, source preference, and the variability in output. We choose the GPT 4o-mini and Gemini 1.5-flash since they have been used for misinformation research
Lucas et al. 2023
;
Huang and Sun 2024
;
Omar et al. 2025
, and Claude 3.5-Haiku as Anthropic has taken measures to curb political misinformation in their models
Anthropic 2024
.

To curate a decent sample size for evaluation, the experiment design utilizes 12 unique statements, covering various political topics of 2024 as identified by Gallup
Brenan 2024
. Each statement is categorized by factuality (examples in Table
1
) and used to generate responses, repeating 160 times per input statement. This results in 1920 data points per model for evaluation (Appendix
C
for example intermediate and final outputs).

3.1
Evaluation of Generated Sources

At first, the LLMs are asked to provide the headline and source for its supporting information in order to verify whether the exact or similar article exists. To compare against, we utilize GNews, an API that returns information about news articles, including the source, the headline, and the date of publication. GNews is then fed the (source, headline) pair generated by the LLM and returns the top 10 results based on that search. Each returned headline is compared to the generated one using cosine similarity, and the best result of the 10 is taken. As there can be varying levels of accuracy, we created the following tiers to categorize our evaluation results, considering [2023, 2024] as the time frame:

•

Tier 1: One of the returned headlines matches the generated headline, falls within time frame.

•

Tier 2: One of the returned headlines matches the generated headline, falls outside time frame.

•

Tier 3: One of the returned headlines is similar to the generated headline but not exact, falls within time frame.

•

Tier 4: One of the returned headlines is similar but not exact to the generated headline, falls outside time frame.

•

Tier 5: All of the returned headlines are not similar to the generated headline.

•

Incomplete Entry: Either the source or the headline is missing, thus a proper search cannot be conducted.

3.2
Evaluation of Source Preference

As LLMs can choose any of the 10 sources, we quantify the sources mentioned in the final output. If none of the 10 sources are found within the final output, the statement is categorized as ‘Other’.

3.3
Measuring Variability in Outputs with regard to Sources

Finally, as we expect the LLMs to choose some variety of the 10 sources we provide, we would also expect instances of outputs from the model to have some diversity. We compare the final outputs of each of the 12 statements using cosine similarity.

4.1

A
). Excluding the incomplete entries because they lack proper citation, our analysis of source-headline pairs shows that for the ChatGPT model, 3990 out of 4096 citations fall into tier 5; Gemini has 322 out of 344, and Claude has 1795 out of 1805 (Figure
2
). It is also important to note the number of incomplete entries, especially in Gemini (1575 out of 1920 generations) and Claude (907 out of 1920 generations). The incomplete entries can arguably be seen as a positive, since the model is at least addressing that it cannot accurately pull from external sources. Our results indicate that most model-generated headlines are hallucinated and not grounded in their cited sources.

4.2
LLMs tend to have Source Selection Bias, Preferring Left Leaning Sources

We find that the distribution of source citations is not uniform across all models (p < 0.001 for all models under Chi-Square Test), implying that the models have some preference in source selection (Figure
3
). Moreover, models’ preference appear to be left-leaning, with most citations from
Washington Post
,
The New York Times
, and
CNN
, and minimal from
Washington Examiner
,
New York Post
, and
Fox News
. This bias is clear in ChatGPT and Claude, but less obvious in Gemini. Finally, the models often deviate from our selected 10 sources. This is most prominent in Gemini (99.4% were labelled ‘Other’), but also prevalent in ChatGPT (24.9%) and Claude (62.5%). Further analysis suggests that removing the outputs with no attributions (“Incomplete Entry" in Figure
2
), 168 from ChatGPT, 335 from Gemini, and 710 outputs from Claude contain an attribution outside of our selected sources, such as Pew Research Center, the Department of Homeland Security, the CDC, and
Reuters
. These findings highlight significant source bias within the models and a tendency to diverge from the original prompt.

4.3
LLM Models Differ in Response Similarity

The average cosine similarity across all statements is 0.617 for ChatGPT, 0.446 for Gemini, and 0.576 for Claude. Table
2
shows the average cosine similarity per statement. Using our set threshold of 0.5 for cosine similarity, ChatGPT has the most similar responses, followed by Claude. Notably, Gemini outputs are on average dissimilar from one another, with all but statement 0 and 4 falling below 0.5. This could potentially stem from the lack of attribution in the source generation phase, which grounds the ChatGPT and Claude models. However, diversity of responses is also statement-dependent (Appendix
A.2
).

5
Discussion

Our results show that LLMs struggle to draw from real, credible sources of information, and tend to prefer left-leaning sources, but vary in response diversity, thus highlighting potential risks of relying on LLMs for fact-checking. This finding is important for technical researchers working in the misinformation domain, but also for non-technical users as prompting is most accessible to them
Knoth et al. 2024
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
.

LLMs not only struggle to draw from external information sources, but when prompted to cite specific sources, it has a tendency to fabricate information and cite said source. Current research suggests that users tend to trust all model responses, including deceptive ones
Sharma et al. 2024
;
DeVerna et al. 2024
, and so, it may negatively impact users unaware of these pitfalls.

Our finding that LLMs tend to cite left-leaning sources more validates existing literature that suggest that LLMs have a left-leaning bias
Santurkar et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2023
;
Motoki et al. 2024
. For example,
Motoki et al. 2024
suggested that in terms of question-answering, the LLM’s responses aligned with left leaning political views more.

The evaluation on consistency of LLM outputs highlight both the importance of grounding outputs while still allowing diversity between response generations. This is critical to study because misinformation can be debunked in several ways, and different arguments may be more convincing for different individuals. For instance, the claim that "Electric and hybrid vehicles make up 30% of cars in the US" can be debunked using data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration tracking EV sales over time
Abboud 2024
, or
The New York Times
highlighting regional differences in EV sales
Popovich 2024
.

Overall, our findings can be used to better design pipelines to counter misinformation, while also highlighting potential limitations of current models. Users should consider these limitations when using LLMs for fact-checking purposes.

Limitations

A wider representation of news sources could be tested.
While we have studied 10 different news sources, there are still many news sources that are not tested. It is possibly, although unlikely, that LLMs might perform differently with those news sources. Further experiments are needed to test this out.

Limited context scope.
Our experiments focused on US-based political statements. Further research could be done with other domain-related misinformation such as health, science, world news and so on. This would help us understand whether our results hold for statements in other domains.

Exploration of external source generation.
Our experiments revolve around evaluating performance using the 10 news sources listed within the prompts. However, the LLMs often bring in other sources as discussed in the results. While the LLM is breaking the constraints of the prompting, it is unclear whether it is ultimately doing so to best support its argument with “real sources". Further expansion of our results would be to identify all external sources brought in (not within our 10 provided) to determine whether those articles exist and if the external sources continue to follow a left-leaning bias.

We only focus on news articles.
In our experiments, we focus on whether the LLMs can cite news articles accurately. However, in the real world, news articles are not the only source of ground truth. LLMs have been shown to perform decently citing academic articles
Byun et al. 2024
. Further research could be done comparing different sources of information.

A wider range of LLMs could be tested.
For this paper, we selected ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude. However, it would be worthwhile to experiment with a wider range of LLMs including Deepseek, Mistral, Llama and so on to increase generalizability of our results. Moreover, multiple variations of the models could also be tested for increased robustness.

B.1
Prompt Templates

Provided below are the prompt templates used for the generations of each model. All prompts and parameters are made to be identical when the mechanism existed within the model. For source generation, we chose 1000 tokens to allow the model to fully expand on facts and sources related to the prompt, but for the rhetorical styling only allowed 150 tokens to ensure a concise response. The temperature was set to 0.3 to to allow for some diversity within the statements but to mitigate potential hallucination of facts. The frequency_penalty and presence_penalty are set to 0 and top_p is set to 1, which are the default settings for ChatGPT (and Claude for top_p) and replicated for the Gemini model. Claude did not appear to have easy accessibility to change frequency_penalty and presence_penalty.

B.2
Environment and Packages

In addition to the parameters mentioned and prompts utilized in the previous section, other information important to model setup is listed here. While the generation and analysis did not require any GPU usage, generating the responses took approximately 5 hours cumulatively across all models, GNews took approximately 6 hours cumulatively across all models, and all other analysis / evaluations required only seconds to complete. Payment occurred for use of all APIs through their respective parent companies. The models were used in line with their respective licenses which include non-commercial use.

To complete cosine similarity analysis, the Python package sklearn was utilized, pulling both the TfidfVectorizer method in order to vectorize the text and the cosine_similarity function to compute the result. The sklearn package version is 1.5.0.

Input

Intermediate Output (Source Generation)
: The statement that “United States population trends have never recovered since COVID in 2020” is misleading. In fact, recent data indicates that the U.S. population has shown signs of recovery and growth since the initial impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau, the population growth rate has rebounded as immigration has increased and birth rates have stabilized. For further details, you can refer to the following sources:
- New York Times: “U.S. Population Growth Rebounds After Pandemic Slowdown” (https://www.nytimes.com/2022/12/22/us/population-growth-census.html)
- U.S. Census Bureau reports on population estimates.

These sources provide evidence that the U.S. population trends are indeed recovering post-COVID, contradicting the original statement.

Final Output (Rhetorical Styling)
: I understand the concern about population trends post-COVID, but the claim that they have never recovered is simply not accurate. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, there was a notable increase of 1.2 million people in the U.S. population from 2021 to 2022, indicating a clear rebound. Additionally, reports from reputable sources like the New York Times confirm that both birth rates and immigration have risen, contributing to this growth.
