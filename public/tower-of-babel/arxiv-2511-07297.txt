On the Leading Order Term of the Lattice Yang-Mills Free Energy
Christian Brennecke

Abstract
In [ 24 ] , the leading order term of the free energy of U(N) lattice Yang-Mills theory in Λ n = { 0 , … , n } d ⊂ ℤ d \Lambda_{n}=\{0,\ldots,n\}^{d}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d} was determined, for every N ≥ 1 N\geq 1 and d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . The formula is explicit apart from a contribution K d K_{d} which corresponds to the limiting free energy of lattice Maxwell theory with boundary conditions induced by the axial gauge. By suitably adjusting the boundary conditions, we provide an equivalent characterization of K d K_{d} that admits its explicit computation.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite subset and let
G
denote a closed subgroup of the group of
N\times N
unitary matrices,
G\subset\text{U(N)}=\{U\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}:U^{*}U=\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\}
, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. We denote by
E_{\Lambda}
the set of oriented edges
e=(x,y)\in E_{\Lambda}
, which are pairs of adjacent vertices
x,y\in\Lambda
such that
x\prec y\,(=x+e_{i}
for some
i\in[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}
) in the lexicographic ordering. The state space of the theory is
G^{E_{\Lambda}}
, the set of maps from
E_{\Lambda}
to
G
, called lattice gauge fields. If
U=(U_{e})_{e\in E_{\Lambda}}\in G^{E_{\Lambda}}
is a field configuration,
e=(x,y)\in E_{\Lambda}
and
e^{\prime}=(y,x)
denotes the negatively oriented version of
e
, we set
U_{e^{\prime}}=U_{e}^{*}=U_{e}^{-1}
. A plaquette
p
is a square that is bounded by four edges in
E_{\Lambda}
. It can be characterized uniquely by the triple
p=(x,j,k)\in\Lambda\times[d]^{2}
, if its vertices are given by
x,x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k},x+e_{k}\in\Lambda
for some
1\leq j<k\leq d
. Here and in the following,
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that in this characterization,
x\in\Lambda_{n}
is the smallest (with regards to the lexicographic ordering) vertex contained in
p
. The set of plaquettes in
\Lambda
is denoted by
P_{\Lambda}
. For a configuration
U\in G^{E_{\Lambda}}
and a plaquette
p=(x,j,k)\in P_{\Lambda}
, we set

\begin{split}U_{p}&=U_{(x,x+e_{j})}U_{(x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}U_{(x+e_{k},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}^{*}U_{(x,x+e_{k})}^{*}\\
&=U_{(x,x+e_{j})}U_{(x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}U_{(x+e_{j}+e_{k},x+e_{k})}U_{(x+e_{k},x)}\in\text{U(N)}.\end{split}

Then, the Wilson action
S_{\Lambda}:G^{E_{\Lambda}}\to[0,\infty)
is defined by

S_{\Lambda}(U)=\sum_{p\in P_{\Lambda}}\text{Re}\big(\text{tr}\,(\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}-U_{p})\big)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\in P_{\Lambda}}\|\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}-U_{p}\|^{2}.

(1)

Here,
\|A\|^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|a_{ij}|^{2}
, for
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, equals the square of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm which is induced by the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\text{tr}\,A^{*}B
. Notice that the second equality in (
1
) uses that
U_{p}\in\text{U(N)}
so that
\langle U_{p},U_{p}\rangle=\langle\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}},\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\rangle
.

Given the above notions, the Euclidean lattice (pure) Yang-Mills measure
\mu_{\Lambda,g}
with coupling strength
g>0
is the measure on
U^{E_{\Lambda}}
defined by

\mu_{\Lambda,g}(dU)=\frac{1}{Z_{\Lambda,g}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{g^{2}}S_{\Lambda}(U)\Big)\sigma_{\Lambda}(dU),

(2)

where
\sigma_{\Lambda}(dU)=\prod_{e\in E_{\Lambda}}dU_{e}
denotes the normalized product Haar measure on
G^{E_{\Lambda}}
. The normalizing constant
Z_{\Lambda,g}
in (
2
) is the partition function of the model and it equals

Z_{\Lambda,g}=\int_{G^{E_{\Lambda}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{g^{2}}S_{\Lambda}(U)\Big)\,\sigma_{\Lambda}(dU).

The corresponding free energy per site
F_{\Lambda,g}
is defined by

F_{\Lambda,g}=\frac{\log Z_{\Lambda,g}}{|\Lambda|}.

From now on, we focus on the lattice
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}=[0,n]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{0,\ldots,n\}^{d}
so that
|\Lambda_{n}|=n^{d}
. For simplicity, we abbreviate
E_{n}=E_{\Lambda_{n}},P_{n}=P_{\Lambda_{n}},\mu_{n,g}=\mu_{\Lambda_{n},g},Z_{\Lambda_{n},g}=Z_{n,g}
and
F_{n,g}=F_{\Lambda_{n},g}
. The main result of
[
24
]
is a derivation of the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty,g\to 0}F_{n,g}
(where
n\to\infty
and
g\to 0
may vary independently). As pointed out in
[
24
]
, the small coupling limit
g\to 0
is relevant for lattice Yang-Mills theory on the scaled lattice
\Lambda_{n,\epsilon}=\{0,\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon n\}^{d}\subset\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. By relabeling the edges, this is equivalent to (
2
) for
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}
with
g^{2}
replaced by
g^{2}_{\epsilon}=g^{2}\epsilon^{4-d}
. Hence, as long as
d<4
, we have that
g_{\epsilon}^{2}\to 0
as the lattice spacing
\epsilon\to 0
tends to zero. A precise understanding of
Z_{n,g}
and
\mu_{n,g}
as
n\to\infty,g\to 0
might therefore be helpful for the construction of continuum Yang-Mills measures, following the strategy proposed in
[
17
]
.

In
[
24
]
, the computation of the leading order term of
F_{n,g}
is obtained by fixing the axial gauge and by approximating
\mu_{n,g}
through an effective Gaussian theory called lattice Maxwell theory (see e.g.
[
32
, Chapter 22]
for more on this). To state this more precisely, we need to introduce some further notation. Consider the maximal tree
T_{n}
in
\Lambda_{n}
with root
(0,\ldots,0)\in\Lambda_{n}
and edges in
E_{n}
. It contains an edge
e=(x,y)\in E_{n}
if and only if its vertices
x,y\in\Lambda_{n}
are of the form

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{j},0,\ldots,0),\;\;\;y=x+e_{j}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{j}+1,0,\ldots,0),

(3)

for some
j\in[d]
. This includes in particular every edge of the form
e=(x,x+e_{d})\in E_{n}
. We denote the set of edges in
T_{n}
by
E_{n}^{0}=E_{T_{n}}\subset E_{n}
and we identify

\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}\simeq\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}:u_{e}=0,\,\forall e\in E_{n}^{0}\big\}\;\text{ for }\;E_{n}^{1}=E_{n}\setminus E_{n}^{0}.

Loosely speaking, elements in
\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}
correspond to a Lie algebra component of the gauge field connections in the axial gauge. For
e=(x,y)\in E_{n}
, we set
u_{e^{\prime}}=-u_{e}
if
e^{\prime}=(y,x)
denotes the negatively oriented version of
e\in E_{n}
and for
p=(x,j,k)\in P_{n}
, we set

\begin{split}u_{p}&=u_{(x,x+e_{j})}+u_{(x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}-u_{(x+e_{k},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}-u_{(x,x+e_{k})}\\
&=u_{(x,x+e_{j})}+u_{(x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k})}+u_{(x+e_{j}+e_{k},x+e_{k})}+u_{(x+e_{k},x)}.\end{split}

(4)

Finally, we define the non-negative symmetric quadratic form
\Sigma_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{E}_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{E}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Sigma_{n}(u,v)=\sum_{p\in P_{n}}u_{p}v_{p}

(5)

and we denote its restriction to
\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}
by
\Sigma_{n}^{0}:\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}\to\mathbb{R}
. For simplicity of notation, we denote the associated symmetric matrices
\Sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E_{n}|\times|E_{n}|},\Sigma_{n}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E_{n}^{1}|\times|E_{n}^{1}|}
again by
\Sigma_{n}
and, respectively,
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
. Since
\Sigma_{n}\geq 0
(and hence
\Sigma_{n}^{0}\geq 0
), it is clear that

-\log\det\Sigma_{n}^{0}=-\sum_{j=1}^{|E_{n}^{1}|}\log\lambda_{j}\big(\Sigma_{n}^{0}\big)=-\text{tr}\log\Sigma_{n}^{0}\in(-\infty,\infty]

is well-defined. Here,
\lambda_{j}(\Sigma_{n}^{0})\geq 0
denotes the
j
-th min-max value of
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
, for
1\leq j\leq|E_{n}^{1}|
, recalled in (
22
) below. Since the restriction from
E_{n}
to
E_{n}^{1}
is based on the choice of the axial gauge, we refer in the sequel to the Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}
with covariance
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
as lattice Maxwell theory in the axial gauge. The main result of
[
24
]
reads as follows.

Theorem 1

(
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
N\geq 1
and set
G=\emph{U(N)}
. Then the limit

K_{d}=\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{-\emph{tr}\log\Sigma_{n}^{0}}{2n^{d}}\in\mathbb{R}

(6)

exists and we have that

F_{n,g}=\frac{|E_{n}^{1}|}{2n^{d}}N^{2}\log g^{2}+(d-1)\log\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}j!}{(2\pi)^{N/2}}+N^{2}K_{d}+o(1),

(7)

where
E_{n}^{1}=(d-1)n^{d}-dn^{d-1}+1
and where
\lim_{n\to\infty,g\to 0}o(1)=0

The proof of Theorem
1
in
[
24
]
combines various tools from probability theory, lattice gauge theory and statistical mechanics. The interpretation of the terms on the right hand side in (
7
) is as follows. Locally, one can change integration over
|E_{n}^{1}|
copies of
G
with regards to Haar measure to integration over
|E_{n}^{1}|
copies of
\mathfrak{g}
against a measure that is absolutely continuous with regards to Lebesgue measure. Its Radon-Nikodym derivative is to taken into account by the second term on the right hand side in (
7
). What remains are, to leading order,
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{g})=N^{2}
independent Gaussian fields which are distributed according to lattice Maxwell theory, rescaled by the factor
-1/g^{2}
. A change of variables in
\mathbb{R}^{|E_{n}^{1}|N^{2}}
then yields the first term on the right hand side and we are left with the free energy
N^{2}K_{d}
of
N^{2}
independent Gaussian fields with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
. In particular, as pointed out in
[
24
, Section 2]
, it is interesting to note that the constant
K_{d}
in (
6
) depends only on the dimension
d\geq 2
, but not on the specific gauge group
G
and it was left open whether it can be determined explicitly. The purpose of this note is to address this question and to provide a formula for
K_{d}
.

Let
E_{n},E_{n}^{0}
and
E_{n}^{1}
be defined as above and let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}
. In the sequel, it is useful to identify
u\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}
with a discrete connection one form. To this end, for simplicity of notation, it turns out convenient to embed
\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{E_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}
by setting
u_{e}=0
whenever
e\in E_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\setminus E_{n}
, a convention we adopt from now on. Notice that this implies in particular that
u_{(x,x+e_{d})}=0
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
, because either
(x,x+e_{d})\in E_{n}^{0}
or
(x,x+e_{d})\not\in E_{n}
, for every
x\in\Lambda_{n}
. As a consequence, the map

\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}\ni u=(u_{e})_{e\in E_{n}}\mapsto w^{(u)}=\big(w_{i}^{(u)}\big)_{i=1}^{d},\;\big(w_{i}^{(u)}\big)(x)=u_{(x,x+e_{i})},\forall x\in\Lambda_{n},i\in[d],

(8)

defines a linear isomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}^{1}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}
and the space

\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}=\Omega_{\Lambda_{n}}^{1,\text{a}}=\Big\{w=(w_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in(\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{n}})^{d}:(w_{i})_{|V_{n,i}^{0}}=0,\,\forall i\in[d]\Big\},

(9)

where

V_{n,i}^{0}=V_{\Lambda_{n},i}^{0}=\big\{x\in\Lambda_{n}:(x,x+e_{i})\in E_{n}^{0} \big\}\cup\big\{x\in\partial\Lambda_{n}:(x,x+e_{i})\not\in E_{n} \big\}.

(10)

Note that
w_{d}=0
for
w\in\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}
. In the sequel, we therefore identify
\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}
with a subspace

\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{n})\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

Here,
w\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{n})
is canonically identified with
w\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
by setting
w_{|\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\Lambda_{n}}=0
and
w_{d}=0\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
. The inner product in (real)
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is

\langle w,w^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{i}(x)w_{i}^{\prime}(x),\;\forall w,w^{\prime}\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

The key observation that enables an explicit computation of
K_{d}
from Theorem
1
is to identify the covariance
\Sigma_{n}
, defined in (
5
), with a suitable lattice differential operator on
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, restricted to
\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}
. Such an identification was used quite recently in the massive setting (i.e. with
\Sigma_{n}
replaced by
\Sigma_{n}+\varepsilon\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{E_{n}}}
for
\varepsilon>0
) in
[
27
, Section 4.3]
to derive the massive Proca field from
\text{SU}(2)
lattice Yang-Mills-Higgs theory. The form of the differential operator is dictated by the perturbative emergence of lattice Maxwell theory from lattice Yang-Mills theory (see e.g.
[
32
, Chapter 22]
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and define
Q_{d}:\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\to\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
by

\big(Q_{d}w\big)_{i}=-\Delta w_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}^{*}w_{j},\;\forall i\in[d].

(11)

Then,
Q_{d}
defines a non-negative symmetric quadratic form in
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
such that

\langle w,Q_{d}w\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\big\|\partial_{i}w_{j}-\partial_{j}w_{i}\big\|^{2},\;\forall w\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

(12)

Moreover, denoting by
\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}}
the orthogonal projection of
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
onto
\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}
, we have

K_{d}=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\emph{tr}\big(\log\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}}Q_{d}\,\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}}\big),

(13)

where
\emph{tr}=\emph{tr}_{|\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}}
is understood as the trace over
\Omega_{n}^{1,\emph{a}}
. As a consequence, we find that

\begin{split}K_{d}&=-\frac{d-1}{2}\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\emph{d}x\,\log\big(1-\cos(2\pi x)\big)\\
&\hskip 11.38092pt-\frac{d-2}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\emph{d}x_{1}\ldots\emph{d}x_{d}\,\log\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-\cos(2\pi x_{k})\big).\end{split}

(14)

In Theorem
2
, the operators
\partial_{i},\partial_{i}^{*}
and
-\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{*}\partial_{i}
refer to the standard lattice derivatives and, respectively, Laplacian. We recall their definitions in Section
2
below.

The computation of
K_{d}
is based on a comparison of the spectrum of
\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}}Q_{d}\,\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}}
with the spectrum of a related operator

\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p,+}}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\,\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p,+}}},

which corresponds to
Q_{d}
with periodic boundary conditions above its zero ground state energy. Loosely speaking, our guiding principle is that
K_{d}
corresponds to a limiting free energy density, which often does not depend on the specific boundary conditions, and that
Q_{d}
can be diagonalized explicitly in the periodic setting. The intuition behind the formula (
14
) is then as follows. The kernel of
Q_{d}
is essentially given by the space of gradients. The axial gauge ensures the positivity of
Q_{d}
and sets the
d
-th field component equal to zero so that, in this gauge,
Q_{d}
acts on gradients like
\partial_{d}^{*}\partial_{d}
. For the
d-2
remaining orthogonal field components, on the other hand,
Q_{d}
acts like the free Laplacian. Splitting the field into a gradient and
d-2
orthogonal components, we arrive at (
14
).

In Section
2
, we provide an elementary and self-contained proof of the reduction to the periodic case and we outline the computation that leads to (
14
). A key ingredient from
[
24
]
that is used in our proof is that the axial gauge ensures the positivity of the lowest eigenvalue of
\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}}Q_{d}\,\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a}}}
. As a side remark, notice that given this input, our derivation of (
14
) provides an alternative existence proof of
K_{d}
(cf.
[
24
, Section 15]
).

2
Free Energy of Lattice Maxwell Theory

In this section, we provide a detailed proof of Theorem
2
. We proceed in several steps. The first lemma identifies
\Sigma_{n}
, defined in (
5
), with a suitable restriction of the operator
Q_{d}
from Theorem
2
. Before stating this precisely, let us recall that

\begin{split}(\partial_{i}\phi)(x)&=\phi(x+e_{i})-\phi(x),\hskip 14.22636pt(\partial_{i}^{*}\phi)(x)=\phi(x-e_{i})-\phi(x)\end{split}

(15)

for all
\phi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
,
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
i\in[d]
. Moreover, we recall that the discrete Laplacian
-\Delta
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined by

-\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{*}\partial_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\partial_{i}^{*}

so that

(-\Delta\phi)(x)=2d\phi(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi(x-e_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi(x+e_{i})

for all
\phi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Notice that
[\partial_{i},\partial_{j}^{*}]=[\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]=0
for all
i,j\in[d]
and that
\langle\phi,(-\Delta)\phi\rangle=\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}\geq 0
as well as
\langle\phi,\partial_{i}\psi\rangle=\langle\partial_{i}^{*}\phi,\psi\rangle
for all
\phi,\psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
.

In the first lemma, we compute the matrix representation
\Sigma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E_{n}|\times|E_{n}|}
of the quadratic form
\Sigma_{n}
, defined in (
5
), explicitly. Up to a few modifications related to the boundary edges in
\Lambda_{n}
, we argue as in the proof of
[
27
, Theorem 4.6]
. Following the latter, let us recall the following terminology. Let
p=(x,j,k)\in P_{n}
be a plaquette, for some
1\leq j<k\leq d
, so that
x\in\Lambda_{n}
is its smallest vertex in lexicographic ordering. Let us abbreviate its edges by
e^{(1)}=(x,x+e_{j}),e^{(2)}=(x+e_{j},x+e_{j}+e_{k}),e^{(3)}=(x+e_{k},x+e_{j}+e_{k}),e^{(4)}=(x,x+e_{k})\in E_{n}
so that
e^{(2)}
is the edge that touches
e^{(1)}
at its right endpoint
x+e_{j}\in\Lambda_{n}
while
e^{(4)}
is the edge that touches
e^{(1)}
at its left endpoint
x\in\Lambda_{n}
. We say that the edges
e^{(1)},e^{(2)},e^{(3)},e^{(4)}
of
p
are neighbors and according to the signs in the plaquette’s weight
u_{p}
defined in (
4
), i.e.

u_{p}=u_{e^{(1)}}+u_{e^{(2)}}-u_{e^{(3)}}-u_{e^{(4)}},

we say that the edges
e^{(j)},e^{(k)}
are positive neighbors if
\{j,k\}=\{1,2\}
or
\{j,k\}=\{3,4\}
. In all other cases, we say that
e^{(j)},e^{(k)}
are negative neighbors. Given an edge
e\in E_{n}
, we denote the set of all its positive neighbors by
N_{+}(e)\subset E_{n}
and the set of all of its negative neighbors by
N_{-}(e)\subset E_{n}
.

In order to compute the matrix representation of
\Sigma_{n}
from (
5
) explicitly, it turns out useful to decompose the set of edges
E_{n}
as follows. First, viewing
\partial\Lambda_{n}=\partial[0,n]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as a subgraph of
\Lambda_{n}
, we split the set of edges

E_{n}=E_{\Lambda_{n}}=E_{\Lambda_{n}}^{\circ}\cup E_{\partial\Lambda_{n}}

disjointly into its boundary edges
E_{\partial\Lambda_{n}}=\{e=(x,y)\in E_{n}:x,y\in\partial\Lambda_{n}\}
and its complement

\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{V}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{V}\big)=\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}}\big)+O(\log n/n).

(43)

Finally, we characterize
K_{d}
in terms of
Q_{d}^{\text{per}}
, which uses (
33
), (
34
) and (
43
). First of all, it is clear that
\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\big)^{\bot}\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})
satisfies

\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\big)^{\bot}\leq Cn^{d-1},

because
\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})
in (
30
) is characterized by
O(n^{d-1})
zero constraints. Since
\text{dim}\ker\big(Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\big)\leq Cn^{d-1}
as well, we also have that

\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\cap\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)^{\bot}\leq Cn^{d-1}.

This follows e.g. from the dimension formula

\begin{split}&\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\cap\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)+\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}+\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)=\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\big)+\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)\end{split}

which, combined with
\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}+\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)\leq\text{dim}\,\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})
, implies that

\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\cap\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)=\text{dim}\,\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})-\text{dim}\,\big(\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\cap\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\big)^{\bot}\geq\text{dim}\,\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d-1}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})-Cn^{d-1}.

We can thus apply (
43
) to
V=\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}\cap\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}\subset\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}
. Since also
V\subset\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}
, we can also apply (
34
). Combined with (
33
), this proves that

\begin{split}K_{d}=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{a,p}}}\big)&=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{V}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{V}\big)\\
&=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}}\big).\end{split}

This implies (
40
) and concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Based on Proposition
6
, we are finally prepared to compute
K_{d}
.

Corollary 7
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Then, we have that

\begin{split}K_{d}&=-\frac{d-1}{2}\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\emph{d}x\,\log\big(1-\cos(2\pi x)\big)\\
&\hskip 11.38092pt-\frac{d-2}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\emph{d}x_{1}\ldots\emph{d}x_{d}\,\log\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-\cos(2\pi x_{k})\big).\end{split}

Proof.

We use the same notation as in the proof of Proposition
6
and denote by
\Pi_{V_{p}}
, for
p\in\Gamma_{n}^{*}
, the orthogonal projection of
\Omega_{n}^{1,\text{p},+}
to
V_{p}
. From the proof of Proposition
6
, we recall in particular that for every
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\Gamma_{n}^{*}
with
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\neq 0
, we have

\lambda_{1}\big(\Pi_{V_{p}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{V_{p}}\big)=2\big(1-\cos(2\pi p_{d})\big)<\lambda_{k}\big(\Pi_{V_{p}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{V_{p}}\big)=\epsilon_{p},\;\forall\,2\leq k\leq d-1.

Combined with (
40
) and the mutual orthogonality of the spaces
V_{p}
, we readily conclude

\begin{split}K_{d}&=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in\Gamma_{n}^{*}:\\
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\text{tr}\log\big(\Pi_{V_{p}}Q_{d}^{\text{per}}\Pi_{V_{p}}\big)+\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n^{d}}O(n^{d-1}\log n)\\
&=-\frac{d-1}{2}\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{n^{d}}\log\big(1-\cos(2\pi p_{d}/n)\big)\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{d-2}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{n^{d}}\log\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-\cos(2\pi p_{k}/n)\big)\\
&=-\frac{d-1}{2}\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\emph{d}x\,\log\big(1-\cos(2\pi x)\big)\\
&\hskip 11.38092pt-\frac{d-2}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\emph{d}x_{1}\ldots\emph{d}x_{d}\,\log\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(1-\cos(2\pi x_{k})\big).\end{split}

(44)

∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The identity (
12
) was verified in Lemma
4
, Proposition
5
proves the characterization (
13
) of
K_{d}
and from Corollary
7
we conclude (
14
).
