Simplicial covering dimension of extremal concept classes
Ari Blondal, Hamed Hatami, Pooya Hatami, Chavdar Lalov, Sivan Tretiak

Abstract
Dimension theory is a branch of topology concerned with defining and analyzing dimensions of geometric and topological spaces in purely topological terms. In this work, we adapt the classical notion of topological dimension (Lebesgue covering) to binary concept classes. The topological space naturally associated with a concept class is its space of realizable distributions. The loss function and the class itself induce a simplicial structure on this space, with respect to which we define a simplicial covering dimension. We prove that for finite concept classes, this simplicial covering dimension exactly characterizes the list replicability number (equivalently, global stability) in PAC learning. This connection allows us to apply tools from classical dimension theory to compute the exact list replicability number of the broad family of extremal concept classes.

Introduction

m=n

Motivated by this connection, we introduce a new notion of dimension for concept classes, inspired by the classical Lebesgue covering dimension and the simplicial structures that naturally arise in learning problems. We focus on binary classification, the task of learning an unknown function that maps the elements of a domain
\mathcal{X}
to one of two possible labels, typically denoted by
\pm 1

binary concept class

\mathcal{C}
, a set of functions from a domain
\mathcal{X}
to the label set
\{\pm 1\}
. Throughout this work, we restrict attention to finite domains
\mathcal{X}

,

,

]

In the finite-domain setting, the simplicial covering dimension turns out (

of the class:

(1)

1.1
Preliminaries and definitions

In this section, we provide precise definitions of the notions discussed in the introduction.

1.1.1
PAC learning, VC dimension, and list replicability

In probably approximately correct (PAC) learning, the learner is given parameters
\delta,\epsilon>0
and receives training data
S
consisting of
n=n(\mathcal{C},\delta,\epsilon)
independent labeled examples drawn from an unknown but fixed distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{X}\times\{\pm 1\}
. We work in the
realizable
setting: there exists some concept
c\in\mathcal{C}
that correctly labels all examples in the support of
\mu
. The learner’s task is to use the training data
S
to output, with probability at least
1-\delta
, a
hypothesis

h:\mathcal{X}\to\{\pm 1\}
whose
population loss

\loss_{\mu}(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{(x,b)\sim\mu}[h(x)\neq b]

is at most
\epsilon
.

Throughout this work, a
learning rule
refers to a (randomized) function
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
that maps any sample

S\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}\times\left\{\pm 1\right\})^{n}

to a hypothesis
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Since our primary focus is sample complexity rather than computational efficiency, we impose no computability constraints on
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
.

VC dimension.

A concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
shatters a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if
\{c|_{S}:~c\in\mathcal{C}\}=\{\pm 1\}^{S}
, where
c|_{S}
denotes the restriction of
c
to
S
. The
Vapnik–Chervonenkis
(VC) dimension of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as

\VCdim(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\sup\{|S|:~S\subseteq\mathcal{X}\text{ is shattered by }\mathcal{C}\}.

The fundamental theorem of PAC learning states that the sample complexity of PAC learning is determined by the VC dimension. More precisely, the optimal sample size for PAC learning a binary class
\mathcal{C}
with accuracy parameter
\epsilon
and confidence parameter
\delta
is
\Theta_{\epsilon,\delta}(\VCdim(\mathcal{C}))
.

List replicability.

List replicability, introduced in
[
CMY23
,
DPVWV24
]
, is an elegant reformulation of global stability
[
CMY23
, Definition 1]
.

Definition 1.1

(List replicability)
.

The
list replicability number
of a concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, denoted
\LR(\mathcal{C})
, is the smallest integer
L
such that the following holds. For every
\epsilon,\delta>0
, there exists a sample size
n(\mathcal{C},\epsilon,\delta)
and a learning rule
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
such that:

For every realizable distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}\times\{\pm 1\}
, there exists a list of hypotheses
h_{1},\dots,h_{L}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bullet\ \loss_{\mu}(h_{i})\leq\epsilon\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,L;

\displaystyle\bullet\ \mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mu^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)\not\in\left\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L}\right\}]\leq\delta\ \text{ where }n=n(\epsilon,\delta).

1.1.2
Extremal classes

A class
\mathcal{C}
over a domain
\mathcal{X}

strongly shatters
a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}

if there exists a labeling
a\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}\setminus S}
such that

\{c|_{S}:~c\in\mathcal{C},\ c|_{\mathcal{X}\setminus S}=a\}=\{\pm 1\}^{S}.

In other words,
\mathcal{C}
realizes all
2^{|S|}
labelings on
S
while fixing the labels outside
S
in some prescribed way. A class
\mathcal{C}
is called
extremal
if every set shattered by
\mathcal{C}
is also strongly shattered.

Many natural concept classes are either extremal or admit natural extremal extensions.
We refer the reader to
[
CCMW22
, Section 3.2]
for a more comprehensive list of known examples; here, we present a few illustrative ones.

Example 1.2

(Sign patterns of convex sets
[
Law83
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set. The class

C(K)\coloneqq\{\sign(v):~v\in K,\ v_{i}\neq 0\ \forall i\leq n\},

\sign(v)\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
denotes the sign pattern of

,

Example 1.3

(Homogeneous half-spaces)
.

Let

be a finite set of points.
Define the concept class of homogeneous half-spaces
\mathcal{H}_{P}

each homogeneous half-space in

whose defining hyperplane avoids

induces a labeling of

.
Explicitly, the label of

1.2

Example 1.4

(Axis-parallel boxes)

Let

be a finite set of points, no two of which share a coordinate. Each axis-parallel box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
induces a labeling of

Example 1.5

(Median classes)

A class

is called

if it is closed under taking the majority of three concepts.
More precisely, for any

, the concept

While not every class is extremal, a well-known open problem asks whether every class can be extended to an extremal one without significantly increasing its VC dimension.

1.1.3
Topological dimension theory

For two families
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of subsets of a set

More generally, a family

.
Every shrinkage of
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}
.

The
order
of a family
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of sets is defined as

\ord(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\max_{x}(|\{i\in I:\ x\in A_{i}\}|-1),

i.e., one less than the maximum number of sets in
\mathcal{A}
that have a nonempty intersection.

Now suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a
finite open cover
of a topological space
X
. The
Lebesgue covering number
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined

Definition 1.7

(Topological dimension)
.

The Lebesgue covering dimension (or simply the topological dimension) of

is defined by

\dim(X)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\LC(\mathcal{A}):~\mathcal{A}\text{ is a finite open cover of }X\right\}.

(3)

Remark 1.8
.

In this paper, we will always work with topological spaces of the form

endowed with the subspace topology inherited from

. Thus, a set

1.1.4
Simplicial covering dimension of binary concept classes

In this section, we describe how the notion of topological dimension can be naturally adapted to the space of realizable distributions associated with a concept class. Our starting point is to represent every realizable distribution as a point in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
.
Every vector
\mu
on the
\ell_{1}
-unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
naturally encodes a distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\{\pm 1\}
: for each
x\in\supp(\mu)
, the distribution assigns mass
|\mu(x)|
to the labeled example
(x,\sign(\mu(x)))
. This correspondence identifies the realizable distributions of a concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
with the subset

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}:~\left|\!\left|\mu\right|\!\right|_{1}=1\text{ and }\exists c\in\mathcal{C}\text{ with }c(x)=\sign(\mu(x))\ \forall x\in\supp(\mu)\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}.

(4)

Note, however, that the topology of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
does not capture the connection between
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
and the binary classification task, since the relevant notion of proximity in learning is not the Euclidean distance but rather the population loss, which only measures discrepancies on points where the given
\pm 1
labels differ. For instance, a given hypothesis
h
incurs zero population loss on all the realizable distributions in

B_{h}\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}:~\left|\!\left|\mu\right|\!\right|_{1}=1\text{ and }\loss_{\mu}(h)=0\},

(5)

even though these distributions may be far apart geometrically in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
. We refer to
B_{h}
as the
zero-loss set
around
h
.

Each
B_{h}
is a
(|\mathcal{X}|-1)
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}=\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}B_{c}
. Hence, unless
\mathcal{C}
is empty,
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, equipped with the Euclidean topology of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, has the same topological dimension as each simplex
B_{c}
, namely
|\mathcal{X}|-1
. However, from the perspective of population loss, it is natural to regard all distributions within a zero-loss set
B_{c}
as being at “distance” zero from one another, since they all induce the same labeling of
\mathcal{X}
. Consequently, each
B_{c}
should be viewed as a
0
-dimensional set in this context.

In other words, a suitable notion of topological dimension for
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
should consider only those covers
\mathcal{A}
in (
3
) that respect the equivalence relation induced by each zero-loss set
B_{h}
. We formalize this idea in the following definition.

Definition 1.11

(Simplicial covering dimension of a binary concept class)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a binary concept class over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{h}\}_{h\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
be the cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
defined above. The
simplicial covering dimension
of
\mathcal{C}
is

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\LC(\mathcal{A}):~\mathcal{A}\text{ is a finite open cover of }\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\text{ satisfying }\mathcal{A}\prec\mathcal{B}\right\}.

1.2
Main contributions

Our first theorem establishes that, for binary concept classes over finite domains, the simplicial covering dimension exactly characterizes the list replicability number.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a concept class over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
. Then

\LR(\mathcal{C})=\SCdim(\mathcal{C})+1.

Combining
A
with the known value of
\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})
from
[
CMY23
]
yields the following.

Theorem 1.15
.

For any finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, we have

\SCdim(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})=|\mathcal{X}|-1\text{ and }\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})=|\mathcal{X}|.

Consequently, every binary class
\mathcal{C}
over a finite domain satisfies

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\geq\VCdim(\mathcal{C})-1\ \text{ and }\ \LR(\mathcal{C})\geq\VCdim(\mathcal{C}).

Proof.

The equality
\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})=|\mathcal{X}|
is due to
[
CMY23
]
. Combined with
A
, we obtain
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})=|\mathcal{X}|-1
. Now, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a class over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, and let
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a finite set. Trivially, we have
\LR(\mathcal{C})\geq\LR(\mathcal{C}|_{S})
, and on the other hand, by considering any finite shattered set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we have

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})+1=\LR(\mathcal{C})\geq\LR(\mathcal{C}|_{S})=\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{S})=|S|.\qed

Remark 1.16
.

In
[
CMY23
]
, the lower bound
\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})\geq|\mathcal{X}|
is proved using the Poincaré-Miranda theorem and the upper bound
\LR(\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}})\leq|\mathcal{X}|
is proved by designing an explicit algorithm. In our framework, the upper bound is immediate, since
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})
is by definition upper bounded by the topological dimension of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, and
\Delta_{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
is the empty cross-polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, which has topological dimension
|\mathcal{X}|-1
.
Moreover, as shown in
Lemma

5.2
, the lower bound can alternatively be recovered from the Lebesgue covering theorem.

The difficulty of computing the list replicability number of a concept class is in part due to the markedly different techniques required for upper and lower bounds. Upper bounds typically use explicit learning algorithms engineered to output from a small list, whereas lower bounds rely on ad hoc topological arguments.

In light of
A
, a natural alternative is to work directly with the simplicial covering dimension of the class. Using this approach, we determine the exact list replicability number for all extremal classes.

Theorem B

(Main theorem)
.

If
\mathcal{E}\neq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
is an extremal class over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, then

\SCdim(\mathcal{E})=\VCdim(\mathcal{E})\ \text{ and }\ \LR(\mathcal{E})=\VCdim(\mathcal{E})+1.

Note that the lower bound
\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\geq\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
in
B
improves by one upon the general lower bound
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\geq\VCdim(\mathcal{C})-1
from
Theorem

1.15
that holds for all finite-domain concept classes.

Proof overview of
B
.

The proof analyzes the simplicial structure of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
and its relation to a cubical complex
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
defined by the strongly shattered sets of
\mathcal{E}
. The key property of extremal classes is that
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
is contractible
[
CCMW22
]
. It was shown in
[
CMW25
, D.5]
that this contractibility implies a deformation retraction of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
onto
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
.

Here, we construct a different retraction tailored to our notion of dimension. This allows us to reduce the problem of computing
\SCdim(\mathcal{E})
to analyzing
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
, where classical tools from dimension theory apply. The simplicial covering dimension of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
is equal to the size of the largest strongly shattered subset of
\mathcal{X}
, and matches the dimension required for the upper bound. To obtain the matching lower bound, we apply Lebesgue’s covering theorem
[
HW41
, Theorem IV 2]
to the maximal solid cube within
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
.

The difference between the cases
\mathcal{E}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{E}\neq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
is essentially explained by the following observation. If
S\subsetneq\mathcal{X}
is strongly shattered by
\mathcal{E}
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
contains a full copy of the solid
|S|
-dimensional cube, as
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
can allocate an arbitrary portion of its probability mass on coordinates outside
S
. In contrast, when
S=\mathcal{X}
, the normalization condition
\left|\!\left|\mu\right|\!\right|_{1}=1
forces the entire mass to lie on
S
, so only the boundary of the
|S|
-dimensional cube is realized.
∎

The extremal class of homogeneous half-spaces, discussed in
Example

1.3
, naturally connects to the notion of
sign-rank
, a key geometric measure of complexity in learning theory. The
sign-rank
of a class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, denoted
\signrank(\mathcal{C})
, is the minimum
d
such that
\mathcal{C}
can be realized as points and homogeneous half-spaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Equivalently, it is the minimum rank of a real
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{X}
matrix whose sign pattern encodes
\mathcal{C}
.

Since for any
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the corresponding class of homogeneous half-spaces
\mathcal{H}_{P}
is extremal and satisfies
\VCdim(\mathcal{H}_{P})\leq d
,
B
and
A
imply the following bounds as a corollary.

Corollary 1.17
.

Every binary concept class
\mathcal{C}
over a finite domain satisfies

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\leq\signrank(\mathcal{C})-1\ \text{ and equivalently }\ \LR(\mathcal{C})\leq\signrank(\mathcal{C}).

The inequality
\LR(\mathcal{C})\leq\signrank(\mathcal{C})
was conjectured by Chase et al.
[
CMY23
]
, who verified it in the case
\signrank(\mathcal{C})=2
. The full conjecture was later resolved in
[
BHH+25
]
using an algorithmic argument based on averaging multiple runs of a large-margin classifier and rounding to an
\epsilon
-net. In contrast,
B
provides a purely topological proof. Furthermore, rather than the geometry of points and half-spaces, it only relies on the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{P}
is extremal.

1.3
Concluding remarks

Arguably, the most intriguing open question regarding simplicial covering dimension (equivalently, list replicability
[
CMY23
, Section 3.4]
) is whether it can be bounded by a function of the VC dimension.

Question 1.18
.

Is there a function
t:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that, for every concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
,

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\leq t(\VCdim(\mathcal{C}))?

By
B
, a positive answer to
1.6
would immediately imply a positive answer to
1.18
.

The dual class
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of
\mathcal{C}
is obtained by swapping the roles of hypotheses
c\in\mathcal{C}
and points
x\in\mathcal{X}
, or equivalently, by transposing the matrix
(c(x))_{c\in\mathcal{C},x\in\mathcal{X}}
. It follows from
[
CCMY24
, Theorem D]
and
A
that

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\VCdim(\mathcal{C}^{*})}{2}\right\rfloor.

For the cube concept class
\mathcal{Q}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, we have

\VCdim(\mathcal{Q})=|\mathcal{X}|\text{ and }\VCdim(\mathcal{Q}^{*})=\lfloor\log_{2}(|\mathcal{X}|)\rfloor.

Therefore, its dual class
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
exhibits an exponentially large gap between the VC dimension and the simplicial covering dimension. Consequently, if the function
t(\cdot)
of
1.18
exists, it must grow at least exponentially.

It is straightforward to verify that every class
\mathcal{C}
satisfies
\VCdim(\mathcal{C}^{*})\leq 2^{\VCdim(\mathcal{C})}
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{E}
is an extremal class, then it was shown in
[
CCH+24
]
that a stronger upper bound
\VCdim(\mathcal{E}^{*})\leq 2\VCdim(\mathcal{E})+1
holds. It would be interesting to investigate whether an analogous relationship exists between
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})
and
\SCdim(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.

Question 1.19
.

What is the relation between
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})
and
\SCdim(\mathcal{C}^{*})
, where
\mathcal{C}^{*}
denotes the dual class? For example, is it true that
\SCdim(\mathcal{C}^{*})\leq 2^{\SCdim(\mathcal{C})}
?

Finally, let us discuss the relationship between simplicial covering dimension and two other learning-theoretic complexity measures: Littlestone dimension and spherical dimension.

Littlestone dimension.

Littlestone dimension is a refinement of VC dimension that determines the optimal number of mistakes in
online learning
. In particular, VC dimension is a lower bound on Littlestone dimension. The celebrated result of
[
ABL+22
, Theorem 23]
, together with the relation between global stability and list replicability, implies that every class with Littlestone dimension
d
satisfies

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\leq 2^{2^{O(d)}}.

On the other hand,
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})
does not provide any upper bound on the Littlestone dimension: for
t>2
, the class
\mathcal{T}_{t}
of threshold functions over the domain
[t-1]
has Littlestone dimension
\lfloor\log_{2}(t)\rfloor
, yet it is an extremal case, and therefore, by
B
satisfies
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})=1
.

Spherical dimension.

The spherical dimension
\SDdim(\mathcal{C})
of a class
\mathcal{C}
, introduced in
[
CMW25
]
, is the largest integer
d
such that there exists a continuous antipodal map
f:\mathbb{S}^{d}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.
In the notation of Matoušek
[
Mat03
]
, this quantity is the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-coindex
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
. Spherical dimension provides a lower bound for simplicial covering dimension:

\SCdim(\mathcal{C})\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\SDdim(\mathcal{C})}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

It is open whether the list replicability number and equivalently the simplicial covering dimension can be bounded above by a function of the spherical dimension. A positive resolution of
1.18
would imply such a bound, since the spherical dimension admits tight lower bounds in terms of both VC and dual VC dimensions
[
CMW25
]
.

Background

Notation.

For a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is finite, all norms on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
define the same topology. In this paper, we use the
\ell_{1}
norm as it is more natural when working with distributions. Given
x\in X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a radius
\epsilon>0
, we denote the
\ell_{1}
-open ball of radius
\epsilon
around
x
by

B_{\ell_{1}}(\epsilon,x)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~\left|\!\left|x-y\right|\!\right|_{1}<\epsilon\}.

Given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
, define

A^{(\epsilon)}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~\left|\!\left|x-y\right|\!\right|_{1}<\epsilon\text{ for some }x\in A\}.

For
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we also define

d_{\ell_{1}}(X,Y)\coloneq\inf_{x\in X,y\in Y}\left|\!\left|x-y\right|\!\right|_{1}

to be the minimal
\ell_{1}
distance between points of
X
and
Y
.

We will denote the abstract simplicial and cubical complexes with capital English letters, such as
K,L,Q
. We use capital Greek letters such as
\Gamma
and
\Delta
to denote geometric simplicial and cubical complexes.

2.1
Simplicial complexes

A simplex
\sigma
is the convex hull of a
finite
affinely independent set
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The elements of
A
are called the vertices of
\sigma
, and the dimension of
\sigma
is defined by
\dim(\sigma)\coloneqq|A|-1
. Following the convention of
[
Mat03
]
, we assume that the empty set is a simplex of dimension
-1
.

The convex hull of any subset of the vertices of
\sigma
is called a face of
\sigma
; note that every face is itself a simplex, and according to our convention, the empty set is a face of every simplex.

Definition 2.1

(Geometric Simplicial Complex)
.

A nonempty collection
\Delta
of simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a (geometric) simplicial complex if the following two conditions hold:

1.

Every face of a simplex
\sigma\in\Delta
also belongs to
\Delta
;

2.

The intersection of any two simplices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Delta
is a face of both
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

The dimension of
\Delta
is the maximum of the dimensions of simplices in
\Delta
.

Every geometric simplicial complex
\Delta
gives rise to a topological space on
\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Delta}\sigma
, called its
polyhedron
or
underlying space
. Giving each simplex its natural topology as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one then topologizes
\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|
by declaring a subset
C\subseteq\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|
to be closed
if and only if
C\cap\sigma
is closed in
\sigma
, for each
\sigma\in\Delta
. In general, this topology does not coincide with the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; however, for
finite
simplicial complexes, the two topologies agree, and we may simply regard
\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The simplicial structure of a geometric simplicial complex is captured by a combinatorial object called an abstract simplicial complex.

Definition 2.2

(Abstract simplicial complex)
.

An
abstract simplicial complex
on a set
V
is a collection
K
of finite subsets of
V
such that
s\in K
and
t\subseteq s
imply
t\in K
.
The elements of
K
are called
simplices
of
K
, and the elements of
V
are called the vertices of
K
. The dimension of
K
is defined by
\dim(K)\coloneqq\max_{s\in K}|s|-1
.

Every geometric simplicial complex
\Delta
determines an abstract simplicial complex
K
with vertex set
V=V(\Delta)
, where each
s\in K
is the set of vertices of a simplex in
\Delta
. In this case, we say that
\Delta
is a
geometric realization
of
K
, and the corresponding topological space
\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|
is called a
polyhedron
of
K
.

Conversely, every
finite
abstract simplicial complex
K
admits a geometric realization in some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For instance, one may map the vertices of
K
to an affinely independent set of
|V|
points in
\mathbb{R}^{|V|-1}
and realize each simplex
s\in K
as the convex hull of the corresponding points. This yields a geometric realization of
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{|V|-1}
.

The following simple proposition implies that all polyhedra of a finite abstract simplicial complex are homeomorphic, so the associated topological space is unique up to homeomorphism.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
Mat03
, Proposition 1.5.4]
)
.

Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be geometric simplicial complexes, and let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be their associated abstract simplicial complexes. Suppose
f\colon V(K)\to V(K^{\prime})
is a simplicial map; that is, it maps every face of
K
to a face of
K^{\prime}
. Then there exists a continuous map
\rho\colon\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|\to\left|\!\left|\Delta^{\prime}\right|\!\right|
such that:

•

if
f
is injective, then
\rho
is injective;

•

if
f
is an isomorphism, then
\rho
is a homeomorphism.

Finally, we discuss the barycentric subdivision of a simplicial complex. Suppose
K
is an abstract simplicial complex and
\Delta
is its geometric realization. Let
\sigma\in\Delta
be a simplex and let
x\in\sigma
be a point. Then the
barycentric coordinates
of
x
in
\sigma
are the unique solution to the equation
\sum_{v\in V(\sigma)}\alpha_{v}v=x
and
\sum_{v\in V(\sigma)}\alpha_{v}=1
. It is not hard to see there is a smallest simplex
\sigma
, called the
support of
x
and denoted
\supp_{\Delta}(x)
, that contains
x
. In that case, all barycentric coordinates satisfy
\alpha_{v}>0
.

Definition 2.4

(Abstract subdivision)
.

The barycentric subdivision of an abstract simplicial complex
K
, denoted
K_{1}
, is defined as the abstract simplicial complex whose vertices are nonempty simplices of
K
, i.e.
V(K_{1})=K\setminus\{\varnothing\}
, and whose simplices are chains of non-empty simplices of
K
ordered by inclusion.

The geometric realization of
K_{1}
is natural.

Definition 2.5

(Geometric subdivision)
.

Let
\Delta
be the geometric realization of
K
. For each nonempty simplex
\sigma\in\Delta
, define
v_{\sigma}
to be its
barycenter
, that is
v_{\sigma}=\tfrac{1}{|\sigma|}\sum_{v\in\sigma}v
. Having defined the coordinates of each vertex in the subdivision, we extend linearly for points in each simplex and obtain a geometric realization of
K_{1}
, denoted
\Delta_{1}
.

Note that
\left|\!\left|\Delta_{1}\right|\!\right|
and
\left|\!\left|\Delta\right|\!\right|
are homeomorphic.

2.2
Cubical complexes

Cubical complexes serve as a combinatorial and geometric framework analogous to simplicial complexes, but built from cubes instead of simplices.

Definition 2.6

(Elementary cubes)
.

An
elementary interval
in
\mathbb{R}
is a closed interval of the form
[a,a]
or
[a,a+1]
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. An
elementary cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a product
\kappa=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{d}
, where each
I_{i}
is an elementary interval. The
dimension
of
\kappa
is the number of factors
I_{i}
of the form
[a,a+1]
.

Definition 2.7

(Geometric cubical complex)
.

A
geometric cubical complex

\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
finite
non-empty collection of elementary cubes such that:

1.

If
\kappa\in\Gamma
and
\tau
is a face of
\kappa
(obtained by replacing one or more factors
[a,a+1]
with
[a,a]
or
[a+1,a+1]
), then
\tau\in\Gamma
;

2.

If
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\Gamma
, then
\kappa_{1}\cap\kappa_{2}
is either empty or is a face of both
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
.

The
polyhedron

\left|\!\left|\Gamma\right|\!\right|
is the union of all cubes in
\Gamma
, equipped with the subspace topology from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Note that sometimes we allow the elementary geometric cubes in a geometric cubical complex to be scaled by some factor. Nevertheless, this does not change any of the topological properties of the cubical complex.

We proceed with the corresponding theory of abstract cubical complexes. The
standard abstract
n
-cube
is represented by the set
\{0,1\}^{n}
. A
face
of the standard abstract
n
-cube is a product
q_{1}\times\cdots\times q_{n}
, where each
q_{i}
is a non-empty subset of
\{0,1\}
.

Definition 2.8

(Abstract cubical complex
[
Far03
]
)
.

An abstract cubical complex on a non-empty vertex set
V
is a collection
Q
of non-empty subsets of
V
, called cubes, satisfying:

1.

Q
is a cover of
V
.

2.

For any
q_{1},q_{2}\in Q,q_{1}\cap q_{2}\in Q
or
q_{1}\cap q_{2}=\varnothing
.

3.

For any
q\in Q
there is a bijection
\phi_{q}:q\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
, for some
n
, satisfying: if
q_{1}\subseteq q
, then
q_{1}\in Q
if and only if
\phi_{q}\left(q_{1}\right)
is a face of
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

The elements of
V
are called the vertices or
0
-cubes of
\mathcal{Q}
. In general, we say
q\in\mathcal{Q}
is an
n
-cube
if
|q|=2^{n}
.

As for simplicial complexes, each geometric cubical complex
\Gamma
has a corresponding abstract cubical complex
Q
: each abstract cube represents the set of vertices of its respective geometric cube. We again say
\Gamma
is a geometric realization of
Q
. To better understand the structure of an abstract cubical complex, it is often useful to study its barycentric subdivision, which is a
simplicial complex
defined as follows.

Definition 2.9

(Subdivision of a cubical complex
[
Far03
]
)
.

The barycentric subdivision of an abstract cubical complex
Q
, denoted
Q_{1}
, is the abstract
simplicial complex
whose vertices are the cubes in
Q
and whose simplices are chains of cubes ordered by inclusion.

2.3
Simplicial complex of a class

To describe the simplicial structure of the set of realizable distributions, we need a few notations.

A
partial concept
on a domain
\mathcal{X}
is a labeling
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{\pm 1,*\}
, where
h(x)=*
means that
h
is
undefined
at
x
. The
support
of
h
is
\supp(h)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{X}:h(x)\neq*\}
. Denote by
h_{*}
the partial concept with empty support.

If
c\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and
h\in\{\pm 1,*\}^{\mathcal{X}}
is a partial concept such that
c
and
h
agree on
\supp(h)
, then
c
is called a
completion
of
h
. More generally, we say that a partial concept
h_{1}

h_{2}
if
\supp(h_{2})\subseteq\supp(h_{1})
and
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
agree on
\supp(h_{2})
. We denote this by
h_{1}\geq h_{2}
.

Given a concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}

is

if there exists a concept

Definition 2.10

(Abstract simplicial complex of a class)

Given a concept class

In particular, note that

where

.

Since there is a bijective correspondence between simplices of

and partial concepts realizable by

directly as simplex of

Notice

of

For each realizable partial concept

For convenience, we will also use

2.4
The cubical complex of a class

Definition 2.12

(Abstract cubical complex of a class)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class. The abstract cubical complex
Q_{\mathcal{C}}
of
C
consists of all subsets
q=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
q
strongly shatters some subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

Dimension theory toolkit

In this section, we prove a few elementary dimension theory facts, which we will invoke repeatedly in the proofs of our main theorems (
B

).

Recall that given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\epsilon>0
, we defined

A^{(\epsilon)}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:~\left|\!\left|x-y\right|\!\right|_{1}<\epsilon\text{ for some }x\in A\}.

As discussed in
Remark

1.8
, when working with topological spaces of the form
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}\prec\{B_{j}\}_{j\in J}
to mean
\{A_{i}\cap X\}_{i\in I}\prec\{B_{j}\cap X\}_{j\in J}
.

The following lemma gives a useful metric tool for constructing refinements.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}\prec\{B_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be covers of
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

•

A_{i}\cap X
are open for all
i\in I
;

•

J
is finite and
B_{j}\cap X
are compact in
X
for all
j\in J
.

There is some
\alpha>0
such that
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}\prec\left\{B_{j}^{(\alpha)}\right\}_{j\in J}
.

Proof.

Since
\{B_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is a refinement of the open cover
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, each
B_{j}
is contained in some open
A_{i}
. If
B_{j}
is empty, then
B_{j}^{(\alpha)}
is also empty and is therefore contained in
A_{i}
for any
\alpha>0
. Likewise, if
A_{i}\supseteq X
, then
B_{j}^{(\alpha)}\cap X\subseteq A_{i}
for any
\alpha>0
.

Otherwise,
X\setminus A_{i}
is a nonempty closed set,
B_{j}\cap X
is a nonempty compact set, and
B_{j}\cap(X\setminus A_{i})=\varnothing
. Together these imply that the distance
\beta_{j}\coloneqq d_{\ell_{1}}(X\setminus A_{i},B_{j}\cap X)
is positive, and so
B_{j}^{(\beta_{j})}\cap X\subseteq A_{i}
. Since there are finitely many
j\in J
, taking
\alpha\coloneqq\min_{j\in J}\beta_{j}
completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma allows us to pass from an open cover to a closed refinement without increasing order.

Lemma 3.2
.

Any open cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of a compact set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a closed refinement
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, and in particular,
\ord(\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I})\leq\ord(\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I})
.

Proof.

Since
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a cover of
X
, for each
x\in X
there exists an
i\in I
such that
x\in U_{i}
. Because
U_{i}
is open and
X
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists some open
V_{x}
such that

x\in V_{x}\subseteq\overline{V_{x}}\subseteq U_{i}.

By the compactness of
X
, the open cover
\{V_{x}\}_{x\in X}
admits a finite subcover
\{V_{x_{j}}\}_{j\in J}
. Hence,
\{\overline{V_{x_{j}}}\}_{j\in J}
is a finite closed cover. Using this cover, we define for each
i\in I
a closed set

F_{i}\coloneq\bigcup\left\{\overline{V_{x_{j}}}:j\in J,\overline{V_{x_{j}}}\subseteq U_{i}\right\}.

By construction, each
\overline{V_{x_{j}}}
is contained in some
F_{i}
, and so
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a closed cover of
X
. Furthermore, for each distinct
F_{i}
and point
x\in X
such that
F_{i}
contains
x
, there is a distinct
U_{i}
such that
x\in F_{i}\subseteq U_{i}
. We conclude that
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
refines
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
without increasing order, as claimed.
∎

Here we present the usual formulation of the Lebesgue covering theorem with closed sets, which can be used to derive the open version stated in
Theorem

1.9
.

Theorem 3.3

(Lebesgue covering theorem
[
HW41
, Theorem IV 2.]
)
.

Suppose a
d
-dimensional cube
[-1,1]^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is covered by a finite family
\mathcal{A}
of closed sets, none of which contains points of opposite faces of the cube.
Then
\ord(\mathcal{A})\geq d
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.9
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a finite family of open sets covering the cube
[-1,1]^{d}
such that no member of
\mathcal{A}
contains points of opposite faces of the cube.
By
Lemma

3.2
, there exists a closed refinement
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{A}
that is also a cover, such that
\ord(\mathcal{F})\leq\ord(\mathcal{A})
.

Since
\mathcal{F}
refines
\mathcal{A}
, it also has no member set containing points of opposite faces of the cube. Applying
Theorem

3.3
to the closed cover
\mathcal{F}
yields that
\ord(\mathcal{F})\geq d
. We conclude that
\ord(\mathcal{A})\geq d
as claimed.
∎

Finally, we record the following useful lemma, which shows that for any closed cover of order
d
of a compact space, there exists a sufficiently small radius
\alpha>0
such that every
\alpha
-neighbourhood around any point meets at most
d+1
sets of the cover.

Lemma 3.4
.

If a finite closed cover
\mathcal{F}
of a compact set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has order
d
, then there is some
\alpha>0
such that, for any
x\in X
, the ball
B_{\ell_{1}}(\alpha,x)\cap X
intersects at most
d+1
sets of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that for every
\alpha>0
there is some
x\in X
such that
B_{\ell_{1}}(\alpha,x)\cap X
intersects at least
d+2
sets of
\mathcal{F}
. Then we may pick some sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
B_{\ell_{1}}(\frac{1}{2^{k}},x_{k})\cap X
intersects at least
d+2
sets of
\mathcal{F}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since
X
is compact,
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
has a subsequence
\{x_{k_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
which converges to some
x^{*}\in X
. Furthermore
\mathcal{F}
is finite, so there are sets
F_{1},\dots,F_{d+2}\in\mathcal{F}
, each of which intersects
B_{\ell_{1}}(\frac{1}{2^{k_{i}}},x_{k_{i}})\cap X
for infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Putting these observations together, we see that for any
j\in[d+2]
, we have

d_{\ell_{1}}(F_{j},x^{*})\leq d_{\ell_{1}}(F_{j},x_{k_{i}})+d_{\ell_{1}}(x_{k_{i}},x^{*}),

where the sum of the right-hand terms can be made arbitrarily small for an appropriate choice of
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, each of the closed sets
F_{1},\dots,F_{d+2}
has
x^{*}
as a limit point, whereby

is contained in

\mathcal{F}
, which contradicts that
\mathcal{F}
has order

.

4
A
: Simplicial covering dimension and replicability

In the introduction, when discussing the problem of learning a class
\mathcal{C}
, we assumed that the learner is allowed to output any hypothesis
h\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
with small loss. However, in some settings, one restricts the learner to output hypotheses from some class
\mathcal{H}\supseteq\mathcal{C}
. For example, in
proper learning
, the output of the learner must be from the original class
\mathcal{C}
.

A more general setting.

Let us denote by
\LR_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})
the list replicability number of
\mathcal{C}
, with the extra requirement that the list in
Definition

1.1
must consist of hypotheses in
\mathcal{H}
. With this notation, the unrestricted case is
\LR(\mathcal{C})=\LR_{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{C})
.
Similarly, define

\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})\coloneq\SCdim_{\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}(\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}),

so that
\SCdim(\mathcal{C})=\SCdim_{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{C})
in the unrestricted setting.

The following theorem, a generalization of
A
, establishes a precise quantitative connection between simplicial covering dimension and list replicability.

Theorem 4.1

(General form of
A
)
.

For any finite domain
\mathcal{X}
, concept class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, we have
\LR_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})=\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})+1
.

Proof.

For convenience, let
d\coloneqq\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})
and let
L\coloneqq\LR_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})
. Recall that by an abuse of notation, we use
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
to denote both the simplicial complex and its polyhedron
\left|\!\left|\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\right|\!\right|
. Since we are working with the topological space
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, recall from
Remark

1.8
that we write
\mathcal{A}\prec\mathcal{B}
to mean
\{A_{i}\cap\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\}_{i\in I}\prec\{B_{j}\cap\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}\}_{j\in J}
.

First, we will show that
d\leq L-1
. This amounts to proving that if
\mathcal{U}
is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, such that

\mathcal{U}\prec\{\sigma\}_{\sigma\in\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}
,
then
\mathcal{U}
has a refinement of order at most
L-1
that is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.
We begin by applying
Lemma

3.1
to produce an
\alpha>0
such that

\mathcal{U}\prec\{\sigma^{(\alpha)}\}_{\sigma\in\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}
.
Fix
0<\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{4}
and
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2(L+1)}
.
Since the list replicability number of
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
\mathcal{H}
is
L
, there exists an
(\epsilon,L)
-list replicable learner
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
for
\mathcal{C}
with outputs in
\mathcal{H}
and with sample complexity
n\coloneqq n(\epsilon,\delta)
.
Using this learning rule, for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
, define an open set

V_{h}\coloneqq\left\{\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}:~\Prob_{S\sim\mu^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)=h]>\frac{1-2\delta}{L}\text{ and }\loss_{\mu}(h)<2\epsilon\right\}.

We will show that the family
\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, and is also indeed a refinement of
\mathcal{U}
with order at most
L-1
.

The sets

are open in
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
by continuity of

and

in

.
To see why
\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
covers
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, first recall that every
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
is a distribution realizable by
\mathcal{C}
, and so the list replicable learner
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
guarantees a list

such that

\Prob_{S\sim\mu^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)\in\{h_{1},\dots,h_{L}\}]\geq 1-\delta

and
\loss_{\mu}(h_{i})\leq\epsilon
for each
i\in[L]
. It follows that there is some
h\in\{h_{1},\dots,h_{L}\}
such that

\Prob_{S\sim\mu^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)=h]\geq\frac{1-\delta}{L}>\frac{1-2\delta}{L}\quad\text{and}\quad\loss_{\mu}(h)\leq\epsilon<2\epsilon,

whereby
\mu\in V_{h}
. Hence,
\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
covers
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

As for the order of
\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
, suppose for contradiction that
h_{1},\dots,h_{L+1}\in\mathcal{H}
are distinct hypotheses such that

\mu\in\bigcap_{i\in[L+1]}V_{h_{i}}

for some
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
. It follows that the probability of outputting each of the distinct hypotheses
h_{1},\dots,h_{L+1}
is greater than
\frac{1-2\delta}{L}
. As these events are disjoint, we deduce that

\Prob_{S\sim\mu^{n}}[\bm{\mathcal{A}}(S)\in\{h_{1},\dots,h_{L+1}\}]>(L+1)\cdot\frac{1-2\delta}{L}>\frac{L+1}{L}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{(L+1)}\right)=1,

which gives the desired contradiction.

It remains to argue that

\mathcal{U}\prec\{\sigma^{(\alpha)}\}_{\sigma\in\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}\prec\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}.

The first refinement is already verified by the choice of
\alpha
, and the second refinement holds by construction since
\loss_{\mu}(h)<2\epsilon<\frac{\alpha}{2}
and

\loss_{\mu}(h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\supp(\mu)\\
h(x)\neq\sign(\mu(x))\end{subarray}}|\mu(x)|=\frac{1}{2}\left|\!\left|\mu-|\mu|h\right|\!\right|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}d_{\ell_{1}}(\mu,\sigma_{h}),

imply
\mu\in\sigma_{h}^{(\alpha)}
.

The second half of the proof is to show that
L\leq d+1
. To this end, fix
\epsilon>0
. If the relative simplicial covering dimension of
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
\mathcal{H}
is
d
, then there exists a refinement
\mathcal{U}
of

\{B_{h}^{(\epsilon/2)}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}

such that
\mathcal{U}
has order no more than
d
and is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
. Now pick any ordering on
\mathcal{H}
and for

let

V_{h}\coloneqq\bigcup\left\{U\in\mathcal{U}:~\text{$h$ is the first hypothesis in $\mathcal{H}$ satisfying $U\subseteq B_{h}^{(\epsilon/2)}$}\right\}.

Note that by definition,
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\{V_{h}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
is a refinement of
\{B_{h}^{(\epsilon/2)}\}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
and has order no more than
d
.
By

3.2
, this open cover
\mathcal{V}
has a closed refinement
\mathcal{F}
(also indexed by
\mathcal{H}
) of order at most
d
. Lastly, applying

3.4
guarantees a

such that

intersects at most
d+1
sets of
\mathcal{F}
for any
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Now we are ready to give an
(\epsilon,d+1
)-list replicable learner for
\mathcal{C}
. For any distribution
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
and sample
S\sim\mu^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\hat{\mu}
denote the empirical estimate of
\mu
using
S
. Since the distributions in
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
are defined on a finite domain, there exists, for any
\delta>0
, some positive integer

such that

\Prob_{S\sim\mu^{n}}\left[\left|\!\left|\mu-\hat{\mu}\right|\!\right|_{1}<\min\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2},\beta\right)\right]\geq 1-\delta

for all
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Given a replicability parameter
\delta>0
, an unknown distribution
\mu
, and a sample
S\sim\mu^{n}
, the learning rule is as follows:

1.

Let
\hat{\mu}
be the empirical estimate of
\mu
using
S
.

2.

Select an arbitrary closed set
F_{h}\in\mathcal{F}
containing
\hat{\mu}
.

3.

Output
h
.

First, let us check accuracy. We have that

\loss_{\mu}(h)\leq\loss_{\hat{\mu}}(h)+\left|\!\left|\mu-\hat{\mu}\right|\!\right|_{1},

where
\loss_{\hat{\mu}}(h)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
because

, and
\left|\!\left|\mu-\hat{\mu}\right|\!\right|_{1}\leq\epsilon/2
with probability at least

.
Second, we check list-replicability. With probability at least

we have
\left|\!\left|\mu-\hat{\mu}\right|\!\right|_{1}<\beta

at most
d+1
closed sets of
\mathcal{F}
that could contain
\hat{\mu}
.
∎

.
The classification of

5.1
Discussion and overview of the proof

The cubical complex
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
of a class
\mathcal{E}
is composed of the union of all its cubes, where a cube of dimension
k
corresponds to a strongly shattered set of size
k
.
By
Theorem

1.10
,
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
has topological dimension equal to that of any maximum cube, which in the case of extremal
\mathcal{E}
is exactly
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
.

Proposition 5.1

(Dimension of the cubical complex)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
, the topological dimension of its cubical complex
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
is equal to the size of the largest strongly shattered set by
\mathcal{C}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{E}
is an extremal class, then

\dim(\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}})=\VCdim(\mathcal{E}).

In view of
Proposition

5.1
, the lower bound of
B
can be achieved by embedding
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
into
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
and applying Lebesgue’s covering theorem (
Theorem

1.9
).
The upper bound follows from a retraction from
\Delta_{\mathcal{E},1}
to the embedding of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E},1}
, which we denoted by
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
in (
7
). In particular, each vertex
h\in\Delta_{\mathcal{E},1}
is mapped to a point in a simplex of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
supported on concepts extending
h
.
The topological dimension of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
is used to construct a suitable open cover of order
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
, which is pulled back to
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
without increasing the order.

When
\mathcal{E}={\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
, however, the embedding given in
Lemma

2.14
fails. In fact,
\Gamma_{{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}}
is the geometric cube of dimension
|\mathcal{X}|
, whereas
\Delta_{{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}}
is the cross-polytope of dimension
|\mathcal{X}|-1
, so no one-to-one embedding exists. We can recover our argument by instead considering the boundary of
\Gamma_{{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}}
, which is homeomorphic to
\Delta_{{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}}
, and has dimension one less than the cube.

\dim(\partial\Gamma_{{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}})=\VCdim({\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}})-1.

This modification leads to the off-by-one case in
B
.

5.2
Lower bound for simplicial covering dimension of extremal classes

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
be an extremal class. Then

\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\geq\begin{cases}\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}\\
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\mathcal{E}
is not the binary cube
{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
, let
Q
be a largest cube within the cubical complex
Q_{\mathcal{E}}
. If
\mathcal{E}={\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
, then instead take
Q
to be an arbitrary
(\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1)
-dimensional cube in
Q_{\mathcal{E}}
. Let

be the geometric realization of
Q
.
By
Proposition

5.1
,
\dim(\Pi)=\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
if
\mathcal{E}\neq{\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\dim(\Pi)=\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1
if
\mathcal{E}={\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
.

Since
f_{\mathcal{E}}
, defined in (
6
), is an injection, any cover
\mathcal{B}
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
induces a cover
g(\mathcal{B})=f^{-1}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{B})\cap\Pi
of
\Pi
with
\ord(\mathcal{B})\geq\ord(g(\mathcal{B}))
.
Furthermore, any refinement of
\mathcal{B}
induces a refinement of
g(\mathcal{B})
.
We will construct a cover
\mathcal{A}
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
which is refined by the zero-loss sets
\{B_{h}\}_{h\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
and such that
g(\mathcal{A})
fulfills the conditions for
Theorem

1.9
.
Thus, any refinement of
\mathcal{A}
must have order at least
d
.

Let
\alpha<1/|\mathcal{X}|
. We define the open cover
\mathcal{A}
of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{B_{h}^{(\alpha)}:~h\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}\}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{A}
is refined by the zero-loss sets
\{B_{h}\}_{h\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
.

Let
y
lie on some face of
\Pi
, so that without loss of generality
y_{x}=1
for some
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and let
h
be any hypothesis in
\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
such that

.
Set

, so that
y^{\prime}_{x}\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}
.
Since any point

has
z_{x}\leq 0
,

is too far from

to lie in

.

\displaystyle\min_{z\in B_{h}}\left|\!\left|y^{\prime}-z\right|\!\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{z\in B_{h}}|y^{\prime}_{x}-z_{x}|\geq|y^{\prime}_{x}|\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}>\alpha.

Thus, if

and

are two points on opposite faces of

This means that

satisfies the conditions of

This lower bound requires
\mathcal{E}
to be extremal, as we require the largest shattered set to form a solid cube in the cubical complex.

5.3
Upper bound for simplicial covering dimension of extremal classes

The upper bound utilizes a “topological equivalence” between the simplicial complex of realizable distributions
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
and the cubical complex of strongly shattered sets
\Gamma_{\mathcal{E}}
. This equivalence is made precise by the following definition. A
retraction
from a topological space
X
to a subspace
A
is a continuous mapping
r\colon X\rightarrow A
such that the restriction of
r
to
A
is the identity, i.e.
r(a)=a
for all
a\in A
.

The following theorem is related to
[
CMW25
, Theorem 9]
, and the proof uses similar ideas, particularly the inductive construction of retractions. However, both the theorem and its proof include new elements and techniques that place the result in the framework of simplicial covering dimension. For instance, in
[
CMW25
]
, the retraction is mainly concerned with preserving antipodality, while in our case, the retraction is designed to produce a suitable open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
, which requires subtle analysis.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\neq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be an extremal class, and let
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Then there is a retraction
f
from
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
to
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}}
and an open cover
\{U_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{E}}
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}}
such that the collection
\{f^{-1}(U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}})\}_{g\in\mathcal{E}}
is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
of order
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
and
f^{-1}(U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}})\subseteq\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon_{0})}
for all
g\in\mathcal{E}
, where
\sigma_{g}
is the maximal simplex in
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
corresponding to the concept
g
.

We defer the proof of
Theorem

5.3
to the following subsections in order to focus immediately on its consequences. Namely, the theorem provides an upper bound on the simplicial covering dimension of
\mathcal{E}
that is needed to complete the proof of our main result.

Recall that
\LR_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})
denotes the list replicability number of
\mathcal{C}
, with outputs in
\mathcal{H}
. Similarly, we have
\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{C})\coloneq\SCdim_{\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}}(\Delta_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Theorem 5.4

(General form of
B
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be an extremal class. Then for every
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, we have

\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{E})=\begin{cases}\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}\\
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Note that
\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\leq\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{E})\leq\SCdim_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E})
for any
\mathcal{H}\supseteq\mathcal{E}
. Hence, to determine
\SCdim_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{E})
, we bound
\SCdim(\mathcal{E})
from below and
\SCdim_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E})
from above. Immediately by
Lemma

5.2
, we have the lower bounds

\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\geq\begin{cases}\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}\\
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

As for the upper bounds, consider first the case where
\mathcal{E}={\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
. Hence,
\SCdim_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E})=\SCdim(\mathcal{E})
. Recall that

\SCdim(\Delta_{\mathcal{E}})=\sup\left\{\SCdim(\mathcal{A}):\mathcal{A}\text{ is a finite open cover of }\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}\right\},

whereas
\SCdim(\mathcal{E})
is defined by taking the supremum over covers
\mathcal{A}
that are refined by particular zero-loss sets.
It follows that

\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\leq\SCdim(\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}).

Since
\mathcal{E}={\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
, the space
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
is the full cross polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, which has topological dimension
|\mathcal{X}|-1
. Furthermore,
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})=|\mathcal{X}|
because the binary cube shatters its entire domain. Together, these imply that

\SCdim(\mathcal{E})\leq\SCdim(\Delta_{\mathcal{E}})=|\mathcal{X}|-1=\VCdim(\mathcal{E})-1.

For the second case, let
\mathcal{E}\neq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any finite open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
such that
\mathcal{A}\prec\{\sigma\in\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}\}
. By
Lemma

3.1
there exists an
\alpha>0
such that
\mathcal{A}\prec\{\sigma^{(\alpha)}\}_{\sigma\in\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}}
. This implies
\mathcal{A}\prec\{\sigma_{g}^{(\alpha)}:~g\in\mathcal{E}\}
where
\{\sigma_{g}:g\in\mathcal{E}\}
are the maximal simplices of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
. Pick
\epsilon_{0}<\alpha
and let
f
and
\{U_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{E}}
be as in
Theorem

5.3
. Then
\{f^{-1}(U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}})\}_{g\in\mathcal{E}}
is an open cover of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
of order
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
that refines
\mathcal{A}
, whereby we conclude that
\SCdim_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E})\leq\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
.
∎

As a consequence, we can exactly classify the proper (
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{E}
) and improper list replicability number

of extremal concept classes:

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be an extremal class. Then for every

, we have

\LR_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{E})=\begin{cases}\VCdim(\mathcal{E})&\text{ if }\mathcal{E}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathcal{X}}\\
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})+1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

The corollary follows directly by combining

5.4
and
Theorem

4.1
.
∎

Lemma

5.12
and the minimality of
w
in
W
.

Observe that
\mathrm{St}_{W}(w)
naturally inherits the geometry of a cone. Indeed, by (
8
) we have

\mathrm{St}_{W}(w)=\{\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}:\alpha\in[0,1],\mu^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w)\}

which is precisely the geometric cone
C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
. Consequently,

\Lambda_{W}=\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}\cup C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w).

The retraction
f_{W}
will be defined as the composition of two intermediate retractions

p_{W}\colon\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}\cup C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)\to\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\cup C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w,1}},w)\ \text{ and }\ q_{W}\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\cup C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)\to\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}.

We begin by defining

p_{W}(\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}f_{W^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mu)&\text{if~}\mu\in\Lambda_{W^{{}^{\prime}}}\\
\alpha w+(1-\alpha)f_{W^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mu^{\prime})&\text{if~}\mu=\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)\end{cases}

Firstly, note that
\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w)=\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}\cap C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
, which shows that
p_{W}
is well-defined. Next, we verify that
p_{W}
has the correct image:

•

If
\mu\in\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}
, then by the induction hypothesis
\imaginary(f_{W^{\prime}})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
.

•

If
\mu=\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
, then
by
Item

1
,
f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime})\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{h[\mu^{\prime}]},1}
, where recall that
h[\mu^{\prime}]
is the minimal partial concept in the support of
\mu^{\prime}
. Since
w
is minimal in
W
, we have
h[\mu^{\prime}]\geq w
. By
Lemma

5.12
this implies
f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime})\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
. Hence, the image of
p_{W}
restricted to
C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
is contained in
C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)
.

Note
p_{W}
is continuous on
\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}
by induction and is continuous on
C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
by the universal property of quotient spaces,
Theorem

5.6
. Since both sets are closed,
p_{W}
is continuous. Finally,
p_{W}
is a retraction as
f_{W^{\prime}}
is the identity map on
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
and, hence, on
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\cap\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w)
.

We proceed with the construction of the retraction

q_{W}\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\cup C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)\rightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}.

By
Proposition

5.13
, there exists a contraction map
F_{w}\colon\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
to some
\mu_{w}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
. We may, if necessary, modify the contraction map and assume, without loss of generality, that

F_{w}(\mu^{\prime},\alpha)=\mu^{\prime}\ \ \forall\mu^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}\text{~and~}\alpha\in[0,1-\epsilon].

(9)

Define

q_{W}(\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mu&\text{if~}\mu\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\\
F_{w}(\mu^{\prime},\alpha)&\text{if~}\mu=\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}\in C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)\end{cases}.

Since
F_{w}(\mu^{\prime},0)=\mu^{\prime}
, the map
q_{W}
is well-defined on
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}\cap C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
. Moreover, as
\imaginary(F_{w})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
, the image of
q_{W}
is contained in
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
as desired. By
Lemma

5.9
,
F_{w}
is continuous on
C(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1},w)
, and therefore,
q_{W}
is also continuous. Finally,
q_{W}
is a retraction as it acts as the identity on
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
.

Since the composition of two retractions is also a retraction, we conclude that
f_{W}\coloneq q_{W}\circ p_{W}
is a retraction from
\Lambda_{W}
to
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1}
.

Item

1
:
By the induction hypothesis, the case
\mu\in\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}
is immediate. It remains to consider the case
\mu\coloneq\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
. Observe

\begin{split}f_{W}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime})&=q_{W}\circ p_{W}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime})\\
&=q_{W}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime}))\\
&=F_{w}(f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime}),\alpha).\end{split}

Hence,
f_{W}(\mu)\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
. Since every
\mu\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
satisfies
h[\mu]\geq w
, the property follows.

Item

2
:
Since
f_{W}
is continuous, it follows immediately that the collection

\{f_{W}^{-1}(U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1})\}_{g\in\mathcal{E}}
is an open cover of
\Lambda_{W}
of order
\VCdim(\mathcal{E})
.

We now show that for every
g\in\mathcal{E}
,
f_{W}^{-1}(U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E},1})\subseteq\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon_{0})}
. Fix
g\in\mathcal{E}
and suppose
f_{W}(\mu)\in U_{g}
. If
\mu\in\Lambda_{W^{\prime}}
, then
f_{W}(\mu)=f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu)\in U_{g}
, and, by the induction hypothesis,
\mu\in\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon_{0})}
.

It remains to prove the same for
\mu\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
. To do this, we use the following claim

Claim 5.14
.

Pick any
g\in\mathcal{E}
. Then
U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}\neq\varnothing
implies that
\sigma_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}\neq\varnothing
. In particular,
g
extends
w
, and
w\in\sigma_{g}
.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive. Assume that
\sigma_{g}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
are disjoint. Then by
Lemma

5.10
we have
d_{\ell_{1}}(\sigma_{g},\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1})\geq\gamma_{0}
. Recall
U_{g}\subseteq\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon)}\subseteq\sigma_{g}^{(\gamma_{0})}
where the second containment follows from our choice of
\epsilon<\gamma_{0}
. Hence,
U_{g}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
are disjoint.

Finally,
\sigma_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}\neq\varnothing
implies that these two simplicial complexes have a common vertex
v
. All vertices in the barycentric subdivision of
\sigma_{g}
are extended by
g
. Hence, by
Lemma

5.12
we have
g\geq v\geq w
, and
w\in\sigma_{g}
.
∎

Now let
\mu\coloneq\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime}\in C(\mathrm{Lk}_{W}(w),w)
and suppose
f_{W}(\mu)\in U_{g}
. By
Item

1
,
f_{W}(\mu)\in U_{g}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{E}_{w},1}
. By
5.14
, we have
w\in\sigma_{g}
. This implies

d_{\ell_{1}}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})\leq(1-\alpha)d_{\ell_{1}}(\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})

where we used the convexity of
\sigma_{g}
. It remains to bound
(1-\alpha)d_{\ell_{1}}(\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})
. We consider two cases:

First case
: Let
\alpha\in[0,1-\epsilon]
. By
Equation

9
we have

f_{W}(\mu)=F_{w}(f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime}),\alpha)=f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime})

Since
f_{W^{\prime}}(\mu^{\prime})\in U_{g}
, by the induction hypothesis, we have that
\mu^{\prime}\in\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon_{0})}
. Hence,

d_{\ell_{1}}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})\leq(1-\alpha)d_{\ell_{1}}(\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})<1\times\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}.

Second case
: Let
\alpha\in(1-\epsilon,1]
. Then

d_{\ell_{1}}(\alpha w+(1-\alpha)\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})\leq(1-\alpha)d_{\ell_{1}}(\mu^{\prime},\sigma_{g})<\epsilon\times 2<\epsilon_{0}.

where we used the fact that the maximum
\ell_{1}
-distance between two points of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
is two, together with our choice of
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}/2
.

Both cases are complete. Hence,
\mu\in\sigma_{g}^{(\epsilon_{0})}
and the induction is complete.
