Virtual reality-based therapeutic intervention to prevent delirium in the intensive care unit: a systematic review of participant experiences, acceptability, and implementation implications
Authors: Surui Liang, Fei Xiong, Jing Jing Su, Eliza Mi Ling Wong, Lorna Kwai Ping Suen, Ladislav Batalik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDelirium is common in intensive care units (ICUs) and is associated with adverse outcomes. Virtual reality (VR) is a non-pharmacological modality that may mitigate contributory factors such as sensory deprivation, anxiety, and immobility. This study aims to synthesise acceptability, feasibility, safety/tolerability, and implementation‑relevant outcomes of VR for ICU delirium prevention.MethodsSystematic review of 11 databases (PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, PsycINFO, AMED, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, Weipu, CBM) from January 2012 to June 2026. Eligible studies evaluated VR-based interventions for delirium prevention in ICUs and reported outcomes on acceptability (e.g., satisfaction, feedback, immersion, comfort), feasibility (e.g. recruitment, completion), and safety/tolerability.ResultsOf 877 articles, seven met the criteria, including 190 patients and 21 healthcare professionals. Most interventions delivered nature-based audiovisual VR content via head-mounted displays, typically calm landscapes accompanied by ambient sounds. Patients consistently reported favourable acceptability, describing VR as enjoyable, immersive, and relaxing, while clinicians highlighted realism, engagement, and compatibility with existing ICU workflows. For feasibility, recruitment rates ranged from 12.6% to 67.6%, with a mean completion rate of 71%, despite substantial heterogeneity in intervention frequency and session duration (5–20 min per session). Safety profiles were favourable, with only mild, transient adverse effects such as dizziness or nausea, none leading to discontinuation.ConclusionVR-based therapeutic interventions in ICU settings may be safe, feasible, and acceptable as adjuncts to multicomponent delirium-prevention strategies, but current estimates are preliminary and subject to substantial uncertainty. Fully powered clinical trials are needed to determine effectiveness and safety. Healthcare professionals might begin with calming, low-exertion, nature-based VR experiences; implement real-time and ongoing monitoring for cybersickness; and embed VR sessions within existing ICU delirium-prevention protocols. Structured staff training and standardised equipment-cleaning procedures will support safe, sustainable adoption while ensuring that technology enhances, rather than replaces, the therapeutic human interaction central to patient-centred ICU care.Clinical trial numberClinical trial numberPROSPERO registration numberCRD420251086766Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15177-8.

Introduction

Delirium is a common, acute neuropsychiatric syndrome marked by fluctuating levels of consciousness, impaired attention, and cognitive dysfunction [1]. It is particularly prevalent in intensive care unit (ICU) settings, where environmental stressors, mechanical ventilation, sedative medications, and disrupted circadian rhythms converge to increase vulnerability [2, 3]. The estimated prevalence of ICU delirium ranges from 20% [4] to 80% [5], particularly affecting older, mechanically ventilated, or severely ill patients [6]. ICU delirium is associated with a host of adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospital stays, long-term cognitive decline, increased morbidity and mortality, and higher healthcare costs [7–9]. Given these consequences, prevention of delirium in the ICU remains a critical and unresolved clinical challenge.

Virtual Reality (VR) refers to the use of digital technology to create immersive, three-dimensional environments that engage multiple senses, most commonly visual and auditory, through devices such as head-mounted displays (HMDs), mobile platforms, or projection systems [10]. Recently, VR has been considered a promising nonpharmacological approach to ICU delirium prevention because it may reduce stress, promote relaxation, counter sensory deprivation, and enhance cognitive engagement, which could improve patients’ psychological health and lower delirium incidence [11, 12]. Such benefits may be partly explained by evidence that exposure to virtual natural settings and awe-inspiring immersive experiences can restore attentional capacity and modulate physiological stress responses through parasympathetic activation and sensory reorientation [13, 14].

Although virtual reality (VR) therapeutic interventions have shown promise in preventing ICU delirium through clinical and pilot studies [15–17], there is currently no systematic synthesis evaluating participation experiences and implementation-related outcomes, particularly acceptability, feasibility, safety, and tolerability. Existing reviews have examined VR for psychological symptoms in fields such as ICU setting [18] oncology [19, 20] and mental health [21, 22], but none have specifically addressed its application for ICU delirium prevention or analysed how intervention components (e.g., session duration, delivery methods, VR content) influence delivery and uptake. Emerging evidence indicates that VR interventions can enhance psychological well-being [23], yet consistent reports highlight challenges in engaging users comfortably and implementing these interventions in diverse healthcare settings [24]. Common issues include discomfort (e.g., dizziness, headaches) [25] and concerns over limited digital literacy [26]. Some studies suggest a dose–response relationship in which higher intervention acceptability is associated with greater health benefits [27], whereas others have not supported this association [28]. Broader social and contextual factors also contribute to inconsistent findings on acceptability, including divergent definitions of intervention use (e.g., completion rate vs. satisfaction) [29] and variability in intervention elements delivered via VR platforms [30]. Understanding acceptability in ICU-focused VR interventions for delirium prevention is critical to uncovering mechanisms, optimizing implementation, and ensuring patient-centred care. Without such evidence, scaling VR for delirium prevention in ICU settings will remain limited.

Objective of the review

This review aims to fill this gap by systematically evaluating the participant experiences and acceptability of VR interventions for ICU delirium prevention, with a primary focus on acceptability. Secondary outcomes include recruitment and completion rates, safety and tolerability.

Methods

This review protocol adhered to the PRISMA-P guidelines and was reported according to PRISMA standards [31, 32]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251086766). Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria based on the PICOS framework:

Participants: Studies involving adult patients (≥ 18 years old) admitted to ICU settings, including medical, surgical, trauma, emergency, and cardiac ICUs, as well as high-dependency units.

Interventions: Studies employing VR therapeutic interventions in ICU settings that included delirium-related outcomes as either primary or secondary outcome measures.

Comparators: Studies with or without control or comparator groups were eligible, as the review focused on acceptability and feasibility rather than comparative effectiveness.

Outcomes: Studies were required to report acceptability as an outcome, assessed either quantitatively (e.g., satisfaction, engagement, immersion, or usability scores) or qualitatively (e.g., patient or healthcare provider experiences and perspectives). Eligible studies also needed to include delirium-related outcomes (either as primary or secondary outcomes), such as delirium incidence, severity, duration, or validated delirium assessment measures. Additional outcomes included feasibility (e.g., recruitment and/or completion rates), safety, and tolerability (e.g., adverse events or physiological changes such as heart rate, respiratory rate, or blood pressure).

Study design: Interventional and mixed-method studies reporting acceptability outcomes of VR interventions for ICU delirium prevention were included. This encompassed randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, feasibility and pilot studies, and qualitative studies with relevant findings.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) targeted paediatric populations (participants under 18 years old); (2) used VR solely for educational purposes or family support without a direct aim to prevent or manage delirium; (3) did not report any acceptability-related outcomes of VR intervention for ICU delirium prevention (either quantitative or qualitative); or (4) were non-original research, including editorials, narrative reviews, study protocols without results, or case reports.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, PsycINFO, AMED, CINAHL Complete, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, Weipu, and CBM, covering publications from January 2012 to June 2026 in English or Chinese. The year 2012 was selected as representing the advent of accessible, high-quality VR technology that enabled more rigorous and widespread research into VR therapeutic interventions [33].

Search terms included variations of the following keywords: “virtual reality,” “delirium,” and “ICU”. As many ICU VR studies do not explicitly use the term “delirium” in their titles, abstracts, or keywords, additional terms related to delirium-relevant outcomes and risk factors (e.g., sleep, pain, anxiety, stress, and cognitive function) were incorporated into the search strategy to improve sensitivity. Studies were subsequently screened for eligibility, and only those reporting delirium-related outcomes as either primary or secondary outcomes were included in the review. Both MeSH terms and free-text terms were incorporated for English and Chinese databases to accommodate the indexing and search functionalities of each. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. Following the updated literature search, all records underwent independent duplicate screening to ensure consistency and completeness of study selection. The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Data extraction

A structured data extraction form was developed, incorporating elements from the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist [34]. Extracted information included the following domains: (1) Study details: First author, publication year, and country or region. (2) Participant characteristics: Sample size, age, gender, and whether the population included subgroups of interest (e.g., older adults, mechanically ventilated patients). (3) VR intervention characteristics: Description following the TIDieR framework, including materials, procedures, delivery method, timing, tailoring, and any modifications. (4) Acceptability and other outcome indicators: Acceptability, recruitment and completion rates (including reported reasons for dropout), and assessment of safety and tolerability. One reviewer independently performed data extraction, and a second reviewer verified the entries for accuracy and consistency. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or by a third party if needed.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using the appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, tailored to study design (e.g., RCTs, quasi-experiments, case series, and qualitative studies) [35]. Each domain was assessed systematically, and judgments were recorded as “Yes,” “No,” or “Unclear.” Any disagreements between assessors were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the characteristics and findings of the included studies, as the variability in study designs, intervention modalities, and outcome measures was expected to preclude quantitative meta-analysis. Acceptability was evaluated based on patient and provider feedback, headset comfort, usability, and immersion ratings. Feasibility indicators such as recruitment, completion rates, and reasons for dropout were descriptively analysed. Safety and tolerability were assessed by summarizing reported adverse events such as dizziness, nausea, or fatigue, as well as any documented physiological changes.

Results

Study selection

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 897 records were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 549 studies remained. Based on title and abstract screening, 102 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 95 were excluded for various reasons. Ultimately, seven studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this review [15–17, 36−39]. Additionally, three corresponding authors were contacted via email (twice each) to request supplementary data [17, 37, 38]. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the seven included studies were conducted in four countries—China, Spain, the United States, and Switzerland—and were published between 2017 and 2025. Study designs included one RCT, one qualitative study and five single-arm feasibility studies. The sample sizes range from 10 to 50, totalling n = 190 participants. The average participant age across studies was 56.2 years, with male participants accounting for 63%. The included studies involved diverse ICU patient populations, including mechanically ventilated patients [37], non-intubated adult ICU patients [16, 17], postoperative cardiac surgery patients [36], elderly patients [38], and general ICU patients without specifying ventilation or intubation status [15].

VR content: Natural scenes

VR interventions typically utilized immersive audiovisual equipment such as (HMDs), all-in-one VR headsets, or VR helmets, including the Pico G2 4 K S VR headset with in-ear earphones [38], the Samsung Gear VR headset with earplugs [15], and smartphone-based systems like the Google Daydream headset with Bluetooth headphones [16, 17], featuring apps such as Pearl, RelaxVR, Google Spotlight Stories, and Bait!.

VR content was primarily delivered through visual and auditory stimuli. The most common element was natural scenery (n = 6), covering landscapes such as mountains, rivers, beaches, blue skies and sunlight accompanied by auxiliary auditory elements including natural ambient sounds, soft light music and classical music, alongside ambient sounds such as running water and wind, Dream Beach by Atmosphaeres [15], and RelaxVR, which offers immersive beach wave scenes [36]. Only one study used passive/attention/memory tasks without nature exposure [37]. Guided meditation (n = 2) was delivered via voice prompts and breathing exercises as a complementary relaxation module [16, 17]. Other intervention components included interactive gaming (n = 1), specifically the fishing game Bait! 17 and VR-based physical exercise (n = 2) [37, 39].

Interventions were commonly delivered by trained professionals, including ICU nurses, clinical researchers, or interdisciplinary teams [15, 17, 37]. Most VR interventions were conducted in the ICU at the patient’s bedside.

Intervention frequency varied, with once-daily sessions being most common, such as daily sessions until ICU discharge [37], up to seven daily sessions [16], two sessions per day over one week [38, 39], and once-daily sessions with at least 24 h between sessions [17]. Some studies used one-time interventions [15], whereas others applied multiple sessions pre-ICU, during ICU, and post-discharge [36]. Session durations mostly ranged between 15 and 20 min, including 15–20-minute sessions [37], 15-minute sessions [38], and shorter or flexible sessions of 5–10 min [15, 17, 36].

Tailoring was achieved by adapting VR contents to the patient’s physical condition, preferences, or cognitive status, such as adjusting VR contents based on alertness and limb mobility [37], adjusting meditation content duration according to patient preference [16], and modifying visual focus based on physical restraints [15]. Two studies reported minor modifications to the intervention protocol: Gerber et al. (2019) substituted CERAD with MoCA due to feasibility and comparable effectiveness, while Jawed et al. (2021) modified VR contents and equipment disinfection procedures based on focus group feedback to ensure infection control and reduce adverse stimulation.

All included studies reported appropriate adherence evaluation strategies. These included tracking completion rates, documenting interruptions and their causes, assessing physiological and behavioural responses [16, 17, 37, 38], and collecting patient feedback using Likert scales [15]. To promote adherence, studies employed multiple strategies: selecting lightweight and user-friendly devices, adapting content and duration to individual needs, providing patient-centred care, and building trust through pre-intervention education [15, 16, 38].

Acceptability, feasibility, safety and tolerability

Acceptability

High acceptability was defined quantitatively based on criteria from Appel et al. (2020) and Glegg & Levac (2018), including: at least 75% of patients providing positive feedback (e.g., describing the experience as “enjoyable” or expressing willingness to continue), immersion or satisfaction scores of 3 or higher out of 5 (or 5 or higher out of 7), and at least 75% of patients being able to clearly recall the intervention content or wishing to continue its use [41, 42]. High acceptability was considered present when studies reported positive participant evaluations meeting thresholds adapted from Appel et al. (2020) and Glegg & Levac (2018).

For studies using Likert-type scales with different score ranges, thresholds were converted proportionally to equivalent values across scales. For example, a score of ≥ 3/5 (60% of the maximum possible score) corresponded to ≥ 2.4/4 (60%) or ≥ 4.2/7 (60%).

Overall, findings across the seven studies suggested favourable acceptability of VR interventions, although acceptability was assessed using heterogeneous measures, including Likert-scale ratings, open-ended questionnaires, and qualitative interviews. Four studies reported quantitative indicators [15, 36–38]: Jawed et al. (2021) found that 80% of patients completed a 15-minute VR session, with 86% rating headset comfort as moderate and 79% rating the experience ≥ 3/5. Ouyang et al. (2023) observed high immersion scores (overall 6.26/7; spatial 5.76/7). Gerber et al. (2019) reported high usability (3.57/4), satisfaction (3.13/4), and immersion (2.70/4), with 84.8% of patients recalling session content and demonstrating focused visual engagement. Turon et al. (2017) showed significantly higher relaxation (P = .002) and interaction (P = .004), and lower boredom (P = .041) among ICU patients compared to healthy volunteers. Two studies using open-ended questionnaires further supported high acceptability [16, 17]: most participants described DREAMS as comfortable, enjoyable, and relaxing, with several requesting longer sessions or expressing interest in purchasing the system, and patients emphasized immersion, distraction, and meaningful escape from the ICU [12, 13]. One qualitative study provided additional insights: patients reported perceived inner positive experiences (e.g., happiness, vitality, enhanced self-control), improved executive effectiveness (e.g., stronger rehabilitation beliefs, cognitive ability, respiratory endurance, and physical activity), and enriched relationships with staff (e.g., emotional support, trust, shared goals) [39].

Despite high acceptability, few participants reported distress from emotionally evocative content such as the Pearl VR film [17]. Identified obstacles included technical limitations (e.g., VR not adapting to ICU patient positioning, spatial constraints, network lag, or unclear visuals), individual preferences (with some patients indicating that the content did not meet their personal needs), and poor physical condition (as poor health status or disease-related discomfort occasionally impacted the VR experience) [39].

Two studies also assessed ICU staff acceptability through open-ended questionnaires [15, 37]. In Jawed et al., 86% of clinicians reported moderate or greater involvement in the VR environment, 81% perceived realism, and all reported moderate or greater spatial presence; 43% rated the headset moderately comfortable and 57% very comfortable, with 90.5% experiencing no equipment issues. Minor adverse events (slight dizziness, nausea, headache) did not prevent completion of the 15-minute session [15]. Turon et al. reported high ratings for compatibility with pharmacologic treatment and physiotherapy, with lower scores only for physical infrastructure [37].

Feasibility

Recruitment rate was calculated as the number of enrolled participants divided by the number of individuals screened for eligibility. Variability in enrolment efficiency was observed, ranging from 12.6% [16] to 67.6% [38]. Completion rate refers to the proportion of recruited participants who completed data analysis. The average completion rate was around 71.0%, varying across studies from 27% (10/37), [17] 57.9% (33/57), [36] 78% (46/59), [16] 86.8% (17/20), [37] and 92% (23/25), [38] to 100% [15]. The most common reasons for non-completion were patient refusal or being too ill to participate; other reasons included ICU readmission and death during ICU stay.

Safety and tolerability

Based on criteria from Appel et al. (2020) and Glegg & Levac (2018), interventions were considered well-tolerated if at least 80% of patients completed treatment without serious adverse events (such as syncope or vomiting) and only experienced low rates of mild side effects (e.g., dizziness or nausea) that resolved spontaneously and did not interrupt treatment. Mild adverse events were defined as mild side effects occurring in 25% or more of patients, including dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, or anxiety, which might lead to temporary interruption or a discontinuation rate of 10% or more [41, 42].

Regarding safety and tolerability, five out of six studies reported VR interventions as safe. Ouyang et al. (2022) observed mild dizziness in 7 of 23 patients (16 episodes) and mild nausea in 4 patients (4 episodes), all resolving within five minutes without serious events. Jawed et al. (2021) described occasional mild dizziness or headaches but overall good tolerance without physiological changes. Turon et al. (2017) reported an 86.8% session completion rate without serious adverse events. Additionally, Gerber et al. (2019) and Ong et al. (2020) confirmed the safety of VR interventions by demonstrating no significant changes in vital signs before and after sessions. A supplementary table (Supplementary Table 1) summarizing the included studies, covering study design, intervention characteristics, and acceptability-related outcomes, is presented.

Qualitative findings

We identified one qualitative study reporting four core themes: perceived inner positive experiences (i.e. happiness, vitality, self-control and active engagement), executive effectiveness, positive relationships, and intervention obstacles. They also perceived enhanced executive effectiveness, such as greater rehabilitation motivation, improved cognition, better respiratory endurance and increased limb mobility. Participants additionally highlighted positive rapport with clinical staff, characterized by emotional support, shared rehabilitation goals and trust in care providers. Finally, key barriers mentioned included VR technical limitations, content mismatches with personal preferences, and adverse physical symptoms.

Discussion

Across the studies, VR therapeutic interventions have been identified as acceptable, feasible, and safe tools for facilitating the prevention of delirium in ICU patients; however, these findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. Several key components were delivered via VR technology, including nature-based environments, relaxation techniques, and elements of cognitive or physical engagement. This review suggests that VR design is acceptable, feasible and well-tolerated in ICU settings, where patients typically experience limited mobility and are confined to sealed ICUs without access to outdoor environments. Furthermore, healthcare professionals have also indicated the acceptability and compatibility of VR interventions with existing workflows, highlighting the potential sustainability of this care model. Safety profiles are favourable overall; reported adverse effects (e.g., dizziness, headache, nausea) are typically mild and transient.

Both quantitative and qualitative findings imply that the acceptability of VR delirium prevention interventions among ICU patients largely hinges on their ability to provide a transformative experience within the confines of a restricted environment. Capturing patients’ experiences and perceptions of the VR intervention adds knowledge beyond standard effectiveness outcomes [43]. It explains how the intervention is encountered by ICU patients who often face significant psychological stress and delirium risk stemming from prolonged immobility, sedation, and the sterile, isolated care environment [44]. VR offers a unique opportunity to mitigate these stressors by immersing patients in calming, nature-based environments or engaging activities that can distract them from the immediate clinical setting, thereby helping to reduce the incidence of delirium. This approach does not violate the ethical principle of prioritizing human-to-human interaction [45]; rather, it aims to transform the restrictive nature of ICU settings by introducing a virtual environment designed to relax and engage patients. patients from included studies generally perceive VR therapeutic interventions as enjoyable and beneficial, appreciating the break from the monotony of their hospital stay [15, 46]. The positive reception is often attributed to the engaging and interactive nature of VR, which can evoke feelings of normalcy and provide mental stimulation. However, such subjective satisfaction should be interpreted with caution, as participation and completion rates varied substantially across the included studies.

The acceptability and feasibility of VR for delirium prevention observed in this review align with findings from existing reviews focused on psychological symptoms across diverse patient populations and clinical settings. Two reviews show that VR interventions are considered acceptable among patients with cancer, as the distracting nature of VR can help reduce pain and worry due to chemotherapy treatment procedures [19], allowing patients to temporarily disconnect from the medical environment and immerse themselves in a pleasant atmosphere [20]. One review reported high acceptance rates and minimal side effects for VR use among ICU patients aimed at alleviating anxiety and depression [18]. Another review indicated overall feasibility and acceptability of VR among patients with psychiatric disorders, with participants reporting VR as calming, helpful, enjoyable, relaxing, or useful, and cybersickness as tolerable [21]. Differences in VR duration were also observed: across the included studies, VR for delirium prevention had shorter durations (≤ 20 min) than in cancer patients (2–60 min) [19, 20] and psychiatric patients (3–75 min) [21].

The feasibility of implementing VR in ICU settings to prevent delirium involves considering technological, logistical, and safety factors. Technologically, the high completion rate indicates that VR systems are generally delivered in a user-friendly approach, requiring minimal patient effort, especially those with low digital literacy, to avoid exacerbating stress or discomfort. Logistically, implementing VR requires integration into existing ICU workflows without disrupting routine care. This involves training healthcare staff to operate VR equipment and manage sessions that are easy to set up, enjoyable for patients, and intuitive to operate, while ensuring that VR use does not interfere with medical procedures or monitoring [16]. This aligns with existing literature indicating that, with proper planning and resource allocation, VR can be seamlessly incorporated into ICU care, offering a feasible adjunctive therapy to enhance patient well-being [47].

Safety and tolerability are critical considerations for the enrolment and completion of VR interventions. While VR offers significant benefits, potential side effects such as dizziness, headaches, and nausea have been reported in the literature and were observed in the reviewed studies. However, these side effects were generally mild, transient, and did not lead to serious consequences such as delirium. Recommendations from broader VR interventions suggest that seated interaction and teleportation-based locomotion [48], alongside recent improvements in display latency, optics, refresh rates, and tracking [49], may further mitigate cybersickness; however, this requires further investigation among ICU patients. This underscores the importance of monitoring and managing these symptoms through established protocols to enhance the overall feasibility and safety of VR therapeutic interventions in the ICU setting specifically for delirium prevention.

Ultimately, the goal of implementing VR in ICU settings to prevent delirium is to create a more holistic care experience that leverages technology to improve patient outcomes while respecting and preserving the irreplaceable value of human interaction [45]. By offering an additional layer of support, VR provides a virtual setting that transforms the ICU environment. This approach aligns with ethical healthcare practices by prioritizing patient-centred care and ensuring that technological advancements are used to enhance, rather than diminish, the quality of human connections and the overall therapeutic environment.

Limitations

This review included only seven studies published in English and Chinese, reflecting the limited evidence on VR therapeutic interventions for delirium prevention in ICU settings and potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Most included studies were quantitative, with only limited qualitative feedback, and the scarcity of qualitative research may have led to missed nuanced insights into participant experiences and contextual factors. Measurement tools varied widely across studies, with most acceptability and feasibility outcomes derived from study-specific or nonvalidated questionnaires. To harmonize results, we proportionally converted thresholds across different Likert scales, a process that may introduce bias. Most included studies did not designate delirium prevention, incidence, duration, or severity as primary outcomes; instead, they emphasized broader psychological, experiential, or feasibility endpoints, which may limit insights specific to delirium prevention. The inclusion of grey literature, such as conference proceedings and a graduate thesis, enhanced the comprehensiveness of the evidence base; however, it may also introduce variability in methodological reporting and potential publication bias. Variability in intervention design, dosage, and outcome measures across the limited number of included studies prevents firm conclusions about component acceptability.

Implications for practice and/or further research

Several implications for future research are noteworthy. Adequately powered randomized controlled trials and subsequent systematic reviews are needed to assess the effects of VR interventions on delirium incidence, duration, and severity, along with downstream outcomes including length of stay, sedative use, functional recovery, and patient satisfaction. Comparative effectiveness studies are warranted to examine nature-based, relaxation-focused, and cognitive/physical engagement modules to identify the most effective content for delirium prevention. In addition, pragmatic study designs should be employed to assess real-world adoption, fidelity, workflow impact, and sustainability across diverse ICU contexts.

For clinical practice, clinicians may start with calming, nature-based, low-exertion VR experiences that are intuitive, minimize cognitive load, and are appropriate for ICU patients with limited mobility and fluctuating alertness. Implement structured checklists to monitor cybersickness symptoms (e.g., dizziness, nausea, headache) and establish clear stop criteria and recovery procedures.

Conclusion

VR therapeutic interventions featuring nature-based environments, relaxation techniques, and cognitively or physically engaging activities appear acceptable, feasible, and safe adjuncts for delirium prevention in ICU settings; however, these findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. Fully powered clinical trials are needed to determine effectiveness and to confirm safety. Within the included studies, patients often reported enjoyment and perceived benefits (e.g., relief from ICU monotony and psychological stressors), and healthcare professionals indicated that, with appropriate training and planning, VR could be integrated into workflows—findings that remain tentative given small samples and heterogeneity. Safety profiles were favourable overall, with adverse events (e.g., dizziness, headache, nausea) typically mild and transient.
