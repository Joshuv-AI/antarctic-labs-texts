High-throughput monitoring of free Mg2+ in IVT reaction reveals effects of free Mg2+ on mRNA synthesis and DNA digestion
Authors: Tjaša Marušič, Tina Faganel, Rok Sekirnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractmRNA vaccines and therapeutics are produced with an in vitro transcription reaction (IVT), a condensation of nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs) into a nascent mRNA chain templated by DNA and catalyzed by RNA polymerase. While tools exist to monitor NTP consumption, the role of the essential cofactor Mg2+ in IVT has not been extensively studied due to the lack of suitable analytical methods. In this study, we report the development of a high-throughput analytical method for monitoring free Mg2+ using selective fluorescent indicators. We combine this free Mg2+ monitoring method with previously reported rapid at-line monitoring of NTP and mRNA concentrations and apply it in batch and fed-batch IVT systems, varying Mg:NTP ratios and NTP concentrations to assess their effects on mRNA yield via free Mg2+. We experimentally demonstrate that free Mg2+ is critical for efficient mRNA production, with low free Mg2+ correlating with slow reaction progression or reaction stalling. For the first time, we experimentally show that under certain IVT conditions, the released free Mg2+ can be effectively utilized to continue the IVT reaction by feeding NTPs only. We also show that pyrophosphatase concentration significantly affects free Mg2+ levels and relate this to IVT kinetics and dsRNA content. Additionally, we demonstrate for the first time that free Mg2+ affects downstream DNase digestion of the pDNA template, resulting in residual pDNA when free Mg2+ levels are insufficient for DNase activity. Our findings provide an accessible methodological platform to improve the efficiency of mRNA production by optimizing IVT conditions and ensuring DNase activity.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00216-026-06542-x.Keywords: IVT, mRNA, Mg2+, pyrophosphatase, DNase

Introduction

In vitro transcription (IVT) is an enzymatic reaction for the synthesis of mRNA from a DNA template, which guides the condensation of nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs) into a nascent mRNA chain, catalyzed by RNA polymerase enzyme (RNAP), such as T7 RNA polymerase (T7 RNAP) [1]. T7 RNAP is a magnesium-dependent enzyme. In solution, Mg2+ binds to NTPs, primarily forming MgNTP2− complexes, with smaller amounts in the form of Mg2NTP, MgHNTP−, and free Mg2+ [2]. Since two Mg2+ ions are needed in the active site of T7 RNAP [3], not only MgNTP2− complexes, but also free Mg2+ ions are crucial for effective transcription [2]. After incorporation of an NTP into the nascent mRNA strand, one H+ and one pyrophosphate ion (PPi) are released. Pyrophosphate ions can form insoluble complexes with Mg2+ and precipitate out of aqueous solution. To avoid precipitation, pyrophosphatase (PPase) is often used to convert one pyrophosphate ion into two soluble phosphate ions (Pi) [4]. Some authors suggest that a low concentration of free magnesium, rather than the presence of PPi, is most detrimental to transcription and therefore recommend performing IVTs with higher initial Mg2+ concentration when PPase is not used [5]. However, recent studies have shown that Mg2PPi, in the presence of DNA, can also form insoluble Mg2PPi-DNA complexes, which sequester DNA from solution and thus reduce the reaction rate [6–8].

The concentrations of NTPs and Mg2+ have been repeatedly cited to be among the most important factors governing IVT yields [9–16]. In particular, we have shown that the ratio between Mg2+ concentration and total NTP concentration (Mg:NTP ratio), rather than Mg2+ concentration itself, drives IVT yield, with an optimal Mg:NTP ratio of 1–2 [17]. An excess of Mg2+ over the total NTP concentration is recommended to ensure a sufficient concentration of free Mg2+ in the reaction; at least 5 mM and optimally 10–20 mM of free Mg2+ have been reported for effective transcription [4]. However, the increased ionic strength and higher concentrations of the magnesium counterion (usually chloride or acetate) have an inhibitory effect on the progression of the reaction [4, 16, 18]. This leads to a preference for a lower Mg:NTP ratio (~ 1) when high NTP concentrations are used [17]. Furthermore, lowering the Mg:NTP ratio below 1.0 can reduce the content of immunogenic double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) by-product, without a detrimental effect on mRNA yield [17]. While Mg2+ is also necessary for folding and stabilization of mRNA structure [19], high concentrations of free Mg2+ can contribute to mRNA hydrolysis [20, 21], which can be further enhanced by longer reaction time [22]. Additionally, Mg2+ contributes to 5′ cap hydrolysis which can negatively impact protein expression [23].

Several authors have shown that mRNA production in an IVT reaction can be improved by a fed-batch approach, i.e., by the stepwise addition of NTPs and Mg2+ [5, 12, 24, 25]. In fed-batch IVT, the total mass of DNA template and enzymes remains unchanged, thereby improving the cost efficiency of the reaction. In addition, a lower initial concentration of NTPs in the fed-batch process delays inhibitory effects due to the high ionic strength, allowing the reaction to proceed faster than in a batch process, where the full amount of NTPs is added at the beginning of reaction [5]. To effectively perform fed-batch IVT, application of transcription solution equilibrium (TSE) analysis was suggested, which combines the calculation of solution equilibrium with reaction stoichiometry to predict changes in solution equilibrium as the reaction proceeds [5]. Experimental optimization of feeds has focused on NTP addition, because suitable analytical techniques are available for at-line NTP monitoring, e.g., with HPLC analytics [12, 25, 26]. Optimization of Mg2+ in the feed is less well studied, presumably due to a lack of suitable analytics of free Mg2+. It was shown that the concentration of free Mg2+ increases during the reaction [27], but apparently not enough for the reaction to proceed without the addition of Mg2+ [12]. There is thus significant potential to improve IVT conditions by optimizing the feeding regime of Mg2+ more precisely.

The function of Mg2+ in IVT is therefore both important and complex, while analytical methods to study it are lacking. To date, only one study has addressed the challenge of measuring free Mg2+ in IVT, utilizing capillary zone electrophoresis (CZE) to separate free Mg2+ from other IVT components based on the difference in charge-to-size ratio [27]. The authors showed that increasing free Mg2+ up to 10 mM improves mRNA yield while further increase reduces the yield. Additionally, they showed that dsRNA content increases with increasing free Mg2+. However, due to low throughput of CZE, elaborate reaction optimization was not possible, and long per-sample analysis time precluded at-line decisions on Mg2+ feeds in fed-batch scenarios.

Herein, we report the development of a high-throughput method for monitoring free Mg2⁺ in the IVT reaction using the fluorescent indicator mag-indo-1, designed to work with accessible analytical equipment, minimal sample consumption, simple sample preparation, and rapid readout. We show that the method can be used to monitor the release of free Mg2+ during the IVT reaction and its sequestration by addition of NTPs in fed-batch. When coupled with previously reported at-line monitoring of NTP and mRNA concentration, we show how the new method can support the investigation of pyrophosphatase effects on the IVT reaction and the detection of insufficient concentrations of free Mg2+, which have previously been overlooked despite being critical for the progress of the IVT. Finally, we demonstrate how the method can predict the effects of IVT reaction conditions on subsequent DNase digestion of pDNA template: low concentration of free Mg2+ in combination with high concentration of Mg-binding species (e.g., NTPs and PPi) can prevent DNase from digesting pDNA template, potentially resulting in high amounts of residual template in mRNA products.

Materials and methods

In vitro transcription

RNase inhibitor (40 U/µL), pyrophosphatase (1000 U/mL), T7 RNA polymerase (1000 U/µL), ATP, UTP, CTP, and GTP (100 mM stocks) were from Roche. ATP, UTP, CTP, and GTP (200 mM stocks) used for feeding were from Mebep. The pDNA template encoding eGFP mRNA (pDNA length: 3280 bp, mRNA length: 942 nt) was provided by BioMay. Mg acetate (1 M) was from Sigma-Aldrich; 10× IVT buffer (400 mM Tris-acetate, 100 mM DTT, pH 7.9) was prepared in-house. DNase I (2 U/µL) and 10× DNase reaction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 25 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.6) were from NEB.

Fed-batch IVT reactions

The concentrations of NTPs and Mg acetate for the batch IVT reactions were as indicated for each reaction; the other parameters were set to 1× IVT buffer, 1 U/µL RNase inhibitor, 2 U/mL pyrophosphatase, 75 ng/µL pDNA, and 50 U/µL T7 RNAP. IVT reactions were mixed in 1.5 mL low-binding plastic tubes (Eppendorf), with T7 RNAP added last, and incubated in a Thermomixer™C (Eppendorf) at 37 °C with shaking at 300 rpm. Prior to sampling, reactions were mixed by pipetting; 4 µL aliquots were then quenched with 4 µL 100 mM EDTA pH 8.0 for chromatographic analysis, and 10 µL aliquots were frozen at − 80 °C for analysis of free Mg2+ every 30 min. When ATP concentration dropped below 10%, the reactions were divided into four parts and mixed with feeds containing Mg2+ and NTPs. The feeds were prepared from 200 mM NTPs to minimize dilution of the reaction. To maintain the same dilution of the reaction regardless of the Mg2+ concentration in the feed, the Mg2+ volumes were replaced by ddH2O in feeds with lower Mg2+ concentrations. The reactions were quenched with 100 mM EDTA pH 8.0 (1:1 volume ratio) when the fastest proceeding reaction was completed.

DOE batch IVT reactions

The concentrations of NTPs, Mg acetate, and pyrophosphatase were as indicated for each reaction; the other parameters were set to 1× IVT buffer, 1 U/µL RNase inhibitor, 75 ng/µL pDNA, and 50 U/µL T7 RNAP. Reactions were prepared as described above and sampled every 30 min until 240 min. After 240 min, DNase digestion of the pDNA template was performed by incubating the reaction mixture with DNase I (1 U enzyme per μg pDNA) in 1× DNase reaction buffer for 15 min at 37 °C. Ten-microliter aliquots were frozen at − 80 °C for analysis of free Mg2+; the remainder was quenched with 100 mM EDTA pH 8.0 (1:1 volume ratio).

Monitoring free Mg2+ with mag-indo-1 tetrapotassium salt

Immunoblotting

Anti-dsRNA monoclonal antibody J2 dot blot was used for the detection of dsRNA in 1 µg of RNA samples. The analysis was performed as previously described [30]. Chemiluminescent signal was visualized using WesternBright ECL on the iBright FL1500 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). dsRNA content was estimated on an arbitrary scale of 0–2, where 0 corresponded to the intensity of the negative control (ddH2O) and 10 corresponded to the intensity of the Magi dsRNA standard (10 ng).

Agarose gel electrophoresis for detection of residual pDNA template

Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to semi-quantitatively determine the amount of residual linear pDNA template in DNase-treated samples as described previously [31]. Five micrograms of RNA from each DNase-treated IVT sample were incubated with 10 ng RNase A (NEB) for 1 h at 37 °C prior to analysis, which enabled visualization of residual pDNA template in RNA samples.

Results

Development of analytical method for monitoring free Mg2+ in IVT

To develop an analytical method for monitoring free Mg2+ levels in IVT, we initially tested two ratiometric indicators, mag-fura-2 (Invitrogen) and mag-indo-1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), both as water-soluble tetrapotassium salts. Excitation and emission spectra for Mg-bound and Mg-free indicators were scanned at wavelengths recommended by providers; Mg-bound and Mg-free indicators showed the highest signal at Ex1 = 330 nm, Em1 = 500 nm, Ex2 = 380 nm, and Em2 = 510 nm for mag-fura-2 (Fig. S1a) and Ex1 = 330 nm, Em1 = 410 nm, Ex2 = 360 nm, and Em2 = 490 nm for mag-indo-1 (Fig. 1a), which were selected for further experiments.

One hundred millimolar Mg2+ was used for determination of Mg-bound spectra. Mg-bound and Mg-free indicator showed the highest signal at Ex1 = 330 nm, Em1 = 410 nm and Ex2 = 360 nm, Em2 = 490 nm, respectively. b Fluorescence measurements at two different wavelength sets for mag-indo-1 (Ex1 = 330 nm, Em1 = 410 nm; Ex2 = 360 nm, Em2 = 490 nm). Typically, 1–10 µM of indicator is used in the fluorescence-based assays. A concentration of 10 µM resulted in lower difference between Rmin and REDTA (standard containing 0 mM Mg2+ and control standard containing 2 mM EDTA, respectively) and led to more consistent measurements compared to 5 µM and 1 µM, presumably due to the lower effect of indicator degradation caused by exposure to light (Table S1, Table S2), and was therefore used for further experiments. Sample volume requirement was minimized by using 384-well low-volume microplates (total volume of 15 µL per well). Titration of different concentrations of Mg2+ in 40 mM Tris resulted in a wider linear range for mag-indo-1 than mag-fura-2 (0.05–10 mM and 0.05–6 mM, respectively, Fig. 1b, c and Fig. S1b-c), making it more suitable for the detection of free Mg2+ in IVT, where concentration range of free Mg2+ can be up to 20–30 mM. However, high concentrations of IVT components together with changing conditions during the reaction had a strong effect on indicator response, requiring dilution of IVT samples.

Twenty-fold dilution of all IVT samples was selected to reduce the non-specific effects of IVT components on the indicator response while maintaining sufficiently high concentrations of free Mg2+ for effective detection. All concentrations reported herein refer to the concentration of free Mg2+ in 20-fold diluted IVT. Spiking the calibration curve with all IVT components (except the NTPs) at 10- and 20-fold dilution had no effect on the slope of the calibration curve (Fig. 1d), suggesting that 20-fold dilution is sufficient to reduce potential interference of the IVT components on the fluorescence response, even when higher concentrations of IVT components are used.

Free Mg2+ in batch and fed-batch IVT

We then tested the analytical method on IVT samples (Fig. 2a). First, we performed batch IVT reactions with an initial concentration of 8 mM of each NTP (total concentration 32 mM) and Mg:NTP ratios of 0.75, 1.00, or 1.25. A higher initial Mg:NTP ratio resulted in faster mRNA production and NTP consumption (Fig. 2b, c). Consistent with a previous report [27], we detected an increase in free Mg2+ as the reaction progressed (Fig. 2d); higher free Mg2+ concentration was detected for Mg:NTP ratio of 1.25 compared to 1.00 and 0.75.

ATP is shown as the representative NTP (the limiting NTP)

We then transitioned to a fed-batch system. To study if reaction progression correlates with free Mg2+ levels in the system, we divided batch IVT reaction mixtures (8 mM of each NTP, initial Mg:NTP ratios of 0.75, 1.00, or 1.25) into four parts when ATP concentration dropped below 10% (Fig. 3, middle column). Each part was fed with 4 mM of each NTP and Mg2+ at Mg:NTP ratios of 0.00, 0.25, 0.50, or 0.75. When the initial Mg:NTP ratio was 1.25, no differences in NTP consumption or mRNA production were observed after Mg-NTP additions (Fig. 3a). Lower Mg:NTP ratios in the feed resulted in a larger decrease in free Mg2+; when no Mg2+ was present in the feed, free Mg2+ concentration dropped to 0.3 mM. When the initial Mg:NTP ratio was 1.00, comparatively slower NTP consumption and mRNA production were observed when no Mg2+ was present in the feed (Fig. 3b); after feeding, free Mg2+ concentration was < 0.2 mM. When the same feeding strategy was used for IVT with initial Mg:NTP ratio of 0.75, the differences in reaction progression were highest (Fig. 3c): the lower the Mg2+ content in the feed, the slower the mRNA production. Free Mg2+ concentration after feeding was < 0.2 mM for all reactions except for the highest Mg-NTP feed ratio of 0.75 (free Mg2+ concentration was 0.3 mM). When no Mg2+ was added to the feed, free Mg2+ concentration was < 0.1 mM and less than 50% of added ATP was consumed.

To achieve chelation of all free Mg2+, we then increased NTP concentration in the feeds (4, 6, or 8 mM of each NTP). Reaction mixtures with initial concentration of 8 mM of each NTP and Mg:NTP ratio of 1.00 were again split into four parts at 90 min. We observed that lower Mg2+ levels in the feed resulted in lower free Mg2+ concentration after feeding and consequently slower reaction progression. The differences in reaction progression after feeding were more pronounced when more NTPs were added (Fig. 4). Reactions that progressed substantially more slowly (less than 50% of ATP was consumed in the time needed for the fastest progressing reaction from the same series to finish) all had very low free Mg2+ (< 0.1 mM). Feeding 8 mM of each NTP with no Mg2+ in the feed stalled the reaction (Fig. 4c).

We then investigated whether a general rule can be established regarding the minimal starting concentration of free Mg2+ (either the initial concentration in batch reaction or the concentration immediately after feed addition) that does not negatively affect mRNA production and ATP/NTP consumption. Analysis of all reactions in first 90 min revealed that the concentration of free Mg2+ > 0.2–0.3 mM results in rapid mRNA production and ATP/NTP consumption, 0.1–0.2 mM leads to a slower reaction rate, and < 0.1 mM stalls IVT reaction (Fig. S2). Additionally, we tested whether the measured free Mg2+ concentration could be a predictor of reaction progression. A linear relationship (R2 > 0.95) and approximately constant slopes were found between measured free Mg2+ and consumed ATP, NTP, as well as mRNA concentration (Fig. S3).

Monitoring free Mg2+ reveals the influence of PPase on IVT progression and DNA digestion

Encouraged by the ability to monitor free Mg2+ levels as a function of IVT conditions, we then studied the effect of PPase on free Mg2+ concentration. Standard PPase concentration (1 U/mL) and various lower PPase concentrations were added to IVT reactions with 12 mM of each NTP and Mg:NTP ratio of 1.2 (Fig. 5). We found that a 10-fold reduction in PPase concentration (to 0.1 U/mL) resulted in the same mRNA yield after 240 min, but at a lower reaction rate. Further reducing PPase concentration (to 0.06 and 0.03 U/mL) decreased the reaction rate, which correlated with lower free Mg2+ levels. The absence of PPase inhibited of the IVT reaction, which correlated with a rapid decrease in free Mg2+ concentration – at 90 min, free Mg2+ concentration was < 0.1 mM, at which point mRNA concentration started to plateau, reaching 6 mg/mL in 240 min compared to reactions with 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase, which reached 12 g/L. Dot blot analysis detected higher dsRNA content in IVT with the highest PPase (Fig. S4).

PPase drives mRNA production through releasing Mg2+ from Mg-PPi complexes. Twelve millimolar of each NTP was used; initial Mg:NTP ratio was 1.2. mRNA production and ATP consumption kinetics were monitored with CIMac PrimaS; free Mg2+ in 20-fold diluted IVT was monitored with mag-indo-1

Combinatorial effects of PPase concentration (0–1 U/mL), NTP concentration (8–16 mM each), and Mg:NTP ratio (0.8–1.6) on free Mg2+ levels were then investigated in a DOE study (study design and results for final time point are in Table S3, J2 dot blot results are in Fig. S5 and time-course data in Fig. S6). PPase concentration was log-transformed before modeling. The models for free Mg2+ and dsRNA at the final time point were good (R2 = 0.87, Q2 = 0.73 and R2 = 0.88, Q2 = 0.76, respectively, Fig. S7b-c and Fig. 6). However, mRNA production and relative yield at the final time point could not be modeled well (R2 = 0.78, Q2 = 0.49, Fig. S7a), as the difference in mRNA concentrations at 240 min between 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase was very small (Fig. 5 and Fig. S8a-b). More localized models (from 0 to 0.1 U/mL PPase) were created for mRNA production and relative yield at the final time point (Fig. 6a, b), which significantly improved model quality (R2 = 0.94, Q2 = 0.82; Fig. S9). Models confirmed that without PPase, mRNA concentration and relative yield are lower due to the absence of free Mg2+ (Fig. 6, left panels). With addition of low amounts of PPase, concentration of free Mg2+ was higher, resulting in higher concentrations of mRNA and higher relative yield (Fig. 6, middle panels). When PPase was in excess, free Mg2+ concentration was highest, leading to increased mRNA production and relative yield, but also the highest dsRNA content (Fig. 6, right panels).

Modeling the effects of PPase, Mg:NTP ratio, and NTP concentration on a mRNA yield, b relative yield (% of theoretical maximal yield), c free Mg2+ concentration, and d dsRNA content. Since the difference in final mRNA production and relative yield was very low between 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase, more localized models (up to 0.1 U/mL PPase) were created for mRNA production and relative yield

Additionally, since the difference in reaction kinetics between 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase was clearly visible (Fig. 5), we created a time-resolved model to discriminate between 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase and to confirm observations from Fig. 5 on a wider range of NTP concentrations and initial Mg:NTP ratios. Models were significantly improved when time resolution was included (R2 = 0.91, Q2 = 0.89 for mRNA production and R2 = 0.93, Q2 = 0.92 for free Mg2+, Fig. S10). Results confirmed that 0.1 U/mL PPase achieves a comparable relative yield at 240 min to 1 U/mL PPase, but with slower kinetics (Fig. S11b). We confirmed that in the absence of PPase, the reaction slows/stops, which is more pronounced at lower initial Mg:NTP ratios (Fig. S11a). Additionally, we experimentally confirmed that in the absence of PPase, free Mg2+ concentration decreases with time; in the case of 0.1 U/mL PPase, it remains relatively constant; and it increases with time for 1 U/mL PPase (Fig. S11c).

After 240 min, all IVT reactions were treated with DNase I according to the standard protocol to remove pDNA template. High amounts of residual pDNA template were detected in reactions with the lowest amounts of free Mg2+ after addition of DNase and DNase buffer (0.1 mM; IVT6 and IVT7; Fig. S12). Interestingly, no residual template was detected in DNase reactions where slightly more free Mg2+ (0.2 mM) was present. Although there were several IVT reactions that contained 0.0 mM free Mg2+ after 240 min of reaction progression, only two (IVT6 and IVT7) contained 0.1 mM free Mg2+ after addition of DNase buffer (Table S3), suggesting that the presence of high residual concentrations of Mg-binding species from IVT (NTPs and PPi) prevents increase in free Mg2+ to levels sufficient for DNase activity. We surmised that Mg-binding species from IVT might also chelate Ca2+ from DNase buffer, which is also essential for DNase activity [32], but that monitoring free Mg2+ may be sufficiently informative to predict DNase activity. To confirm that lack of DNase activity is due to chelation of divalent ions from DNase buffer by species from the IVT reaction, we repeated these two IVT reactions (now termed IVT6-r and IVT7-r): after 240 min, each reaction was divided into five parts for DNase digestion, and various amounts of Mg2+ with or without Ca2+ were added to the standard reaction protocol (Table S4). Agarose gel electrophoresis analysis revealed the presence of residual pDNA template in reactions where measured free Mg2+ concentration was < 0.3 or < 0.2 mM for IVT6-r and IVT7-r, respectively, while pDNA was completely digested when a sufficient amount of Mg2+ and/or Ca2+ ions was added (Fig. S13, Table S5).

Discussion

Magnesium ions (Mg2+) are crucial for many cellular processes, including protein and nucleic acid synthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, signal transduction, cell metabolism, and proliferation. Due to its positive charge, which acts as a bridge between anionic centers of high charge density, Mg2+ binds to macromolecules such as polyphosphates, e.g., pyrophosphates and NTPs. Additionally, it associates with membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby having a stabilizing and protective effect. It also promotes DNA replication and transcription and influences RNA translation, probably acting as an allosteric or catalytic modulator of key enzymes such as topoisomerases, endonucleases, and polymerases. Mg2+ also acts as a cofactor in numerous enzymatic reactions, particularly those involving nucleotides, including kinases, G-proteins, and ATPases, as the biologically active form of ATP is actually the MgATP2− complex [33]. In solution, Mg2+ ions are mainly present as MgNTP2− (Kd = 30 µM), with smaller amounts in the form of Mg2NTP (Kd = 25 mM), MgHNTP− (Kd > 25 mM), and free Mg2+ [2].

To date, only one study has measured free Mg2+ in IVT reaction, using capillary zone electrophoresis [27]. Fluorescent indicators specific for free Mg2+, widely used for visualizing and measuring free magnesium in living cells, have never been applied to study free Mg2+ in IVT reaction, despite their higher throughput, low sample consumption, and ease of implementation. It is important to distinguish between non-ratiometric indicators (such as magnesium green and mag-fluo-4), where fluorescence is measured at a single wavelength, and ratiometric indicators (such as mag-fura-2 and mag-indo-1). Ratiometric indicators exhibit a shift in the excitation or emission spectrum upon ion binding and can be calibrated using the ratio of fluorescence intensities at two different wavelengths. This calibration eliminates artifactual fluctuations in the fluorescence signal that might otherwise be misinterpreted as changes in ion concentration [33]. When quantitative rather than qualitative data from the fluorescence signal are required, ratiometric probes are preferred over non-ratiometric probes. For this reason, we selected ratiometric indicators mag-fura-2 and mag-indo-1 for measuring free Mg2+ in an IVT reaction.

Ten micromolar of indicator was used for experiments, as it led to more consistent results after different light exposure times (20 min, 2 h, 4 h) compared to 1 or 5 µM (Tables S1, S2). This made the method useful regardless of sample number and suitable for rapid at-line analytics since estimates of free Mg2+ levels could be obtained even without application of a calibration curve. Further increasing the indicator concentration was considered unlikely to be beneficial, due to higher reagent consumption and increased chelation of free Mg2+ by the indicator. It is important to note that spermidine, which has previously been reported to have strong effects on Mg-chelating indicators [34], was not used in our reactions, as no positive impact was observed on IVT kinetics (Fig. S14 and recent IVT modeling report [35]). However, at 20-fold dilution, the effect of spermidine at concentrations typically used in IVT reaction (1–4 mM) is expected to be minimal. For implementation in spermidine-containing production processes, the assay can be readily adapted by including spermidine at the corresponding concentration in the calibration curve. It is also important to note that measurements can be affected by the presence of other divalent ions. The REDTA value was therefore always used as a control for comparison with the Rmin value. As neither of these two standards contained Mg2+, their values should not differ substantially. A larger difference between them, i.e., REDTA being lower than Rmin, would indicate contamination with divalent ions from chemicals, glassware, or plastic (during assay development, a leach was detected from plastic containers from one provider, data not shown).

An important indicator property is its ion-binding affinity, expressed by the dissociation constant (Kd), which determines its sensitivity to changes in ion concentration. Typically, the ion concentration in an experiment should be between 0.1 and 10 times the Kd to ensure a linear response from the indicator. Most magnesium indicators have a Kd in the millimolar range, which allows them to optimally respond to free Mg2+ concentrations in cells, usually between 0.2 and 1 mM [33]. Since Kd is dependent on pH, temperature, viscosity, ionic strength, protein binding, and the amount of Mg2+ and other ions present, it is necessary to empirically measure Kd for each specific system of interest [36]. A relatively high Kd, combined with a low indicator concentration, decreased the potential interference with the equilibrium of bound-to-free Mg2+, i.e., by competing for Mg2+ bound to NTPs, T7 RNAP, PPi, and mRNA. Kd for mag-fura-2 was 0.6 mM and Kd for mag-indo-1 was 1.1 mM, when a range of Mg2+ concentrations was titrated in 40 mM Tris (Fig. S1c, Fig. 1c). However, high concentrations of IVT components together with numerous possibilities of initial IVT conditions as well as differences between initial and final reaction conditions resulted in variations in pH, viscosity, and ionic strength, which made application of uniform Kd impossible without dilution of samples. IVT samples were therefore diluted 20-fold to decrease non-specific effects on indicator response, and Kd was determined from a calibration curve containing 0.05 to 1.5 mM Mg2+ in 40 mM Tris (Fig. 1d). It is important to note that dilution can alter the equilibrium of Mg2+ with Mg2+-binding species, meaning that accurate quantification of free Mg2+ in the IVT reaction cannot be achieved by simply multiplying measured values by the dilution factor. Therefore, all results are reported as free Mg2+ in 20-fold diluted samples. Twenty-fold dilution of IVT samples provided sufficiently high concentrations of free Mg2+ for discrimination between different reaction conditions and detection of all significant changes in free Mg2+ levels.

Using this method, we were able to measure the release of free Mg2+ during the IVT reaction. The pronounced difference in reaction progression between 0.75 and 1.00 Mg:NTP ratios underscored the essential role of free Mg2+ in efficient reaction progression (Fig. 2b–d). Interestingly, there was almost no difference between reaction progression with 1.00 and 1.25, suggesting that an excess of free Mg2+ does not provide additional benefits under these experimental conditions. We speculated that the threshold for efficient mRNA production is around 0.2 mM of measured free Mg2+ while < 0.1 mM free Mg2+ drastically slows down the reaction.

We then designed a set of fed-batch reactions to determine the minimal amount of free Mg2+ required after feeding for a fed-batch reaction to proceed efficiently. Initial reaction conditions were selected based on reports that initial concentration of approximately 8 mM of each NTP and Mg:NTP ratio of about 1 result in both high mRNA production and high reaction rate (up to 8 mg/mL mRNA in 60–90 min), making these conditions suitable for achieving high final yields after subsequent feed additions [24, 25, 28]. Only two studies have focused on feed optimization. Pregeljc et al. showed that IVT stalls when only NTPs are added to the reaction, while feeding IVT with both NTPs and Mg2+ allows the reaction to progress [12]. Similarly, Guo et al., who studied several different Mg-NTP combinations, observed substantially lower mRNA yields after feeding NTPs without Mg2+, or reaction stalling when NTP concentration in the feed was high [24]. To study the effect of free Mg2+ on the fed-batch reaction kinetics, we varied the Mg:NTP ratio in the initial reaction as well as in the feed. The results confirmed that the fed-batch reaction can proceed without Mg2+ in the feed, but only when the Mg:NTP ratio in the initial reaction is sufficiently high or when sufficiently low amounts of NTPs are added with the feed so that not all of the free Mg2+ from the batch reaction is chelated. The threshold for efficient reaction progression appeared to be 0.2–0.3 mM of measured free Mg2+ after feeding. With our analytical method, the concentration of Mg2+ in IVT can be easily optimized, which can be particularly valuable for the fed-batch IVT. Additionally, we show that the concentration of free Mg2+ can serve as a predictor of reaction progression. A linear relationship (R2 > 0.95) was found between the measured free Mg2+ concentration and the amounts of consumed ATP, NTPs, and mRNA concentration (Fig. S3). Although the slope varied depending on reaction conditions, it can provide valuable insight into reaction progression, especially when the same IVT conditions (either batch or fed-batch) are repeatedly used and measurements can be instantly compared with those from previous reactions.

We then experimentally demonstrated the impact of PPase on free Mg2+ concentration and linked it to progression of IVT. Five IVT reactions were performed, all starting with the same amount of free Mg2+ (Fig. 5). With an excess of PPase (1 U/mL), there was sufficient free Mg2+ to allow incorporation of available NTPs, and the concentration of free Mg2+ increased as the reaction progressed. When all ATP was consumed, the reaction stopped, with a concomitant stabilization of the free Mg2+ concentration. In reactions with lower PPase concentrations, a slower increase or decrease in free Mg2+ levels was observed. This agreed well with reports that PPase prevents sequestration of free Mg2+ by PPi by converting PPi into Pi [4, 8]. Precipitation in reactions with lower PPase concentrations or without PPase was clearly observable, as the solution became cloudy. However, since the initial free Mg2+ was the same in all reactions, there was virtually no difference in ATP consumption and mRNA production between reactions until 30 min. After 30 min, the differences in free Mg2+ concentration between reactions became apparent. High free Mg2+ concentration in IVT with the highest PPase (1 U/mL) resulted in much higher ATP consumption and mRNA concentration at 60 min, while ATP consumption and mRNA concentration were comparable for other reactions, where free Mg2+ concentration was 0.2–0.5 mM. Between 60 and 90 min, lower PPase concentrations led to lower free Mg2+ levels, which halted mRNA production. When the free Mg2+ concentration reached < 0.1 mM (in IVT without PPase), mRNA production stopped.

Interestingly, lower amounts of PPase (0.06 and 0.1 U/mL) resulted in relatively constant levels of free Mg2+ in the early reaction phase, indicating that some, but not all, of the PPi was converted to phosphate, resulting in complexation of the free Mg2+ and preventing an increase in its concentration. Lowering the amount of PPase (0.03 U/mL) led to a sharp drop in free Mg2+ concentration (from 0.4 to 0.1 mM) in the early reaction phase; free Mg2+ then remained relatively constant at 0.1 mM at later time points. As some free Mg2+ was still present, the reaction progressed, albeit more slowly. Faster decrease in free Mg2+ at the beginning and the constant or slightly increased levels of free Mg2+ at later time points in the reactions with 0.03, 0.06, and 0.1 U/mL PPase, are likely due to the faster initial progression of the reaction. This led to higher concentration of PPi that could not be completely hydrolyzed with a limited amount of PPase. Slower progression of the reaction at later time points resulted in slower formation of PPi and thus enabled the PPase to cleave PPi more effectively. Dot blot analysis of mRNA indicated that IVTs with lower PPase concentrations contained less dsRNA per microgram of mRNA (Fig. S4). The observed effect on dsRNA content is most likely directly related to lower free Mg2+ levels; however, it could also be associated with altered reaction kinetics. Since IVTs with 0.1 and 1 U/mL PPase both produced the same amount of mRNA in 4 h, we propose that a lower PPase concentration and an approximately constant level of free Mg2+ during IVT reaction may reduce dsRNA, albeit at the expense of a lower reaction rate.

Similar trends were observed across a wider range of NTP concentrations and Mg:NTP ratios (Fig. S6). When an excess of PPase was used, free Mg2+ concentration increased as long as the mRNA concentration increased; the reactions stopped when ATP was depleted. When 0.1 U/mL PPase was used, free Mg2+ concentration increased slightly or remained approximately stable (depending on the initial parameters and the rate of the reaction); the reaction stopped when ATP was depleted. When little or no PPase was used, free Mg2+ decreased during the reaction and in some cases reached 0.0 mM. We show that a low initial free Mg2+ (< 0.1 mM) leads to a slow or inhibited reaction (e.g., when in combination with high NTPs and/or a low PPase concentration). In reactions where free Mg2+ was depleted to 0.0 mM, mRNA production stopped completely even though NTPs were still present. This confirms that the presence of free Mg2+ is as important as the availability of NTPs for the IVT reaction to proceed.

Interestingly, some reactions stopped or significantly slowed down even though both NTPs and free Mg2+ were still present in the reaction. We hypothesize that this may be due to higher mRNA production leading to higher PPi. This could result in the formation of more Mg2PPi, which has previously been reported to sequester DNA and thus remove it from the reaction [8]; however, we do not have sufficient evidence to support this. After incorporation of each NTP into the mRNA chain, a PPi and an H+ are released, with the former binding Mg2+ when PPase is not used. MgPPi complex can further bind Mg2+ and form Mg2PPi, which crystallizes and eventually sequesters DNA, leading to inhibition of transcription. When PPase is used, PPi is hydrolyzed to two phosphates and Mg2+ is released from the MgPPi complex. Part of the released Mg2+ is then buffered with phosphate, part is bound by mRNA, and part is returned to the pool of free Mg2+. Since a large excess of PPase is usually used in the IVT reaction (1–2 U/mL, up to 5 U/mL) [37], our analytical method for determination of free Mg2+ could be used to optimize the PPase concentration and thereby reduce the cost of IVT reaction. This could be achieved by titrating PPase and measuring free Mg2+ in the reaction: as long as the rate of increase in free Mg2+ does not change when the PPase concentration is lowered, one can assume that reducing PPase concentration has no effect on the course of the IVT reaction. Finally, we show that low concentrations of free Mg2+ in combination with Mg-binding species such as NTPs and PPi at the end of the reaction, can inhibit downstream enzymatic reactions dependent on Mg2+, e.g., DNase digestion of residual DNA template. Residual pDNA template is a process-related impurity, typically removed from mRNA product by digestion with DNase or by chromatographic purification [38]. A maximum of 10 ng of residual DNA is recommended per dose of vaccine or therapeutic mRNA [39]. We are the first to show that final IVT conditions influence the efficiency of pDNA digestion with DNase: high amounts of residual pDNA template were detected in IVT reactions with the lowest measured free Mg2+ (0.1 mM) after DNase treatment, despite using standard DNase buffer, which should provide sufficient Mg2+ and Ca2+ for digestion. Interestingly, no residual template was detected in DNase reactions with free Mg2+ of 0.2 mM or more (Fig. S12, IVT8 and IVT16). The observation can be explained by comparing the differences in residual NTPs after reaction termination – low residual NTPs chelate less divalent ions from DNase buffer, enabling DNase to function. High residual NTPs together with PPi chelate divalent ions from DNase buffer, inhibiting DNase activity and resulting in residual pDNA template after digestion. We show that the problem can be resolved by addition of higher amounts of divalent ions, either Mg2+ alone or together with Ca2+. Similarly to IVT reactions where < 0.1–0.2 mM free Mg2+ substantially reduced activity of T7 RNAP, 0.1–0.2 mM of free Mg2+ was consistently observed as a threshold for efficient DNase reaction.

Multiple studied examples of IVT reaction progression indirectly demonstrate that, although measurements in 20-fold diluted samples do not provide an exact thermodynamic value of free Mg2⁺ in the original high-concentration environment, the observed trends are reliable and highly informative. Similar trends are seen in DNase reactions, where insufficient free Mg2⁺ leads to incomplete pDNA digestion and residual pDNA. The main advantages of the fluorescence method are its accessibility, minimal sample consumption, simple sample preparation, high throughput, and rapid readout, enabling fast, near real-time at-line analysis with potential for on-line analysis and continuous monitoring. While capillary zone electrophoresis, the only other reported method for monitoring free Mg2+ in IVT, allows measurement and quantification in undiluted IVT samples, it lacks these practical advantages of the fluorescence-based assay. For future quantification of free Mg2⁺ directly in undiluted samples with fluorescent indicators, we anticipate the development of probes insensitive to variations in pH, viscosity, and ionic strength. Nevertheless, our methodology successfully predicted the progression of both IVT and DNase reactions by controlling free Mg2+. The same methodology could in the future be applied to other important Mg-dependent enzymatic reactions, including 5′ capping and polyadenylation of mRNA.

Conclusions

We have developed a high-throughput, fluorescence-based method for measuring free Mg2+ in IVT reaction. We show that this method can be used to monitor the release of free Mg2+ when IVT is performed as a batch reaction and that it can be implemented for rapid at-line analysis of IVT alongside the established NTP and mRNA measurement techniques. We experimentally demonstrate the requirement for sufficient free Mg2+ levels for effective mRNA production: a low Mg:NTP ratio in an IVT reaction results in slow reaction progression, which correlates with low free Mg2+. To our knowledge, we are the first to experimentally show the effect of Mg:NTP ratio on free Mg2+ and its impact on fed-batch IVT: we show that Mg2+ in the feed should be adjusted based on initial Mg:NTP ratio – high starting ratio can result in sufficiently high free Mg2+ levels such that no additional Mg2+ is required in the feed, while lower initial Mg:NTP ratio requires a higher concentration of Mg2+ in the feed. We show that the amount of required Mg2+ in the feed also depends on NTP concentration in the feed.

Furthermore, we are the first to experimentally demonstrate a concentration-dependent PPase effect on free Mg2+ levels. We show how PPase concentration affects the subsequent progression of the IVT reaction, in particular the difference between 0.1 and 1 U/mL, which impacts IVT reaction kinetics but not overall yield. Additionally, we demonstrate how final IVT conditions can affect free Mg2+ levels and subsequent digestion of the pDNA template with DNase.

Practical significance of our work is manifold. It is particularly valuable in the production of mRNA therapeutics in case of unexpectedly low yields or slow progression of the IVT reaction, e.g., if no/too little Mg2+ or PPase were added to the IVT reaction, or due to batch-to-batch variation in PPase activity: low initial Mg2+ concentrations can be detected by low initial free Mg2+ levels, while insufficient PPase activity can be detected by a decrease or insufficient increase in free Mg2+ during the reaction. We foresee the development of continuous low-level NTP feeding while maintaining free Mg2+ at optimal steady-state concentration, supported with our analytical approach, which will inform adding an amount of NTP that chelates free Mg2+ released during mRNA elongation. Such feeding would allow efficient reaction progression while preventing potential negative effects of excess free Mg2+. In addition to in vitro transcription, we show that free Mg2+ is critical for activity of DNase, with important implications for mRNA vaccine purity. Similarly, the method could be used for measuring the free Mg2+ concentration in other posttranscriptional reactions, such as polyadenylation or the 5′ capping reaction.

We show that monitoring free Mg2+ provides valuable insight into the progression of the IVT reaction and postulate the development of next-generation fluorescent indicators or the use of Mg2+ soft sensors as control tools for monitoring mRNA production, either integrated in bioreactors or off-line.
