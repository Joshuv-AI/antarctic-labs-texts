Transcriptomic analysis of Thai rice varieties at the reproductive stage uncovers potential genetic determinants of salt tolerance
Authors: Navarit Jitsamai, Nattana Thongsima, Rujana Suwandaeng, Supachitra Chadchawan, Isabelle M Henry, Luca Comai, Teerapong Buaboocha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil salinity represents a major environmental constraint limiting crop productivity and threatening global food security. Previous evaluation of Thai rice varieties under reproductive stage salinity stress revealed a substantial genetic variation in salt tolerance, providing valuable germplasm for identifying novel tolerance genes. This study aimed to characterize molecular mechanisms underlying salt tolerance differences among contrasting Thai rice varieties through comparative transcriptome analysis.ResultsFour local Thai rice varieties with distinct salt tolerance phenotypes were subjected to transcriptomic analysis during their reproductive stages. Physiological and yield-related parameter assessment under salt stress confirmed that ‘Daw Dawk Mai (DD)’, ‘Nahng Nuan (NN)’, and ‘Tah Bahn (TB)’ exhibited greater tolerance than ‘Ta Pow Lom (TP)’ under extreme salt stress conditions. RNA sequencing of flag leaves on days 1 and 3 following salt treatment revealed 5975, 2620, 1017, and 958 differentially expressed genes in ‘TP’, ‘TB’, ‘NN’ and ‘DD’, respectively, indicating salt-sensitive varieties exhibit more extensive transcriptional perturbation. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and pathway analysis demonstrated that all salt-tolerant varieties (DD, NN, and TB) shared uniquely enriched GO terms, including cold acclimation, protein folding and response to reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the biological process category; chitinase activity in the molecular function category; and cell wall and external encapsulating structure in the cellular component category. Comparative transcriptome analysis indicated three potential salt-tolerant genes, including receptor-like protein kinase (Os07g0534100), OsEULD2 (Os07g0683600), and hypothetical protein (Os11g0160400) that were significantly upregulated under salinity stress uniquely in all three salt-tolerant varieties and exhibited higher expression relative to the salt-sensitive variety (‘TP’) under salt stress conditions. Furthermore, cis-element analysis revealed that an indel within the OsEULD2 promoter may have affected its expression and salt tolerance.ConclusionsComparative transcriptome analysis successfully identified three salt tolerance candidate genes with potential applications in rice breeding programs. The discovery of conserved stress response pathways and variety-specific regulatory elements advances our understanding of reproductive-stage salt tolerance mechanisms in rice. These findings provide molecular tools for developing salt-tolerant cultivars through marker-assisted selection and contribute to global efforts addressing salinity challenges in rice production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07732-y.Keywords: Oryza sativa, Rice, RNA-seq, RNA sequencing, Salt-tolerant gene, Transcriptomic analysis

Background

Soil salinity is an abiotic stress factor that limits crop production and affects the global food security. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Land and Plant Nutrition Management Service reported that over 6% of the world’s total land area is threatened by saline soils. This issue adversely affects rice production, which is a staple food for 50% of the human population. Saline soil primarily causes osmotic and ionic stresses [1]. Osmotic stress resulting from external salts reduces water absorption in plants and disrupts cellular and physiological processes. Ionic stress, resulting from subsequent ion accumulation (Na+ and Cl-), stimulates excess production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative stress. These effects inhibit rice development, specifically during the seedling, reproductive stages, and seed production. A sustainable solution involves the development of salt-tolerant cultivars through rice breeding programs [2]. However, traditional plant breeding is time consuming and labor intensive. Salt tolerance is a quantitative trait that is governed by multiple genes that participate in numerous critical processes. Therefore, identifying salt-tolerant genes and understanding their underlying salt response mechanisms can offer precise insights for breeding, and potentially provide an alternative approach for effective genetic engineering to obtain salt-tolerant rice [3, 4]. An effective strategy for identifying salt-tolerant genes is high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), which can precisely detect and quantify all transcripts compared with microarray and cDNA sequencing [5]. Through comparative analysis, this approach reveals salt-responsive pathways and novel gene(s) involved in the regulation of salt stress responses in rice [6–9].

To identify the significant genes responsible for salt tolerance during the seedling stage, we previously assessed the transcriptomes of ‘Luang Pratahn’ rice, which is a local salt-tolerant Thai rice variety [10]. However, the transcriptomes of Thai rice varieties with contrasting salt tolerance abilities during the reproductive stage remain unexplored. Therefore, in this study, we assessed four local Thai rice varieties under saline conditions during the reproductive stage. The results demonstrated that ‘Daw Dawk Mai’, ‘Nahng Nuan’, and ‘Tah Bahn’ were highly resistant to salt stress when compared with ‘Ta Pow Lom’. A comparative analysis of the tolerant and susceptible transcriptome varieties identified common and specific salt-responsive genes. We observed that ten genes were differentially expressed under salt stress across all three salt-tolerant varieties. Among these, three genes were significantly upregulated and exhibited higher expression levels in the salt-tolerant varieties under salinity stress. These findings indicate that these three genes have potential for further characterization.

Methods

Plant materials and salt stress treatment

Rice seeds of the ‘Daw Dawk Mai (DD)’, ‘Nahng Nuan (NN)’, ‘Tah Bahn (TB)’, and ‘Ta Pow Lom (TP)’ varieties were obtained from the Pathum Thani Rice Research Center, Rice Department, Ministry of Agriculture. For salt stress treatment at the seedling stage, the plants were grown in a hydroponic solution. Salt stress treatment was then performed using different NaCl concentrations (0, 100, 150, and 200 mM) for 5 days. A standard evaluation system (SES) score was used to assess their salt tolerance abilities on the fifth day. For salt stress treatment at the reproductive stage, the seeds were soaked in water and individually grown on clay soil until the reproductive stage using a randomized complete block design with a minimum of three biological replicates. Each variety was subjected to control and salt stress treatments (500 mM NaCl for 5 days) at heading stage. The variable fluorescence/maximum fluorescence (Fv/Fm) ratios and soil-plant analysis development (SPAD) values were monitored during salt stress treatment using a Pocket PEA chlorophyll fluorometer (Hansatech Instruments, UK) and SPAD-502 Plus chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta, UK), respectively. Yield components were measured at the end of the experiment. Flag-leaf tissues from the control and salt stress treatments were harvested from three biological replicates for RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analysis on the first and third day after starting the treatment.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA from ‘DD’, ‘NN’, ‘TB’, and ‘TP’ varieties under control and salt stress conditions were isolated from flag leaf tissue using PureLink reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity and quality of total RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. Total RNA (500 ng) from each sample was used for library preparation using the KAPA Stranded mRNA-Seq Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Poly(A) RNA was extracted using magnetic oligo-dT beads and was fragmented by heating. Fragmented RNA was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis using random primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis to generate double-stranded cDNA (dscDNA), where the second strand was marked with deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP). The resulting dscDNA was subjected to bead-based cleanup and A-tailing. Unique dsDNA adapters were ligated to the library fragments, followed by bead-based cleanup. The adapter-ligated library was amplified through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and purified using a bead-based cleanup. Each library was quantified using qPCR and pooled with the other samples. The pooled library was single-end sequenced using Illumina NextSeq (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identification

Raw reads from each sample were trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic-0.36 [11] with default parameters. High-quality reads from each sample were mapped to the rice Os-Nipponbare-Reference-IRGSP-1.0 genome (release-57) [12] using Hisat2 [13] and counted using HTSeq-count [14]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each sample were identified using DESeq2 [15]. Transcripts with an adjusted P-value (Padj) ≤ 0.05 and log2fold change ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 were considered as significantly upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively. The identified DEGs were compared among varieties using Venn diagrams through a web-based software (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and pathway analysis

GO enrichment and pathway analyses of DEGs were performed using ShinyGO 0.77 [16]. Genes with detectable expression in all the tested samples were included in the background. The DEGs of each variety were compared to their respective backgrounds to identify enriched GO terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Fold enrichment represents the percentage of DEGs associated with a specific GO term or pathway divided by the corresponding percentage in the background. FDR was calculated based on the nominal P-value from the hypergeometric test. The GO terms across various categories or KEGG pathways with FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from flag leaves with TRI reagent® (Molecular Research Center, Inc.). cDNA was synthesized from 700 ng of total RNA using iScriptTM reverse transcription supermix (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and then used as template for qPCR using Luna® universal qPCR master mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Three technical replicates were performed for each sample. qPCR reaction (10 µl) contained 1X Luna universal qPCR master mix, 0.25 µM of forward and reverse primers (Additional file 12) and cDNA. qPCR reaction was performed at 95 °C for 60 s followed by 45 cycles (95 °C, 15 s; 60.8 °C, 30 s). Melt curve analysis (60–95 °C, 5 s; 0.5 °C increment) was performed at the end of the PCR cycles. EF-1α gene was used as internal control. Relative expression levels of target gene under normal and salt stress conditions were calculated using 2-ΔCt method [17]: relative expression = 2–(Ct target gene – Ct internal control).

Results

The local Thai rice varieties demonstrate contrasting salt tolerance ability

We previously assessed 104 Thai rice varieties at the reproductive stage under salinity stress. The stability index (SI), which is the ratio of a plant’s physiological parameter under salt stress to that under normal conditions, was used to assess salt tolerance [18]. Our analysis revealed that the Thai rice varieties exhibited a wide range of salt tolerance, from those comparable to the highly tolerant ‘Pokkali’ to those similar to the sensitive ‘IR29’. Here, we evaluated salt tolerance levels of four Thai rice varieties: ‘Daw Dawk Mai (DD)’, ‘Nahng Nuan (NN)’, ‘Tah Bahn (TB), and ‘Ta Pow Lom (TP)’ at both seedling and reproductive stages. At the seedling stage, a standard evaluation system (SES) score was used to assess the degree of salt tolerance after treatment with different concentrations of NaCl (Additional file 1). The SES ranged from 1 to 9 depending on the symptom severity, where 1 represented highly tolerant plants (normal growth) and 9 represented highly sensitive plants (almost all plants dead or dying). Compared to other varieties, ‘TP’ exhibited more severe symptoms after salt treatment with 100 mM and 150 mM NaCl for 5 days indicating its higher sensitivity to salinity at the seedling stage.

To investigate agronomic performance under severe salt stress, the four rice varieties were grown until the reproductive stage and subjected to salt treatment for 5 days. Fig. 1 illustrates their Fv/Fm ratios, SPAD values, and grain production, with additional yield-related traits shown in Additional file 2. We measured Fv/Fm, the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (PSII), to monitor photosynthetic performance during salt treatment. Under normal conditions, all varieties exhibited Fv/Fm values of approximately 0.8, whereas salt stress significantly reduced Fv/Fm specifically in ‘TP’.

The variable fluorescence/maximum fluorescence (Fv/Fm) ratios, and soil-plant analysis development (SPAD) values during salt stress; and yield following salt stress after recovery of the varieties: ‘Daw Dawk Mai (DD)’ (A), ‘Nahng Nuan (NN)’ (B), ‘Tah Bahn (TB)’ (C), and ‘Ta Pow Lom (TP)’ (D). For Fv/Fm ratios and SPAD values, data represent means ± SD (n ≥ 4)

SPAD values, which were proportional to total chlorophyll content, were also measured as indicators of plant health. Under normal conditions, all varieties had SPAD values of approximately 50. Following salt treatment, SPAD values of ‘DD’ and ‘NN’ were only slightly reduced, whereas those of ‘TB’ and ‘TP’ were reduced moderately and severely, respectively. Importantly, ‘DD’, ‘NN’, and ‘TB’ produced filled grains after recovery from extreme salt conditions, whereas ‘TP’ did not survive (Additional file 3). These results demonstrate that ‘DD’, ‘NN’, and ‘TB’ maintain superior photosynthesis, chlorophyll content, and reproductive capability under extreme salt stress compared to ‘TP’. Consequently, varieties with contrasting salt tolerance abilities serve as valuable resources for identifying salt-tolerant genes through comparative transcriptome analysis.

Principal component analysis of RNA-seq data reveals specific salt responses of a salt-sensitive variety

To determine the early transcriptional response, flag leaf transcriptomes of the four varieties were assessed using RNA sequencing on days 1 and 3 following salinity stress treatment during the reproductive stage. Sequencing libraries generated raw and high-quality reads ranging from approximately 9.5–26.0 M and 9.4–25.7 M, respectively (Additional file 4 and 5). After mapping the high-quality reads to the rice reference genome, four ‘TB’ libraries with low mapping rates were excluded from downstream analysis. The remaining libraries (‘DD’, ‘NN’, ‘TB’, and ‘TP’) demonstrated average read mapping percentage of 91.25%, 93.18%, 93.68%, and 92.93%, respectively, indicating high-quality data suitable for analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of normalized read counts revealed clear separation patterns (Fig. 2). Libraries from control and salt-stressed samples of each variety were distinctly separated along principal component 1 (PC1), specifically in ‘TB’ and ‘TP’ varieties. Under normal conditions, libraries from all varieties clustered together across both time points. However, under stress conditions, libraries showed greater dispersion, especially between days 1 and 3, with the notable exception of ‘TP’, which maintained closer clustering across time points.

Differential gene expression analysis reveals varying salt stress responses among rice varieties

Comparative transcriptome analysis identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by comparing salt-stressed versus control conditions at corresponding time points (Fig. 3A). On day 1 following salt treatment, ‘DD’ showed the fewest DEGs (133), while ‘TP’ exhibited the highest number (4510). The DEGs counts for ‘NN’ and ‘TB’ were intermediate (761 and 2,476, respectively), with ‘TB’ showing approximately half the number observed in ‘TP’. By day 3, ‘DD’ increased its DEG count, contrasting with decreases in ‘NN’ and ‘TB’, while ‘TP’ maintained a similar high number due to the substantial response already initiated on day 1. Across all varieties and time points, upregulated genes consistently outnumbered downed regulated genes, with 74 genes shared by two or more tolerant varieties (Fig. 3B). Additionally, 576 common DEGs were identified across all varieties, indicating shared fundamental salt response mechanisms.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) on the first- and third-day following salinity stress treatment in various varieties (A), and common and specific DEGs under salt stress in various varieties (B). White circles indicate DEGs shared among at least two salt-tolerant varieties

To identify variety-specific responses, we compared each salt-tolerant variety against the salt-sensitive ‘TP’ under both normal and salt stress conditions (Figs. 4A and B). Under normal conditions, DEG numbers between salt-tolerant varieties and ‘TP’ ranged from 350 to 550 genes across both time points, with 129 genes commonly differential across all comparisons (Fig. 4C). Under salt stress, however, dramatic differences emerged: ‘DD’ showed nearly 3000 DEGs relative to ‘TP’ on day 1, while ‘NN’ and ‘TB’ exhibited approximately ~ 1400 and ~ 500 in ‘NN’ and ‘TB’, respectively.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in salt-tolerant varieties under normal (A) or salt stress (B) condition and comparison of the overall DEGs in salt-tolerant varieties under normal (C) or salt stress (D) condition

Notably, ‘DD’ and ‘NN’ demonstrated fewer DEGs on day 3 compared to day 1, while ‘TB’ maintained consistent numbers across both time points. The direction of expression changes also shifted: on the day 1, downregulated genes outnumbered upregulated genes, whereas this pattern reversed by day 3. Under salt stress conditions, the three salt-tolerant varieties (‘DD’, ‘NN’, and ‘TB’) shared 227 DEGs, highlighting genes of particular importance for salt tolerant mechanisms (Fig. 4D).

GO enrichment and pathway analysis demonstrate common and uniquely enriched GO terms and pathways in distinct varieties

To understand the functional significance of genes exclusively responsive in salt-tolerant varieties, we performed GO classification and enrichment analysis of shared DEGs (Fig. 5). On both days 1 and 3, the most prevalent biological process terms related to abiotic stress including response to stress, stimulus, and chemical compounds. Molecular function categories were dominated by binding terms, (protein binding, small molecule binding, and carbohydrate derivative binding), while cellular component terms emphasized membrane and extracellular structures. Significantly enriched GO terms on day 1 confirmed stress-related responses, particularly cellular responses to oxidative and chemical stress (Fig. 5E). Detoxification processes emerged as key post-stress mechanisms, and cell wall/external structure components appeared crucial for salt stress response. However, no significantly enriched GO terms were identified for shared DEGs on day 3.

Comprehensive GO enrichment analyses of all DEGs in each variety under salinity stress was also performed (Figs. 6 and 7). Altogether, 147 biological process terms, 88 molecular function terms, 48 cellular component terms, and 33 pathways were enriched (Additional files 6 and 7). Twenty-one commonly enriched biological process terms involved abiotic stress responses including osmotic stress, oxidative stress, and salt stress responses. Common molecular function terms included hydrolase activities targeting glycosyl bonds and O-glycosyl compounds. Cellular component terms highlighted anchored membrane components and extracellular region.

Remarkably, all three salt-tolerant varieties shared unique GO terms enrichments: cold acclimation, protein folding, and response to ROS in the biological process category, chitinase activity in the molecular functions; and cell wall plus external encapsulating structure in the cellular component. However, no pathways were shared commonly enriched among salt-tolerant varieties, suggesting variety specific response to salt stress. Unique pathways included cutin/suberin/wax biosynthesis in ‘DD’, and multiple metabolic pathways (limonene and pinene degradation, lysine degradation, histidine metabolism, glutathione metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism) were unique pathways in ‘TB’.

In addition to identifying the uniquely enriched GO terms or pathways of salt-responsive genes in all salt-tolerant varieties, we assessed the enriched GO terms or pathways of DEGs when comparing each salt-tolerant variety with the salt-sensitive variety (‘TP’). We assessed the GO terms and pathways of the 388 DEGs in a minimum of two comparisons under non-stress conditions (Fig. 4C). Three GO terms, including polysaccharide binding, carbohydrate binding, and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) binding, in the molecular function category were enriched, whereas GO terms in other categories or pathways were not significantly enriched (Additional file 8). GO enrichment and pathway analysis of 1276 DEGs observed in a minimum of two comparisons under salt stress (Fig. 4D) demonstrated 20 terms in the biological process category, 15 in the molecular function category, six in the cellular component category, and five pathways (Additional file 9). The majority of the biological process terms were involved in abiotic stress responses, such as response to temperature stimulus, response to inorganic substance, and response to oxidative stress. The response to ROS was enriched, corresponding to the uniquely enriched GO terms of the salt-responsive genes in all three salt-tolerant varieties. Although cold acclimation and protein folding were not significantly enriched, response to cold remained significant. However, chitinase activity in the molecular function category, and cell wall and external encapsulating structure in the cellular component category were not significantly enriched. These results indicate that, compared to the salt-sensitive variety, none of the salt-tolerant varieties expressed genes specific to any biological processes and pathways under normal conditions. However, they highly regulate the genes responsible for stress response under salt stress.

Comparison of salt-responsive and tolerant variety-specific genes identified potential salt-tolerant genes

We applied three stringent criteria to identify potential salt tolerant genes (1) differential expression under salinity stress only in the salt-tolerant varieties; (2) differential expression between salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive varieties under salt stress conditions; and (3), upregulated and downregulated genes under salinity stress in salt-tolerant varieties with higher and lower expression levels, respectively, when compared with the salt-sensitive variety. Among DEGs responding to salt stress in all three tolerant varieties, ten genes met the first criterion (Fig. 3B; Table 1). These genes showed consistent expression across all salt-tolerant varieties following salt treatment. Nine out of 10 genes were significantly upregulated, under salt stress, while one was downregulated.

An indel within the OsEULD2 promoter is associated with salt tolerance

To investigate potential regulatory mechanism underlying differential gene expression, we examined single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertion/deletion (indels) polymorphisms in the three candidate genes. A significant indel was identified in the OsEULD2 promoter at −684 to −672 relative to the transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. 9A). No polymorphisms were detected in the promoters of the other two candidate genes. Compared to the ‘Nipponbare’ rice reference genome, only the salt-sensitive ‘TP’ variety exhibited a homozygous deletion (T/T) in the OsEULD2 promoter at this position, whereas the salt-tolerant ‘DD’ and ‘NN’ varieties had no deletion (TAAAGACAAGACA/TAAAGACAAGACA). The PlantPAN 4.0 analysis [19] predicted this indel region as a binding site of OsBP-5, a MYC transcription factor, suggesting potential regulatory significance. To validate the association between this indel and salt tolerance, we analyzed 43 Thai rice accessions from our previous study during the reproductive stage [18] based on their OsEULD2 promoter genotypes. Rice accessions without the deletion exhibited significantly higher stability indices (SI) for filled grains per plant under salt stress compared to those with a homozygous deletion (Fig. 9B).

In another set of experiments, we assessed the indel in 89 Thai rice varieties, which their salt tolerance ability was previously examined during the seedling stage [20]. We selected three varieties with the deletion (overlapping with those examined during the flowering stage) and six varieties without the deletion but having similar genetic backgrounds based on a previous PCA of their genome sequences. Consistently, the SI for key growth parameters−shoot fresh weight (SFW), shoot dry weight (SDW), root fresh weight (RFW), and root dry weight (RDW)−was significantly higher in rice accessions without the deletion (Fig. 10A). The presence of this deletion was visually confirmed by mapping previously sequenced Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) reads against the IRGSP-1.0 reference genome (Figs. 10B-D). Furthermore, an analysis of this indel across a wider set of 1,934 rice accessions from the Rice SNP-Seek database [21] revealed a broader trend; accessions lacking the deletion generally exhibit higher salt tolerance, as indicated by lower Salt Injury Scores (SIS), compared to those carrying the deletion (Fig. 10E). Therefore, these results strongly support the presence of this indel and its robust association with salt tolerance. Taken together, the OsEULD2 gene and the transcription factor binding site on its promoter are proposed as a key candidate gene and regulatory element for further molecular characterization of salt tolerance mechanism in indica rice.

Discussion

Soil salinity represents a major abiotic stress that significantly limits rice productivity worldwide. Salt tolerance is a complex, quantitative trait controlled by multiple genes, and developing salt-tolerant cultivars through precision breeding requires comprehensive knowledge of various salt-tolerance genes [18]. Building on these findings, we selected four local Thai varieties representing distinct tolerance phenotypes to identify novel salt-tolerant genes through comparative transcriptome analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) of RNA-seq libraries revealed that salt stress samples from the sensitive variety ‘TP’ clustered more closely across time points compared to the tolerant varieties, suggesting a more limited transcriptional response repertoire. This pattern corresponded with ‘TP’ exhibiting the highest number of differential expressed genes on day 1 following salt treatment, indicating an intense but potentially ineffective early stress response. For example, the salt-susceptible variety ‘Chao 2R’ showed 7,208 DEGs under salt stress, whereas the tolerant variety ‘RPY geng’ exhibited only 3,874 DEGs during the seedling stage [7]. Additionally, the ‘TB’ and ‘TP’ samples exhibited a high degree of similarity, especially under control conditions. This result is consistent with our previous analysis on the structure of the Thai rice population by PCA based on their genomic sequences, in which ‘TB’ and ‘TP’ clustered together, suggesting these two rice varieties share a similar genetic background. In addition, compared with the salt-tolerant varieties, the salt-sensitive ‘TP’ exhibited an early response in the expression of OsHKT and OsHAK genes including OsHKT1;1, OsHKT1;3, OsHKT1;5, OsHAK1, and OsHAK16, thus confirming its heightened sensitivity to salt stress and providing molecular validation of the physiological observations (Additional file 10). The highest number of overlapping DEGs between days 1 and 3 was observed in ‘TP’ indicating sustained activation of similar genes sets throughout the stress period. However, the salt tolerant varieties demonstrated significantly higher numbers of DEGs on day 3, representing an additional wave of gene expression potentially crucial for establishing upregulation of known salt-responsive genes.

Comparative analysis of significantly enriched GO terms among the four varieties to identify biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components specifically associated with salt-tolerant varieties. Three uniquely enriched biological process emerged: cold stress, like salinity stress, promotes ROS accumulation in chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes, and apoplasts, resulting in oxidative stress [22]. Additionally, freezing temperatures impede water absorption causing osmotic stress and dehydration similar to salt stress effects. All three salt-tolerant varieties shared seven genes associated with cold acclimation, all encoding dehydrins that confer freezing stress tolerance [23]. These dehydrin genes were significantly upregulated under salinity stress in both the salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive varieties, but their unique association with tolerance suggests quantitative rather than qualitative differences in expression. Beyond cold tolerance, dehydrins play crucial roles in multiple abiotic responses. In rice, overexpression of Rab16A (Os11g0454300) and OsDhn-Rab16D (Os11g0453900) enhanced salt and drought tolerance, respectively [24, 25], whereas the overexpression of OsDhn1 (Os02g0669100) enhanced both drought and salt stress tolerance [26]. These findings indicate that plants have evolved shared protective mechanisms to combat multiple environmental stresses through common molecular pathways.

Protein folding emerged as another critical process shared among salt-tolerant varieties. High salt concentrations disrupt protein structure by interfering with weak interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions, leading to misfolded proteins aggregation and cellular dysfunction. We identified seven genes with protein-folding GO terms in all three salt-tolerant varieties, four of which also responded to the ROS. These genes were significantly upregulated under salinity stress across all varieties, with the salt-sensitive “TP’ showing higher expression of OsHsp18.0 (Os01g0184100) compared to tolerant varieties ‘DD’ and ‘NN’. This pattern suggests that sensitive varieties accumulate more misfolded proteins under salt stress requiring greater chaperone activity for damage control. Previous studies confirmed that OsHsp18.0 overexpression in ‘Nipponbare’ enhanced tolerance to both salt and heat stress, while silencing increased sensitivity [27], highlighting the critical importance of protein quality control in stress tolerance. While many chaperone proteins have been identified, our previous GWAS could not detect them at the seedling stage. This indicates that protein folding appears to be more important during the reproductive stage than the seedling stage.

Chitinase activity was exclusively enriched in the salt tolerant varieties. OsChia2b (Os04g0493400), encoding a chitinase enzyme, was upregulated approximately 39-fold in all salt-tolerant varieties. While chitinases are traditionally associated with antifungal defense through chitin degradation, emerging evidence suggests broader roles in abiotic stress tolerance. Despite lacking a chitin-binding domain, OsChia2b efficiently hydrolyzes chitooligomers (GlcNAcn) and exhibits antifungal activity against Trichoderma reesei [28]. The gene is directly activated by the transcription factor WRKY114, a positive regulator of Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) [29], and shows higher expression in bacterial blight-resistant compared to susceptible rice lines [30]. The connection between chitinase and salt tolerance extends beyond rice. Overexpression of chitinase 2 (LcCHI2) gene from Leymus chinensis in tobacco and maize enhances fungal pathogen resistance and increases salt stress tolerance [31], indicating evolutionary conserved links between biotic and abiotic stress response pathways. This dual functionality may reflect shared signaling cascades or downstream protective mechanisms activated during various stress conditions.

Additionally, we compared the transcriptomes of the salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive varieties to identify genes whose expression was specific to each variety. GO enrichment and pathway analyses of these genes demonstrated that, under non-stress conditions, these genes were not involved in specific biological processes or pathways shared by salt-tolerant varieties. However, under salinity-stress conditions, these genes were associated with stress responses. Based on the enriched GO terms of salt-responsive genes observed in salt-tolerant varieties, responses to ROS and cold stress play crucial roles in salt tolerance. Based on the three criteria described earlier, we identified 10 salt-responsive genes specifically found in the salt-tolerant varieties. Although seven of the 10 genes (Additional file 11) did not meet all criteria, some of them demonstrated experimental evidence of salt responses. OsPsbR1 (Os07g0147500) was downregulated under salt stress in the salt-tolerant varieties; however, its expression level was higher than that in the salt-sensitive variety. This gene encodes a protein that is a crucial component of the PSII complex in the chloroplasts. OsPsbR1 is downregulated under drought, salt, and heat stress conditions in Nipponbare rice [32]. Additionally, the downregulation of OsPsbR1 has been observed in CSR27, a salt-tolerant variety, under salt stress [33].

Through stringent filtering criteria, we identified three genes specifically upregulated in salt salt-tolerant varieties with higher expression than that of the salt-sensitive variety under salt stress: receptor-like protein kinase (Os07g0534100), OsEULD2 (Os07g0683600), and hypothetical protein (Os11g0160400). These genes represent promising candidates for marker-assisted breeding and functional characterization. Kinases are signaling proteins central to the salt tolerance mechanism. They act as signal transducers that receive stress-related signals and relay them to downstream target proteins via phosphorylation. Our GWAS identified many kinases as key salt-tolerance genes at both the seedling [20] and reproductive [18] stages. This indicates that kinase-mediated signaling appears to be crucial from the seedling through the reproductive stage, especially in indica Thai rice. The receptor-like protein kinase (RLK) belongs to a large gene family that contains over 1000 genes in rice [34]. RLKs consist of extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular protein kinase domains [35]. RLKs function as receptors on the cell membrane to perceive extracellular signals or stimuli through their extracellular domains, resulting in dimerization followed by autophosphorylation of the intracellular protein kinase domains. Active RLKs activate downstream effectors through transphosphorylation [36]. One of the targets of RLKs is receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK), which lacks the extracellular domain. Zhou, Liu (37) observed that under salinity stress, RLK perceives extracellular signals and phosphorylates salt tolerance receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase 1 (STRK1). Subsequently, the activated STRK1 stimulates catalase C (CatC) activity through phosphorylation, resulting in the elimination of excess H2O2, thereby reducing oxidative damage and facilitating salt tolerance in rice [37]. Unlike other potential genes, Os07g0534100, which encodes RLK, was upregulated in the salt-tolerant varieties but was not expressed in the salt-sensitive variety. Functional domain analysis revealed that RLK contains kinase domain and domain of unknown function 26 (DUF26), also known as the salt stress response/antifungal domain. This DUF26 domain was also found in DUF26 kinase (Os07g0541000), which is one of the candidate salt-tolerant genes identified by GWAS at the seedling stage [20]. Therefore, RLK may serve as an upstream receptor that activates downstream targets to enhance salt tolerance.

We observed the upregulation of two OsEULs, OsEULD1b (Os03g0327600) and OsEULD2 (Os07g0683600), in all salt-tolerant varieties under salinity stress. Among the three-candidate salt-tolerant genes, we identified an indel on the promoter of the OsEULD2 gene. The region corresponding to the indel, which was exclusively present in the salt-tolerant varieties, was also predicted to be a binding site of the transcription factor OsBP-5. Therefore, OsBP-5 might promote the expression of OsEULD2 gene under salinity stress. The euonymus-related lectin (EUL) gene family, defined as carbohydrate-binding proteins in rice, consists of five functional members. Lambin, Demirel Asci (38) demonstrated that the expression of OsEULD1b and OsEULD2 was induced under drought and salt stress during the seedling stage. Subcellular localization by fusion with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) in tobacco leaves indicated that both OsEULD1b and OsEULD2 were localized in the nucleus and cytoplasm, whereas the other OsEULs were only observed in the nucleus [38]. Overexpression of OsEULD1b significantly enhanced drought and salt stress tolerance in indica rice [39]. These results indicated that both OsEULD1b and OsEULD2 have similar responses to abiotic stress and localization; therefore, OsEULD2 may also facilitate salt tolerance.

Similar to the other two genes, the expression levels of the third potential gene (Os11g0160400) were induced by salinity stress in all salt-tolerant varieties and were higher than those in the salt-sensitive variety under salt stress. This gene is considered a hypothetical protein gene encoding a proline-rich protein (PRP); however, its expression is supported by transcript and protein evidence from the rice annotation project database (RAP-DB). PRP contains a high amount of proline, which accounts for approximately 28% of the total amino acids. PRPs are one of the five classes of plant cell wall proteins and are characterized by the presence of proline-proline repeats [40]. Zhan, Wang (41) observed that the sickle (SIC) gene, which encodes a PRP, is essential for development and abiotic stress tolerance in Arabidopsis. The sic-1 mutant plants exhibited defects, such as serrated and sickle-like leaves, reduced height, delayed flowering, and abnormal inflorescences. Additionally, sic-1 mutant plants were sensitive to chilling and salt stresses [41], indicating that PRPs play crucial roles in both normal development and stress adaptation.

Among the three candidate genes, only OsEULD2 exhibited promoter polymorphisms, specifically a 12-bp indel at positions − 684 to −672 relative to the transcription start site. This indel was present exclusively in salt-tolerant varieties, with the salt-sensitive ‘TP’ showing a homozygous deletion. This region was predicted to be a binding site for OsBP-5, a MYC transcription factor, suggesting that the indel affects transcriptional regulation under salt stress conditions. Validation using 43 Thai rice accessions confirmed that varieties retaining the full promoter sequence exhibited significantly higher salt tolerance indices compared to those with deletions. This finding provides compelling evidence for the functional significance of the OsEULD2 promoter polymorphism and establishes a direct link between genetic variation and phenotypic differences in salt tolerance. The identification of this regulatory variant offers immediate applications for marker-assisted selection in rice breeding programs targeting salt tolerance improvement.

Our findings contribute to understanding salt tolerance mechanisms in rice from multiple perspectives. First, the identification of conserved stress response pathways (cold acclimation, protein folding, ROS response) suggests that breeding strategies targeting broad stress tolerance may be more effective than single-stress approaches. Second, the discovery of chitinase upregulation in salt-tolerant varieties highlights previously unrecognized connections between biotic and abiotic stress responses, potentially enabling simultaneous improvement of multiple stress tolerance traits. The three candidate genes identified provide valuable molecular markers for precision breeding programs. The OsEULD2 promoter indel, in particular, offers a simple PCR-based assay for screening germplasm collections and breeding populations. Furthermore, these genes represent targets for functional validation through overexpression and knockout studies, which could provide definitive proof of their roles in salt tolerance and facilitate the development of improved varieties through biotechnological approaches.

Conclusions

This comparative transcriptome analysis of four local Thai rice varieties with contrasting salt tolerance abilities during the reproductive stage revealed variety-specific and conserved molecular responses to salinity stress. Salt-tolerant varieties demonstrated unique enrichment of biological processes including cold acclimation, protein folding and ROS response, along with chitinase activity in molecular functions. These findings indicate that salt tolerance involves coordinated activation of multiple stress response pathways that extend beyond salt specific adaptations.

We identified three genes (Os07g0534100, Os07g0683600, and Os11g0160400) that were specifically upregulated in salt-tolerant varieties and showed higher expression than the sensitive variety under stress conditions. Among these, OsEULD2 contained a functionally significant 12-bp promoter indel that was associated with salt tolerance across 43 Thai rice accessions. This polymorphism represents an immediately applicable molecular marker for breeding programs.

The identification of shared stress response mechanisms and specific tolerance genes provides new insights into the molecular basis of salt tolerance in rice and offers practical tools for developing improved varieties through marker-assisted selection and biotechnological approaches. These findings contribute to global efforts addressing food security challenges posed by increasing soil salinity in rice-growing regions worldwide.
