Performance of Allplex™ MG & AziR Assay for Detecting Macrolide-Resistant Mycoplasma genitalium
Authors: Gilho Lee, Yumi Seo, Donghoon Lim, Seung-Ju Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Mycoplasma genitalium is a sexually transmitted pathogen for which azithromycin is recommended as the first-line therapy. However, the prevalence of macrolide resistance has increased globally, reaching peak levels in Asia-Pacific countries. Macrolide resistance-guided therapy (MRT) is cost-effective, with minimal collateral damage and a high cure rate. We aimed to validate the performance of the Allplex™ MG & AziR Assay (AziR), which was designed to detect genetic variations in MRT.MethodsUsing 279 M. genitalium-positive genitourinary samples, the performance of the AziR assay was compared with that of Sanger sequencing of the 23S rRNA gene of M. genitalium.ResultsThe AziR assay targeting positions 2058 and 2059 in the 23S rRNA gene revealed that 133 and 146 cases, respectively, carried the wild and mutant types of adenosine, while sequencing detected the wild and mutant types in 134 and 145 cases, respectively. The AziR assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 100% (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.968–1) and specificity of 99.254% (95% CI, 0.953–1) in distinguishing wild type from mutant variants. Of the 279 samples, only six showed genetic discrepancies between the two tests: one wild type by sequencing was found to have A2059G by the AziR assay, two A2059Gs were discordant as A2058Ts, one A2059G was discordant as A2058G, and two A2058Gs by sequencing were reported as A2059Gs by the AziR assay.ConclusionOur results show that the AziR assay can identify specific mutants in a single reaction and demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity for detecting macrolide resistant M. genitalium. Therefore, the AziR assay can be reliably used for MRT.

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma genitalium is a sexually transmitted pathogen associated with an increased risk of pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility in females, and urethritis in males.1 Despite its limited genetic content and simple structure, only two antimicrobials, azithromycin and moxifloxacin, are clinically effective against M. genitalium. 23

Owing to the challenges in culturing M. genitalium,4 assessing genetic variations at positions 2058 and 2059 of the 23S rRNA gene (Escherichia coli numbering) has become the gold standard for determining macrolide resistance (MR). For example, an A > G transition at these positions (e.g., A2058G or A2059G) definitively correlates with MR, indicating likely failure of first-line azithromycin treatment.34

The prevalence of macrolide resistant M. genitalium worldwide has shown a rapid increase in recent years, from 35.5% before 2010 to 51.4% in 2016–2017. Additionally, the prevalence of MR mutations is higher in the Western Pacific, Eastern Asian, and American regions than in the European region.2 The increasing global prevalence of MR has intensified the need for MR-guided treatment strategies. This involves the identification of M. genitalium and concurrent conduct resistance testing. Treatment is then adjusted based on the results; switching to either azithromycin or moxifloxacin depending on the resistance testing outcomes.56

Traditionally, Sanger sequencing is the gold standard for determining resistance-associated genetic variations. Unfortunately, this method involves several time-consuming critical steps, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for amplification, DNA electrophoresis to identify the amplified product, purification of PCR products, and DNA sequencing using the automatic capillary method in specialized laboratories. In addition, interpretation of sequencing data requires further expertise.78

In contrast, multiplex real-time PCR offers a more streamlined approach, allowing simultaneous amplification of multiple genetic targets in a single reaction. Furthermore, standardized multiplex real-time PCR can rapidly detect traces of multiple targets within 2–3 hours, enabling point-of-care testing. This efficiency is in contrast to the more laborious and time-intensive nature of Sanger sequencing.910

In this study, the Allplex™ MG & AziR Assay (AziR) developed by Seegene (Seoul, Korea) was used to identify genetic variations at positions 2058 and 2059 of the 23S rRNA gene in M. genitalium. The platform utilizes two fluorescence detection systems at 60°C and 72°C, using four different fluorophores for eight targets, including six mutants (A2058T, A2058C, A2058G, A2059T, A2059C, A2059G), internal control, and M. genitalium. This method detected all possible mutant types in a single tube with a shorter turnaround time than conventional sequencing.78910

To date, only a few studies have evaluated the performance of CE-IVD-marked AziR assays. This study compared the efficacy of the AziR assay with that of Sanger sequencing in determining MR in M. genitalium and assessed its applicability in macrolide resistance-guided therapy (RGT).

METHODS

Materials

This study utilized two sample sources: 1) 127 stored M. genitalium-positive DNA samples collected between 2018 and 2019 from primary care clinics in various areas in Korea,1112 and 2) 159 first-void urine samples obtained from male with M. genitalium infection who visited Dankook University Hospital between January 2020 and December 2023.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from the urine samples.11 The presence of M. genitalium was confirmed by in-house PCR using consensus primers (MgPa-1 and MgPa-3) for M. genitalium.13

Sanger sequencing

To sequence the 23S rRNA gene of M. genitalium, a 312 bp region containing the MR-determining site in domain V of the 23S rRNA gene was amplified first.11 The amplified product was then loaded onto a 2% agarose gel for visualization. Subsequently, the PCR product was sent to Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) for Sanger sequencing using a forward primer. Sequencing data were analyzed using BioEdit version 7.2 (Isis Pharmaceuticals, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Two sequences from the 127 stored DNA samples and 5 sequences from the 159 urine samples showed ambiguous results and were excluded from further analysis.

AziR assay

The performance of the AziR assay was evaluated using 279 DNA samples. A reaction mixture of 20 µL was prepared using 5 µL of M. AziR MOM, 5 µL of EM4, 5 µL of EM4 buffer, and 5 µL of sample DNA as a template and loaded into a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). After an initial denaturation at 95°C for 15 min, the samples were denatured at 95°C for 3 seconds, followed by the sequential release of two fluorescence signals at 60°C and 72°C for 10 seconds each. Amplification and fluorescence release were performed for 45 cycles. Data were analyzed using Seegene Viewer version 1.6 (Seegene Inc.).

Statistical analysis

Genetic data were dichotomized into wild type and mutant types. Pearson’s χ2 test was used to assess the clinical significance of genetic differences between the AziR assay and Sanger sequencing. Sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dankook University Hospital (IRB No. 2023-07-019), and informed consent was obtained from all the participants. Any clinical information that could be used to trace personal information was either deleted or anonymized.

RESULTS

The AziR assay detected M. genitalium and provided MR results for all 279 samples. Sanger sequencing, used as a reference method, revealed that positions 2058 and 2059 in the 23S rRNA gene contained an adenosine (A2058 and A2059, wild type) in 134 cases but were mutated (A2058G, A2059G, or A2058T) in 145 cases. Specifically, there were 115 cases of A2059G, 26 cases of A2058G, and four cases of A2058T mutations (Fig. 1).

To evaluate the performance of the AziR assay, the detection of 23S rRNA mutants was compared with the Sanger sequencing results. Only one sample reported as wild type by Sanger sequencing was identified as a mutant (A2059G) by the AziR assay. All other mutants identified using both methods were categorically concordant. The sensitivity and specificity of the AziR assay for detecting 23S rRNA mutants in M. genitalium were 100% (95% CI, 96.783–100%) and 99.254% (95% CI, 95.296–99.961%), respectively (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The rapid increase in the prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant M. genitalium 2 has prompted the Korean Association of Urogenital Tract Infection and Inflammation and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency to revise the Korean sexually transmitted infection guidelines.5 According to the updated guidelines, molecular MR testing is strongly recommended. Secondly, when the MR test is feasible, RGT is primarily recommended due to its cost-effectiveness and high cure rate.5614 When MR testing is not feasible, empirical therapy is recommended which consists of oral administration of 100 mg doxycycline or minocycline twice daily for 7 days, followed by azithromycin for 4 days.5 However, empirical therapy may quickly deplete the two antimicrobials, leading to high treatment failure and collateral damage.61214

According to national guidelines, empirical antimicrobial therapy must be designed based on the national prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and the genetic diversity of the 2058 and 2059 sites in the 23S rRNA gene of M. genitalium in Korea between 2014 and 2019. M. genitalium-positive urogenital samples were obtained from female (G1) and male (G2) patients visiting primary care clinics and referred male patients with antibiotic-resistant urethritis (G3) visiting a referral center. The mutation rates at the 2058 and 2059 sites in G1, G2, and G3 were 1.85, 5.81, and 48.53%, respectively.5 If MR testing is not feasible, the updated Korean guideline-based therapy may achieve a high cure rate in patients visiting primary care clinics. However, this recommended empirical regimen may not effectively eradicate infections in referral clinics with a high prevalence of MR.

To implement RGT, an accurate assay capable of detecting MR must be the initial step for appropriate selection of susceptible antimicrobials.5614

Although Sanger sequencing has been considered the gold standard for reading nucleotide sequences, it is time-consuming and not standardized.8 Indeed, the entire process of Sanger sequencing, from amplification to interpretation using the BioEdit program, takes 3–4 days and involves multiple critical steps to ensure successful results, typically performed by molecular biology specialists. In contrast, certified multiplex real-time PCR offers a more robust approach for detecting genetic variations than Sanger sequencing.9 The entire multiplex real-time PCR process for RGT in this study was conducted using a single CFX96 system and took 2–3 hours. The multiplex real-time PCR-based AziR was developed by Seegene, Korea, and has already obtained European certification for detecting MR in M. genitalium. As the AziR assay is relatively new, limited data are available to evaluate its performance in MR detection.151617 For instance, Le Roy et al.15 reported a clinical sensitivity of 74.5% for the AziR assay but Peris et al.18 reported a clinical sensitivity of 95%. These disparate results make it difficult to accurately and objectively evaluate the performance of the AziR assay. Therefore, we evaluated the effectiveness of the AziR assay in identifying MR M. genitalium and found that the AziR assay showed excellent performance for the detection of 23S rRNA mutations compared with Sanger sequencing with the overall sensitivity and specificity of the AziR assay for distinguishing wild and mutant types being 100% (95% CI, 96.783–100%) and 99.254% (95% CI, 95.296–99.961%), respectively.

The sensitivity of the AziR assay may vary across studies1518 owing to differences in the DNA concentrations of the samples used in each study. The mean Ct value for M. genitalium in our AziR assays was 25.19 (95% CI, 24.792–25.601). The excellent performance observed in this study may be due to high load of M. genitalium in each sample.19

The AziR assay uses multiplex real-time PCR to detect mutations using mutation-specific primers and probes.910 Therefore, it can simultaneously amplify all possible mutations associated with MR within a single reaction and report each mutant separately.

A separate report for each mutant, rather than a binary report (e.g., wild-type and mutant type), may have advantages in M. genitalium research. Because genetic transitions occur more easily than transversions, they are frequently observed during genome evolution. Therefore, an ‘A > G’ variant is much more common than an ‘A > T’ variant.20 Only two mutants, A2028G and A2059G in the 23S rRNA gene, have definitively shown high minimum inhibitory concentration levels for macrolides in M. genitalium culture systems, suggesting macrolide treatment failure.3 However, it is not possible to estimate the antimicrobial resistance effect on rare transversion types, such as A2058T. Finally, although treatment decisions are primarily based on the detected mutation, detailed information provided by the AziR assay may also be useful for surveillance or research on genetic mutations in M. genitalium. 16 Therefore, we consider that the variant information from the six variants provided by the AziR assay may be more informative than a simple binary result.

Our study had some limitations. First, most of the enrolled participants were male. As the personal information used to characterize sex was deleted or anonymized, we could not determine the exact sex distribution in this study. Although M. genitalium is dominant in male patients, prospective sex-balanced sampling is required for future studies. Second, the high performance observed in this study may not be guaranteed with lower infectious loads of M. genitalium. Theoretically, certain mutants that must be reported as “not detectable” may be reported as “wild type” in samples with extremely low concentrations of M. genitalium when using the AziR assay.

In conclusion, M. genitalium easily acquires MR, which can lead to the failure of macrolide therapy in patients. In this study, the AziR assay demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 99.254% specificity in distinguishing between wild-type and mutant MR-determining sites in M. genitalium compared with Sanger sequencing. In addition, the AziR assay uses a robust multiplex real-time PCR technology to detect all possible genetic variations at positions 2058 and 2059 of the 23S rRNA gene, facilitating the rapid initiation of appropriate treatment.
