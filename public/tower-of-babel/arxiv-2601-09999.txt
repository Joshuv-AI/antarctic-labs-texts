Abstract
This paper proposes corrected forecast combinations when the original combined forecast errors are serially dependent. Motivated by the classic 4 example, we show that combined forecast errors can be strongly autocorrelated and that a simple correction—adding a fraction of the previous combined error to the next-period combined forecast—can deliver sizable improvements in forecast accuracy, often exceeding the original gains from combining. We formalize the approach within the conditional-risk framework of 8 , in which the combined error decomposes into a predictable component (measurable at the forecast origin) and an innovation. We then link this correction to efficient estimation of combination weights under time-series dependence via GLS, allowing joint estimation of weights and an error-covariance structure. Using the U.S. Survey of Professional Forecasters for major macroeconomic indices across various subsamples (including pre/post-2000, GFC, and COVID), we find that a parsimonious correction of the mean forecast with a coefficient around 0.5 is a robust starting point and often yields material improvements in forecast accuracy. For optimal-weight forecasts, the correction substantially mitigates the forecast combination puzzle by turning poorly performing out-of-sample optimal-weight combinations into competitive forecasts. 14

This information can be used in different ways, but the simplest option is to correct the combined forecast with the error from the previous period, i.e., if we denote the actual observation

at time

(where we use the correction of 0.5 for simplicity)

This motivating example shows that, although the vast forecast combination literature operates in the back plane and has made substantial progress over the years, see
14
, a new dimension focusing on the properties of the combined forecast can be highly beneficial.

Furthermore, the optimal forecasting weights introduced by
4
exhibit poor empirical performance, leading to the forecast combination puzzle of
13
: “repeated finding that simple combination forecasts outperform sophisticated adaptive combination methods in empirical applications”. The optimal weight problem can be estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) in a linear regression, as noted by
9

Generalized least squares (GLS) estimation is more efficient than OLS in the presence of autocorrelated errors, a point recently reiterated by
12
. By focusing on the properties of the combined optimal forecast, we should be able to shed more light on the forecast combination puzzle.

Theory

Assume that we wish to forecast
y_{T+h}\in\mathbb{R}
using the vector of
h
-step-ahead forecasts

\bm{f}_{T+h|T}=(f_{1,T+h|T},f_{2,T+h|T},\dots,f_{n,T+h|T})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Following
7
, we assume that
y_{t}
may follow a complex process marked by measurement issues, structural changes, and nonstationarity induced by distribution changes.
Following
2
, we map all forecasts to the real number line and limit our analysis to linear combinations with weights
\bm{w}=(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

to produce the combined forecast
f_{c,T+h|T}=\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T+h|T}
.
We denote the vector of forecasting errors as
\bm{e}_{T+h|T}=y_{T+h}\bm{\iota}-\bm{f}_{T+h|T},

where
\bm{\iota}
is a vector of ones, and the error of the combined forecast as
e_{c,T+h|T}=y_{T+h}-f_{c,T+h|T}=\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{e}_{T+h|T}.

We first formalize the correction to the mean forecast used in the motivating example. We then embed it in the conditional framework of
8
and conclude with the generalization.

2.1
Correction of the mean forecast

Let us consider the equal weights
\bm{w}^{\text{EW}}=\bm{\iota}/n
(where
\bm{\iota}
is a
n\times 1
vector of ones), a generalization of the motivating example with two forecasts in the introduction. The combined forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}=\bm{\iota}^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T+h|T}/n
and the corresponding forecasting error
e^{\text{EW}}_{T+h|T}=y_{t+h}-f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}
can be corrected using a correction factor
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}
available at time
T
to produce the corrected combined forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}}=f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}+b_{T}^{\text{EW}}
. In our motivating example,
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=0.5\,e^{\text{EW}}_{T|T-1}
with
h=1
, a simple function of the previous forecasting error. However, a general correction
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}(I_{T},\bm{\gamma})
that involves information
I_{T}
, including additional variables, and parameters
\bm{\gamma}
is also possible and presented in Section
2.4
.
Here, we show a simple possibility of estimating one parameter.

When dealing with the simple correction
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=\gamma\,e^{\text{EW}}_{T|T-1}
, it seems that the correction factor of 0.5 is a reliable starting point in many applications, but with sufficient data available, one can use historical data to find the optimal correction. In each period
t
, one needs to solve the optimization

\widehat{\gamma}_{t}=\arg\min_{\gamma}\sum_{\tau=t_{0}}^{t-h}\left[y_{\tau+h|\tau}-(f^{\text{EW}}_{\tau+h|\tau}+\gamma\,e^{\text{EW}}_{\tau|\tau-h})\right]^{2}

(1)

and use the historically optimal (in the period
[t_{0},t]
) factor
\widehat{\gamma}_{t}
to correct forecast
f^{\text{CEW}}_{t+h|t}=f^{\text{EW}}_{t+h|t}+\widehat{\gamma}_{t}\,e^{\text{EW}}_{t|t-h}
at the future moment
t+h
.

The optimal correction, in terms of the mean squared error (MSE), is achieved by
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=\operatorname{E}(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}|I_{T})
as given by the following theorem.

Theorem 1

Given the additional available information
I_{T}
, the corrected forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}}
using
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=\operatorname{E}(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}|I_{T})
achieves lower conditional and unconditional MSE, i.e.,

\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{EW}}_{T+h|T})^{2}|I_{T}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}})^{2}|I_{T}]\text{ and }\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{EW}}_{T+h|T})^{2}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}})^{2}].

Since

y_{T+1}=f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}+e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}=f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}+b_{T}^{\text{EW}}+\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}},

where
\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}}=e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}-\operatorname{E}(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}|I_{T})
, then the corrected combined forecast error

e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}}=y_{T+1}-f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}}=e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}-b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}}

has zero conditional mean and achieves lower conditional MSE as

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{EW}}_{T+h|T})^{2}|I_{T}]=

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[(b_{T}^{\text{EW}}+\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}})^{2}|I_{T}]=(b_{T}^{\text{EW}})^{2}+\operatorname{E}[(\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}})^{2}|I_{T}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[(\xi_{T+h}^{\text{EW}})^{2}|I_{T}]=\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}})^{2}|I_{T}]

and by taking the unconditional expectation, we have the same inequality for the unconditional MSE,

\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{EW}}_{T+h|T})^{2}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CEW}})^{2}].

In applications, one needs to specify how to model
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}=\operatorname{E}(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}|I_{T})
and estimate it, which will affect the MSE. Therefore, keeping the model simple and minimizing additional estimation, as in our motivating example, should be a favorable correction strategy.

2.2
Correction of the (unconditionally) optimal forecast

We now turn our attention to the optimal weights. Assuming that the loss function
L(\cdot)
depends only on
e_{c,T+h|T}
, the classical
unconditionally
optimal weights solve the problem

\min_{\bm{w}}\operatorname{E}[L(e_{c,T+h|T})].

(2)

Under mean squared error loss,
L(e)=e^{2}
, only the first two conditional moments influence the optimal weights, and the optimization problem can be solved explicitly. We also assume that the forecasts are unbiased,
\operatorname{E}(\bm{e}_{T+h|T})=\bm{0}
; thus, we solve the optimization problem (
2
) subject to the restriction that the weights sum up to one,
\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{\iota}=1
, which produces the optimal weights of
4

\bm{w}^{\text{BG}}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\bm{\iota}}{\bm{\iota}^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\bm{\iota}},

(3)

where
\Sigma=\operatorname{E}[\bm{e}_{T+h|T}\bm{e}^{\prime}_{T+h|T}]
, and the optimal BG forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{BG}}=(\bm{w}^{\text{BG}})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T+h|T}
.
The existence of the first two moments of the errors,
\bm{e}_{T+h|T}
, is sufficient for the results derived in this section to be valid under our general data assumptions.

If a suitable correction
b_{T}^{\text{BG}}
is available, then the conditional and unconditional inequalities for the reduction in error will hold for the corrected BG forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CBG}}=f_{T+h|T}^{\text{BG}}+b_{T}^{\text{BG}}=(\bm{w}^{\text{BG}})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T+h|T}+b_{T}^{\text{BG}}
, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2

Given the additional available information
I_{T}
, the corrected forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CBG}}
using
b_{T}^{\text{BG}}=\operatorname{E}(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{BG}}|I_{T})
achieves lower conditional and unconditional MSE, i.e.,

\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{BG}}_{T+h|T})^{2}|I_{T}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CBG}})^{2}|I_{T}]\text{ and }\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{BG}}_{T+h|T})^{2}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{CBG}})^{2}].

The same as before.

In fact, any other combination (not necessarily optimal) or even the original individual forecasts can be corrected in a similar manner to improve performance. All previous corrections were two-step procedures, in which the original forecasts were combined in the first step and corrected in the second step.

The correction term, such as
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}
or
b_{T}^{\text{BG}}
, will generally depend on a vector of unknown parameters
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

that need to be estimated or selected. From now on, we explicitly denote this dependence as
b_{T}^{\text{EW}}(\bm{\gamma})
or
b_{T}^{\text{BG}}(\bm{\gamma})
. In our motivating example in the introduction, this is a scalar first-order autoregressive parameter, and its value is simply set to 0.5. However, if
\bm{\gamma}
is estimated, then the two-step procedure is the simplest option, i.e.,
given the weights,
\bm{w}^{\text{EW}}
,
\bm{w}^{\text{BG}}
(or any other chosen weights) and their corresponding combinations
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{EW}}
,
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{BG}}
, we can use the corresponding combined forecasting errors to estimate
\bm{\gamma}
and correct the combined forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{CZZ}}=f_{T+h|T}^{\text{ZZ}}+b_{T}^{\text{ZZ}}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{ZZ}})
, where ZZ stands for EW, BG or any other combination that one decides to use. This procedure may be effective in many situations due to its simplicity.

Conditional Framework of
8

One expects benefits from jointly estimating
\bm{w}
and
\bm{\gamma}
, at least in theory, so
we now turn our attention to a one-step procedure in which weights and corrections are determined simultaneously.
Given
I_{T}
, the

conditionally
optimal combination weights of
8
solve the problem

\min_{\bm{w}}\operatorname{E}[L(e_{c,T+h|T})|I_{T}].

(4)

For the conditional expectation in the optimization (
4
) to be well defined, one must specify how the conditional information is used.
While
8
used
I_{T}
, the information set available at time
T
, to extract the conditional bias of individual models producing the conditionally optimal weights, we will focus on the combined error.

We assume that the combined forecast error,
e_{c,T+h|T}
, can be decomposed into two parts,
e_{c,T+h|T}=b_{T}+\xi_{T+h}
, where
b_{T}=\operatorname{E}(e_{c,T+h|T}|I_{T})
and
\operatorname{E}(\xi_{T+h}|I_{T})=0
. A simple example where this decomposition arises naturally is when forecasting errors exhibit autocorrelation, as in our motivating example in the introduction.
In this case, the information set
I_{T}
consists of previous forecasting errors, i.e.,
I_{T}=\{e_{c,T|T-1}\}
, and the conditional bias follows a first-order autoregressive model
e_{c,T+1|T}=\gamma e_{c,T|T-1}+\xi_{T+1}
(for
h=1
), with
b_{T}=\gamma e_{c,T|T-1}
and the remainder
\xi_{T+1}=e_{c,T+1|T}-\gamma e_{c,T|T-1}
.

We now approach the optimal weight problem by noting that, as
9
observed, the unconditional optimal weights can be estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) in the linear regression

y_{t+h}=w_{1}f_{1,t+h|t}+\dots+w_{n}f_{n,t+h|t}+e_{c,t+h|t},\;t=1,\dots,T-h

(5)

subject to the restriction
w_{1}+\dots+w_{n}=1
. The implicit assumption is that
e_{c,t+h|t}
are independent and identically distributed (
iid
). If the errors are correlated, i.e., the
iid
assumption does not hold, then the OLS estimator,
\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{OLS}}
(and its corresponding forecast
f_{T+h|T}^{\text{OLS}}
), remains unbiased and consistent but is not necessarily efficient. We can therefore estimate the corrected forecast combination using

y_{t+h}=w_{1}f_{1,t+h|t}+\dots+w_{n}f_{n,t+h|t}+\gamma\left[y_{t}-(w_{1}f_{1,t|t-h}+\dots+w_{n}f_{n,t|t-h})\right]+\xi_{t+h},\;

which is equivalent to

y_{t+h}-\gamma y_{t}=w_{1}(f_{1,t+h|t}-\gamma f_{1,t|t-h})+\dots+w_{n}(f_{n,t+h|t}-\gamma f_{n,t|t-h})+\xi_{t+h}.

12
refer to this transformation as the Hildreth–Lu method and also present other alternatives that may be more attractive. We will focus on the simple version and
the corresponding estimators
(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}},\widehat{\gamma}^{\text{GLS}})
, which can be used to combine and correct at the same time

f_{T+h|T}^{\text{GLS}}=(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T+h|T}+\widehat{\gamma}^{\text{GLS}}(y_{T}-(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})^{\prime}\bm{f}_{T|T-h}).

(6)

When considering the forecasting errors,
e^{\text{OLS}}_{T+h|T}=y_{T}-f_{T+h|T}^{\text{OLS}}
and
e_{T+h|T}^{\text{GLS}}=y_{T}-f_{T+h|T}^{\text{GLS}}
, one naturally expects the GLS to perform better. This result is formalized by the following theorem.

Theorem 3

The conditionally optimal forecast achieves lower conditional and unconditional MSE, i.e.,

\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{OLS}}_{T+h|T})^{2}|I_{T}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{GLS}})^{2}|I_{T}]\text{ and }\operatorname{E}[(e^{\text{OLS}}_{T+h|T})^{2}]\geq\operatorname{E}[(e_{T+h|T}^{\text{GLS}})^{2}].

See Theorem 1 of
8
.

2.4
Generalization

We now extend our approach to allow general time-series dependence in the forecasting error term.
Let
\bm{y}=(y_{1+h},\dots,y_{T})^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{F}=(\bm{f}_{1+h|1},\dots,\bm{f}_{T|T-h})^{\prime}
,
\bm{f}_{t+h|t}=(f_{1,t+h|t},\dots,f_{n,t+h|t})^{\prime}
, and
\bm{e}_{c}=(e_{c,1+h|1},\dots,e_{c,T|T-h})^{\prime}
. In matrix notation, we write the model (
5
) as
\bm{y}=\mathbf{F}\bm{w}+\bm{e}_{c}
. The time-series dependence in the forecasting errors can be modeled with a general covariance matrix
\operatorname{E}[\bm{e}_{c}\bm{e}_{c}^{\prime}]=\Omega(\bm{\gamma})
. Suppose that
\bm{e}_{c}
follows a stationary ARMA
(p,q)
process and that
\Omega(\bm{\gamma})
is known. Then we solve the following optimization problem

\min_{\bm{w}}(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})^{\prime}\Omega^{-1}(\bm{\gamma})(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})

(7)

subject to the restriction
\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{\iota}=1
, which produces the optimal weights

\bm{w}^{\text{opt}}=\frac{\widetilde{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\iota}}{\bm{\iota}^{\prime}\widetilde{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\iota}},

(8)

where
\widetilde{\Sigma}=\widetilde{\bm{e}}^{\prime}\,\widetilde{\bm{e}}
,
\widetilde{\bm{e}}=\Omega^{-1/2}(\bm{\gamma})\bm{e}
, and
\bm{e}=(\bm{y}\bm{\iota}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F})
.

In practice,
\Omega(\bm{\gamma})
is unknown, but the efficient generalized least squares
(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}^{\text{GLS}})
can be estimated simultaneously using the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method proposed by
10
or a two-step procedure proposed by
15
. Let
R(\bm{w})=(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})^{\prime}\Omega^{-1}(\bm{\gamma})(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})
and
R_{n}(\bm{w})=(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})^{\prime}\Omega^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})(\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w})
denote the theoretical and empirical risks, respectively. They differ only in the use of the true parameter
\gamma
versus its estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
. The following theorem shows that the theoretical minimum risk
R(\bm{w}^{\text{opt}})
and the empirical GLS risk
R_{n}(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})
are approximately equal as long as the covariance matrix estimate
\Omega^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})
is close to the true value
\Omega(\bm{\gamma})
.

Theorem 4

Let
a_{T}=\|\bm{e}^{\prime}(\Omega^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})-\Omega^{-1}(\bm{\gamma}))\bm{e}\|_{\infty}
. Suppose that
\|\bm{w}\|_{1}\leq{c}
, then, we have

\displaystyle|R(\bm{w}^{\text{opt}})-R_{n}(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})|

\displaystyle\leq a_{T}c^{2},

\displaystyle|R(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})-R_{n}(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})|

\displaystyle\leq a_{T}c^{2},

\displaystyle|R(\widehat{\bm{w}}^{\text{GLS}})-R(\bm{w}^{\text{opt}})|

\displaystyle\leq 2a_{T}c^{2}.

Under the restriction
\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{\iota}=1
, we have
\bm{e}_{c}=\bm{y}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w}=\bm{y}\bm{\iota}^{\prime}\bm{w}-\mathbf{F}\bm{w}=(\bm{y}\bm{\iota}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F})\bm{w}=\bm{e}\bm{w}
. Therefore, we can rewrite the theoretical and empirical risks as
R(\bm{w})=\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{e}^{\prime}\Omega^{-1}(\bm{\gamma})\bm{e}\bm{w}
and
R_{n}(\bm{w})=\bm{w}^{\prime}\bm{e}^{\prime}\Omega^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})\bm{e}\bm{w}
, respectively. Following a similar argument to the proof of Theorem 1 of
6
, we can show the results.

While our theory allows for general correction, parsimonious models often deliver good forecasts in practice. Therefore, the practical advice is to use simple corrections, as in our motivating example in the introduction, before adding complexity and estimation variability that could harm forecasting performance.

4
Conclusion

This paper shows that an important and largely overlooked source of forecast improvement lies not in refining combination weights, but in correcting combined forecasts when their errors are serially dependent. Building on the classic
4
example, we demonstrate that combined forecast errors often exhibit substantial autocorrelation and that exploiting this predictability can deliver gains that exceed those achieved by forecast combination itself. We formalize this idea within the conditional-risk framework of
8
and link forecast correction to efficient estimation of combination weights under time-series dependence using GLS. Empirical evidence from the U.S. Survey of Professional Forecasters across multiple variables and subsamples strongly supports the effectiveness of this approach.

A central empirical finding is the robustness of a simple, easily implementable correction: adding a fraction of the previous combined forecast error to the next-period combined forecast. Across most variables and subsamples—excluding periods dominated by extreme structural disruptions such as COVID—a correction factor near 0.5 consistently yields sizable improvements in forecast accuracy. The gains from this parsimonious adjustment are comparable to those from estimating historically optimal correction factors, while avoiding additional estimation uncertainty. As a result, the corrected mean forecast is a practical and robust default choice for applied forecasting.

The correction perspective also offers new insight into the forecast combination puzzle. Although optimal-weight combinations estimated via OLS often perform poorly out of sample, correcting these forecasts substantially improves their accuracy and frequently restores their theoretical advantage over simple averages. Jointly estimating weights and the correction factor via GLS further improves efficiency and eliminates residual autocorrelation in forecast errors, although in our application, the corrected mean forecast remains highly competitive.

These findings point to a promising and largely unexplored direction for future research. We encourage researchers to give explicit attention to the time-series properties of combined forecast errors and to treat forecast correction as a complementary dimension to forecast combination. Rather than replacing existing techniques, correction can be naturally integrated into the multitude of established combination methods, as well as newer approaches, including machine-learning-based forecast ensembles. Incorporating simple correction mechanisms into both classical and modern forecasting frameworks has the potential to deliver substantial accuracy gains at minimal additional cost, and we view this as a fertile area for further theoretical and empirical work.
