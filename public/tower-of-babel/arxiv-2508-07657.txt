MoRoCo: Multi-operator-robot Coordination, Interaction and Exploration under Restricted Communication
Zhuoli Tian, Yuyang Zhang, Jinsheng Wei and Meng Guo

Abstract
Fleets of autonomous robots are increasingly deployed alongside multiple human operators to explore unknown environments, identify salient features, and perform complex tasks in scenarios such as subterranean exploration, reconnaissance, and search-and-rescue missions. In these contexts, communication is often severely limited to short-range exchanges via ad-hoc networks, posing challenges to coordination. While recent studies have addressed multi-robot exploration under communication constraints, they largely overlook the essential role of human operators and their real-time interaction with robotic teams. Operators may demand timely updates on the exploration progress and robot status, reprioritize or cancel tasks dynamically, or request live video feeds and control access. Conversely, robots may seek human confirmation for anomalous events or require help recovering from motion or planning failures. To enable such bilateral, context-aware interactions under restricted communication, this work proposes MoRoCo, a unified framework for online coordination and exploration in multi-operator, multi-robot systems. MoRoCo enables the team to adaptively switch among three coordination modes: spread mode for parallelized exploration with intermittent data sharing, migrate mode for coordinated relocation, and chain mode for maintaining high-bandwidth connectivity through multi-hop links. These transitions are managed through distributed algorithms via only local communication. Extensive large-scale human-in-the-loop simulations and hardware experiments validate the necessity of incorporating human-robot interactions and demonstrate that MoRoCo enables efficient, reliable coordination under limited communication, marking a significant step toward robust human-in-the-loop multi-robot autonomy in challenging environments.

Introduction

Autonomous robots such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
and ground vehicles (UGVs) are becoming common companions
of human operators in various scenarios,
especially to explore unknown and hazardous sites
before entry,
or search and rescue after earthquakes,
see
[
Couceiro(2017)
,
Petráček et al.(2021)Petráček, Krátkỳ,
Petrlík, Báča, Kratochvíl and Saska
,
Klaesson et al.(2020)Klaesson, Nilsson, Vaquero, Tepsuporn, Ames and
Murray
]
.
The robots become extended eyes and arms of the operators
by fusing their perception results to the operators
and performing actions as instructed by the operators.
Existing strategies for collaborative
exploration in
[
Hussein et al.(2014)Hussein, Adel, Bakr, Shehata and
Khamis
,
Colares and Chaimowicz(2016)
,
Zhou et al.(2023)Zhou, Xu and Shen
,
Patil et al.(2023)Patil, Zheng, Gupta, Song, Sriram and
Sycara
]

often assume a perfect all-to-all communication
among the robots.
In other words, any local observation by one robot
is immediately available to other robots,
which is equivalent to one robot with multiple moving sensors.
This is however impractical in many aforementioned scenes
where the communication facilities are unavailable or severely degraded.
In such cases, the robot-robot and robot-operator communication
is often limited to ad-hoc networks in close proximity.
This imposes great challenges for the operators
to coordinate, supervise and interact with the robotic fleet,
as communications would not be feasible unless actively planned.

Furthermore, the role of the human operator and
the online interaction with the fleet during exploration
are less studied and mostly replaced by a static base station for visualization.
However, under restricted communication, the operator might be
completely
unaware
of the progress and results of the ongoing exploration
if none of the robots actively return to the operator
to relay such information.
Consequently, the operator can not supervise the exploration task in real-time,
e.g., checking the system status such as battery and liveness;
and dynamically adjusting the priorities of the areas to be explored.
In addition, the operator often needs direct access to
the video stream and control of a particular robot, e.g.,
to inspect certain regions close-up for important semantic features
that are otherwise overlooked during exploration;
or assist a robot through tough terrain via manual control.
In another direction, the robots often need confirmation from the operator
regarding actions or detected features, or immediate assistance.
In these cases, a reliable high-bandwidth information flow is often required
from the operator to the robot even during motion,
which is particularly challenging in unknown environments
without global communication.

To overcome these challenges,
this work proposes a general algorithmic framework called MoRoCo,
for the coordination, supervision and interaction of
multi-operator multi-robot systems
that operate in unknown environments under restricted communication.
Bilateral and multi-modal interactions during exploration are explicitly modeled:

four types
of requests from the operators to the robots,
including timely feedback, prioritized regions to explore,
dynamic movement, and direct access to a chosen robot;

two types
of requests from the robots to the operators,
including confirmation before and after action, assistance
amid failures;
and
one type
of request among the operators to bound
the time interval for information exchange.
To facilitate the delivery and responses of these requests
via only local communication,
a distributed and hierarchical intermittent communication protocol
is proposed within multiple teams of one operator and multiple robots,
and between the teams, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Explicit upper bounds on the maximum information latency within each team
and among the teams are imposed at all time.
Based on this communication protocol, a distributed
information exchange and coordination strategy is proposed to
co-optimize the exploration progress and timely response to these requests.
Particularly,
three different operation modes are introduced for the teams:
(I) the “
spread mode
”,
where the pairwise intermittent communication events
(including time, location and content of the messages)
and exploration tasks are planned simultaneously,
to increase the exploration efficiency and fulfill
the online requests, subject to
close-range communication and tight latency constraints;
(II) the “
migrate mode
”,
where the operator moves along with the sub-team
for fast migration to a distant region,
while maintaining connectivity;
(III) the “
chain mode
”,
where the communication topology is temporarily decomposed
into connected sub-networks.
An assignment algorithm is proposed to select some relay robots
and form a reliable communication chain
from the operator to the designated robot, and the other
robots continue exploration.
It is shown that a high-bandwidth communication is maintained
when fulfilling the requests,
after which the hierarchical communication topology is restored.
Extensive human-in-the-loop simulations are conducted for
more than
10
robots and
3
operators,
within typical office scenes and subterranean caves of varying scales.
Moreover, hardware experiments are performed for a fleet of
3
UGVs
and
1
mobile manipulator, along with
2
operators
in indoor environments.

Main contribution of this work is threefold:
(I) the novel problem formulation of
multi-operator multi-robot systems
that operate in unknown environments under restricted communication.
Seven types of explicit bilateral and multi-modal requests are addressed among
the robots and operators.
To the best of our knowledge, this problem
has not been addressed
in related work;
(II) the proposed distributed strategy for coordination,
communication and interaction
that can co-optimize the collaborative exploration task
and the fulfillment of the online contingent requests;
(III) the proposed three operation modes as the
“spread, migrate and chain modes”,
and the transition phases among these modes.
They are universal and can be potentially applied to other applications,
where the inter-robot communication is severely
limited to local exchange.

2
Related Work

2.1
Collaborative Exploration

The problem of collaborative exploration has a long history in robotics.
The frontier-based method from
[
Yamauchi(1997)
]

introduces an intuitive yet powerful metric for guiding the exploration.
Originally proposed for a single robot, it was later extended to multi-robot teams
by assigning these frontiers to different robots for concurrent exploration in different ways,
e.g., greedily to the nearest robot by distance in
[
Yamauchi(1999)
]
,
centralized assignment based on Voronoi partitioning in
[
Bi et al.(2023)Bi, Zhang, Wen, Pan, Zhang, Wang and
Yuan
]
,
distributed and sequential auction in
[
Hussein et al.(2014)Hussein, Adel, Bakr, Shehata and
Khamis
]
,
or the multi-vehicle routing for the optimal sequences in
[
Zhou et al.(2023)Zhou, Xu and Shen
]
,
or based on the expected information gain in
[
Burgard et al.(2005)Burgard, Moors, Stachniss and
Schneider
,
Fang et al.(2019)Fang, Ding and Wang
,
Batinovic et al.(2021)Batinovic, Petrovic, Ivanovic, Petric and
Bogdan
,
Baek et al.(2025)Baek, Moon, Kim, Cao, Ho, Scherer
et al.
,
Colares and Chaimowicz(2016)
,
Patil et al.(2023)Patil, Zheng, Gupta, Song, Sriram and
Sycara
]
.
More recent learning-based methods such as
[
Calzolari et al.(2024)Calzolari, Sumathy, Kanellakis and
Nikolakopoulos
,
Sygkounas et al.(2022)Sygkounas, Tsipianitis, Nikolakopoulos and
Bechlioulis
,
Hu et al.(2020)Hu, Niu, Carrasco, Lennox and Arvin
,
Smith and Hollinger(2018)
]

demonstrate further advantages by leveraging learned heuristics for prioritizing different frontiers or even predicting the unexplored map directly.
Inter-robot communication is crucial for collaborative exploration,
enabling coordinated planning and avoiding redundant exploration.
To achieve this, robots must merge their local maps into a global map, requiring a shared coordinate system.
If the relative coordinates are known, the map merge can be done rather efficiently and accurately as in
[
Hörner(2016)
]
.
Otherwise, more advanced methods like feature matching or geometric optimization can help, see
[
Yu et al.(2020)Yu, Fu, Gostar and Hu
]
for a recent review.
The work in
[
Asgharivaskasi et al.(2025)Asgharivaskasi, Girke and
Atanasov
]
proposes ROAM, a decentralized optimization scheme to ensure consistency and consensus among the local maps.
Additionally, the SlideSLAM framework from
[
Liu et al.(2024)Liu, Lei, Prabhu, Tao, Spasojevic, Chaudhari,
Atanasov and Kumar
]
enables a multi-robot map fusion scheme via semantic labelling and matching to improve simultaneously map merging and inter-robot localization.
However, the aforementioned work all assume that all robots can
exchange information via wireless communication
perfectly and instantly at all time.
Thus, all robots always have access to
the same global map

and further the same set of frontiers.
This is often impractical or infeasible without pre-installed infrastructures for communication.
As discussed in
[
Esposito and Dunbar(2006)
]
,
due to obstructions of obstacles in subterranean environments such as
[
Ohradzansky et al.(2022)Ohradzansky, Rush, Riley, Mills, Ahmad,
McGuire, Biggie, Harlow, Miles, Frew et al.
,
Rouček et al.(2020)Rouček, Pecka, Čížek,
Petříček, Bayer, Šalanskỳ, Heřt,
Petrlík, Báča, Spurnỳ et al.
,
Dahlquist et al.(2025)Dahlquist, Nordström, Stathoulopoulos,
Lindqvist, Saradagi and Nikolakopoulos
]

or indoor structures such as
[
Shen et al.(2012)Shen, Michael and Kumar
,
Al Khawaldah and Nüchter(2015)
]
,
the inter-robot communication is severely limited both in the communication range
and in the bandwidth of information exchange.
Consequently, these approaches are not suitable anymore
and can not be applied directly to communication-constrained and unknown scenarios.

2.2
Limited Communication

To overcome the challenge of limited communication,
many recent works focus on the joint planning of inter-robot communication and autonomous exploration.
The straightforward solution would be to establish a communication network covering the entire environment by pre-deploying communication devices,
such as
[
Bi et al.(2023)Bi, Zhang, Wen, Pan, Zhang, Wang and
Yuan
,
Yu et al.(2021)Yu, Tong, Xu, Xu, Dong, Yang and Wang
]
.
However, this approach is limited to known environments or small-scale laboratory settings and cannot be applied to large-scale unknown environments.
A more practical approach is to design exploration strategies that explicitly take into account the communication constraints among the robots.
The survey in
[
Banfi et al.(2015)Banfi, Li, Basilico and
Amigoni
]
provides a quantitative comparison of different communication-constrained exploration strategies,
including
[
Rooker and Birk(2007)
]
and
[
Pei et al.(2013)Pei, Mutka and Xi
]
.
The work in
[
Klaesson et al.(2020)Klaesson, Nilsson, Vaquero, Tepsuporn, Ames and
Murray
]
reports the systematic techniques developed for the DARPA subterranean challenge,
where a mixed integer program is formulated over the mobility-communication network.
Furthermore, the work by
[
Asgharivaskasi et al.(2025)Asgharivaskasi, Girke and
Atanasov
]
introduces a Riemannian optimization approach for efficient exploration and inter-robot communication.
A scalable strategy based on reinforcement learning is proposed in
[
Wang et al.(2024)Wang, Xie and Atanasov
]
with one-hop communication to reduce the planning complexity.
The work in
[
Asgharivaskasi and Atanasov(2024)
]
presents a distributed optimization method
for the collaborative mapping of semantic octrees in order to limit the communication overhead while ensuring consistency.

On the other hand, different intermittent communication strategies have been proposed under various performance metrics , e.g.,
the “Zonal and Snearkernet” relay algorithm in
[
Vaquero et al.(2018)Vaquero, Troesch and Chien
]
,
the four-state strategy (including explore, meet, sacrifice and relay) in
[
Cesare et al.(2015)Cesare, Skeele, Yoo, Zhang and
Hollinger
]
,
the distributed assignment of relay tasks for static goals in
[
Marchukov and Montano(2019)
]
,
a centralized integer program to optimize rendezvous points in
[
Gao et al.(2022)Gao, Wang, Zhong, Yang, Wang, Xu, Wang, Lin, Xu and
Gao
]
,
and a method in
[
Schack et al.(2024)Schack, Rogers and Dantam
]
that predicts the positions of other robots via the un-availability of communication,
to improve coordination in the exploration tasks.
Our earlier work in
[
Guo and Zavlanos(2018)
,
Kantaros et al.(2019)Kantaros, Guo and
Zavlanos
]
proposes a fully distributed intermittent communication protocol for pairs of robots within the fleet.
Moreover, fully-connected communication networks at all time are imposed
in
[
Rooker and Birk(2007)
,
Zavlanos et al.(2011)Zavlanos, Egerstedt and
Pappas
]
by collaborative motion planning,
and further in
[
Pei et al.(2013)Pei, Mutka and Xi
,
Marcotte et al.(2020)Marcotte, Wang, Mehta and
Olson
]
and
[
Xia et al.(2023)Xia, Deng, Pan, Zhang, Duan, Zhou and
Cheng
]
by placing some front and relay nodes to ensure a lower-bounded bandwidth for the fleet.
Droppable radios are utilized in
[
Saboia et al.(2022)Saboia, Clark, Thangavelu, Edlund, Otsu, Correa,
Varadharajan, Santamaria-Navarro, Touma, Bouman et al.
]
as extended communication relays between robots and a static base station.
Recent work in
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Yu, Tang, Xu and Wang
]
proposes efficient representations of the explored map to reduce the communication overhead.
Our previous work
[
Tian et al.(2024)Tian, Zhang, Wei and Guo
]
tackles the hard constraint of lower-bounded information latency
for all robots under limited communication.
It can be observed that the aforementioned methods typically assume the existence of a static base station and consider only a single communication mode,
either intermittent communication among the multi-robot system (including the base station) as a whole
or full connectivity at all times.
These approaches are often designed for particular scenarios
and could be limited in communication range and bandwidth.
More importantly, they mostly neglect the crucial role of human operators during the process of collaborative exploration,
and the potential interactions between the operators and the fleet.

2.3
Human-Fleet Interaction

Indeed, the human operator plays
an indispensable role

in the operation of robotic fleets,
even when these systems exhibit significant autonomous capabilities,
see
[
Dahiya et al.(2023)Dahiya, Aroyo, Dautenhahn and
Smith
]
for a recent survey.
In
[
Gombolay et al.(2015)Gombolay, Gutierrez, Clarke, Sturla and
Shah
]
, the authors discuss how the allocation of decision-making authority between humans and robots affects the overall efficiency of the team.
In many scenarios,
the operators are not only required to monitor the online status of the fleet,
but also to intervene or supervise critical procedures when necessary.
Robots, in turn, may need human confirmation before executing risky or difficult movements, or may seek direct assistance when encountering unforeseen situations.
Most aforementioned work neglects these aspects and assume a static base station for visualization,
see
[
Klaesson et al.(2020)Klaesson, Nilsson, Vaquero, Tepsuporn, Ames and
Murray
,
Vaquero et al.(2018)Vaquero, Troesch and Chien
,
Marchukov and Montano(2019)
,
Gao et al.(2022)Gao, Wang, Zhong, Yang, Wang, Xu, Wang, Lin, Xu and
Gao
,
Pei et al.(2010)Pei, Mutka and Xi
,
Saboia et al.(2022)Saboia, Clark, Thangavelu, Edlund, Otsu, Correa,
Varadharajan, Santamaria-Navarro, Touma, Bouman et al.
,
Schack et al.(2024)Schack, Rogers and Dantam
,
Dahlquist et al.(2025)Dahlquist, Nordström, Stathoulopoulos,
Lindqvist, Saradagi and Nikolakopoulos
]
,
yielding a rather uniform behavior of the fleet until the area is fully explored.
An interactive exploration strategy is proposed in our previous work
[
Tian et al.(2024)Tian, Zhang, Wei and Guo
]
,
which is however limited to simple exploration tasks and single communication mode.
Recent advances also explore these multi-modal paradigms for the operator-fleet interactions,
incorporating voice, gesture, gaze, or touch inputs to enrich the expressiveness and efficiency of communication between human
and the robots as in
[
Viswanathan et al.(2024)Viswanathan, Saucedo, Satpute, Kanellakis and
Nikolakopoulos
,
Riley and Frew(2023)
,
Nikolaidis et al.(2018)Nikolaidis, Kwon, Forlizzi and
Srinivasa
,
Rosenfeld et al.(2017)Rosenfeld, Agmon, Maksimov and
Kraus
]
.
Moreover, an augmented-reality (AR) device is proposed in
[
Reardon et al.(2019)Reardon, Lee, Rogers and
Fink
]

to facilitate more immersive human-fleet real-time interaction.
The recent work in
[
Chen et al.(2024)Chen, Sun, Pollefeys and Blum
]
presents a 3D mixed-reality interface overlaying the SLAM map and robot positions to enable drag-and-drop commands.
A joystick and wristbands is adopted in
[
Villani et al.(2020)Villani, Capelli, Secchi, Fantuzzi and
Sabattini
]

that allow the operator to manage multiple robots while monitoring their own cognitive and physical workload.
These approaches however all rely on high-bandwidth and reliable communication
between the operator and the fleet.
These aforementioned bilateral and online interactions bring numerous challenges
for communication-constrained scenarios,
which are not yet addressed formally in the literature, e.g.,
how to ensure a timely exchange between the operators
and the fleet (including the newly explored map and requests);
how to choose different modes of communication (intermittent or long-chain)
to fulfill different requests and further switch between modes.

,

Each team

The consideration of multiple teams that consist of

4
Proposed Solution

As illustrated in Fig.
3
,
the proposed scheme MoRoCo consists of four main components:
(I) The intermittent communication protocol, which serves as a fundamental
building block for the subsequent components;
(II) The proposed strategy in the “spread mode”
and the “migrate mode”,
for simultaneous exploration and intermittent communication,
to ensure the lower-bounded latency among the robots and the operators;
(III) The topology optimization and connectivity maintenance
in the “chain mode”,
while minimizing the impact on the exploration efficiency;
and (IV) The online interaction and adaptation during execution,
where the robots switch between different roles and communication modes
to accommodate different requests and the dynamic behaviors of the operators.

4.1
Intermittent Comm. with Hyper-Ring Graph

4.1.1
Hyper-ring Communication Graph.

To ensure that the information can be propagated among the robots and to the operators,
the robots are required to meet and communicate via the protocol
of

To begin with, a hyper-ring graph is adopted as the communication topology
for the whole fleet, as illustrated in Figure

The

4.1.2
Intermittent Communication Protocol.

As shown in Figure
4
,
the protocol of intermittent communication under the hyper-ring topology
is as follows:
(I)
Intra-team Communication
. Initially at
t=0
,
all robots in one team
\mathcal{T}_{k}
are in close proximity but
each robot only communicates with its neighbors (called an
internal event
);
during each local communication, each pair of robots
determines locally the “time and place” for
their
next
communication event,
and whether one of them needs to return and propagate
data to the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}
before they meet (called a
return event
);
then they depart and do not communicate until the next communication event;
(II)
Inter-team Communication
.
Initially, all teams are in close proximity.
The operators of two neighboring teams would communicate with each other
and decide the “time and place” for the next communication event
between two teams,
however without specifying the robots from each team involved.
Then, the teams depart and do not communicate until the next communication event,
where two selected robots from each team would meet and communicate
(called an
external event
),
to decide the next communication event between two teams.
This procedure repeats indefinitely until termination.

Remark 5
.

Compared with all-time connectivity in
[
Guo et al.(2016)Guo, Tumova and Dimarogonas
]
and
subgroup-wise connectivity in
[

Zavlanos

]

a pair-wise intermittent communication offers a higher flexibility
and potentially less impact on the task execution,
as also motivated in
[
Guo and Zavlanos(2018)
,
Tian et al.(2024)Tian, Zhang, Wei and Guo
]
.
More numerical comparisons are given in the sequel.

\blacksquare

4.1.3
Estimation of the Communication Quality.

To plan for inter-robot communication events,
it is essential to evaluate the feasibility of inter-robot communication
at different area of the explored workspace.
Thus, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is employed to
estimate the communication quality between any two points within the explored map.
As shown in Figure
5
,
the ad-hoc communication devices are often capable of measuring signal strength in real time between the units.
In other words, the electromagnetic properties such as attenuation and distortions
are comparable in similar environments such as office buildings and subterranean caves.
Thus, the training data can be collected during past exploration missions
in similar scenes or known environments,
which includes the explored map, semantic features,
positions of the transmitting or receiving robots, and the communication quality.
An example of the data collection process is presented in Figure
2
.
Consequently, the MLP network can be trained in a supervised way beforehand,
to predict the communication quality
\texttt{Qual}(p_{i},p_{j},\mathcal{A})
in (
2
),
given the transmitting position
p_{i}
and receiving position
p_{j}

within the explored map
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

An example of the training results for typical office environments
is shown in Figure
5
,
with a MLP network of three hidden layers.
It can be seen that the predicted results closely match the actual measurements,
and the communication quality degrades drastically with increased distance
and the number of obstructing walls.

4.2.1
Distributed Frontier-based Exploration.

The frontier-based method as pioneered in
[
Yamauchi(1997)
]

allows a robot to explore the environment gradually,
by recursively reaching the frontiers on the boundaries between
the explored and unexplored areas within its local map.
Briefly speaking,
given the local map
M_{i}(t)
of robot
i\in\mathcal{N}
at time
t>0
,
these boundaries can be identified via a Breadth-First-Search (BFS)
from any explored cell,
which are then clustered or ranked to a few frontiers by various metrics,
as summarized in
[
Holz et al.(2010)Holz, Basilico, Amigoni and
Behnke
]
.
Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)\triangleq\{f_{1},\cdots,f_{H}\}
the set of local frontiers,
where each frontier
f_{h}\in\partial M_{i}
is on the boundary of explored areas
\partial M_{i}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}_{i}(t)
is empty if
M_{i}(t)
is fully explored.
Note that due to the distributed nature of the system and limited communication,
the set of local frontiers often differ greatly across robots and teams.

the operator is not allowed to move via the spread mode nor the migrate mode.
Otherwise,
the messenger could not be aware of the new position of the operator
after it returns from the external event, thus losing contact with its team.

\blacksquare

Local Adaptation by
\{\xi^{i}_{5}\}
.

The requests by
\xi^{i}_{5}
from robot

often require immediate feedback from the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}

yielding a high priority request during execution.
To this end, a local adaptation algorithm is proposed to minimize
the response time to this request,
by allowing the choice of an optimal robot to return to the operator.
More specifically, assume that robot

\xi^{i}_{5}

at time

If the next communication event

is a return event, or there is enough

\texttt{h}_{k}

before
\mathbf{c}^{0}_{i}
, then it can directly return to fulfill the request.
Otherwise, robot
i
should meet at
\mathbf{c}^{0}_{i}
and obtain the next round of planned
communication events
C_{r}
as defined in (
6
).
Then, it iterates to find the smallest index
\ell\in\{1,\,2,\,\cdots,L_{r}\}

such that the following condition is satisfied:

t_{n_{\ell}\hat{n}_{\ell}}+T^{\texttt{nav}}_{\hat{n}_{\ell}}(p_{n_{\ell}\hat{n}_{\ell}},\,p_{\texttt{h}_{k}},\,p_{n_{\ell+1}\hat{n}_{\ell+1}})\leq t_{n_{\ell+1}\hat{n}_{\ell+1}},

(32)

where
t_{n_{\ell}\hat{n}_{\ell}}
and
p_{n_{\ell}\hat{n}_{\ell}}
are the time and place of the
\ell

If a solution is found, a return event is added to the plan of robot
\hat{n}_{\ell}
at time
t_{n_{\ell}\hat{n}_{\ell}}
,
which is then propagated to robot
n_{\ell}
through subsequent communication events.
However, if no feasible solution is found, then a return event is added to the
local plan of robot
i
before its next communication event
\mathbf{c}^{0}_{i}
.

4.3
Chain Mode: Multi-hop Communication Relays

As motivated earlier,
both the requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
require a reliable communication chain
between an operator and a particular robot,
e.g., to transmit a video stream or to take over the robot control.
The key to fulfill such requests is to optimize the number of robots
and their locations,
which serve as relays in the communication chain between the operator
and the particular robot.
Meanwhile, the rest of the robots in the fleet should adapt to this change
of communication topology and continue in the spread mode
to satisfy other requests.
More importantly, after the requests are fulfilled, the relay robots should
return to the spread mode to maximize the exploration efficiency.
This section first describes
the hybrid optimization algorithm for the chain mode,
which includes the communication topology for the replay
and the assignment of relay roles.
Then, it presents the transition strategy from the spread mode to the chain mode,
and vice versa.

4.3.1
Hybrid Optimization of the Chain Mode.

Without loss of generality, consider that a reliable communication is desired
between the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}
and the robot
i^{\star}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
,
which implies that the following communication-ensured chain (CEC)
exists between them.

Definition 12
.

A communication-ensured chain (CEC) from the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}

to the robot
i^{\star}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
is a sequence of anchor points
denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\triangleq p_{0}p_{1}\cdots p_{C}
.
It satisfies the following three conditions:
(I)
p_{0}=p_{i^{\star}}
and
p_{C}=p_{\texttt{h}_{k}}
;
(II) Each anchor point
p_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{h}_{k}}
,

\forall\ell=0,\cdots,C
;
and (III)
\texttt{Qual}(p_{\ell},\,p_{\ell+1},\,{M}_{i^{\star}})>(\underline{\delta}+\rho_{\texttt{c}})

as in (
2
) with
\rho_{\texttt{c}}>0
,
\forall\ell=0,\cdots,(C-1)
.

\blacksquare

In other words, if there is a robot located at each anchor point
of
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}

that communicates with its preceding and succeeding robots
(or the operator) on the chain,
the CEC provides a reliable chain of communication
from robot
i^{\star}
to the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}
.
Note that
\rho_{\texttt{c}}>0
is a design parameter as
the
margin
for the communication quality,
which not only effects how many relay robots are required but also
how much they can move within this communication chain.
As illustrated in Figure
12
,
the CEC is generated in two main steps:
(I) First, the collision-free shortest path from
p_{i^{\star}}
to
p_{\texttt{h}_{k}}

within
M_{\texttt{h}_{k}}
is computed as
\mathbf{p}^{\star}_{k}
,
with the same resolution as the underlying map;
(II) Second,
starting from the first anchor point
\widehat{p}_{\ell}=p_{i^{\star}}=\mathbf{p}^{\star}_{k}[j_{\ell}]
where
\ell=0
and
j_{\ell}=0
,
the next anchor point
\widehat{p}_{\ell+1}=\mathbf{p}^{\star}_{k}[j_{\ell}+1]

is given by the largest index
j_{\ell+1}>j_{\ell}
such that

\texttt{Qual}(p_{\ell},\,\mathbf{p}^{\star}_{k}[j_{\ell+1}],\,{M}_{\texttt{h}_{k}})>(\underline{\delta}+\rho_{\texttt{c}})
holds.
The above procedure terminates when
j_{L}=|\mathbf{p}^{\star}_{k}|
,
namely the last anchor is the operator
\widehat{p}_{L}=p_{\texttt{h}_{k}}
.
The resulting chain is denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}_{k}
,
which has a total length of
L
.
Clearly, the larger the range for effective communication is,
the shorter CEC is, thus fewer relay robots are required.

Remark 7
.

The condition on communication quality
\texttt{Qual}(\cdot)>\underline{\delta}+\rho_{\texttt{c}}
above
can be estimated by the heuristic rules given the communication module
within the latest map
M_{\texttt{h}_{k}}
,
or learned via the empirical data collected offline or
during the exploration phase, as shown in Figure
5
.

\blacksquare

Given the CEC
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}
above,
the next objective is to choose some robots as
relay robots
and
further assign them to the respective anchor points within
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}
.
To begin with, if the required number of relay robots is more than the number of
robots in the team,
i.e.,
C>(N_{k}-1)
, then this communication chain is
infeasible
for
the current team
\mathcal{N}_{k}
.
In this case, either the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}
should move along the chain
to reduce the number of relay robots required,
or relay robots from other teams can be recruited,
which is discussed in the sequel.
For now, assume that
C\leq N_{k}-1
and consider the assignment problem
for relay robots below.

Problem 2
.

Given the CEC
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}
above,
determine the optimal
assignment
\Pi^{\texttt{c}}_{k}:\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
such that the maximum time when all assigned relay robots reach the
corresponding anchor points is minimized, i.e.,

\mathop{\mathbf{min}}\limits_{\Pi_{\texttt{c}}}\Big\{\mathop{\mathbf{max}}\limits_{\ell\in[1,C]}\;\big\{t_{\ell}+T_{i_{\ell}}^{\texttt{Nav}}\big(p_{i_{\ell}}(t_{\ell}),\,p_{\ell}\big)\big\}\Big\},

(33)

where
\Pi^{\texttt{c}}_{k}(p_{\ell})\triangleq(i_{\ell},\,t_{\ell})
,
\forall\ell=1,\cdots,C
;
robot
i_{\ell}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
is the robot assigned to the anchor point
p_{\ell}
;

t_{\ell}
is the time when robot
i_{\ell}
starts navigating from its
location
p_{i_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})
towards
p_{\ell}
;
and
T_{i_{\ell}}^{\texttt{Nav}}(\cdot)
is the estimated duration of navigation.

\blacksquare

In other words, the objective is to find a subset of robots in
\mathcal{N}_{k}

to serve as relay robots and assign them to the anchor points,
such that the maximum time for all robots to reach their anchor points is minimized,
or equivalently, the time when the requests can be fulfilled is minimized.
Given the current position of all robots in
\mathcal{N}_{k}
, the above problem
resembles the unbalanced linear bottleneck assignment problem
(LBAP) from
[
Burkard et al.(2012)Burkard, Dell’Amico and
Martello
]
,
where the objective is to minimize the maximum cost of an agent-task assignment
(i.e., not the summation).
A centralized solution is proposed for the first return robot after the request has been received,
due to two main reasons:
(I) the communication among
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is too limited for a distributed solution;
(II) the local plans of all robots in
\mathcal{N}_{k}
are known to the return robot.
In particular,
the return robot can first compute the
C
-by-
N_{k}
cost matrix
\{w_{\ell,i}\}
given the local
plans
\{\Gamma_{i}\}
, where
w_{\ell,i}>0
is the time of arrival
if robot
i
leaves from its tailing planned communication event
\mathbf{c}^{L_{i}}_{i}

and reaches the anchor point
p_{\ell}\in\widehat{\mathbf{p}}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}
,
i.e.,
w_{\ell,i}\triangleq t^{L_{i}}_{i}+T_{i}^{\texttt{Nav}}\big(p^{L_{i}}_{i},\,p_{\ell}\big)
,
which holds for all
\ell=1,\cdots,C
and
i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
.
Consequently, a binary linear problem is formulated following
[
Burkard et al.(2012)Burkard, Dell’Amico and
Martello
]

given the one-to-one mapping constraints and the objective function.
It can be then solved by an off-the-shelf solver,
e.g.,
GLOP
from
[
OR-Tools(2022)
]
.
The solution is mapped to the desired assignment for Problem
2
,
denoted by
\Pi^{\texttt{c},\star}_{k}
.
Thus, for each anchor point
p_{\ell}
, the robot
i_{\ell}\triangleq\Pi^{\texttt{c},\star}_{k}(p_{\ell})\in\mathcal{N}_{k}

is assigned as the relay robot,
which navigates to
p_{\ell}
after its tailing planned communication event.
By then, the communication chain is established between the
operator
\texttt{h}_{k}
and the robot
i^{\star}
,
by which the requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
can be fulfilled
given the desired data exchange and duration.

Consequently, as illustrated in Figure
13
,
the communication topology of
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is decomposed into two sub-graphs:

\mathcal{G}_{k}\triangleq\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\cup\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{k},

(34)

where the chain topology
\mathcal{G}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\triangleq(\mathcal{N}^{\texttt{c}}_{k},\,\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{c}}_{k})

is a line graph over the assigned
robots
\mathcal{N}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\triangleq\{\Pi_{k}^{\texttt{c},\star}(p_{\ell}),\,\forall\ell\}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{k}

and neighbors
(i_{\ell},\,i_{\ell+1})\in\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\subset\mathcal{N}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}\times\mathcal{N}^{\texttt{c}}_{k}

given

and

and the new spread topology

derived from the original ring topology

by removing all robots in
\mathcal{N}_{k}^{\texttt{c}}
and connecting
consecutive remaining robots in
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{k}

Due to the limited communication, a smooth transition of the communication topology
is non-trivial, which is explained in the sequel.

4.3.3
Connectivity Maintenance within Chain Mode.

After the transition to chain mode is completed,
the remaining robots in
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{k}

to
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{k}

Due to the latency constraints imposed by

\texttt{h}_{k}

4.4
Overall Framework and Discussion

4.4.1
Online Execution and Adaptation.

As summarized in Figure
3
,
initially the teams are close and start from the spread mode.
Inter-team and intra-team communications follow the intermittent
communication protocol by Definition
8

and Algorithm
3
,
during which the local maps of all robots and operators are updated.
The latency constraints by
\{\xi^{k}_{1}\}
and
\{\xi^{k_{1}k_{2}}_{7}\}

are ensured by Theorem
1
.
Meanwhile, the online requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{2}\}
,

\{\xi^{k}_{3}\}
,
\{\xi^{i}_{5}\}

can be received and fulfilled by the spread mode,
following Algorithm
4
and
5
.
Furthermore, if online requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
are
received, the teams need to transit between the spread mode
and the chain mode, following Algorithm
6
.
This procedure continues until all operators
\texttt{h}_{k}
have
received the complete map and all requests are fulfilled,
as stated in (
5
).
Note that the fleet can still react to online requests
even after the complete map is fully explored.

4.4.2
Complexity Analysis.

For the inter-robot coordination,
to determine the return event and the external event only requires searching for a single path
within the explored map,
which has a computation complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N_{m}\log N_{m})
, where
N_{m}
is the number of nodes in the explored map.
Note that a topological map as proposed in
[
Zhang et al.(2022)Zhang, Yu, Tang, Xu and Wang
]
can be constructed and maintained
during the exploration, which greatly reduces the number of nodes in the map.
The complexity of optimizing the next internal communication event depends primarily on the construction of
the cost matrix, which has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{F}_{ij}|^{2}(N_{m}\log N_{m}))
,
where
|\mathcal{F}_{ij}|
is the number of frontiers within the merged map of robots
i
and
j
.
Note that the frontier detection can be implemented in an incremental manner
by only considering the newly explored areas at each cycle,
thus can be efficiently computed with a constant time complexity related to the sensor range.
Another computation task is the generation and assignment of the CEC during human-robot interaction,
which has a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N_{m}\log N_{m})+\mathcal{O}(N_{p})
,
with
N_{p}
being the number of nodes within the reference path.
Generally, the proposed framework is computationally efficient due to its event-based design,
which can be implemented on embedded systems with limited computational resources,
as demonstrated in the hardware experiments.

4.4.3
Generalization.

There are several notable generalizations of the proposed framework,
in terms of heterogeneous fleets, failure detection and recovery,
multiple requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
,
and spontaneous meeting events.

(I)
Heterogeneous Fleets
. During collaborative exploration, there often exists
areas that are not accessible by one type of robots but suitable for another.
For instance, UGVs might be stuck climbing hills while UAVs can reach higher altitude.
However, UAVs have limited battery and can be difficult to navigate through narrow and low ceilings.
Thus, it is beneficial to deploy heterogeneous robots within each subteam, e.g.,
wheeled UGVs, quadruped robots and UAVs as in
[
Siegwart et al.(2015)Siegwart, Hutter, Oettershagen, Burri,
Gilitschenski, Galceran and Nieto
,
Asadi et al.(2020)Asadi, Suresh, Ender, Gotad, Maniyar, Anand,
Noghabaei, Han, Lobaton and Wu
]
.
The proposed framework can be conveniently applied to heterogeneous fleets, i.e.,
the estimation of navigation time is modified
to take into account the heterogeneous mobility and resource constraints
(such as battery and capability), i.e.,

T_{i}(p_{s},\,p_{g})=T_{i}^{\texttt{Nav}}(p_{s},\,p_{g},M_{i}),\,\text{ if }\,R_{i}(p_{s},\,p_{g})<r_{s};

(38)

where
T_{i}^{\texttt{Nav}}(\cdot)
is the estimated time from
p_{s}
to
p_{g}

within the local map
M_{i}
;

R_{i}(p_{s},\,p_{g})
is the estimated resource required for the navigation
of robot
i
;
and
r_{s}
is the estimated resource level of robot
i
at position
p_{s}
.
In this way, frontiers and meeting locations would not be assigned to
robots if they are out-of-reach and too costly.

(II)
Failure Detection and Recovery
.
As robots can often fail when operating in unknown and extreme environments,
a failure detection and recovery mechanism is essential for the safety
and integrity of the proposed system.
The key is to ensure that the local communication topology
\mathcal{G}^{k}

can be adjusted online to remove the failure robot,
via only intermittent local communication.
Assume that robot
j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
fails at time
t_{f}>0
,
and its proceeding and succeeding neighbors in
\mathcal{G}

are robot
k\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
and robot
i\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
, respectively.
As illustrated in Figure
15
, consider the following two cases:

Case One
: If robot
j
fails on its way to meet robot
k
,
robot
k
will wait for robot
j
at the communication
location
c_{jk}
agreed from the previous round, for a waiting period of
T^{-}_{\texttt{max}}
.
Then, robot
k
assumes that robot
j
has failed and directly moves to
the meeting location
c_{ij}
of robots
j
and
i
,
which has been propagated to robot
k
via previous communication events.
Afterwards, robots
k
and
i
plan for their next meeting event as before;

Case Two
: If robot
j
fails
before
meeting with robot
i
,
robot
i
detects the failure of robot
j
with a waiting time
T^{+}_{\texttt{max}}

similar to the first case.
However, robot
i
cannot directly meet with robot
k
,
as robot
k
has already communicated with robot
j
and most likely performs exploration.
Moreover, the meeting event between robot
k
and its successor is unknown to robot
i

due to the failure of robot
j
.
Therefore, robot
i
first determines a meeting location
p_{ik}
locally,
and then communicates with its successor to propagate this information.
After several communication events, this location is propagated to robot
k

along with the message that robot
j
has failed.
Thus, robot
i
cancels the meeting event with robot
j
and navigates to
p_{ik}

to meet with robot
k
instead.
In both cases, the local communication topology
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is adjusted to
form a smaller ring, by removing the failed robot.
Note that
T^{+}_{\texttt{max}}
should be larger than
T^{-}_{\texttt{max}}
,
such that robot
i
can meet with robot
k
in the first case above,
before robot
k
judges that robot
j
has failed and moves away.

(III)
Multiple Requests of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
.

With multiple robots in the subteam, it might happen that more than one requests
of
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
and
\{\xi^{i}_{6}\}
are known to the operator
\texttt{h}_{k}

within one return event,
i.e., the operator requests direct access to several robots one after another;
or several robots request direct assistance from the operator.
In this case, the assigned robots by
\Pi^{\texttt{c}}_{k}
in the chain mode might navigate
directly to the next assistance task as relay robots,
i.e., without transition back first to the spread mode.
This can be achieved in a similar way to the scheme of connectivity maintenance
during motion as described in the chain mode.
Namely, if the next task is within the accessible region,
the operator can directly control the target robot and move it to target position,
given the optimized CEC.
If the next task can not be reached directly,
the whole chain can be transited to connect operator and the next assistance task.

(IV)
Spontaneous Meeting Events
.
During the real-time execution,
it is possible that robot
i
meets with another robot
k

on its way to meet robot
j
, which is called a
spontaneous
meeting event.
Consider the following three cases depending on which teams the robots
i
and
j

belong to, respectively.

Case One
: robot
i
and robot
j
belong to the same team
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
.
Similar to our previous work in
[
Guo and Zavlanos(2018)
]
,
robot
i
can coordinate with robot
k
for the next meeting event
even though they are not connected initially.
However, here they only exchange their local data
D_{i}
and
D_{k}
,
without extending their local sequence of confirmed meetings,
so that our predefined communication topology within the
robotic team
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
can be maintained.

Case Two
: robot
i
and robot
k
belong to different
teams and they are
not
neighboring teams,
i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
,
and
(\mathcal{T}_{m},\mathcal{T}_{n})\notin\mathcal{E}
.
Similar to the first case,
they only share their local data without further coordination.

Case Three
: robot
i
and robot
k
belong to different teams
which are neighboring teams,
i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
,
and
(\mathcal{T}_{m},\mathcal{T}_{n})\in\mathcal{E}
.
In this case,
they act as temporary messengers to determine the next external event between two teams.
The next meeting event is determined following the same procedure in (
26
),
and the original external meeting event is removed via the subsequent
intra-team communication events within both teams.
Thus, they continue to execute their original plans,
and propagate the new external event to other team members and operators.

(V)
Corner Cases
.
When the team size is relatively small,
it is possible that there are one or two robots within each team or subteam,
particularly during the mode transitions.
In this case, there are inadequate number of robots to form a ring communication
topology for the spread mode.
Consequently, these corner cases can be handled as follows:

Case One:
If there is only one robot in the spread mode, it enters a special case called the
single-explore
case,
where it explores the environment independently and returns to the operator periodically to share the map and data, according to the maximum latency
T^{k}_{\texttt{h}}
.
When other robots in this team need to be restored to the spread mode, for example, when a communication chain is disbanded,
this robot transits to the spread mode and restores communication with other robots.

Case Two:
If there are two robots in the spread mode, they can still follow the intermittent communication protocol,
but the topology is a line instead of a ring.
In this case, the local plan of each robot
i
is simplified to
\Gamma_{i}\triangleq\tau_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}
,
i.e., it contains only one communication event
\mathbf{c}_{i}
.
However, the coordination process during the intermittent meeting still follows Algorithm
3
,
and the same transition phase is activated when the command for migrate mode or chain mode is received.

5.1.2
Spatial-temporal Tree of Data Flow.

For better visualization of the results,
a novel data structure is designed here to illustrate the process of
exploration and data propagation,
as the spatial-temporal tree of data flow (
SDF-tree
).
As shown in Figure
17
,
it is a tree that originates from where the team initially starts
and grows in the spatial-temporal space of
x-y-t
,
along the trajectories of each robot and the operator.
Namely, each leaf node (colored squares) of the SDF-Tree
represents the area explored by a robot,
all of which connects to the spatial-temporal trajectory of
the corresponding robot via the so-called
E-edge
.
Thus, the height of a leaf node on the
t
-axis represents
the time that it is explored.
Moreover, when the explored area of a leaf node becomes known to the operator,
this node is connected to the spatial-temporal trajectory of the operator
via the so-called
R-edge
.
Thus, the difference in their projection onto the
t
-axis represents
the latency of this area,
i.e., the time received by operator minus the first time explored by any robot.
When a scheduled meeting event or a return event happens,
the spatial-temporal trajectories of two robots or a robot and one operator
are connected via the so-called
M-edge
,
to indicate the data exchange among them.
Lastly, to highlight the largest latency within each team and between teams,
an additional edge called
L-edge
that is parallel to t axis is created.

It is generated by first iterating over all the R-edges to find the one with the maximum latency.
Without loss of generality, assume that the leaf node is at
(x^{\star},y^{\star},t^{\star})
and
the latency is
\delta
, then the L-edge is created by connecting the leaf node
to the new node
(x^{\star},y^{\star},t^{\star}+\delta)
.
Namely, the height of L-edge represents the maximum latency of the corresponding area.

Thus, the structure of a SDF-Tree encapsulates rich and essential information regarding
the online progress of exploration and data-propagation,
which are instrumental for our numerical validation.

5.5
Comparisons

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework (as
MOROCO
),
a quantitative comparison is conducted against baseline methods in both single-team and multi-team scenarios.
Since current methods can not handle the spread mode and the chain mode simultaneously,
the comparison is mainly focused on the exploration task in the spread mode.

5.5.1
Single-team Experiments.

For fair comparison, each method is required satisfy the same latency constraints in (
11
)
to provide timely updates to the operator.
Since most methods can not address this constraint, minor modifications are made
to these baseline methods accordingly, while preserving their original strategies for exploration.
More specifically, our method
MOROCO
is compared with four baseline methods:

(I)
CMRE
, a method for coordinated multi-robot exploration proposed in
[
Burgard et al.(2005)Burgard, Moors, Stachniss and
Schneider
]
.
It is a representative method that does not take into account communication constraints where each robot explores the environment independently.
To satisfy the latency constraints, a return event is added for each robot before the maximum latency is reached.

(II)
M-TARE
, a distributed exploration framework proposed in
[
Cao et al.(2023)Cao, Zhu, Ren, Choset and Zhang
]

that handle communication constraints implicitly.
It adopts a pursuit strategy where the robots opportunistically pursue each other to
communicate and thus improve the overall exploration efficiency.
A modification similiar to CMRE is made to satisfy the latency constraints.

(III)
JSSP
, a framework based on intermittent communication, as proposed in
[
Da Silva et al.(2024)Da Silva, Chaimowicz, Silva and
Hsieh
]
.
It generates a global plan for intermittent rendezvous by representing the
multi-robot exploration task as an instance of the Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSSP)
over the frontiers and sampled rendezvous locations and minimizing the waiting times.
Different from our method, it adopts a time-varing communication topology which is optimized
by one robot during a synchronization event where all robots meet at a common point.
Due to the latency constraints, additional edges connecting the operator to each robot are added to the communication graph,
each of which is associated with a return event before the maximum latency is reached.

(IV)
MOROCO-NI
, which is our method without considering the human-robot interaction.
In other words, the operator is not allowed to send any requests to the robots,
including dynamic movement
\{\xi^{k}_{3}\}
, prioritized regions
\{\xi^{k}_{2}\}
,
and communication chains
\{\xi^{k}_{4}\}
.

The compared metrics are as follows:
the total explored area;
the mission time, measured as the time that the operator receives the complete map;
the time of last update, measured as the time when no more area is explored;
the return rate, measured as the number of return events per
T_{\texttt{h}}
;
and whether online interactions are allowed.
Each method is tested 5 times in three different environments: the building complex in Figure
18
,
the indoor environment in Figure
20
, and the cave environment in Figure
25
.
The team consists of 4 robots and one operator, with the maximum latency
T_{\texttt{h}}
set to
160s
in all scenarios.

.
Consequently, CMRE and M-TARE achieves only
50.5\%

A similiar outcome can be found for JSSP, which have even lower exploration rate due to
the bad choices of rendezvous points and excessive waiting there.
Moreover, since the meeting locations are sampled randomly without checking for feasibliity,
the robots may not be able to reach the meeting points within the specified latency constraints.
In contrast, the static method MOROCO-NI achieves a better performance than CMRE, M-TARE and JSSP,
increasing the maximum coverage by
1.5
to
2
times owing to the simultaneous coordination of exploration area, meeting events and return events.
Moreover, the average return rate of MOROCO-NI is significantly lower than these three methods,
revealing a higher efficiency of data propagation among the robots and the operator.
However, MOROCO-NI is not able to explore the whole environment due to the lack of human-robot interaction,
particularly due to the large area and the latency constraint.

In comparison, when the human interactions are allowed,
including prioritized regions, dynamic movement and migration,
MOROCO achieves a coverage of
100\%
with the highest exploration efficiency
and the lowest return rate in all three environments.

In addition, MOROCO provides the operator with a more timely update of the map compared with other methods,
which is crucial for the operator to interact with the fleet and collaborate in real-time.

5.5.2
Multi-team Experiments.

As described earlier, since there are no existing work that can address simultaneously
the intra-team and inter-team latency constraints in the multi-team scenario,
the following three ablation baselines are adopted for the numerical comparisons:
(I)
MOROCO-NM
: the proposed method without the migrate mode;
(II)
MOROCO-NP
: the proposed method without prioritized regions,
namely the operator is not allowed to specify the prioritized regions for exploration;
(III)
MOROCO-NE
: the proposed method without external events and inter-team latency constraints.

The compared metrics are similar to the single-team case:
the total explored covered area;
the mission time measured as the time that both operators obtain the complete map;
the exploration time measured as the time when the complete map is explored by two teams;
the maximum inter-team latency;
and the overlapping rate measured as the percentage of overlapping explored
area between two teams.
Three scenarios including the building, indoor and cave are considered as the single-team case,
where each team consists of 2 robots and one operator.
The inter-team latency is set to
T_{\texttt{c}}=360s
and
the intra-team latency
T_{\texttt{h}}=160s
for all scenarios.

Each method is tested
5
times, and the strategies of the interactions
by two operators remain unchanged during each test.

The results are summarized in Table
6
and Figure
26
.
It can be seen that MOROCO achieves the largest coverage, the shortest mission time and the lowest overlapping rate in all three environments.
In addition, the inter-team latency is strictly below the specified bound.
Moreover, the necessity of the “migrate mode” is apparent from the results, since MOROCO-NM fails to cover the whole environment
in the indoor scenario, ending with only
67.4\%
coverage rate.
On the other hand, MOROCO-NP gives the lowest exploration efficiency in the building complex and cave environments,
due to lack of prioritized regions, which leads to a larger overlapping area of more than
70\%
between two teams.
It validates that the online supervision from the operator via the requests of
“prioritized regions” are important to improve the efficiency of inter-team coordination within small-scale environments.
However, MOROCO-NP achieves a better performance in the large-scale indoor environment than in the other two scenes,
as the two operators can initially move away from each other and overlapping is rather small.
Last but not least, the inter-team latency under MOROCO-NE increases drastically as the environment becomes larger,
i.e., from
359.0s
in the building complex and
482.7s
in cave to
1030.3s
in the large-scale indoor scenario,
all of which exceeds the specified bound
T_{\texttt{c}}=360s
.
Without timely communication between the teams,
the local update at both operators are much slower by 19%
and the exploration efficiency within the large-scale environments
is lower by 8%,
as shown in Figure
26
.
These results demonstrates that the designed requests
of
\{\xi^{k}_{2}\}
,
\{\xi^{k}_{3}\}
and
\{\xi^{k_{1}k_{2}}_{7}\}

and the migration mode are essential to achieve
the best performance in the multi-team scenarios,
i.e., by enabling a faster exploration, more timely updates of local maps,
and more efficient inter-team coordination.

7
Conclusion

This section concludes with some limitations of the current framework,
and future research directions.

7.1
Limitations

7.1.1
Efficiency and Accuracy of Map Merge.

Efficient and accurate map
merge is an essential block during the intermittent pair-wise communication.
It is taken for granted via off-the-shelf packages, e.g.,

multirobot_map_merge
(
[
Hörner(2016)
]
) and

FRAME
(
[
Stathoulopoulos et al.(2023)Stathoulopoulos, Koval, Agha-mohammadi
and Nikolakopoulos
]
),
which offers efficient map merging for robots with and without initially-known
positions.
However for the latter case without known initial positions,
accuracy of the map merging relies heavily on the amount of overlapping space
between the local maps of each robot, i.e.,
larger overlapping area increases the number of matched features and thus the accuracy.
Thus, it is preferred to have more overlapping areas explored by both neighboring robots.
However, as discussed earlier, an efficient scheme for collaborative exploration
instead would minimize the overlapping area among the robots to maximize the exploration efficiency.
This trade-off has not been considered in the current framework,
and part of our future work.

7.1.2
Semantic Information.

Semantic information within the workspace has been mostly neglected in the
proposed framework, which however can be utilized in several aspects.
First, such semantic information can be essential for the operator
to trigger certain requests, e.g., areas with valuable resources to prioritize,
and key features to inspect.
Providing these information and even automating the generation of potential
requests could significantly improve the task efficiency.
Second, as demonstrated in numerous recent work from
[
Chang et al.(2023)Chang, Hughes, Ray and
Carlone
,
Tao et al.(2024)Tao, Liu, Spasojevic, Agarwal and Kumar
,
Liu et al.(2024)Liu, Lei, Prabhu, Tao, Spasojevic, Chaudhari,
Atanasov and Kumar
]
,
incorporating semantic information in the map can drastically improve the
accuracy and efficiency of map merging, by treating the semantic objects
as anchor points.
Third, related to the first aspect, semantic information explored in the environment is
closely associated with high-level collaborative tasks for the fleet,
e.g., search and rescue in
[
Miller et al.(2022)Miller, Cladera, Smith, Taylor and
Kumar
]
, dynamic capture in
[
Bajcsy et al.(2024)Bajcsy, Loquercio, Kumar and
Malik
]
.
Thus, besides exploration, the robots need to coordinate to f ulfill these collaborative tasks
that are generated online.
Some of our earlier works focus on the task coordination under complex temporal
tasks and communication constraints in
[
Guo and Zavlanos(2018)
,
Wang et al.(2023)Wang, Guo and Li
]
,
which however do not consider collaborative exploration under latency constraints simultaneously.

7.1.3
Multi-modal and User-friendly Interaction.

The human-fleet interaction in the proposed framework are mostly via touch on
the integrated GUI panel or typing directly in the terminal,
which can be challenging especially for layman.
Thus, the means of human-fleet interaction can be extended to be multi-modal and user-friendly:
(I) Large Language Models (LLMs) could be adopted to convert verbal commands to structured requests as defined here;
(II) Templates of requests w.r.t. typical scenarios can be designed before-hand and stored,
through which the operator could directly revise;
(III) Although a wide range of operator-fleet requests are incorporated in the
proposed framework,
there could be task or scenario-specific requests not yet addressed,
which might even require a new collaboration mode.
For instance, instead of a uniform exploration, the operator might have a temporary goal
in the unexplored area, which should be reached in minimum time.
Such requests can not be directly handled by the proposed collaboration modes,
and remain part of our future work.

7.1.4
Map Prediction and Heuristics Learning.

As discussed in
[
Wang et al.(2025)Wang, Yu, Xu, Gao, Yang, Tang, Yu, Chen, Gao, Jian,
Chen, Gao, Zhou and Wang
]
,
the efficiency of collaborative exploration depends largely on the structure
of the underlying map, i.e., there is hardly a exploration strategy that is
optimal for all scenarios.
Consequently, there has been numerous work in
[
Shrestha et al.(2019)Shrestha, Tian, Feng, Tan and Vaughan
,
Ericson and Jensfelt(2024)
,
Tan et al.(2025)Tan, Narasimhan and
Nejat
]

that predicts the unexplored map given the explored region
and further learns the heuristics to prioritize certain region.
Similar concepts can be applied to the proposed framework in terms of the choice
of frontiers and communication points during intermittent communication,
thus to accelerate the exploration process and the fulfillment of different requests.
Furthermore, related to the previous aspect, the predicted map and the learned heuristics
can be utilized to generate templates of interaction online according to the progress.

7.2
Conclusion and Future Work

The proposed MoRoCo framework enables effective coordination between the human operators
and the robotic fleets in communication-restricted scenarios like search-and-rescue
or subterranean exploration.
By adaptively and optimally switching between the spread, the migrate, and the chain modes,
the system balances exploration, dynamic interaction, and intermittent data exchange
through only ad-hoc networks without the need for pre-installed infrastructure.
Distributed coordination ensures timely responses to seven different bidirectional
operator-fleet requests, such as region prioritization and remote inspection.
Extensive human-in-the-loop simulations and experiments demonstrate the effectiveness
for online coordination, interaction and exploration.
Future work includes the consideration of these aspects raised in the limitations.
