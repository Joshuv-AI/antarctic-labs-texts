The functional lumen opening with self-forming magnetic anastomosis (FLOWS) study: results from the North American pivotal trial
Authors: Andre Teixeira, Michael Seger, Nestor De la Cruz Munoz, Dana Portenier, Pearl Ma, Muhammad Ghanem, Richard Englehardt, Melissa Felinski, Kelvin Higa, Manoel Galvao Neto, Shinil K Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the safety and effectiveness of using self-forming magnets (SFM) with an immediately functional lumen for the creation of small bowel anastomoses.BackgroundPrevention of short-, medium-, and long-term gastrointestinal anastomotic complications remains a clinical challenge. Magnetic compression techniques with the ability to provide an immediately functional lumen may provide safer and more consistent anastomoses with reduced risk of leak, bleed, and/or stricture.MethodsThis multicenter, prospective, single-arm pivotal clinical study evaluated the Flexagon 25 mm SFM system plus OTOLoc in adult patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery requiring the creation of a small bowel anastomosis. The primary endpoint was defined as successful anastomosis created with the SFM with immediate functional lumen technique without placement procedure, device, or target anastomosis-related reoperation through 30 days. Adverse events were monitored and classified per Clavien–Dindo criteria.ResultsAcross six centers, 79 patients underwent laparoscopic small bowel anastomosis creation with an SFM that provided an immediately functional lumen. All patients achieved the primary study endpoint. There were no reported cases of small or large bowel obstruction, anastomotic bleed, leak, re-intervention, or death. The most common adverse events included abdominal pain (35 cases), nausea (21), and incision site pain (21), with only four serious adverse events. There were no serious device-related adverse events. Median (interquartile range) procedure and anastomosis creation times were 92 (76, 116) and 13 (9, 17) minutes, respectively.ConclusionThis clinical trial highlights the technical and clinical success and the short-term safety profile of an SFM system that provides an immediately functional lumen for the creation of a small bowel anastomosis. The results provide a robust platform for future controlled and comparative studies to more comprehensively define the role of magnetic compression anastomosis in gastrointestinal surgery.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13211-8.Keywords: Anastomosis, Magnet, Compression, Small intestine, Laparoscopy, Robotic, Surgery

Strategies to prevent gastrointestinal anastomotic complications include standardizing technique (staplers), adapting to predicted tissue thickness (differing staple heights), assessing tissue perfusion, and decreasing variability in technique to allow for appropriate tissue accommodation (powered staplers that take control of fastest firing speed from the surgeon) [1–5]. Beyond short-term events (leaks and bleeds), mid to long-term complications, including stricture and ulcers, are not well quantified but add potential risk of harm (intervention) and increased costs.

Initially described in 1978, magnetic compression anastomosis may be the most adaptable of techniques, as compression takes place over hours/days (compared to seconds/minutes), with the potential to allow for enhanced tissue accommodation. Preclinical work demonstrates that as compared to sutured/stapled anastomoses, magnetic anastomoses result in less fibrosis, tissue distortion, bacterial ingress, and chronic inflammatory changes [6–9]. Anastomotic burst pressures are significantly higher than sutured/stapled anastomoses and approach native tissue. This has been demonstrated when measured at 60 min in an ex vivo model utilizing explanted porcine intestine as well as in animal survival studies when measured at 6 weeks [7, 10]. A limitation of the magnetic technique has been the lack of ability to create an immediately functional lumen. This has relegated this technique largely to procedures in which an immediately functional lumen is not necessary (primarily bipartition procedures for bariatric/metabolic indications) [8, 9, 11–16].

The Flexagon self-forming magnet (SFM) system (GI Windows, Westwood, MA) (Supplementary Fig. 1A) consists of a chain of eight tube-shaped magnet segments. After the surgeon makes an enterotomy, a temporary silicone implant (OTOLoc) is placed to capture and provide a uniform fixed opening for the enterotomy (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The two magnets are then connected after being introduced and deployed within the lumen. (Supplementary Fig. 1B).The outer diameter of the formed magnet is 25 mm. The silicone implant within the coupled magnets provides an immediately functional lumen (8–10 mm) that allows for immediate enteral flow (Supplementary Fig. 1C). A matured anastomosis is fully created after 8–14 days, after which the magnets will pass into the lumen and naturally excreted. Preclinical and early clinical trials have demonstrated feasibility and safety [7, 17].

The primary objective of this trial was to investigate the safety and effectiveness of the SFM system with an immediately functional lumen when used to create a small bowel anastomosis in subjects undergoing laparoscopic small bowel surgery.

Methods

This US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Investigational Device Exemption pivotal study was approved by a central Institutional Review Board (IRB) (WCG, Cary, NC) and by the individual institutional IRBs. This trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06454916). All safety data were reviewed by a Data Monitoring Committee (DMC).

Study design and participants

Adults (≥ 22 years) who were undergoing laparoscopic/robotic surgery requiring the creation of a small bowel anastomosis were screened for inclusion in this prospective, multicenter, single-arm pivotal clinical study. Inclusion/exclusion criteria are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Interventions

Intraoperative exclusion criteria are noted in Supplementary Table 2. The small bowel anastomosis was performed using the Flexagon 25 mm SFM system plus OTOLoc. The surgeon first measured the distance from the end of the stapled bowel to the planned enterotomy site at the anastomosis site. An antimesenteric enterotomy was created. If a duodenal anastomosis was being completed, the enterotomy was planned for the corner of the duodenal staple line. A silicone implant (OTOLoc) was placed through the enterotomy to completely capture, support, and provide a uniform opening for the enterotomy. The SFM was then deployed into the lumen. The second silicone implant and SFM were deployed in similar fashion. The surgeon then coupled the two SFMs together to initiate the creation of the magnetic anastomosis with an immediately functional lumen. The anastomosis was inspected to ensure a complete anastomosis without clamping of any associated mesentery. If the SFMs were not in the desired location, they were decoupled and the connection process was repeated.

All patients were followed for 60 days postoperatively (Supplementary Table 3). Patients underwent abdominal X-ray and upper gastrointestinal (UGI) series with small bowel follow through (SBFT) on postoperative day 7. If the SFM device was noted on imaging on day 7, an additional abdominal X-ray was conducted at postoperative day 30. All patients underwent an UGI with SBFT on day 30. If the SFM was noted at day 30, an additional abdominal X-ray was conducted at day 60. If the SFM was noted at day 60, an endoscopic removal procedure would be considered if deemed clinically indicated. The subject was followed until the device was removed/excreted.

Outcomes

The study’s primary endpoint was defined as successful anastomosis created with the SFM without procedure, device or target anastomosis-related reoperation through 30 days. Conversion to an open procedure due to inability to create the anastomosis using laparoscopic methods, intraoperative rescue procedure such as stapled/sutured rescue of failed SFM placement or intraoperative repair of adjacent organ was considered failure to achieve the primary endpoint. These failure events were defined as secondary outcomes. Additional data collected included procedure time, target anastomosis creation time (time to place/connect magnets defined as the time from enterotomy to magnet coupling), and measured distance between the target anastomosis and staple line.

All adverse events (AE) were documented and graded in accordance with Clavien–Dindo surgical classification. Additional safety outcomes are listed in Table 1. Any serious adverse event (SAE) that resulted in death or any three or more SAEs that were Clavien–Dindo classification of 3b or higher would result in immediate evaluation by the DMC for consideration for immediate suspension/termination of the trial.

Analysis

Enrolled patients were analyzed in two cohorts, the primary analysis cohort and a roll-in training cohort. A maximum of 15 roll-in patients (1–2 patients per site) were allotted for enrollment. As this technology was new to all of the participating surgeons, the roll-in training cohort of patients was implanted prior to enrollment of the primary analysis cohort at each site.

Sample size determination

Sample size was calculated using a precision approach for the primary endpoint of patient success (expected rate of 97%). Assuming 97% of patients meeting the primary endpoint, the sample size is determined to establish a precision of the estimate of the primary endpoint with a simple asymptotic (Wald) continuity-corrected confidence interval width no larger than 10%. An attrition rate of 5% was accounted for. A sample size of 74 treated patients was determined to be required to provide this level of precision (with the goal of having 70 evaluable patients). Under these conditions, the simple asymptotic (Wald) continuity-corrected 95% confidence interval around the primary endpoint would be 92.3 to 100.0%. Up to 15 patients were planned for enrollment in the roll-in cohort for training sites on the implant procedure.

Statistical analysis plan

The modified intention-to-treat (mITT) analysis set consisted of all primary analysis cohort patients with an attempt to place the device (defined as enterotomy performed). This population was utilized for the primary analysis for all study endpoints. The percentage of patients from the mITT (primary) population who achieved the primary endpoint was reported along with a two-sided 95% simple asymptotic (Wald) continuity-corrected confidence interval. Continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages of subjects in each category. All statistical tests were performed either as a two-sided test at the 0.05 level of significance or as a one-sided test at the 0.025 level of significance, unless otherwise specified. SAS® software was used for statistical analysis.

Role of the funding source

The funding source provided grant support to the participating centers for study design and data collection/analysis. Study investigators independently performed analysis, interpretation, writing, and final approval of the manuscript.

Results

Between September 2024 and March 2025, 102 patients (six centers) were evaluated for inclusion. 23 patients were screen failures or withdrew from the study prior to surgery. Eight patients were included in the roll-in cohort, which resulted in 71 patients in the primary analysis cohort (mITT cohort). Within the mITT cohort, there were 0 patients in the no implant cohort [patients for whom SFM device placements was abandoned intraprocedurally (enterotomy made but without implant)] (Fig. 1).

In the subset of patients undergoing jejunojejunostomy during REYGB, the median (IQR) time from initial enterotomy to magnet coupling was 18 (15.5, 21.5) minutes in the roll-in cohort (n = 8) as compared to 12 (8.5, 15.5) minutes in the mITT cohort (n = 55) (p = 0.08). Similar results are noted when evaluating only centers that performed 10 or more jejunojejunostomy procedures. The difference in time between the first five procedures as compared to the last five procedures (including all roll-in and mITT patients) was 15 (12, 16.25) minutes versus 11.5 (7, 14) minutes (p = 0.07). The median time (all patients) to complete each type of anastomosis (jejunojejunostomy, duodenoileostomy, and ileoileostomy) was 13.0, 20.5, and 9.0 min, respectively.

Out of 79 patients in the mITT and roll-in cohorts, 78 patients completed day 7 and day 30 imaging. One patient did not have an UGI with SBFT performed at day 30. Only 2 patients had a SFM noted within the GI tract at 30 days. 29 patients (mITT and roll-in cohorts) reported awareness of excretion of the magnets (asymptomatic). All patients who underwent the 30-day UGI with SBFT achieved anastomotic success (patent anastomosis) as evaluated by site-specific independent radiologists.

There were five device malfunctions noted in the entire cohort, none that resulted in an AE. In all reported malfunctions, either the OTOLoc cartridge or SureFire Flex laparoscopic delivery device for the SFM was the component that malfunctioned and most happened before the device was inserted into the patient. In all cases, another replacement device was successfully used.

At 60 days (78/79 patients), there were no reoperations. There were four SAEs noted in the entire cohort (mITT and roll-in cohorts). They included intraoperative small bowel perforation (Clavien–Dindo Class IIIb, n = 2, both recognized and treated at index operation), pneumonia (Clavien–Dindo Class II, n = 1), and Clostridium difficile colitis (Clavien–Dindo Class II, n = 1). Three of the SAEs occurred less than 7 days from the index operation with the final SAE (pneumonia) occurring at day 15.

Discussion

In this study, all patients achieved the primary endpoint of successful creation of a magnetic anastomosis with an immediately functional lumen without any anastomotic leaks, bleeding, or obstruction. The mechanism of magnetic anastomoses and preclinical data predict low expected anastomotic complications rates, secondary to decreased tissue trauma, distortion, inflammatory changes, bacterial infiltration, and fibrosis. There is also no residual foreign body/material [6–9].

Aside from imaging studies performed at postoperative day 7 and 30 (trial protocol), there were no changes in the usual postoperative pathway. The immediate functional lumen is approximately 8–10 mm in size with a final outlet size of approximately 25 mm after anastomotic maturation. Given the size of the lumen, early postoperative obstruction is a concern. In this cohort, there were no reports of obstruction related to either the lumen or final outlet size. Given an immediately functional lumen, no changes were/are required as it relates to postoperative diet progression. It is important to note that as per the participating surgeons’ standard postoperative bariatric surgery diet protocols, patients were maintained on a liquid diet for the first 2 weeks postoperatively. In the event of a stricture, standard endoscopic treatments can be utilized. In preclinical studies, the burst strength of magnetic anastomoses approaches that of native tissue [7, 10].

In this study, 8 patients underwent duodenal-Ileal anastomosis in which a portion of the duodenal staple line was incorporated into the completed anastomosis. There were no anastomotic complications with overlap of the SFM and staple lines. There were two intraoperative small bowel perforations during the trial, both occurring during the magnet placement procedure. Both were immediately recognized/repaired, and the magnetic technique was completed without need for conversion to another anastomotic technique. Both perforations were related to errors in technique related to introduction of the SFM system components.

The study protocol excluded patients with prior major surgery on the stomach, duodenum, hepatobiliary tree (excluding laparoscopic cholecystectomy/sleeve gastrectomy), pancreas or right colon. 48/79 (60.8%) patients in this study had prior abdominal surgery, including hysterectomy, myomectomy, cesarean section, ventral hernia repair, and appendectomy. There was one patient with an incidentally noted Meckel’s diverticulum (not resected) and two patients with intraoperative notation of intestinal rotational disorders without obstruction. There were no patients who had an obstruction or retained SFM suggesting safety in patients who have had prior abdominal/pelvic surgery.

The SFMs must pass through the ileocecal valve. There were no retained SFMs proximal to the ileocecal valve in this trial. No patient required endoscopic retrieval of SFMs. The pediatric surgery/gastroenterology literature provides insight [18]. The rate of intervention for blunt foreign bodies (i.e., coins) that have passed the pylorus is extremely low [19]. For comparison, the diameter of the SFM is approximately the size of a quarter.

Patients cannot undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies until the magnets have passed; in patients who may require an MRI soon after surgery, surgeons should exercise caution in using SFMs. For abdominal procedures, there are no contraindications for use of SFMs in patients with pacemakers and/or defibrillators. In our cohort, one patient had a pacemaker.

Given the diversity in procedures, anastomotic locations, and variable volume per center, it is difficult to estimate the learning curve for the SFM technique described in this paper. All of the investigators used the SFMs in patients for the first time during this trial. Although we will be able to more fully elucidate the learning curve with increasing number of procedures performed, it appears to be relatively short with experienced surgeons. Although the authors believe that this technology increases ease of performing intracorporeal anastomoses, this technology can be used in open or minimally invasive surgery.

Long-term/late complications related to small intestinal anastomoses, specifically with Roux-en-Y type reconstruction, are rare but include intussusception and obstruction. Risk factors for intussusception are poorly understood, but larger final anastomotic size may be a risk factor. In one of the largest series published on this topic, jejunojejunostomy length greater than 60 mm may be a contributing factor [20]. The final outlet size of the magnetic anastomosis is approximately 25 mm. Long-term follow-up data are required to determine if this has a protective effect as it relates to intussusception.

Magnetic techniques do not eliminate the need for staplers or for sutured/stapled anastomotic techniques. Staplers are still widely used for consistency during resection procedures. The magnetic technique adds to a portfolio of anastomotic techniques with a common goal of improving outcomes. With the current technology, in patients who require more than one anastomosis, only one can be done using the SFM.

There are a number of limitations of this study. As a single-arm safety/feasibility study, there is a lack of a comparison group. A criticism of this trial is the use of the magnetic technique in small bowel anastomosis, given the low incidence of early complications with standard stapled/sutured techniques. Demonstrating safety and feasibility of the technique is the first important step toward application in higher risk and more challenging cases, including esophageal, gastric, colon and rectal, as well as in acute care indications with diseased intestine.

Enabling technologies do tend to add immediate cost; however, when analyzing overall impact to the health care system, it is important to consider both immediate and long-term costs. Short-term (30–60 day) follow-up is important for early anastomotic complications. However, long-term complications in small bowel anastomosis are infrequent and poorly quantified (i.e., intussusception). Registry studies, specifically as it relates to incidence of short-, medium-, and long-term anastomotic-related complications will better quantify the cost associated with the magnetic technique. Increasingly, cost need to be viewed not as a single event, but in the global scale of a patient’s medical lifespan.

Common themes across all technological advances to reduce anastomotic complications include personalization to tissue characteristics, decreasing variability, and improving consistency in technique. Despite advances in staplers as well as technologies to assess tissue perfusion, anastomotic complications persist. In the presence of tissue perfusion, other factors including tissue thickness, trauma, edema, and the wound healing response likely contribute to outcomes. Although a large focus remains on high-risk anastomoses (esophagus, colorectal, and acute care), medium- and late-term complications (e.g., marginal ulcers and strictures) do not have easy prevention mechanisms. The anastomotic technique that is most enabling has to have several characteristics, including reproducibility, consistency, and the ability to customize the healing process to a patient’s individual tissue thickness and inflammatory response. This is where the preclinical data suggest the magnetic anastomosis with immediate functional lumen may have the biggest benefit without the need to change procedure technique.

Future directions include long-term registry studies (to capture real world experience and outcomes as it relates to medium- and longer-term small intestinal anastomotic issues), studies on learning curve and safety/outcome in surgeons with little experience with minimally invasive anastomoses, potential advantages in higher risk foregut, hindgut, and high-risk small bowel procedures, development of a portfolio of sizes of the immediately functional lumen as well as end-anastomotic size, hybrid endoscopic and minimally invasive techniques for foregut and colorectal applications, as well as the use in patients with more extensive intestinal surgical history (safety in patients with extensive bowel adhesions).

Conclusion

This multicenter study demonstrates the technical feasibility, clinical success, and short-term safety profile of a magnetic anastomosis technique with an immediately functional lumen when used for the creation of laparoscopic small intestinal anastomoses. Further study is required to determine the medium- and long-term outcome advantages of this technique.
