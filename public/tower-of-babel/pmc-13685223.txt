The interplay of gender, culture, and psychology in shaping entrepreneurial intentions in Sidama, Ethiopia: a mixed-methods, TPB-informed investigation in a collectivist African context
Authors: Samuel Hewiso, Wogene Markos Dumo, Abate Yesigate Taye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYouth unemployment is a pressing challenge in Ethiopia, and understanding the socio-psychological drivers of entrepreneurial intention is critical for policy design. However, most entrepreneurial intention research has been conducted in Western contexts, and the applicability of dominant models like the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to collectivist African settings remains undertheorized and empirically underexamined.MethodsThis study investigates how psychological factors and gender shape entrepreneurial intentions among urban youth in Sidama, Ethiopia, using a TPB-informed framework adapted for a collectivist context. A sequential mixed-methods design combined a survey of 384 young people (60% female, mean age 24.3 years) with 12 key informant interviews and 6 focus group discussions (48 participants). Binary logistic regression was employed after diagnostic checks confirmed model adequacy, with robustness checks including probit, linear probability, ordered logit, bootstrap, and subsample analyses.ResultsBinary logistic regression showed that self-efficacy (OR = 2.10, p < 0.001), risk tolerance (OR = 1.80, p < 0.001), and proactiveness (OR = 2.41, p < 0.001) significantly increased the likelihood of high entrepreneurial intention. Contrary to findings from many Western contexts, female respondents reported significantly higher entrepreneurial intentions than males (OR = 0.35, p < 0.001). Qualitative data revealed that this counter-intuitive pattern is driven by gendered economic necessity, culturally sanctioned trading niches for women, communal safety nets that enhance women's perceived behavioral control, and social stigma (Lawa/shame) that deters men from informal entrepreneurship.ConclusionsThe study demonstrates that entrepreneurial intention in collectivist African settings is not merely an individual cognition but a socially negotiated stance shaped by gendered norms, communal expectations, and culturally specific definitions of risk and control. We propose that TPB constructs, particularly perceived behavioral control and subjective norms, require cultural adaptation to incorporate communal resources and social sanctions. Policy implications include gender-differentiated entrepreneurship support and leveraging community structures (e.g., idir groups) for youth enterprise development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05236-4.

Introduction

Ethiopia’s rapidly growing youth population faces an acute unemployment crisis, with urban youth unemployment exceeding 25% [33]. In the Sidama region, a recently established regional state with a predominantly post-agrarian economy, formal wage employment is scarce, and policymakers increasingly look to entrepreneurship for job creation. However, entrepreneurial activity remains mostly necessity-driven and confined to the informal sector. While structural barriers such as limited access to finance, inadequate infrastructure, and burdensome regulations are well documented [15, 23], the individual-level motivational factors that precede venture creation entrepreneurial intentions remain underexplored, particularly in studies of youth career transitions [25].

Research on entrepreneurial intentions has been dominated by studies conducted in Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies [16]. The uncritical application of Western models like Ajzen’s [2] Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to settings such as Sidama a collectivist society with strong communal ties, patriarchal norms, and unique institutions like idir (community insurance) and lawa (shame) raises concerns about cultural validity. This study addresses these concerns by proposing a TPB-informed framework that explicitly theorizes how collectivist cultural features specifically communal safety nets and social sanctions may reshape the meaning and operation of perceived behavioral control and subjective norms, rather than simply applying Western measures in a new context. Moreover, the gender-intention link, typically showing a male advantage in Western samples [30], may not hold in contexts where women’s economic participation is simultaneously necessitated by survival needs and enabled by culturally sanctioned market roles [1, 27].

Despite a growing body of entrepreneurial intention research globally (see recent systematic reviews by [20, 26], empirical evidence from post-agrarian, collectivist regions of sub-Saharan Africa remain scant. Specifically, little is known about (a) how core TPB constructs and psychological traits operate in a context where perceived behavioral control is deeply embedded in communal safety nets, (b) how gender intersects with cultural scripts to produce counter-intuitive patterns of entrepreneurial intention, and (c) whether standard quantitative measures of risk and proactiveness capture locally meaningful constructs. The Sidama region provides a particularly instructive case because of its strong collectivist institutions, recently devolved regional governance creating new economic opportunities, and high youth unemployment that makes entrepreneurship a pressing policy concern. The region's distinct cultural features, including the idir system and lawa social sanctions, offer an ideal setting to examine how cultural context moderates the relationship between psychological antecedents and entrepreneurial intention. Literature review and theoretical framework

The theory of planned behavior in cross-cultural perspective

Ajzen’s [2] Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) identifies three proximal determinants of intention: attitude toward the behaviour, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control (PBC). Meta-analyses confirm the TPB’s predictive power for entrepreneurial intentions across diverse settings [20, 29]. However, the relative weight and even the meaning of its components are not universal. Autio et al. [5] demonstrated that cultural practices particularly individualism versus collectivism moderate the strength of TPB predictors, with subjective norms exerting greater influence in collectivist societies. In such environments, the opinion of family, elders, and the broader community may outweigh personal attitudes, and PBC may encompass the ability to mobilise communal resources rather than pure individual agency.

Despite these insights, the vast majority of TPB-intention studies have been conducted in WEIRD contexts; very few have rigorously tested or adapted the model in collectivist, low-income African settings where communal obligations and social embeddedness redefine the nature of control [18, 27]. As Wigren-Kristoferson et al. [32] argue, entrepreneurial action is deeply embedded in social structures that both enable and constrain, yet this embeddedness is rarely theorised within TPB-based intention models. The present study directly addresses this gap by proposing a culturally adapted TPB framework that explicitly incorporates communal resources (as a component of PBC) and social sanctions (as a component of subjective norms) in the Sidama context.

Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework guiding this study, illustrating how the standard TPB constructs are extended to incorporate collectivist cultural features. Specifically, we propose that perceived behavioral control in this context comprises both individual agency (self-efficacy) and access to communal safety nets; subjective norms are shaped not only by family and peer expectations but also by culturally sanctioned shame (lawa) and approval; and attitudes toward entrepreneurship are inflected by gendered cultural scripts that define appropriate economic roles for men and women. Entrepreneurial self-efficacy: universal predictor, culturally shaped antecedents

Entrepreneurial self-efficacy (ESE) the belief in one’s ability to successfully execute entrepreneurial tasks [22] is widely regarded as the motivational core of PBC. Grounded in Bandura’s [8] social cognitive theory, ESE consistently emerges as one of the strongest predictors of entrepreneurial intention in meta-analyses [26, 34]. Recent studies in developing country contexts have confirmed this relationship: Bahaw et al. [6], in a longitudinal study of vocational students in Trinidad and Tobago, found that exposure to entrepreneurship education significantly enhanced entrepreneurial self-efficacy, which in turn predicted entrepreneurial intentions. Similarly, Bahaw et al. [7] demonstrated that post-training outcomes, including enhanced self-efficacy, mediated the relationship between entrepreneurship training and micro-enterprise development. In challenging environments, ESE also interacts with resilience to sustain entrepreneurial ambition [12].

However, the sources of ESE are context-dependent. In Western settings, mastery experiences and personal accomplishments are primary; in many African societies, ESE may be nurtured through observational learning within extended families, traditional apprenticeship, and community validation [18]. In Sidama, where small-scale trading is often transmitted intergenerationally, ESE may be built through witnessing same-gender relatives succeed in culturally approved ventures. Risk tolerance and proactiveness: traits re-contextualized

Trait-based approaches have been debated, yet meta-analyses confirm that risk propensity and proactiveness are robustly associated with entrepreneurial entry and intention [28]. Foundational work by Kihlstrom and Laffont [17] theorized that less risk-averse individuals are more likely to become entrepreneurs. Risk tolerance is conventionally defined as the willingness to accept uncertain outcomes [31]. In subsistence-level economies, however, risk is pervasive and often managed collectively. Assmann and Ehrl [4] argue that collectivist cultures share risk through familial and communal networks, which may inflate self-reported risk tolerance because the individual’s perceived exposure is buffered. Thus, in Sidama, “risk tolerance” may partly capture confidence in the communal safety net rather than a purely personal trait. Recent work by Bahaw et al. [7] in the Caribbean context similarly found that risk perception among micro-entrepreneurs was shaped by social networks and community support structures, suggesting that risk tolerance in developing economies is socially embedded rather than purely individual.

Proactiveness taking initiative, anticipating future opportunities, and acting to shape one’s environment is a cornerstone of entrepreneurial orientation [21] and a strong predictor of entrepreneurial intentions [28]. Bolton and Lane [10] developed a validated individual entrepreneurial orientation scale that includes proactiveness. In low-opportunity environments, proactiveness may be not merely advantageous but essential, as individuals must actively create opportunities rather than wait for formal employment. Gender and entrepreneurial intentions: a contested terrain

A substantial body of literature from Western and Middle Eastern contexts reports that men exhibit higher entrepreneurial intentions and higher ESE than women [30]. In Saudi Arabia, for example, restrictive social norms and institutional barriers suppress female entrepreneurial intention even when personal attitudes are favourable [3]. However, evidence from sub-Saharan Africa challenges this universal male advantage. Adom [1] documented that women in Ghana are highly active in necessity-driven entrepreneurship, often outnumbering men in small-scale trade, a pattern linked to gendered poverty and the necessity to provide for households [13]. Muhammad [24] found that gender differences in intention among Nigerian students were nuanced and depended on cultural and family expectations. A comprehensive review by Ojong et al. [27] concluded that female entrepreneurship in Africa is deeply rooted in economic necessity, culturally sanctioned markets (particularly food and retail), and extended family support systems that lower entry barriers.

The gender-intention relationship in Africa is therefore not simply a matter of individual disposition but is fundamentally shaped by what African feminist scholars term "socially embedded agency" [1], the way women's economic actions are simultaneously constrained and enabled by cultural norms, household responsibilities, and community expectations. This perspective, rooted in African feminist economics [13], challenges the individualistic assumptions of Western entrepreneurial models and highlights the need for contextualized gender analysis. These findings suggest that the direction and magnitude of gender differences in entrepreneurial intention are highly context-specific. In Sidama, where women have long controlled certain market niches and face strong social expectations to contribute to household income, female intention may equal or exceed that of men an outcome that would contradict standard Western predictions. Therefore, we do not hypothesize a directional gender effect a priori, but treat gender as an exploratory variable whose influence is expected to be moderated by cultural context [30].

The Sidama cultural setting: collectivism, communal safety nets, and gendered scripts

Sidama society is characterised by collectivism and a dense web of communal institutions. The idir (community-based insurance association) provides financial and social support during crises, while lawa (shame) acts as a powerful social sanction. These features directly shape entrepreneurial behaviour. On the one hand, the safety net reduces the personal cost of business failure; on the other hand, ventures that deviate from community norms risk bringing shame upon the entire family. Wigren-Kristoferson et al. [32] highlight how such embeddedness can both enable and constrain entrepreneurial action.

Gender-specific cultural scripts are particularly pronounced. Women’s involvement in small-scale trading selling spices, vegetables, coffee is not only accepted but expected, forming part of their identity as providers. For young men, however, the ideal remains formal salaried employment, especially in government. Informal entrepreneurship is often stigmatised as a sign of academic failure. Consequently, the same cultural fabric produces opposing effects: it legitimises and de-risks entrepreneurship for women, while stigmatising it for men.

In light of the foregoing, three clear gaps emerge. First, TPB-based intention models have rarely been tested in collectivist African settings where communal resources and social sanctions fundamentally redefine PBC and subjective norms. Second, the interaction between psychological traits and gender in such contexts is poorly understood; existing studies that document a female advantage often lack the mixed-methods depth to explain the underlying mechanisms. Third, the measurement of risk tolerance and proactiveness may require cultural reinterpretation, but empirical studies that pair quantitative surveys with qualitative exploration are scarce. The present study addresses these gaps by integrating a context-adapted TPB framework with qualitative thematic analysis.

Methodology

Research design

A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (QUAN → qual) was employed, following established procedures for mixed-methods research [14]. The quantitative phase surveyed a stratified random sample of urban youth to identify predictors of entrepreneurial intention. The qualitative phase used focus groups and interviews to explain quantitative patterns, particularly the unexpected gender result. This design was chosen because it allows statistical patterns to be identified first, and then explored in depth through participants' own narratives, providing both breadth and depth of understanding.

Sampling and participants

Quantitative sample

The target population comprised urban youth aged 18–34 in seven towns across Sidama (Hawassa, Yirgalem, Leku, and four other major towns). Using Cochran’s formula for a finite population with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, a minimum sample of 384 was determined. Stratified random sampling ensured proportional representation by town, gender, and educational background. To enable robust gender-disaggregated analysis, females were deliberately oversampled (60%) relative to their estimated population proportion in the target age group (approximately 52%). Females were deliberately oversampled (60%) to allow robust gender-disaggregated analysis.

Qualitative sample

Six focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted, stratified by gender and entrepreneurial status (aspiring vs. active), involving a total of 48 participants (24 female, 24 male). Additionally, 12 key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with successful entrepreneurs (n = 4), Micro and Small Enterprise (MSE) development officers (n = 4), and community elders (n = 4). All qualitative participants were selected purposively for their depth of relevant experience. Recruitment was conducted through local administrative offices and community networks; participants were approached based on their roles and expertise. Data saturation was determined when successive interviews and focus groups yielded no new themes, which occurred after the 10th interview and the 5th focus group; the additional two interviews and one focus group were conducted to confirm saturation.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using maximum likelihood estimation

The hypothesized factor structures fitted the data adequately: comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.94, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.06, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0.05 for the combined measurement model. Factor loadings ranged from 0.58 to 0.82, all significant at p < 0.001. Average variance extracted (AVE) values were: entrepreneurial intention = 0.61, self-efficacy = 0.54, risk tolerance = 0.50, and proactiveness = 0.57 all exceeding the recommended 0.50 threshold (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Discriminant validity was confirmed as the square root of each AVE exceeded the inter-construct correlations, and the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratios were all below 0.85, indicating distinct constructs. Detailed fit indices and factor loadings are available in the supplementary material.

Qualitative protocols

Semi-structured guides explored perceptions of entrepreneurship as a career, perceived barriers and facilitators, the role of family and community, the meaning of risk, and gender-specific experiences. All interviews and FGDs were conducted in Sidamuafoo, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English by bilingual researchers.

Data analysis

Model specification

The dependent variable, entrepreneurial intention, was originally measured as a continuous average of five items (range 1–5). Preliminary inspection revealed a heavily skewed distribution: 65.4% of respondents fell in the "high intention" category (mean ≥ 4), 32% in "medium" (mean 3–3.9), and only 2.6% in "low" (mean < 3). This extreme imbalance caused quasi-complete separation in the initially planned generalized ordered logit model, producing highly inflated and unstable odds ratios. We therefore constructed a binary outcome:Y = 1 if average intention score ≥ 4 (“high entrepreneurial intention”)Y = 0 if average intention score < 4 (“low/medium entrepreneurial intention”)

Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s [11] six-phase approach. Two researchers independently coded the translated transcripts using NVivo 12, achieving initial inter-coder agreement of 91%. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Themes were developed both inductively from the data and deductively by mapping onto an adapted TPB framework.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was granted by Hawassa University's College of Business and Economics Ethical Review Board on 15 March 2025 (reference HU/CBE-07/2017). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; verbal consent was audio-recorded for those with limited literacy. Confidentiality was ensured by using identification codes, and data were stored on encrypted devices.

The robustness checks (Table 4) confirmed the stability of the results. The probit model produced odds-ratio equivalents that were almost identical to the logistic model, and all predictors remained significant (p < 0.01). Qualitative findings

Thematic analysis uncovered three overarching themes that illuminate the quantitative results.

Theme 1: gendered social scripts and opposed normative pressures

Women consistently described small-scale trading as an expected, intergenerational activity. One female participant in Hawassa explained: “My mother and grandmother were sellers in the market. For me, it is not really a choice; it is a duty. Selling spices lets me feed my children today without relying on a husband who may leave or have no work himself.” Another female FGD participant in Yirgalem added: "Every woman in my family has a small business; it is how we survive. When I told my mother I wanted to start selling vegetables, she was proud. She gave me her old market spot." For women, entrepreneurship was thus a socially legitimate and often unavoidable livelihood strategy.

In contrast, young men faced intense pressure to secure formal, salaried employment. A male participant in Leku stated: “Starting a small business is what you do if you have failed in school. It is a sign that you could not get a good education. For me, failure in business would bring shame (lawa) to my family. I would rather wait for a government job, even for years, than face that.”

Another male participant in Hawassa echoed this sentiment: "My father worked for the government for 30 years. He expects me to do the same. If I start selling in the market, people will say, 'What happened to his education?' It would shame my entire family." These opposing normative currents explain why men reported lower entrepreneurial intention despite a generally favorable societal attitude toward entrepreneurship.

Theme 2: communal safety nets as a form of perceived behavioral control

Both genders acknowledged that collective institutions, especially idir and extended family networks, provide tangible and psychological safety nets. An elder noted: “We support our young people, especially our daughters and sisters. If a young woman wants to start a trade, her family and her idir will give her a small loan. We are her security. She knows she will not starve if her business has a bad month.” A female entrepreneur in Leku explained how this works in practice: "When I started my small grocery shop, my idir group gave me 2,000 birrs. I didn't have to go to a bank. And my sisters help me run the shop when I am sick. I am not alone." This communal backing directly increased women’s PBC, making the prospect of business failure less catastrophic. For men, however, accessing such support was often conditional on pursuing socially approved ventures, further narrowing their permissible entrepreneurial pathways.

Theme 3: the social re-framing of risk

When discussing risk, participants spoke not of financial loss but of social consequences. For men, risk was almost synonymous with shame and loss of status; for women, the greater risk was failing to provide for their children. A female FGD participant summarized: “If I do nothing, my children go hungry. That is the real risk. The market may be bad some days, but we survive together.” Another female participant added: "We women are used to managing risk every day we stretch a small amount of money to feed the family. Running a business is the same skill. "Thus, the high self-reported “risk tolerance” among women, as captured by the survey, may not reflect a personality trait but a rational response to the high social and economic cost of inaction.

Taken together, these themes demonstrate that entrepreneurial intention in Sidama is not merely an individual cognition but a socially negotiated stance, shaped by gendered norms, communal expectations, and culturally specific definitions of risk and control. Discussion

This study sought to understand the psychological and gendered drivers of entrepreneurial intention among urban youth in Sidama. The findings both confirm the relevance of the TPB-informed framework and reveal important contextual specificities.

First, self-efficacy and proactiveness emerged as the strongest psychological predictors of high intention (supporting H1 and H3), consistent with global meta-analyses [26, 28]. However, the qualitative findings suggest that ESE in Sidama is not built solely through individual mastery experiences, as Western theories propose. Instead, it is nourished by observational learning within entrepreneurial families and by community validation, echoing Kuada's [18] emphasis on the communal nurturing of entrepreneurial capabilities in Africa. The robust effect of proactiveness aligns with the notion that, in opportunity-scarce environments, a proactive disposition is not merely helpful but essential for creating any viable livelihood. This extends the findings of Bahaw et al. [6], who found that entrepreneurship education enhanced self-efficacy in a Caribbean context, by showing that in the Sidama context, self-efficacy is primarily built through family and community channels rather than formal training.

Second, the gender reversal women reporting significantly higher intentions than men extends the emerging African evidence that challenges the universal male advantage in entrepreneurial intention [1, 27]. Our mixed-methods design allowed us to disentangle the mechanisms. The qualitative narratives revealed that female intention is fuelled by economic necessity, culturally sanctioned trading roles, and the safety net provided by communal institutions, all of which enhance PBC. For men, by contrast, subjective norms pull in the opposite direction: informal entrepreneurship is stigmatised, and waiting for a formal job carries less social risk than starting a "shameful" business. These findings imply that subjective norms and PBC are not gender-neutral,they are laden with different meanings and weights for men and women. This aligns with and extends Shinnar et al.'s [30] argument that culture moderates the gender-intention relationship not just in degree but in direction.

Third, the results compel a re-examination of the risk tolerance construct (supporting H2, but with important caveats). The high odds ratio for risk tolerance must be interpreted in light of the qualitative data showing that risk is socially buffered. When communal institutions absorb a significant portion of potential losses, the self-reported willingness to take risks may be a proxy for trust in these institutions rather than an innate preference. This aligns with Assmann and Ehrl's [4] argument that collectivist cultures share and thus reduce individual risk, and with Bahaw et al.'s [7] finding that risk perception among micro-entrepreneurs is shaped by social networks. Future research should develop culturally sensitive scales that separate personal risk propensity from perceived communal support.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes three distinctive theoretical contributions to the entrepreneurial intention literature.

First, we contribute to the cross-cultural adaptation of TPB by demonstrating that in collectivist African settings, the construct of perceived behavioral control (PBC) requires extension beyond individual agency to include communal resources. Our qualitative evidence shows that the idir system and extended family networks function as a form of "collective PBC" a shared confidence that community support will be available in times of business difficulty. This extends Autio et al.'s [5] work by specifying how collectivism moderates TPB relationships: not merely by increasing the weight of subjective norms, but by fundamentally redefining the nature of control itself from an individual to a communal resource.

Second, we refine the understanding of the gender-intention relationship in non-Western contexts. While much of the literature frames gender differences in entrepreneurial intention as a problem of female deficit [30], our findings reveal that in Sidama, female intention surpasses male intention and this is not a sign of empowerment in the Western feminist sense, but rather a reflection of gendered economic necessity and culturally sanctioned roles. This aligns with African feminist perspectives [1, 13] that emphasise women's agency as socially embedded and constrained by, rather than independent of, cultural norms.

Third, we contribute to the trait literature by problematising the measurement and interpretation of risk tolerance in collectivist settings. Our mixed-methods approach reveals that risk tolerance, as measured by standard scales, conflates individual risk propensity with confidence in community safety nets. This has implications for cross-cultural comparisons: observed differences in risk tolerance between WEIRD and non-WEIRD samples may reflect differences in social structures as much as differences in individual psychology.

Contextual boundaries and generalizability

The findings of this study are situated within the specific context of urban Sidama, a collectivist, post-agrarian society with strong communal institutions and gendered market roles. Several features of this context are likely to be shared with other parts of sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in the East African region: strong extended family ties, community-based insurance (similar to chamas in Kenya or tontines in West Africa), and culturally sanctioned roles for women in small-scale trade. However, other findings may be specific to Sidama: the particular strength of the idir system, the recent regional devolution that has created new economic opportunities, and the distinct cultural scripts around lawa and entrepreneurship.

We suggest that the core theoretical insights that PBC is communally embedded, that subjective norms are gendered, and that risk tolerance is socially buffered may be transferable to other collectivist African settings where similar institutions exist. However, the specific direction and magnitude of gender differences, the strength of family influences, and the particular forms of social sanction may vary across countries and regions. The findings are less likely to be directly transferable to individualistic contexts or to collectivist societies with different institutional arrangements (e.g., East Asian collectivism, where family expectations may operate differently). Future research should conduct comparative studies across multiple African countries to establish the generalizability of our findings and to identify the specific cultural and institutional factors that moderate the relationships we have identified.

Policy implications

The study underscores the inadequacy of one-size-fits-all entrepreneurship programs. For young men, interventions should explicitly target the stigma attached to informal business and present entrepreneurship as a viable, high-status career, perhaps through role-model campaigns and formalization pathways. For women, the priority is to move beyond microcredit and survivalist trading toward growth-oriented support affordable childcare, market infrastructure, and protection from harassment. Importantly, policy should leverage, rather than ignore, existing communal structures; channeling support through idir groups and cooperatives can capitalize on local notions of control and collective efficacy. Given that formal sector job creation in Ethiopia has not kept pace with population growth, promoting youth entrepreneurship in culturally appropriate ways may be the most feasible pathway to employment generation.

Conclusion, limitations, and future research

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the entrepreneurial intentions of urban youth in Sidama are powerfully shaped by a complex interplay of psychological traits (self-efficacy, proactiveness), demographic factors (gender), and the mediating influence of a collectivist culture that simultaneously enables and constrains. The dramatic reversal of typical gender patterns underscores the profound danger of importing Western psychological and entrepreneurial models without critical adaptation. Entrepreneurship in this context is not merely an individual career choice but a socially embedded and culturally scripted livelihood strategy, the contours of which are defined by a nexus of cultural norms, gendered expectations, and economic necessity. We propose that future applications of the TPB in collectivist African settings should explicitly operationalise "collective PBC" (confidence in community support) and gendered subjective norms as culturally adapted constructs, and should employ mixed-methods designs to capture the meaning-making processes that quantitative scales cannot reveal.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design captures intention at a single point in time, not eventual startup behavior or venture success. Second, the focus on urban youths in one Ethiopian region, while providing depth, limits the generalisability of the findings to rural populations or other countries with different cultural structures. Third, although the mixed methods approach is a strength, the qualitative sample, while rich and information-heavy, is not statistically representative of the larger population. Fourth, we acknowledge that the dichotomisation of entrepreneurial intention, while necessary for statistical reasons, results in a loss of information that could obscure meaningful variation among respondents in the medium-intention category. We have mitigated this through multiple robustness checks and by discussing the implications, but future research should consider alternative approaches such as ordered categorical models with larger samples or continuous measures with appropriate transformations. Fifth, our study did not include separate measures of the full TPB constructs (attitude toward entrepreneurship and subjective norms were not measured in the quantitative survey), limiting our ability to test the complete TPB model quantitatively. We have addressed this by reframing our contribution as a TPB-informed investigation with qualitative exploration of these constructs, and we encourage future research to incorporate validated measures of all TPB components for more comprehensive testing.
