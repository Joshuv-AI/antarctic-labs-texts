Efficacy and safety of sublingual cilostazol and dexborneol for the treatment of acute ischaemic stroke: a randomised phase 2 trial in China
Authors: Yicong Wang, Yi Sui, Hui Qu, Yuesong Pan, Ling Guan, Baoshan Qiu, Liping Liu, Zhongrong Miao, Lei Wang, Philip M. ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEndovascular treatment has emerged as a cornerstone intervention for acute ischaemic stroke caused by large-vessel occlusion. However, a subset of patients experience futile recanalisation, correlating with unfavourable long-term outcomes. We aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of Y-6 sublingual tablets (cilostazol and dexborneol) in ischaemic stroke patients with endovascular treatment.MethodsThis randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial in 33 centres in China prospectively enrolled patients aged 35–80 years, who had ischaemic stroke with large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation within 24 h of onset, with an NIHSS score 7–25 at randomisation. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1:1:1:1) to the Y-6 high-dose group, Y-6 low-dose group, cilostazol high-dose group, cilostazol low-dose group, and placebo group for 28 days. The modified intention-to-treat analysis included the patients receiving at least one dose of the intervention. The primary efficacy outcome was the 90-day mRS 0–1 score, and the primary safety outcome was symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage at 28 days. The trial has been registered on ClinicalTrials.govNCT06138834.FindingsBetween Dec 14, 2023, and Dec 25, 2024, of 300 enrolled Chinese patients, 294 patients were included in the intention-to-treat analysis with 213 (72.4%) male. A favourable functional outcome (mRS 0–1) at 90 days occurred in 25 patients (44.6%) in the Y-6 high-dose group (RR 1.25, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.79–1.97; p = 0.34), 29 patients (46.0%) in the Y-6 low-dose group (RR 1.29, 95% CI 0.83–2.00; p = 0.25), 25 patients (41.7%) in the cilostazol high-dose group (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.74–1.85; p = 0.51), 22 patients (37.3%) in the cilostazol low-dose group (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.64–1.69; p = 0.86), and 20 patients (35.7%) in the placebo group. Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage at 28 days occurred in 1 patient (1.8%) in the Y-6 high-dose group, 3 patients (4.8%) in the Y-6 low-dose group, 2 patients (3.3%) in the cilostazol high-dose group, 4 patients (6.8%) in the cilostazol low-dose group, and 2 patients (3.6%) in the placebo group. No significant differences in efficacy and safety outcomes were found compared with that in the placebo group. Serious adverse events within 90 days occurred in 14 patients (25.0%) in the Y-6 high-dose group, 18 patients (28.6%) in the Y-6 low-dose group, 18 patients (30.0%) in the cilostazol high-dose group, 24 patients (40.7%) in the cilostazol low-dose group, and 14 patients (25.0%) in the placebo group.InterpretationOur findings indicate that, for patients with ischaemic stroke undergoing endovascular treatment for large vessel occlusion, Y-6 sublingual tablets (cilostazol and dexborneol) were safe with tolerance. Although the trial did not demonstrate statistically significant efficacy on the primary outcome, the signal toward improved functional outcomes without haemorrhage risk supports the biological plausibility of the intervention and justifies further investigation. Large-scale phase 3 clinical trials are warranted to further this research.FundingThe National Natural Science Foundation of China; Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project; Beijing Municipal Science & Technology Commission; Capital’s Funds for Health Improvement and Research; National Key R&D Program of China.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

To improve functional prognosis in patients with acute ischaemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion, a sublingual tablet combining cilostazol and dexborneol together was used to facilitate absorption and ameliorate cerebral microcirculation. We conducted a literature search of PubMed using the search term “(endovascular therapy or endovascular treatment) AND (cilostazol and dexborneol)” for the period of database inception up to May 4th, 2026, without any language restrictions. We found no clinical study that explored the efficacy of cilostazol and dexborneol in acute large vessel occlusion patients with large infarction.

Added value of this study

This phase 2 trial with randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, placebo-controlled design was to evaluate Y-6 sublingual tablets (containing cilostazol and dexborneol) of patients aged 35–80 years with acute ischaemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion. Participants were randomised in a 1:1:1:1:1 ratio to receive either Y-6 high-dose group, Y-6 low-dose group, cilostazol high-dose group, cilostazol low-dose group, and placebo group for 28 days. This study enrolled 300 patients and demonstrated that Y6 sublingual tablets did not improve the 90-day functional outcome and not associated with the risk of bleeding.

Implications of all the available evidence

Although the findings from this phase 2 trial of Y-6 sublingual tablets did not meet its primary efficacy outcome, and the findings should be regarded as strictly hypothesis-generating, the signal toward improved functional outcome supports the safety and tolerability of this intervention in this study population of patients with acute ischaemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion within 24 h of symptom onset. These results support the rationale for proceeding to a phase 3 trial to confirm efficacy and explore underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Stroke imposes a substantial global burden of disability and death, and endovascular treatment has been widely adopted for the management of patients with ischaemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 However, despite achieving varying degrees of vascular recanalization following endovascular treatment, around 50% of patients failed to show improvement in their prognosis.7,8 Cytoprotection is important in ischaemic stroke research and may be an effective treatment in those not responding well to recanalisation.9 The Stroke Treatment Academic Industry Roundtable X proposed that brain cytoprotection could enhance thrombolysis and thrombectomy, and that clinical trials should consider its use with reperfusion therapy.10,11 The concept of cytoprotection emphasises the protection of neurovascular unit brain cells, including endothelial and smooth muscle cells, neurones, astrocytes, microglia, and other cells.12 It involves preserving the structural integrity of the neurovascular unit, rendering multi-target drug research a promising prospect.

Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase 3 (PDE3) inhibitor, exerts vasodilatory and antiplatelet effects while also protecting cerebral endothelial cells, thereby stabilising the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and preserving neurovascular unit integrity.13,14 Dexborneol inhibits inflammatory factors, and combination edaravone and dexborneol injection and sublingual tablets have been utilised as neuroprotective drugs for ischaemic stroke.15 The doses-response relationship and synergistic effect between cilostazol and dexborneol had been discovered in the middle cerebral artery occlusion rat models, and dose exploration studies had also been conducted. Notably, sublingual tablets are non-invasive, absorbed quickly, and present a convenient and quick option in patients with stroke.

The objective of the phase 2 trial was to evaluate the safety and tolerability of Y-6 sublingual tablets, which are composed of cilostazol and dexborneol, and to assess the efficacy in ischaemic stroke patients with large vessel occlusion who are eligible for endovascular treatment.

Methods

Study design

The EFfects of Y-6 SUblingual Tablets for PaTients with AcUte Ischaemic StRokE (FUTURE) trial was a randomised, double-blind, double-dummy, placebo-controlled, multicentre trial conducted in 33 hospitals in China, which prospectively enrolled participants (Appendix pp. 2). Detailed methodological rationale and design specifications have been published previously.16

The results from our previous phase 2a study (NCT05836766) for Y-6 sublingual tablets have not been published currently. The phase 2a study was primarily conducted to gather data on the safety, tolerability, and potential MRI-based imaging exploratory outcomes (DCE and FEXI) of Y-6 in 60 patients. This phase 2 trial included several committees, as outlined within the Supplementary appendix. This includes a Steering Committee, an Executive Committee, a Data Safety and Monitoring Committee, a Statistical and Data Management Centre and a Clinical Event Adjudication Committee. The Steering Committee oversaw the study design and protocol amendments. An Executive Committee was responsible for the management of trial. A Data and Safety Monitoring Committee regularly reviewed safety data, holding meetings every 3 months. Statistical analyses and data management were conducted by the Statistical and Data Management Centre, which ensured trial data accuracy and integrity. The Clinical Event Adjudication Committee performed blinded adjudication of endpoint events including stroke and death. The protocol and statistical analysis plan were included in the Supplementary appendix.

Ethics

The protocol of the FUTURE trial has been approved by the Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. YW 2023- 038-02), and the ethics committees of all participating centres. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorised representatives prior to enrolment.

Participants

The main inclusion criteria for this study were ischaemic stroke with large-vessel occlusion within 24 h of onset and planned for endovascular treatment, age 35–80 years, a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 7–25, and signature of an informed consent form by themselves or their legal representatives. The lower age cutoff of 35 years was adopted in line with established thresholds in previous study; and the aetiology of intracranial large-vessel stenosis or occlusion in Chinese patients younger than 35 years is relatively complex and heterogeneous.17, 18, 19 Notably, patients with posterior circulation or bilateral lesions, NIHSS consciousness score 1a ≥2, history of intracranial haemorrhage or rare stroke causes, bleeding tendency, recent major trauma and surgery, and short expected survival were excluded. Patients who had used anticoagulants, tirofiban or cilostazol, and dexborneol were also excluded. A complete list of the exclusion criteria has been included in Appendix pp. 4–5. The sex of the patients in this study was based on the patient's self-report as male or female.

Randomisation and masking

Randomisation was implemented via a centralized, interactive web-based response system. A randomisation sequence was generated centrally using SAS 9.4 statistical software and stratified by participating centres. The groups consisted of patients dosed with 2 sublingual tablets and 2 oral tablets.

Participants were instructed to not directly chew or swallow any undissolved sublingual tablets. After an adequate assessment of swallowing, for patients with severe dysphagia, oral medications were administered via nasogastric tube feeding. In the Y-6 low-dose sublingual tablets group (25 mg cilostazol and 6.06 mg dexborneol), patients were required to take one Y-6 sublingual tablet (25 mg of cilostazol and 6 mg of dexborneol) with one Y-6 placebo tablet (0.06 mg of dexborneol) sublingually each time, and orally administer 2 half-tablets of cilostazol placebo (containing no cilostazol). In the Y-6 high-dose sublingual tablets group, patients were required to take two Y-6 sublingual tablets sublingually each time and orally take two half-tablets of cilostazol placebo. In the cilostazol low-dose group (25 mg cilostazol and 0.12 mg dexborneol), patients were required to take two Y-6 placebo tablets sublingually each time and orally take half a tablet of cilostazol (one tablet containing 50 mg of cilostazol) and half a tablet of cilostazol placebo. In the cilostazol high-dose group, patients were required to take two Y-6 placebo tablets sublingually each time and two half-tablets of cilostazol. In the placebo group (0.12 mg dexborneol), patients were required to take two Y-6 placebo tablets sublingually each time and orally take two half-tablets of cilostazol placebo.

Patients were unaware of treatment allocation. Throughout the drug production process, stringent measures were implemented to guarantee that the appearance, taste, and overall sensory characteristics of both the active drug and the placebo were indistinguishable. This was achieved through meticulous formulation adjustments and rigorous quality control checks, aimed at eliminating any potential biases that could arise from differences in tablet appearance or palatability.

To uphold the integrity of the double-blind study design, all individuals involved in drug administration including pharmacists, physicians, and nursing staff were blinded to treatment groups. Statisticians for data analysis and investigators collected data were also blinded to treatment allocation. The documentation would be maintained to record for both the dispensation and retrieval of investigational drugs.

Procedures

The first dose had to be taken as soon as possible after randomisation and before intravascular treatment. The second dose was administered at least 10 h after the first dose. The drugs were taken twice daily (Q12 ± 1 h) for a 28-day treatment period. Face-to-face visits were conducted at 24 ± 2 h, 7 ± 2 days, 28 ± 3 days, and 90 ± 7 days after randomisation; the number of visits could be increased as needed. During the visit, demographic and clinical information was collected, including detailed and accurate records of medication use, adverse events, and serious adverse events.

If there is a deterioration in neurological function or severe headache of participants, imaging should be performed to exclude intracranial haemorrhage. For cases of asymptomatic intracranial haemorrhage, medication was continued as normal following clinical assessment. Study medication was stopped after symptomatic haemorrhage. Additionally, in cases of serious adverse events or when patients were unable to continue taking the medication, the investigator took one of the following actions as clinically appropriate: suspending drug administration, adjusting the dose to once daily, or completely discontinuing the medication.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the proportion of patients with modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores of 0–1 at 90 ± 7 days after randomisation. The secondary efficacy outcomes were mRS score at 90 ± 7 days, changes in the NIHSS score from baseline to 24 ± 2 h, 7 ± 2 days, and 28 ± 3 days; early neurological deterioration (END), defined as an increase in the NIHSS score of ≥2 points or an increase in the motor function ≥1 point or an increase in consciousness level of ≥1 point and excluding the progression of disease due to intracranial haemorrhage by examination of cranial computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and exacerbation of disease due to other non-stroke causes at 24 h; combined vascular events (including stroke, myocardial infarction, or vascular death) at 90 days. Outcomes were evaluated by the clinical-event adjudication committee, which was blinded to treatment group.

The primary safety outcome was symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage at 28 ± 3 days after randomisation (Heidelberg haemorrhage classification). Other safety outcomes included any bleeding events, vascular deaths, all-cause deaths within 90 days after randomisation and adverse events/serious adverse events within 90 days. Outcome definitions are given in the Appendix pp. 6–9.

Statistical analysis

With no comparable study data from previous research to serve as a reference, determination of sample size was not solely based on the power for the primary efficacy outcome in phase 2 trial. This study is phase 2 research of an exploratory nature, designed to provide initial efficacy and safety data for Y-6 in patients with acute ischaemic stroke undergoing endovascular treatment, and, as such, we did not adjust the sample size for multiple groups.

The study is an exploratory trial designed with a prespecified statistical power of 80% and a two-tailed α level of 0.05. The primary efficacy outcome is the proportion of patients achieving a mRS score of 0–1 on day 90. We hypothesized that the effect size of Y-6 will be at least 28% higher than that of the placebo, assuming a placebo response rate of 35%. With a 1:1 allocation ratio between groups, approximately 47 participants are required per group. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the number of participants per group is increased to 60. Additionally, two dosage groups of cilostazol are included in the study.

Data were analysed according to the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle in the full analysis set, defined as all randomised patients who received at least one dose of study product. An additional analysis was done in a per-protocol group, defined as all patients finishing the treatment without major violation of the trial protocol. For the primary efficacy analysis, intergroup differences were examined via the chi-squared (χ2) test. The relative risk (RR) along with the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mRS score of 0–1 at 90 ± 7 days were computed using a generalised linear model. The primary outcome was also analysed in prespecified subgroups of participants. A sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome was additionally performed with the exact binomial test.

Secondary efficacy outcomes, including early neurological deterioration at 24 h and combined vascular events at 90 days (binary variables), were analysed as per the primary efficacy outcome. The mRS scores of all groups at 90 ± 7 days were compared using ordinal logistic regression to derive a common odds ratio and its corresponding 95% CI. Changes in the NIHSS score from baseline to 24 ± 2 h, 7 ± 2 days, and 28 ± 3 days within each group were presented as the least square mean (LS Mean) and standard error (SE). The mean differences between groups were estimated using a covariance analysis model.

For missing data in efficacy outcomes, if a participant had at least one post-treatment mRS score (excluding death as the reason), the last observation carried forward method would be applied. If no mRS scores were available, the missing score would be considered as 6 when referred to the analysis. No imputation would be applied for secondary efficacy outcomes. The post hoc analyses were adjusted for the stratification variable (research centre), with additional analyses further adjusting for sex, clinical history, pre-stroke mRS, NIHSS score, and time from stroke onset to endovascular puncture and treatment administration. Missing data were addressed via multiple imputation using a cumulative logistic regression model with 100 imputations (seed = 100070).

For safety-related outcomes, including symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage at 28 ± 3 days (Heidelberg haemorrhage classification), any bleeding events, vascular deaths, and all-cause deaths within 90 ± 7 days, intergroup differences were analysed using Fisher's exact test. The risk differences along with the 95% CI for safety-related outcomes were computed using a generalised linear model.

The widths of the CIs for the primary efficacy assessment have not been adjusted for multiple comparisons and, therefore, should not be utilised for hypothesis testing. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant, and the analysis were performed using SAS 9.4 software.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Results

Between Dec 14, 2023, and Dec 25, 2024, 314 patients with acute ischaemic stroke were screened, of whom 14 patients who did not meet the eligibility criteria or declined to participate and were excluded. Consequently, a total of 300 patients meeting the eligibility criteria were enrolled; six untreated patients were excluded from the analysis set: two withdrew by decision, two failed to receive thrombectomy, and the remaining two inappropriately enrolled. Among the 294 patients who received at least 1 dose of study products, 213 (72.4%) patients were men, and the median age was 65.5 (57.0–72.0) years. Overall, 30 (10.2%) of 294 patients showed premature permanent drug discontinuation, 7 (2.4%) patients used prohibited concomitant medications that significantly impacted the efficacy evaluation (Fig. 1). The median age across the 5 groups ranged from 64.0 to 66.5 years. The proportion of female patients ranged from 19.0% to 35.7% across the groups. At some study centres, investigators directly performed digital subtraction angiography for the assessment of large vessel occlusion without computed tomography angiography, which resulted in a shorter interval from stroke onset to endovascular puncture relative to the time from stroke onset to administration. And the baseline characteristics were similar in five groups as well as in per-protocol set population (Table 1 and Appendix pp. 15–16). Discussion

In this phase 2 clinical trial of ischaemic stroke patients with large vessel occlusion undergoing endovascular treatment, Y-6 was safe and well tolerated. Most clinical studies on neuroprotective agents for ischaemic stroke have ultimately failed to demonstrate clear benefits.20, 21, 22 The combination formulations containing dexborneol, such as edaravone dexborneol injection or sublingual tablets, have shown improved 90-day functional outcomes compared to the placebo and edaravone alone in the TASTE (The Treatment of Acute Ischaemic Stroke with Edaravone Dexborneol) and TASTE-SL (The Treatment of Acute Ischaemic Stroke with Sublingual Edaravone Dexborneol) trials without increasing safety risks.23,24 Regarding this trial, the results suggest Y-6 sublingual tablets are a potentially promising neuroprotective therapy, with demonstrated safety in this phase 2 study, warranting further investigation in phase 3 trials. The FUTURE trial builds on these findings and STAIR consortium recommendations by evaluating a multitarget neuroprotective strategy combining dexborneol (anti-inflammatory, BBB-stabilizing properties) and cilostazol (antiplatelet, endothelial-protective properties).

Following reperfusion therapy for ischaemic stroke, reperfusion injury may occur with pathophysiological pathways involving inflammatory activation, microvascular obstruction, and BBB disruption contributing to microcirculatory dysfunction and persistent poor outcomes despite reperfusion.25 Dexborneol, a small molecule with excellent BBB permeability, reduces inflammation during the acute ischaemic phase by improving cerebral blood flow, inhibiting neuroexcitotoxicity, modulating the Ca2+ overload, and regulating nitric oxide synthase (NOS) expression.26, 27, 28 Cilostazol, an antiplatelet agent, has demonstrated reduced stroke recurrence and major adverse cardiovascular events with favourable safety profiles compared to traditional antiplatelets in meta-analyses of 18 randomised trials involving over 11,000 patients.29 Animal studies further suggest that cilostazol may exert endothelial protection and vasodilation beyond platelet inhibition, with potential cytoprotective effects in the brain.14 In cerebral small vessel disease, recent clinical trials indicated that cilostazol could improve Montreal Cognitive Assessment results while slowing white matter lesion progression likely through enhanced cerebral perfusion and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.30,31 Compared to secondary prevention regimens with 100 mg cilostazol for antiplatelet therapy, the 25 mg cilostazol component in Y-6 sublingual tablets provides a safety margin for combination use with other antiplatelet agents. Furthermore, the sublingual tablet formulation enables rapid drug disintegration upon saliva contact, facilitating quick absorption through the sublingual venous plexus into systemic circulation and the brain. This route avoids invasive administration while providing a rapid onset of action, circumventing swallowing difficulties common in some patients with stroke.32,33

The FUTURE study has several limitations. First, our inclusion criteria focused on patients with ischaemic stroke caused by large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation who underwent endovascular treatment, and did not include patients with posterior circulation vascular occlusion, such as basilar artery occlusion, who underwent endovascular treatment. Therefore, extrapolating the conclusions of the study may require consideration of the different types of ischaemic stroke and treatments. Second, in the follow-up of clinically stable patients, routine repeat CT scans were unnecessary, which may have resulted in underdetection of intracranial haemorrhage. Third, although the phase 2 study included 5 groups of patients, the sample size was small with limited statistical power and exploratory efficacy analyses. In the future design of phase 3 trial, the results of this study and more standardized calculation will be referred to enhance the sample size.

In summary, for ischaemic stroke with large-vessel occlusion within 24 h who undergo endovascular treatment, the results of this phase 2 study showed that Y-6 sublingual tablets did not improve the efficacy outcomes and were not associated with an increased bleeding risk. Although the trial did not demonstrate statistically significant efficacy on the primary outcome, and the findings should be regarded as strictly hypothesis-generating, the signal toward improved functional outcomes without haemorrhage risk supports the biological plausibility of the intervention and justifies further investigation. A phase 3 trial may be justified.
