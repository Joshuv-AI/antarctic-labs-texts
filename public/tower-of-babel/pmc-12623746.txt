Clinical and electrophysiological study of polyneuropathy in patients with multiple sclerosis
Authors: Alina Flintere-Flinte, Daniels Urbanovics, Staņislavs Mironovs, Solvita Berzina, Jolanta Kalnina, Elizabete Kenina, Maksims Zolovs, Viktorija Kenina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although multiple sclerosis (MS) primarily affects the central nervous system, studies suggest peripheral nervous system involvement, leading to polyneuropathy (PNP). However, its prevalence and associated clinical factors remain unclear. This study addresses this gap. We enrolled 149 people with MS (pwMS) based on McDonald criteria (2017), including 54 males (36.2%) and 95 females (63.8%). Patients were classified as relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS, n = 130), secondary progressive MS (SPMS, n = 14), and primary progressive MS (PPMS, n = 5). Assessments included the total neuropathy score (TNSc), neuropathic pain scale (DN4), expanded disability status scale (EDSS), and generalized anxiety disorder scale-7 (GAD-7). Nerve conduction studies (NCS) were conducted to define PNP. PNP was diagnosed in 16% (n = 24) of subjects, with the highest prevalence in the SPMS group (35.7%). Higher TNSc scores, cerebral MS disease form, and male gender significantly increased PNP risk. Women had a 54% lower risk than men. NCS findings indicated significantly longer M latency and reduced nerve conduction velocity in the PNP group, suggesting a demyelinating nature. This study provides descriptive insights into the prevalence and neurophysiological features of polyneuropathy in multiple sclerosis, suggesting a complex interplay between disease-related and clinical factors. While limitations such as small sample size and single-center design warrant cautious interpretation, the findings underscore the need for larger, multicenter studies to clarify underlying mechanisms and guide improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS) with a variety of clinical presentations and unclear pathogenesis. More than 2.8 million people have MS worldwide, and the prevalence of MS per 100,000 population in Europe in 2020 is 142.811. The onset of symptoms is generally between the ages of 20 and 402. Neurological symptoms can manifest in various forms, including sensory changes, motor weakness, cognitive problems3, and autonomic dysfunction, affecting multiple body functions4.

Although MS is generally considered restricted to the CNS, several studies have reported the involvement of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) that causes peripheral nerve lesions resulting in polyneuropathy (PNP)5. Demyelination in the PNS has been demonstrated by peripheral nerve conduction abnormalities6, nerve biopsy (segmental demyelination, etc.)7, and magnetic resonance neurography studies where the highest occurrence of PNS abnormalities was in the group of patients with MS compared to the control group8. There have been several reports of axonal lesions (axonal PNP) in pwMS9. Also, several studies have described possible autoimmune reactivity to PNS and CNS myelin antigens based on the fact that central and peripheral myelin share many molecules, such as myelin basic protein (MBP) and myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG)10.

However, the relationship between the probability of PNP in pwMS, and clinical factors such as MS forms, progression, severity, age, and gender is not clear. This study aims to address this gap. The study was based on a population-wide cohort in Latvia.

Materials and methods

Study sample

One hundred forty-nine patients were evaluated and diagnosed with definite multiple sclerosis (MS) based on the McDonald criteria (2017); these patients were recruited from Pauls Stradins Clinical University Hospital between December 2022 and September 2023 using the hospital registry. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was approved by Rīga Stradiņš University Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals before participation in the study.

Availability of data materials

The datasets used and/or analysed during the current study available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the hospital registry and selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure a homogeneous study population. To be eligible for inclusion, individuals must have provided signed informed consent, be aged between 18 and 70 years, and possess a confirmed diagnosis of multiple sclerosis (MS) according to the McDonald criteria (2017), encompassing any form of MS. Additionally, participants were required to have an Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score ranging from 0 to 6.5, indicating varying levels of disability without severe impairment.

Individuals were excluded from the study if they had a history of substance abuse, diabetes mellitus or other conditions associated with neuropathy (e.g., chronic kidney disease), current or prior use of medications known to influence neuropathy risk (e.g., chemotherapy agents, antiretrovirals, or chronic corticosteroid use) or any contraindications to nerve conduction studies. These exclusion criteria were set to prevent potential confounding factors that might influence the outcomes of the study or the safety of participants during the assessments.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were assessed using patients’ medical histories available in the hospital registry.

The neurological status of MS subjects was assessed using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS).

Enrolled subjects underwent a uniform evaluation of the peripheral nervous system. First, patients were screened using the shortened and revised total neuropathy scoring criteria (srTNS)11, which consists of three components of symptom extension (numbness, tingling, and neuropathic pain) and two objective testing components (tendon reflex and vibration sensibility). The total neuropathy score (srTNS) was calculated for all participants.

The Douleur Neuropathique en 4 (DN4) interview questionnaire was used to identify neuropathic pain12. All subjects enrolled completed the Latvian version of the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale to assess anxiety symptoms13. Patients who scored four or more points on the DN4 questionnaire were defined as having neuropathic pain. More than four points in the GAD-7 questionnaire indicate an increased risk of generalised anxiety.

Electrodiagnostic approach

The patients underwent nerve conduction studies (NCS) performed by certified neurophysiology experts. The neuroconduction studies were performed on motor and sensory nerves according to the polyneuropathy examination protocol. Each patient underwent bilateral NCS of the upper extremities (motor and sensory components of the ulnar and median nerves)14 and bilateral NCS of the lower extremities (motor components of the peroneal and tibial nerves and sensory components of a sural nerve)15. Polyneuropathy was diagnosed when abnormalities were identified in at least two peripheral nerves across two limbs, based on deviations from normative values used in Latvian clinical neurophysiology. Demyelinating patterns were characterized by slowed conduction velocity and/or prolonged distal motor latencies, whereas axonal patterns were indicated by reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) or sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes. Diagnosis was based on the overall pattern of abnormalities, not strict percentage thresholds, and aligned with typical features of length-dependent, symmetrical large fiber polyneuropathy. To minimize misclassification due to spinal cord or root lesions, polyneuropathy was diagnosed only when abnormalities were present in multiple nerves involving at least two limbs. In addition, sensory nerve conduction abnormalities were observed in all polyneuropathy cases, which supports a peripheral rather than radicular etiology. Conduction velocities of ≥ 50 m/s for arm nerves and ≥ 45 m/s for leg nerves were considered normal. Distal motor latencies for the median nerve (≤ 4 ms), ulnar nerve (≤ 3 ms), peroneal nerve (≤ 5 ms), and tibial nerve (≤ 4 ms) were considered within normal limits. Normal amplitudes were ≥ 4 mV for the median nerve, ≥ 6 mV for the ulnar nerve, ≥ 2 mV for the peroneal nerve, and ≥ 4 mV for the tibial nerve.

For consistency, stimulation and recording distances were standardized across patients according to clinical guidelines for example, 80 mm between stimulation and recording sites for the median nerve. All measurements were obtained using surface electrodes, with limb temperatures maintained at ≥ 32 °C. Standardized distances between stimulation and recording points ensured accurate results.

People with multiple sclerosis who were diagnosed with large fiber polyneuropathy, based on nerve conduction studies, were assigned to the PNP group.

Statistical data analysis

The data distribution was assessed by inspection of the normal Q–Q plot and with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data homogeneity was assessed with the Levene test. The Mann–Whitney U test, the Welch test, or the chi-squared homogeneity test were used to compare multiple parameters of demographic and neurological assessment between the MS with PNP group and the MS without PNP group. Electrophysiological parameters were compared between the MS with PNP and MS without PNP groups using the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) or Welch’s t-test (for normally distributed data). Binomial logistic regression was performed to determine the factors (demographic and neurological) related to the presence or absence of polyneuropathy in patients. Explanatory variables were selected by evaluating the p-value and performing filter methods, which evaluated each variable’s importance based on statistical measures such as correlation coefficients. To build the regression model, stepwise forward and backward regression methods were used. All possible models and interactions were analysed. To select the best model, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), p-values and confidence intervals of the odds ratio were used.

The final regression model was validated by assessing key assumptions. First, the Box–Tidwell procedure was applied to test whether the relationship between the TNSc scores and the logit of the dependent variable was linear. Second, variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to assess multicollinearity. In addition, the area under the curve (AUC) was computed to evaluate the model’s ability to correctly classify outcomes.

Statistical data analysis was performed with Jamovi (v.2.3). The results were considered statistically significant when the p value was < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and neurological evaluation

In our study, 149 patients aged Mean = 42.2, SD = 11.3 years (male 43.9 ± 10.4; female 41.2 ± 11.8, p = 0.042) were enrolled in the study and classified into categories according to multiple sclerosis type: 130 with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), 14 with secondary progressive MS (SPMS), and five with primary progressive MS (PPMS). Polyneuropathy (PNP) was identified in 24 participants, representing 16% of the total group. The highest prevalence was in the SPMS groups, where 5 of 14 patients (35.7%) were diagnosed with PNP. The final regression model was statistically significant and identified three key predictors (see Table 3). The Total Neuropathy Score (TNSc) was a strong predictor; for each one-point increase in the TNSc, the odds of a patient having PNP increased by 59% (OR 1.59, 95% CI [1.47, 1.71]).

The MS disease form was also a significant factor. Patients with the cerebral form of MS were 2.3 times more likely to have PNP than those with the cerebrospinal form (OR 2.30, 95% CI [1.54, 3.45]).

Finally, gender was a significant predictor, with female patients having 54% lower odds of having PNP compared to male patients (OR 0.46, 95% CI [0.34, 0.62]).

Electrodiagnostic results

Analysis of the electrophysiological parameters of the leg nerves (n.peroneus, n.tibialis, and n.suralis) between individuals with MS and PNP and those with MS without PNP revealed statistically significant differences for each nerve (p < 0.05). Specifically, there were markedly higher values of amplitude and velocity in the MS without PNP group compared to the MS with PNP group, while M latency was significantly longer in the MS with PNP group (Fig. 1). For example, amplitude of N. peroneus in individuals with PNP (median (Q1–Q3): 3.2 (2.35–4.15)) was significantly lower than in people without PNP (3.6 (2.8–5.2)), p < 0.001 r = 0.27. The amplitude of N. tibialis in individuals with PNP (8.4 (6.47–11.83)) was significantly lower than in people without PNP (10.8 (8–13.5)), p < 0.001 r = 0.28. The amplitude of N. suralis in individuals with PNP (9.3 (6.8–12.2)) was significantly lower than in people without PNP (13.75 (10–17.3)), p < 0.001 r = 0.46. Additional results are visualized in Fig. 1. Although amplitudes and latencies remained within clinically normal ranges, the observed differences may suggest early or subclinical large fiber involvement rather than typical polyneuropathy.

There was no difference in the electrophysiological parameters of the ulnar nerve motor conduction study (p > 0.05), while the electrophysiological parameters of the sensory conduction study of n. ulnaris differed significantly between individuals with MS and PNP and those with MS without PNP (p < 0.05). S-Latency was significantly prolonged in individuals with PNP (mean ± SD: 2.4 ± 0.4) than in non-PNP individuals (1.9 ± 0.2) (p < 0.001). The amplitude was significantly higher in people without PNP (27.9 ± 13.6) than in people with PNP (19.3 ± 12.3) (p < 0.001). The velocity was significantly higher in people without PNP (54.6 ± 3.6) than in people with PNP (48.1 ± 6.3) (p < 0.001). Despite observed differences, both groups remained within clinically accepted normative ranges for amplitudes and latencies; however, velocities were an exception, falling below the established norms utilized in Latvian clinical practice.

Discussion

The prevalence of PNP among PwMS

Prevalence of PNP and representativeness of the study sample

Our study identified a prevalence of polyneuropathy (PNP) of 16% among people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS), which aligns with findings from similar studies but also contrasts with other reports where prevalence rates ranged from 5%16 to 77.8%9. While this variability may stem from differences in study designs and sample sizes, it is essential to consider the representativeness of our study population.

Specifically, the findings in our study should be regarded with caution regarding their generalizability, particularly in terms of age and MS course. As a significant predictor of PNP in pwMS, age was shown to correlate with disease duration and the progression type of MS, potentially influencing the observed PNP prevalence. Given that the mean age in our study was 42.2 years, which is higher than the general population prevalence of PNP in the same age range, it is plausible that age-related factors contribute to our findings. However, MS is strongly associated with changes in peripheral nerve function, which may contribute to the earlier onset of PNP compared to the general population. Thus, while the findings provide valuable insights into the prevalence of PNP, further studies are necessary to confirm the representativeness of our sample, particularly in terms of age distribution and MS progression type.

MS and earlier onset of PNP

Regarding the potential for an earlier onset of PNP in pwMS, it is relevant to compare our findings with those from previous studies, which report higher prevalence rates of PNP. These findings suggest that MS may be associated with an earlier onset of PNP than other conditions or even the general population. This highlights the need for further investigation into the pathophysiological mechanisms that may accelerate the development of PNP in MS. Although our study provides a combined estimate of PNP prevalence, future research with a more diverse, larger cohort could offer greater clarity on whether MS accelerates the onset of peripheral neuropathy and how this interacts with disease type, disease duration, and other clinical factors.

Interrelationship between age, gender, MS type, disease duration, and EDSSIn this study, we used both univariate and multivariate statistical methods. Univariate analysis looks at one factor at a time, for example comparing age or gender between groups (Table 1). This approach is useful for identifying simple differences, but it does not take into account how factors may be related to one another. In this study, age, time from the first symptoms and disability scores showed significant associations with polyneuropathy when analyzed separately.

However, health outcomes are often influenced by several factors acting together. For this reason, we also applied multivariate analysis, which allows us to examine many factors simultaneously and see which ones remain important after adjusting for others. Using this approach, we found that only the total neuropathy score, MS disease form, and gender were independent predictors of polyneuropathy. This means that while age and disability seem important on their own, their effect could be explained by other related variables. To address this, future studies should use statistical models that account for these interdependencies, such as multivariate regression analysis or structural equation modeling, which can provide more accurate estimates of the independent effects of each factor. This approach would help clarify the precise mechanisms by which age, MS type, and disease duration contribute to the onset of PNP, and would improve the understanding of how these factors interact in pwMS.

In the evaluation of prescribed medications in our study, no significant difference was observed between patients with MS and polyneuropathy compared to those with MS alone, except for siponimod treatment. Siponimod use was more frequent in the PNP group (20.83%) compared to the non-PNP group (5.22%). However, this discrepancy does not imply a causal relationship between siponimod administration and the onset of polyneuropathy. Rather, it likely reflects its preferential use in secondary progressive MS, which itself carries a higher incidence of polyneuropathy, as discussed above.

Differences in nerve conduction studies

The NCS findings, while statistically different, often remained within normative values. These results likely reflect subtle or subclinical nerve involvement rather than overt, clinically apparent polyneuropathy. In the study of length-dependent polyneuropathy, a pattern has been noted whereby the longest peripheral nerves, typically those in the legs, are affected first. This observation aligns with the disease’s progression, characterised by initial distal nerve damage that gradually moves proximally, often leaving nerves in the hands unaffected17. Indicators such as prolonged M-latency and reduced nerve conduction velocity signify mild demyelination, underscoring the involvement of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) in a manner similar to that of the central nervous system (CNS), where demyelination usually marks the beginning of the disease process.

Moreover, the observation of a significantly lower amplitude in pwMS with PNP compared to those without suggests axonal damage, even though these amplitude values may remain within the normal range. This finding is consistent with the presence of early axonal lesions, mirroring CNS pathology, where axonal changes are a hallmark of advanced disease stages. However, the possibility of secondary axonal damage, stemming from the impact of demyelination on axonal metabolism, cannot be discounted18.

Limitations and future studies

One limitation of the present study is the omission of F-wave assessments. F-wave latency and persistence could provide important information on proximal nerve involvement and early demyelination and should be included in future research. Another limitation is that body mass index (BMI), which can influence conduction parameters, particularly sensory amplitudes, was not recorded. Future studies should consider BMI as a potential covariate. This parallel between CNS and PNS pathology raises essential questions about the mechanisms underlying PNS alterations in MS. Future research aimed at identifying specific PNS targets is crucial for understanding these changes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides descriptive data that improve our understanding of the prevalence and neurophysiological characteristics of polyneuropathy in the multiple sclerosis population, highlighting differences between patients with and without PNP of MS. This suggests a potentially complex relationship between MS and PNP. Our results indicate that variables such as gender, age, MS course, and clinical factors may play a role in the risk of PNP in pwMS, but these findings should be interpreted with caution given the study design.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-center design, relatively small subgroup sizes, absence of control groups, and potential selection bias restrict the generalizability of the results and preclude drawing definitive conclusions about causal associations.

Despite these constraints, the study highlights important gaps in knowledge and opens avenues for future research. Further investigations, ideally involving multicenter cohorts with larger and more diverse populations, are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying PNP in MS. Such insights may ultimately support improved diagnostic approaches and treatment strategies for affected patients.
