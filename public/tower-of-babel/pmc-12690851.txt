Associations between mediterranean diet adherence, binge eating behavior, and oral health-related quality of life in adults
Authors: Emine Merve Ekici, Sena Yücel, Ümmügülsüm Güler, Emine Tuğçe Ersoy, Özge Mengi Çelik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite growing recognition of the complex interplay between eating patterns and health outcomes, few studies have simultaneously examined the associations between Mediterranean diet adherence, binge eating behavior, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). This study aimed to address this gap by exploring these variables in a large adult population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1006 adults (aged 18–65 years) in Türkiye using an online survey. Validated instruments included the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Scale (MEDAS), Binge Eating Scale (BES), and the OHQoL-UK. Spearman correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 32.52 ± 12.8 years. Based on MEDAS scores, 46.7% of participants were classified as adherent to the Mediterranean diet. Binge eating behavior was significantly and negatively correlated with MD adherence (r = − 0.112, p < 0.001) and OHRQoL (r = − 0.078, p < 0.05), and positively correlated with BMI (r = 0.235, p < 0.001). Regression analysis identified BMI (β = 0.425, p < 0.001), gender (female; β = − 0.169, p < 0.001), marital status (single; β = 0.259, p < 0.001), income status (β = − 0.061, p = 0.034), MEDAS score (β = − 0.100, p < 0.001), and OHQoL-UK score (β = − 0.069, p = 0.017) as significant predictors of binge eating behavior, collectively explaining 20.5% of the variance (R² = 0.205).ConclusionThis study is among the first to explore these variables simultaneously and demonstrates that, beyond sociodemographic and weight-related factors, dietary and oral health indicators also independently contribute to binge eating behavior. These findings highlight the need for multidisciplinary prevention strategies integrating nutritional, psychological, and oral health dimensions.Keywords: Mediterranean diet, Binge eating behavior, Oral health, Dietary patterns, Nutrition, BMI

Introduction

Oral and dental health is a fundamental component of overall health, nutritional status, and quality of life. Conditions such as dental caries, tooth loss, and periodontal diseases can impair chewing function, compromise dietary intake, and increase susceptibility to infection—thereby negatively impacting nutritional status, systemic health, and individual well-being [39, 40]. Periodontal diseases affect approximately 60% of adults worldwide, with about 24% experiencing moderate to severe forms. This high prevalence makes periodontal disease a significant global public health concern [19]. A balanced and adequate diet plays a central role in maintaining oral structure and preventing oral diseases, particularly through its effects on inflammation, nutrient supply, and oral microbiota [5]. The composition and frequency of food consumption directly influence the health of teeth and surrounding tissues, as demonstrated by multiple recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses that highlight the association between healthy dietary patterns and improved periodontal outcomes [43, 46, 48].

In addition to diet, oral health is also influenced by individual behavioral patterns such as oral hygiene habits and eating behaviors. Periodontal diseases, in particular, are multifactorial conditions in which nutrition, immune response, and microbial balance interact [5, 21]. Poor dietary quality may contribute to systemic inflammation and worsen periodontal status even in the absence of poor hygiene [21], Türkoğlu et al., [49]. Thus, evaluating dietary behavior in the context of oral health is essential.

In addition to general dietary patterns, psychological eating behaviors, such as binge eating, may also have indirect negative effects on oral health. Binge eating is typically characterized by recurrent episodes of consuming large amounts of food in a short period, often involving energy-dense, sugar-rich, and ultra-processed foods, along with a perceived loss of control and the absence of compensatory behaviors [2]. Binge Eating Disorder (BED) is considered the most prevalent eating disorder among adults, with lifetime prevalence estimates of approximately 2.8% in women and 1.0% in men [10]. However, the global burden of BED remains underexplored due to limited cross-national data.

These eating episodes increase cariogenic exposure by providing fermentable substrates for acidogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, leading to enamel demineralization and elevated risk of dental caries [32]. Furthermore, frequent consumption of these foods may alter the oral microbiota, reducing microbial diversity and favoring pathogenic species associated with periodontal disease [51]. Psychological comorbidities commonly linked with binge eating, such as depression and anxiety, may also indirectly affect oral health by negatively impacting oral hygiene behaviors and increasing the risk of inflammation [36, 50]. Although binge eating has been studied extensively in relation to obesity and mental health, its potential impact on oral health mechanisms has received comparatively less attention and warrants further exploration.

In contrast to eating behaviors that negatively impact health, such as binge eating, there is growing evidence that certain structured dietary patterns —particularly the MD may exert protective effects on oral health [9, 45, 46]. The MD is characterized by a high intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil; moderate consumption of dairy products and fish; and a low intake of red meat and sweets [9]. These dietary components possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and microbiota-regulating properties. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that high adherence to the MD is associated with reduced risk and severity of periodontitis, as well as improvements in periodontal parameters [45, 46]. Although further long-term and interventional studies are needed to clarify causal relationships, current evidence underscores the supportive role of the MD in promoting periodontal health.

Recent evidence suggests that adherence to the MD may have protective effects on maladaptive eating behaviors such as binge eating, potentially through mechanisms involving satiety, glycemic regulation, and neurotransmitter modulation [7, 22]. The high fiber content of the MD enhances satiety by prolonging chewing—which increases gastric distension—and by stimulating the secretion of anorexigenic hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) [4]. Furthermore, functional components of the MD such as omega-3 fatty acids, monounsaturated fats, folate, and B vitamins may influence serotonin production, transport, and binding, contributing to mood regulation [7]. Through these neurobiological pathways, the MD may play a supportive role in regulating eating behaviors.

Despite increasing interest in the interplay between diet, eating behaviors, and oral health, comprehensive investigations addressing all three domains within a unified framework are still scarce. Existing research often focuses on pairwise associations, lacking a comprehensive framework. Given the multidimensional impacts of maladaptive eating behaviors on both psychological well-being and oral health, identifying modifiable dietary patterns such as the MD is critically important. By addressing all three variables together, this study fills a significant gap in the literature and provides insights into how diet quality may influence both behavioral and oral health outcomes. Accordingly, we hypothesized that higher adherence to the MD would be associated with lower levels of binge eating and better OHRQoL-UK.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2024 and January 2025 among 1006 adults (698 females, 308 males) aged 18–65 years residing in Ankara, Türkiye. Data were collected using a web-based survey form (Google Form) developed by the researchers. Participants were recruited through a snowball sampling method, which involved sharing the survey link via social media platforms such as Twitter, Facebook, WhatsApp, and Instagram. Initially, the link was distributed within the researchers’ personal and professional networks, and participants were encouraged to share the link further, allowing for broader participation.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) being aged 18 to 65, (2) residing in Türkiye, (3) having internet access, (4) voluntarily agreeing to participate by checking the informed consent box at the beginning of the survey, and (5) completing the entire survey. Exclusion criteria included: (1) being pregnant or breastfeeding, (2) following a medically prescribed diet due to a specific disease, (3) having diagnosed eating disorders other than binge eating, (4) having severe dental conditions that interfere with eating (e.g., dysphagia, significant tooth loss), and (5) providing incomplete responses.

Before the study commenced, ethical approval was obtained from the University of Health Sciences Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024/571). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The survey included questions on sociodemographic characteristics (gender, age, marital status, education level, occupation, and income), self-reported anthropometric measurements (body weight and height), and standardized tools to evaluate binge eating behavior, adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and oral health-related quality of life.

Anthropometric measurements

Body weight and height data were obtained via self-report by the participants. To enhance the accuracy of these self-reports, detailed instructions were provided within the online survey to guide participants on proper measurement techniques, such as using a flat surface and a wall for height measurement and a calibrated scale for weight. Participants were encouraged to provide recent and precise values and to avoid estimations. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula: weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Following Madden and Smith’s [26] classification, BMI was categorized as underweight (< 18.50 kg/m²), normal weight (18.50–24.99 kg/m²), overweight (25.00–29.99 kg/m²), or obese (≥ 30.00 kg/m²).

Several studies support the validity of self-reported anthropometric measurements. Lassale et al. [23], using data from the Nutrinet-Santé study, found high validity of web-based self-reported height and weight, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) exceeding 0.9. Similarly, Fayyaz et al. [14] conducted a systematic review demonstrating good agreement between measured and self-reported weight and height, reporting ICC values greater than 0.9 and acceptable limits of agreement.

Binge eating scale (BES)

The Binge Eating Scale (BES) was developed by Gormally, Black, Daston, and Rardin [15] to assess the severity of binge eating behaviors among obese individuals. It has been widely used in non-clinical populations to evaluate binge eating behaviors (e.g., Duarte et al., [12, 16]).

The scale consists of 16 items, with eight items assessing emotions and cognitions related to binge eating (e.g., guilt, feeling out of control), and eight items evaluating binge eating behaviors (e.g., eating quickly, eating in secret). Most items have four response options scored from 0 to 3, except for items 6 and 16, which have three response options scored from 0 to 2. Higher total scores on the scale indicate greater severity of binge eating behaviors The total score of the scale ranges from 0 to 46, with higher scores indicating greater severity of binge eating behavior. Scores are commonly categorized as follows: 0–17 = no binge eating, 18–26 = mild to moderate binge eating, and ≥ 27 = severe binge eating, based on the original classification by Gormally et al. [15].

The Turkish adaptation was conducted by Baykan and Durak Batıgün [6], demonstrating a unidimensional factor structure consistent with the original scale. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the Turkish version was 0.83, and the test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.87, indicating the scale’s reliability and validity [6].

Oral health-related quality of life (OHQoL-UK)

Oral health-related quality of life was assessed using the OHQoL-UK scale, which was originally developed by McGrath and Bedi [29] based on the WHO’s “structure-function-ability-inheritance” model. The Turkish adaptation and validation of the scale were conducted by Mumcu et al. [34], who reported a high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96), indicating excellent reliability of the Turkish version.

The scale includes 16 items distributed across four dimensions: symptoms (2 items), physical condition (5 items), psychological condition (5 items), and social condition (4 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“very bad effect”) to 5 (“very good effect”), allowing for both negative and positive evaluations of oral health impact. The total score ranges from 16 to 80, with higher scores reflecting a better oral health-related quality of life [29]; Mumcu et al., [34].

Mediterranean diet adherence scale (MEDAS)

In order to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet, the 14-item Mediterranean Diet Adherence Scale (MEDAS) was used. The scale was originally developed by Martínez-González et al. [28] within the framework of the PREDIMED trial, and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Pehlivanoğlu et al. [38].

The scale includes 14 questions on key components of the Mediterranean dietary pattern, such as olive oil, fruits, vegetables, legumes, fish, red and processed meats, and wine consumption. Each item is scored either 0 or 1 according to compliance with the recommended intake levels. The total score ranges from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating better adherence. A score of ≥ 7 indicates sufficient adherence to the MD [28]; Pehlivanoğlu et al., [38]. The Turkish validation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.829, indicating good internal consistency for the MEDAS [38].

Statistical analysis

The data analysis for this study was performed using SPSS 27.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As the variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric methods were applied. For comparisons between two independent groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for numerical variables due to non-normal distribution, while the Pearson chi-square test was applied for categorical data. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationship between oral health-related quality of life, MD adherence, and binge eating behaviors total scores. Figure 1 presents the correlation heatmap, illustrating significant associations among these variables. A linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive factors binge eating behaviors total scores. Figure 2 provides a visualization of the regression coefficients for binge eating behavior total score. Total scores from the BES and MEDAS were analyzed as continuous variables in correlation and regression models. However, predefined cut-off points were used to categorize participants in descriptive statistics. To meet the assumptions of linear regression, non-normally distributed data were transformed logarithmically to approximate a normal distribution. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. All regression assumptions, including linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity, were tested and met.

Results

In this study, 1006 adults aged 18–65 years (female: 698, male: 308) participated. The general characteristics of the individuals are given in Table 1. The mean age of the individuals was 32.52 ± 12.8 years, and the mean BMI was 24.69 ± 4.82 kg/m². 57.4% of the individuals were single, 68.9% had university/graduate education, and 32.1% were students. 49.2% of the individuals stated that their income was higher than their expenses. According to BMI classification, 53.3% of the individuals were classified as normal weight, 28.3% as overweight, 12.7% as obese, and 5.7% as underweight. It was observed that 53.3% of the individuals did not comply with the MD and 78.1% did not have binge eating disorder. The mean score of the MEDAS was 6.33 ± 1.92, the mean total score of oral health-related quality of life was 55.6 ± 14.04, and the mean total score of the binge eating scale was 11.11 ± 7.92. In the study, individuals were also evaluated according to compliance with the MD and marital status, smoking habit and regular physical activity showed a significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05) (data not shown).

Discussion

Although previous studies have addressed diet–oral health or diet–eating behavior relationships separately, few have integrated these domains into a single model [7, 18, 48]. By examining Mediterranean diet adherence, binge eating behavior, and oral health-related quality of life together, this study provides a broader perspective on the interplay between lifestyle and health outcomes. Correlational analyses revealed that higher MD adherence and better OHRQoL were both significantly associated with lower levels of binge eating behavior. Moreover, MD adherence and OHRQoL were positively correlated with each other, suggesting a potential shared behavioral or lifestyle background. Additionally, nearly half of the participants (46.7%) demonstrated adequate MD adherence, which offers a meaningful context for exploring how diet quality relates to psychosocial and oral health indicators.

In the multiple linear regression model, gender, body mass index (BMI), marital status, income status, MD adherence, and OHRQoL emerged as significant predictors of binge eating behavior. Specifically, being male, having a higher BMI, and being single were associated with greater binge eating tendencies, while higher adherence to the MD and better OHRQoL were linked to lower binge eating scores. These findings underscore the multidimensional nature of binge eating behavior, influenced by physiological (e.g., BMI), sociodemographic (e.g., gender, marital and income status), and lifestyle-related (e.g., diet quality and oral health) factors.

The significant associations observed between binge eating behavior, MD adherence, and OHRQoL may be explained by several behavioral and physiological mechanisms. Structured dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet can promote appetite regulation, reduce emotional eating, and support mood stabilization due to their high fiber content, low glycemic index, and richness in anti-inflammatory components [7]; Martínez-González et al., [27]. The act of prolonged chewing and slow digestion triggered by fiber-rich foods increases satiety hormones such as GLP-1 and cholecystokinin, helping reduce binge episodes [4]. Additionally, oral discomfort or pain resulting from poor oral health may lead to disrupted eating patterns, which in turn contribute to the onset or persistence of maladaptive eating behaviors [32, 42].

Interestingly, this study identified a weak yet positive correlation between MD adherence and BMI, a finding that contrasts with some previous studies. Several investigations have reported that adherence to the MD is inversely associated with body weight and obesity-related outcomes [8, 37, 41]. The MD rich in dietary fiber, unsaturated fats, and low-glycemic index foods—has been associated with improved satiety, reduced overall energy intake, and anti-inflammatory effects, all of which contribute to weight management [11, 25]. The divergence observed in our findings may be explained by the sample characteristics, as a considerable proportion of participants were within the normal weight range. It is also plausible that individuals with higher BMI may be more inclined to adopt healthier eating patterns, such as the MD, in an effort to manage their weight, which could partly explain the positive association observed.

In addition to the weak positive correlation observed between MD adherence and BMI, the current study revealed a significant negative association between BMI and oral health-related quality of life, and a positive correlation with binge eating behavior. These results align with prior findings indicating that higher BMI is often associated with poor oral health outcomes and increased risk of maladaptive eating patterns [13, 30, 35, 47]. Obesity-related factors such as poor diet quality, reduced salivary flow, and low oral hygiene have been linked to increased dental caries and periodontal disease risk [35, 44]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation and medication use common among individuals with obesity may contribute to oral health deterioration [13, 47].

The positive correlation between BMI and binge eating behavior may reflect psychological distress or compensatory eating patterns, as reported in previous studies [10]. These intertwined pathways highlight the importance of considering both physiological and behavioral aspects of weight-related health outcomes.

In both correlation and regression analyses, binge eating behavior was significantly negatively associated with MD adherence and OHRQoL. These findings suggest that individuals who follow a healthier dietary pattern and maintain better oral health may be less prone to engage in maladaptive eating behaviors. The Mediterranean diet promotes appetite regulation and emotional stability through its high content of fiber, healthy fats, and anti-inflammatory compounds [4, 7, 22].Fiber-rich foods prolong digestion and stimulate satiety hormones such as GLP-1 and cholecystokinin, which help reduce the urge to binge [4]. Additionally, polyphenols and omega-3 fatty acids improve mood and reduce psychological stress, factors commonly linked to binge episodes [7, 22].

Similarly, poor oral health may cause pain or discomfort during eating, resulting in irregular or compensatory intake patterns. In line with this, our findings revealed a significant negative association between OHRQoL and binge eating. Prior studies have reported increased rates of dental erosion, gum bleeding, and tooth pain among individuals with binge eating disorder, often due to frequent consumption of acidic and calorie-dense foods [18, 20]. These oral issues may both reflect and perpetuate disordered eating behaviors.

Gender was a significant predictor of binge eating behavior, with female participants reporting higher binge eating scores than males. This finding is consistent with the broader literature, which consistently shows that women are more likely to experience binge eating than men. For instance, a systematic review covering studies between 2008 and 2018 reported a lifetime prevalence of 2.8% for women and 1.0% for men, indicating that women are approximately two to three times more likely to develop binge eating disorder (BED) compared to men [10]. Although BED affects both genders, women are disproportionately impacted, potentially due to a combination of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors such as emotional dysregulation, body dissatisfaction, and greater exposure to societal appearance pressures.

Marital status also emerged as a significant predictor in the regression model, with single individuals demonstrating higher binge eating scores than their married counterparts. Being single may be associated with increased emotional stress, loneliness, or lack of social support, which in turn may contribute to maladaptive eating patterns such as binge eating. Previous studies suggest that married individuals tend to exhibit more stable dietary habits and better overall well-being, potentially buffering them against disordered eating behaviors [1, 24].

Furthermore, higher income status was significantly associated with lower binge eating scores. This finding aligns with studies highlighting the influence of socioeconomic factors on eating behavior. Individuals with lower income may be at greater risk for stress, food insecurity, and limited access to nutritious food, all of which are linked to higher rates of binge eating [3, 33]. Although some studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding income level and binge eating, the overall evidence suggests that economic disadvantage can exacerbate disordered eating through both psychological and environmental pathways [17, 31].

This study has several notable strengths. First, it employed a large and diverse adult sample (N = 1006), enhancing the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Second, the study simultaneously examined the interrelationships among Mediterranean diet adherence, binge eating behavior, and oral health-related quality of life—three domains that are often studied in isolation. This integrative approach offers a more comprehensive understanding of how lifestyle, dietary behavior, and oral health interact. Furthermore, validated and culturally adapted instruments (e.g., MEDAS, BES, and OHQoL-UK) were used, ensuring the reliability and validity of the measurements.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and all data were based on self-reported measures, which may be subject to social desirability and recall bias. This includes anthropometric data such as height and weight, which may not accurately reflect participants’ actual BMI status. Moreover, potential confounders such as physical activity level, psychological distress, or oral hygiene practices were not assessed, which may have influenced the observed associations. Additionally, since the sample was recruited through web-based survey distribution, participants with higher digital access or health awareness may be overrepresented, potentially leading to selection bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings. In particular, the sampling method may have led to an overrepresentation of younger individuals with higher education levels and health consciousness, which could have influenced the observed behavioral patterns and reduced the external validity of the findings.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to simultaneously investigate the associations between Mediterranean diet adherence, binge eating behavior, and oral health-related quality of life within a single analytic model. Our findings indicate that while sociodemographic and weight-related variables remain prominent predictors of binge eating, lifestyle-related factors specifically adherence to the Mediterranean diet and oral health-related quality of life also significantly contributed to the variance in binge eating behavior. These results support the emerging view that eating behaviors are influenced not only by psychological and biological factors but also by dietary patterns and oral health status.

By integrating these three domains in a large adult sample from Türkiye, the study contributes a novel perspective to the literature and emphasizes the need to address eating behaviors through a broader lifestyle and public health lens. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to the study’s cross-sectional nature and reliance on self-reported data.

Future research should explore these interrelationships through longitudinal and interventional designs. In clinical and public health settings, collaborative programs involving dietitians, oral health professionals, and mental health providers may help address factors associated with disordered eating and encourage approaches aligned with holistic well-being. Although based on cross-sectional data, the modest associations identified in this study may still offer useful insights for shaping future preventive efforts targeting binge eating behavior.
