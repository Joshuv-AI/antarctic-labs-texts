Association of NMR metabolic signatures of healthy lifestyle with hypertension: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Xi Zhang, Li Zhang, Zhixing Fan, Chuying Huang, Jie Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLifestyle factors play a critical role in the prevention and management of hypertension, while metabolomics provides valuable insights into the biological mechanisms underlying disease development. However, the relationships among lifestyle-related metabolic signatures, lifestyle behaviors, and hypertension risk remain incompletely understood.MethodsThis prospective cohort study used data from the UK Biobank. A composite healthy lifestyle score was calculated, and a lifestyle-related metabolic signature was developed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics and elastic net regression. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations between the healthy lifestyle score and the metabolic signature and incident hypertension. Causal mediation analysis based on a counterfactual framework (CMAverse package) was performed to quantify the mediating effect of the metabolic signature on the association between lifestyle and hypertension. Subgroup, interaction, and sensitivity analyses were also conducted.ResultsA total of 126,677 eligible participants (mean age, 54.92 ± 8.12 years; 46.47% male; 96.09% White) were included. During a median follow-up of 13.43 years, 18,720 participants developed hypertension. A healthy lifestyle-related metabolic signature comprising 76 metabolites was identified and showed a strong correlation with the healthy lifestyle score. Both a higher healthy lifestyle score (HR: 0.927; 95% CI: 0.913–0.940) and a higher metabolic signature level (HR: 0.675; 95% CI: 0.641–0.711) were independently associated with a significantly lower risk of incident hypertension, with significant inverse dose-response trends. These associations remained generally consistent across subgroups stratified by age, education, and income. Mediation analysis indicated that the metabolic signature explained 57.08% (95% CI: 31.89%–83.15%) of the protective association between a healthy lifestyle and hypertension risk. The primary findings remained robust across multiple sensitivity analyses.ConclusionA healthy lifestyle is associated with a distinct metabolomic profile that substantially mediates its protective association with hypertension. These findings provide new insights into the metabolic mechanisms linking lifestyle behaviors to hypertension risk and highlight the importance of metabolic health in hypertension prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06363-x.

Introduction

Hypertension remains a major global public health concern. It is one of the leading modifiable risk factors for serious cardiovascular and renal complications, including stroke, coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), imposing a considerable burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. Its widespread prevalence and substantial contribution to global morbidity and mortality highlight the urgent need for effective preventive strategies [3]. Lifestyle modification, including adherence to a healthy diet, regular physical activity, moderation of alcohol consumption, and smoking cessation, has been consistently recommended by clinical guidelines and supported by extensive evidence as a cornerstone for the prevention and management of hypertension [4–7]. However, the biological mechanisms through which these lifestyle behaviors exert long-term effects on blood pressure regulation remain incompletely understood. This knowledge gap hinders the development of mechanism-based preventive interventions and more accurate approaches to hypertension risk prediction.

Recent advances in high-throughput metabolomics have provided a powerful approach for characterizing systemic biochemical alterations associated with lifestyle exposures [5]. Circulating metabolites reflect the combined influences of genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and gut microbial activity, making them valuable indicators of the biological consequences of health-related behaviors [8, 9]. Therefore, a multi-metabolite signature derived from these profiles may serve as a more objective and biologically relevant measure of healthy lifestyle exposure than self-reported lifestyle scores alone, while also offering insight into the metabolic pathways through which lifestyle influences hypertension risk [10]. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics has particularly demonstrated considerable potential for improving the prevention, diagnosis, and management of chronic diseases, including diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and CKD [11–14]. Despite these advances, prospective studies evaluating lifestyle-related metabolomic signatures as mediators of the association between healthy lifestyle behaviors and incident hypertension remain scarce.

Therefore, using data from the large-scale prospective UK Biobank cohort and its comprehensive NMR metabolomics platform, this study aimed to: (1) develop and validate an NMR-based metabolic signature associated with a composite healthy lifestyle score using elastic net regression; (2) prospectively examine the associations of both the healthy lifestyle score and its corresponding metabolic signature with incident hypertension; and (3) quantify the extent to which the association between healthy lifestyle and hypertension is mediated through this metabolomic pathway using causal mediation analysis. This prospective cohort study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROCSS guidelines [15]. By elucidating the metabolic mechanisms underlying the protective effects of a healthy lifestyle, this study may help identify intermediate biomarkers that could support hypertension prevention.

Methods

Study design and participants

The UK Biobank is a large, population-based, multicenter, prospective cohort study [16]. Between 2006 and 2010, approximately 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years were recruited from 22 assessment centers across the United Kingdom. At baseline, participants completed comprehensive touchscreen questionnaires covering sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors, underwent standardized physical assessments, and provided biological samples for subsequent analyses [16]. The UK Biobank study received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

For this study, participant selection was performed in several stages. Of the 502,175 individuals initially enrolled in the UK Biobank, those who did not undergo NMR metabolomics testing (n = 227,938) or had incomplete NMR metabolomics biomarker measurements (n = 26,052) were first excluded, leaving 248,185 participants with complete NMR metabolomics data. Participants with missing lifestyle information (n = 67,382) and those with prevalent hypertension at baseline (n = 54,126) were excluded. After applying the exclusion criteria, 126,677 eligible participants were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Lifestyle score

Given the interrelationships among lifestyle factors and their collective influence on mortality and morbidity, this study constructed a composite healthy lifestyle score based on evidence from the United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and in accordance with recommendations from the World Health Organization and the American Heart Association (AHA) for the prevention of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [17–19]. The score comprised four modifiable lifestyle components: smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and diet [20]. Detailed definitions and assessment methods for each lifestyle factor are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Table 1).

Smoking status at baseline was classified as either current or non-current smoking, with non-current smoking considered the healthy category [5]. Participants who reported either no alcohol consumption or drinking only on special occasions were classified as irregular drinkers. In the United Kingdom, one standard alcoholic drink contains 8 g of ethanol. Healthy alcohol consumption was defined as no more than one standard drink per day for women and no more than two standard drinks per day for men [21]. Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity activity, 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of both per week [21, 22]. A healthy diet was defined as meeting recommendations for at least five of the ten predefined food categories, emphasizing higher consumption of vegetables, fruits, fish, and whole grains while limiting refined grains, unprocessed meat, and processed meat intake [23, 24].

Each lifestyle component was assigned a score of 1 for a healthy level and 0 for an unhealthy level. The individual component scores were summed to generate a composite healthy lifestyle score ranging from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyle behaviors. To facilitate the evaluation of dose-response patterns and the presentation of baseline characteristics, participants were further categorized into three lifestyle groups according to their total scores: 0–1, 2, and 3–4 points [10].

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics

A high-throughput NMR metabolomics platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd (Finland) was used to quantify metabolic biomarkers in randomly selected EDTA plasma samples. Following quality control procedures, 251 metabolites were retained for analysis, including 14 subclasses of lipoprotein lipids, fatty acids and their components, as well as a range of low-molecular-weight metabolites (see Supplementary Table 2 for details) [25]. These biomarkers represent multiple metabolic pathways and provide a comprehensive characterization of individual metabolic status. All NMR-derived metabolite concentrations were log10-transformed before analysis. Detailed information on metabolite measurements and detection procedures is available through the UK Biobank online resource (https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/label.cgi? id=220).

Determination of hypertension outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was incident hypertension occurring during follow-up (median follow-up: 13.43 years). The definition of hypertension and the corresponding UK Biobank codes are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Table 3). Incident hypertension was identified using a comprehensive and rigorous case ascertainment approach that integrated multiple data sources, including the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10), the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures version 4 (OPCS-4), self-reported physician diagnoses, and Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) records [10, 26]. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of recruitment until the earliest occurrence of incident hypertension, end of follow-up, loss to follow-up, or death.

Covariates

The covariates included in this study comprised sociodemographic characteristics, health status, genetic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and medical history. Sociodemographic variables included age (years), sex (female/male), race (White/other), educational attainment (university degree/no university degree), and household income (<£18,000/≥£18,000). Health status was assessed using body mass index (BMI), categorized as < 25.0, 25.0–29.9, and ≥ 30.0 kg/m². Genetic susceptibility was evaluated using a hypertension-specific standardized polygenic risk score (PRS) obtained directly from the UK Biobank (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/field.cgi? id=26244).

Lifestyle-related factors included smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and diet. Medical history included diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD; including ischemic heart disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke), and cancer.

Information on these variables was obtained from the UK Biobank through baseline touchscreen questionnaires, interviewer-administered assessments, nurse-conducted physical examinations, laboratory measurements, and participant self-reports [21, 24]. BMI and PRS were determined through physical measurements and genetic analyses. In comparison, age, sex, race, educational attainment, household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, dietary habits, and histories of diabetes, CVD, and cancer were collected from baseline questionnaires, interviews, or self-reported records.

To minimize bias and improve the robustness of the statistical analyses, missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) and multiple imputation based on latent class (MILC) [27]. The chained equations approach was used to impute missing values for age, sex, race, education, household income, BMI, PRS, and histories of diabetes, CVD, and cancer. A total of five imputed datasets were generated for the analyses. All imputations were performed using the original observed data, and no passive imputation was applied to transformed variables.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics stratified by incident hypertension status are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Differences in continuous variables between groups were assessed using Student’s t-tests, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

To construct lifestyle-related metabolic signatures, all NMR-derived metabolites were first standardized, and elastic net regression was applied to develop metabolic signature scores associated with the healthy lifestyle score. Elastic net regression combines the strengths of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO; L1 regularization) and ridge regression (L2 regularization), allowing simultaneous variable selection by identifying the most informative metabolites while effectively addressing multicollinearity by retaining correlated metabolite groups. This approach generated a robust and interpretable metabolic signature. The resulting metabolic signature score was subsequently standardized to a mean of 0 and an SD of 1 within the analytical sample. Therefore, a one-SD increase represented a moderate population-wide improvement in the overall metabolite score, reflecting a substantially healthier metabolic profile. Spearman correlation analysis was then performed to evaluate the relationships between the metabolic signature, individual lifestyle factors, and the overall healthy lifestyle score.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the associations between the healthy lifestyle score, its corresponding metabolic signature, and incident hypertension. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race, educational attainment, and household income. Model 2 was further adjusted for BMI and the polygenic risk score (PRS). Model 3 additionally included histories of diabetes mellitus, CVD, and cancer. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify these associations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were further performed to assess potential non-linear associations between the healthy lifestyle score, the metabolic signature, and hypertension risk after adjustment for potential confounders.

To investigate whether the metabolic signature mediated the association between healthy lifestyle and incident hypertension, a counterfactual causal mediation analysis was conducted using the CMAverse package to estimate the mediation proportion and its corresponding 95% CI [28]. Subgroup and interaction analyses were also performed to assess the consistency of the associations across different participant characteristics.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. First, participants with missing covariate data were excluded to minimize potential bias arising from incomplete information. Second, participants who developed hypertension within the first two years of follow-up were excluded to reduce the possibility of reverse causation, as these individuals may have modified unhealthy lifestyle behaviors after diagnosis. Third, participants with a history of CVD at baseline were excluded to confirm that the primary findings were not driven by pre-existing cardiovascular disease.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with participants who remained free of hypertension (non-hypertensive participants), those who developed hypertension differed significantly across most baseline characteristics. Participants in the hypertension group were older (58.74 ± 7.48 vs. 54.26 ± 8.05 years, P < 0.001), were more likely to be male (54.71% vs. 45.04%, P < 0.001), had a higher prevalence of not holding a university degree (69.99% vs. 60.35%, P < 0.001), had a higher mean BMI (28.06 ± 4.58 vs. 26.37 ± 4.12 kg/m², P < 0.001), and were more likely to have an annual household income below £18,000 (24.63% vs. 16.39%, P < 0.001).

Participants who developed hypertension also showed a higher mean polygenic risk score (PRS) (0.02 ± 0.91 vs. −0.20 ± 0.93), along with higher prevalences of diabetes (4.36% vs. 1.46%), CVD (4.89% vs. 1.91%), and cancer (10.68% vs. 7.76%) than those without incident hypertension (all P < 0.001). The hypertension group had a lower mean healthy lifestyle score (2.18 ± 0.99 vs. 2.34 ± 0.98; P < 0.001). They also had lower proportions of non-current smoking (49.38% vs. 58.93%, P < 0.001) and healthy alcohol consumption (51.58% vs. 52.41%, P = 0.037), together with higher proportions of insufficient physical activity (39.38% vs. 37.25%, P < 0.001). Unhealthy dietary patterns not meeting the DASH-based criteria (43.29% vs. 39.66%, P < 0.001). No significant difference in racial distribution was observed between the two groups (P = 0.095).

Identification of lifestyle-related metabolites

Elastic net regression was applied to 251 metabolites, yielding 76 metabolites associated with the healthy lifestyle score (Supplementary Fig. 2). Figure 1 presents these metabolites, ranked by their positive and negative elastic net regression coefficients. Among the positively weighted metabolites, linoleic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids (LA_pct), omega-3 fatty acids as a percentage of total fatty acids (Omega_3_pct), and linoleic acid (LA) showed the largest positive coefficients. In comparison, glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA), omega-6 fatty acids (Omega_6), and the degree of unsaturation (Unsaturation) showed the largest negative coefficients. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3, the lifestyle-related metabolic signature comprised a diverse range of metabolites, with lipids, lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, and amino acids representing the predominant components.

The lifestyle-related metabolic signature was constructed by weighting the selected metabolites according to their elastic net regression coefficients (Supplementary Table 4). Correlation analyses demonstrated clear associations between the metabolic signature, the healthy lifestyle score, and individual lifestyle factors (Supplementary Fig. 4). Among the selected metabolites, the average diameter of high-density lipoprotein particles (HDL-size) and docosahexaenoic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids (DHA_pct) showed the strongest positive correlations with the healthy lifestyle score. In comparison, monounsaturated fatty acids as a percentage of total fatty acids (MUFA_pct) and the triglycerides-to-phosphoglycerides ratio (TG_by_PG) presented the strongest negative correlations.

When individual lifestyle components were examined, non-current smoking showed the strongest positive correlation with LA_pct and the strongest negative correlation with MUFA_pct. Healthy alcohol consumption was most strongly positively correlated with phospholipids in medium HDL (M-HDL-PL) and most strongly negatively correlated with the apolipoprotein B-to-apolipoprotein A1 ratio (ApoB_by_ApoA1). Regular physical activity demonstrated the strongest positive correlation with the polyunsaturated-to-monounsaturated fatty acid ratio (PUFA_by_MUFA) and the strongest negative correlation with GlycA. A healthy diet showed the strongest positive correlation with DHA_pct and the strongest negative correlation with MUFA_pct (Supplementary Fig. 4). The distribution of lifestyle-related metabolic signature scores in participants with and without incident hypertension is summarized in Supplementary Table 5.

Association between lifestyle and associated metabolic signatures and hypertension

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine the associations of individual lifestyle factors, the composite healthy lifestyle score, and the lifestyle-related metabolic signature with incident hypertension. The associations between smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and adherence to the DASH diet and hypertension risk across the three progressively adjusted models are summarized in Supplementary Table 6. After sequential adjustment for potential confounders, current smoking (Model 3: HR, 0.808, [95% CI: 0.785–0.832], p < 0.0001) showed an inverse statistical association with incident hypertension, regular physical activity (Model 3: HR, 0.934; 95% CI: 0.907–0.962; P < 0.0001) and adherence to the DASH diet (Model 3: HR, 0.958; 95% CI: 0.930–0.987; P = 0.004) were identified as independent protective factors. In comparison, healthy alcohol consumption was not significantly associated with hypertension risk after full adjustment (Model 3: HR, 1.063; 95% CI: 1.000–1.094; P = 0.051).

The associations between the lifestyle-related metabolic signature and incident hypertension are presented in Table 2. In Model 1, each one-standard-deviation (SD) increase in the metabolic signature score was associated with a 51% lower risk of hypertension (HR, 0.493; 95% CI: 0.470–0.517; P < 0.0001), indicating that a population-wide one-SD increase in the overall metabolic signature reflecting healthier metabolic characteristics corresponded to a substantial reduction in hypertension risk. Although the magnitude of the association was attenuated after further adjustment for BMI, PRS, and disease history, it remained highly significant in both Model 2 (HR, 0.675; 95% CI: 0.641–0.711) and Model 3 (HR, 0.699; 95% CI: 0.664–0.736) (both P < 0.0001). Compared with participants in the low metabolic signature group, those in the medium- and high-score groups consistently demonstrated lower risks of hypertension across all models, with significant dose-response relationships (all trend P < 0.0001). In the fully adjusted Model 3, the medium and high metabolic signature groups had 15% (HR, 0.845; 95% CI: 0.816–0.875) and 24% (HR, 0.758; 95% CI: 0.720–0.798) lower risks of hypertension, respectively (Table 2).

A similar pattern was observed for the healthy lifestyle score. Each one-SD increase in the lifestyle score was associated with an 11% lower risk of hypertension in Model 1 (HR, 0.892; 95% CI: 0.879–0.906; P < 0.0001), and this association remained statistically significant after full adjustment in Model 3 (HR, 0.935; 95% CI: 0.921–0.949; P < 0.0001). Relative to the low lifestyle score group, participants with medium and high lifestyle scores also had significantly lower risks of hypertension, with significant dose-response trends across categories (trend P < 0.001 in Model 3). In the fully adjusted model, the risk of hypertension was reduced by approximately 9% in the medium-score group (HR, 0.907; 95% CI: 0.843–0.975; P = 0.009) and by approximately 12% in the high-score group (HR, 0.879; 95% CI: 0.818–0.944; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

As shown in Fig. 2A, the metabolic signature demonstrated a significant non-linear association with hypertension risk (overall P < 0.001; non-linearity P = 0.025). In comparison, the healthy lifestyle score showed a significant linear inverse association with hypertension risk (overall P < 0.001; non-linearity P = 0.716) (Fig. 2B). The hazard ratio declined steadily with increasing lifestyle score, producing an approximately linear downward trend, indicating that higher lifestyle scores were consistently associated with progressively lower hypertension risk across the observed range without evidence of a significant non-linear threshold.

To further characterize the contribution of individual metabolites, Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed for each of the 76 lifestyle-related metabolites (Supplementary Table 7). Higher concentrations of several metabolites were significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension. Among these, the systemic inflammatory biomarker glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) showed the strongest positive association (HR, 1.145; 95% CI: 1.128–1.161; P < 0.0001). Several lipid- and lipoprotein-related biomarkers also demonstrated significant positive associations, including the apolipoprotein B-to-apolipoprotein A1 ratio (ApoB_by_ApoA1; HR, 1.048; P < 0.0001) and very-low-density lipoprotein particle size (VLDL_size; HR, 1.074; P < 0.0001).

Multiple metabolites were inversely associated with hypertension risk. The strongest protective associations were observed for albumin (HR, 0.945; 95% CI: 0.931–0.959; P < 0.0001), average HDL particle diameter (HDL_size; HR, 0.950; 95% CI: 0.933–0.967; P < 0.0001), the polyunsaturated-to-monounsaturated fatty acid ratio (PUFA_by_MUFA; HR, 0.865; 95% CI: 0.851–0.879; P < 0.0001), and linoleic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids (LA_pct; HR, 0.862; 95% CI: 0.849–0.874; P < 0.0001). In comparison, metabolites including pyruvate, alanine (Ala), and leucine (Leu) were not significantly associated with incident hypertension (P > 0.05).

A higher healthy lifestyle score was significantly associated with a lower risk of hypertension (total effect: HR, 0.886; 95% CI: 0.811–0.920; P < 0.001). This overall association comprised both a direct effect (HR, 0.951; 95% CI: 0.870–0.986; P < 0.001) and an indirect effect mediated through the lifestyle-related metabolic signature (HR, 0.931; 95% CI: 0.927–0.940; P < 0.001). The mediation analysis estimated that 57.08% (95% CI: 31.89%–83.15%; P < 0.001) of the protective association between a healthy lifestyle and hypertension was explained by the metabolic signature, indicating that more than half of the observed benefit was mediated through metabolomic pathways.

Among the individual lifestyle components, adherence to the DASH diet demonstrated the largest mediation proportion. Although adherence to the DASH diet was independently associated with a lower risk of hypertension (total effect: HR, 0.958; P < 0.001), 66.51% (95% CI: 43.56%–80.37%; P < 0.001) of this protective association was mediated through the metabolic signature. Likewise, the protective association between regular physical activity and hypertension (total effect: HR, 0.935; P < 0.001) was mediated by the metabolic signature to an estimated extent of 35.09% (95% CI: 25.87%–38.68%; P < 0.001). Although non-current smoking was also associated with a lower risk of hypertension (total effect: HR, 0.809; P < 0.001), its indirect effect through the metabolic signature was minimal (HR, 0.991), with only 3.85% (95% CI: 3.24%–4.22%; P < 0.001) of the association explained by metabolomic mediation.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, a series of sensitivity analyses was performed. These analyses excluded participants with missing covariate data (final sample size: N = 112,415) (Supplementary Table 8, Supplementary Tables 9, and Supplementary Fig. 5), participants who developed hypertension within the first two years of follow-up (final sample size: N = 125,568) (Supplementary Table 10, Supplementary Tables 11, and Supplementary Fig. 6), participants with a history of CVD at baseline (final sample size: N = 123,696) (Supplementary Table 12, Supplementary Tables 13, and Supplementary Fig. 7), and participants of non-White racial/ethnic backgrounds (final sample size: N = 121,726) (Supplementary Table 14, Supplementary Tables 15, and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Across all sensitivity analyses, the results were highly consistent with the primary analyses. The inverse associations of a healthy lifestyle and its corresponding metabolic signature with incident hypertension remained statistically significant; the metabolic signature continued to mediate a substantial proportion of the protective association between lifestyle and hypertension, and the distinct dose-response relationships observed for the healthy lifestyle score and the metabolic signature were preserved. These consistent findings provide strong support for the robustness and reliability of the study’s main conclusions.

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective cohort study based on the UK Biobank, we comprehensively examined the associations of healthy lifestyle behaviors, lifestyle-related metabolic signatures, and their combined relationship with the risk of incident hypertension. The mediating role of the metabolic signature in the association between lifestyle and hypertension was further evaluated, and confirmed the robustness of the findings through multiple sensitivity analyses. Our results showed that both a healthier lifestyle and a more favorable lifestyle-related metabolic signature were independently associated with a lower risk of incident hypertension. These associations also demonstrated heterogeneity across specific population subgroups. The lifestyle-related metabolic signature mediated 57.08% of the protective association between a healthy lifestyle and hypertension, indicating that metabolomic alterations account for more than half of the observed lifestyle-related benefit.

The mediation analysis was conducted within a counterfactual framework, which relies on four key assumptions: (1) the absence of unmeasured confounding between the exposure (healthy lifestyle) and the mediator (metabolic signature); (2) the absence of unmeasured confounding between the mediator and the outcome (incident hypertension), conditional on the exposure; (3) the absence of unmeasured confounding between the exposure and the outcome; and (4) the absence of mediator–outcome confounders that are themselves influenced by the exposure. To minimize potential bias, a comprehensive range of demographic, socioeconomic, genetic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates was adjusted in the mediation models. No significant interaction between exposure and the mediator was observed, supporting the assumptions underlying the mediation analysis. Although residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, the consistency of the findings across multiple sensitivity analyses provides reassurance regarding the robustness of the estimated mediation effects.

These results offer new evidence that metabolomic alterations represent an important biological pathway through which healthy lifestyle behaviors influence hypertension risk. By identifying metabolic signatures as key intermediates linking lifestyle to hypertension, our study provides mechanistic insight into this relationship. It supports the development of metabolomics-informed strategies for the prevention and management of hypertension.

A healthy lifestyle is widely recognized as a cornerstone of chronic disease prevention, with substantial evidence demonstrating its ability to reduce all-cause mortality and prolong life expectancy [29, 30]. As the most prevalent modifiable cardiovascular risk factor, hypertension is strongly influenced by lifestyle behaviors. Extensive epidemiological studies have shown that unhealthy dietary habits, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking contribute to elevated blood pressure through multiple biological mechanisms, including the promotion of obesity, activation of the sympathetic nervous system, vascular endothelial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation [31–33]. Adherence to an overall healthy lifestyle, particularly regular physical activity and the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, has consistently been shown in randomized controlled trials and prospective cohort studies to lower blood pressure and reduce the risk of hypertension [5, 34].

Our findings reinforce this well-established evidence while extending current knowledge by suggesting that the protective effects of healthy lifestyle behaviors may be mediated, at least in part, through favorable alterations in systemic metabolism. The substantial mediation effect observed in this study indicates that metabolic remodeling may represent an important biological mechanism linking lifestyle behaviors to hypertension prevention.

An unexpected finding was the inverse statistical association between smoking status and incident hypertension, which appears inconsistent with the well-established evidence identifying smoking as a major cardiovascular risk factor. A similar paradoxical observation has been reported by Fu et al., who found an inverse association between alcohol consumption and the incidence of coronary artery disease [10]. This should not be interpreted as evidence that smoking protects against hypertension. Instead, it is more likely explained by inherent limitations of observational cohort studies, including survivor bias and competing-risk bias. This interpretation is further supported by our mediation analysis, which showed that smoking had only a minimal indirect effect through the metabolic signature, with a mediation proportion of just 3.85%, suggesting that metabolomic pathways account for little of the observed association between smoking and hypertension risk.

In this study, the association between alcohol consumption and incident hypertension did not reach conventional statistical significance (P = 0.051), although the point estimate suggested a modest increase in risk (HR = 1.063). This finding is consistent with emerging evidence indicating that even moderate alcohol consumption may contribute to elevated blood pressure. However, the confidence interval narrowly crossed the null value (95% CI: 1.000–1.094), indicating considerable statistical uncertainty. Consequently, neither a true null association nor a modest protective effect can be definitively excluded based on the current data. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating more detailed alcohol consumption categories and repeated exposure assessments are needed to characterize this relationship.

These results further underscore the importance of maintaining a comprehensive healthy lifestyle, including non-smoking, regular physical activity, and adherence to a healthy dietary pattern, as a fundamental strategy for the primary prevention of hypertension.

Metabolites represent the downstream products of gene expression, protein activity, and environmental exposures, including lifestyle factors such as diet and physical activity. As a result, they provide a dynamic and integrated reflection of an individual’s physiological and pathological state [35]. In this study, 76 lifestyle-related metabolites, primarily comprising lipids, lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, and amino acids, were identified, which collectively formed the core of the lifestyle-related metabolic signature. Among these metabolites, linoleic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids (LA_pct) showed the strongest positive association with hypertension protection, whereas glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) showed the strongest inverse association.

Linoleic acid is an essential polyunsaturated fatty acid that has been shown to lower blood pressure through multiple mechanisms, including improving vascular endothelial function by enhancing nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, suppressing vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, and attenuating systemic inflammation [36–38]. In comparison, GlycA is a well-established biomarker of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation that reflects the cumulative inflammatory burden within the body [39, 40]. Elevated GlycA concentrations have been associated with vascular endothelial injury, increased arterial stiffness, and vascular calcification, all of which contribute to the development of hypertension [41, 42]. The predominance of lipid- and inflammation-related metabolites within the identified metabolic signature suggests that improvements in lipid metabolism and maintenance of inflammatory homeostasis largely mediate the beneficial effects of healthy lifestyle behaviors on hypertension.

Previous studies have demonstrated that regular physical activity reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease by mitigating chronic inflammation, enhancing lipid oxidation, and improving lipoprotein metabolism [43, 44]. Likewise, dietary modification plays a central role in maintaining cardiovascular health [45]. Consistent with previous reports [46], our findings further demonstrated that distinct lifestyle behaviors were associated with specific metabolomic profiles. For example, regular physical activity was most strongly associated with the polyunsaturated-to-monounsaturated fatty acid ratio (PUFA_by_MUFA) and the inflammatory biomarker GlycA. In comparison, adherence to the DASH diet was most closely related to docosahexaenoic acid as a percentage of total fatty acids (DHA_pct) and monounsaturated fatty acids as a percentage of total fatty acids (MUFA_pct). Large-scale population studies have also reported that elevated circulating MUFA levels are associated with an increased risk of hypertension, with this relationship being even more pronounced among individuals with a high genetic predisposition to the disease [47]. These findings provide a plausible molecular basis for the differential effects of individual lifestyle behaviors on hypertension risk.

In this study, both the healthy lifestyle score and the corresponding lifestyle-related metabolic signature were independently associated with a lower risk of incident hypertension, with significant inverse dose-response relationships observed for both. The healthy lifestyle score demonstrated a linear inverse association with hypertension risk, indicating that each incremental improvement in lifestyle behaviors was associated with a progressive reduction in hypertension risk. This finding is consistent with the concept, supported by numerous studies, that even modest lifestyle improvements can yield meaningful cardiovascular health benefits [48], including a reduced risk of hypertension[49].

Moreover, the lifestyle-related metabolic signature showed a significant non-linear association with hypertension risk, consistent with previous reports [50]. This finding suggests that the protective effect of favorable metabolic profiles may increase more rapidly or reach a plateau beyond a certain threshold. Such a non-linear relationship indicates that the influence of metabolic signatures on hypertension is not simply the cumulative effect of individual metabolites, but rather reflects the integrated interactions among multiple metabolic pathways, giving rise to emergent biological properties [51]. These patterns likely result from the saturation of key physiological pathways, intrinsic feedback mechanisms that maintain metabolic homeostasis, and synergistic or antagonistic interactions among different metabolites [52]. Accordingly, the observed non-linearity highlights the complex pathophysiology of hypertension as a systemic disorder and suggests the existence of an optimal metabolic range that may maximize the benefits of preventive interventions.

The inverse associations of both the healthy lifestyle score and the lifestyle-related metabolic signature with hypertension remained statistically significant after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, including BMI, PRS, and baseline comorbidities. However, the effect estimates were modestly attenuated. This attenuation suggests that adiposity and genetic susceptibility partially contribute to the lifestyle–metabolite–hypertension pathway but do not fully account for the independent protective effects of healthy lifestyle behaviors and favorable metabolic profiles. These findings further indicate that individuals may still derive meaningful reductions in hypertension risk through lifestyle modification and metabolic optimization, even in the presence of excess body weight or a high genetic predisposition, supporting the development of personalized strategies for hypertension prevention [53, 54].

A key finding of this study is that the lifestyle-related metabolic signature serves as an important biological mediator linking healthy lifestyle behaviors to the risk of incident hypertension. However, the magnitude of mediation varied across individual lifestyle components. The metabolic signature accounted for 57.08% of the protective association between the composite healthy lifestyle score and hypertension, suggesting that more than half of the benefits of a healthy lifestyle may be explained by favorable metabolic alterations. Among the individual lifestyle factors, adherence to the DASH diet showed the greatest mediation proportion (66.51%), followed by regular physical activity (35.09%), whereas non-current smoking demonstrated the smallest mediation proportion (3.85%). These findings indicate that different lifestyle behaviors may influence hypertension through distinct biological pathways, reflecting the heterogeneous metabolic mechanisms underlying their health effects [55, 56].

The substantial mediation effect observed for the DASH diet suggests that its antihypertensive benefits are largely driven by metabolic remodeling, including favorable changes in fatty acid composition, reductions in systemic inflammatory markers such as GlycA, and improvements in lipoprotein metabolism. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the DASH dietary pattern lowers blood pressure through coordinated regulation of multiple metabolic pathways [57, 58]. In comparison, the minimal mediation effect observed for smoking implies that its association with hypertension is mediated predominantly through mechanisms other than the metabolic profile captured in this study. These mechanisms likely include sympathetic nervous system activation, nicotine-induced vasoconstriction, and endothelial injury, all of which have been well documented as contributors to elevated blood pressure and vascular dysfunction [59]. These results advance our understanding of the biological mechanisms linking lifestyle to hypertension and highlight metabolomic signatures as potential intermediate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for hypertension prevention.

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated significant effect modification according to age, sex, educational attainment, and household income, suggesting that the associations between healthy lifestyle, metabolic signatures, and hypertension are not uniform across the population. These findings have important public health implications. Although the promotion of healthy lifestyle behaviors should remain a universal strategy for hypertension prevention, greater emphasis should be placed on older adults, men, and individuals with lower socioeconomic status. These populations may face additional biological, behavioral, and environmental barriers to maintaining healthy lifestyles. They may therefore benefit from more intensive, tailored, and supportive interventions to improve cardiovascular health and reduce health disparities [60, 61].

This study has several important strengths. First, the large sample size (126,677 participants) and extended follow-up period (median, 13.43 years) provided substantial statistical power to detect modest associations and a sufficient number of incident hypertension events. Second, by integrating behavioral epidemiology with high-throughput metabolomics, lifestyle-related metabolic signatures were identified and comprehensively characterized their mediating role in the association between healthy lifestyle behaviors and hypertension risk. Third, the robustness of the findings was strengthened through comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders and a series of sensitivity analyses, including excluding participants with missing covariate data, those who developed hypertension during the early follow-up period, and those with pre-existing CVD at baseline. These analyses consistently supported the primary findings and reduced the potential influence of residual confounding and reverse causation.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, as with all observational cohort studies, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, and causal relationships cannot be definitively established. Future interventional studies are needed to determine whether modifying lifestyle-related metabolic signatures can directly reduce the risk of hypertension. Second, metabolomic profiling was performed only at baseline, precluding longitudinal assessment of metabolite concentrations and potentially underestimating the mediating effects of dynamic metabolic alterations. Third, the healthy lifestyle score incorporated only four conventional lifestyle factors and did not include other potentially relevant health behaviors, such as sleep duration and quality, sedentary behavior, or BMI. Fourth, lifestyle information was obtained through self-reported questionnaires, making it susceptible to recall and reporting bias. Finally, because the study population consisted predominantly of individuals of European ancestry, the generalizability of these findings to other racial and ethnic populations may be limited, particularly given potential differences in metabolic profiles across diverse populations.

Conclusion

This large-scale prospective cohort study identified a distinct lifestyle-related metabolic signature. It demonstrated that it plays a substantial mediating role in the association between healthy lifestyle behaviors and incident hypertension risk. These results suggest that the protective effects of healthy lifestyle behaviors are, in part, mediated by favorable metabolic alterations, providing new mechanistic insights into hypertension prevention and highlighting the metabolome as a potential target for future preventive strategies.
