Use of the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline for labor and delivery in China: implications for clinical practice
Authors: Chengjuan Sun, Shaofei Su, Wei Song, Haili Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is an unmet need for a large comprehensive population-based dataset documenting national birthing trends in China and a partograph specifically tailored to Chinese women. This study assessed the impact of using the World Health Organization (WHO) partograph or Zhang’s guideline to manage labor and delivery in China and inform the development of a partograph that specifically aligns with the progression of labor in Chinese women.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included low-risk nulliparous women with a singleton, full-term fetus in cephalic presentation entering spontaneous labor at a specialized obstetric hospital in China between January 2010 and June 2022. Pregnant women were managed according to the WHO partograph (January 2010-August 2014, n = 31,286) or Zhang’s guideline (September 2014-June 2022, n = 49,821).ResultsRates of assisted reproduction (4.57% vs. 1.05%; p < 0.0001) and hypertension (7.44% vs. 6.71%; p < 0.0001) were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline compared to the WHO partograph. Rates of labor intervention (35.31% vs. 13.95%; p < 0.0001), including induction of labor by oxytocin, artificial rupture of membranes, lateral episiotomy and conversion to cesarean section (all, p < 0.0001), were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph. Rates of forceps assisted vaginal deliveries (12.67% vs. 6.42%; p < 0.0001) and postpartum hemorrhage (10.9% vs. 6.2%; p < 0.0001) were significantly higher, and birth asphyxia (0.15% vs. 0.09%; p = 0.02) was significantly lower, for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable insights into the utilization of the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline in managing labor and delivery among Chinese women. Findings indicate that women managed according to Zhang’s guideline had higher rates of assisted reproduction and hypertension, suggesting a potentially different demographic profile or underlying health conditions compared to women managed according to the WHO partograph. Notably, the use of the WHO partograph was linked to a significant increase in labor interventions, while Zhang’s guideline resulted in higher rates of forceps-assisted vaginal deliveries and postpartum hemorrhage, yet interestingly, a lower incidence of birth asphyxia. These contrasting outcomes underscore the importance of aligning labor management tools with the specific needs and progression of labor in Chinese women. The results advocate for a tailored partograph that could better reflect the unique characteristics of Chinese women and optimize decision making and maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Introduction

Accurately evaluating progression of labor and identifying dystocia are critical for implementing timely obstetric interventions that improve maternal and neonatal outcomes [1]. The need for obstetric interventions is rising due to changes in the sociodemographic characteristics and health profiles of women, including increased maternal age, higher maternal and fetal body mass index, decreased maternal physical activity, and a rise in maternal metabolic and reproductive immune disorders. The decision to opt for cesarean section, especially in cases of suspected dystocia, must be based on strict criteria. Original descriptions of normal and abnormal labor, which accurately describe patterns of cervical dilatation and fetal descent, were based on clinical observations [2]. Contemporary care during labor and delivery relies on guidelines, labor graphs, and partographs. These tools are essential for delineating the stages of labor, discerning causes of dystocia, and determining when to intervene.

The World Health Organization (WHO) partograph, established in 1994, effectively differentiates normal and abnormal progress in labor, allowing early detection of aberrant labor patterns. The WHO advocates for universal use of the partograph during labor [3]. More recently, a notable study by Zhang examined labor patterns across 19 hospitals in the US using data from the Consortium on Safe Labor. Findings revealed that cervical dilation in women with singleton births, spontaneous labor onset, vertex presentation, vaginal delivery and normal perinatal outcomes, progressed slower than anticipated before reaching 6 cm. Zhang proposed a labor curve and suggested that allowing labor to continue for longer before 6 cm could decrease cesarean deliveries [4]. Zhang’s guideline informed recommendations for the clinical management of labor by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the International Association of Maternal and Fetal Medicine.

In China, there is an unmet need for evidence-based standards guiding the management of labor and delivery. Currently, most healthcare settings in China use recommendations developed for European and American populations. Specifically, the population used to develop Zhang’s guideline included only a small percentage (< 5%) of Asian participants; therefore, it does not adequately reflect the demographic traits, cultural characteristics, body type, lifestyle habits and maternity care unique to childbearing women in Asia. Given that China comprises one-fifth of the global population, the absence of a large comprehensive population-based dataset documenting national birthing trends and the lack of a partograph specifically tailored to Asian mothers, particularly Chinese women, are notable deficiencies.

The objective of this study was to assess the impact of using the WHO partograph or Zhang’s guideline to manage labor and delivery in China and inform the development of a partograph that specifically aligns with the progression of labor in Chinese women.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective review of the medical records of low-risk nulliparous women who entered spontaneous labor at a specialized obstetric hospital in China between January 2010 and June 2022. Women were managed according to the WHO partograph (January 1, 2010, to August 31, 2014) or Zhang’s guideline (September 2014 to June 30, 2022) based on the hospital’s routine practice at the time. Inclusion criteria were: (1) nulliparous, term, singleton pregnancy; (2) vertex presentation; and (3) absence of significant cephalopelvic disproportion assessed by pelvimetry, estimation of fetal biparietal diameter, and ultrasound. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe pregnancy complications and comorbidities, including but not limited to major cardiovascular, pulmonary, liver, kidney, and endocrine diseases, tumors, and severe anemia; or (2) history of uterine surgery, placenta previa, or fetal malformations.

The study was conducted by a team of experienced midwives, who ensured consistent and rigorous adherence to the study protocol. The study protocol was approved by the Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Capital Medical University (2024-KY-013-01) Institutional Review Board.

Management of labor

The WHO partograph, based on the work of Friedman, was used to monitor labor and delivery and identify complications in pregnant women between January 1, 2010 and August 31, 2014 [3]. Zhang’s guideline, applied according to the New Standard and Management of Labor: Expert Consensus (2014; Obstetrics Subgroup, Chinese Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Chinese Medical Association), was used to manage pregnant women between September 2014 and June 30, 2022 [5, 6]. Labor analgesia was provided according to the directives on standardizing intraspinal labor analgesia, which increased its coverage in 2018 [7].

Outcomes

Maternal outcomes included duration of labor and rates of labor intervention, fever during labor, postpartum hemorrhage, and urinary retention. According to the WHO partograph, arrest of the active phase is defined as no progress in cervical dilation (from 3 to 4 centimeters to full dilation) during the active phase of labor for more than 4 h, even with adequate contractions (at least three contractions every 10 min) [3]. Zhang’s guideline considers the active phase to begin when cervical dilation reaches 6 centimeters. Therefore, arrest of the active phase is defined as no further progress in dilation for 2 h after cervical dilation reaches 6 centimeters, despite adequate contractions [5, 6].

Postpartum hemorrhage was diagnosed as vaginal bleeding > 500 ml within 24 h after vaginal delivery, or 1000 ml after a cesarean section, as per WHO guidelines [8].

Neonatal outcomes included: birth weight and birth asphyxia. Severity of birth asphyxia was categorized based on Apgar 5-minute scores of > 7 (normal)， 4–7 (mild) and ≤ 3 (severe) [9].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS v26.0. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range, and were analyzed with non-parametric tests. Count data were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and were analyzed using chi-square tests. Ordinal data were analyzed using non-parametric testing methods. All tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 220,907 low-risk pregnant women were eligible for inclusion in this study. After screening, 81,107 pregnant women were included in the analysis, comprising 31,286 low-risk pregnant women who were managed according to the WHO partograph (January 1, 2010, to August 31, 2014), and 49,821 low-risk pregnant women who were managed according to Zhang’s guideline (September 2014 to June 30, 2022) (Fig. 1). The number of pregnant women who were managed according to Zhang’s guideline was larger than those managed according to the WHO partograph due to the longer time period over which Zhang’s guideline was applied in the hospital’s routine clinical practice.

(8.22% vs. 4.94%; p < 0.0001) was significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph. Discussion

The goals of labor curves, partographs and guidelines are to ensure maternal and neonatal safety, minimize unnecessary labor interventions, and promote natural childbirth. This study assessed the impact of using the WHO partograph or Zhang’s guideline to manage labor and delivery in China. Findings showed total duration of labor was significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline. Labor intervention rates, including induction of labor by oxytocin, artificial rupture of membranes, lateral episiotomy, and conversion to cesarean section were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph. Rates of fever during labor, forceps assisted vaginal deliveries, postpartum hemorrhage, requirement for blood transfusion, and urinary retention were significantly higher, and birth asphyxia was significantly lower, for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guidelines. These findings provide epidemiological data on childbirth in China from a specialized obstetric hospital that spans the changing paradigm from the WHO partograph to Zhang’s guideline. Findings should inform development of a partograph specifically tailored to Chinese pregnant women.

Standards for management of the labor process are crucial for detecting difficulties early and allowing timely interventions. The impact of differences between previous and more recent standards guiding labor and delivery on maternal and fetal outcomes is not well understood [10]. According to the WHO, issues with labor progression that may lead to cesarean section include no cervical change after adequate uterine contractions, persistent occiput posterior and transverse positions, and protracted labor [11]. Zhang’s guideline recognizes that the active stage of labor may start at 6 cm. This led to the removal of previous recommendations for cesarean section based on the duration of latent labor, resulting in a reduction in the cesarean section rate, with no concomitant increase in fetal distress [12–14].

In the present study, demographic and clinical characteristics for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph compared to Zhang’s guideline showed maternal age, and the rates of advanced maternal age, assisted reproduction, maternal obesity, uterine fibroids, hypertension and hyperglycemia were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline. This may be attributable to the end of China’s one-child policy, which is resulting in increased births to older mothers. Older women are more likely to suffer from comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension that may affect their ability to conceive and could negatively impact their pregnancies and outcomes [15]. Rates of hyperglycemia may have been influenced by changes in diagnostic criteria for glucose tolerance tests during the study period. In August 2013, the WHO adopted new diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), which substantially increased the number of GDM diagnoses [16].

In the present study, rates for labor interventions, including induction of labor by oxytocin, artificial rupture of membranes and lateral episiotomy were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph. This may be because Zhang’s guideline adheres more closely to the natural labor process, allowing extensive dilation of the birth canal, which reduces the need to induce or augment labor and delivery, and may minimize associated pain and trauma. Analysis of maternal outcomes showed the total duration of labor, bleeding volume, and rates of fever during labor, postpartum hemorrhage, requirement for blood transfusion, and urinary retention were significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline. Consistent with this, evidence suggests that protracted labor is associated with an increased risk of maternal and neonatal complications [12, 17], including chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony, postpartum urinary retention due to neurologic impairment of micturition, and endometritis [18–25].

Most women included in this study had a vaginal delivery, but the rate of forceps assisted vaginal deliveries was significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guidelines. This may be because protracted labor is associated with increased use of forceps delivery [13], which may replace the use of cesarean section when there is arrest of the second stage of labor [26, 27]. Accordingly, a retrospective cohort study of nulliparous women (N = 1131) at two delivery units in Sweden showed a longer duration (> 2 h) of the passive second stage of labor was associated with an increased risk for an instrumental vaginal delivery [28]. In addition, National Health and Wellness Committee directives issued in 2018 to standardize intraspinal labor analgesia and improve coverage, resulted in an increase in the rate of epidural analgesia. Clinical trials suggest that epidural analgesia does not increase the risk of cesarean delivery, overall or for dystocia, but can significantly increase the risk of instrumental vaginal delivery [7].

Over the past 12 years, professional organizations and medical institutions in China have attached increasing importance to collaborative training on the use of forceps for obstetric teams and promoted the application of forceps deliveries. This has enabled medical institutions to acquire the ability to proficiently carry out forceps deliveries, thus reducing the rate of conversion to cesarean section. The increased acceptance of forceps deliveries by pregnant women has made physicians more inclined to choose this delivery method when making decisions. The physical strength and endurance of pregnant women have improved, enabling them to better cooperate with the exertion and breathing that is required during forceps delivery, thereby increasing the success rate of such deliveries.

In the present study, the rate of conversion to cesarean section was significantly higher for pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph. In contrast to these findings, the Labor Progression study (LaPS), a multicenter, cluster-randomized controlled trial at obstetric units in Norway, found no significant difference in the frequency of intrapartum caesarean section use between the obstetric units assigned to adhere to the WHO partograph compared to Zhang’s guideline [29]. In this study, the application of Zhang’s guideline had an impact on the indications for cesarean section. This guideline recognizes that the active phase of labor may start when the cervix dilates to 6 cm, which negates the recommendation for cesarean section based on the length of the latent phase, reducing the cesarean section rate without an increase in fetal distress. The criteria for cesarean section have evolved over time, with a more comprehensive and evidence-based approach being adopted. Clinicians now consider a multitude of factors, including maternal and fetal conditions, as well as the progress of labor, rather than relying solely on traditional indicators.

With respect to neonatal outcomes, birth asphyxia was significantly lower for pregnant women managed according to Zhang’s guideline. Protracted labor may result in maternal fatigue, causing accumulation of acidic metabolites such as lactic acid. Combined with compromised placental blood flow, this can result in fetal acidosis and neonatal asphyxia. In the present study, the longer duration of labor in women managed using Zhang’s guideline did not appear to increase fetal asphyxia. Similarly, a retrospective, single-center cohort study in a tertiary hospital in Portugal found changing the criteria of labor arrest from the WHO partograph to Zhang’s guideline was not associated with more maternal or neonatal morbidity [30].

Limitations of the study

The study exhibits notable strengths. Its clinical relevance is evident as it pertains directly to labor management in a specific population. The large sample size enhances the reliability of the findings, and the comprehensive comparative analysis provides a detailed understanding of the differences and similarities between the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline. These aspects emphasize the significance of such partographs and guidelines for optimizing decision-making and maternal and neonatal outcomes. The observed variations in factors such as labor intervention rates, mode of delivery, and maternal and neonatal characteristics across women managed according to the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline, offer a foundation for further exploration and potential refinement of labor management strategies.

Nevertheless, the study was not without limitations. The retrospective design inherently brought potential for biases and data inaccuracies. The single-center nature of the study restricted the generalizability of the results.

Future research should focus on addressing these limitations. Prospective studies spanning multiple centers would generate robust evidence regarding the efficacy of different labor management partographs and guidelines. A meticulous examination of both the theoretical and practical aspects of the partographs and guidelines, accompanied by long-term follow-up of maternal and neonatal health, would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on the childbirth process and outcomes. This, in turn, would facilitate the development of a customized and effective partograph for labor management within China, potentially enhancing clinical practice and maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Conclusion

Labor curves, partographs and guidelines represent important advances that can optimize decision making in labor management. While labor curves, partographs and guidelines are unable to account for all factors that influence labor and delivery in every patient, training healthcare providers in the competent use of these tools to ensure labor is closely monitored and life-threatening complications are identified and treated may avoid adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [10, 29, 31]. In this retrospective study, we compared the application of the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline in managing labor and delivery among Chinese low-risk nulliparous women. Our analysis encompassed a comprehensive set of maternal and neonatal demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as a wide range of outcomes, providing a detailed understanding of the impact of these two approaches.

Our findings revealed significant differences in various aspects. Maternal characteristics such as age, rates of advanced maternal age, assisted reproduction, obesity, uterine fibroids, hypertension, and hyperglycemia differed between the pregnant women managed according to the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline. Neonatal birth weight and macrosomia rates also varied. Regarding outcomes, total labor duration, intervention rates, delivery modes, and associated complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and neonatal asphyxia showed distinct patterns.

These results have important implications for clinical practice. The differences suggest that the choice of labor curve, partograph or guideline can significantly influence the management and outcomes of labor and delivery. The unique associations observed with the WHO partograph and Zhang’s guideline highlight the need for a more tailored approach to labor management in Chinese women. A partograph specifically designed for this population could potentially optimize decision-making and improve maternal and neonatal health.

Prospective, multi-center studies with long-term follow-up are essential to further validate and refine our understanding of the most effective labor management strategies for Chinese women. By doing so, we can continue to improve the quality of obstetric care and outcomes in this population.
