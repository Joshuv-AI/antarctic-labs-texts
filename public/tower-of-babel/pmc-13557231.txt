Thin Equivocal Melanocytic Lesions and Melanoma in Situ: The Monitoring Versus Excision Dilemma in Daily Clinical Practice
Authors: Saturnino Gasparini
Published: 2026

The Clinical Dilemma of Thin Equivocal Melanocytic Lesions

Advances in dermoscopy and digital imaging, including sequential digital dermoscopy, total body photography, and more recent applications of artificial intelligence, have significantly influenced dermatological practice, increasing the detection of thin atypical melanocytic lesions. Among these, a substantial proportion show equivocal dermoscopic features that do not allow a reliable distinction between atypical nevi and melanoma in situ (MIS). The evolution of in vivo diagnostics has improved the ability to detect lesions at an early stage but has also made a management dilemma increasingly common: deciding which melanocytic lesions should be excised immediately and which can be monitored over time.

Despite the use of advanced diagnostic tools, the distinction between atypical nevus and melanoma remains challenging, particularly in thin melanocytic lesions. This uncertainty frequently results in a precautionary excisional approach, placing dermatopathologists in a position similar to that of clinicians. Lesions excised at very early stages often lack clearly discriminative histopathological features [1], and several studies have demonstrated significant interobserver variability in the diagnosis of early melanocytic proliferations [2].

In a context increasingly characterized by medicolegal concerns in many countries [3], this uncertainty contributes to a cautious attitude among both clinicians and dermatopathologists, with a tendency toward an interventional approach and the frequent classification of such ambiguous lesions as melanoma.

Overdiagnosis and Biological Risk

Over recent decades, the incidence of melanoma has markedly increased, particularly due to the rising diagnosis of thin melanomas and melanoma in situ, while mortality has remained relatively stable. This epidemiological trend has raised concerns about possible overdiagnosis, defined as the detection of biologically indolent lesions that may never evolve into clinically significant disease [4].

Melanoma in situ is not a biologically homogeneous entity. Recent evidence suggests that many lesions are biologically indolent [5] and that MIS often represents a risk factor rather than an obligate precursor of invasive melanoma [6].

Despite this evidence, translating it into individual clinical decision-making remains challenging in daily practice. As a result, even minimally atypical lesions suspected of MIS are generally managed as potentially dangerous, implicitly accepting the risks of overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

The Role of Dermoscopy in Management Decisions

In light of the epidemiological and biological evidence, particularly the low risk of progression of melanoma in situ, the diagnostic difficulties of early thin melanocytic lesions, and the growing signs of overdiagnosis, it appears reasonable to question whether the excision threshold for less suspicious melanocytic lesions could be modulated more selectively.

In this setting, sequential digital dermoscopy represents an important tool to address the dilemma between monitoring and excision.

Monitoring allows changes in equivocal pigmented melanocytic lesions to be tracked over time and facilitates the detection of modifications indicative of early melanoma development. Longitudinal assessment also enables lesions to evolve toward more informative features, supporting a reliable histopathological diagnosis.

However, the effective use of this strategy still faces practical challenges. While established indications exist for selecting patients for dermoscopic follow-up [7], lesion-based dermoscopic criteria, equally relevant for guiding the choice between monitoring and excision, remain poorly defined. In the absence of shared lesion-based dermoscopic criteria, many thin melanocytic lesions are excised due to the fear of missing melanoma, even when they could be safely managed through monitoring.

The Role of the Atypical Reticular Pattern in Management Decisions

Among equivocal dermoscopic patterns, the atypical reticular pattern is frequently observed in daily clinical practice.

From a diagnostic perspective, an irregular pigment network is a dermoscopic feature with high sensitivity but limited specificity in the differential diagnosis between melanoma in situ and atypical nevi [8]. In addition, slow-growing melanomas frequently display a predominant reticular pattern [9], whereas some thin melanomas with greater invasive potential typically lack a pigment network [10].

Taken together, these findings suggest that thin melanocytic lesions characterized exclusively by an atypical reticular pattern may represent a subgroup of lesions with low immediate biological risk for which dermoscopic monitoring represents a valid management approach.

A first step toward more robust and scientifically grounded clinical decisions could be the recognition of dermoscopic follow-up as a legitimate management option for a subgroup of thin equivocal melanocytic lesions characterized exclusively by this pattern, in the absence of additional dermoscopic risk criteria.

Many experienced dermatologists already adopt this approach in daily clinical practice; however, the lack of formal recommendations limits its broader implementation and often promotes an excessively interventional attitude.

Formalizing the indication for monitoring in this subgroup of lesions, which are too often directed toward excision, would not diminish the diagnostic value of the atypical reticular pattern in screening. Rather, it would allow a distinction between criteria useful for detecting a lesion and those sufficient to justify immediate intervention.

Toward a More Balanced Management Approach

The development of formal management recommendations based on lesion-specific dermoscopic criteria, integrated with patient-based risk stratification, may help achieve more uniform and shared clinical decision-making.

Formal recognition of dermoscopic monitoring as a management option for thin lesions characterized exclusively by an atypical reticular pattern, in the absence of additional dermoscopic risk criteria, represents a first step toward more rational, better supported, and risk-adapted clinical decisions.

Clinical experience suggests the need for validation studies aimed at verifying and consolidating this approach, contributing to more robust decision-making between monitoring and excision.
