Advancing global mental health through social media engagement: lessons from an international professional organization (IACAPAP)
Authors: Lakshmi Sravanti, Hesham M. Hamoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since the emergence of social media in 2004, it has become an increasingly influential medium for communication, education, and advocacy within healthcare. For child and adolescent mental health, it offers unique opportunities to engage a diverse global audience, promote mental health literacy, galvanize partners in advocating for children’s mental health and counter misinformation and stigma. At the same time, the use of social media by professional medical organizations raises important questions related to ethics, governance, credibility, and responsible engagement, particularly when working with vulnerable populations such as children and adolescents. Despite the widespread adoption of social media by health professionals and organizations, there is limited guidance on how international professional bodies can strategically and ethically navigate this evolving digital landscape. Here, we draw on our experience managing the social media platforms of the International Association for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Allied Professions (IACAPAP), a global organization representing mental health professionals across disciplines and regions. We describe the objectives and scope of IACAPAP’s social media activities, including mental health promotion, professional education, and advocacy, and outline key strategies used and challenges encountered to tailor content across platforms. Based on these experiences, we propose the “ABLE-GA” framework to support responsible social media engagement by professional medical organizations. ABLE-GA stands for Alignment, Boundaries, Language, Ethics, Governance and Adaptation. By sharing lessons learned from IACAPAP’s experience, this commentary aims to contribute to the emerging discourse on the role of social media in child and adolescent mental health and to offer practical insights for professional organizations seeking to use digital platforms effectively and ethically.

Background

For professional organizations, social media can play a critical role in engaging its members as well as educating the general public. Health-related organizations generally use social media to disseminate news and information, for professional education, to advertise new opportunities or member services, and for health promotion [3, 4]. The use of social media has increased tremendously in child mental health including families/carers, civil society organizations, funding agencies, policymakers and other professionals working with children [1]. A professional and credible online presence has become increasingly important for mental health organizations, particularly in a context marked by widespread misinformation, stigma, and unregulated mental health content. From a broader sociotechnical perspective, social media platforms function not merely as communication tools but as dynamic ecosystems shaped by user behavior, algorithms, and sociocultural context [2]. This underscores the need for professional organizations to engage with an awareness of how content is interpreted, shared, and amplified across networks.

Emerging evidence also supports the potential of social media as a tool for mental health intervention and behavior change. A recent meta-analysis by Zhang et al. [6] suggests that social-media-based mental health interventions can be effective and scalable when thoughtfully designed. Similarly, a Cochrane review [3] highlights the role of interactive social media interventions in influencing health behaviors and outcomes. While these findings are largely drawn from adult populations, they reinforce the importance of strategic and evidence-informed engagement by professional organizations in the digital space.

The International Association for Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Allied Professions (IACAPAP) was established in 1937 with a mission to promote child and adolescent psychiatry and the mental health and development of children and adolescents, through policy, practice, training and research. IACAPAP currently maintains five social media platforms, including Facebook, X (formally Twitter), YouTube, Instagram, and LinkedIn. Facebook has the largest following (~ 9.7 K), followed by Instagram (~ 2.8 K), X (~ 2.1 K) and YouTube (~ 800). Instagram demonstrates the most rapid growth, increasing from approximately 780 to 2784 followers within less than one year. LinkedIn shows a steady presence (~ 690 followers). The accounts were created with the aim to reach out to multiple audiences involved in child and adolescent mental health and well-being. The purpose of this commentary is to outline the role of social media in promoting child mental health, outline principles of social media engagement for different social media platforms, and highlight lessons learnt from managing social media activities for a large professional organization like IACAPAP. Despite the growing literature on social media use among mental health professionals, this is, to our knowledge, the first article to examine social media engagement at the level of an professional international mental health organization.

Social media activities of IACAPAP

The social media activities of IACAPAP are guided by three overarching objectives: mental health promotion, professional education, and advocacy. These activities are coordinated by volunteers as part of the IACAPAP Communications team and are designed to engage a diverse, global audience while maintaining scientific credibility and organizational identity.

A core function of IACAPAP’s social media presence is the dissemination of organizational updates. This includes promoting key IACAPAP activities such as congress-related announcements, calls for nominations for awards and mentorship programs, and updates on ongoing events, initiatives and programs. Social media platforms serve as an efficient channel to enhance visibility and participation in these activities across member organizations and regions. Another important activity involves promoting the IACAPAP Bulletin, which functions as a central medium for sharing scholarly, educational, and practice-oriented content. Social media posts highlight Bulletin articles that showcase initiatives undertaken by member organizations, and ongoing work in the field of child and adolescent mental health from different parts of the world, thereby fostering a sense of global community and shared learning. IACAPAP also uses social media to promote creative and participatory initiatives, such as calls for competitions, calls for artwork created by children and adolescents and “mindscapes” a space for teenagers to express themselves through art, poetry and essays. Selected works are featured in the IACAPAP Bulletin, reinforcing child-centered perspectives and providing a platform for children’s voices within a professional mental health organization. Commemoration of important international days related to mental health, neurodevelopmental conditions, and child well-being forms another key component of IACAPAP’s social media strategy. These posts aim to reduce stigma, initiate meaningful conversations, and enhance mental health literacy by presenting evidence-informed information in accessible formats tailored to diverse audiences.

While the core objectives and themes remain consistent across platforms, content is strategically adapted to suit the specific audience, technical constraints, and communicative style of each platform. This includes variation in message length, use of hashtags, and incorporation of visual elements such as images or short videos. Such platform-specific tailoring allows IACAPAP to optimize engagement while maintaining consistency in messaging and organizational values. Theme selection is guided by alignment with IACAPAP’s strategic priorities, ongoing activities of member organizations and collaborating societies, and international observance days, allowing for both consistency and responsiveness to emerging needs. Scientific content is not curated through systematic literature surveillance. Where included, it is drawn from peer-reviewed literature, guidelines, or outputs from reputable organizations, and content from Bulletin submissions, including contributions from clinicians and researchers and research updates, is reviewed by bulletin editors with domain expertise to ensure accuracy prior to dissemination.

The flipside of social media use: risks and the need for caution

While social media offers significant opportunities for mental health communication, it also carries inherent risks. These include the potential for misinformation, oversimplification, and misinterpretation of complex mental health concepts, particularly when content is consumed outside its intended context. Ethical concerns related to confidentiality, professional boundaries, and unintended clinical implications are especially salient for medical and mental health organizations. While no single universally accepted international guideline exists for social media use by professional mental health organizations, guidance from bodies such as the World Health Organization [5] on health information credibility and infodemic management highlights converging principles of accuracy, confidentiality, professional boundaries, and responsible communication. Additionally, social media platforms can amplify polarization, expose organizations to online harassment, and pose reputational risks. Structural challenges such as unequal digital access and language barriers further limit equitable reach, particularly in low-resource settings. Despite these challenges, social media remains necessary due to its unparalleled reach, immediacy, and capacity for advocacy, making cautious and responsible engagement essential.

Proposed guidelines for social media engagement for professional medical organizations

In response to the opportunities and challenges of managing social media for an international professional organization, and the need for contextually relevant frameworks to guide ethical and effective engagement, we propose the “ABLE-GA” framework as a set of guiding principles. These guidelines are informed by our experiential learning within IACAPAP and are intended to be adaptable across diverse cultural, legal, and resource settings while maintaining scientific rigor and professional integrity. ABLE-GA stands for Alignment, Boundaries, Language, Ethics, Governance and Adaptation.

Learnings from our journey: challenges and the way forward

Our experience suggests that strategic curation and consistency in messaging are more effective than high posting frequency in sustaining meaningful engagement. A balance between original content as well as sharing relevant content curated by others is essential. Visual and narrative-based content can enhance reach and interaction while maintaining scientific rigor, particularly when tailored to diverse audiences. Ethical vigilance remains paramount, especially when addressing sensitive topics involving children and adolescents, where issues of confidentiality, consent, and potential harm require careful consideration. Ongoing reflection, incorporation of feedback from the audience, and capacity-building within communications teams are essential to ensure responsible and effective social media engagement. Feedback is obtained through platform analytics (e.g., engagement metrics), direct user interactions (comments and messages), and inputs from member organizations and professional networks, informing iterative refinement of content and strategy. Moving forward, professional organizations must strike a careful balance between visibility and responsibility, leveraging social media as a tool for informed advocacy, education, and global collaboration in child and adolescent mental health.
