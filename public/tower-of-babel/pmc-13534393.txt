Optimized LC–MS/MS method for simultaneous determination of multiclass veterinary anthelmintic residues in animal-derived matrices
Authors: Ye-Bin Jang, Yeong-Ju Jo, Mi-Ok Kim, Gui-Hyun Jang, Jae-Eun Mun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthelmintics are veterinary drugs used to treat or prevent diseases in livestock and fishery products. However, their residues in animal-derived foods pose potential risks to human health. In Korea, maximum residue limits (MRLs) have been established to regulate such residues; however, for certain substances, including niclosamide, a widely used anthelmintic, only qualitative analytical methods are available, limiting effective residue monitoring. This study developed a multiresidue quantitative analytical method for 18 anthelmintic compounds. Method validation was conducted in accordance with CODEX guidelines (CAC/GL-71) using six livestock products (beef, pork, chicken, eggs, milk, and porcine fat) and three fishery products (flatfish, eel, and shrimp). Sample preparation was performed using the QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) extraction method followed by dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) clean-up. Simultaneous determination of the 18 anthelmintics was performed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The matrix-matched calibration curves exhibited coefficients of determination (R²) exceeding 0.99 for all target compounds. Furthermore, recovery rates at all target concentrations satisfied CODEX performance criteria, demonstrating acceptable accuracy and precision. The developed method can enhance the efficiency of residue analysis and provide a technical basis for establishing safety management standards for livestock and fishery products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44463-026-00098-1.Keywords: Anthelmintics, Maximum residue limits (MRLs), Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), Matrix-matched calibration curves

Introduction

Veterinary drugs are increasingly administered to food-producing animals, including livestock and aquaculture species, to promote growth, prevent disease, and enhance overall productivity (Lulijwa et al. 2020). However, the use of these drugs poses an inherent risk of residual accumulation in edible tissues (Rana et al. 2019). Such residues are often attributed to excessive use or noncompliance with established withdrawal periods (Tajick and Shohreh 2006). Additionally, certain animal species may metabolize or eliminate these compounds inefficiently, resulting in their accumulation in muscle or organ tissues. The consumption of products with residual veterinary drug levels may pose significant risks to human health (Babapour et al. 2012).

Parasitic diseases continue to represent a major challenge on livestock productivity across agricultural ecosystems worldwide, particularly in regions such as sub-Saharan Africa (Waller 1997). Gastrointestinal helminths—including cestodes (tapeworms), nematodes (roundworms), and trematodes (flukes)—cause considerable economic losses due to infections in both livestock and farmed fish (Kinsella et al. 2009, Enejoh and Suleiman 2017). The administration of anthelmintics is a well-established approach for preventing and treating helminth infections in both animals and humans. Globally, anthelmintics are routinely administered not only to maintain body weight and reproductive performance but also to mitigate the economic impact of parasitic infections (Tuck et al. 2016).

Anthelmintic drugs are classified into six major chemical groups based on their mode of action: benzimidazoles, macrocyclic lactones (including avermectins and milbemycins), salicylanilides, substituted phenols, tetrahydropyrimidines, and imidazothiazoles (Mooney et al. 2019). For example, triclabendazole, a benzimidazole compound, is effective against both larval and adult stages of endo- and ectoparasites (Rúbies et al. 2015). Salicylanilides, such as closantel, rafoxanide, and niclosamide, are effective against a broad spectrum of ectoparasites, including ticks (Swan 1999). Tetrahydropyrimidines such as morantel and pyrantel, function as nicotinic receptor agonists and are used in both veterinary and human medicine (Enejoh and Suleiman 2017). Imidazothiazoles, such as levamisole, are mainly prescribed for treating nematode infections (Jedziniak et al. 2009). Additionally, other agents such as praziquantel and nitroxinil are widely used in antiparasitic therapy (Enejoh and Suleiman 2017).

The over-the-counter availability of numerous anthelmintic products raises concerns regarding their widespread and unregulated use (Kinsella et al. 2009). Consuming food with anthelmintic residues can cause toxic effects and hypersensitivity reactions; moreover, the overuse of these drugs in animals may also result in the emergence of drug-resistant parasite strains (Dasenaki and Thomaidis 2015). To mitigate these risks, governments have established stringent regulatory frameworks to monitor and control residue levels in food products (Beyene 2016). In the Republic of Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) enforces maximum residue limits (MRLs) for veterinary drugs. These MRLs are determined from the theoretical maximum daily intake (TMDI), calculated using national food consumption data and the acceptable daily intake (ADI) for each compound (Oh et al. 2009).

Although MRLs have been established for several anthelmintics in meat and seafood, compounds such as niclosamide, rafoxanide, morantel, and pyrantel remain unregulated. Internationally, MRLs for anthelmintics in edible animal tissues, such as muscle, milk, and eggs, vary across regulatory bodies: 50–200 ng/g (Food Chemicals Codex), 100–1000 ng/g (United States Department of Agriculture), 10–50 ng/g (European Union), and 1–2000 ng/g (Australian Agricultural and Veterinary Chemicals Code) (Yoo et al. 2021). In Korea, although MRLs have been established to regulate veterinary drug residues, for certain substances, including niclosamide, only qualitative analytical methods are currently available. This limitation poses significant challenges to effective residue monitoring and regulatory enforcement. Accordingly, the absence of sensitive and robust quantitative methods represents a critical research gap, reinforcing the necessity of developing improved LC–MS/MS approaches applicable to diverse animal-derived matrices.

Various analytical methods have been developed for detecting anthelmintic residues in animal-derived matrices using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Sample extraction methods commonly include liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) and QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) techniques, which have been widely applied to matrices such as muscle tissue and eggs (Zhang et al. 2016, Cooper et al. 2012, Whelan et al. 2010). Dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) is often incorporated as a clean-up step to eliminate matrix interferences. Previous studies have shown that d-SPE effectively removes coextracted lipids and proteins from high-fat, high-protein matrices such as meat and fish (Wang et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2021).

Several LC–MS/MS-based multiresidue methods have been reported for the analysis of anthelmintic residues in animal-derived foods; however, most have focused on commonly monitored compounds or a limited range of matrices. The method developed in this study expands the analytical scope by including not only niclosamide, for which quantitative analysis has been limited in Korea, but also compounds that have been relatively less frequently analyzed in previous studies, such as derquantel, bithionol, and nitroxinil. In addition, while previous studies have often been limited to specific matrices, such as livestock products, fishery products, or milk, this study is significant in that it established a QuEChERS-based LC–MS/MS method capable of simultaneously quantifying structurally diverse anthelmintic and antiparasitic-related residues in both livestock and fishery products.

This study aimed to develop a rapid and reliable multiresidue method for the simultaneous determination of 18 anthelmintics in a diverse array of animal-derived food matrices. The method utilizes QuEChERS extraction followed by d-SPE clean-up and was validated across six livestock products (beef, pork, chicken, eggs, milk, and porcine fat) and three aquaculture species (flatfish, eel, and shrimp). By broadening both the target analyte coverage and matrix applicability, the proposed method provides a practical framework for comprehensive monitoring of anthelmintic residues in complex food matrices, thereby contributing to regulatory practice and future methodological advancements in food safety analysis.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Standards for chlorfluazuron, derquantel, and rafoxanide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA; Steinheim, Germany). All other standards were obtained from Dr. Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Germany): abamectin, bithionol, closantel, doramectin, eprinomectin, ivermectin, levamisole, moxidectin, morantel, niclosamide, nitroxinil, oxyclozanide, praziquantel, pyrantel, triclabendazole, and triclabendazole-keto. Methanol (MeOH) and acetonitrile (MeCN) in LC–MS grade were acquired from Merck Inc. (Darmstadt, Germany). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), formic acid, MgSO4, and NaCl were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), while primary secondary amine (PSA) was purchased from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Ammonium formate was obtained from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA, USA) and octadecylsilane (C18; 55–105 μm, 125 Å) was acquired from Waters (Milford, MA, USA). A syringe filter from Teknokroma (Barcelona, Spain) with a 0.2 μm-pore polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane was used.

Each standard (1,000 µg/mL) was prepared in MeOH (abamectin, bithionol, chlorfluazuron, derquantel, doramectin, ivermectin, levamisole, moxidectin, morantel, nitroxinil, praziquantel, rafoxanide, eprinomectin) or DMSO (closantel, niclosamide, oxyclozanide, pyrantel, triclabendazole, triclabendazole-keto) and stored at − 20 ℃ in amber bottles.

LC–MS/MS instruments and analytical conditions

Chromatographic conditions were optimized to achieve efficient separation, sensitivity, and selectivity by considering the various chemical properties of the target analytes. Instrumental analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu LCMS-8060 (Osaka, Japan) equipped with a Waters Xbridge C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 3.5 μm). The C18 column is widely used for veterinary drug analysis (Kanda et al. 2015, Balizs and Hewitt 2003, Stolker et al. 2007) because its hydrophobic stationary phase provides strong retention for structurally diverse anthelmintics, thereby improving resolution and reproducibility (Kim et al. 2021). Chromatographic separation employed the following mobile phases: (A) 5 mM ammonium formate with 0.1% formic acid in water, and (B) 0.1% formic acid in MeCN. The LC gradient was programmed as follows: 5% B from 0 to 0.2 min, 70% B at 1.0 min, 95% B from 6.0 to 7.0 min, 5% B at 7.2 min, and reequilibration until 12 min. Additional parameters included a 5 µL injection volume, 40 °C column temperature, and 0.4 mL/min flow rate.

Sample preparation

In this study, analytical methods were validated for 18 types of veterinary drugs in livestock products (beef, pork, chicken, eggs, milk, and porcine fat) and fishery products (flatfish, eel, and shrimp). All samples were purchased from the domestic market in Korea and prepared according to the Food Code guidelines to ensure representative sampling, followed by homogenization using a mixer. The homogenized samples were stored in sealed containers at − 20 °C until analysis. A preliminary screening confirmed the absence of all 18 target analytes in the test samples.

Method validation

The developed analytical method and its matrix effects were evaluated according to the CODEX CAC/GL-71 and National Institute of Food and Drug Safety Evaluation of Korea (NIFDS) guidelines. The validation parameters included selectivity, accuracy, precision, linearity, and limits of quantification (LOQ).

Selectivity was confirmed using blank samples from nine types of livestock and fishery products, in which no interference peaks were observed at the retention time of the target analyte. Accuracy (recovery) refers to the proportion of analyte quantified in the final result relative to the amount added into a blank sample prior to extraction, generally expressed as a percentage. Precision is the closeness of agreement between independent replicate test results obtained under specified conditions. Both accuracy and precision were evaluated by repeatability (intraday) and reproducibility (interday, three consecutive days) at three spiking levels through recovery and coefficient of variation (CV), respectively. The repeatability was assessed from intra-day measurements, using five replicate injections obtained on the same day (n = 5). The inter-assay (or intermediate) precision, which applies to within-laboratory variations, was evaluated by two analysts on different days to allow comparison of the method’s performance (n = 5).

Validation was conducted at three concentration levels: 1×LOQ, 1×MRL, and 2×MRL for analytes with established MRLs, and 1×, 2×, and 10×LOQ for analytes without MRLs. Linearity was expressed as the coefficient of determination (R2) and assessed using six points on the calibration curve at concentrations of 0.125×, 0.25×, 0.5×, 1×, 2×, and 4×MRL for each analyte, or 0.5×, 1×, 2×, 4×, 10×, and 20×LOQ. If the 1×LOQ was not included in the curve containing the MRL, an additional calibration curve including 1×LOQ was constructed, and recovery and CV values were calculated for this curve.

The LOQ of the method was calculated statistically by averaging the results from seven calibration curves. LOQ was calculated according to the Standard Deviation of the Response and the Slope method (ICH Q2(R1)), using the standard deviation of the y-intercept as σ and the average slope (S) of the calibration curve.

The matrix effect refers to analytical signal suppression or enhancement resulting from coextracted matrix components, which can affect the sensitivity and reliability of the method (Trufelli et al. 2011). The matrix effect of the analytical method developed in this study was assessed utilizing the slope ratio between calibration curves prepared in solvent (standard solution) and those prepared in matrix-matched standards (tissue extracts). Results and discussion

Optimization of LC conditions

Chromatographic separation was optimized by injecting a standard mixture of all 18 target components. An optimal instrumental method was established by evaluating mobile phase combinations using different additives. Ammonium formate and formic acid are commonly used mobile phase additives (Gao et al. 2005); for the analysis of mectin compounds, several studies have utilized 5 mM ammonium formate in the aqueous mobile phase (Wang et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2020, Zeleke et al. 2022). Additionally, a series of preliminary experiments was conducted to select the organic phase: (1) 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in MeOH, (2) 5 mM ammonium formate in MeCN, and (3) 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in MeCN. Optimal intensity for all analytes was achieved when a combination of 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid was used as the aqueous mobile phase, along with MeCN containing 0.1% formic acid as the organic phase.

In a previous study, MeCN was selected as the mobile phase for the analysis of veterinary drugs (Kokkalis et al. 2012) because it provided superior peak resolution compared to MeOH, particularly producing more distinct peaks for early-eluting compounds (Rafferty et al. 2011). Formic acid enhances the ionization efficiency of analytes in the electrospray ionization (ESI) source, thereby improving mass spectrometry (MS) sensitivity (Gao et al. 2005). Under these optimized liquid chromatography (LC) conditions, the retention times (RT) of the 18 anthelmintics ranged from 1.9 min for morantel to 6.4 min for ivermectin.

Optimization of electrospray ionization MS/MS

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was primarily used as the detection method for the quantitative determination of the 18 anthelmintics separated by LC. Triple quadrupole MS/MS analysis was performed using an electrospray ion source in both positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI−) modes. The capillary voltages were set at 4.0 and − 3.0 kV for the positive and negative modes, respectively, with argon as the collision gas.

The LC–MS/MS system established in this study acquired data in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, using Shimadzu software for data management and control to enhance the selectivity of qualitative and quantitative results and to improve detection sensitivity. Standard solutions were directly injected into the mass spectrometer to optimize precursor ions, product ions, and collision energies.

In both ESI + and ESI− modes, precursor ions were selected for each analyte, and collision energies were optimized to generate product ions. The transition with the highest intensity was used for quantification, while all other transitions, with lower intensities than the most intense transition, were used for confirmation. Optimization of extraction conditions

Sample preparation comprised organic solvent extraction to isolate analytes from livestock and fishery products, followed by a clean-up step to remove interferences from the solvent extract (Nunes et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2011). According to established food code guidelines and prior studies, QuEChERS d-SPE is commonly used for the analysis of anthelmintic residues (Kinsella et al. 2009, Kim et al. 2021) and is widely used for multiresidue analysis of various veterinary drugs in animal foods (Wang et al. 2021). Salting-out agents, such as sodium acetate (NaOAc), MgSO4, and NaSO4, enhance the solubility of multiresidue molecules in the organic phase (Hou et al. 2014, Kim et al. 2016).

The choice of extraction solvent strongly influences extraction efficiency. Key considerations when selecting a solvent include analyte polarity, selectivity, partitioning behavior, purification effectiveness, and compatibility with chromatographic separation techniques (Chen et al. 2019). The three organic solvents most commonly used in the QuEChERS method are acetone, MeOH, and MeCN (Pszczolińska and Michel 2016). Among these, MeCN is preferred because it readily separates from water when an appropriate salt is added (Song et al. 2019). Furthermore, it is widely used as an extraction solvent for analyzing contaminants and residues in food samples because it minimizes matrix interference (Lazartigues et al. 2011). Additionally, the addition of anhydrous MgSO4 to MeCN is particularly effective for removing residual water after the partitioning step, as MgSO4 is virtually insoluble in MeCN (Choi and Kang 2021, Arias et al. 2018).

Acidified MeCN has been widely employed for veterinary drug extraction from animal tissues (Xu et al. 2019, Aguilera-Luiz et al. 2012). The addition of 0.1% formic acid to the extraction solvent has been reported to enhance analytical performance by reducing matrix effects and improving both recovery and precision (Stubbings and Bigwood 2009, Yang et al. 2024, European Committee for Standardization (CEN) 2018). Therefore, 0.1% formic acid in MeCN was selected as the extraction solvent in this study, and the effects of different QuEChERS salt compositions were subsequently evaluated.

In this study, three QuEChERS methods were compared: the Original method (4 g MgSO4, 1 g NaCl), the EN 15,662 method (4 g MgSO4, 1 g NaCl, 1 g trisodium citrate, 0.5 g disodium hydrogen citrate), and the AOAC method (6 g MgSO4, 1 g NaOAc), all of which are commonly used for the extraction of veterinary drugs.

Flatfish samples were extracted using all three methods, and calibration curves for 18 target analytes were evaluated within a concentration range of 5–50 ng/g. For the EN and AOAC methods, the R² values for abamectin were only 0.2304 and 0.0385, respectively, indicating poor linearity. In contrast, the Original method yielded values exceeding 0.99 for all anthelmintic compounds and was therefore selected as the optimal extraction method for this study.

The Original method represents the unbuffered version of the QuEChERS method, whereas the EN and AOAC versions utilize citrate and acetate buffer systems, respectively, to maintain the extract pH at approximately 5.0–5.5 and 4.8, thereby stabilizing extraction conditions (Lehotay et al. 2010). However, such buffered conditions may not be optimal for all target compounds in a multiresidue analytical method. Moreover, under the acidified MeCN extraction conditions used in this study, citrate or acetate ions present in the buffered salt mixtures may have complicated the ionic environment of the extraction system and altered salting-out efficiency, potentially promoting the co-extraction of endogenous matrix components such as lipids, pigments, proteins, and phospholipids (Kalinke et al. 2024). These effects may be particularly relevant for macrocyclic lactone compounds such as abamectin, which, owing to their high molecular weight and lipophilic properties, can readily interact with lipid- and protein-rich matrix components. Increased co-extraction of these components may enhance matrix-derived ion suppression during the ESI process and destabilize concentration-dependent responses, ultimately resulting in poor calibration linearity.

In contrast, the combination of acidified MeCN and unbuffered Original QuEChERS salts may have provided more favorable extraction conditions for target analytes with diverse physicochemical properties. Acidified MeCN can promote the release of analytes from matrix components and improve their partitioning into the organic phase, while the simpler MgSO4/NaCl salt composition may reduce variability associated with buffer-derived matrix interactions. Therefore, the Original QuEChERS method was considered to provide more efficient phase separation and more consistent extraction and ionization behavior across the 18 target analytes. This interpretation is also consistent with previous reports indicating that extraction salts are useful for purifying high-lipid extracts (Kalinke et al. 2024) and that the unbuffered QuEChERS method is commonly employed for veterinary drug residue analysis (Zhao et al. 2021, Kang et al. 2014, Li et al. 2020).

Optimization of purification conditions

Livestock and fishery muscles are fat-based matrices, necessitating purification to eliminate interferences. PSA and C18 are commonly employed in sample preparation for this purpose. PSA is effective owing to the numerous primary and secondary amino groups on its surface, while C18 selectively adsorbs interferences through ion exchange, removing nonpolar compounds such as fats, proteins, volatile oils, and fat-soluble substances (Chen et al. 2012). Graphitized carbon black (GCB) effectively adsorbs pigments and various impurities; however, its use may cause target loss and reduced recovery (Huang et al. 2023). Furthermore, adding a certain amount of MgSO4 to the homogenized sample can facilitate stable and efficient d-SPE purification. Specifically, MgSO4 plays a crucial role in absorbing water molecules mixed with the analyte in the organic phase (Chen et al. 2012). Therefore, this study evaluated the combination of primary secondary amine (PSA) and C18 adsorbents in conjunction with MgSO₄ to optimize the purification process. The results showed that when C18 (400 mg) was used alone with MgSO₄, the coefficients of determination (R²) for chlorfluazuron, levamisole, triclabendazole, and keto-triclabendazole ranged from 0.920 to 0.979, failing to meet linearity criteria. When PSA was used alone, R² values for chlorfluazuron (0.898) and triclabendazole (0.986) were also below 0.99. In contrast, the simultaneous use of PSA and C18 as sorbents resulted in R² values of ≥ 0.99 for all 18 anthelmintics, indicating excellent linearity. Accordingly, the d-SPE composition selected for purification consisted of 1,200 mg MgSO₄, 400 mg PSA, and 400 mg C18. Furthermore, the extract volume required for effective purification was standardized. Since a QuEChERS kit containing salts was used during extraction, the final extract volume varied depending on the matrix. Matrix effect

The matrix effects of 18 anthelmintics analyzed using an optimized analytical method varied depending on the sample type. Differences in fat and protein content among livestock and fishery products may result in differing levels of signal enhancement and suppression during LC–MS/MS analysis (Lawal et al. 2018). The matrix effects observed for the 18 anthelmintics across nine matrices are summarized in Table S3. Negative values indicate ion suppression, whereas positive values reflect ion enhancement. Ion suppression or enhancement is typically attributed to endogenous matrix constituents or coextracted interfering components that influence chromatographic behavior and alter the ionization efficiency of target analytes during the ESI process (Barbieri et al. 2019). Despite the low injection volume, significant matrix effects, defined as signal changes of ≥ ± 20%, were observed for the majority of the analytes. Most analytes exhibited substantial ion suppression, with values ranging from − 96.08 to − 20.58%. In particular, abamectin, levamisole, pyrantel, and triclabendazole exhibited ion suppression greater than 20% in all livestock and fishery matrices. In contrast, significant ion enhancement was relatively limited, with values ranging from 20.17 to 97.75%. Notably, moxidectin showed ion enhancement in several animal-derived food matrices, including three types of fishery products, chicken, and milk.

A reliable analytical method must efficiently extract target analytes from livestock and fishery samples. However, coextraction of matrix components such as lipids and proteins can induce matrix effects, including signal enhancement or suppression, during LC–MS/MS analysis and may also affect column lifespan and quantitative accuracy (Mei et al. 2003). These matrix effects can serve as important sources of error in the quantitative analysis of residues in animal-derived food products (Song et al. 2019). Furthermore, ESI is particularly susceptible to matrix effects when using generic extraction protocols with relatively simple sample clean-up, such as the QuEChERS method (Fedorova et al. 2014, Romero-González et al. 2007). Coeluting interfering compounds may suppress or enhance the ion signal of target analytes during the ionization process (Santos et al. 2016).

In this study, matrix-matched calibration was applied to minimize the influence of these matrix effects on quantitative results. Matrix-matched calibration is a method in which calibration standards are prepared not in pure solvent but in blank matrix extracts, thereby allowing standards and real samples to be ionized under similar matrix environments (Niessen et al. 2006, Planas et al. 2006). Accordingly, ion suppression or enhancement caused by lipids, proteins, and other endogenous matrix components coextracted during sample preparation is reflected in the calibration curve, thereby correcting systematic quantitative errors arising from matrix effects (Hewavitharana 2011, Picón Zamora et al. 2003). In other words, the matrix effects shown in Table S3 represent the degree of signal variation relative to solvent-based standards; however, actual quantification was performed using matrix-matched calibration curves corresponding to each matrix, preventing these signal variations from directly leading to bias in quantitative results.

The use of internal standards is generally regarded as an effective means for correcting matrix effects and variations that may occur during sample preparation. However, in a multiclass method such as the present study, which simultaneously analyzes 18 anthelmintics, the use of isotope-labeled internal standards for all analytes is limited by cost and technical constraints, and suitable analogs may not be available for certain target compounds (Dasenaki and Thomaidis 2015). Therefore, considering the applicability of the method to routine residue analysis and the diversity of the target analytes, matrix-matched calibration for each sample matrix was selected as a practical and feasible correction strategy. In particular, even for compounds such as abamectin, levamisole, pyrantel, and triclabendazole, which showed strong ion suppression in all matrices, matrix-induced signal variation could be corrected at the quantification stage by applying calibration curves prepared in the same matrix.

The applicability of this approach was also confirmed by the method validation results. The developed method showed satisfactory recovery and precision in each matrix, indicating that reliable and robust quantification of anthelmintic residues in livestock and fishery products can be achieved by applying matrix-matched calibration, even in the presence of substantial matrix effects. Nevertheless, matrix-matched calibration requires the availability of appropriate blank matrices of the same type and assumes consistency in matrix composition. Therefore, additional validation may be necessary when the method is applied to processed foods or complex samples with markedly different matrix compositions. In future studies, the introduction of isotope-labeled internal standards or surrogate internal standards for major target analytes could further improve precision and long-term robustness, particularly for samples with greater matrix-to-matrix variability.

Validation

Selectivity, Linearity, and LOQ

The developed method for 18 types of anthelmintics was validated using nine livestock and fishery product matrices in accordance with the CODEX guidelines. The validation results for selectivity, linearity, and LOQ are presented in Fig. S1. The qualitative and quantitative ions of the 18 veterinary drugs were confirmed in the chromatograms of each sample.

All analytes showed excellent linearity (R² > 0.99) in matrix-matched calibration curves, confirming that the method complied with the CODEX guidelines. The LOQ values ranged from 0.0033 to 0.0054 mg/kg, all below half of the established MRLs. These values were comparable to or lower than those reported in prior studies. For instance, a multiresidue analysis of anthelmintic and antiprotozoal drugs in livestock products reported LOQ values ranging from 1 to 10 µg/kg (Rimayi et al. 2015), while the analysis of anthelmintics in mutton yielded LOQs of 0.5 µg/kg for ivermectin, levamisole, and oxyclozanide, and 10 µg/kg for rafoxanide, albendazole, and closantel (Park et al. 2023). Therefore, this method is considered sufficiently suitable for the analysis of anthelmintics in livestock and fishery products.

Accuracy and precision

The accuracy (recovery) and precision (CV) results are presented in Table 1. The intraday accuracy and precision of all analytes ranged from 72.0 to 119.1% (CV: ≤ 18.2%) in nine representative livestock and fishery matrices. These results confirmed that the method is suitable for determining the residue levels of anthelmintics in these products. According to the CODEX guidelines, at concentrations of 1–10 ppb, the recovery rate should be 60–120% and the CV should be ≤ 30%. At concentrations of 10–100 ppb, the recovery rate should be 70–120% and the CV should be ≤ 20% (Within-Lab).

Validation results of the developed analytical method (Intra-day and inter day)

1) Day 1 data were used for intra-day validation (n = 5) and were also included as Day 1 in the inter-day validation, in accordance with international guidelines (AOAC, 2016; ICH Q2(R1); SANTE/2023/11312), which state that intra-day repeatability data may also serve as the first day in inter-day precision assessment

The applicability of the method was verified via intraday validation. Intra-day validation was performed using five replicates on Day 1, and the same Day 1 dataset was also included as the first day in the inter-day validation. Subsequently, interday validation was also conducted, and the validation results satisfied the performance criteria recommended by the CODEX guidelines. Recovery and CV values ranged from 72.0 to 118.7% (CV: ≤ 18.8%) on Day 2 and 72.0 to 117.1% (CV: ≤ 19.0%) on Day 3. According to the CODEX guidelines, at concentrations of 1–10 ppb and 10–100 ppb, the CV should be ≤ 45% and ≤ 32%, respectively (Between-Lab). These findings confirmed that the developed method meets the acceptable ranges required at each spiking level specified in the CODEX guidelines, demonstrating its reliability as a multiresidue analytical method.

Application to real samples

The applicability of the developed analytical method was further assessed by analyzing real animal-derived food samples purchased from the domestic market. A total of 60 samples, comprising 30 livestock products and 30 fishery products, were selected based on high consumption patterns in Korea and analyzed for the 18 target anthelmintic residues using the optimized QuEChERS-based LC–MS/MS method.

For the application study, the presence or absence of the target analytes was determined by comparing the MRM transitions, retention times, and chromatographic profiles of blank samples, matrix-matched calibration standards, and test sample solutions.

None of the 18 target analytes were detected in any of the 60 samples analyzed. The method was successfully applied to livestock and fishery product samples without interfering peaks, confirming its suitability for veterinary drug residue analysis in these matrices. These results demonstrate that the developed method can be successfully applied to real livestock and fishery product samples, supporting its practical applicability for residue monitoring. Although no target residues were detected in the present survey, continued monitoring is necessary to ensure the safety of animal-derived foods and to strengthen the management of veterinary drug residues. Therefore, the analytical method developed and validated in this study is expected to be useful as a practical tool for routine surveillance of multiclass anthelmintic residues in livestock and fishery products.

Conclusions

In this study, a QuEChERS-based LC–MS/MS method was developed and validated for the simultaneous determination of 18 multiclass veterinary anthelmintic residues in animal-derived food matrices. The optimized method used acidified acetonitrile extraction, the Original QuEChERS salt composition, and d-SPE clean-up with MgSO₄, PSA, and C18, providing effective extraction and purification for both livestock and fishery products. The method was successfully validated in six livestock matrices and three fishery matrices, including beef, pork, chicken, eggs, milk, porcine fat, flatfish, eel, and shrimp. The developed method showed satisfactory analytical performance in terms of selectivity, linearity, LOQ, accuracy, and precision. Matrix-matched calibration curves exhibited good linearity for all target analytes, and the recovery and precision results met the CODEX performance criteria. These results demonstrate that the proposed method is reliable for quantitative multiresidue analysis of anthelmintics in complex animal-derived food matrices. In particular, the method enables the quantitative determination of structurally diverse anthelmintics, including compounds such as niclosamide, for which quantitative analytical approaches have been limited. This study demonstrated the applicability of the developed method for the simultaneous determination of 18 anthelmintics in livestock and fishery product samples, and the method was applied to animal-derived foods purchased from the domestic market. Based on the analysis of 60 livestock and fishery products with high consumption rates, none of the 18 target analytes were detected, supporting the practical applicability of the proposed method. This indicates that the method can be applied not only to validation studies but also to practical residue monitoring of livestock and fishery products. Nevertheless, substantial matrix effects were observed for several analytes, suggesting that matrix-derived ion suppression or enhancement remains an important analytical challenge in LC–MS/MS-based multiresidue analysis. Although isotope-labeled internal standards were not used in this study, matrix-matched calibration was applied to compensate for matrix effects and improve quantitative reliability. Overall, the proposed method provides a rapid, sensitive, and practical analytical framework for the simultaneous determination of veterinary anthelmintic residues in animal-derived foods. The method may contribute to strengthening routine residue surveillance programs and provide useful technical support for the establishment, revision, and implementation of food safety management standards for livestock and fishery products.
