Identification of ice loads on ship structure using a hybrid regularization strategy
Authors: Chenyan Zhou, Ling Chen, XiaoQuan Li, Weiting Liu, Qun Yin, Jianing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately identifying ice loads acting on a ship is crucial for ensuring the structural safety of polar vessels. To mitigate the severe ill-posedness caused by limited measurement data and high sensitivity to noise, regularization techniques are widely used in ice load identification. However, existing regularization strategies often struggle to balance the preservation of transient impact peaks with the reconstruction of smooth background fluctuations, leading to ice load identification results that lack physical plausibility. To address this, this study proposes an ice load identification method based on a hybrid regularization strategy. First, Green’s kernel functions are used to establish a dynamic mapping between the structural strain response and impact ice loads, thereby formulating the inverse problem model for ice load identification. On this basis, a hybrid regularization model combining the advantages of the L1 and L2 norms is introduced, designed to simultaneously capture the peaks of impact loads while maintain the continuity of low-amplitude background loads. The corresponding objective function is efficiently solved using a coordinate descent algorithm, and the optimal set of regularization parameters is selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Finally, the performance of the proposed method is evaluated through high-fidelity numerical simulations of the local structure of an ice-class research vessel, and its engineering applicability is validated through scaled model experiments. The results indicate that the hybrid regularization strategy not only enhances the accuracy of peak load identification but also effectively mitigates signal truncation in low-amplitude regions, demonstrating a balanced overall performance. In multi-source impact scenarios, it exhibits high robustness and low sensitivity to interference. The proposed method provides an effective solution for ice load identification in polar vessels.Keywords: Polar vessels, Ice load identification, Hybrid regularization, Numerical simulation, Scaled model experimentsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As global temperatures continue to rise, the polar regions have drawn widespread international attention for their strategic value in resource development, maritime transportation, scientific research, and military operations. It is well known that vast amounts of sea ice drift throughout the polar regions, existing in various forms such as level ice, brash ice, ridged ice, and icebergs. When a ship navigates in these areas, the hull is subjected to continuous interactions with the surrounding ice, which becomes a major concern for the vessel’s structural safety. Therefore, accurately and effectively obtaining ice loads is essential for structural design and navigational safety of polar ships.

Currently, the research on ice loads primarily relies on empirical formulations, numerical simulations, and experimental investigations1. The empirical formula approach is widely used in engineering practice for its simplicity2–5. However, it has limited applicability and cannot capture the complexities of all ship types or operating conditions, making it most suitable for preliminary design stages6. With the advancement in computational technology, numerical simulation methods have gained increasing attention from researchers in recent years. Common approaches such as the finite element method (FEM)7, the discrete element method (DEM)8, and coupled CFD-DEM techniques9 can provide high-fidelity ice load predictions when adequate computational resources are available, and the modeling assumptions are appropriate. However, it is important to note that numerical simulations inevitably involve condition-specific assumptions and simplifications, which depend on the researcher’s understanding of ship-ice interaction mechanisms10. Consequently, results obtained by different researchers may vary significantly. Experimental testing is currently the most direct approach for obtaining ice loads and can be categorized into scaled model tests and full-scale ship measurements. Compared to empirical and numerical methods, experimental approaches require significant human and material resources, resulting in substantially higher costs. Although scaled model tests are convenient for mechanism studies, the characteristics of artificial ice, limitations of similarity criteria, and idealized test environments limit their ability to reproduce local responses and extreme loads accurately11. In contrast, full-scale ship measurements are considered by the industry as the most accurate method for obtaining ice loads, effectively revealing their magnitude, distribution patterns, and statistical characteristics.

For full-scale ship measurements, the most direct way to capture ice-hull interaction forces is to install mechanical sensors at the contact interface. However, implementing this approach in practice is often challenging for two main reasons. First, the regions that experience ice loads, including the bow, the waterline area, and the reinforced ice belt, are narrow, highly curved, and structurally complex, which makes it difficult to reserve flat and stable surfaces for installing force sensors. Second, the strong impact forces generated during ship-ice collisions can easily damage measurement devices installed on the exterior of the hull, preventing continuous and reliable monitoring of ice loads. To address these challenges, load identification techniques have gained increasing attention12–14. This approach uses sensors to record structural responses on the inner surface of the loaded region or in adjacent areas, and then applies specific algorithms to infer the external loads acting on the structure. The influence coefficient matrix (ICM) method, based on beam theory, is a classical approach for local load identification, and many current load inversion algorithms are derived from it. Kujala15 was the first to obtain full-scale ice load measurements using sensors installed on the icebreaker I.B. Sisu. These datasets later served as essential reference data for developing and validating the ICM method. Mincheul et al.16 applied the ICM method, based on beam theory, to identify the ice loads acting on the bow of the research vessel ARAON. Their results indicated that the error between the inverted mean ice load and the measured mean value was within 15%. Li et al.17 applied this method to identify ice loads on a cold-region offshore platform, and the reliability of the results was confirmed through numerical simulations and scaled model tests. However, it should be noted that the ICM method, based on beam theory, relies on static assumptions and cannot capture the dynamic behavior of ice loads during ship-ice collisions. To overcome this limitation, Ji et al.18,19 introduced Green’s kernel function to establish a dynamic relationship between local strains and impact ice loads, replacing the traditional static influence matrix with a Green structural matrix. Bao et al.20 derived a dynamic stiffness matrix based on Euler-Bernoulli beam theory and proposed a dynamic stiffness matrix method to replace the traditional static influence coefficient approach. Liu et al.21 used the response function of a Heaviside step excitation as the inversion kernel to capture the dynamic characteristics of the loads, and applied a hybrid numerical method to identify the load distribution. These research advances have significantly enhanced the applicability of the ICM method for dynamic load identification.

However, in practical measurement scenarios, limitations in sensor placement, incomplete correspondence between measurement points and the loaded region, and other constraints keep the influence matrix highly ill-conditioned, regardless of any improvements made to the ICM method. As a result, the stability of the identified load is poor. To address this issue, researchers need to introduce prior constraints to improve the problem’s solvability, with regularization strategies being one of the key approaches22,23. The Tikhonov regularization method (L2 norm) is the earliest strategy applied in ice load identification24,25, valued for its simple mathematical form, computational stability, and ease of implementation. However, studies have found26 that although the Tikhonov regularization strategy can mitigate the ill-conditioning of the transfer matrix, it often overly smooths the load variations and fails to capture the transient or local peak load characteristics during ship–ice collisions. In recent years, the sparse regularization (L1 norm) strategy has attracted increasing attention27,28, providing a promising approach for ice load identification. Liu et al.29 proposed a grouped sparse regularization method for identifying dynamic external loads. By constructing a dictionary atom group structure based on prior knowledge, this approach markedly improves both the accuracy and computational efficiency of load identification. Chen et al.30 introduced non-convex sparse regularization in the frequency domain and incorporated spatial sparsity priors to improve the accuracy of impact load inversion. However, the sparse regularization strategy also has notable drawbacks. In load identification, it can produce nonphysical spike solutions, representing a continuous load as a few high-amplitude points, which misrepresents the true structural loading behavior. In summary, effectively balancing the preservation of local peaks, the capture of small perturbations, and the suppression of inversion instability remains a key scientific challenge in ice load identification research.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an ice load identification method based on a hybrid regularization strategy, which is designed to simultaneously capture the global smooth characteristics and local abrupt variations in the ice load time history. First, a dynamic mapping between the structural strain response and impact ice loads is established using Green’s kernel functions. Subsequently, a hybrid regularization inversion model that combines the respective advantages of the L1 and L2 norms is constructed, and the objective function is efficiently solved using a coordinate descent algorithm. To ensure the physical plausibility of the identified results, the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) is used to select the optimal set of regularization parameters. Finally, the performance and engineering applicability of the proposed method are systematically validated through high-fidelity numerical simulations and scaled model experiments on the local structure of an ice-class research vessel. Hybrid regularization strategy

As discussed in the previous section, regularization techniques are commonly applied in ice load identification to address the inherent ill-posedness by introducing additional constraints into the solution process. At present, widely adopted regularization strategies comprise Tikhonov regularization, which imposes an L2-norm constraint, and sparse regularization, which imposes an L1-norm constraint. Nevertheless, both strategies face significant limitations in addressing the problem of ice load identification for ships operating in polar environments.

During navigation in ice-covered waters, the bow of the vessel is the first to come into direct contact with sea ice. Accordingly, the bow region is selected as the area of ice load identification in this study. A total of 20 strain sensors are installed near the design waterline at the bow, arranged in a 4 × 5 array, with all sensors oriented toward the starboard side. The load application region is located above the strain measurement area, and the ice load impact area is discretized into 16 regions. Figure 3 illustrates the load application area and the corresponding region of strain measurement points.

Load application areas and the strain measurement points.

Based on the analysis of full-scale experimental data from the IBRV ARAON16, the dominant spectral energy of ice loads is primarily concentrated in the 5-10 Hz range. To effectively capture the dynamic response characteristics of ice loads, the sampling frequency of the strain sensors in this study is set to 20 Hz, with a time-history duration of 5 s. We employ a coupled Computational Fluid Dynamics-Discrete Element (CFD-DEM) method to generate the time histories of ice loads, which are then applied as external inputs to the finite element model of the hull. The corresponding strain time histories at specified locations are calculated using the Finite Element Method (FEM), thereby creating a complete high-fidelity dataset. Figure 4 illustrates the numerical simulation framework for ship–ice interaction. The underlying principles and computational procedures are detailed in our previous work 33 and are not repeated here. To construct the influence coefficient matrix, a unit load is applied sequentially at each load application region i, and the resulting strain responses at the measurement points are computed to form the i-th column of the influence coefficient matrix G. By repeating this procedure for all regions, the final structural matrix G with dimensions 20 × 16 is obtained.

Numerical simulation framework for ship–ice interaction.

To determine the optimal regularization parameters, it is first necessary to define appropriate ranges for the regularization parameters λ and α and to discretize them to ensure an effective BIC-based search. Considering the system noise level and the accuracy of strain measurements, the regularization strength parameter λ is sampled uniformly on a logarithmic scale within the interval [10-5, 10-1], resulting in 21 discrete values: λ∈{ 10-5, 10-4.8, 10-4.6, …, 10-1.2, 10-1 }. This logarithmically uniform sampling strategy enables a broad parameter search while maintaining local refinement, thereby reducing the risk of missing the optimal solution. For the hybrid regularization control parameter α, given that the ice load time histories are generally smooth with localized abrupt changes (exhibiting both sparsity and smoothness), this study performs uniform sampling over the interval [0, 1], resulting in a total of 11 discrete values.

By traversing different combinations of (λ, α) and calculating the corresponding BIC values, the parameter set that minimizes the BIC, (λ*, α*), is selected as the optimal regularization parameter. Figure 5 presents the corresponding BIC heat map. It can be observed that when λ ≈ 10-3 and α is around 0.7, the BIC reaches its minimum value of 42.5, indicating that the model achieves an optimal balance between fitting capability and complexity.

Results and discussion

Analysis of model identification performance

In this study, hybrid regularization, L1 regularization, and L2 regularization strategies are employed to identify ice loads in discrete regions of the ship bow. Figure 6 presents the ice load identification results obtained using the three regularization strategies. It can be observed that the ice loads identified using the hybrid regularization strategy achieve significantly better overall fitting quality compared with either L1 or L2 regularization alone. Further comparison reveals that L1 regularization is particularly effective at capturing instantaneous load peaks, whereas L2 regularization provides greater stability in reconstructing the smooth, background portions of the load.

Comparison of ice load inversion results in discrete regions of the ship bow using three regularization strategies.

To quantitatively evaluate the proposed ice load identification method, three metrics are selected: overall root mean square error (O-RMSE), RMSE of the impact segments (I-RMSE), and RMSE of the non-impact segments (NI-RMSE). Impact segments are defined as the time instants at which the ice load is greater than or equal to 0.5Fmax, representing high-load events during ship-ice interaction. All remaining time intervals are defined as non-impact segments. Tables 2–4 present the results of the three evaluation metrics for each identified region. It can be observed that there are notable differences in ice load identification accuracy among the different methods. As indicated by the O-RMSE values in Table 2, the hybrid regularization strategy demonstrates the highest inversion accuracy, with an average RMSE of 3.72 across the 16 identified regions. This value is markedly lower than those obtained using L1 regularization (11.87) and L2 regularization (26.73). Compared with L1 regularization, the hybrid regularization approach improves identification accuracy by approximately 68.6%, while an improvement of about 86.1% is achieved compared with L2 regularization. This significant improvement in accuracy indicates that, compared with L1 or L2 regularization strategies, the hybrid regularization strategy proposed in this study is more suitable for identifying ice load time histories characterized by sharp peaks and high-frequency fluctuations.

From Table 3 and Table 4, it can be seen that the hybrid regularization strategy exhibits a more balanced performance across different load characteristic segments. Specifically, the average RMSE for peak loads is 5.05, while that for non-impact load segments is 0.85. Compared with the L1 regularization strategy, the hybrid regularization approach improves the inversion accuracy of peak loads by approximately 37% and that of non-impact loads by about 97.5%. These results clearly demonstrate that the hybrid regularization method effectively integrates the sparsity of L1 regularization with the smoothness of L2 regularization, capturing sharp peaks during high-load events while maintaining stability in the low-load background, thereby avoiding the extreme deviations that may result from using a single regularization strategy. In contrast, the L1 regularization strategy excels at capturing ice load peaks, but this often comes at the expense of reconstruction accuracy in the non-impact segments, limiting its adaptability to smooth ice load time histories. On the other hand, L2 regularization performs well in identifying non-impact loads, but its excessive smoothing weakens the transient characteristics of ice impacts, leading to large errors in peak load identification. Such limitations can result in overly conservative estimates of extreme loads or the neglect of potential risks in practical structural design.

Experimental validation

To verify the feasibility and identification accuracy of the hybrid regularization strategy in engineering applications, a scaled model experiment was conducted on a representative local ship hull structure24. The experimental model incorporates typical structural components, including outer plating, frames, and longitudinal stiffeners, and is designed to reproduce the local structural characteristics of the ship side. The experimental model was fabricated from 6061 aluminum alloy with a uniform plate thickness of 3 mm. Considering that aluminum alloys are prone to significant deformation under high temperatures during welding, welding was not adopted for the connections in the model. Instead, a high-strength methacrylate-based structural adhesive was used for bonding, thereby ensuring geometric accuracy and overall structural stability. Figure 7(a) illustrates the overall schematic of the scaled model, while Fig. 7(b) shows the locations of the 12 load application points. External loads were applied at each inversion location using random impact excitation with an impulse hammer of 3.1 mV/N sensitivity. The corresponding impact loading conditions are summarized in Table 5. In Cases 3-6, multiple impacts were applied sequentially at different locations within a single acquisition window using a single impulse hammer, without strict temporal synchronization. The resulting time differences between impacts naturally introduce signal superposition, which is representative of realistic ice-structure interaction scenarios. The strain responses at the frame locations of the model were measured using resistive strain gauges. The gauges were arranged in a half-bridge configuration with temperature compensation, and their specific installation positions are illustrated in Fig. 6(a). During the experiment, force and strain signals were recorded using a DH5922 data acquisition system. Considering that the natural frequencies of the scaled model structure are primarily below 5 Hz, the sampling frequency of the strain signals is set to 10 Hz, with a measurement range of -1000 με to 1000 με. To reduce electrical noise, the acquisition system was equipped with an anti-aliasing filter.

By comparing the time-series correlation coefficient R between single-source and multi-source load inversions, it can be seen that the accuracy of all three regularization strategies decreases as the number of load sources increases. This indicates that mutual interference among multiple load sources is inevitable, which can exacerbate the ill-conditioning of the inversion equations and increase the error in load inversion. However, it is noteworthy that the sensitivity to load interference differs significantly among the various regularization strategies. Specifically, in the multi-source load inversion scenario, the correlation coefficient R obtained using hybrid regularization, L1 regularization, and L2 regularization decreases by 0.012, 0.035, and 0.115, respectively, compared with the single-source case. These results indicate that the hybrid regularization strategy proposed in this study exhibits the lowest sensitivity to interference from multiple load sources and demonstrates superior robustness in multi-source impact load inversion problems. In contrast, the widely used L2 regularization strategy, due to its penalty term favoring uniformly distributed loads, has limited capability in distinguishing multiple load sources and thus shows the highest sensitivity to interference.

Furthermore, we observed that an increase in the number of impact events also leads to a slight reduction in the accuracy of load inversion. This effect is attributable to the increased number of impacts, which reduces the spacing between individual events within the same time window. This promotes signal superposition and mutual coupling, exacerbating the ill-posedness of the inversion equations and increasing inversion errors. Experimental results indicate that the three hybrid regularization strategies have essentially comparable sensitivity to the number of impacts.

Conclusions

To accurately identify ice loads acting on the surface of polar vessels, this study proposes an ice load identification method based on a hybrid regularization strategy. The proposed method combines the sparsity-promoting advantage of the L1 norm with the smoothing property of the L2 norm, thereby enabling accurate capture of transient impact peaks while preserving the continuity of low-amplitude background loads. The main contributions are summarized as follows:

By incorporating the BIC, the optimal weighting of the L1 and L2 penalty terms is determined, effectively avoiding the uncertainty associated with empirical parameter selection. This mechanism suppresses noise amplification and overfitting while maintaining inversion accuracy, significantly enhancing the physical plausibility of the identified ice loads.

In particular, under multi-source interference scenarios, the correlation coefficient decreases by only 0.012, indicating that the method exhibits the lowest sensitivity to multi-source interference and the highest robustness.

The proposed method is based on a linear elastic structural model, assuming that the system is time-invariant and that multiple point loads satisfy the principle of superposition. Experimental results indicate that, under moderate ice impact conditions, these assumptions are well suited to metallic ship structures. Compared with existing identification approaches, the proposed method offers clear advantages in terms of load inversion accuracy and physical consistency. Nevertheless, its applicability is subject to several limitations:

The present method is developed under the assumption of linear structural behavior, which is appropriate within the scope of this study. The scaled model remained in the linear elastic regime throughout all experiments, and the Green’s function framework is well suited to metallic hull structures subjected to moderate ice impact loads. Nevertheless, this assumption may become invalid under conditions involving large plastic deformation, local buckling of hull plating, or extreme ice impact events at full scale. Under such conditions, the identification accuracy could degrade, and extensions such as piecewise linearization or nonlinear system identification techniques would be required. Extending the method to strongly nonlinear ship-ice interaction scenarios represents an important direction for future research.

Overall, the ice load identification method proposed in this study demonstrates significant advantages in peak capture, background reconstruction, and noise-robust stability, providing a more effective technical approach for structural safety assessment of polar vessels and navigation risk prediction in ice-covered waters. Future research could explore nonlinear effects, validation with full-scale ship data, and real-time adaptive parameter optimization, aiming to advance the application of real-time online ice load monitoring.
