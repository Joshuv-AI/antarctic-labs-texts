Morphological and Multilocus Phylogenetic Characterization of Septoria Species from Dioscorea, Eclipta, and Taraxacum in East Asia
Authors: Joon Ho Choi, Bora Nam, Anysia Hedy Ujat, Chiharu Nakashima, Seong-Jin Lee, Young-Joon Choi, Hyeon-Dong Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMore than 90 species of Septoria have been reported from Korea, but the identity of many recorded taxa remains uncertain owing to the lack of detailed morphological documentation and multilocus sequence data. In this study, isolates collected from symptomatic leaves of economically and medicinally important plants between 2006 and 2024 were examined using morphological observations and multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on six loci (internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, actin (ACT), β-tubulin (Btub), calmodulin (CAL), elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1-α), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2)). The concatenated dataset resolved three well-supported lineages corresponding to Septoria dioscoreicola on Dioscorea polystachya, S. eclipticola on Eclipta prostrata, and S. miurae on Taraxacum officinale, of which S. dioscoreicola and S. eclipticola are newly recorded in Korea, whereas Korean materials previously reported as S. taraxaci on Taraxacum are re-identified here as S. miurae. Multilocus analyses further demonstrated that S. taraxaci and S. miurae represent two distinct species associated with Taraxacum, with Korean and Japanese isolates corresponding to S. miurae, whereas the third Taraxacum-associated taxon, S. taraxacicola, is regarded as a synonym of S. miurae. Given the economic and medicinal importance of these hosts involved, the occurrence of Septoria leaf spot on these species may represent a potential concern for their cultivation in East Asia. These results provide an updated taxonomic basis for Septoria species occurring in Korea and clarify species boundaries within the Taraxacum-associated complex.Keywords: Leaf spot, multilocus phylogeny, plant-pathogenic fungi, species delimitation

Introduction

Septoria represents one of the largest genera of coelomycetous plant-pathogenic fungi and includes more than 2900 published names associated with leaf spot diseases on a wide range of herbaceous and woody plants worldwide [1,2]. Species of Septoria sensu stricto are characterized by immersed to subepidermal pycnidial conidiomata producing hyaline, filiform, septate conidia that are often exuded in mucilaginous cirrhi under humid conditions [3,4]. Many species are of phytopathological importance because they infect agricultural crops, ornamental plants, medicinal herbs, and wild vegetation, often reducing plant growth, yield, and marketability, e.g., S. lactucae on lettuce, S. lycopersici on tomato, and S. tritici (now, Zymoseptoria tritici) on wheat [5–7].

Traditionally, Septoria species were identified primarily on the basis of morphological characters such as conidial size, septation, pycnidial structure, and host association [8,9]. However, these characters frequently overlap among taxa and may vary depending on host and environmental conditions, making species delimitation difficult [10]. Large-scale phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that Septoria sensu lato is polyphyletic, and several distinct genera, including Parastagonospora, Sphaerulina, and Zymoseptoria, have been recognized [1,11]. Within Septoria sensu stricto, reliable species delimitation therefore requires polyphasic approaches integrating morphology, culture characteristics, host associations, and multilocus DNA phylogeny, particularly from protein-coding genes such as ACT, β-tubulin (Btub), elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1-α), and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) [12].

In Korea, members of the Septoria sensu lato reported to date include Caryophylloseptoria, Cercospora, Phloeospora, Septoria, Sphaerulina, Stagonospora, and Zymoseptoria [13]. Within Septoria sensu stricto, 94 species have been listed from 145 host plants [6,14]. However, most historical records were established primarily on morphological observations, and only a limited number of taxa have been supported by cultural characteristics and multilocus phylogenetic data. As a result, the identity of many previously reported taxa remains uncertain and requires reevaluation using modern systematic approaches [1].

Taxonomic complexity is particularly evident in Taraxacum officinale (common dandelion), on which three names of Septoria, including S. miurae, S. taraxaci, and S. taraxacicola, have historically been applied [15,16]. Because early descriptions were based only on morphological characters and comparative molecular data were unavailable, species boundaries within this complex have remained unclear. Clarification of their relationships requires phylogenetic comparison of Septoria materials from Europe and East Asia, where these taxa were originally described.

Field surveys conducted in Korea revealed previously undocumented Septoria-like leaf spot diseases on Dioscorea polystachya (Chinese yam) and Eclipta prostrata. Dioscorea polystachya is widely cultivated in East Asia as an important food and medicinal crop [15,16], whereas E. prostrata is a medicinal plant commonly used in traditional herbal medicine. As Septoria leaf spot has not previously been documented on Dioscorea polystachya and Eclipta prostrata in Korea, accurate identification of the causal fungi is necessary for reliable disease diagnosis and control. In addition, previous Korean samples of Septoria on Taraxacum, originally identified as S. taraxaci [6,8], are reevaluated here based on morphological comparison and multilocus phylogenetic analyses.

The objective of this study is to characterize Septoria species associated with Dioscorea polystachya, Eclipta prostrata, and Taraxacum officinale in Korea using morphological observations, cultural characteristics, and multilocus phylogenetic analyses based on six loci (internal transcribed spacer (ITS), actin (ACT), Btub, calmodulin (CAL), EF1-α, and RPB2). In addition, the taxonomic relationships among Taraxacum-associated taxa were reassessed through phylogenetic comparison of East Asian materials, and the nomenclatural status of S. taraxacicola was evaluated in relation to S. miurae.

Materials and methods

Fungal isolates

Leaves exhibiting typical Septoria-like leaf spot symptoms were collected from diverse locations in Korea between 2006 and 2024. Sampling sites were distributed across Gyeonggi-do, Gangwon-do, and Jeollabuk-do provinces, and included forest margins, mountain trails, and cultivated fields. Collections were conducted primarily between May and October. All voucher specimens were deposited at the herbaria of Korea University (KUS-F) and Kunsan National University (KSNUH). Single-spore isolation was performed using fresh conidial masses (cirrhi) extruded from pycnidia on naturally infected leaves. Conidial masses were carefully placed onto a drop of sterile distilled water on a slide glass and gently dispersed to obtain a dilute suspension. The suspension was streaked onto potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco, Detroit, MI) supplemented with streptomycin sulfate (200 ppm; Kisanbio, Seoul, South Korea) to suppress bacterial growth. Plates were incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 3–5 days. Germinating single conidia were transferred onto fresh PDA plates and cultured for morphological observation and DNA extraction. Representative Korean isolates were preserved in the Korean Agricultural Culture Collection (KACC) and the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute (CBS) (Table 1). For comparison, the Japanese strain MAFF 237805, isolated from Taraxacum officinale and deposited in the MAFF Genebank (Research Center of Genetic Resources, National Agriculture and Food Research Organization, Tsukuba, Japan), was included in the phylogenetic analysis of the Taraxacum-associated Septoria complex in East Asia.

Morphological and cultural characterization

Morphological characteristics were examined using both fresh symptomatic materials and dried herbarium specimens. Fresh samples were mounted in sterile distilled water for observation of conidiomata and conidia, whereas dried specimens were mounted in lactic acid and gently heated before examination. Conidia used for measurements were obtained from sporulation occurring naturally on infected host tissues. At least 30 conidia were measured for each specimen and presented as minimum–maximum ranges. Hand sections of symptomatic tissues were prepared to examine the structure of conidiomata, ostioles, and conidiogenous cells. Microscopic observations were performed using a differential interference contrast (DIC) microscope (Zeiss Imager M2 AX10; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) equipped with an AxioCam MRc5 camera (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) at magnifications of ×40, ×63, and ×100. Digital images were captured and processed using Zeiss imaging software. Measurements are reported as (minimum–) standard deviation toward the minimum–mean–standard deviation toward the maximum (–maximum).

Cultural characteristics were examined on PDA after incubation at 25 °C in the dark for four weeks. Colony diameter, color, texture, margin characteristics, aerial mycelium development, and production of exudates were recorded.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from mycelium harvested from PDA cultures using the MagListo 5M Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). PCR amplification targeted six loci: the ITS region, ACT, Btub, CAL, translation EF1-α, and the RPB2. Primer pairs used for amplification are listed in Table 2. PCR reactions were performed using AccuPower® PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). Amplification conditions followed a previously published protocol [11], with minor adjustments. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels and purified using the AccuPrep® PCR/Gel Purification Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). Purified amplicons were sequenced bidirectionally by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea). Forward and reverse sequences were assembled and edited using DNASTAR Lasergene software version 7.1.0 (DNASTAR, Inc., Madison, WI) [27]. Consensus sequences were deposited in GenBank, and accession numbers are provided in Table 1.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

Multilocus phylogenetic analyses were conducted using a concatenated dataset consisting of ITS, ACT, Btub, CAL, EF1-α, and RPB2 sequences. Reference sequences of Septoria species were retrieved from GenBank based on previously published phylogenetic studies (Table 1). Sequences of each locus were aligned individually using MAFFT v7 [28] with the default algorithm and manually inspected for alignment errors. The individual alignments were concatenated using SequenceMatrix v1.8 [29] to produce a combined dataset of the six markers. Maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analyses were performed using the IQ-TREE v1.6.12 [30] with a partitioned dataset. The best-fit nucleotide substitution models for each partition were selected using ModelFinder [31]. Branch support was assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap method with 1000 replicates [32]. Sphaerulina aceris (CBS 183.97) was selected as the outgroup based on its placement outside Septoria sensu stricto in a previous phylogenetic study [12]. The inferred phylogenetic tree was visualized using iTOL v7 [33].

Results

Phylogeny

The concatenated six-locus dataset comprised 37 ingroup taxa and 2214 aligned nucleotide positions, including ITS (498 bp), ACT (206 bp), Btub (305 bp), CAL (473 bp), EF1-α (390 bp), and RPB2 (344 bp). The ML phylogeny resolved three well-supported lineages corresponding to Septoria dioscoreicola, S. eclipticola, and S. miurae, each clearly separated from other representatives of Septoria sensu stricto included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree inferred from a concatenated dataset of six loci (ITS, ACT, β-tubulin, CAL, EF1-α, and RPB2) representing species of Septoria sensu stricto. Bootstrap support values (1000 replicates) greater than 70% are shown at the nodes. Isolates obtained in the present study are indicated in bold. The three focal taxa, Septoria miurae, S. eclipticola, and S. dioscoreicola, are highlighted. Sphaerulina aceris (CBS 183.97) was used as the outgroup. The scale bar indicates substitutions per site.

The isolate KACC 411317, obtained from Dioscorea polystachya, was located in an independent basal lineage of a major clade, composed of Septoria sensu stricto. Although no directly comparable dataset of Septoria species on D. polystachya was available in public databases, its distinct phylogenetic position, together with morphological agreement with the original description, supports the identification of the Korean isolate as S. dioscoreicola.

Septoria eclipticola isolates obtained from E. prostrata, including three Korean isolates (KACC 43079, 410927, and 411037) and two Iranian reference isolates (CCTU 1153.2 and CBS 136118), formed a strongly supported clade corresponding to S. eclipticola. No sequence divergence was detected between Korean and Iranian isolates across the six loci analyzed.

Four Korean isolates (KACC 42901, 410958, 411139, and CBS 128650) obtained from Taraxacum officinale, together with the Japanese isolate MAFF 237805, formed a well-supported monophyletic clade. No sequence divergence was detected among these isolates across the six loci analyzed. This clade was clearly separated from Septoria taraxaci CBS 567.75 isolated from Taraxacum sp. in Armenia and from other Asteraceae-associated species of Septoria included in the analysis. This lineage corresponds to the taxon previously reported from Korea as Septoria taraxaci and is identified here as S. miurae based on combined morphological comparison and multilocus phylogenetic evidence.

Discussion

The present study confirms the occurrence of three species of Septoria, namely S. dioscoreicola on Dioscorea polystachya, S. eclipticola on Eclipta prostrata, and S. miurae on Taraxacum officinale, in Korea based on combined morphological observations and multilocus phylogenetic analyses. Septoria dioscoreicola and S. eclipticola are newly recorded in Korea, whereas previous Korean records of S. taraxaci on Taraxacum officinale are shown here to represent S. miurae. Multilocus phylogenetic analyses clearly separated S. taraxaci and S. miurae, despite their occurrence on the same host genus and partially overlapping morphological characters.

The confirmation of S. dioscoreicola on Dioscorea polystachya is noteworthy because molecular data for Septoria species associated with Dioscorea remain scarce. Earlier records were based mainly on conidial morphology, which partially overlaps among two species reported from this host genus [34]. The availability of multilocus sequence data for Korean material therefore provides an important reference for future taxonomic and comparative studies of Dioscorea-associated Septoria-like fungi. Given the agricultural and medicinal importance of D. polystachya in East Asia, accurate identification of the causal species is also essential for evaluating potential phytopathological risks.

The record of S. eclipticola from Korea extends the known distribution of this species, which had previously been documented only in Iran. The similarity of sequences across all six loci between Korean and Iranian isolates, including the ex-type, suggests that S. eclipticola is a stable species with a broader distribution than previously recognized. This finding highlights the importance of direct comparison with authenticated reference isolates when documenting newly recorded taxa within a genus that lacks characters on those morphological distinctions, such as Septoria.

The most taxonomically significant result concerns Septoria species associated with Taraxacum officinale. Multilocus phylogenetic analyses clearly separated S. taraxaci and S. miurae as two independent species despite their occurrence on the same host genus and partially overlapping morphological characters. The consistent clustering of Korean and Japanese isolates confirms that S. miurae represents an East Asian lineage distinct from the European species S. taraxaci. These findings demonstrate that host association alone does not provide sufficient resolution for species delimitation within Septoria.

Nomenclatural clarification further supports recognition of S. miurae as the correct name for the East Asian taxon associated with Taraxacum. S. taraxaci Hollós (1907) remains the earliest legitimate name for a Septoria species on Taraxacum in European countries, whereas two later homonyms, Septoria taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. (1913) from Japan and Septoria taraxaci J.W. Ellis from the UK (1914), are illegitimate under ICN Art. 53.1. S. taraxacicola Miura (1928) had been recognized as a validly published name for a new species from Manchuria, mainland China. However, judging from his notes in the protologue, S. taraxacicola is an avowed substitute name (ICN Art. 6.11) of S. taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. described based on the specimens, which he collected from Aomori, Japan [15]. That is, he proposed “a new species” cited with S. taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. as a synonym, although he would propose “a new name”. In addition, the type locality of S. taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. in the protologue and specimens’ records in the herbarium S (https://herbarium.nrm.se/specimens/F63127) is Sapporo, Hokkaido Island, not Aomori, Honshu Island. This may be his mistake. S. miurae Trotter (1931) was proposed as a new name (avowed substitute) based on the type of S. taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. collected by Miura and cited S. taraxaci Syd. & P. Syd. as the replaced synonym. Therefore, as a legitimate name, S. miurae is applicable to the East Asian lineage confirmed in this study.

The present study refines the taxonomic circumscription of three Septoria species occurring in Korea, including the re-identification of Korean Taraxacum-associated material previously referred to S. taraxaci as S. miurae, and provides multilocus reference data for taxa that had previously been poorly characterized at the molecular level. These results contribute to a more stable species framework for Septoria in East Asia and provide a foundation for future studies addressing host specificity, geographic distribution, and disease epidemiology of septorioid fungi.
