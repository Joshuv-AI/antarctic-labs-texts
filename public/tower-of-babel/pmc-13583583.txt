Efficacy of plasma exchange and exchange transfusion in the clinical management of severe hypertriglyceridemia in emergency
Authors: C. Stefanutti, G. Zeppa, G. Perrone, G. Labbadia, P. Galoppi, V. Demarco, E. Fornari, A. Bordugo, C. Santelia, V. Mancioppi, M.G. Zenti, M. Cossu, B. Sovrano, A. Vivenzio, H. Cohen, H. Soran, L. Pisciotta, C. Maffeis, B. Bjelakovic, K. Widhalm, G. Rizzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe management involving genetic evaluation and therapy for SHTG of 21 subjects having SHTG either associated with AP or not is discussed within the context of a multinational, clinical case record analysis.MethodsA retrospective case series study of 21 patients with severe hypertriglyceridemia (SHTG) with or without acute pancreatitis (AP) involving recurrent AP cases was performed. The clinical profile, genetic background, and results of treatments using therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and/or exchange transfusion (ET) were analyzed among a mixed group of adult patients, children, pregnant females, and one neonate.ResultsPatients were symptomatic and were admitted via the ER. Patients who received therapy using TPE or ET had a significant reduction in plasma lipid and lipoprotein levels with improvement in their symptoms. Other treatments, such as TG-lowering agents, can also be used but are less beneficial at this stage in the management of rapidly lowering TG levels.ConclusionThis choice of TPE or ET for 16 patients with clinical signs of acute pancreatitis induced by SHTG was proper. Considering that this is a retrospective study without randomization, the results are more a confirmation of successful use rather than an indication of superiority over standard therapy.

Introduction

Hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) is a relatively prevalent metabolic syndrome marked by an increase in the concentration of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), triglycerides (TG), and chylomicrons in the blood plasma [1], [2]. Severe hypertriglyceridemia (SHTG) is associated with TG concentration greater than 882 mg/dL (>9.96 mmol/L), accompanied by a high concentration of chylomicrons and TG-rich VLDL. Familial chylomicronemia syndrome (FCS) is a rare autosomal recessive condition arising from loss-of-function mutations of genes related to the metabolism of chylomicrons, with major contribution of the gene for lipoprotein lipase (LPL) [3], [4]. Multifactorial chylomicronemia syndrome (MCS) includes genetic predisposition and secondary factors such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, and alcoholism contributing to the development of variable accumulation of VLDL, chylomicrons, and IDL [5]. Persistent chylomicronemia implies the prolonged presence of chylomicrons in fasted plasma [6], [7]. The etiology of HTG can be primary or secondary (abuse of alcohol, diabetes, medication, pregnancy, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome), with HTG occurring in affected individuals as a child or adult, both females and males [8].

There is also a genetic basis for HTG in children and adults, which presents as chylomicrons in an advanced clinical presentation called FCS as a result of LPL deficiency or apoC-II deficiency [9]. Some other promising TG-lowering drugs have been studied recently, such as antisense oligonucleotides against ApoC-III (volanesorsen, olezarsen) and monoclonal antibodies against ANGPTL3 (evinacumab) [10]. The significant lowering effect on TG was noted in clinical trials, but none of these drugs have been used in any emergencies, according to any scientific publication [11].

Diagnosis (phenotypic)

The complications associated with SHTG include not only acute pancreatitis but also eruptive xanthoma or lipemia retinalis [13]. Chylomicronemia syndrome may also develop when there are high amounts of chylomicrons in the blood, which is defined by the presence of recurrent attacks of abdominal pain (colic), nausea, vomiting, and pancreatitis [14]. Triglycerides in such cases are usually more than 2000 mg/dL (22.58 mmol/L). Among diseases leading to secondary SHTG and AP are chronic renal failure, diabetes mellitus, alcoholism, pregnancy, drugs (antihypertensive drugs, glucocorticoids, oral estrogens, etc.), obesity, and hypothyroidism. Clinically diagnosed patients with SHTG, with or without AP, were treated in the emergency department via TPE and were provided with the second and third treatments after 24 and 48 h, respectively. During hospital stay, all patients were given dietary and medication interventions (fibrates, ω-3 fatty acids). Among 21 patients, 12 had AP and 11 had recurrent AP. One 23-day-old patient received ET treatment.

Therapeutic apheresis method

According to our protocol, we utilized a centrifugal apheresis system (OPTIA, TERUMO BCT, Lakewood, CO 802015, USA). Most patients were submitted to urgent treatment with TPE (a written informed consent was always obtained). Approximate duration of procedure: 90 min. 3000–4000 mL of blood were processed with the extraction of about 1300 mL of plasma, and 2000 mL of human albumin 50 g/L at 5%, 250 mL, and succinyl gelatin 500 mL as a replacement fluid were infused. In TPE, the whole blood is usually separated into plasma and related corpuscular components through centrifugation or, rarely, filtration [17]. The corpuscular components mixed with albumin solution plus saline (NaCl 0.9%) solution at 5%–25% are then reinfused to the patient to substitute the plasma previously removed. TPE removes atherogenic lipid, lipoproteins, but also other plasma proteins, such as albumin, immunoglobulins, and hemocoagulatory mediators [18].

Exchange transfusion (ET) method

After setting up a femoral central venous catheter (CVC), the patient was submitted to ET. The total volume infused was 574 mL; aspirated 278 mL. The whole procedure, 4 h in total, was well tolerated. Glycemia strictly monitored, Hb and electrolytes in range. Transient hypocalcemia, ionized calcium, 105 mg/dL (26.25 mmol/L), occurred and was corrected with oral calcium gluconate. Progressive decrease in platelet count (90.000/mmc) was observed and managed with blood transfusion.

Results

We have not reported the medical history and clinical evolution of all the patients belonging to this cohort, although all are in clinical remission and in good general condition. The main demographic and anthropometric parameters, the phenotypic and genetic diagnosis, their triglyceride levels, and the most feared complication, i.e., acute pancreatitis, have been widely reported (see the tables). The patients treated with extracorporeal technique (TPE) showed a complete remission of symptoms, significant reduction of triglycerides, and after a period of clinical observation in the department of internal medicine, were discharged with dietary intervention and pharmacological therapy, essentially fibrates and ω-3 fatty acids (Table 7). Among the group receiving TPE/ET, a quick improvement in the clinical status was noted within 24–48 h post-procedure, especially in terms of abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, and oral intake. There were no cases of sustained organ dysfunction due to the SHTG-associated AP among those who underwent the treatment during their admission [19]. Nevertheless, owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the variability in the patient population, there were no standard comparisons made concerning the length of stay, ICU admission, organ dysfunction scoring, and/or recovery time between treated and untreated individuals. Also, patients not treated with TPE and the newborn treated with ET have all been treated in the emergency room and later in the department of internal medicine with usual medical care [20]. Since the current investigation is retrospective and descriptive in its approach, and because those individuals not receiving TPE were a heterogeneous group in terms of severity, time of treatment, and associated conditions, a quantitative analysis of the effects of TG reduction between the two groups was not possible [21].

In order to give a summary of results at the level of cohorts, patients receiving TPE or ET showed marked biochemical and clinical improvement. There was an improvement in symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and oral feeding. This improvement was achieved within 24–48 h following the extracorporeal procedures. Organ dysfunction that was due to SHTG-associated AP did not occur in any of the patients receiving the treatments in the hospital setting. In this group of 21 patients who were involved in this study, 12 patients were identified as suffering from AP, and 11 others were diagnosed with a history of recurrent AP attacks. All patients who underwent treatment reported a significant decrease in triglyceride levels and an improvement in their condition; however, triglyceride levels post-treatment were not always available for analysis owing to the retrospective and multi-center nature of the study. Thus, no mean or median percentage of triglyceride reduction was calculated.

There were a few cases of adverse events related to extracorporeal therapy, and those that occurred were temporary. For the one neonate under extracorporeal therapy (ET), he had mild temporary hypocalcemia and thrombocytopenia and was effectively treated with no adverse effects. One pregnant individual had respiratory distress temporarily and had to be under intensive care, but fully recovered.

Notwithstanding, we thought it was useful for the reader to report in extenso three very interesting clinical cases from the cohort, covering all ages of life, gender, and, for women, pregnancy: an adult, a newborn, and a pregnant patient.

Case # 1: a 46-year-old male, diagnosed with SHTG, relapsing AP. Treated with PEX. (Unpublished).

A 46-year-old patient (male, Caucasian, height: 178 cm, weight: 87 Kg) was diagnosed with SHTG at age 15. Evidence of familiarity with dyslipidemia, AP, and diabetes. However, no genetic mutation was identified. He faced four AP bouts: first episode in 2001 treated with strict diet and hydration, a second event in 2011, a necrotic hemorrhagic AP (surgically treated), a further AP episode in 2014 managed with a nasogastric (NG) tube, and lastly a fourth AP episode in 2020, managed, once again, with an NG tube. Creatinine increased to 4 mg/dL (353.6 mmol/L) (ref. range: 0.8–1.2 mg/dL and 70.7–106 mmol/L). The failure of any attempt to reduce TG, which were very high, and the lack of response to fibrates and ω-3 fatty acids, led to a referral to our Extracorporeal Therapeutic Techniques Unit [22], where the patient underwent serial weekly treatment with TPE with progressive reduction of plasma TG levels (Figs. 3) and improvement of gastrointestinal symptoms. The patient received TPE for 20 weeks and decided not to undergo further extracorporeal treatments. It was not possible to arrange a follow-up, but we received news of his sudden death due to an acute myocardial infarction after a few months, from his wife. Case # 2: 23-day-old female newborn diagnosed with LPL deficiency, AP. Treated with ET. (Unpublished).

A 23-day-old newborn (currently 5 months), exclusively breastfed, attended the pediatric emergency room after evidence of bright blood in the feces; no evidence of further symptoms. Blood tests showed evidence of milky plasma. For the first 48 h, reliable TG and LDL- cholesterol (LDL-C) load was unavailable because of lipemia. Following a period of fasting and hydration, the TG level was observed to be 21,160 mg/dL (238.91 mmol/L). The LDL-C level, when calculated, was found to be inaccurate owing to the presence of severe hypertriglyceridemia and lipemia. After setting a femoral central venous catheter (CVC), the patient was submitted to exchange transfusion (ET) for 3 days (plasma volume infused, 574 mL). No side effects occurred, except for slight hypocalcemia, treated with oral calcium gluconate. Platelet count decrease was managed with blood transfusion. Post-operative TG levels rose to 3573 mg/dL (40.3 mmol/L). LDL-C results were still hard to evaluate due to the presence of lipemia. The patient was given Monogen (Monogen is low-fat, whey protein powdered feed, which is low in long-chain triglycerides and high in medium-chain triglycerides) from the next day, at a well-tolerated, progressively increased dose. TG levels improved to 2133 mg/dL (24.0 mmol/L) and 811 mg/dL (9.1 mmol/L), respectively, 24 and 48 h after ET. Four days after this procedure, TG levels decreased to 373 mg/dL (4.2 mmol/L). CVC was removed, and feeding was supplied with Monogen 150 mL/Kg/day. Six days after ET, TG levels slightly increased to 683 mg/dL (7.7 mmol/L). The newborn showed a normal brain ultrasound. Age-appropriate alertness and behavior. Genetic test confirmed the diagnosis of LPL deficiency (Table 3, case # 19).

Case # 3: 35-year-old pregnant woman, familial history of HTG and Arterial Hypertension; AP. Treated with PEX.

A 35-year-old Caucasian pregnant woman at 25 weeks of gestational age (GA) was referred to the emergency room, reporting severe abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. The familial history was positive for familial HTG and arterial hypertension. Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography confirmed the presence of AP. Laboratory showed increased lipase activity, 200 U/L (ref. range 28–100 U/L), and severely elevated values of both plasma TG 4425 mg/dL (49.9 mmol/L) and total cholesterol (TC) 1488 mg/dL (38.48 mmol/L). The patient was diagnosed with Hyperlipidemic Pancreatitis, and the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) (tachycardia 120 pulses per minute, leukocytosis (22 × 10 [9]/L), tachypnea, respiratory rate 21/min, and abnormally high C-reactive Protein (237 mg/L)) was also assessed. Therapeutic apheresis, by means of PEX, was initiated. During the first cycle of PEX, 3000 mL of plasma were substituted with 5% albumin, leading to a significant reduction of TG and TC levels and of C-reactive protein concentration (237 mg/L). Soon after the end of PEX, the patient developed an episode of adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring admission to the Intensive Care Unit. A complete recovery was obtained after a few hours, and two additional PEX procedures were performed with an overall improvement of the patient's clinical and metabolic condition. During all PEX sessions, albumin levels, electrolyte and acid-base balance were strictly monitored and corrected when necessary; fetal heart rate was continuously recorded. After four days, the patient's treatment was switched to oral therapy (fenofibrate 200 mg/day and omega-3 fatty acids 4 g/day). TG levels progressively returned to relatively acceptable values (ranging between 542 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) and 984 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L). At 38 weeks GA, the patient entered spontaneous labor, delivering a healthy male newborn weighing 2860 g. Gene profiling assessed a combined heterozygous state for the variants pSer19Trp of the APOA5 gene and pCys130Arg of the APOE (allele E4) gene (Table 3, case # 21).

Discussion

Several treatments have been used in the treatment of SHTG and AP patients. Management of AP in emergencies includes fasting, intravenous fluid administration, and analgesics. The primary objective in the management of SHTG in AP is the quick reduction of TG-rich lipoproteins from plasma.

TPE and ET can be described as two rapid cytoreductive rescue therapies that have the capacity to quickly reduce TG levels in a matter of hours. However, recent pharmacological therapies, including ApoC-III inhibitors such as volanesorsen and olezarsen, as well as ANGPTL3 inhibitors like evinacumab, which are considered game-changing developments in SHTG prevention and management in the long run, are not used in emergency settings owing to their inability to offer quick results, making them inappropriate for use as rescue therapies. Approval of antisense oligonucleotide against apoC-III, volanesorsen, for the treatment of FCS was recently granted in Europe. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted approval for olezarsen for the treatment of FCS in adults. ANGPTL3 inhibitors are also promising agents. Notably, none of the available clinical evidence supports the use of any of these agents in an emergency setting. Accordingly, the current results can only be regarded as descriptive information about feasibility and prompt biochemical changes without indicating any advantage compared to conventional medicine.

Limitations

This multinational case record study provides valuable insights into the acute management of severe hypertriglyceridemia (SHTG) using therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and exchange transfusion (ET); however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective nature of the analysis, based on a convenience sample of 21 patients, introduces potential selection bias and limits generalizability. The cohort, while diverse in age, gender, ethnicity, and clinical presentation (including children and pregnant women), is relatively small and drawn from specialized centers, which may not fully represent broader populations with SHTG or familial chylomicronemia syndrome (FCS). Second, the absence of a control group precludes direct comparisons between TPE/ET and conventional therapies alone. Triglyceride reduction rates and clinical endpoints could not be compared between the groups receiving treatment and those not receiving treatment since patient assignment to treatment was determined by clinical emergencies, and not according to study criteria. Moreover, there was no standardized data on the degree of organ dysfunction, the time of ICU stay, and the duration of overall hospitalization, which made the comparison of results impossible. Although all patients achieved clinical remission, the lack of randomization, blinding, or standardized follow-up protocols is a further limiting factor in order to confirm the outcomes obtained with the extracorporeal methods. Variability in treatment protocols (e.g., number of TPE sessions, replacement fluids) and the inclusion of patients with heterogeneous genetic profiles (e.g., 13 with FCS-compatible mutations) may complicate causal inferences. Long-term follow-up data were incomplete for some patients, including one case of sudden death unrelated to the intervention, which hinders evaluation of sustained efficacy and safety. Additionally, while novel therapies like ApoC-III inhibitors were discussed, their emergency use was not directly compared in this cohort, as the focus was on real-world acute settings. Future studies should address these limitations through prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, standardized protocols, and comprehensive genetic and biochemical profiling to better validate TPE/ET's role in SHTG management. Further, the lack of triglyceride levels after procedures and standardized follow-up prevented the calculation of percentage reductions and recurrence of conditions in the overall population, as well as a proper statistical comparison between groups.

Conclusions

Results from this case series study conducted in real-life conditions across multiple countries show the effectiveness and success of TPE and ET in the management of SHTG in difficult-to-treat patient populations, such as pediatric patients, pregnant women, and neonates. As it was mentioned, the case series is retrospective and not randomized, and thus, these results have to be considered in light of the effectiveness of these therapies rather than as evidence of their superior efficiency when compared to standard treatments. New drugs lowering TG levels can prove helpful in future long-term management of this condition, but at the moment, they cannot be used as emergency medications. Future registries or multicenter trials should investigate whether rapid TG reduction obtained using TPE/ET results in any decrease in organ failure, ICU admission, and length of hospital stay.
