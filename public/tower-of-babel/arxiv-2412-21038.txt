Sparse PCA: Phase Transitions in the Critical Regime
Michael J. Feldman, Theodor Misiakiewicz, and Elad Romanov

Abstract
This work studies sparse principal component analysis (PCA) in high dimensions. Given n n independent p p -dimensional Gaussian samples with covariance 𝚺 ≔ ( λ − 1 ) ​ 𝒗 ​ 𝒗 ⊤ + 𝐈 p , {\bm{\Sigma}}\coloneqq(\lambda-1){\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{I}}_{p}, our goal is to estimate 𝒗 {\bm{v}} under the assumption of sparsity. On the one hand, if the sparsity level m ≔ ‖ 𝒗 ‖ 0 m\coloneqq\|{\bm{v}}\|_{0} satisfies m ≲ n m\lesssim\sqrt{n} , algorithms such as covariance thresholding (Krauthgamer et al., 2015) consistently outperform PCA. On the other hand, if m ≫ n m\gg\sqrt{n} , it is conjectured that no polynomial-time algorithm can recover 𝒗 {\bm{v}} below the detection threshold of PCA. We investigate the “critical” high-dimensional regime, where n , p , m → ∞ n,p,m\rightarrow\infty with m / n → β m/\sqrt{n}\rightarrow\beta and p / n → γ p/n\rightarrow\gamma , and study estimators based on kernel PCA, generalizing covariance thresholding. Within this framework, we achieve a fine-grained understanding of signal detection and recovery. Our main result establishes a detection phase transition, analogous to the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché (BBP) transition for PCA: above a signal strength threshold—depending on the kernel function, γ \gamma , and β \beta —kernel PCA is informative. Conversely, below the threshold, kernel principal components are asymptotically orthogonal to the signal. Notably, (1) above this threshold, consistent support recovery is possible with high probability, (2) for all β ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) \beta\in(0,\infty) , kernel PCA strictly outperforms PCA, and (3) as β → ∞ \beta\rightarrow\infty , kernel PCA and PCA coincide. We identify optimal kernel functions for detection and support recovery, and numerical calculations suggest that soft thresholding is nearly optimal. Our key technical contribution is approximation guarantees for deterministic equivalents of kernel random matrices, which enable sharp estimates of coordinate fluctuations of kernel principal components.

Introduction

From factor analysis to covariance estimation to matrix factorization, principal component analysis (PCA) is a standard tool for dimensionality reduction and low-rank signal recovery. Given data with
n
observations and
p
variables, the principal components are the eigenvectors of the
p\times p
sample covariance matrix. PCA, however, has two key drawbacks: first, the principal components are generally non-sparse linear combinations of all
p
variables. This limits interpretability, and fails to capture the fact that signal matrices are often simultaneously low-rank and sparse—such matrices arise in diverse fields, including genetics, computer vision, imaging, and neuroscience
[
21
,
22
,
29
,
56
,
66
]
. Second, in high dimensions (when
p
is comparable to or greater than
n
), PCA is inconsistent: the principal components are inconsistent estimators of the eigenvectors of the population covariance
[
58
]
.

These issues have spurred the development of numerous alternatives to classical PCA that produce sparse principal components, collectively referred to as sparse PCA methods
[
38
,
23
,
68
,
21
,
14
,
62
,
42
]
. In this literature, the assumption of a low-rank and sparse ground truth is often implicit in applied and methodological papers, and explicit in theoretical analyses
[
45
]
. Whether or not there is evidence in a particular problem that the ground truth is sparse, Friedman et al. advise “betting on sparsity”: “Use a procedure that does well in sparse problems, since no procedure does well in dense problems”
[
29
,
32
]
.

PCA has been extensively studied in high dimensions, revealing several phenomena that are absent in classical fixed-
p
statistics. A prototypical model in the theoretical literature is
Johnstone’s spiked covariance model (henceforth referred to as the “spiked model”)
[
36
]

n
independent samples are observed from a
p
-dimensional Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}})

{\bm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a rank-one perturbation of identity:

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}\coloneqq(\lambda-1){\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{I}}_{p}.

(1.1)

Here,
\lambda>1
is the maximum eigenvalue of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and our goal is to estimate the corresponding eigenvector or “spike”
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}

{\bm{Z}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denote the data matrix with the samples as rows, where
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
contains i.i.d. standard Gaussian elements.

PCA estimates
{\bm{v}}
by the leading eigenvector
{\bm{u}}_{1}
of the sample covariance

{\bm{Y}}\coloneqq n^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\hskip-0.80002pt{\bm{Z}}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{1/2}

(which is a sufficient statistic for

In low dimensions, where

and

As

p/n\rightarrow\gamma\in(0,\infty)

[(1-\sqrt{\gamma})^{2},(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}]

of
{\bm{Y}}
and

satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\begin{cases}\lambda+\frac{\lambda\gamma}{\lambda-1}&\lambda>1+\sqrt{\gamma},\\
(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}&\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{\gamma},\end{cases}

(1.2)

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{v}}\rangle^{2}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\begin{cases}\Big(1-\frac{\gamma}{(\lambda-1)^{2}}\Big)\Big/\Big(1+\frac{\gamma}{\lambda-1}\Big)&\lambda>1+\sqrt{\gamma},\\
0&\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{\gamma}.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Remarkably, PCA experiences a
phase transition

\lambda_{1}

{\bm{v}}
. On the other hand, if

\lambda_{1}
is an
outlier eigenvalue

. Key papers in the development of (

This work focuses on the “critical” regime

Accordingly, we assume

This section investigates the statistical implications of the results in Sections

First, we introduce a hypothesis test based on the maximum eigenvalue

[

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}y_{\ell}^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1,i\neq\ell}^{p}x_{i}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{i=1,i\neq\ell}^{p}|\kappa^{\prime}(\sqrt{n}{\bm{Y}}_{i\ell})|^{2}v_{i}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bm{v}}\|_{\infty}^{2}\max_{1\leq\ell\leq p}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{\ell\}}|\kappa^{\prime}(\sqrt{n}{\bm{Y}}_{i\ell})|^{2}.

Combining the above bounds yields the lemma.
