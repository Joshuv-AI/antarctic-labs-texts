Abstract
In this paper, we perform asymptotic analyses of the widely used ESPRIT direction-of-arrival (DoA) estimator for large arrays, where the array size N N and the number of snapshots T T grow to infinity at the same pace. In this large-dimensional regime, the sample covariance matrix (SCM) is known to be a poor eigenspectral estimator of the population covariance. We show that the classical ESPRIT algorithm, that relies on the SCM, and as a consequence of the large-dimensional inconsistency of the SCM, produces inconsistent DoA estimates as N , T → ∞ N,T\to\infty with N / T → c ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) N/T\to c\in(0,\infty) , for both widely- and closely-spaced DoAs. Leveraging tools from random matrix theory (RMT), we propose an improved G-ESPRIT method and prove its consistency in the same large-dimensional setting. From a technical perspective, we derive a novel bound on the eigenvalue differences between two potentially non-Hermitian matrices, which may be of independent interest. Numerical simulations are provided to corroborate our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Direction-of-arrival (DoA) estimation is a fundamental task in array signal processing with various applications, ranging from radar, sonar, and wireless communications, to medical imaging.
Accurate DoA estimation enables systems to localize sources, optimize resource allocation, and enhance signal quality in complex environments.
Among the numerous methods developed for DoA estimation, subspace-based methods such as MUSIC and its variants, as well as the ESPRIT approach, have gained significant popularity
[
25
,
21
,
19
]
.
Their effectiveness and computational efficiency (compared to, e.g., maximum likelihood estimators) have made them mainstays in modern array signal processing.

Subspace-based methods such as MUSIC and ESPRIT exploit the inherent orthogonality between the signal and noise subspace of the
population covariance
to estimate DoAs from noisy observations.
Since the population covariance is practically inaccessible, eigenspectral decomposition is performed on the sample covariance matrix (SCM) of the received signals to extract DoA information.
When the number of snapshots
T
is
much larger
than the array size
N
, the SCM provides an accurate estimate of the population covariance, and the statistical performance of, e.g., MUSIC
[
28
]
, total least squares ESPRIT
[
17
]
, and least squares ESPRIT
[
36
]
has been well-studied in prior work.

However, these subspace-based methods are known to suffer from the so-called “threshold effect,” where their performance degrades drastically when either the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) or the number of snapshots falls below a certain threshold
[
29
]
.
This limitation has sparked renewed interest in analyzing these methods in the regime of
large arrays and limited snapshots
, where both
N
and
T
grow to infinity at the same pace, i.e.,

\displaystyle N,T\to\infty,\quad N/T\to c\in(0,\infty),

a setting that models scenarios where large sensing arrays acquire data within relatively short sampling durations.

With the progress of random matrix theory (RMT) over the past decade, many methods in statistics, signal processing, and machine learning have been revisited in the large-dimensional regime, leading to novel insights and improved algorithms tailored for large-dimensional data
[
18
,
5
]
.
A key takeaway from these developments is that when
T
is
not much larger
than
N
, the SCM becomes a poor eigenspectral estimator of the population covariance (see
[
5
]
and
Section

II-C
for a brief review).
In such cases, the sample eigenvectors/eigenspaces are
biased
from their population counterparts.
Consequently, subspace methods
cannot
be expected to provide consistent estimates of the true DoAs in scenarios where
N,T
are both large and comparable.
Notably, the empirically observed “threshold effect” of these methods is a direct manifestation of the
phase transition
behavior inherent in the large-dimensional SCM eigenspace.

In the case of MUSIC, it has been shown in
[
35
]
that despite the eigenspectral
inconsistency
of the SCM in the large
N,T
regime, MUSIC still provides consistent DoA estimates in widely-spaced DoA scenarios (see
Assumption

3

However, for closely-spaced sources (see
Assumption

4

O(N^{-1})
, the classical MUSIC approach
fails to deliver accurate estimates
.
To address this limitation, a modified MUSIC algorithm, G-MUSIC, was introduced by “correcting” the sample signal subspace using RMT.
G-MUSIC is guaranteed to provide
N
-consistent

II-C

Eigenspectral Inconsistency for Large-dimensional SCM

ESPRIT relies on the assumption that the signal subspace
{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
can be accurately estimated by
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
.
This is typically valid when the number of observations
T
is much larger than the array size
N
, making the sample covariance
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
a good “proxy” of population covariance
{\mathbf{C}}
in the sense that
\|\hat{\mathbf{C}}-{\mathbf{C}}\|\to 0
as
T\to\infty

However, in the case of large arrays and/or limited snapshots, where
N
and
T
are of the same order of magnitude,
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
is
not
a consistent estimator of
{\mathbf{C}}
in a spectral norm sense.
Consequently, we should
not
expect that the top subspace
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
used in ESPRIT is a good estimate of the true signal subspace
{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
.

In the following, we recall a few results from large-dimensional RMT that provide precise eigenspectral characterizations of SCM in the large

We note that throughout this paper, we consider a regime in which the number of sources
K
is fixed as

This assumption is standard in large-dimensional analyses of subspace-based methods and spiked covariance models
[
8
,
1
]

Allowing

4\sigma^{2}\sqrt{c}\gg 0
.
Moreover, under the additional
Assumption

2
, it is known that the largest eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
(that are due to the “signal”
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}
) are guaranteed to “separate” from those due to the random white noise.
However, even in this case, the empirical eigenvalues
\hat{\lambda}_{i}
of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
are larger than the population ones (i.e.,
\sigma^{2}(1+\ell_{i})
), by a quantity that is proportional to
c=\lim N/T
, and eigenvectors
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
are
biased
estimate of the population eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{k}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{A}}^{{\sf H}})
, in the sense that for arbitrary deterministic
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, the eigenspace
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\sf H}
is
biased
by a factor of
(1-c\ell_{k}^{-2})/(1+c\ell_{k}^{-1})
as in (
16
).

Theorem

1
can be proven using the so-called “Deterministic Equivalent for resolvent” analysis framework.
This approach will be constantly exploited in the proof of our technical results in this paper.
We refer the interested readers to
Appendix

B
of the appendix as well as
[
5
, Chapter 2]
for a detailed discussion of this approach.

III
Inconsistency of ESPRIT for Large Arrays

In this section, we present our main result in
Theorem

2
on the large-dimensional inconsistency of classical ESPRIT in
Section

III-A
.
The proof of
Theorem

2
relies on a novel bound on eigenvalue difference between two matrices derived in
Section

III-B
, and is given in detail in
Section

III-C
.

Built upon recent advances in RMT, we perform in this section an in-depth analysis of the classical ESPRIT method in
Algorithm

1
in the large array and limited snapshot setting of
Assumption

1
.
We show that, in general, classical ESPRIT provides
inconsistent
estimates of the DoAs in the following two scenarios:
widely-spaced
DoAs and
closely-spaced
DoAs, defined respectively as follows.

Assumption 3

(Widely-spaced DoAs)
.

The DoAs
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}
are fixed as
N\to\infty
, and they have angular separation much larger than a beam-width
2\pi/N
.

Assumption 4

(Closely-spaced DoAs)
.

The DoAs
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}
are spaced with a “distance” of order
O(N^{-1})
, that is

|\theta_{k}-\theta|=O(N^{-1}),\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

(17)

for some
\theta>0
as
N\to\infty
.

In the case of closely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

4
, the DoAs are within a “distance” of order
O(N^{-1})
.
As such, for an estimate
\hat{\theta}_{k}
of the true DoA
\theta_{k}
to be distinguished from other DoAs, one must have an estimation error of order
o(N^{-1})
.
We say, in this case, that the estimate
\hat{\theta}_{k}
is
N
-consistent if
N(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})\to 0
as
N,T\to\infty
, see also
[
35
]
.
Note that this differs from the widely-spaced DoA scenario in
Assumption

3
, in which case we only need
\hat{\theta}_{k}
to be a consistent estimation of
\theta_{k}
, that is
\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k}\to 0
as
N,T\to\infty
.

The fundamental distinction between widely- and closely-spaced DoAs can be further understood from the structure of the array response.
Under
Assumption

1
and in the case of widely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

3
, we have, as
N,n,T\to\infty
at the same pace that
\|{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{I}_{K}\|=O(N^{-1})
, so that the steering matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
is (approximately for
N
large) the same as the signal subspace
{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
, and that both
{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}{\mathbf{A}}
are asymptotically diagonal.
On the contrary, in the case of closely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

4
,
{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{A}}
is no longer asymptotically identity, and
{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}{\mathbf{A}}
are no longer asymptotically diagonal.
See
Lemma

5
in
Appendix

A
of the appendix for a detailed characterization of these quantities.

III-A

Large-dimensional behavior of ESPRIT

According to
Algorithm

1
, the ESPRIT DoA estimates depend on the angles of the eigenvalues of

\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}=(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}\equiv\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{2},

(18)

where
\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}},\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{1},\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{2}
are empirical estimates of their population counterparts in (
9
).

In the following result, by studying the behavior of
\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, we provide a precise large-dimensional characterization of the classical ESPRIT method in the large array and limited snapshot regime.
The key idea and technical challenges to prove
Theorem

2
will be discussed in
Section

III-C
.

Theorem 2

(Large-dimensional behavior of ESPRIT)
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
In addition, suppose that either
Assumption

3
or
Assumption

4
is satisfied.
Define the deterministic matrix
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}=\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{1}^{-1}\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{2}
with

\begin{split}\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{1}&=\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})\mathbf{\Phi}_{1}\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})+\tau\left(\mathbf{I}_{K}-\diag(\mathbf{g})\right),\\
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{2}&=\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})\mathbf{\Phi}_{2}\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}}),\end{split}

(19)

where
\sqrt{\mathbf{g}}=[\sqrt{g_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{g_{K}}]^{\sf T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
and

g_{k}\equiv\frac{1-c\ell_{k}^{-2}}{1+c\ell_{k}^{-1}}\in(0,1).

(20)

Further assume that
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}
has distinct eigenvalues, and denote by

\lambda_{k}(\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}})
its
k
-th largest eigenvalue (in amplitude).
Let
\hat{\theta}_{k}
denote the DoA estimate obtained from the classical ESPRIT method in
Algorithm

1
, and let
\bar{\theta}_{k}\equiv\arg(\lambda_{k}(\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}))/\Delta
.
Then, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, we have

\Delta(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\bar{\theta}_{k})\to 0,

(21)

almost surely as
N,T\to\infty
.

Theorem

2
tells us that in the large
N,T
regime, the estimates
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
, due to the large-dimensional inconsistency of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
discussed in
Section

II-C
, are
biased
from their population counterparts
{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
defined in (
9
), and connect instead to
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
in (
19
).
As a direct consequence of
Theorem

2
, we have, in the case of large arrays, that ESPRIT method diverges from its original design discussed in
Section

II-A
and should in general
not
be able to provide consistent DoA estimates in neither widely- nor closely-spaced DoAs scenarios.

In the following, we discuss special cases where the aforementioned large-dimensional inconsistency of classical ESPRIT holds or, by chance, fails.
We first note that, in the limit of infinite snapshots or sufficiently high SNR, the large-dimensional corrections characterized in
Theorem

1
disappear.
Specifically, as
c=\lim N/T\to 0

or
as the SNR
\ell_{k}\to\infty
, one has
g_{k}\to 1
, which implies
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}={\bm{\Phi}}_{1}^{-1}{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}={\bm{\Phi}}
.
In this regime, the sample-based ESPRIT estimator converges to its population counterpart and yields consistent DoA estimates.
One might also conjecture that a similar consistency could be recovered in the
small-subarray
limit

\tau=\lim n/N\to 0
, since the large-dimensional bias term
\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})

in both
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{1}
and
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{2}
appears to vanish.
However, this intuition is misleading, since under both Assumptions
3
and
4
, one has
{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
, so that
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}=\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}^{-1}\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
is no longer well defined.

Beyond the limiting case discussed above, classical ESPRIT also holds consistency in the uncorrelated and widely-spaced DoAs scenario, as in the following remark.

Remark 1

(Special case: widely-spaced DoAs with uncorrelated sources)
.

For widely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

3
, assume in addition that the sources are
uncorrelated
so that
\mathbf{P}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(t)\mathbf{s}(t)^{{\sf H}}]
is diagonal.
In this case, the top-
K
subspace
{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
is approximately the same as that spanned by the steering vectors, and that
{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}=\tau\mathbf{I}_{K}+O_{\|\cdot\|}(N^{-1})
,
{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}=\tau\diag\{e^{\imath\Delta\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{K}+O_{\|\cdot\|}(N^{-1})
, so that
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}
defined in (
19
) writes

\bar{\mathbf{{\Phi}}}=\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})\diag\{e^{\imath\Delta\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{K}\diag(\sqrt{\mathbf{g}})+O_{\|\cdot\|}(N^{-1}),\vskip-1.0pt

for
\mathbf{g}
a real vector.
As such,
\bar{{\bm{\Phi}}}
has the same eigenvalue
angles
as
{\bm{\Phi}}
, and that
\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k}\to 0
almost surely.
Classical ESPRIT thus provides consistent DoA estimation in this setting.

Beyond the
small-subarray
limiting case and the special scenarios discussed in Remark
1
, where the classical ESPRIT estimates
\hat{\theta}_{k}
obtained from
Algorithm

1
are “lucky” enough to be consistent, it can be shown that
\hat{\theta}_{k}
in general deviates from the true DoA
\theta_{k}
in the large
N,T
regime.
We provide in the following two examples: widely-spaced DoAs with correlated sources and closely-spaced DoAs with equal power sources.

Remark 2

(Special case: widely-spaced DoAs with correlated sources)
.

In the case of widely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

3
, consider a general scenario where the sources are
correlated
with generic
\mathbf{P}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}(t)\mathbf{s}(t)^{{\sf H}}]
.
This is in contrast to the uncorrelated source setting in
Remark

1
.
Denote
{\mathbf{P}}={\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}\mathbf{L}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\sf H}
the eigen-decomposition of
{\mathbf{P}}
, it then follows from
Lemma

5
in
Appendix

A
of the appendix that
{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}={\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{A}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}+O_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(N^{-1})=\tau\mathbf{I}_{K}+O_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(N^{-1})
and similarly that
{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}=\tau{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\sf H}\diag\{e^{\imath\Delta\theta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{K}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}+O_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(N^{-1})
, so that

\bar{\mathbf{{\Phi}}}=\diag(\mathbf{g}){\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\sf H}\diag\{e^{\imath\Delta\theta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}\diag(\mathbf{g})+O_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(N^{-1}).

As such,
\bar{\mathbf{{\Phi}}}
has, in general, its eigenvalues different from those of
{\bm{\Phi}}
.
This, by
Theorem

2
, leads to
inconsistent
ESPRIT estimates such that
\arg(\lambda_{k}(\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}))/\Delta-\theta_{k}\nrightarrow 0
as
N,T\to\infty
.

It can be checked, in the case of
K=2
sources with different DoAs
\Delta\theta_{1}\neq\Delta\theta_{2}+m\pi
for positive integer
m
, that the classical ESPRIT estimates
cannot
be consistent
unless

{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
, that is, when the two sources are
uncorrelated
.
See
Section

C-A
in the appendix for a detailed exposition of this counterexample.

Remark 3

(Special case: closely-spaced DoAs with equal power sources)
.

In the case of closely-spaced DoAs in
Assumption

4
, we consider
K=2
sources with
\theta_{2}=\theta_{1}+\alpha/N
for some
\alpha>0
, and assume uncorrelated signals with equal powers, that is,
{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
.
It can be checked that classical ESPRIT is
not

N
-consistent in this case, that is
N(\arg(\lambda_{k}(\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}))/\Delta-\theta_{k})\nrightarrow 0
as
N,T\to\infty
, see
Section

C-B
in the appendix for a detailed proof.

III-B

A novel bound on eigenvalue difference

In this section, we first establish sufficient conditions for eigenvalue approximation to prove Theorem 2, which are summarized in
Theorem

2
.
In particular, these conditions are shown to hold for
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}
and
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}
in
Theorem

3
of
Section

III-C
, and may also be of independent interest.
We then provide the proof of
Theorem

2
.
The major technical challenge in characterizing the large-dimensional behavior of ESPRIT in
Algorithm

1
lies in the fact that the corresponding DoA estimates, which are the angles of the complex eigenvalues of the
K
-by-
K
random matrix
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}
defined in (
18
), depend on the entries of two
strongly dependent
random matrices
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}
and
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
in a non-trivial manner.
In addition, the off-diagonal complex entry of
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
in (
18
), for
i\neq j
, is given by

[\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}]_{ij}=\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}=\textstyle\sum_{m=\ell}^{n+\ell-1}\mathbf{e}_{m+\Delta}^{\sf H}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{\sf H}\mathbf{e}_{m},

(22)

and cannot be handled using standard RMT techniques.
The same holds true for
[\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}]_{ij}
, the off-diagonal entries of
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}
.
Indeed, standard RMT and contour integration techniques provide direct access to the following bilinear forms in the large
N,T\to\infty
limit,

\mathbf{a}^{\sf H}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\sf H}\mathbf{b},

(23)

for
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
of bounded norm, see for example
[
5
, Section 2.5]
.
This thus provides access to (limits of) the diagonal entries of
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}
and
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
, but
not
their off-diagonal entries.

To resolve this technical challenge, we introduce the following bound on the eigenvalue difference between two (possibly non-Hermitian) matrices, using the products of their entries with indices forming a circle.

Theorem 3

(Eigenvalue approximation between two matrices)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K\times K}
be two matrices, and assume that
{\mathbf{A}}
has only simple eigenvalues. Further assume that for every
m
-cycle of indices

1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq K
, their entries satisfy, for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

|A_{i_{1}i_{2}}A_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots A_{i_{m}i_{1}}-B_{i_{1}i_{2}}B_{i_{2}i_{3}}\ldots B_{i_{m}i_{1}}|\leq\varepsilon.

(24)

Then there exists a permutation
\Pi
on
\{1,\ldots,K\}
and a constant
C>0
, depending only on
K
and

\max\{\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|,\;\max_{i,j}|B_{ij}|\}
, such that

\bigl|\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{A}})-\lambda_{\Pi(k)}({\mathbf{B}})\bigr|\;\leq\;C\,\varepsilon^{1/K},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.

(25)

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

To prove
Theorem

3
, we work on the characteristic polynomials of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
.
It is known, e.g., from
[
10
]
that the characteristic polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K\times K}
writes

\!\!\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{K}-{\mathbf{A}})=\lambda^{K}-S_{1}({\mathbf{A}})\lambda^{K-1}\ldots+(-1)^{K}{S}_{K}({\mathbf{A}}),

(26)

for
S_{k}({\mathbf{A}})
the sum of all
k
-by-
k
principal minors of
{\mathbf{A}}
, with
S_{1}({\mathbf{A}})=\tr({\mathbf{A}})
and
S_{K}({\mathbf{A}})=\det({\mathbf{A}})
.
Note that this is a polynomial (of
\lambda
) of degree
K
, and it suffices to evaluate its coefficients (i.e., the sum of principal minors).

Consider one of the
k
-by-
k
principal minors of two matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
, denoted by
{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]
and
{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]
, respectively, with ordered indices
\mathcal{I}_{k}:1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq K
, we have, by definition of principal minor, that

\displaystyle|{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]-{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{\sigma}\sign(\sigma)\Big(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}A_{j\sigma(j)}-\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}B_{j\sigma(j)}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma}\Big|\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}A_{j\sigma(j)}-\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}B_{j\sigma(j)}\Big|,

(27)

where we denote
\sigma\colon\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\to\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
the permutations of the index set
\mathcal{I}_{k}
as in
[
10
, Section 0.3.2]
(that are
k!
distinct permutations).
It is known that every permutation
\sigma
of a finite set can be uniquely decomposed into a product of disjoint cycles (and the uniqueness is up to the order of the cycles), see for example
[
26
]
.
See
Example

1
in
Appendix

A
of the appendix for an example of such decomposition.
Then, it follows from (
27
) that

\displaystyle|{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]-{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma}\Big|\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}A_{j\sigma(j)}-\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}B_{j\sigma(j)}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\Big|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\underbrace{A_{j_{1}j_{2}}\ldots A_{j_{m_{i}}j_{1}}}_{\text{indices form an $m_{i}$-node circle}}-\prod_{i=1}^{p}B_{j_{1}j_{2}}\ldots B_{j_{m_{i}}j_{1}}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\Big|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}-\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}\Big|,

where, in the second line, we use the fact that each permutation
\sigma
can be decomposed into
1\leq p\leq k
disjoint cycles of lengths
m_{1},\ldots,m_{p}
, and we denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=A_{j_{1}j_{2}}\ldots A_{j_{m_{i}}j_{1}},\;\beta_{i}=B_{j_{1}j_{2}}\ldots B_{j_{m_{i}}j_{1}}.

Since we assume that (
24
) holds for any
m
-node circle, we have
|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}|\leq\varepsilon,i=1,\ldots,p
. Meanwhile, let
\rho\equiv\max\{\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|,\max_{i,j}|B_{ij}|\}
, then

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}|\leq\rho^{m_{i}},|\beta_{i}|\leq\rho^{m_{i}}.

Using the standard telescoping expansion for product differences, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\prod_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}-\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}\Big|=\sum_{s=1}^{p}(\alpha_{s}-\beta_{s})\Big(\prod_{i<s}\alpha_{i}\Big)\Big(\prod_{i>s}\beta_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{p}|\alpha_{s}-\beta_{s}|\Big|\prod_{i<s}\alpha_{i}\Big|\Big|\prod_{i>s}\beta_{i}\Big|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{p}\varepsilon\rho^{k-m_{s}}\leq p\rho^{k-1}\varepsilon.

Summing over all permutations yields

\displaystyle|{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]-{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]|\leq\textstyle\sum_{\sigma}p\rho^{k-1}\varepsilon\leq k!p\rho^{k-1}\varepsilon.

(28)

As such, for the
k
-th order coefficient of the characteristic polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
as in (
26
), we have

\displaystyle|S_{k}({\mathbf{A}})-S_{k}({\mathbf{B}})|=\left|\sum\nolimits_{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=k}^{\binom{K}{k}}\left|{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]-{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\binom{K}{k}\max_{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=k}\left(\left|{\mathbf{A}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]-{\mathbf{B}}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]\right|\right)\leq C_{K}\varepsilon,

for some constant
C_{K}
that only depends on
\rho
and
K
.
When
K
is fixed under Assumption 1,
C_{K}
is an absolute constant independent of
N
and
T
, and one has
|S_{k}({\mathbf{A}})-S_{k}({\mathbf{B}})|=O(\varepsilon)
.
To convert this coefficient bound into a bound on the eigenvalues, we invoke the following quantitative root-continuity theorem.

Theorem 4

(Continuity of roots of a polynomial,
[
16
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Let
f(z)
be a polynomial of degree
K
with only simple roots, whose coefficients are denoted by
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}
.
Then, there exist constants
C^{\prime}>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
, depending only on the coefficients
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}
, such that for every
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
,
whenever a polynomial
g(z)
with coefficients
\{b_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}
satisfies
|a_{i}-b_{i}|\leq\delta
for
i
, the polynomial
g(z)
also has only simple roots and, moreover, each root
r_{f}
of
f(z)
corresponds to a root
r_{g}
of
g(z)
with
|r_{f}-r_{g}|\leq C^{\prime}\,\delta^{1/K}
.

Hence, by combining the bound in (29) with Theorem 4, we obtain that each root of the characteristic polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}
admits a corresponding root (up to a permutation
\Pi(\cdot)
) of that of
{\mathbf{B}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bigl|\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{A}})-\lambda_{\Pi(k)}({\mathbf{B}})\bigr|\;\leq\;C^{\prime}(C_{K}\varepsilon)^{1/K}\;\leq\;C\,\varepsilon^{1/K},

for
k=1,\ldots,K,
and some constant
C
that depends only on
K
and
\rho
.
This completes the proof of Theorem 3.
∎

III-C

Proof of
Theorem

2

Theorem

, following the same line of arguments as that of
Theorem

3
.
The major difference is that, to prove

2
, we need to evaluate the eigenvalues of the

of two matrices
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}=\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
as in (

{\mathbf{A}}
) in

.

Following the idea of

, we provide, in the following result, characterization of the diagonal entries of
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}

and
the product of the off-diagonal entries,
when their indices form a circle
.

Theorem 5

(Large-dimensional characterization of
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}
and
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
)
.

Under the same settings and notations of
Theorem

2
, we have, for
\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
defined in (
18
), that

1.

their diagonal entries satisfy, for

,

[\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}]_{kk}-[\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1}]_{kk}\to 0,\quad[\hat{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}]_{kk}-[\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}]_{kk}\to 0,

(29)

almost surely as
N,T\to\infty
, for
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1},\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{2}
defined in (
19
);

2.

for indices
1\leq k_{1}<\ldots<k_{m}\leq K
that form an
m
-node cycle, denote
\mathbf{M}_{k_{j}}={\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}~\mbox{or}~{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{J}}_{1},j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, so that the off-diagonal entries
[\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{1}]_{k_{i}k_{j}}
or
[\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{2}]_{k_{i}k_{j}}
of

IV
Consistent DoA Estimation with G-ESPRIT

We have seen in
Theorem

2
and the discussions thereafter that classical ESPRIT in
Algorithm

1
is, in general,
incapable
of providing consistent DoA estimates in the large array and limited snapshot regime.
In this section, we present an improved approach: the generalized ESPRIT (G-ESPRIT) method that fixes the large-dimensional inconsistency of classical ESPRIT in this regime.

The G-ESPRIT method is as simple as classical ESPRIT, but with the large-dimensional “bias” terms of latter consistently estimated and removed.
Precisely, it follows from
Theorem

2
that the top subspace
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{K}
of SCM contains additional large-dimensional bias terms (in
\bar{\bm{\Phi}}
) of the form
g_{k}=\frac{1-c\ell_{k}^{-2}}{1+c\ell_{k}^{-1}}
defined in (
20
).
These quantities, for known dimension ratio
N/T
, can be consistently estimated from the SCM per the following result.

Lemma 2

(Consistent estimates of
g_{k}
)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, let
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
be the
k
th largest eigenvalue of SCM
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
,
g_{k}
be defined in (
20
),
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
Define

\hat{g}_{k}=\frac{1-\frac{N}{T}\hat{\ell}_{k}^{-2}}{1+\frac{N}{T}\hat{\ell}_{k}^{-1}},

(31)

where the estimated SNR
\hat{\ell}_{k}
is defined as

\hat{\ell}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{k}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}-1-\frac{N}{T}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{k}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}-1-\frac{N}{T}\right)^{2}-\frac{4N}{T}}\Big),

with
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
denoting the noise variance estimate
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{N-K}\sum_{i=K+1}^{N}\hat{\lambda}_{i}
.
Then,
\hat{\ell}_{k}-\ell_{k}\to 0
and
\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}\to 0
almost surely as
N,T\to\infty
.

Proof of
Lemma

2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, it follows from
Theorem

1
that
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
satisfies
\hat{\lambda}_{k}\to\sigma^{2}\!\big(1+\ell_{k}+c\frac{1+\ell_{k}}{\ell_{k}}\big)
almost surely as
N,T\to\infty
.
In practice, the true noise variance
\sigma^{2}
is not known and is replaced by the consistent estimator
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{N-K}\sum_{i=K+1}^{N}\hat{\lambda}_{i}
, which satisfies
\hat{\sigma}^{2}\!\to\!\sigma^{2}
almost surely.
Inverting the expression and using continuous mapping theorem, we have that
\hat{\ell}_{k}-\ell_{k}\to 0
.
Finally, by the definition of
g_{k}
and continuous mapping theorem, we obtain
\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}\to 0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

(Consistent DoA estimation with G-ESPRIT)

\lambda_{k}(\hat{{\bm{\Phi}}}^{G})
the
k

V-A

On the choice of subarray size
n
and distance
\Delta

The performance of ESPRIT-type algorithms depends critically on the subarray distance
\Delta
and the subarray size
n
.
To ensure unambiguous estimation,
\Delta
must satisfy the
angular unambiguity condition

-\pi\leq\Delta\theta\leq\pi
, so that the phase difference between the two subarrays remains within the
principal interval.
Accordingly, in the simulations performed in this section, we choose,

1.

in the case of widely-spaced DoAs and without prior information about the DoAs,
\Delta=1
to avoid this phase ambiguity, and
n=N-1
; and

2.

in the case of closely-spaced DoAs with a narrow field-of-view
\theta\in[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]

\Delta
is chosen close to, but strictly smaller than
\lfloor\frac{\pi}{\max_{\theta\in[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]}|\theta|}\rfloor
, so as to avoid phase ambiguity while maintaining high angular resolution.

When widely- and closely-spaced DoAs coexist, no single displacement
\Delta
is optimal for all sources.
A practical solution is to use multiple shift-invariant subarray pairs with different spacings and to fuse the resulting estimates, see also
[
12
]

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we perform a large-dimensional analysis of the classical ESPRIT DoA estimation method in the regime of large arrays and limited snapshots, where the array length
N
and the number of samples
T
are both large and comparable.
Our study covers both widely-spaced and closely-spaced DoA scenarios.
We show that while classical ESPRIT is generally
inconsistent
in these settings, this bias can be corrected using the proposed G-ESPRIT approach.
G-ESPRIT exhibits a pronounced performance advantage over conventional ESPRIT and G-MUSIC methods especially in the closely spaced DoA scenario, demonstrating its ability to resolve extremely small angular separations between sources.

From a technical perspective, we establish a novel bound on the eigenvalue differences between two possibly non-Hermitian matrices, which may be of independent interest in non-Hermitian spectral analysis and related structured matrix problems, for example, approximately gauge-equivalent weighted digraphs and gain-graph switching models. In these models, vertex gauge transformations or switching operations naturally preserve cycle products of edge weights, making cycle-product control more suitable than standard entrywise or norm-based perturbation measures in approximate settings; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
4
]
.
Numerical simulations validate the consistency of G-ESPRIT and highlight its reduced variance compared to classical ESPRIT – though a rigorous theoretical characterization of this variance reduction is beyond the scope of this paper.

It would be of future interest to extend the RMT analysis framework in this paper to characterize the (e.g., CLT-type) second-order fluctuation of both ESPRIT and G-ESPRIT, as to assess quantitatively their performance gaps from the CRB.

Appendix B
Deterministic Equivalent for Resolvent

In this section, we present the Deterministic Equivalent as a convenient technical tool to assess the asymptotic behavior of (eigenspectral) scalar observations of large random matrices.
We refer the interested readers to
[
5
, Chapter 2]
for a review.

Definition 3

(Deterministic Equivalent)
.

For Hermitian random matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, we say a deterministic matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a Deterministic Equivalent of
{\mathbf{Q}}
and denote

{\mathbf{Q}}\leftrightarrow\bar{\mathbf{Q}},

(41)

if for all deterministic matrices
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
of unit spectral and Euclidean norm, respectively, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\tr({\mathbf{A}}({\mathbf{Q}}-\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}))\to 0,\quad\mathbf{a}^{\sf H}({\mathbf{Q}}-\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}})\mathbf{b}\to 0,

almost surely as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 6

(First- and second-order Deterministic Equivalents for resolvent,
[
5
, Theorem 2.4]
)
.

For random matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times T}
having i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
entries,
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
not eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}/T
and deterministic matrix
\mathbf{B}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times N}
of bounded spectral norm, then, for the resolvent
{\mathbf{Q}}(z)=({\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}/T-z\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}
, the following deterministic equivalents hold

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1})

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow m(z_{1})\mathbf{I}_{N},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1})\mathbf{B}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2})

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow m(z_{1})m(z_{2})\mathbf{B}+\eta(z_{1},z_{2})\frac{1}{T}\tr(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{I}_{N},

with

\eta(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\sigma^{4}m^{2}(z_{1})m^{2}(z_{2})}{(1+\sigma^{2}cm(z_{1}))(1+\sigma^{2}cm(z_{2}))-c\sigma^{4}m(z_{1})m(z_{2})},

(42)

and

Proof of

The proof of Deterministic Equivalents generally comes in two steps:

1.

approximation (in a spectral norm sense) of the expectation of the random matrix model of interest; and

2.

concentration of trace and bilinear norms as in
Definition

3
around the corresponding expectations.

Here, we provide detailed derivation of the first step for the results in

7

[
5

We evaluate the expectation
\frac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}){\mathbf{Z}}]
, and that of
\frac{1}{T}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{2}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}){\mathbf{Z}}
can be derived similarly.

Consider the
(i,i)
th diagonal entry of the expectation
\frac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}){\mathbf{Z}}]
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{i}]=\frac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{i}}{(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}))(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}))}\right]+o(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}\tr({\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{2}))/T}{(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}))(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}))}+o(1)=\frac{\sigma^{2}(\frac{n}{T}m(z_{1})m(z_{2})+c\eta(z_{1},z_{2})\frac{n}{T})}{(1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{1}))(1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{2}))}+o(1)=\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})+o(1),

where we used in the second line the Woodbury identity in
Lemma

3
(the rank-one case is known as the Sherman–Morrison formula) to write

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{z}_{i}=\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}\mathbf{z}_{i}}{1+\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}\mathbf{z}_{i}}=\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}\mathbf{z}_{i}}{1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}}+o(1),

for
{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}=(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\sf H}-z\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}
independent of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
so that
\|{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}-{\mathbf{Q}}\|=O(N^{-1})
, and then
Lemma

6
in the last line.

For off-diagonal entries of
\frac{1}{T}{\mathbf{Z}}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}){\mathbf{Z}}
, we write, for
i\neq j
that by Lemmas
3
and
6
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{j}=\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-j}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{j}}{(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{1}))(1+\frac{1}{T}\tr{\mathbf{Q}}(z_{2}))}+o(1)=\frac{1}{T}\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-j}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{j}}{(1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{1}))(1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{2}))}+o(1).

Note that
\mathbf{x}_{j}
still depends on
{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}
, so we further write
{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}=({\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}+\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{j}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\sf H})^{-1}={\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}-\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{j}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}}{1+\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}\mathbf{z}_{j}}
for

that is independent of both
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-i}(z_{1}){\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-j}(z_{2})\mathbf{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{1})-\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{1})\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{j}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{1})}{1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{1})}\right)

\displaystyle\times{\mathbf{J}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{2})-\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{2})\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\sf H}{\mathbf{Q}}_{-ij}(z_{2})}{1+c\sigma^{2}m(z_{2})}\right)\mathbf{z}_{j}+o(1),

\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j}
