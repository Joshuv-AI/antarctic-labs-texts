Dynamic multi-strategy Grey Wolf optimizer and its applications
Authors: Yueqi Wang, Jiazhi Song, Haiyan Zhao, Jingqing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the shortcomings of the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) in solution accuracy, convergence speed, and search capability, this paper proposes a Dynamic Multi-Strategy Grey Wolf Optimizer (DMSGWO). Based on GWO, DMSGWO introduces four improvement strategies. First, a nonlinear convergence factor strategy is adopted to better balance global exploration and local exploitation. Second, a population dynamic grouping strategy is employed to dynamically adjust the population sizes of exploration and exploitation groups during the iteration process. Third, a random position update strategy is applied to the exploration group to enhance the global exploration capability. Finally, an adaptive perturbation position update strategy is applied to the exploitation group to improve the local exploitation capability. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of DMSGWO, it is compared with GWO, four other swarm intelligence optimization algorithms, and four other improved GWO algorithms on 23 benchmark test functions and the CEC2022 test suite. The comparative results demonstrate that DMSGWO exhibits excellent solution accuracy, stability, and convergence speed. Further validation through Friedman ranking tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirms that DMSGWO ranks first in overall performance. The test results show statistically significant performance differences from the other algorithms on most test functions, and further verify the superior performance of DMSGWO. To validate the practical application value of DMSGWO, it is applied to two typical engineering design optimization problems and WSN coverage optimization problem alongside the comparative algorithms. The results show that DMSGWO can effectively handle constraints and achieve the best objective values. Thus, these results demonstrates the effectiveness and practicality of DMSGWO in solving real-world complex application problems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54428-w.

Introduction

Swarm Intelligence (SI) is a kind of meta-heuristic algorithms that solve optimization problems by simulating the collective behaviors observed in nature. These algorithms originate from observations of the phenomena and rules governing the behavior of biological groups in nature. They emphasize local interactions and information sharing among individuals and achieve global optimization through self-organization and adaptive mechanisms. Classic algorithms such as the Genetic Algorithm (GA)1, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)2, and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)3 have achieved significant results. In recent years, with a deeper understanding of natural behaviors, new swarm intelligence algorithms such as the Artificial Bee Colony Algorithm (ABC)4, Firefly Algorithm (FA)5, Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)6, and Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA)7 have been successively proposed and have demonstrated excellent performance in solving complex optimization problems.

Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)8 was proposed by Mirjalili et al. in 2014. Inspired by the social hierarchy and cooperative hunting behavior of grey wolves9, it has gained widespread attention due to its good convergence, few parameters, and easy implementation. In GWO, the convergence factor a linearly decreases from 2 to 0 to regulate the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation10. This linear change cannot flexibly adapt to different optimization stages and results in weaker search capabilities. Furthermore, during position updates in each iteration, the algorithm assigns equal weight to the three leading wolves. All individuals follow the same update strategy without establishing a clear collaboration mechanism between exploration and exploitation11. This makes the algorithm prone to premature convergence or insufficient exploitation when facing complex problems12. To address limitations such as low solution accuracy, slow convergence, and being easily trapped in local optima, researchers have proposed various improvements13. Focusing on enhancing algorithm performance, researchers mainly improve GWO from two directions. The first is designing a non-linear convergence factor to more precisely regulate the search behavior of the algorithm at different iteration stages, thereby better balancing exploration and exploitation. The second is improving the position update formula to enhance the algorithm’s search capability.

Regarding improvements to the convergence factor: Yihui Qiu et al.14 introduced a nonlinear convergence strategy. However, a single nonlinear convergence factor mainly varies with iterations and lacks real-time feedback on population stagnation and individual fitness differences. Hongbo Wang et al.15 redefined the linear parameter and combined crossover and mutation strategies to enhance the balance between exploration and exploitation. However, crossover and mutation have multiple combination forms, requiring appropriate selections for different tasks, and a unified dynamic adaptive grouping mechanism is absent.

Regarding improvements to the position update formula: Jiashuo Chen et al.16 proposed a random position update formula to reduce the wolves’ dependence on leader wolves and increase diversity, but this may cause convergence oscillations in later stages. Yufei Zhang et al.17 integrated DE-inspired mutation operators, evolutionary population dynamics, and nonlinear population size reduction into GWO, allowing the mutation process to switch between exploration and exploitation modes based on iteration probability. However, the algorithm primarily improves position updates through mutation, repositioning, and population size reduction, without directly constructing exploitation-stage perturbations based on individual fitness differences.

Although many GWO variants have been proposed to improve the original algorithm, several limitations remain insufficiently addressed. First, the linear convergence factor in GWO cannot flexibly regulate the transition between global exploration and local exploitation, which may lead to premature convergence in complex search spaces. Second, most GWO variants still update all individuals using similar leader-guided mechanisms, resulting in limited population diversity and insufficient functional differentiation among individuals. Third, although some multi-strategy GWO variants integrate multiple improvement strategies, the interaction among these strategies is often not explicitly coordinated. Therefore, the main research question of this study is how to construct a coordinated GWO framework that can dynamically balance global exploration and local exploitation. To address this question, this paper proposes a Dynamic Multi-Strategy Grey Wolf Optimizer (DMSGWO). Unlike existing GWO variants that simply combine multiple strategies, DMSGWO is designed as a coordinated and dynamically adjusted search framework. Its novelty does not lie in the independent originality of each individual strategy, but in the coordinated integration of dynamic population-level task division and nonlinear convergence regulation. Specifically, DMSGWO dynamically assigns exploration and exploitation tasks according to the change in the population best solution, while the nonlinear convergence factor regulates the transition between global exploration and local exploitation. Through this mechanism, DMSGWO establishes a clearer collaboration between global exploration and local exploitation. The main contributions are as follows:

Grey Wolf Optimizer

Inspiration

GWO is inspired by the social hierarchy of grey wolves18. In nature, they form stable packs of 5 to 12 individuals with a strict hierarchy19, as shown in Fig. 1. Based on the different roles during hunts, they can be classified into four levels: the α wolf serve as leaders and primary decision-makers; the β wolf assist in decision-making and maintain order; the δ wolf rank below the former two but lead the ω wolf and the ordinary wolves20. This hierarchy dynamically adjusts based on individual conditions, ability, and social interactions, which reflects natural collective intelligence built on leadership, cooperation, and feedback.

GWO is also inspired by the hunting behavior of grey wolves21. When hunting, grey wolves follow three key steps: chasing, encircling, and attacking. The coordinated and adaptive strategies wolves employ during this process provide a biological model for GWO.

Nonlinear convergence factor strategy

As a swarm intelligence optimization method, the performance of GWO depends on the regulation of the convergence factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document} largely29. In GWO, the convergence factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document} decreases linearly from 2 to 0 as the iteration number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} increases30. Although this linear decrease method is simple and intuitive to implement, its relatively rapid decline in the early stage of iteration can cause the algorithm to shift from the global exploration phase to the local exploitation phase prematurely. The consequence of this is that the algorithm does not search the solution space sufficiently, easily converge to local optima prematurely, thereby reducing the possibility of finding the global optimum31. To address this issue, scholars have focused on dynamically adjusting the convergence factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document} to better balance the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation capabilities in recent years, which has become one of the key research directions for improving GWO32. Therefore, DMSGWO designs a nonlinear convergence factor as shown in Eq. (9). Its variation trend illustrated in Fig. 4. Compared with that of GWO, the proposed nonlinear convergence factor decreases slowly in the early stage and rapidly in the later stage. This design maintains strong global exploration capability in the early stage, allowing thorough traversal of different regions of the solution space and avoiding premature convergence to local optima. In the later stage, it accelerates the decrease, prompting the algorithm to focus on promising regions and enhancing local fine-grained search, thereby achieving a better balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Population dynamic grouping strategy

In GWO, all individuals follow the same position update formula, without establishing a clear collaborative mechanism for exploration and exploitation within the population. The balance between exploration and exploitation relies solely on the single parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document}, which can easily lead the algorithm to trap in local optima or converge slowly when tackling complex optimization problems. To more precisely and proactively coordinate exploration and exploitation, DMSGWO introduces the population dynamic grouping strategy. This strategy dynamically divides all individuals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} into an exploration group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{1}$$\end{document} and an exploitation group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{2}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N=N}_{1}+{N}_{2}$$\end{document}). The exploration group focuses on broad searches to identify potentially optimal regions and enhance the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima. And the exploitation group concentrates on fine-grained refinement around current better solutions to improve convergence accuracy and search efficiency. The sizes of the two groups are not fixed. Instead, their proportional scale is adaptively adjusted through iterative feedback to make the population structure more flexible.

The specific working mechanism is as follows: Firstly, the population is divided after random initialization such that the top 50% of individuals are assigned to the exploration group and the bottom 50% to the exploitation group. This setting allocates the same population size to exploration and exploitation at the beginning of the optimization process, thereby avoiding an initial bias toward either search behavior. Meanwhile, the two groups execute completely different position update formulas to ensure the efficiency of the grouping operation and assign distinct search tasks to each group. During iterations, the group sizes are not static but are dynamically adjusted after each iteration based on changes in the fitness value of the optimal solution. Under normal conditions, DMSGWO gradually reduces the exploration group size while increasing the exploitation group size to enhance local search capability and improve convergence accuracy. Moreover, if the best fitness remains unchanged for three consecutive iterations, the population is considered likely trapped in a local optimum, enabling timely response to stagnation. The three-iteration criterion is used because one or two unchanged iterations may only reflect normal temporary pauses, while waiting for more iterations would delay adjustments. In such cases, the exploration group size is incrementally increased while the exploitation group size is decreased to enhance the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima. Through this strategy, DMSGWO can adaptively adjust the size ratio between exploration and exploitation groups at different iteration stages.

Random position update strategy for exploration group

In GWO, individual position updates rely entirely on the combined guidance of the three leading wolves. However, in complex optimization problems, such guided convergence can easily lead to premature population clustering and entrapment in local optima. To enhance the algorithm’s global search capability, DMSGWO designs a random position update strategy for individuals within the exploration group. The position update direction in this strategy is determined either by a Lévy flight perturbation based on the optimal solution or by a random individual differential perturbation. This approach reduces reliance on the three leading wolves and enables more effective exploration of potential optimal solution regions.

During each iteration, DMSGWO uses a probability threshold of 0.5 to randomly select one of two methods from Eq. (10) for each individual in the exploration group. This threshold gives the two position update directions equal probabilities, preventing the algorithm from biasing toward either one. If the randomly generated probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p>0.5$$\end{document}, the individual performs a Lévy flight perturbation update. The update direction is perturbed based on the difference between the current individual’s position and the position of the optimal α wolf to explore regions distinct from the current best solution. Lévy flight exhibits heavy-tailed characteristics. In most cases, it generates short step lengths, which are beneficial for fine-grained searches near the individual. Occasionally, it produces extremely long jump steps, which allow the individual to achieve long-distance leaps. This enhances the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima and explores unknown regions significantly. If the random probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\le\:0.5$$\end{document}, the individual adopts a random individual differential perturbation update. This mechanism randomly selects two individuals from the current entire population and uses their positional difference as the update direction. The individual’s position update direction is entirely determined by these two randomly chosen individuals, which make the search direction highly randomized and free from attraction to the current optimal solution region. This effectively promotes random information exchange among different individuals within the population, thereby increasing population diversity.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X_{new}}(t+1)=\left\{ \begin{gathered} X(t)+rand \cdot \left( {X(t) - {X_\alpha }(t)} \right) \cdot Levy(s){\text{ , }}p>0.5 \hfill \\ X(t)+rand \cdot \left( {{X_1}(t) - {X_2}(t)} \right){\text{ , }}otherwise \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

Here, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:rand$$\end{document} are random numbers within the interval [0, 1]. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{\alpha\:}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denotes the position of the current optimal individual, the α wolf. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{1}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}_{2}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} represent the positions of two individuals randomly selected from the current population. The step size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document} of Lévy flight follows the distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{L}\stackrel{{\prime }}{\mathrm{e}}\mathrm{vy}\left(\beta\:\right)$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} set to 1.5. This value is commonly used in swarm intelligence optimization because it can generate a typical heavy-tailed search pattern, in which most steps are short-distance moves and a few steps are long-distance jumps. Therefore, β = 1.5 helps maintain local search continuity while preserving the ability to escape local optima and explore distant regions of the search space. Adaptive perturbation position update strategy for exploitation group

In GWO, individual position updates assign equal weight to the three leading wolves, causing all individuals to converge toward the same central region. This does not allow for differentiated fine-grained searches based on the varying quality of different individuals’ positions within the search space. Additionally, exploitation behavior is entirely controlled by the convergence factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document}. It lacks adaptive adjustment capabilities based on the search state. Once the population falls into a local optimum early in the iteration, it becomes difficult to rely on internal driving forces to actively escape. To overcome these limitations, DMSGWO designs an adaptive perturbation position update strategy for individuals in the exploitation group. This strategy follows the cooperative hunting approach of GWO, where the three leading wolves guide the calculation of an essential target position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X$$\end{document} using Eq. (8). This ensures that the algorithm inherits the fundamental cooperative pack-hunting framework of grey wolves. Building upon this, the strategy designs a relative fitness factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varphi\:$$\end{document} and an adaptive perturbation factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w$$\end{document}, as shown in Eqs. (12) and (13), respectively. Finally, the adaptive perturbation position update formula for the exploitation group is derived as Eq. (14).12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi ={\left( {\frac{{{f_i}}}{{{f_{ave}}}}} \right)^3}$$\end{document}13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w=0.5+0.5\cos \left( {\pi \cdot \frac{t}{T}} \right)$$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X(t+1)=(1 - w) \cdot X+w \cdot X \cdot \varphi$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the fitness value of the current individual, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{\mathrm{avg}}$$\end{document} denotes the average fitness value of the population. The relative fitness factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varphi\:$$\end{document} enables differentiated searches among individuals. For individuals with poorer fitness, it amplifies the magnitude of their position updates. This encourages larger leaps to potentially escape local optima. Conversely, for individuals with better fitness, it reduces their update step size for stable exploitation and finer refinement around high-quality solutions. The adaptive perturbation factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w$$\end{document} varies adaptively with the iteration number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} through a cosine function. In the early stage, a larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w$$\end{document} allows the differentiated perturbation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X\bullet\:\varphi\:$$\end{document} to dominate. This allows the exploitation group to maintain strong exploration and escape capabilities during local search. The algorithm gradually shifts toward a cooperative hunting mode primarily guided by the three leading wolves. This dynamically balances the weights between the traditional position update \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X$$\end{document} and the differentiated perturbation update \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X\bullet\:\varphi\:$$\end{document}. Pseudocode of DMSGWO

To enhance the search and optimization capabilities of GWO, this paper builds upon the GWO framework and incorporates four improvement strategies to construct DMSGWO. The framework of the four improvement strategies is illustrated in Fig. 5.

DMSGWO first randomly initializes a population of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} individuals and evenly divides the initial population into an exploration group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{1}$$\end{document} and an exploitation group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{2}$$\end{document}. Then, the iterative loop begins. The fitness values of all individuals in the current population are calculated. Based on these fitness values, the three best individuals are selected and updated. Next, the nonlinear convergence factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a$$\end{document} is computed, and group-based position updates are performed. The individuals in the exploration group undergo the random position update strategy, while those in the exploitation group undergo the adaptive perturbation position update strategy. Under normal circumstances, the algorithm gradually reduces the number of individuals in the exploration group and increases the number in the exploitation group. However, if the optimal solution remains unchanged for three consecutive iterations, a reverse adjustment is triggered to adjust the population structure dynamically. Finally, when the iteration number reaches the preset maximum, the algorithm exits the loop and records the global optimal solution. Experimental design and result analysis

Experimental setup

Comparative algorithms and test functions

To validate the performance of DMSGWO, it is compared with GWO, four other swarm intelligence optimization algorithms and four other improved GWO algorithms: Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)6, Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO)34, Black-winged Kite Algorithm (BKA)35, LSHADE_EpSin36, MSGWO37, ISGWO38, BAGWO39, MYIGWO40. Experimental environment and parameters

To ensure a fair comparison of all algorithms, the experiments were conducted using MATLAB R2024a on a computer with an 13th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i7-13650HX (2.60 GHz) processor. The parameters were consistent across all runs: a population size of 30 and a maximum of 500 iterations. Each algorithm was executed independently 30 times to reduce randomness. The optimal value (Best) was recorded to represent the theoretical optimal performance of the algorithm. The average value (Avg) was used to measure the overall optimization accuracy and average convergence level. The standard value (Std) was employed to evaluate the stability and robustness of the algorithm. The best results are highlighted in bold.

Ablation experiments and result analysis

To verify the effectiveness of the four proposed improvement strategies and further investigate the internal design mechanism of the algorithm, ablation experiments were conducted in 30 dimensions to clearly demonstrate the contribution of each strategy. DMSGWO_1 denotes the variant that only introduces the nonlinear convergence factor. DMSGWO_23 incorporates the dynamic population grouping strategy and applies the random position update strategy only to the exploration group. DMSGWO_24 incorporates the dynamic population grouping strategy and applies the adaptive perturbation position update strategy only to the exploitation group. DMSGWO_123 adds the nonlinear convergence factor to DMSGWO_23, while DMSGWO_124 adds the nonlinear convergence factor to DMSGWO_24. DMSGWO_234 introduces dynamic population grouping, applies random position update strategy to the exploration group, and applies adaptive perturbation position update strategy to the exploitation group.

According to the ablation results in Table 1, each improvement strategy achieves a significant performance enhancement compared with the original GWO. DMSGWO_1, which only introduces the nonlinear convergence factor, improves the performance of GWO on most functions, indicating that this strategy can better balance exploration and exploitation and alleviate the premature convergence caused by the original linear convergence factor. However, its improvement is still limited, suggesting that the optimization capability of a single strategy has inherent limitations. The comparisons of DMSGWO_123 with DMSGWO_23, DMSGWO_124 with DMSGWO_24, and DMSGWO with DMSGWO_234 further demonstrate the positive contribution of the nonlinear convergence factor to algorithm performance. The results of DMSGWO_23 show that the random position update strategy for the exploration group can improve the algorithm’s performance on multimodal functions, especially on fixed-dimensional multimodal functions, where it exhibits strong global search ability and effectively escapes local optima. However, its local exploitation capability on unimodal functions remains insufficient. In contrast, DMSGWO_24 shows that the adaptive perturbation position update strategy for the exploitation group provides high solution accuracy and stability on unimodal functions and some multimodal functions, although its exploration diversity may be limited. DMSGWO_234 compensates for the limitations of DMSGWO_23 and DMSGWO_24 and achieves a substantial performance improvement on most functions. This indicates that targeted improvements to both the exploration and exploitation groups can generate a strong synergistic effect, enabling the algorithm to possess both strong global exploration capability and refined local exploitation ability.

To verify the significance of each strategy in DMSGWO, a Friedman rank test was conducted based on the results of 30 independent runs of all algorithms to evaluate their overall differences. The Friedman test was used to comprehensively analyze the performance of all algorithms on 23 benchmark functions at a 5% significance level. Box plot analysis

Figure 8, Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Fig. S3 show the boxplot results of DMSGWO and nine comparison algorithms on benchmark functions with 30, 100 and 500 dimensions. Each boxplot is based on the best fitness values from 30 independent runs. For minimization problems, a lower box position indicates higher accuracy, while a shorter box indicates better stability13. As shown in the figure, DMSGWO achieves lower median values and relatively compact box ranges on most benchmark functions, indicating that the algorithm can obtain high-quality solutions stably across 30 independent runs. As the problem dimension increases from 30 to 100 and 500, some comparison algorithms exhibit significantly wider boxes and more outliers, suggesting a decline in their stability in high-dimensional search spaces. In contrast, DMSGWO maintains a compact result distribution and low fitness values on most functions, demonstrating good dimensional scalability and robustness. Furthermore, for the CEC2022 test suite, Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. S4 provide boxplot results under 10 and 20 dimensions, further validating the comprehensive performance of DMSGWO. Overall, DMSGWO exhibits competitive convergence accuracy, stability, and robustness on the CEC2022 test suite. Compared with most competing algorithms, the boxes of DMSGWO is generally located in the low-fitness region and has a shorter length, indicating that it can stably obtain superior solutions across multiple independent runs. When the dimension increases from 10 to 20, although all algorithms are affected by the high-dimensional search space, DMSGWO does not always produce the lowest box position on every CEC2022 function. Nevertheless, its overall boxplot distribution indicates competitive robustness and dimensional scalability across different dimensions.

Convergence analysis

Convergence curves serve as a visual tool for evaluating algorithm search performance and convergence speed47. Figure 10 presents the convergence curves of DMSGWO and comparison algorithms on 23 benchmark functions in 30 dimensions, with local magnification for selected functions. On most unimodal functions, especially \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{3}$$\end{document}, DMSGWO exhibits faster convergence and higher final accuracy than most comparison algorithms. In particular, DMSGWO reaches stable convergence within the early iterations on several functions, which can be attributed to the nonlinear convergence factor strategy. This strategy enables the algorithm to maintain sufficient global exploration in the early stage and gradually strengthen local exploitation in the later stage. For multimodal functions, DMSGWO shows clear convergence advantages on several representative functions, especially \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{8}\sim{F}_{11}$$\end{document}, indicating that the random position update strategy improves the ability of the algorithm to escape local optima and search promising regions. Meanwhile, on fixed-dimensional multimodal functions, DMSGWO also maintains stable convergence behavior on most functions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the dynamic coordination between exploration and exploitation. The convergence curves for the 100- and 500-dimensional cases are provided in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Fig. S6. These results further show that DMSGWO can maintain competitive convergence speed and final accuracy as the problem dimension increases, although it does not achieve the best performance on every single function. As shown in Fig. 11 and Supplementary Fig. S7, DMSGWO also exhibits competitive convergence performance on the CEC2022 test suite for 10 and 20 dimensions. Its convergence curves generally decrease rapidly in the early iterations and continue to improve steadily in the middle and later stages on several functions, indicating that the algorithm can effectively coordinate global exploration and local exploitation in complex search spaces. Compared with some algorithms that show slow convergence or stagnation on certain CEC2022 functions, DMSGWO demonstrates stronger overall search robustness. However, DMSGWO does not always obtain the lowest final fitness value on every CEC2022 function. For example, on some functions such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{5}$$\end{document}, BAGWO, LSHADE_EpSin, or MYIGWO can achieve comparable or slightly better final accuracy. Therefore, the results indicate that DMSGWO has strong overall convergence performance and dimensional scalability, rather than absolute superiority on all individual functions. Overall, DMSGWO shows a stable convergence process and strong comprehensive optimization performance across benchmark functions and the CEC2022 test suite.

Applications of DMSGWO to engineering design optimization problems

Experimental setup

Engineering design optimization refers to the systematic mathematical methods to identify the optimal set of design parameters that satisfy all specified engineering constraints, thereby maximizing or minimizing a particular performance metric (such as cost, weight, or volume)48. To validate the practical application value of the proposed DMSGWO algorithm, it is tested on two engineering design optimization problems: tension/compression spring design, speed reducer design, three-bar truss design and multiple disk clutch brake design. The comparative algorithms and experimental environment remain the same as in Section “Experimental Design and Result Analysis”, with a population size of 50, a maximum of 500 iterations, and each algorithm is independently run 200 times. The best results are highlighted in bold.

Application of DMSGWO to WSN coverage optimization problem

Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) are distributed sensing networks featuring low power consumption, low cost, wide coverage, and functional integration53. They are widely used in smart healthcare, urban traffic management, agricultural monitoring, and other fields, and are considered a key technology for future human life54. However, sensor nodes have limited energy and are often randomly scattered in harsh monitoring environments, leading to coverage holes in some areas and excessive overlap in others. This degrades both monitoring quality and network reliability55. Therefore, adaptive deployment of nodes to improve distribution uniformity and increase coverage is critical56.

The 2D WSN coverage optimization problem is defined as follows57. In a monitoring area of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M\times\:N$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n$$\end{document} sensor nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S=\left\{{s}_{1},\:{s}_{2},\cdots\:,{s}_{n}\right\}$$\end{document}are randomly deployed, each with the same sensing radius \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R$$\end{document}, to maximize coverage. To simplify the problem, the area is discretized into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M\times\:N$$\end{document} grids, with each grid center as a target point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O=\left\{{o}_{1},\:{o}_{2},\cdots\:,{o}_{M\times\:N}\right\}$$\end{document}. Conclusion

This paper conducts systematic research on the performance improvement of the GWO and its application expansion. Firstly, to address the shortcomings of the standard GWO, DMSGWO is proposed by integrating four innovative improvement strategies. Secondly, in terms of algorithm performance, DMSGWO is evaluated using 23 benchmark test functions and the authoritative CEC2022 test suite. Experimental results demonstrate that DMSGWO exhibits significant advantages in solution accuracy, convergence speed, and stability compared to other comparative algorithms. DMSGWO ranks first in the overall algorithm ranking. Finally, in terms of application expansion, DMSGWO is applied to two typical engineering design optimization problems and WSN coverage optimization problem. These results verify its effectiveness and practicality in constrained optimization problems. Future work can further optimize the algorithm and expand its application to other types of optimization problem domains.
