Genetic and clinical characterization of two families with severe venous thromboembolism due to nonsense mutations in the SERPINC1 gene
Authors: Xuqian Wei, Weibin Chen, Jian Zhang, Jianhua Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntithrombin (AT) III is a key physiological anticoagulant, and its hereditary deficiency represents one of the most severe forms of inherited thrombophilia. However, as a rare disorder, AT III deficiency is frequently underdiagnosed due to the limitations of current clinical algorithms. In this study, we describe two cases of hereditary AT III deficiency accompanied by multiple venous thromboembolic events: a 39-year-old male with extensive lower limb deep venous thrombosis (DVT) involving the iliac, femoral, and popliteal veins, and a 21-year-old female with intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism (PE). Laboratory evaluation revealed reduced AT III activity levels of 51.9% and 52.7%, respectively. Quantitative ELISA analysis further confirmed a corresponding reduction in AT antigen levels. Both patients showed suboptimal responses to initial low-molecular-weight heparin treatment but responded favorably to oral rivaroxaban. Genetic testing identified two nonsense mutations in the SERPINC1 gene: NM_000488.4:c.906dupT (p.Glu303Ter), a previously unreported variant, and NM_000488.4:c.481 C > T (p.Arg161Ter), reported here for the first time in an Asian individual. Family analyses confirmed that the variants were inherited from the proband’s parents, who had a history of venous thromboembolism (VTE). These findings underscore the importance of assessing AT activity in patients with unexplained thrombotic events, particularly at a young age, and support the use of genetic testing to guide personalized anticoagulation strategies in AT III deficiency.Keywords: Antithrombin III, Venous thromboembolism, SERPINC1 gene, Nonsense mutation, Anticoagulation therapy

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), which includes deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), ranks among the top five most common vascular disorders, increasing the risk of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. VTE arises from a complex interaction between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers [1–3]. While inherited risk factors such as the factor V Leiden (FV R506Q; rs6025) mutation and the prothrombin G20210A (PTGM; rs1799963) polymorphism are common in Western populations [1, 4], they are relatively rare in East Asians [5, 6].

Deficiencies in natural anticoagulant proteins—particularly antithrombin (AT), protein C, and protein S—are increasingly recognized as important contributors to venous thromboembolism (VTE) in Asian populations, where they occur more frequently than common Western mutations such as factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A [5, 7–9]. Among these, AT (also referred to as AT III) plays a central role in regulating the coagulation cascade. As a member of the serpin superfamily, AT is a serine protease inhibitor whose anticoagulant activity is markedly enhanced by glycosaminoglycans, particularly heparin and heparan sulfate [10–13]. Once activated, AT irreversibly inhibits key proteases in both the intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation pathways, including thrombin (factor IIa), factor Xa, and, to a lesser extent, factors VIIa, IXa, and XIa [10, 14–17].

Hereditary AT deficiency is a rare but highly thrombogenic disorder, recognized as one of the most severe inherited thrombophilias [18]. Individuals with this deficiency are at a substantially increased risk for thrombotic events, especially when additional risk factors such as surgery, trauma, prolonged immobilization, hospitalization, aging, long-haul travel, hormone replacement therapy, or oral contraceptive use are present [6]. Although its prevalence is low, the clinical impact of AT deficiency is profound due to its strong association with life-threatening VTE events [19].

The SERPINC1 gene, located on chromosome 1q25.1 and spanning over 13 kb, encodes the AT protein. It comprises 7 exons and 6 introns [20, 21]. Mutations in SERPINC1 leading to AT deficiency are genetically diverse, including point mutations, large deletions, and nonsense variants, all of which have been documented in patients with recurrent VTE [22]. To date, more than 500 pathogenic variants associated with type I and type II AT deficiencies have been cataloged in the Human Gene Mutation Database (http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk) [22, 23]. Type I deficiency, the more severe form, is typically caused by nonsense or frameshift mutations that reduce or eliminate AT protein expression. In contrast, type II deficiency results from missense mutations that impair AT’s functional activity without significantly affecting its plasma concentration [23].

In this study, we describe two unrelated families affected by severe VTE due to novel nonsense mutations in SERPINC1. One mutation (NM_000488.4:c.906dupT, p.Glu303Ter) is previously unreported, while the other (NM_000488.4:c.481 C > T, p.Arg161Ter) is identified for the first time in an Asian individual. By integrating clinical and genetic data, our findings expand the mutational spectrum of SERPINC1 and reinforce the importance of genetic screening in patients with unexplained or familial VTE.

Subject

Family 1

The first case involved a 39-year-old man diagnosed in 2023 with extensive DVT of the lower extremity. During his hospitalization, multiple emboli in the right main pulmonary artery and bilateral distal branches were identified. Genetic screening of immediate family members—including his father, mother, and son—revealed that both his father and son carried the same SERPINC1 mutation. Notably, his father had experienced DVT in the lower limb at the age of 54.

Family 2

The second patient was a 21-year-old woman who developed intermediate- to high-risk PE in 2023 following approximately two months of drospirenone–ethinylestradiol (Busta) use. During treatment, she also suffered DVT in the lower limb. Genetic testing revealed that her mother, but not her father, carried the same SERPINC1 mutation. Her mother had previously experienced DVT in the lower extremity at age 37, which had also followed contraceptive use. The patient is currently unmarried and has no children.

Clinical course and treatment

Patient 1

In June 2023, a 39-year-old male presented to the Department of Vascular Surgery at Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital with severe swelling of the left lower limb for three days. The initial evaluation revealed markedly elevated D-dimer levels. Ultrasonography revealed extensive DVT involving the left posterior tibial, popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins (Fig. 1). Due to a history of mild, transient chest tightness, pulmonary artery computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed multiple emboli in the right main pulmonary artery and bilateral distal branches (Fig. 2). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) via popliteal vein puncture confirmed thrombus extension from the left popliteal to the common iliac vein.

Vascular ultrasound image of the lower extremities in patient 1. Ultrasonography showed extensive DVT involving the left posterior tibial, popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins of patient 1

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) image of patient 1. CTA obtained at the time of admission for patient 1, revealing multiple pulmonary emboli in the right main pulmonary artery and bilateral distal branches

An inferior vena cava filter was placed for thromboembolic prophylaxis, followed by percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy using the AngioJet system. And the filter was removed at the seventh month after placement. The procedure achieved near-complete thrombus removal from the left popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins (Figs. 3 and 4), resulting in substantial improvement in lower extremity edema. The patient received standard postoperative anticoagulation with subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) (Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg, q12h) during the two-day inpatient period, followed by oral rivaroxaban for long-term management.

DSA image before thrombectomy of patient 1. It showed extensive thrombus from the left popliteal vein to the common iliac vein of patient 1

DSA image after thrombectomy of patient 1. It showed near-complete thrombus removal from the left popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins of patient 1

At the three-month follow-up, the patient showed no residual swelling. Ultrasound confirmed the resolution of thrombi in the popliteal and posterior tibial veins, but the recurrence of a thrombus in the left superficial femoral vein. We suspect that the recurrence occurred within two days post-thrombectomy may be due to suboptimal anticoagulation, likely related to reduced AT III activity (47%) and the limited efficacy of LMWH under such conditions. Given the absence of clinical symptoms, rivaroxaban 20 mg was continued once daily with close monitoring.

At both six- and twelve-month follow-ups, the thrombus in the superficial femoral vein persisted without progression, and no new thrombotic events were observed. Due to the confirmed SERPINC1 nonsense mutation and persistently reduced AT III activity, lifelong anticoagulation was recommended. One year postoperatively, the dosage of rivaroxaban was adjusted to 15 mg once daily for long-term secondary VTE prevention, deviating from the standard 10 mg due to the patient’s AT deficiency and inherent predisposition to VTE.

Patient 2

In September 2023, a 21-year-old woman presented to the Department of Cardiology at Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital with one week of sudden-onset chest tightness and palpitations. She had no clear precipitating factors. Initial labs showed elevated D-dimer levels, and CTA revealed PE involving the distal main pulmonary artery and its bilateral branches (Fig. 5). Anticoagulation with LMWH was initiated immediately. Due to moderate hypoxemia, the patient was classified as intermediate-high risk and was treated with intravenous alteplase per standard thrombolysis protocols.

Contrast-enhanced CTA image of patient 2. Contrast-enhanced CTA performed upon admission of patient 2, showing multiple pulmonary emboli located in the distal main pulmonary artery and its bilateral branches

One day post-thrombolysis, she developed mild swelling in the left calf, with a 3 cm increase in circumference compared to the contralateral side. Doppler ultrasound confirmed extensive DVT involving the left iliac, femoral, popliteal, and peroneal veins (Fig. 6). However, no vascular surgery consultation was obtained at the time. The patient remained on LMWH therapy, which failed to achieve adequate anticoagulation. Her AT III activity was later found to be 50.1%, indicating a hypercoagulable state that likely contributed to the ineffectiveness of LMWH. Rivaroxaban therapy was initiated four days later, just before discharge.

Vascular ultrasound image of the lower extremities in patient 2. Ultrasonography showed extensive DVT involving the left iliac, femoral, popliteal, and peroneal veins of patient 2

Fortunately, there was no further progression of the PE or lower limb thrombosis during this interval. Following the switch to rivaroxaban, anticoagulation was more effective. At three months, ultrasound showed complete resolution of thrombi in both the pulmonary arteries and lower limb veins. No recurrence of thromboembolic events was detected at six- and twelve-month follow-ups.

Genetic analysis revealed that both the patient and her mother carried the same SERPINC1 nonsense mutation. The mother’s history of DVT at age 37 was similarly associated with contraceptive use. The father tested negative for the mutation.

Inferior vena cava filter placement procedure

Following successful regional anesthesia, the common femoral vein on the unaffected side was punctured, and a vascular sheath was placed. Angiography confirmed unobstructed inferior vena cava blood flow and identified the location of the renal vein. Subsequently, under DSA guidance, the filter was advanced along the guidewire and deployed in the inferior vena cava below the renal vein.

Laboratory examinations and etiological investigation

All procedures of this study were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. And this work was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (2025-KY-123). In addition to standard coagulation profiles conducted during hospitalization, both patients underwent an extensive panel of thrombophilia-related tests after recovering from the acute phase of VTE and discontinuing their respective anticoagulants for the guideline-recommended durations: vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for at least two weeks; direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) for at least 2 days (preferably longer); and unfractionated heparin (UFH) or LMWH for more than 24 h [24]. These assessments included AT activity, protein C (PC) and protein S (PS) activity, levels of various clotting factors, von Willebrand factor (vWF) activity, lupus anticoagulants, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies, and plasma homocysteine concentration (Table 1).

Phenotypic analysis revealed decreased AT III activity in both patients. Subsequent direct sequencing of the SERPINC1 gene identified pathogenic variants. To further elucidate the genetic basis of the observed thrombophilia, we conducted a retrospective screening of immediate family members.

Methods

Measurement of AT III activity

AT III activity was quantified using a chromogenic assay (Berichrom Antithrombin III (A) Kit, Siemens) on a Sysmex CS-5100 automated coagulation analyzer, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification of AT III antigen by ELISA

The expression level of AT III antigen was measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (JL19564, JONLNBIO, Shanghai, China) based on a double-antibody sandwich format. Microplate wells pre-coated with an anti-human AT III capture antibody were incubated sequentially with standards or patient samples, followed by biotin-labeled detection antibodies and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated enzymes. After thorough washing, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added for color development. The HRP catalyzed the conversion of TMB into a blue product, which turned yellow upon acidification. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader ELX800 (Bio Tek, VT, USA). Each sample was tested in triplicate, and AT III concentrations were determined from a standard curve.

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral whole blood using standard protocols. The purity and concentration were assessed, and samples were diluted to a working concentration of 100 ng/µL.

SERPINC1 gene sequencing

All exons and flanking intronic regions of the SERPINC1 gene were PCR-amplified from genomic DNA of both patients and their family members. PCR products were subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing using an ABI-3730 automated sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Sequencing was conducted by BioSune Biotechnology Co., Ltd., located in Shanghai, China. Sequencing chromatograms were analyzed using the Sequence Analysis software package (Applied Biosystems).

The pathogenicity of the identified candidate variants was evaluated based on the guidelines provided by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP).

Nucleotide changes were described according to Human Genome variation nomenclature, with position + 1 corresponding to the “A” of the ATG translation start codon (reference sequence: NM_000488.4). Amino acid positions were annotated using both the Human Genome Variation Society (where + 1 refers to the methionine of the ATG initiation codon) and the AT mutation database, which counts residues of the mature protein starting from the + 1 histidine in exon 2, after removal of the 32-residue signal peptide.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). p < 0.05 represents statistically significant.

Identification of SERPINC1 mutations in patients and their relatives

Based on these findings, we performed genetic screening of the SERPINC1 gene in both individuals. Direct sequencing of all exons identified mutations in the SERPINC1 coding region, resulting in premature protein termination.

The first patient, a 39-year-old male, was found to harbor the novel SERPINC1 mutation p.Glu271Ter (NM_000488.4 SERPINC1:c.906dupT p.Glu303*) (Fig. 8), which is reported here for the first time. This patient first presented with extensive DVT in the lower limbs in 2018, at age 34. In 2023, he experienced a recurrent DVT episode, accompanied by multiple pulmonary embolisms, and was admitted to Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital. The patient reported a history of prolonged air travel with limited leg movement prior to each thrombotic event. Family screening, including his father, mother, and son, revealed that both his father and son also carried the same SERPINC1 mutation (Fig. 9). His father had a history of DVT at age 54 and suffered a cerebral infarction at age 69.

Discussion

This study reports two cases of severe venous thrombosis, both associated with novel nonsense mutations in the SERPINC1 gene, which encodes antithrombin III (AT III), a critical regulator of coagulation. These cases highlight the complex clinical presentation of hereditary AT III deficiency and underscore the value of genetic screening, particularly in individuals with unexplained thrombotic events (Visual Summary).

Case 1: novel p.Glu271Ter mutation in an AT III-deficient patient

The first patient, a 39-year-old male, presented with extensive DVT and PE, severe manifestations of VTE. This case is notable for the identification of a previously “unreported SERPINC1 mutation, p.Glu271Ter (NM_000488.4 SERPINC1:c.906dupT p.Glu303*) (Fig. 8). This mutation expands the genetic landscape of AT III deficiency and its association with thrombophilia. The patient’s history of prolonged air travel, coupled with recurrent thrombotic events, suggests that environmental factors, such as immobility during long-haul flights, may exacerbate an underlying genetic predisposition to thrombosis. Previous studies have established that individuals with AT III deficiency are at higher risk of thromboembolic events, particularly when exposed to additional risk factors such as immobility and extended travel [6].

Despite prompt intervention with mechanical thrombectomy and anticoagulation, the patient experienced a subsequent thrombotic recurrence, emphasizing the challenges in managing anticoagulation in individuals with hereditary thrombophilia. His AT III level was significantly low (approximately 50%), consistent with previous studies showing that reduced AT III activity correlates with an increased risk of VTE and more severe thrombotic events [18, 19]. Initial treatment with LMWH failed to provide optimal anticoagulation, consistent with previous studies showing a higher rate of asymptomatic thrombus propagation during the early phase of VTE treatment with heparin and VKAs [25]. However, switching to rivaroxaban proved more effective, underscoring the need for personalized anticoagulant therapy tailored to an individual’s genetic and clinical profile. These findings suggest that LMWH may be associated with early asymptomatic thrombus propagation during initial VTE anticoagulation therapy. In case one, the suboptimal response to LMWH may also be partially related to the patient’s low AT III activity. Given the heparin’s therapeutic effect relies on normal AT III levels [26], we recommend that clinicians assess AT III activity before initiating LMWH treatment to optimize treatment outcomes.

Case 2: first report of p.Arg129Ter mutation in an Asian patient

The second patient, a 21-year-old female, presented with PE and extensive DVT— involving the left iliac, femoral, popliteal, and peroneal veins— following the use of drospirenone-ethinylestradiol tablets (Busta). Genetic screening revealed the presence of the SERPINC1 mutation p.Arg129Ter (NM_000488.4 SERPINC1:c.481 C >T p.Arg161*) (Fig. 10), a mutation previously reported in Western populations [27–29]. This is the first reported case of this mutation in an individual of Asian descent. This case suggests the importance of genetic screening, particularly in younger patients (< 50 year) with thromboembolic events, as oral contraceptives containing estrogen or progestin are known to increase the risk of VTE among all women, with an additive and even synergistic increase in VTE risk in those with inherited thrombophilias [24].

Similar to the first patient, her AT III level was low (50.1%), further supporting a genetic predisposition to thrombosis. Notably, the patient developed an unexpected DVT in the left lower limb following thrombolytic therapy with alteplase. The condition arose from a complex interplay of multiple factors, including hereditary thrombophilia due to AT III mutations, suboptimal anticoagulation with LMWH and the procoagulant effects of oral contraceptives (OCPs). First, the AT III mutation reduced activity and antigen level (approximately 50%), constituting the fundamental cause of severe hereditary thrombophilia [18, 19] and a persistent hypercoagulable state. Second, AT III, the most important natural anticoagulant, neutralizes thrombin and factor Xa, forming the mechanistic basis of heparin-based drugs [26]. When AT III activity falls to 50.1% (moderate to severe deficiency), the efficacy of heparin-based therapy is substantially reduced or lost, making standard LMWH treatment insufficient. Third, exogenous estrogens and combination estrogen-progesterone OCPs are associated with an increased risk of VTE in all women, with an additive or even synergistic effect in those with inherited thrombophilias [24]. The procoagulant risk associated with OCPs is driven by the elevation of specific coagulation factors (II, VII, IX, X and XII) and fibrinogen levels [30]. Although the patient discontinued OCPs at admission, the procoagulant effects may persist transiently. In this case, the combination of low AT III activity, suboptimal response to LMWH therapy, and the OCPs usage contributed to the development of unexpected DVT.

Family screening revealed that both the patient and her mother carried the same SERPINC1 mutation, suggesting a hereditary basis for her thrombosis. Her mother, diagnosed with DVT at age 37, likely experienced similar hypercoagulability, exacerbated by the use of contraceptive pills. This familial pattern highlights the importance of genetic testing in patients with a strong family history of VTE. Identifying carriers of AT III deficiency can guide clinical management and help prevent future thrombotic events. This case also serves as a cautionary tale: once genetic testing reveals reduced AT III activity, the use of estrogen-containing contraceptives should be strictly avoided due to the heightened risk of VTE.

Genetic insights and clinical implications

The SERPINC1gene, located on the long arm of chromosome 1 within the q23-q25.1 region, encodes AT III, a key coagulation inhibitor. Spanning 13.57 kb of genomic DNA, SERPINC1 consists of seven exons separated by six introns. The mRNA transcript produced is 1594 nucleotides long, translating into a protein of 464 amino acids, which includes a 32-residue signal peptide and a 432-amino acid mature protein. Notably, the intronic regions of SERPINC1 contain one partial and nine complete Alu repeat elements, comprising 22% of the intronic sequence, which is notably higher than the average 5% found in the human genome [23]. A previous study examined SERPINC1 mutations in 272 patients with AT III deficiency, revealing a mutation profile comprising 59% missense, 10% nonsense, 8% splice site mutations, 15% small deletions/insertions/duplications, and 8% large deletions [2 7]. This data indicates that nonsense mutations are relatively infrequent. In this study, we identified two such mutations: the first was an insertion of “T” that resulted in a premature termination of translation at TGA, while the second mutation, a “C” to “T” substitution, also led to a premature termination at TGA. Notably, the first mutation represents the initially reported variant, while the second mutation has been previously reported [27–29].

AT III deficiency is classified into two types based on the antigen levels and functional activity of AT. Type I AT deficiency involves a concurrent decrease in functional activity and antigen concentration, with antigen levels typically below 70 IU/dL. In contrast, Type II AT deficiency is characterized by a qualitative defect, where mutations produce a dysfunctional protein that impairs AT activity despite normal antigen levels [31].

In this study, both identified cases of AT III deficiency resulted from mutations causing premature termination of the AT III protein. Both mutations were heterozygous (Figs. 8 and 10), the activities of AT III were reduced by approximately 50% (Table 1), and the antigen levels showed a corresponding reduction of approximately 50% (Fig. 7). This reduction in activity was primarily due to impaired protein expression resulting from premature termination, thus classifying these cases as Type I AT deficiency.

In addition to nonsense mutations, specific missense mutations can lead to Type I AT deficiency. These mutations may cause glycosylation defects or intracellular retention of AT, or they may reduce conformational stability, impairing the protein’s functionality [32, 33]. Previous studies have identified specific missense mutations, such as Pro80Ser, Gly424Arg, and Met284Arg, which impact the stability of the AT shutter region. These mutations can induce conformational changes that affect the protein’s structure, stability, and function, further demonstrating how missense mutations can contribute to the dysfunction of AT III [10, 34].

Both patients in this study carried mutations in the SERPINC1 gene, leading to frameshifts and premature termination of AT III expression. These mutations resulted in reduced or dysfunctional AT III levels, contributing to a hypercoagulable state. The identification of these mutations in the context of familial thrombophilia points to the importance of genetic screening in patients with unexplained thrombosis, particularly when traditional risk factors, such as the use of oral estrogen-containing contraceptives, cannot fully account for thrombotic events.

AT III’s anticoagulant activity is enhanced through its highly specific binding site for heparin, which induces conformational changes that significantly increase AT III’s ability to inhibit FXa and FIXa [14, 34–36]. Additionally, heparin can form ternary complexes that further boost AT III’s thrombin inhibition capacity [26]. Thus, the efficacy of heparin as an anticoagulant depends on both normal AT III levels and its functional activity.

Management of anticoagulation in hereditary AT III deficiency must be individualized. As demonstrated in both cases, LMWH alone was insufficient to achieve optimal anticoagulation. Similar to heparin, the mechanism of action of LMWH involves enhancing AT III’s inhibition of FXa and thrombin. Still LMWH has a shorter molecular chain, resulting in stronger and more prolonged anti-FXa activity, with weaker thrombin inhibition [14, 34–37]. LMWH is primarily used to prevent and treat DVT and PE. However, in cases of congenital AT III deficiency, resistance to heparin or LMWH may occur [38], underscoring the importance of monitoring AT III activity when these anticoagulants are used.

PE, a life-threatening condition resulting from the obstruction of the pulmonary artery by a thrombus, typically originates from DVT in the lower extremities or pelvis [39]. Accurate diagnosis and prompt treatment are crucial, as PE is associated with various risk factors, including congenital AT III deficiency. In patients with AT III deficiency, resistance to heparin, a conventional treatment for acute PE, may occur. DOACs such as dabigatran, apixaban, and rivaroxaban have emerged as effective treatments for PE in patients with AT III deficiency [40, 41]. Studies have shown that rivaroxaban effectively treats massive venous thrombosis in AT III-deficient patients and helps prevent long-term recurrence [40]. DOACs are now recommended as standard treatment for DVT and PE in multiple clinical guidelines [42].

In this study, both patients with AT III deficiency appeared to show suboptimal responses to LMWH therapy. Switching to oral anticoagulants, such as rivaroxaban, was associated with improved clinical outcomes. This emphasizes the need for personalized anticoagulation strategies, particularly the use of DOACs in patients with hereditary thrombophilia. Additionally, as recommended in both cases, long-term anticoagulation therapy is essential to prevent further thrombotic events, especially in individuals with genetic mutations that predispose them to lifelong hypercoagulability.

Recently, alongside oral anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents like aspirin have emerged as key players in the long-term prevention of venous thrombosis, primarily by inhibiting platelet activation. Clinical trials have shown that aspirin significantly reduces the risk of VTE recurrence after discontinuing anticoagulation therapy in patients with unprovoked VTE [43, 44]. Furthermore, antiplatelet agents have demonstrated efficacy in preventing venous thrombosis and reducing PE incidence following joint replacement surgeries [45]. These findings suggest that antiplatelet agents may provide additional long-term protection against both VTE recurrence and PE incidence after joint arthroplasty.

Limitations and future directions

Although these cases provide valuable insights into the clinical management of hereditary AT III deficiency, several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, both patients were relatively young, and it remains unclear whether these findings would be applicable to older individuals with additional comorbidities. Second, while genetic mutations were identified in these cases, the full spectrum of genetic variants associated with AT III deficiency is still incompletely understood. Further research is needed to investigate the range of mutations in SERPINC1 and their effects on thrombotic risk across diverse ethnic populations. Finally, larger cohort studies are required to refine anticoagulation strategies and assess the long-term outcomes of patients with hereditary AT III deficiency, particularly regarding the use of DOACs.

Long-term anticoagulation is crucial for preventing recurrence in patients with hereditary AT III deficiency. In both cases, the decision to continue lifelong anticoagulation therapy was based on the identified genetic mutations and the patient’s persistent thrombotic risk. Furthermore, close monitoring of anticoagulation status is critical to ensure therapeutic levels are maintained, minimizing the risk of recurrence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, these cases highlight the importance of genetic screening in diagnosing hereditary thrombophilia and optimizing anticoagulation therapy for patients with AT III deficiency. Managing such patients requires a thorough understanding of both genetic factors and anticoagulation efficacy, underscoring the necessity for personalized treatment plans. Further research is essential to fully delineate the genetic landscape of antithrombin deficiency and its implications for thrombosis management.
