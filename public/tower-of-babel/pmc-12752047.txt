Examination of the correlation between clinical anxiety and self-confidence among dental students undergoing clinical training in Türkiye
Authors: Nihan Kaya Acar, Enes Bardakci, Merve Pelin Dur Sarıgüzel, Peris Celikel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the relationship between clinical anxiety and self-confidence in dental education is crucial for enhancing students’ perceptions of professional competence and improving educational processes. This study aimed to examine the association between clinical anxiety and self-confidence experienced by dental students during clinical training.MethodsA cross-sectional, multi-center survey was conducted among 4th- and 5th-year undergraduate dentistry students (n = 548) enrolled in faculties across Türkiye. Data were collected online using a structured three-part questionnaire comprising demographics, 10 Likert-type items assessing clinical anxiety, and 10 Likert-type items evaluating clinical self-confidence. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics and Kendall’s Tau correlation, were employed to examine the associations between clinical anxiety and self-confidence variables, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 548 students participated (57.7% [n = 316] 4th-year, 42.3% [n = 232] 5th-year), with 64.4% (n = 353) female. Clinical anxiety was most pronounced regarding fear of making mistakes (37.8%, n = 207), difficult cases (45.3%, n = 248), time pressure during treatment (43.8%, n = 240), and complex procedures such as endodontics, prosthodontics, and surgery (39.1%, n = 214). In contrast, self-confidence was higher in areas such as professional competence (50.9%, n = 279), patient management (52.6%, n = 288), rapid decision-making (51.3%, n = 281), and skill development through faculty feedback (56.4%, n = 309). Correlation analyses demonstrated a weak but statistically significant negative relationship between clinical anxiety and self-confidence (τ = −0.07 to − 0.34, p < 0.05), indicating that higher anxiety levels were associated with lower self-confidence across multiple domains.ConclusionsThis study indicates that clinical anxiety is associated with lower levels of self-confidence among dental students; however, self-confidence tends to increase with greater experience and constructive feedback. The findings suggest that teaching technical skills alone is insufficient in clinical education programs; curricula should also incorporate interventions aimed at enhancing students’ psychological resilience, including stress management, mentorship, and psychosocial support.Keywords: Clinical anxiety, Clinical Self-Confidence, Clinical training, Dental education

Introduction

The primary objective of dental education programs is to equip students with the ability to perform fundamental dental treatments from a professional perspective. Ultimately, the aim is to cultivate competent and reliable practitioners who, grounded in a solid theoretical foundation, can independently diagnose and manage treatments with adequate practical experience [1–4]. To achieve this objective, students are engaged in both preclinical and clinical training. The transition from preclinical to clinical education constitutes a particularly challenging milestone, as it necessitates the concurrent application of theoretical knowledge, technical proficiency, and communication skills. This critical phase often heightens students’ anxiety and may undermine their self-confidence [5]. In line with these challenges, previous literature has consistently reported elevated levels of anxiety, depression, and even suicidal ideation among dental students, with prevalence rates in certain studies approaching those documented in psychiatric inpatient populations [6].

Anxiety is defined as a generalized and nonspecific form of fear that arises in situations perceived as threatening to an individual’s sense of integrity [7]. The concept of self-confidence has been defined in various ways, but is generally described as an individual’s subjective belief in their ability to successfully perform a given activity [8–10]. The impact of anxiety and self-confidence on dental students’ educational experiences is substantial, as the psychological challenges they encounter can differ markedly across various stages of their training. For instance, psychological distress has been reported to increase markedly during the initial patient interactions when transitioning from preclinical to clinical training [11], whereas in the final year, stress levels rise due to the simultaneous demands of advancing clinical competencies and continuing theoretical coursework [12]. Such anxiety arising during the educational process can have significant adverse effects on students, including hindering learning, reducing performance, and potentially compromising the quality of care provided [13]. Recognizing these detrimental effects, researchers have increasingly emphasized the importance of integrating psychological support frameworks into dental education to help mitigate students’ anxiety and strengthen their coping capacities. Interventions such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, cognitive-behavioral strategies, and structured mentorship programs have been reported to be associated with lower levels of anxiety and greater clinical self-confidence within learning environments [14–18]. However, despite growing interest in this area, many existing studies have remained limited to specific disciplines or single-center contexts [6, 11, 19]. However, the current literature lacks studies that directly examine the relationship between anxiety and self-confidence during the clinical training process while simultaneously considering multiple variables. Building upon this background, the present study aims to investigate the relationship between clinical anxiety and self-confidence among dental students during their clinical training, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological dimensions of dental education.

Methods

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional study among 4th- and 5th-year students receiving clinical education in dentistry faculties in Türkiye. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ataturk University Faculty of Dentistry in April 2025 (Approval No: 2025/04–29). The survey was carried out in accordance with the Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2008). Following ethics approval, the research team shared the approval letter and study protocol with dentistry faculties in Türkiye. Faculties that consented to participate distributed the survey link (Google Forms) to their student representatives. These representatives then posted the link in official class WhatsApp™ and Telegram™ groups on 1 June 2025. Two reminder messages were sent to the same groups after 7 and 14 days. The questionnaire remained accessible for four weeks (1–31 June 2025). Participation was completely voluntary; no e-mail addresses were collected, and no incentives were provided. The survey questions underwent content validity assessment, and participants received an informed consent form outlining the study’s purpose and procedures. The inclusion criteria for the study were defined as dental students aged between 22 and 25 years, enrolled in the 4th or 5th year, and voluntarily participating in the survey. The exclusion criteria included students currently on academic leave or with interrupted clinical training and exchange or visiting students to ensure data reliability and sample homogeneity. As the participating faculties were those that voluntarily agreed to take part, and students self-selected by choosing to complete the online questionnaire, the study employed a convenience sampling approach rather than random selection within faculties.

The sample size calculation was based on a type-I error probability (α) of 0.05, a test power (1-ß) of 0.80, and an effect size of 0.25. A power analysis conducted under these parameters determined that data needed to be collected from a minimum of 124 students. However, the final sample included 548 participants, which substantially exceeded the minimum required sample size, thereby increasing the statistical power and robustness of the findings. In total, students from the 4th (n = 316) and 5th (n = 232) years participated in the online survey. Data for the study were collected using an online survey form created via Google Forms. The questionnaire was designed in three sections. In the first section, six structured multiple-choice items were included to gather information on the students’ demographic and educational background, covering gender, age, year of study, length of clinical experience, frequency of clinical practice, and their evaluation of the guidance provided by faculty members during clinical training. The second section consisted of 10 Likert-type questions assessing students’ clinical anxiety. The third section comprised 10 Likert-type items evaluating students’ self-confidence in relation to their clinical training experiences. The survey items were developed following a comprehensive review of the relevant literature and adapted from previously validated instruments commonly used in medical and dental education research. Specifically, the 10 clinical anxiety and 10 self-confidence items were derived from the thematic and conceptual frameworks of established questionnaire identified in prior studies [20–22]; however, the individual items were newly developed in Turkish to ensure linguistic clarity and contextual relevance for Turkish dental students. Accordingly, the questionnaire was not a direct translation or replication of any existing tool but rather a literature-informed and contextually tailored instrument designed to reflect key dimensions of clinical anxiety (e.g., fear of making mistakes, time pressure, patient interaction) and self-confidence (e.g., technical competence, decision-making, stress management). Content validity of the questionnaire was ensured through expert review. A panel of specialists in dental education and educational sciences examined all items in terms of clarity, relevance, and appropriateness, and necessary revisions were made in line with their feedback. To further confirm comprehensibility and feasibility, the draft instrument was pilot-tested with a small group of dental students who were not included in the main study. Based on the feedback from this process, minor wording adjustments were introduced, and the final version of the questionnaire was prepared for formal data collection.

The statistical analyses of the study were carried out using the SPSS version 27.0 software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics of the data were expressed in terms of numbers and percentages. Kendall’s Tau correlation was used to examine the relationships between ordinal and continuous measurements. Statistical significance was determined at the threshold of p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the relationship between clinical anxiety levels and clinical self-confidence among dental students undergoing clinical training. To the best of our knowledge, it also represents the first study in the literature to specifically investigate the association between clinical anxiety and self-confidence in dental students. The findings of this study revealed that a considerable proportion of students experienced anxiety both before and during clinical practice, with this anxiety predominantly centered around fear of making mistakes, challenging cases, faculty criticism, and time pressure. Nevertheless, the majority of students reported feeling confident regarding their professional competence, patient management, and clinical skills. Correlation analyses further revealed that higher levels of anxiety were associated with lower levels of self-confidence. In addition, the development of clinical skills and the receipt of constructive feedback from faculty members were found to enhance students’ self-confidence. The manner in which faculty feedback is delivered appears to play a decisive role in shaping students’ emotional and cognitive responses during clinical training. Positive reinforcement appears to be associated with higher motivation and self-confidence, whereas appropriately structured constructive feedback is linked to improved performance without exacerbating anxiety. Accordingly, adopting balanced and stage-appropriate feedback practices may foster not only learning outcomes but also students’ psychological well-being.

These observations are consistent with research from other health disciplines, which has similarly emphasized the importance of supportive supervision and constructive feedback as key determinants of students’ confidence, satisfaction, and professional growth [23] .Comparable findings have been reported among midwifery and nursing students, where structured mentorship and well-designed clinical environments have been shown to enhance learning outcomes despite contextual challenges [24, 25]. Furthermore, resilience-oriented interventions have been identified as effective strategies for mitigating stress and sustaining self-efficacy, particularly in demanding or conflict-affected clinical settings [26]. Collectively, these studies reinforce the importance of integrating psychological support frameworks and feedback-based mentorship models within dental curricula to promote both academic and emotional development.

A study conducted on nursing students reported that self-confidence was generally at a high or moderate level, whereas anxiety was observed at low or moderate levels. Although the relationship between self-confidence and anxiety was weak, the findings indicate that these two factors interact in a complex manner during the clinical training process [27]. Similarly, this study found that dental students, particularly those newly beginning clinical treatments, experienced uncertainty in coping with stress and managing complex cases, yet still demonstrated a sense of self-confidence. Therefore, clinical experience, social support mechanisms, and feedback processes appear to be associated with higher self-confidence and lower levels of anxiety among students.

Previous studies have reported that students who did not receive sufficient preclinical training experienced higher levels of anxiety [28], whereas increased professional experience and higher educational attainment positively influenced clinical competence, patient communication, and job satisfaction [29]. In this study, sources of clinical anxiety were found to be related to students’ lack of clinical knowledge, fear of making mistakes, and receiving criticism from faculty members. Additionally, approximately half of the participants reported that their self-confidence increased as their clinical skills improved. This study also suggests that clinical anxiety is related to self-confidence, and that dental students’ clinical anxiety can potentially be mitigated through the self-confidence they possess.

In the study conducted by Alamoush et al. [30], the stress levels of students from different dental disciplines were compared, and it was found that students in the prosthodontics clinic exhibited higher levels of stress. This has been attributed to the longer duration of prosthetic treatment, the difficulty in finding suitable patients, and the complex, multifactorial nature of prosthetic procedures. In addition, this study reported that fifth-year students experienced higher stress levels due to increased workload. Consistently, more than 60% of the students in the present study indicated that they experienced greater anxiety during endodontic, prosthetic, or surgical procedures. This suggests that students’ anxiety levels may be linked to the complexity of clinical procedures and the intensity of academic workload.

In the survey study conducted by Malghani et al. [22] clinical situations that could potentially induce anxiety among dental students were examined. The most prominent clinical factors contributing to heightened anxiety among dental students were identified as the risk of patient infection, tooth extraction errors, and misdiagnosis. Honey J. et al. [31] in their study conducted on dental students, reported that the highest levels of self-confidence were observed in frequently performed simple procedures, whereas the lowest levels were noted in complex procedures where lack of experience was more evident. They further suggested that extending clinical exposure in these areas could contribute to enhancing students’ confidence. This finding aligns with the results of the present study, which indicate that students experience increased anxiety in the clinical setting due to fear of making errors and encountering challenging cases.

Tokuc et al. [32] in their study on dental students, examined clinical situations that provoke anxiety and reported that deficiencies during the diagnostic and treatment processes were associated with elevated anxiety levels. In this study, 25.3% of students undergoing clinical training reported feeling inadequately knowledgeable during clinical practice. Moreover, this study suggested that anxiety levels decrease as clinical experience and expertise increase, and that female students exhibit higher anxiety levels compared to their male counterparts. In current study, it is plausible that gender-related factors may have contributed to the overall patterns observed in the present study.

In the study conducted by Kishawi et al. [33] on dental students in the endodontics clinic, it was reported that stress factors did not have a negative impact on students’ clinical performance, and that students under stress demonstrated shorter treatment times. It has been suggested that this may be related to the adaptive mechanisms developed by dental students in response to stressful conditions through the training they receive during both their preclinical and clinical education. Similarly, in this study, the high proportion of students responding positively to the statements “I feel sufficiently confident in my ability to develop and improve my technical skills,” “I acknowledge that making mistakes is a natural part of the learning process,” and “I feel confident in managing the stress I experience in the clinical setting” appears to support the presence of this adaptive mechanism.

In conclusion, the present study evaluates the relationship between clinical anxiety and self-confidence among dental students, establishing a statistically significant inverse correlation between these constructs, thereby underscoring the complex interplay of psychological factors within clinical educationn. Although the correlations observed in this study were statistically significant, their magnitude was generally weak. This pattern is not unexpected, as psychological constructs such as anxiety and self-confidence are multifactorial in nature and influenced by diverse academic, personal, and environmental factors. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as reflecting statistical significance rather than strong practical impact, emphasizing the complex and nuanced relationship between these variables within clinical dental education. Furthermore, it was found that students who experienced high levels of anxiety prior to commencing clinical practice demonstrated lower confidence in patient management, rapid decision-making, and technical skills. These results imply that psychological determinants inherent to the process of adapting to the clinical environment may exert a substantial influence on students’ perceptions of their professional competence. The results suggest that, in addition to technical skill training, dental curricula may need to integrate approaches that foster students’ psychological resilience. Another notable finding of the study is that students’ confidence markedly increases as they gain more clinical experience. Based on the findings, it is recommended that stress management and psychosocial support programs aimed at reducing students’ clinical anxiety be implemented in dental education to strengthen students’ self-confidence. These recommendations are consistent with established psychological and pedagogical frameworks. Interventions grounded in cognitive-behavioral approaches may help students regulate anxiety through adaptive coping and cognitive restructuring, whereas mentorship and feedback processes informed by Bandura’s self-efficacy theory can promote the development of confidence by reinforcing mastery experiences and constructive feedback [34].

This study has several limitations. Given its cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established. Moreover, the voluntary nature of participation may have introduced selection bias, while the employed measurement instruments capture only the students’ immediate emotional states, thereby limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding long-term clinical performance or professional competence. Additionally, as data collection was conducted within a one-month period, the findings may have been influenced by seasonal or academic workload variations inherent to that specific time frame. Furthermore, the predominance of female participants and the study’s focus on a single national context (Türkiye) may limit the generalizability of the results to more diverse or international populations. Future studies are recommended to longitudinally investigate the relationship between clinical anxiety and self-confidence, include dental students from diverse countries, and evaluate the effectiveness of educational intervention programs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that clinical anxiety negatively impacts self-confidence among dental students. Students’ anxieties were primarily centered on fear of making mistakes, challenging clinical procedures, and time pressure, whereas their self-confidence was observed to increase through gradual skill acquisition and constructive feedback from faculty members. The findings indicate that clinical education should emphasize not only technical skills but also the psychological support and preparedness of students. Incremental patient exposure, simulation exercises, structured feedback, and stress management strategies may help strengthen students’ self-confidence while mitigating anxiety. Future research should assess the sustained impact of these interventions.
