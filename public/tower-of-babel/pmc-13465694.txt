Motion-Steered Noise Management in Hearing Aids Reduces Listening Effort and Stress Indexed by Pupil Size and Heart Rate
Authors: Jeppe H. Christensen, Valentina Zapata-Rodríguez, Sébastien Santurette, Axel Ahrens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Head and body movements influence real-world hearing outcomes, yet they are often overlooked in clinical evaluations of hearing aids. This reduces ecological validity and limits assessments of motion-based features. To overcome this, the present study introduces a novel experimental approach that incorporates natural human behavior during listening, utilizing virtual reality (VR) in an anechoic chamber equipped with a spherical 64-channel loudspeaker array and a naturalistic two-stage listening task. We hypothesized that heart rate, obtained unobtrusively with a wrist-worn device, and pupillometry would be sensitive to differences in listening difficulty and hearing aid technology.Design:Participants (N = 32) freely moved their head and body orientation to complete two listening tasks with a natural transition between them. First, they located a target talker among 15 source locations, then comprehended a 33-sec news clip from the target location. Task difficulty was manipulated by introducing either two or four interfering talkers at various spatial locations. Beat-to-beat heart rate was recorded, and listening stress was calculated as the relative change in beats per minute based on the number of interfering talkers. In addition, pupil dilation was recorded with head-mounted cameras in the VR headset to estimate sustained listening effort. Head and gaze direction were tracked throughout the experiment. Participants used two different hearing aid models, both featuring noise management systems based on Deep Neural Networks. However, only one model utilized head movement information from an embedded accelerometer to adjust noise management in response to detected motion activity.Results:Speech comprehension performance decreased, and heart rate and pupil dilation increased in the four interfering talkers condition compared with the two interfering talkers condition. Comparing the two hearing aid models, speech comprehension was similar, but heart rate and pupil dilation were significantly higher with the model that did not incorporate head movement information in its noise management. We also found that pupil dilation and heart rate were significantly and positively associated across trials within participants.Conclusions:VR combined with unobtrusive heart rate monitoring is an effective method for evaluating hearing aids in naturalistic conditions. This approach allows for head movements and changes to body orientation, which are necessary for assessing hearing aid features that steer by motion detection. Relative heart rate was sensitive to both task difficulty and hearing aid technology, aligning with changes in pupil dilation and suggesting that motion-steering of hearing aid noise management can benefit speech comprehension following a localization task by reducing listening stress and effort.

INTRODUCTION

Head and body movements significantly influence real-world (Borschke et al. 2024) and naturalistic hearing outcomes (Gessa et al. 2025). For example, individuals typically orient themselves toward the speaker or at an angle close to speaker, adjusting their posture and keeping their head still to optimize listening outcome (Grange & Culling 2016; Hadley et al. 2021; Higgins et al. 2023). While in complex environments, when looking for a conversational partner, larger head movements and turns are necessary for increasing speech reception (Slomianka et al. 2024). However, traditional listening tests typically require participants to remain still while listening, facing the target, so that the acoustics can be controlled for and so that objective measures of, for example, listening effort, which are typically burdensome and obtrusive (e.g., pupillometry), can be applied. These restrictions limit natural behavior. As a solution, virtual sound environments (VSEs) have been utilized to achieve a balance between having a controlled auditory scene and a high level of acoustic realism within the scene while allowing for movement. These environments use multiple loudspeakers around the listener, generating a high level of acoustic realism within the scene. While some differences still exist between virtual and real sound environments, VSEs have been validated as a viable option for testing hearing aids and offer a higher level of ecological relevance compared with common measurement approaches (Cubick & Dau 2016; Oreinos & Buchholz 2016; Ahrens, Marschall, et al. 2019). To further enhance the degree of naturalism, VSEs have been combined with visual scenes presented using Virtual Reality (VR) (Stecker et al. 2018; Ahrens, Lund, et al. 2019; Hohmann et al. 2020). These virtual audiovisual environments can then introduce visual cues, for example, relevant for listening tasks and evaluation of hearing aid processing features (Wu & Bentler 2010). Combining VSE with VR has been applied in several studies that replicate natural human behavior and performance in controlled listening tasks (Hendrikse et al. 2019). Fichna et al. (2021) evaluated the influence of scene complexity and visual representation of the scene on psychoacoustic metrics, including sound source localization and speech comprehension, where the virtual environment demonstrated an ecologically appropriate performance. Similarly, Ahrens & Lund (2022) investigated listeners’ speech comprehension by asking them to locate a target story among interfering speech sources, which revealed that increasing the number of talkers as well as the reverberation makes it harder for normal hearing listeners to analyze scenes as expected from the real-world (Slomianka et al. 2024).

Thus, VSEs combined with VR for visual scenes are a promising method for evaluating more naturalistic listening tasks because they allow participants to move to locate a talker and orient their head to optimize for speech reception, as opposed to traditional speech comprehension tests where subjects must remain still and front-facing. This may be particularly relevant when evaluating devices that use automatic motion-based steering. Using naturalistic settings where participant movement is less restricted allows for a stronger contrast of hearing aid features and noise management (e.g., beamforming and noise reduction) that are dependent on movement registration (Branda & Wurzbacher 2021; Braendgaard et al. 2024). But the approach still does not resolve the issue that most objective outcome measures (e.g., desk-mounted pupillometry) are obtrusive and require participants to remain still or fixated while listening. This is unfortunate because hearing aid noise management features have previously been shown to reduce objective markers of listening effort (e.g., pupil dilation) in people with hearing loss (Fiedler et al. 2021) who typically experience increased listening effort during speech perception in comparison to people with normal hearing (Ohlenforst et al. 2017). Therefore, objective measures relying on less obtrusive modalities, such as wristbands, could be a desirable alternative for evaluating hearing aid noise management under less restricted and more naturalistic conditions. Especially when considering listening tasks of equal behavioral performance, physiological responses measured peripherally could help reveal differences in arousal representing stress and effort activation (Mackersie & Cones 2011; Mackersie & Calderon-Moultrie 2016). Effort has been defined as “the deliberate allocation of mental resources to overcome obstacles in goal pursuit when carrying out a task,” with listening effort referring to tasks that involve listening (Pichora-Fuller et al. 2016). As such, indices of listening effort can be helpful in evaluating the difficulty of listening situations in the absence of behavioral effects. Traditionally, listening effort has been quantified by the change in pupil dilation evoked by auditory stimuli, with increases reflecting increased cognitive resource allocation (Zekveld et al. 2018). On the other hand, physiological stress is a general phenomenon characterized by changes in physiological arousal and hormonal release (cortisol and corticosterone) following intrinsic or extrinsic triggers (Romero 2004; Gaidica & Dantzer 2020). It is not clear how well measures of general stress (e.g., changes in heart rate) can be used as an alternative to traditional pupillometry when assessing listening-related effects. While the pathways between the pupil size mediating locus coeruleus–norepinephrine system and the cardiovascular system are unclear (Papesh & Goldinger 2024), it has been proposed that both pupil size and heart rate are under the common influence of changes in the parasympathetic and sympathetic autonomic nervous system activity (Bradley et al. 2008; Finke et al. 2017; Jerčić et al. 2020). In psychophysiology, Wang et al. 2018 documented a positive association between heart rate and pupil dilation during the processing of emotional faces. But pupil dilation and heart rate have even been found to be directly related under specific circumstances and positively correlate on a trial-by-trial basis during emotional conflict and valence processing (Chang et al. 2025). Thus, arousal during sustained listening under difficult conditions is potentially reflected in both pupil size (representing listening effort) and heart rate (representing listening stress). This idea is further corroborated by Sarangi and Johnson (2024), who presented data suggesting that wristbands could be an alternative to research-grade physiological monitoring in representing a psychophysiological response to listening difficulty, that is, listening stress. In their study, a speech perception task was repeated under varying SNR (−4 to +8 dB) while recordings of heart rate, skin conductance, and respiratory rate were obtained with wearable sensors and compared with laboratory-grade recordings. Their results show that mainly the heart rate (both from the wearable and the laboratory-grade recordings) was sensitive to the differences in objective difficulty of the listening condition.

The present study combines minimally obtrusive objective measures of physiological arousal (heart rate using a wristband and pupil dilation using inward facing cameras in a VR headset) with a listening task in a VSE for reproducing an ecologically valid listening experiment. The VSE is implemented in an anechoic chamber with a spherical 64-channel loudspeaker array and the listening task is presented via VR. We hypothesized that objective measures, heart rate, and pupil dilation, would be able to determine task difficulty and hearing aid technology, particularly for hearing aids differing in their ability to steer by motion detection. Namely, we expected increases in pupil dilation and heart rate for the more difficult listening condition, and that effects would be moderated by hearing aid technology. Moreover, we hypothesized that trial-wise changes in pupil dilation and heart rate would correlate within individuals, indicating that both measures are under common influence by changes in the autonomic nervous system. While the pupillometry applied in the present study can be considered somewhat obtrusive (e.g., requiring a VR headset), it still allows for movement, in contrast to desk-mounted pupillometry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Danish Science-Ethics Committee with the reference number H-16036391. All participants gave their informed written consent before being enrolled in the study and were offered hourly monetary compensation.

Participants

A total of 32 native Danish-speaking participants (13 female) were recruited. They were on average 69.4 years of age (range = [48, 82]; SD = 7.3), were all experienced hearing aid users with at least 6 months of hearing aid use, and had stable bilateral sensorineural hearing loss with no more than 15 dB asymmetry across the maximum of three frequencies. Their pure-tone average (across 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz) was on the right ear 41.33 dB HL (SD = 11.10) and on the left ear 39.96 dB HL (SD = 11.50). Exclusion criteria included eye diseases or conditions influencing constriction or dilation of the pupil; eye surgery; intake of medication impacting the autonomic nervous system; dexterity difficulties; and active ear disease.

Equipment

Visual scenes were reproduced by an HTC Vive Pro Eye VR headset that includes pupillometry using an integrated camera (Fig. 1A). Pupil dilation, and azimuth and elevation of head and eye-gaze were continuously recorded with a sampling frequency of approximately 90 Hz. The Vive Pro Eye has previously been shown to generate head- and eye-tracking data suitable for behavioral research (Niehorster et al. 2017; Schuetz & Fiehler 2022). Moreover, participants wore the Empatica E4 medical-grade wristband (Empatica Srl, Italy, see Fig. 1A) and beat-wise heart rate was derived from peak-to-peak detection of the continuously recorded photoplethysmogram using Empatica’s proprietary software. The experimental task is coded in MATLAB (version 2020a), and the visual scenes presented in VR are coded in Unity (version 2018.3.5f1).

Hearing Aids and Settings

Participants either wore Oticon Real (hearing aid A) or Oticon Intent (hearing aid B) hearing aids bilaterally fitted. In both cases, all settings were set to default with adaptive noise management active, and power domes were used for acoustic coupling. Gain was adjusted to each participant’s hearing loss using the manufacturer’s updated Voice-Aligned Compression (VAC+) fitting rationale, designed to optimize speech comprehension and sound quality based on loudness data from Buus and Florentine (2001).

The hearing aid models had similar features (i.e., wind noise management, feedback management strategies, equal fitting bandwidth, 64 processing channels) but differed in their implementation of adaptive noise management. They both used a fast-acting 24-channel noise reduction system with a high-resolution Minimum Variance Distortionless Response beamformer combined with the processing of a deep neural network-based postfilter (Andersen et al. 2021) trained to enhance the contrast between speech and noise using across-channel information from real-life sound scenes. But while hearing aid A uses acoustic information about the sound environment complexity to adapt processing, hearing aid B also includes motion information from a hearing aid embedded accelerometer (Braendgaard et al. 2024). Thus, depending on registration of movement, hearing aid B can adjust beamforming and provide a higher or lower SNR benefit compared with hearing aid A in the same noisy setting, while the SNR benefit with hearing aid A is the same regardless of motion (Braendgaard et al. 2024).

Procedure

The test was divided into two blocks, each consisting of 24 trials with one hearing aid model. The order of hearing aid models was randomized and balanced across participants. After fitting and familiarization, the participant was seated in the center of an anechoic chamber (Fig. 1A) with a spherical configuration of 64 loudspeakers (see Ahrens, Marschall, et al. 2019 for details). The VR headset (Fig. 1A) then reproduced the visual scene (Fig. 1B) simulating an empty room with wooden floor and wall panels (see screenshot in Fig. 1B). In the frontal plane (±105° visual angle), 15 avatars represented by semi-transparent humanoid shapes were arranged in an evenly distributed semi-circle spaced with 15° of visual angle, aligning with the physical loudspeakers in the horizontal plane. Each experimental session took roughly 1.5 hr and an additional 30 min were spent on hearing aid fitting and test familiarization. Trials differing in the number of interfering talkers (two or four) were presented in a pseudo-randomized way with equal order for each participant. A trial began with the onset of background noise and speech from both the target to be found and the interfering talkers (t = 0 sec in Fig. 1B), followed by the presentation of a visual symbol (t = 3 sec) cuing the story of the target speaker. Following the localization response (clicking on an avatar with the handheld controller), a 7-sec countdown with only the first three numbers pronounced audibly was presented before the onset of the newsclip for the speech comprehension task. This countdown allowed participants to get reoriented in case they did not identify the correct target talker. There were no visual changes in the scene between the speech localization and speech comprehension tasks. Throughout the trial, speech-shaped background noise was played from five loudspeakers. Target speech audio material was randomized across trials and hearing aid models, but counterbalanced between conditions with two or four interfering talkers. After each block, participants reported their perceived level of exerted effort, their engagement with the newsclip, and their perception of the overall sound quality on continuous rating scales (range 0 to 100) presented centrally. The questions were (translated from their Danish wording): “How much effort did you put into understanding the news clips?”; “How engaged were you in understanding the news clips?”; and “How do you rate the overall sound quality of the hearing aids?.” Participants were familiarized with the questions before the experiment.

Speech Perception and Localization Task

Each trial started with the participant having to identify the location of a target talker among the 15 avatars. The story spoken by the target talker was cued by having a visual icon appear in front of the participant (i.e., at 0° of visual angle) representing a depiction of the content of the story (e.g., a “guitar” cues that the target story is related to music and guitar play, see Fig. 1C). The number of interfering talkers presented simultaneously varied between two and four with the sparsity, that is, the visual angle encompassing all talkers, being either moderate (45° to 75° for two interfering talkers; 75° to 90° for four interfering talkers) or sparse (>90° for two interfering talkers; >135° for four interfering talkers). The absolute location of the target talker varied pseudo-randomly (but consistently for all participants) either in the frontal area (±60°) or toward the sides (±75° to ±105°). Accuracy and completion time of the task were emphasized by instructing participants to find the correct story as fast as possible. Following a response, the correct avatar would turn green.

Speech Comprehension Task

After responding to the speech localization task, the speech comprehension task would begin by first presenting an auditory 7-sec countdown. During this time, participants were instructed to re-adjust their posture and attention accordingly in case they answered incorrectly in the localization task. During the speech comprehension, participants were instructed to remain still, with eyes focused on the target talker and to listen carefully to a 33-sec news clip while ignoring the other two or four interfering talkers. After each trial, a statement about the content of the news clip was presented for example, “Der kommer et stigende antal krydstogtturister til København” (“An increasing number of cruise tourists come to Copenhagen”). Participants were asked to indicate whether this statement was correct or wrong (i.e., chance level was 50%).

Audio Material

The speech material used for the speech localization task was taken from a database of anechoically recorded monologues in Danish (Ahrens & Lund 2022). The database consists of 10 stories, each spoken by 10 native Danish speakers, resulting in 100 unique combinations of content and speaker, which were used as targets in the speech localization task and interfering talkers during both tasks. Five of the speakers are female, and five are male. One of the female talkers was excluded from the present study, as it was the test leader’s voice. Each story had a different topic that could be distinguished easily by its content and was assigned an identifying visual icon. For the speech comprehension task, only the target talker was replaced by a 33-sec-long, anechoically recorded, publicly available news clip of neutral content in Danish by a female and male speaker (Fiedler et al. 2021). The gender of the speaker remained constant between the two tasks. Last, the background noise consisted of speech-shaped noise reproduced from five loudspeakers located at the back plane of the listener at ±120°, ±150°, and 180°. The overall level of the background noise, as well as that of all competing talkers (target and interferer), was individually calibrated at 65 dB sound pressure level.

Data Preprocessing and Filtering

Raw pupil dilation was processed according to recommendations in the literature (Winn et al. 2018). Namely, data from the right eye was chosen as it preserved most of the data; blink artifacts were removed by interpolating samples from −30 to +100 msec around each detected blink; single pupil traces were then baseline corrected to isolate the transient component by subtracting the mean dilation observed in the 3 sec before audio onset for each trial and participant; only traces with >70% of original samples preserved were kept for further processing. Lastly, traces were down-sampled to 1 Hz and low-pass filtered.

For visualization and within-subject analysis, heart rate was referenced to the mean heart rate observed during the easier speech comprehension condition with only two interfering talkers. This removed the effect of inter-individual differences in resting heart rate.

For both pupil and heart rate data, traces were cut to 35 sec, which is 5 sec before the news clip offset. This was done to reduce the effects of motion and response preparation. In addition, data were collapsed across target location angle. After preprocessing, data from 24 participants were included in the pupil dilation analysis, and 20 participants were included in the heart rate analysis. Of the participants removed from the pupil dilation analysis, three were excluded due to preprocessing and filtering of the raw traces, one due to extreme baseline pupil size only for hearing aid B (Fig. 2A), and four participants did not have pupillometry recorded (see Limitations). All 12 participants who were removed from the heart rate analysis were removed because they did not have enough observations retained after preprocessing. Specifically, participants were excluded when more than 30% of all their observations were not usable.

Statistical Analysis

Pupil dilation and heart rate traces were modeled as dependent variables in separate generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) implemented in R (version 4.4.3) with the mgcv package version 1.9 (Wood 2017), which have been particularly useful for pupillometry (van Rij et al. 2019). In both cases, the number of interfering talkers (2 or 4) and hearing aid model (A or B) were coded in a joint 4-level categorical variable Condition and included as a parametric term to account for overall changes in outcome magnitude. To account for conditional variations in pupil size and heart rate over time (i.e., the time courses), smooth terms included Time by Condition. Moreover, non-interesting experimental conditions were included as both parametric and smooth terms (using the “by” argument in the bam() function) to account for their effects on the overall magnitude and time course of pupil dilation and heart rate. This included task performance (Performance, wrong/correct), sparsity of the talkers (Sparsity, low/high), and gender of the target talker (Target gender, male/female). Random effects terms included factor smooths for Participant and Trial number over Time. Lastly, to account for participant-specific behavior potentially influencing pupil dilation and heart rate traces on a within-trial basis, smooth terms for gaze (azimuth and level) and hand movement (SD of accelerometer vector magnitude) were included as smooth terms. Autocorrelation in residuals was accounted for by including a random slope for each unique trace (coded with categorical variable Event) in combination with an autoregressive 1st order error term with value rho determined by the lag 1 coefficient of the models fitted first without the autoregressive term added (van Rij et al. 2019). These steps effectively reduced autocorrelation in the residuals of both pupil dilation and heart rate traces to a level deemed acceptable (<0.3 at lag 1, and rapid decay for lag 1+) without compromising model fit (Porretta et al. 2018). The check.gam() function from the itsadug R package (version 2.4.1) was used to validate the appropriate number of knots (argument k, set to default 9) for each smooth, and to visually inspect that the default distribution family (Gaussian) produced normally distributed residuals. Statistical inference on the difference in pupil size or heart rate between levels of Condition was then based on summary statistics of the parametric terms with re-coding of the baseline condition, and visualization of the time courses with difference plots estimated with the itsadug package in R.

To supplement the model-based investigation of the time course of pupil dilation and heart rate, we applied a cluster-based permutation analysis (CPA) (Maris & Oostenveld 2007), which is a nonparametric approach for statistical inference on time series (Colizoli et al. 2025). Thus, the CPA does not constitute a separate test of differences in the pupil dilation and heart rate traces. Rather, it serves to strengthen the statistical support for true differences in the traces—that is, “conjunction testing” (Rubin 2021). The CPA based on mixed-effects models is implemented in R using the clusterperm.lmer function (Lee & Braun 2012) from the permutes package (version 2.8).

Subjective ratings of listening effort, engagement, and sound quality were evaluated with linear mixed-effect (LME) models with fixed factors for hearing aid model and random intercepts for participants. Trial-wise speech comprehension performance was evaluated with a generalized linear mixed-effects model implementing a binomial link function with fixed effects for hearing aid model, number of interfering talkers, and their interaction in combination with random intercepts for participants. Physiological baselines were evaluated by analysis of variances applied to LME models to test systematic differences between sessions (e.g., hearing aid models) and the number of interfering talkers within participants.

Last, trial-wise mean heart rate and pupil dilation were subjected to association analysis using an LME model. Specifically, the model predicts heart rate from pupil dilation and additionally includes a fixed effect for the hearing model to account for differences in intercepts between the different test sessions. The random effects terms include intercepts for participants as well as slopes for the hearing aid model within each participant.

LME and generalized linear mixed-effect models were implemented in R using the lme4 package (version 1.1-37) with p value approximations based on t-statistics using the sjPlot package version 2.8 (Lüdecke 2021) and referenced to hearing aid model A and the two interfering talkers condition. Pairwise comparisons on the speech comprehension performance were estimated using the emmeans R package (version 1.11). In all cases, LME model suitability was validated by visually confirming that the raw conditional residuals fell within the 95% confidence interval of a straight line (y = x) in normal quantile plots (i.e., QQ plots).

RESULTS

Behavioral Performance

Performance in the speech comprehension task (proportion of correct answers) was for two interfering talkers 73.96% (SD = 14.47%) with hearing aid A and 76.56% (SD = 15.47%) with hearing aid B. For four interfering talkers, these performance rates were 63.02% (SD = 15.69%) and 66.15% (SD = 17.19%). Performance was not significantly different with hearing aid A compared with hearing aid B (two interfering talkers: odds ratio = 0.868, SE = 0.147, p = 0.396, Cohen d = 0.144; four interfering talkers: odds ratio = 0.866, SE = 0.133, p = 0.357, Cohen d = 0.141). However, performance was significantly worse in the more difficult condition with four interfering talkers compared with in the two interfering talkers condition (hearing aid A: odds ratio = 0.590, SE = 0.094, z = −3.305, p ≤ 0.001, Cohen d = 0.527; hearing aid B: odds ratio = 0.589, SE = 0.097, p = 0.001, z = −3.231, Cohen d = 0.530). This pattern in performance did not change when evaluating only participants with valid pupillometry data (N = 24) or with valid heart rate data (N = 20). The full model outcomes are available in Table 1 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B891.

In terms of self-reports, only the ratings of listening engagement differed significantly between hearing aid models (see summary of statistical model in Table 1), with lower listening engagement observed for hearing aid A. As with comprehension performance, the pattern of effects did not change when considering different participant groups (i.e., all participants versus participants with valid pupillometry data versus participants with valid heart rate data).

Physiological Baseline Effects

Pupil dilation traces were baseline corrected by subtracting the mean dilation during the 3 sec before news clip onset. Heart rate was corrected by subtracting the mean heart rate during the entire news clip presentation with two interfering talkers. Because changing from one hearing aid model to another required repositioning the hearing aids, we compared the baselines for each to evaluate any systematic bias. One participant had elevated baseline pupil dilation with hearing aid B for both the two and four interfering talkers conditions compared with hearing aid A (Fig. 2A) and was removed from further analysis. For the remaining participants, baselines did not systematically differ between the hearing aid models (pupil dilation: F = 0.012, p = 0.915; heart rate: F = 2.018, p = 0.161) nor by the number of interfering talkers (pupil dilation: F = 0.963, p = 0.330; heart rate: F = 0.543, p = 0.464), and the interactions between hearing aid model and number of interfering talkers were not significant (pupil dilation: F = 0.003, p = 0.958; heart rate: F = 0.007, p = 0.935). The full model outcomes are available in the Table 2 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B892.

Changes in Pupil Dilation With Listening Condition

Average pupil dilation from baseline over time can be seen in Figure 3 by hearing aid model (Figs. 3A, B) or number of interfering talkers (Figs. 3C, D). In all cases, the pupil constricts following the news clip onset at the 7-sec mark, indicating sustained listening (Fiedler et al. 2021). The overall level, quantified by the parametric terms in the GAMM (Table 2), did not differ from the initial baseline condition (hearing aid A and two interfering talkers) to all other conditions. However, re-coding the baseline condition revealed that pupil dilation was significantly higher with hearing aid A and four interfering talkers compared with hearing aid B and both two and four interfering talkers (Est. = 0.026, SD = 0.011, t = 2.370, p = 0.018; Est. = 0.024, SD = 0.011, t = 2.181, p = 0.029). As with heart rate, there was no significant overall difference in pupil dilation between the interfering talkers’ conditions with hearing aid B (Est. = 0.002, SD = 0.011, t = 0.203, p = 0.839). The time courses (Fig. 3) revealed that only hearing aid A exhibited a period of significant difference in pupil dilation between the two and the four interfering talker’s conditions, which occurred in the early part of the trial (approximately between 12 and 20 sec). Moreover, when comparing the hearing aid models directly, the time courses revealed higher pupil dilation with hearing aid A in both the two and the four interfering talkers’ conditions. For the former, the difference was significant only during the latter part of the listening task (25 to 30 sec), whereas for the latter, significance was observed only in the earlier part (10 to 22 sec). The GAMM difference curves and the CPA agreed—that is, showing overlapping results despite differences in the time intervals each method identified as significant.

Correlation of Trial-Wise Pupil Dilation and Heart Rate

Pupil dilation and heart rate were both sensitive to task manipulations, represented by the number of interfering talkers and hearing aid models. Because both are physiological measures hypothetically originating from a common underlying mechanism related to arousal (Wang et al. 2018), we evaluated the trial-wise correlation between them. The scatter plot in Figure 5 shows the mean heart rate and pupil dilation averaged across the news clip playback during the speech comprehension task for each participant and hearing aid model. Besides a few instances, most associations were positive. An LME model predicting pupil dilation with heart rate revealed a significant within-participant association (Est. = 6.01 bpm/mm, confidence interval = 4.43 to 7.60, p ≤ 0.001, marginal R2 = 7.01%, conditional R2 = 92.79%, see full model outcomes in Table 3 in Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/EANDH/B893). DISCUSSION

We evaluated speech comprehension in a novel naturalistic listening experiment that was applied in a VSE with virtual reality for reproducing visual scenes. In this setup, participants can freely move their upper body and head position, which allows for natural behaviors and transitions that can enhance listening outcomes and heighten the ecological validity of testing hearing aids that steer by motion detection. Throughout the experiment, unobtrusive objective outcome measures were acquired by recording continuous heart rate using a wearable wristband and pupil dilation using inward-facing cameras in the VR headset.

Speech comprehension was computed as the proportion of correctly answered 2AFC questions concerning the 33-sec news clip played in the presence of either two or four interfering talkers. The performance was lower with four compared with two interfering talkers, but did not otherwise differ by hearing aid model. Thus, the manipulation of the number of interfering talkers successfully moderated the task difficulty similarly for both hearing aid models. This also means that objective measures can be used for comparing the two hearing aids. Namely, because the same performance levels were achieved, objective measures can help shed light on the listening effort involved without confounding from differences in other cognitive processes, for example, memory or attention (McGarrigle et al. 2014).

Pupil dilation and heart rate were sensitive to the number of interfering talkers, but depending on the hearing aid model. Specifically, in the condition with four interfering talkers, both pupil dilation and heart rate exhibited periods of significant elevation compared with the condition with only two interfering talkers with hearing aid A (Figs. 3A and 4A). With hearing aid B, pupil dilation was unaffected by the number of interfering talkers, while heart rate was increased with four interfering talkers, but only for the first approximately 8 sec of the 33-sec-long news clip (compared with the first 14 sec with hearing aid A), whereafter it decreased to the level observed with only two interfering talkers present. It is important to note that overall pupil dilation and heart rate were significantly higher with hearing aid A than with hearing aid B in the more difficult four interfering talkers’ condition. This was evident in both the overall level differences (parametric terms) and in the time course differences (Figs. 3D and 4D).

Both pupil dilation and heart rate traces were analyzed using GAMMs. While GAMMs have been successfully applied to pupil size data in previous research (Johns et al. 2024), their use in modeling relative heart rate traces during listening tasks remains rare, if not entirely absent. To ensure comparability, we applied consistent parameterization across models for both pupil size and heart rate and complemented this with a nonparametric test to assess differences in time courses unaffected by potential model misspecification. Visually, the model predictions closely align with the observed trajectories of pupil dilation and relative heart rate (Figs. 3 and 4). Furthermore, the difference curves derived from the GAMMs and the results from the CPA revealed conjunct regions of significant condition-related differences. This convergence suggests that effects in the time courses identified by the GAMMs are robust and not confounded by inadequate model specification.

Pupil dilation, when conditioning on the listening condition, has previously been linked to sustained exerted listening effort (Fiedler et al. 2021). Specifically, the pupil shows greater dilation for situations with higher task demands, leading to cognitive strain represented by inhibition of the parasympathetic pathway (Steinhauer et al. 2004; van der Wel & van Steenbergen 2018). We propose that the sustained increase in pupil dilation observed in the condition with four interfering talkers—particularly when using hearing aid A—reflects greater listening effort compared with the condition with only two interfering talkers. Moreover, this effort-related increase in pupil dilation appears to be attenuated by hearing aid B, as indicated by the lower dilation levels in the four interfering talkers’ condition when using hearing aid B (Fig. 3D). This suggests that hearing aid B may be more effective in mitigating the cognitive load associated with complex auditory environments.

Changes in heart rate in response to environmental factors are commonly interpreted as indicators of stress (Gaidica & Dantzer 2020). Here, we propose that the observed increase in heart rate with the number of interfering talkers (i.e., the level of background noise) for hearing aid A represents “listening stress,” which aligns with previous findings showing that various types of noise, whether in laboratory or real-world settings, modulate physiological responses measured via heart rate (Burns et al. 2016; Shoushtarian et al. 2019; Christensen et al. 2021; Sarangi & Johnson 2024). Thus, the observed increase in heart rate in the four interfering talkers’ condition, either sustained throughout the listening task with hearing aid A or occurring primarily in the early phase with hearing aid B, reflects periods of listening stress. Notably, hearing aid B appears to mitigate this effect after approximately 8 seconds of continuous listening, as heart rate returns to baseline levels.

Taken together, the systematic changes in objective listening effort and listening stress suggest contrasting benefits of hearing aid A and B. We propose that this is driven by the fact that hearing aid B includes motion detection in the automatic adaptation of noise reduction and directionality. Namely, in contrast to hearing aid A, hearing aid B optimizes the perceived SNR considering both the environment and the motion of the wearer. This means that for the speech localization task, hearing aid B provides a less strict directionality and noise suppression when head rotations are detected, while in the comprehension task, the noise reduction will be enhanced, and the directionality will be focused on the target. In turn, this optimizes the perceived SNR of all talkers (localization task) or of the target talker (comprehension task). On the other hand, hearing aid A only considers acoustic cues from the listening environment and, thus, must provide a balanced benefit regardless of head motion. This potentially results in less favorable SNR enhancement during the speech comprehension task because the transition from one listening intent to another is not supported by adaptive noise management.

It is interesting that the self-reported effort, engagement, and sound quality with the two tested hearing aid models revealed only a significant difference in engagement. Namely, participants rated that they were more engaged with the news clips in the speech comprehension task when listening with hearing aid B, which is consistent with less listening effort and stress being exerted. However, the self-reports were only given once after each experimental block; they might not capture trial-wise fluctuations in either task difficulty or listening motivation.

We also found a trial-wise agreement between absolute pupil dilation and heart rate, pointing toward the possibility that a common mechanism affects both measures (C. A. Wang, Baird, et al. 2018). Specifically, on trials with higher pupil dilation, there was a tendency to also have a higher heart rate, which corroborates the main findings of this study. The fact that pupil dilation and heart rate are associated potentially means that future studies under even more naturalistic conditions or even field trials could apply heart rate measures in combination with knowledge of the listening condition to assess listening stress in the real-world (Andersson et al. 2023) as a proxy for listening effort.

Similar localization experiments have been used to evaluate hearing aid performance before, but without applying objective outcome measures. For instance, Brimijoin et al. (2014) evaluated the impact of hearing aid microphone mode (omnidirectional and directional) on performance in an auditory orienting task, where participants needed to locate the female talker among some interfering male talkers. The study found that participants performed better in the task when using the omnidirectional mode, which provided greater awareness and detection of sound sources from targets located more than 90° away from the front of the listener. Conversely, an improved performance with the directional mode was found, which facilitated better sound localization toward target angles closer to the center, thus consistent with the results presented here.

Limitations

A total of 32 participants completed the experiment; however, due to poor data, we had to remove eight participants for the pupil dilation analysis and 12 participants for the heart rate analysis. This is most likely due to the fact that participants in the present study were less restricted in their movement, thus introducing more noise and artifacts, which also suggests that best practices in how to filter and process pupillometry data might be too conservative for more ecologically valid setups (Winn et al. 2018). However, four of the eight excluded participants completed the experiment while pupillometry was not recorded due to technical issues with the VR glasses and associated software, and data from one of the eight participants was removed from further analysis due to extreme baseline levels (Fig. 2), leaving the number of participants rejected due to poor pupillometry data to three. For the heart rate analysis, the 12 participants who were removed all had too infrequent or poor sampling of the photoplethysmogram signal, which might have been caused by small movements of the hand, potentially carried over from the speech localization task, reducing the contact between skin and the Empatica device sensors. While the proportion of excluded participants is relatively high, the number of participants remaining for each modality (24 for pupil data, 20 for heart rate data) is still comparable to similar studies (Johns et al. 2024; Sarangi & Johnson 2024).

Contrary to the finding that objective listening effort was sensitive to the hearing aid model, the self-reported listening effort was not. However, participants only evaluated their perceived listening effort at the end of each experimental block, thereby neglecting fluctuations from changing task difficulty and rendering the self-report less momentary. As the largest difference in objective listening effort and stress was observed for the more difficult four interfering talker condition, we expect that the reason this was not revealed on the self-reports is due to low sensitivity. Alternatively, as previously argued (Seeman & Sims 2015), participants might have relied on their perception of task difficulty, which was the same for the two experimental blocks, when rating their perceived listening effort, or not have been consciously aware of their physiological responses.

CONCLUSION

Virtual reality, combined with unobtrusive objective outcome measures (here, heart rate), is a viable method for evaluating hearing aids under naturalistic conditions that allow for movement in a virtual sound environment. Listening stress, quantified by relative heart rate, was sensitive to both task difficulty and hearing aid technology, aligning with sustained listening effort quantified here by pupillometry. Wrist-worn wearables can be used as an unobtrusive alternative to traditional, more obtrusive recordings of physiological arousal and effort in listening tasks using functional near-infrared spectroscopy, pupillometry, or electroencephalography, for example, in longitudinal ambulatory studies or clinical trials lacking resources for medical-grade equipment.
