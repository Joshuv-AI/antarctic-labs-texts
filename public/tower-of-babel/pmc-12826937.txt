Integrating Vascular Aging and Genetic Risk: The Combined Impact of Estimated Pulse Wave Velocity and Genetic Predisposition on Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Chang Sheng, Rui Zhou, Hongcai Wang, Jin Ling, Guoqiang Lin, Pu Yang, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEstimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV), a noninvasive marker of arterial stiffness, reflects vascular aging and has been associated with increased coronary artery disease (CAD) risk. However, the interplay between ePWV and genetic factors, including polygenic risk score (PRS) and apolipoprotein E genotypes, in determining CAD susceptibility remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed data from the HRS (Health and Retirement Study), including 5856 participants (4741 White and 1115 Black individuals) without baseline CAD. ePWV was calculated, and genetic risk was assessed using PRS and apolipoprotein E genotyping. Cox proportional hazards models evaluated the associations between ePWV, genetic predisposition, and CAD incidence, with stratified analyses by race and sex. Mediation analyses explored underlying mechanisms.ResultsElevated ePWV (≥10 m/s) was significantly associated with increased CAD risk (hazard ratio [HR], 1.50 [95% CI, 1.25–1.81], P<0.001). Among White participants, both high PRS and elevated ePWV independently predicted CAD, with a potential mitigating interaction effect. In Black participants, PRS was not significantly associated with CAD risk, whereas apolipoprotein E ε4 carriers with high ePWV exhibited a markedly increased risk (HR, 2.62 [95% CI 1.09–6.32], P=0.032). Sex‐stratified analyses indicated that White women with high PRS and high ePWV had a lower‐than‐expected CAD risk. Additionally, changes in total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol mediated the association between ePWV and CAD.ConclusionsVascular aging and genetic predisposition interact in complex ways to influence CAD risk, with notable variations across racial and sex subgroups. These findings highlight the need for personalized prevention strategies incorporating both vascular health and genetic risk profiling.Keywords: apolipoprotein E, coronary artery disease, estimated pulse wave velocity, polygenic risk scoreSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Precision Medicine

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, driven by a complex interplay of physiological and genetic factors. 1 Identifying these factors and understanding their combined effects is essential for developing effective prevention strategies and mitigating the global health burden of CAD.

Emerging evidence suggests that vascular aging plays a crucial role in cardiovascular health. 2 Estimated pulse wave velocity (ePWV), a noninvasive marker of arterial stiffness, is recognized as an indicator of vascular aging and has been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Arterial stiffness has also been linked to renal function decline, underscoring its systemic impact. 7 In CAD, higher ePWV has been linked to increased all‐cause mortality, particularly when it exceeds 11.15 m/s. 8 The Framingham Heart Study demonstrated that elevated PWV is associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk in the general population, supporting the clinical utility of arterial stiffness measurements for cardiovascular risk assessment in community‐based populations. 9 In addition, previous studies have investigated the incorporation of ePWV into existing cardiovascular risk prediction models and have reported significant improvements in predictive performance. 10 However, the extent to which ePWV independently predicts CAD risk, and whether genetic predisposition modifies this association, remains unclear.

Genetic factors, particularly polygenic risk score (PRS) and specific genetic variants such as apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotypes, may further contribute to CAD susceptibility. Multiple studies have highlighted the importance of PRS in predicting CAD risk. For example, a blood lipid‐related PRS was independently associated with CAD susceptibility 11 and CAD incidence. 12 Similarly, the ApoE gene, which exists in 3 major types (ε2, ε3, and ε4), has been linked to lipid metabolism and cardiovascular outcomes. The ApoE ε2 allele is associated with lower low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, reduced CAD risk, and longer survival, 13 whereas the ApoE ε4 allele is linked to an increased risk of CAD and hyperlipidemia. 14 Despite these insights, the interactions between vascular aging (ePWV) and genetic risk (PRS, ApoE) in shaping CAD incidence remain poorly understood, particularly across diverse racial and sex‐based subgroups.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between ePWV and CAD risk while considering the modifying effects of genetic predisposition. Specifically, we examined the joint influence of ePWV and PRS on CAD incidence in both White and Black populations and assessed the role of ApoE genotypes in modulating this relationship. Furthermore, we explored potential sex differences in these associations. Additionally, we conducted mediation analyses to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

By integrating vascular aging and genetic risk factors, this study provides novel insights into the pathophysiology of CAD and enhances our understanding of how these factors interact to shape disease susceptibility. Our findings may inform precision prevention strategies, particularly for high‐risk populations.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The HRS (Health and Retirement Study) is sponsored by the National Institute on Aging (grant number NIA U01AG009740) and is conducted by the University of Michigan. The data can be accessed and obtained upon reasonable application through the official HRS website: https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/.

Patient and Public Involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Design and Participants

We analyzed clinical and genetic data from the HRS, a biennial longitudinal cohort study of a nationally representative sample of noninstitutionalized US residents aged >50 years. Detailed study design and logistics have been described elsewhere. 15 , 16 Briefly, the HRS collects economic, social, and health data, including biosamples for genomic and biomarker analyses. Since 2006, the HRS has adopted a mixed‐mode follow‐up design, in which half of the participants were randomly assigned to receive an enhanced face‐to‐face interview, complete a psychosocial questionnaire, and provide saliva samples for DNA extraction in 2006, and the remaining half completed these procedures in 2008. This rotating design allows for the periodic collection of richer biomarker and psychosocial data while maintaining biennial follow‐up for the entire cohort.

The HRS was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan, with informed consent obtained from all participants or their representatives. This study focused on participants with available genetic data. Blood biomarker data were classified as sensitive health information, requiring a formal application for access (https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/). As all data were deidentified before access, this study was deemed exempt from review by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Baseline data were obtained from nonproxy participants between 2006 and 2012, as biomarker, genetic, and PRS data were collected during this period. Participants with CAD at baseline (N=592) were excluded. Additional exclusions were applied for missing data on sociodemographic, health behavior, or health condition (N=1717). Outliers were further excluded based on age≤50 years (N=314), body mass index<16 kg/m2 (N=9), or survey weight=0 (N=229). Only participants with baseline ePWV data and follow‐up data for CAD incidence (2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, or 2020) were included. After exclusions, the final analytic sample comprised 5856 participants, including 4741 White and 1115 Black individuals. The sample selection process is depicted in Figure 1.

Participants with missing covariate data were further excluded: missing sociodemographic information (N=277), missing health behavior data (N=541), or missing health condition information (N=899). Coronary Artery Disease

CAD was identified according to responses to the following questions in each wave: “Since [last interview date] has a doctor told you that you had a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” [If so] “Did you have a heart attack, myocardial infarction, angina, or chest pains due to your heart?” In the 2010 to 2020 waves, participants who responded “Yes” to the 2 questions were considered to have CAD. The outcome was time to incident CAD, calculated as the number of years from the interview date to the date reporting CAD. Participants were censored at the date of the last contact or by the end of the follow‐up period, whichever came first.

Polygenic Risk Score for CAD

The HRS Polygenic risk Score for CAD was derived from the 2011 genome‐wide association studies results. 24 It encompassed all CAD‐associated single nucleotide variants shared between the genome‐wide association studies meta‐analysis and the HRS genetic data set. Each overlapping single nucleotide variants was assigned a weight based on its effect size from the genome‐wide association studies meta‐analysis, and the PRS was computed as the standardized weighted sum of these single nucleotide variants for Black and White participants. To stratify genetic risk, we divided the PRS into 3 categories: low (bottom quintile), intermediate (quintiles 2–4), and high (top quintile), a widely adopted method in epidemiological research. 25 , 26 , 27

ApoE Genotyping

HRS participants who provided consent and successfully completed salivary DNA collection in 2006 (Phase 1), 2008 (Phase 2), 2010 (Phase 3), or 2012 (Phase 4) were considered eligible for inclusion. Comprehensive details on DNA collection, genotyping methods, and quality control procedures can be found elsewhere (https://hrsonline.isr.umich.edu/sitedocs/genetics/HRS2_qc_report_SEPT2013.pdf). Genotyping was performed using predesigned TaqMan allelic discrimination single nucleotide variant assays C___3084793 (rs429358) and C___904973 (rs7412) to determine ApoE genotypes. In accordance with standard analytical approaches, ApoE genotype classification was binary: carriers (1 or 2 copies) versus noncarriers (no copies) for both the ε4 and ε2 variants. 28 , 29 Furthermore, individuals homozygous for the ApoE ε3 allele (ε3/ε3 genotype) were designated as the reference group. The ApoE ε4 category included participants with ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4 genotypes, and the ApoE ε2 group comprised those with ε2/ε3 or ε2/ε2 genotypes.

Covariates

The covariates included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, wealth, race, marital status, and education), health status (history of hypertension and diabetes), and health behaviors (smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, and physical activity). In addition to self‐reported measures, the model incorporated cardiovascular disease‐related blood biomarkers obtained from dried blood spots collected via finger prick during in‐person visits. These biomarkers included the total cholesterol‐to‐high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol ratio (lipid metabolism), glycated hemoglobin A1c (glucose control), cystatin C (global kidney function), and CRP (C‐reactive protein; systemic low‐grade inflammation). Additionally, 2 psychological indicators—Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale and sleep status—were included. 30

Statistical Analysis

Discrete variables were presented as counts (percentage [%]), abd continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) for nonnormally distributed data, and as means±SD) for normally distributed data. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. One‐way ANOVA was used to compare means of continuous variables across multiple groups, with Tukey’s test applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. We applied cumulative incidence curves with log‐rank tests, followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate time to CAD across ePWV levels, reporting hazard ratios (HR) and CIs while adjusting for potential confounders. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were detected for any covariate or the global model (all P>0.05). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic variables, including sex, race, marital status, education, wealth, and behavioral factors such as body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity. Model 3 further accounted for cardiometabolic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, total cholesterol (TC)/HDL‐C, and cystatin C (as a marker of renal function). Model 4 additionally included psychosocial and sleep‐related variables, namely Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale and sleep status, to capture broader health determinants that may influence cardiovascular outcomes. These models were selected based on prior evidence demonstrating the influence of behavioral, metabolic, renal, and psychosocial factors on vascular aging and cardiovascular risk and are in line with established modeling strategies used in studies of arterial stiffness and cardiovascular disease. 31 , 32 , 33 To evaluate the overall association between PRS categories and CAD incidence, we computed global P values based on Wald tests from Cox proportional hazards models. These P values assess whether there is a statistically significant difference in CAD risk across all levels of the categorical PRS variable.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the association between ePWV and CAD, including adjustment for sampling weights, the use of an alternative ePWV estimation equation, and multiple imputation to address missing covariate data. Multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R, and the first imputed data set was used for analysis. 34 , 35 , 36

To assess the role of genetic susceptibility in the association between ePWV and CAD risk, we examined both multiplicative and additive interactions between genetic susceptibility and ePWV. 27 Multiplicative interaction was assessed by including a product term between 2 categorical exposures in multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with adjustments for relevant sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates. Additive interaction was evaluated by calculating the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), using model‐derived regression coefficients and variance–covariance matrices. Significance was determined using Wald tests, and the analytic approach followed standard methods for additive interaction in time‐to‐event data. 37 In addition to analyzing the full sample using a fully adjusted model, we performed stratified analyses by sex and race. Given the lack of a significant association between PRS and CAD in Black participants, some analyses were restricted to White participants. 27 , 38

For mediation analysis, we selected a subgroup of participants with repeated blood biomarker measurements at both baseline and follow‐up. Causal mediation analysis was conducted to estimate the average causal mediation effect and average direct effect, using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 simulations to derive 95% CIs. 39 Using these biomarker values and their changes over time as mediators, we explored potential mechanisms underlying the association between ePWV and CAD. Changes in biomarker values were defined as the difference between the second measurement and the baseline measurement. All tests were 2 sided with a significance level of 0.05, and all statistical analyses were conducted in R 4.2.1.

RESULTS

Study Population Characteristics

The final analytical sample included 5856 participants, comprising 4741 White and 1115 Black individuals (Figure 1; Table 1). The participants had a mean age of 62.48±10.46 years, and 57.45% were female. The median follow‐up duration was 10.00 years (interquartile range, 8.00–12.00). During the follow‐up period, 8.57% (502 out of 5856) of participants developed CAD. Participants with high baseline ePWV (n=2476) exhibited significantly older age (70.47 versus 56.63 years, P<0.001), elevated blood pressure (mean blood pressure: 108.07 versus 94.07 mm Hg, P<0.001; systolic blood pressure: 142.47 versus 119.25 mm Hg, P<0.001; diastolic blood pressure: 85.13 versus 77.28 mm Hg, P<0.001), lower lipid profiles (TC/HDL‐C ratio: 3.64 versus 3.68, P=0.010), lower TC (220.23 versus 236.57 mg/dL, P<0.001), lower HDL‐C (64.19 versus 67.99 mg/dL, P<0.001), higher cystatin C (0.66 versus 0.55 mg/L, P<0.001), higher glycated hemoglobin (5.87% versus 5.74%, P<0.001), and lower Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale scores (1.40 versus 1.54, P<0.001) compared with those with low ePWV.

Additionally, individuals with high ePWV had a higher prevalence of hypertension (64.66% versus 42.54%, P<0.001) and diabetes (19.47% versus 16.60%, P=0.005). Notably, the proportion of individuals with body mass index≥30 was lower in the high ePWV group (41.16% versus 45.95%, P<0.001), whereas the proportion of individuals who rarely or never engaged in moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity was higher (17.08% versus 10.62%, P<0.001). Association Between ePWV and CAD Risk

The distribution of ePWV is presented in Figure S1. Cumulative incidence curves confirmed that elevated ePWV was associated with an earlier onset and higher incidence of CAD (Figure 2). Further analysis revealed that participants with ePWV ≥10 m/s had a significantly higher risk of CAD compared with those with ePWV <10 m/s across all models (HR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.25–1.81], P<0.001; Model 4) (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses using an alternative ePWV equation derived from a European population yielded consistent results, reinforcing the robustness of these findings (Table S1). Additional sensitivity analyses, including adjustment for sampling weights and multiple imputation for missing covariates, also demonstrated consistent findings (Table S1).

Effect of ePWV on CAD Incidence in Different Polygenic Risk Score Groups

The distribution of the PRS is illustrated in Figure S2, showing a normal distribution. Among White individuals in the low and intermediate PRS groups, participants with ePWV ≥10 m/s exhibited a higher incidence of CAD compared with those with ePWV <10 m/s (Figures 3A and 3B). However, in the high PRS group among White individuals and across all PRS groups among Black individuals, ePWV was not significantly associated with CAD incidence (Figure 3C through 3F).

Sex‐stratified analysis further demonstrated that, among women, elevated ePWV correlated with earlier and higher CAD incidence in both the low and intermediate PRS groups. Among men, this association was observed only in the intermediate PRS group (Figure S3A through S3F). These findings suggest that the incidence curves for CAD differ by PRS, race, and sex.

Association Between Polygenic Risk Score and CAD Risk in Different Racial Groups

We examined the association between PRS and CAD in the overall population, as well as separately in White and Black participants (Table S2). In the total population, PRS as a continuous variable was borderline significant for CAD risk (HR, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.99–1.18], P=0.083). When analyzed categorically, the high PRS group exhibited a slightly increased risk of CAD compared with the low PRS group (HR, 1.31 95% CI, 1.00–1.71], P=0.052).

Race‐specific analyses revealed a significant positive association between PRS and CAD among White individuals (HR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.01–1.23], P=0.025), with the high PRS group demonstrating a significantly increased CAD risk (HR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.04–1.90], P=0.029). In contrast, no significant association was found between PRS and CAD risk among Black participants (P>0.05). These results suggest that PRS may have greater predictive value for CAD in White populations, whereas its applicability in Black populations requires further investigation. 27

Joint Association of ePWV and Polygenic Risk Score With CAD by Race

In the overall population, compared with individuals with both low PRS and ePWV <10 m/s, those with high PRS but ePWV <10 m/s exhibited an increased risk of CAD (HR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.09–2.34], P=0.017), as did those with low PRS but ePWV ≥10 m/s (HR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.07–2.42], P=0.023). The interaction between ePWV and PRS was statistically significant with an RERI score of −0.54 (P for additive interaction=0.046), suggesting that their combined effect on CAD risk was not simply additive but rather exhibited a potential mitigating effect (Table S3).

This pattern was more pronounced among White participants. Notably, in White individuals, the CAD risk in the high PRS and ePWV ≥10 m/s group was significantly lower than in the low PRS and ePWV <10 m/s group (HR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.29–1.00], P=0.048), further suggesting a possible buffering effect (Table 3). Individuals with high genetic risk may have already been exposed to certain pathophysiological mechanisms, such as vascular aging and inflammation. When elevated ePWV is also present, these mechanisms may be detected earlier, potentially triggering compensatory responses or clinical interventions that ultimately reduce the risk of disease onset. However, in Black individuals, there was no interaction between PRS and ePWV (Table S4).

Joint Association of ePWV and Polygenic Risk Score With CAD Among White Individuals of Different Sexes

To further explore the combined impact of ePWV and PRS on CAD risk, we conducted sex‐stratified analyses in White individuals (Table 4). In women with ePWV <10 m/s, a high PRS significantly increased CAD risk (HR, 2.37 [95% CI, 1.28–4.39], P=0.006). Conversely, in women with low PRS, ePWV ≥10 m/s was associated with an increased CAD risk (HR, 2.16 [95% CI, 1.13–4.16], P=0.021). Interestingly, when both ePWV and PRS were elevated, CAD risk was reduced (HR, 0.40 [95% CI, 0.17–0.91], P=0.029). This trend was consistent with findings from the overall White population and the total study population. However, in men, no significant interaction was observed between ePWV and PRS (P for additive interaction=0.357). These results suggest a sex‐specific joint effect of ePWV and genetic predisposition on CAD risk.

Impact of ePWV on CAD Incidence in Different ApoE Genotypes

We first analyzed the distribution of ApoE genotypes (Figure S4), which was consistent with previous studies. 40 Compared with the ApoE ε2 group, the ApoE ε4 group exhibited higher TC/HDL‐C and TC levels, whereas HDL‐C levels remained similar (Figure S5A through S5C).

Subsequently, we found that among White individuals in the ApoE ε3 and ApoE ε4 groups, ePWV ≥10 m/s was associated with earlier onset and higher incidence of CAD. However, among Black individuals, this trend was observed only in the ApoE ε4 group, whereas in the ApoE ε2 and ApoE ε3 groups, ePWV had no significant impact on CAD incidence (Figure S6A through S6F).

Furthermore, we performed sex‐stratified analyses. Regardless of sex, individuals in the ApoE ε3 and ApoE ε4 groups with ePWV ≥10 m/s had a significantly higher CAD incidence than those with ePWV <10 m/s. In contrast, in the ApoE ε2 group, ePWV did not influence CAD incidence in either men or women (Figure S7A through S7F). These results suggest that the association between ePWV and CAD incidence varies across ApoE genotypes, racial groups, and sex subgroups.

Joint Association of ePWV and ApoE ε4 Genotype With CAD Across Racial Groups

We further examined the combined effect of ePWV and ApoE ε4 genotype on CAD risk and explored potential racial differences in their interaction.

In the overall population and among White individuals ApoE ε4 noncarriers, participants with ePWV ≥10 m/s had a significantly increased CAD risk (overall: HR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.21–1.87], P<0.001; White: HR, 1.64[ 95% CI, 1.29–2.09], P<0.001). Additionally, we did not observe a significant interaction between ePWV and ApoE ε4 in the overall or White populations (P for additive interaction >0.05) (Tables S5 and S6).

Among Black individuals, however, a distinct pattern emerged. ApoE ε4 carriers with high ePWV (≥10 m/s) exhibited a markedly increased CAD risk (HR, 2.62 [95% CI, 1.09–6.32], P=0.032), with an RERI of 0.90 (P for additive interaction=0.008). This suggests a potential synergistic effect between high ePWV and ApoE ε4 genotype in Black individuals, where elevated ePWV may have a more pronounced impact on CAD risk in ApoE ε4 carriers (Table 5). These results indicate that the interaction between ApoE ε4 and ePWV on CAD risk varies by race.

Joint Association of ePWV and ApoE ε4 Genotype With CAD in Black Individuals by Sex

Further sex‐stratified analysis among Black individuals (Table S7) revealed that in Black women, ApoE ε4 carriers with ePWV ≥10 m/s had a relatively high CAD risk (HR, 2.25 [95% CI, 0.72–7.10], P=0.165), although the result did not reach statistical significance.

In contrast, in Black men, ApoE ε4 carriers with high ePWV had a more pronounced increase in CAD risk (HR, 3.18 [95% CI, 0.72–14.05], P=0.126), with a significant interaction effect (RERI=1.37, P for additive interaction=0.042). These findings suggest that Black men may be more susceptible to the combined effect of high ePWV and ApoE ε4 genotype, potentially amplifying CAD risk. This underscores the need for tailored risk assessment in specific populations, such as Black men with high ePWV and ApoE ε4 carrier status, to improve cardiovascular disease prevention.

Joint Association of ePWV and ApoE ε2 Genotype With CAD Across Racial Groups

We next analyzed the interaction between ePWV and ApoE ε2 genotype on CAD risk in different racial groups.

In the overall population and among White individuals, although ePWV ≥10 m/s was associated with a higher CAD risk (overall: HR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.25–1.87], P<0.001; White: HR, 1.55 [95% CI, 1.23–1.94], P<0.001), no significant interaction was observed between ApoE ε2 genotype and ePWV (P for additive interaction >0.05) (Tables S8 and S9). Similarly, no significant interaction was found in Black individuals (P for additive interaction >0.05) (Table S10).

Mediating Role of Blood‐Based Biomarkers

To further investigate potential mechanisms linking ePWV to CAD risk, we performed mediation analyses with blood‐based biomarkers (Table 6). Findings were consistent with partial mediation of the association between ePWV and CAD by changes in TC (ΔTC) and HDL‐C (ΔHDL‐C), accounting for 5% (average causal mediation effect=0.17, P=0.010) and 7% (average causal mediation effect =0.24, P=0.006) of the total effect, respectively. Although HDL‐C is generally cardioprotective, the observed positive mediation effect of ΔHDL‐C may reflect compensatory increases in dysfunctional HDL particles under conditions of arterial stiffness. Prior evidence suggests that vascular dysfunction can impair HDL’s antiatherogenic properties (eg, reduced cholesterol efflux capacity) even when circulating levels rise, potentially explaining this paradoxical association. 41 , 42 In contrast, ΔTC’s mediation was consistent with established pathways linking lipid metabolism to atherosclerosis progression. Other biomarkers, including glycated hemoglobin, CRP, and cystatin C, did not show evidence of significant mediation effects (Table 6). These findings suggest that monitoring longitudinal changes in lipid profiles may provide superior predictive value for CAD risk assessment compared with static measurements.

DISCUSSION

Principal Findings

This study provides compelling evidence that ePWV, a marker of vascular aging, is significantly associated with an increased risk of CAD. Notably, this relationship is influenced by genetic predisposition, including PRS and ApoE genotypes, with racial and sex differences in these associations. Specifically, we observed a more pronounced joint effect of ePWV and PRS in White female individuals, whereas the combined effect of ePWV and the ApoE ε4 genotype was stronger in Black male individuals. Sex‐specific differences observed in the interaction between ePWV and PRS may suggest underlying biological or behavioral heterogeneity. In women, the significant interaction suggests that vascular aging and genetic predisposition may jointly influence CAD risk in a nonlinear or compensatory manner, possibly due to earlier clinical recognition, differential treatment responses, or sex‐related hormonal and inflammatory pathways. In contrast, the absence of interaction in men suggests that the relationship between vascular aging and genetic risk may be more additive and less modulated by compensatory mechanisms. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in cardiovascular risk prediction. The interaction between ePWV and APOE ε4 on CAD risk seen only in Black participants may suggest racial differences in genetic architecture, environmental exposures, and health disparities. These factors may influence how vascular aging and genetic risk interact, highlighting the importance of ancestry‐specific analysis in cardiovascular risk prediction. Mediation analyses further suggest that lipid profiles partially mediate the relationship between ePWV and CAD risk. Notably, longitudinal changes in total cholesterol and HDL‐C levels appear to be more sensitive indicators of arterial stiffness‐related cardiovascular risk than static measurements, highlighting the importance of dynamic lipid monitoring in clinical practice.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Our findings align with previous research indicating that arterial stiffness, as measured by ePWV, is associated with cardiovascular mortality and cardiovascular disease outcomes. 43 Moreover, prior studies have demonstrated that elevated ePWV is linked to increased all‐cause mortality in patients with CAD. 8 However, these studies primarily focused on clinical outcomes among individuals with existing CAD, without specifically addressing the role of ePWV in the risk of CAD onset. Our study is among the first to comprehensively investigate the association between elevated ePWV and the incidence of CAD, reinforcing the role of vascular aging as a critical determinant of CAD risk.

Beyond physiological risk factors, genetic predisposition is also closely associated with CAD risk. 24 , 44 , 45 Given that genetic factors are determined at birth and remain stable throughout life, independent of age or risk factor levels, an increasing number of studies suggest that PRS assessment could facilitate early risk prediction and the identification of high‐risk individuals for preventive interventions. 46 A large‐scale cohort study based on the UK Biobank has demonstrated a significant association between CAD PRS and CAD incidence, 47 a finding that aligns with our study. Furthermore, our results extend these observations by identifying a stronger association among White individuals. Racial differences in genetic predisposition to CAD have also been observed in other cardiovascular disease studies. For instance, a previous study using data from the HRS found that higher genetic risk, as quantified by PRS, was independently associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction in White participants but not in Black individuals. 27 This disparity may stem from differences in the genetic architecture of CAD across racial groups.

In our study, the combined effect of high CAD PRS and high ePWV on CAD risk was less pronounced than the sum of their individual effects, particularly in White women. This suggests that in the presence of a high genetic risk, the impact of vascular aging may be attenuated, possibly due to physiological compensatory mechanisms or other unidentified factors. The underlying mechanisms, however, warrant further investigation and validation in future studies. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies investigating the interaction between CAD PRS and traditional clinical risk factors. For instance, individuals with high CAD PRS were found to exhibit a reduced impact of type 2 diabetes, elevated body mass index, and increased CRP on CAD risk. 47 These observations suggest that genetic predisposition may modify the effects of physiological and metabolic risk factors on CAD development.

Regarding ApoE genotypes, extensive research has demonstrated the interaction between ApoE polymorphisms and various lifestyle and environmental factors in cognitive function, memory, and Alzheimer’s disease. 48 , 49 , 50 In the context of cardiovascular disease, the ApoE ε4 allele has been shown to interact with smoking, enhancing the risk of CAD. 51 Interestingly, the increased risk of CAD associated with smoking is attenuated upon smoking cessation among ApoE ε4 carriers. 52 Notably, sex differences in these interactions have been reported, with ApoE ε4 primarily amplifying the smoking‐related risk of CAD in women. 53

A study involving White European participants from the UK Biobank investigated the potential interaction between lifestyle factors (eg, high physical activity levels and increased fish and polyunsaturated fatty acid intake) and ApoE ε4 genotype in modulating CAD risk. 54 While these lifestyle factors were associated with a reduced risk of CAD, no significant interaction with ApoE genotypes was observed. 54 Similarly, in our study, we did not find significant interactions between ApoE ε2 or ApoE ε4 genotypes and ePWV in the overall population. However, our study extends previous findings by demonstrating that the joint effect of ApoE ε4 and high ePWV is particularly pronounced in Black male individuals. These results suggest that gene‐physiological interactions influencing CAD risk may vary across racial and sex groups, underscoring the importance of precision medicine approaches in cardiovascular risk assessment.

Potential Mechanisms

The interplay between ePWV and genetic risk in CAD development is likely driven by multiple mechanisms. Increased arterial stiffness, reflected by high ePWV, induces hemodynamic changes such as elevated pulse pressure, which contribute to endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis progression, and ultimately CAD. 55 Genetic predisposition exacerbates these effects by influencing lipid metabolism, inflammation, and arterial wall integrity. 56 Furthermore, inflammation and oxidative stress may serve as common biological pathways linking genetic susceptibility, vascular aging, and CAD progression. 57 , 58 Future research should explore additional mediators, such as immune activation and endothelial repair mechanisms, to gain deeper insights into these complex interactions.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This study has several strengths. First, we used a large, diverse cohort with extensive genetic and vascular health data, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of interactions between ePWV and genetic factors. Second, we incorporated race‐ and sex‐specific analyses, revealing important differences in CAD risk stratification. Third, mediation analysis provides mechanistic insights into the role of lipid profiles in linking vascular aging and CAD.

However, our study also has limitations. First, although ePWV has been validated as a surrogate marker of vascular aging, it may not fully capture the complexity of arterial stiffness compared with directly measured parameters such as cfPWV. 5 , 22 Second, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, particularly in racial subgroup analyses. Third, our findings may not be generalizable to other populations, particularly those of Asian ancestry. Moreover, this study needs to involve younger populations to further assess its generalizability. In addition, due to the lag in the study design, we were unable to adopt more representative methods for PRS construction. 59 , 60 This may have attenuated the differences observed among Black participants and may also have contributed to the sex‐based disparities in CAD risk. Finally, we did not include chronological age as a separate covariate in our models, as our primary objective was to evaluate ePWV as a composite indicator of vascular aging. However, this modeling choice may limit the ability to disentangle the independent effect of age on CAD risk and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future research should validate these findings using diverse populations and direct arterial stiffness measurements.

Further investigations should explore the potential benefits of integrating ePWV and genetic risk assessments into precision medicine approaches for CAD prevention. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and the effectiveness of targeted interventions, such as lifestyle modifications or pharmacologic strategies, in individuals with high vascular aging and genetic risk.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings underscore the importance of considering both ePWV and genetic factors in CAD risk assessment, particularly in racial and genetically diverse populations. These results have potential implications for precision prevention strategies, highlighting the need for tailored interventions to mitigate CAD risk in high‐risk subgroups.
