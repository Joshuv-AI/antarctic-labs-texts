Bioefficacy of Indian Beauveria bassiana and Lecanicillium lecanii isolates against Lipaphis erysimi (Hemiptera: Aphididae) and their safety to Apis cerana (Hymenoptera: Apidae)
Authors: Abhinandan Yadav, Anjumoni Devee, Ataur Rahman, Awaneesh Kumar, Shimantini Borkataki, Jehan Zeb, Mohammed E Gad, Mohamed A Awad, Mohamed M Baz, Abdelfattah Selim, Mohammed H Alruhaili, Hattan S Gattan, Mohammed E M Esmaeil, Haytham Senbill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental issues associated with the excessive use of chemical substances have received increasing attention globally, particularly regarding their effects on non-target/beneficial insects such as pollinators. To overcome such drawbacks, alternative control methods are being increasingly evaluated. A study was conducted to assess the effectiveness of two Indian biopesticidal isolates against the mustard aphid, Lipaphis erysimi, on rapeseed crops and to investigate their potential risks to the pollinator honeybee, Apis cerana. Laboratory bioassays showed that Beauveria bassiana AS-4220 was the most effective material against L. erysimi after 24 h (LC50 = 95.5 ppm); however, neem seed oil was the most toxic to A. cerana after the same time interval (LC50 = 185.4 ppm). The highest reduction in the aphid population under field conditions was observed with B. bassiana AS-4220 at 3 × 108 spores mL− 1 ten days after field treatment (71.9%), followed by B. bassiana AS-4220 at 2 × 108 spores mL−1 ten days after field treatment (70.7%). The highest PT (persistent toxicity index) and RPT (relative persistent toxicity) values were observed for B. bassiana at 3 × 108 spores mL− 1 (799.98 and 1.06, respectively). The benefit: cost ratio among the treatments was highest for B. bassiana at 3 × 108 spores mL− 1 (4.90:1). However, the highest honeybee mortality was observed following treatment with neem seed oil at 3 mL L− 1 and B. bassiana at 3 × 108 spores mL− 1 ten days of treatment (100%). No significant inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity was observed in honeybees 24 h after treatment. The study demonstrates that entomopathogenic fungi effectively control L. erysimi while posing some risk to A. cerana, particularly at higher concentrations, necessitating careful timing of application . Therefore, these treatments may be used to manage L. erysimi in rapeseed crops while minimizing effects on A. cerana bees, provided that application is scheduled during periods of low foraging activity.Keywords: Biopesticides, India, Myco-insecticides, The turnip aphid, The eastern honeybee, AcetylcholinesteraseSubject terms: Biotechnology, Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences, Zoology

Introduction

Indian mustard (Brassica juncea) is a valuable oilseed crop from the rapeseed-mustard group and is predominantly grown in the Indian subcontinent, as well as in some regions of Canada, China, Australia, and Russia1. Apart from its culinary uses, Indian mustard oil has several applications in the food, chemical, biofertilizer, and paint industries. Moreover, rapeseed is a necessary crop that improves the productivity of subsequent cereal crops (Lu et al. 2022; FAO 2022)2–5.

According to Patel, et al.6, around 50 insect species have been reported infesting Brassica spp. in India. Of these, the mustard aphid, Lipaphis erysimi (Kaltenbach) is the most harmful pest that reduces yield in rapeseed-mustard crops. The aphids’ ability to feed on phloem sap and quick reproduction contributes to their status as a global threat to crop yield. Infestation by these aphids can lead to retarded plant growth, poor seed formation, and low oil content7,8. India contributes 11.3% of the global rapeseed-mustard production; however, L. erysimi remains a major pest, causing yield losses of up to 83% under severe infestations9. The timing and severity of L. erysimi infestations on oilseed Brassicas can vary depending on the season and region. In the absence of stable and resistant Brassica cultivars, the impact of L. erysimi infestation may be even more pronounced. Furthermore, the mustard aphid transmits at least 10 plant viruses, including cabbage black ring spot, cauliflower mosaic, radish mosaic, and turnip mosaic viruses (Bragard et al. 2023).

Honeybees are vital pollinators for open-pollinated crops like rapeseed-mustard due to the abundant availability of nectar and pollen on the flowers10–12. They are primary pollinators of Brassicacrops and play a vital role in maximizing seed production as their sticky pollen requires insect vectors for transfer13,14.. Studies have shown that honeybee pollination can significantly increase the seed set and yield of crops such as cabbage, cauliflower, radish, and mustard, particularly with A. cerana pollination15. Apis cerana (Hymenoptera: Apidae) is a native bee species in East Asia and exhibits greater foraging activity at lower temperatures compared to A. mellifera, making it an important pollinator of Brassica crops in Asia16,17. This highlights the importance of understanding the unique characteristics of different bee species for effective pollination efforts18.

Entomopathogenic fungi act as sustainable alternatives to synthetic insecticides by suppressing insect pests while maintaining lower toxicity levels for non-target organisms. Among these fungi, Beauveria bassiana and Lecanicillium lecanii exhibit notable efficacy against aphids and are frequently included in integrated pest management programs. Undoubtedly, the impact of pesticides on honeybees is a growing concern for sustainable agricultural practices19,20. Moreover, it is crucial to prioritize the safety of pollinators when implementing pest control measures. Understanding the unique characteristics of different bee species, such as their foraging behavior, can improve pollination management. Likewise, choosing insecticide formulations that minimize risks to pollinators while maintaining effective pest control is necessary (Tosi et al. 2022; Sanchez-Bayo and Goka 2023)21. Through mitigating the effects of pesticides on pollinators, we can ensure a healthy balance between pest control and the preservation of natural resources22.

Although entomopathogenic fungi are increasingly utilized for aphid control, empirical data on their field performance against L. erysimi and their safety regarding the pollinator A. cerana remain scarce. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy of Indian isolates of B. bassiana and L. lecanii against L. erysimi and assess their non-target effects on A. cerana under both laboratory and field conditions.

Methods

Preparation of different concentrations of biopesticides

Three commercial biopesticides: B. bassiana, L. (= Verticillium) lecanii, and Azadirachta indica (Neem oil) were used as source solutions for the present study. The details of commercialized products used in this study are shown in Table 1. All tested products were oil-based formulations as per the manufacturer’s specifications. Prior to each experiment, spore viability was determined by evaluating its germination on potato dextrose agar (PDA). After being incubated in an incubator at 25 ± 1° C for 18 to 24 h, a minimum of 300 spores were microscopically evaluated for germination. Only those spore suspensions which had a germination rate of ≥ 90% were used for the bioassays. Spore concentrations used in the laboratory and field experiments were determined and adjusted using a hemocytometer under a light microscope (Vision 2000, Labomed, India) to obtain the desired concentrations before application. The required concentrations were prepared by diluting the stock solution with distilled water to obtain final concentrations of 50, 150, 250, 350, 450 and 500 ppm.

Source of experimental insects

The mustard aphids, L. erysimi (Hemiptera: Aphididae) were collected from naturally infested mustard crops (Brassica campestris var. toria), whereas the nurse bees of A. cerana (Hymenoptera: Apidae) were collected from the apiary of the All India Coordinated Research Project (AICRP) on honeybees, Department of Entomology, Assam Agricultural University, Jorhat-13, Assam, India. Both insect species were brought to the Apiculture Laboratory, Department of Entomology, AAU to perform the laboratory bioassays following the methods of Gupta and Rawlins (1966); Eid et al. (2011); Xavier et al. (2015). Young nurse bees were selected because they are less active than foraging worker bees, making them easier to collect and handle during laboratory bioassays.

Laboratory bioassays

Values of the lethal concentration 50 (LC50) were determined by the application of dry film residual contact of each biopesticide concentration. Briefly, thin and uniform films of different concentrations of each biopesticides were prepared by adding 1 ml of each solution on the inner surface of the Petri dishes , followed by rotation until dryness. Ten individuals of L. erysimi were released in the Petri dishes of four replications of each concentration. Simultaneously, a control set was also run using only dry film of distilled water. For avoiding desiccation, fresh leaf discs of rapeseed were kept over moist filter papers in the Petri dish to feed the aphids. Mortality rates of L. erysimi were recorded 24, 48 and 72 h post treatment, and corrected mortality was applied using the Abbott’s formula23. In a similar experiment, ten 7-days old nurses of A. cerana were released in Petri plates and covered with aerated plastic container (25 cm x 10 cm) to provide free movement of bees and to provide artificial food source (Xavier et al., 2015). Three replicates were maintained for each concentration.. Sugar solution (75 g/ one liter water) was provided to serve as food source. Petri plates were kept at room temperature (28 ± 2 °C and 75–80% RH). In parallel, a control film of distilled water was applied. The mortality of honeybees was recorded after 24, 48 and 72 h of treatment, and corrected using the Abbott’s formula23.

Mortality data obtained from both the experiments were subjected to Probit analysis according to Finney (1952) to find out the LC50 values against each biopesticides at different time of exposure and at different concentrations. Dead honeybees were surface-sterilized and incubated on PDA to confirm mycosis (fungal growth) as the cause of death. The value of relative toxicity of biopesticide was calculated by considering neem oil as standard check.

Field trials

Location, design, and layout of the experiment

Field experiments were conducted in the apiary field of AICRP on honeybees, Department of Entomology, AAU (29 ± 1°C and 87 ± 2% RH). The apiary is located at an altitude of 91.0 meters (26°45’28.053’’ N; 94°12’28.672’’ E) with an annual rainfall of around 2000 mm and relative humidity around 80%. The experiment was laid out in Randomized Block Design (RBD) consisting of 24 treatments with three replications per treatment. A total area of 392 m2 was divided into three replications and each replication was further divided into 24 plots having 12.0 m2 (3 m x 2 m) each. Interspacing between replication and between plots were 1 m and 0.75 m, respectively. A space of 0.5 m was left surrounding the whole experimental area. The design and layout of the experiment is shown in Figs. 1 and 2.

Crop selection, sowing and intercultural operations

The rapeseed crop variety TS-36 was selected as the host plant. TS-36 is a highly yielding variety (10 to 12 q/ha., with non-shattering pods, short duration (90–110 days to maturity), and average oil content of 44% known in Assam, India; moreover, it is more susceptible to aphids. Seeds of rapeseed were grown following the standard agronomic practices (Package of practices; 2015, AAU). The seeds were sown on each plot in five parallel rows with row-to-row distance of 25 cm, and plant to plant distance of 10 cm by thinning. The distance from the edge of the plot to the nearest row was 10 cm on both sides of each plot.

Application of biopesticides on the mustard aphid, L. erysimi

All the three biopesticides were applied by spraying on rapeseed crop (B. juncea ; Brassicaceae) at appearance of the pest/flowering stage (Fig. 3). The treatments were worked out on the basis of available literature and concentrations were prepared accordingly as: T1: B. bassiana @ 1 × 108 spore mL− 1; T2: B. bassiana @ 2 × 108 spore mL− 1; T3: B. bassiana @ 3 × 108 spore mL− 1; T4: L. lecanii @ 1 × 108 spore mL− 1; T5: L. lecanii @ 1.5 × 108 spore mL− 1; T6: L. lecanii @ 2 × 108 spore mL− 1; T7: Neem oil @ mL L− 1 and T8: Distilled water (Control). Polyethylene sheet partitions were erected during the spraying operation between the adjoining plots to another. Twenty-four treatments including control were performed, each of them replicated three times. Population sampling technique was applied according to for following up the L. erysimi population. Briefly, the sample of the aphids was counted in situ from randomly selected five plants of each plot out of the top ten cm portion. Relative density of the aphids per plant was recorded as well. A pre-treatment count of the aphids was performed in each plot one day before the biopesticide treatment. Post-treatment counts were performed after 1,3,5,7, and 10 days. Percentage reduction in aphid population was calculated using the formula: [ (Pre-treatment count − Post-treatment count) / Pre-treatment count ] × 100

Infestation of Lipaphis erysimi aphids on mustard flowers (A) and siliques (B).

To determine residual toxicity, a stock culture of aphids infesting rapeseed plants was maintained in the laboratory to determine the residual toxicity of the biopesticides. The plants were grown in pots (50 cm x 38 cm) following the standard agronomic practices. The top shoot portion (10–15 cm) of non-infested three random plants were selected from each plot of the field and cut after 1,3,5,7, and 10 days post-spraying and kept in specimen tube (10 cm x 3.5 cm). Three tubes were considered as one replication. In each tube, ten aphids were placed with three replications and mortality rates were recorded after 24, 48 and 72 h of exposure. Persistence toxicity (PT) values were calculated from corrected mortalities data as per the method given by Sarup, et al.24 and Singh25. By using these PT values as a base, relative persistence of toxicity (RPT) values was calculated following Saini26, Pradhan and Venkatraman27.

Application of biopesticides on the Asian honeybee, Apis cerana

Ten honeybees were released in each plot and exposed to the same treatment conditions as those applied to aphids. Mortality rates were recorded at 1,3,5,7, and 10 days post-treatment (Fig. 4). The activity of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in treated honeybees was determined following the method previously described by Hanley, et al.28. Briefly, honeybees were kept in ice (0 °C) for 30 min before dissection of the head region in the presence of Bodenstein insect Ringer’s solution29.The heads were homogenized in a fixed volume of pre-cooled phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.8). The homogenate was filtered through muslin cloth, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used as the crude acetylcholinesterase enzyme extract. Five milliliters of substrate solution (1.33 mM acetylcholine chloride) was added to 0.4 ml of crude extract, mixed, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, along with a reference tube containing substrate only. The reaction was stopped by the addition of one ml of alkaline hydroxylamine solution (prepared by mixing equal volumes of 2.5 N NaOH and 1 N hydroxylamine solution).

Foraging activity of Apis cerana bees on mustard flowers during the field evaluation of different biopesticide treatments.

One ml of citrate buffer (1 M, pH 1.4) was added to each tube, followed by the slow addition of 2 ml of ferric chloride solution (0.7 M). The final volume was adjusted to 10 ml with distilled water. The tubes were incubated for 20 min at room temperature, then filtered, and optical densities were measured at 490 nm. Protein concentration analysis was performed using the Lowry assay (Lowry et al. 1951) with BSA (bovine serum albumin) used as the protein standard. The activity of AChE was then normalized to the total protein concentration in each sample and expressed as micromoles of acetylcholine hydrolyzed per minute per milligram of protein. All assays were performed in triplicate. AChE activity was death with as one unit corresponding to one µmol of acetylcholine hydrolyzed/minute/insect head/mg protein (Fig. 5).

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity assay in Apis cerana bees collected from biopesticide-treated plots, showing the laboratory determination of enzyme inhibition.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting statistical analysis on the data, we verified that it was normally distributed by applying the Shapiro–Wilk test and that variance between samples was homogeneously distributed by employing Levene’s test. The data was analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in a randomized block design (RBD) using the Web AgriStat program, v. 1.0 (ICAR Research Complex for Goa, Old Goa 403402, India). Comparisons of treatment means were conducted by using the least significant difference (LSD) method at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. For all statistical analyses, the significance level (α) was established at 0.05. LC₅₀ values, slopes ± standard errors and regression equations were estimated via probit analysis using PoloPlus software (LeOra Software, LLC, 2003) and reported in accordance with the Finney (1971) method26. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity data were analyzed by employing IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 16.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Laboratory bioassays

The LC50 values against A. cerana bees were 185.4, 326 and 250 ppm after 24 h of treatment by neem oil, L. lecanii and B. bassiana, respectively. The order of toxicity was found to be B. bassiana, followed by L. lecanii then neem seed oil. In comparison, values LC50 of against L. erysimi after 24 h were found to be 115.2, 102 and 95.5 ppm for neem oil, L. lecanii and B. bassiana treatments, respectively with the same order of toxicity. Field experiments

Based on the LC50 values bio-efficacy, B. bassiana at concentrations viz. 1 × 108 spores mL− 1, 2 × 108 spores mL− 1 and 3 × 108 spores mL− 1, L. lecanii @ 1 × 108 spores mL− 1, 1.5 × 108 spores mL− 1, and 2 × 108 spores mL− 1 and neem seed oil @ 3 mL L− 1 as standard check, was evaluated against L. erysimi on field. Pre-treatment count of aphid was made one day prior to spraying and post treatment counts were made at one, three, five, seven and ten days after spraying.

The mustard aphids, L. erysimi

The number of aphids per 10 cm terminal shoot was found to be 62.0%, 70.7%, and 71.9% when treated with B. bassiana @ 1 × 108 spores mL− 1, 2 × 108 spores mL− 1, 3 × 108 spores mL− 1, respectively; 46.8%, 58.7%, 62.5% in case of L. lecanii @ 1 × 108 spores mL− 1, 1.5 × 108 spores mL− 1, 2 × 108 spores mL− 1, respectively r; and 67.1% in case of neem seed oil @ 3 mL L− 1 ten days after spraying (Table 4). Furthermore, results revealed that all the biopesticidal treatment suppressed the populations of aphids significantly over control.

Discussion

India’s agriculture industry is crucial to its economy and the livelihood of its population30. However, pest-related crop losses are a significant issue, with estimates of on-farm yield losses ranging from 10 to 30%31,32. Additionally, post-harvest losses can be significant, with up to 18% of fruits and 13% of vegetables lost during handling and storage. As a result of chemical control issues, non-chemical pest management strategies such as microbial biopesticides are becoming more popular (Table 7). The biopesticide market in India is growing due to concerns about environmental toxicity and insecticide resistance. The global biopesticide market was expected to grow to $4.5 billion by 2023, in increasing pattern through the next years33. In India, at least 15 microbial control agents have been developed as biopesticides with 970 commercial formulations registered. However, the market growth of biopesticides in India is hindered by slow adoption, limited resources for large-scale production, unregistered product sales, and regulatory and commercialization challenges34. Despite these challenges, the use of biopesticides as part of integrated pest management programs is expected to increase in India; however, it becomes questionable about their effects on non-target or beneficial insects35.

The study reveals that using B. bassiana as a biopesticide can considerably lower L. erysimi populations. All concentrations examined exhibited high decrease rates ranging from 46.8 to 71.9% compared to pre-treatment counts, whereas untreated control plots showed an increase of 22.8%. There were no significant differences between the B. bassiana concentrations at 2 and 3 × 108 spores mL− 1; however, a significant difference was observed between the concentration of 1 × 108 spores mL−1and the higher concentrations tested. These findings suggest that B. bassiana is a promising biocontrol agent for aphid pests with only minor effects on the co-existing (A) cerana honeybees36,37. The greater pathogenicity to L. erysimi by (B) bassiana than A. cerana highlights that a certain target-specific selectivity is of value to biocontrol agents and likely occurs due to differential host vulnerability, cuticle structure, and immune capability between aphids and honeybees. The concentration-dependent effect of disease severity indicates that, under field conditions, it is essential for spores of the fungus to be applied in high number on the host cuticle and the fungus to be able to adhere and germinate, and further research is needed to optimize its use and application in the field.

In context of the present data, efficacy of B. bassiana found in this study concurs with literature, it showed potent efficacy (LC50 of 2.7 × 105 cfu) and it resulted to be highly virulent towards aphids. Akmal et al.38 previously found an aphid nymphal mortality with an application rate using B. bassiana whereas Mohamed et al.39 was able to establish very useful aphid control with a relatively low spore numbers (3 to 5 × 103 spores/ml). Differences in spore counts that were used in different trials along with variety of host, isolate, environmental conditions, application device might have contributed for the minute differences in efficacies found. Therefore B. bassiana is considered as reliable candidate within the Integrated aphid management approach programs.

This higher efficiency of B. bassiana on the insects in the present study can be attributed to effective infection by spore adhesion to the insect cuticle, subsequent germination, penetration of the insect body through a set of cuticles degrading enzymes such as proteases, chitinases and lipases, followed by multiplication into the haemocoel of the insect and then production of fungal toxins, accumulation into the insect body tissues, leading to death. These mechanisms can explain the concentration-dependent increase in aphid mortality in both the laboratory and field conditions36,38.

In this study, we found that L. lecanii also caused significant reduction of aphid populations both in laboratory and field conditions though to a slightly lower extent compared to B. bassiana. This variation may be associated with isolate virulence or adaptability in the environment because entomopathogenic fungi infection relies heavily on the temperature and relative humidity in environment. Likewise, many researchers indicated similar findings about the control of aphids and other pests of Hemiptera, L. lecanii successfully controlled many hemipteran species, though at a significantly slower pace of infection than B. bassiana.

L. lecanii proliferates throughout the insect’s body, leading to the death of the host in around 48–72 h. This entomopathogenic fungus is commonly used as a biological pesticide for controlling various pests, including aphids, whiteflies, and thrips, in greenhouses39. The mycotoxins produced by L. lecanii include bassianolide, vertilecanin-A1, decenedioic acid, and 10-hydroxy-8-decenoic acid40–42. As the host nutrients are depleted, the blastopores differentiate into elongated hyphae, which extend outward from the body forming a mycelial mat of conidiophores over the surface of the integument, resulting in mummification. It employed a similar strategy of infection to the other studied species, hence the observed lower effect may have been due to isolate-specific features, such as enzyme activity, conidial germination ability or adaptation to the environment, as well documented in these types of agents and relevant for practical application of highly active strains36,37.

Neem seed oil has shown promising results as a biopesticide against L. erysimi aphids, with a concentration of 3 mL L−1after 24 h (LC50 = 115.2 ppm), proving to be effective in reducing aphid populations. However, it resulted in higher levels of bee mortality than the fungal biopesticides although the inhibition of aphids was significant. It therefore appears likely that some of the microbial biopesticides would offer a superior balance between controlling the pest without negatively impacting the pollinators, especially if treatments are carried out at the flowering stage.

Under field conditions, a concentration of 3mL L−1of neem seed oil showed the highest reduction of L. erysimi populations, recording a mean of 22.53 ± 4.06 after ten days of treatment. The tested concentration reduced the entire population by 67.1% over pre-treatment counts, whereas untreated control plots showed an increase of 22.8%. In addition to its effectiveness against aphids, neem oil has also been shown to have broad-spectrum insecticidal properties, making it a useful tool for integrated pest management (IPM) strategies43. However, as with any biopesticide, it is important to consider potential negative impacts on non-target organisms, such as bees, before widespread use. Further research is needed to optimize neem oil application and assess its impact on the environment. It is also important to explore other biopesticides, such as L. lecanii and B. bassiana, to determine their potential in pest management strategies while minimizing risks to non-target organisms and the environment. Ultimately, a balanced approach that considers both the benefits and risks of biopesticides is crucial for sustainable pest management44.

Azadirachtin is a limonoid that acts as a growth regulator by interfering with endocrine pathways and as an antifeedant and ovipositional deterrent through chemosensory pathways45. It can cause larval gut degeneration, disrupt oogenesis and vitellogenesis in female mosquitoes, cause immune deficiencies and act as genetic regulators affecting protein and enzyme production46.

Honeybees play a crucial role in crop production, with their pollination efforts contributing to 80% of insect pollinators. Apart from their role in pollination, honeybees are economically important due to the market for honey and beeswax. However, honeybees are also sensitive bioindicator species that are greatly affected by environmental changes and pollutants, including pesticides47. Research has shown that the fungal bio-fungicide Trichoderma does not have any adverse effects on both Western honeybees (A. mellifera)48 and Asian honeybees (A. cerana)49. On the other hand, other fungi like B. bassiana, Cordyceps fumosorosea, Hirsutella spp., and L. lecanii proved toxic for bees at different developmental stages50,51. Our study revealed no significant changes in the AChE activity of any of the treated bees (3.47 ± 0.19 to 3.62 ± 0.005 U/min/g insect) compared to the control (3.51 ± 1.02 U/min/g insect), showing no extra detoxification or sensitivity to the products used in this study. This is in accordance with prior findings that observed few sublethal physiological impacts from entomopathogenic fungi against honeybees when utilized per agricultural application guidelines. For instance, B. bassiana exhibited low rates of adult mortality when fielded against honeybees [68] and the relative virulence of infection against bees appears highly dependent on fungal strain, dose, route of infection [69]. While the products were observed to inflict a certain degree of bee death in this investigation, no changes to AChE levels occurred in the infected organisms which may serve as an indicator of neurotoxicity under the controlled conditions employed herein.

The increased sensitivity of L. erysimi over A. cerana can be attributed to variations in their physiologies, their cuticles composition, grooming behavior, immune defenses, and ecological exposure to the pathogen. Honeybees can remove fungi via grooming and exhibit strong antimicrobial immune defenses, limiting their susceptibility of establishment of fungus, unlike aphids who have relatively less behavioral defense, and keep continuously in surface of plants leading to a high probability of attachment and infection of spores. It is most likely due to this reason why higher LC50 was detected in A. cerana in our study.

In combination, laboratory-based and field-based studies have revealed a good profile of selectivity for each entomopathogenic fungi species that causes significantly higher mortality to the target aphid compared to the non-target pollinator. This level of selectivity, however, remains a requirement to achieve Integrated Pest and Pollinator Management (IPPM) in the sense that we must control the pest effectively without negative impacts on the pollination service providers. Notwithstanding, the occurrence of some mortality in the bees under field conditions indicates that there should be some restriction to applications occurring at the peak of foraging time [70].

Despite evidence for its effectiveness against L. erysimi and a measure of its acute impact on A. cerana colonies here, caution is advised in reaching conclusions about the applicability of the results to practice. Firstly, the study was conducted over a short post-treatment interval (ten days), and so we cannot recommend from our data how well either fungal treatment will continue, or how it will perform throughout the foraging season. Secondly, in the pollinator safety trials we relied only on measures of acute lethality and activity levels (AChE assay). To adequately characterize potential sublethal effects of these fungicides on the health and functioning of honeybee colonies we would need to incorporate assays of additional measures including effects on foraging behavior, colony health, development, reproductive output and immune status (and thus a number of physiological and immunological markers). Lastly, the ability of an entomopathogenic to cause death is to some extent dependent on climatic factors that exist at the site and time that treatment occurs, and that can affect survival and the viability of the fungal inoculum. Hence, we also urge further, multi-location studies over multiple seasons before our findings can be considered applicable throughout Europe or elsewhere in the world.

Despite promising field performance shown for B. bassiana and L. lecanii in the current work, success of entomopathogenic fungi can be significantly impacted by both the properties of formulated products and the prevailing environmental factors. The viability and persistence of fungal spores are strongly associated with formulation, oil-based forms tending to be more resistant to UV radiation and desiccation than water-based formulations, resulting in enhanced conidia viability and efficacy in the field (Faria & Wraight, 2007; Mascarin & Jaronski, 2016). Direct solar ultraviolet irradiation is one of the main factors influencing reduction in spores viability andinfectivity after field application. Solar UV irradiation is believed to limit spore persistence on plant surfaces38. In addition, various other environmental parameters, such as temperature, humidity, rainfall and wind, can have substantial effects on conidial germination, infectivity of the host and activity of EPF remaining on plants, which leads to considerable variation of the field results in different locations and across growing seasons52. Consequently, advancements in formulation types including utilization of oil-based vehicles, protection of conidia against UV radiation and use of microencapsulation, are expected to provide solutions for more stable persistence and reproducible performance of EPF in commercial field conditions36,53–55.

In conclusion, both the entomopathogenic fungi, B. bassiana and L. lecanii, effectively controlled L. erysimi in laboratory and field conditions, displaying a considerably high toxicity against target aphid species as compared with non-target pollinator, A. cerana. The potential of these fungi as biopesticides would appear promising as a substitute for conventional synthetic insecticides; however, they have resulted in 100% mortality of the honeybees at the highest concentration under field conditions, indicating a significant risk to pollinators. Therefore, their application must be carefully timed to avoid peaks in pollinator foraging activity (e.g., early morning or late evening). The present findings conclude that these fungi have a place in Integrated Pest and Pollinator Management (IPPM) strategies, serving as a more environmentally friendly alternative for control of Brassica’s damaging pest, provided that application guidelines strictly consider pollinator safety. These findings must be validated in further multilocation trials and over longer terms.
