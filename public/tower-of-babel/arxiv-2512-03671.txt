Generative AI Practices, Literacy, and Divides: An Empirical Analysis in the Italian Context
Beatrice Savoldi, Giuseppe Attanasio, Olga Gorodetskaya, Marta Marchiori Manerba, Elisa Bassignana, Silvia Casola, Matteo Negri, Tommaso Caselli, Luisa Bentivogli, Alan Ramponi, Arianna Muti, Nicoletta Balbo, Debora Nozza

Abstract
The rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) language technologies, particularly generative AI (GenAI) chatbots accessible via conversational interfaces, is transforming digital interactions. While these tools hold societal promise, they also risk widening digital divides due to uneven adoption and low awareness of their limitations. This study presents the first comprehensive empirical mapping of GenAI adoption, usage patterns, and literacy in Italy, based on newly collected survey data from 1,906 Italian-speaking adults. Our findings reveal widespread adoption for both work and personal use, including sensitive tasks like emotional support and medical advice. Crucially, GenAI is supplanting other technologies to become a primary information source: this trend persists despite low user digital literacy, posing a risk as users struggle to recognize errors or misinformation. Moreover, we identify a significant gender divide—particularly pronounced in older generations—where women are half as likely to adopt GenAI and use it less frequently than men. While we find literacy to be a key predictor of adoption, it only partially explains this disparity, suggesting that other barriers are at play. Overall, our data provide granular insights into the multipurpose usage of GenAI, highlighting the dual need for targeted educational initiatives and further investigation into the underlying barriers to equitable participation that competence alone cannot explain.



1
Introduction

The rapid advancement of AI language technologies (LTs)—computational models that process and generate human language—has entered a transformative phase with the rise of powerful “GenAI chatbots” such as ChatGPT
(
OpenAI 2023
)
, which are reshaping how we work, access information, and interact with technology.
Unlike prior LTs designed for a dedicated activity or modality, these new tools are general-purpose, capable of performing numerous tasks—from writing and translation to coding and tutoring—across languages without domain-specific training. This makes them uniquely accessible to diverse users and adaptable across contexts.
As a result, GenAI chatbots have achieved unprecedented adoption: By 2025, the ChatGPT platform had become one of the five most visited websites in the world
(
Exploding Topics 2025
)
,
leaving researchers, policymakers, and developers to grapple with fundamental questions about adoption, usage, and global impact.

As a new commodity, access to GenAI chatbots offers promises for personalized learning
(
Abbes et al. 2024
)
, personal convenience
(
Skjuve et al. 2024
)
, and workforce productivity
(
Noy and Zhang 2023
;
Brynjolfsson et al. 2025
)
.
The impact of access—or lack thereof—can be substantial: a temporary ChatGPT ban in Italy produced a significant drop in coding productivity, with output levels recovering only after users developed strategies to circumvent the restrictions
(
Kreitmeir and Raschky 2023
)
.
However, alongside these benefits come significant risks.
GenAI can propagate bias and stereotypes
(
Hofmann et al. 2024
;
Mitchell et al. 2025
)
, spread misinformation
(
Chen and Shu 2024
)
, and critically, also widen digital divides
(
UNESCO 2024
)
. Recent research suggests that uneven adoption and varying levels of AI proficiency threaten to deepen existing inequalities
(
Bentley et al. 2024
;
Capraro et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Suárez and García-Mariñoso 2025
)
, with higher uptake concentrated among populations of higher socioeconomic status
(
Bassignana et al. 2025
)
, urban residents
(
Daepp and Counts 2025
)
, and more educated individuals
(
Hartley et al. 2024
)
.

The escalating interest in understanding how individuals engage with these technologies
has prompted surveys to grasp user attitudes towards AI more broadly
(
Scantamburlo et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, as well as analyses of user conversations to specifically document GenAI chatbots’ emerging applications
(
Ouyang et al. 2023
;
Trippas et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. The largest and recent analyses, however, rely on proprietary company data from single platforms—e.g., Microsoft Bing Copilot
(
Tomlinson et al. 2025
)
, ChatGPT
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
or Claude
(
Handa et al. 2025
)
—and lack reported social, geographical, and demographic information on users. Also, such studies inherently focus on current users only, overlooking those who do not engage with these tools—a potentially distinct demographic group with different needs or technological constraints
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.
Another limitation of these studies is their predominant focus on English-speaking contexts, particularly the United States, despite evidence that sociocultural norms and contexts shape users’ expectations and attitudes toward technology
(
Murayama et al. 2025
)
. This gap is especially relevant given that GenAI performance varies across languages
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Holtermann et al. 2024
)
, potentially reinforcing linguistic and cultural asymmetries in how these tools are experienced and adopted.

In this paper, we focus on the Italian-speaking context, where comprehensive research on GenAI chatbot adoption and usage is critically lacking. Italy presents a compelling case, also due to its challenges in digital competence, ranking 23rd of 27 EU Member States in the Digital Economy and Society Index (DESI) for basic digital skills.
1

Specifically to AI technologies, Ipsos’ 2025 AI Monitor
(
Ipsos 2025
)
reveals that while a substantial portion of the Italian population has heard of AI, only half feel they truly understand it—placing Italy 29th out of 30 ranked countries.
These aggregate figures, however, may mask important disparities across social groups. For instance,
Sublime and Renna 2024
identify a gender gap in GenAI chatbot usage among Italian students, yet their analysis is confined to educational settings, leaving the broader population unexplored.

We provide the
first comprehensive empirical mapping of GenAI chatbot adoption, usage patterns, and literacy in Italy
. Drawing on newly collected survey data (
n=1,906
) that include both adopters and non-adopters and rich demographic information, we combine quantitative statistical analyses with qualitative examination of open-ended responses to pursue four research objectives.
(1)
First, we examine GenAI chatbot adoption by comparing it with other longstanding LTs to understand how GenAI fits into existing technological ecosystems and if and why it replaces existing LTs.

(2)
Second, we document GenAI chatbots usage patterns—including activities, intents, and contexts (i.e., work versus personal use).
(3)
Third, we assess awareness of the benefits and risks to identify literacy gaps.
(4)
Finally, we explore whether GenAI adoption and usage divides emerge across sociodemographics, and the role of literacy and experience with other LTs in predicting such gaps.

Our data represent a rich snapshot of 2025 for the Italian context. Aware that GenAI and its applications continue to evolve rapidly, we make all survey data publicly available to enable longitudinal monitoring.

2
Related Work

The field of LTs has undergone a paradigm shift with the rise of highly capable Large Language Models (LLMs), which form the backbone of modern GenAI chatbots.
2

Departing from traditional LT solutions based on task-specific models (e.g., dedicated to summarization or machine translation), LLMs are inherently a general-purpose LT, capable of capturing complex language patterns and addressing multiple tasks with minimal adaptation. This versatility—across modalities (e.g., voice, text) and languages—coupled with high accessibility of friendly conversational interfaces, has driven rapid public adoption of GenAI chatbots and a vast expansion of potential use cases. This, in response, has sparked intense institutional, media, and scholarly interest across disciplines.
In this section, we summarize recent works across thematic areas aligned with our contribution.

GenAI chatbot Usage and Language Technology Replacement

Research on the real-world applications of GenAI chatbots has focused on analyzing large-scale user conversations.
Early work by
Ouyang et al. 2023
identifies novel user requests, such as design and planning, that were unaddressed by traditional LTs. Recent studies confirm rapidly evolving patterns, with a focus on software development and writing using Claude
(
Handa et al. 2025
)
, professional use cases supported by Microsoft Bing Copilot
(
Tomlinson et al. 2025
)
, and everyday information-seeking via ChatGPT, where non-work usage is growing faster than professional use
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
.
However, these findings are constrained by a focus on English and proprietary, non-public datasets. Evidence on usage patterns for the Italian context is particularly scarce, limited to younger demographics in descriptive reports
(
Eurispes 2025
)
. More fundamentally, the question of technological substitution—whether GenAI is augmenting or replacing existing tools—remains open. Our work addresses these gaps by analyzing usage patterns across the Italian adult population and investigating the potential replacement of existing technologies, thereby providing insights into GenAI’s consolidating impact in the current technological ecosystem.

Literacy and Risks

Understanding public awareness of GenAI’s risks and limitations, as well as literacy levels, is crucial for contextualizing its societal impact. Public concerns are significant; a global study found that 54% of Italians have experienced errors from GenAI, and 84% support laws against generated misinformation
(
Gillespie et al. 2025
)
. Such worries are not unique to Italy—both the public and experts in the U.S. have expressed concerns about insufficient oversight
(
Pew Research Center 2025c
)
.
The relationship between literacy in GenAI and usage, however, presents a complex picture. On one hand, frequent use can increase trust, while a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanics can reduce it
(
Bouyzourn and Birch 2025
)
.
These findings contrast with those of
Gillespie et al. 2025
, which found that higher levels of training and literacy correlated with greater trust and acceptance. Furthermore,
Tully et al. 2025
find that individuals with lower literacy were more receptive to GenAI, perceiving it as “magical”—a finding that challenges the assumption that education uniformly promotes adoption.
Given these ambiguous findings, we investigate the state of literacy in GenAI and LTs in the Italian context, and examine its relation to engagement with GenAI chatbots across sociodemographic segments and usage scenarios.

Sociodemographic Divides

Existing research has identified age, education, and income as significant adoption predictors of GenAI.
U.S. surveys find increasing use is most notable among younger, more educated individuals
(
Pew Research Center 2025a
)
, a trend reinforced by workplace data
(
Hartley et al. 2024
)
. The pattern is documented by

Gillespie et al. 2025
in a global study across 47 countries, finding that younger, more educated, and higher-income individuals are more trusting and accepting of GenAI.
Gender has also emerged as a relevant factor.
A synthesis of 18 studies by
Otis et al. 2024
reports persistent gender disparities across regions and occupations, even when access to technology is equalized.
These differences manifest in both adoption rates and engagement patterns.
Sublime and Renna 2024
find significant gender gaps among French and Italian students, with males reporting higher usage rates across academic fields. Similarly,
Møgelvang et al. 2024
observe that male Norwegian students use GenAI chatbots more frequently, while female students express greater concern for critical thinking and a stronger need to learn appropriate use. While gender is a key demographic lens, socioeconomic status also shapes interactions.
Bassignana et al. 2025
uses qualitative methods to find that users with lower status tend to anthropomorphize GenAI, suggesting that background influences not just whether people use the technology, but also
how
they engage with it.
Understanding these demographic patterns raises questions about what drives adoption in the first place.
While surveys of early adopters identify key motivators like productivity and novelty
(
Skjuve et al. 2024
)
, the reasons for non-adoption—and the characteristics or technological constraints of those that do not currently use GenAI—remain overlooked
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
. Our work addresses this gap by examining factors for both adoption and non-adoption. Through a dual approach, we analyze whether sociodemographic divides manifest differently at the stage of initial GenAI
adoption
versus the frequency and nature of
usage
.

3
Design and Methods

We provide here a brief outline of our study design, followed by detailed information about our sample, measures, and analytical methods.

3.1
Study Design

Our survey design reflects our four main research objectives and is organized as follows.

First, we measure adoption rates of GenAI chatbots alongside five longstanding task-specific LT applications: machine translation (e.g., Google Translate), voice assistants (e.g., Siri), assisted writing (e.g., Grammarly), speech transcription (e.g., Zoom captions), and text-to-speech generation (e.g., Adobe Read Out Loud). These applications were selected for their widespread commercial use and allow us to assess
i)
participants’ familiarity and experience with other task-specific LTs,
ii)
whether GenAI is complementary or also replacing existing tools. For each technology that participants reported using, we asked whether usage had changed since the introduction of GenAI chatbots and about the reasons for this shift. Additionally, we explicitly ask about potential behavioral shifts in the use of Web search queries.

Second, for participants who adopted GenAI chatbots (henceforth GenAI
users
), we asked questions mapping how, when, and for what purposes they use these tools. We provided a list of
activities
identified as significant in prior research
(
Ouyang et al. 2023
)
(e.g., email writing, coding, travel planning), asked participants to indicate whether they perform these activities for work or personal use, and inquired about interaction modalities (e.g., using spoken language or written text) and the language (e.g. Italian or English) used in such interactions.
To move beyond specific activities, we also analyzed users’ interaction
intent
, as a single activity like “revising a text” can serve divergent goals—from polishing an email to practicing a language. We classified these underlying purposes using established taxonomies of usage intent
(
Bodonhelyi et al. 2024
;
Shah et al. 2025
)
.

Third, to assess literacy in AI language technologies, we collected information on prior training, self-reported knowledge and competence, awareness of these technologies’ benefits and risks, and desiderata for future education.

Finally, we gathered sociodemographic information, including age, gender, geography, socioeconomic status, educational level, and professional area to investigate their relationships with adoption, usage of the technology, and divides.

To ensure accessibility for non-expert participants, we follow
Bassignana et al. 2025
and provide a friendly definition of LT (i.e., computational models that process and generate human language), distinguishing between general-purpose GenAI chatbots and task-specific LTs, alongside examples and descriptions for each of our five specific LT applications.
The survey—distributed in Italian and translated here for dissemination purposes—was extensively piloted and refined to enhance clarity, construct validity, and reliability. We provide the

3.2
Participants and Distribution

The questionnaire was distributed between May 23rd and August 27th, 2025, using the Qualtrics platform
(
Qualtrics 2025
)
. We use a non-probability sampling strategy
(
Vehovar et al. 2016
)
, where respondents voluntarily self-select into the survey.
To ensure broad coverage and approximate the Italian population more closely, the survey was disseminated through multiple online channels, including social media, mailing lists, and messaging apps such as WhatsApp and Telegram. Broader outreach was achieved via national and local media outlets, including
Corriere della Sera
,
3

TP24
,
4
and
Il Fatto Quotidiano
.
5
Participants were encouraged to share the survey within their networks, fostering a snowball sampling effect. They completed the survey anonymously on a device of their choice.

Eligibility required participants to be at least 18 years old and have native or C1 level proficiency in Italian (standard CEFR language proficiency scale). Responses failing this language filter, representing 4.55% of submissions (
n=100
), were excluded. Additional filters were applied to enhance data quality. We removed responses completed in less than 2.78 minutes (
n=3
)—below the lower interquartile range of the median survey duration of 8.7 minutes—as well as responses flagged by Qualtrics as potential duplicates (
n=66
). Participants with missing responses on the GenAI chatbot adoption question (
n=10
) were also excluded.
After applying these criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 1,906 respondents. Key sample characteristics are summarized in Table
2
. We report age, gender, geography (including Italian people living abroad), education, and socioeconomic status. This was operationalized by asking respondents “In which socioeconomic bracket do you place yourself?” The responses were then recoded into three categories: Lower (“Low” and “Middle-low”), Mid (“Middle”), and Higher (combining “Mid-high” and ”High”). “I prefer not to answer” responses (2.7%) were proportionally distributed across the three categories.

Our sample demonstrates a reasonable alignment with the key demographic characteristics of the Italian population (comparison with national statistics in Supplementary S1.3, Fig. S2 based on Italian National Statistics Institute data—i.e. ISTAT 2025
6
).
Gender distribution is balanced and closely mirrors national statistics. Age shows typical patterns of online surveys, with a slight underrepresentation (65+ years old; 20.06% in our sample vs. 28.7% in the general population).
Educational attainment shows the largest deviation from a representative sample (30.6% of non-graduates, vs. 85% in the general population), likely due to a higher representation of younger individuals in our sample.
Importantly, this over-representation of educated respondents makes our first exploratory findings more conservative, likely providing lower bound estimates: if significant digital divides and modest literacy levels are evident even in this highly educated sample, they are likely more pronounced in the broader population.
7

Our sample shows a higher proportion of GenAI users (80.4%,
n=1533
) compared to non-users. We acknowledge that, as an opt-in survey, individuals who are not interested in the technology may have been less inclined to participate. Nevertheless, the number of non-users (
n=373
) remains sufficient for robust analysis of adoption barriers and usage patterns.

To test the robustness of our results, we performed sensitivity analyses using post-stratification weights based on nationally representative ISTAT data for gender, age, and geography; the main findings remain substantively unchanged (results in Supplementary S2.1). Therefore, we retain all data to maximize statistical power.

3.3
Measures

In this study, we operationalize key variables of interest.

GenAI chatbot adoption.

GenAI chatbot adoption is treated as a binary variable based on participants’ self-reported use of GenAI chatbots in the past 12 months. To facilitate understanding, we provided a multiple-choice list of specific chatbot models, such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini (list available in Table S1 in the Supplementary materials). Participants were classified as
users
if they selected at least one of these models and as
non-users
if they did not select any.

LT adoption and experience.

To compare GenAI chatbot adoption with the use of longstanding task-specific LTs, we collected data on the use of five well-established applications: machine translation (e.g., Google Translate), voice assistants (e.g., Siri), assisted writing (e.g., Grammarly), speech transcription (e.g., Zoom captions), and text-to-speech (e.g., Adobe Read Out Loud). Responses for each application were coded as binary variables (has/does not have experience).
An index of prior LT experience was then constructed by summing participants’ responses for each of the five applications, with the resulting score ranging from 0 (indicating no use of any application) to 5 (indicating use of all five applications).

Usage intent and frequency.

For GenAI users, we collected self-reported data on the frequency of GenAI chatbot use across six specific intents: Information Retrieval, Problem Solving, Learning, Content Creation, Leisure, and Creativity (see Table
1
). The response options were recoded to represent frequency on a scale of 0 (never), 1 (less than monthly), 2 (monthly), or 3 (weekly).

LT literacy.

We assessed self-reported competence and knowledge of GenAI and AI language technologies more broadly using six items on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree). The items included: (1) “I have a good knowledge of language technologies,” (2) “I can recognize errors in language technologies,” (3) “I can distinguish automatically generated text from text written by humans,” (4) “I feel prepared to use language technologies,” (5) “I know the limitations of language technologies,” and (6) “I know the potential of language technologies.” Responses to these items were recoded into a mean unified score on a 0-1 linear scale, with higher values indicating greater self-assessed LT literacy.

3.4
Analysis and Methods

3.4.1
Statistical Analysis

Our analytical approach combines descriptive and inferential methods to address our research objectives.
For research objectives 1–3 (GenAI and LT adoption, GenAI chatbot usage, and LT literacy and education), we employ descriptive statistics—e.g., frequencies, means, standard deviations, and cross-tabulations to characterize broad patterns across the sample. For research objective 4 (digital divides), we use inferential methods to examine whether adoption and usage differ systematically across sociodemographic groups, and if they can be explained by LT experience and literacy.

We use logistic regression to predict GenAI chatbot adoption (users vs. non-users) and estimate odds ratios, which provide a more intuitive measure of the likelihood of adopting GenAI based on the predictor variables.
To identify intent-specific predictors of usage frequency, we run separate ordinary least squares (OLS) models for each of the six intents. While this is formally an ordered measure (0-3),
it can be reasonably interpreted as approximating a continuous frequency of use. In fact, ordered logistic and OLS regressions yield virtually identical results in terms of sign, magnitude, and significance of the coefficients (see S6 and S7 in the Supplementary materials). For parsimony and ease of interpretation, we therefore report OLS estimates.

All models incorporate sociodemographic variables as controls, and include LT experience and literacy as additional interdependent variables to account for technology familiarity and digital competence.

We verified that the predictor variables do not exhibit problematic multicollinearity using Variance Inflation Factors
(
O’brien 2007
, VIF;)
. All VIF values are below 1.2, well below conventional thresholds, indicating no significant multicollinearity among predictor variables (full results reported in Supplementary S2.2, Table S3).

3.4.2
Free-Form Response Analysis

Our survey included optional open-ended questions to capture qualitative insights into participants’ perceptions of the technology’s usability, experience with potential errors, and preferences for use (e.g., language of the interaction or modality). We include in our analyses three questions that received at least 300 responses (
\sim
20% of the users’ response pool).

Following standard textual modeling practices
(
Alipour et al. 2023
;
Bhandarkar et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, we search for and group similar answers into clusters, and then describe each cluster’s topic to infer trends and insights.
In practice, given all open-text responses of a question, we use BERTopic
(
Grootendorst 2022
)
, an established topic modeling tool. The software uses embedding models to transform text into numerical representations, clustering and dimensionality reduction algorithms, and LLMs to generate textual descriptions of the discovered topics.
We report in the Supplementary material (S.1.4) more details about BERTopic, as well as the complete information on the preprocessing applied to texts, our choices of model parameters, and steps taken to verify the extracted topics.

GenAI Chatbot Adoption Surpass most Longstanding Language Technology Applications

Figure
1
.a presents adoption rates across six language technologies. GenAI chatbots have rapidly achieved widespread adoption (80.5%), surpassing established LTs such as voice assistants (72%) and approaching the near-universal usage (94%) of machine translation. This is particularly notable given that voice assistants like Siri and Alexa have been popular for years.
Other speech-related applications (speech transcript at 27% and text-to-speech at 15%) remain considerably less prevalent, suggesting that longevity alone does not guarantee widespread adoption.
These figures attest to the explosive growth of GenAI chatbots. Our data further allow us to examine the specific composition of the GenAI chatbots adopted: ChatGPT emerges as the dominant platform, used by nearly three-quarters (74.5%) of respondents in our sample. Gemini is a distant second, used by 37.4%. This significant disparity points to limited platform diversification among users and suggests that ChatGPT’s first-mover advantage has cemented a strong incumbent position for this emerging technology.

GenAI Chatbots Drive Shift from existing Technologies

When GenAI users were asked whether the onset of GenAI chatbots had affected how and if they used other digital technologies, 34.6% reported a definitive shift, while an additional 18.3% were unsure. Specifically for LTs, we find technology-specific displacement patterns (Figure
1
.b). Assisted writing tools face the most substantial replacement by GenAI chatbots (24.5% partial, 19.8% complete replacement) — an expected outcome given their text-generation capabilities, which also allow drafting text from scratch. Notably, web search experiences meaningful displacement (34% partial, 5% complete), suggesting that users bypass traditional query-based information retrieval in favor of conversational interfaces that provide targeted, synthesized responses.
8

Usability as a Pathway to GenAI Chatbot Usage

To understand the mechanisms driving GenAI chatbot growth, we asked participants who reported replacing other technologies with GenAI (
n=624
) to select reasons from six multiple-choice options (i.e., convenience, flexibility & personalization, accuracy, reliability, transparency, besides “other”). The results reveal that the shift stems primarily from practical advantages rather than higher performance or trustworthiness: convenience (38%) and flexibility/personalization (30%) dominate users’ motivations, while accuracy (19%), reliability (8%), and transparency (2%) play secondary roles.
Accordingly, the displacement of existing specialized technologies seems driven more by usability improvements and novel interaction paradigms in user-friendly interfaces that reduce friction in user workflows across multiple functionalities rather than a sense of trust.

4.2
GenAI Chatbot Usage Results

Having established adoption rates, here we focus on GenAI users (
n=1,533
) and examine how they engage with the technology in practice. We analyze which intents and activities dominate usage, whether interactions serve professional or personal needs, and the extent to which users employ GenAI chatbots as productive instruments, creative assistants, or emotional companions
(
Andersson 2025
)
.
Finally, for more pragmatic insights, we explore how the general-purpose, multilingual, and multimodal nature of the tools is leveraged by examining in which languages and modalities users interact with them, and why, by analyzing participants’ open-text responses.

GenAI chatbots are a Multi-Purpose Tool

Figure

Dominance of Text Interactions and Context-Driven Language Choice

We asked users which language and modality (writing or speaking) they use when interacting with GenAI chatbots.
Regarding languages, while almost all users report using Italian (95%), a substantial portion also relies on English (52%)—a notably high figure likely driven by the high representation of graduates and younger segments in our sample.
10
Also, there is a strong preference for writing (85%) over speaking (2.2%) or alternating between voice and text modalities (13.2%) when interacting with GenAI chatbots.

Our clustering analysis of free-form responses exposes the main users’ attitudes. Among those who left an open comment about the
preferred language for interactions
(
n=529
), we identified several driving factors.
Most commonly (
n=207
), it is a contextual choice. Frequently, users prefer English for work and Italian for personal use.
Moreover, users associated queries in English with higher reliability in model outputs, especially for image generation.
A second trend (
n=95
) centers on a strong preference for Italian because it is the respondents’ native language. Such explicit preference co-occurs with mentions to “convenience,” “ease of use,” “practicality,” and “simplicity.”
In smaller clusters, language choice is explicitly related to
i) the desired output language (
n=45
)—i.e., a pragmatic reason rather than a preference—
ii) task-dependent conditions (
n=32
), including the “topic discussed,” “target audience” or even their “mood;”
iii) the superior availability of technical information in English (
n=27
) (e.g., for programming), and
iv) educational purposes for themselves (
n=26
), e.g., to practice English.
A final group (
n=26
) uses GenAI chatbots almost exclusively in Italian due to a lack of competence in other languages, preventing them to interact as they would like to.

Among those who commented on the
preferred modality for interacting with GenAI chatbots
(
n=464
), most explained why writing is superior.
Many (
n=106
) underline the perceived benefits of control, precision, and clarity that the written medium facilitates. They consistently highlight the ability to refine and revise prompts before submission as a key advantage, mentioning that writing allows for a “more pondered” choice of words and greater clarity.
Another group (
n=97
) prefers written input due to established habits and a sense of comfort. In this cluster, two respondents identify as dyslexic, suggesting that writing may be a more accessible or preferred mode of interaction for individuals with reading or writing challenges.
Two clusters explicitly highlight the shortcomings of voice inputs, driven by a combination of practical, social, and cognitive factors. Many report perceived limitations in accuracy, reliability, and overall user experience, citing “transcription errors” and a sense that the system does not fully understand them, leading to repetition.
Voice is also deemed less suitable in various environments (e.g., a shared workspace or a crowded environment) than text, as it risks disturbing others or sharing unwanted information. Interestingly, some respondents display discomfort or self-consciousness when speaking to a device, even when alone (translated: “When I’m alone talking to the phone, I feel dumb”).
A small portion of participants (
n=14
) highlight privacy concerns. Upon manual inspection, we noticed a strong emphasis on the potential misuse of audio data. A key concern is the activation of microphones and the permissions required by chatbot platforms. This worry extends to uncertainty about
how
recorded audio is being used—specifically, whether it is “being used for training purposes” or other “undisclosed applications.”
The few respondents (
n=12
) who exhibited a clear preference for speaking to chatbots are primarily driven by perceived efficiency gains and convenience compared to typing.
Despite acknowledging an initial awkwardness (“I feel awkward […]”) when using voice, they explicitly state they can “talk faster” or that voice is “faster overall” compared to typing (“I’m slow with the qwerty”). The practical benefit of using vocal inputs on a smartphone is also mentioned.

These findings reveal a strong user preference for the control, precision, and privacy afforded by written text. Language selection, meanwhile, emerges as a deliberate, context-dependent choice, balancing the convenience of native Italian with the perceived reliability and technical utility of English.

Social Bias and Hallucination Awareness

Despite limited overall literacy, participants demonstrate comparatively higher awareness that LTs can reproduce biases and stereotypes (Figure
4
.c: users
M=0.96
, non-users
M=0.56
). This awareness also comes with smaller divides between users and non-users, suggesting broader public discourse on AI ethics and social impact has reached beyond the experts and adopters community.
Overall bias awareness (All
M=0.89
) also scores higher than the ability to spot errors (All
M=0.27
), potentially signaling a gap between awareness and actual ability to recognize such issues.
This gap is further evidenced by GenAI users’ reported experiences: when asked if they had ever encountered errors or biased examples in the output of GenAI chatbots, 39.5% of GenAI users confirmed encountering them, whereas 24.3% were unsure.

In a free-form question, we asked participants to expand on their experienced errors and stereotyped outputs. Among those who commented (
n=334
), the most commonly reported issues centered on factual inaccuracies and fabrications.
The largest cluster (
n=100
) describes experiences with hallucinations—instances in which GenAI chatbots “present false information as facts.” Issues frequently concern ontological knowledge about Italian culture, e.g., historical events or bibliographic details. Crucially, some (
n=6
) report issues in high-stakes contexts, e.g., legal and fiscal requests about the Italian system, with
GenAI generating “references to non-existent legal articles,” “fabricating supporting precedents,” or offering information that “contradicts established Italian law.”
Several users (
n=55
) documented pervasive gender and racial bias in AI outputs.
In image generation, users reported managerial roles depicted as “handsome middle-aged men,” teachers as young, blond, “Barbie-like” women figures, and caregivers as inherently feminine.
Requests for “images of poor people consistently generated people of color,” while successful people yielded white individuals.
Linguistic biases were also prevalent (
n=24
), with users reporting a systematic defaulting to masculine forms in Italian, even when gender-neutral options existed in the source language—e.g., “translating a doctor and a nurse” consistently produced “un medico e un’infermiera,” (masculine and feminine forms in Italian, respectively). Additionally, some users (
n=11
) identified cultural homogenization, describing responses as “anglocentric” and noting the GenAI’s tendency toward excessively polite outputs that avoid genuine depth or critical perspectives.

Lack of Competence and Loss of Critical Thinking as Barriers to Adoption

Given the attested moderate levels of LT literacy and previous education reported, Figure

GenAI users express particularly strong support (
M=1.17
) compared to non-users (

). This pattern suggests that while current users recognize gaps in their knowledge and desire structured learning opportunities, non-users have a lower interest in improving their LT literacy.

Non-users (
n=373
) were asked about the reasons for not using GenAI chatbots and conditions that might facilitate future adoption. Regarding non-adoption reasons, we find that this subsample segments into distinct groups: those with knowledge barriers (40.7%,
n=152
)—unaware of chatbot existence (19.3%) or feeling that they don’t know how to use them (21.4%)—and those who deliberately choose not to adopt them either by preference (35.1%) or perceived lack of utility (26.3%).
11
For future adoption, change lies on LT education and support infrastructure (option selected by 43.4% of non-users), followed by accuracy guarantees (27.3%), privacy assurances (24.1%), and, notably, social and work pressure (12.9%).
However, 24.1% of non-users express complete disinterest in future use, representing a resistant segment that raises concerns about the potential of GenAI chatbots to undermine critical thinking and diminish self-sufficiency, which explains their lower support for LT education integration.

GenAI Chatbot Divides Results

To conclude our analysis, we employ regression modeling to explore GenAI chatbot divides at two stages: adoption (user vs non-users) and usage frequency across intents among users. This dual approach reveals whether factors predicting uptake also shape engagement patterns, distinguishing between barriers to entry versus differences in engagement. All models (see details in Sec.
3.4.1
) include age, gender, geography, socioeconomic status, and education as independent variables, as well as LT prior experience and literacy to explore their role in predicting potential divides.

LT literacy is strongly predictive of GenAI Adoption

Figure
5
presents odds ratios from a logistic regression predicting GenAI chatbot adoption (logarithmic scale). Values above 1.0 indicate increased adoption likelihood, and vice versa.
Consistent with earlier waves of digital technology diffusion
(
Francis et al. 2019
, e.g.)
, adoption declines sharply with age. Compared to 18-34 year-olds, those aged 35-54 show 56% lower odds (OR = 0.44), while 55-64 and 65+ groups face odds ratios of 0.37 and 0,.20 respectively. Gender also matters: women have half the odds of men (OR = 0.50). Socioeconomic gradients follow expected patterns—graduates show 48% higher odds (OR = 1.48) and higher-income individuals 73% higher odds (OR = 1.73)—while no differences are observed between low and mid income participants, suggesting income effects concentrate at the upper end.
Geography shows no significant regional variation within Italy nor with participants living abroad.
Most notably, prior LT experience and competence emerge as very strong positive predictors.
Each point increase on the 0-5 LT experience scale raises odds by 72% (OR = 1.72), while LT literacy exhibits an exceptionally strong effect (OR = 20.82).
However, causality likely runs in both directions: prior LT literacy and experience lower adoption barriers, while usage of GenAI chatbots might further increase competence and knowledge of such technology. This reinforcing dynamic might bring cumulative advantage, where early adopters pull further ahead.

Having explored gaps in GenAI chatbot adoption, we now examine whether these disparities extend to usage frequency among actual adopters.

The results reveal heterogeneous patterns with limited consistency across sociodemographic groups, likely reflecting GenAI chatbot’s general-purpose nature serving diverse functions for different users.
Unlike the clear age decline in adoption, usage patterns among adopters vary substantially by intent.
Content Creation
follows the expected pattern, with older users predicting significantly lower engagement (55-64:
\beta=-0.19
; 65+:
\beta=-0.66
).
Information Retrieval
, however, exhibits the opposite trend: usage increases with age, peaking among those 65+ (

This presents a double-edged sword:

Overall, our results suggest an important distinction between barriers of adoption and patterns of usage across those who have adopted GenAI chatbots, which are highly intent-dependent. However, as we explore in the following, gender is the exception, with a persistent divide that structures both who uses GenAI chatbots and how intensively they engage with it across all domains.

Gender Divides in Chatbot Adoption and Usage

In Figure

, we isolate the effect of gender in adoption and usage frequency to examine gender disparities, by showing predicted probability differences between women and men of our regression models using average marginal effect (AME).
Concerning adoption rates, we find a clear age gradient in Figure
7
.a: among 18-34 year-olds, women have 4.1 percentage points lower adoption probability than men (AME = -0.041), reaching 13.5 points for those 65+ (AME = -0.135). Thus, we identify that gender gaps compound with age, but also suggest that gender divides in adoption might be reducing in younger generations.
Among GenAI users, gender gaps persist (Figure

.b), with women consistently using chatbots less frequently than men across all intents.
The most significant disparities appear in

(AME = -0.38 on the 0-3 frequency scale), and the smallest ones in

(AME = -0.09). Notably, even for
Information Retrieval
—the most accessible intent—women engage less frequently.

Notably, all our results control for sociodemographic variables, LT literacy, and prior experience. Yet sequential decomposition reveals our models explain only 34% of the baseline gender gap in adoption (i.e., gender without controls) and on average 15% of usage frequency across all intents (Tables with decompositional analyses in Supplementary Fig. S8 and Fig. S9). Thus, the majority of gender disparities remain unexplained by measured factors, suggesting the gap operates through mechanisms beyond technical competence, education, or socioeconomic status—potentially including differences in interest, perceived utility, privacy concerns, or gendered norms around technology adoption and use that require future investigation.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive, quantitative, empirical analysis of how Italian-speaking adults (i) adopt GenAI chatbots, comparatively to other language technologies, as well as (ii) their usage patterns, and (iii) literacy.
Using original 2025 survey data from nearly 2,000 individuals, we examine how GenAI chatbots are used, by whom, and for what purposes.
Moreover, we investigate how sociodemographic characteristics are associated with different degrees of adoption and use, and their relationship with prior
technological experience and literacy.

Our findings confirm the disruptive and fast-growing role of GenAI chatbots, hinting at renewed potential to
supplant
other technologies, as anecdotal evidence suggests
(
Honan 2025
, e.g.)
.
Among Italian speakers, GenAI chatbots have gained a larger user base than established technologies such as voice assistants (Siri, Cortana), despite their longstanding and pervasive presence in everyday applications.
In our data, web search and information seeking are the most compelling examples of this phenomenon. Indeed, 39% of GenAI users reported to have replaced traditional search engines to at least a partial extent—a trend likely to accelerate as AI-generated summaries gain prominence atop search results
(
Pew Research Center 2025b
)
.
Stark adoption, however, is not driven by the pursuit of more accurate and trustworthy results. Rather, versatility and convenience attract users who favor a general-purpose, multimodal, multilingual technology that lets them switch between tasks seamlessly.

Ease of use, while potentially democratizing access, can pose significant threats when matched with poor awareness of the technology’s limitations—especially in light of frequent accounts of hallucination and sycophancy.
12

In our sample, regarding literacy in AI language technologies, competence and knowledge gaps are pronounced even among GenAI users. This finding mirrors Italy’s nationwide low levels of overall digitalization skills
(
Kralj 2024
)
and broad understanding of AI technologies
(
Beccalli et al. 2024
;
Ipsos 2025
)
. One in two users has never received any training whatsoever, whether formal or informal, on GenAI or other AI technologies. Self-reported literacy is low, with a mean score across several skill categories of 0.23 (on a scale from
-1.5
to
+1.5
, with 0 indicating uncertainty about one’s competence). Crucially, users are mostly unsure whether they can spot potential errors in GenAI outputs (score: 0.41).
Low literacy is a particularly urgent issue because GenAI’s impact on the workplace
(
Handa et al. 2025
;
Brynjolfsson et al. 2025
)
is rapidly extending to personal life (our sample shows a balanced distribution of GenAI chatbots use between work and personal time, echoing recent trends
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
).
We observe activities such as seeking medical advice, verifying “facts,” and seeking information more broadly, which were not reported in the most recent accounts from Italian agencies
(
Eurispes 2025
, e.g.,)
.
These activities are prevalent among older users (65+) who have the lowest literacy levels and a higher risk of being particularly vulnerable to errors and misinformation.
We also find emerging use cases in companionship and emotional support across all age groups, applications previously reported among US teens
(
Common Sense Media 2025
)
. While GenAI may address shortages of accessible and affordable support, it also raises concerns about appropriate boundaries and dependencies.

Concerning GenAI’s potential to exacerbate existing divides, we are interested in the factors that contribute to adoption barriers
and
usage frequency among adopters, with particular attention to the role of prior language technology experience and self-perceived competence. This dual approach reveals whether the same factors that predict uptake also shape engagement patterns.
Concerning adoption, our findings largely echo existing literature: men, more highly educated individuals, those with higher socioeconomic status, and progressively younger cohorts are more likely to adopt chatbots
(
Bassignana et al. 2025
;
Hartley et al. 2024
;
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
.
Notably, we find no regional differences. This null finding should be interpreted cautiously: our online survey sample equalizes internet access across regions, masking Italy’s documented north-south digital divide
(
Serrecchia 2024
)
. Additionally, our analysis did not capture urban-rural distinctions, which concurrent research identifies as a meaningful predictor of GenAI adoption in the US
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
, thus suggesting an important avenue for future investigation.
However, once users cross the adoption threshold, these patterns shift substantially. Age, educational, and socioeconomic factors lose consistent predictive power, and usage frequency becomes scattered and highly intent-dependent.
For example, LT literacy—the strongest predictor of GenAI adoption—has no predictive power for how frequently a user would rely on GenAI to search for new information on the web.
This divergence highlights how GenAI chatbots serve diverse needs. A lack of technical skill does not prevent engagement for many of these purposes. Consequently, studies that only measure overall usage frequency without distinguishing between user intents risk overlooking this critical variation.
Still, self-reported competence on AI and language technologies is low, and both current users and around half of non-users express desire for more education on the matter.

Gender is the striking exception: women lag in both adoption and usage frequency. Importantly, literacy only partially explain the disparities, suggesting competence alone cannot account for gender differences in GenAI chatbots engagement. This pattern points to trust issues and social and cultural factors beyond competence
(
Otis et al. 2024
)
. In educational settings, female students exhibit lower trust in GenAI
(
Møgelvang et al. 2024
)
or fear being penalized and perceived as cheating when using it
(
Carvajal et al. 2024
)
. By taking an intersectional approach, we find that gender differentials in adoption are lower among younger cohorts, suggesting that when the use of GenAI chatbots becomes normalized gender divide decreases. However, future work and interventions are needed to understand such divides and avoid entrenching inequalities. Moreover, the underrepresentation of women in user data may contribute to biased data used to train and improve future models.

Our study provides novel empirical evidence and data on GenAI chatbot adoption, use, and literacy that can inform interventions. As these technologies become embedded in daily life, these insights can guide policymakers, educators, and developers in ensuring equitable access while protecting vulnerable users from the risks of uninformed adoption.
