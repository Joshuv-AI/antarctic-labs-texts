Telomere-to-Telomere genome assembly of an endangered tree Berchemiella wilsonii (Rhamnaceae)
Authors: Jianghao Pang, Zhiqiang Xiao, Yeqing Wang, Yufen Cheng, Yunlong Tang, Shuaishuai Song, Hao Wu, Xinzeng Wei, Mingxi Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Berchemiella wilsonii (Schneid.) Nakai is one of the National Key Protected Wild Plants in China, and one of the 120 Plant Species with Extremely Small Populations in China. By combining PacBio HiFi sequencing, DNBSEQ sequencing and Hi-C sequencing, we have assembled a chromosome-scale, haplotype-resolved genome for B. wilsonii. The final assembled haplotype A and haplotype B genomes were 216.84 Mb and 217.69 Mb, anchored to 2n = 24 chromosomes, all chromosomal ends contain telomeric characteristic motifs (TTTAGGG), only haplotype A has one gap, both close to a T2T genome assembly, with contig N50 lengths of 18.24 Mb and 18.03 Mb, respectively. Further, BUSCO analysis showed an extremely high assembly completeness (98.9% complete BUSCO genes). The genome contains a total of 22,828 coding genes, of which 21,992 (96.34%) were functionally annotated. This is the first report of the genome of B. wilsonii and the high-quality genome will provide new insights into evolutionary history and taxonomic classification challenges of endangered plant species.

Background & Summary

Berchemiella wilsonii (Schneid.) Nakai is a deciduous broad-leaved shrub or tree species. The mature trees produce numerous small bisexual flowers in May and June. The ellipsoidal drupes ripen in July and August and are mainly dispersed by gravity and birds. B. wilsonii is an endangered tree species in the China Plant Red Data Book1, one of the National Key Protected Wild Plants in China, and one of the 120 Plant Species with Extremely Small Populations in China. This tree species was first recorded in 1907 by Ernest Wilson in western Hubei, central China. However, after the first record, it was not found for more than 90 years and was presumed extinct in the wild. Until 2001, it was rediscovered in the Hubei Houhe National Nature Reserve2, which is ca. 130 km away from its type locality. Since then, several populations have been discovered, but most are very small3.

Berchemiella Nakai, a genus of the Rhamnaceae family, is endemic to East Asia. It comprises three species and one variety. B. wilsonii, B. yunnanensis Y. L. Chen & P. K. Chou, and B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata H. Qian are endemic to China, while B. berchemiifolia (Makino) Nakai is found in Japan and Korea. All the four taxa are restricted to extremely narrow geographic ranges and isolated small populations4. Field surveys at different time periods found that some local populations of B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata have become extinct within the last few decades, probably caused by habitat destruction by human activities4–6. Furthermore, B. yunnanensis Y. L. Chen & P. K. Chou was first sampled in 1940 at Funing county, Yunnan province, but after that it has not been discovered in the wild. In this situation, the four taxa were all classified as endangered plant species in the countries where they were found7–9. However, the external and internal factors that drive the endangered status of this genus have been poorly understood, except some cues from seed germination tests and conservation genetics of B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata. The seed vigor and seed germination rate of B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata are both very low10. Conservation genetic studies with various molecular markers demonstrated low to high within-population genetic diversity and restricted gene flow among populations5–8. In contrast to the low resolution of traditional markers, genome assembly and re-sequencing can provide new insights for how these species became endangered by revealing evolutionary history11,12.

The phylogenetic relationships of the genus of Berchemiella with its closely related genera, such as Berchemia and Chaydaia, was still debated for a long time4, and until now it is still lack of phylogenomic evidence. Similarly, the phylogenetic relationships of the four taxa within the genus of Berchemiella are still unclear now. For example, B. koreana from Korea was viewed as a synonym of B. berchemiifolia. Additionally, the variety B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata was described based on only one morphological character4. B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata from Zhengjiang and Anhui provinces, but B. wilsonii from Hubei province, has trichomes on petioles. In 2010, a new population was found in Shengzhou, Zhejiang province and the discoverer named it as B. wilsonii13. This is debated because the geographic distribution of this population was far from the other populations of B. wilsonii and all the four known populations of B. wilsonii var. pubipetiolata located between them3. The unclear species delimitation can hamper effective conservation management. Genome re-sequencing can substantially address the classification challenges resulted from only morphological observation14,15.

In the last two decades, high-throughput DNA sequencing technology (e.g., genome re-sequencing) has substantially improved the availability of genomic data of non-model species and largely broken through the technical limits of conservation genomics and species delimitation14,16. Furthermore, genome assembly has entered the telomere-to-telomere (T2T) era and revolutionized genomics research17. However, the T2T genome of the genus of Berchemiella is still not available. Until now, only two species (i.e., Ziziphus jujuba and Rhamnella rubrinervis) of the Rhamnaceae family have available reference genomes18. This impedes the investigation of the drivers of population decline or even local extinction of this endangered genus, and the enhancement of the resolution in species delimitation of this genus for effective conservation.

Here, T2T and gap-free genome of B. wilsonii was de novo assembled using PacBio HiFi, DNBSEQ and Hi-C sequencing with a variety of assembly strategies (Fig. 1). The high-quality genome of B. wilsonii will provide a valuable genomic resource for endangered plant species conservation and facilitate studies in deciphering the evolutionary history of declining populations and in addressing the taxonomic classification challenges of the genus of Berchemiella, and even the Rhamnaceae family.Fig. 1Synteny and genomic features of haplotype A of Berchemiella wilsonii. a, Pseudochromosomes; b, Gene number density; c, Repeat density; d, GC density.

Methods

Plant materials

Samples of B. wilsonii were collected from Wuhan Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Wuhan City, Hubei Province, China. Photos were taken during the sampling process for preservation as vouchers. Tissues, including leaves, stems, and roots were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in refrigerator at −80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves using the modified CTAB method for genome sequencing19.

PacBio HiFi sequencing

Libraries for single-molecule real-time (SMRT) Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) genome sequencing were constructed following the standard protocols of PacBio at Wuhan Benagen Technology Company Limited (Wuhan, China). DNA quality and concentration were assessed using the NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Then, 7 μg of high-quality genomic DNA was sheared to ∼15 kb targeted size, followed by damage and end repair, blunt-end adapter ligation, size selection, and binding with sequencing primers and enzymes. Finally, placed into the Revio system (Pacbio, USA) sequencer for sequencing. A total of 5,237,074 HIFI reads with ~99.46 Gb (~454 × the assembled genome) sequencing data were generated, with an average read length of 18.99 kb. The N50 was 19.04 kb (Supplementary Table S1).

DNBSEQ sequencing

After quality confirmation, the DNA sample was randomly fragmented using a Covaris ultrasonicator, and size selection of 200–400 bp fragments was carried out with magnetic beads. This was followed by terminal repair, A-tailing, sequencing adapter addition, purification, and PCR amplification to complete the whole library preparation process. The read length of the library is about 450 bp. After the construction of the library, Qubit 3.0 was used for preliminary quantification, and the library was diluted. Then Agilent 2100 was used to detect the inserted fragments of the library. After the size of the inserted fragments met the expectation, the effective concentration of the library was accurately quantified using Q-PCR to ensure library quality. Qualifying libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI Inc., China). Raw reads were filtered using Fastp v0.21.020. A total of 2,717,116,828 raw reads were generated, producing approximately 402.69 Gb (about 1838 × assembled genome) of clean sequencing data, with a GC content of 35.38% (Supplementary Table S2). In addition, we used the tool BLASTN v2.11.0 + to compare the first 50,000 reads generated by sequencing with the NT database v202107. Among 50,000 reads, 11,813 could be aligned to the database. The assignment results showed that the species with the most alignments was B. wilsonii, with a total of 2,386 reads, accounting for 4.77%. No obvious exogenous contamination was found.

Hi-C sequencing

Plant tissues were crosslinked with formaldehyde, digested with DPNII endonuclease, and labelled with biotin. The interacting DNA fragments were then connected to each other to ensure that the location of the DNA is determined during subsequent sequencing and analysis. DNA was purified and captured, and the purified DNA was mechanically sheared into 200–400 bp fragments by Fragmentase, and the ends of the fragments were repaired. The Hi-C libraries were enriched, treated with A tail, and then amplified by PCR. Subsequently, the paired-end sequencing process (PE150) was run on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI Inc., China) to obtain 150 bp reads. A total of 2,264,122,316 raw reads were generated. This produced ~339.01 Gb (~1548 × assembled genome) of clean sequencing data for subsequent pseudochromosome assembly (Supplementary Table S3).

Transcriptome sequencing

One tissue sample was collected from each of the roots, leaves and stems. Total RNA was extracted using the R6827 Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), and RNA samples were examined using the NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, DE) and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). mRNA was extracted from total RNA, and the library was constructed through a series of steps including enrichment, cutting, cDNA synthesis, PCR amplification, and cyclization. After library detection was completed by using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, sequencing was performed at the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI Inc., China). In the end, a total of ~ 37.64 Gb (125,469,026 reads) of RNA-seq data was obtained (Supplementary Table S4).

Estimation of genome size and heterozygosity

The basic features of the genome including genome size, heterozygosity, and proportion of repeated sequences were predicted using the k-mer counting method21. DNBSEQ paired-end reads were utilized to calculate the counts of k-mers with a size of 19 bp using Jellyfish v2.2.10. Genomescope v2.0 was then employed to predict the genome features based on the frequency of k-mers22. The estimated genome sizes of B. wilsonii were 199.38 Mb, with heterozygosity values of 0.53% (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S5).Fig. 2Genome survey of B. wilsonii based on k-mer analysis.

De novo genome assembly

Using the assembler Hifiasm v0.16.1-r37523, two assembly strategies of HiFi reads and HiFi + Hi-C reads are used to generate the assembly of preliminary haplotype parsing (Supplementary Table S6). First, Hifiasm was used to perform all-versus-all alignment, clustering and error correction on reads. Subsequently, haplotype phasing was performed on the read segments based on nucleotide differences at heterozygous sites, that is, the sequences of the vast majority of reads share identical sequences, but there were stable base differences at specific sites. If each distinct nucleotide at a certain variant site is supported by at least three reads, these reads are assigned to distinct haplotypes. During subsequent assembly, reads assigned to haplotype 1 are classified as haplotype A, and reads assigned to haplotype 2 are classified as haplotype B. After a comprehensive evaluation, the Contig-level assembly version of the best quality pure HiFi strategy was selected as the best component for further polishing and scaffolding of the T2T genomic skeleton. In this selected primary assembly version, the assembled genome size was 219.57 Mb and 218.77 Mb in haplotype A and haplotype B distributed across 41 and 30 contigs with N50 of 17.46 Mb and 17.33 Mb, and BUSCO scores of 98.9 and 99.0, respectively.

Hi-C interaction was used to cluster Contig sequences into n chromosome groups by using the software ALLHiC v0.9.824, and the Contigs within the n chromosome groups were ordered and oriented, and then manually refined with Juicebox v1.11.0825. A total of 436,124,312 bp (~99.49%) contig sequences were anchored to 24 chromosomes. Minimap2 v2.17-r94126 was used to compare mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, remove sequences with more than 50% base alignment, remove bacterial contamination through blast refseq database, and remove contigs with low sequencing depth. Winnowmap v1.1127 was used to fill gaps, and the reliability of gap was verified by comparing with other assembled version. Finally, HiFi reads ≥ 10 kb were compared with Gap-filled genomes to correct errors and further improve genome assembly. Identification of telomeric and centromeric regions

For telomeres recognition, Winnowmap v1.1127 was used to align all PacBio HiFi reads to pseudochromosomes, and reads that were aligned once within 50 bp of the end of the chromosome were retained. The occurrences of telomere repeat motifs (‘CCCTAAA’/‘TTTAGGG’) in each read were calculated, and the read with the most occurrence was defined as the telomeric reference and the others were defined as the telomeric query, medaka_consensu v1.2.1 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka), was used to generate consensus sequences using the telomeric reference and the telomeric query, nucmer v3.128 was used to align the consistency sequences to each chromosome, and the original telomere sequences were replaced with the best alignment results (Supplementary Table S7). For centromere identification, Tandem repeats(TR)were identified on the genome using software TRF29 with parameters 27 7 80 10 50 500-d-h. Tandem repeat monomer clustering using cd-hit30 with parameters -c 0.9-n 5-d 0-g 1-aS 0.9. Tandem repeat monomers with greater than 90% similarity will be assigned to the corresponding Tandem Repeat Clusters (TRC), with the representative sequence in the longest TRC as lib, using Repeatmasker with parameters - nolow-no_is-norna-parallel 2-lib trc_lib.fa) searches representative sequences on chromosomes to reduce overlap between the longest TRC and others. Finally, bedtools merge was used to integrate the centromere regions of each chromosome, and bedtools was used to calculate gene density with 50 kb as a window, and the RIdeogram package was used to map the centromere regions of all chromosomes (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S8).Fig. 3Distribution of centromeric regions, telomeres, 45S_rDNA, and gene density on chromosomes of haplotype A (a) and haplotype B (b).

Distribution of centromeric regions, telomeres, 45S_rDNA, and gene density on chromosomes of haplotype A (a) and haplotype B (b).

DNA repeats annotation

RepeatModeler v2.0.431 was used based on de novo method, and the model sequence was predicted based on its genome sequence. LTR-FINDER vOfficial release of LTR-FINDER-parallel32 and LTRharvest v1.6233 were used to predict LTR sequence. Use LTR-retriever v2.9.034 to de-redundant the predicted results from the above two methods to obtain a non-redundant LTR sequence. Use TEclass v2.1.335 to combine the two denovo sequences to obtain a denovo repeat library. The Unknown content was redefined by the software, and then the RepBase library v20181026 and de novo library were combined and the software RepeatMasker v4.1.536 was used to compare and predict the repeat sequence to get the De novo + repbase result. RepeatProteinMask v4.1.5 from the RepeatMasker package was used to identify transposable element (TE)-related proteins based on protein homology. Finally, all the repetitive prediction results were combined to remove the redundancy. The results of the Combined TEs of the final genome scattered repeat set were obtained. Tandem repeat prediction was made using software TRF v4.0929 and software MISA v2.137. In summary, in haplotype A and haplotype B, the repeats are estimated to account for 43.77% (94.89 Mb) and 43.85% (95.45 Mb) of the genome size, of which 2.73% and 2.49% are uncharacterized repeats. Tandem repeats account for 5.23% and 5.199% of the genome size, with 201,996 and 202,247 microsatellites. Long terminal repeats (LTR) were dominant (45.57 Mb, 21.01% and 42.92 Mb, 19.72%), with Gypsy (16.47 Mb and 14.22 Mb) and Copia (12.20 Mb and 10.74 Mb) being the most abundant subtypes, accounting for 7.59% (6.53%) and 5.62% (4.93%) of the assembly of haplotype A (haplotype B), respectively.

Genome annotation

For gene structure annotation, a combination of transcript-, de novo- and homology-based methods was applied. The DNBSEQ RNA clean reads were compared to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.138, the transcript was constructed with StringTie v2.2.139, and then the coding DNA sequences (CDS) were predicted using TransDecoder v5.7.0. For the homology-based method, Tblastn v2.13.040 with default settings was employed to predict homologous sequences by mapping protein sequences of Ziziphus jujuba, Arabidopsis thaliana, Ficus carica, Morus notabilis, wild and cultivated Boehmeria nivea, and Exonerate v2.4.041 was used to predict transcripts and coding regions based on the mapping results. After the repetitive sequences were masked, AUGUSTUS v3.5.042 and GlimmerHMM v3.0.443 were used for de novo gene prediction based on the hidden Markov model, and the model was trained based on homology and self-transcriptome data. maker v3.01.0344 was used to merge the predicted gene models by the three methods. Firstly, verify the accuracy of the open reading frame (ORF) region, coding region initiation and termination, and filter gene length of all the above predicted genes, based on the verification results, genes supported by multiple evidence were preferentially selected, then the single evidence results were selected according to the sequence of transcript-, homology- and de novo-based evidence. Finally, AGAT v1.4.145 was used to statistically analyze the complete set of genes. A total of 22,828 coding genes were identified in haplotype A, with an average of 5.59 exons per transcript, and single-exon genes accounted for 18.20% of all genes. Similarly, 22,945 coding genes were identified in haplotype B, with an average of 5.55 exons per transcript. Single-exon genes accounted for 18.5% of all genes (Supplementary Table S9).

The BUSCO v5.8.3(viridiplantae_odb10 database)46 assessment showed that both haplotype A and haplotype B were 99.8% Complete BUSCOs (99.1% Complete and Single-Copy BUSCOs) matched. Furthermore, the quality and consistency were evaluated using OMArk v2.1.047 and PSAURON v1.0.448 (Supplementary Table S10).

Gene functional annotations were assigned based on sequence and Motif conservation. For the assignment based on sequence conservation, DIAMOND was used to align the predicted protein sequence with several protein databases (Uniprot49, NR50, KOG51), and the highest proportion of annotation was 95.36% and 95.22% in haplotype A and haplotype B, respectively (database NR). For the assignment based on Motif conservation, InterProScan v5.55-88.052 identifies motifs and domains by matching them with the subdata Pfam in the database InterPro. KEGG pathway annotations were obtained using KofamKOALA53. EggNOG-mapper v2.0.13754 was used for GO annotation. The highest proportion of conservative annotation based on Motif was 93.67% and 93.06% in haplotype A and haplotype B, respectively (Interpro database). A total of 21,992 (96.34%) and 22,079 (96.23%) functional genes were annotated in haplotype A and haplotype B by aligning with public protein databases, respectively (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S11). For non-coding RNA, tRNAscan-SE V.2.0.1255 was used to annotate tRNA sequences in the genome, RNAmmer v1.256 was used to annotate rRNA, and INFERNAL v1.1.457 was used to annotate NcRNA. In haplotype A/B, a total of 2,552/2,775 non-coding RNAs were identified, including 1,406/1,691 rRNAs and 378/379 tRNAs sequences.Fig. 4Functional annotation of genes for haplotype A (a) and haplotype B (b).

Functional annotation of genes for haplotype A (a) and haplotype B (b).

Genome evolution analysis

Orthofinder v2.3.1258 performed gene family clusters based on the amino acid sequences of the selected species for subsequent phylogenetic and gene family contraction and expansion analysis. A total of 15 species were selected, these include Glycine max, Medicago truncatula, Ficus erecta, M. notabilis, B. nivea, Cannabis sativa, Z. jujuba, Rhamnella rubrinervis, Pyrus pyrifolia, Rosa chinensis, A. thaliana, Brassica rapa, Oryza sativa, Euphorbia peplus, and Salix brachista. Using BLASTP v2.6.040 with an E-value of 10−5, a total of 74,501 orthologous gene families with 542,463 genes were identified in all species, and 7,846 gene families with 253,534 genes were shared by all species. The gene families specific to haplotype A and haplotype B were 664 (715 genes) and 775 (797 genes), respectively (Supplementary Table S12).

Based on the 110 identified protein-coding single-copy genes, MUSCLE v3.8.3159 was used to perform multiple sequence alignment for the protein sequences of each single-copy gene family, and then TRIMAL v1.2rev5960 was used to filter the comparison results, the filtered comparison results were combined and linked to super gene. Finally, RAxML v8.2.10 (model PROTGAMMAWAG)61 was used to construct Multi-Locus species phylogenetic tree based on super gene. Based on topological structure and fossil time node of phylogenetic tree, the MCMCTREE subroutine v4.9 of PAML package was used with parameters: nsample = 3000000; burnin = 8000000; seqtype = 0; model = 462 to estimate the divergence time. Using CAFE v3.163, the birth-mortality model was used to estimate the number of genes in the gene family of each branching ancestor based on the results of species phylogenetic tree and gene family clustering, so as to predict the contraction and expansion of the gene families of this species relative to the ancestor. The criterion for defining significance is P-value ≤ 0.05. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed the closest relationship between B. wilsonii and R. rubrinervis, with an estimated divergence time of ~ 10.6 Mya. There were 176 gene families (641 genes) and 143 gene families (539 genes) expanded, 260 (451 genes) and 318 (612 genes) gene families contracted in haplotype A and haplotype B, respectively (Fig. 5). The expanded and contracted gene families enriched 3084 GO terms and 22 KEGG pathways.Fig. 5Phylogenetic tree, divergence time, and gene family expansion or contraction.

The previously identified orthologous and paralogous genes of A. thaliana, Z. jujuba, M. notabilis and the species in this study were selected. MCScanX64 with default parameters was used for synteny analysis of the genome. Each syntenic block required ≥5 gene pairs, and then the yn00 module in PAML v4.962 was used to calculate the synonymous nucleotide substitution (Ks) of the syntenic gene pairs. Density maps were also drawn using R package ggplot2 v2.2.1 to determine possible events of whole-genome duplication (WGD). The Ks density distribution map showed that the peak value of B. wilsonii and other species was ~1.6 (Fig. 6). Technical Validation

We evaluated the level of genome completeness of the final genome assembly using BUSCO v5.3.0 (embryophyta_odb10 database). The results showed that among the 1641 expected embryogenic genes, the Complete genes accounted for 98.9% in both haplotype A and haplotype B, among which the complete and single-copy genes accounted for 98% /97.8% in the two haplotypes, and only 0.2%/0.1% were identified as fragment genes (Table 2). The Merqury77 (k = 19) was used to build the k-mer database based on DNBSEQ sequencing data to evaluate the quality of genome assembly. Results showed that the consensus quality value (QV) and the completeness values of haplotypes A/B were 60.72/59.65 and 92.57%/92.73%, respectively, demonstrating high accuracy and completeness (Fig. 8). The clean reads obtained from each sequencing platform were mapped to the final haplotype A and haplotype B, and the mapping rates of the whole DNBSEQ short reads by BWA v0.7.17-r118878 were 97.62% and 97.55%, respectively, which means almost all sequencing data were represented (covering 100% of the total genome length, 99.99% with a coverage depth ≥ 4 × ; 99.98% with a coverage depth ≥ 10 × ; and 99.97% with a coverage depth ≥ 20 × , showing high coverage). Meanwhile, mapping of PacBio reads by minimap2 v2.17-r94126 was 99.98% (covering 99.99% of the total length of genome, in which 99.95% with a coverage depth ≥ 4 × ; and 99.92%/ 99.91% with a coverage depth ≥ 10 × , 99.90% / 99.89% with a coverage depth ≥ 20 × , respectively). The LTR assembly index (LAI) was calculated using LTR_retriever34 to evaluate the continuity of assembly based on the proportion of complete LTR retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) existing in the assembly genome to the total LTR-RTs. The results showed that at the whole-genome level, the LAI scores of haplotype A and haplotype B were 20.3% and 18.76% respectively, indicating extremely high assembly continuity.
