Fair Algorithms with Probing for Multi-Agent Multi-Armed Bandits
Tianyi Xu, Jiaxin Liu, Zizhan Zheng

Abstract
We propose a multi-agent multi-armed bandit (MA-MAB) framework aimed at ensuring fair outcomes across agents while maximizing overall system performance. A key challenge in this setting is decision-making under limited information about arm rewards. To address this, we introduce a novel probing framework that strategically gathers information about selected arms before allocation. In the offline setting, where reward distributions are known, we leverage submodular properties to design a greedy probing algorithm with a provable performance bound. For the more complex online setting, we develop an algorithm that achieves sublinear regret while maintaining fairness. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets show that our approach outperforms baseline methods, achieving better fairness and efficiency.

1
Introduction

The multi-agent multi-armed bandit (MA-MAB) framework models a scenario where
M
agents compete for
A
arms over discrete time steps. In each round, a decision-maker assigns arms to the agents and observes the corresponding rewards. One such application is ridesharing, where drivers (agents) independently choose geographic regions (arms) to serve based on factors such as demand and availability, which directly influence their rewards.

Maximizing total expected reward is a common objective in the MA-MAB framework, but it can lead to inequalities in practice
[
Joseph et al. 2016
]
. Optimizing for aggregate performance often results in a concentration of profitable arms among a few agents, disadvantaging others
[
Kleinberg et al. 2018
,
Agarwal et al. 2014
]
. This is especially concerning in applications where fair resource allocation is essential, such as in ridesharing platforms, where drivers need equitable access to profitable areas
[
Bubeck and Cesa-Bianchi 2012
,
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
]
, and in content recommendation systems, where creator exposure should not be monopolized.

Many MA-MAB studies aim to improve fairness, often by modifying allocation strategies or imposing fairness constraints on overall performance
[
Joseph et al. 2018
,
Patil et al. 2020
]
. Others adopt fairness-aware objectives, such as the Nash Social Welfare (NSW) criterion, which balances rewards among agents by penalizing allocations where any agent’s reward is too low
[
Heidari et al. 2018
,
Zhang and Conitzer 2021
]
. Notably,
[
Jones et al. 2023
]
demonstrated that applying NSW in MA-MAB frameworks can balance efficiency and fairness, preventing any agent from being consistently left out of rewarding arms.

However, a common limitation of existing approaches is their reliance on instantaneous reward feedback
[
Li et al. 2016
]
to update estimates and guide allocation decisions, whereas, in many practical settings, critical information remains unknown. For example, in ridesharing, uncertainties in passenger requests and real-time road conditions can significantly compromise reward estimates and fairness-oriented strategies. In such uncertain environments, probing offers a promising approach to gather additional information before making decisions
[
Oh and Iyengar 2019
]
. Probing was originally studied in economics
[
Weitzman 1979
]
as a cost-incurring method for acquiring additional information to improve decision-making under uncertainty. It has since been applied in database query optimization
[
Deshpande et al. 2016
,
Liu et al. 2008
]
, real-time traffic monitoring for vehicle routing
[
Bhaskara et al. 2020b
]
, and wireless network scheduling
[
Xu et al. 2021
,
Xu et al. 2023
]
.

Our work extends the NSW-based multi-agent multi-armed bandit (MA-MAB) framework by incorporating a probing mechanism to gather extra information, refining reward estimates and improving the exploration-exploitation balance while ensuring fairness. The decision-maker first probes a subset of arms for detailed reward data, then allocates agents fairly. In the offline setting, where reward distributions are known, we develop a greedy probing algorithm with a provable performance bound, leveraging the submodular structure of our objective. For the online setting, we propose a combinatorial bandit algorithm with a derived regret bound.

Integrating probing into MA-MAB poses the challenge of balancing exploration and exploitation while maintaining fairness. Related probing problems have been shown to be NP-hard
[
Goel et al. 2006
]
, and while previous works, such as
[
Zuo et al. 2020b
]
, have explored probing strategies in MA-MAB, they have simplified assumptions, such as limiting rewards to Bernoulli distributions and ignoring fair allocation. Our framework overcomes these limitations by considering general reward distributions, ensuring fairness, and introducing a probing budget to optimize performance under exploration constraints.

The primary contributions of our work are as follows:

(1) We extend the multi-agent MAB framework with a novel probing mechanism that tests selected arms before allocation. This approach ensures fairness through Nash Social Welfare optimization, departing from previous work that focuses solely on aggregate rewards
[
Zuo et al. 2020a
]
.

(2) For known reward patterns in offline setting, we develop a simple yet effective greedy probing strategy with provable performance guarantees, while maintaining fairness across agents.

(3) For the online setting where rewards are unknown, we propose an algorithm that balances exploration and fairness, proving that probing and fair allocation do not compromise asymptotic performance.

(4) Experiments on both synthetic and real-world data demonstrate that our method achieves superior performance compared to baselines, validating the effectiveness of the probing strategy and our algorithm.

2
Related Work

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) framework has been key for sequential decision-making under uncertainty
[
Lai and Robbins 1985
,
Auer et al. 2002
,
Audibert et al. 2009
,
Garivier and Cappé 2013
]
. While early MAB models typically involve a single decision-maker choosing one arm per round, many real-world problems involve multiple agents acting simultaneously
[
Martínez-Rubio et al. 2019
]
, each potentially choosing different arms
[
Hossain et al. 2021
]
. Most existing multi-agent MAB methods focus on maximizing total rewards, but this can unfairly favor some agents. To address fairness, researchers have proposed approaches to protect individual agents
[
Joseph et al. 2016
]
. Among these, optimizing Nash Social Welfare (NSW)
[
Kaneko and Nakamura 1979
]
has been effective, as it maximizes the product of all agents’ utilities, ensuring no agent is left without rewards.

In many practical settings, some critical information about reward distributions is unknown
[
Slivkins 2019
,
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
]
. For example, in ridesharing, the exact distribution of passenger requests or traffic conditions may be uncertain. Existing fair MA-MAB methods typically rely on immediate feedback to update estimates, pulling arms without exploring these unknown factors
[
Liu and Zhao 2010
,
Gai and Krishnamachari 2014
]
. The concept of probing, or ”active exploration”
[
Chen et al. 2015
,
Amin et al. 2014
]
, offers a way to gather additional information by testing a subset of arms before fully committing. Instead of randomly allocating resources, a decision-maker picks a subset of arms to ”test,” observes partial feedback, and thus refines its understanding of the reward distributions, particularly when pulling poorly understood arms involves significant risk or opportunity cost
[
Golovin and Krause 2011
,
Bhaskara et al. 2020a
]
. However, most probing work focuses on efficiency-based metrics, like coverage or total reward, and does not directly address fairness across agents. As a result, there is a gap between fair multi-agent MAB solutions relying on passive exploration and probing strategies focusing on aggregate objectives. We bridge this gap by integrating probing into a fair multi-agent setting, using submodular properties for effective arm selection while prioritizing Nash Social Welfare to ensure fairness.

3
Problem Formulation

In this section, we define the fair multi-agent multi-armed bandit (MA-MAB) problem by extending the classical multi-armed bandit framework to incorporate fairness, multi-agent interactions, and the effect of probing decisions. The goal is to optimize both fairness and utility while accounting for probing overhead.

3.1
Agents, Arms, and Rewards

We consider a set of
M
agents (denoted by
[M]=\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
) and a set of
A
arms (denoted by
[A]=\{1,2,\ldots,A\}
). For each agent
j\in[M]
and each arm
a\in[A]
, let
D_{j,a}
denote the unknown reward distribution with cumulative distribution function
F_{j,a}
and mean
\mu_{j,a}
. At each time step
t\in[T]
, the decision-maker selects a probing set
S_{t}\subseteq[A]
and assigns an arm
a_{t}\in[A]
to each agent
j\in[M]
. For every probed arm
a\in S_{t}
, a reward realization
R_{j,a,t}
is drawn i.i.d. from
D_{j,a}
; for unprobed arms
a\in[A]\setminus S_{t}
, only the mean reward
\mu_{j,a}
is available. Let

R_{t}\coloneqq\{\,R_{j,a,t}:j\in[M],\,a\in[A]\,\}

denote the collection of reward realizations at time
t
. We assume that every reward realization
R_{j,a,t}
drawn from
D_{j,a}
lies in the interval
[0,1]
; consequently, the mean rewards satisfy
\mu_{j,a}\in[0,1]
.

3.2
Fairness Objective: Nash Social Welfare

To balance fairness and efficiency, we aim to maximize the expected Nash Social Welfare (NSW). For a given probing set
S_{t}
, an allocation policy
\pi_{t}=[\pi_{j,a,t}]\in[0,1]^{M\times A}
represents the probability that agent
j
is assigned arm
a
at time step
t
. Given the observed rewards
R_{t}
and the mean reward matrix

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\bigl[\mu_{j,a}\bigr]_{j\in[M],\,a\in[A]},

we define the NSW objective as

\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t})=\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\begin{split}&\sum_{a\in S_{t}}\pi_{j,a,t}\,R_{j,a,t}\\[2.84526pt]
&\quad+\sum_{a\notin S_{t}}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\mu_{j,a}\end{split}\right).

This objective ensures that both fairness (by preventing any agent’s expected reward from being too low) and overall efficiency are considered.

The allocation policy
\pi_{t}
must satisfy the following constraints:

\sum_{a\in[A]}\pi_{j,a,t}=1,\quad\forall j\in[M],

\sum_{j\in[M]}\pi_{j,a,t}\leq 1,\quad\forall a\in[A].

The first constraint ensures that each agent distributes their allocation probability mass across available arms, meaning that in expectation, each agent selects one arm. The second constraint ensures that the total probability mass assigned to each arm does not exceed one, preventing over-allocation. However, since
\pi_{j,a,t}
represents probabilities rather than deterministic assignments, partial allocations are allowed: an agent may be assigned multiple arms probabilistically, and an arm may be assigned to multiple agents in fractional proportions. This random allocation assumption is common in previous work, such as in
[
Jones et al. 2023
]
, where a similar allocation structure is used.

3.3
Probing Overhead

Probing incurs a cost that increases with the size of the probing set. Since probing provides additional reward realizations but also consumes resources, we model the effective (instantaneous) reward at time
t
as follows:

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{total}}=\Bigl(1-\alpha(|S_{t}|)\Bigr)\,\mathbb{E}_{R_{t}}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t})\Bigr],

where
\alpha:\{0,1,\ldots,I\}\to[0,1]
is a non-decreasing overhead function satisfying
\alpha(0)=0
and
\alpha(I)=1
. Here, the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{R_{t}}[\cdot]
is taken over the reward realizations
R_{t}
(with rewards drawn i.i.d. from the corresponding
D_{j,a}
).

This formulation accounts for the trade-off between exploration and exploitation. When more arms are probed, the decision-maker obtains more accurate information about reward distributions, leading to better allocation decisions. However, probing incurs costs, such as time delays, energy consumption, or computational overhead, which reduce the net benefit. For instance, in a wireless scheduling scenario, probing more channels provides better estimates of channel conditions but increases latency, reducing the system’s effective throughput
[
Xu et al. 2021
]
. This formulation is appropriate because the probing impact often scales with the system’s overall performance, and similar scaling effects have been modeled in prior work
[
Xu et al. 2021
]
. The function
\alpha(|S_{t}|)
captures this diminishing return, ensuring that excessive probing is discouraged.

3.4
Decision Problem

At each round
t
, the decision-maker proceeds in two stages:

Probing Stage:
Select a probing set
S_{t}\subseteq[A]
that balances exploration (gathering new information) and exploitation (leveraging current knowledge). Upon selecting
S_{t}
, the system probes the corresponding arms and obtains their reward realizations. That is, for each
a\in S_{t}
and each agent
j\in[M]
, a reward
R_{j,a,t}
is sampled i.i.d. from the distribution
D_{j,a}
.

Allocation Stage:
Based on the observed rewards
R_{t}
for arms in
S_{t}
(and the known mean rewards
\mu_{j,a}
for arms not in
S_{t}
), choose a probabilistic allocation policy
\pi_{t}\in\Delta^{A}
to assign arms to agents.

The decision-maker’s goal is to select
(S_{t},\pi_{t})
in each round so as to maximize
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{total}}
and thereby achieve sublinear cumulative regret.

3.5
Regret and Performance Measure

To evaluate the performance of our online learning approach, we define regret by comparing the achieved reward with the optimal reward obtained in an offline setting.

In the offline setting, the decision-maker has full knowledge of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and can directly compute the optimal probing set and allocation policy
(S_{t}^{*},\pi_{t}^{*})
that maximizes the expected NSW objective:

(S_{t}^{*},\pi_{t}^{*})=\arg\max_{S,\pi}\Bigl(1-\alpha(|S|)\Bigr)\mathbb{E}_{R_{t}}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi)\Bigr].

This serves as a performance benchmark.

In contrast, the online setting requires the decision-maker to learn
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
over time while making sequential decisions based on observed rewards. The cumulative regret measures the performance gap between the online strategy and the offline optimal policy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{regret}}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl[\,\Bigl(1-\alpha\bigl(|S_{t}^{*}|\bigr)\Bigr)\,

\displaystyle\cdot\;\mathbb{E}_{R_{t}}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*})\Bigr]\;-\;\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{total}}\Bigr].

Efficient algorithms aim to ensure that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{regret}}(T)
grows sublinearly with
T
, thereby balancing fairness, overall utility, and the cost of probing.

4
The Offline Setting

In the offline setting, all reward distributions are known in advance, reducing the problem to a static optimization over the probing set
S
without the time index
t
. Given any fixed
S
, the optimal allocation policy
\pi

[
Cole and Gkatzelis 2015
]
can be computed, allowing the effective reward to be expressed as a function of
S
.

This optimization is computationally challenging, as similar problems have been shown to be NP-hard
[
Goel et al. 2006
]
. To address this, we develop a greedy algorithm based on submodular maximization techniques to obtain an approximate solution while accounting for probing costs.

4.1
Optimization Objective and Probing Utility

Since the offline setting is static, we drop the time index
t
. The decision-maker is now tasked with selecting a probing set
S\subseteq[A]
from the
A
available arms. For each agent
j\in[M]
and each arm
a\in[A]
, let
D_{j,a}
denote the known reward distribution with CDF
F_{j,a}
and mean
\mu_{j,a}
. When an arm
a
is probed (i.e.
a\in S
), a reward realization
R_{j,a}
is observed (drawn i.i.d. from
D_{j,a}
); otherwise, the decision-maker relies on the mean reward
\mu_{j,a}
.

Given a probing set
S
, an allocation policy
\pi=[\pi_{j,a}]
can be applied to assign arms to agents. For any fixed
S
, one may compute the optimal allocation policy that maximizes the expected Nash Social Welfare
[
Cole and Gkatzelis 2015
]
. Hence, we define the effective objective as

\mathcal{R}(S)=\Bigl(1-\alpha(|S|)\Bigr)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr],

\pi^{*}(S)
denotes the optimal allocation policy given
S
, and
\alpha(|S|)
is the probing overhead function.

Directly optimizing
\mathcal{R}(S)
is challenging due to both the combinatorial nature of the set
S
and the multiplicative form of
\mathrm{NSW}
. To address this, we decompose
\mathcal{R}(S)
into two components.

Defining a simplified utility
g(S)
.

To isolate the contribution of probed arms and simplify the multiplicative structure, we define

g(S)\;=\;\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{S}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\Biggl[\prod_{j\in[M]}\Bigl(\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a}\,R_{j,a}\Bigr)\Biggr],

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{A}_{S}=\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times A}\mid

\displaystyle\pi_{j,a}=0,\quad\forall a\notin S,\;\forall j\in[M],

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a}\leq 1,\quad\forall j\in[M]\Bigr\}.

Since only arms in
S
yield random rewards, this formulation ensures that
g(S)
is naturally monotonic in
S
.

Defining the non-probing utility
h(S)
.

Complementarily, we define the non-probing utility as

h(S)\;=\;\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}}\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\,\mu_{j,a}\right),

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}=\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times A}\mid

\displaystyle\pi_{j,a}=0,\quad\forall a\in S,\;\forall j\in[M],

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus S}\pi_{j,a}\leq 1,\quad\forall j\in[M]\Bigr\}.

This formulation captures the baseline utility achievable by allocating exclusively among the unprobed arms. Similar to
g(S)
, the allocation policy ensures that each agent’s total allocation probability does not exceed one, allowing partial assignments across multiple arms.

Log Transformation and Piecewise-Linear Approximation.

A typical approach for handling the product
\prod_{j}(\dots)
is to consider the logarithmic transform, i.e.,
\log\bigl(g(S)\bigr)
, which converts the product into a sum. However, directly working with
\log\bigl(g(S)\bigr)
is challenging. Hence, inspired by classical piecewise-linear approximations of concave functions in global optimization
[
Horst and Tuy 1996
,
Tawarmalani and Sahinidis 2002
]
, we construct a piecewise-linear, concave, and non-decreasing function
\phi:[0,x_{\max}]\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x>0
,

\phi(x)\geq\log(x).

We then define

f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S)=\phi\bigl(g(S)\bigr).

This construction yields an objective that (i) upper-bounds
\log\bigl(g(S)\bigr)
and (ii) exhibits diminishing returns, as explained next.

4.2
Piecewise-Linear Upper Bound Construction

To approximate
\log\bigl(g(S)\bigr)
in a tractable manner, we construct
\phi
as follows:

•

Upper Bound Assumption:
Assume that
x_{\max}
is an upper bound on
g(S)
, i.e., for all
S
,
g(S)\leq x_{\max}
.

•

Partitioning:
Partition the interval
[0,x_{\max}]
into segments with breakpoints
\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{L}
(with
0<\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{L}=x_{\max}
). Moreover, choose the partition sufficiently fine so that there exists a constant
\eta>0
(with
\eta
sufficiently small) such that

\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}\leq\eta\quad\text{for all }i,

and, importantly, for any
S\subseteq[A]
and any arm
a\notin S
,

g(S\cup\{a\})-g(S)\leq\eta.

This guarantees that for every
S
and
a
, both
g(S)
and
g(S\cup\{a\})
lie within the same linear segment of
\phi
.

•

Tangent Lines:
For each breakpoint
\tau_{i}
, define the tangent line

T_{\tau_{i}}(z)=\log(\tau_{i})+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}(z-\tau_{i}).

By the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
, for every
z\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
we have

\log(z)\leq T_{\tau_{i}}(z).

We then define

\phi(z)=\max_{0\leq i<L}T_{\tau_{i}}(z),

so that for all
z>0
,

\phi(z)\geq\log(z).

By construction,
\phi
is concave and non-decreasing. In particular, if
x\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
and
y\in[\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
with
x<y
(so that
\tau_{i}\leq\tau_{j}
), the slopes on these segments are given by
1/\tau_{i}
and
1/\tau_{j}
, respectively. Since
\tau_{i}<\tau_{j}
, we have

\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}.

Thus, for any
0<x<y\leq x_{\max}
and any increments
d,d^{\prime}\geq 0
(with
d,d^{\prime}\leq\eta
so that
x+d
and
y+d^{\prime}
lie within single linear segments), it holds that

\frac{\phi(x+d)-\phi(x)}{d}=\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}=\frac{\phi(y+d^{\prime})-\phi(y)}{d^{\prime}}.

Additional Assumption.
In order to handle the case when the increments differ (i.e., when
d<d^{\prime}
), we assume (see Appendix for a detailed proof) that the partition is sufficiently fine so that if
x\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
and
y\in[\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
with
\tau_{i}\leq\tau_{j}
, then

\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}\geq\frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau_{j}}.

Finally, define the composed function

f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S)=\phi\bigl(g(S)\bigr).

By the properties above, we will show in Section 4.3 that
f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S)
is submodular. This submodularity property enables us to analyze the greedy probing strategy and derive its approximation guarantee. Specific details are shown in the Appendix.

Utilizing the construction above, we establish the following key properties (proofs are in the Appendix). These lemmas are fundamental for proving Theorem

For any

g(S)\leq g(T).

Lemma 2

(Monotonicity)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]

f_{\text{upper}}(S)\leq f_{\text{upper}}(T)

Lemma 3

(Submodularity)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]

f_{\text{upper}}(S\cup\{a\})-f_{\text{upper}}(S)\;\;\geq\;\;f_{\text{upper}}(T\cup\{a\})-f_{\text{upper}}(T).

(Monotonicity of
h(S)
)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]

h(S)\geq h(T).

Lemma 5
.

For any probing set
S

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(S)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha(|S|))\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha(|S|))(g(S)+h(S)).

5
The Online Setting

In the online setting, we consider a system with
M
agents and
A
arms over
T
rounds. Unlike the offline setting where rewards are known, here they are unknown, requiring a balance between exploration and exploitation.

Analysis

In this section, we present our regret analysis for the proposed OFMUP algorithm. Detailed proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

Smoothness of the NSW Objective

A key ingredient in our analysis is the smoothness of the NSW objective. We have the following result.

Lemma 6

(Smoothness of the NSW Objective)
.

Let
\pi\in\Delta^{M\times A}
be any allocation matrix where
\pi_{j,a}
denotes the probability that agent
j
is assigned arm
a
. Let
\mu,\nu\in[0,1]^{M\times A}
be two reward matrices. For any probing set
S\subseteq[A]
and observed rewards
R
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\nu,\pi)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a}\,\Bigl|\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}\Bigr|.

Concentration of Reward Estimates

Lemma 7

(Concentration of Reward Estimates)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, for all
t>A
,
a\in[A]
, and
j\in[M]
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mu_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{N_{j,a,t}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta}\Bigr)}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}=w_{j,a,t}\

Aggregate Reward Bound

Next, we introduce a bound relating the aggregated expected reward (computed with true means) to the confidence widths.

Lemma 8

(Aggregate Reward Bound)
.

Let

\displaystyle\gamma_{j,t}\;=\;\sum_{a\in[A]}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr),

\displaystyle Q(t,p)\;=\;\bigl\{\,j\in[M]:\gamma_{j,t}\,\geq\,2^{-p}\bigr\}\,.

Suppose that for all integers
p\geq 0
, we have

|Q(t,p)|<2^{p}\cdot 3\,\ln T.

Then the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\left(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+6\ln T\log M

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}w_{j,a,t}.

This lemma is crucial in controlling the error introduced by using upper confidence bounds.

Main Regret Guarantee

Combining the smoothness of the NSW objective, the concentration bounds, and the auxiliary technical results, we obtain our main regret guarantee.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the cumulative regret
R_{T}
of the Online Fair multi-Agent UCB with Probing algorithm (Algorithm 2) satisfies

R_{T}\;=\;O\!\Bigl(\,\zeta\;\bigl(\sqrt{M\,A\,T}\;+\;M\,A\bigr)\,\ln^{c}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{M\,A\,T}{\delta}\Bigr)\Bigr),

for some constant
c>0
.

Theorem
2
establishes that the cumulative regret of our probing-based algorithm is sublinear in
T
. Hence, as
T
grows, the average regret per round tends to zero. Although the probing mechanism does not alter the
\sqrt{T}
growth rate in the worst case, it substantially improves empirical performance by enabling faster elimination of suboptimal arms and more informed decision-making, as confirmed by our experimental results. Full proofs of Lemmas

–
8
and Theorem
2
are provided in the Appendix.

6
Experiments

We evaluate our framework using controlled simulations and a real-world ridesharing case study, with results demonstrating that the probing mechanism yields superior performance.

In our simulated environment, we consider a multi-agent multi-armed bandit (MA-MAB) setting with
M
agents and
A
arms (e.g.,
M=12,A=8
for small-scale and
M=20,A=10
for large-scale scenarios). For each agent–arm pair
(j,a)
, rewards are generated as i.i.d. samples from a fixed distribution
D_{j,a}
with mean
\mu_{j,a}
.

In the real-world setting, we apply our framework to the NYYellowTaxi 2016 dataset
[
Shah et al. 2020
]
, treating vehicles as agents and discretized pickup locations (binned into 0.01° grids) as arms. Rewards are determined by the normalized Manhattan distance between vehicles and pickup points—closer distances yield higher rewards. Vehicle locations are randomly pre-sampled within city bounds and remain fixed, underscoring the practical effectiveness of our approach.

To test different conditions, we consider two reward distributions: a Bernoulli distribution (with rewards 0 or 1 and probabilities based on
\mu
values in
[0.3,0.8]
) and a discrete distribution (with rewards sampled from
\{0.3,0.4,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8\}
). Cumulative regret is computed as in Equation (
3.5
), with the optimal reward determined via exhaustive search. For numerical stability, cumulative Nash social welfare is aggregated using the geometric mean of per-agent rewards. We further verify that our offline objective
f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S)
closely approximates
\log(g(S))
(difference 0.03), confirming the validity of our formulation. All experimental settings satisfy the assumptions outlined in our paper.

6.1
Baselines

Three algorithms serve as our comparison baselines:
Non-Probing
, a fair MAB algorithm from
[
Jones et al. 2023
]
without probing capability, focusing only on optimal allocation with current information;
Random Probing with Random Allocation (Random P+A)
, which randomly selects a fixed number of arms for probing and then allocates the arms randomly; and
Greedy Probing with Random Allocation (Greedy P+A)
, which uses the same greedy probing strategy as our algorithm but performs random allocation after probing.

6.2
Results

Figure 1 shows OFMUP’s performance across different tests. In small-scale tests (M = 12, A = 8), OFMUP reduces regret by 85% vs. Random P+A and 60% vs. Greedy P+A after 3000 steps. The advantage is greater in medium-scale tests (M = 20, A = 10), where regret is 88% lower than Random P+A and 80% lower than Greedy P+A at T=3000, showing improved scalability.
For discrete rewards, OFMUP continues to outperform. The gap with other methods grows as it learns reward patterns, reducing regret by 85% vs. Random P+A and 65% vs. Non-Probing at T=3000. Notably, Random P+A’s regret is slightly higher in small-scale tests due to fairness computation via the geometric mean and increased variability with smaller samples. These results validate our theoretical analysis—OFMUP effectively balances exploration and exploitation while ensuring fairness. The experiments further confirm the crucial role of probing in gathering information and guiding allocation. We further examine scalability by independently varying agent numbers (Figure 2). OFMUP maintains superior performance across all configurations, with its relative advantage becoming more pronounced as problem complexity increases.

7
Conclusion

We introduce a fair multi-agent multi-armed bandit framework that integrates selective probing with Nash Social Welfare objectives, enabling efficient information gathering and balanced decision-making. Our offline greedy probing algorithm provides theoretical guarantees, while the online extension ensures sublinear regret. Experiments on synthetic and real-world ridesharing data validate its effectiveness, demonstrating superior performance over baselines. The results confirm that probing refines reward estimates and improves the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, making our framework suitable for practical resource allocation.

Lemma 1

(Monotonicity of
g(S)
)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]
, we have

g(S)\leq g(T).

Proof.

Recall that

g(S)=\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{S}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a,t}\,R_{j,a}\right)\right],

where

\Delta^{A}_{S}=\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times A}:\;\pi_{j,a,t}=0\text{ for all }a\notin S,\quad\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a,t}\leq 1\text{ for all }j\in[M]\Bigr\}.

If
S\subseteq T
, then any allocation
\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{S}
satisfies
\pi_{j,a,t}=0
for all
a\in T\setminus S
. Consequently, for each
j\in[M]
we have

\sum_{a\in T}\pi_{j,a,t}=\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a,t}\leq 1.

That is,
\pi
is also a feasible solution in
\Delta^{A}_{T}
. Since the maximization defining
g(T)
is taken over a larger set than that for
g(S)
, we immediately obtain

g(S)=\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{S}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a,t}\,R_{j,a}\right)\right]\leq\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{T}}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\in T}\pi_{j,a,t}\,R_{j,a}\right)\right]=g(T).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2

(Monotonicity)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]
, we have
f_{\text{upper}}(S)\leq f_{\text{upper}}(T)
.

Proof.

Since
g(S)\leq g(T)
(Lemma
1
), and
\phi(\cdot)
is non-decreasing, we get

f_{\text{upper}}(S)=\phi\bigl(g(S)\bigr)\leq\phi\bigl(g(T)\bigr)=f_{\text{upper}}(T).

∎

Lemma 3

(Submodularity)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]
and
a\notin T
,

f_{\text{upper}}(S\cup\{a\})-f_{\text{upper}}(S)\;\;\geq\;\;f_{\text{upper}}(T\cup\{a\})-f_{\text{upper}}(T).

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]
and
a\in[A]\setminus T
. Define

x=g(S),\quad y=g(T),\quad d=g(S\cup\{a\})-g(S),\quad d^{\prime}=g(T\cup\{a\})-g(T).

By the monotonicity of
g
, we have
x\leq y
.

Based on the assumption made in the offline analysis that the partition
\{\tau_{i}\}
used in constructing
\phi
is sufficiently fine so that for any
S
and any
a\notin S
the marginal gain satisfies

g(S\cup\{a\})-g(S)\leq\eta,

for some small
\eta>0
, it follows that
x
and
x+d
lie in some interval
[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
and
y
and
y+d^{\prime}
lie in some interval
[\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
. Based on the construction of
\phi
, on the interval
[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
we have

\phi(z)=T_{\tau_{i}}(z)=\log(\tau_{i})+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}(z-\tau_{i}),

so that

\phi(x+d)-\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\,d.

Similarly, on
[\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
,

\phi(y+d^{\prime})-\phi(y)=\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}\,d^{\prime}.

Since
x\leq y
and by monotonicity of
g
the corresponding breakpoint indices satisfy
\tau_{i}\leq\tau_{j}
, it follows that

\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}.

In the case
d\geq d^{\prime}
, the desired inequality immediately holds:

\phi(x+d)-\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\,d\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}\,d^{\prime}=\phi(y+d^{\prime})-\phi(y).

In the case
d<d^{\prime}
, we further rely on the additional assumption that the partition is chosen sufficiently fine so that even if the absolute increment
d
is smaller than
d^{\prime}
, the relative difference in slopes compensates. Specifically, we assume that

\frac{d}{d^{\prime}}\geq\frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau_{j}}.

Under this condition, we have

\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\,d\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\Bigl(\frac{\tau_{i}}{\tau_{j}}d^{\prime}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\tau_{j}}\,d^{\prime},

so that

\phi(x+d)-\phi(x)\geq\phi(y+d^{\prime})-\phi(y).

Thus, in both cases, we conclude that

\phi\bigl(g(S\cup\{a\})\bigr)-\phi\bigl(g(S)\bigr)\geq\phi\bigl(g(T\cup\{a\})\bigr)-\phi\bigl(g(T)\bigr).

This shows that
f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S)=\phi(g(S))
is submodular.
∎

Lemma 4

(Monotonicity of
h(S)
)
.

For any
S\subseteq T\subseteq[A]
, we have

h(S)\geq h(T).

Proof.

Recall that

h(S)\;=\;\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}}\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\,\mu_{j,a}\right),

where

\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}\;=\;\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times A}\,\Bigm|\;\pi_{j,a}=0\text{ for all }a\in S,\quad\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus S}\pi_{j,a}\leq 1\text{ for all }j\in[M]\Bigr\}.

If
S\subseteq T
, then clearly

[A]\setminus T\subseteq[A]\setminus S.

Thus, any allocation
\pi
that is feasible for
\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus T}
(i.e.,
\pi_{j,a}=0
for all
a\in T
and
\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus T}\pi_{j,a}\leq 1
for all
j

\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}
.

To see this in detail, note that we can partition the set
[A]\setminus S
as

[A]\setminus S\;=\;\Bigl(([A]\setminus S)\cap T\Bigr)\cup\Bigl(([A]\setminus S)\cap([A]\setminus T)\Bigr).

Since
\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus T}
implies that
\pi_{j,a}=0
for all
a\in T
, it follows that for any
j\in[M]
,

\sum_{a\in([A]\setminus S)\cap T}\pi_{j,a}=0.

Therefore, we obtain

\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus S}\pi_{j,a}\;=\;\sum_{a\in([A]\setminus S)\cap T}\pi_{j,a}+\sum_{a\in([A]\setminus S)\cap([A]\setminus T)}\pi_{j,a}\;=\;\sum_{a\in([A]\setminus S)\cap([A]\setminus T)}\pi_{j,a}.

Since
([A]\setminus S)\cap([A]\setminus T)\subseteq[A]\setminus T
, we have

\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus S}\pi_{j,a}\leq\sum_{a\in[A]\setminus T}\pi_{j,a}\leq 1.

This shows that any
\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus T}
automatically satisfies the constraint for
\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}
.

Since the maximization in the definition of
h(S)
is taken over the larger set
\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}
(which contains
\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus T}
as a subset), it follows that

\displaystyle h(S)

\displaystyle=\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}}\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\,\mu_{j,a}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\pi\in\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus T}}\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\notin T}\pi_{j,a}\,\mu_{j,a}\right)=h(T).

This completes the proof.

Lemma 5
.

For any probing set
S
, we have

\mathcal{R}(S)=(1-\alpha(|S|))\,\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr]\leq(1-\alpha(|S|))\Bigl(g(S)+h(S)\Bigr).

Proof.

Recall that by definition,

\mathcal{R}(S)=(1-\alpha(|S|))\,\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr],

where the Nash Social Welfare (NSW) under the optimal allocation
\pi^{*}(S)
is given by

\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))=\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)R_{j,a}+\sum_{a\notin S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\mu_{j,a}\right).

For each agent
j
, define

X_{j}:=\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)R_{j,a}\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{j}:=\sum_{a\notin S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\mu_{j,a}.

Since
R_{j,a},\,\mu_{j,a}\in[0,1]
and

\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)+\sum_{a\notin S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\leq 1,

it follows that for each
j
we have
X_{j},\,Y_{j}\in[0,1]
and
X_{j}+Y_{j}\leq 1
.

We now upper bound the expectation of the NSW by separating the contributions from the probed arms and the unprobed arms.

(1) Contribution from Probed Arms.

Define the allocation
\pi^{S}
on
S
by

\pi^{S}_{j,a}=\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\quad\text{for }a\in S.

Then for each
j
,

\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{S}_{j,a}=\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\leq 1,

so that
\pi^{S}
is a feasible allocation in the set
\Delta^{A}_{S}
. By the definition of
g(S)
(the maximum expected NSW attainable using only the probed arms), we have

\mathbb{E}_{R}\left[\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\in S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)R_{j,a}\right)\right]\leq g(S).

(2) Contribution from Unprobed Arms.

Similarly, define the allocation
\pi^{\bar{S}}
on the unprobed arms by

\pi^{\bar{S}}_{j,a}=\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\quad\text{for }a\notin S.

For each
j
,

\sum_{a\notin S}\pi^{\bar{S}}_{j,a}\leq 1,

so that
\pi^{\bar{S}}
is a feasible allocation in
\Delta^{A}_{[A]\setminus S}
. By the definition of
h(S)
(the maximum NSW attainable using only the unprobed arms), we obtain

\prod_{j\in[M]}\left(\sum_{a\notin S}\pi^{*}_{j,a}(S)\mu_{j,a}\right)\leq h(S).

(3) Upper Bound for the Mixed Allocation.

Under the optimal allocation
\pi^{*}(S)
, each agent
j
obtains a contribution of
X_{j}+Y_{j}
so that the overall NSW is

\prod_{j\in[M]}(X_{j}+Y_{j}).

We now show that

\prod_{j\in[M]}(X_{j}+Y_{j})\leq\prod_{j\in[M]}X_{j}+\prod_{j\in[M]}Y_{j}.

To see this, fix an agent
j
and consider the function

f_{j}(\lambda)=\ln\Bigl(\lambda X_{j}+(1-\lambda)Y_{j}\Bigr),\quad\lambda\in[0,1].

Since the logarithm is strictly concave on
(0,1]
and the mapping
\lambda\mapsto\lambda X_{j}+(1-\lambda)Y_{j}
is linear, it follows that
f_{j}(\lambda)
is strictly concave and attains its maximum at one of the endpoints, namely at
\lambda=0
or
\lambda=1
. This means that for each
j
,

\max\{X_{j},\,Y_{j}\}\geq\lambda X_{j}+(1-\lambda)Y_{j}\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in[0,1].

In other words, for each
j
the best strategy is to allocate all probability either to the contribution
X_{j}
or to
Y_{j}
. Thus, the maximum possible product is achieved by one of the pure strategies:

\prod_{j\in[M]}(X_{j}+Y_{j})\leq\max\Bigl\{\prod_{j\in[M]}X_{j},\;\prod_{j\in[M]}Y_{j}\Bigr\}.

Since for any nonnegative numbers
a
and
b
we have
\max\{a,b\}\leq a+b
, it follows that

\prod_{j\in[M]}(X_{j}+Y_{j})\leq\prod_{j\in[M]}X_{j}+\prod_{j\in[M]}Y_{j}.

Taking expectations (noting that
X_{j}
depends on the randomness in
R
while
Y_{j}
is deterministic since it depends on
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
), and using the bounds established in parts (1) and (2), we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr]\leq g(S)+h(S).

(4) Conclusion.

Finally, since
1-\alpha(|S|)\geq 0
, multiplying the above inequality by
1-\alpha(|S|)
yields

\mathcal{R}(S)=(1-\alpha(|S|))\,\mathbb{E}_{R}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi^{*}(S))\Bigr]\leq(1-\alpha(|S|))\Bigl(g(S)+h(S)\Bigr).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Let
S^{*}
be an optimal subset maximizing
\mathcal{R}(S)
.
Then the set
S^{\mathrm{pr}}
returned by Algorithm
1
, for any
\zeta\geq 1
, satisfies

\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})\;\;\geq\;\frac{\,e-1\,}{\,2e-1\,}\;\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{R}(S^{*}).

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, we have:

\mathcal{R}(S)\leq(1-\alpha(|S|))\bigl[g(S)+h(S)\bigr].

Applying this to
S^{*}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(S^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha(|S^{*}|))\,\bigl[g(S^{*})+h(S^{*})\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha(|S^{*}|))\,g(S^{*})+h(\emptyset)\quad\text{(by Lemma \ref{lemma:monotonicity_h})}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha(|S^{*}|))\,f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S^{*})+h(\emptyset)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{e-1}(1-\alpha(|S^{*}|))f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S_{|S^{*}|})+h(\emptyset)\quad\text{(submodular factor $1-1/e$ on $f_{\mathrm{upper}}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{e-1}(1-\alpha(|\widetilde{S}^{*}|))f_{\mathrm{upper}}(\widetilde{S}^{*})+h(\emptyset)

\displaystyle\text{(by selection of $\widetilde{S}^{*}$ such that $|S_{|S^{*}|}|=|S^{*}|$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{e-1}(1-\alpha(|S_{j}|))f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S_{j})+h(\emptyset)

\displaystyle\text{(by definition of $j=\arg\max\{(1-\alpha(|S_{i}|))f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S_{i})\}$)}.

From the algorithm, we have that the chosen set
S^{\mathrm{pr}}
satisfies:

S^{\mathrm{pr}}=S_{j},

which implies:

\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})=(1-\alpha(|S_{j}|))\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{j},\dots)].

By the algorithm’s filtering condition,

(1-\alpha(|S_{j}|))f_{\mathrm{upper}}(S_{j})\leq\zeta\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}}),

which leads to:

\mathcal{R}(S^{*})\leq\frac{e}{e-1}\zeta\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})+h(\emptyset).

If
S^{\mathrm{pr}}=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})=h(\emptyset)
satisfies the bound trivially. Otherwise, we conclude:

\mathcal{R}(S^{*})\leq\frac{e}{e-1}\zeta\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})+\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}}).

This simplifies to:

\mathcal{R}(S^{\mathrm{pr}})\geq\frac{e-1}{2e-1}\frac{1}{\zeta}\mathcal{R}(S^{*}).

∎

Lemma 6

(Smoothness of the NSW Objective)
.

Let
\pi\in\Delta^{M\times A}
be any allocation matrix where
\pi_{j,a}
denotes the probability that agent
j
is assigned arm
a
. Let
\mu,\nu\in[0,1]^{M\times A}
be two reward matrices. For any probing set
S\subseteq[A]
and observed rewards
R
, define

\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\mu,\pi)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\!\left(\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a}R_{j,a}+\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\mu_{j,a}\right).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\nu,\pi)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a}\Bigl|\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}\Bigr|.

Proof.

Define for each agent
j
the aggregated reward under the policy
\pi
as

s_{j}:=\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a}R_{j,a}+\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\mu_{j,a},\quad t_{j}:=\sum_{a\in S}\pi_{j,a}R_{j,a}+\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\nu_{j,a}.

Then, the NSW objective can be written as

\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\mu,\pi)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}s_{j},\quad\mathrm{NSW}(S,R,\nu,\pi)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}.

Our goal is to show that

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left|s_{j}-t_{j}\right|,

and then use the triangle inequality to relate
|s_{j}-t_{j}|
to
\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a}|\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}|
.

We proceed by induction on
M
.

Base case (
M=1
):

When
M=1
, we have

\left|s_{1}-t_{1}\right|=\left|s_{1}-t_{1}\right|,

so the inequality holds with equality.

Inductive step:

Assume the inequality holds for
M-1
. For
M
, write

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}\right|=\left|s_{M}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}s_{j}-t_{M}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|.

Adding and subtracting
s_{M}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}
yields

\left|s_{M}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right)+\left(s_{M}-t_{M}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|.

By the triangle inequality,

\leq|s_{M}|\cdot\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|+\left|s_{M}-t_{M}\right|\cdot\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|.

Since
\mu_{j,a}
and
\nu_{j,a}
lie in
[0,1]
and
\pi_{j,a}
is a probability matrix, both
s_{j}
and
t_{j}
are convex combinations of numbers in
[0,1]
and hence lie in
[0,1]
. In particular,
|s_{M}|\leq 1
and
\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|\leq 1
. Therefore,

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}\right|\leq\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|+\left|s_{M}-t_{M}\right|.

By the inductive hypothesis,

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}t_{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}|s_{j}-t_{j}|.

Hence,

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{M}s_{j}-\prod_{j=1}^{M}t_{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}|s_{j}-t_{j}|+|s_{M}-t_{M}|=\sum_{j=1}^{M}|s_{j}-t_{j}|.

Finally, by the triangle inequality applied to
s_{j}-t_{j}
, we have

|s_{j}-t_{j}|=\Bigl|\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\bigl(\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}\bigr)\Bigr|\leq\sum_{a\notin S}\pi_{j,a}\,\Bigl|\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}\Bigr|.

Summing over
j
and extending the sum from
\sum_{a\notin S}
to
\sum_{a=1}^{A}
, we obtain:

\sum_{j=1}^{M}|s_{j}-t_{j}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a}\,\Bigl|\mu_{j,a}-\nu_{j,a}\Bigr|.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, for all
t>A
,
a\in[A]
, and
j\in[M]
, we have

\bigl|\mu_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr|\;\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{N_{j,a,t}}}\;\;+\frac{\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{3N_{j,a,t}}\;=\;w_{j,a,t},

where
N_{j,a,t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\mathbf{1}\{a_{j,\tau}=a\}
denotes the number of times agent
j
has pulled arm
a
up to time
t-1
.

Proof.

Step 1. Define key quantities and apply Freedman’s inequality.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}
be the empirical mean reward for agent
j
pulling arm
a
up to time
t
:

\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}=\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}R_{j,a,\tau}\mathbf{1}\{a_{j,\tau}=a\}.

Define the sum of differences between observed rewards and the true mean:

Z_{j,a,t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\Bigl(R_{j,a,\tau}\mathbf{1}\{a_{j,\tau}=a\}-\mu_{j,a,t}\mathbf{1}\{a_{j,\tau}=a\}\Bigr).

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
be the information available up to time
\tau
, and define the sum of conditional variances:

V_{j,a,t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\text{Var}\Bigl(R_{j,a,\tau}\mathbf{1}\{a_{j,\tau}=a\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\Bigr).

Since rewards are bounded in
[0,1]
, we upper bound the variance as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Var}(R_{j,a,\tau}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[R_{j,a,\tau}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}]-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[R_{j,a,\tau}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}]-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}.

Summing over all rounds where arm
a
was pulled:

V_{j,a,t}\leq N_{j,a,t}(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}).

Applying Freedman’s inequality:

\Pr\Bigl\{\bigl|Z_{j,a,t}\bigr|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2\,V_{j,a,t}\,\ln\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}}\;+\tfrac{1}{3}\ln\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\Bigr\}\;\;\geq\;\;1-\frac{\delta}{2MAT}.

Step 2. Apply Union Bound.

Applying the union bound across all
j\in[M]
,
a\in[A]
, and
t>A
:

\Pr\Bigl\{\forall j,a,t:\quad\bigl|Z_{j,a,t}\bigr|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2\,V_{j,a,t}\,\ln\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}}\;+\tfrac{1}{3}\ln\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\Bigr\}\;\;\geq\;\;1-\frac{\delta}{2}.

Step 3. Relate to empirical mean.

Dividing Freedman’s bound by
N_{j,a,t}
:

\bigl|\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\mu_{j,a,t}\bigr|\;\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2V_{j,a,t}\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{N_{j,a,t}^{2}}}\;+\frac{\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{3N_{j,a,t}}.

Since
V_{j,a,t}\leq N_{j,a,t}(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})
, we obtain:

\bigl|\mu_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr|\;\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{N_{j,a,t}}}\;+\frac{\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{3N_{j,a,t}}.

Applying a union bound across all
t,a,j
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let

\gamma_{j,t}\;=\;\sum_{a\in[A]}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr),\quad Q(t,p)\;=\;\bigl\{\,j\in[M]:\gamma_{j,t}\,\geq\,2^{-p}\bigr\}.

Suppose that for all integers
p\geq 0
, we have

|Q(t,p)|<2^{p}\cdot 3\,\ln T.

Then the following inequality holds:

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\;-\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)\;\leq\;1\;+\;6\,\ln T\,\log M\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

Proof.

Step 1:

\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\;-\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\;=\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr).

From the confidence-bound definition,

U_{j,a,t}\;=\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}+w_{j,a,t},\quad 1-U_{j,a,t}\;=\;\bigl(1-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;-\;w_{j,a,t}.

Hence,

\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;=\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl[(1-U_{j,a,t})+w_{j,a,t}\Bigr]\;=\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;+\;\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

Therefore,

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

Step 2: Relate it to
\gamma_{j,t}
and use
\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\leq 1
.

The lemma definition is

\gamma_{j,t}\;=\;\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr).

Because
\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\in[0,1]
, we have

\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;\;\leq\;\;\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;=\;\gamma_{j,t}.

Summing over
j
from
1
to
M
yields

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;\;\leq\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr)\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

Step 3: Use the layer argument to bound
\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}
.

By assumption,

Q(t,p)\;=\;\bigl\{\,j:\gamma_{j,t}\,\geq\,2^{-p}\bigr\},\quad|Q(t,p)|<2^{p}\cdot 3\,\ln T.

We partition it into

Q^{\prime}(t,p)\;=\;Q(t,p+1)\setminus Q(t,p),

so for each
j\in Q^{\prime}(t,p)
,

2^{-p-1}\leq\gamma_{j,t}<2^{-p}.

Then

\sum_{j\in Q^{\prime}(t,p)}\gamma_{j,t}\;\;\leq\;\;|Q(t,p+1)|\cdot 2^{-p}\;\;\leq\;\;(2^{p+1}\cdot 3\,\ln T)\,2^{-p}\;=\;6\,\ln T.

Summing over
p=0,1,\dots,\lfloor\log M\rfloor
, we split

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}\;=\;\sum_{j\notin Q(t,\lfloor\log M\rfloor)}\gamma_{j,t}\;+\;\sum_{p=0}^{\lfloor\log M\rfloor}\sum_{j\in Q^{\prime}(t,p)}\gamma_{j,t}.

First term:
If
j\notin Q(t,\lfloor\log M\rfloor)
, then
\gamma_{j,t}<2^{-\lfloor\log M\rfloor}\leq 1/M
. Thus:

\sum_{j\notin Q(t,\lfloor\log M\rfloor)}\gamma_{j,t}\;\leq\;1.

Second term:
Each layer contributes at most
6\ln T
, and there are at most
\lfloor\log M\rfloor+1
layers:

\sum_{p=0}^{\lfloor\log M\rfloor}\sum_{j\in Q^{\prime}(t,p)}\gamma_{j,t}\;\leq\;\sum_{p=0}^{\lfloor\log M\rfloor}6\ln T\;\leq\;6\ln T\,\log M.

Therefore:

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}\;\leq\;1+6\ln T\,\log M.

Step 4: Conclude the proof.

Putting everything together:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j,t}\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+6\,\ln T\,\log M\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the cumulative regret
R_{T}
of the Online Fair multi-Agent UCB with Probing algorithm (Algorithm 2) satisfies

R_{T}\;=\;O\!\Bigl(\,\zeta\;\bigl(\sqrt{M\,A\,T}\;+\;M\,A\bigr)\,\ln^{c}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{M\,A\,T}{\delta}\Bigr)\Bigr),

for some constant
c>0
.

Proof.

Recall that the effective (instantaneous) reward at round
t
is

\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{total}}\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \bigl(1-\alpha\bigl(|S_{t}|\bigr)\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr],

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
is taken over the random reward realizations
R_{t}
.
Let
\bigl(S_{t}^{*},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)
be the optimal probing set and allocation policy (with full knowledge of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
).
The cumulative regret is then

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{regret}}(T)\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl[\bigl(1-\alpha\bigl(|S_{t}^{*}|\bigr)\bigr)\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*})\bigr]\;-\;\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\mathrm{total}}\Bigr].

Since
\bigl(1-\alpha(|S_{t}|)\bigr)\leq 1
for all
t
, bounding
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{regret}}(T)
up to a constant factor reduces to bounding

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*})\;-\;\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t})\Bigr].

Step 1: Transition from
\bigl(S_{t}^{*},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)
to
\bigl(S_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)
.

By Theorem
1
, for each
t
there is a factor
\rho=\frac{\,e-1\,}{\,(2e-1)\zeta}

such that

\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi\bigr)\;\;\leq\;\;\tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi\bigr),\quad\forall\,\pi.

Hence,

\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\;\;\leq\;\;\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].

Next, since
U_{t}
is the upper-confidence estimate for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
at round
t
,

U_{j,a,t}\geq\mu_{j,a}
for each agent
j
and arm
a
,
we have the monotonicity property:

\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr).

Moreover, by Algorithm
2
,
\pi_{t}
is chosen better than
\pi_{t}^{*}
under
U_{t}
,
so

\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr).

Combining these two inequalities yields

\tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr).

Substituting back into (1), we get

\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t}^{*},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}^{*}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\;\;\leq\;\;\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{\,\rho\,}\,\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].

Therefore
, up to the factor
\tfrac{1}{\rho}
,
in bounding the regret over
K
,
it suffices to handle

\sum_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].

Step 2: Large-
\gamma_{j,t}
Rounds
(K^{\prime})
.

Warm-up phase and definition of
\boldsymbol{K}
.

Recall that the first
MA
rounds (indices
t=1,\dots,MA
) form a warm-up phase,
where each agent-arm pair
(j,a)
is explored exactly once. This phase incurs at most a constant regret
MA
.
From round
t=MA+1
onward, the algorithm operates under the UCB-based procedure of Step 1.
Let

K\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \{MA+1,MA+2,\dots,T\}

denote these subsequent rounds.
Thus, aside from the
MA
-cost warm-up, we only need to bound

\sum_{t\in K}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\;-\;\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].

Definition of large-
\gamma
rounds
\boldsymbol{K^{\prime}}
.

For each
t\in K
and integer
p\geq 0
, define

Q(t,p)\;=\;\bigl\{\,j\in[M]:\,\gamma_{j,t}\,\geq\,2^{-p}\bigr\}\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma_{j,t}\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum_{a\in[A]}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl(1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr).

Intuitively,
\gamma_{j,t}
measures the total “probability mass” on arms whose upper confidence
U_{j,a,t}
is significantly below 1.
We collect the “large-
\gamma
” rounds into

K^{\prime}\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \Bigl\{\,t\in K:\exists\,p\geq 0\text{ s.t.\ }\lvert Q(t,p)\rvert\,\geq\,2^{p}\cdot 3\,\ln T\Bigr\}.

Thus
K^{\prime}\subseteq K
.

Bounding the large-
\gamma_{j,t}
contribution.

Observe that for each agent
j
,

\sum_{a\in S_{t}}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\cdot 1\;+\;\sum_{a\notin S_{t}}\pi_{j,a,t}\,U_{j,a,t}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \sum_{a\in[A]}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{a\in S_{t}}+\mathbf{1}_{a\notin S_{t}}\,U_{j,a,t}\bigr].

Using the identity

\mathbf{1}_{a\in S_{t}}+\mathbf{1}_{a\notin S_{t}}\,U_{j,a,t}=1-\mathbf{1}_{a\notin S_{t}}[\,1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr]
,
it follows that

\sum_{a\in[A]}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{a\in S_{t}}+\mathbf{1}_{a\notin S_{t}}\,U_{j,a,t}\bigr]\penalty\ \;\leq\;1\penalty\ -\;\sum_{a=1}^{A}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\bigl[1-U_{j,a,t}\bigr]\penalty\ =\;1-\gamma_{j,t}.

Hence,

\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\penalty\ =\,\prod_{j=1}^{M}\Bigl[\sum_{a\in S_{t}}\!\pi_{j,a,t}\,R_{j,a,t}+\sum_{a\notin S_{t}}\!\pi_{j,a,t}\,U_{j,a,t}\Bigr]\penalty\ \;\leq\;\prod_{j=1}^{M}\!\bigl(1-\gamma_{j,t}\bigr).

Whenever
\lvert Q(t,p)\rvert
is large (i.e. many agents have
\gamma_{j,t}\geq 2^{-p}
), the product
\prod_{j\in Q(t,p)}(1-\gamma_{j,t})
becomes extremely small.

Therefore,

\sum_{t\in K}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\penalty\ \;\leq\;\sum_{t\in K^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\penalty\ +\;\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\ldots

and focusing on
K^{\prime}
,

\sum_{t\in K^{\prime}}\!\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\penalty\ \;\leq\;\sum_{t\in K^{\prime}}\!\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{j\in Q(t,p)}\bigl(1-\gamma_{j,t}\bigr)\Bigr]\penalty\ \;\leq\;T\,\bigl(1-2^{-p}\bigr)^{2^{p}\,3\,\ln T}\penalty\ \;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{\,T^{2}\,},

where we used
\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)^{x}\leq e^{-1}
for
x\geq 1
.

Conclusion of Step 2.

Thus, summing over large-
\gamma

\tfrac{1}{T^{2}}
.
Including the warm-up cost
MA

MA

\begin{split}\Bigl[\text{Cost from }t\in[MA]\Bigr]&\;+\;\sum_{t\in K}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)-\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\;\leq\;MA+\frac{1}{T^{2}}\\
&\quad+\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)-\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].\end{split}

(1)

Hence, it remains to bound the small-
\gamma
rounds
t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}
,
which will be handled in Step 3.

Step 3: Small-
\gamma_{j,t}
Rounds
\boldsymbol{(K\setminus K^{\prime})}
.

Recall from Step 2 that,
it remains to bound

\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)\;-\;\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)\Bigr].

On the high-probability event ensured by Lemma
7
, we have for every
j,a,t
:

\bigl\lvert\mu_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr\rvert\;\leq\;\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{2\,\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\bigr)\,\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{N_{j,a,t}}}}_{\text{root term}}\;\;+\;\;\underbrace{\frac{\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2MAT}{\delta}\bigr)}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}}_{\text{linear term}}\;\;=\;w_{j,a,t}.

(2)

Moreover, by definition
U_{j,a,t}=\min\bigl\{\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}+w_{j,a,t},\,1\bigr\}
,
so

\lvert U_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\rvert\leq w_{j,a,t}
.
Hence, for each
t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t}\bigr)-\mathrm{NSW}\bigl(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[M]}\sum_{a\in[A]}\pi_{j,a,t}\Bigl\lvert U_{j,a,t}-\mu_{j,a,t}\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j,a}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,\Bigl(\bigl\lvert U_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\bigr\rvert+\bigl\lvert\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\mu_{j,a,t}\bigr\rvert\Bigr),

which is at most

2\sum_{j,a}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}\penalty\ \;\leq\;2\sum_{j,a}\,\pi_{j,a,t}\Bigl[\!\sqrt{\frac{2\,(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})\,\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{\,N_{j,a,t}\,}}\;+\;\frac{\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}\Bigr].

Summing this over
t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{NSW}(S_{t},R_{t},U_{t},\pi_{t})\penalty\ -\penalty\ \mathrm{NSW}(S_{t},R_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\pi_{t})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2})\,\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{N_{j,a,t}}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ 2\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\frac{\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}

\displaystyle\quad=:\penalty\ \textsf{(RootTerm)}+\textsf{(LinearTerm)}.

(3)

Bounding the linear part.

By
[
Jones et al. 2023
, Lemma 4.4]
,
there is an event of probability at least
1-\tfrac{\delta}{2}
on which

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\,\sum_{a\in[A]}\frac{\pi_{j,a,t}}{N_{j,a,t}}\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ 2\,A\,\bigl(\ln\tfrac{T}{A}+1\bigr)\;+\;\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2}{\delta}\bigr).

(4)

Hence,

\textsf{(LinearTerm)}\penalty\ =\penalty\ 2\,\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\frac{\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}\penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \frac{2\,\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{3}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j,a}\frac{\pi_{j,a,t}}{N_{j,a,t}}

\quad\leq\frac{2\,\ln(\tfrac{2\,M\,A\,T}{\delta})}{3}\,M\Bigl[2\,A\,(\ln\tfrac{T}{A}+1)\;+\;\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{2}{\delta}\bigr)\Bigr],

Bounding the root term.
We begin with

\textsf{(RootTerm)}\;=\;2\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)L}{N_{j,a,t}}},

with

L\;=\;\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{2MAT}{\delta}\Bigr).

Since

\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}}{N_{j,a,t}}}\;=\;\sqrt{\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}},

we apply Young’s inequality,

a\,b\;\leq\;\frac{q\,a^{2}}{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{2q}\quad(\text{for any }q>0),

with

a=\sqrt{\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}},\quad b=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}},

to obtain

\sqrt{\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}}\;\leq\;\frac{q}{2}\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2q}\,\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}.

Thus,

\textsf{(RootTerm)}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2L}\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\left[q\,\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{q}\,\frac{1}{N_{j,a,t}}\right].

(1) Application of Lemma
8
:

For each
t

\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)\;\leq\;1+6\ln T\,\log M+\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

Summing over
t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}
yields

\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\Bigl[1+6\ln T\,\log M\Bigr]+\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}.

(2) Application of
[
Jones et al. 2023
, Lemma 4.4]
:

There is an event with probability at least
1-\tfrac{\delta}{2}
on which

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a\in[A]}\frac{\pi_{j,a,t}}{N_{j,a,t}}\leq 2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}.

Thus,

\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\frac{\pi_{j,a,t}}{N_{j,a,t}}\leq M\Bigl[2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr].

(3) Further bound on the
w
-term:

Since

w_{j,a,t}=\sqrt{\frac{2\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\Bigr)L}{N_{j,a,t}}}+\frac{L}{3\,N_{j,a,t}},

and using
\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}-\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}^{2}\leq 1
, we have

\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2L}{N_{j,a,t}}}+\frac{L}{3\,N_{j,a,t}}.

Thus, by summing over
t,j,a

[
Jones et al. 2023

\sum_{t\in K\setminus K^{\prime}}\sum_{j,a}\pi_{j,a,t}\,\hat{\mu}_{j,a,t}\,w_{j,a,t}\leq M\Biggl[\sqrt{2L}\,\sqrt{2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}+\frac{L}{3}\Bigl(2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr)\Biggr].

(4) Final Bound:

Substituting the bounds from (1), (2) and (3) into our original inequality gives

\displaystyle\textsf{(RootTerm)}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2L}\Biggl\{\,q\Biggl[T\Bigl(1+6\ln T\,\log M\Bigr)+M\Biggl(\sqrt{2L}\,\sqrt{2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{L}{3}\Bigl(2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr)\Biggr)\Biggr]+\frac{M}{q}\Bigl[2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr]\Biggr\}.

Choosing

q=\frac{\sqrt{A\,M}}{(\sqrt{A\,M}+\sqrt{T})\,\sqrt{L}},

one obtains (after some algebra) the final bound

\textsf{(RootTerm)}=O\!\Biggl(\Bigl(L\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigl(1+\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{A\,M}}\Bigr)\Bigl[MA\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+M\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr]+\frac{T\sqrt{A\,M}}{\sqrt{A\,M}+\sqrt{T}}\Biggr).

Recalling that
L=\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{2MAT}{\delta}\Bigr)

\textsf{(RootTerm)}=O\!\Biggl(\Bigl(\ln\frac{2MAT}{\delta}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigl(1+\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{A\,M}}\Bigr)\Bigl[MA\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+M\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr]+\frac{T\sqrt{A\,M}}{\sqrt{A\,M}+\sqrt{T}}\Biggr).

The simplifying process is:
Recalling that

\displaystyle+\;\frac{2L}{3}\,M\Bigl[2A\Bigl(\ln\frac{T}{A}+1\Bigr)+\ln\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr].
