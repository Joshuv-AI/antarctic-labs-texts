Comparative pathological analysis of systemic atherosclerosis in two cases of Japanese bantams infected with Marek’s disease virus
Authors: Meiya OKANO, Aoi KUROKAWA, Hiroka TSUTSUMI, Tomoyuki HARADA, Takuya KISHIMOTO, Maki DAIGAKU, Kazumi NIBE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfection with Marek’s disease virus (MDV) primarily causes neurological disorders and lymphomas; however, vascular syndrome rarely occurs. We herein report two cases of Japanese bantams submitted for a pathological examination, with multiple atherosclerotic lesions observed in the muscular arteries throughout the body. In the first case, diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes were formed in the spleen and liver and were considered Marek’s disease (MD) with vascular syndrome. In contrast, in the second case, no obvious diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes were observed. Viral proteins expressed upon MDV infection, such as MDV-EcoRI-Q (Meq) and phosphoprotein 38 (pp38), were detected by immunohistochemical staining, confirming that vascular syndrome can occur with MDV infection, regardless of the presence of diffuse proliferation of neoplastic lymphocytes characteristic of MD. In addition, it was confirmed that MDV-infected lymphocytes infiltrated the arterial lesions, and viral proteins were observed in the smooth muscle cells of the arterial lesions, suggesting that the infected lymphocytes may be transporting MDV to the arterial lesion sites.Keywords: atherosclerosis, Japanese bantam, Marek’s disease, MDV-EcoRI-Q (Meq), phosphoprotein 38 (pp38)

Marek’s disease virus (MDV) is classified within the Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily of the Herpesviridae family, genus Mardivirus, and is further divided into three serotypes (GaAHV2, GaAHV3, HVT) based on its pathogenicity, oncogenicity, and antigenicity in chickens [11]. Among these, Marek’s disease (MD) is characterized by neurological disorders such as paralysis of the legs in chickens and lymphomas caused by infection with GaAHV2. MD is divided into the classical neurological form (also known as fowl paralysis) and the lymphoproliferative form [12]. In the classical form, neurological disorders such as wing paralysis, leg paralysis, and torticollis are observed, with a mortality rate of less than 10%. In contrast, the lymphoproliferative form is characterized by the occurrence of lymphomas in multiple internal organs such as the liver, spleen, and proventriculus regardless of the presence or absence of neurological signs, with a mortality rate of 10–30% [16].

After latent infection in T cells, MDV induces the transformation of T cells by integrating its viral genome into the telomeres of the host chromosomes, sometimes causing fatal lymphomas [2]. The proliferative findings of neoplastic lymphocytes of various sizes observed at this time are important diagnostic features for identifying MD [14, 16]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) targeting MDV-EcoRI-Q (Meq) protein, which is highly expressed in CD4+ T cells transformed by MDV infection, as well as other GaAHV2-specific proteins such as phosphoprotein 38 (pp38), viral interleukin 8 (vIL8), and glycoprotein B (gB), has been used to confirm MDV infection [3, 7, 8, 10, 15].

Other non-neoplastic conditions caused by MDV infection include lymphodegenerative, transient paralysis, and vascular syndromes [1]. Vascular syndrome is rare, and there are only a few case reports of naturally occurring MD with vascular syndrome [9, 13]. It has been reported that in vascular syndrome, sclerotic changes develop in various arteries such as the coronary artery, celiac artery, mesenteric artery, and gastric artery [4, 9]. However, pathological analyses of arterial lesions associated with naturally occurring Marek’s disease have only reported arteriosclerotic lesions without atheromatous changes. To the best of our knowledge, no histopathological analyses of atherosclerosis arising in naturally occurring cases have been reported.

To discuss the relationship among MDV infection, neoplastic lymphocytes, and the formation of arterial lesions, this report describes a comparative histopathological analysis of two Japanese bantams with naturally occurring MDV infections. Both cases demonstrated similar vascular syndromes; however, they differed in the presence of diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes.

The cases involved two Japanese bantams kept at a zoo in Tokyo, both of which were unvaccinated against MD. The two birds were housed simultaneously in the same poultry house during their lifetimes.

The first case was a 5-year-old female Japanese bantam who died suddenly without any obvious clinical signs. At necropsy, enlargement and white lesions were observed in the spleen, and the liver was generally pale with scattered white lesions (Fig. 1A). In addition, bright yellow, firm thickened lesions were observed in the arterial walls throughout the body. No notable abnormalities were observed in any other organ.

Gross findings. (A) Case 1. In the spleen, enlargement and white lesions were observed (arrow), and in the liver, pallor and white spots were seen. Numerous multiple bright yellow, firm thickened lesions were observed in the arterial walls around the gizzard and intestines (black arrowhead). (B) Case 2. Multiple bright yellow, firm thickened lesions were observed in the walls of the abdominal aorta (white arrowheads).

The second case was a 10-year-old male Japanese bantam who was being treated for an impaired leg motor function but repeatedly had trouble walking and loss of appetite, followed by death with the onset of neurological signs. At necropsy, although no significant abnormalities were observed in most organs, bright yellow, firm thickened lesions, as in the first case, were observed in the arterial walls throughout the body (Fig. 1B).

Based on the clinical signs and necropsy, MD was suspected in the first case, and samples from the heart, spleen, and liver were collected.

In case 2, although no enlargement of the internal organs was observed, similar bright yellow lesions were found in the blood vessels throughout the body. Therefore, similar to case 1, samples from the heart, liver, skin, digestive tract (from the esophagus to the large intestine), and hind limbs were collected.

All tissues were embedded in paraffin, sliced into sections of approximately 3–4 µm thick, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). For the heart tissue sections, Elastica Van Gieson (EVG) staining was performed to evaluate the structure of the elastic lamina in arterial lesions. Additionally, to assess lipid accumulation in arterial lesions, frozen sections were prepared from formalin-fixed tissues and stained with Oil Red O (ORO). The process from the preparation of frozen sections to ORO staining was performed by Kyodo Byori Co., Ltd. (Kobe, Japan). IHC was conducted on the spleen, liver, heart, and other arterial lesions using anti-Meq monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and anti-pp38 mAbs as MDV infection markers, anti-CD3 polyclonal antibodies (FLEX Polyclonal Rabbit Anti-Human CD3 (Ready-to-Use), Dako-Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) as a marker for T lymphocytes, and anti-Alpha Smooth Muscle Actin (α-SMA) mAbs (monoclonal mouse, clone 1A4, catalog #904,601, RUO; previously Covance catalog #MMS466S; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) as a marker for smooth muscle cells. IHC for Meq and pp38 was performed according to the method described in reference [10].

IHC for CD3 and α-SMA were performed as follows. For antigen retrieval, slides were heated at 120°C for 5 min in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for pre-diluted anti-CD3 antibodies incubation at 37°C for 60 min. Detection was carried out using Histofine Simple Stain MAX PO (R; rabbit) (Nichirei Bioscience Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and DAB Substrate Solution (Histostar, MEDICAL & BIOLOGICAL Laboratories Co., Ltd., Ina, Japan). Similarly, antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides at 120°C for 5 min in Dako Target Retrieval Solution (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) diluted 1:10, followed by incubation with anti-α-SMA mAbs diluted 1:1,000 at 37°C for 60 min. Secondary antibody and visualization were performed using Histofine Simple Stain MAX PO (M; mouse) (Nichirei Bioscience Inc.) and DAB Substrate Solution (MEDICAL & BIOLOGICAL Laboratories Co., Ltd.).

In the first case, a histological examination by HE showed diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes were formed in areas corresponding to white lesions in the spleen and liver, with most of the normal tissue architecture lost (Fig. 2A, 2B). No obvious diffuse proliferative lesions of the neoplastic lymphocytes were observed in the heart. The neoplastic lymphocytes were medium-sized with scant cytoplasm, coarse chromatin in the nuclei, and occasional prominent nucleoli. Occasionally, mitosis was observed. Degeneration or disruption of the arterial wall from the intima to the media was observed in the trabecular and central arteries of the spleen, the hepatic artery, and the muscular arteries of the myocardium and epicardium in the heart, with severe lipid accumulation in these areas. In addition, macrophages with foamy cytoplasm heavily infiltrated the lesion sites, cholesterol deposits were often observed, and atherosclerotic lesions formed. In the arterial lesions of the spleen and liver, neoplastic lymphocytes infiltrated significantly. In the heart, no diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes were observed; however, scattered infiltration of neoplastic lymphocytes was seen in the arterial lesions (Fig. 2C).

In case 2, multiple atherosclerotic lesions, such as those in case 1, were observed in the arteries located in the myocardium and epicardium of the heart, skin, proventriculus, gizzard, hepatic, mesentery, and ischial artery. However, obvious diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes were not found in any tissue (Fig. 2D). Small lymphocyte infiltration was observed to a small extent in the arterial lesions and feather follicle epithelium.

In both cases 1 and 2, ORO staining of the arterial lesions revealed accumulation of lipid components stained red in foam macrophages, lipid vacuoles, and cholesterol clefts (inset of Fig. 2D). EVG staining of the arterial lesions revealed partial or circumferential disruption of the elastic lamina from the intima to the media, and long spindle-shaped and short flattened smooth muscle cells stained yellow were observed in the media (Fig. 3A, 3B).

IHC revealed that proliferating neoplastic lymphocytes in the spleen and liver of case 1 were positive for CD3 on the cell membrane and for Meq in the nucleus (Fig. 4A, 4B). Neoplastic lymphocytes infiltrating the arterial lesions of the spleen, liver, and heart were also positive for CD3 on the cell membrane and positive for Meq in the nucleus (Fig. 4C). In addition to the obvious neoplastic lymphocytes, the small lymphocytes found in the normal vascular lumen and arterial lesions were also positive for Meq. The infiltration of small Meq-positive lymphocytes was limited to arterial lesions, with no infiltration observed in other tissues. In the media of the arterial lesion, long spindle-shaped and short flattened smooth muscle cells among cholesteryl clefts and accumulated lipids were positive for α-SMA. In the same lesions, long spindle-shaped cells and infiltrating lymphocytes showed scattered positivity for pp38 (Fig. 4D).

In case 2, small Meq-positive lymphocytes were observed in the arterial lesions (Fig. 4E) and feather follicle epithelium. In the media of the arterial lesions, similar staining patterns to those in the first case for α-SMA and pp38 were observed (Fig. 4F). Additionally, positivity for pp38 was observed in the feather follicle epithelium. No positive infiltrating cells for Meq or pp38 were observed in other organs.

Meq is a viral protein involved in lymphomagenesis caused by MDV infection [10]. The anti-Meq mAbs used in this study specifically react with tumor cells and do not react with non-tumor cells [10]. In the first case, diffuse proliferation of tumor lymphocytes positive for both CD3 and Meq was observed, leading to a definitive diagnosis of MD caused by GaAHV2 infection. In contrast, in the second case, mild infiltration of small lymphocytes was detected in arterial lesions and the feather follicle epithelium, but no apparent proliferative lesion was observed in the HE findings. However, because the infiltrating small lymphocytes were positive for Meq, it was considered that transformation due to MDV infection had occurred, resulting in neoplastic changes. Furthermore, multiple atherosclerotic lesions were identified in the muscular arteries of various organs in both cases, consistent with vascular syndrome associated with MDV.

In previous reports of naturally occurring MD in Japanese bantams, although arteriosclerotic changes were observed, atheromatous changes like those seen in experimental cases were not evident, suggesting that Japanese bantams are not susceptible to atherosclerotic changes with lipid accumulation following arteriosclerosis [9]. However, since both cases reported here exhibited atherosclerotic lesions similar to those seen in experimental studies, it is considered that the same pathological condition can also occur in Japanese bantams.

In experimental infection studies related to MDV and arteriosclerotic lesions, arteriosclerotic lesions equivalent to atherosclerosis develop, and a significant difference in the incidence of arteriosclerosis between infected and uninfected chickens has been reported regardless of whether they were fed a diet supplemented with high cholesterol. Regarding the sites of lesion formation, lesions develop systemically in infected chickens in areas such as the coronary arteries, mesenteric arteries, and gastric arteries, whereas in uninfected chickens fed a high-cholesterol diet, arterial lesions were observed only in the distal descending aorta, which represented a significant difference [5]. At the zoo where this case occurred, all Japanese bantams previously housed had been fed the same diet, and this is the first time systemic arterial lesions have been confirmed. Based on these findings, the two cases identified are considered unlikely to have lesions caused by nutritional factors, and the presence of multiple arterial lesions suggests involvement of MDV in their pathogenesis.

Arterial lesions can occur in MD; however, the detailed pathogenic mechanisms by which MDV infection contributes to arteriosclerotic lesion formation remain unclear [4, 5]. In the multiple arteriosclerotic lesions observed in this case, long spindle-shaped cells positive for pp38 were scattered in the media of the muscular arteries. The media of muscular arteries is mainly composed of smooth muscle cells, and since α-SMA highlights both long spindle-shaped and short flattened smooth muscle cells, the pp38-positive spindle-shaped cells are considered to be arterial smooth muscle cells, indicating that MDV has infected these arterial smooth muscle cells. In an experimental study in which MDV infected cultured chicken smooth muscle cells, infected cells showed a significant increase in the accumulation of lipids and cholesterol crystals compared to non-infected cultured smooth muscle cells [6]. Similarly, in naturally occurring cases such as these two, it was suggested that MDV infection of arterial smooth muscle cells increases lipid accumulation and contributes to the formation of arterial lesions. However, based on the histological findings in this study alone, the detailed mechanism of lipid accumulation remains unclear, and since the cholesterol levels of the two Japanese bantams in this case are unknown, the possibility that lipid accumulation occurred secondarily due to vascular wall damage caused by MDV infection cannot be ruled out.

MDV infection involves entry through the respiratory tract, followed by transport of the virus to various lymphoid organs, such as regional lymph nodes, the bursa of Fabricius (BF), thymus, and spleen, where MDV establishes either latent infection or lytic infection in lymphocytes. Subsequently, infected T lymphocytes transport MDV to the skin and feather follicle epithelium, where cell-free MDV is produced [2]. In the two cases reported here, Meq-positive lymphocytes of various types, regardless of morphological atypia, were observed infiltrating the arterial lesions and feather follicle epithelium. The presence of Meq-positive lymphocytes infiltrating the feather follicle epithelium is considered consistent with the transport of MDV to the feather follicle epithelium by latently infected lymphocytes during the MDV infection cycle. By a similar mechanism, it is possible that MDV is transported to muscular arteries through lymphocyte infiltration. Furthermore, in the second case, arterial lesions were observed in the absence of diffuse proliferative lesions of neoplastic lymphocytes caused by MDV infection, suggesting that the formation of diffuse proliferation of neoplastic lymphocytes and the occurrence of vascular lesions due to MDV are independent phenomena.

Vascular syndrome in naturally occurring cases of MD is rare, and to the best of our knowledge, detailed histopathological analyses have not been previously reported. In this study, it was revealed that MDV infects smooth muscle cells of muscular arteries, even in naturally occurring cases, suggesting that transformed T cells may transport MDV to smooth muscle cells in a manner similar to the transfer of the virus to the feather follicle epithelium. Furthermore, this transport was suggested to be the pathogenic mechanism underlying the development of vascular syndrome in MD.
