Single-Stage Coverage of Exposed Critical Structures With Intact Decellularized Fish Skin and Simultaneous Epidermal Autograft Application: The “Sandwich” Technique
Authors: Katherine C Benedict, John M Sullivan, Laura I Galarza, Marc E Walker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoverage of exposed tendon and bone in upper-extremity injuries presents a reconstructive challenge, often requiring staged procedures or complex microsurgical reconstruction techniques. We present a case series using a single-stage approach with intact decellularized fish skin graft laminated with a simultaneous split-thickness skin autograft. This technique aims to reduce the number of surgeries and decrease time to complete healing. Two cases are reported: a dog-bite forearm injury with exposed extensor tendons devoid of peritenon and a work-related crush injury of the hand with exposed metacarpal fracture. Following definitive debridement, both cases ultimately achieved successful wound healing with simultaneous placement of decellularized fish skin graft laminated with split-thickness skin autograft without further interventions.Key words: Autograft, Coverage, Single-Stage, Split-Thickness, Xenograft

Stable soft tissue coverage remains an important goal in upper-extremity reconstruction. However, in the presence of exposed critical structures this often is difficult to achieve. Coverage of exposed tendons without peritenon or bone devoid of periosteum often requires staged procedures or flap coverage to achieve a durable and functional reconstruction.1 An additional consideration in the reconstruction of exposed tendons is the requirement for postoperative tendon gliding to prevent development of adhesions and the need for secondary tenolysis.2 In these cases, when local tissue is not available for coverage of the exposed structure, skin substitutes often are used to provide a vascularized layer of soft tissue before application of an epidermal autograft.2 A disadvantage of this technique is the additional time and surgery required between placement of the skin substitute and eventual skin graft to allow for revascularization of the wound bed to support the autograft.

Numerous options for skin substitutes exist, each with their own positive and negative characteristics in the management of upper-extremity wounds.2 The typical strategy for dermal substitute use involves placement of the dermal matrix onto the wound bed followed by a 5-7 day delay before application of the skin graft, allowing for neovascularization of the skin substitute before autograft.2,3 To avoid this need for staged procedures, prior reports have described successful single-stage placement of dermal matrices on wounds of the upper extremity and immediately covering the matrix with a 0.011 to 0.015-inch split-thickness skin autograft.3

Within the sphere of regenerative tissue matrices, the use of acellular intact fish skin grafts (Kerecis; Ísafjörður) has become a viable option for coverage of complex upper-extremity wounds with the literature showing faster wound healing rates and better functional outcomes.4 These decellularized fish skin grafts have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties that have been attributed to their concentration of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids.5 Additionally the fish dermis is similar chemically and structurally to human skin, which precludes the development of autoimmune reactions in their use.6 The species of wild North Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) used to manufacture these acellular fish skin grafts also has a unique microporous extracellular matrix structure. 7 This characteristic suggests that the xenograft can be used in a single-stage procedure with placement of the fish skin graft to the wound bed and simultaneous lamination with a split-thickness skin autograft; however, to our knowledge this has not been described previously in the literature.

We describe a technique of successful tendon and bone coverage with a single-stage intact decellularized fish skin graft and simultaneous lamination with split-thickness skin autograft. Two patients are included in this report. One patient underwent single-stage coverage of an exposed extensor tendon devoid of peritenon using a meshed fish skin xenograft and simultaneous coverage with a thin split-thickness skin autograft, which healed uneventfully. The second patient underwent a similar procedure with single-stage skin grafting over fish skin xenograft for coverage of exposed metacarpal after metacarpal fracture fixation with a concomitant soft tissue crush injury. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case Reports

Patient A

A 61-year-old woman presented with a large soft tissue injury to the left forearm from multiple dog bites (Fig. 1). She underwent serial debridement of the wound resulting in a dorsal forearm wound (150 cm2) with exposed extensor tendons without intact peritenon. Initial soft tissue reconstruction was attempted with bilayered acellular dermal matrix over the exposed tendons. While this healed well over most of the wound, the dermal matrix did not revascularize over the exposed tendons resulting in a recurrent wound that, despite aggressive local wound care, persisted for several weeks. Six weeks after initial bilayered dermal matrix placement, she underwent repeat debridement followed by placement of a meshed dermal repair scaffold composed of cross-linked bovine tendon collagen with glycosaminoglycans layered with a polysiloxane layer. Initially, this scaffold demonstrated good apposition to the wound bed, but after 2 weeks, the wound returned once more with exposed extensor tendons.

A Wounds to the dorsal left forearm after dog bites. B Wounds to the volar forearm after dog bites.

She ultimately required repeat debridement of this scaffold and final reconstruction with placement of a meshed intact decellularized fish skin xenograft (Kerecis SurgiClose; Ísafjörður) and simultaneous coverage with a 0.010-inch split-thickness skin autograft covered by a negative pressure bolster. The negative pressure bolster was removed at postoperative day 6 with complete adherence of the skin graft overlying the exposed tendon. Daily dressing changes were performed using nonadherent gauze for the following 14 days. The laminated coverage progressed to heal completely at 30 days after surgery without further intervention and the patient achieved coverage of the exposed extensor tendons with appropriate extensor tendon gliding and no limitations in wrist or finger extension. The patient’s wrist motion improved from 10°–35° of flexion and from 15°–45° of extension following her final reconstruction (Fig. 2)

Patient B

A 23-year-old man with a past medical history of type I diabetes mellitus presented after sustaining a penetrating crush trauma to the hand from an industrial accident resulting in a ring finger metacarpal fracture and extensive soft tissue injury. He initially underwent debridement of the left dorsal hand wound with provisional percutaneous fixation of the metacarpal fracture and placement of a negative pressure dressing. Fourteen days later, he underwent definitive fixation of the metacarpal fracture with placement of bone allograft and coverage of the majority of the exposed extensor tendons and metacarpals with a third dorsal metacarpal artery perforator fasciocutaneous flap. The majority of this healed well. However, 1 month later there was noted to be a wound at the site of the distal flap, which upon further probing revealed exposure of the ring finger metacarpal. This was reconstructed with placement of micrograft intact decellularized fish skin xenograft (Kerecis SurgiClose Micro; Ísafjörður) and immediate coverage with a split-thickness skin autograft covered by a negative pressure bolster. This healed without further intervention resulting in successful coverage of the exposed bone (Fig. 3).

A Dorsal hand wound in patient B with exposed ring finger metacarpal. B Healed wound 6 months after surgery following application of micrograft fish skin xenograft and simultaneous split-thickness autograft over exposed metacarpal.

Both patients achieved healed wounds of the upper extremity with no further operative procedures following simultaneous application of decellularized fish skin graft laminated with a simultaneous split-thickness skin autograft (Table 1, Table 2).

Discussion

Two patients who underwent coverage of exposed critical structures in the upper extremity with a single-stage fish skin graft and split-thickness skin autograft are presented. Prior studies have reported experience with the use of decellularized intact fish skin grafts for wound coverage as well as single-stage use of other dermal matrices with simultaneous skin grafting; however, to our knowledge this case series represents the first successful report of the use of acellular intact fish skin grafts with immediate laminated skin grafting to achieve wound coverage in a single operation.

The goal of all reconstructive procedures in the upper extremity is the ability to obtain a stable soft tissue envelope that allows restoration of normal function of the limb as quickly as possible. This helps to limit tendon adhesions and joint stiffness, which ultimately can result in joint contractures. Prior studies have shown the success of dermal substitutes in the management of these wounds; however, most of these procedures are performed in a staged fashion to allow revascularization of the dermal matrix before skin graft application.3,8,9 The additional time required for return to regular activity for the patient as well as the added cost of a secondary surgical procedure must be considered whenever a staged procedure is planned as some studies have estimated that the cost of a single minute of operating room time may be as high as $113.10

An additional consideration in the management of hand and forearm injuries is mitigating the risk of tendon adhesions to achieve the best postoperative results. The literature has shown use of dermal matrices over tendon repairs in the hand results in a decrease in peritendinous adhesions.3 The benefits of this improvement in function cannot be overstated, as the development of tendon adhesions invariably results in some degree of functional impairment.3

In this study we attempt to combine these two concepts. Single-stage fish skin graft with simultaneous skin autograft allows the benefits of a skin substitute over the exposed tendon or bone while eliminating the drawback of needing a secondary staged skin graft to cover the xenograft (Fig. 4). To our knowledge, this study presents the first reported cases to date of simultaneous application of xenograft fish skin substitute and split-thickness skin graft using the “sandwich” technique with successful coverage of exposed extensor tendons and bones in the forearm and hand as a useful single-stage reconstructive option of exposed critical structures.
