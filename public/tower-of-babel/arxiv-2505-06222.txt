Equalizing Closeness Centralities via Edge Additions
Alex Crane, Sorelle A. Friedler, Mihir Patel, Freda Shi, Blair D. Sullivan, Madelyn J. Warr

Abstract
Graph modification problems with the goal of optimizing some measure of a given node’s network position have a rich history in the algorithms literature. Less commonly explored are modification problems with the goal of equalizing positions, though this class of problems is well-motivated from the perspective of equalizing social capital, i.e., algorithmic fairness. In this work, we study how to add edges to make the closeness centralities of a given pair of nodes more equal. We formalize several versions of this problem: Closeness Ratio Improvement , which aims to maximize the ratio of closeness centralities between two specified nodes, and Closeness Gap Minimization , which aims to minimize the absolute difference of centralities. For the former, we present a quasilinear-time 6 11 \frac{6}{11} -approximation, complemented by a bicriteria inapproximability bound. In contrast to this positive result, we show that Closeness Gap Minimization admits no multiplicative approximation, unless P = NP \textnormal{{P}}=\textnormal{{NP}} . We also establish NP -hardness for All-Pairs Closeness Ratio Improvement , which aims to maximize the minimum ratio of closeness centralities across all node pairs.

Our Results:
We introduce and study optimization problems related to balancing the closeness centrality of a given pair of nodes. In
Closeness Ratio Improvement
, the goal is to maximize the ratio of the closeness centralities of two given vertices with a budgeted number of edge additions. Our main result is a quasilinear-time
\frac{6}{11}
-approximation for this problem. We also prove that the problem is
NP
-hard for any target ratio
\tau
in the interval
(\frac{1}{2},1]
, and trivial when
\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Additionally, we show that it is
NP
-hard to approximate
Closeness Ratio Improvement
within any factor better than
\frac{5e}{5e+1-\varepsilon}\approx 0.932
, and we generalize this lower bound to give a
\frac{5e^{c}}{5e^{c}+1-\varepsilon}
approximation bound for any algorithm which is allowed to add
ck
edges, where
c\geq 1
is a constant. In
Closeness Gap Minimization
, the goal is to minimize the absolute difference between the closeness centralities of two nodes, again with a budgeted number of edge additions. We prove that this problem is
NP
-hard and, in contrast to
Closeness Ratio Improvement
, does not admit any multiplicative approximation algorithm.
Finally, in
All-Pairs Closeness Ratio Improvement
the goal is to use a budgeted number of edge additions to maximize the minimum ratio of closeness centralities across all vertex pairs. We establish that this problem is
NP
-hard.

Preliminaries

We introduce the problem of
Closeness Ratio Improvement
on a social network where people are modeled as vertices and edges represent connections between people. Specifically, let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with vertices
V
and edges
E
. Our goal will be to add a budget of
k
edges to
G
to equalize the closeness centrality of two vertices. We denote a graph
G=(V,E)
augmented with an edge set
S\subseteq V^{2}\backslash E
as
G+S=(V,E\cup S)
. Within
G
, the shortest path length between two vertices
u,v\in V
is represented as
\text{d}_{{G}}({u},{v})
. Finally, we define the closeness centrality of a vertex
v\in V
in a graph
G=(V,E)
as
\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({v})=\sum_{u\in V}\text{d}_{{G}}({u},{v})
, the sum of
v
’s shortest-path distances to each vertex in the graph. Note that having a smaller closeness centrality value means a node is closer to more vertices in the graph, and thus more important or central in the graph.

In the problem of
Closeness Ratio Improvement
, we are given a graph
G
and vertices
a,b
, and the goal is to add the
k
edges to
G
which will maximize the ratio of
a
and
b
’s closeness centralities. We define the closeness ratio of
a
and
b
as
\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G}}({a},{b})=\frac{\min(\penalty\ \textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({a})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({b})\penalty\ )}{\max(\penalty\ \textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({a})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({b})\penalty\ )}
, so that we can guarantee our ratio is never more than 1.

Closeness Ratio Improvement

Input:

A graph
G=(V,E)
, vertices
a,b\in V
, and a positive integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Task:

Find a set of edges
S
of size at most
k
which maximizes
\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G+S}}({a},{b})

A key aspect of the problem definition is the choice to consider the ratio of closeness centralities instead of their difference. Another natural formulation is the following:

Closeness Gap Minimization

Input:

A graph
G=(V,E)
, vertices
a,b\in V
, and a positive integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Task:

Find a set of edges
S
of size at most
k
which minimizes
|\textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({a})-\textnormal{cc}_{{G+S}}({b})|
.

Within the fairness literature, both the ratio and difference are commonly considered variants for equalization of various measures
[
29
]
, but recent work supports the use of ratio by default unless domain-specific concerns indicate otherwise
[
53
]
. In the context of closeness centrality equalization, we additionally find justification for the use of the ratio instead of the difference as, unlike
Closeness Ratio Improvement
,

is unlikely to admit any multiplicative approximation algorithm, as we show in Section
3
(
Corollary

1
).

We also propose the problem of adding edges to a graph to maximize the minimum pairwise closeness ratio. A ratio close to 1 indicates that all nodes are similarly advantaged. For a vertex subset
X\subseteq V
, we write
\textsc{best-cc}_{G}(X)\coloneqq\min_{x\in X}\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({x})
and
\textsc{worst-cc}_{G}(X)\coloneqq\max_{x\in X}\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({x})
. Formally, the problem is as follows.

All-Pairs Closeness Ratio Improvement

Input:

A graph
G=(V,E)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Task:

Find
S\subseteq V^{2}\setminus E
,
|S|\leq k
, maximizing
\frac{\textsc{best-cc}_{G+S}(V)}{\textsc{worst-cc}_{G+S}(V)}
.

In
Section

3

is

-hard.

A
\frac{6}{11}
-Approximation

In this section we show that a surprisingly simple strategy can improve upon the

-approximation yielded by adding the edge

a
to
b

Recall that we assume
\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({b})<\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({a})

is actually much larger than
\frac{1}{2}
unless a significant portion of the graph is in

is already

-approximate.
These two insights impose significant structural restrictions on hard instances, and the achievement of this section is to show how to reason about these instances.
We do so via careful arguments to bound the sizes of relevant vertex sets.
Generally, the intuition behind these arguments will be straightforward, and the challenge will be to appropriately formalize the necessary inequalities.

Lemma 2

Let

,

\textnormal{cc}_{{G+au}}({a})\leq\textnormal{cc}_{{G+au}}({b})

4.3
Analysis III: Proving the Sufficient Condition Occurs

By
Lemma

2
, if
Algorithm

1
returns on Line
1

\frac{2}{3}
-approximation. Similarly, by
Lemma

1
, if
Algorithm

returns on Line

\frac{6}{11}

Thus, we may assume that

1
returns on Line

By design, if

\frac{6}{11}

Recall that other than the edge

It now suffices to show that if neither

\frac{6}{11}

Lemma 4
.

Let
(G=(V,E),a,b,k)
be an instance of
Closeness Ratio Improvement
with
\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({b})<\textnormal{cc}_{{G}}({a})
and
\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({b})<\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({a})
. Let
G^{*}
be an optimal solution, and assume that
\max\{\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G}}({a},{b})

\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G+ab}}({a},{b})\}<\frac{6}{11}\cdot\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})

k+|N_{G}(a)|\geq\frac{n}{6}

The full proof is in
Section

A.3
. Here, we sketch the argument. Recall the notation

for the private neighbors of

We begin by establishing some additional structure.

Claim 4
.

Under the hypotheses of the lemma, all of the following hold:

(i)

\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({b})<\frac{6(n-2)}{5}
,

(ii)

\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})>\frac{11}{12}

(iii)

|B^{p}|>\frac{4(n-2)}{5}

To see part (i), observe that since
\text{d}_{{G+ab}}({a},{b})=1
, the triangle inequality yields
\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({a})\leq\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({b})+n-2
. This bound implies that if
\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({b})
is large, then the rato
\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({b})/\textnormal{cc}_{{G+ab}}({a})
must be large, i.e., at least
\frac{6}{11}
, in contradiction of our hypothesis. The particular quantity

is a consequence of the arithmetic used to check this intuition.
Part (ii) follows from the fact that
\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G+ab}}({a},{b})>\frac{1}{2}
(recall
Section

2.2

is not
\frac{6}{11}
-approximate.
Part (iii) follows from an argument in the style of

3

We will also need an upper bound on

This bound is a consequence of several creative combinations of

4

Claim 5

\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({b})<d_{G^{*}}(a,b)+\frac{4(n-2)}{5}+(d_{G^{*}}(a,b)+1)\cdot\frac{n-2}{5}
.

Observe that condition (ii) of
Claim

4
implies the inequality
\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({a})\leq\frac{12}{11}\cdot\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({b})

It is easiest to perform this analysis with a specific value of

We begin by combining Claims

and

Since

Claim 6

There is no optimal solution

with
\text{d}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})\geq 3

To formally prove

to write a lower bound for

in terms of

\text{d}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})

gives us an upper bound for
\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({b})

The result then follows by substituting these bounds into the inequality
\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({a})\leq\frac{12}{11}\cdot\textnormal{cc}_{{G^{*}}}({b})
(condition (ii) of
Claim

4

Thus, either
\text{d}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})=1

\text{d}_{{G^{*}}}({a},{b})=2

Claim 7

If there exists an optimal

Meanwhile, we substitute

If

5
Conclusion and Open Problems

In this paper, we used closeness centrality to represent social capital in networks and introduced several novel problems aimed at equalizing the capital of two specified nodes via edge additions. For
Closeness Ratio Improvement
, we proved
NP
-hardness for all target ratios
\tau\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
, while observing that the problem is trivially solvable
\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
: just add the edge
ab
between the relevant vertices. We also showed how to quantify and harness the structure of non-trivial instances, i.e., instances where adding
ab
is not sufficient, leading to a scalable (quasilinear time)
\frac{6}{11}
-approximation. Additionally, we proved the problem is inapproximable within any factor greater than
\frac{5e}{5e+1-\varepsilon}\approx.932
. For
Closeness Gap Minimization
, we also showed
NP
-hardness and further ruled out any multiplicative approximation.
Finally, we proposed an extension,
All-Pairs Closeness Ratio Improvement
, in which the goal is to equalize the closeness centralities of all node pairs, rather than two specified nodes. We established the
NP
-hardness of this problem.

Amongst the advantages of our framework are the elegance of our combinatorial model, and the technical simplicity of our algorithm and its accompanying analysis. The choice of closeness centrality as the measure for social capital was motivated in part by these considerations, but this does not mean that it is the only compelling choice. Indeed, we suggest that while much algorithmic work has been devoted to graph modification with the intent of optimizing some characteristic of a given node, modification to
equalize
node characteristics is both well-motivated and relatively under-explored, whatever the characteristic of interest. For example, it may be interesting to consider variants of our framework that equalize betweenness centrality
[
8
]
or PageRank
[
44
]
.

We also emphasize that our model, in which a pair of nodes is assumed as input, is intended as a starting point for a broader set of algorithmic questions motivated by the goal of promoting fairness across a social network.
We have already taken one step in this direction by studing
All-Pairs Closeness Ratio Improvement
. A more direct generalization of
Closeness Ratio Improvement
considers two
groups
of vertices.
The goal is to add at most
k
edges to the graph to maximize the ratio between the least central node in each group.

Group Closeness Ratio Improvement

Input:

A graph
G=(V,E)
, two groups
A,B\subseteq V
,
A\cap B=\emptyset
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Task:

Find
S\subseteq V^{2}\setminus E
,
|S|\leq k
, maximizing
\textnormal{cc-ratio}_{{G+S}}({\textnormal{cc-worst}_{{G+S}}({A})},{\textnormal{cc-worst}_{{G+S}}({B})})
.

This is a generalization of
Closeness Ratio Improvement
, but it seems that new insights will be needed to tackle it. For example, if
|A|,|B|
are unbounded with respect to
k
, then there is not a clear way to lift the trivial approach outlined in
Section

2.2
, which is also a crucial component of the algorithm in
Section

4
. Even when
|A|,|B|
are bounded, it is not clear that useful generalizations of our Lemmas
1
and
2
can be obtained, and the lower bound on
k
required for a straightforward generalization of
Lemma

3
is likely to be very large.
One might also consider optimizing the ratio of mean or best centralities within each group; the minimax formulation presented above is common in the fairness literature, e.g.,
[
7
]
, usually with the justification that it focuses on aiding the most disadvantaged individuals.
