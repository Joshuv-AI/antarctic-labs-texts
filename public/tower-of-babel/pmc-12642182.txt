Intralobar pulmonary sequestration masquerading as a giant lung abscess in a 16-year-old male: a case report and literature review
Authors: Fei Yuan, Chiqing Ying, Yang Lou, Dan Zhu, Xiaoli Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary sequestration (PS) is a rare congenital pulmonary dysplasia. Due to atypical clinical symptoms, PS is frequently challenging to differentiate from other common respiratory diseases.Case presentationA 16-year-old male presented with acute fever and expectoration as chief complaints. The patient had undergone tooth extraction more than one month prior. Background

Pulmonary sequestration (PS) is a rare congenital malformation, accounting for 0.15% to 6.4% of all congenital pulmonary malformations [1]. A descriptive epidemiological study of the Chinese population showed that the prevalence of pulmonary sequestration was 0.42 per 10,000 births from 2010 to 2019 [2]. PS consists of a nonfunctioning mass of lung tissue that lacks normal tracheobronchial connections and is supplied by an anomalous systemic artery [3]. It is classified into two main types: intralobar sequestration (ILS) and extralobar sequestration (ELS). Due to atypical clinical symptoms, PS is frequently misdiagnosed. Wei et al. [4] reported a preoperative misdiagnosis rate of 58.63% on average. To enhance clinical awareness and improve understanding of this condition, we present a rare case of intralobar pulmonary sequestration misdiagnosed as a giant lung abscess in an adolescent. Notably, the patient exhibited significantly elevated levels of carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) and was found to have Aspergillus colonization. Accordingly, we also provide a review of the relevant literature.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male was admitted with a four-day history of fever and expectoration. The patient had a tooth extraction over a month ago but no history of recurrent pulmonary infections. Four days before admission, he developed a fever peaking at 39.5 °C, accompanied by expectoration, but no chest pain or hemoptysis. The patient expectorated purulent and malodorous sputum. He was diagnosed with pneumonia at a local clinic and treated with medications including antibiotics, antipyretics, cough suppressants, and expectorants. Although body temperature normalized after treatment, cough and sputum production worsened, with the expectoration of a large volume of purulent and malodorous sputum. The patient developed left-sided dull chest pain one day before admission, worsened by breathing and coughing. Chest computed tomography (CT) at a local hospital showed a large cavity in the left lower lobe containing an air-fluid level and surrounding patchy consolidative opacities, measuring approximately 8.5 × 10.1 × 14.5 cm (Fig. 1A and B). Therefore, the patient was admitted to the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at our hospital for further management.

Chest CT shows a large cavity with an air-fluid level inside in the left lower lobe. A, B: Chest CT before admission; C, D: Reexamined chest CT

Upon admission, physical examination revealed normal vital signs. The thorax was symmetrical with no deformity. Breath sounds were diminished and percussion note was dull in the left lower lung field. No prominent rales were detected in either lung. Cardiac and abdominal examinations revealed no significant abnormalities.

Post-admission laboratory tests revealed a white blood cell count of 10.54 × 10⁹/L (normal range: 4.0–10.0 × 10⁹/L) and a neutrophil percentage of 82.6% (normal range: 50%–70%). C-reactive protein was elevated at 152.37 mg/L (normal range: 0–8 mg/L), and procalcitonin was increased to 0.65 ng/mL (normal range: 0–0.5 ng/mL). Liver and kidney function indexes were within normal limits. Serum CA19-9 was elevated at 393.13 U/mL (normal range: 0–35.0 U/mL), and carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) was 66.13 U/mL (normal range: 0–35.0 U/mL). Serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was within the normal range (0–5.0 ng/mL). Sputum cultures showed no bacterial or fungal growth, and acid-fast staining of sputum failed to detect any acid-fast bacilli. Abdominal color Doppler ultrasound revealed a solitary gallbladder polyp with a long-axis diameter of 5 mm. No abnormalities were detected in the liver or pancreas. Bronchoscopy showed purulent secretions occluding the basal bronchi of the left lower lobe. The bilateral bronchial mucosa appeared hyperemic, and no neoplastic lesions were observed in either bronchus (Fig. 2). Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid showed a predominance of neutrophils, and the results of bacterial and fungal cultures were negative.

Based on the chest CT findings, a diagnosis of lung abscess was made. Following admission, the patient received empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Imipenem/cilastatin 500 mg was administered intravenously every 8 h for an initial 5 days, followed by Cefoperazone/sulbactam 2000 mg intravenously every 12 h for another 5 days. Given the importance of lung abscess drainage, the patient underwent percutaneous transthoracic tube drainage under local anesthesia with ultrasound guidance. Analysis of the drained purulent fluid revealed markedly elevated tumor markers, including CA19-9 at 2001.00 U/mL, CA125 at 1385.48 U/mL, and CEA at 323.53 ng/mL. A large number of neutrophils were observed, with no evidence of malignant cells. Purulent fluid cultures revealed no bacterial or fungal growth. Over the course of two days, a total of 700 mL of purulent fluid was drained, after which no further fluid output was observed. A follow-up chest CT scan was performed one week after the pre-admission CT scan. It showed a slight decrease in the size of the cavitary lesion in the left lower lobe (Fig. 1C and D), measuring approximately 6.6 × 8.0 × 10.5 cm. Due to the limited efficacy of the drainage procedure, surgical intervention was recommended by a cardiothoracic surgeon following consultation, and the patient was subsequently transferred to the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery for further treatment.

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery was planned to perform a left lower lobectomy. During the operation, suppurative inflammation and abscess formation were observed in the left lower lobe. Additionally, an anomalous artery approximately 2 cm in diameter was identified, originating from the descending thoracic aorta and supplying the left lower lobe (Fig. 3). Intralobar pulmonary sequestration was considered intraoperatively. Considering the risk of bleeding from the large artery supplying the sequestered segment, a posterolateral thoracotomy was performed, and a left lower lobectomy was carried out. Postoperative histopathological analysis revealed irregularly dilated bronchi, Aspergillus hyphal masses in alveolar cavities, chronic suppurative inflammation, interstitial organization, and alveolar epithelial hyperplasia (Fig. 5). No malignant cells were identified. Based on the intraoperative findings and histopathological results, the final diagnosis was established as infected intralobar pulmonary sequestration with Aspergillus colonization.

The patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day, and no anti-Aspergillus medication was administered following discharge. Two months after discharge, the patient’s serum CA19-9 level had returned to the normal range. At the 14-month follow-up, the patient had not developed any complications, and the reexamined chest CT only revealed postoperative changes in the left lung (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Pulmonary sequestration (PS) is a congenital lung dysplasia. Its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. The most widely accepted theory is the embryologic hypothesis, which suggests that PS likely develops between the 4th and 8th weeks of gestation. The theory provides a single mechanism for the spectrum of pathology observed in PS, suggesting that a supernumerary lung bud arises caudal to the normal lung bud and subsequently migrates caudally in conjunction with the esophagus [5, 6]. According to whether the abnormal lung tissue has its own complete pleura and is separated from normal lung tissue, PS is classified into ILS and ELS. Mixed sequestration is rarely observed. ILS occurs within normal lung tissue without an independent visceral pleural covering, while ELS is separated by its own visceral pleura. ILS is more common, making up 75.0%–92.8% of reported cases in the literature [1, 4, 7]. ILS shows no gender predominance in incidence, whereas ELS demonstrates a male predominance with a reported male-to-female ratio of approximately 3–4:1 [8, 9]. ELS is typically diagnosed in infancy or childhood [10] and is often asymptomatic. It is frequently associated with other congenital anomalies, including congenital diaphragmatic hernia, airway malformations, and congenital heart disease [11]. Some cases of ELS diagnosed in adults are accidentally discovered on imaging examinations [12]. In contrast, ILS typically manifests during adolescence or early adulthood; however, up to 50% of cases remain undiagnosed until after age 20 [4, 12]. While most ILS cases are not connected to the normal tracheobronchial tree, approximately 15%–20% of cases do have such a connection [13]. As a result, patients with ILS are prone to recurrent pulmonary infections, which lead to the most common clinical manifestations, including cough, expectoration, fever, hemoptysis, and chest pain [4, 7]. In this case, the patient was an adolescent with ILS connected to the bronchi. He presented with fever, expectoration, and chest pain, but no hemoptysis. However, the patient denied a history of recurrent pulmonary infections.

ILS almost always occurs in the lower lobe, more frequently in the left lung than the right, with the posterior basal segment of the left lower lobe being most commonly affected [1, 4], as seen in our case. By contrast, ELS is typically found in the left posterior costophrenic sulcus between the lower lobe and the diaphragm [8, 14]. Sequestered lung tissue typically receives blood supply from a single abnormal artery [15]. The most common abnormal artery originates from the descending thoracic aorta, followed by the abdominal aorta, celiac trunk or splenic artery [11, 14]. Abnormal arteries originating from other vessels, such as intercostal, renal, subclavian, left gastric, and coronary arteries, are relatively rare [16–20]. The feeding artery averages 6.3–6.6 mm in diameter but can reach up to 2.5 cm [1, 11]. In our case, the abnormal feeding artery of ILS originated from the descending aorta and had a diameter exceeding the average, measuring 2 cm.

PS typically appears on chest CT scans as mass-like, cystic, cavitary, pneumonic lesions, and bronchiectasis [4]. Due to its atypical clinical presentation, PS is frequently difficult to differentiate from common respiratory diseases, such as lung cysts, abscesses, malignancies, pneumonia, and bronchiectasis with concurrent infection. In our case, the patient, who had a tooth extraction more than one month before the onset of symptoms, presented with fever, purulent and malodorous sputum, chest pain, but no hemoptysis. Chest CT showed a large cavitary lesion in the left lower lobe, with an air-fluid level and surrounding patchy consolidation. No evidence of fungus (e.g., Aspergillus) or acid - fast bacilli was found in sputum or purulent fluid during laboratory tests. Blood tests revealed elevated inflammatory markers. Without a history of recurrent lung infections, congenital lung cysts and PS were considered less likely in the differential diagnosis. Consequently, the lesion was initially misdiagnosed as a simple lung abscess caused by odontogenic flora, based on clinical judgment. Thus, anti-infective therapy combined with abscess drainage was initiated. There have been reports of PS being misdiagnosed as a lung abscess. However, the reported patients were aged between 18 and 58 [21–24]. In contrast, the patient in our case was a 16-year-old adolescent presenting with a giant lung abscess. To the best of our knowledge, this unique combination of a young age and a giant lung abscess has never been reported.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) has long been regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing PS, as it provides a clear visualization of the abnormal arterial blood supply. With advances in imaging technology, DSA has been replaced by non-invasive modalities such as computed tomography angiography (CTA). Currently, CTA is recognized as the preferred imaging technique for preoperative evaluation of PS [14]. It provides surgeons with a detailed preoperative vascular roadmap, thereby reducing the risk of inadvertent vascular injury and ensuring safer resections. In this case, the patient was initially misdiagnosed with a lung abscess before surgery, resulting in the omission of a comprehensive preoperative vascular assessment via CTA. Fortunately, the surgeon identified the aberrant blood vessel intraoperatively and promptly modified the surgical approach to avoid the risk of massive hemorrhage. Based on the intraoperative findings and the absence of pathological evidence indicating a malignant tumor, we conclusively confirmed the diagnosis of infected intralobar pulmonary sequestration.

PS is a rare benign disease. Since 1988, numerous studies have reported elevated tumor marker levels in patients with PS, and CA19-9 elevation was the most frequently observed [25–28]. Additionally, concurrent elevations in markers such as CA125, CEA, carbohydrate antigen 242, and carbohydrate antigen 50, have also been reported in the literature [29–32]. In our case, the serum CA19-9 level of this male patient was significantly elevated, CA125 was slightly elevated, and CEA was normal. As is well known, serum CA19-9 is a tumor marker mainly associated with pancreatic and biliary tract malignancies, whereas CA125 is primarily associated with gynecological malignancies, and CEA is most commonly elevated in colorectal cancer and lung cancer. Therefore, we carried out a preliminary assessment aimed at determining the cause of the elevated CA19-9 level; however, the abdominal ultrasound imaging did not show any apparent malignant lesions in the pancreas or biliary tract.

In the reported cases, serum tumor marker levels normalized after the sequestered pulmonary tissue was resected, which was also observed in our case. The exact mechanism of elevated serum tumor markers in PS remains uncertain. CA19-9, CA125 and CEA have been reported to be synthesized and secreted by normal bronchial epithelial cells [33]. The immunohistochemical results reported in multiple studies have shown that these tumor markers are positively stained in the bronchial epithelium, alveolar epithelium, and mucus of the resected sequestered lung tissue [26, 29, 30, 32]. Some studies have suggested that the chronic inflammatory process caused by Aspergillus or other pathogen infections in PS may elevate tumor markers [27]. Through these studies, we can hypothesize that the bronchial epithelial cells within sequestered pulmonary tissue are capable of synthesizing these tumor markers. Chronic inflammation or infection may increase their secretion. Due to the lack of normal bronchial drainage in sequestered lung tissue, these markers tend to accumulate at high concentrations within the lesion. Recurrent inflammatory processes can damage the mucosal barrier, allowing antigens originally confined to the epithelial cells to enter the bloodstream. These hypotheses require further in-depth investigation.

Aspergillus is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that commonly colonizes pre-existing pulmonary cavities, especially those caused by tuberculosis [34]. It has been identified in the resected sequestered lung tissue of patients with ILS, primarily through postoperative histopathological analysis [13]. A similar scenario was observed in our case. Histopathological analysis of this patient revealed Aspergillus hyphae masses within the alveolar cavities, with no evidence of invasion into the surrounding lung tissue or blood vessels. The patient exhibited no hemoptysis, a defining clinical feature of Aspergillus infection. Based on these findings, we considered the presence of Aspergillus as colonization rather than infection. For immunocompetent patients undergoing complete resection without intraoperative fungal spillage, postoperative antifungal therapy is not routinely required [35]. We believed that lobectomy had effectively eliminated the risk of further colonization; therefore, this patient did not receive antifungal agents. After 14 months of postoperative follow-up, the patient had no complications at all.

Conclusions

We can learn some lessons from this case, which can assist us in preventing delayed diagnoses in our future clinical work. This case illustrates the importance of considering ILS diagnosis when treating lung abscess in adolescents. Lung abscess may not just be a simple case of lung abscess. When clinicians encounter adolescents with lung abscesses accompanied by elevated tumor markers, such as CA19-9, they should consider the possibility of PS. CTA should be performed, which can clearly show the number and diameter of abnormal blood vessels, thereby reducing the risk of misdiagnosis and intraoperative fatal bleeding. Surgical resection is often the curative treatment for patients with symptomatic PS.
