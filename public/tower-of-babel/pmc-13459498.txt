Cryotherapy combined with standard rehabilitation and early functional recovery after knee arthroplasty: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiuxin Zhang, Dalong Wang, Shumin Yin, Wei Wang, Xu Chen, Demeng Kong, Yunmeng Li, Shanshan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pain, swelling, and limited mobility are major barriers to early functional recovery after knee arthroplasty. Cryotherapy is widely used during postoperative rehabilitation, but evidence regarding its role when combined with standard rehabilitation remains inconsistent. This study examined whether the addition of cryotherapy to a standard rehabilitation protocol was associated with improved early postoperative outcomes after knee arthroplasty.MethodsThis retrospective, non-randomized cohort study included 100 patients who underwent primary knee arthroplasty between April and July 2024. Patients were allocated to a cryotherapy group (n = 58) or a control group (n = 42) receiving standard rehabilitation alone, based on clinical practice patterns and patient preference. Primary outcomes included Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) scores, range of motion, and knee circumference. Secondary outcomes included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores, opioid consumption, length of hospital stay, complications, and patient satisfaction.ResultsAt 1 week postoperatively, the cryotherapy group had higher HSS scores (P < 0.001), greater knee flexion (P = 0.006), less extension deficit (P = 0.005), and smaller knee circumference (P = 0.003) than the control group. These between-group differences remained significant at 2 weeks (all P < 0.05) but were no longer significant at 6 weeks. VAS pain scores were lower in the cryotherapy group at 1 week (P < 0.001) and 2 weeks (P < 0.001). The cryotherapy group also used fewer opioid analgesics in the first 48 h (P < 0.001), had shorter hospital stays (P = 0.001), and reported higher patient satisfaction at 6 weeks (P = 0.003). No significant between-group difference in complications was observed (P = 0.121), and no cold-related adverse events were reported.ConclusionsCryotherapy combined with standard rehabilitation was associated with better early postoperative recovery after knee arthroplasty, particularly during the first 2 postoperative weeks. Cryotherapy may serve as a practical adjunct in early postoperative rehabilitation, although further prospective studies are needed to confirm its clinical benefit.

Introduction

Knee arthroplasty, including unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA), has become one of the most effective and widely performed surgical procedures for end-stage knee osteoarthritis, with the number of cases increasing worldwide [1]. While knee arthroplasty has proven highly effective in relieving pain and restoring joint function, the postoperative period presents significant challenges that directly impact both short-term recovery and long-term outcomes [2, 3].

Patients often experience pain, swelling, and limited range of motion (ROM) following knee arthroplasty, that can substantially hinder early functional recovery and patient satisfaction [4]. Effective management of these early postoperative symptoms is crucial for optimizing rehabilitation outcomes. Standard rehabilitation protocols following knee arthroplasty typically include early mobilization, progressive ROM exercises, muscle strengthening, gait training, and restoration of functional activities [5–7]. These rehabilitation strategies are essential for improving functional recovery, preventing complications such as deep vein thrombosis and joint stiffness, and facilitating safe discharge. However, pain and swelling often limit patient participation in rehabilitation during the critical early postoperative period [8, 9].

Cryotherapy, defined as the therapeutic application of cold to reduce tissue temperature, has long been used in orthopedic surgery as an adjunctive treatment [10].

Proposed mechanisms include vasoconstriction, reduced tissue metabolism, attenuation of inflammatory responses, and analgesic effects related to decreased nerve conduction velocity [11]. In this context, cryotherapy may serve as a supportive measure to help control pain and swelling during early recovery and potentially facilitate participation in rehabilitation.

However, the evidence regarding cryotherapy after knee arthroplasty remains inconsistent. Some studies have reported benefits in pain reduction and decreased analgesic consumption [12, 13], whereas others have found limited or no clinically significant effects [14]. A recent systematic review concluded that, although cryotherapy appears safe, evidence for its clinical benefit remains inconsistent [15].

These discrepancies may reflect variation in cryotherapy protocol, treatment duration, device type, and outcome measurement across studies [16, 17]. In addition, many previous studies have focused mainly on pain outcomes, with less attention to a broader range of early postoperative functional and recovery-related measures.

Therefore, the purpose of this retrospective study was to examine whether cryotherapy combined with a standard rehabilitation protocol was associated with better early postoperative recovery after knee arthroplasty, compared with standard rehabilitation alone. Specifically, we assessed differences in pain intensity, range of motion, functional scores, length of hospital stay, and patient-reported outcomes during the first 6 weeks postoperatively. These findings may help inform postoperative rehabilitation strategies after knee arthroplasty.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a retrospective, non-randomized observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Orthopedics, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: 20240230). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All patient data were anonymized and handled in accordance with local data protection regulations.

Participants

This study retrospectively reviewed the medical records of all patients who underwent primary knee arthroplasty at our institution between April 1, 2024, and July 31, 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 50 and 80 years; (2) primary unilateral knee arthroplasty (either UKA or TKA); (3) diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or post-traumatic arthritis; (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I-III; [18] (5) complete clinical and follow-up data available.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) bilateral or revision knee arthroplasty; (2) severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, or hepatic or renal insufficiency; (3) preoperative knee infection or skin lesions; (4) contraindications to cryotherapy including Raynaud’s syndrome, cryoglobulinemia, cold urticaria, or peripheral vascular disease; (5) intraoperative or postoperative severe complications requiring special management; (6) psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment preventing cooperation with assessment.

Surgical procedures

All operations were performed by five experienced knee surgeons using standardized techniques. For UKA, a minimally invasive anteromedial approach was used with implantation of the Oxford Unicompartmental Knee System (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). For TKA, a standard medial parapatellar approach was employed with implantation of the P.F.C. Sigma system (DePuy Synthes, Johnson & Johnson, Warsaw, IN, USA). All TKA procedures aimed for neutral mechanical alignment using conventional instrumentation. All patients received the same perioperative management protocol including spinal or general anesthesia, multimodal analgesia (combining oral analgesics, local infiltration analgesia, and patient-controlled intravenous analgesia as needed), prophylactic antibiotics, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis according to institutional protocols.

Group assignment and interventions

Patients were assigned to two groups according to the postoperative rehabilitation protocol received during hospitalization. The cryotherapy group (n = 58) received cryotherapy in addition to the standard rehabilitation protocol, whereas the control group (n = 42) received the standard rehabilitation protocol alone. Group assignment was non-randomized and reflected clinical practice patterns and patient preference during the study period.

Cryotherapy protocol

The cryotherapy group received cold therapy using standardized ice packs. Ice packs were prepared by the nursing staff using crushed ice sealed in plastic bags and wrapped in a single layer of dry towel to prevent direct skin contact and frostbite. The ice packs were applied circumferentially around the knee joint, covering the anterior, medial, and lateral aspects. Cold therapy was initiated within 2 h postoperatively and continued for 20 min per session, 4 times daily (morning, noon, afternoon, and evening) for the first 14 days postoperatively. The temperature at the skin-towel interface was monitored at the beginning of each session and maintained between 10 and 15 °C. Sessions were discontinued if patients reported excessive discomfort, numbness, or skin color changes. A minimum interval of 2 h between sessions was maintained to allow tissue rewarming. All cryotherapy applications were documented by nursing staff, including session duration, patient tolerance, and any adverse reactions.

Standard rehabilitation protocol

Both groups followed the same structured rehabilitation program: (1) Immediate postoperative period (0–24 h): Lower limb elevation at 30°, ankle pump exercises (20 repetitions per hour), isometric quadriceps contractions (10 repetitions every 2 h). (2) Early phase (Days 1–7): Continuous passive motion (CPM) machine use starting at 30–60° for 30 min twice daily, active-assisted ROM exercises, bedside sitting training, gentle weight-bearing as tolerated with walking aids from postoperative day 2. (3) Intermediate phase (Days 8–14): Progressive increase in ROM targets to 90°, assisted standing and walking training, muscle strengthening exercises for quadriceps and hamstrings. (4) Late phase (Weeks 3–6): Further ROM progression, gait training, activities of daily living (ADL) training, progressive strengthening exercises. All patients received identical multimodal pain management including scheduled oral acetaminophen, NSAIDs as appropriate, and rescue opioids if needed. Anticoagulation therapy (low molecular weight heparin) and compression stockings were used for thromboprophylaxis according to institutional protocols.

Outcome measures

Because early postoperative recovery is multidimensional, we included complementary outcome measures reflecting pain, swelling, range of motion, functional status, and patient-reported recovery. Data were extracted from electronic medical records, surgical logs, nursing records, physiotherapy records, and outpatient follow-up records by two independent researchers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Demographic data collected included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diagnosis, surgery type (UKA or TKA), prosthesis type, surgical approach, operative time, estimated blood loss, and drainage volume. Baseline and follow-up outcome measures were recorded as specified above.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics between the cryotherapy and control groups were compared using the independent-samples t test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For outcomes measured repeatedly over time (preoperatively and at 1, 2, and 6 weeks postoperatively), linear mixed-effects models were used to assess the effects of group, time, and group × time interaction while accounting for within-patient correlation. Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), surgery type (UKA vs. TKA), and baseline outcome values, as appropriate. For outcomes measured at a single postoperative time point, multivariable linear regression or logistic regression models were used with adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and surgery type, as appropriate. Sensitivity analyses restricted to TKA patients were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. Only patients with complete clinical and follow-up data were included in the analysis; therefore, no imputation for missing data was performed. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The two groups were generally comparable at baseline. There were no significant differences in age, sex distribution, BMI, ASA classification, diagnosis distribution, or surgery type distribution (P > 0.05 for all).

Primary outcomes

Overall primary outcome analysis

Linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, surgery type, and baseline outcome values showed significant group × time interaction effects for all primary outcomes, including HSS score (P = 0.003), knee flexion (P = 0.038), extension deficit (P = 0.046), and knee circumference (P = 0.037), indicating that postoperative recovery trajectories differed between the two groups.

HSS score

HSS scores improved progressively in both groups throughout follow-up, with significant increases from baseline at all postoperative time points (all P < 0.001 vs. baseline). The cryotherapy group had higher HSS scores than the control group at 1 week (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P < 0.001) and 2 weeks (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P = 0.003), whereas the between-group difference was no longer significant at 6 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.122; adjusted P = 0.142) (Fig. 1A).

Knee flexion

Knee flexion improved significantly from baseline in both groups at all postoperative time points (all P < 0.001 vs. baseline). The cryotherapy group had greater knee flexion than the control group at 1 week (unadjusted P = 0.006; adjusted P = 0.010) and 2 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.036; adjusted P = 0.043), with no significant between-group difference at 6 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.899; adjusted P = 0.994) (Fig. 1B).

Extension deficit

Extension deficit decreased progressively in both groups, with significant reductions from baseline at all postoperative time points (all P < 0.05 vs. baseline). The cryotherapy group had less extension deficit than the control group at 1 week (unadjusted P = 0.005; adjusted P = 0.012) and 2 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.023; adjusted P = 0.035), whereas no significant between-group difference was observed at 6 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.957; adjusted P = 0.974) (Fig. 1C).

Knee circumference

Knee circumference showed different postoperative patterns between groups. In the cryotherapy group, knee circumference increased slightly at 1 week and then decreased at 2 and 6 weeks, with no significant difference from baseline (P > 0.05). In the control group, knee circumference increased at 1 week compared with baseline (P < 0.001) and then decreased from 2 weeks onward, with no significant difference from baseline thereafter (P > 0.05). Between-group comparisons showed smaller knee circumference in the cryotherapy group at 1 week (unadjusted P = 0.003; adjusted P = 0.009) and 2 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.030; adjusted P = 0.041), but not at 6 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.935; adjusted P = 0.941) (Fig. 1D).

Secondary outcomes

VAS pain

VAS pain scores were lower in the cryotherapy group during the first 2 postoperative weeks, with significant between-group differences at both 1 week (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P < 0.001) and 2 weeks (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P < 0.001), but not at 6 weeks (unadjusted P = 0.092; adjusted P = 0.118). Linear mixed-effects model analysis also showed a significant group × time interaction effect for VAS pain (P < 0.001), indicating greater early pain relief in the cryotherapy group.

Other secondary outcomes

The cryotherapy group also consumed fewer opioid analgesics in the first 48 h (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P < 0.001) and required analgesics for a shorter duration (unadjusted P < 0.001; adjusted P = 0.001). Length of hospital stay was shorter in the cryotherapy group (unadjusted P = 0.001; adjusted P = 0.003). No significant between-group difference in overall complication rates was observed (unadjusted P = 0.121; adjusted P = 0.142), including superficial wound infection, deep vein thrombosis, and joint stiffness requiring manipulation. No cold-related adverse events were reported in the cryotherapy group. Patient satisfaction at 6 weeks was higher in the cryotherapy group (unadjusted P = 0.003; adjusted P = 0.006) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis: TKA patients only

To address the heterogeneity introduced by including both UKA and TKA patients, sensitivity analyses were performed in patients who underwent TKA only (n = 69; cryotherapy group n = 40, control group n = 29). The findings were broadly consistent with the overall analysis, with between-group differences remaining most evident during the first 2 postoperative weeks (Table 3). At 1 week, patients in the cryotherapy group had higher HSS scores (60.12 ± 9.23 vs. 54.35 ± 9.87, P = 0.010), greater knee flexion (95.00° [IQR, 85.00-105.00] vs. 85.00° [IQR, 80.00–95.00], P = 0.024), and smaller knee circumference (43.17 ± 3.08 cm vs. 44.88 ± 3.62 cm, P = 0.037) than those in the control group. Similar patterns were observed at 2 weeks, whereas most between-group differences were attenuated by 6 weeks.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, the addition of cryotherapy to standard rehabilitation was associated with more favorable early postoperative recovery after knee arthroplasty. The between-group differences were most evident during the first 2 postoperative weeks and involved pain, swelling, range of motion, functional scores, opioid consumption, and length of hospital stay. By 6 weeks, most outcomes had become comparable between groups, suggesting that cryotherapy may mainly accelerate recovery during the acute postoperative phase rather than substantially affect later functional status.

Current evidence on cryotherapy after knee arthroplasty remains mixed, although more recent higher-level evidence generally supports a beneficial effect on early postoperative outcomes. A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis by Liang et al. found that cryotherapy after total knee arthroplasty was associated with lower early pain scores, reduced opioid consumption, and favorable short-term recovery indicators, although effect sizes varied across outcomes [11]. Wyatt et al. similarly concluded that cryotherapy appears to reduce postoperative pain and opioid requirements after total knee arthroplasty [15]. Other recent evidence syntheses have also suggested short-term benefits, particularly for pain, swelling, and range of motion, while emphasizing heterogeneity in treatment protocols and outcome definitions [17, 23]. In contrast, earlier evidence syntheses reported more modest or inconsistent effects on pain, analgesic use, and length of stay [24]. These discrepancies likely reflect variation in cryotherapy modality, duration, timing, treatment intensity, perioperative analgesic protocols, and rehabilitation pathways across studies.

The temporal pattern observed in the present study is biologically plausible. Cryotherapy has been proposed to reduce local blood flow, tissue metabolism, edema formation, and nociceptor activity, thereby attenuating postoperative inflammation and pain during the acute recovery period [25, 26]. This interpretation is also supported by recent work emphasizing that treatment parameters such as timing, duration, and temperature may influence the magnitude of postoperative benefit [25]. The finding that between-group differences were concentrated in the first 2 postoperative weeks and diminished thereafter is consistent with this early-phase mechanism and with prospective studies showing that cryotherapy-related differences are most apparent during the immediate postoperative period [27, 28].

The lower opioid consumption observed in the cryotherapy group may be clinically relevant in the context of contemporary multimodal perioperative pain management, in which reducing opioid exposure is an important goal [29]. The lower knee circumference observed in the cryotherapy group is likewise consistent with prior evidence suggesting that cryotherapy may help reduce early postoperative edema after total knee arthroplasty [17, 20]. Because pain and swelling can limit early mobilization and exercise participation, improvements in these early symptoms may facilitate rehabilitation during the acute postoperative phase [30]. The shorter hospital stay observed in the cryotherapy group may also be relevant from a perioperative management perspective, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because discharge timing is influenced not only by symptom recovery but also by institutional practice, discharge logistics, and social factors [31].

No cold-related adverse events were observed in the cryotherapy group, and no significant between-group difference in overall complication rates was identified. This is broadly consistent with the current literature, which suggests that cryotherapy is generally well tolerated when applied with appropriate precautions and attention to contraindications [11, 15]. Higher patient satisfaction was also observed in the cryotherapy group at 6 weeks, which may reflect the more favorable early postoperative course in this group. At the same time, satisfaction is a subjective outcome and may be influenced by patient expectations, particularly in a nonblinded study setting [32, 33].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The retrospective nonrandomized design introduces the possibility of selection bias and residual confounding, particularly because group assignment was influenced by clinical practice patterns and patient preference. Although the two groups were generally comparable in measured baseline characteristics and adjusted analyses were performed, baseline comparability and statistical adjustment can reduce, but not eliminate, the risk of confounding by indication and residual confounding. The inclusion of both unicompartmental and total knee arthroplasty patients introduced clinical heterogeneity, as recovery trajectories differ between these procedures. Although the TKA-only sensitivity analysis showed broadly consistent findings, the primary conclusions still relied on pooled analysis and should therefore be interpreted with caution. This was also a single-center study, which may limit generalizability to other institutions and care pathways. In addition, follow-up was limited to 6 weeks, so the findings support only early postoperative differences and do not allow conclusions regarding sustained benefit, longer-term function, or prosthesis-related outcomes. The clinical relevance of the observed between-group differences also remains uncertain because this study was not designed to assess minimal clinically important differences. In addition, knee circumference is an indirect surrogate measure of postoperative swelling and should be interpreted cautiously. Because the study was nonblinded, subjective outcomes such as VAS pain and patient satisfaction may also have been influenced by patient expectations. Finally, adherence to both cryotherapy and rehabilitation was not formally quantified, and the use of manually prepared ice packs may limit generalizability to device-based cryotherapy systems, which differ in treatment delivery and standardization.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides clinically relevant real-world data on the use of cryotherapy as an adjunct to standard rehabilitation after knee arthroplasty. The relatively broad assessment of pain, swelling, range of motion, functional recovery, opioid use, complications, and patient-reported outcomes allowed a more comprehensive evaluation of early postoperative recovery than studies focused primarily on pain alone. Further prospective randomized studies with standardized cryotherapy protocols, blinded outcome assessment where feasible, and longer follow-up are needed to clarify the clinical significance of these early differences and to better define the role of cryotherapy in postoperative rehabilitation after knee arthroplasty.

Conclusion

Cryotherapy combined with standard rehabilitation was associated with better early postoperative recovery after knee arthroplasty, particularly during the first 2 postoperative weeks, with improvements in pain, swelling, range of motion, and opioid consumption. The convergence of outcomes by 6 weeks suggests that cryotherapy may primarily accelerate early recovery rather than substantially influence later short-term functional status. These findings support the potential role of cryotherapy as a practical adjunct in early postoperative rehabilitation after knee arthroplasty, although further prospective studies are needed to confirm its clinical benefit.
