Hierarchical proof of trust a Byzantine fault tolerant federated learning framework for industrial IoT applications
Authors: Amit Chaurasia, Sandeep Kumar Sharma, Pramod Singh Rathore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) presents significant challenges for training machine learning models due to data privacy concerns, heterogeneous data distributions, and limited bandwidth. This paper proposes HPoT (Hierarchical Proof-of-Trust), a novel federated learning-based consensus algorithm for IIoT infrastructure that addresses these limitations while ensuring data integrity and model fidelity. HPoT integrates blockchain technology with federated learning to create a decentralized, privacy-preserving framework enabling collaborative model training without raw data sharing. The algorithm features: (1) robust aggregation with weighted averaging and anomaly detection for heterogeneous datasets, (2) adaptive weighting prioritizing updates from trustworthy devices, (3) reputation scoring to detect malicious nodes, and (4) hierarchical three-tier architecture optimized for IIoT constraints. Experimental evaluation demonstrates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$70\%$$\end{document} reduction in communication rounds, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document} decrease in per-node energy consumption, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$75\%$$\end{document} lower consensus latency compared to traditional federated learning, while maintaining comparable accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$94.2\%$$\end{document} vs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$94.8\%$$\end{document}). The algorithm effectively handles data heterogeneity, communication constraints, and adversarial attacks common in IIoT environments. HPoT provides a scalable, secure, and energy-efficient solution for privacy-preserving machine learning on resource-constrained IIoT devices, advancing practical deployment of collaborative AI in industrial settings.

Introduction

In the face of the problem of data privacy, it is possible to train a machine learning model that uses decentralized IIoT data training via federated learning without disclosing such data. Integrating federated learning and blockchain would bring an increase in security and privacy in IIoT applications and secure data aggregation and model training. Industrial Internet of Things is an automation system that combines automated machines, sensors, and cloud-based platforms to support manufacturing facilities with intelligent factory implementations1. The federated learning method allows training models collaboratively among the distributed edge devices without sharing their data with each other, and consensus algorithms process local models to make a global model.

The advantages of merging the federated learning and blockchain concepts have some possible implications in the industrial IoT infrastructure, such as data privacy, better security, and transparency. Federated learning can facilitate the joint training of a model without sharing data and blockchain offers a secure and transparent environment in which to manage and to audit the aggregation of the model training process2. Integrating the two technologies, one can construct a decentralized and privacy-aware AI infrastructure to use in the IIoT systems3.

In IIoT setting, blockchain may be utilized to build a safe and clear information market where customers are in a position to donate their data towards the federated learning process in exchange of awards. Blockchain helps to increase the security and privacy of the data, which is especially valuable when it comes to federated learning in healthcare4. In addition, the incorporation of AI and blockchain can increase data security and integrity, as the blockchain has the permanent record to guarantee the accuracy of data and prevent manipulation of data by AI applications, particularly in the sectors where data security and accuracy are priorities. The combination of blockchain and federated learning has potential in overcoming these issues of trust and security in collaborative model training5.

Compared to the traditional centralized databases, blockchain does not require a third party and decreases fraud and enhances transparency. The most common use of blockchain is the introduction of Satoshi Nakamoto into Bitcoin, the first truly feasible implementation of blockchain, a digital cryptocurrency created in 2008 to operate without the influence of a central authority. Stated briefly, blockchain is a decentralized, distributed ledger that considers transactions securely, transparently and immutably and employs encryption and consensus mechanisms. Hashes connect each block with the previous one thus forming a chain. Validators or miners append the new blocks through the proof of work or proof of stake thus ensuring the security and consensus in the network.

Federated learning fundamentals

Federated learning has emerged as a promising distributed machine learning paradigm, especially suited for industrial Internet of Things infrastructures where data is inherently decentralized and privacy concerns are paramount6. In traditional centralized machine learning, data from various devices is aggregated on a central server for model training, but this approach poses significant challenges regarding data privacy, security, and communication bandwidth in IIoT settings7. Federated learning addresses these challenges by enabling model training to occur locally on each device, using its data, and only sharing model updates with a central server8. This approach minimizes the amount of data that needs to be transmitted, thus preserving data privacy and reducing communication overhead. The distributed nature of federated learning aligns well with the architecture of IIoT systems, where devices are often geographically dispersed and have limited communication capabilities6.

Recent work on blockchain-enabled IoT has demonstrated the potential of trust-based consensus mechanisms. The GLSB-IoT framework9 employs Grey Wolf Optimization for lightweight blockchain, achieving 30% energy reduction. However, it lacks federated learning integration and Byzantine fault tolerance. Similarly, the smart city framework10,11 provides strong privacy guarantees but assumes homogeneous data distributions, limiting IIoT applicability where data heterogeneity is inherent.

Consensus algorithms

A number of consensus schemes have been offered to blockchain systems, Proof-of-Work, Proof-of-Stake, and Delegated Proof-of-Stake. In Proof-of-Work approach, participants are forced to order their transaction verification and generation of newly added blocks by solving computationally demanding puzzles, which can be energy-intensive and time-consuming. Instead, Proof-of-Stake is more energy efficient by selecting validators depending on the number of tokens held which generally can result in centralization. Delegated Proof-of-Stake gives tokens the opportunity to assign their voting rights to a reduced body of sensors, a scheme that might enhance throughput and effective performance. Another Byzantine fault tolerant algorithm is the Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (pBFT) where nodes can exhibit malicious or arbitrary failures12.

Consensus algorithms are essential in the reliability and security of such distributed systems like blockchain networks and federated learning systems. Likewise in federated learning, consensus algorithms may be leveraged to combine model updates across client devices, and to make the global model consistent and correct. When developing a consensus algorithm to federated learning inside IIoT infrastructure the requirements should be taken into the account, the number of clients, the data distribution scale, and the threat to the data security.

Literature survey

Federated learning is a distributed machine-learning paradigm, where multilateral clients share a common model and train it without sharing their personal information. Decentralized training in which the central server coordinates the training by collecting updates sent by the clients reduces the risks of centralized data collection and data storage. However, being used as a performance bottleneck, a central parameter server may also present privacy issues as the number of participants rises25. The nature of distributing data among many clients also allows the inclusion of a more diverse body of data, resulting in stronger and generalized model performance26. In areas where data privacy matters most, e.g., in healthcare, finance, sensitive research, the decentralized feature of federated learning offers important advantages by restricting transferring sensitive data to flow of model parameters instead of actual data, thus leading to greater data privacy and security26,27. However, the federated learning faces a number of adversities, which consist in significant communication overhead, possible data bias, and necessity to establish trust between users26,28. Blockchain integration is especially attractive since it presents a transparent, mutation-resistant and distributed check with which transactions and amendments can be tracked and cleared in federated-learning settings, enhancing trusts and credibility5.

One of the most noticeable studies of applications of federated learning to healthcare demonstrates the benefits of the framework. The importance of training powerful models using a range of datasets, and the advantages to adhere to restrictive data-privacy restrictions like GDPR, makes the use of federated learning interesting to use, as it allows collecting the knowledge without having to centralize sensitive patient data29,30. This is drastically different compared with the conventional centralized machine-learning, in which all data settles on a solitary server, and poses high privacy threat related to data leakage, misuse, and abuse risks31. All in all, federated learning can serve as a very promising method of safeguarding data privacy and secure computation given some modern threats of a model-inference attack and the reconstruction of data on the basis of gradients.

Federated learning is particularly well-suited for IIoT environments, where data is often generated and stored on edge devices with limited communication capabilities. The federated learning approach ensures that sensitive data remains on the devices where it originates, which enhances data privacy and reduces the risk of data breaches32. By training models locally, federated learning minimizes the need to transmit large volumes of data over the network, which reduces communication overhead and improves efficiency33.

Despite significant advancements in distributed ledger technologies, a definitive consensus mechanism that concurrently optimizes for scalability, security, and decentralization remains elusive, particularly when considering the inherent trade-offs between these critical performance metrics. This ongoing challenge necessitates further exploration into novel consensus protocols and refinements of existing ones to address current limitations effectively. The “Byzantine Generals Problem” exemplifies this difficulty, highlighting the challenge of achieving agreement in decentralized systems where malicious actors or unreliable communication can disrupt coordination34,12. While various consensus mechanisms like Proof of Work and Proof of Stake have been widely adopted, each presents distinct vulnerabilities and efficiency concerns12. For instance, the computational costs associated with Proof of Work can hinder widespread adoption, especially in high-transaction environments35.

Federated Byzantine Fault Tolerance and Federated Proof of Stake have received particular attention among consent algorithms related to federated learning36. Federated Proof of Stake mitigates the drawback of the traditional Proof of Stake through the integration of the concepts of federated-learning to improve security, decentralization and scalability of distributed ledger technologies8. The synergistic integration also provides additional security to data integrity and traceability, reduces vulnerability to inference, poisoning, and Sybils attacks37. However, federated proof of stake suffers a massive communication overhead and the failure to resist or offend the malicious actors to harm the global model38. To address these limitations, complex algorithmic techniques are required, such as multi-scale feature fusion strategies, and robust aggregation methodologies, as a means of realizing high performance and developing reliable federated-learning systems39,40. Creation of strong incentive mechanisms and privacy-friendly techniques that reward participation and discourage bad behaviour or attacks, as well as maintaining data integrity and model credibility, are an active research area41.

Due to the necessity to train machine-learning models across multiple organizations in the same setting as well as complying with privacy limitations, the challenges of federated learning have become a well-known research field33. However, the problems of the statistical heterogeneity and the diversity of the working devices still require solutions that were as innovative as possible to guarantee the optimal performance of the system and its applicability to a wide ensuring scope of domains8. No existing approach simultaneously addresses data heterogeneity, Byzantine resilience, and energy efficiency in resource-constrained IIoT environments.

The first, by Zhang et al., proposes a privacy-preserving federated learning framework specifically designed for heterogeneous data distributions in 6G mobile networks. Their scheme introduces a noise-calibrated local differential privacy mechanism combined with a dynamic client selection strategy to handle non-IID data across heterogeneous devices. While Zhang et al. demonstrate strong privacy guarantees (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}-differential privacy) and improved convergence under data heterogeneity, their framework is designed for a flat (single-tier) federated learning topology and does not incorporate Byzantine fault tolerance or a trust scoring mechanism. In contrast, HPoT addresses heterogeneity through its hierarchical cluster architecture and trust-based adaptive weighting, while simultaneously providing Byzantine resilience up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lfloor N/3 \rfloor$$\end{document} malicious nodes—a capability absent from Zhang et al.’s scheme. HPoT additionally targets resource-constrained IIoT devices (ARM Cortex-M4 class hardware), whereas Zhang et al. assume 6G mobile devices with substantially greater computational resources.

The second work, by Chen et al.52, proposes a robust secure blockchain-based hierarchical asynchronous federated learning (HAFL) scheme for IoT environments. Their approach shares several structural similarities with HPoT: both employ a hierarchical two-tier architecture (edge layer + cloud layer), blockchain-based integrity guarantees, and Byzantine fault tolerance. Key distinctions are as follows. First, Chen et al.’s scheme is asynchronous—local updates are aggregated as they arrive, without waiting for a synchronous round—which improves latency under heterogeneous device speeds but complicates convergence analysis. HPoT uses a synchronous round structure within each cluster, which provides stronger convergence guarantees under the BFT bound. Second, Chen et al. do not incorporate a dynamic trust scoring mechanism; their robustness relies on cryptographic proofs alone, without adaptively penalizing persistently unreliable devices. HPoT’s four-component trust score (Eq. 20) provides a complementary behavioural filter that detects stealthy, low-magnitude poisoning attacks (89.2% data poisoning detection rate) that cryptographic proofs alone cannot identify. Third, HPoT’s energy-adaptive participation strategy (Eq. 27) and battery-dependent epoch scheduling (Algorithm 1) are not present in Chen et al.’s scheme, making HPoT more suitable for severely resource-constrained IIoT deployments. Problem statement

Reaching consensus among the local models under a federated learning framework is very important in maintaining convergence and accuracy of the global model, and the heterogeneous nature of the IIoT devices and their data distributions present severe complications to reaching a consensus among local models. The differences in hardware capacity, data quality and bandwidth could cause differences in local model updates, which in turn will diminish convergence of global model. Moreover, the existence of malicious or defective devices may bring biased or inaccurate updates, degrading the state of the global model.

To counter these weaknesses this paper proposes consensus algorithm that utilizes federated learning applied to IIoT infrastructure. The described algorithm already implements such a strong aggregation protocol, which involves weighting averaging and the method of anomaly detection to reduce effects of heterogeneous data distributions and malicious updates.

Proposed methodology

Consensus algorithm design

There are several factors to put into consideration when designing a consensus algorithm to handle federated learning in the IIoT infrastructure. IIoT devices collect heterogeneous data and need sufficient advancing algorithms to manage different arrangements and quality of data. Limited network bandwidths and intermittent connectivity may be a problem in IIoT networks and therefore require communication-efficient consensus protocols. IIoT systems can be exposed to hackers and need agreement mechanism to be resistant to cyberspace attackers as well as data tampering.

This will enable to use decentralized and immutable properties of blockchain and keep the integrity of data and avoid tampering and federated learning which allows model training in collaboration without sharing sensitive data. Furthermore, techniques of differential privacy can also be interpolated to provide further boosts to privacy of data through addition of noise to the model updates that complicates the inference of information about individual instances. The nature of training machine learning models in IIoT is the fact that it is complicated with data privacy, data heterogeneity, and communication restrictions. A hopeful alternative is federated learning, which allows training multiple models based on data on many decentralized IIoT devices and providing data privacy.

Nonetheless, existence of malicious or faulty clients may jeopardize quality and correctness of the global model, thus, it is important to have a robust design of the consensus algorithms, which should withstand such situations. The task is to design federated learning-based consensus algorithm, which allows properly integrating local updates of IIoT devices to generate a global model, and maintain data privacy, robustness to malicious attacks, and tolerance of communication constraints. Security analysis framework

This section summarizes the attack implementation tooling, the detection mechanisms considered, the evaluation metrics used for performance assessment, the testing scenarios devised to probe detector robustness, and a concise summary of the experimental results.

Attack implementation tools. Adversarial attacks were implemented using a combination of established libraries and custom code. The Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (ART) v1.12.0 was used for standard adversarial primitives and baseline manipulations, while a bespoke Byzantine attack simulator (Python 3.8) was developed to model coordinated and custom attack behaviors. Two primary attack modules were integrated into the simulator: (i) a model-poisoning module that injects random noise into local model updates, and (ii) a data-poisoning module that flips training labels at a configurable flip rate (here set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$40\%$$\end{document}).

Cosine Similarity Analysis: A pairwise cosine similarity matrix was computed across all submitted model updates in each communication round to quantify directional agreement between updates. Evaluation metrics. Detection performance and attack impact were quantified using the following metrics: (i) True Positive Rate (TPR)—fraction of Byzantine devices correctly identified; (ii) False Positive Rate (FPR)—fraction of honest devices misclassified as Byzantine; (iii) Detection Latency—number of communication rounds until an adversary is identified; and (iv) Attack Success Rate—fractional contribution or influence of malicious updates retained in the aggregated global model.

Testing scenarios. We designed four adversarial scenarios to stress-test the detectors:Gradual attack: malicious intensity is increased progressively over rounds to simulate stealthy escalation.Coordinated attack: multiple adversaries act simultaneously and in a coordinated manner to amplify effect.Stealthy attack: adversaries use low-magnitude perturbations designed to remain below simple statistical thresholds.Adaptive attack: adversaries adapt their strategies in response to observed detection behavior (e.g., reduce magnitude after partial detection).Across the considered scenarios and detectors, the combined pipeline achieved high detection effectiveness. For model-poisoning attacks the observed detection rate was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97.3\%$$\end{document}, while for data-poisoning attacks it was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$89.2\%$$\end{document}. The false positive rate remained low, at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<2\%$$\end{document} across all scenarios. Detection latency exhibited a mean of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.1$$\end{document} rounds with a 95th-percentile of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5$$\end{document} rounds, indicating that most adversaries were identified within a small number of communication rounds. These results demonstrate that the ensemble of similarity-based, statistical, and density-based detectors provides robust and timely identification of Byzantine and poisoning behaviors under a variety of realistic adversarial patterns.

Attack parameter justification

The specific parameter values used in each attack scenario were selected through a combination of standard adversarial machine learning practice and preliminary validation experiments, as detailed below.The 40% label-flip rate was selected based on two considerations. First, it represents a realistic upper bound for stealthy attacks: flip rates exceeding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document} cause accuracy degradation severe enough to be detected trivially through standard accuracy monitoring, making them operationally non-stealthy. Second, it aligns with the range used in the canonical adversarial federated learning benchmark study Tolpegin et al.44—where flip rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$30-50\%$$\end{document} are standard for evaluating detection systems under moderate-to-high intensity poisoning. We conducted preliminary experiments with flip rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(10\%, 20\%, 30\%, 40\%)$$\end{document} and selected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$40\%$$\end{document} as the most challenging rate at which the detection pipeline could be meaningfully stress-tested without becoming trivially detectable.The value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma = 5.0$$\end{document} was chosen to produce a high-intensity perturbation clearly distinguishable from honest model-update noise arising from data heterogeneity, which in our experimental setting yields gradient norms with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma < 1.0$$\end{document}. This separation is important to ensure that the model poisoning scenario tests the detector under genuinely challenging conditions rather than noise indistinguishable from IID variance. The experiments confirm this: at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma = 1.0$$\end{document} (low intensity), the attack is largely self-limiting—gradient norms remain within the natural distribution, and detection rate with cosine similarity alone reaches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$96.1\%$$\end{document}. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma = 5.0$$\end{document}, the single-detector cosine similarity rate drops to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$81\%$$\end{document}, rising to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97.3\%$$\end{document} only when the full ensemble (cosine + statistical outlier + DBSCAN) is applied. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma = 5.0$$\end{document} condition was therefore selected as the most informative operating point for evaluating the ensemble’s added value. This justification is consistent with model-poisoning parameterizations in related Byzantine-robust FL literature45,46.The DBSCAN hyperparameters were selected via a grid search on a held-out validation network of 100 devices (disjoint from the test network used for all reported results). We evaluated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$eps~\in ~{0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$min\_samples~ \in ~{3, 5, 10},$$\end{document} yielding 12 candidate configurations. The combination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$eps = 0.3, min\_samples = 5$$\end{document} achieved the best F1-score (0.94) on the validation set across all three attack types, balancing sensitivity (avoiding missed detections that occur at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$eps = 0.5$$\end{document}, where outlier clusters merge with honest ones) against specificity (avoiding false positives that spike at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$eps = 0.1$$\end{document}, where the neighbourhood is too tight). Attack scenarios

evaluate system robustness under three detailed adversarial threat models: model poisoning (Type I), data poisoning (Type II), and Sybil (Type III) attacks. Each attack is parameterized to emulate realistic adversarial behavior in distributed IIoT learning and to stress different aspects of the detection pipeline.

Model poisoning attack. A subset of compromised devices deliberately perturbs their local model parameters by injecting additive Gaussian noise to bias the global aggregation. For a compromised device \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document}, the reported parameters are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta '_{i} \;=\; \theta _{i} \;+\; \mathscr {N}\!\big (\theta ,\; \sigma ^{2} I\big ),$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {N}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes a multivariate Gaussian with mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} and covariance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ^{2}I$$\end{document}. In our experiments we set the noise magnitude \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma =5.0$$\end{document} to represent a high-intensity perturbation and sweep the fraction of compromised devices over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0\%,10\%,20\%,33\%\}$$\end{document}. This parameter sweep allows assessment of both low-incidence (sparse) and high-incidence (widespread) model poisoning and their effects on convergence, accuracy degradation, and false-positive detection rates.

Data poisoning attack. Data poisoning is performed by altering local training labels on compromised devices to intentionally mislead the learning objective. Concretely, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$40\%$$\end{document} of the local training labels on selected devices are randomly flipped within a set of targeted classes (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2--3$$\end{document} classes in multiclass tasks). To increase stealth, poisoned samples are injected gradually across communication rounds: a device increments the number of poisoned examples over a 20-round window rather than introducing all poisoned data at once. This gradual injection is designed to evade detection heuristics that rely on abrupt distributional shifts and to evaluate the sensitivity of temporal detection methods.

Sybil attack. The Sybil attack model simulates a single adversary that controls \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} distinct fake identities (Sybil nodes) within the network. Each Sybil identity submits coordinated, oftentimes identical, malicious updates to amplify adversarial influence on the global model. Attack coordination includes synchronous submission times and replicated update vectors, which increases the likelihood of the malicious updates surviving aggregation rules that weight by frequency. Detection for Sybil scenarios in our pipeline leverages clustering of model-update hashes or low-dimensional embeddings to identify groups of highly similar (or identical) submissions; the experimental design varies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} and the degree of synchronization to probe the limits of clustering-based defenses.

For all attack types we explore multiple axes of variability (e.g., intensity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}, proportion of compromised devices, number of Sybil identities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}, and injection schedules) to comprehensively evaluate detection performance under realistic and adversarially adaptive conditions. Performance is measured with respect to detection accuracy, false-positive rate, detection latency (in communication rounds), and the residual attack success rate (fractional influence of malicious updates in the final global model). Results and analysis

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.8.10 with the PyTorch 1.9.0 deep learning framework. The simulations were executed on a high-performance computing platform equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB) and a 64-core AMD EPYC 7742 processor. For network-level experimentation, a custom Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) network simulator was employed, which is available for public access at50.

The simulated IIoT environment consisted of 100–10, 000 IoT nodes organized in a hierarchical topology comprising 10–50 clusters and 5–20 edge coordinators. The dataset was distributed across devices using a Dirichlet distribution with parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.5$$\end{document} to induce non-IID characteristics. Device communication followed the IEEE 802.15.4 standard, offering a bandwidth of 250 kbps for inter-node transmissions. Each simulated IoT device was modeled with an ARM Cortex-M4 CPU operating at 80 MHz, 512 KB of RAM, and a 2000 mAh battery with simulated discharge behavior. Depending on the experiment, each device held between 1000 and 5000 local data samples to emulate realistic IIoT workloads.

The performance evaluation of the proposed HPoT algorithm was conducted using a comprehensive simulation environment comprising 10 to 10, 000 IIoT devices distributed across various industrial scenarios. The experimental setup included heterogeneous data distributions, varying network topologies, and different levels of Byzantine node presence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0\%$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$33\%$$\end{document}). All experiments were repeated 50 times with different random seeds to ensure statistical significance, and results are reported with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$95\%$$\end{document} confidence intervals (Figs. 5 and 6).

In the environment of resource limitation, energy efficiency optimization can act as one of the key IIoT device performance metrics. A practical analysis reveals that HPoT algorithm would provide \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document} lower energy consumption per device as compared to the traditional federated learning mechanisms.

Fig. 7 presents the outcomes of the energy consumption investigation displaying how the energy consumption is distributed among the different types of devices. The results show that it is possible to reduce the energy consumption of the devices by about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60 \%$$\end{document} and the devices as shown in (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_i < 0.2$$\end{document}) can keep contributing meaningfully to the federated learning phase.

Moreover, the statistical significance of the observed reduction energy in each category of devices was proved with the help of a paired t-test. The obtained results indicate that the t value is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t =-12.47$$\end{document} and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p-value$$\end{document} is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p-value~is~<0.001$$\end{document}, and the difference between energy consumption is significant.

Cryptographic overhead in the energy budget. An important clarification regarding the reported 50% energy reduction is warranted. The energy consumption figures reported throughout this paper and specifically the per-device values in Section “Sensitivity analysis of trust score weights” and Table 10 - include the full computational and communication energy budget for each device per federated learning round. This explicitly encompasses: (i) local model training, (ii) gradient compression, (iii) model update transmission and reception, and (iv) all cryptographic operations mandated by the HPoT protocol, including SHA-256 model hashing, ECDSA digital signature generation and verification, and consensus proof verification.

To ensure full transparency and reproducibility, Section “Cryptographic overhead in the energy budget” provides a component-level energy breakdown comparing HPoT against Traditional FL (FedAvg). Measurements were obtained by profiling individual operations on the simulated ARM Cortex-M4 device model (80MHz, 512 KB RAM), averaged across 50 experimental runs on a network of 1000 devices.

As shown in Section “Cryptographic overhead in the energy budget”, cryptographic operations (hashing + ECDSA signature + proof verification) account for approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20.5\%$$\end{document} of HPoT’s total per-device energy expenditure (14.1 mJ of 67.0 mJ per round). The dominant energy saving arises from the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$70\%$$\end{document} reduction in communication rounds—each avoided round eliminates training, compression, and transmission energy simultaneously. The cryptographic overhead is nearly identical between HPoT and Traditional FL in absolute terms (14.1 mJ vs. 14.1 mJ), since Traditional FL also performs basic hashing for integrity checks in our implementation. Consequently, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document} overall energy reduction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$46.9\%$$\end{document} precisely, as shown in Section “Cryptographic overhead in the energy budget”) is attributable to the hierarchical communication architecture and adaptive device participation, not to any reduction in cryptographic cost. Security and trust evaluation

Trust-based filtering

The security evaluation confirms that the HPoT algorithm successfully tolerates up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$33\%$$\end{document} Byzantine nodes while maintaining system integrity.The trust scoring mechanism, defined by Eq. (20), computes the device trust score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{i}(t)$$\end{document} as a weighted combination of four components with trust threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust = 0.5$$\end{document} for filtering malicious nodes during aggregation. The trust threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust$$\end{document} serves as the minimum acceptable trust score for including a device’s update in aggregation:

Aggregation Set: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A(t) = {n_i \in N: T_{i}(t) \ge \tau \_trust}$$\end{document}

Threshold Selection Rationale: - \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust = 0.3$$\end{document}: Too permissive, 8% Byzantine updates included - \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust = 0.5$$\end{document}: Optimal balance, 2% false positives - \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust = 0.7$$\end{document}: Too restrictive, excludes 15% honest new devices

We set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \_trust = 0.5$$\end{document} based on validation set performance across 1000 simulation runs with varying Byzantine ratios.

To empirically validate the security of the preheating period (rounds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-5$$\end{document}), we conducted additional experiments injecting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$33\%$$\end{document} Byzantine nodes from round 1—the worst-case adversarial scenario during cold-start. Three protections remain active throughout this window: (i) cryptographic integrity—digital signatures (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Sig_i$$\end{document}, Eq. (3)) and SHA-256 model hashes prevent identity spoofing from round 1; (ii) proportional dilution—each device contributes weight proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{|D_i|}{\sum |D_k|}$$\end{document}, so a Byzantine device with a typical dataset size exerts no disproportionate influence; and (iii) cosine pre-screening—Algorithm 2 (lines \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$8-11$$\end{document}) still applies FilterTrusted() against the initialized neutral score, rejecting extreme outliers even before differentiation begins. The global model accuracy at the end of round 5 under this \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$33\%$$\end{document}-Byzantine cold-start scenario was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$89.3\%$$\end{document}. From round 6 onward, the full trust-scoring pipeline activated and corrected for the accumulated bias: by round 8, accuracy recovered to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$93.1\%$$\end{document}, and by round 15 it reached the fully-converged value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$94.2\%$$\end{document} —an accuracy gap that closes within 3 rounds of trust activation. These results confirm that the cold-start window, while intentionally permissive, does not produce irreversible model degradation.

Conclusion

This paper presents HPoT (Hierarchical Proof-of-Trust), a novel hierarchical consensus algorithm designed for federated learning in IIoT environments. The proposed framework effectively addresses three critical challenges in decentralized industrial intelligence: Byzantine fault tolerance, energy efficiency, and data heterogeneity. By introducing a multi-tier trust-based hierarchy, HPoT enables reliable and efficient collaborative model training among a large number of heterogeneous IoT devices while preserving security and scalability. HPoT achieves significant performance gains across several key metrics. The hierarchical communication model reduces consensus complexity from O(N) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\log N)$$\end{document}, resulting in a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$70\%$$\end{document} reduction in communication rounds. Furthermore, adaptive trust management and selective participation mechanisms lead to a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document} decrease in per-device energy consumption and a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$75\%$$\end{document} reduction in consensus latency, while maintaining a global model accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$94.2\%$$\end{document}. The algorithm provides resilience against adversarial behaviors by tolerating up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lfloor \frac{N}{3} \rfloor$$\end{document} Byzantine nodes with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$97\%$$\end{document} detection rate for malicious activities. These results collectively demonstrate the capability of HPoT to operate efficiently under real-world IIoT constraints.

The hierarchical trust-scoring mechanism not only enhances reliability but also ensures scalability across large and diverse device networks. This design allows HPoT to be deployed on resource-constrained IoT nodes without compromising security or model performance. The algorithm’s distributed verification process prevents single points of failure and mitigates trust manipulation attacks, making it highly suitable for industrial domains where operational continuity and trustworthiness are paramount.

Future work

To enhance the robustness and applicability of HPoT, several research directions are proposed as Introducing dynamic cluster formation and reconfiguration mechanisms based on real-time network conditions, device capabilities, and energy availability. Extending HPoT to multi-organizational contexts such as inter-factory collaboration and supply chain coordination. Incorporating formal privacy guarantees using noise injection and secure aggregation techniques. Exploring FPGA and ASIC-based implementations for energy-efficient deployment on edge and gateway devices. Conducting pilot studies within manufacturing plants, smart city infrastructures, and industrial automation networks to assess scalability and adaptability in operational settings.

In summary, HPoT advances the state-of-the-art in privacy-preserving, trustworthy, and energy-efficient federated learning for IIoT systems. Its open-source implementation fosters reproducibility, transparency, and continued research, establishing a strong foundation for secure and scalable collaborative AI in next-generation industrial ecosystems.
