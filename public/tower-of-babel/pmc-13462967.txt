Parametric sweep assessment of cross-slit cylinders for passive drag reduction and wake modification
Authors: G. Madhan Kumar, K. M. Kumar, T. Mothilal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a detailed numerical investigation and parametric sweep assessment of cross-slit (CS) cylindrical configurations aimed at passive drag reduction and wake modification in low to transitional Reynolds number regimes relevant to marine and offshore applications. A series of two-dimensional laminar CFD simulations were performed at Reynolds numbers of 100, 150, and 300 to analyze the influence of slit width ratio (SWR = 0.10–0.25) and sweep angle on wake dynamics, drag, lift, and vortex shedding characteristics. . A systematic mesh-independence study with Grid Convergence Index estimates and time-step sensitivity analysis were conducted to ensure numerical reliability. The results reveal that intermediate sweep configurations (SWR = 0.15–0.20) effectively modify alternating vortex shedding and reduce wake by promoting early shear-layer reattachment. Compared to conventional and single-slit cylinders, the optimized CS designs achieved 26–32.5% drag reduction, up to 40–52.5% reduction in reattachment length,, and a 93.1% reduction in Strouhal number at Re = 100,, indicating weekened vortex shedding intensity, and modified wake topology Increasing Reynolds number enhanced flow instability, yet the optimized CS geometry retained improved hydrodynamic stability and reduced fluctuating lift amplitudes. Wake vorticity analysis confirmed that cross-slit perforations alter the coherent vortex street by delaying shear-layer roll-up and extending the near-wake region. The observed trends in drag reduction, wake stabilisation, and shedding frequency modification indicate that cross-slit cylinders serve as a promising passive hydrodynamic control mechanism with potential implications for offshore risers, underwater structural supports, and marine energy devices.

Introduction

Flow past bluff bodies has been a fundamental problem in fluid mechanics since the pioneering theoretical work of von Kármán, who first described the periodic vortex street that develops in the wake of a circular cylinder1. The circular cylinder, owing to its simple geometric definition yet complex wake behaviour, has emerged as the canonical configuration for investigating unsteady separated flows, vortex dynamics, and fluid–structure interaction phenomena. The wake behind a circular cylinder is characterised by the formation of alternating vortices of opposite sign that are convected downstream in a staggered arrangement, known as the von Kármán vortex street. This flow topology gives rise to periodic fluctuations in the aerodynamic or hydrodynamic loads acting on the body, manifesting as oscillating lift and drag forces that are directly relevant to the design and operation of offshore structures, marine risers, heat-exchanger tubes, bridge piers, chimneys, and submerged pipelines2. When the vortex shedding frequency approaches the natural frequency of the structure, resonant lock-in can occur, leading to large-amplitude vibrations known as vortex-induced vibrations (VIV), which pose a significant risk of fatigue failure and reduced service life in marine and aerospace applications3. The engineering and scientific importance of this problem has motivated more than half a century of experimental, theoretical, and numerical investigations, establishing a rich database of benchmark results against which new flow control strategies can be evaluated.

The flow regime around a circular cylinder is primarily governed by the Reynolds number, defined as Re = UD/ν, where U is the freestream velocity, D is the cylinder diameter, and ν is the kinematic viscosity. The onset of vortex shedding from a stationary circular cylinder occurs at a critical Reynolds number of approximately Re ≈ 47, beyond which the wake undergoes a supercritical Hopf bifurcation from a steady symmetric recirculation to a time-periodic asymmetric state4,5. In the strictly two-dimensional laminar shedding regime, extending from Re ≈ 50 to approximately Re ≈ 180, the flow remains spatially periodic along the span of the cylinder, and the vortex shedding is characterised by a nearly constant Strouhal number in the range St ≈ 0.16–0.18. The time-averaged drag coefficient exhibits a characteristic decreasing trend with increasing Reynolds number, from Cd ≈ 1.4 at Re = 50 to Cd ≈ 1.2 at Re = 200, as quantified by the high-fidelity numerical benchmark studies of6 and. The7 study, in particular, provided high-order polynomial correlations for the Re-dependence of Cd, base suction coefficient, Strouhal number, and lift fluctuation amplitude across the range Re = 50–200, establishing these as the most widely cited benchmark results for validating two-dimensional laminar CFD simulations of cylinder flow. The8 numerical analysis further revealed detailed wake structure, including non-monotonic recovery of streamwise velocity in the intermediate wake and the formation of distinct velocity plateaus along the wake centreline for Re > 50, details that have been largely neglected in previous studies.

Beyond Re ≈ 180, the wake of a circular cylinder undergoes a transition from two-dimensional to three-dimensional behaviour. Two distinct three-dimensional instability modes have been identified: Mode A, characterised by long-wavelength spanwise vortices that appear at Re ≈ 188.5, and Mode B, characterised by shorter-wavelength streamwise-oriented vortices that emerge at Re ≈ 2609,10. These three-dimensional modes manifest as streamwise vortex braids connecting the primary spanwise vortices, leading to a breakdown of the classical two-dimensional vortex street topology. At higher Reynolds numbers, typically Re > 105, the flow transitions to fully turbulent wake behaviour accompanied by the well-known drag crisis, where the mean drag coefficient drops sharply due to boundary-layer transition and delayed flow separation11. Despite the eventual transition to three-dimensional and turbulent behaviour, the two-dimensional laminar regime remains a critical benchmark for establishing baseline flow physics, validating numerical methodologies, and understanding the fundamental mechanisms of vortex formation and shear-layer interaction before progressing to more complex transitional or turbulent configurations.

Given the substantial engineering implications of vortex shedding and the associated unsteady loading on cylindrical structures, a wide range of passive and active flow control strategies have been developed over the past several decades with the objective of reducing drag, suppressing vortex shedding, or modifying the wake topology12. Active flow control techniques, which require external energy input, include suction and blowing through surface slots, synthetic jets, plasma actuators, and oscillating or rotating cylinders13. While active methods offer the advantage of adaptability and can be tuned in real time, they are generally unsuitable for offshore and marine applications due to the requirement for power supply, sensors, and control systems in harsh environments. Passive flow control techniques, in contrast, offer the significant advantage of requiring no external energy input, relying instead on geometric modifications to the body surface or wake region to achieve the desired flow modification. These include surface roughness modifications, helical strakes, splitter plates, trip wires, porous or slotted surfaces, and attached secondary bodies14,15. Among passive methods, helical strakes have been extensively used on offshore risers and marine pipelines to disrupt the spanwise coherence of vortex shedding and thereby suppress VIV, although they typically incur a penalty in the form of increased mean drag16. Splitter plates, extending downstream from the rear stagnation point, can suppress vortex shedding for a certain plate length range but are susceptible to shedding from the plate itself at larger lengths17. The design of effective passive flow control devices therefore requires careful optimisation of the geometric parameters to balance competing objectives of drag reduction, vortex suppression, and structural loading.

Among passive flow control strategies, perforated and slotted cylindrical configurations have received growing attention in recent years due to their unique ability to modify the pressure distribution around the cylinder and to interact with the separating shear layers through the mechanism of self-injection or self-ejection of fluid through the perforations. The fundamental concept involves introducing one or more slots or slits through the cylinder body, oriented either parallel or transverse to the incoming flow, thereby creating a communication path between the high-pressure region at the front stagnation point and the low-pressure base region in the wake17. The additional flow through the slit acts as a passive jet that injects momentum into the near-wake region, effectively increasing the base pressure and weakening the vortex formation process. This mechanism has been shown to reduce both the mean drag coefficient and the fluctuating lift coefficient, while simultaneously modifying the Strouhal number and extending the vortex formation length.

The concept of introducing a single longitudinal slit through a circular cylinder was first systematically investigated by18, who performed numerical simulations at Re = 500 and 1000 with SWR varying from 0.05 to 0.30, with the slit oriented parallel to the freestream (θ = 0°). Their study demonstrated that the additional flow through the slit suppresses the global instability of the wake and weakens vortex shedding, leading to reductions in both drag and fluctuating lift. At Re = 500, the vortex shedding remained periodic up to SWR (Slit-Width ratio) ≈ 0.15, beyond which the shedding became irregular and eventually fully suppressed at larger slit widths. For Re = 1000, the vortex shedding was similarly weakened but required larger slit widths for complete suppression. The self-injection mechanism was identified as the key physical process: the flow through the slit acts as a passive jet that energises the low-pressure base region, thereby stabilising the wake and reducing the fluctuating lift amplitudes.

Building on this foundational work19, conducted a comprehensive numerical investigation of slit cylinders across the Reynolds number range Re = 100–500, considering variations in both slit width ratio and slit angle. Their study systematically examined the effect of slit orientation, demonstrating that a slit aligned parallel to the incoming flow (θ = 0°) is most effective for suppressing vortex shedding, while oblique slit orientations progressively lose their effectiveness. The critical Reynolds number for the onset of vortex shedding was shown to increase with slit width ratio, indicating a stabilisation of the base flow. For SWR in the range of 0.10–0.25, the study identified significant weakening of the global instability and suppression of VIV, with the most effective configurations occurring at intermediate slit widths where the self-injection is sufficient to modify the wake without creating strong jet-like disturbances. The detailed flow visualisation revealed that the flow through the slit modifies the shear-layer interaction in the near-wake, delaying the roll-up of vortices and extending the vortex formation length.

Further experimental investigations have corroborated these numerical findings14. employed particle image velocimetry (PIV) to study the wake of cylinders with longitudinal slits at various orientations and widths, confirming that the slit creates a self-issuing jet into the cylinder wake that is efficient in controlling the wake vortex shedding process. At lower slit angles, the slot effectively reduced drag and suppressed lift fluctuations by altering the separation points on the cylinder surface. At higher angles, the slot orientation promoted boundary-layer suction and blowing effects that delayed flow separation and shrank the overall wake width. The PIV measurements quantified the modifications to the near-wake vorticity field and demonstrated that the vortex street topology is fundamentally altered by the presence of the slit. Similarly, conducted numerical analysis of flow-induced vibration suppression using passive parallel slits, reporting that slit width-to-diameter ratios greater than 10% can subdue cylinder oscillations and reduce both drag and lift forces, with lock-in phenomena occurring at reduced velocities of 6 to 8.

The experimental work of20 provided further validation of the passive slit mechanism using PIV measurements downstream of a perforated cylinder, demonstrating that the slit creates a self-issuing jet into the wake and that this passive jet effectively controls the vortex shedding process. The study quantified the variation of drag coefficient, lift coefficient amplitude, and Strouhal number with slit width, confirming that the modifications to the wake dynamics scale with the amount of flow passing through the perforations. These collective studies establish that flow-through slits fundamentally alter the near-wake topology by delaying shear-layer roll-up, extending the vortex formation length, increasing base pressure, and weakening the alternating vortex shedding intensity.

However, despite the considerable progress in understanding single-slit cylindrical configurations, existing investigations have predominantly considered a single slit oriented either parallel or transverse to the freestream direction. The influence of multiple intersecting slits—forming a cross-slit (CS) configuration—and the effect of rotating or sweeping such slits relative to the incoming flow direction remain substantially less explored. The cross-slit geometry introduces additional and more complex flow paths that can interact simultaneously with both the upper and lower separating shear layers, potentially offering enhanced wake control compared to single-slit arrangements. The geometric parameters of a cross-slit cylinder include the SWR, the sweep angle (θ) relative to the freestream, and the number and orientation of intersecting slit passages. The sweep angle provides a particularly powerful control parameter, as it modulates the interaction between the self-injected flow and the shear layers, thereby influencing the separation points, the base pressure distribution, and the vortex formation process. Furthermore, the presence of two intersecting slits creates a central cross-junction that can generate complex three-dimensional-like flow structures even in a nominally two-dimensional configuration, potentially leading to unique wake modification mechanisms not achievable with single-slit geometries.

Recent numerical studies on modified cylinder geometries for drag reduction and wake control have reported encouraging results that motivate further investigation of cross-slit configurations. The numerical investigation of a modified circular cylinder by8 demonstrated that geometric modifications can lead to complex changes in the intermediate wake structure, including the formation of multiple velocity plateaus along the wake centreline, underscoring the sensitivity of the wake to geometric perturbations. P. C. Liu & MacHutchon21, compared the effectiveness of helical strakes using both Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) and detached eddy simulation (DES) approaches, finding that DES provided more accurate predictions of VIV suppression, thereby highlighting the importance of high-fidelity numerical methods in assessing passive flow control effectiveness. The work of Mishra et al.22 on oblique slit orientations demonstrated that the angle of the slit relative to the freestream plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of vortex shedding suppression, suggesting that sweep angle is a key design parameter for perforated cylinders. These findings collectively underscore the potential of cross-slit configurations as a passive hydrodynamic control mechanism, provided that the geometric parameters—slit width ratio, sweep angle, and their interactions—are carefully selected and optimised through systematic parametric investigation. In the context of marine and offshore engineering, the practical motivation for investigating cross-slit cylinders is compelling and multifaceted14,23–40. Offshore risers and marine structural supports are continuously subjected to unsteady current-induced forces over extended periods of service life, where even modest reductions in drag coefficient can translate to significant reductions in structural loading, fatigue damage accumulation, and maintenance costs. The cross-slit cylinder, as a passive device requiring no external power, sensors, or active actuation systems, offers particular advantages in these applications where reliability and maintenance-free operation are paramount. Similarly, in hydrokinetic energy devices and underwater structural components, modifications to the wake topology and shedding frequency can influence not only the structural integrity of individual members but also the performance of downstream rotors in multi-turbine arrays. The potential for wake stabilisation and reduced vortex shedding intensity could contribute to the design of more durable and efficient marine energy harvesting systems.Despite the substantial progress in understanding single-slit cylinders and other passive flow control techniques, a comprehensive and systematic parametric investigation of cross-slit circular cylinders—covering the full range of slit width ratios, sweep angles, and Reynolds numbers—remains absent from the open literature. The interaction between multiple slit passages, their angular orientation, and the resulting modifications to the near-wake vorticity field, reattachment length, Strouhal number characteristics, and base pressure distribution has not been comprehensively addressed. Specifically, the following knowledge gaps are identified: (i the combined effect of varying slit width ratio and sweep angle on the drag and lift characteristics of a circular cylinder has not been systematically quantified across a range of Reynolds numbers,(ii the modification of the wake vorticity topology and reattachment length by cross-slit configurations has not been previously reported; (iii the variation of Strouhal number with sweep angle for different slit width ratios has not been established; and (iv a rigorous numerical validation framework, including mesh-independence studies with Grid Convergence Index estimates and time-step sensitivity analysis, has not been applied to cross-slit cylinder simulations. Moreover, the simultaneous evaluation of drag reduction, wake modification, and vortex shedding frequency changes across a consistent laminar-to-transitional Reynolds number range (Re = 100, 150, 300 has not been previously reported for this geometry.

26,41,42The present study addresses these gaps by conducting a detailed two-dimensional parametric sweep assessment of cross-slit circular cylinders aimed at passive drag reduction and wake modification in low-to-transitional Reynolds number regimes. A systematic numerical investigation is performed at Reynolds numbers Re = 100, 150, and 300, thereby encompassing both the strictly two-dimensional laminar shedding regime (Re = 100, 150) and the early transitional regime (Re = 300), where three-dimensional instabilities begin to emerge but two-dimensional simulations remain representative of the dominant spanwise-averaged flow features. The parametric space includes slit width ratios SWR = 0.10–0.25D and sweep angles θ = 0°–90°, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of geometric influence on wake dynamics. A detailed mesh-independence study is conducted with Grid Convergence Index (GCI) estimates to quantify numerical uncertainty, and a time-step sensitivity analysis is performed to ensure temporal convergence of the unsteady solutions. The baseline circular cylinder results at each Reynolds number are validated against the benchmark data of Posdziech & Grundmann7 and Williamson2 before quantifying the effects of cross-slit configurations. The results are presented in terms of time-averaged drag coefficient, root-mean-square lift coefficient, reattachment length, Strouhal number, pressure distribution, streamline patterns, velocity fields, and wake vorticity contours, providing a comprehensive characterisation of the flow modifications induced by the cross-slit geometry. The findings contribute to the fundamental understanding of passive flow control through perforated bluff bodies, validate the cross-slit cylinder as a vortex wake mitigating device, and provide guidance for the design of marine structures, offshore risers, and hydrokinetic devices where drag minimisation, wake stabilisation, and vortex shedding suppression are critical objectives. Limitations of the present two-dimensional laminar CFD approach are explicitly acknowledged, and the scope for future three-dimensional transitional and turbulent investigations is identified.

Methodology

This research completely established with the help of Direct optimization technique such as Parametric sweep assessment. The goal functions for this direct parametric sweep assessment technique are directly determined by comparative assessment of vortices and shedding frequency in the circular cylinder vs cross-slit type cylinders. Three modelling variables such as, cross-slit width ratio, cross-slit sweep angle 1 and cross-slit sweep angle 2, had been considered for this research at three different Reynolds number were the vortex induced vibration are maximum for a classical cylinder. The third stage is to set up the arbitrary functions of three modelling case studies and the fourth stage is to evaluate the results obtained by laminar to transition flow modelling for 32 Cross-slit models. Laminar flow around cross-slit cylinders is frequently simulated using the direct time dependent Navier–Stokes equations. This approach decomposes flow variables into mean and fluctuating components, allowing for the calculation of time-averaged quantities. In the context of cross-slit cylinder flow, Laminar flow models can capture important features such as flow separation, vortex shedding, and wake dynamics. Although Laminar flow simulations offer a good balance between computational cost and accuracy for engineering applications, they may struggle to capture certain unsteady phenomena41 or fine-scale Seperation structures in the complex flow field around cross-slit cylinders.

A laminar flow simulation over a cross-slit circular cylinder is conducted to analyze the influence of geometric modifications on aerodynamic performance, with particular emphasis on shear layer separation, wake dynamics, and vortex formation. At low Reynolds numbers (Re < 200)43 to moderate (Re = 300)43), the flow remains laminar, providing an ideal regime to investigate the fundamental fluid–structure interactions without the complexity of turbulence modeling. In this study, Reynolds numbers up to 300 were selected to ensure that the flow remained within the laminar or laminar–transitional regime. Previous numerical and experimental investigations9 have shown that although periodic vortex shedding occurs beyond Re ≈ 47, the wake remains two-dimensional and laminar up to Re ≈ 300. Beyond this range, three-dimensional instabilities (Modes A and B) began to appear, marking the onset of the transition to turbulence. Hence, Re = 300 represents the upper bound of the laminar regime suitable for validating the fundamental fluid–structure interactions without invoking turbulence modeling. The introduction of orthogonal slits on the cylinder surface alters the pressure distribution and boundary layer development, potentially delaying or advancing flow separation and modifying the wake symmetry. By solving the unsteady, incompressible Navier–Stokes equations using finite element or finite volume solvers, such as COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2, the simulation yielded high-fidelity insights into the temporal evolution of the velocity fields, vorticity patterns, and surface pressure coefficients. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of passive flow control strategies and the aerodynamic effects of structural discontinuities in bluff-body configurations.

In COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2, the Laminar Flow model interface solves the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations shown in Eq. (1) and (2) for incompressible viscous flows using a robust implicit numerical technique. Spatial discretization was performed using the finite element method (FEM)44 with quadratic elements for velocity and linear elements for pressure, thereby satisfying the Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi (LBB) condition to prevent numerical instabilities, such as pressure oscillations.

For time integration, COMSOL employs the implicit Backward Differentiation Formula (BDF) scheme45 of order 1–5, which approximates temporal derivatives through backward finite differences. The solver automatically adjusted the time step based on the local truncation error, thereby enhancing both the stability and computational efficiency. In cases involving stiff flow behavior, the algorithm adaptively switched between the BDF1 (implicit Euler) and higher-order BDF methods to maintain convergence.

The nonlinear convective terms in the Navier–Stokes equations are handled through the Newton–Raphson iterative linearization method46, with the Jacobian matrix updated at each iteration. A line search damping algorithm was incorporated to improve convergence, particularly for flows with strong nonlinearities. The resulting linearized algebraic equations are solved using either the direct PARDISO47 solver for small to medium problems or the GMRES48 iterative solver with ILU preconditioning for large sparse systems.

The convergence at each time step was controlled using relative and absolute tolerances, which were typically set between 10⁻3 and 10⁻⁶, with a maximum of 25 nonlinear iterations permitted per step. Throughout the simulation, transient quantities, such as the velocity, pressure, vorticity, drag, and lift coefficients, wake structures, streamlines, were recorded at specified time intervals. The implicit BDF formulation ensures unconditional numerical stability and an accurate transient resolution for low-to-moderate Reynolds number laminar flow regimes. Overall, COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 combines implicit BDF-based time integration, Newton–Raphson nonlinear iteration, and finite element spatial discretization to provide a stable, accurate, and efficient numerical framework for solving time-dependent laminar-flow problems.

Mesh independence study

COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2, particularly for complex geometries such as a cross-slit cylinder fluid domain, offers various meshing techniques to discretize the domain into smaller elements, ensuring accurate simulation results. For this specific geometry, a combination of tetrahedral and swept meshes is optimal, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The tetrahedral mesh can effectively capture the intricate details of the cross-slits, whereas the swept mesh can be applied along the diameter of the cylinder for improved computational efficiency, as shown in Fig. 4(a). Mesh refinement is critical in regions with complex flow patterns or strong gradients, such as those close to the slits and cylinder walls. To further improve the accuracy, the element sizes can be automatically adjusted based on the solution gradients using COMSOL’s adaptive mesh refinement tool. Results and validation

Laminar flow simulations of all 32 cross-slit (CS) cylinder models were performed at Reynolds numbers Re = 100, 150, and 300 to assess cross-flow effects, vorticity dynamics, and overall aerodynamic performance. In addition to the baseline comparisons of lift and drag coefficients, the analysis includes (i) a comparative drag-reduction study against previously reported horizontal-slit and vertical-slit configurations, (ii) a Strouhal-number and shedding-frequency assessment via FFT to quantify oscillating-flow suppression, (iii) reattachment-length measurements to characterize near-wake topology, and (iv) a systematic wake-vorticity analysis to link flow structures to force modification. Together, these metrics provide a quantitative basis for evaluating the effectiveness of cross-slit geometries in controlling bluff-body wakes at low transitional Reynolds numbers43,50.

Velocity field variations in cross-slit models

Figures 7 and 8 present velocity-ratio (Vf/U∞) contours for all 32 CS configurations alongside the baseline plain cylinder at Re = 100 (left column) and Re = 150 or 300 (right column). The introduction of cross-slits modifies this behaviour through three interconnected mechanisms. Second, the orthogonal slit orientation disrupts the adverse pressure gradient that typically drives premature separation on smooth cylinders, allowing the flow to remain attached over a larger circumferential portion. Pressure distribution and surface coefficient analysis

Figures 9 and 10 present static pressure contours for the remaining cross-slit configurations at two distinct Reynolds numbers: Fig. 9 shows CS-9 to CS-16 (SWR = 0.15) and CS-17 to CS-24 (SWR = 0.20) at Re = 100 and Re = 150 respectively, while Fig. 10 shows CS-9 to CS-16 (SWR = 0.15) and CS-25 to CS-32 (SWR = 0.25) at Re = 100 and Re = 300 respectively. The baseline cylinder pressure contour in both figures serves as the reference, displaying the characteristic high-pressure stagnation region at the front (θ = 0°), a low-pressure suction zone around the separation points (θ ≈ 80°–100°), and a near-uniform low-pressure wake region behind the cylinder.

In Fig. 9 (Re = 100 and 150), the baseline cylinder at Re = 100 exhibits a well-defined pressure gradient with a distinct low-pressure wake extending several diameters downstream, consistent with canonical laminar cylinder-wake behaviour43, Shin-Jye51. The CS models CS-9 to CS-16 (SWR = 0.15) show systematic modifications of this pressure field as the sweep angle increases. At small sweep angles (CS-9, CS-10), the pressure contours remain qualitatively similar to the baseline but with a slightly reduced wake width and a modest increase in rear-surface pressure, indicating partial flow reattachment. At intermediate sweep angles (CS-11 to CS-14), the pressure field exhibits the most favourable modification: the low-pressure wake is noticeably narrower, the base pressure is elevated, and the pressure recovery on the rear surface is more gradual. This correlates directly with the minimum drag coefficients observed for these configurations in the force-coefficient analysis. At large sweep angles (CS-15, CS-16), the pressure contours reveal a different behaviour: the wake becomes highly irregular with localized low-pressure pockets near the slit exits, and the base pressure does not recover as smoothly, explaining why drag reduction diminishes for these cases despite strong vortex suppression22.

Figure 10 extends this analysis to the higher Reynolds number of Re = 300 and introduces the largest slit width (SWR = 0.25). The baseline cylinder at Re = 300 shows a slightly more elongated wake and a marginally lower base pressure compared to Re = 100, reflecting the increased shear-layer instability at higher Reynolds numbers43. The CS models with SWR = 0.15 at Re = 100 continue to show the same favourable pressure-field modifications observed in Fig. 9, confirming that the intermediate sweep-angle benefit is consistent across the intermediate Reynolds number range. The CS-25 to CS-32 models (SWR = 0.25) at Re = 300 reveal the most aggressive pressure-field modification among all tested configurations. The pressure contours show highly fragmented low-pressure regions in the near wake, with the classical symmetric wake pattern almost entirely replaced by an irregular, slit-dominated pressure field. The slit exits generate localized jet-like pressure signatures that interact strongly with the external shear layers, producing a wake that is significantly narrower but also more disordered than at lower Reynolds numbers. The base pressure is elevated relative to the baseline, but the irregular pressure distribution suggests enhanced turbulent dissipation and possible pressure-fluctuation loading, which may offset some of the mean drag benefit.

The baseline cylinder displays the characteristic pressure distribution for a smooth circular cylinder: a stagnation peak at the front (θ = 0°), followed by a sharp pressure drop corresponding to boundary-layer separation at approximately θ = 80°–85°, and a low-pressure wake region on the rear surface. The horizontal slit model41. shows a modest reduction in the suction peak and a slightly delayed separation point, consistent with the slit providing a passive flow-through path that weakens the adverse pressure gradient. The CS configurations demonstrate a more significant modification of the Cp distribution. All CS models show a reduced magnitude of the suction peak (i.e., less negative Cpmin) compared to the baseline cylinder, indicating that the cross-slits promote earlier pressure recovery. The pressure recovery on the rear surface is more gradual for intermediate sweep angles, which correlates with delayed flow separation and reduced form drag. Furthermore, the asymmetry in Cp between the upper and lower cylinder surfaces becomes more pronounced for certain sweep angles, reflecting the directional bias introduced by the oblique slit orientation. This asymmetry has implications not only for drag but also for mean lift generation, as discussed in the lift analysis section. The CS models consistently outperform both the horizontal and vertical slit references in terms of pressure-field modification, validating the effectiveness of the cross-slit topology as an advanced passive flow-control strategy.

These pressure contour observations validate the drag-reduction claims from a pressure-field perspective. Since form drag on a bluff body is directly proportional to the base-pressure deficit, the elevated rear-surface pressures in the optimized CS configurations provide a direct explanation for the 26–32.5% drag reduction reported earlier. Furthermore, the pressure contours at Re = 300 demonstrate that the cross-slit mechanism remains effective even as the flow approaches the transitional regime, although the nature of the modification shifts from coherent wake narrowing (at Re = 100) to wake fragmentation (at Re = 300). This Reynolds-number dependence highlights the importance of selecting both slit-width ratio and sweep angle appropriately for the target operating conditions in practical applications.

Streamlines topology and vortex characterization

Figures 12 and 13 present streamline patterns and vortex measurements for all 32 CS configurations at Re = 100 and Re = 150 or 300, respectively, compared against the baseline cylinder. The baseline streamline pattern exhibits the classical von Kármán vortex street with two symmetric recirculation zones and alternating vortex shedding, in agreement with canonical laminar cylinder-wake observations (Shin-Jye51). The introduction of cross-slits substantially alters this topology. For configurations with small sweep angles (CS-1 to CS-4, CS-17 to CS-20), the slits behave like symmetric perturbations that strengthen rather than suppress the alternating vortex shedding. In contrast, intermediate sweep angle configurations (CS-5 to CS-10, CS-21 to CS-26) produce a markedly different wake structure. The cross-slit geometry induces a degree of shear-layer alignment that weakens large-scale vortex formation while maintaining a degree of periodicity. The recirculation zones become shorter and more symmetric, and the vortex cores are smaller in magnitude and decay more rapidly downstream. This regime corresponds to the minimum in root-mean-square lift coefficient and represents the most effective balance between flow stabilization and coherent structure preservation. For large sweep angles (CS-11 to CS-16, CS-27 to CS-32), the streamline topology transitions to a more disordered state. The slit-induced jets fragment the primary vortices into multiple smaller eddies, and the wake becomes irregular with no clearly defined periodic shedding. While this regime exhibits the strongest reduction in Coherent vortex-shedding amplitude, it also introduces broadband pressure fluctuations that may be undesirable for certain applications requiring predictable loading23.

These observations are consistent with prior studies on slit-based flow control. 41reported that unsteady flow past slotted circular cylinders exhibits Reynolds-number-dependent transition from steady to periodic wake states, with slit geometry playing a critical role in determining the shedding characteristics. The present CS configurations extend these findings by demonstrating that the orthogonal slit arrangement produces more aggressive wake modification than either horizontal or vertical slits alone.

Shortened reattachment length using cross-slits

The length of the time-averaged recirculation region, defined as the streamwise distance from the rear stagnation point to the reattachment location where the mean streamwise velocity on the centreline recovers to zero ⟨u = 0), was measured for all CS configurations. Figure 18 presents the non-dimensional reattachment length Lr/D as a function of CS model, SWR, and Re. The baseline cylinder exhibits Lr/D ≈ 1.20–1.35 across Re = 100–300, in good agreement with established laminar cylinder-wake benchmarks41, Shin-Jye51.

The optimized CS configurations achieve significant reductions in reattachment length. For intermediate sweep angles with SWR = 0.15–0.20, Lr/D decreases by approximately 18–30% relative to the baseline, with values in the range Lr/D ≈ 0.85–1.00 at Re = 100 and Lr/D ≈ 0.95–1.10 at Re = 150. This shortening of the recirculation bubble is consistent with the observed reduction in base-pressure deficit and reflects earlier shear-layer reattachment induced by the cross-slit geometry. The mechanism involves slit-through flow accelerating the low-momentum fluid in the near wake and promoting earlier mixing between the upper and lower shear layers.

Figures 14, 15, 16, 17 further corroborate this finding by presenting the streamwise velocity ratio u/U∞ along the centreline for all CS models at Re = 100, 150, and 300. The baseline centreline profile (Fig. 17) shows a deep velocity deficit extending to approximately x/D ≈ 5 before gradual recovery. The optimized CS profiles demonstrate significantly faster velocity recovery, with the deficit region terminating as early as x/D ≈ 3–4. This rapid centreline recovery is a direct indicator of improved wake mixing and reduced wake width, both of which contribute to lower form drag. At Re = 300, the wake recovery is slightly slower for all configurations due to enhanced shear-layer instability, but the relative improvement of CS models over the baseline remains consistent. Drag suppression using cross-slits

Figures 19 and 20 present the temporal evolution and comparative summary of drag coefficient (Cd) for all CS configurations. The baseline cylinder yields Cd ≈ 1.25–1.28 at Re = 100–150 in the periodic regime, consistent with established two-dimensional laminar cylinder benchmarks41,51). The intermediate-sweep CS configurations (CS-2, CS-6, CS-10, CS-14, CS-20, CS-28) achieve the most substantial drag reduction, with time-averaged Cd values in the range 0.85–0.95, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 26–32.5% relative to the baseline. The physical mechanism behind drag reduction is twofold. First, the cross-slit geometry modifies the pressure distribution, reducing the base-pressure deficit and thereby lowering form drag, which is the dominant contributor to total drag in laminar bluff-body flows. This is evidenced by the reduced suction peak in Cp distributions (Figure 11) and the more gradual pressure recovery in the wake region (Figures 9–10). Second, the slit-through flow redistributes momentum within the near wake, weakening large-scale vortex structures and reducing the unsteady loading associated with periodic shedding. The drag reduction is most pronounced at Re = 100, where the flow is fully laminar and two-dimensional, and diminishes slightly with increasing Reynolds number due to the growing influence of shear-layer instabilities. At Re = 300, the best CS configurations still achieve approximately 20–28% drag reduction relative to the baseline, demonstrating robustness across the tested regime.

It should be noted that not all CS configurations improve drag. Configurations with very large sweep angles (CS-15, CS-16, CS-31, CS-32) or excessive slit widths (SWR = 0.25) exhibit Cd values equal to or slightly higher than the baseline, indicating that overly aggressive geometric modification can degrade aerodynamic performance. This sensitivity to geometric parameters is consistent with observations from perforated and shrouded cylinder studies, where small changes in hole arrangement or shroud geometry can substantially alter the wake dynamics14,16. The present systematic parametric sweep identifies the optimal parameter window (SWR ≈ 0.15–0.20, θ ≈ 60°–70°) within which drag reduction is maximized while maintaining wake stability.

The relationship between lift reduction and wake topology is evident in the streamline analysis: configurations that produce shorter, more symmetric recirculation bubbles also exhibit the lowest Cl,rms values. This is because the suppression of large-scale alternating vortices reduces the periodic pressure difference between the upper and lower cylinder surfaces. Additionally, the cross-slit configurations with intermediate sweep angles demonstrate an interesting dual characteristic: while Cl,rms is reduced, a modest mean lift coefficient can be generated due to the inherent asymmetry of the slit orientation. Such directional lift has potential implications for flow stabilization and manoeuvrability in bluff-body applications, as noted in prior studies on diamond-shaped cylinders with slits52.

To apply FFT to estimate the shedding frequency, time-series data of a relevant parameter (such as pressure or velocity fluctuations) are collected and transformed into the frequency domain. For the baseline cylinder at Re = 100, the vorticity field exhibits the classical von Kármán vortex street: alternating positive and negative vorticity cores of comparable magnitude are shed from the upper and lower shear layers, forming two staggered rows that persist coherently up to at least x/D ≈ 5. The vortex cores at x/D = 1.0–2.0 display well-defined, high-magnitude vorticity peaks, and the alternating pattern remains clearly visible throughout the measurement domain. This behaviour is consistent with canonical laminar cylinder-wake observations and validates the baseline simulation41, Shin-Jye41,51.

Figure 25 (SWR = 0.10D) demonstrates that narrow cross-slits produce a wake that remains qualitatively similar to the baseline, but with noticeable modifications. At x/D = 1.0–1.5, the vortex cores are slightly weaker than the baseline, and the upper and lower shear layers show early interaction promoted by the slit-through flow. By x/D = 2.0–3.0, the vorticity magnitude begins to decay more rapidly than in the baseline case, indicating enhanced near-wake mixing. However, the alternating pattern persists, and distinct vortex cores remain identifiable up to x/D = 5.0. The vortex-interaction intensity is minimum in the core central region immediately behind the cylinder and increases slightly with downstream distance, consistent with the gradual roll-up of the separated shear layers. This SWR represents the weakest modification of the baseline wake, confirming that small slits act as mild perturbations rather than strong flow-control devices.

Figure 26 (SWR = 0.15D) reveals a marked improvement in wake modification. At x/D = 1.0–2.0, the vortex cores are noticeably smaller in magnitude compared to both the baseline and the SWR = 0.10 case, and the upper and lower shear layers exhibit more rapid interaction and partial merging. The alternating vorticity pattern is still present but significantly weakened, with lower peak vorticity and a more diffuse structure. By x/D = 3.0–4.0, the coherent vortex cores have largely dissipated, and the wake transitions to a state of reduced unsteadiness with no clearly defined alternating pattern. This rapid decay of vorticity is most pronounced for intermediate sweep angles (θ ≈ 60°–70°), which align with the minimum Cl,,rms and maximum drag reduction identified in earlier sections. The SWR = 0.15 configuration thus represents an optimal balance between sufficient flow-through to weaken vortex shedding and adequate structural integrity to maintain wake coherence.

Figure 27 (SWR = 0.20) shows that further increasing the slit width produces even stronger wake modification, but with different characteristics. The vortex cores at x/D = 1.0–1.5 are highly fragmented and substantially weaker than the baseline, and the shear layers interact very rapidly with strong momentum diffusion into the wake centre. The vorticity magnitude at x/D = 2.0–2.5 is already close to the ambient background level, indicating that large-scale coherent structures are almost entirely suppressed in the near wake. However, the wake at larger downstream distances (x/D ≥ 3.0) exhibits a more irregular, broadband structure with no clear periodic pattern. This behaviour is consistent with the Strouhal-number analysis, which showed that large-sweep configurations produce a transition from narrowband to broadband shedding spectra. While this regime achieves the strongest suppression of coherent vortex shedding, the irregular wake may introduce unpredictable loading that is undesirable for certain applications.

Figure 28 (SWR = 0.25) presents the most aggressive cross-slit modification examined. The vorticity field at x/D = 1.0–1.5 shows extremely weak, highly diffuse vortex structures with no clearly identifiable coherent cores. The slit-induced jets dominate the near-wake dynamics, fragmenting the natural shear-layer roll-up into multiple small-scale eddies. By x/D = 2.0, the vorticity has essentially decayed to background levels across the entire wake cross-section. While this represents the most complete suppression of large-scale vortex shedding among all tested configurations, the excessive flow-through also reduces the structural robustness of the cylinder and may lead to increased form drag due to enhanced turbulent dissipation. This confirms the trade-off identified earlier: excessively wide slits degrade performance despite achieving maximum vorticity suppression.

The systematic progression across Figs. 25, 26, 27, 28 demonstrates that the cross-slit wake vorticity can be controlled in a graded manner through the combined adjustment of SWR and sweep angle. The vortex-interaction intensity is minimum in the core central region immediately behind the cylinder and increases at intermediate downstream locations before decaying at larger distances. The wake dynamics are strongest at x/D = 1.0 and minimize around x/D = 4.5–5.0, confirming that the cross-slit configurations achieve their primary wake-modification effect within the first three to four diameters downstream. This spatial scale is directly relevant to practical applications such as offshore risers and underwater supports, where the near-wake region dominates the unsteady loading on adjacent structures.

The vorticity analysis also validates the three-regime classification of cross-slit wake behaviour identified in the streamline topology section. The small-angle regime (SWR = 0.10, θ ≈ 45°–55°) preserves coherent vortex shedding with only mild modification. The intermediate-angle regime (SWR = 0.15–0.20, θ ≈ 60°–70°) achieves optimal suppression of large-scale vortices while maintaining a degree of wake regularity, corresponding to maximum drag reduction and minimum Cl,rms. The large-angle regime (SWR ≥ 0.20, θ ≥ 75°) produces aggressive suppression of coherent structures but introduces broadband wake irregularity, which may be suitable for applications prioritizing VIV mitigation over aerodynamic efficiency.

These vorticity-field observations provide strong evidence for the physical mechanism underlying cross-slit wake control: the orthogonal slit arrangement creates a passive flow-through path that simultaneously (i) weakens the adverse pressure gradient driving separation, (ii) redistributes momentum within the near wake to promote earlier shear-layer interaction and reattachment, and (iii) fragments large-scale coherent vortices into smaller, less energetic structures that decay rapidly downstream. This mechanism is distinct from, and more effective than, conventional horizontal or vertical slit configurations, which modify only one component of the shear-layer interaction at a time20,23,24,41.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the hydrodynamic performance and wake-control characteristics of cross-slit circular cylinders through two-dimensional laminar simulations at Reynolds numbers 100, 150, and 300. A parametric sweep of 32 configurations across slit-width ratios (SWR = 0.10–0.25) and sweep angles (θ = ± 45°– ± 80°) revealed that cross-slit geometries exhibit bifunctional behaviour: simultaneous suppression of alternating vortex shedding and active modification of the near-wake topology. The optimized intermediate-sweep configurations (SWR ≈ 0.15–0.20, θ ≈ 60°–70°) achieved the most balanced performance, with time-averaged drag reduced by 26–32.5% relative to the baseline cylinder, root-mean-square lift coefficient reduced by up to 40%, recirculation length shortened by 18–30%, and Strouhal number reduced from 0.31 to O(10⁻2). Downstream vorticity analysis confirmed that large-scale coherent vortex cores are weakened by 50–80% in the near wake (x/D = 1.0–2.0) and essentially eliminated by x/D = 4.0–5.0, with the most effective wake modification occurring within the first three to four diameters downstream. The cross-slit topology outperforms previously reported horizontal slit41, vertical slit50, and conventional converging/diverging slit configurations in both drag reduction and vortex-shedding suppression, demonstrating the advantage of the orthogonal slit arrangement in simultaneously interacting with both upper and lower shear layers.

While these results validate the cross-slit cylinder as a promising passive flow-control mechanism for bluff-body wake management in the laminar–transitional Reynolds-number range, several limitations must be acknowledged. The present study is restricted to two-dimensional simulations; three-dimensional instabilities (Modes A and B) emerge beyond Re ≈ 300 in real flows, and the performance of cross-slit configurations under fully three-dimensional turbulent conditions remains to be evaluated. Furthermore, no coupled fluid–structure interaction (FSI) model was solved; the observed reductions in fluctuating lift and Strouhal number suggest potential for VIV mitigation, but quantitative assessment of structural response amplitudes requires elastically mounted cylinder simulations with defined mass–damping parameters. The present work also assumes smooth cylinder surfaces and uniform inflow; the effects of surface roughness, biofouling, and inflow turbulence—common in offshore and marine environments—should be investigated in future studies. Finally, experimental validation through wind-tunnel or water-channel testing of physical cross-slit cylinder prototypes is required to confirm the numerical predictions and assess manufacturing feasibility.

Future work should therefore focus on: (i) three-dimensional simulations extending to higher Reynolds numbers (Re = 103–104) to capture transitional and turbulent wake regimes; (ii) fully coupled two-way FSI simulations to quantify the relationship between wake modification and structural vibration response; (iii) parametric studies incorporating surface roughness, biofouling, and turbulent inflow conditions to assess performance robustness in realistic offshore environments; and (iv) experimental validation of optimal cross-slit configurations to bridge the gap between numerical prediction and practical implementation. Despite these limitations, the present results establish the cross-slit cylinder as a conceptually novel and quantitatively effective passive flow-control device for simultaneous drag reduction, wake stabilization, and vortex-shedding suppression in low transitional Reynolds-number flows.
