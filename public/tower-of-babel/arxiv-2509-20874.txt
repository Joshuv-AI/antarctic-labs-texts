Thermal quarks and Polyakov loops in two-color dense QCD
Yugo Kurebayashi, Toru Kojo, Daiki Suenaga *

Abstract
We study confinement and deconfinement in dense two-color QCD by analyzing the dynamics of thermal quarks and gluons. The Polyakov loop is used as a probe of the relevant thermal excitations, distinguishing quark- and hadron-dominated regimes in dense matter. To describe the Polyakov loop, we adopt both lattice-informed phenomenological models and the massive Yang–Mills framework. After calibrating these models at zero density, we investigate in-medium modifications of the Polyakov loops and gluon propagators at finite temperature and density. Diquark gaps control the screening at zero temperature, whereas the screening due to thermal quarks is sensitive to the Polyakov loop. Inclusion of the Polyakov loop helps to reproduce lattice data at low temperature, suggesting that thermal excitations are predominantly hadronic rather than uncorrelated quarks.

Two-color QCD; confinement; Polyakov loop; massive gluons

In recent years, much effort has been devoted to studies of the equation of state (EOS) of dense matter.

By construction, such models implement the essential features of the previous crossover approaches in which hadronic and quark EOS were interpolated phenomenologically

;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
;
Kojo 2025
;
Tajima et al. 2025
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
;
Ivanytskyi 2025
.

As mentioned, simple quark-hadron models
have not directly addressed the nature of confinement-deconfinement transitions; rather this most subtle and intriguing question has been detoured.
In this paper, taking dense two-color QCD as our platform,
we investigate confinement/deconfinement in dense matter by studying thermal properties of dense matter.
We delineate the lattice data for the Polyakov loop and gluon propagators
and confront them with analytic results.
Our central question is whether thermal excitations are dominated by quarks or hadrons,
and answering to this question helps to understand the confinement/deconfinement in dense matter.

In QC
2
D, quarks acquire the color-singlet gap and thus generate neither Debye nor Meissner masses

Kojo and Baym 2014
;
Suenaga and Kojo 2019
;
Kojo and Suenaga 2021
;
Contant and Huber 2020
;
Contant and Huber 2017
.
As a consequence, infrared gluons are protected from screening and remain confining.
With increasing density, the protected phase space gradually shrinks, but it disappears only asymptotically—a scenario known as asymptotic confinement
Rischke et al. 2001
;
Cohen and Sen 2015
.
Quark matter with confining gluons provides a concrete realization of the quarkyonic matter proposed by McLerran and Pisarski
McLerran and Pisarski 2007
.
Although qualitative arguments for asymptotic confinement are plausible, a detailed characterization remains lacking.
Given the significant progress in lattice simulations since the original proposal of asymptotic confinement,
it is timely to revisit this concept in a more quantitative manner.
In this paper, we pursue this goal through a set of analyses based on effective models.

In thermal models of QCD, the Polyakov loop plays a central role
Polyakov 1978
; see Ref.
Fukushima and Skokov 2017
for a review.
The expectation value of the Polyakov loop is related to the free energy of an infinitely heavy quark,
\langle L\rangle\sim e^{-F_{Q}}
.
In pure Yang–Mills (pYM) theory, the flux string from an isolated color charge extends to infinity, implying
F_{Q}\to\infty
and hence
\langle L\rangle\to 0
.
By contrast,
\langle L\rangle
becomes finite when the medium is populated with color fields, allowing the flux string of a heavy quark to dissolve.
In QCD with light quarks, the string can also be terminated by dynamical quark–antiquark creation, so that
\langle L\rangle
may already be finite even before the medium is saturated with color fields.
A key question is how rapidly
\langle L\rangle
grows with temperature, which directly reflects whether the dominant thermal excitations are hadrons or quarks.

In this paper, we examine two models for the Polyakov loop.
The first is the so-called inverse Weiss potential, derived from nonperturbative gluon and ghost propagators
Braun et al. 2010
;
Fukushima and Kashiwa 2013
;
Fukushima and Su 2013
;
Reinosa et al. 2015a
;
Reinosa et al. 2015b
.
In pYM theory, this model successfully reproduces the critical temperature of the confinement–deconfinement transition.
Here we investigate how its predictions are modified once quarks are coupled.
The second model is a phenomenological construction calibrated to lattice simulation data, and it has been widely used in
Polyakov-Nambu-Jona-Lasinio (PNJL) and Polyakov-Quark-Meson (PQM) frameworks.
Although both models describe pYM well, the inclusion of quarks clearly differentiates them:
the inverse Weiss potential becomes excessively modified and deviates from lattice results, whereas the phenomenological model continues to reproduce the data.
This distinction becomes even more pronounced in the regime of dense quark matter.

We further investigate gluon propagators at various values of
\mu_{q}
and
T
,
incorporating Polyakov loop effects that suppress thermal quarks.
Lattice studies of gluon propagators in two-color dense QCD have been reported in Ref.
Boz et al. 2019
,
while model analyses with massive gluon propagators have been carried out both at zero temperature
Suenaga and Kojo 2019

and at finite temperature with quark one-loop corrections
Kojo and Suenaga 2021
.
These analytic computations represent finite-
\mu_{q}
applications of the framework
successfully developed for pYM
Tissier and Wschebor 2011
;
Tissier and Wschebor 2010
;
Reinosa et al. 2017
;
Gracey et al. 2019

and later extended to QCD with dynamical quarks
Peláez et al. 2014
;
Peláez et al. 2017
; see Ref.
Peláez et al. 2021
for a review.
In the present work, we extend these earlier analyses for QC
2
D by explicitly including the Polyakov-loop suppression of thermal quarks,
and we argue that this effect is helpful to explain the lattice data at low temperature.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.

In Sec.

In Sec.

, the model is calibrated at
\mu_{q}=0

In Sec.
5

In Sec.

In Sec.

In Sec.
8

Finally, Sec.
9

2
Polyakov loop potential in pure Yang-Mills

We begin with the gluon sector in the presence of background fields
\mathcal{A}_{0}\equiv\mathcal{A}_{0}^{a}\frac{\,\lambda_{a}\,}{2}
where
\lambda_{a}
are the Pauli matrices for color.
The background field
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is assumed to be constant in space-time,
and we consider amplitudes along the
\lambda_{3}
direction.
The mean-field Polaykov loop is then given by

\displaystyle\Phi=\frac{1}{\,2\,}{\rm tr}[\mathcal{P}e^{\mathrm{i}g\beta\mathcal{A}_{0}^{3}\lambda_{3}/2}]=\cos\frac{\,\beta\mathcal{R}\,}{2}\,,~~~~~~~\mathcal{R}\equiv g\mathcal{A}_{0}^{3}\,.

(1)

In the high temperature limit (
\beta\rightarrow 0
),

\Phi
approaches unity, corresponding to the deconfined phase.
At low temperatures,
\mathcal{A}_{0}
is expected to scale as
\sim T
.
If
\beta\mathcal{R}\to\pi
in this limit, the system is in the confined phase (
\Phi=0
).
Thus, as the temperature increases, the system undergoes a transition from the confined to the deconfined phase.

Below, we examine two typical models for the Polyakov loop potential:
a phenomenological model commonly employed in PNJL and PQM frameworks, and
the inverse Weiss potential, which can be computed from nonperturbative gluon and ghost propagators.

2.1
Phenomenological potential

The phenomenological potentials used in pNJL or pQM models
Fukushima and Skokov 2017

are constructed to reproduce lattice results both with and without quarks.
For example, the two-color potential used by Brauner, Fukushima, and Hidaka (BFH)
Brauner et al. 2009
is

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm BFH}=-bT\big[\,24\Phi^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta a}+\ln(1-\Phi^{2})\,\big]\,,

(2)

where
b=(0.2105\,{\rm GeV})^{3}
and
a=0.8581\,{\rm GeV}
.
The potential vanishes for the confined solution
\Phi\rightarrow 0
.
The first term, proportional to
-Te^{-\beta a}
, can be interpreted as an entropic term favoring the deconfined solution.
The second term,
\ln(1-\Phi^{2})
, favors the confined solution; it diverges as
\Phi\to 1
, preventing the system from reaching complete deconfinement (
\Phi=1
).
The logarithmic form originates from strong-coupling expansions, where the Haar measure yields a logarithmic contribution, and it is related to the presence of ghosts.
The three-color version of this model is known to successfully reproduce lattice data at
N_{\rm c}=3
,
and this situation is also true at
N_{\rm c}=2
, as we see in the following.

2.2
Inverse Weiss potential

To construct the inverse Weiss potential, we compute the effective potential in the presence of the background field
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{3}
.
For the potential derived from the original perturbative propagators, see Refs.
Gross et al. 1981
;
Weiss 1981
.
The Lagrangian for the pYM part is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm pYM}=-\frac{\,1\,}{\,4\,}G_{\mu\nu}^{A}G^{A\mu\nu}\,,~~~~~~~G_{\mu\nu}^{A}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}^{A}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}^{A}+gf_{ABC}A_{\mu}^{B}A_{\nu}^{C}\,,

(3)

where
f_{ABC}
is the structure constant and
g
coupling constant.
To construct the one-particle-irreducible (1PI) effective potential as a function of
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{0}^{3}\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}
,
we first separate the gauge field as
A_{\mu}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\delta_{\mu 0}+a_{\mu}
with
a_{\mu}
being quantum fields.
The
G_{\mu\nu}
can be rewritten as

G_{\mu\nu}^{A}=[\mathcal{D}_{\mu}a_{\nu}]^{A}-[\mathcal{D}_{\nu}a_{\mu}]^{A}+gf_{ABC}a_{\mu}^{B}a_{\nu}^{C}

with the background covariant derivative defined by

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}a_{\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}a_{\nu}-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}\delta_{\mu 0}\big[\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2},\frac{\lambda_{C}}{2}\big]a_{\nu}^{C}
.
Using the Cartan bases
\lambda_{3}/2
and
\lambda_{\pm}/2=(\lambda_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\lambda_{2})/2
, one can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}a_{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}a_{\nu}^{3}\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}+\big(\partial_{\mu}-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}\delta_{\mu 0}\big)a_{\nu}^{-}\frac{\lambda_{+}}{2}+\big(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}\delta_{\mu 0}\big)a_{\nu}^{+}\frac{\lambda_{-}}{2}\,.

(4)

We impose the background gauge fixing condition,
\mathcal{D}^{\mu}a_{\mu}=0
,
that breaks the gauge invariance of
a_{\mu}
but maintain the invariance for
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
.

To model the non-perturbative propagators in the IR, we add a mass term for gluons
to capture the qualitative trend of the Landau gauge propagators in vacuum,
see Ref.
Peláez et al. 2021
for a recent review and Refs.
Gribov 1978
;
Vandersickel and Zwanziger 2012
which give the physical motivation based on the Gribov-Zwanziger framework.
We can either add a term
\propto m_{g}^{2}a_{\mu}^{2}
which is local but not gauge invariant, or a term which is non-local but gauge invariant,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{m_{g}}=-\frac{\,m_{g}^{2}\,}{2}a_{\mu}\bigg(g^{\mu\nu}-\frac{\,\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\,}{\,\partial^{2}\,}\bigg)a_{\nu}\,.

(5)

Including all these elements and using the Faddeev-Popov trick, the massive YM model is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm mYM}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pYM}+\frac{\,(\mathcal{D}^{\mu}a_{\mu})^{2}\,}{\,2\xi\,}+\mathcal{L}_{m_{g}}+\bar{c}\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}c+\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}\,,

(6)

where
c
and
\bar{c}
are ghost and anti-ghost fields.
Taking
\xi\rightarrow 0
, the gluon and ghost propagators are (the gluon propagator
D_{\mu\nu}^{AB}\propto\langle(a^{A}_{\mu})^{*}(-k)a^{B}_{\nu}(k)\rangle
)

\displaystyle[D_{AB}^{\mu\nu}(k)]_{\rm tree}=-\frac{\,\delta_{AB}\,}{\,k_{A}^{2}-m_{g}^{2}\,}P^{\mu\nu}_{k_{A}}\,,~~~~~~~~[D^{AB}_{c}(k)]_{\rm tree}=-\frac{\,\delta^{AB}\,}{\,k_{A}^{2}\,}\,,

(7)

where
\delta_{AB}=1
for
(A,B)=(3,3),(+,+),(-,-)
with the other components zero.
The projection operator for the transverse component is
P^{\mu\nu}_{k}=g^{\mu\nu}-k^{\mu}k^{\nu}/k^{2}
.
The momentum for
(3,-,+)
-components are given by

\displaystyle(k_{3})_{\mu}=k_{\mu}\,,~~~~(k_{\pm})_{\mu}=k_{\mu}\pm\mathcal{R}\delta_{\mu 0}\,.

(8)

With these propagators one can construct the one-loop effective potential.
We define

\displaystyle F(r;m)=T\sum_{n}\int_{\vec{k}}\ln\big[(\omega_{n}+r)^{2}+\vec{k}^{2}+m^{2}\big]\,,

(9)

where
\omega_{n}=2n\pi T(n\in{\bf Z})
is the Matsubara frequency for bosons.
The free energy densities for gluons and ghosts can be written as

\displaystyle\Omega_{g}^{\rm mYM}(T;\mathcal{R})=\frac{1}{\,2\,}\sum_{r=0,\pm\mathcal{R}}\big[3F(r;m_{g})+F(r;0)\big]\,,~~~~~\Omega_{gh}^{\rm mYM}(T;\mathcal{R})=-\sum_{r=0,\pm\mathcal{R}}F(r;0)\,,

(10)

where
T
-independent constants are neglected.
The factor
3
in the first term accounts for the trace over the projectors
P^{\mu\nu}
, while the last term arises from the longitudinal component,
which decouples from radiative corrections and hence remains massless.
Ghosts contribute with the opposite sign to gluons.

Summing the gluon and ghost contributions, the one-loop potential reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{g+gh}^{\rm mYM}(T;\mathcal{R})=\frac{1}{\,2\,}\sum_{r=0,\pm\mathcal{R}}\big[3F(r;m_{g})-F(r;0)\big]\,,

(11)

where the sum over
r
can be explicitly evaluated as

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0,\pm\mathcal{R}}F(r;m)=T\int_{\vec{k}}\bigg(\ln\big[1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{m}}\big]+\ln\big[1-2\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{m}}(2\Phi^{2}-1)+\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta E_{m}}\big]\bigg)\,.

(12)

In standard perturbation theory, ghosts cancel two gluon modes, leaving only the two transverse modes.
In the present massive theory, the massless longitudinal mode is cancelled,
but the cancellation of one mode is incomplete due to the gluon mass.
At low temperatures, massive gluons are suppressed, and the ghost contribution, which favors the confining solution, dominates the potential.

It should be emphasized that the Polyakov loop in the fundamental representation does not suppress thermal excitations in the adjoint representation.
Physically, dynamical gluons screen adjoint charges and form color-singlet states.
Indeed, the logarithmic terms with Boltzmann factors can be rewritten as
{\rm Tr}_{\rm adj.}\big[1-L_{\rm adj}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E}\big]
.
Here
L_{\rm adj}
denotes the Polyakov loop operator in the adjoint representation which
can be expressed in terms of the fundamental representation
L_{\rm fund}

through a group-theoretic relation,
(N_{c}^{2}-1)L_{\rm adj}=N_{c}^{2}L_{\rm fund}\overline{L}_{\rm fund}-1
.
At the mean-field level, a vanishing Polyakov loop for quarks (
\Phi=0
) necessarily implies a non-vanishing Polyakov loop for gluons and ghosts.
As a result, the thermal pressure (
=-\Omega^{\rm mYM}
) is unphysically large at low temperature.

In addition, we examine the temperature and
\Phi
-dependence of the mass
m_{g}
.
Such dependences have been studied in Ref.
Canfora et al. 2015
,
see also Ref.
Mei et al. 2025
.
We adopt the following parametrization

\displaystyle m_{g}^{T}=\sqrt{\big(m_{g}^{\rm vac}\big)^{2}+c_{g}\Phi^{n_{g}}T^{2}}\,,

(13)

which interpolates between the computations at
\Phi=0
and
1
within the Gribov-Zwanziger framework.
We choose
n_{g}=2
and
c_{g}=0
and 4.
At
\Phi=1
, the mass at large
T
eventually scales as
m_{g}\sim g_{s}T
,
while for
\Phi=0
the mass is saturated,
m_{g}\sim{\rm const}
for
\Phi=0
.
For the vacuum mass we choose
m_{g}^{\rm vac}=800
MeV
which is consistent with the lattice studies for the Landau gauge propagators in pYM
Reinosa et al. 2015a
;
Reinosa et al. 2015b
.

3
Including quarks: PNJL model

3.1
Quasi-quark excitations

We now include quarks within a quasi-particle description.
To be concrete we use the PNJL model.
As a first step, we examine the
\mu_{q}=0
case to calibrate our models against lattice data.
In particular, the lattice data were often obtained for a nonzero diquark source
with which quarks form color-singlet diquark condensates and acquire a gap even at
\mu_{q}=0
.
Without the diquark source term, the diquark condensate is formed
only for
\mu_{B}\geq m_{\pi}
(equivalently
\mu_{q}\geq m_{\pi}/2
),
as predicted by the chiral perturbation theory
Son and Stephanov 2001a
;
Son and Stephanov 2001b
.

For later convenience, from the very beginning we derive the general expression of quark spectra at nonzero
\mu_{q}
and
\Delta
.
In the presence of a temporal background field, the kinetic term reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{kin,q}=\bar{q}\big[\mathrm{i}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$\partial$\crcr}}}+g{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$a$\crcr}}}+\mathcal{R}T_{3}\gamma_{0}+\mathrm{i}\mu\gamma_{0}-M_{q}\big]q-q^{T}{\bf\Delta}q\,,

with
T_{3}=\lambda_{3}/2
and
{\bf\Delta}=\gamma_{5}\tau_{2}\lambda_{2}\Delta
, where
\tau_{2}
is the Pauli matrix in flavor space.
Employing the Nambu-Gor’kov formalism, we define
\psi=q
and its charge-conjugated field as
\psi_{C}(p)=\lambda_{2}\tau_{2}C\bar{q}^{T}(-p)
.
Then,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(p){\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$p$\crcr}}}\psi(p)=\bar{\psi}_{C}(p){\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$p$\crcr}}}\psi_{C}(p)\,,~~~~~\bar{\psi}(p)\gamma_{0}\mathcal{A}^{0}T_{3}\psi(p)=\bar{\psi}_{C}(p)\gamma_{0}\mathcal{A}^{0}T_{3}\psi_{C}(p)\,,

where we used
\lambda_{2}(-T_{a}^{T})\lambda_{2}=T_{a}
.
The momentum space representation of the Lagrangian now reads

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{kin,q}=\bar{\Psi}(p)\begin{bmatrix}~{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$p$\crcr}}}+\big(\mathcal{R}T_{3}+\mathrm{i}\mu\big)\gamma_{0}-M_{q}~&~\Delta\gamma_{5}~\\
~-\Delta^{*}\gamma_{5}~&~{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$p$\crcr}}}+\big(\mathcal{R}T_{3}-\mathrm{i}\mu\big)\gamma_{0}-M_{q}~\end{bmatrix}\Psi(p)\,,

(24)

where
\Psi(p)=(\psi(p),\psi_{C}(p))^{T}/\sqrt{2}
.

We note that
p_{0}
and
\mathcal{R}T_{3}
always appear in the combination
p_{0}+\mathcal{R}T_{3}
,
while the chemical potential
\mu
enters the quasiparticle poles as

\xi_{\rm p,a}(\vec{p})=\sqrt{(E_{q}(\vec{p})\mp\mu)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
.
In the thermodynamic potential, the shift
p_{0}\rightarrow p_{0}+\mathcal{R}T_{3}
modifies
the standard entropic logarithm according to

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{c}\ln\big[1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\xi_{k}^{i}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}T_{3})}\big]=\ln\big[1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\xi_{k}^{i}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}/2)}\big]+\ln\big[1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\xi_{k}^{i}-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}/2)}\big]\,,

(25)

where the trace is taken over color indices.
Combining these logarithms, the thermal quark contribution to the free energy (or equivalently, minus the pressure) becomes

\displaystyle\Omega_{kin,q}^{T}=-\mathcal{P}^{T}_{kin,q}=-4T\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\ln\big(1+2\Phi\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}+\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}\big)\,.

(26)

The pressure at
\Phi=0
(confined) or
\Phi=1
(deconfined) are

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{kin,q}^{T}\rightarrow\left\{\begin{matrix}&4T\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\ln\big(1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta N_{\rm c}\xi_{k}^{i}}\big)&~~(\Phi\rightarrow 0)\\
&4N_{\rm c}T\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\ln\big(1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}\big)&~~(\Phi\rightarrow 1)\end{matrix}\right.

where we write
N_{\rm c}=2
.
The thermal quark pressure is larger at least by a factor
N_{\rm c}
in the deconfined phase than in the confined phase.
The difference would be even greater in the high temperature limit;
for
\Phi=0
with
\xi_{k}\sim|\vec{k}|
, we rescale the variable as
N_{\rm c}|\vec{k}|=|\vec{k}^{\prime}|
to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{kin,q}^{T\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi=0)~\sim~N_{\rm c}^{-4}\mathcal{P}_{kin,q}^{T\rightarrow\infty}(\Phi=1)\,.

(27)

In order to examine the impact of the Polyakov loop, in Fig.

Thermal hadronic contributions rise rapidly around

3.2
Evolution of condensates

To determine the evolution of the in-medium gaps,
we include both the zero-point energy and the potential terms associated with the gaps and the Polyakov loop.
For this purpose, we adopt the pNJL-type thermodynamic potential

\displaystyle\Omega_{q}=-4\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\big[~\xi_{k}^{i}+T\ln\big(1+2\Phi\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}+\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}\big)~\big]+\frac{\,(M_{q}-m_{q})^{2}+(\Delta-\Delta_{\rm ext})^{2}\,}{\,2G\,}\,,

(28)

where
G
denotes the NJL coupling constant,
m_{q}
the current quark mass, and
\Delta_{\rm ext}
an external diquark source term.
The current quark mass
m_{q}
is adjusted to reproduce the pion mass employed in the lattice simulations.
In the lattice setup, the diquark source term
\Delta_{\rm ext}
is introduced to stabilize the numerical calculations;
we take its value accordingly to ensure consistency with the lattice data.

Including the pYM term, the gap equations for
\Phi
,
M_{q}
, and
\Delta
are
(
\Omega_{\rm QC_{2}D}=\Omega_{\rm pYM}+\Omega_{q}
)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\partial\Omega_{\rm QC_{2}D}\,}{\,\partial\Phi\,}

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\partial\Omega_{{\rm pYM}}\,}{\,\partial\Phi\,}-4T\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\bigg[\frac{\,\Theta(\Lambda_{\rm NJL}-|\vec{k}|)\,}{\,\cosh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})+\Phi\,}+\frac{\,\Theta(|\vec{k}|-\Lambda_{\rm NJL})\,}{\,\cosh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i}\big|_{\eta=\eta_{\rm ext}})+\Phi\,}\bigg]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\,\partial\Omega_{\rm QC_{2}D}\,}{\,\partial\eta\,}

\displaystyle=-4\sum_{i={\rm p,a}}\int_{\vec{k}}\Theta(\Lambda_{\rm NJL}-|\vec{k}|)\frac{\,\partial\xi_{k}^{i}\,}{\,\partial\eta\,}\frac{\,\sinh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})\,}{\,\cosh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})+\Phi\,}+\frac{\,\eta-\eta_{\rm ext}\,}{\,2G\,}\,,

(29)

where
\eta=M_{q}
or
\Delta
.
In the low temperature limit (
\beta\rightarrow+\infty
),
the thermal factors in the integrand depend on
\Phi
very weakly;
the thermal factor behaves as

1/(\cosh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})+\Phi)\simeq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{k}^{i}}
for the
\Phi

and
\sinh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})/(\cosh(\beta\xi_{k}^{i})+\Phi)\simeq 1
for the
M_{q}
and
\Delta
parts.

Here we have assumed that the gaps damp at high energy and parametrize such damping scale as
\Lambda_{\rm NJL}
.
This scale appears automatically if we use momentum-dependent interactions, e.g., the
\sim 1/p^{2}
type.
In the NJL model the contact interactions yield only momentum-independent gaps.
Thus we have to impose such a scale
\Lambda_{\rm NJL}
by hand so that
\partial\xi_{k}^{i}/\partial\eta
is nonzero only for
|\vec{k}|\leq\Lambda_{\rm NJL}
.
This results in the standard NJL gap equations.
Meanwhile, the integral in the equation for
\Phi
should include both thermal quarks with and without the effective masses,
so we should keep the integral for
|\vec{k}|\geq\Lambda_{\rm NJL}
.

6
Gluon propagators at one-loop with Polyakov loops

We focus on medium corrections to the static gluon propagators,
D(k)
, at zero frequency (
k_{0}=0
).
Processes with
k_{0}\ll\mu_{q}
keep quarks near the Fermi surface and hence are most relevant for non-perturbative effects.
At the same time, the momentum transfer
|\vec{k}|
can be large if quarks remain close to the Fermi surface before and after scattering.
Therefore, we concentrate on small
k_{0}
while considering a wide range of
|\vec{k}|
to
\sim 2\mu_{q}
.

To reproduce the lattice results of Ref.
Boz et al. 2019
, it is crucial to suppress quark excitations in the infrared region.
Gapless quarks would otherwise over-screen the electric gluons
Contant and Huber 2020
.
Including a diquark gap of
\Delta\sim 200
MeV reduces the electric screening, improving agreement with lattice data
Suenaga and Kojo 2019
;
Kojo and Suenaga 2021
.
In contrast, in the magnetic sector, static screening remains absent for any value of
\Delta
, as in the normal phase.
The magnetic screening is usually regarded as non-perturbative effects.

At finite temperatures, thermally excited particles—either quarks or hadrons—respond to external momenta as gapless modes
Kojo and Suenaga 2021
;
Suenaga and Kojo 2021
.
If the thermal excitations are quark-dominated, they induce static electric screening as in the normal phase, while the static magnetic modes remain unscreened.
In contrast, if the thermal excitations are confined, the electric screening is expected to be weaker, since color-singlet objects of typical size
R_{\rm size}
cannot respond effectively to external fields at wavelengths much longer than their size (
k\ll R_{\rm size}^{-1}
).

We account for the suppression of thermal quarks by including the Polyakov loops.
The gluon self-energy is described by the polarization function

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{AB}(k_{A})\equiv(\mathrm{i}g_{s})^{2}\int_{x,y}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k(x-y)}\big\langle\big(\bar{q}\gamma_{\mu}\frac{\,\lambda_{A}\,}{2}q\big)(x)\big(\bar{q}\gamma_{\nu}\frac{\,\lambda_{B}\,}{2}q\big)(y)\big\rangle\,.

(36)

where
k_{A}
was defined in Eq. (
8
).
Explicit evaluation shows that
\Pi^{AB}
depends on
k_{A}
and is diagonal in color space,
with nonzero components only for
(A,B)=(3,3),(+,+),(-,-)
. All other combinations vanish.

As usual, we decompose the gluon self-energy into electric and magnetic components.
In the presence of the background field, the momenta entering the projection tensors are
k_{A}
,
which ensures the Ward-Takahashi identity,
k^{\mu}_{A}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{AB}=0
, is satisfied.
The decomposition then reads

\displaystyle\Pi^{AB}_{\mu\nu}(k_{A})=\delta^{AB}P^{E}_{\mu\nu}(k_{A})\Pi_{E}(k_{A})+\delta^{AB}P^{M}_{\mu\nu}(k_{A})\Pi_{M}(k_{A})

(37)

where
k_{A}
was defined in Eq. (
8
).

6.1
The vacuum subtraction

The polarization function is formally UV divergent.
We extract the UV divergent vacuum contribution and combine it with the counter terms for the renormalization,

\displaystyle\big[\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{AB}(k_{A})\big]^{R}\equiv\big[\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{AB}(k_{A})\big]+\delta Z_{g}k_{A}^{2}P_{\mu\nu}^{k_{A}}

\displaystyle=\delta^{AB}P_{\mu\nu}^{k_{A}}\big[\Pi_{\rm vac}(k_{A})+\delta Z_{g}k_{A}^{2}\big]+P^{E}_{\mu\nu}(k_{A})\delta\Pi_{E}^{AB}(k_{A})+P^{M}_{\mu\nu}(k_{A})\delta\Pi_{M}^{AB}(k_{A})\,,

(38)

where the renormalized vacuum part is (
K^{2}\equiv-(k_{A})^{2}
,
\alpha_{s}=g_{s}^{2}/4\pi
)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm vac}^{R}(k_{A})=\Pi_{\rm vac}(k_{A})+\delta Z_{g}k_{A}^{2}=-K^{2}\frac{\,2\alpha_{s}^{q}\,}{\,\pi\,}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x(1-x)\ln\frac{\,\big(M_{q}^{\rm vac}\big)^{2}+x(1-x)K^{2}\,}{\,\big(M_{q}^{\rm vac}\big)^{2}+x(1-x)\mu_{R}^{2}\,}\,,

(39)

with
\mu_{R}
being the renormalization scale.
The medium part is defined by subtracting the vacuum part,

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{E,M}\equiv\Pi_{E,M}-\Pi_{\rm vac}\,,

(40)

where the subscript
AB
is omitted for the moment.

This subtracted function is UV convergent, but its finite part depends on regularization methods.
In particular, if we integrate
q_{0}
first to pick up poles and then integrate
\vec{q}
,
there emerges UV finite terms which violate the conservation law
k_{A}^{\mu}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{AB}(k_{A})=0
,
when the quark propagators in
\Pi_{E,M}
and
\Pi_{\rm vac}
contain, e.g., different
M_{q}
or
\Delta

Kojo and Baym 2014
.

One way to restore the conservation law is to introduce counterterms that cancel regularization artifacts
Kojo and Baym 2014
.
Another approach is to split the vacuum subtraction into two steps
Kojo and Suenaga 2021
:

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{E,M}=\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}+\delta_{\mu_{q}}\Pi_{E,M}=\big[\Pi_{\rm vac}^{M_{q}\rightarrow\tilde{M}_{q}}-\Pi_{\rm vac}\big]_{\rm dim\,reg}+\big[\Pi_{E,M}-\Pi_{\rm vac}^{M_{q}\rightarrow\tilde{M}_{q}}\big]_{\rm 3d\,reg}\,.

(41)

Here, the
\delta_{\mu_{q}}\Pi_{E,M}
, which is the difference between
\Pi_{E,M}
and
\Pi_{\rm vac}
evaluated with the same gap parameters but different
\mu_{q}
,
has no regularization artifacts, and hence one can evaluate it in the standard residue method with a three dimensional cutoff.
Meanwhile, each term in
\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}
can be evaluated via dimensional regularization, and hence
\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}
can be made artifact-free.
The parameter
\tilde{M}_{q}(\mu_{q},T)\equiv\sqrt{M_{q}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
, determined at a given
\mu_{q}
,
is uniquely fixed to ensure that the conservation law is maintained.
Explicitly,
\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}
reads

\displaystyle\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}=-K^{2}\frac{\,2\alpha_{s}^{q}\,}{\,\pi\,}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x(1-x)\ln\frac{\,\tilde{M}_{q}^{2}+x(1-x)K^{2}\,}{\,\big(M_{q}^{\rm vac}\big)^{2}+x(1-x)K^{2}\,}\,.

(42)

If the gap parameters in vacuum and medium remain the same, i.e.,
\tilde{M}_{q}=M_{q}^{\rm vac}
,
then
\delta_{S}\Pi_{E,M}
vanishes by construction.

6.2
The medium corrections

Physical medium effects are largely reflected by the
\delta_{\mu_{q}}\Pi_{E,M}
.
Decomposing the normal and anomalous quark propagators into the particle and antiparticle components,
and then performing the residue integral for
q_{0}
, we find (before the vacuum subtraction)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\Pi_{E,M}^{AB}(k_{A})=\frac{\,g_{s}^{2}\,}{2}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}=p,a}\int_{\vec{q}}K_{E,M}^{ss^{\prime}}\big[C_{E,M}^{ss^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}^{ss^{\prime}}_{AB}+\tilde{C}_{E,M}^{ss^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{ss^{\prime}}_{AB}\big](\vec{q}_{+},\vec{q}_{-};k_{0})\,,

(43)

where
\vec{q}_{\pm}=\vec{q}\pm\vec{k}/2
.
We call
K_{E,M}^{ss^{\prime}}
,
C_{E,M}^{ss^{\prime}}
, and
\mathcal{G}^{ss^{\prime}}_{AB},\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{ss^{\prime}}_{AB}

the kinematic, coherence, and propagator factors.
The propagator
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
is non-vanishing only at nonzero temperature.

6.2.1
Kinematic factors

The explicit forms of the kinematic factors are

\displaystyle{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}={\cal K}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{a}}_{E}=-\frac{k^{2}}{\vec{k}^{2}}\bigg(1+\frac{\,\vec{q}^{2}-\vec{k}^{2}/4+M_{q}^{2}\,}{E_{q_{+}}E_{q_{-}}}\bigg)\ ,

\displaystyle{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E}={\cal K}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{p}}_{E}=-\frac{k^{2}}{\vec{k}^{2}}\bigg(1-\frac{\,\vec{q}^{2}-\vec{k}^{2}/4+M_{q}^{2}\,}{E_{q_{+}}E_{q_{-}}}\bigg)\ ,

\displaystyle{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{M}={\cal K}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{a}}_{M}=-1+\frac{\,(|\vec{q}|\cos\theta)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2}/4+M_{q}^{2}\,}{E_{q_{+}}E_{q_{-}}}\ ,

\displaystyle{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{M}={\cal K}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{p}}_{M}=-1-\frac{\,(|\vec{q}|\cos\theta)^{2}-\vec{k}^{2}/4+M_{q}^{2}\,}{E_{q_{+}}E_{q_{-}}}\ ,

(44)

where
\theta
in
\cos\theta
is the angle between
\vec{q}
and
\vec{k}
.
These kinematic factors depend on
M_{q}
and the spatial momenta, and they are common across different states of matter.
It is useful to note that, at low momenta, the electric components behave as

{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}\rightarrow 2+O(\vec{k}^{2})

and
{\cal K}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E}\rightarrow O(\vec{k}^{2})
.
This indicates that in-medium corrections to the electric part are dominated by particle–hole excitations.

6.2.2
Coherence factors

The coherence factors, which characterize the diquark condensed phase, are

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}-\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}-\mu_{q})\pm|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{-}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}+\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}+\mu_{q})\pm|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{-}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}-\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}+\mu_{q})\mp|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{-}}\right)\,,

(45)

and the factor relevant only at finite temperature (since
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
is nonzero only at finite temperature, see below),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}-\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}-\mu_{q})\pm|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{-}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}+\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}+\mu_{q})\pm|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{-}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\,(E_{q_{+}}-\mu_{q})(E_{q_{-}}+\mu_{q})\mp|\Delta|^{2}\,}{\xi_{\mathrm{p}}^{+}\xi_{\mathrm{a}}^{-}}\right)\,,

(46)

where
{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}(q_{+},q_{-})={\cal C}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{p}}_{E,M}(q_{-},q_{+})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}}_{E,M}(q_{+},q_{-})=\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{p}}_{E,M}(q_{-},q_{+})
.
It is important to note that, at zero temperature, the electric component is vanishing in the small momentum limit,
{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}\sim O(\vec{q}\cdot\vec{k})
.
Later, we see that the propagator factor
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}
remains finite for
\vec{k}\rightarrow 0

so that the product
{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}
vanishes,
meaning that the electric screening is absent in the diquark condensed phase.
This situation differs from the normal phase
where the propagator exhibits a singularity
\vec{k}
as
\sim 1/\vec{q}\cdot\vec{k}
for small
|\vec{k}|
and hence makes the product

{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{E}
finite.
In this case the Debye mass emerges, cutting off gluons in the IR.
In contrast, the particle-hole contributions to the magnetic component is finite,
{\cal C}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}_{M}\sim\Delta^{2}/\xi_{p}^{2}
.

6.2.3
Propagator factors

Finally, we examine the propagator factors where the effects of the Polyakov loop enter.
First, we generalize the Fermi-Dirac distribution for quarks as

\displaystyle n_{F}(x\pm\mathrm{i}Q/2)=\frac{\,1+\Phi\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\,}{\,\mathrm{e}^{2\beta x}+2\Phi\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}+1\,}\pm\mathrm{i}\frac{\,\tilde{\Phi}\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\,}{\,\mathrm{e}^{2\beta x}+2\Phi\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}+1\,}\equiv L_{F}^{\bf\Phi}(x)\pm\mathrm{i}\tilde{L}_{F}^{\bf\Phi}(x)\,,

(47)

where
{\bf\Phi}=\Phi+\mathrm{i}\tilde{\Phi}=\cos(\beta\mathcal{R}/2)+\mathrm{i}\sin(\beta\mathcal{R}/2)
.

For deconfined quarks at
(\Phi,\tilde{\Phi})=(1,0)
,
this function is reduced to the usual Fermi-Dirac distribution,
L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=1}(x)\rightarrow n_{F}(x)
and
\tilde{L}^{{\bf\Phi}=1}_{F}(x)\rightarrow 0
.
The low temperature behavior of the real part is
L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=1}(\xi_{s})\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{s}}
.

For confined quarks at
(\Phi,\tilde{\Phi})=(0,1)
, we find
L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=\mathrm{i}}(x)\rightarrow n_{F}(2x)
and
\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=\mathrm{i}}(x)\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}n_{F}(2x)
.
At low temperatures, their behaviors are
L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=\mathrm{i}}(\xi_{s})\sim\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\xi_{s}}
and
\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}=\mathrm{i}}(\xi_{s})\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{s}}
, respectively.
For low energy excitations,
the real part exhibits a stronger suppression,
\sim\mathrm{e}^{-2\beta\Delta}
, which can be interpreted as the Boltzmann factor for hadronic excitations.
Meanwhile, the imaginary part is suppressed as
\sim e^{-\beta\Delta}
, reflecting quark excitations decaying into the colored background.

Using these generalized distribution functions, the
A=B=3
components of the propagator factors are

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{A=B=3}_{ss^{\prime}}(q_{+},q_{-};{\bf\Phi})

\displaystyle=\big[1-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}+\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}+\frac{\,1\,}{\,-k_{0}+\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{A=B=3}_{ss^{\prime}}(q_{+},q_{-};{\bf\Phi})

\displaystyle=\big[L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}+\frac{\,1\,}{\,-k_{0}-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)\,,

(48)

which contain only the real part of the thermal distribution functions.
On the other hand, the
(A,B)=(\mp,\mp)
components are

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\mp\mp}_{ss^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\big[1-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}+\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}+\frac{\,1\,}{\,-(k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R})+\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)

\displaystyle~~~~\pm\mathrm{i}\big[\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})+\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}-\frac{\,1\,}{\,-(k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R})-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mp\mp}_{ss^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\big[L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})-L_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}+\frac{\,1\,}{\,-(k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R})-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)

\displaystyle~~~~\pm\mathrm{i}\big[\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s}^{-})+\tilde{L}_{F}^{{\bf\Phi}}(\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+})\big]\bigg(\frac{\,1\,}{\,k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}-\frac{\,1\,}{\,-(k_{0}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R})-\xi_{s}^{-}+\xi_{s^{\prime}}^{+}\,}\bigg)\,,

(49)

which contain the real and imaginary parts of the thermal distribution functions.
It is important to note that
\mathcal{G}^{AB}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{AB}
are functions of
k_{A}
;
this verifies Eq. (
36
) where
we wrote
\Pi^{AB}
as functions of
k_{A}
.
We also note that the imaginary parts of
\mathcal{G}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
are common,

{\rm Im}\mathcal{G}^{\mp\mp}_{ss^{\prime}}={\rm Im}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mp\mp}_{ss^{\prime}}
.
These functions vanish at
(k_{A})_{0}=0
, and we will therefore not discuss them further.

6.2.4
The full expression after adding the pYM type self-energy

Below, we examine the electric and magnetic propagators at
K^{2}=-k_{A}^{2}
.
It is known that the vacuum propagators in the pYM theories

Here,
\mu_{R}
is the renormalization scale for which we take

GeV,
and

which generally differs from the quark-gluon coupling
\alpha_{s}^{q}

In the following, we begin with the vacuum propagators fitted to lattice data and

The propagators are expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\mu\nu}^{AB}(k_{A})=D_{E}^{AB}P_{\mu\nu}^{E}+D_{M}^{AB}P_{\mu\nu}^{M}\,,~~~~~~~~~~D_{E,M}^{AB}(k_{A})=\frac{\,\delta^{AB}\,}{\,K^{2}+m_{g}^{2}+\Pi_{\rm vac}^{G}+\Pi_{\rm vac}^{R}+\delta\Pi_{E,M}\,}\,,

(50)

where
\Pi_{\rm vac}^{R}
and

are defined in Eqs. (

leads to a serious disagreement between the analytic and lattice results.
Furthermore, extrapolating the one-loop results with fixed
\alpha_{s}^{q}
to very high density eventually causes the chromo-magnetic instability

;
Huang and Shovkovy 2004b
, which has been extensively discussed in the context of gapless color superconductivity
Shovkovy and Huang 2003
;
Huang and Shovkovy 2003
.
A possible way to avoid such an instability is to invoke a “gluon saturation” mechanism,
as in the Gribov–Zwanziger model
Gribov 1978
;
Vandersickel and Zwanziger 2012
,
which would drive
m_{g}
to increase with
\mu_{q}
before the paramagnetic enhancement causes the instability.
This conceptual issue, however, lies beyond the scope of the present paper.

Now we turn to the case of
T=118
MeV.
Our analytic results show substantially weaker electric screening compared to the lattice results.
This discrepancy may be understood as follows.
As mentioned earlier, the
\Delta

Consequently, while
T=118

T_{s}

T_{s}
by about 20–50 MeV.

9
Discussion and summary

In this paper, we delineate the role of thermal quarks in dense QC
2
D and their impact on gluon dynamics,
focusing specifically on the Polyakov loop and gluon propagators.

Our initial approach was to start from the massive gluon propagators constrained by lattice data and then use them to compute the Polyakov loop potential.
However, the resulting potential for the pYM turns out to be too flat;
the inclusion of quark contributions drastically deforms the structure of the Polyakov loop potential,
leading to an overly rapid deconfinement that disagrees with lattice QCD results.
We therefore suspect that the gluons responsible for confinement and those appearing in the propagators are somewhat distinct:
the latter corresponds to excitations above the confining vacuum, while the gluons forming the Polyakov loop potential appear to be more robust against quark-induced perturbations.

To investigate the nature of thermal excitations in dense matter, we employ phenomenological Polyakov loop potentials used in the literature and examine their correlation with thermal quarks, in a manner similar to the PNJL and PQM models.
We tune our setup to be consistent with lattice results;
in particular, we adjust our model parameters to reproduce a pion mass of
m_{\pi}\sim 640
MeV
and introduce a finite diquark source term,
\Delta_{\rm ex}=45
MeV, which is commonly used to stabilize lattice simulations.
The renormalization of the Polyakov loop is also taken into account.

After calibrating our model to reproduce lattice results at
\mu_{q}=0
, we study the Polyakov loop at finite
\mu_{q}
and find reasonable agreement with the lattice data.
Achieving this agreement requires a diquark gap of
\Delta\sim 200
–
300
MeV and a finite
\Delta_{\rm ex}
.
If
\Delta
is artificially set to zero, the Polyakov loop reaches the deconfined solution too rapidly due to the large abundance of gapless quarks.

We next delineate in-medium gluon propagators in the Landau-DeWitt gauge.
At the one-loop level, it is known that at zero temperature the color-singlet diquark gap protects gluons from screening by quarks.
Thermal quarks overcoming the gap energy contribute to the screening as in the normal phase;
hence, the thermal modifications of the gluon mass can be used to study the nature of thermal excitations and how confinement affects them.

The Polyakov loop effects quenches the colored quarks and suppresses their contributions to the screening.
These confining effects are important at the lowest temperature measured on the lattice,
T\sim 44
MeV,
and should be taken into account when confronting theoretical computations with the lattice data.
The thermal evolution of the screening masses at various
\mu_{q}
appears reasonably consistent with the lattice results for the range of our parameters,

\alpha_{s}^{q}=0.5
–2.0 and
m_{g}=400
–500 MeV.

Overall, our analyses with the current lattice data suggest that thermal colored excitations in dense two-color QCD matter are strongly suppressed.
In the
\Phi=1
case, the electric screening grows too rapidly compared to lattice results,
unless
\Delta
is considerably larger than
\sim 200
MeV (this is unlikely however),
allowing thermal quarks to be effectively neglected even without Polyakov loop suppression.
Including the Polyakov loop increases the minimum excitation from
\sim\Delta
to
\sim 2\Delta
,
corresponding to the energy scale of baryonic (diquark) excitations.

To draw more definitive conclusions about confinement and deconfinement in dense QC
2
D matter, however,
lattice data with smaller statistical fluctuations and better-controlled finite-volume and lattice-spacing effects are called for.
In the theoretical side, the analytic computations should be improved by including higher-order loop corrections and/or genuinely non-perturbative effects.
Much remains to be done.
