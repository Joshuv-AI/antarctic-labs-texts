Methylation regulatory networks in osteoarthritis: mechanisms and targeted therapies
Authors: Siman Tian, Xiaoqian Men, Yi Zheng, Yu Liu, Zhikuan Li, Chien-Wei Lee, Shangchun Guo, Hongtao Xu, Jiangtao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA), the most common degenerative joint disease worldwide, currently lacks effective disease-modifying therapies that can halt its progression. This therapeutic challenge stems from the complexity of multiple pathological mechanisms, including chondrocyte metabolic imbalance, inflammatory responses, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. In recent years, epigenetics, particularly reversible methylation modifications, has provided a new perspective for understanding OA.Main bodyThis review systematically analyzes the multi-dimensional methylation regulatory network in OA from a “pan-methylation” perspective. DNA methylation regulates the transcriptional activity of key genes (e.g., SOX9, MMP13) through the DNMTs/TETs enzyme system. RNA epitranscriptomic modifications (e.g., m6A, m5C, m7G methylation) precisely controls mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and splicing processes via its “Writers-Readers-Erasers” machinery (METTL3, FTO, YTHDFs, etc.), influencing autophagy, inflammation, and metabolic balance. Histone methylation (e.g., H3K27me3, H3K79me2) directly regulates catabolic gene expression by altering chromatin states. These multi-layered methylation networks collectively form a complex epigenetic regulatory system in OA.ConclusionBased on these findings, targeting specific methyltransferases has shown great therapeutic potential in preclinical studies. This review not only deepens the understanding of OA pathogenesis but also provides a theoretical basis and innovative strategies for developing disease-modifying therapies targeting the methylation network. Future research should focus on joint-specific drug delivery systems and epigenetic precision therapy to promote a fundamental shift in OA treatment paradigms.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Methylation, Epigenetics, Regulatory network, Targeted therapy

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most disabling degenerative joint disease worldwide, with a steadily increasing prevalence that affects over 500 million people globally. It places a enormous burden on society and poses a major challenge to public health [1–3]. It is characterized by chronic pain and whole-joint pathology, including articular cartilage damage, synovitis, subchondral bone remodeling, and osteophyte formation [4]. However, clinical treatments are still limited to symptomatic interventions such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain relief and joint replacement, lacking effective methods to halt disease progression [5, 6]. This therapeutic dilemma stems from the complexity of OA’s pathological mechanisms: it is essentially a cascade of joint destruction driven by an imbalance in chondrocyte metabolism, synovial inflammatory responses, degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM), and abnormal remodeling of subchondral bone [7].

In recent years, epigenetics has gradually emerged as a key research field, providing new perspectives for understanding the pathogenesis of OA. Epigenetic modifications are reversible and dynamic mechanisms of gene expression regulation that do not alter the DNA sequence itself. They support proper cell development and function and are associated with various diseases [8–10]. Recent research on epigenetics in OA has primarily focused on DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs, with numerous genome-wide methylation studies being published [11, 12]. Methylation changes not only respond to inflammatory microenvironments and mechanical stress stimuli in OA joints but also form an important part of positive feedback regulatory loops during OA pathology, thereby continuously driving cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling, influencing OA progression [13–15]. Therefore, in-depth research into the specific mechanisms of methylation modifications in OA will not only help reveal the molecular etiology of OA but also provide a theoretical basis for developing novel targeted epigenetic therapeutic strategies.

This review adopts a “whole methylome” perspective to systematically elaborate on the multi-layered methylation regulatory network in OA. We summarize the current research status of classical methylation modifications (DNA, RNA, and histone methylation) and explore the cross-talk between different levels of methylation. Furthermore, we discuss the translational potential of targeting methyltransferases in OA treatment and offer prospects for future intervention strategies based on epigenetics, aiming to promote the development of fundamental therapeutic strategies that modify the progression of OA (Fig. 1).

The mechanism and therapeutic targets of different methylation in OA

Classical epigenetic framework

DNA methylation

DNA methylation is a fundamental epigenetic mechanism that regulates gene expression by covalent addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon position of cytosine within CpG dinucleotides, forming 5-methylcytosine (5mC). This modification is established and maintained by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and can be reversed by Ten–eleven translocation (TET) proteins [16]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have focused on the mechanism of action of DNA methylation regulatory enzymes in OA (Table 1).

DNA Methylation Regulatory Enzymes in Osteoarthritis Regulation Mechanisms and Therapeutic Target

Importantly, the methylation alterations in OA cartilage are not the result of stochastic epigenetic drift, but reflect a coordinated and structured reprogramming of the epigenetic landscape. Previous studies have reported three catalytically active DNMTs with biological activity: DNMT1, DNMT3a, and DNMT3b. DNMT1 acts as a maintenance methyltransferase, binding to hemi-methylated DNA during cell division and proliferation to preserve the DNA methylation landscape in the genome. DNMT3a and DNMT3b are considered de novo DNMTs, as they establish new methylation patterns during development [17, 18]. Genome-wide DNA methylation analyses of normal and OA cartilage tissues using sequencing technologies [19–21]have revealed altered DNA methylation in OA tissues for several key factors known to be involved in chondrocyte homeostasis. Multiple studies have demonstrated aberrant DNA methylation in the promoter regions of genes relevant to OA chondrocytes, including genes encoding extracellular matrix molecules (e.g., Col2A1, Col9A1, Col10A1, Acan), matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., MMP2, MMP3, MMP9, MMP13, Adamts4/5), and key chondrogenic transcription factors (e.g., Sox9, Sox4, Runx2) [16, 22]. Catabolic genes generally exhibited widespread hypomethylation; for instance, reduced methylation levels in the MMP13 promoter region led to a significant increase in its transcriptional activity, accelerating type II collagen degradation. In contrast, protective anabolic genes were silenced by hypermethylation. The methylation level of the SOX9 promoter was significantly elevated in OA cartilage, inhibiting its binding to the COL2A1 enhancer, while hypermethylation also resulted in decreased mRNA expression of COL2A1 and ACAN [16]. Furthermore, alterations in DNA methylation have also been identified in genes associated with signaling pathways. IL-1β recruits TET2 to the MMP13 promoter via the NF-κB pathway, mediating active demethylation [16]. In addition to classical catabolic genes, DNA methylation can directly regulate core hubs of the inflammatory network in osteoarthritis (OA). Sun et al. revealed that in OA cartilage, the promoter regions of the transcriptional co-regulators CtBP1/CtBP2 are hypomethylated, leading to their upregulated expression. The overexpressed CtBP forms a transcriptional activation complex with p300 and AP1, which then binds to the promoter of the NLRP3 inflammasome and drives its transcription. This activates the Caspase-1/IL-1β axis, establishing an inflammatory amplification pathway described as “DNA hypomethylation—CtBP overexpression—NLRP3 activation.” [23](Fig. 2).

Recent studies highlight DNMTs as central regulators of OA epigenetics. We have learned that the dysregulation of DNMTs in OA leads to an epigenetic bias, promoting the progression of the disease towards a degenerative state. Both DNMT1 and DNMT3A are upregulated in human OA cartilage and in surgically induced murine OA, leading to hypermethylation of the PPARγ promoter and suppression of its chondroprotective functions [24]. In traumatic OA, DNMT1 is recruited by GSK3B to the NR4A3 promoter, inducing its methylation and silencing transcription; this attenuates JAK2/STAT3 signaling and thereby limits ECM degradation and inflammatory injury [25]. In contrast, DNMT3B expression is consistently reduced in OA cartilage. Loss of DNMT3B decreases promoter methylation of 4-aminobutyrate aminotransferase (ABAT), resulting in altered mitochondrial metabolism [26]. Furthermore, DNMT3B represses miR-34a expression through promoter methylation; in OA, elevated miR-34a promotes ECM degradation and inflammation via MCL1 targeting and PI3K/AKT activation. Inhibition of miR-34a alleviates cartilage damage, whereas silencing DNMT3B reverses this effect, establishing a DNMT3B–miR-34a–MCL1 axis in OA pathogenesis [27].

Apart from chondrocytes, emerging evidence has begun to characterize DNA methylation in other tissues, but research remains at the broad level of omics analysis, and there are still many unknowns regarding the specific regulatory mechanisms. A recent study constructed methylation mQTL maps in low-grade cartilage, high-grade cartilage, synovium, fat pad and blood through large-scale integrative omics research, and found that certain methylation sites were only co-localized with GWAS in synovium but not in blood, suggesting tissue specificity [28]. This team also discovered that there were global differences in the methylation profiles of cartilage and synovium in OA patients, indicating that different cell types have their own unique methylation patterns, which may be related to their developmental origin, function and microenvironment [29]. Kawarai et al. found that the expression of DNMT3a and TET1/3 was significantly upregulated in the synovium of OA models, accompanied by an increase in the global DNA methylation level; while the changes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells were relatively mild, with only a slight increase in the expression of DNMT1 and TET1 [30]. This also corresponds to the different methylation patterns in different tissues proposed by the above study, explaining why certain methylation sites are significantly associated with diseases only in synovium but not in other tissues. In addition, Yang et al.‘s study on OA synovial fibroblasts found that overexpression of IL-6 was accompanied by hypomethylation of the IL-6 promoter, and overexpression of DNMT3a could increase methylation and inhibit IL-6 expression [31].

Collectively, methylation acts not only as a consequence of inflammation, but also as an amplifying element within the inflammatory network. These findings demonstrate that DNMTs exert bidirectional roles in OA progression by coordinating catabolic gene activation, anabolic gene silencing, and metabolic reprogramming. DNMTs not only function as modifying enzymes, but also become the core nodes in the epigenetic architecture of OA, and are very promising therapeutic targets.

RNA methylation and epitranscriptomic

RNA methylation, a central component of epitranscriptomics, regulates RNA fate through dynamic and reversible chemical modifications. Epitranscriptomics broadly refers to the ensemble of chemical modifications on coding and non-coding RNAs that fine-tune gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence. In the context of OA, epitranscriptomic marks integrate inflammatory, metabolic signals to shape chondrocyte homeostasis and joint-tissue remodeling. This process is precisely coordinated by three functional classes of molecules: methyltransferases (Writers), demethylases (Erasers), and recognition proteins (Readers). Methyltransferases catalyze the transfer of a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to specific sites on RNA, demethylases remove the modifications to maintain dynamic equilibrium, and reader proteins specifically bind the methylation marks to mediate downstream functions [32, 33]. Among the various modifications, three major types—N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), and 7-methylguanosine (m7G)—function through their specific regulatory systems. (Table 2). m6A is the most abundant and functionally versatile internal mRNA modification. It is catalyzed by a multiprotein writer complex comprising METTL3 (catalytic core), METTL14 (structural support), WTAP (localization), and accessory proteins such as ZC3H13 and VIRMA. The modification is reversible through the demethylases FTO and ALKBH5, while YTH domain-containing proteins act as readers to regulate mRNA splicing, translation, or decay [34–36]. The METTL3 and FTO/YTHDF2 pathways have been jointly identified by multiple studies as a core regulatory axis in OA. METTL3 has been shown to mediate m6A modification on the mRNA of the autophagy gene ATG7, recruiting YTHDF2 to bind and accelerate its degradation, leading to disrupted autophagy and chondrocyte senescence [37] (Fig. 3). This finding directly links m6A regulation to the core pathological process of autophagic dysfunction in OA. Similarly, Zhou et al. found that FTO expression was downregulated in OA cartilage. This reduction led to increased m6A modification levels on SMAD2 mRNA, which was subsequently recognized and degraded by YTHDF2, thereby attenuating the SMAD2-mediated cartilage-protective signaling [38] (Fig. 4). Research on meniscal cells further supports this mechanism, demonstrating that FTO downregulation decreased the mRNA stability of the key autophagy gene ATG16L1 via YTHDF2, inhibiting autophagy and promoting degeneration [39]. Both pathways converge on YTHDF2-mediated decrease in the stability of key gene mRNAs, directly impacting chondrocyte homeostasis and highlighting the pivotal role of the writer/eraser-reader system in OA chondrocyte dysfunction. In addition to these consistent core findings, research on some regulatory molecules presents discrepancies. For instance, regarding WTAP, An et al. reported its significant upregulation in OA cartilage, where it enhances the m6A modification level of FRZB mRNA and reduces its stability, thereby relieving the inhibition on Wnt/β-catenin signaling and exacerbating chondrocyte inflammation and ECM degradation [40]. In contrast, Yang et al. indicated that WTAP was downregulated in OA and could affect chondrocyte proliferation and function by mediating the m6A methylation of IL-33 and reducing IL-33 mRNA stability [41]. This discrepancy may stem from the high heterogeneity of OA itself, differences in the clinical samples used, or variations in experimental models, suggesting that WTAP’s function might be specific to the disease stage or cellular microenvironment. Furthermore, other studies have revealed the roles of additional methyltransferases in OA. METTL14-mediated m6A modification also participates in OA progression by enhancing the m6A modification of the lncRNA FAS-AS1 to promote its expression, which subsequently activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway and exacerbates cartilage ECM degradation [42]. Nie et al. demonstrated that ALKBH5 expression is upregulated in an inflammatory microenvironment. It stabilizes the mRNA of the transcription factor Runx2 and enhances its expression through demethylation, directly promoting the transcription of various matrix-degrading enzymes [43]. Another study found that tRF16 targets and reduces ALKBH5 expression, leading to an overall increase in m6A modification levels, which in turn activates the NF-κB pathway and aggravates cartilage inflammation and degradation [44]. Collectively, these studies illustrate how m6A modifications influence OA progression by affecting key factors involved in cellular senescence, ECM degradation, and inflammatory responses.

In contrast, research on m5C and m7G modifications is currently more focused on the characterization of global landscapes and bioinformatic predictions, lacking clearly defined effector gene-signaling pathway axes as established for m6A. m5C is a significant epigenetic mark in RNA, present primarily in tRNAs, rRNAs, mRNAs, and non-coding RNAs, where it influences RNA stability, transcription, and translation processes [45, 46]. Its methyltransferases, mainly the NSUN family (e.g., NSUN2, NSUN6) and DNMT2, catalyze the addition of a methyl group to the cytosine base in RNA, thereby modulating RNA function. This modification can be reversed by demethylases, such as members of the TET family and ALKBH1 [47, 48]. The functional outcomes of m5C modification are ultimately mediated by reader proteins like ALYREF and YBX1 [49]. Yu et al. were the first to map the m5C RNA methylation landscape in OA cartilage tissue. Their study identified numerous differentially methylated genes in OA cartilage, which were significantly enriched in pathways related to collagen degradation and extracellular matrix degradation, suggesting that m5C modification may contribute to OA cartilage destruction by regulating the expression of matrix degradation-related genes [50]. Through bioinformatic analysis, Li et al. found aberrant expression of various m5C regulatory factors in OA tissues and further identified two distinct m5C modification patterns. These patterns showed significant correlations with endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitophagy, and immune cell infiltration (e.g., T cells, B cells, macrophages), implying that m5C modification might be involved in OA progression by modulating the immune microenvironment [51].

m7G modification actively participates in biological and pathological functions by influencing the metabolism of various RNA molecules, including messenger RNA (mRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) [52]. Currently known m7G regulatory factors in mammals include the methyltransferases METTL1/WDR4, RNMT/RAM, and WBSCR22/TRMT112, which catalyze the transfer of a methyl group to the N7 position of guanosine to form the m7G modification [53]. The role of m7G in OA remains in the early stages of investigation. One study indicated that METTL1-mediated m7G modification levels were significantly elevated in OA chondrocytes. This increase promoted the expression of mt-tRF3b-LeuTAA, which in turn inhibited SIRT3-mediated deSUMOylation and mitophagy, ultimately exacerbating chondrocyte degeneration and mitochondrial dysfunction [54]. Research by Chen et al. showed that m7G hub genes were enriched in MAPK, mTOR, and HIF-1 signaling pathways, whose aberrant activation promotes synovial inflammation and chondrocyte metabolic disturbances [55]. Li, using integrated bulk and single-cell transcriptome analyses, found that the m7G-related gene LSM1 was significantly upregulated in OA and promoted inflammatory responses [56]. Another study, based on OA databases, identified 7 m7G-related hub genes involved in mRNA metabolism and immune regulation. Their differential expression was validated in OA synovial tissues, suggesting that m7G modification may contribute to OA progression by regulating immune infiltration [57].

Beyond these three major modifications, several novel RNA methylations also show regulatory potential in OA. m1A (N1-methyladenosine) modification is catalyzed by the writers TRMT6/TRMT61A and can be removed by the erasers ALKBH3 and FTO [58]. The expression of m1A modification-related genes was found to be downregulated in the cartilage of OA patients, suggesting their potential involvement in chondrocyte dysfunction and dysregulated extracellular matrix metabolism by affecting RNA stability and translation efficiency [59]. Furthermore, studies have observed significant downregulation of m1A and uridylation modifications alongside significant upregulation of pseudouridine (Ψ) modification in OA, indicating their collective involvement in the pathological process. This suggests that various RNA modifications work through cross-talk to form a complex post-transcriptional regulatory network underlying chondrocyte degradation and immune microenvironment disruption in OA [56].

The studies summarized above collectively demonstrate that RNA methylation modifications are involved in regulating the pathological process of OA. A deeper understanding of the dynamic interplay within these RNA modification networks holds significant potential for developing precise diagnostic methods and effective therapies for OA.

Histone methylation

Histone modifications are key epigenetic regulatory mechanisms that play a central role in various cellular processes. Histone methylation is particularly important, with common modification sites located on lysine residues of histone H3, including monomethylation, dimethylation, and trimethylation states, denoted as me1, me2, and me3, respectively. These modifications directly regulate gene transcriptional activity by influencing chromatin accessibility. Typically, activating marks (e.g., H3K4me, H3K36me, H3K79me) promote transcription, while repressive marks (e.g., H3K9me, H3K27me, H4K20me) are involved in gene silencing. The dynamic balance between these states is co-regulated by histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and lysine demethylases (KDMs) [18, 60]. In OA research, existing data are highly concentrated on the regulation of chondrocytes by histone methylation, while studies on other joint tissues (e.g., synovium, bone) are relatively limited, which constrains our understanding of the overall mechanisms within the joint microenvironment (Table 4).

Regarding activating methylations, H3K4 methylation is one of the more extensively studied modifications [61]. However, related research in OA is not abundant. One relevant study showed that IL-1β can induce H3K4 dimethylation (H3K4me2) and trimethylation (H3K4me3) at the promoter regions of inflammatory genes COX-2 and iNOS in OA chondrocytes, with specific recruitment of the methyltransferase SET-1 A. Inhibition of SET-1 A blocked this methylation process and the expression of COX-2/iNOS, indicating that H3K4 methylation is a key epigenetic mechanism in OA inflammation [62]. Furthermore, Shao et al. found that H3K36 methylation levels were significantly reduced in the cartilage of OA patients. The methyltransferase NSD1, by regulating H3K36me1/me2 levels, promotes the expression of the transcription factor OSR2, thereby helping to maintain cartilage homeostasis [63] (Fig. 5). Mechanically loaded macrophage-induced M1 polarization can release exosomes rich in miR-350-3p, which directly target and inhibit NSD1 expression, leading to decreased H3K36me1/me2 levels, resulting in chondrocyte catabolism and senescence, and accelerating OA progression. This study revealed a novel mechanism by which mechanical stress regulates OA via an exosomal-miRNA-epigenetic axis [64]. Assi et al. discovered that H3K79 methylation (H3K79me) levels were significantly decreased in OA, and the demethylases KDM7A/B were specifically highly expressed in OA cartilage, directly causing the reduction in H3K79 methylation. Inhibition of KDM7A/B restored H3K79me levels, promoted cartilage anabolism, and alleviated OA pathological damage [65].

Repressive methylations have also been investigated in OA. It has been reported that methylation of H3K9 and H3K27 is increased at the promoter region of the key anabolic gene Sox9 and is associated with the suppression of Sox9 in human OA chondrocytes [66]. Fahmi et al. found that under inflammatory conditions, the demethylase LSD1 is recruited to the promoter region of microsomal prostaglandin E synthase-1 (mPGES-1), leading to decreased H3K9 methylation in OA chondrocytes. Pharmacologic blockade of LSD1 prevented IL-1β-induced H3K9 demethylation and mPGES-1 expression [67]. JMJD3, an H3K27me3-specific demethylase, is upregulated in OA induced by abnormal mechanical stimulation. It promotes the transcription of NR4A1 by removing the H3K27me3 modification at its promoter region. The elevated NR4A1 further activates the Akt signaling pathway, driving chondrocyte apoptosis, release of inflammatory factors, and extracellular matrix degradation, ultimately accelerating joint degeneration [68].

These findings collectively indicate that histone methylation participates in the pathogenesis and progression of OA by regulating the anabolic and catabolic activities of chondrocytes.

Crosstalk within methylation networks and their application prospects in targeted OA therapy

During OA progression, DNA methylation, histone methylation, and RNA methylation (primarily m6A) constitute a multi-layered epigenetic regulatory network. Studies show that during BMSC chondrogenic differentiation, the 3’UTR region of Sox9 mRNA undergoes simultaneous Nsun4-mediated m5C and Mettl3-mediated m6A methylation modifications, which synergistically recruit YTHDF2 and eEF1α-1 to form a complex, significantly enhancing Sox9 translation efficiency and promoting chondrogenic marker expression [69]. Furthermore, in OA progression, RNA m6A methylation and DNA methylation form a cascade regulatory axis: METTL3-mediated m6A modification enhances the stability of lncRNA IGFBP7-OT, promoting its binding to and inhibition of DNA methyltransferases DNMT1/DNMT3a, leading to IGFBP7 promoter hypomethylation and upregulation, ultimately accelerating cartilage degeneration [70].

These interaction mechanisms suggest that epigenetic regulation in OA is not a simple linear process, but rather involves the cross-layer coordination of multiple methylation modifications that jointly regulate cellular physiological and pathological processes. This intricate network not only governs disease progression but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel targeted intervention strategies and precision delivery approaches for OA treatment. This suggests that focusing on the inhibition or activation of relevant methyltransferases may offer a more effective molecular-level approach to delay or reverse the pathological progression of OA.

Specifically, based on the key role of DNA methylation in OA, researchers have begun exploring the therapeutic potential of DNMT inhibitors. Studies show that Diacerein and the anticancer drug 5-azacytidine are widely used as tool compounds in OA research; they can reverse the hypermethylation status of protective gene promoters like PPARγ in OA chondrocytes, restoring their expression and thereby alleviating cartilage destruction [24, 71]. Furthermore, using DNA methyltransferase inhibitors like AZA or 5-Aza-dC can effectively reverse the hypermethylation status in the promoter regions of key genes (e.g., CtBPs) and miRNAs (e.g., miR-140-5p, miR-146a) in OA chondrocytes or synovial cells, restoring their expression, and subsequently downregulating downstream catabolic and inflammatory factors, demonstrating the potential of epigenetic therapy in OA [23, 72]. Besides DNMT inhibitors, STAT3, as a key transcription factor, maintains chondrocyte hypomethylation and an epigenetically youthful state by directly inhibiting DNMT3b expression; it may mediate an intrinsic regenerative attempt in OA, thus precise regulation of the STAT3-DNMT3b axis is expected to become a new epigenetic therapeutic strategy to delay OA progression [73]. Glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3B) overexpression can recruit DNMT1 to the promoter region of the pro-inflammatory factor NR4A3, promoting its hypermethylation and thus inhibiting expression, further suppressing JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway activation, and alleviating chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammatory factor release, and ECM degradation. Therefore, regulating the methylation status of specific genes can delay OA progression [74].

In the field of RNA methylation, the m6A modification regulatory network provides multiple promising targets for OA therapy. WTAP, as an m6A methyltransferase, promotes OA progression via the m6A-FRZB-Wnt/β-catenin axis; in vivo silencing of WTAP significantly delays mouse OA progression, and this protective effect can be reversed by a Wnt pathway activator [40]. Zhou et al. showed that human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (hucMSCs-EVs) can carry miR-1208 to target and inhibit METTL3, reducing m6A modification of NLRP3 mRNA, thereby reducing inflammatory factor release, delaying cartilage degradation, and promoting chondrocyte proliferation [75]. Chen et al. constructed a fibroblast-like synoviocyte (FLS)-targeting adeno-associated viral vector (AAV9.HAP-1) loaded with si-METTL3; targeted inhibition of synovial METTL3 restores autophagy, alleviates senescence, and reduces cartilage destruction, representing a potential OA therapeutic strategy [37]. Furthermore, studies report that extracellular vesicles carrying the m6A demethylase FTO can promote protective autophagy and inhibit apoptosis in chondrocytes by reducing the m6A levels of autophagy genes Atg5/Atg7 and the apoptosis gene BNIP3. This suggests FTO not only serves as a potential target for OA therapy but also that engineered extracellular vesicle-based FTO delivery strategies provide a new direction for targeted epitranscriptomic therapy of OA [76]. ALKBH5, relying on its demethylase activity, regulates the m6A modification of CYP1B1, thereby alleviating MSC senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction; in vivo experiment demonstrates that MSCs overexpressing ALKBH5 significantly enhance the therapeutic effect on OA [77].

The dynamic balance of histone methylation modifications provides another important direction for OA therapy. Histone demethylases KDM7A/B are specifically highly expressed in OA cartilage tissue; treating OA models with the KDM7A/B inhibitor Daminozide effectively restores H3K79me2 levels and promotes the expression of cartilage ECM synthesis genes like COL2A1 and ACAN [65]. JMJD3, as an H3K27me3-specific demethylase, inhibiting its activity (e.g., using GSK-J4) or delivering si-JMJD3 using nanocarriers can significantly alleviate the OA pathological process [68].

It is worth noting that current clinical management of OA relies primarily on symptomatic treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain relief and inflammation control, and joint replacement surgery for end-stage disease. While these approaches can alleviate symptoms, they do not halt or reverse disease progression. In contrast, epigenetic intervention targeting methylation-related enzymes can delay or reverse the disease process by restoring the balance of the methylation regulatory network. This suggests that in the early stage of OA, the combination of epigenetic therapy and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can not only control symptoms but also modify the progression of the disease. As disease-modifying epigenetic agents move toward clinical translation, combination regimens that integrate symptomatic relief with mechanism-driven intervention may offer a more comprehensive and personalized treatment paradigm for OA patients.

Conclusion and outlook

This review systematically summarizes the pivotal roles and interactive regulatory networks of DNA, RNA, and histone methylation modifications in the pathogenesis and progression of osteoarthritis (OA). Substantial evidence indicates that various methylation modifications play critical roles in regulating chondrocyte metabolism, inflammatory responses, and extracellular matrix degradation, providing a crucial foundation for understanding OA mechanisms.

It is important to note that current research data in this field are predominantly derived from chondrocytes. Studies on other key components of the joint microenvironment, such as the synovium, subchondral bone, and immune cells, remain relatively scarce, which somewhat limits a comprehensive understanding of the epigenetic regulatory network governing OA as a whole-organ disease. Future studies should prioritize integrative analyses of cartilage-synovium-bone crosstalk to better characterize OA as a system-level epigenetic disorder, rather than a pathology confined to cartilage. Nevertheless, existing findings sufficiently demonstrate that targeting these methylation-modifying enzymes (e.g., DNMTs, METTL3, FTO, KDMs) presents novel opportunities for developing disease-modifying therapies. It should be noted that the relevant clinical applications still require rigorous validation through human tissue organoids, longitudinal cohort studies, and spatially resolved transcriptomics.

Furthermore, specific methylation markers, such as PPARγ promoter methylation, SOX9 promoter methylation, or the m⁶A modification level of ATG7 mRNA, have the potential to serve as biomarkers for the early diagnosis of osteoarthritis. However, these markers still need to be validated through longitudinal cohort studies and standardized detection methods, such as liquid biopsy techniques based on blood or synovial fluid. On this basis, future clinical strategies should further incorporate patient stratification based on epigenetic characteristics, thereby promoting the shift from simple symptomatic relief to targeted and mechanism-driven precision medical intervention in the treatment of osteoarthritis.

However, current research primarily focuses on individual types of methylation modifications. OA is a complex disease driven by multi-factorial interactions; its pathogenesis is likely not driven by alterations in a single epigenetic layer but rather results from the interplay and co-regulation of different methylation modifications. Therefore, future research must transition from a “single-dimension” focus to a “multi-dimensional integration” approach. On the basis of expanding the research scope to encompass all joint tissues, it is essential to leverage multi-omics, bioinformatics, and other technologies to systematically decipher how different types of methylation modifications form synergistic or antagonistic regulatory networks across spatial (different joint cells) and temporal (different disease stages) dimensions, collectively influencing OA progression. Prioritizing single-cell multi-omics, epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS), and integrative methylome-transcriptome modeling will help to identify master regulatory nodes within OA’s epigenetic architecture.

Ultimately, interdisciplinary collaboration will be pivotal for translating epigenetic insights into clinical therapies that could fundamentally reshape the OA treatment landscape.
