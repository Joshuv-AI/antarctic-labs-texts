An early prediction model for high-quality blastocyst development based on ANA titer and baseline demographic characteristics in the first IVF/ICSI cycle
Authors: Kai Ding, Yidan Chen, Wei Jiang, Xin Li, Xiufeng Ling, Chun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-quality blastocysts with good developmental potential are crucial for successful pregnancy, yet the factors influencing their formation remain unclear in clinical practice, and no predictive model is available for early clinical use. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), which are closely associated with autoimmune diseases but are also commonly found in the infertile patients, may significantly impact embryo quality. Therefore, evaluating the effect of ANA on blastocyst quality in infertile patients is of great importance, as it could facilitate the establishment of an early predictive model for the formation of high-quality blastocysts prior to oocyte retrieval.MethodThis study aims to investigate the potential factors influencing high-quality blastocyst formation in infertile patients undergoing their first IVF/ICSI cycle with ANA titer screening, and to assess whether ANA titer impacts the formation and predictive significance of high-quality blastocysts. A total of 2,876 eligible infertility patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 1,725) and a validation set (n = 1,151) at a 6:4 ratio. Clinical characteristics of the optimal blastocyst development group (OBD group) and suboptimal blastocyst development group (SBD group) were compared to initially screen potential predictive variables (P < 0.1). Variables were further selected using RF, LASSO, and XGBoost machine learning methods, and those common to all three methods were identified as key predictors of optimal blastocyst development. A predictive model was then constructed using a multivariate logistic regression model, and its performance was evaluated through ROC and calibration curves in both the training and validation sets. Internal validation was performed using the Bootstrap method, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test assessed the goodness-of-fit between predicted and actual outcomes. A nomogram was developed to visualize the model and facilitate early clinical application. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical utility of the nomogram.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences in clinical characteristics between the training and validation sets after 6:4 randomization (P > 0.05). In the training set, female age, duration of infertility, baseline LH level, T level, FSH level, FSH/LH ratio, sperm concentration, normal morphology rate, male age, AMH level, AFC, infertility type, ANA titer, number of spontaneous abortions, and MCL were compared with the SBD group (P < 0.1) and considered as potential influencing factors. Five predictive variables closely related to OBD were identified using RF, LASSO, and XGBoost machine learning methods: female age, AMH level, AFC, ANA titer, and number of spontaneous abortions. The prediction model based on these variables achieved an AUC of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.66–0.71) in the training set and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.73) in the validation set, demonstrating good discriminative ability. The calibration curve for both the training set (P = 0.735) and validation set (P = 0.099) showed that the predicted probabilities closely aligned with the actual probabilities. The nomogram and DCA further enhance the clinical applicability of this model.ConclusionA high-quality blastocyst prediction model was constructed using five easily obtainable clinical baseline characteristics, demonstrating clinically acceptable predictive ability. This model facilitates individualized diagnosis and treatment for infertility patients before embryo culture. Additionally, this study is the first to highlight the importance and predictive potential of ANA in high-quality blastocyst formation, underscoring the need for ANA-related screening and treatment in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01830-z.Keywords: Antinuclear antibodies, In vitro fertilization, Intracytoplasmic sperm injection, Embryo quality, Predictive learning models

Introduction

In 2021, the global prevalence of infertility was reported to affect 12.6–17.5% of couples of reproductive age, with assisted reproductive technology (ART) achieving a live birth rate of approximately 30% per initiated cycle [1]. Compared to cleavage-stage embryos, blastocyst-stage embryos exhibit better implantation potential, which can be attributed to their enhanced synchronization with the window of endometrial receptivity and the ability to self-select embryos with high developmental potential [2]. These advantages have contributed to the widespread adoption of blastocyst transfer in clinical practice, particularly for optimizing single-embryo transfer (SET) strategies in both in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) cycles [3–5]. Nevertheless, extended in vitro culture carries inherent risks, including developmental arrest of viable embryos and subsequent cycle cancellations, which may negatively impact cumulative pregnancy rates [6]. Therefore, to enhance reproductive outcomes in infertile patients and alleviate unnecessary financial burdens, there is an urgent need to identify potential risk factors influencing high-quality blastocyst formation in order to enable personalized treatment strategies.

Multiple studies have discovered potential clinical and laboratory predictors of blastocyst development, including maternal age, serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels, ovarian response (e.g., oocyte yield), paternal factors (age and semen parameters), gonadotropin dosage, fertilization method, and cleavage-stage embryo morphology [3, 7, 8]. However, the clinical utility of these variables in constructing predictive models for blastocyst formation has not been fully explored. While current models predominantly focus on preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) cycles, leveraging blastocyst euploidy status as a key determinant of implantation potential [9–11], PGT is not commonly used in clinical practice, and some centers even prohibit embryo biopsy, making it unsuitable for many patients [12]. Other models related to blastocyst prediction incorporate laboratory data related to embryo culture, such as the number of oocytes retrieved, cleavage embryo symmetry, and fragmentation rate [3, 7]. Although these models have shown good predictive efficacy, the data are only available after oocyte retrieval or ovarian stimulation, which limits their clinical application. Therefore, a more comprehensive method is needed to assess key factors influencing high-quality blastocyst formation in infertile patients. This would enable earlier prediction and facilitate the integration of personalized treatment strategies.

ANA refers to a class of autoantibodies that target intracellular structures and organelles, including components of the cell nucleus, cytoplasm, and mitotic structures [13]. Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), utilizing human epithelioma-2 (HEp-2) cells as the substrate, is commonly employed to detect ANA positivity (titer ≥ 1:80) [14]. Although ANA is closely associated with autoimmune diseases (AIDs), it can also be present in the general population due to the body’s normal immune response. The positivity rate with a titer ≥ 1:80 is approximately 10% to 12%, and the rate with a titer ≥ 1:160 is only 5% [15]. In infertility patients, ANA is found in approximately 20% of cases, and its positivity is correlated with impaired embryo developmental competence, as evidenced by reduced blastocyst formation rates and lower morphological quality [16–18]. Although changes in ANA titer are not clearly linked to AIDs activity or treatment response [19], elevated ANA titers in infertility patients appear to be associated with a lower number of oocytes retrieved and fewer available embryos [20, 21]. Mechanistic studies implicate ANA-mediated interference in oocyte maturation through dysregulation of RNA processing and DNA replication fidelity [22], as well as through direct disruption of meiotic spindle integrity via centromere protein interactions [23, 24]. Consequently, ANA may impair the development of oocytes and early embryos, with this effect exacerbated as the titer increases. While ANA has been incorporated into predictive models for recurrent implantation failure (RIF) risk stratification [25], its potential as a standalone predictor of embryo developmental competence remains unexplored.

Given the correlation between ANA and embryo quality, as well as the importance of establishing early predictive models for high-quality blastocyst development, this study aims to evaluate whether ANA can serve as an early and reliable predictor of blastocyst quality. Based on these findings, the study also seeks to develop an effective tool for predicting high-quality blastocyst formation in IVF/ICSI cycles prior to oocyte retrieval.

Materials and methods

Study design

A total of 10,171 patients were recruited, all of whom underwent assisted reproductive technology treatment at the Reproductive Center of Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital between January 2021 and February 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the current IVF/ICSI cycle was the first cycle; (2) autologous oocytes were used, without the need for egg donation; (3) blastocyst culture was performed. Patients were excluded if they did not undergo ANA titer testing (n = 6,354), had chromosomal abnormalities (n = 152), a history of ovarian surgery (n = 238), a history of systemic chemotherapy or radiotherapy (n = 14), autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus or Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (n = 54), underwent embryo transfer and/or cryopreservation on day 3 (n = 442), or underwent sperm retrieval or cryopreservation (n = 61). The remaining dataset was randomly divided into a training set (60% of patients, n = 1,725) and a validation set (40% of patients, n = 1,151). Figure 1 presents the flowchart of patient inclusion. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital (2024KY-116).

Detection of ANA expression

Prior to the initiation of IVF/ICSI treatment, ANA testing was performed by the Department of Laboratory Medicine at Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital, where positive samples were initially identified using this method. Venous blood samples were collected, and serum was separated. The test serum was then incubated with antigen slides using HEp-2 cells as the substrate (Shenzhen YHLO, China). ANA reacts with the antigens in the HEp-2 cell substrate, forming an antigen-antibody complex. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, analyses were conducted using the iSlide 240 fully automated fluorescence immunoassay analyzer (Shenzhen YHLO, China). ANA testing was considered positive when characteristic fluorescence signals were detected in the sample at a serum dilution of 1:80 [14].

Data collection

All required information was retrieved from our electronic medical record system (EMRS). Data collected from patient history surveys included the age, body mass index (BMI), baseline sex hormone levels (baseline follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH], prolactin [PRL], luteinizing hormone [LH], testosterone [T], estradiol [E2], progesterone [P]), AMH levels, and duration of infertility for female patients. For male patients, the data included age and semen analysis parameters, such as sperm concentration, sperm morphology, progressive motility (PR), non-progressive motility (NP), and immotility (IM). Additionally, to comprehensively analyze factors related to high-quality blastocyst formation, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, TyG-BMI, and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) were calculated. The specific formulas are as follows: TyG = ln(fasting triglycerides [mg/dL] × fasting plasma glucose [FPG] [mg/dL]/2), TyG-BMI = TyG index × BMI, HOMA-IR = (FPG [mmol/L] × fasting insulin [µU/mL])/22.5. The individual decrease in menstrual cycle length (MCL) over time may be considered the most sensitive biomarker of diminishing ovarian reserve. MCL is classified into four main duration categories: short (21–27 days), normal (28–31 days), long (32–35 days), and other MCL categories [26].

Ovarian stimulation protocol and embryo quality evaluation

All patients in this study underwent a flexible gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist (GnRH-ant) protocol, as previously described in our earlier publication [27]. Ovarian stimulation was initiated with daily administration of gonadotropins (Gn) starting on the second or third day of the menstrual cycle. When the leading follicle reached a diameter of 12–14 mm or serum estradiol (E2) levels exceeded 300 ng/L, GnRH-ant (Cetrorelix, Merck Serono, Darmstadt, Germany) was commenced by subcutaneous injection and continued until the day of ovulation trigger. Follicular growth was monitored through transvaginal ultrasound and sex hormone levels, including FSH, LH, E2, and P, with Gn dosages adjusted accordingly. Final oocyte maturation was induced when at least two follicles reached a diameter greater than 18 mm, either by a single dose of human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) (Lizhu Pharmaceutical Factory, China) or by dual triggering with 2000 IU HCG in combination with a GnRH agonist (0.2 mg Decapeptyl, Ferring International Center SA). For high-risk ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) patients, an antagonist protocol was chosen with a GnRH-a trigger. The definition and grading of OHSS followed the guidelines of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) [28].Oocyte retrieval was performed approximately 36–37 h later. Retrieved oocytes were fertilized using either IVF or ICSI 4–6 h after follicular aspiration, depending on sperm quality. Embryos were subsequently cultured in vitro for 3–6 days prior to fresh embryo transfer (ET) or cryopreservation, depending on the patient’s preference, clinical indications, embryo development, and other factors.

Fertilization was assessed using an inverted microscope at 10× magnification. Oocytes exhibiting two pronuclei (2PN) within the cytoplasm after insemination were considered normally fertilized, while those lacking this feature were classified as abnormally fertilized.The fertilized eggs were then transferred to the culture medium (G1; Vitrolife) and overlaid with mineral oil (Ovoil; Vitrolife) for an additional 2 days (until the 2-cell or 8-cell stage). On Day 3, the embryos were scored according to previously published criteria [29]. A high-quality cleavage-stage embryo was defined as having at least six blastomeres, less than 20% fragmentation, and no significant morphological abnormalities. After reclassifying the embryos based on developmental stage and morphological scoring, they were cultured in fresh medium (G2; Vitrolife) until reaching the blastocyst stage (Day 5–6).The oocytes and embryos were cultured in a sequential medium (IVF, G1 and G2, Vitrolife, Sweden) under conditions of 37 °C, 6% carbon dioxide, 5% oxygen, 89% nitrogen, and saturated humidity (Astec, Japan) [30].

Morphological observation and grading were performed on blastocysts formed on Day 5 and Day 6 post-fertilization. According to the Gardner blastocyst grading system [31], blastocysts were staged and rated based on three factors: the degree of blastocyst expansion, the number and morphology of the inner cell mass (ICM) cells, and the number and morphology of the trophoblast cells (TE). The degree of blastocoel expansion was graded on a scale from 1 to 6. When the blastocyst reached an expansion level of 3 or higher, both ICM and TE were evaluated based on cell size and density (A/B/C). High-quality blastocysts were defined as those at stage 3 or above, with an expanded blastocoel and both ICM and TE graded as B or higher (i.e., ≥3BB).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.2.1.To unify the format of continuous variables, all continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) [32], and categorical variables were presented as n (%). Intergroup comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test, while count data were analyzed with Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. The high-quality blastocyst rate was defined as the ratio of high-quality blastocysts to the total number of blastocysts per patient. Due to the lack of a clear reference range for the proportion of high-quality blastocysts [33], we determined a threshold of 50% for high-quality blastocyst rate based on previous studies [34] and the high-quality blastocyst rate in our center (as shown in Table S1). Infertile patients with a high-quality blastocyst rate of ≥ 50% were assigned to the optimal blastocyst development group (OBD group), while those with a rate < 50% were categorized into the suboptimal blastocyst development group (SBD group).

If the missing values for a variable were less than 20%, we considered imputing the missing data. We used the mice package in R and applied Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) to impute numeric features [35]. This method was applied to impute the missing values for the following variables: BMI (6, 0.2%), bFSH (14, 0.5%), bE2 (13, 0.5%), bP (15, 0.5%), bPRL (152, 5.3%), bLH (14, 0.5%), bT (180, 6.3%), AMH (9, 0.3%), TSH (27, 0.9%), Cr (15, 0.5%), BUN (18, 0.6%), TC (238, 8.3%), TG (221, 7.7%), FPG (82, 2.3%), insulin (270, 9.4%), sperm concentration (149, 5.2%), spermatozoa with normal morphology (423, 14.7%), and progressive motile sperm (72, 2.5%). Sensitivity analysis was performed on the imputed dataset to assess its impact on the original dataset. Additionally, the remaining dataset was randomly split into a training set (60% of patients, n = 1,725) and a validation set (40% of patients, n = 1,151) using the caret package in R.

To enhance the clinical applicability of the predictive model, antral follicle count (AFC), AMH and FSH were divided into four groups based on quartiles. Variables with P values < 0.1 in group comparisons were considered potential factors for optimal blastocyst development and selected for further analysis. Three machine learning models—random forest (RF), LASSO, and XGBoost—were employed to identify factors independently associated with optimal blastocyst development.

To identify the importance of factors and rank them, the random forest algorithm was implemented. This is a powerful, tree-based machine learning method. We configured the model to generate 1,000 random trees and assessed model performance by calculating the out-of-bag (OOB) error, where the OOB error ranges from 0 (better performance) to 1 (worse performance). Additionally, we evaluated variable importance by examining the Gini index, reported as “MeanDecreaseGini,” which measures disorder. Higher values indicate greater importance in the model, as scores closer to 0 signify higher disorder, while scores closer to 1 reflect lower disorder and more consistent contribution to the outcome. Finally, variables with the most significant impact were selected through permutation testing.

For LASSO regression, we conducted multivariable analysis using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator with an L1 penalty, complemented by 10-fold cross-validation for internal validation. This logistic regression model applies a penalty to the absolute magnitude of the regression coefficients based on the value of λ. As the penalty increases, the estimated values of weaker factors are reduced to zero, leaving only the most influential predictive variables in the model. The most predictive covariates were selected using the minimum λ plus one standard error (λ.1se). LASSO regression was performed using the glmnet package in R software.

A multivariate logistic regression model was built based on the selected factors, and its performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the calibration curve. Internal validation was conducted using the Bootstrap method, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test assessed the goodness-of-fit between the model’s predictions and actual outcomes. A nomogram was developed to visualize the clinical application of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated the clinical utility of the nomogram. All tests were two-tailed, with P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

There were no statistically significant differences in clinical characteristics between the training and validation sets (P >0.05); detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table S1. To ensure a more comprehensive inclusion of potential influencing factors, variables with P values < 0.1 in the training set used to construct the predictive model were included in the next step of analysis [36, 37], when comparing the OBD group with the SBD group: female age, duration of infertility, baseline LH level, T level, FSH level, FSH/LH ratio, sperm concentration, normal morphology rate, male age, AMH level, AFC, infertility type, ANA titer, number of spontaneous abortions, and MCL. Screening and identification of factors associated with optimal blastocyst development

The identified potential influencing factors were incorporated into the RF model, which generated 1,000 trees, with each tree split using a random selection of three predictor variables. The OOB error rate was 38.26%, as shown in Figure S1.This indicates that the model made approximately 38.26% incorrect predictions on the data samples not selected by each decision tree, which we consider acceptable for the purpose of variable selection. The mean decrease in Gini (MDG) index, suitable for high-dimensional or noisy classification problems [38], was selected for this study. Permutation importance testing revealed that AMH, AFC, female age, number of spontaneous abortions, ANA titer, and infertility type significantly influenced the model’s predictive outcomes. These variables were ranked in descending order of importance, as shown in Figure S2.

LASSO regression was applied to identify factors closely associated with the optimal blastocyst development in the training set. The optimal λ was set at 0.061, shrinking some variables’ coefficients to zero, while retaining non-zero coefficients as key factors. The following baseline factors were initially assessed: duration of infertility, FSH/LH ratio, female age, AMH, AFC, ANA titer, and number of spontaneous abortions, as shown in Figure S3a and S3b.

XGBoost demonstrated excellent computational speed and predictive accuracy, with all included variables showing considerable importance for optimal blastocyst development. Ultimately, 15 predictor variables were identified, with the importance of key variables ranked from highest to lowest as follows: AMH, AFC, FSH/LH ratio, sperm concentration, normal morphology, T, number of spontaneous abortions, female age, LH, male age, ANA titer, infertility type, duration of infertility, FSH, and MCL, as shown in Figure S4.

Common predictor variables identified by RF, Lasso, and XGBoost were selected for the predictive model development. These included five variables: female age, AMH, AFC, ANA titer, and number of spontaneous abortions. To further assess their importance, trigger day-related indicators were also considered after ovarian stimulation, and variable selection was performed using LASSO regression (Table 2 and S2, Figure S5a and S5b). The results confirmed that after adding trigger day follicular status and related hormone levels, these five predictor variables still significantly influenced optimal blastocyst development and continued to be independent factors for predicting optimal blastocyst development, as shown in the Fig. 2.

Development and validation of a model for predicting optimal blastocyst development

Female age, AMH, AFC, ANA titer, and number of spontaneous abortions were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to evaluate their impact on blastocyst development outcomes (Table 2). The results confirmed that these variables were significantly associated with the occurrence of optimal blastocyst development. To optimize the patient counseling model, a visual nomogram was developed, converting the effects of the five non-zero coefficient variables into measurable points. As shown in Fig. 3, the points for each variable are determined at the intersection of the vertical line between the variable and the point scale. The points for all variables are then summed to calculate the total score. Finally, the probability of optimal blastocyst development is determined from the total score scale.

Visualization of the nomogram for predicting optimal blastocyst development. The points for each variable are read at the intersection of the vertical line drawn from the variable to the point scale. The points for each variable are then summed to calculate the total score. The probability of a patient developing optimal blastocyst development is determined from the total score scale. The range for each line’s automatic generation is based on the variable ranges of patients included in this study. The red vertical line represents example data

ROC analysis demonstrated that the predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.66–0.71) in the training set and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.73) in the validation set, indicating acceptable predictive performance (Fig. 4). Additionally, the calibration curves for both sets closely matched the ideal curve. After internal validation using the Bootstrap method with 1,000 resamples, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit, with P = 0.735 for the training set and P = 0.099 for the validation set (Fig. 5a and b). This demonstrates that the predicted values closely align with the observed values, confirming the model’s accuracy. The DCA demonstrates that the nomogram prediction model outperforms both the None and ALL lines when the threshold probability (Pt) for the training set ranges from 20% to 79%, and the Pt for the validation set ranges from 16% to 76%. This further affirms that our model offers excellent clinical net benefit and holds significant clinical application value (Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of the results, we performed a sensitivity analysis. A comparison between the original dataset and the imputed dataset showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the two datasets in the imputed variables, including BMI, bFSH, bE2, bP, bPRL, bLH, bT, AMH, TSH, Cr, BUN, TC, TG, FPG, insulin, sperm concentration, spermatozoa with normal morphology, and progressive motile sperm (P > 0.05, see Table S3). Since we imputed AMH, which is a key variable affecting optimal blastocyst development, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of the model using both the imputed dataset and the original dataset. The results showed that the association between AMH and optimal blastocyst development remained consistent in direction and statistical significance, indicating that our main findings are robust (Table 3).

Discussion

This study identified five key predictive factors for optimal blastocyst development through RF, XGBoost, and LASSO regression analysis: female age, AMH, AFC, ANA titer, and number of spontaneous abortions. Specifically, female age ≥ 38 years, AMH < 1.81 ng/mL, AFC < 8, ANA titer ≥ 1:80, and a history of no spontaneous abortions were independent risk factors for optimal blastocyst development. A visual nomogram model was developed based on these factors to facilitate personalized prediction, showing strong discriminatory ability and accuracy in both the training and validation sets.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to specifically explore the importance and predictive ability of ANA in optimal blastocyst development. ANA-related AIDs, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), are closely linked to fertility issues in infertile patients, including ovarian insufficiency and embryo implantation failure [39, 40]. Cohort studies have shown that the presence of ANA is associated with decreased oocyte maturation rates, fertilization rates, and high-quality embryo rates in infertile patients, with further deterioration in embryo outcomes as ANA titers increase [17, 20, 41]. The mechanisms by which ANA affects embryo quality may involve interference with RNA transcription in oocytes and embryos, as well as damage to DNA topoisomerase, leading to DNA replication disorders and impaired embryo development [22]. Furthermore, ANA in follicular fluid has been shown to hinder oocyte maturation, interfere with spindle-centromere interactions, and cause severe chromosomal misalignment during metaphase of meiosis [23, 24]. These findings suggest that ANA may serve as a biomarker for autoimmune-related infertility, closely linked to embryo quality. Through various machine learning methods, we identified potential factors influencing high-quality blastocyst formation, and the results further indicate that ANA, particularly its titer, may be a predictive factor for high-quality blastocyst formation.

Common causes of spontaneous abortion include chromosomal abnormalities in the embryo [42], maternal immunological factors, thrombophilic factors, reproductive tract anatomical abnormalities, and endocrine disorders [43]. Some embryos with chromosomal abnormalities can still develop to the blastocyst stage [44]. Therefore, the blastocyst quality in infertile patients with a history of spontaneous abortion may not necessarily be compromised. Studies have shown that in infertile patients undergoing their first IVF/ICSI cycle, a history of one or two spontaneous abortions does not significantly affect embryo quality [45]. Furthermore, the incidence of aneuploidy in recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA) and sporadic miscarriage groups was not significantly different [46]. Clinically, about 60% of RSA cases have an unclear etiology, with potential mechanisms including abnormal decidual reactions, improper trophoblast invasion, and imbalances in endometrial function homeostasis [47, 48]. This suggests that RSA and sporadic miscarriage may be more closely related to endometrial status, such as endometrial receptivity [49]. Current research indicates that in RSA patients, abnormal expression of cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)−6 and IL-8, associated with endometrial receptivity, disrupts the window of endometrial receptivity (ER) [50, 51]. Since embryos with good developmental potential are critical for a successful pregnancy [52], patients with a history of spontaneous abortion appear to retain the ability to produce high-quality embryos. However, for patients with multiple spontaneous abortions, comprehensive evaluations and treatments related to miscarriage are conducted prior to IVF/ICSI treatment [43]. This helps explain why an increased number of spontaneous abortions is correlated with a higher probability of optimal blastocyst development.

AMH is a peptide growth factor produced by the granulosa cells of ovarian follicles [53]. Due to its stable levels throughout the menstrual cycle, AMH has gained attention as a reliable marker for assessing ovarian reserve [54]. AFC, the total number of antral follicles in both ovaries, can be measured through transvaginal ultrasound during the early follicular phase. When combined, AFC and AMH offer a more specific indicator for predicting ovarian reserve function [55]. Studies have shown that AMH and AFC are closely related to ovarian reserve and also serve as predictors for embryo quality, quantity, and fertilization rates following ovarian stimulation [56–60]. A decrease in antral follicle number may impair the selection of mature oocytes, thus affecting embryo quality [61]. However, some studies have reported conflicting results regarding the predictive value of AMH and AFC for embryo quality [55]. For instance, in IVF cycles, AMH levels did not predict oocyte quality in women under 38 years of age [62]. This discrepancy may be explained by the inclusion of a large number of patients undergoing preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A), who likely received comprehensive examinations and treatments. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the impact of these interventions could not be excluded, potentially contributing to the observed differences. In our study, we aimed to assess the overall relationship between AMH, AFC, and the occurrence of optimal blastocyst development, without the need for age-stratified analysis. We found that the likelihood of optimal blastocyst development increased as AMH levels and AFC rose, consistent with previous findings [3].

The likelihood of optimal blastocyst development decreases with female age, likely due to the higher incidence of aneuploidy and the potential decline in mitochondrial activity associated with aging [63]. Previous studies have shown that over 90% of euploid embryos can develop to the blastocyst stage, whereas fewer than 50% of aneuploid embryos can reach this stage [10]. Specifically, for women aged 38 years or older with infertility, the rates of aneuploidy and embryo arrest significantly increase after age 38 [64]. Our analysis further confirms that, compared to women under 30, the likelihood of optimal blastocyst development is lower after age 38. However, for women aged 30–38 years, no significant difference was observed in the occurrence of optimal blastocyst development. A possible explanation is that embryos with greater fragmentation are less likely to reach the blastocyst stage [65]. Furthermore, studies on infertility patients under 35 years of age have shown that female age does not significantly affect the relationship between embryo fragmentation and blastocyst formation, suggesting that maternal age within this age group does not impact blastocyst development [66].

High-quality blastocysts are critical indicators for improving clinical pregnancy outcomes in infertile patients. In a study by VerMilyea et al. [67], an artificial intelligence (AI)-based model trained on day-5 blastocyst images to predict clinical pregnancy achieved a weighted overall accuracy of 64.3% (sensitivity 70.1%, specificity 60.5%). Additionally, Lee et al. [68] found that high AI scores of blastocysts were significantly associated with live birth rates in SET cycles. Although these studies demonstrate the immense potential of AI as an emerging tool to improve embryo selection and predict implantation and pregnancy outcomes, they still rely on data from the blastocyst development stage and remain closely tied to the traditional Gardner scoring system used by embryologists [31]. Therefore, obtaining morphological parameters at the blastocyst stage (e.g., ≥3BB) remains of critical importance. Notably, VerMilyea et al. reported that the accuracy of both AI model predictions and embryologists’ assessments of high-quality blastocysts (≥ 3BB) ranged from 47.4% to 59.2%. This suggests that relying solely on static images captured by optical microscopy during IVF cycles is still insufficient to effectively differentiate and predict high-quality blastocysts, whether assessed by AI or embryologists. Thus, early prediction of high-quality blastocysts and analysis of their influencing factors remain crucial for improving pregnancy success in infertile patients.

Previous studies have developed predictive models for high-quality blastocyst formation. Xiong et al. [3] created a model using maternal age, AMH levels, and the number of oocytes retrieved to predict the number of high-quality blastocysts, showing good performance (AUC >0.7). However, in Xiong’s study, the key variable, the number of oocytes retrieved, was only available after the oocyte retrieval procedure, limiting early clinical application. Additionally, no new variables related to high-quality blastocyst formation were identified, and their calibration curve lacked internal validation. From a visual perspective, the predicted probabilities differed significantly from the actual probabilities, suggesting that further validation of the clinical predictive accuracy is needed. Liao et al. [69] used time-lapse monitoring (TLM) and deep learning models in artificial intelligence (AI) to predict blastocyst formation, while Liu et al. [7] employed embryo laboratory outcome-related indicators such as the number of oocytes retrieved, pronuclear morphological score, and cleavage ball count. Although these models showed good predictive performance (AUC >0.7), they also shared the limitation of only being applicable after oocyte retrieval, hindering early clinical intervention. In this study, we conducted variable selection using multiple machine learning models and identified five easily obtainable predictive factors. To enhance clinical applicability, all included variables were categorical. Our model demonstrated strong accuracy and discriminative ability, with internal validation performed. Notably, ANA titer was specifically identified for its predictive potential regarding high-quality blastocyst rates. This model facilitates early clinical intervention, helping to identify infertility patients at higher risk of suboptimal blastocyst development, ultimately improving embryo quality and assisted reproductive outcomes.

Several studies have explored the impact of male factors on embryo quality. Increasing paternal age is associated with higher DNA fragmentation rates and reduced sperm motility [70]. Sperm DNA integrity is critical for successful IVF and healthy embryo development, as compromised sperm DNA hinders early embryo development [71] and leads to lower blastocyst formation rates [72]. Other factors, such as semen parameters, have also been shown to influence embryo quality. For instance, couples with male partners suffering from oligospermia or azoospermia exhibit significantly lower blastocyst formation rates per fertilized egg compared to those with normal sperm concentration [73]. In ICSI, severe oligospermia (below 0.2 million/ml) reduces blastulation rates. Decreased rapid progressive motility particularly affects IVF, resulting in fewer embryos on day 2 when progressive motility is below 32% [74]. However, a related meta-analysis of 12 studies on the impact of paternal age on oocyte donation outcomes, after excluding maternal age bias, found no significant correlation between advanced paternal age and blastocyst formation or related clinical outcomes [75]. Similarly, a study using donor oocytes to assess the effect of male age on ICSI outcomes found that paternal age did not impact reproductive outcomes, suggesting that high-quality oocytes can overcome the reduced reproductive potential of aged sperm [76]. Furthermore, sperm characteristics appear to be weak predictors of embryo quality, with female factors playing a more dominant role in determining embryo quality [11].

This study is the first to elucidate and demonstrate the predictive significance of ANA in high-quality blastocyst formation. Using baseline demographic information available before embryo culture, we developed a model with good predictive performance. The model has been validated with both training and validation datasets, ensuring its accuracy. This tool allows clinicians to implement early interventions for factors influencing embryo outcomes in infertile patients, facilitating personalized treatment strategies.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective, single-center design and the lack of external validation for model assessment. Additionally, the study focused on infertility patients undergoing their first IVF/ICSI cycle with ANA-related testing, which excluded many potential infertility patients. Due to its retrospective nature, we were unable to obtain information regarding the immune medications these patients were receiving, which may have led to inaccuracies in the model evaluation. Furthermore, the model was based on variables available in our EMRS, which may not include other relevant factors, such as sperm DNA fragmentation index and embryological variables. Embryological variables, such as embryo morphology scores and fragmentation rates, are significantly associated with embryonic developmental potential [33]. However, these variables were not included in our study, primarily because the study design aimed to identify factors related to blastocyst development as early as possible, specifically before oocyte retrieval. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that the exclusion of these variables may have compromised the accuracy of our model. To simplify the model, we selected five variables that were strongly correlated with high-quality blastocysts, excluding other potentially relevant factors, such as serum E2 levels on the ovulation trigger day [11]. While this simplification improved the model’s usability, it may have reduced its predictive performance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential impact and predictive role of ANA in high-quality blastocyst formation and develops a multivariable predictive model for the occurrence of optimal blastocyst development. The model is based on five independent risk factors: female age, AMH concentration, AFC, number of previous miscarriages, and ANA titer. It demonstrates strong predictive ability and accuracy in both training and testing sets. Additionally, because the variables are easily obtainable early in the treatment process, the model significantly improves clinical decision-making.
