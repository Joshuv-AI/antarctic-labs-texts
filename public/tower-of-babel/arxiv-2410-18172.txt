Holographic Generalised Gukov-Witten Defects
Pieter Bomans, Lorenzo Tranchedone

Abstract
Spindle and disc solutions have received significant attention in recent literature. However, it is not entirely clear what these supergravity solutions represent in the dual SCFT. In this work we elucidate this issue by considering a different global completion of the spindle solution, focusing on a single conical defect. We argue that these solutions can be interpreted as the insertion of a single (generalised) Gukov-Witten defect in the bulk SCFT. We give compelling evidence for this claim by uplifting the solutions to type IIB supergravity and showing that the five-dimensional 1 2 \frac{1}{2} -BPS solutions uplifts to the bubbling surface operators solutions. Their less supersymmetric cousins give rise to a large class of holographic generalised Gukov-Witten surface operators for which we perform a detailed geometric analysis. To corroborate these claims we compute a variety of holographic observables such as the renormalised action, stress tensor one-point function and defect entanglement entropy. In addition, we extend the holographic inflow mechanism to account for bulk-to-defect inflow to study the symmetries and anomalies of the bulk and defect.

Summary of this work

In this work we focus on surface defect operators in four-dimensional

Unlike for the spindle or disc discussed above, we consider a non-compact global completion with an asymptotic

Uplifting our solutions to ten-dimensional type IIB supergravity, this class of

Including the additional scalars breaks the axial symmetry and therefore allows us to describes a larger class of surface defects.

Outlook

Our work makes progress on the outstanding question to identify the field theory interpretation of spindle solutions. This paper identifies the conical defects appearing in the spindle geometries as generalised Gukov-Witten defects. Similarly, in
[
5
,
6
,
17
,
18
,
27
,
24
]
, defects in the seven-dimensional spindle and disc solution were interpreted as co-dimension two disorder defects in the
\mathcal{N}=(2,0)
theory. A natural conjecture extending our work is that spindle and disc solutions in any dimension should be interpreted as adding co-dimension two defects to the bulk theory. In the four-dimensional gravitation setup, dual to the three-dimensional ABJM theory we make progress in this direction by identifying the conical defects in the spindle solution as vortex lines
[
54
]
. See also
[
35
]
for a holographic discussion of a class of closely related monodromy defects in ABJM. A similar interpretation should exist in the six-dimensional spindle solutions dual to the Seiberg SCFTs. These defects are not well studied in the literature making it an interesting objective for further research.

One notable difference with the setup discussed here and the five-dimensional spindle solutions is that in the latter setup the uplifted geometry is completely regular. This difference arises from our choice to have no conical defects at the boundary, which fixes the
z
-coordinate to be
2\pi
-periodic. Related to this, we expect that if a full holographic RG flow solution, connecting the spindle solution with an asymptotic
{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{5}
region, would be known, this solution would contain conical singularities on the
\mathbf{R}^{1,1}\times S^{2}
boundary. Forcing these singularities to be absent we expect to retrieve the conical singularities in the ten-dimensional bulk.

This difference is reminiscent of the story proposed in
[
55
]
, where two alternative prescriptions to describe surface defects were proposed. On the one hand, surface defects are described by specifying the singularities along a co-dimension two locus, similar as the disorder defects discussed in this work, while on the other hand they show that there is an equivalent description of these defects by including conical defects in space-time. It would be interesting to understand the connection of this work with our setup to make the above story more precise. A starting point to understand this setup would be to study the spindle index discussed in
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
. In these works ABJM is localised on
S^{1}\times\Sigma
, giving rise to an index that closely resembles the standard index on
S^{1}\times S^{2}
with the insertion of vortex lines at the poles of the
S^{2}
.

In this work we constructed five-dimensional supergravity solutions and subsequently uplifted them to ten dimensions. In the
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS case, we found that our solutions can be embedded into the bubbling surface defect setup and give rise to a subset of possible Gukov-Witten defects. The ten-dimensional setup is richer and allows for generalisation to holographically describe any Gukov-Witten surface defect in
\mathcal{N}=4
. It would be interesting to study the ten-dimensional setup corresponding to the
\frac{1}{4}
-BPS defects and embed them in a generalised bubbling ansatz derived in
[
59
]
. The PDEs arising in this setup are much more involved and a similar classification of boundary conditions is currently beyond reach. However, starting from the seed solutions provided by our five-dimensional setup could provide a concrete point to generalise our solutions and obtain holographic control over a extended class of surface operators.

Finally, in this work we discussed defect solutions with a conical defect in the bulk and conjectured these to be dual to a class of Gukov-Witten defects. Focussing on the
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS case, this class of defects are known as tame or regular defects, since they correspond to tame ramifications in an associated Hitchin system
[
28
,
29
]
. Similar to the class
\mathcal{S}
story, there is a whole set of irregular or wild punctures, corresponding to wild ramifications
[
60
]
. These defects are not well-studied hence it would be very interesting to get a holographic grip on them. A natural guess would be that they correspond to
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS disc-defect solutions with a disc-like boundary instead of a conical defect in the bulk. However, a preliminary analysis suggests that these solutions behave rather different than expected. We leave this as an open question for future explorations.

3  Holographic defect solutions

In the remainder of this work we will propose a holographic description of the (generalised) Gukov-Witten defects introduced above. Since these defects in general have large scaling weight, the probe approximation is not valid. In order to overcome this we will construct novel backreacted solutions corresponding to these defects. We proceed in two steps, first describing the solutions in maximal gauged supergravity in five dimensions and in the next section uplifting and analysing them in type IIB supergravity.

3.1  The gauged supergravity theory

Since we are studying solutions dual to surface operators in
\mathcal{N}=4

\mathrm{SO}(6)

3.3  Regularity

Before we continue, let us discuss the possible singularities arising in these solutions in some more detail. In the large
w

{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{5}

\mathcal{N}=4

The boundary is conformal to

7
7

7

Simple root:
For generic values of the parameters

Therefore we find that near the largest root the two dimensional metric represents a

(3.13)

Our solutions therefore correspond to orbibundles over an (infinite) disc with a conical singularity at the origin. To fully specify the
\mathrm{U}(1)^{3}

(\mathbf{R}^{2}\times(\mathbf{C}^{*})^{3})/\mathbf{Z}_{k}

\mathbf{Z}_{k}:(z,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\rightarrow\left(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{k}}z\,,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}m_{1}}{k}}x_{1}\,,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}m_{2}}{k}}x_{2}\,,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}m_{3}}{k}}x_{3}\right)\,,

(3.14)

where

\mathcal{N}=(2,0)
supersymmetry we have to demand that the orbifold action
\Gamma\in\mathrm{SU}(4)
. Hence, we have to demand that

. With extended

Double root:
In this case we have two possibilities. Either we have a double root at

and a simple root at
w<w_{0}
. In the former case we necessarily have to put
h_{2}=h_{3}=w
and

. In this case the metric near the double root at

(3.15)

where we introduced the coordinate

H(w)^{1/3}
also shrinks near this point so that we can still think of the space as capping off at

{\rm hol}_{0}(A^{(1)})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{w=0}A^{(1)}=\alpha_{I}-1\,.

(3.16)

When allowing for two or more non-trivial functions the double root can also occur at a non-zero value

. In all such cases the metric near the root reduces to the metric on the hyperbolic plane (compactified on a circle). This behaviour was already noticed as the limit of spindle solutions in
[

Triple root:
Similar to the double root there are two options. When

w=0
. Alternatively, with three non-vanishing parameters we also have the option to have a triple zero at a non-zero value

. In both these cases it is unclear what the physical interpretation of these singularities is hence we won’t discuss them further in the main text and refer to Appendix
B
for more details.

Comments on
Y^{(I)}
:
The above analysis is independent of whether the scalars
Y_{I}
are vanishing or not. However, when two or more
h_{I}
are non-trivial we do not have analytic solutions. Moreover, the analytic solution (
3.9
) does not have the appropriate behaviour near the root. For this reason we are limited to using numerical methods to analyse these cases. Turning on the additional scalars breaks some of the global symmetries including the rotation transverse to the defect, possibly to a discrete subgroup. It would be interesting to get better analytic control over these cases.

3.4  Supersymmetry

The solutions considered in this work preserve varying degrees of supersymmetry, depending on how many gauge fields are turned on
[
83
,
9
,
10
]
. The presence or absence of
Y_{I}
s does not significantly change this discussion. Each
Y_{I}
is associated to the breaking of a particular
\mathrm{U}(1)
, so the only constraint we impose is that the
Y_{I}
s associated to R-symmetries vanish. To simplify the discussion we therefore ignore the
Y
s in the following discussion. Similarly, in general we are allowed to turn on flat connections for non R-symmetries without altering the discussion below.

For ease of exposition, we simply summarise the various cases here, while an in depth discussion of the BPS equations is deferred to Appendix
A
. With vanishing
Y_{I}
, the preserved supersymmetry is entirely determined by the values of the parameters
q_{I}
. In the generic case, all
q_{I}
are distinct and non-vanishing. In this case the solution preserves
\mathcal{N}=(0,2)
superconformal symmetry (or
\mathcal{N}=(2,0)
depending on the choice of chirality of the preserved supercharge). Specialising to the case with one vanishing parameter, the solutions preserve
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
superconformal symmetry while the case with two vanishing parameters preserves
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
superconformal symmetry.

In addition to the supersymmetry, all solutions with vanishing
Y_{I}
preserve at least
\mathrm{U}(1)^{3}
global symmetry. if two of the parameters are equal, this symmetry in enhanced to
\mathrm{U}(1)^{2}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
, while with three equal parameters, the global symmetry is enhanced to
\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{SU}(3)
.

Finally, the flat connections associated to R-symmetries have to vanish. In the generic case this means that we need to impose
\sum_{I}\alpha_{I}=0
. In the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
case the R-symmetry is given in
[
9
]
. Lastly, when we preserve
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
superconformal symmetry, the Cartan generators of the two
\mathrm{SU}(2)
R-symmetries are given by the sum and difference of the
\mathrm{U}(1)
generators corresponding to the vanishing
q_{I}

q_{2}=q_{3}=0
, we have to impose
\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=0
.

4.1  Generic case

For the generic

Moreover, note that we have fixed the periodicity of the Reeb vector of

This once again corroborates the general idea put forward in this work, that disorder surface operators can be equivalently be described as a surface operator in flat space or by putting the theory on flat space with a conical deficit.

5  Holographic defect observables

In the previous section we identified our defect solutions as holographically describing a class of generalised Gukov-Witten surface defects. In this section we corroborate this conjecture by computing a variety of holographic observables in the presence of the defect. For the class of
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS defects we recover the expected field theory results, while for the
\frac{1}{4}
- and
\frac{1}{8}
-BPS defects our computations provide new predictions.

5.1  Symmetries and anomalies

In the defect setting, the bulk anomaly polynomial should be amended with a defect anomaly polynomial, localised at the location of the defect.

\mathcal{I}^{\rm bulk-defect}=\mathcal{I}_{6}^{\rm bulk}+\delta(X_{4})\,\mathcal{I}_{4}^{{\rm defect}}\,,

(5.1)

where in our case

\mathcal{I}^{\rm defect}_{4}=\frac{1}{2}k_{ab}c_{1}(F^{a})\wedge c_{1}(F^{b})-\frac{k}{24}p_{1}(R)\,,

(5.2)

The bulk + defect anomaly polynomial can be obtained systematically from IIB extending the anomaly inflow methods developed in

\mathcal{I}^{\rm bulk-defect}=\int\mathcal{I}_{11}

\mathcal{I}_{11}={\rm d}\mathcal{I}_{10}\,,\qquad\quad S_{\rm top.}^{\rm IIB}=\int{\rm vol}_{10}\mathcal{I}_{10}\,.

In the absence of a fluctuating axio-dilaton, the eleven form

5.2  Renormalised Action

Having discussed the symmetries and anomalies, a second interesting holographic observable is given by the on-shell action of the defect background. In the dual field theory this quantity is related to the partition function in the presence of the dual defect.

Inserting the equations of motion, we can rewrite the on-shell action as

S_{\mathrm{on-shell}}=-\frac{1}{16\pi G_{N}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{5}x\sqrt{|g|}\sum_{I=3}^{3}\left[\frac{1}{6}X_{I}^{-2}|F^{(I)}|^{2}+\frac{2}{3}V\right]+S_{\rm GH}\,.

(5.14)

where

S_{GH}=\frac{1}{8\pi G_{N}}\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}d^{4}x\sqrt{|\gamma|}K

(5.15)

is the usual Gibbons-Hawking boundary term. As usual, the on-shell action diverges and needs to be renormalised. Following
[
88
,
89
,
90
]
we can regularise the action by adding a superpotential and curvature counterterm to the action,

S_{W}=\frac{1}{8\pi G_{N}}\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\rm d}^{4}x\sqrt{|\gamma|}W\,,\qquad S_{\rm curv.}=\frac{1}{32\pi G_{N}}\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\rm d}^{4}x\sqrt{|\gamma|}R[\gamma]\,,

(5.16)

where
R[\gamma]
is the boundary Ricci tensor. Adding these counterterms to the diverging action, we obtain a finite result for the on-shell action. Inserting our solution we find,

S_{\rm ren.}=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{N}}\left(2w_{0}-\frac{3}{4}\right)\mathbf{V}_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}}\mathbf{V}_{S^{1}}

(5.17)

where
\mathbf{V}_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}}
is the regularised
{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}
volume and
\mathbf{V}_{S^{n}}
the volume of the
n
-sphere. Subtracting the contribution from the
{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}
vacuum solution we find the remaining defect contribution

S_{\rm defect}=\frac{1}{8\pi G_{N}}\left(w_{0}-1\right)\mathbf{V}_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}}\mathbf{V}_{S^{1}}\,.

(5.18)

The partition function in the presence of a defect scales as

\left\langle{1}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}=R^{b/3}\,,

(5.19)

where
R
here is the
{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}
length scale
[
40
,
44
]
. Comparing this with our on-shell action we have

\left\langle{1}\right\rangle_{\Sigma}=\mathrm{e}^{-S_{\rm defect}}=R^{\frac{\pi}{2G_{N}}(1-w_{0})}\,,

(5.20)

where we used the regularised volume
\mathbf{V}_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}}=-2\pi\log R
. As we will see below this matches the expected value of
b
.

More generally, we can expand the solution around the asymptotic boundary to explicitly observe the dependence on the sources and vevs. Using Fefferman-Graham coordinates, the metric can always be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\rho^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}+\frac{1}{\rho}g_{ab}{\rm d}x^{a}{\rm d}x^{b}

(5.21)

\displaystyle g_{ab}=

\displaystyle g_{(0),ab}+g_{(2),ab}\rho+g_{(4),ab}\rho^{2}+h_{(4),ab}\rho^{2}\log\rho+\dots\,.

Where
g_{(0),ab}
is the metric on
\partial\mathcal{M}
. Similarly, we can expand the scalar and gauge fields as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\log{X_{I}}&=(\chi_{1}^{I}+\chi_{2}^{I}\rho+\dotsm)\rho+(\zeta_{1}^{I}+\zeta_{2}^{I}\rho+\dotsm)\rho\log{\rho}\\
A^{(I)}&=(\alpha_{0}^{I}+\alpha_{1}^{I}\rho+\alpha_{2}^{I}\rho^{2}+\alpha_{3}^{I}\rho^{2}\log{\rho}+\dotsm)dz\end{split}

(5.22)

Where the expansion coefficients can be related to the vevs and sources of the dual fields. We can find the appropriate coordinate transformation from our solution perturbatively around
\rho=0
. Up to linear order in
\rho
it takes the form,

\displaystyle w=\frac{1}{\rho}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})

(5.23)

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{144}\left[8(q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}+q_{3}^{2}-q_{1}q_{2}-q_{1}q_{3}-q_{2}q_{3})-24(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})-9\right]+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{2})\,,

Comparing with our solution we immediately see that there are no sources turned on for the scalar fields, while the monodromy parameters
\alpha_{I}
provide sources for the gauge fields.

Finally, in the above we restricted to the case with vanishing
Y_{I}
. However, note that the analysis remains identical with non-vanishing
Y_{I}
. In particular, the same counterterms should cancel all divergences. However, unlike the inflow analysis above, the approach here requires having an explicit solution. For the explicit solution (
3.9
) we evaluated the on-shell action and found that it remains unchanged.

5.3  Stress-Energy Tensor 1-pt function

With the on-shell action at hand we can compute a variety of one-point functions by differentiating with respect to the relevant source and subsequently setting the source to zero
[
89
,
91
]
. Schematically we have

\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g^{(0)}|}}\frac{\delta S_{\rm ren}}{\delta J}\Big|_{J=0}\,.

(5.24)

In particular, we are interested in computing the stress tensor one-point function in the presence of a defect,

\langle T_{ij}\rangle=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{|g_{(0)}|}}\frac{\delta S_{\rm ren}}{\delta g_{(0)}^{ij}}

(5.25)

Evaluating the resulting expressions on our background yields

\langle T_{ij}\rangle dx^{i}dx^{j}=-\frac{1}{16}\left[1-\frac{8}{3}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})\right](ds_{AdS_{3}}^{2}-3dz^{2})

(5.26)

which is traceless, as expected for a conformal defect. The first term is the vacuum contribution, corresponding to
q_{I}=0
. Subtracting the vacuum contribution, we find the defect contribution to be

\langle T_{ij}\rangle\big|_{\Sigma}dx^{i}dx^{j}=\frac{1}{6}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})(ds_{AdS_{3}}^{2}-3dz^{2})

(5.27)

As discussed in Section
2
the stress tensor one-point function is related to the scaling weight of the defect, which is given by

h=\frac{1}{6}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})

(5.28)

The scaling weight in turn is related to the anomaly coefficient
d_{2}
through (
2.10
). Assuming the ANEC holds in the presence of defects yields the condition
d_{2}\leq 0
, meaning that for a physical defect the parameters
q_{I}
must obey the sign constraint

q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}\geq 0

(5.29)

It can be shown that this constraint is indeed obeyed by all
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS and
\frac{1}{4}
-BPS spindle solutions, while for the
\frac{1}{8}
-BPS solution with three independent parameters
q_{I}
it is an additional condition that must be imposed.

5.4  Defect Entanglement Entropy

As a final defect observable we consider the defect entanglement entropy. This section closely follows the formalism of
[
92
]
adjusted to our context. In a holographic setting the EE is computed using the Ryu-Takayanagi (RT) formula
[
93
]
:

S_{EE}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{min}}{4G_{N}}

(5.30)

Where
\mathcal{A}_{min}
is the area of the extremal surface on a fixed time slice that minimizes the bulk area functional, anchored to the entangling region on the boundary, and
G_{N}
is the gravitational constant in the bulk theory. By using the Ryu-Takayanagi formula we can reduce the problem of calculating the entanglement entropy in the defect boundary theory to a purely geometric problem.

We take the entangling region to be a Euclidean 3-ball
\mathcal{B}
of radius
R
centred on the defect. The bulk geometry belongs to a class of ten-dimensional metrics which can be written in the form

{\rm d}s^{2}=s(w,x^{a})^{2}{\rm d}s_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{d}}^{2}+\rho(w,x^{a})^{2}{\rm d}w^{2}+G_{bc}(w,x^{a}){\rm d}x^{b}{\rm d}x^{c}\,,

(5.31)

Where the
x^{a}
are coordinates on a compact manifold
\mathcal{M}_{9-d}
with metric
G_{ab}
, and the
{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}
component is written in Poincaré coordinates

ds^{2}_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{d}}=\frac{1}{u^{2}}({\rm d}u^{2}-{\rm d}t^{2}+{\rm d}r^{2}+r^{2}{\rm d}\Omega_{d-3})

(5.32)

With the defect located at the boundary
u=0
. Parametrising the hypersurface as
u=u(r,w,x^{a})
, the area functional is given by:

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}=\mathbf{V}_{S^{d-3}}\int&{\rm d}x^{a}{\rm d}r{\rm d}w\sqrt{\mathrm{det}G}\\
&\times r^{d-3}\rho\left(\frac{s}{x}\right)^{d-2}\sqrt{1+(\partial_{r}x)^{2}+\frac{s^{2}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{(\partial_{w}x)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}+G^{ab}\partial_{a}x\partial_{b}x\right)}\end{split}

(5.33)

Without loss of generality we can pick a time slice
t=0
. It was shown in
[
94
]
that the extremal surface obtained by variations of the above functional satisfies
w^{2}+r^{2}=R^{2}
, and furthermore that this is a global minimum of the area. The minimal area is then given by

\mathcal{A}_{\rm min}=\mathbf{V}_{S^{d-3}}R\int{\rm d}x^{a}{\rm d}u{\rm d}w\sqrt{\det G}\rho s^{d-2}\frac{(R^{2}-x^{2})^{(d-4)/2}}{x^{d-2}}

(5.34)

Note that the integral neatly splits into an integral over
u
and an integral over
w
and
\mathcal{M}_{9-d}
.

The solution describing a surface operator has
d=3
, and the metric functions were found through the uplift in the previous section (see Section
4
). Note that for ease of computation in this section we redefine
\mu=\cos{\theta}
. Then, substituting and integrating over the angular coordinates
(z,\phi_{I})
yields

\mathcal{A}_{min}=32\pi^{4}R\int_{\epsilon_{x}}^{R}dx\frac{1}{x\sqrt{R^{2}-x^{2}}}\mathcal{I}=32\pi^{4}\left(\ln{\frac{2R}{\epsilon_{x}}}+\dotsm\right)\mathcal{I}

(5.35)

The cut-off
\epsilon_{x}
must be introduced to regulate the divergences caused by correlations between UV modes near the boundary of the entangling region
\partial\mathcal{B}
. Postponing momentarily the question of choosing appropriate integration bounds, the remaining integral
\mathcal{I}
is given by

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{4}\int{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\psi\int{\rm d}wf_{{\mathop{\rm AdS}\nolimits}_{3}}f_{w}f_{\phi_{1}}f_{\phi_{2}}f_{\phi_{3}}\sqrt{(f_{\theta\psi}^{4}-4f_{\psi}^{2}f_{\theta}^{2})\left(\frac{f_{z\phi_{1}}^{4}}{f_{\phi_{1}}^{2}}+\frac{f_{z\phi_{2}}^{4}}{f_{\phi_{2}}^{2}}+\frac{f_{z\phi_{3}}^{4}}{f_{\phi_{3}}^{2}}-4f_{z}^{2}\right)}

(5.36)

This integral looks rather intimidating, but plugging in the metric functions and simplifying yields the miraculously simple result

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\psi\int_{w_{0}}^{\infty}{\rm d}w\cos{\theta}^{3}\cos{\psi}\sin{\theta}\sin{\psi}\,,

(5.37)

The
w
integral is clearly divergent, as it runs from the root
w_{0}
up to infinity. We must then introduce a second regulator located at
\rho=\epsilon_{\rho}\ll 1
, with
\rho
the radial Fefferman-Graham coordinate. Since we are working in ten dimensions, the cut-off for the
w
integral is really a hypersurface
\Lambda
, which we can obtain using a ten-dimensional Fefferman-Graham transformation between
\rho
and
w
. The full transformation is discussed in Appendix
C
. The resulting cut-off can be written as,

\begin{split}\Lambda(\epsilon_{\rho},\theta,\psi)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{8}[&4+2\cos^{2}(\theta)(q_{1}+q_{2}\cos^{2}(\psi)+q_{3}\sin^{2}(\psi))\\
&-4(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})+2\sin^{2}(\theta)(q_{2}+q_{3})]+\dots\,.\end{split}

(5.38)

Performing the integration in (
5.37
) then yields

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{16}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})-w_{0}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\rho})

(5.39)

The divergence in
\epsilon_{\rho}
is eliminated by vacuum subtraction. Taking the limit
\epsilon_{\rho}\rightarrow 0
we finally obtain:

\mathcal{A}_{\min}-\mathcal{A}_{min,0}=2\pi^{4}\left[(1-w_{0})-\frac{1}{3}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})\right]\left(\ln{\frac{2R}{\epsilon_{x}}}+\dotsm\right)

(5.40)

To wrap up the calculation, we use the Ryu-Takayanagi formula and compare the result with the expression for the universal part of
S_{EE}
given in (
2.13
). We find

S_{EE}^{\mathrm{def}}\Big|_{\mathrm{log}}\equiv\frac{1}{3}\left[b+\frac{1}{3}d_{2}\right]=\frac{\pi^{4}}{2G_{N}^{(10)}}\left[(1-w_{0})-\frac{1}{3}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})\right]

(5.41)

Where
G_{N}^{(10)}
is the ten-dimensional gravitational constant.

Reintroducing
Y_{1}\neq 0
, the uplifted ten-dimensional metric is given in (
4.11
). It can be verified that the metric functions still simplify, but now the introduction of the new scalar breaks rotational invariance over the
\phi_{1}
coordinate. Therefore,
\mathcal{I}
is modified to become

\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\psi{\rm d}\phi_{1}\int_{w_{0}}^{\Lambda(\epsilon_{\rho},\theta,\phi_{1})}\cos^{3}\theta\cos\psi\sin\theta\sin\psi

(5.42)

Hence, the only difference from the previous calculation is the form of
\Lambda
. The appropriate Fefferman-Graham transformation is discussed in Appendix
C
. We find

\Lambda(\epsilon_{\rho},\theta,\phi_{1})=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{2}[2+q_{1}\cos^{2}\theta-2q_{1}+K\sin^{2}\theta\cos 2\phi_{1}]+\dotsm

(5.43)

Clearly, the integration over
\phi_{1}
makes the last term drop out, so we recover the same result as for the un-deformed case.

Summarising the above, and converting to field theory quantities, we find the
b
and
d_{2}
anomaly coefficients as

b=3N^{2}(1-w_{0}),\quad d_{2}=-3N^{2}(q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3})

(5.44)

where we used the holographic relations

G_{N}^{(10)}=\frac{1}{2}\pi^{6}l_{P}^{8}\,,\qquad L_{AdS}^{4}=\pi Nl_{P}^{4}\,,\qquad G_{N}^{(10)}=\mathbf{V}_{S^{5}}G_{N}^{(5)}\,.

(5.45)

It is now straightforward to compare these results with the expressions given in
(
2.14
)
for the
\frac{1}{2}
-BPS surface defects. Using the explicit identification between supergravity and defect parameters obtained in (
4.24
), we verify that our results match the
\frac{1}{2}-
BPS field theory calculation. In addition, our holographic analysis offers a predictions for the values of
b
and
d_{2}
for a large class
\frac{1}{4}
- and
\frac{1}{8}
-BPS surface defects.
