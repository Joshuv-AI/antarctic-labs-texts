Spectral properties of the Laplacian of Scale-Free Percolation models
Rajat Subhra Hazra, Nandan Malhotra

Abstract
. We consider scale-free percolation on a discrete torus 𝐕 N \mathbf{V}_{N} of size N N . Conditionally on an i. i. d. sequence of Pareto weights ( W i ) i ∈ 𝐕 N (W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}} with tail exponent τ − 1 > 0 \tau-1>0 , we connect any two points i i and j j on the torus with probability p i ​ j = W i ​ W j ‖ i − j ‖ α ∧ 1 p_{ij}=\frac{W_{i}W_{j}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\wedge 1 for some parameter α > 0 \alpha>0 . We focus on the (centred) Laplacian operator of this random graph and study its empirical spectral distribution. We explicitly identify the limiting distribution when α < 1 \alpha<1 and τ > 3 \tau>3 , in terms of the spectral distribution of some non-commutative unbounded operators.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, many random graph models have been proposed to model real-life networks. These models aim to capture three key properties that real-world networks exhibit: scale-free nature of the degree distribution, small-world property, and high clustering coefficients
(
van der Hofstad, 2023
)
. It is generally difficult to find random graph models which incorporate all the three features. Classical random graph models typically fail to capture scale-freeness, small-world behavior, and high clustering simultaneously. For instance, the Erdős-Rényi model only exhibits the small-world property, while models like Chung-Lu, Norros-Reittu, and preferential attachment models are scale-free (
Chung and Lu (2002)
,
Barabási and Albert (1999)
and small-world but have clustering coefficients that vanish as the network grows. In contrast, regular lattices have high clustering but large typical distances. The Watts-Strogatz model (
Watts and Strogatz (1998)
) was an early attempt to create a network with high clustering and small-world features, but it does not produce scale-free degree distributions.

Scale-free percolation, introduced in
Deijfen et al. (2013)
, blends ideas from long-range percolation (see e.g.
Berger (2002)
) with inhomogeneous random graphs such as the Norros–Reittu model. In this framework, vertices are positioned on the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
lattice, and each vertex
x
is independently assigned a random weight
W_{x}
. These weights follow a power-law distribution:

\mathbb{P}(W>w)=w^{-(\tau-1)}L(w),

where
\tau>1
and
L(w)
is a slowly varying function at infinity.

Edges between pairs of vertices
x
and
y
are added independently, with a probability that increases with the product of their weights and decreases with their Euclidean distance. The edge probability is given by

p_{xy}=1-\exp\left(-\lambda\frac{W_{x}W_{y}}{\|x-y\|^{\alpha}}\right),

(1.1)

where
\lambda,\alpha>0
are model parameters and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. This model has been proposed as a suitable representation for certain real-world systems, such as interbank networks, where both spatial structure and heavy-tailed connectivity distributions are relevant (
Deprez et al. (2015)
). Various properties of the model are now well known and we refer to the articles by
Jorritsma et al. (2024)
,
Cipriani and Salvi (2024)
,
Cipriani et al. (2025)
,
Heydenreich et al. (2017)
,
Dalmau and Salvi (2021)
for further references.

In recent times, there has been a lot of interest in the models which have connections probabilities similar to (
1.1
). Kernel-based spatial random graphs encompass a wide variety of classical random graph models where vertices are embedded in some metric space. In their simplest form (see
Jorritsma et al. (2023)
for a more complete exposition) they can be defined as follows. Let
V
be the vertex set of the graph and sample a collection of weights
(W_{i})_{i\in V}
, which are independent and identically distributed (i. i. d.), serving as marks on the vertices. Conditionally on the weights, two vertices
i
and
j
are connected by an undirected edge with probability

\mathbb{P}\left(i\leftrightarrow j\mid W_{i},W_{j}\right)=\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})\|i-j\|^{-\alpha}\wedge 1\,,

(1.2)

where
\kappa
is a symmetric kernel,
\|i-j\|
denotes the distance between the two vertices in the underlying metric space and
\alpha>0
is a constant parameter. In a recent article, the present authors with A. Cipriani and M. Salvi (
Cipriani et al. (2025)
) proved the spectral properties of the adjacency matrix when
\alpha<d
and the weights have a finite mean. In the above setting, the model was considered on a torus of side length
N
so that the adjacency operator as a matrix was easier to describe. In this article, we aim to extend this study to the case of a Laplacian matrix. Although our approach would extend to general kernel-based models, we shall stick to the product form kernel, that is,
\kappa(x,y)=xy
, so that the ideas can be clearly presented. It is one of the few cases where the limiting distribution can be explicitly described.

The Laplacian of a graph with
N
vertices is defined as
A_{N}-D_{N}
where
A_{N}
is the adjacency matrix and
D_{N}
is the diagonal matrix where the
i
-th diagonal entry corresponds to the
i
-th degree. When the entries of the matrix are not restricted to
0
or
1
, the matrix is also referred to as the
Markov matrix
(
Bryc et al. (2006)
,
Bordenave et al. (2014)
). The graph Laplacian serves as the discrete analogue of the continuous Laplacian, essential in diffusion theory and network flow analysis. The Laplacian matrix has several key applications: The Kirchhoff Matrix-Tree Theorem relates the determinant of the Laplacian to the count of spanning trees in a graph (
Chung (1997)
), the multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue indicates the number of connected components (
Chung (1997)
), the second-smallest eigenvalue, known as the Fiedler value or the algebraic connectivity, measures the graph’s connectivity; higher values signify stronger connectivity
De Abreu (2007)
. For a comprehensive overview of spectral methods in graph theory, refer to Chung’s monograph
Chung (1997)
and Spielman’s book
Spielman (2012)
. In modern machine learning, spectral techniques are pivotal in spectral clustering algorithms, where the techniques use the Laplacian eigenvalues and eigenvectors for dimensionality reduction before applying clustering algorithms like
k
-means (
Abbe et al. (2020)
,
Abbe (2018)
). It is particularly effective for detecting clusters that are not linearly separable. Recent advancements integrate spectral clustering with graph neural networks to enhance graph pooling operations (
Bianchi et al. (2020)
). Spectral algorithms are also crucial for identifying communities within networks by analysing the spectral properties of the graph (
Chung (1997)
).

Bryc et al. (2006)
established that for large symmetric matrices with independent and identically distributed entries, the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of the corresponding Laplacian matrix converges to the free convolution of the semicircle law and the standard Gaussian distribution. In the context of sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs,
Huang and Landon (2020)
studied the local law of the ESD of the Laplacian matrix. They demonstrated that the Stieltjes transform of the ESD closely approximates that of the free convolution of the semicircle law and a standard Gaussian distribution down to the scale
N^{-1}
. Additionally, they showed that the gap statistics and averaged correlation functions align with those of the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble in the bulk.
Ding and Jiang (2010)
investigated the spectral distributions of adjacency and Laplacian matrices of random graphs, assuming that the variance of the entries of an
N\times N
adjacency matrix depends only on
N
. They established the convergence of the ESD of these matrices under such conditions. These results of the Erdős-Rényi random graphs were extended to the inhomogeneous setting by
Chakrabarty et al. (2021)
. In a recent work,
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022)
derived a combinatorial way to describe the limiting moments for a wide variety of random matrix models with a variance profile.

Our contribution:

The empirical spectral distribution of the (centred) Laplacian of a graph that incorporates spatial distance has not been studied before. For example, we are not aware of a result that describes the spectral properties of the Laplacian for the long-range percolation model. Our main contribution is that we establish this result for the scale-free percolation model on the torus. We restrict ourselves to the dense regime, that is,
\alpha<1
. We show that under mild assumptions on the weights (having finite variance), we establish the existence of the limiting distribution. It turns out to be a distribution that involves the Gaussian, the semicircle, and the Pareto distribution. In a symbolic (and rather informal) way, it is given by the spectral law of

W^{1/2}SW^{1/2}+\sqrt{m_{1}}W^{1/4}GW^{1/4},

where
W
is an unbounded operator with spectral law given by the Pareto distribution,
S
is a bounded compact operator whose spectral law is the semicircle law, and
G
is an unbounded operator whose law is given by the Gaussian distribution. Finally,
m_{1}
is the first moment of
W
. The interaction between these operators comes from the fact that in the non-commutative space,
\{W,G\}
is a commutative algebra, freely independent of
S
. Similar results have been established when the weights are bounded and degenerate, and no spatial distances are involved (
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022)
and
Chakrabarty et al. (2021)
). The present work extends the results to settings that involve random heavy-tailed weights and spatial distances. Both the conditions require many new techniques.

Outline of the article:

In section
2
we explicitly describe the setup of the model and state our main results. In Theorem
2.1
we show the existence of the limiting spectral distribution, and in Theorem
2.5
, we identify the measure and state some of its properties. In Section
3
we first introduce a Gaussianised version of the matrix, and this helps us to simplify the variance profile. We then truncate the weights and decouple the diagonal, which allows us to apply the moment method. In Section
4
we identify the limiting moments of the decoupled Laplacian and show that it does not depend on the spatial parameter
\alpha>0
, which is crucial in the identification of the limiting measure of the original Laplacian. Finally, in Section
5
we identify the limiting measure using results from free probability.
In Appendix
6
we provide references to some of the results we use in our proofs, which are collections of results from other articles and are stated here for completeness.

2.
Setup and main results

In this section we describe the setup of the model and also state the main results.

2.1.
Setup

(a)

Vertex set:
the vertex set is
\mathbf{V}_{N}\coloneqq\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,N\}
. The vertex set is equipped with torus the distance
\|i-j\|
, where

\|i-j\|=|i-j|\wedge(N-|i-j|).

(b)

Weights:
the weights
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
are i. i. d. random variables sampled from a Pareto distribution
W
(whose law we denote by
\mathbf{P}
) with parameter
\tau-1
, where
\tau>1
. That is,

\mathbf{P}(W>t)=t^{-(\tau-1)}\one_{\{t\geq 1\}}+\one_{\{t<1\}}.

(2.1)

(c)

Long-range parameter:

\alpha>0
is a parameter which controls the influence of the distance between vertices on their connection probability.

(d)

Connectivity function:
conditional on the weights, each pair of distinct vertices
i
and
j
is connected independently with probability
P^{W}(i\leftrightarrow j)
given by

P^{W}(i\leftrightarrow j)\coloneqq p_{ij}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(i\leftrightarrow j\mid W_{i},W_{j})=\frac{W_{i}W_{j}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\wedge 1.

(2.2)

We will be using the short-hand notation
p_{ij}
for convenience. That is,
Note that the graph does not have self-loops.

In what follows, we denote by
\mathbb{P}
the joint law of the weights and the edge variables. Note that
\mathbb{P}
depends on
N
, but we will omit this dependence for simplicity. Let
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$},\mathbf{E}
, and
E^{W}
denote the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P},\mathbf{P}
, and
P^{W}
respectively.

The associated graph is connected, as nearest neighbours with respect to the torus distance are always linked. Let us denote the random graph generated by our choice of edge probabilities by
\mathbb{G}_{N}
. Let
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}
denote the adjacency matrix (operator) associated with this random graph, defined as

\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\leftrightarrow j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since the graph is finite and undirected, the adjacency matrix is always self-adjoint and has real eigenvalues.
Let

\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}=\diag(d_{1},\cdots,d_{N})

where
d_{i}
denotes the degree of the vertex
i
and in this case given by

d_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}(i,j).

We will consider the Laplacian of the matrix, which is denoted as follows:

{\Delta}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}=\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}-\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}.

In general, when
0<\alpha<1
, the eigenvalue distribution requires scaling in order to observe meaningful limiting behavior. In
Cipriani et al. (2025)
, it was shown that an appropriate scaling of the adjacency matrix, under which the convergence of the bulk eigenvalue distribution can be studied, is given by

c_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}\frac{1}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\sim c_{0}N^{1-\alpha},

(2.3)

where
c_{0}
is a positive constant and
0<\alpha<1
. The scaled adjacency matrix is then defined as

\mathbf{A}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{G}_{N}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}.

(2.4)

We define the corresponding (scaled) Laplacian as

\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}=\mathbf{A}_{N}-\mathbf{D}_{N},

where
\mathbf{D}_{N}
is given by

\mathbf{D}_{N}=\diag(\mathbf{d}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{d}_{N})

with

\mathbf{d}_{i}=\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{N}(i,k).

The empirical measure that assigns a mass of
1/N
to each eigenvalue of the
N\times N
random matrix
\mathbf{M}_{N}
is called the Empirical Spectral Distribution (ESD) of
\mathbf{M}_{N}
, denoted as

\ESD\left(\mathbf{M}_{N}\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}},

where
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{N}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}_{N}
.
We are interested in the centred Laplacian matrix for the bulk distribution. So define

\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}=\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}]

(2.5)

where
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}](i,j)=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}(i,j)]
. If we define
\mathbf{A}_{N}^{\circ}=\mathbf{A}_{N}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mathbf{A}_{N}]
and
\mathbf{D}_{N}^{\circ}
is the diagonal matrix
\diag(\mathbf{d}_{1}^{\circ},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{N}^{\circ})
where
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\circ}=\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{N}^{\circ}(i,k)
, then it is easy to see that

\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}=\mathbf{A}_{N}^{\circ}-\mathbf{D}_{N}^{\circ}.

In this article we will be interested in understanding the behaviour of
\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ})
as
N\to\infty
.

2.2.
Main Results

The Lévy-Prokhorov distance
d_{L}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined as

d_{L}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\big\{\varepsilon>0\mid\mu(A)\leq\nu\left(A^{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon\,\text{ and }\,\nu(A)\leq\mu\left(A^{\varepsilon}\right)+\varepsilon\quad\forall\,A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\big\},

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
, and
A^{\varepsilon}
is the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
A
. For a sequence of random probability measures
(\mu_{N})_{N\geq 0}
, we say that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu_{N}=\mu_{0}\text{ in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability}

if, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(d_{L}(\mu_{N},\mu_{0})>\varepsilon)=0.

Our first main result is existential, and is as follows.

Theorem 2.1.

Consider the random graph
\mathbb{G}_{N}
on
\mathbf{V}_{N}
with connection probabilities given by (
2.2
) with parameters
\tau>3
and
0<\alpha<1
. Let
\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ})
be the empirical spectral distribution of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}
defined in (
2.5
). Then there exists a deterministic measure
\nu_{\tau}
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{o})=\nu_{\tau}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

The characterisation of
\nu_{\tau}
is achieved by results from the theory of free probability. For convenience, we state some technical definitions. We refer the readers to
(
Anderson et al., 2010
, Chapter 5)
and
Mingo and Speicher (2017)
for further details.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a
W^{*}
-probability space, where
\mathcal{A}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded operators on a Hilbert space (closed in the weak operator topology), and
\varphi
is a state. A densely defined operator
T
is said to be
affiliated
with
\mathcal{A}
if for every bounded measurable function
h
, we have
h(T)\in\mathcal{A}
. The law (or distribution)
\mathcal{L}(T)
of such an affiliated operator
T
is the unique probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\varphi(h(T))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)\,d\mathcal{L}(T)(x).

For a collection of self-adjoint operators
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
, their joint distribution is described by specifying

\varphi(h_{1}(T_{i_{1}}),\ldots,h_{k}(T_{i_{k}})),

for all
k\geq 1
, all index sequences
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and all bounded measurable functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a
W^{*}
-probability space, and suppose
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are said to be
freely independent
if

\varphi(p_{1}(a_{i_{1}}),\ldots,p_{n}(a_{i_{n}}))=0,

for every
n\geq 1
, every sequence
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\in\{1,2\}
with
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, and all polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
in one variable satisfying

\varphi(p_{j}(a_{i_{j}}))=0,\quad\text{for all }j=1,\ldots,n.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
be operators affiliated with
\mathcal{A}
. The families
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
and
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
are freely independent if and only if

p(h_{1}(a_{1}),\ldots,h_{k}(a_{k}))\quad\text{and}\quad q(g_{1}(b_{1}),\ldots,g_{m}(b_{m}))

are freely independent for all bounded measurable functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
, and for all polynomials
p
and
q
in
k
and
m
non-commutative variables, respectively.

We are now ready to state our second main result.

Theorem 2.5.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, the limiting measure
\nu_{\tau}
can be identified as

\nu_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}\left(T_{W}^{1/2}T_{s}T_{W}^{1/2}+\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W]}T_{W}^{1/4}T_{g}T_{W}^{1/4}\right).

Here,
T_{g}
and
T_{W}
are commuting self-adjoint operators affiliated with a
W^{\star}
-probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
such that, for bounded measurable functions
h_{1},h_{2}
from
\mathbb{R}
to itself,

\varphi\left(h_{1}\left(T_{g}\right)h_{2}\left(T_{W}\right)\right)=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h_{1}(x)\phi(x)dx\right)\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}h_{2}(u)(\tau-1)u^{-\tau}du\right)

with
\phi
the standard normal density. The integral factorization is simply classical independence of the commuting selfadjoint operators
T_{g}
and
T_{W}
. Furthermore,
T_{s}
has a standard semicircle law and is freely independent of
\left(T_{g},T_{W}\right)
.

In particular, when
W
is degenerate, say
W\equiv 1
, then
\nu_{\tau}
is given by the free additive convolution of semicircle and Gaussian law.

2.3.
Discussion and simulations.

(a)

We now briefly describe the main steps of the proof.

1.

Gaussianisation.
In the first step, we show that replacing the Bernoulli entries with Gaussian entries having the same mean and variance results in empirical spectral distributions that are close.

2.

Simplification of the variance profile.
In this step, we show that the variance profile can be simplified to
W_{i}W_{j}/\|i-j\|^{\alpha}
, effectively removing the truncation at
1
.

3.

Truncation.
Here, we show that in the Gaussian matrix, the weights
W_{i}
can be replaced by the truncated weights
W_{i}^{m}=W_{i}\one_{W_{i}\leq m}
.

4.

Decoupling the diagonal.
In this step, we show that the Laplacian can be viewed as the sum of two independent random matrices (conditionally on the weights). Thus, we replace the diagonal matrix
D_{N}
with an independent copy
Y_{N}
, which has the same variance profile.

5.

Moment method.
With truncated weights and decoupled matrices, we apply the moment method to show convergence of the empirical spectral distribution and identify the limiting moments. A key observation is that the limiting measure and the method are independent of
\alpha
, so the results remain valid even when
\alpha=0
.

6.

Identification of the limiting measure.
Finally, we first identify the limiting measure in the case of truncated weights. These are typically associated with bounded operators (except in the Gaussian case). We then use techniques from
Bercovici and Voiculescu (1993)
to remove the truncation and identify the limiting measure in the general case.

(b)

We now present some simulations that illustrate how the proof outline aligns with a specific value of
\alpha
. In Figure

, we plot the eigenvalue distribution of the centred Laplacian matrix, with the parameter range

We remark that our results can be extended in two directions. Although we state and prove them for the case
d=1
and
\alpha<1
, they naturally generalize to any dimension
d\geq 1
and
\alpha<d
. In that case, the scaling constant requires an adjustment, with

to

\frac{\kappa_{\sigma}(W_{i},W_{j})}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\wedge 1,

3.
Gaussianisation: Setup for the proof of Theorem
2.1

To prove Theorem
2.1
, we construct a Laplacian matrix with truncated weights and a simplified variance profile, with the diagonal decoupled from the adjacency matrix. We follow the ideas of
Cipriani et al. (2025)
, albeit with a slightly modified approach, as follows:

(1)

We begin by Gaussianising the matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{o}
to obtain a matrix
\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}
, using the ideas of
Chatterjee (2005)
. Since we have
\tau>3
, the proof proceeds without the need to truncate the weight sequence
\{W_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
.

(2)

We then tweak the entries of
\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}
further through a series of lemmas to obtain the Laplacian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
, whose corresponding adjacency has mean-zero Gaussian entries and a simplified variance profile.

(3)

Next, we truncate the weights
\{W_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
at
m\geq 1
, and construct the corresponding matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}
. We show that, in
\mathbb{P}
-probability, the Lévy distance vanishes in the iterated limit
m\to\infty
and
N\to\infty
.

(4)

We conclude by decoupling the diagonal of the matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}
from the off-diagonal terms. This follows from classical results used in studying the spectrum of Laplacian matrices.

3.1.
Gaussianisation

Suppose
(G_{i,j})_{i>j}
is a sequence of i. i. d.
\gauss(0,1)
random variables and independent of the sequence
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
. Define

\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{p_{ij}(1-p_{ij})}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}+\frac{\mu_{ij}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}&i\neq j\\
0&i=j,\end{cases}

where
\mu_{ij}=p_{ij}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[p_{ij}]
. Let
\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}
be the corresponding Laplacian of the matrix
\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}
. Let
h
be a
3
times differentiable function on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\max_{0\leq k\leq 3}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|h^{(k)}(x)|<\infty
, where
h^{(k)}
is the
k
-th derivative of
h
. Define the resolvent of the
N\times N
matrix
\mathbf{M}_{N}
as

R_{M_{N}}(z)=\left(\mathbf{M}_{N}-z\Id_{N}\right)^{-1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where
\Id_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix and
\mathbb{C}^{+}
is the upper-half complex plane. Further, define

H_{z}(\mathbf{M}_{N})=\St_{M_{N}}(z)=\tr(R_{M_{N}}(z))
for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

Lemma 3.1 (Gaussianisation of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}
).

Consider
\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{o}
defined as above. Then for any
h
as above,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[h(\Re H_{z}(\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}))]-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[h(\Re H_{z}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{o}))]\right|=0\,,

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[h(\Im H_{z}(\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}))]-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[h(\Im H_{z}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{o}))]\right|=0\,.

The proof is very similar to the one presented in
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022)
and is modified along the lines of
Cipriani et al. (2025)
and hence it is moved to Appendix
6
. It uses the classical result of
Chatterjee (2005)
, and we only give a brief sketch by showing the estimates of the error probabilities in this setting. In
Cipriani et al. (2025)
, the Gaussianisation was done with truncated weights, but here we will not need that.

3.2.
Simplification of the variance profile.

We now proceed with a series of lemmas to simplify the variance profile of our Gaussianised matrix. First, we construct a new matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}
as the Laplacian corresponding to the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{N,g}
, defined as follows:

Suppose
(G_{i,j})_{i>j}
is a sequence of i. i. d.
\gauss(0,1)
random variables as before, and independent of the sequence
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
. Define

\mathbf{A}_{N,g}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{p_{ij}(1-p_{ij})}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}&i\neq j\\
0&i=j.\end{cases}

We now have the following result.

Lemma 3.2.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}
be as defined above. Then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(d_{L}(\ESD(\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N}),\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}))>\varepsilon)=0.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

By the Hoffman–Wielandt inequality (Proposition
6.1
),

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Big[d_{L}^{3}\big(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}),\ESD(\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\,\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Tr\Big((\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}-\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\Big(\sum_{k\neq i}(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,k))\Big)^{2}\right].

Recall that for
i\neq j
,

\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{p_{ij}(1-p_{ij})}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\;G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}+\frac{\mu_{ij}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}},\qquad\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{p_{ij}(1-p_{ij})}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\;G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j},

with
\mu_{ij}:=p_{ij}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[p_{ij}]
and
c_{N}
as in (
2.3
). Hence

\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,j)=-\frac{\mu_{ij}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}.

Off–diagonal term.

Using
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mu_{ij}^{2}]\leq\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[p_{ij}^{2}]
and the bound
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[p_{ij}^{2}]\leq C\|i-j\|^{-2\alpha}
(from
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W^{2}]<\infty
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{N}(i,j))^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mu_{ij}^{2}]\leq C\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\|i-j\|^{2\alpha}}.

By Lemma
6.4
with
\beta=2\alpha
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\|i-j\|^{2\alpha}}\asymp\begin{cases}N^{1-2\alpha},&0<\alpha<\tfrac{1}{2},\\[2.0pt]
\log N,&\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2},\\[2.0pt]
1,&\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

while
c_{N}\asymp N^{1-\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
. Therefore

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(A_{N,g}(i,j)-\bar{A}_{N}(i,j))^{2}\right]\leq C\begin{cases}N^{-\alpha},&0<\alpha<\tfrac{1}{2},\\[2.0pt]
(\log N)\,N^{-1/2},&\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2},\\[2.0pt]
N^{-(1-\alpha)},&\tfrac{1}{2}<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

(3.1)

and clearly, all the cases go to zero.

Diagonal (row–sum) term.

Set
S_{i}:=\sum_{k\neq i}\mu_{ik}
, so that

\sum_{k\neq i}(A_{N,g}(i,k)-\bar{A}_{N}(i,k))=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\,S_{i}.

Thus

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\!\left[\Big(\sum_{k\neq i}(A_{N,g}(i,k)-\bar{A}_{N}(i,k))\Big)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\big[S_{i}^{2}\big].

We bound
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[S_{i}^{2}]=\Var\!\big(\sum_{k\neq i}p_{ik}\big)
by the Efron–Stein inequality (
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
, Theorem 3.1)
) applied to the independent family
\{W_{k}:k\neq i\}
(with
W_{i}
kept fixed as a parameter):

\Var\!\Big(\sum_{k\neq i}p_{ik}\Big)\ \leq\ \sum_{k\neq i}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Big[\big(p_{ik}(W_{i},W_{k})-p_{ik}(W_{i},W_{k}^{\prime})\big)^{2}\Big]\ \leq\ 2\sum_{k\neq i}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\big[p_{ik}^{2}\big],

where
W_{k}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
W_{k}
. Using again
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[p_{ik}^{2}]\leq C\|i-k\|^{-2\alpha}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[S_{i}^{2}]\ \leq\ \frac{C}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq k}\frac{1}{\|i-k\|^{2\alpha}},

which is the same quantity already handled in the off–diagonal term. Hence it also goes to
0
at the rates displayed above.

Combining the two bounds gives

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Big[d_{L}^{3}\big(\ESD(\Delta_{N,g}),\ESD(\bar{\Delta}_{N})\big)\Big]\longrightarrow 0,

and the claim follows by Markov’s inequality.
∎

Define for
i\neq j

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{p_{ij}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}

and put zero on the diagonal. Here
(G_{i,j})_{i\geq j}
are the i. i. d.
\gauss(0,1)
random variables used in the previous result. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
be analogously defined.
The next lemma shows that
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
have asymptotically the same spectrum.

Lemma 3.3.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{L}(\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}),\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}))>\varepsilon\right)=0.

Proof.

Again using Propostion
6.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[d_{L}^{3}(\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}),\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Tr\left((\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}-\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}(i,j)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)^{2}\right]

Dealing with the last two terms separately as before, we proceed by bounding the first term. Since
G_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent of
(W_{i})

and of each other,

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\sqrt{p_{ij}}-\sqrt{p_{ij}\left(1-p_{ij}\right)}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[p_{ij}\left(1-\sqrt{1-p_{ij}}\right)^{2}\right].

Using
0\leq p\leq 1
and the elementary bound
0\leq 1-\sqrt{1-p}\leq p
we have
\big(1-\sqrt{1-p}\,\big)^{2}\leq p^{2}
, hence

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Big[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,j)-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\Big]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{c_{N}}\,\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\big[p_{ij}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C}{c_{N}}\,\frac{\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{2}]^{2}}{\|i-j\|^{2\alpha}},

(3.2)

Summing
(
3.2
) over
i\neq j
and applying Lemma
6.4
and using the bounds in (
3.1
) we have that the term goes to zero.

Expanding the square in the second term, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{\ell\neq i,k}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,\ell)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,\ell)\right)\right].

Again the first term in above sum is of the order
N^{-\alpha}
and the expectation in the second term is zero. Indeed, using the independence between
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
and
G_{i,j}
we have for
k\neq\ell
,

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g}(i,\ell)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,\ell)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\sqrt{p_{ik}(1-p_{ik})}-\sqrt{p_{ik}})(\sqrt{p_{i\ell}(1-p_{i\ell})}-\sqrt{p_{i\ell}})\right]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[G_{i\wedge k,i\vee k}G_{i\wedge\ell,i\vee\ell}\right]=0,

where the last term is zero for
\ell\notin\{i,k\}
due to independence. This completes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

We conclude this subsection with one final simplification. For any
i\neq j
, let

r_{ij}=\frac{W_{i}W_{j}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\,,

and let
r_{ii}=0
. Define the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}
as follows: for
i\neq j
,

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{r_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}

and put
0
on the diagonal. Define Laplacian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
accordingly with
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}
.

Lemma 3.4.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{L}(\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}),\ESD(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}))>\varepsilon\right)=0.

Proof.

For any
1\leq i\neq j\leq N
, define the set
\mathcal{C}_{ij}=\{r_{ij}<1\}
. Let
(X_{i,j})_{i\geq j}
be defined as follows

X_{ij}=\frac{\sqrt{r_{ij}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G^{\prime}_{ij},

where
(G^{\prime}_{ij})_{i\geq j}
be a sequence of independent
\gauss(0,1)
random variables, independent of the previously defined
(G_{ij})
and
(W_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
. Define a symmetric matrix
L_{N,g}
as follows: for
1\leq i<j\leq N
,

L_{N,g}(i,j)=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}}+X_{ij}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c}}.

We put zero on the diagonal and consider the
\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}
as the Laplacian matrix corresponding to
L_{N,g}
. Note that
L_{N,g}
has the same distribution as
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}
and hence the
\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}
has the same distribution as
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
.

By Proposition
6.1
, we again have

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[d^{3}\left(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}),\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g})\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}(i,j)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(L_{N,g}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\sum_{k\neq i}L_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(L_{N,g}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{\ell\neq i,k}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(L_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)\left(L_{N,g}(i,\ell)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,\ell)\right)\right]

Again, we deal with the two sums separately. The first sum can be bounded above as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{4}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(L_{N,g}(i,j)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)-X_{ij})^{2}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)^{2}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}}\right]+\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[X_{ij}^{2}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}^{N}\mathbf{E}[G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}^{2}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c}}]+\mathbf{E}[X_{ij}^{2}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}^{N}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c})+\mathbf{E}[X_{ij}^{4}]^{1/2}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}^{N}\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c})+\frac{3\mathbf{E}[W_{i}^{2}W_{j}^{2}]^{1/2}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\,\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C(N^{-\alpha(\tau-2)}+N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}(\tau-1)})=\mathrm{o}_{N}(1),

where we have used in the last line the following estimate:

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c})\leq\mathbf{P}\left(W_{i}W_{j}\geq\|i-j\|^{\alpha}\right)\leq\frac{c}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha(\tau-1)}}

which follows from Lemma
6.2
. For the second term note that

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(L_{N,g}(i,k)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)\left(L_{N,g}(i,\ell)-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,\ell)\right)\right]=\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbf{E}[\sqrt{p_{ik}}\sqrt{p_{i\ell}}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c}}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{i\ell}^{c}}]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{ik}G_{i\ell}]

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbf{E}[\sqrt{p_{ik}}\sqrt{r_{i\ell}}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{c}}\one_{\mathcal{C}_{i\ell}^{c}}]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{ik}G_{i\ell}^{\prime}]-\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbf{E}[\sqrt{r_{ik}}\sqrt{p_{i\ell}}\one_{C_{ij}^{c}}\one_{C_{i\ell}^{c}}]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{ik}^{\prime}G_{i\ell}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{c_{N}}\mathbf{E}[\sqrt{r_{ik}}\sqrt{r_{i\ell}}\one_{C_{ij}^{c}}\one_{C_{i\ell}^{c}}]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{ik}^{\prime}G_{i\ell}^{\prime}],

and since
k\neq\ell
, all the above terms are zero. Thus the proof follows.
∎

3.3.
Truncation

Let
m>1
be a truncation threshold and define
W_{i}^{m}=W_{i}\one_{W_{i}\leq m}
for any
i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}
. For all
N\in\N
, we define a new random matrix as follows:
Let

r_{ij}^{m}=\frac{W_{i}^{m}W_{j}^{m}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\qquad i\neq j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}\,,

and let
\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}
be defined for
i\neq j
as

\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{r_{ij}^{m}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j},

and put
0
on the diagonal. Analogously define
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}
.

Lemma 3.5 (Truncation).

For every
\delta>0
one has

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{L}(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}),\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}))>\delta\right)=0\,.

Proof.

The proof follows the same idea as the previous lemmas. Recall that

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j)=\frac{\sqrt{r_{ij}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j}

for all
i\neq j
, with 0 on the diagonal, and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
is the corresponding Laplacian. Once again, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[d^{3}\left(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}),\ESD(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m}(i,j)-\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}(i,j)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,j)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\sum_{k\neq i}\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,k)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,j)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq i}\sum_{\ell\neq i,k}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,k)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,k)\right)\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,\ell)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,\ell)\right)\right].

The proof of Lemma
3.4
aids us by taking care of the second factor in the last line, which turns out to be equal to 0 by the independence of Gaussian terms. For the first term, the common Gaussian factor pulls out by independence, yielding the upper bound

\frac{4}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\sqrt{W_{i}W_{j}}-\sqrt{W_{i}^{m}W_{j}^{m}}\right)^{2}\right]}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{4}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{\mathbf{E}[W_{i}W_{j}-W_{i}^{m}W_{j}^{m}]}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}},

where the inequality follows by using the identity
(a-b)^{2}\leq|a^{2}-b^{2}|
for any
a,b\geq 1
. Adding and subtracting the term
W_{i}W_{j}^{m}
inside the expectation gives us that

\displaystyle\frac{4}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}(i,j)-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{N,g}(i,j))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{\mathbf{E}[W_{i}]\mathbf{E}[W_{j}\one_{\{W_{j}>m\}}]+\mathbf{E}[W_{j}^{m}]\mathbf{E}[W_{i}\one_{\{W_{i}>m\}}]}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\tau}}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\frac{m^{2-\tau}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}=\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}_{m}(m^{2-\tau}),

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
6.3
, with
C_{\tau}
a
\tau-
dependent constant. Markov inequality concludes the proof.
∎

Decoupling

Since we now have bounded weights, the decoupling result follows from the arguments from

. See also the proof of
(
Chakrabarty et al., 2021

for the inhomogeneous extension.

Lemma 3.6.

Let
(Z_{i}:i\geq 1)
be a family of i. i. d. standard normal random variables, independent of
(G_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq j)
. Define a diagonal matrix
Y_{N}
of order

by

Y_{N}(i,i)=Z_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k\neq i}r_{ik}^{m}}{c_{N}}},\quad 1\leq i\leq N.

and let

\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}={\mathbf{A}}_{N,g,m}+Y_{N}.

(3.3)

Then for every
m>1
, and for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left[(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})^{2k}-(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m})^{2k}\right]\right)=0.

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left(\operatorname{Tr}^{2}\left[(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})^{k}\right]-\operatorname{Tr}^{2}\left[(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m})^{k}\right]\right)=0.

4.
Moment method: Proof of Theorem
2.1

We begin by stating a key proposition that describes the limit of the empirical spectral distribution of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}
. The majority of this section will be devoted to the proof of this proposition, and so, we defer the proof of the proposition to page
4
.

Proposition 4.1.

Let
\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})
be the empirical spectral distribution of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}
defined in (
3.3
). Then there exists a deterministic measure
\nu_{\tau}
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})=\nu_{\tau,m}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

We now use Proposition
4.1
and tools from Appendix
6
and Section
3
to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Combining Proposition
4.1
with Lemma
3.6
gives us that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,m})=\nu_{\tau,m}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

(4.1)

To show the existence of the limit
\nu_{\tau}:=\lim_{m\to\infty}\nu_{\tau,m}
, we wish to apply Lemma
6.5
. Equation (
4.1
) satisfies Condition
(1)
of Lemma
6.5
. Moreover, Condition
(2)
can be easily verified by Lemma
3.5
. Thus, there exists a unique limit
\nu_{\tau}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\tilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g})=\nu_{\tau}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

(4.2)

Combining equation (
4.2
) with Lemma
3.4
, and subsequently with Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\bar{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N})=\nu_{\tau}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

(4.3)

We now wish to show that the limiting empirical spectral distribution for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}
is
\nu_{\tau}
in
\mathbb{P}
–probability. To this end, note that for any
h
satisfying conditions of Lemma
3.1
, and
H_{z}
as in subsection
3.1
, we have by the means of Lemma
3.1
that

\lim_{N\to\infty}h\left(\Re(H_{z}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}))\right)=h\left(\Re\St_{\nu_{\tau}}(z)\right).

The above characterises convergence in law. However, since
\nu_{\tau}
is a deterministic measure, the above convergence holds in
\mathbb{P}
–probability, and analogously for
\Im(H_{z}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ}))
. This gives us that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\St_{\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ})}(z)=\St_{\nu_{\tau}}(z)\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

Since convergence of Stieltjes transforms characterises weak convergence, we obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ})=\nu_{\tau}\qquad\text{ in $\,\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,,

completing the proof.
∎

We now provide the proof of Proposition
4.1
. We borrow the main ideas of
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Section 5.2.1, 5.2.2)
, and adapt them to our setting using the results of
Cipriani et al. (2025, Section 4.4)
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

The proof of the moment method is valid when the weights are bounded, and so for notational convenience, in this proof we will drop the dependence on
m
from
\{r^{m}_{ij}\}_{i,j\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
. Thus, for the remainder of the proof, we have that

r_{ij}=\frac{W_{i}^{m}W_{j}^{m}}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha}}.

We apply the method of moments to show the convergence to the law
\nu_{\tau,m}
. The proof is split up into three parts as follows:

(1)

For any
k\geq 1
, we compute the expected moment

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{k}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})(\dd{x}),

and show that as
N\to\infty
, the above quantity converges to a value
0<M_{k}<\infty
for
k
even, and 0 otherwise.

(2)

We then show concentration by proving (under the law
\mathbb{P}
) that

\Var\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{k}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})(\dd{x})\right)\to 0\quad\text{as $N\to\infty$}.

(3)

Lastly, we show that the sequence
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
uniquely determises a limiting measure.

Step 1.

We begin by considering that
k
is even. By using the expansion for
(a+b)^{k}
, it is easy to see that

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{k}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})(\dd{x})=\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\Tr\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{k}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k},\\
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\end{subarray}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\Tr\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{1}}Y_{N}^{n_{1}}\ldots\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{k}}Y_{N}^{n_{k}}\right)\right],

where
\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}
and
Y_{N}
are as in Lemma
3.6
, and
\{m_{i},n_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
are such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}+n_{i}=k.

Let
M(p)
and
N(p)
be defined as

M(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}m_{i},\quad N(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}n_{i}

for any
1\leq p\leq k
. To expand the trace term, we sum over all
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{M(k)+N(k)+1})\in[N]^{M(k)+N(k)+1}
, where
[p]:=\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
, and we identify
i_{M(k)+N(k)+1}\equiv i_{1}
. Then, from
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Eq. 5.2.2)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\Tr\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{1}}Y_{N}^{n_{1}}\ldots\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{k}}Y_{N}^{n_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{M(k)}=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\,,

(4.4)

where also in (
4.4
) we identify
i_{M(k)+1}\equiv i_{1}
.
Taking expectation in (
4.4
), we have that

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\tr\left(\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{1}}Y_{N}^{n_{1}}\ldots\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{m_{k}}Y_{N}^{n_{k}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{M(k)}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\right]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}\right]\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\right].

(4.5)

It is well known that the expectation over a product of independent Gaussian random variables is simplified using the Wick’s formula (see Lemma
6.7
). In particular, if one were to partition the tuple
\{1,\ldots,K\}
for some nonnegative integer
K
, the contributing partitions are typically non-crossing pair partitions (
Nica and Speicher (2006)
).

We now introduce some notation from
Cipriani et al. (2025)
. For any fixed non-negative even integer
K
, let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)
and
NC_{2}(K)
be the set of all pair partitions and the set of all non-crossing pair partitions of
[K]
respectively. Let
\gamma=(1,\ldots,K)\in S_{K}
be the right-shift permutation (modulo
K
), and for any
\pi
which is a pair-partition, we identify it as a permutation of
[K]
, and read
\gamma\pi
as a composition of permutations. Further, for any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)
, let
\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
denote the set

\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}:=\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}(K,N)=\{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{K}:i_{r}=i_{\gamma\pi(r)}\text{ for all $r\in[K]$}\}.

Let
C(K,N)=\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}^{c}
, the complement of
\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
, wherein we have
i_{r}=i_{\pi(r)}
for any
r
. By Wick’s formula for the Gaussian terms
\{G_{i,j}\}
, since the the sum over tuples
\mathbf{i}
would be reduced to the sum over pair partitions
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)
and the associated tuples
\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}\cup C(K,N)
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[K]^{N}}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in C(K,N)}+\sum_{\pi\in NC_{2}(K)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}}+\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)\setminus NC_{2}(K)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}}.

(4.6)

To analyse further, we use a key tool in the proof which is the following fact (
Cipriani et al. (2025, Claim 4.10)
).

Fact 4.2
.

Let
K
be an even nonnegative integer. Then, we have the following to be true:

(1)

For any
\pi\in NC_{2}(K)
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{K/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}}\prod_{(r,s)\in\pi}\frac{1}{\|i_{r}-i_{r+1}\|^{\alpha}}=1\,.

(2)

For any pair partition
\pi
, if
\mathbf{i}\in C(K,N)
, then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{K/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in C(K,N)}\prod_{(r,s)\in\pi}\frac{1}{\|i_{r}-i_{r+1}\|^{\alpha}}=0\,.

(3)

For a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(K)\setminus NC_{2}(K)
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{K/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}\cup C(K,N)}\prod_{(r,s)\in\pi}\frac{1}{\|i_{r}-i_{r+1}\|^{\alpha}}=0\,.

Let
\tilde{\pi}:=\gamma\pi
for any choice of
\pi
. From
Chatterjee (2005, Eq. 5.2.5)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi}):=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\right]=\prod_{u\in\tilde{\pi}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
1+M(j)\in u\end{subarray}}Z_{\ell_{u}}^{n_{j}}\right]<\infty,

(4.7)

where
u
is a block in
\tilde{\pi}
and
\ell_{u}
its representative element. Note that this does not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{i}
, and to obtain a nonzero contribution, we must have that for all
u\in\tilde{\pi}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:1+M(j)\in u\end{subarray}}n_{j}\equiv 0\quad(\mathrm{mod}\,2).

(4.8)

Observe that
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\right]
depends only on
\tilde{\pi}
and not the choice of
\mathbf{i}
, and as a consequence, we can define

\displaystyle\Phi(\tilde{\pi}):=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\right]<\infty\,.

(4.9)

Next, note that the sum

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}=\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}_{N}(1)

(4.10)

by definition of
c_{N}
(and the weights are uniformly bounded). Finally, if we look at the terms

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}{c_{N}}\right)^{1/2}\right]\,,

(4.11)

we can again bound the weights above by
m
. Recall that Wick’s formula on the Gaussian terms imposes the restriction on choices of
\mathbf{i}
. Using these facts, in combination with (
4.7
), (
4.9
), and (
4.10
), we have that (
4.5
) gets bounded by

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:Laplacian moment expectation expansion}\leq\frac{C}{Nc_{N}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(M(k))}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}\cup C(M(k),N)}\Phi(\tilde{\pi})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})\prod_{(r,s)\in\pi}\frac{m^{2}}{\|i_{r}-i_{r+1}\|^{\alpha}}\,.

(4.12)

If we split (
4.12
) as (
4.6
), then using Fact
4.2
, we see that in the cases when
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(M(k))
and
\mathbf{i}\in C(M(k),N)
, and when
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(M(k))\setminus NC_{2}(M(k))
for all
\mathbf{i}
, the contribution in the limit
N\to\infty
is 0.

We are now in the setting where we take
\pi\in NC_{2}(M(k))
and
\tilde{\pi}:=\gamma\pi
, and
\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
. First, note that
\tilde{\pi}
is a partition of
[M(k)]
. We remark that if
M(k)\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,2)
then
NC_{2}(M(k))=\emptyset
, and so,
M(k)
must be even.

Next, we focus on analysing the product
\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}^{m}}
appearing in (
4.5
). We wish to show that this depends only on
\pi
, and not on the choice of
\mathbf{i}
. We follow the idea of
Cipriani et al. (2025)
, wherein one constructs a graph associated to a chosen partition
\pi
, and any tuple
\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
is equivalent to a tuple
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
with as many distinct indices as the number of vertices in the constructed graph. First, note that the coordinates are pairwise distinct (we take
r_{ii}=0
for all
i
). Next, we construct a preliminary graph from the closed walk
i_{1}\to i_{2}\to\ldots i_{M(k)}\to i_{1}
. Lastly, we collapse vertices and edges that are matched in
\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
, and we denote the resulting graph as
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
, since it does not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{i}
but rather the choice of
\pi
itself. The resulting graph
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
is the graph associated to the partition
\pi
, and we refer the reader to Definition
6.8
for a formal description. For clarity, consider the following example:

Let
M(k)=4
, and let
\pi=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\}
. Then,
\tilde{\pi}=\{\{1,3\},\{2\},\{4\}\}
. For any
\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
, we see that
i_{1}=i_{3}
, and
i_{2},i_{4}
are independent indices. Now,
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
is a graph on 3 vertices, which are labelled as
\{\{1,3\}\}
,
\{2\}
and
\{4\}
, and so its corresponding tuple
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
is exactly the same as
\mathbf{i}
.

We then have, from
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Eq. 5.2.12)
, that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}^{m}}=\prod_{e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}r_{e}^{t_{e}/2},

(4.13)

where
E_{\tilde{\pi}}
is the edge set of
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
and
t_{e}
is the number of times an edge
e
is traversed in the closed walk on
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
. Also observe

\Phi(\tilde{\pi})=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}G_{e}^{t_{e}}\right].

Consequently, we must have that
t_{e}
to be even for all
e
, since the Gaussian terms are independent and mean 0. We claim that
t_{e}=2
for all
e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}
. Indeed, if for all
e
,
t_{e}\geq 2
with at least one
e^{\prime}
such that
t_{e^{\prime}}>2
, then,
\sum_{e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}t_{e}>2|E_{\tilde{\pi}}|
. Since
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
is connected,
|E_{\tilde{\pi}}|\geq|V_{\tilde{\pi}}|-1=M(k)/2
, where
V_{\tilde{\pi}}
is the vertex set. Thus,
\sum_{e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}t_{e}>M(k)
. But,
\sum_{e}t_{e}=M(k)
, gives a contradiction. We conclude that
t_{e}=2
for all
e\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}
.

A similar contradiction arises when we assume that there exists a self-loop in
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
. Thus
G_{\tilde{\pi}}
is a tree on
\frac{M(k)}{2}+1
vertices with each edge traversed twice in the closed walk. As a consequence, every Gaussian term in
\Phi(\tilde{\pi})
appears exactly twice, and so,
\Phi(\tilde{\pi})=1
.

Let
b_{s}
be the
s^{\text{th}}
block of
\tilde{\pi}
and let
\ell_{s}
its representative element. Define

\gamma_{s}:=\#\left\{1\leq j\leq k:1+M(j)\in b_{s}\right\},

and

\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{\gamma_{s}}\}=\left\{1\leq j\leq k:1+M(j)\in b_{s}\right\}.

We then have

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)t}}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}=\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{\ell_{s}t}\right)^{\sum_{j=1}^{\gamma_{s}}n_{s_{j}}/2}.

(4.14)

Note that

\sum_{j=1}^{\gamma_{s}}n_{s_{j}}=\sum_{j\in[k]:1+M(j)\in b_{s}}n_{j}.

Let us define
\tilde{n}_{s}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\gamma_{s}}n_{s_{j}}/2
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{s:b_{s}\in\tilde{\pi}}\tilde{n}_{s}=\frac{N(k)}{2}.

(4.15)

Using
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Eq. 5.2.16)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{\frac{M(k)+N(k)}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1}\neq\ldots\neq\ell_{M(k)/2+1},\\
p_{(s,1)},\ldots,p_{(s,\tilde{n}_{s})}:s\in\left[\frac{M(k)}{2}+1\right]\end{subarray}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}r_{\ell_{u}\ell_{v}}\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\prod_{t=1}^{\tilde{n}_{s}}r_{\ell_{s}p_{(s,t)}},

(4.16)

where for any two blocks
b_{s_{1}}
and
b_{s_{2}}
,
\{p(s_{1},1),p(s_{1},2),\ldots\}
and

are non-intersecting sets of indices

and
\{p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,p_{\tilde{n}_{s_{2}}}^{\prime}\}
. Note that for

,

as before, but we rewrite in terms of representative elements to indicate common factors with the terms

. Taking expectation in (

) gives us

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\eqref{eq:Laplacian variance term expansion}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{\frac{M(k)+N(k)}{2}}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\neq\ldots\neq\ell_{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}\frac{W_{\ell_{u}}^{m}W_{\ell_{v}}^{m}}{\|\ell_{u}-\ell_{v}\|^{\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{(s,1)},\ldots,p_{(s,\tilde{n}_{s})}:\\

The vertex set
V_{\tilde{\pi}}
of the graph

yields

distinct indices, due to the tree structure. Using Fact

\sum_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}\frac{1}{\|\ell_{u}-\ell_{v}\|^{\alpha}}

is of the order of
\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}_{N}\left(c_{N}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}}\right)
since the weights are uniformly bounded in the range

. For the second summand in (

already appears in the graph

, and for any

many distinct indices from the sequence

We claim that as

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}W_{\ell_{u}}^{m}W_{\ell_{v}}^{m}\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\prod_{t=1}^{\tilde{n}_{s}}W_{\ell_{s}}^{m}W_{p_{(s,t)}}\right]\,.

First, note that the weights are bounded, and so, (

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{Nc_{N}^{M(k)/2}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\neq\ldots\neq\ell_{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}\frac{W^{m}_{\ell_{u}}W^{m}_{\ell_{v}}}{\|\ell_{u}-\ell_{v}\|^{\alpha}}\right]=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}W^{m}_{\ell_{u}}W^{m}_{\ell_{v}}\right]\,,

. Thus, combinatorially, the first summand in (

, as defined in Definition

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{c_{N}^{N(k)/2}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left.\sum_{p_{(s,1)},\ldots,p_{(s,\tilde{n}_{s}})}\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\prod_{t=1}^{\tilde{n}_{s}}\frac{W^{m}_{\ell_{s}}W^{m}_{p_{(s,t)}}}{\|\ell_{s}-p_{(s,t)}\|^{\alpha}}\right|W^{m}_{\ell_{s}}\right]=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\left.\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\prod_{t=1}^{\tilde{n}_{s}}W^{m}_{\ell_{s}}W^{m}_{p_{(s,t)}}\right|W^{m}_{\ell_{s}}\right]\,.

Due to the compact support of the weights, it is now easy to conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\eqref{eq:Laplacian varterms expectation}=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\tilde{\pi}}}W_{\ell_{u}}^{m}W_{\ell_{v}}^{m}\prod_{s=1}^{\frac{M(k)}{2}+1}\prod_{t=1}^{\tilde{n}_{s}}W_{\ell_{s}}^{m}W_{p_{(s,t)}}\right]=:t(\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}},W^{m})

(4.18)

where
W^{m}=(W_{1}^{m},W_{2}^{m},\ldots)
and
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}}
is the modified graph as described above and illustrated in Figure
3
.

We can therefore conclude that for all even
k
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\tr(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{k})\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k},\\
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in NC_{2}(M(k))}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})t(\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}},W^{m})\,.

(4.19)

Now, consider the case when
k
is odd. Due to (
4.8
), we have that
M(k)
must be odd. Thus,
\pi
cannot be a pair partition, and in particular,
\pi\not\in NC(M(k))
. Consider the term
\Phi(\tilde{\pi})
in (
4.9
), and notice that by Wick’s formula, this term is identically 0 if
M(k)
is odd. Since the other expectations in (
4.5
) are of order
\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}_{N}(1)
, we conclude that the odd moments are 0 in expectation.

Step 2.

We now wish to show concentration of the moments. Define

P(\mathbf{i}):=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\right]\,,

and

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i_{j}\wedge i_{j+1},i_{j}\vee i_{j+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\right.

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left.\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}G_{i^{\prime}_{j}\wedge i^{\prime}_{j+1},i^{\prime}_{j}\vee i^{\prime}_{j+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{\sqrt{r_{i^{\prime}_{j}i^{\prime}_{j+1}}}}{\sqrt{c_{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{c_{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_{i^{\prime}_{1+M(j)}t}\right)^{\frac{n_{j}}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}Z_{i^{\prime}_{1+M(j)}}^{n_{j}}\right]\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\Var\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})(\dd{x})\right)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k},\\
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime}:[M(k)]\to[N]}\left[P(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime})-P(\mathbf{i})P(\mathbf{i}^{\prime})\right]\,,

(4.20)

and we would like to show
\eqref{eq:Laplacian moment variance step 1}\to 0
.
If
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
have no common indices, then
P(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime})=P(\mathbf{i})P(\mathbf{i}^{\prime})
by independence. If there is
exactly
one common index, say
i_{1}=i^{\prime}_{1}
, then by independence of Gaussian terms, the factors
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{i_{1},i_{2}}]
and
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[G_{i_{1},i_{2}^{\prime}}]
would pull out, causing (
4.20
) to be identically 0. Thus, we have
at least
one matching of the form
(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2}^{\prime})
.

Let us begin by taking
k
to be even. Consider exactly one matching, which we take to be
(i_{1},i_{2})=(i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2}^{\prime})
without loss of generality. Let
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
be partitions of
\{1,2,\ldots,M(k)\},\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime},\ldots,M(k)^{\prime}\}
respectively. Let
\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{(1)}}
denote the sum over index sets
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
with exactly one matching. Then, we have by an extension of the previous argument

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{(1)}}_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime}:[M(k)]\to[N]}P(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}c_{N}^{M(k)}}\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}\Phi(\tilde{\pi})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})\Phi(\tilde{\pi}^{\prime})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[r_{i_{1}i_{2}}\prod_{j=2}^{M(k)}\sqrt{r_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}}\prod_{j=2}^{M(k)}\sqrt{r_{i_{j}^{\prime}i_{j+1}^{\prime}}}\right]\,.

(4.21)

Expanding the expression for
r_{ij}
and using the fact that
W_{i}^{m}\leq m
gives us that

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:Laplacian moment variance bound}\leq\frac{m^{2M(k)}}{N^{2}c_{N}^{M(k)}}\sum_{\pi}\Phi(\tilde{\pi})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})\Phi(\tilde{\pi}^{\prime})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi}^{\prime})\sum_{\mathbf{i,i}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\|i_{1}-i_{2}\|^{\alpha}}\prod_{j=1}^{M(k)}\frac{1}{\|i_{j}-i_{j+1}\|^{\alpha/2}}\frac{1}{\|i_{j}^{\prime}-i_{j+1}^{\prime}\|^{\alpha/2}}\,.

(4.22)

We are now precisely in the setting of
Cipriani et al. (2025)
, and in particular, following the ideas from
Cipriani et al. (2025, p24)
and using Fact
4.2
, we obtain that the right-hand side of (
4.22
) is of order
\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}_{N}(c_{N}^{-1})
. For
t
matchings in
\mathbf{i,i}^{\prime}
, the order is
\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}(c_{N}^{-t})
, giving us that (
4.16
) is of order
\mbox{${\mathrm{O}}$}(c_{N}^{-1})
when
k
is even.

The argument for the case where
k
is odd is similar. Since the optimal order is achieved when we take
\mathbf{i}\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi}
and
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\setminus\{i_{1}^{\prime},i_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\mathrm{Cat}_{\pi^{\prime}}
, with
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in NC_{2}(M(k))
, one cannot construct these partitions with
k
being odd with the restriction from (
4.8
) imposing that
M(k)
must be odd. Consequently, we have convergence in
\mathbb{P}
–probability of the moments of
\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c})
. Thus, we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\tr(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{k})=M_{k}\quad\text{in $\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,,

where

\displaystyle M_{k}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\mathcal{M}(k)}\sum_{\pi\in NC_{2}(M(k))}t(\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}},W^{m})\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})\,,&\text{$k$ even},\\
0\,,&\text{$k$ odd}\,,\end{cases}

(4.23)

where
\mathcal{M}(k)
is the multiset of all numbers
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
that appear in the expansion
(a+b)^{k}
for two non-commutative variables
a
and
b
.

Step 3.

We are now left to show that these moments uniquely determine a limiting measure. This follows from
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Section 5.2.2)
, but we show the bounds for the sake of completeness.

First, from
Chatterjee and Hazra (2022, Section 5.2.2)
, we have that
\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\pi})\leq 2^{k}k!
. Next, observe from (
4.18
) that
|t(\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}},W^{m})|\leq(m^{2})^{\frac{k}{2}}=m^{2}
, since
W_{i}\leq m
for all
i
and
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\pi}}
is a graph on
\frac{k}{2}+1
vertices with
\frac{k}{2}
edges. Lastly,
|NC_{2}(M(k)|\leq|NC_{2}(k)|=C_{k}
, where
C_{k}
is the
k^{\text{th}}
Catalan number, and moreover,
|\mathcal{M}(k)|\leq 2^{k}
. Combining these, we have

\beta_{k}:=|M_{k}|\leq 2^{k}.C_{k}.m^{k}.2^{k}k!=(4m)^{k}C_{k}k!\,.

Using Sterling’s approximation, we have

\frac{1}{k}\beta_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq\frac{4m}{(k+1)^{\frac{1}{k}}}.\frac{4\mathrm{e}^{-\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{k}}}\,,

where
\pi
here is now the usual constant, and subsequently, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{2k}\beta_{2k}^{\frac{1}{2k}}<\infty\,.

(4.24)

Equation (
4.24
) is a well known criteria to show that the moments uniquely determine the limiting measure (see
Lin (2017, Theorem 1)
).
This completes the argument.
∎

5.1.
The case
\alpha=0

In Section

, we show the existence of a unique limiting measure
\nu_{\tau}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N}^{\circ})=\nu_{\tau}\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

We have also shown that
\nu_{\tau}
is the limiting measure for the
\ESD
of the Laplacian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
.
In particular, through the proof of Proposition
4.1

\nu_{\tau,m}
is independent of the choice of
\alpha
, and consequently,
\nu_{\tau}
is
\alpha

\alpha=0
from
Cipriani et al. (2025, Section 6)
in the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g}
, to obtain the Laplacian matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}^{\circ}
, which corresponds to the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}_{N,g}^{\circ}
with entries given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{N,g}^{\circ}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{W_{i}W_{j}}}{\sqrt{N}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j},\,&i\neq j\\
0,\,&i=j.\end{cases}

Then,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}^{\circ})=\nu_{\tau}
in
\mathbb{P}
–probability. Recall that for all
1\leq i\leq N

W_{i}^{m}:=W_{i}\one_{W_{i}\leq m}
for any

3.5
and
3.6
to contruct a matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}=\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{\circ}+Y_{N}^{\circ}
such that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{L}(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g}^{\circ}),\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}))>\delta\right)=0\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{\circ}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{W_{i}^{m}W_{j}^{m}}}{\sqrt{N}}G_{i\wedge j,i\vee j},\,&i\neq j\\
0,\,&i=j,\end{cases}

and

is a diagonal matrix with entries

Y_{N}^{\circ}(i,i)=Z_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k\neq i}W_{i}^{m}W_{k}^{m}}{N}}\,.

By Proposition
4.1
, we have that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ})=\nu_{\tau,m}

\mathbb{P}
–probability. Thus, we begin by identifying
\nu_{\tau,m}
. To that end, consider the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}:=\mathbf{A}_{N,g,c}+\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\circ}

\mathbf{A}_{N,g,c}

\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\circ}
a diagonal matrix with entries

\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\circ}(i,i)=Z_{i}\sqrt{W_{1}^{m}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]}\,.

We now have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1.

Let
\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}
be as defined above. Then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{L}(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}),\ESD(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}))>\delta\right)=0\,.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
6.1
to obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[d_{L}(\ESD(\mathbf{\Delta}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}),\ESD(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Y_{N}^{\circ}(i,i)-\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\circ}(i,i)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[Z_{1}^{2}]\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{k\neq i}W_{k}^{m}}}{\sqrt{N}}-\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\left[\left|\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}^{m}}{N}-\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]\right|\right]\,.

(5.1)

We have that
(W_{i}^{m})_{i\in\mathbf{V}_{N}}
is a bounded sequence of i. i. d. random variables, and in particular have finite variance. By the strong law of large numbers, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}^{m}}{N}=\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]\quad\text{$\mathbb{P}$--almost surely}\,.

However, by the boundedness of the weights, we have that
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}^{m}
is uniformly bounded by
m
, which is integrable (with respect to
{\mathbb{E}}
). By the dominated convergence theorem, we have convergence in
L^{1}
, and consequently, (
5.1
) goes to 0 as
N\to\infty
. We conclude with Markov’s inequality.
∎

We can now conclude that
\nu_{\tau,m}
is the limiting measure of the
\ESD
of the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}
.

5.2.
Identification of
\nu_{\tau,m}

We have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\ESD(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ})=\nu_{\tau,m}\quad\text{in $\mathbb{P}$--probability}\,.

Notice that
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}
can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{N,g,c}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{N,g,m}^{\circ}+\widehat{Y}_{N}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{G}\right)\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]}\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/4}\mathrm{Z}\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/4}\,,

where
\mathrm{W}_{m}=\diag(W_{1}^{m},\ldots,W_{N}^{m})
,
\mathrm{G}
is a standard Wigner matrix with i. i. d
\gauss(0,1)
entries above the diagonal and 0 on the diagonal, and
\mathrm{Z}
is a diagonal matrix with i. i. d.
\gauss(0,1)

First we need to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\ESD\left(\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{G}\right)\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{m}]}\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{Z}\right)\mathrm{W}_{m}^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}T_{s}T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}+\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{m}]}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}T_{g}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}\right)\text{ weakly in probability }.

This easily follows by retracing the arguments in the proof of
(
Chakrabarty et al., 2021
, Theorem 1.3)
and using the Lemma
6.6
presented in the appendix. This shows that

\nu_{\tau,m}=\mathcal{L}\left(T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}T_{s}T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}+\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{m}]}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}T_{g}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}\right).

5.3.
Identification of
\nu_{\tau}

We now conclude with the proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We work in a tracial
W^{*}
-probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
. Let

T_{g}
and
T_{s}
denote, respectively, a standard Gaussian and a standard semicircular self-adjoint element.
For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
T_{W_{m}}
(resp.
T_{W}
) be self-adjoint affiliated operators with
distributions
\mu_{W_{m}}
(resp.
\mu_{W}
). We assume
T_{s}
is free from the abelian von Neumann
algebras
W^{*}(T_{W_{m}},T_{g})
and
W^{*}(T_{W},T_{g})
. By the freeness result stated in
Bercovici and Voiculescu (1993, Proposition 4.1)
, this implies that for any bounded Borel
u,v
the elements
u(T_{W_{m}})v(T_{g})
and
u(T_{W})v(T_{g})
are free from
T_{s}
.

Fix the constant
c:=\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W]}
. If one writes
c_{m}:=\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{m}]}
, then
c_{m}\to c
. The replacement

c_{m}\mapsto c
is justified at the end. For
R>0
define bounded truncations

u_{R}(x):=(x\wedge R)^{1/2},\quad v_{R}(x):=(x\wedge R)^{1/4}.

Define affiliated (self-adjoint) operators

Q_{R}^{(m)}:=u_{R}(T_{W_{m}})\,T_{g}\,u_{R}(T_{W_{m}})+c\,v_{R}(T_{W_{m}})\,T_{s}\,v_{R}(T_{W_{m}}),

(5.2)

and

Q_{R}:=u_{R}(T_{W})\,T_{g}\,u_{R}(T_{W})+c\,v_{R}(T_{W})\,T_{s}\,v_{R}(T_{W}),

(5.3)

noting that
u_{R}(\cdot)
and
v_{R}(\cdot)
are bounded while
T_{g}
may be
unbounded, so
Q_{R}^{(m)},Q_{R}
are in general affiliated rather than bounded.

Let

\varepsilon_{m}:=d_{\infty}(\mu_{W_{m}},\mu_{W})=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{\mu_{W_{m}}}(t)-F_{\mu_{W}}(t)|\to 0.

Theorem 3.9 of
Bercovici and Voiculescu (1993)
allows us to represent
T_{W_{m}}
and
T_{W}
in the same space together with a projection
p_{m}\in W^{*}(T_{W_{m}},T_{W},T_{g})
such that

\varphi(p_{m})\geq 1-\varepsilon_{m},\qquad p_{m}\,T_{W_{m}}\,p_{m}=p_{m}\,T_{W}\,p_{m}.

(5.4)

Since
p_{m}
lies in the abelian algebra generated by
T_{W_{m}},T_{W},T_{g}
, it commutes with

T_{g}
and with bounded Borel functions of
T_{W_{m}},T_{W}
. Hence
p_{m}u_{R}(T_{W_{m}})=u_{R}(T_{W})p_{m}
and

p_{m}v_{R}(T_{W_{m}})=v_{R}(T_{W})p_{m}
, and therefore

p_{m}\,Q_{R}^{(m)}\,p_{m}\ =\ p_{m}\,Q_{R}\,p_{m}.

(5.5)

From the estimate (
Bercovici and Voiculescu (1993, Theorem 3.9 (i))
)
if
A,B
are selfadjoint and
pAp=pBp
with
\varphi(p)\geq 1-\delta
, then

\sup_{t}|F_{A}(t)-F_{B}(t)|\leq\delta

we obtain, for each fixed
R>0
,

d_{\infty}\big(\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}^{(m)}),\mathcal{L}(Q_{R})\big)\ \leq\ 2\varepsilon_{m}\ \xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\ 0.

(5.6)

We introduce the following affiliated operators

H_{m}:=T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}T_{g}T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}+cT_{W_{m}}^{1/4}T_{s}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4},\quad H:=T_{W}^{1/2}T_{g}T_{W}^{1/2}+cT_{W}^{1/4}T_{s}T_{W}^{1/4}.

Define the projections
q_{m,R}:=1_{[0,R]}(T_{W_{m}})
and
q_{R}:=1_{[0,R]}(T_{W})
. On the range
\mathrm{Ran}(q_{m,R})
we have

u_{R}(T_{W_{m}})=T_{W_{m}}^{1/2}
and
v_{R}(T_{W_{m}})=T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}
, and similarly with
W

W_{m}
. Consequently,

q_{m,R}Q_{R}^{(m)}q_{m,R}=q_{m,R}H_{m}q_{m,R},\quad q_{R}Q_{R}q_{R}=q_{R}Hq_{R}.

Applying the same compression–distribution estimate we have

d_{\infty}\big(\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}^{(m)}),\mathcal{L}(H_{m})\big)\ \leq\ 2\,\varphi(1-q_{m,R})=2\,\big(1-F_{\mu_{W_{m}}}(R)\big),

(5.7)

and

d_{\infty}\big(\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}),\mathcal{L}(H)\big)\ \leq\ 2\,\big(1-F_{\mu_{W}}(R)\big).

(5.8)

Since
\mu_{W_{m}}\Rightarrow\mu_{W}
, the family
{\mu_{W_{m}}}
is tight; hence
\sup_{m}(1-F_{\mu_{W_{m}}}(R))\to 0
and
1-F_{\mu_{W}}(R)\to 0
as
R\to\infty
. Therefore,

\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}^{(m)})\Rightarrow\mathcal{L}(H_{m})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}(Q_{R})\Rightarrow\mathcal{L}(H)\qquad(R\to\infty),

(5.9)

with convergence uniform in

along the first convergence.

Fix
\eta>0
. Choose

so large that both bounds in
(

) and (
5.8
) are less than
\eta

. Then choose

d_{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}^{(m)}),\mathcal{L}(Q_{R}))\leq\eta
. The triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(H_{m}\right),\mathcal{L}(H)\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(H_{m}\right),\mathcal{L}\left(Q_{R}^{(m)}\right)\right)+d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(Q_{R}^{(m)}\right),\mathcal{L}\left(Q_{R}\right)\right)+d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(Q_{R}\right),\mathcal{L}(H)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3\eta

As
\eta\downarrow 0

\mathcal{L}(H_{m})\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{L}(H)\qquad(m\to\infty).

(5.10)

This identifies
\nu_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}(H)
.

Finally, to replace
c_{m}=\sqrt{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{m}]}
by

, note that

H_{m}^{(c)}-H_{m}^{(c_{m})}=(c-c_{m})\,T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}T_{s}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}.

Fix
M>0
and set
r_{m,M}=1_{[0,M]}(T_{W_{m}})
. Then

\|r_{m,M}(H_{m}^{(c)}-H_{m}^{(c_{m})})r_{m,M}\,\|\leq|c-c_{m}|\,M^{1/2}\,\|T_{s}\|.

Therefore, for the bounded–Lipschitz distance
d_{BL}
,

d_{BL}\left(\mathcal{L}(H_{m}^{(c)}),\mathcal{L}(H_{m}^{(c_{m})})\right)\leq 2\,\varphi(1-r_{m,M})+|c-c_{m}|\,M^{1/2}\,\|T_{s}\|.

(5.11)

Indeed, on
\operatorname{Ran}\left(r_{m,M}\right)
,

\left\|r_{m,M}\left(H_{m}^{(c)}-H_{m}^{\left(c_{m}\right)}\right)r_{m,M}\right\|=\left|c-c_{m}\right|\left\|r_{m,M}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}T_{s}T_{W_{m}}^{1/4}r_{m,M}\right\|\leq\left|c-c_{m}\right|M^{1/2}\left\|T_{s}\right\|=:\varepsilon.

For any bounded 1 -Lipschitz

\displaystyle\left|\varphi\left(f\left(H_{m}^{(c)}\right)\right)-\varphi\left(f\left(H_{m}^{\left(c_{m}\right)}\right)\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\varphi\left(r_{m,M}f\left(H_{m}^{(c)}\right)r_{m,M}-r_{m,M}f\left(H_{m}^{\left(c_{m}\right)}\right)r_{m,M}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+2\varphi\left(1-r_{m,M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(f)\varepsilon+2\varphi\left(1-r_{m,M}\right)\leq\varepsilon+2\varphi\left(1-r_{m,M}\right)

where we used

on the range of

Since

and

Consequently, the limit law is the same whether we use
