Ecoregional and altitudinal distribution of the principal malaria vectors in Colombia
Authors: María C. Carrasquilla, Ranulfo González, Sergio R. Pineda, Clara B. Ocampo, James Montoya-Lerma, Paula Pareja-Loaiza, Martha L. Ahumada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Colombia, with over 100,000 cases reported in 2023, malaria is a significant vector-borne disease. The wide range of climates and highly variable topography and land cover of the country support a high species richness, favouring different scenarios of transmission. This study characterizes the ecoregional and altitudinal distribution of the principal malaria vectors in Colombia, Anopheles albimanus, Anopheles darlingi, Anopheles nuneztovari sensu stricto (s.s.), Anopheles neivai sensu lato (s.l.) and Anopheles calderoni, and represent their historic and current distribution.MethodsA database of the distribution of the principal malaria vectors in Colombia was constructed using information from diverse sources dating 1929 to 2023. Elevation and ecoregion were obtained using the U.S. Geological Service (USGS) and the Ecoregions2017©Resolve shapefile, respectively. Distribution maps of the principal malaria vectors regarding elevation and ecoregion were generated.ResultsA total of 2,371 records of the main malaria vectors were obtained. This study shows that the principal anopheline species are widely distributed. In the coastal regions of the country, An. albimanus (Atlantic and Pacific regions) and An. neivai s.l. (Pacific region) are highly represented. However, records of these species are also associated with inland areas. Anopheles darlingi, An. nuneztovari s.s. and An. calderoni are mainly inland species. Besides its wide distribution, An. darlingi presents a high association with forests. Anopheles nuneztovari s.s. is mainly associated with the Middle Magdalena Valley, Urabá-Bajo Cauca, Atrato River, and Catatumbo, and An. calderoni is found far inland, especially in the Cauca River Valley. Regarding altitude, these malaria vectors are predominantly distributed below 500 m a.s.l. Nevertheless, An. albimanus and An. calderoni are also present at elevations over 1,000 m a.s.l. Notably, An. albimanus and An. neivai s.l. are the two species that suggest elevation increase through time.ConclusionsThe distribution of the principal malaria vectors in Colombia, encompasses both coastal and inland areas, and a variety of ecoregions, particularly the Chocó-Darién and Magdalena-Urabá moist forests. The comprehensive identification of ecoregions and elevations in which malaria vectors have historically been and are currently distributed will contribute to determining receptivity areas for designing malaria stratification plans and the prioritization of zones for its prevention and control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05707-w.

Background

Globally, malaria is a significant vector-borne disease. In 2023, an estimated 263 million cases were reported in endemic countries worldwide [1]. Particularly, in the Americas 505,642 cases were registered and Venezuela, Brazil, and Colombia, accounted for 80% of these cases. Notably, Colombia was responsible for 20.7% of these cases. Regarding the Plasmodium species of these infections, Colombia reported 66,035 Plasmodium vivax cases (62.98%) and 37,774 Plasmodium falciparum cases (36.03%), corresponding to the highest number of P. falciparum cases in the Americas during 2023 [1].

The wide range of climates, variable topography, and land cover of Colombia support a high mosquito species richness, favouring malaria transmission [2, 3]. An estimated 40–48 anopheline species have been reported in the country [4–6]. Of these, three species from the Nysorrhynchus subgenus account for most records of natural infections: Anopheles (Nyssorhynchus) albimanus, Anopheles (Nyssorhynchus) nuneztovari, and Anopheles (Nyssorhynchus) darlingi [7]. However, Anopheles (Kerteszia) neivai s.l., and Anopheles (Anopheles) calderoni are considered local vectors, due to their presence in regions with active malaria transmission, anthropophilic behavior, and detection of natural infection with Plasmodium spp. [8–13].

Concerning investigations of the distribution of malaria vectors in Colombia, there are two major reports: one in 1957 conducted by the Malaria Eradication Service (SEM for its acronym in Spanish) [14] and another from 2001 by Olano et al. [15]. Additionally, some research studies have included information on Anopheles distribution [5–7, 16]. However, despite the importance of regional, ecological, and altitudinal distributions of these vectors, a comprehensive characterization of their distributions in Colombia has not previously been undertaken.

Herein, this study characterizes the ecoregional and altitudinal distribution of An. albimanus, An. darlingi, An. nuneztovari s.s., An. neivai s.l. and An. calderoni, the principal malaria vectors in Colombia and present their historical and current distribution.

Methods

Database construction

A database was compiled from records obtained through Instituto Nacional de Salud, Colombia (INS), Museo de Entomología de la Universidad del Valle (MUSENUV), Servicio de Erradicación de la Malaria (SEM), the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), the Barcode of Life Data Systems (BOLDSYSTEMS), the Malaria Project. In addition, an extensive bibliographic review, using the search terms “Anopheles”, “Colombia”, and “Each of the names of the Colombian departments” in the public repositories Google Scholar, PubMed, and SciELO, was performed. The database was constructed specifying Anopheles species, department, municipality, locality, latitudinal and longitudinal position, collection year, ecoregion, altitude, and references. Records were obtained between 1929 and 2023, and to visualize the distribution of malaria vectors through time, they were organized in three periods: 1) 1929–1969, 2) 1970–1999, 3) 2000–2023.

Records from 1929 to 2023 were assembled into one large dataset. These records were collected during entomological surveillance to identify the malaria vectors in endemic areas over these 95 years. Surveillance activities occurred in different years, with irregular periods between them. Over the 95 years, researchers from several organizations collected records, which explains why some decades between 1929 and 2023 have more records than others.

Records in the dataset were classified into three groups. The first group spans from 1929 to 1969, the second from 1970 to 1999, and the third from 2000 to 2023. The intervals between these groups are unequal because there are very few records for the decades 1920s, 1930s, 1960s, and 1970s. The decision to use only three groups facilitates comparison across periods.

Geographic position of records in the database

For each record, a latitudinal and longitudinal position was included. Geographic locations registered in studies were checked to confirm accuracy; if a study did not specify latitude/longitude positions, but the name of a locality was reported, coordinate tuples were obtained, when possible, using ArcMap (version 10.8.2) and Google Earth Pro.

Colombia is made up of thirty-two first-level administrative divisions, referred to as departments (“departamentos”) (Fig. 1). Departments are composed of municipalities (“municipios”), which are second-level administrative divisions. Each municipality has a main town, referred to as the "cabecera municipal." Municipalities are further divided into “veredas”, a third-level administrative division. All geographic information, including boundaries of administrative divisions, was obtained from the Colombian National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE). DANE regularly publishes a geodatabase called the Marco Geoestadístico Nacional (MGN) [17], which provides updated shapefiles for “departamentos”, “municipios”, “cabecera municipales”, and “veredas”.Fig. 1Colombian map

Colombian map

The precise geographic positions (latitude and longitude) of records that specified “vereda”, the third-level administrative division, were calculated using the “vereda” layer in the Marco Geoestadístico Nacional (MGN) [17]. Since each vereda has a center and centers are points in space, the Add Geometry Attributes tool in ArcMap was used to obtain the centroid of each “vereda”. The geographic coordinates of “Cabecera Municipal” for each Colombian municipality was calculated using the same tool. The latitude and longitude of records in the dataset that did not indicate a “vereda” or a “cabecera municipal” but included a geographic name were obtained using ArcMap or Google Earth Pro. If a geographic position was not recovered, the record was not included.

Since this study comprises a review of records since 1920s and geographical boundaries of departments and municipalities have changed over the last 100 years, an overlay of the Colombian municipality layer from 1966 (Instituto Geográfico Agustin Codazzi (IGAC) [18] and the current Colombian municipality layer was performed to identify the actual department and municipality of older records using ArcMap.

Elevation

Elevation was obtained using the U.S. Geological Service (USGS)—Digital Elevation Model- Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) 1 Arc-Second Global [19], which has a resolution of approximately 30 m.

To integrate the 30-m SRTM Digital Elevation Model’s data with the records containing latitude and longitude, the Extract Values to Points tool in ArcMap was used. The output was a database that incorporates elevation for each corresponding record.

Ecoregions

Ecoregions are large land areas characterized by unique natural communities and species, with boundaries reflecting their original extent before significant land-use changes [20]. In Colombia, there are 35 ecoregions (Fig. 2). In this project, the ecoregion of each record was obtained using the Ecoregions2017©Resolve shapefile, licensed under CC-BY 4.0 (Ecoregion 2017) [21], and the Select by Location tool in ArcMap. This software tool selects the ecoregions where there are records of mosquitoes.Fig. 2Map of Colombian ecoregions

Map of Colombian ecoregions

In the Ecoregion shapefile, most ecoregion names include references to countries (first-level administrative boundaries), departments (second-level administrative boundaries), cities, or regions. For example, the names of two ecoregions are “Western Ecuador moist forest” and “South American Pacific mangroves”. Although these names mention Ecuador and the South American Pacific region, it is important to note that ecoregions often cross political boundaries, extending beyond the areas they are named after.

A few records do not report elevation data since the Digital Elevation Model (SRTM) has some areas with no-data or void data. As a result, the total number of records with elevation data is fewer than the total number of ecoregion records.

Results

A total of 2,371 records were obtained (859 for An. albimanus, 501 for An. darlingi, 607 for An. nuneztovari s.s., 240 for An. neivai s.l., and 164 for An. calderoni) in 24 ecoregions of the 35 ecoregions reported for Colombia.

Anopheles albimanus

This species is mostly distributed in the Pacific and Atlantic regions (Fig. 1), extending to Isla Fuerte, and the Archipelago of San Andrés and Providencia and it is mainly found in lowlands below 100 m a.s.l. This species has been encountered in 12 ecoregions, being particularly abundant in the Magdalena-Urabá moist forests (n = 262 registries), the Chocó-Darién moist forests (n = 177), South American-Pacific mangroves (n = 156) and the Guajira-Barranquilla xeric scrubs (n = 105) (Fig. 3A) (Table 1). Records of this species above 500 m a.s.l., particularly since the 2000s, have been documented, including reports above 1,000 m a.s.l. in the Cauca Valley Dry Forest, which is between the Central and West Andes mountain ranges (Departments of Cauca, Valle del Cauca, Risaralda and Caldas) (Figs. 3B, 8).Fig. 3Map of the distribution of Anopheles albimanus in different time periods A. Anopheles darlingi

This species occurs at altitudes of less than 530 m a.s.l. without evident changes along the different periods (Fig. 4B, Fig. 8). It is mainly registered inland and has a wide distribution across the Colombian territory, presenting a high association with forests. It has been found in diverse ecoregions with a high number of records documented in moist forests (n = 331), followed by dry forests, (n = 65), and montane forests (n = 28). It is also found in the eastern savannas (n = 56) and xeric shrublands (n = 13). Records in moist forests from Eastern Colombia are mainly registered since 2000, particularly in the Solimões-Japura moist forest (n = 18), Negro-Branco moist forests (n = 11), Caquetá moist forests (n = 8) and Japurá-Solimões-Negro moist forests (n = 1) (Fig. 4A) (Table 1).Fig. 4Map of the distribution of Anopheles darlingi in different time periods A. Anopheles darlingi has been found naturally infected with P. vivax and P. falciparum across the Colombian territory, including Urabá-Bajo Cauca, the Pacific and Atlantic coast, the Magdalena Middle Valley, the Orinoquia, and the Amazon. Interestingly, mosquitoes of this species have also been found naturally infected with Plasmodium malariae in two localities in Puerto Nariño, in the southern area of the Amazon: Tipisaca and Doce de Octubre. Infection rates have been detected between 0.086% and 1.587% for P. vivax and ranged between 0.036% and 1.3% for P. falciparum (Table 2).

Anopheles nuneztovari s.s.

This species mainly has a wide inland distribution, bordering the mountains, particularly in the Middle Magdalena Valley, Urabá-Bajo Cauca, Atrato River, and Northeastern Andes along the Venezuela frontier, with most of the records registered in moist forests, mainly in the Magdalena-Urabá moist forest with 214 reports and the Chocó-Darién moist forest with 226 reports. It is also abundant in the Northwest Andean montane forests (n = 88), the Catatumbo moist forests (n = 30), and the Apure-Villavicencio dry forests (n = 20) (Fig. 5A) (Table 1). Concerning elevation, this mosquito has been reported below 700 m a.s.l., without noteworthy changes through time (Fig. 5B, Fig. 8).Fig. 5Map of the distribution of Anopheles nuneztovari s.s. in different time periods A. Most reports of natural infection in An. nuneztovari s.s. mosquitoes, correspond to P. vivax with infection rates ranging from 0.047% to 0.649% in three regions: the Pacific coast (Buenaventura, Valle del Cauca), Urabá-Bajo Cauca (Antioquia, Córdoba) and the Middle Magdalena Valley (Cimitarra, Santander). Mosquitoes of this species have also been found infected with P. falciparum in Urabá-Bajo Cauca (Córdoba) with infection rates of 0.12 and 0.18% (Table 2).

Anopheles neivai s.l.

This species complex is distributed along the Colombian Pacific region from Nariño to Chocó. It is found at or near coastal areas in mangroves (n = 76) and in the adjacent Chocó-Darién moist forests (n = 149), where the species is more predominant, mainly near the border with the mangrove region. In eastern Colombia, close to the border with Venezuela, two records have been found since 2000, and in the Atlantic region, one record was reported in 1955 (Fig. 6A) (Table 1). Few records of natural infection with Plasmodium species have been reported for An. neivai s.l. on the Pacific coast. In particular, mosquitoes have been found infected with P. falciparum in Buenaventura, Valle del Cauca (Infection rate 9.5%) and El Litoral de San Juan, Chocó (Infection rate = 0.17%) and with P. vivax in collections from Santa Bárbara, Nariño (Infection rate = 0.04%) and El Litoral de San Juan, Chocó (Infection rate = 0.02%) (Table 2).

Anopheles calderoni

Most of the reports of An. calderoni correspond to the 2000s, except for some previous records of museum specimens, as the species was first confirmed in Colombia in 2010. This species is mainly found in Western and Northern Colombia. Nonetheless, a few records have also been reported in Orinoquia, Catatumbo, and Tolima. Most of the records of this species are far inland and many of them are in the Cauca River Valley. This species has been recorded in areas up to 1,480 m a.s.l. (Figs. 7B, 8). Discussion

This study characterized the ecoregional and altitudinal distribution of the principal malaria vectors in Colombia, An. albimanus, An. darlingi, An. nuneztovari s.s., An. neivai s.l. and An. calderoni, using information from multiple sources since the 1920s. The results of our study provide a comprehensive overview of their distribution. However, it is important to emphasize that data have not been collected systematically and periodically. Furthermore, based on national case report records, studies on Anopheles mosquitoes in the country have been mainly conducted in malaria-endemic areas in relation to control efforts. Therefore, records of their presence largely represent locations where malaria cases have been reported [10].

In Latin America, An. albimanus has mostly a coastal distribution [22]. In the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico, it is found from Southern Texas to Venezuela, and in most of the Caribbean islands, and the Pacific coast region, it is established from Mexico to northern Perú [7]. This study shows that, in Colombia, An. albimanus also has this distribution pattern and is predominant in localities along the Pacific and Caribbean coast, as has been previously reported by Olano et al. and Montoya-Lerma et al. [7, 15]. Throughout this broad distribution in Latin America, An. albimanus is considered to be ecologically adaptable [23]. In the present study, it was detected that records have been reported in montane forests, dry forests, moist forests, mangroves, and xeric scrublands. Of the principal Colombian malaria vectors, An. albimanus is the only one found in San Andrés and Providencia, specifically in the Cayos Miskitos-San Andrés and Providencia moist forests. Regarding elevation, in our study, this species has been mainly found in lowlands below 100 m a.s.l. However, records above 500 m a.s.l. have been documented, including reports above 1,000 m a.s.l., particularly since the 2000s in the Cauca Valley Dry Forest, increasing malaria receptivity in this ecoregion. In Latin America, this species has also been primarily found in areas below 400 m a.s.l. with a higher abundance in areas below and up to 100 m a.s.l. However, the species has also been collected above 1,000 m a.s.l., particularly it was collected in 1964 in Escuintla, Amatitlan, Guatemala at 1,200 m a.s.l. [24].

Throughout its wide distribution in Latin America, An. albimanus is an important malaria vector [23]. In this extensive review, An. albimanus mosquitoes have been found infected with P. vivax and P. falciparum in both the Atlantic and Pacific coast regions of Colombia. It has been incriminated as the principal vector in the Pacific coast region. However, its presence represents a risk factor in other regions, including areas where malaria transmission is low [22].

Anopheles darlingi is an anthropophilic species with a broad distribution in South America, extending from Colombia to Argentina. It is also found in Panamá, Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala, Belize, and southern Mexico [7, 22]. In the present review, it was found that in Colombia, this species is widespread in areas less than 530 m a.s.l. Further, it has an inland distribution, being almost absent at or near coastal areas. According to the records, it is present in the Atlantic region, Chocó, Urabá, Bajo Cauca, Magdalena Wetlands, Orinoquia and Amazon regions. Interestingly, records from the Amazon region have been mainly registered since 2000. This study shows that An. darlingi is associated with wetlands in the Magdalena and Cauca rivers, located in central Bolívar and southern Magdalena departments, particularly in the confluence area where the Magdalena, Cauca, San Jorge, and Cesar rivers converge. Its association with wetlands is also observed in the upper Sinú River. In the Orinoquia region, this anopheline vector, is found in the upper part of the Negro and Ariari rivers. In the Pacific region, records of this mosquito are found in the upper part of the Atrato River, where human settlements are located.

In Latin America, An. darlingi is considered the most important malaria vector species in the Amazon basin [25], being incriminated in many localities in different countries, including Brazil, French Guiana, Perú, Suriname, and Venezuela [22]. In Colombia, it is the main malaria vector in the Amazon and Orinoquia Regions, Urabá-Bajo Cauca, and Middle Magdalena Valley [15]. Although An. darlingi has been mainly associated with forest environments, it also has been found in periurban areas, such as Quibdó (Chocó) in the Pacific Coast region, and Villavicencio and Puerto Gaitán (Meta) in the Orinoquia region, where malaria transmission has been detected [14, 26]. Interestingly, this anopheline species has also been found infected with P. malariae in the Amazon region, as has also been reported in French Guiana and Venezuela [27, 28].

In Latin America, Nuneztovari Complex (Anopheles nuneztovari s.l.), is restricted to Panamá, the northern region of South America, and the Amazon [7, 22]. Along its distribution, it is proposed that Nuneztovari Complex of Anopheles comprises four species: An. nuneztovari s.s. Gabaldon, An. goeldii Rozeboom & Gabaldon, An. dunhami Causey and An. nuneztovari species A [29]. In Colombia, the current evidence confirms the presence of An. nuneztovari s.s. [30–32], and An. dunhami, restricted to the Colombian Amazon [33]. None of our records correspond to the Amazon region, where the other species of the Nuneztovari Complex are reported.

In this review, An. nuneztovari s.s. is found along the upper and middle part of some rivers, including Atrato, Sinú, Carare, and Arauca, following the linear course of rivers. Interestingly, the species has not been reported in the lower part of these rivers, concurring with its inland distribution, where human settlements are located.

Anopheles nuneztovari s.l. is recognized as a key malaria vector in Colombia, Perú, and Venezuela [7]. In Colombia, An. nuneztovari s.s. has been incriminated as a vector in Sarare, Arauca Department and Catatumbo, Norte de Santander Department [15]. This species is suspected to maintain malaria transmission with An. darlingi in other areas, such as Bajo Cauca-Nechi, Urabá, and Atrato river regions [15].

Anopheles neivai s.l. is a species complex widely distributed in coastal areas from the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico through the northern Pacific coast to Ecuador, where it inhabits lowland tropical forests and mangroves in coastal areas [36]. Along its distribution An. neivai s.l. is a highly diverse species complex, and at least eight tentative species have been proposed. In Colombia, four populations have been suggested: In the Pacific, An. neivai s.s. and An. neivai 8, in the North east, at the Colombian-Venezuelan border, An. neivai 2 and An. neivai 3 [34]. Vargas et al. confirmed the presence of An. neivai s.s. and An. neivai 8 in the Colombian Pacific coast [35]. This database was created reviewing previous records. Therefore, it is not possible to specify to which particular tentative species these records correspond, and more studies would be necessary to clarify the species diversity of this complex along Colombia.

In the present study, this species complex was found distributed along the Colombian Pacific coast and highly associated with mangroves and the adjacent Chocó-Darién moist forests. These findings concur with previous studies where An. neivai s.l. has been found in human settlements associated with these ecoregions on the Colombian Pacific coast and it is characterized for using bromeliads as breeding sites [37, 38].

Records of infection incriminate this species as an important local vector in the Departments of Valle del Cauca and Chocó along the Colombian Pacific coast [7], but also in Santa Bárbara, Department of Nariño, on the Southwest Colombian Pacific coast proximal to Ecuador [9].

Anopheles calderoni was first described in 1991 by Wilkerson in Perú [39] and first confirmed in Colombia in 2010 by González et al. [40]. Therefore, most of the records of this species are from the last 15 years. However, retrospective identification was performed, and previous records correspond to specimens collected between 1948 and 2009 that were previously identified as Anopheles punctimacula and Anopheles malefactor [40]. Anopheles calderoni has also been reported in Venezuela and Ecuador [11]. The compilation of records in Colombia shows that An. calderoni is an inland species. The only records of natural infection for this species in Colombia have been reported in Nariño, on the Southern Pacific coast.

Regarding elevation, in Colombia, An. calderoni was found at varying altitudinal ranges below 1,480 m a.s.l. In Perú, this species has been reported between coastal areas to 1,080 m a.s.l. [41], and in Venezuela, it has been found between 200 and 400 m a.s.l. [42].

It is important to highlight that we have created a database of records since the 1920’s. For the generation of this database, as geographical boundaries of departments and municipalities have changed over the last 100 years, an overlay of previous and current Colombian municipality layer was performed. Also, the latitude/longitude positions were obtained, when possible, and elevation for each record was extracted from the U.S. Geological Service (USGS) Digital Elevation Model-Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) 1 Arc-Second Global—which has a resolution of 30 m. In countries, such as Colombia, characterized by the presence of the Andes mountains, land elevations are highly irregular in many regions. By integrating the SRTM 30-m Digital Elevation Model with the dataset of a century of observations, this manuscript developed a unique database that comprises time, latitude, longitude, and altitude.

The manuscript also highlights the importance of An. calderoni as a local vector, describes its distribution, and summarizes its natural infection status, an aspect that remains underreported in the literature.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals that the principal malaria vectors in Colombia, An. albimanus, An. darlingi, An. nuneztovari s.s., An. neivai s.l. and An. calderoni, are widely distributed in the territory, encompassing both coastal and inland areas, and a variety of ecoregions, highlighting the Chocó-Darién moist forests and the Magdalena-Urabá moist forests. Besides, regarding altitude, these malaria vectors are mainly distributed below 500 m a.s.l. Nevertheless, An. albimanus and An. calderoni are also present at elevations over 1,000 m a.s.l. Noteworthy, this research suggests that An. albimanus and An. neivai s.l. have increased their elevation range over time. The presence of these two vector species at higher altitudes since the 2000s, suggests that human activities and climate variation and change might be influencing this altitudinal expansion. However, caution is needed as collections through time have not been performed in the same locations, therefore it cannot be ruled out that the species were present previously at the collection sites and altitudes. Further and robust data is needed to evaluate this issue.

This study characterized the ecoregional and altitudinal distribution of the main malaria vectors over ten decades in Colombia. Identifying the ecoregions and elevation in which malaria vectors are distributed will contribute to determining receptivity areas for designing malaria stratification plans in the country. This will lead to the identification of prioritized areas for the prevention and control of malaria.
