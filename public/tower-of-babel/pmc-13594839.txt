Screening and identification of genes for cell autonomous sex identity in chicken somatic cells
Authors: Zidi Jin, Xiaolin Wu, Aneeqa Imtiaz, Jing Ge, Haizhou Gong, Minmeng Zhao, Daoqing Gong, Long Liu, Tuoyu Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChicken somatic cells exhibit cell autonomous sex identity (CASI), where sexual features are determined by sex chromosomes rather than hormones. CASI arises from sex‑biased gene expression due to lack of dosage compensation on sex chromosomes. To identify CASI‑determining genes (CASIDGs), we analyzed transcriptomes of wattles (phenotypically hormone‑sensitive) and pectoral muscles (phenotypically hormone‑unresponsive) from 6‑week‑old roosters and hens, with or without sex hormone treatment. Inter‑sex differentially expressed genes (DEGs) mainly located on the Z chromosome and autosomes 1‑2, while hormone‑affected DEGs predominantly on autosomes 1‑3. After removing hormone‑affected DEGs from the inter‑sex DEGs, 285 DEGs (51.23% on sex chromosome) remained for pectoral muscle, and 255 DEGs (63.93% on sex chromosome) for wattle. Out of the 89 DEGs shared by these two tissues, 88 were located on sex chromosomes and were considered candidate CASIDGs. These candidate CASIDGs were mainly enriched in mitochondrial protein synthesis and isomerase activity-related pathways. Among the CASIDGs, HINT1 and MED18 were validated according to the screening criteria. Overexpressing these two genes in primary muscle cells could affect immune/inflammatory responses, cell proliferation and migration, cytoskeleton, and extracellular matrix interactions. Additionally, 139 autosomal DEGs for pectoral muscle and 92 for wattle were identified as candidate sex marker genes. Among these, IRF9 and CCL4 were co‑regulated by HINT1 and MED18. In conclusion, the sex characteristics of chicken somatic cells reflect the combined effects of CASIDGs and sex hormones. HINT1 and MED18 are confirmed as candidate CASIDGs. These findings lay a solid foundation for elucidating the CASI mechanism in avian somatic cells.Keywords: Chicken, Cell autonomous sex identity, Sex hormone, Sex determining gene, Sex marker gene

Introduction

Sex is closely related to many biological traits in birds, especially economic traits. The chicken (Gallus gallus) is an important economic animal and model organism. Discovering sex-determining genes and elucidating the relevant mechanisms have important theoretical and practical values for understanding sex evolution in avian species and for sex regulation in poultry. Currently, there are few studies on the determination of sex characteristics in non-gonadal tissues of chickens, such as wattles and muscles. Our recent study indicates that non-gonadal tissues in chickens differ in their sensitivity to sex hormones, for example, wattles are phenotypically sensitive to sex hormone treatment, while muscles are phenotypically unresponsive. This sensitivity is related to the protein abundance of the sex hormone receptor ERα in the tissues (Jin et al., 2026). However, muscles and other phenotypically hormone-unresponsive non-gonadal tissues still show obvious sex differences between roosters and hens. This suggests the presence of other determining factors.

To explain this, previous researchers proposed the concept of cell autonomous sex identity (CASI) (Zhao et al., 2010). The CASI hypothesis was initially based on studies of three naturally occurring gynandromorphic chickens. In these chickens, the levels of sex hormones circulating in the blood were the same on the left and right sides of the body, but the sex characteristics on the two sides were different. In situ hybridisation investigations revealed that the body side exhibiting male traits predominantly had ZZ sex chromosome constitution, while the female phenotype side predominantly had ZW constitution. This discovery indicates that the genes responsible for sex traits in chicken somatic cells reside on the sex chromosomes.

The prevailing consensus holds that sex chromosomes harbor genes responsible for sexual characteristics in chicken somatic cells, i.e., CASI-determining genes (CASIDGs). Genes located on the chicken Z chromosome typically lack dosage compensation (Kuroda et al., 2001; Kuroiwa et al., 2002; Ellegren et al., 2007; Sun et al., 2019). Therefore, the expression level of CASIDGs in males is about twice that in females. This dosage difference causes females to exhibit female characteristics and males to show male characteristics(Clinton et al., 2012). In addition, because males lack the W chromosome, genes on the W chromosome may also be candidates for CASIDGs due to dosage effects.

Previous studies have analyzed transcriptome of chicken blastoderms at Hamburger‑Hamilton stage 4 (HH4), when gonadal sex has not yet differentiated. More than 200 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) on sex chromosomes were identified between males and females (Zhang et al., 2010). At this stage, undifferentiated gonads cannot produce sex hormones. Therefore, the expression differences of these DEGs are not caused by sex hormones but by gene dosage effects. These DEGs on sex chromosomes can be considered candidates for CASIDGs. In addition, other studies have sequenced gonads of 4.5-day-old male and female embryos and brains at different embryonic stages. It was estimated that there are about 300 candidate CASIDGs (Ayers et al., 2013). However, most of the candidate CASIDGs have been investigated in studies on sex differentiation of chicken gonads. For example, DMRT1 on the Z chromosome has been demonstrated to be involved in testis differentiation (Ioannidis et al., 2021). miR‑2954 plays a key role in dosage compensation of avian sex chromosomes (Fallahshahroudi et al., 2025). Except for the gonads, the roles of these genes in sex determination of other non-gonadal tissues have not been reported.

Given that differentiation of different tissues involves epigenetic mechanisms, different tissues may share common CASIDGs and also have distinct ones. Previously identified candidate CASIDGs were based on chicken embryos at very early developmental stages. At those stages, many tissues have not yet begun to differentiate or have not completed differentiation. Therefore, such screening has certain limitations. This study aimed to screen for DEGs between sexes in the wattles and pectoral muscles of 6‑week‑old chickens, and then to remove those genes affected by sex hormones. In this way, putative CASIDGs situated on sex chromosomes could be identified, which will establish a basis for clarifying the mechanisms of sex determination in non-gonadal tissues.

Materials and methods

Construction of sex hormone injection model chickens

Fifty Hy-Line White commercial day-old chicks with similar body weights were selected. Their sex was determined by vent sexing (25 roosters and 25 hens). They were raised under conventional management conditions until 2 weeks of age. Healthy roosters and hens were selected (24 of each sex) for sex hormone injection. Roosters were randomly divided into two groups: a control group (injected with saline) and a hormone treatment group (injected with β‑estradiol). Hens were also randomly assigned to a control group (saline injected) and a hormone treatment group (injected with testosterone). Starting at 2 weeks of age, each chicken received an intramuscular injection into the pectoral muscle every two days. The injection was either saline or the corresponding sex hormone. The dosage was 5 mg per kg body weight (according to preliminary unpublished data).

The injections continued until 6 weeks of age. During this period, all experimental chickens were raised on wire floors in the same room, managed under conventional conditions, and had free access to food and water.

The β‑estradiol (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and testosterone (Solarbio, Beijing, China) solutions were both dissolved in DMSO (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Before injection, they were diluted to the appropriate concentration with saline. Before slaughter, the chickens were fasted for 12 h. After fasting, each chicken was weighed. The comb and wattle were photographed and measured. The chickens were then euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. The pectoral muscle was dissected and weighed. Tissue samples of the gonads, pectoral muscle, and wattle were collected from the same site of each tissue. These samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a -80 °C freezer for long‑term storage. In addition, a small piece of pectoral muscle tissue was taken from each chicken. PCR-based sex identification was then performed to confirm the sex determined by vent sexing.

PCR-based sex identification

Sex identification was performed following the manufacturer's instructions. The reagents used were MightAmpTM DNA Polymerase Ver.2 (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) and chicken CHD1 primers. The samples tested were pectoral muscle tissue samples or blood samples from E6 chicken embryos(Ellegren, 1996).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of muscle and wattle tissues

The general procedure for transcriptome sequencing was as follows. Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent. The RNA was then treated with DNase I to remove genomic DNA. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The RNA Integrity Number (RIN) for all samples was ensured to be ≥ 7.0. Next, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The mRNA was fragmented and used as a template to synthesize cDNA. After constructing the sequencing library, paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control and filtering to obtain clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (GRCg6a) using HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified using featureCounts. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with different filtering criteria based on the experimental requirements.

To identify the DEGs between untreated males (roosters) and females (hens) separately for pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, the screening criteria were |log₂ (Fold Change) | > 0.585 and P < 0.05.

To identify the DEGs regulated by different sex hormones separately for pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, the transcriptomic data of β-estradiol treated-roosters were compared with the untreated-roosters, or the transcriptomic data of testosterone treated-hens were compared with the untreated-hens. The screening criteria were (|log₂ (Fold Change) | > 0.585 and P < 0.05).

To identify the putative CASIDGs separately for pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, substracting the genes regulated by sex hormones from inter-sex DEGs was performed. The remaining DEGs on the sex chromosomes are putative CASIDGs. It is noteworthy that here the criteria for identification of genes regulated by sex hormones were only |log₂ (Fold Change) | > 0.585 without considering P value. Applying this lenient threshold allows for capturing as many hormone-affected genes as possible (including those with marginal significance), ensuring that the remaining CASIDGs retain high specificity. This approach prioritizes sensitivity in removing hormone-affected genes rather than excluding false positives in the traditional statistical sense. The putative CASIDGs common to the two tissues were also identified.

To identify the pathways enriched by DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and KEGG pathway annotation were performed using R 4.5.1. Pathways or biological processes with P < 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Screening method for sex marker genes in chicken somatic tissues

To identify candidate sex marker genes separately for pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, the gene sets obtained after removal of hormone-affected genes using the lenient criterion described above were used. From these two gene sets, genes located on autosomes were retained. This yields the candidate sex marker gene set for chicken pectoral muscle tissue and the candidate sex marker gene set for chicken wattle tissue, respectively. The obtained candidate sex marker genes were further validated by quantitative PCR (qPCR).

Collection of chicken embryo tissue samples

Thirty fertilized eggs of similar size were selected from Hy‑Line White commercial layer hens. The eggs were incubated using a standard incubation method. On day 6 and day 12 of incubation, the fertilized eggs were taken out and the eggshells were wiped with 75% alcohol for disinfection. The eggs were then opened, and the embryos were removed. The embryos were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Pectoral muscle tissue was carefully dissected and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples were then transferred to a -80 °C freezer for storage. For embryos collected on day 6 of incubation, a small amount of blood was taken, and PCR-based sex identification was performed to confirm the genetic sex of each embryo. For embryos collected on day 12 of incubation, the morphology of the gonads was observed to confirm the genetic sex.

For the nucleocytoplasmic separation experiment, one male and one female embryo were collected at 12 days of incubation. Fresh gonad and pectoral muscle tissues were carefully dissected and placed in sterile PBS. These tissues were immediately used for nuclear and cytoplasmic separation.

Nucleocytoplasmic separation of tissues

Nuclear and cytoplasmic separation was performed using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Fresh gonad and pectoral muscle tissues from 12-day-old chicken embryos were used. The general procedure was as follows. Under hypotonic conditions, a cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent was used to fully swell the cells and disrupt the cell membranes., releasing the cytoplasmic proteins. After centrifugation, the cytoplasmic supernatant and the nuclear pellet were collected. Then, a high-salt nuclear protein extraction reagent was used to extract the nuclear proteins.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRNzol Universal (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The general procedure was as follows. Tissues were fully homogenized or cells lysed in TRNzol reagent. Chloroform was added to achieve phase separation. After centrifugation, the upper aqueous phase containing RNA was collected. Isopropanol was added to precipitate the RNA. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol to remove impurities. Finally, the RNA was dried and dissolved in nuclease-free water to obtain total RNA.

A commercial kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to reverse transcribe total RNA into cDNA. The general procedure was as follows. Total RNA was mixed with primers and heated for denaturation. Then reverse transcriptase, dNTPs, buffer, and RNase inhibitor were added. The mixture was incubated at 42 °C to synthesize first-strand cDNA. Finally, the reverse transcriptase was inactivated by heating.

All qPCR primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software. They were synthesized by GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Primer information is listed in Supplementary Table 1. qPCR was performed on a real-time quantitative PCR instrument using a commercial kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The reaction mixture contained SYBR Green master mix, forward and reverse primers, and cDNA template. 28S was used as the internal reference gene. Results were analyzed using the 2‑ΔΔCt method.

Protein extraction and western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (APPLYGEN, Beijing, China). The general procedure was as follows. Cell pellets or homogenized tissues were mixed with pre-chilled RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and lysed on ice for 20 minutes. The lysate was then centrifuged at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected.

Protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions to ensure equal loading amounts of protein. The general procedure was as follows. BCA working solution was prepared. The test samples and standards were added to a 96-well plate. The working solution was then added and mixed. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 25 minutes. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm. Protein concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

Protein samples with measured concentrations were mixed with protein loading buffer. The mixture was heated in a metal bath at 95 °C for 10 minutes for denaturation. Protein samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. The separated proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer method. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk. The membrane was then incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After that, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding HRP-labelled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. The antibodies used in this study were as follows. SMAD2Z antibody (12570-1-AP), HINT1 antibody (10717-1-AP), CDK7 antibody (27027-1-AP), MED18 antibody (15352-1-AP), and GAPDH antibody (60004-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Goat anti rabbit IgG (CW0103S) and Goat anti mouse IgG (CW0102S) were purchased from Jiangsu Coen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Isolation and culture of primary muscle cells

Ten fertilized eggs of similar size were selected from Hy‑Line White commercial layer hens. The eggs were incubated using a standard incubation method. After 12 days of incubation, primary muscle cells were isolated. The fertilized eggs were taken out and wiped with 75% alcohol for disinfection. The eggshells were opened, and the embryos were removed. The embryos were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The sex of each embryo was confirmed by observing the morphology of the gonads under a stereomicroscope. The pectoral muscle tissue was carefully dissected and placed in sterile PBS. The tissue was washed twice with sterile PBS. Pre-warmed trypsin (Gibco, NY, USA) was then added. The mixture was digested in a 37 °C-water bath. After complete digestion, DMEM (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, NY, USA) was added to stop digestion. The mixture was then centrifuged. The cell pellet was washed once with sterile PBS. Complete medium (DMEM, 10% FBS, and 100× penicillin streptomycin) was added to resuspend the cells. The cell suspension was filtered through a 40μm cell strainer. The cells were then counted and diluted to an appropriate density. The cells were seeded into cell culture plates and cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2.

Overexpression of HINT1 or MED18 in chicken primary muscle cells

The coding sequences (CDS) of chicken HINT1 (GenBank: NM_204689.2) and MED18 (GenBank: XM_003642960.6) were amplified by RT‑PCR from chicken ovarian cDNA. EcoRI and HindIII restriction sites were introduced at both ends of each amplicon. Each PCR product and the pcDNA3.1(+) vector were double‑digested with EcoRI and HindIII, then ligated and transformed into DH5α competent cells. Expression of the exogenous sequences in pcDNA3.1(+) is driven by the CMV promoter and terminated by the bGH poly(A) signal. The recombinant plasmids (named pcDNA3.1‑HINT1 and pcDNA3.1‑MED18) were verified by DNA sequencing and then used in subsequent experiments.

Transfection of chicken primary muscle cells with the recombinant plasmids pcDNA3.1‑HINT1 and pcDNA3.1‑MED18 (separately) was performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The general procedure was as follows. Lipofectamine 3000 reagent and DNA were each diluted with Opti MEM. P3000 reagent was added to the DNA dilution. The two dilutions were then mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes to form liposome-DNA complexes. Finally, the complexes were added to the cell culture medium. The cells were cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Cells in 12-well plates were harvested after 24 hours for transcriptome sequencing. After 48 hours, cells in 6-well plates were harvested, and proteins were extracted to assess the efficacy of overexpression.

Screening of DEGs induced by overexpression of HINT1 or MED18 in chicken primary muscle cells

Considering that cellular studies exhibit greater repeatability and less inter-individual variability relative to tissue samples, a more rigorous screening criterion was employed for the identification of DEGs in the cellular experiments. This facilitated the control of the false positive rate and enhanced the dependability of the outcomes.

After measuring gene expression with featureCounts, DESeq2 software was used to detect DEGs in chicken primary muscle cells. The criteria were |log2(Fold Change) | > 1 and padj < 0.05. GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG signaling pathway annotation were performed on the identified DEGs using R 4.5.1. GO terms and KEGG pathways with padj < 0.05 were considered significant.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using R 4.5.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, an independent samples t-test or Mann‑Whitney U test was used depending on the data distribution. For comparisons among multiple groups, one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test, or the Kruskal‑Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test, was used depending on the data distribution. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. For two‑group comparisons, significant differences are indicated by asterisks (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01). For multiple group comparisons, significant differences among groups are indicated by letters. Groups sharing the same letter are not statistically different (P > 0.05), while groups with completely different letters are statistically different (P < 0.05).

Results

Sex hormone injection causes tissue-specific changes in sex phenotypes in chickens

Starting at 2 weeks of age, hens were injected with testosterone, and roosters were injected with β‑estradiol. The dose was 5 mg per kg body weight. The injections lasted for 4 weeks. The results showed that compared with control hens, the testosterone-injected hens had significantly larger combs and wattles. Their comb and wattle color was the same as that of control roosters. On the other hand, compared with control roosters, the β‑estradiol-injected roosters had significantly smaller combs and wattles. Their comb and wattle color was the same as that of control hens (Fig. 1A). This indicates that the comb and wattle are phenotypically hormone-sensitive somatic tissues, and their sex phenotypes are easily affected by sex hormones. However, measurement of pectoral muscle weight showed that muscle weight was not significantly affected by testosterone or β‑estradiol injection. This suggests that chicken pectoral muscle is a phenotypically hormone-unresponsive somatic tissue (Fig. 1B).

Screening and analysis of hormone-responsive DEGs in the pectoral muscle and wattle of roosters and hens

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of pectoral muscle from control roosters vs. estrogen-treated roosters identified 1090 DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Among these, 608 DEGs were upregulated after estrogen treatment, and 482 DEGs were downregulated (see Table 3 for the top 10 genes with the highest fold change in each group). Validation of candidate CASIDGs

From the 88 shared candidate CASIDGs, we prioritized four genes (HINT1, MED18, CDK7, and SMAD2Z) for Western blot validation. The selection criteria were as follows: (1) these genes are located on the Z chromosome and showed consistent differential expression between males and females in both pectoral muscle and wattle; and (2) they exhibited robust and reproducible expression signals in our transcriptome data, making them suitable for further validation.

Mouse liver protein samples were used as positive controls. We tested the feasibility of antibodies against the proteins encoded by these genes in chicken pectoral muscle and wattle protein samples by Western blotting (WB). As predicted, chicken HINT1 showed a band near 15 kDa; chicken MED18 showed a band near 25 kDa; chicken CDK7 showed a band near the predicted size of 40 kDa; chicken SMAD2Z showed a band between 55 and 70 kDa (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Using these antibodies, we measured the protein expression levels of CDK7, SMAD2Z, HINT1, and MED18 by WB in the wattle and pectoral muscle tissues of control hens, androgen-treated hens, control roosters, and estrogen-treated roosters. The results showed that the protein abundance of CDK7 in wattle tissue was not significantly different among the groups. In pectoral muscle, CDK7 protein abundance was extremely low and could not be detected by WB (n = 6, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). The protein abundance of SMAD2Z in both wattle and pectoral muscle showed no significant differences among the groups either (n = 6, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). For HINT1 and MED18, their protein abundances in both wattle and pectoral muscle showed significant differences or a trend of difference between roosters and hens, and sex hormone injection did not significantly change their protein abundances (n = 6, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Similar results were then obtained for HINT1 and MED18 protein abundances in gonadal tissues (n = 6, Supplementary Fig. 12): both genes showed significant expression differences between roosters and hens and were unaffected by sex hormone treatment. Based on these Western blot results showing that HINT1 and MED18, but not CDK7 or SMAD2Z, exhibited significant sex-differential protein abundance that was unaffected by hormone treatment, we selected HINT1 and MED18 for subsequent overexpression experiments.

Screening of sex marker genes in somatic tissues

Since there is currently a lack of sex marker genes for somatic tissues, this study screened for sex marker genes in somatic tissues. In this study, sex marker genes were defined as autosomal genes that show significant expression differences between roosters and hens and are not affected by sex hormones. These sex marker genes serve as downstream sex-biased biomarkers rather than sex-determining factors per se, as their sex-biased expression is driven by upstream sex-linked CASIDGs. Using transcriptome sequencing data from the pectoral muscles of untreated control roosters and hens, we identified inter-sex DEGs. From these, we removed DEGs affected by estrogen or androgen treatment in the pectoral muscle. This yielded 139 DEGs located on autosomes (see Supplementary Table 7 for the complete gene list). Their distribution is shown in Supplementary Fig. 21A. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (18 DEGs, 12.95%), autosome 2 (11 DEGs, 7.91%), and autosome 4 (10 DEGs, 7.19%). These DEGs were considered sex marker genes for pectoral muscle tissue. Eight genes were randomly selected from these genes for qPCR validation. The results showed that the expression changes of all eight genes were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing data (Supplementary Fig. 21B), indicating that the screening results for sex marker genes were reliable.

Similarly, for wattle tissue, we removed DEGs affected by estrogen or androgen treatment in wattle from the inter-sex DEGs. This yielded 92 DEGs located on autosomes (see Supplementary Table 8 for the complete gene list). Their distribution is shown in Supplementary Fig. 22A. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (16 DEGs, 17.39%), autosome 4 (12 DEGs, 13.04%), and autosome 2 (11 DEGs, 11.96%). These DEGs were considered sex marker genes for wattle tissue. Similarly, eight genes were randomly selected from these genes for qPCR validation. The results showed that among the eight genes, seven genes (TREMB2, STEAP3, CFH, CST3, ADA, PDZK1IP1, and S100A10) showed expression changes consistent with the transcriptome sequencing data (Supplementary Fig. 22B).

By comparing the sex marker genes screened from pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, we found that the two tissues shared almost no common sex marker genes. This indicates that sex marker genes are tissue-specific.

By comparing the sex marker genes of pectoral muscle with the DEGs induced by HINT1 overexpression, we identified five sex marker genes affected by HINT1 overexpression. qPCR validation showed that among these, the expression changes of APOA1, IRF9, and CCL4 were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing data in both roosters and hens (Fig. 12A-B). Similarly, we identified two sex marker genes affected by MED18 overexpression, namely IRF9 and CCL4. qPCR validation confirmed that the expression changes of these two genes were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing data in both roosters and hens (Fig. 12C-D). Combining the results of HINT1 and MED18 overexpression, IRF9 and CCL4 are sex marker genes in pectoral muscle tissue that are regulated by both HINT1 and MED18.

Discussion

Although previous studies have observed the phenomenon of cell autonomous sex identity, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Identifying CASIDGs will help elucidate these mechanisms. Current evidence suggests that CASIDGs are located on sex chromosomes and exhibit dosage effects (Clinton et al., 2012). Previous studies have performed transcriptome sequencing analysis using chicken embryos before gonadal sex differentiation and identified some DEGs located on sex chromosomes. These DEGs can be considered candidates for CASIDGs (Melamed and Arnold, 2007; Zhang et al., 2010; Ayers et al., 2013). Given that tissues are less differentiated at early developmental stage and that tissue differentiation is substantially influenced by epigenetic mechanisms, the present study identified CASIDGs after gonadal sex differentiation using 6-week-old sex hormone-treated and untreated roosters and hens.

The screening strategy was as follows: from the inter-sex DEGs, we removed DEGs affected by sex hormones (using a lenient criterion without P-value threshold to prioritize sensitivity, ensuring that the remaining CASIDGs have high specificity). The remaining DEGs located on sex chromosomes were considered candidates for CASIDGs.

In this study, wattle and pectoral muscle were selected for screening CASIDGs. Our previous results showed that the sensitivity of different tissues to sex hormones is related to the protein abundance of the estrogen receptor ERα(Jin et al., 2026). The protein abundance of ERα in wattle is high, making it sensitive to sex hormones. In contrast, ERαabundance in pectoral muscle is low, making it insensitive. Indeed, androgen injection caused hen wattles and combs to become larger and redder, while estrogen injection caused rooster wattles and combs to become smaller and yellower. This indicates that the sex hormone injection experiments were successful, and that wattle and comb can represent phenotypically hormone-sensitive somatic tissues. In contrast, for both roosters and hens, injection of testosterone or β‑estradiol did not cause significant changes in pectoral muscle weight. This indicates that pectoral muscle can represent a phenotypically hormone-unresponsive somatic tissue. Therefore, using these two tissues allowed us to screen for tissue-specific CASIDGs.

In 6-week-old control roosters and hens, the inter-sex DEGs in somatic tissues reflect the influence of CASIDGs as well as the regulatory impact of sex hormones. We identified a total of 854 DEGs in the pectoral muscle. These genes are mainly located on the Z chromosome (18.50%), autosome 1 (13.00%), and autosome 2 (7.26%). In wattle, we identified a total of 1156 DEGs. These genes are mainly located on the Z chromosome (16.78%), autosome 1 (12.80%), and autosome 2 (7.53%). Upon comparison of DEGs between the pectoral muscle and wattle, we identified 199 shared DEGs. These genes are mainly located on the Z chromosome (46.23%), the W chromosome (15.58%), and autosome 1 (8.54%). Consequently, whether in specific tissues or in the collective DEGs, the DEGs were predominantly localized on the sex chromosomes. The differential expression of these DEGs on sex chromosomes primarily pertains to gene dosage effects; however, some may also result from sex hormone influences.

In contrast, the expression differences of DEGs on autosomes between sexes can be attributed to regulation by sex‑linked DEGs and to sex hormone effects. Although the sex‑linked DEGs had the highest numbers among the inter‑sex DEGs, the majority of DEGs were still distributed on autosomes. DEGs on autosomes 1 and 2 accounted for the highest proportions, which is related to their lengths and gene numbers (Elferink et al., 2010). It is worth noting that the number of DEGs in wattle was 35.36% higher than that in pectoral muscle. This is likely because wattle is more sensitive to sex hormones than pectoral muscle. Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs in pectoral muscle were enriched in pathways involved in protein synthesis, response to hormone stimulus, activation of signaling pathways, regulation of development and growth, muscle structure and contraction, lipid metabolism and transport, cell adhesion, and folate synthesis. These pathways may participate in the formation of sex characteristics in pectoral muscle, such as roosters having heavier pectoral muscle than hens but lower fat content. On the other hand, the DEGs in wattle were enriched in pathways involved in anatomical structure morphogenesis, response to growth factor and hormone signals, regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation, cell migration, extracellular matrix composition and cell adhesion, and folate synthesis. These pathways may participate in the formation of sex characteristics in wattle, such as roosters having larger wattles than hens, and differences in wattle morphology and texture between the sexes. Because there were few common DEGs between the pectoral muscle and wattle, the number of pathways obtained from enrichment analysis was also small. The common pathways involved protein synthesis, folate biosynthesis, and cell morphology regulation. The small number of common DEGs between pectoral muscle and wattle is likely related to epigenetic regulation of somatic tissue differentiation (Yu et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2025). This also suggests that different tissues may share some common CASIDGs while also having distinct ones.

To remove DEGs affected by sex hormones and obtain candidate CASIDGs, we treated young chicks with different sex hormones. In 6‑week‑old roosters, we compared estrogen-treated and untreated control pectoral muscles and screened 1090 DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (12.94%), autosome 2 (10.37%), and autosome 3 (7.52%). In 6‑wk‑old hens, we compared androgen-treated and untreated control pectoral muscles and screened 1008 DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (14.88%), autosome 2 (10.12%), and autosome 3 (9.42%). In comparison to the number of DEGs seen between normal roosters and hens in pectoral muscle, both sex hormones stimulated a greater number of DEGs, as the hormonal influence was more significant in the treated groups.

By comparing DEGs induced by estrogen treatment with those induced by androgen treatment in pectoral muscle, we found 181 common DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (18.78%), autosome 2 (13.81%), and autosome 3 (10.50%). Notably, these DEGs were not concentrated on sex chromosomes. This is because the expression differences of CASIDGs on sex chromosomes and the differences they induce in other sex‑linked genes were smaller than the expression differences induced by sex hormone treatment. Similarly, in 6‑week‑old roosters, we compared estrogen-treated and untreated control wattles and screened 1707 DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (13.83%), autosome 2 (9.61%), and autosome 3 (7.56%). In 6‑week‑old hens, we compared androgen-treated and untreated control wattles and screened 1955 DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (13.40%), autosome 2 (9.62%), and autosome 3 (8.59%). Again, these DEGs were not concentrated on sex chromosomes, and their numbers were clearly higher than the number of DEGs between normal roosters and hens in wattle. By comparing DEGs induced by estrogen treatment with those induced by androgen treatment in wattle, we found 543 common DEGs. They were mainly concentrated on autosome 1 (14.73%), autosome 2 (9.76%), and autosome 3 (8.29%). Compared with the pectoral muscle, the number of DEGs screened in wattle after hormone treatment was higher in roosters, in hens, and among the common DEGs. This is consistent with wattle having higher sensitivity to sex hormones than pectoral muscle. Previous studies have shown that tissue specificity is closely related to epigenetic regulation (Yu et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2025). These DEGs, especially those common to both tissues, reflect the molecular mechanisms of sex hormone action to some extent. Pathway enrichment analysis showed that the effects of estrogen in rooster pectoral muscle were mainly reflected in pathways related to blood vessel formation, cell adhesion, anatomical structure formation, muscle cytoskeleton, actin, muscle contraction, and immunity. The effects of androgen in hen pectoral muscle were mainly reflected in pathways related to cell adhesion, protein synthesis, muscle cytoskeleton, anatomical structure morphogenesis, and response to external stimuli. The effects of estrogen and androgen in muscle exhibits some common features (such as effects on cell adhesion, muscle cytoskeleton, and anatomical structure formation) but also had some distinctions, such as estrogen in roosters being related to muscle movement, whereas androgen in hens was related to protein synthesis. For wattle, the effects of estrogen in roosters were mainly reflected in pathways related to cell adhesion, cell movement and migration, anatomical structure morphogenesis, muscle cytoskeleton, actin, neuroactive ligand signaling, calcium signaling, and response to external stimuli. The effects of androgen in hens were mainly reflected in pathways related to blood vessel formation, cell adhesion, muscle cytoskeleton, anatomical structure formation, fatty acid metabolism, calcium signaling, and response to external stimuli. The effects of estrogen and androgen in wattle also shared some common features (such as cell adhesion, muscle cytoskeleton, anatomical structure morphogenesis, calcium signaling, and fat metabolism) and differences (such as neuroactive ligand signaling for estrogen and blood vessel formation for androgen). These sex hormone effects may lead to phenotypic changes in chicken pectoral muscle and wattle tissues. In addition, by comparison, only 8 DEGs were commonly responsive to sex hormone treatment in both pectoral muscle and wattle. They were SLK, PTGR2, FRMD3, MX1, CD320, CCN3, SECISBP2L, and ASPH. Except for FRMD3, which is located on the Z chromosome, the other 7 genes are located on autosomes. Although no previous studies have directly linked these genes to sex hormone responses, one study showed that CCN3 may participate in the formation of certain female‑specific phenotypes in mammals. It was identified as a brain‑derived maternal hormone highly expressed in lactating women and is essential for maintaining bone health during lactation (Babey et al., 2024).

During the screening process for candidate CASIDGs, we removed DEGs regulated by sex hormones from the inter‑sex DEGs present between normal roosters and hens. After this removal, we obtained 285 DEGs in pectoral muscle. Among these, 43.16% were located on the Z chromosome, and 8.07% on the W chromosome. In wattle, we obtained 255 DEGs. Among these, 53.73% were located on the Z chromosome, and 10.20% on the W chromosome. By comparing the DEGs obtained from pectoral muscle and wattle, we found 89 DEGs common to both tissues. Among these common DEGs, 78.65% were located on the Z chromosome, and 20.22% on the W chromosome. Thus, after removing hormone‑affected DEGs, the vast majority of DEGs (98.88%) were located on the sex chromosomes. This is consistent with the hypothesis that CASIDGs reside on the sex chromosomes. Pathway enrichment analysis showed that the candidate CASIDGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to protein synthesis, especially mitochondrial protein synthesis, and isomerase activity. This suggests that candidate CASIDGs may influence the sex characteristics of chicken somatic cells through mechanisms such as regulating mitochondrial protein synthesis.

Previous studies performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on blastoderms of chicken embryos at HH4 (Zhang et al., 2010) and on gonads of 4.5-day-old chicken embryos of different sexes (Ayers et al., 2013). Based on their results, they estimated that the number of CASIDGs could reach 200 to 300. By comparison, among the candidate CASIDGs identified in this study, 40 DEGs were the same as those found in the HH4 blastoderm analysis. All 40 of these DEGs were located on the Z chromosome. In comparison to the sequencing results from 4.5-day embryonic gonads, 30 DEGs were identical, all of which were situated on the Z chromosome.

However, it is worth noting that there are several differences between this study and previous studies. First, the samples used were different. This study used pectoral muscle and wattle tissues, which are already well differentiated. Previous studies used whole blastoderms (containing all tissues that were not yet fully differentiated) or gonads that had not yet undergone sex differentiation. Second, in this study, the gonads had already differentiated and were capable of producing different sex hormones at the time of sampling. In previous studies, the gonads had not yet differentiated and did not produce different sex hormones. Third, the sequencing technology used in this study was considerably more advanced than that used in previous studies, providing higher sequencing accuracy. Fourth, the reference genome used in this study has been updated many times compared with that used in previous studies, and the gene annotation is more complete. These differences may explain why only some of the candidate CASIDGs overlap between this study and previous studies. For example, DMRT1, a sex determining gene for male gonads identified previously, was not present in the list of candidate CASIDGs identified in this study. This may reflect that different tissues in chickens have different CASIDGs.

In this study, we validated four candidate CASIDGs: HINT1, MED18, CDK7, and SMAD2Z. The results showed that HINT1 and MED18 met the selection criteria for CASIDGs at the protein level (protein abundance showed significant differences between roosters and hens and was not affected by sex hormone treatment). Moreover, sex differences in their protein abundances existed before gonadal sex differentiation. The HINT1 gene located on the chicken Z chromosome (also known as chPKCI, HINTZ) belongs to the histidine triad superfamily. It contains a His-X-His-X-His-X motif (where X is a hydrophobic amino acid). HINT1 plays important roles in various neuropsychiatric diseases (Zimoń et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2017) and is involved in biological processes such as apoptosis regulation, gene transcription, and cell cycle control (Razin et al., 1999; Weiske and Huber, 2006; Cen et al., 2009). In endothelial cells, HINT1 maintains mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism(Gao et al., 2023). Although research on HINT1 in chickens is relatively limited, its family member HINTW located on the W chromosome was previously studied in depth as a candidate gene for ovary determination (Smith et al., 2009; C. Sun et al., 2023; C. Sun et al., 2025). HINT1 is mainly distributed in the cytoplasm, suggesting that it may function through interactions with cytoplasmic proteins. Pathway enrichment analysis after HINT1 overexpression in pectoral muscle cells showed that the DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to immune/inflammatory responses, cell proliferation and development regulation, key growth and survival signaling pathways (such as Wnt and MAPK/ERK), extracellular matrix-cytoskeleton signaling, and intercellular communication. This suggests that HINT1 may influence sex characteristics of somatic tissues through these pathways.

The MED18 gene is located on the chicken Z chromosome. The protein it encodes is an important component of the mediator complex during transcription and plays a key regulatory role in transcription initiation and elongation (Kumafuji et al., 2014). Studies have shown that MED18 can negatively regulate transcription through the CDK/cyclin module (Kumafuji et al., 2014). However, no studies have been reported on the involvement of MED18 in chicken sex determination. Transcriptome sequencing analysis after MED18 overexpression in pectoral muscle cells showed that MED18 may influence sex characteristics of somatic tissues by participating in biological processes such as immune/inflammatory responses, cell chemotaxis and migration, cell proliferation, and regulation of development and growth.

It should be noted that in this study, both HINT1 and MED18 were overexpressed in primary muscle cells of rooster and hen embryos. This was mainly because these two genes are Z‑linked and may have a dosage‑dependent synergistic effect. Roosters have two Z chromosomes, while hens have only one. Furthermore, the dosage compensation system in chickens is imperfect, resulting in nearly all genes on the Z chromosome exhibiting differential expression levels between the sexes. Therefore, when HINT1 and MED18 function in roosters, they may rely on the synergistic effect of genes on the Z chromosome at twice the dose present in hens. Overexpressing them only in hens may not fully reveal this synergistic effect due to the single‑dose background. Overexpressing them in roosters as well can, on one hand, avoid the potential endogenous dosage limitation in hens, and on the other hand, help to more comprehensively analyze the functions of these two genes, making the conclusions more reliable.

In addition to the Z‑linked candidates discussed above, some W‑linked candidate CASIDGs were also identified, such as HINTW and SPIN1W (a spindlin family member implicated in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation). Although these W‑linked genes are exclusively present in females (ZW) and absent in males (ZZ), their potential roles in somatic sex determination have not been experimentally explored. Based on their known or predicted molecular functions, we speculate that they may contribute to the establishment of female somatic sex characteristics through mechanisms such as dosage‑dependent modulation of chromatin structure, RNA processing, and protein ubiquitination. However, we acknowledge that these hypotheses require direct experimental validation in future studies, and the precise functions of W‑linked CASIDGs in somatic tissues remain to be elucidated.

Sex marker genes are important tools in sex research. Sex marker genes for chicken gonads are already well defined, such as DMRT1, SOX9, FOXL2, and CYP19A1(Luo et al., 2024). However, sex marker genes for gonads do not apply to other somatic tissues. This is because the gonads of roosters and hens have obvious morphological differences. The hen's gonad is mainly the ovary, which develops from the cortex, while the rooster's testis develops mainly from the medulla (Guioli et al., 2020; Mizia et al., 2023). Currently known sex marker genes function mainly due to their specific expression in the cortex or medulla. For other somatic tissues, there are no obvious anatomical differences between roosters and hens like those in gonadal tissues. Therefore, sex marker genes for gonads cannot be applied to other somatic tissues. In this study, we screened for sex marker genes in pectoral muscle and wattle according to our defined criteria. A sex marker gene was defined as a gene located on an autosome that shows significant expression differences between the sexes and is not affected by sex hormones, and these genes are downstream sex-biased biomarkers rather than sex-determining factors per se. The results showed 139 candidate sex marker genes for pectoral muscle and 92 for wattle. However, the two tissues shared almost no common sex marker genes, indicating that sex marker genes are tissue specific. Validation showed that 15 out of 16 randomly selected genes had expression patterns consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results. This confirms that the screening results are reliable, and the genes identified in this study can serve as sex marker genes. Consistently, the candidate CASIDGs HINT1 and MED18 both significantly affected the expression of sex marker genes in pectoral muscle. This included their common regulation of the sex marker genes IRF9 and CCL4. These findings further support the view that HINT1 and MED18 are CASIDGs and also support the application value of the identified sex marker genes.

Currently, there are no knockout or gene‑edited chickens available for HINT1, MED18, or the sex marker genes. Therefore, substantial validation of these genes remains to be completed in the future. In addition, the candidate CASIDGs and the somatic sex marker genes identified in this study require further characterization.

Conclusion

This study reveals that the sex characteristics of chicken somatic cells are determined by both the dosage effects of CASIDGs on sex chromosomes and the effects of sex hormones. Genes associated with the effects of sex hormones are predominantly situated on autosomes 1 to 3. Wattle exhibits higher sensitivity to sex hormones than pectoral muscle. The effects of sex hormones in pectoral muscle and wattle involve both common pathways (such as hormone response and cell adhesion) and distinct pathways. For example, the pectoral muscle focuses on muscle contraction and protein synthesis, whereas the wattle focuses on morphogenesis and cell migration.

By removing sex hormone‑affected genes, this study screened out 88 candidate CASIDGs mainly located on sex chromosomes. Among them, 40 overlapped with previous findings in early chicken blastoderms. Both tissues shared common CASIDGs, and each tissue also had its own tissue‑specific CASIDGs. The CASIDGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to mitochondrial protein synthesis and isomerase activity. HINT1 (cytoplasmic) and MED18 (nuclear) were validated as candidate CASIDGs that met the screening criteria. HINT1 may participate in sex determination by regulating cell proliferation, survival, and cytoskeletal signaling. MED18 may mediate immune responses, cell migration, and developmental regulation. This study also found sex marker genes specific to various tissues. These findings clarify the molecular mechanisms of sex hormone action and identify HINT1 and MED18 as significant candidate genes for CASI determination. This work establishes a robust basis for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying cell autonomous sex identity in somatic cells.
