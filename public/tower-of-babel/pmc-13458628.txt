Mechanism of Autophagic Extracellular Vesicles Revealed
Authors: Constantinos Demetriades, Thierry Galli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent work by Mao and colleagues identifies a distinct class of small extracellular vesicles, termed autophagic extracellular vesicles (AEVs), generated from amphisomes upon autophagy induction. In this commentary, we discuss how this study provides important mechanistic insight into the coupling between autophagy and secretion. AEVs are molecularly and functionally distinct from canonical exosomes, being enriched in autophagy‐related components such as LC3 and p62, and dependent on core ATG machinery for their biogenesis. Notably, their secretion is enhanced by autophagy induction and contributes to intercellular communication, particularly in the context of viral infection. These findings position amphisomes as critical sorting hubs that direct cargo toward either degradation or secretion, thereby integrating autophagic and endolysosomal pathways. We further highlight how these results intersect with prior evidence implicating SNARE‐dependent mechanisms, including VAMP7 and stress‐responsive regulators such as GRASP55, in unconventional secretion. Finally, we discuss key unresolved questions, particularly the mechanisms underlying the generation of small intraluminal vesicles within amphisomes and the role of ESCRT machinery in this process. Overall, the identification of AEVs adds a new layer of complexity to extracellular vesicle biology and opens new avenues for understanding how autophagy contributes to intercellular signaling in health and disease.

The study by [1] provides a significant conceptual and mechanistic advance in the field of extracellular vesicle biology by identifying and characterizing a distinct population of autophagy‐derived vesicles, termed AEVs. These secreted vesicles, enriched in the < 100 nm fraction, are generated upon autophagy induction and originate from amphisomes, hybrid organelles formed through the fusion of autophagosomes with multivesicular bodies.

A key contribution of this work lies in the clear molecular distinction between AEVs and canonical exosomes. Mao et al. show that AEVs are enriched in autophagy‐related proteins, including LC3, p62, and components of the autophagic machinery, as well as ESCRT‐III elements, and display a cargo profile that differs from that of classical CD63‐positive exosomes. Interestingly, the authors show that pharmacological induction of autophagy with rapamycin increases the secretion of small extracellular vesicles, specifically enriching the < 100 nm population corresponding to AEVs, without significantly affecting larger vesicle fractions. Importantly, AEV biogenesis depends on core autophagy regulators, including ATG proteins, establishing a direct mechanistic link between autophagosome biogenesis and vesicle secretion.

Beyond their molecular identity, Mao et al. provide functional insight by demonstrating that AEVs contribute to intercellular communication, particularly during viral infection. Their data suggest that AEVs can transport specific protein and signaling cargos, thereby contributing to the propagation of stress or infection‐related signals. This positions AEVs not simply as byproducts of autophagy, but as active mediators of extracellular signaling.

A central implication of this work is that amphisomes serve as critical sorting hubs. Rather than being terminal intermediates en route to lysosomal degradation, these organelles emerge as bifurcation points where cargo can be directed either toward degradation or secretion. The identification of AEVs therefore supports a model in which autophagy is intrinsically coupled to secretory pathways, with cargo selection and vesicle identity determined at the level of endolysosomal–autophagic convergence.

In this context, previous research has suggested that such secretory routes may involve SNARE‐dependent fusion events at the plasma membrane, notably via VAMP7, thereby linking intracellular trafficking to extracellular release [2]. In the latter study, we found that the secretion of LC3 and p62 was decreased in VAMP7 KO and increased in ATG5 KO, aligning well with Mao et al. [1]. In addition, consistent the findings in this new publication [1], we found that Rapamycin could increase axonal growth in neurons, suggesting that AEV secretion may contribute to neuronal development [2]. From our previous work, a role of GRASP55 [3] might also be anticipated in the secretion of the AEVs upon starvation or stress, as demonstrated here by Mao and colleagues [1].

Here, the unresolved issue concerns the mechanism by which small intraluminal vesicles (< 100 nm) are generated within the amphisome from the inner autophagosomal vesicles, which are generally close to 1000 nm in diameter. The authors propose a role for the ESCRT‐III complex within the amphisome lumen, but this raises the question of how the complex would move from the cytosolic face into the lumen across the amphisome‐limiting membrane.

Taken together, Mao et al. establish a new layer of complexity in extracellular vesicle biology by defining a mechanistically distinct vesicle population downstream of non‐degradative autophagy. A key challenge moving forward will be integrating this classification with functional outputs and determining how different vesicle subtypes—exosomes, AEVs, and other endolysosomal‐derived carriers—coordinate to regulate intercellular communication in physiological and pathological contexts.
