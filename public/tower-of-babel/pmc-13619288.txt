Genetically predicted circulating polyunsaturated fatty acids and Crohn’s disease susceptibility
Authors: Tingting Zhu, Qiang Hu, Kaifeng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crohn disease (CD) remains an increasing global health burden. Omega-3 and omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids participate in lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation, but previous observational and interventional studies have produced inconsistent findings. This study aimed to assess whether genetically predicted circulating omega-3, omega-6, and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio are associated with CD susceptibility using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) design. Genome-wide association study summary data for circulating omega-3, omega-6, and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio were obtained from the IEU Open GWAS project (each n = 114,999), while CD outcome data included 12,194 cases and 28,072 controls. Genome-wide significant single-nucleotide polymorphisms (P < 5 × 10−8) were clumped at r2 = 0.001 within 10,000 kb, and all retained instruments had F-statistics > 10. Multiplicative random-effects inverse-variance weighting was used as the primary analysis, supplemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. Heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and robustness were evaluated using Cochran’s Q test, the MR-Egger intercept, leave-one-out analysis, and funnel plots. A total of 46 single-nucleotide polymorphisms for omega-3, 51 for omega-6, and 34 for the omega-6/omega-3 ratio were included. Higher genetically predicted circulating omega-3 levels were associated with lower odds of CD (odds ratio [OR] = 0.823, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.721–0.939, P =.004), whereas omega-6 showed no statistically significant association (OR = 0.885, 95% CI 0.727–1.061, P =.186). A higher genetically predicted omega-6/omega-3 ratio was associated with increased odds of CD (OR = 1.211, 95% CI 1.069–1.372, P =.003). Complementary MR methods showed generally concordant directions, and MR-Egger intercept tests provided no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. However, substantial between-instrument heterogeneity was observed for all three exposures. These findings suggest that genetically predicted higher circulating omega-3 levels and a lower omega-6/omega-3 ratio are associated with lower CD susceptibility, whereas omega-6 alone shows no statistically significant association. Given the substantial between-instrument heterogeneity, these results should be regarded as suggestive genetic evidence rather than definitive proof of causality and should not be interpreted as evidence that dietary omega-3 intake or supplementation prevents CD.

1. Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disorder that can affect any segment of the digestive tract and is characterized by cramp-like abdominal pain, persistent diarrhea, weight loss, and a high rate of complications. The Global Burden of Disease 2021 study estimated that about 5.8 million people worldwide were living with CD in 2021. Although incidence has leveled off in North America and Europe over the past two decades, it continues to rise rapidly in Asia and Latin America.[1] The disease imposes a substantial burden on quality of life, work capacity, and health-care costs, making the early identification of modifiable risk factors and the implementation of primary-prevention strategies increasingly important.

Diet is widely regarded as a key external trigger that can initiate or exacerbate CD. Within the polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) family, omega-3 and omega-6 lipids modulate eicosanoid production, NF-κB signaling, and intestinal barrier function, thereby shaping the balance between mucosal inflammation and epithelial repair.[2,3] Long-chain omega-3 PUFAs, including docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), are generally considered anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and protective of the epithelium, whereas certain omega-6 PUFAs, particularly arachidonic acid, can be metabolized into pro-inflammatory mediators.[4,5] Recent in vitro and animal studies, however, suggest that the upstream omega-6 precursor linoleic acid can, at specific concentrations, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine release, indicating that its effects are not uniformly pro-inflammatory.[6]

Epidemiological findings are similarly inconsistent. Large prospective cohorts have reported an inverse association between marine-derived omega-3 intake and CD risk, yet randomized trials of fish oil supplements have failed to replicate this protective signal.[7] High dietary omega-6 intake or an imbalanced omega-6/omega-3 ratio has been implicated in CD progression by some studies, whereas others have found no significant association.[8] Notably, the omega-6/omega-3 ratio may better capture the net effect of the fatty acid spectrum on mucosal immune and inflammatory networks than absolute intakes alone, but robust causal evidence for this ratio remains limited.

Traditional observational studies are vulnerable to confounding by diet, lifestyle, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, and disease-related changes in food intake. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses germline genetic variants associated with an exposure as instrumental variables and can reduce, but not eliminate, these sources of bias.[9] The validity of causal interpretation depends on three assumptions: the genetic instruments must be associated with the exposure, must be independent of confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship, and must influence the outcome only through the exposure. Consequently, MR estimates should be interpreted as effects of genetically proxied exposure differences rather than as direct estimates of the effect of a specific dietary intervention.

Against this background, we integrated large GWAS datasets and applied a two-sample MR design to evaluate: whether genetically predicted circulating omega-3 levels are associated with CD susceptibility; whether genetically predicted circulating omega-6 levels are associated with CD susceptibility; and whether the genetically predicted omega-6/omega-3 ratio is associated with CD susceptibility. We aimed to provide genetic evidence relevant to PUFA biology in CD while explicitly distinguishing genetically influenced circulating PUFA levels from modifiable dietary intake or supplementation.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

We adopted a two-sample Mendelian-randomization (MR) framework to evaluate the association of genetically predicted circulating omega-3 fatty acids, omega-6 fatty acids, and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio with Crohn’s disease (CD) (Fig. 1). Three assumptions underpin MR (Fig. 2).[10] First, the relevance assumption requires that the instrumental SNPs be robustly associated with the exposure. Second, the independence assumption requires that the instruments are not associated with confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship. Third, the exclusion-restriction assumption requires that the instruments influence CD only through the PUFA exposure being modeled, rather than through an alternative biological pathway. Because the latter two assumptions cannot be proven from GWAS summary data alone, we performed multiple sensitivity analyses and interpreted the findings cautiously.

2.2. Data sources

All summary-level statistics were obtained from the publicly accessible IEU Open GWAS Project. Circulating omega-3 levels were represented by the dataset “met-d-Omega_3” (n = 114,999; 12,321,875 SNPs), circulating omega-6 levels by “met-d-Omega_6” (n = 114,999; 12,321,875 SNPs), and the omega-6/omega-3 ratio by “met-d-Omega_6_by_Omega_3” (n = 114,999; 12,321,875 SNPs). These metabolomic GWAS data derive from large-scale UK Biobank analyses in which multiple genome-wide significant loci were identified for circulating PUFA traits.[11] The CD outcome dataset was “ebi-a-GCST004132,” based on the International IBD Genetics Consortium and comprising 12,194 cases and 28,072 controls (40,266 participants; 9,457,998 SNPs).[12] All participants were of European ancestry. Exposure and outcome summary data were obtained from separate GWAS resources to minimize sample overlap. Because the data were de-identified and publicly available, no additional ethics approval was required.

2.3. SNP selection

Instrumental variants were selected in R using the TwoSampleMR package. SNPs associated with each exposure at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) were clumped using an LD threshold of r2 = 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window. Exposure and outcome alleles were then harmonized, and palindromic or incompatible variants that could not be aligned reliably were excluded. Instrument strength was assessed using F = β2/SE2; all retained variants had F > 10. The statistical association of these variants with circulating PUFA levels satisfies the MR relevance criterion but does not imply that every SNP acts directly on PUFA concentrations. Some variants may influence PUFA levels through fatty-acid desaturation, elongation, transport, or broader lipid-metabolic pathways; therefore, potential horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated in sensitivity analyses and considered in the interpretation.

2.4. Statistical methods

Data import, instrumental-variable selection, Mendelian-randomization (MR) analyses, and sensitivity tests were carried out with the “TwoSampleMR” package (version 0.6.15) in R 4.4.1.

2.4.1. MR analysis

The primary analysis used the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) estimator. In the TwoSampleMR package, the primary IVW analysis was implemented using a multiplicative random-effects model. Briefly, SNP-specific associations with CD were regressed on the corresponding SNP-exposure associations with the intercept constrained to zero, and each variant was weighted according to the inverse variance of its SNP-outcome association.[10,13] This approach combines the individual Wald-ratio estimates across genetic instruments. The multiplicative random-effects IVW model allows for over-dispersion among SNP-specific estimates and adjusts the standard error of the pooled estimate when between-instrument heterogeneity is present. Importantly, this approach accounts for statistical heterogeneity in the uncertainty of the pooled estimate but does not identify the source of heterogeneity or eliminate bias arising from invalid instruments or horizontal pleiotropy. Therefore, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods were used as complementary estimators. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

2.4.2. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the MR estimates and potential violations of the instrumental-variable assumptions. First, directional horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test; a statistically significant nonzero intercept was considered evidence of average directional pleiotropy. Second, Cochran’s Q statistics were calculated under both IVW and MR-Egger models to assess between-instrument heterogeneity. A statistically significant Q statistic was interpreted as evidence that the SNP-specific estimates varied more than expected from sampling error alone. Such heterogeneity was considered an important limitation that reduces the certainty of interpreting the pooled estimate as a homogeneous causal effect. Third, leave-one-out analyses were performed by sequentially removing each SNP to determine whether the overall estimate was disproportionately driven by a single genetic variant. Fourth, funnel plots were inspected for marked asymmetry. Because these sensitivity analyses have limited ability to detect all forms of horizontal pleiotropy, particularly balanced or pathway-specific pleiotropy, concordance across methods was considered supportive but not definitive evidence for causality.

3.1.2. MR analysis results

The multiplicative random-effects IVW analysis showed an inverse association between genetically predicted circulating omega-3 and CD [OR = 0.823, 95% CI 0.721–0.939, P = .004]. MR-Egger, weighted median, and simple mode estimates were directionally consistent and statistically significant (P < .05). 3.1.3. Sensitivity analysis

Cochran’s Q test showed substantial heterogeneity among the omega-3 instruments under both the IVW model (Q = 114.548, P < .001) and MR-Egger regression (Q = 110.620, P < .001). This significant between-instrument heterogeneity indicates that the SNP-specific estimates are not fully homogeneous and therefore reduces the certainty with which the pooled estimate can be interpreted as a single causal effect. We used a multiplicative random-effects IVW model, which allows for over-dispersion among SNP-specific estimates and adjusts the standard error of the pooled estimate in the presence of heterogeneity. However, this approach does not identify the source of heterogeneity and cannot eliminate bias caused by invalid instruments or horizontal pleiotropy. The MR-Egger intercept was 0.009 (P = .259), providing no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy, although this test has limited power. Leave-one-out analysis showed that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the pooled association (Fig. 5), and the funnel plot was broadly symmetrical (Fig. 6). These sensitivity analyses provide some reassurance that the association was not driven by one obvious outlier or strong directional pleiotropy; nevertheless, balanced pleiotropy and heterogeneous biological pathways cannot be excluded. Therefore, the observed inverse association should be regarded as suggestive rather than definitive evidence of causality. The forest plot is shown in Figure 7.

3.2.3. Sensitivity analysis

Cochran’s Q test indicated substantial between-instrument heterogeneity (IVW: Q = 109.273, P < .001; MR-Egger: Q = 108.239, P < .001). Although the multiplicative random-effects IVW model accounts for over-dispersion when estimating the uncertainty of the pooled association, it does not eliminate potential bias arising from heterogeneous biological pathways or invalid instruments. The MR-Egger intercept was 0.007 (P = .535), providing no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analysis did not identify a single influential SNP (Fig. 10), and the funnel plot was broadly symmetrical (Fig. 11). Accordingly, the absence of a statistically significant pooled association should not be interpreted as evidence that omega-6 has no biological relevance to CD, particularly in the presence of substantial between-instrument heterogeneity. 3.3.3. Sensitivity analysis

Cochran’s Q test showed substantial heterogeneity for the omega-6/omega-3 ratio (IVW: Q = 74.402, P < .001; MR-Egger: Q = 71.489, P < .001). Thus, the pooled estimate should not be interpreted as a homogeneous effect across all genetic instruments. Although the multiplicative random-effects IVW model accounts for over-dispersion in estimating the standard error, residual biological heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy may remain. The MR-Egger intercept was − 0.009 (P = .304), providing no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. 4. Discussion

In this two-sample MR study, genetically predicted higher circulating omega-3 was associated with lower odds of CD, omega-6 alone showed no statistically significant association, and a higher omega-6/omega-3 ratio was associated with higher CD odds. These findings provide genetic evidence linking circulating PUFA profiles with CD susceptibility. However, substantial between-instrument heterogeneity limits the strength of causal inference, and the results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that altering dietary omega-3 intake or taking fish-oil supplements prevents CD. The exposure captured by MR represents lifelong genetically influenced differences in circulating PUFA traits, which are biologically and temporally distinct from short-term dietary supplementation.

A central issue raised by the present analysis is whether the selected SNPs are genuinely related to circulating omega-3 levels. The genetic relevance of the selected SNPs to circulating omega-3 levels is supported by large-scale GWAS evidence. The exposure dataset was derived from a UK Biobank metabolomic GWAS of more than 114,000 participants, in which dozens of independent genome-wide significant variants were associated with circulating PUFA traits and explained measurable phenotypic variance.[11] Earlier GWAS also identified biologically plausible loci in fatty-acid metabolism, particularly FADS1/FADS2 and ELOVL2, that are strongly associated with circulating long-chain omega-3 species.[14] In our omega-3 instrument set, rs174564, located in the FADS region, showed a particularly strong exposure association. FADS enzymes catalyze key desaturation steps in the conversion of essential fatty-acid precursors, providing a plausible biological route from genotype to circulating PUFA composition.[14,15]

Nevertheless, a genome-wide association between a SNP and omega-3 concentration does not prove that the variant acts exclusively or directly through omega-3. Genetic variants can influence circulating PUFA concentrations through desaturation, elongation, hepatic lipid handling, lipoprotein transport, or other metabolic pathways, and some of these pathways may independently affect inflammatory disease. This distinction is important for the MR exclusion-restriction assumption. Indeed, analyses of the same large UK Biobank PUFA GWAS in other disease settings have shown that estimates can change after accounting for lipoprotein-related traits or excluding variants near the FADS locus, illustrating the potential for correlated lipid pathways and horizontal pleiotropy.[11] Therefore, our results are most appropriately interpreted as evidence based on genetically proxied circulating PUFA traits, not as proof of a single molecular pathway. Accordingly, the present analysis supports the relevance of the selected variants as statistical instruments for circulating PUFA traits but cannot establish that every instrument influences CD exclusively through omega-3 or omega-6 metabolism. This limitation is particularly important when interpreting the findings causally.

Dietary intake and supplementation add a second layer of complexity. Circulating omega-3 is influenced by both environmental exposure and endogenous metabolism; genetic variation affects absorption, conversion, tissue handling, and baseline concentrations, while diet can modify the same biomarker. The random allocation of alleles at conception helps reduce confounding by adult dietary choices, but MR does not assume that diet is irrelevant. Rather, it estimates the association with the genetically determined component of circulating omega-3. Gene–diet interaction is also biologically plausible: FADS variants can modify the relationship between PUFA intake and circulating fatty-acid profiles, and previous work in CD has reported interactions between dietary PUFA ratios and FADS variants.[16] Thus, an MR estimate cannot be translated directly into a recommended dose of fish, omega-3 supplements, or an optimal dietary omega-6/omega-3 ratio. Therefore, the present MR findings have no direct implication for the dose, formulation, duration, or clinical effectiveness of omega-3 supplementation. At present, they should not be used to guide dietary prevention or therapeutic recommendations for CD.

Our findings should also be viewed alongside the mixed clinical literature. Earlier studies suggested possible benefits of omega-3 preparations, whereas large randomized trials and systematic reviews did not demonstrate a consistent benefit of fish-oil supplementation for maintaining remission in CD.[17–20] Previous MR studies have reported inverse associations between genetically predicted omega-3-related traits and IBD or CD, including signals involving EPA and FADS-region variants.[15,21] These apparently different lines of evidence are not necessarily contradictory because genetic proxies represent lifelong metabolic differences, whereas randomized supplementation trials test specific formulations, doses, treatment durations, disease stages, and adherence patterns. The current MR study therefore contributes suggestive etiologic evidence regarding genetically proxied PUFA traits but does not establish clinical efficacy.

The substantial Cochran’s Q statistics represent an important limitation of the present analysis. For omega-3, significant heterogeneity was observed under both the IVW model (Q = 114.548, P < .001) and MR-Egger regression (Q = 110.620, P < .001). We emphasize that this heterogeneity does affect the certainty of our final interpretation. It indicates considerable variability among SNP-specific estimates and raises the possibility that the genetic instruments operate through heterogeneous biological, metabolic, or pleiotropic pathways. We used a multiplicative random-effects IVW estimator because this approach allows for over-dispersion among SNP-specific estimates and adjusts the uncertainty of the pooled estimate when heterogeneity is present. Nevertheless, random-effects modeling cannot correct bias from invalid genetic instruments and does not eliminate horizontal pleiotropy. The nonsignificant MR-Egger intercepts, broadly symmetrical funnel plots, directionally concordant complementary estimators, and leave-one-out analyses provide some reassurance against strong directional pleiotropy or a single dominant variant. However, none of these analyses can fully exclude balanced pleiotropy, correlated lipid pathways, or other sources of biological heterogeneity. Consequently, we do not interpret the significant IVW estimate as definitive evidence of a homogeneous causal effect. Rather, the omega-3 association should be regarded as suggestive genetic evidence that is compatible with, but does not prove, a causal contribution to CD susceptibility.

Mechanistically, long-chain omega-3 PUFAs can give rise to specialized pro-resolving mediators such as resolvins and protectins that attenuate inflammatory signaling and support epithelial-barrier recovery.[22,23] Conversely, arachidonic-acid-derived mediators may promote inflammatory responses under some conditions, although the biology of omega-6 fatty acids is heterogeneous and cannot be reduced to a uniformly pro-inflammatory effect. The FADS pathway is particularly relevant because it influences the conversion of precursor fatty acids into downstream long-chain products and has been implicated in CD-related inflammatory biology.[16,24] These mechanisms provide biological plausibility, but the present MR analysis does not identify which specific omega-3 species, metabolic step, or inflammatory mediator is responsible for the observed association.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all GWAS participants were of European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, the exposure traits represent aggregate circulating omega-3 and omega-6 measures rather than individual PUFA species; therefore, the effects of EPA, DHA, alpha-linolenic acid, linoleic acid, and arachidonic acid cannot be distinguished. Third, substantial between-instrument heterogeneity was observed for all three exposures, raising the possibility of residual horizontal pleiotropy, correlated lipid pathways, or pathway-specific genetic effects despite nonsignificant MR-Egger intercept tests. Fourth, summary-level data prevented assessment of nonlinear associations, detailed gene–diet interactions, disease phenotype, age at onset, or treatment status. Finally, MR estimates represent lifelong genetically proxied differences in circulating PUFA traits and should not be interpreted as estimates of the clinical benefit of dietary supplementation. Future studies incorporating individual PUFA species, pleiotropy-robust approaches, multivariable MR, colocalization or locus-specific analyses, diverse ancestries, detailed dietary information, and prospective intervention studies are needed.

5. Conclusion

Using large-scale GWAS summary data, this two-sample MR study identified an inverse association between genetically predicted higher circulating omega-3 and CD susceptibility, no statistically significant association for omega-6 alone, and a positive association between a genetically predicted higher omega-6/omega-3 ratio and CD susceptibility. However, substantial between-instrument heterogeneity and the possibility of residual horizontal pleiotropy limit the strength of causal inference. Accordingly, these findings should be regarded as suggestive genetic evidence linking circulating PUFA profiles with CD susceptibility rather than definitive proof of causality. They do not establish that dietary omega-3 intake or supplementation prevents CD and should not be translated directly into clinical or nutritional recommendations. Further validation using individual PUFA species, pleiotropy-robust and multivariable genetic analyses, diverse populations, and prospective intervention studies is required.
