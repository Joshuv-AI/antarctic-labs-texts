An introduction to pitch strength in contemporary popular music analysis and production In Music 2024, Innovation in Music Conference 14-16 June, 2024, Kristiania University College, Oslo, Norway
Emmanuel Deruty

Abstract
Music information retrieval distinguishes between low- and high-level descriptions of music. Current generative AI models rely on text descriptions that are higher level than the controls familiar to studio musicians. Pitch strength, a low-level perceptual parameter of contemporary popular music, may be one feature that could make such AI models more suited to music production. Signal and perceptual analyses suggest that pitch strength (1) varies significantly across and inside songs; (2) contributes to both small- and large-scale structure; (3) contributes to the handling of polyphonic dissonance; and (4) may be a feature of upper harmonics made audible in a perspective of perceptual richness.

Chapter plan.

The chapter is organized as follows: first, a characterization of PS is provided. While the literature suggests that PS can be measured using the MPEG7 feature HarmonicRatio, this claim is shown not to be valid in the general case. Then, examples of variable PS in contemporary popular music are given. In particular, it is demonstrated that contemporary popular music covers a wide range of PS. Finally, relations between PS and musical structure, dissonance, and audible higher harmonics are explored.

2
Pitch strength, characterization

Definition.

Yost, 2009
defines pitch strength as:

‘
Pitch strength
(also called
pitch saliency
) refers to the strength of the perceived pitch of a complex sound as compared with the overall perceived quality (timbre) of the sound.’

Signal features and pitch strength.

Zwicker and Fastl, 1990
report that:

•

Sounds with line spectra generate a strong pitch, while continuous spectra produce a weaker pitch, except for narrow-band noise.

•

PS of pure tones increases with duration and sound pressure, peaking at mid-range frequencies.

•

Expanded bandwidth of noise bands reduces PS.

•

Peaked ripple noise can achieve PS similar to complex tones.

•

Low-pass noise with steep spectral slopes has significantly lower PS than pure tones, and the PS decreases further with less steep slopes.

•

Partial masking sounds can significantly reduce the PS of pure tones.

Wightman, 1973
add:

•

More harmonics result in a stronger pitch.

•

Harmonic complex tones have a stronger pitch than inharmonic ones.

Pitch strength and iterative rippled noise.

PS has been extensively studied in iterative rippled noise (IRN
(
Yost et al., 1978
;
Yost and Hill, 1978
;
Yost et al., 1994
;
Yost et al., 1996
;
Yost, 1996
;
Patterson et al., 1996
;
Patterson et al., 2000
)
. According to
Patterson et al., 1996
;
Yost et al., 1994
;
Yost et al., 1996
, the PS of an IRN wave is related to the height of the first peak in its normalized autocorrelation function (AC1), equivalent to the HarmonicRatio MPEG7 feature
(
ISO, 2001
)
. Specifically, the PS of an IRN wave is an exponential function of AC1:
PS=k\cdot 10^{AC1}
, where
k
is a proportionality constant.

Pitch strength and HarmonicRatio.

HarmonicRatio (equivalent to AC1) has been described as ‘the proportion of harmonic components within the power spectrum’
(
ISO, 2001
, p. 36)
and ‘the ratio of harmonic power to total power’
(
Moreau et al., 2006
, p. 33)
. Given that it measures IRN’s PS, and that harmonic sounds elicit a strong impression of pitch, one might consider using HarmonicRatio to measure PS in the general case. However, Appendix
A
suggests that this measure will be at least partially inaccurate.

4
Pitch strength and structure

Large- and small-scale structure.

Deruty et al., 2013
summarize the distinction between large-scale and small-scale musical structures.

Large-scale structure, ‘semiotic structure’, or ‘sectional form’
(
Bimbot et al., 2012
)
, describes the long-term regularities in a musical piece
(
Bimbot et al., 2014
)
. Segments in this structure are ‘autonomous and comparable blocks’
(
Bimbot et al., 2010
;
Bimbot et al., 2014
)
, with a median duration of about 11.5 seconds
(
Snyder, 2000
;
Deruty et al., 2013
)
. In Western classical music, these segments may be classified as ‘sentences’ and ‘periods’
(
Schoenberg, 1967
;
Caplin, 1998
)
, and their relations can be described through comparison
(
Bimbot et al., 2012
)
.

Small-scale structure refers to the internal organization of these segments, termed ‘form’ by
Caplin, 1998
. This time scale is known as the ‘morpho-syntagmatic level’
(
Bimbot et al., 2014
;
Bimbot et al., 2016
)
or the level of ‘melodic and rhythmic grouping’
(
Monelle, 2014
)
. Functions at this level include antecedent, consequent, basic idea, contrasting idea
(
Caplin, 1998
)
, and contrast
(
Bimbot et al., 2016
)
. Relationships between units are described through implication
(
Bimbot et al., 2016
)
, with the contrast identified by deviations from implied relations.

5
Pitch strength and dissonance

Low PS can help manage dissonance.

Following
Tenney, 1988
,
Sethares, 2005
distinguish between five types of consonance-dissonance, numbered CDC-1 to CDC-5. The dissonance interpretation here primarily relates to CDC-2 (polyphonic consonance/dissonance).

Drums and pitch.

Drums can contain pitched content
(
Richardson, 2010
)
. Drum eigenfrequencies are not necessarily harmonic but can be made harmonic under certain conditions
(
Antunes, 2017
)
. Usually, the tonal elements in drums are not structured like partials in a harmonic series. Their frequency relationship ranges from inharmonic to chaotic
(
Wu et al., 2018
)
.

Drums can be tuned. In popular music, the tuning of acoustic drums can have a significant impact on the quality and contextuality of the instrument
(
Toulson et al., 2009
)
. Some drums, such as the timpani
(
Blades, 2001
)
and roto-toms
(
Holland, 2001
)
, have a stronger pitch than others.

Low PS and non-transposition of out-of-tune samples.

The Hyper Music producers (see Appendix
G
) note that when a sound is out of tune but has low PS, they often avoid transposing it to preserve its timbre – which is made possible by low PS reducing dissonance. This practice aligns with
Frisius, 2010
, who states that a music theory positing neutral transposition — where pitch groups retain their character when transposed – overlooks the transposition of the sounds themselves.
Frisius, 2010
cites the composer Luigi Russolo, who found it challenging to transpose sonic gestures into other registers without losing their identity, particularly when the pitch is not clearly definable.

Summary.

Low PS elements provide greater flexibility in using polyphonic dissonance in contemporary popular music.

6
Low-pitch strength audible harmonics

Examples.

The introduction cited
My Bloody Valentine, 1991
, in which the upper harmonics are loud enough to become audible. Similarly, a key aspect of Hyper Music producers’ work is amplifying upper partials while attenuating the fundamental, making the upper partials audible as carriers of perceivable pitches. For example, in the bass part from
Hyper Music, 2023b
, harmonic 5 (two octaves and a major third) is boosted using equalization. The boosted harmonics’ pitch isn’t necessarily strong. Producers may use these harmonics’ low PS to create ambiguity in the perceived number of pitches.

Harmonic tones may convey multiple pitches.

Perceptual tests asked respondents how many simultaneous pitches they could hear in two bass tracks and a keyboard track
(
Hyper Music, 2023c
)
. The boosted harmonics were perceived as independent pitches, with the number varying from 1 to 4. The mean number of perceived pitches was 1.7, 1.95, and 2.45, respectively. Pitches that are not unanimously perceived may be considered as exhibiting low PS.

Role of distortion.

Hyper Music producers may use distortion to boost or generate harmonics. Distortion applies a non-linear transformation to the signal, causing intermodulation distortion
(
Newell, 2017
, p. 464)
, which produces partials from each input partial and their combinations. If
f_{a}
and
f_{b}
are the input frequencies, the output contains frequencies
k_{a}f_{a}+k_{b}f_{b}
, where
(k_{a},k_{b})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Distorting a sine wave generates harmonics, while distorting a harmonic complex tone boosts harmonics, making them audible. Thus, distortion adds low-PS elements.

In
Massive Attack, 2010
, the two guitars and keyboard are distorted, forming streams in the sense of
Bregman, 1994
. Each stream has pitches of varying strength, with harmonics’ pitches being less audible than the perceived fundamental.

Summary.

Using equalization or distortion to boost upper harmonics so that they become low-PS audible components is a way to add perceptual richness to the music.

7
Conclusion and future work

Summary.

The chapter presents arguments for the importance of pitch strength (PS) in contemporary popular music. Signal analysis, combined with perceptual observations, suggests several conclusions:

•

Peak salience, and therefore, PS, vary significantly across songs.

•

Deviations to small-scale form implications may involve PS, making it part of contemporary popular music’s determinant of form.

•

PS may also contribute to the large-scale form.

•

Polyphonic dissonance may be attenuated if at least one involved element has low PS.

•

Amplified upper harmonics may be individually perceivable elements with low PS.

The notion of PS may improve our understanding of contemporary popular music. As a low-level perceptual parameter, it may be a useful addition to generative AI models to better their alignment with human perception and musical practice.

Perspectives.

Future work should aim to consolidate the content of this chapter. While the chapter suggests the importance of PS in contemporary popular music, it does not provide definitive evidence. Similarly, it proposes – but does not demonstrate – the concept’s potential utility in generative AI models. Although it introduces relationships between PS and the noisiness-inharmonicity space, it stops short of establishing a formal link between them. Finally, most perceptual observations presented are the author’s; to improve their reliability, systematic listening tests should be conducted.

Appendix A
ISO226:2003 weighting

Humans are not equally sensitive to all frequencies
(
Fletcher and Munson, 1933
)
. Models, such as
ISO, 2003
, describe how tones are perceived as equally loud depending on their frequency and sound pressure level. This chapter uses the ISO 226-2003 weighting to ensure that evaluated power spectra more accurately reflect the listener’s perceived spectral profile.
