ℓ 2 / ℓ 2 \ell_{2}/\ell_{2} Sparse Recovery via Weighted Hypergraph Peeling
Nick Fischer, Vasileios Nakos

Abstract
We demonstrate that the best k k -sparse approximation of a length- n n vector can be recovered within a ( 1 + ϵ ) (1+\epsilon) -factor approximation in O ⁡ ( ( k / ϵ ) ​ log ⁡ n ) O((k/\epsilon)\log n) time using a non-adaptive linear sketch with O ⁡ ( ( k / ϵ ) ​ log ⁡ n ) O((k/\epsilon)\log n) rows and O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) column sparsity. This improves the running time of the fastest-known sketch [Nakos, Song; STOC ’19] by a factor of log ⁡ n \log n , and is optimal for a wide range of parameters. Our algorithm is simple and likely to be practical, with the analysis built on a new technique we call weighted hypergraph peeling . Our method naturally extends known hypergraph peeling processes (as in the analysis of Invertible Bloom Filters) to a setting where edges and nodes have (possibly correlated) weights.

Technical Overview

In this section we give a high-level overview of our new sketch. It relies on hashing at its main building component, similarly to
[
17
,
41
]
and in all the heavy hitters literature. However, our specific approach differs quite substantially from previous work. We briefly review the two known approaches.

Known Approaches: Iterative and Hierarchical.

In
[
17
]
the vector
x
is hashed to
O(k/\epsilon)
buckets, augmented with an appropriate error-correcting code in such a way that from all buckets that contain
only one
heavy hitter that heavy hitter is recoverable. However, we only expect a constant fraction, say
k/2
, of the heavy hitters to be isolated in their respective buckets. So letting
x^{\prime}
denote the resulting recovered vector, there are still up to
k/2
heavy hitters remaining in the
residual vector

x-x^{\prime}
. To recover these, one can proceed in an iterative fashion, i.e., we essentially repeat the same hashing process where the number of buckets can be reduced by a constant factor. This process can be set up in a such way that eventually, after
O(\log k)
rounds, it yields a vector satisfying the
\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}
guarantee. Due to the iterative procedure, one has to pay at least one
\log n
factor in the column sparsity and decoding time. For details, we refer to
[
17
]
.

In contrast, the improved algorithm in
[
41
]
builds a
\log(k/\epsilon)/\log\log(k/\epsilon)
-degree interval tree over
[n]
and traverses that tree from top to bottom in order to find where the heavy hitters are, recognizing
heavy
intervals per level. At each level elements belonging to the same interval are hashed to the same bucket and combined with
gaussians
, instead of the usual random signs. The choice of gaussians is crucial in order to reduce the likelihood of cancellation, and using the anti-concentration of the gaussian distribution it is shown that
most
energy from heavy hitters is retained if one sets up that process correctly. The branching factor of this process yields one additional
\log
factor in the decoding time, and changing the branching factor crucially affects correctness. For more details, we refer to
[
41
]
.

New Approach: One-Shot.

In a nutshell, as we are aiming for the best-possible
O((k/\epsilon)\log n)
recovery time, our goal is to perform
one-shot
recovery rather than iterative recovery (as in
[
17
]
) or hierarchical recovery (as in
[
41
]
). That is, we will throughout consider a single hashing scheme.

Before we get into the details of our new approach, let us first fix some definitions. We call
i
a
heavy hitter
(or just
heavy
) if
|x_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}
, and otherwise we call
i

light
. Let
T\subseteq[n]
denote the set of the largest
k
entries in
x
. Following
[
41
]
, instead of computing
x^{\prime}
directly we resort to a slight simplification of the original task: It suffices to recover a subset of heavy hitters
R
with the property that we do not miss too much energy from
T
, i.e., such that

\lVert x_{T\setminus R}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq O(\epsilon)\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

One can then easily refine
R
to derive the desired approximation
x^{\prime}
of
x
. For the rest of this overview we elaborate on how to compute such a set
R
.

Inspiration: Hypergraph Peeling.

Our algorithm is inspired by hypergraph peeling processes. This is a widely-applied technique, e.g. in the design of efficient data structures like Invertible Bloom Filters
[
20
]
, cuckoo hashing
[
42
,
9
,
39
,
13
,
15
]
, but also in the context of error-correcting codes
[
32
,
36
]
and random SAT instances
[
40
]
. Before proceeding, we find it instructive to give a quick recap of this technique. The setup is that there are
K
elements which are supposed to be hashed to
O(K)
buckets in such a way that we can recover all
K
elements given only the buckets. The main idea is to hash each element not just to a single bucket, but to
h>1
buckets, where each bucket stores the sum of the elements that are hashed to it. In order to recover the elements from the buckets, one repeatedly finds a bucket that contains exactly one item, recovers that one item and removes (i.e., subtracts) it from all the
h
bucket it participates in. This creates some new singletons, so the algorithm proceeds to recover these new singletons, and so on.

The analysis hinges on random (hyper-)graph theory. Specifically, one models the hashing scheme as an
h
-uniform hypergraph where each bucket is a vertex, and each element is a hyperedge spanning the
h
buckets it is hashed to. The goal is to show that this hypergraph is
peelable
, i.e., that we can step-by-step remove edges containing some degree-1 node (i.e., a bucket containing just one element). Clearly, as the hash function is uniformly random, so is this hypergraph. And as the number of vertices (i.e., buckets) is larger than the number of hyperedge (i.e., elements) by a constant factor, the hypergraph is quite sparse. In such a situation one can prove that with constant probability all connected components are loosely connected and therefore peelable, at least as long as
h\geq 2
(for
h\geq 3
this event even happens with high probability).

This peeling process technique has already been used before in sparse recovery
[
43
,
12
,
35
]
. In
[
43
]
the support of the vector
x
is known and thus the algorithm is concerned solely about estimation rather than the harder task of identification, whereas in
[
12
,
35
]
the authors assume that the vector is exactly
k
-sparse. In our case, where we assume nor knowledge of the support nor exact sparsity, analyzing this process is quite different and more challenging.

Weighted Hypergraph Peeling.

Back to our context, let us attempt to mimic the Invertible Bloom Filter setup: We hash the
n
coordinates to
O(k/\epsilon)
buckets in such a way that we can ideally recover all
O(k/\epsilon)
heavy elements. Similarly to the Invertible Bloom Filter, we hash each element not just to a single bucket, but instead to some
h\geq 2
buckets.

Our final algorithm succeeds only with constant probability, so it actually suffices to take
h=2
(i.e., to consider graphs). Following related literature we will nevertheless treat the general hypergraph case
h\geq 2
here, also because we are optimistic that this generalization will pay off for future work (possibly to reduce the error probability in
Theorem

1.1

We can augment each bucket with a scheme that can perform
1

O(\log n)
measurements and time with high probablity in

x_{i}
is hashed to a bucket
v
such that the energy
|x_{i}|^{2}
significantly exceeds the total energy of all other entries hashed to the bucket
v
(by some large constant factor), then we can successfully recover
i
. On the side we keep an instance of Count-Sketch to have query access to a point-wise approximation
\hat{x}
of
x

[
5
]
, and we keep a short linear sketch to approximate the tail
\hat{t}\approx\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}

[
41
]
. With these tools we simulate the Invertible Bloom Filter decoding process: For each bucket we run the
1
-sparse recovery procedure to find a candidate
i
. By comparing
\hat{x}_{i}
with
(\epsilon/k)\cdot\hat{t}
we can reliably test if
i
is heavy or light. If we determine that
i
is light, the recovery failed and we discard
i
. If we determine that
i
is heavy, then we store
i
and subtract
\hat{x}_{i}
from all buckets that
i
participates in. The hope is that we can peel the heavy hitters, one by one, as in the Invertible Bloom Filter decoding procedure.

The analysis of this process naturally corresponds to a generalized
weighted
hypergraph peeling process. Specifically, we model the recovery algorithm as a vertex- and edge-weighted
h
-uniform hypergraph
G=(V,E,w)
, where the nodes
V
correspond to buckets and each edge
e
corresponds to some heavy hitter
x_{i}
. We sometimes write
e_{i}
to denote the edge corresponding to
x_{i}
. The edge weights are
w_{e_{i}}=|x_{i}|^{2}
and the vertex weights are
w_{v}=\sum_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}
, where the sum is over all
light
entries hashed to the bucket
v
. So, we have that
\mathbb{E}[w_{v}]=O(\frac{\epsilon}{k}\|x_{-k}\|_{2}^{2})
. In this model, let us call an edge
e\in E

free
if it is incident to some vertex
v\in e
(i.e., bucket) such that

w_{e}\geq\rho\cdot\left\lparen w_{v}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\neq e^{\prime}\in E\\
v\in e^{\prime}\end{subarray}}w_{e^{\prime}}\right\rparen,

where
\rho
is a large constant called the
recovery threshold
. We say that
e
is
peelable
if we can repeatedly remove free edges from the graph so that in the remaining graph
e
itself becomes free.

This definition is indeed a generalization of the unweighted notion from before,
3
3

3

To see this, let all vertex weights be
0
, let all edge weights be
1
and take a recovery threshold
\rho>1
. Then an edge is free if and only if it is incident to a degree-
1
node.
and it is easy to verify that it is chosen in such a way that our new algorithm can recover all peelable edges in
G
. Therefore, ideally we would wish to prove that all edges in
G
are peelable and conclude the proof here. Unfortunately, there are two major problems in what we have outlined so far. In the following we describe these two problems and our solutions.

Problem 1: Not All Edges Are Peelable.

The first serious problem is that, in contrast to the unweighted setting, it is unfortunately not true that all edges are peelable with high probability.
4
4

4

Namely, consider a heavy hitter with energy
|x_{i}|^{2}\approx(\epsilon/k)\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. It corresponds to some edge
e_{i}
with weight
w_{e_{i}}=|x_{i}|^{2}
. The total energy of the light elements is up to
\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, and thus the expected vertex weight
\Ex[w_{v}]
in the hypergraph is roughly
\mu\approx(\epsilon/k)\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Therefore, with constant probability
all
vertices
v
incident to
e
have weight
w_{v}\geq w_{e}
, which effectively means that we can never recover
e
.
Does this mean that our approach is doomed? Recall that it is not strictly necessary to recover
all
heavy hitters, but that we can afford to miss a small fraction (energy-wise). Therefore, the question is whether we can recover edges of sufficiently large total energy.

Our analysis of this step turned out to be surprisingly clean. In fact, we only care about two aspects of
G
: (1) The hyperedges are random and sparse, and (2) the vertex weights are chosen randomly with expected weight
\mu
(though they possibly depend on each other). In such a situation, we show that any edge
e
fails to be peelable with probability at most
O(\mu/w_{e})
.

The proof of this statement involves two steps (see
Lemmas

3.4
and
3.5
). First, fix the randomness of the edges (1) and focus on the randomness of the vertex weights (2). Intuitively, any large-weight edge
e
only fails to be peelable if for all nodes
v
incident to
e
, there are other non-peelable edges
e^{\prime}
of comparable total weight incident to
v
. But for these edges
e^{\prime}
the same argument applies. Hence, by induction
e
only fails to be peelable if it is at the center of a sufficiently large and far-spreading connected component in which the weights slowly decrease as one moves away from
e
. Conversely, edges
e
in smaller components should be peelable. Formally, this shows that each edge
e
is peelable with probability
O(D(e)\cdot\mu/w_{e})
where
D(e)=\sum_{e^{\prime}}\rho^{1+d(e,e^{\prime})}
measures how far-spread the connected component of
e
behaves (the sum is over all edges
e^{\prime}
in the same connected component as
e
and
d(e,e^{\prime})
is the distance between the edges
e
and
e^{\prime}
). In the second step we fix the randomness of the vertex weights (2) and only consider the randomness of the edges (1). As long as the graph is sufficiently sparse, it can be shown that
\Ex[D(e)]=O(1)
. This concludes the proof that each edge is non-peelable with probability at most
O(\mu/w_{e})
.

Getting back to our recovery algorithm, we demonstrate that this probability is indeed small enough so that the expected loss in energy is tolerable:

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert x_{R\setminus T}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i\in T}\Pr(\text{$e_{i}$ not peelable})\cdot|x_{i}|^{2}=\sum_{i\in T}O\left\lparen\frac{\mu|x_{i}|^{2}}{w_{e_{i}}}\right\rparen=O(|T|\mu)=O(\epsilon)\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Problem 2: Accumulated Estimation Errors.

There is a second serious problem with the approach outlined above. In the Invertible Bloom Filter decoder it is crucial that we can
subtract
recovered elements from all relevant buckets—however, in our case this subtraction is
not perfect
. Recall that instead of subtracting
x_{i}
we can only subtract the Count-Sketch approximation
\hat{x}_{i}
. However, this incurs an additive error of up to
\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
to the energy of the respective bucket
v
which affects all future recoveries involving this bucket
v
. Even worse, it could very well happen that these errors
accumulate
. Concretely, in some buckets
v
it could become impossible to perform recoveries—not because the noise
w_{v}
is large, but because the accumulated approximation error of the previously recovered elements in that bucket
v
became too large. In fact, we can tolerate an accumulated error of at most
O(\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2})
, so even after a
constant
number of recoveries per bucket, in the worst case, further recoveries become impossible.

Perhaps a first instinct would be to give up on these buckets, hoping again that the total energy loss is tolerable. Unfortunately, this does not work out at all—if we can only perform up to
O(1)
recoveries per bucket then we effectively lose a constant fraction of the heavy hitters.

Instead, our solution relies on the tighter analysis of Count-Sketch due to Minton and Price
[
38
]
. They proved that a Count-Sketch with column sparsity
r=\Theta(\log n)
and
O(rk/\epsilon)
rows does not only give a point-wise approximation
\hat{x}
satisfying that
|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
(as in the original analysis of Count-Sketch
[
5
]
), but in fact provides an estimate that is more accurate by a factor of
r

on average.
Formally, the guarantee is that for all
i
,

\Pr\left\lparen|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\lambda}{r}\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\rparen\leq\exp(-\Omega(\lambda)).

see
Lemma

3.6
. Intuitively, the estimation error
|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}
behaves like an exponentially distributed random variable. Combining this insight with the fact that with high probability the maximum bucket load from heavy hitters is
O(\frac{\log(k/\epsilon)}{\log\log(k/\epsilon)})=O(\log n)
, it follows that the expected accumulated error per bucket is
O(\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2})
(rather than the naive upper bound which is worse by the bucket load), which is tolerable in our setting. If the errors were independent of each other, a standard concentration bound for sums of independent subexponentially distributed random variables (e.g.,
[
28
]
) would imply that the bound holds in fact with high probability. As the errors are dependent on each other our formal proof looks slightly different; see
Lemmas

4.7
and
4.9
. Curiously we depend on the tighter upper bound of
O(\frac{\log(k/\epsilon)}{\log\log(k/\epsilon)})=O(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
on the maximum bucket load.

Our approach seems to inherently require
(k/\epsilon)\log n
rows, instead of
(k/\epsilon)\log(n/k)
as we need all the
1
-sparse recovery routines to succeed with high probability. With
\log(n/k)
rows there is a
\poly(k/n)
probability of not recovering a heavy hitter from a bucket, and this is not sufficiently small for the peeling process to go through. Nevertheless, the algorithm is very concise and is likely to be relevant in practice as well.

Unweighted Hypergraph Peeling.

We start with two lemmas that are useful in the analysis of unweighted peeling processes. Throughout we consider
uniformly random
hypergraphs that are sampled with repetition—i.e., starting from an empty graph on
N
nodes, we insert
M
uniformly random
h
-uniform hyperedges into the graph. It is possible for the same edge to be sampled multiple times, so the resulting object is strictly speaking a
multi-
hypergraph. But this event happens only with small probability in our parameter regime, and thus we will often implicitly assume simple hypergraphs. For a proof of the following lemma, see e.g. the text book
[
14

.

3.1
.

Let
h\geq 3
and let
G
be a
h
-uniform hypergraph with
N
vertices and
M\leq\frac{N}{2h}
hyperedges sampled uniformly at random (with repetition). Then with probability
1-N^{-1+o(1)}
each connected component is a hypertree or unicyclic.

Notably the same statement also holds for graphs (i.e.,
h=2
), but only with
constant
success probability
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
. From this lemma it is almost immediate that random hypergraphs are peelable with high probability, i.e., that we can repeatedly remove edges involved incident to a degree-1 vertex to remove all edges from the hypergraph. Indeed, this is clear for hypertrees (simply keep removing leaf edges) and is also not hard to check for unicyclic components. In the same spirit, the following lemma will be useful for the treatment of unicyclic components later.

3.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a unicyclic hypergraph. For each edge
e\in E
there is some vertex
v\in e
such that upon removing
e
the resulting connected component

is a hypertree.

Proof.

Let
N
and
M
denote the number of vertices and edges in the connected component
C(e)
, respectively. Since the graph is unicyclic we have that
N=(h-1)M
. Now consider the graph
G\setminus e
obtained from
G
by removing
e
, and let
C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell}
denote its connected components. Suppose for contradiction that all these components are not hypertrees. Letting
N_{i}
and
M_{i}
denote the number of vertices and edges in
C_{i}
, respectively, this means that
N_{i}\leq(h-1)M_{i}
. But clearly
\sum_{i}N_{i}=N
and
\sum_{i}M_{i}=M-1
, which contradicts our initial assumption that
N=(h-1)M
.
∎

Weighted Hypergraph Peeling.

Finally, we consider our new weighted hypergraph peeling processes. Formally, equip a hypergraph
G=(V,E)
with a weight function
w:V\sqcup E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We will write

(for

(for

Definition 3.3

(Peeling)
.

Let
\rho\geq 1
and let

be a vertex- and edge-weighted hypergraph. We say that an edge

is
\rho

if there is some vertex

w_{e}\geq\rho\cdot\left(w_{v}+\sum_{e\neq e^{\prime}\in E}w_{e^{\prime}}\right).

A

-peeling sequence
is a sequence of edges
e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell}
such that, for all

is
\rho

G\setminus\{e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell-1}\}
. We call

\rho
-peelable
if there is a
\rho
-peeling sequence containing
e
.

The next two lemmas give convenient tools to analyze weighted hypergraph peeling. In combination, they essentially state that the probability that any edge is not peelable is at most
O(\mu/w_{e})
, where

is the expected vertex weight.

3.4

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho}{w_{e}}\cdot\Ex\left[w_{v}+\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\delta(v)}X_{e^{\prime}}w_{e^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{w_{e}}\cdot\left\lparen\Ex[w_{v}]+\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\delta(v)}\Ex[X_{e^{\prime}}]\cdot w_{e^{\prime}}\right\rparen

Proof.

To bound

we perform a breadth-first search starting from

E_{0},E_{1},\dots
denote the sets of edges explored in the respective layers of the search. Formally, let
E_{0}=\{e\}
and for

the set

consists of all edges
e^{\prime}\not\in E_{1},\dots,E_{i-1}
for which there is an edge

that shares a common node with

. We view the random construction of

as being revealed step-by-step in the breadth-first search. I.e., initially we only know that
e
is an edge but have not revealed any random choice, and in the
i

E_{i}
.

We will bound
\Ex[|E_{i}|\mid E_{i-1}]
. So focus on the
i
-th step where we have already revealed
E_{i-1}
and are currently revealing the edges
E_{i}
. The only candidates are
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{h}\}
where at least one of these nodes touches an edge in
E_{i-1}
. There are at most
\binom{|V|}{h-1}\cdot h|E_{i-1}|
such sets. Moreover, at least
|V|-M\geq(1-\frac{1}{2h})|V|
nodes are not touched by any of the previously revealed edges. Thus, any fix
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{h}\}
becomes an edge in this step with probability at most

\frac{M}{\binom{|V|-M}{h}}\leq\frac{M}{\binom{|V|}{h}}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{|V|}{|V|-M}\right\rparen^{h}=\frac{M}{\binom{|V|}{h}}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{2h}}\right\rparen^{h}\leq\frac{2M}{\binom{|V|}{h}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Ex[|E_{i}|\mid E_{i-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\binom{|V|}{h-1}\cdot h|E_{i-1}|\cdot\frac{2M}{\binom{|V|}{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\binom{|V|}{h-1}\cdot h|E_{i-1}|\cdot\frac{|V|}{4\rho h^{2}\binom{|V|}{h}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|E_{i-1}|}{4\rho}\cdot\frac{|V|}{|V|-(h-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|E_{i-1}|}{2\rho},

and it follows that

\Ex[|E_{i}|]\leq\frac{1}{(2\rho)^{i}}.

Clearly all edges
e^{\prime}\in E_{i}
have distance at most
i-1
to the designated edge

, and therefore

\Ex[D_{G^{\prime},\rho}(e)]\leq\Ex\left[\rho+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|E_{i}|\cdot\rho^{i}\right]\leq\rho+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{i}}{(2\rho)^{i}}=\rho+1,

completing the proof.
∎

3.3
Error-Correcting Codes

As the third ingredient we rely on an asymptotically good error-correcting code that can be encoded and decoded in
linear
time. Such a result was first obtained by Spielman
[
46
]
, and improved constructions have since lead to improved constants. Specifically, we rely on the following lemma which follows from the error-correcting codes due to Guruswami and Indyk
[
21
]
.
5
5

5

In the language of coding theory, the function
\operatorname{Enc}
is a binary error-correcting code with rate
R:=\frac{1}{2048}
that can be decoded (via
\operatorname{Dec}
) in linear time from a fraction of up to
e:=\frac{1}{8}
errors. In
[
21
, Theorem 5]
it is shown that any rate-distance trade-off below the Zyablov bound is achievable; that is, we have that
e\leq\max_{R<r<1}(1-r-\epsilon)H^{-1}(1-R/r)
(for some constant
\epsilon>0
), where
H^{-1}
is the inverse of the binary entropy function.
The second item in
Lemma

3.8
is non-standard, but can easily be ensured by flipping each bit in
\operatorname{Enc}(\cdot)
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. By Chernoff’s bound, each resulting code word differs from the expected number of 1’s,
1024\log n
, by a fraction of more than
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{16}
with probability at most
\exp(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\cdot 1024\log n)\leq n^{-2}
, and so the statement follows by a union bound over all
n
code words.

3.8
Linear-Time Error-Correcting Code
[
21
]
.

There is a randomized algorithm to evaluate functions
\operatorname{Enc}:[n]\to\{0,1\}^{2048\log n}
and
\operatorname{Dec}:\{0,1\}^{2048\log n}\to[n]
with the following properties:

•

Let
i\in[n]
and let

be such that
\operatorname{Enc}(i)
differs from

\frac{1}{8}

\operatorname{Dec}(x)=i
.

•

With high probability, the fraction of 1’s in

|x_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{k}\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. It follows that
|H|\leq k/\epsilon+|T|=k/\epsilon+k\leq 2k/\epsilon
. The bound on
|I|
is analogous.
∎

Our ultimate goal is to recover a subset of the top-
k
coordinates which suffices for
\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}
sparse recovery. In our one-shot recovery, we settle to recover a subset of the heavy coordinates which contains a desired subset as above. However, due to the approximate nature of the algorithm, it is likely that the algorithm will recover some coordinates that are not heavy but a constant fraction smaller than the threshold of heavy. We call these coordinates intermediate.

4.2
Point and Tail Estimates

The first step is to analyze the accuracy of the point and tail estimates from the first two types of queries in the sketch. The following two lemmas are immediate from
Lemmas

3.6
and
3.7
, but we record both for convenience later on.

4.4
Tail Estimates.

With high probability,
\frac{1}{c}\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\hat{t}\leq\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.

4.5
Point Estimates.

With high probability,
|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{32ck}\cdot\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}
for all
i\in[n]
.

The first consequence is that we can pinpoint which coordinates
i
can pass the test “
|x_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\cdot\hat{t}
” in
8
of the recovery algorithm: In the following lemma we show that
all
heavy coordinates pass the test, and the test is
only
passed by intermediate coordinates.

4.6
Recovery Threshold.

Suppose that the events from
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.5
hold. Then for all
i\in H
it holds that
|\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\hat{t}
, whereas for all
i\not\in I
it holds that
|\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}<\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\hat{t}
.

Proof.

Conditioned on these events we have
\frac{1}{c}\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\hat{t}\leq\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{32ck}\cdot\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}
for all
i\in[n]
. In this case, for each
i\in H
we have

|\hat{x}_{i}|\geq|x_{i}|-|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|\geq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{k}}-\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{32ck}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{k}}-\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{32k}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{2k}},

and for each
i\not\in I
we have

|\hat{x}_{i}|\leq|x_{i}|+|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|<\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{4ck}}+\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\lVert x_{-ck}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{32ck}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{4k}}+\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{32k}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\hat{t}}{2k}},

as claimed.
∎

The following lemma is more interesting. We exploit that Count-Sketch in fact provides point estimates that are better by a factor of
\log n

on average
as proven by Minton and Price
[
38
]
to argue that with good probability the fraction of (intermediate) coordinates that are misestimated by a factor of
\Theta(\lambda)
is exponentially small in
\lambda
.

4.7
Intermediate Point Estimates.

For an integer
\lambda\geq 1
, let
I_{\lambda}\subseteq I
denote the subset of coordinates
i
with

\frac{\epsilon(\lambda-1)}{2^{14}k\log n}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{2^{14}k\log n}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Then with probability at least
0.99
, it holds that
|I_{\lambda}|\leq|I|/2^{\lambda-1}
for all integers
\lambda\geq 1
.

Proof.

Recall that in the sketch we pick the parameters
r=10\log n
and
s\geq 8192ck/\epsilon
for the estimate
\hat{x}
provided by Count-Sketch. Therefore, for any
i\in I
,
Lemma

3.6
implies that:

\Pr\left\lparen|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon(\lambda-1)}{2^{14}k\log n}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\rparen\leq\Pr\left\lparen|x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}|^{2}\geq\frac{10(\lambda-1)}{r}\cdot\frac{\lVert x_{-s}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{s}\right\rparen\leq\exp(-10(\lambda-1)).

Therefore,
\Ex[|I_{\lambda}|]\leq|I|\cdot\exp(-10(\lambda-1))
, and from Markov’s inequality it follows that

\Pr\left\lparen|I_{\lambda}|>\frac{|I|}{2^{\lambda-1}}\right\rparen\leq\frac{\Ex[|I_{\lambda}|]\cdot 2^{\lambda-1}}{|I|}\leq 2^{\lambda-1}\cdot 2^{-10(\lambda-1)}=2^{-9(\lambda-1)}.

Taking a union bound over these error probabilities for
\lambda\geq 2
(noting that for
\lambda=1
the statement is clear), the total error probability is at most
\sum_{\lambda\geq 2}2^{-9(\lambda-1)}\leq 2^{-8}\leq 0.01
and the proof is complete.
∎

This completes the analysis of the point and tail estimates. For the rest of the analysis we will never directly consider the randomness of these estimates, other than conditioning on the previous
Lemmas

4.5
,
4.4
and
4.7
.

4.3
Estimation Error per Bucket

The next step is to bound the total estimation error in each bucket
B_{v}
. In fact, we only care about the estimation error of the intermediate coordinates
I_{v}
(as these are the only potentially recovered elements that influence the values
p_{v,j}
in the recovery algorithm; we will be more precise about this in the next subsection).

4.8
Bucket Load.

With high probability, it holds that
|I_{v}|\leq\frac{2\log n}{\log\log n}
.

Proof.

Fix any bucket
v\in V
. By
Lemma

4.3
the expected bucket load is at most

\Ex[|I_{v}|]=|I|\cdot\frac{3}{|V|}\leq 8ck/\epsilon\cdot\frac{3}{24ck/\epsilon}=1.

Moreover, as across all
i\in I
the events
i\in I_{v}
are independent, from Chernoff’s bound it follows for
\ell:=\frac{2\log n}{\log\log n}
that

\Pr\left\lparen|I_{v}|\geq\ell\right\rparen\leq\frac{e^{\ell+1}}{\ell^{\ell}}\leq\frac{n^{o(1)}}{n^{2-o(1)}}.

Taking a union bound over all
|V|\leq O(n)
buckets, the claim is immediate.
∎

4.9
Estimation Error per Bucket.

Conditioned on the event from
Lemma

4.7
, with high probability it holds that, for each bucket
v\in V
,

\lVert(x-\hat{x})_{I_{v}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2048k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

We condition on the event from
Lemma

4.7
and treat the randomness of the point estimates
\hat{x}
as fixed. The only source of randomness that concerns us here is the randomness of
e_{i}
for
i\in I
. Fix any bucket
v\in V
and suppose that

\lVert(x-\hat{x})_{I_{v}}\rVert_{2}^{2}>\frac{\epsilon}{2048k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\lVert(x-\hat{x})_{I_{v}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\lVert(x-\hat{x})_{B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}|B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}|\cdot\frac{\epsilon\lambda}{2^{14}k\log n}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2},

and thus

\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\lambda\cdot|B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}|>8\log n.

However, we will show that this last inequality holds only with very small probability. To see this, first recall from
Lemma

4.7
that
H_{\lambda}=\emptyset
for all
\lambda>\log n
. Now fix any integers
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{\log n})
with
\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\lambda b_{\lambda}\geq 8\log n
; we show that the probability that
b_{\lambda}=|B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}|
is small. A first observation is that if
\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}b_{\lambda}>\ell:=\frac{2\log n}{\log\log n}
then the bucket load
|I_{v}|
exceeds
\ell
, but this event happens only with small probability by the previous lemma. So instead assume that
\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}b_{\lambda}\leq\ell
. Recall that each element
i\in I_{\lambda}
is participating in
B_{v}
independently with probability
\frac{3}{|V|}
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\Pr(|B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}|=b_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\binom{|I_{\lambda}|}{b_{\lambda}}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{3}{|V|}\right\rparen^{b_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq|I_{\lambda}|^{b_{\lambda}}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{3}{|V|}\right\rparen^{b_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|I|^{b_{\lambda}}}{2^{(\lambda-1)b_{\lambda}}}\cdot\left\lparen\frac{3}{|V|}\right\rparen^{b_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{(\lambda-1)b_{\lambda}}},

where in the final step we used that
|I|\leq 8ck/\epsilon\leq|V|/3
. These events are independent for the different sets
I_{\lambda}
, hence

\displaystyle\Pr(\forall\lambda\in[\log n]:|B_{v}\cap I_{\lambda}|=b_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\lambda}\frac{1}{2^{(\lambda-1)b_{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle=2^{-\sum_{\lambda}(\lambda-1)b_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=2^{-\sum_{\lambda}\lambda b_{\lambda}+\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-8\log n+\ell}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-8+o(1)}.

To bound the total error probability, we take a union bound over the
|V|\leq O(n)
buckets
v
, and also over all integer sequences
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{\log n})
with
\sum_{\lambda}\lambda b_{\lambda}\geq 8\log n
and
\sum_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}\leq\ell
. Each such sequence has at most
\ell
nonzero entries which respectively ranges from
1
to
\ell
, and thus the number of sequences is at most

\binom{\log n}{\ell}\cdot(\ell+1)^{\ell}\leq(\log n)^{\ell}\cdot(\log n)^{\ell}=n^{4}.

It follows that the error probability is
n^{-3+o(1)}
, which completes the proof.
∎

4.4

Definition 4.10

The

4.11
Recovering Free Edges.

Suppose that the events from
Lemmas

4.5
,
4.4
,
4.8
and
4.9
hold. Then with high probability, the graph

does not contain
\rho
-free edges for
\rho=2048

Proof.

Suppose otherwise, and let
e_{i^{*}}
be a
\rho
-free edge in the graph
G\setminus\{e_{i}:i\in R\}
. Let
v\in e_{i^{*}}
be the vertex witnessing that
e_{i^{*}}
is
\rho

2
to

v

v

incident to

has not changed from this iteration until the algorithm has terminated. We show that this is a contradiction, since the algorithm would have recovered

in

i
into
R
in
9

By definition, as
e_{i}
is
\rho

w_{e_{i^{*}}}\geq\rho\cdot\left(w_{v}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in H_{v}\setminus R\\
i\neq i^{*}\end{subarray}}w_{e_{i}}\right).

Plugging in the definitions of the vertex- and edge-weights this becomes

|x_{i^{*}}|^{2}\geq\rho\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in L_{v}}|x_{i}|^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in H_{v}\setminus R\\
i\neq i^{*}\end{subarray}}|x_{i}|^{2}\right)\geq\rho\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B_{v}\setminus R\\
i\neq i^{*}\end{subarray}}|x_{i}|^{2}.

By definition the algorithm computes

\displaystyle p_{v,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{v}}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot x_{i}-\sum_{i\in B_{v}\cap R}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot\hat{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in B_{v}\setminus R}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot x_{i}+\sum_{i\in B_{v}\cap R}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot(x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}),

and we define

\displaystyle p_{v,j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\vphantom{\sum_{i}}p_{v,j}-\sigma_{j}(i^{*})\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i^{*})_{j}\cdot x_{i^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B_{v}\setminus R\\
i\neq i^{*}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot x_{i}+\sum_{i\in B_{v}\cap R}\sigma_{j}(i)\cdot\operatorname{Enc}(i)_{j}\cdot(x_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}).

We view
p_{v,j}^{*}
as a random variable over the randomness of the signs
\sigma_{j}

\sigma_{j}

\Var[p_{v,j}^{*}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in B_{v}\setminus R\\

To derive an upper bound on this variance, we bound the first sum by

4.5
and
4.4

4.6

\Var[p_{v,j}^{*}]\leq\frac{|x_{i^{*}}|^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{\epsilon}{\rho k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2|x_{i^{*}}|^{2}}{\rho},

where in the last step we used that

From Chebyshev’s inequality it follows that

\Pr\left\lparen|p_{v,j}^{*}|\geq\frac{|x_{i^{*}}|}{4}\right\rparen\leq\frac{\Var[p_{v,j}^{*}]}{(\frac{|x_{i^{*}}|}{4})^{2}}\leq\frac{32}{\rho}=\frac{1}{64}.

Let us call an index

otherwise. The previous computation shows that we expect at most a

Now focus on any good index

|p_{v,j}|\geq|x_{i^{*}}|-\frac{|x_{i^{*}}|}{4}=\frac{3}{4}\cdot|x_{i^{*}}|,

whereas if
\operatorname{Enc}(i^{*})_{j}=0

|p_{v,j}|=|p_{v,j}^{*}|\leq\frac{1}{4}\cdot|x_{i^{*}}|.

Recall that the bit-string

of the entries. (Indeed, in the event that all indices are good,

3.8

.

Additionally,
Lemma

4.6
guarantees that (conditioned on the events from

4.5
and
4.4

into

This shows that for any bucket

4.5
The Associated Hypergraph is Well-Peelable

In the previous step we have established that all edges that are peelable in the associated hypergraph can be recovered by the algorithm. In this section we show that almost all edges

are peelable. More specifically, we will show that the total weight of all edges

4.13

Let
P\subseteq H
denote the set of
\rho

in the associated hypergraph. Then with probability at least
0.97
, it holds that

\lVert x_{T\setminus P}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 800\epsilon\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

Observe that the associated hypergraph relies on two sources of randomness:
\{e_{i}:i\in H\}
and
\{e_{i}:i\in L\}

heavy
randomness) determines the edges
E

For the first part of the proof disregard the light randomness and focus on the heavy randomness. On the one hand,
|E|=|H|\leq 2k/\epsilon\leq\frac{|V|}{6}
by
Lemma

4.3
and thus

3.1
states that with high probability each connected component in the associated hypergraph is a hypertree or unicyclic. On the other hand,
|E|\leq 2k\epsilon\leq\frac{|V|}{72\rho}

Lemma

3.5
implies that
\Ex[D(e_{i})]\leq\rho+1
for each

\Ex\left[\sum_{i\in T}D(e_{i})\right]\leq(\rho+1)k\leq 2\rho k,

and by Markov’s bound with probability at least

\sum_{i\in T}D(e_{i})\leq 200\rho k.

Next, fix the heavy randomness and focus on the light randomness. For each vertex

\displaystyle\Ex[w_{v}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L}\Pr(v\in e_{i})\cdot|x_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{|V|}\sum_{i\in L}|x_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{|V|}\cdot\left\lparen\lVert x_{L\setminus T}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert x_{L\cap T}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{|V|}\cdot\left\lparen\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}+k\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{|V|}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{16}k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

In this setting—where the hypergraph and the edge weights are fix, each connected component is a hypertree or unicyclic, and the vertex weights are chosen randomly (though not necessarily independently) with expected weight at most

yields that the probability that an edge

is not

\Pr(i\not\in P)\leq\frac{\mu\cdot D(e_{i})}{w_{e_{i}}}=\frac{\epsilon\cdot D(e_{i})}{2^{16}k\cdot|x_{i}|^{2}}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Thus, the expected weight of the non-peelable edges is

\displaystyle\Ex\left[\lVert x_{T\setminus P}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in T}\Pr(i\not\in P)\cdot|x_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in T}\frac{\epsilon\cdot D(e_{i})}{2^{16}k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon\cdot 200\rho k}{2^{16}k}\cdot\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\vphantom{\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon}}8\epsilon\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

By one more application of Markov’s inequality, we finally obtain that
\lVert x_{T\setminus P}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 800\epsilon\lVert x_{-k}\rVert_{2}^{2}
with probability at least
0.99
. Taking a union bound over the all four error events---the two events that succeed with high probability,
6
