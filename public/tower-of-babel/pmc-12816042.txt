A wave glider for passive acoustic monitoring of cetaceans and anthropogenic sources in the central Mediterranean Sea
Authors: Sara Ferri, Anaëlle Evrard, Simonepietro Canese, Teresa Romeo, Silvestro Greco, Augusto Passarelli, Livio Favaro, Francesco Caruso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Passive acoustic monitoring has become a widely used method to study cetaceans, especially for populations facing escalating threats from noisy human activities, including shipping traffic, fishing industry and marine constructions. Here, we conducted a study using an autonomous surface vehicle to explore the distribution and acoustic behavior of cetaceans and to characterize anthropogenic sound sources in the central Mediterranean Sea. A wave glider equipped with a single-towed acoustic recorder was deployed from 13th September 2022 to 3rd March 2023. The recording yielded 19,115 files of 460s each (approximately 2 TB), a third of which was kept for a preliminary analysis based on spectrogram visualization and audio listening. The results showed that nearly half of the dataset contained delphinid signals (Delphinidae), followed by sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) and fin whales (Balaenoptera physalus), with notable hotspots in the southern Tyrrhenian and the Ionian Sea. Moreover, the almost continuous detection of anthropogenic sources highlighted the widespread acoustic impact of human activities in the area. These findings demonstrate the value of passive acoustics in the use of autonomous vehicles as a versatile tool for large-scale and long-term monitoring, offering a promising approach to support conservation efforts for vulnerable species while advancing strategies to mitigate human impacts on marine ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32142-3.

Introduction

The marine soundscape is shaped by physical and biological sounds as well as anthropogenic ones1, and it is highly variable both in time and space2. The biological activities of marine mammals, fish or crustaceans contribute significantly to the overall ambient sound in marine ecosystems through the production of vocalizations and other acoustic signals3. Anthropogenic sounds, now grouped together under the term “anthropophony,” are derived from a variety of human activities4,5 and can contribute substantially to the ambient soundscape.

In recent years, Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) has been increasingly used as a non-invasive tool to monitor the acoustic marine environment, allowing continuous detection of vocalizing animals across large spatial and temporal scales6, even in remote or unknown areas and under any weather conditions7. Currently, PAM can be implemented through a variety of instruments and sensors8 such as fixed platforms (e.g. moorings, cabled stations), drifting devices (e.g. buoys)9,10, and preset mobile vehicles (e.g. autonomous surface vehicles, autonomous underwater vehicles)11,12. The advancement and widespread implementation of modern technologies have improved the performance of PAM (sensitivity, data storage, real-time transmission), thus expanding its use in ecological assessments, biodiversity monitoring and impact evaluations13–15. Traditional PAM methods, i.e. fixed platforms, remain cost-effective solutions that provide stable, long-term recordings at specific sites but are limited in terms of the coverage of the surveyed area and on the variety of data collected16. In contrast, mobile platforms can collect acoustic data over large areas, following fixed routes from coastal to offshore waters16. Moreover, the ocean gliders are equipped with hydrophones and multiple sensors, making them a flexible and efficient tool, able to record ambient sound even in poor weather conditions and to gather critical information about marine ecosystems17.

Cetaceans are particularly suited for acoustic investigations, contributing largely to the underwater soundscape, as they rely on sound for navigation, orientation, foraging and communication18, and as their signals vary among species-groups19 allowing acoustical identification. The Delphinidae family use echolocation clicks, frequency-modulated whistles for communication and pulsed sounds in both social and foraging contexts, sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) produce broadband clicks for echolocation (i.e. regular clicks), foraging (i.e. creaks), and communication (i.e. codas), while the fin whale (Balaenoptera physalus), the only common baleen whale in the Mediterranean Sea, emits low-frequency (approx. 20 Hz) vocalizations for long-range communication19–21. PAM is particularly efficient because cetaceans’ underwater behavior often limits the effectiveness of traditional visual surveys (e.g. limited by sea state or light conditions)20. Indeed, several studies have proven the efficiency of PAM techniques in investigating cetacean diversity22, their abundance and habitat use23, behavior24, as well as in assessing the impact of anthropogenic noise on their biological activities25. The introduction of significant levels of underwater noise, particularly from human activities, can stress marine ecosystems and affect cetaceans, causing temporary or permanent threshold shifts, behavioral changes, and acoustic masking5. The latter occurs when a signal emitted by a cetacean is overlapped in frequencies by human-made noise, such as shipping traffic or industrial activities, reducing the effectiveness of communication, navigation, or prey detection26.

Although the Mediterranean Sea represents only 0.8% of the world’s ocean surface, it has an extremely high density of maritime traffic, with 30% of all international shipping concentrated in this area27. In this basin, ten species of cetaceans are considered resident, three are occasionally sighted and considered visitors, and seven other species have been classified as vagrants in the Mediterranean, highlighting the region as a cetacean biodiversity hotspot28. These species face numerous anthropogenic threats, including noise disturbance, collisions, and bycatch29. Noise pollution, primarily from maritime traffic, sonars, seismic exploration, and industrial activities (such as offshore wind farms), represents a growing concern for marine conservation in this semi-enclosed basin30. Over the next few decades, the development of offshore wind energy is expected to increase significantly. This growth will require robust acoustic baselines and monitoring programs that can assess the cumulative impact on marine species31. Conservation efforts are therefore increasingly focused on mitigating these threats and maintaining a favorable conservation status of the Mediterranean populations, as required by the EU Habitats Directive [Directive 92/43/EEC]. In this context, PAM provides a critical approach to both ecological research and environmental impact assessment (EIA), supporting the establishment of baseline acoustic conditions and the long-term evaluation of anthropogenic noise impacts13,31,32. Key initiatives such as the PELAGOS Sanctuary and ACCOBAMS have been central to cetacean conservation in the region33,34. Specifically, knowing the distribution and spatio-temporal abundance of cetacean species in each area is essential to promote effective conservation measures35.

This study represents the first deployment of a wave glider for passive acoustic monitoring of cetaceans in the central Mediterranean Sea. The main objective is to analyze cetacean vocalizations alongside anthropogenic sounds, to generate preliminary evidence on species presence, vocal repertoire, and the large-scale distribution of human-generated noise. Previous large-scale acoustic surveys in this area were conducted from research vessels but limited by weather conditions and required extended periods of sampling36–38. The wave glider, unlike other systems, operates autonomously over long time periods and continuously collects acoustic data across wide spatial scales. This approach supports the integration of innovative mobile technologies into marine conservation and EIA frameworks, contributing to both biodiversity protection and the mitigation of human impacts.

Results

Acoustic detections of cetaceans

Preliminary results on cetacean species presence in the study area, as well as the variety of signals produced, were obtained on a large scale using a hydrophone-equipped wave glider for PAM. In total, over the 814.2 h of acoustic data manually analyzed, we identified 3069 files with detections (i.e., presence of at least one signal in a file) of delphinids (48.2% of the analyzed dataset), 125 files with detections of sperm whales (1.96%) and 13 files with detections of fin whales (0.2%) (Fig. 1). Whistles were the most frequently detected signal produced by delphinids (78.1%), followed by echolocation clicks (60.9%) and pulsed sounds (17.7%). Among sperm whales, regular clicks were found in almost all recordings with detections (99.2%), while creaks and codas accounted respectively for 4.8 and 4% of the sperm whale detections.

Anthropogenic sound detections

The main types of anthropogenic sounds detected by the wave glider were categorized into ships, sonar and low-frequency pulses (Fig. 2). Shipping noise included both commercial and recreational vessels, sonar included both military and civil sonars, and low-frequency pulses included both air guns and unknown origin pulses emitters probably linked to seismic exploration. Shipping noise represented the predominant type of sound identified, with 4810 files accounting for 75.6% of the total acoustic file. Next were low-frequency pulses, detected in 1639 files, accounting for 25.7% of the total detections and sonar signals detected in 548 files with 8.6% of the total.

Spatial and temporal distribution of acoustic detections

The preliminary analysis of the acoustic dataset provides a first large-scale characterization of the spatio-temporal distribution of cetacean acoustic detections and anthropogenic sources along the glider track (Figs. 3, 4, 5). Delphinid acoustic detections (clicks, whistles, and pulsed sounds) were detected all along the track, showing a wide distribution across the central Mediterranean basin (Fig. 3). Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) indicated that probability of detection was significantly associated with bathymetry, distance to the coast, and time of day (p < 0.001, R2 = 0.199, deviance explained [d.e.] = 18.6%; Fig. 6a).The different signals produced by Delphinids followed the same spatial distribution, with the same pattern of probability of detection, in different proportions, for both bathymetry and distance to the coast. For all signals, there was a higher probability of detection and therefore presence at a specific range of bathymetry. A daily trend was identified for signal presence in function of the hour of the day following a circadian rhythm where all three signals but specifically clicks and pulsed sounds were more present during nighttime. Sperm whale acoustic signals were detected in specific areas of the central Mediterranean Sea: southern Tyrrhenian Sea, western Ionian Sea and southern Adriatic Sea (Fig. 4a). As for the Delphinids signals, GAMs revealed significant associations between detection probability and the studied spatial and temporal variables (p < 0.001, R2 = 0.304, d.e.= 46.7%; Fig. 6b). Detection probability increased within defined bathymetric and coastal ranges and was slightly higher during daytime. Finally, fin whale detections were found only in few recordings acquired in a restricted area of the southern Tyrrhenian Sea (Fig. 4b).

The spatio-temporal distribution of anthropogenic sound sources recorded by the glider in the central Mediterranean Sea was then investigated. Overall, the data revealed an almost continuous presence of anthropogenic sources along the glider’s track, suggesting that anthropogenic activities contribute significantly to the marine soundscape of this region (Fig. 5). The GAM (R2 = 0.42, d.e.= 35.8%) highlighted the effect of both bathymetry and distance to the coast on the probability of detection of anthropogenic sources (p values < 0.001) and therefore the influence of those spatial variables on the presence of anthropogenic noise along the wave glider track (Fig. 6c). As for shipping noise, the low-frequency pulses were spread in all the study area, with few detections in the Sicily Channel, while sonar was predominantly found in the southern Tyrrhenian Sea with few other sporadic detections. Regarding the temporal distribution, the hour of the day didn’t influence the probability of detection of the shipping noise and low-frequency pulses sources, with a continuous presence over the day (p values > 0.05), while the sonar source was slightly influenced (p value < 0.05).

Discussion

The use of Autonomous Surface Vehicles (ASVs) has demonstrated their versatility and potential in a wide range of scientific fields8. The wave glider has proven its efficiency in underwater acoustic research, demonstrating the ability to collect high quality data on marine soundscapes and applications in diverse marine environments17,39. Previous studies demonstrated its application for cetacean monitoring, including assessments of humpback whale acoustic presence in the Pacific Ocean40 and delphinid diversity in the southwestern Atlantic23. Although autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), such as profiling gliders, have previously been employed in the Mediterranean to detect and monitor cetaceans41,42, this work represents the first application of a hydrophone-equipped autonomous surface vehicle (ASV) in the region.

Specifically, the wave glider used in this study provides a novel platform for long-term, large-scale passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) in the Mediterranean Sea. These preliminary results show their effectiveness in detecting multiple cetacean species based on their vocalizations, in both coastal and oceanic environments. Delphinids signals were detected almost everywhere along the path of the glider, with less probability of detection in shallower waters, as highlighted by the GAM of bathymetry influence. Although this study did not distinguish between species, it is well known that the striped dolphin (Stenella coeruleoalba) is the most abundant cetacean species in the Mediterranean Sea43, with a marked preference for pelagic waters off the continental shelf44. Moreover, its abundance tends to be lower in the Sicily Channel than in the Tyrrhenian and Ionian Seas36,45. Overall delphinid abundance and diversity decrease from west to east in the basin43,46. While our study concentrated on overall trends, further analyses could use advanced techniques to analyze narrow-band signals, such as whistles47,48. This approach could help identify different species and improve our understanding of their distribution and behavior. In this context, the temporal distribution of the types of signals emitted by delphinids, as revealed by the GAMs, showed that the hour of the day is a relevant predictor of their acoustic presence, with evidence of a clear daily pattern covering both day and night. However, these temporal patterns should be interpreted, as the wave glider was continuously moving along its route, although at a very low speed, and did not represent a fixed location. Specifically, echolocation clicks and pulsed sounds, which are related to foraging activity, are more abundant during dark hours, possibly due to trophic chain dynamics, such as nycthemeral migrations49. Previous investigations in different regions of the study area further support this finding of a notable diel pattern in click occurrence49,50. Furthermore, whistles are more frequently produced during early morning and early night, likely reflecting the use of social interactions during traveling and daytime activities, as well as during foraging when associated with clicks and pulsed sounds51. Therefore, PAM is confirmed to be an efficient technique to identify circadian cycles in the acoustic activity of delphinids, gathering more information on their behavior than traditional visual methods20,49,52. It should also be noted that differences in detection rates among call types may partly reflect variations in their acoustic characteristics, such as frequency and source level, which influence their propagation and detectability in the marine environment. The wide distribution of delphinids also provides valuable insights into their ecological role in different ocean zones as fundamental top predators of marine ecosystems.

Moreover, the wave glider was also able to detect acoustic signals from a deep diving species with specific habitat preference, the sperm whale. Sounds from sperm whales were identified in three main areas: the Tyrrhenian Sea, the Ionian Sea, and the Southern Adriatic Sea. These are in line with previous acoustic detections of the species37, reinforcing the ecological importance of these regions as potential hotspots for sperm whales. Furthermore, acoustic and visual detections of the species have previously been shown not to follow a uniform distribution in each area of the Mediterranean53,54, explaining the influence of environmental variables leading to habitat partitioning. In this study, the GAM highlighted the influence as well of both bathymetry and distance to the coast on the probability of detection. The results are consistent with earlier studies showing a strong association between sperm whale distribution and specific topographic features, such as submarine canyons and seamounts55. For example, in the Tyrrhenian Sea, Mussi et al.56 observed the presence of sperm whales in areas characterized by sharp bathymetric gradients, while similar patterns were observed in the Ionian Sea57,58 and Ligurian Sea59. The ecological importance of submarine canyons and seamounts for marine mammals is well established. These features are recognized as keystone habitats due to their role in increasing food availability through oceanographic processes such as nutrient upwelling60. The results of this study highlight the need of preserving these remote areas in the Mediterranean Sea.

Fin whales’ detections were few during this dynamic PAM survey and probably underestimated as only mature males produce vocalizations61 that are usually detected in low background noise conditions62. Indeed, low-frequency anthropogenic noise such as shipping noise or low-frequency pulses could lead to the masking of their calls and prevent their detection63, especially with sensors close to the surface. This highlights one limitation of the study, since detections were based on presence/absence per file and only a subset of the dataset was manually analyzed, which may underestimate both occurrence and species-specific patterns. Nonetheless, this study allowed us to monitor the presence of fin whales in the southern Tyrrhenian Sea, which might coincide with their transit route between a summer feeding ground in the Ligurian Sea64,80 and a winter-feeding ground around Lampedusa65. This transition was also demonstrated through satellite tagging66. The species occurrence is higher during late spring/summer period in the northwestern Mediterranean Sea, following an aggregation-dispersion migration scheme67. Then, traveling southwards to reach the Sicily Channel during winter66. Our preliminary results highlight the potential of PAM methods in monitoring fin whale movements even in a noisy environment.

Among anthropogenic sources, shipping noise was the most prevalent, followed by low-frequency pulses and sonar signals. These types of human-noisy activities were detected almost continuously along the glider’s path, with spatial variability influenced by bathymetry and distance to the coast. Sonar detections were concentrated in the southern Tyrrhenian Sea, while shipping noise and low-frequency pulses were more widely distributed. According to the GAM result, anthropogenic sound source detection did not show a clear daily pattern according to the temporal analysis, underlining their continuous presence. These findings emphasize the pervasive contribution of anthropogenic activities to the Mediterranean soundscape and the need for management measures. Examples include the implementation of speed reduction programs, changing shipping routes or limiting activities in sensitive habitats as promoted by international frameworks (e.g. ACCOBAMS)68. Future work should aim to expand the analysis, integrate automated classification methods and combine PAM detections with additional environmental variables (e.g. productivity and oceanographic features) in order to improve ecological interpretations16.

Autonomous surface vehicles like the wave glider are valuable tools for continuous passive acoustic monitoring, gathering data on sound sources, which can be used to assess mitigation measures’ effectiveness, such as regulating vessel routes69. Although these vehicles are high-cost platforms with limited temporal coverage, they can be very useful to monitor remote and unknown areas, especially for large and long-term spatial surveys, filling gaps left by traditional monitoring methods. While mobile platforms such as the wave glider provide wide spatial coverage, they offer limited temporal resolution compared to fixed recorders, as they continuously move along their track. This trade-off is similar, although at a highly distinctive speed, to that commonly observed in digital aerial surveys70, where extensive spatial coverage is balanced against reduced temporal persistence. Based on our preliminary results, the wave glider can be considered suitable platforms to monitor the spatio-temporal distribution and acoustic behavior of cetaceans, as well as to study potential overlap with the presence of anthropogenic sounds. Indeed, many areas of the Mediterranean Sea are subject to human activities including shipping traffic and offshore operations posing a significant threat to marine life, especially for cetaceans. Among others, human-made noise affects cetacean populations through habitat loss71 and changes in acoustic behavior72. Despite its complexity, recent studies highlight that underwater noise, recognized as a sensory pollutant, can be mitigated through specific management strategies and international cooperative efforts73. In this context, the glider track was designed in relation to environmental impact assessment (EIA) plans for future offshore wind farm developments, with the aim of collecting baseline acoustic data. The results highlight the relevance of autonomous platforms like the wave glider as valuable tools to support EIA and guide mitigation strategies in offshore areas. This study highlights the widespread presence of anthropogenic noise even in remote and offshore areas of the Mediterranean, emphasising the importance of establishing appropriate protected areas to preserve key cetacean habitats and mitigate acoustic impacts on these species.

Methods

Wave glider and study area

The wave glider is a remotely controlled autonomous surface vehicle made of two parts connected to one another by an 8-meter umbilical cable (Fig. 7). The floating part on the surface is equipped with solar panels to provide power to the system, a navigation system programmable and modifiable, satellite communication, and multi-parameter customizable oceanographic sensors (e.g. CTD and fluorometry). The underwater part - called the sub- that captures the wave surface motion and through the rising and falling of its six wings transforms it into forward motion to propel the vehicle forward. In addition, a solar electrical thruster is available for extra speed and mobility in case of challenging ocean conditions. The wave glider used in this study is the SV3 074 model developed by Liquids Robotics, which is particularly suitable for long-term missions with an endurance of up to one year. For our study, it was also equipped with a single towed hydrophone (Porpoise, RS Aqua, UK) with a sensibility of − 193.3 dB re 1 V/µPa.

The vehicle was deployed in the central Mediterranean Sea from the 13th of September 2022 from the island of Minorca (Spain) to 17th December 2022, and from 15th January 2023 to 3rd March 2023 (Fig. 8). The acoustic survey started on the 30th of September, the previous period being a test with no acoustic acquisition of data. This ASV crossed the southern Tyrrhenian Sea, the Sicily Channel, and the Ionian Sea for about 2,000 nautical miles at an average speed of about 1 knot, before reaching the southern Adriatic Sea. The glider transects on the path were defined based on potential future offshore wind farm development areas in the central Mediterranean Sea.

Data collection

Acoustic data acquisition started on the 30th September 2022 (the previous period being a navigation test with no data acquisition) and recordings were acquired continuously except for the following days due to system failure and a time interval between deployments: from 26th to 30th November, the 10th December, from 13th to 15th December, from 18th December 2022 to 14th January 2023, and from 24th February to 26th February 2023. The continuous recording setting yielded a total of 19,115 files of 460 s in a .flac format (approx. 2 TB), acquired at a sampling rate of 192 kHz (Table 1).

Data analysis

A subset of the original dataset (6372 acoustic files, ~ 33%) was selected for manual analysis. This subset was selected to be representative of the total dataset, maintaining the occurrences of cetacean vocalizations and anthropogenic sounds along the wave glider’s path. This approach ensures that the selected files provide a reliable overview of the different sound sources observed, while keeping the manual analysis affordable. The manual analysis consisted of spectrogram visualization (2048-point FFT and 1012-point Hanning Window) and audio listening using the open-source software Raven Lite v. 2.0.5 (Bioacoustics Research Program, Cornell Lab of Ornithology). For each file, we assessed the presence/absence, where presence was defined as the detection of at least one vocalization from cetaceans or one anthropogenic sound with a distinction between broadband and low-frequency acoustic sources. Acoustic signals emitted by odontocetes (delphinids—Fam. Delphinidae or sperm whales) were classified into different categories: echolocation clicks, whistles and pulsed sounds for delphinids; regular clicks, creaks and codas for sperm whales, to provide information on the acoustic behavior of the species. In addition, to identify the low-frequency calls of fin whales, a downsample of each acoustic file, set to 1.6 kHz, was performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Each recording was then displayed as a spectrogram, and the audio file was listened to at accelerated speed (5x). This method improves the clarity of vocalizations by making them more audible and easier to identify. The objective was to report the presence of different cetacean species and anthropogenic sound sources (ships, sonar, low-frequency pulses) along the wave glider track. The entire dataset was analyzed by three expert PAM operators to limit the observer bias. To assess consistency among observers, interrater reliability was assessed on a 1% sample of the dataset using Cohen’s kappa, as described in McHugh74. The analysis resulted in a kappa value of 0.95, indicating an ‘almost perfect’ level of agreement, with 82–100% of the data considered reliable.

Statistical analysis

We retrieved two environmental parameters providing information on the spatial distribution of cetaceans and anthropogenic sources along the wave glider track: the depth of the seafloor (bathymetry) and the distance from the nearest coast. Bathymetric data were retrieved from EMODnet Bathymetry75 and the nearest coastal distance (mainland and islands) was calculated in QGIS v.3.28.15 (QGIS Development Team, 2024). A generalized additive model (GAM)76 was conducted in R version 4.3.177 to explore the effect of both spatial variables (Bathymetry, Distance to the coast) and a temporal one (Hour of the day) on the probability of detection of delphinids and sperm whale signals, and anthropogenic sources along the wave glider track. Binomial based (presence/absence) GAMs with a logit link function and the three variables described before as a smoothed fixed effect were performed using the ‘mgcv’ R package78. Presence/absence of the different types of delphinids signal and the different anthropogenic sources were modelled jointly within their respective GAMs. For the bathymetry and distance to the coast variables, the predictions obtained from the GAM were computed on the 5–95% quantile to better represent the effective time spent by the glider. Furthermore, for statistical analysis, we considered only the regular clicks of Sperm whales, due to the few numbers of detection of the other signals (codas, creaks). Fin whale detections were not included due to the few detections reported, and regarding the long propagation range of their calls62,79. Here, ‘probability of detection’ refers specifically to the likelihood of an acoustic file containing at least one identified signal (i.e., presence of this signal) from the considered category (cetacean vocalization or anthropogenic sound).
