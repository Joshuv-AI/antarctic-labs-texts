Not every right-atrial mass is a myxoma: infiltrative right-atrial lipomatous hypertrophy treated with debulking and patch reconstruction
Authors: Frieda-Maria Kainz, Dominik Wiedemann
Published: 2026

Summary

Right-atrial lipomatous hypertrophy is a rare benign infiltrative fatty lesion that may mimic atrial myxoma and present with arrhythmias or venous inflow obstruction.1 Multimodality imaging is essential for tissue characterization and operative planning.2 When infiltration involves the atrial wall or septum, complete excision may be hazardous and decompression-oriented surgery may be preferable.3

Case description

A 78-year-old woman with two-vessel coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and frequent supraventricular ectopy presented with a bulky right-atrial mass of uncertain origin.1 Transthoracic echocardiography, computed tomography, and positron emission tomography-computed tomography demonstrated a homogeneous low-attenuation lesion with fat-equivalent density (−92 to −136 HU, avg −113 HU, A 300 mm2) and absent relevant FDG uptake, consistent with benign fatty tissue.2 (Figure 1) Surgery was indicated because of the lesion’s size, intracavitary mass effect, and arrhythmic burden.3 After cardiopulmonary bypass and bicaval control, the right atrium was opened above the superior vena cava. The lesion consisted of compact fatty tissue with intramural extension into the interatrial septum. Complete excision was considered unsafe because of septal infiltration and the risk of conduction injury. Therefore, targeted debulking of the intracavitary component was performed, followed by xenopericardial patch reconstruction of the cavity and septum, with selective superior vena cava enlargement to relieve inflow restriction. Concomitant venous bypass grafting to the right coronary artery was completed. Post-operatively, recurrent bradyarrhythmia required VVI pacemaker implantation on Day 5. Discussion

The differential diagnosis of a right atrial mass includes myxoma, thrombus, lipoma, lipomatous hypertrophy, and malignant tumours such as sarcoma.4 In contrast to myxoma, which is typically pedunculated and mobile, lipomatous hypertrophy is non-encapsulated and infiltrative;5 unlike thrombus, it lacks layering morphology and embolic context; and unlike sarcoma, it lacks invasive growth and metabolic activity. Due to septal infiltration, complete excision risked conduction injury and instability; debulking with patch reconstruction relieved obstruction and arrhythmias.3,5
