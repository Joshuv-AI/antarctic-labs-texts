Palladium anchored to magnetic silica functionalized with AP groups as a novel and efficient heterogeneous catalyst for C–C coupling reactions
Authors: Mohammed Asiri, Vicky Jain, Suhas Ballal, Abhinav Kumar, Rami Oweis, Munthar Kadhim Abosaoda, Abhayveer Singh, Parminder Singh, Subhashree Ray, Aashna Sinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel magnetic nanocatalyst has been developed by covalently attaching a palladium complex (AP-Pd) to magnetic Fe3O4 particles, which were coated with a silica layer. This surface functionalization method of magnetic particles offers a promising strategy to bridge heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysis techniques. Various analytical techniques were employed to characterize the structure, morphology, and physicochemical properties of the synthesized particles. These techniques include Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), inductively coupled plasma (ICP), X-ray diffraction (XRD), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area measurements. The Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd nanocatalyst exhibited excellent catalytic performance in Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reactions, demonstrating high activity and recyclability. Introduction

In recent years, magnetic nanoparticles, especially Fe3O4 nanoparticles, have garnered significant research attention owing to their remarkable magnetic properties and ease of synthesis1–4. These nanoparticles have found extensive applications across various domains, including biomedicine, magnetically guided drug and gene delivery, magnetic hyperthermia, enzyme immobilization, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), data storage, catalysis, and environmental remediation5–9. Their versatility stems from attributes such as superparamagnetism, biocompatibility, low toxicity, high magnetic susceptibility, and exceptional saturation magnetization. Moreover, their surfaces can be readily functionalized with a wide range of chemical groups. Alongside these advancements, Fe3O4-based materials have also emerged as promising candidates in the field10–12. More recently, magnetic nanoparticles featuring an iron oxide core encased within a silica shell have attracted growing interest due to their distinctive chemical and physical properties13,14. These include tunable surface chemistry, minimal cytotoxicity, unique magnetic behavior, and the advantage of being easily separated from reaction environments using external magnetic fields15.

Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles offer significant advantages, but their standalone usage is associated with key challenges that demand surface modification16. Due to strong van der Waals and magnetic forces between particles, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles are prone to aggregation, forming large clusters that diminish their specific surface area, degrade magnetic properties, and hinder performance in various applications6,17,18. To address these issues, coating Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles with a thin silica (SiO₂) layer has emerged as a highly effective solution. The silica layer serves as a physical barrier that mitigates attractive forces between the nanoparticles, thereby reducing aggregation19–21. Furthermore, silica surfaces can be functionalized for instance, with charged groups or polymers to enhance static or steric stability, offering additional protection against particle clustering. Beyond aggregation control, silica coatings provide increased resistance to corrosive environments and high temperatures, ensuring that these nanoparticles remain stable under challenging conditions14,22. The sol-gel process, widely utilized for silica coating, enables precise regulation of silica layer thickness, thereby influencing the size and morphology of the resulting composite nanoparticles23,24. These advancements make the coated nanoparticles valuable not only for water purification applications but also as efficient platforms for developing cutting-edge magnetic heterogeneous catalysts25–27.

In this study, magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles were synthesized and utilized as a support to develop an organometallic palladium catalyst (Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd). The catalytic performance of this system was then assessed for carbon–carbon cross-coupling reactions, specifically the Suzuki-Miyaura reactions, under green conditions. Carbon–carbon coupling reactions hold significant importance in the synthesis of natural products, pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, polymers, biologically active compounds, herbicides, and advanced materials28–31. Typically, the formation of C-C bonds is catalyzed by palladium complexes. While homogeneous palladium catalysts have been widely employed due to their excellent catalytic activity, their use comes with notable limitations32–34. These include the challenges associated with catalyst recycling and product purification, which are often time-intensive and require specialized techniques35. Moreover, homogeneous catalysts commonly rely on phosphine ligands that are expensive, toxic, and highly sensitive to air and moisture36,37. As a result, the development of heterogeneous catalysts, particularly magnetic nanocatalysts, has gained attention for their ease of recyclability and environmentally friendly attributes.

Experimental

Preparation of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd

Magnetic nanoparticles were synthesized through the co-precipitation method using Fe(III) and Fe(II) ions in a 2:1 molar ratio, facilitated by ammonium hydroxide as the precipitating agent. Initially, FeCl3·6H2O (5.8 g) and FeCl2·4H2O (2.1 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water at 85 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere while subjected to vigorous mechanical stirring at 500 rpm. Subsequently, 12 mL of 25% ammonium hydroxide solution was injected rapidly into the reaction mixture in a single step. This resulted in the immediate formation of a black precipitate of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs). The reaction continued for an additional 30 min before cooling to room temperature. The resultant MNPs, appearing as a dark solid, were separated from the solution using magnetic separation and washed several times with deionized water. In the next stage, 1.0 g of the isolated Fe3O4 was dispersed in a solvent mixture comprising ethanol (15 mL), ammonia solution (2.5 mL), and deionized water (20 mL). This was followed by the addition of polyethylene glycol-400 (PEG-400, 3.0 g) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 4 mL). The mixture was stirred continuously for 24 h at room temperature to ensure complete reaction. Afterward, the product (Fe3O4@SiO2) was collected using an external magnet, thoroughly washed five times with ethanol and deionized water, and dried at 25 °C. In the next step, 1 g of Fe3O4@SiO2 was dissolved in 20 mL of toluene and stirred for 20 min. Then, 3 mL of 3-chloropropyltriethoxysilane was added to the reaction mixture. The mixture was stirred for another 24 h at 60 °C. After completion, the product was separated using an external magnet, washed with hexane, and dried. Next, 1 g of Fe3O4@SiO2-cl was dissolved in 15 mL of toluene and stirred for 20 min. Then, 2.5 mmol of 4’-aminophthalic acid (AP) was added to the reaction mixture. The reaction mixture was stirred vigorously and refluxed for another 24 h. After completion, the product was separated using an external magnet, washed with ethyl acetate, and dried. To synthesize the Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd catalyst, 2.4 mmol of palladium acetate was introduced into a suspension of 1 g of Fe3O4/n-Pr-AP in 50 mL of ethanol. This mixture was stirred intensively under reflux conditions for 24 h. Subsequently, 0.6 g of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) was added to reduce the palladium, and the reaction continued under reflux for an additional 5 h. The resulting black solid was isolated by external magnet, thoroughly washed with ethanol to eliminate unbound metal ions, and dried under high vacuum to obtain the purified Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd catalyst (Fig. 1).

Synthesis of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

Procedure for the Suzuki reaction using Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd catalyst

Phenylboronic acid (1 mmol), aryl halide (1 mmol), and Na2CO3 (1.2 mmol) were combined in 3 ml of water alongside Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd MNPs (0.02 g). The mixture was stirred under reflux, with the reaction progress monitored via TLC. After completion, the reaction was cooled, and the Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd MNPs were separated using an external magnet, followed by a wash with EtOAc. The remaining solution underwent liquid-liquid extraction using water and ethyl acetate. The organic layer was dried with 1.5 g of anhydrous Na2SO4. Finally, the ethyl acetate was evaporated, resulting in the production of pure biphenyl derivatives in excellent yields (Fig. 2). The synthesized compounds were characterized through H NMR spectroscopy, and the corresponding spectra have been included in the article’s supplementary files.

Catalyst characterizations

The FT-IR spectra of the catalysts Fe3O4 (a), Fe3O4/SiO2 (b), Fe3O4/n-Pr (c), Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP (d), and Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd (e) are depicted in Fig. 3. In Fig. 3a, the spectrum of uncoated Fe3O4 displays a signal at 576 cm⁻¹, corresponding to Fe–O bond vibrations, and a broad band at 3428 cm⁻¹, which is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations. This indicates the presence of hydroxyl groups on the surface of the uncoated Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs). The FT-IR spectrum of SiO2-coated Fe3O4 (Fig. 3b) features a sharp band at 1115 cm⁻¹ and a weaker absorption peak at 962 cm⁻¹, representing the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si and the symmetric stretching of Si–OH bonds, respectively. These findings confirm the formation of a SiO2 layer around the Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Figure 3c illustrates the FT-IR spectrum of Fe3O4@SiO2/n-Pr, showing peaks at 2943 and 2885 cm⁻¹, which are associated with methylene C–H stretching. This confirms the successful immobilization of the linker on the magnetic substrate. In Fig. 3d, corresponding to Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP, a broad absorption band in the range of 2474–3349 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the stretching vibrations of O–H and N–H bonds. Additionally, a band at 1729 cm⁻¹ corresponds to carbonyl group vibrations from acid functionalities, while the peak at 1647 cm⁻¹ is linked to N-H bending vibrations. Peaks at 2942 and 2886 cm⁻¹ are also observed, corresponding to C–H stretching vibrations. These results confirm the presence of 4′-aminophthalic acid functionalities anchored on the Fe3O4/n-Pr substrate. Lastly, after functionalizing the AP group with Pd, as shown in Fig. 3e, a prominent band at 1653 cm⁻¹ exhibits shifts and slight intensity changes. This provides evidence for the successful incorporation of Pd onto the surface of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles1,38–40.

The crystalline structure of Fe3O4 (a) and Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd (b) was examined through XRD analysis, as depicted in Fig. 4. The data displayed distinct, sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ angles of 18.1°, 30.4°, 37.0°, 42.8°, 54.7°, 57.2°, and 62.4°, which correspond to the characteristic crystal framework of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. In Fig. 4b, the peak appearing at region 18.1° is related to the silicate group coated on the surface of the magnetic nanoparticles. These findings confirm the successful synthesis of the intended catalyst and demonstrate that the Fe3O4 crystalline structure remained intact, with no detectable signs of structural degradation (Fig. 4)41–45.

XRD spectrum of a Fe3O4, and b Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

Figure 5 presents the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves for Fe₃O₄@SiO₂, Fe₃O₄/n-Pr/AP, and Fe₃O₄/n-Pr/AP-Pd, detailing their thermal decomposition behaviors. In Fig. 5a, the TGA curve for Fe₃O₄@SiO₂ demonstrates a two-step thermal decomposition process. The first weight loss is observed below 250 °C, attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed solvents. The second weight loss, occurring at higher temperatures, is likely due to the combustion and decomposition of the silicate coating surrounding the Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles. Similarly, Fig. 5b illustrates the TGA curve for Fe₃O₄/n-Pr/AP, also showing two distinct steps of thermal decomposition. The initial weight loss below 250 °C is caused by the removal of solvent molecules weakly bound to the surface. This is followed by a second weight loss between 250 and 700 °C, which arises from the breakdown of organic groups (AP) attached to the catalyst’s surface. In Fig. 6c, the TGA curve for Fe₃O₄/n-Pr/AP-Pd highlights a slight weight loss of 5.89% below 250 °C, attributed to the evaporation of adsorbed solvents similar to observations in Fig. 5. Within the temperature range of 250 °C to 700 °C, an additional weight reduction of approximately 15% occurs, corresponding to the decomposition of the Fe₃O₄/n-Pr/AP-Pd complexes. These findings from the TGA analysis clearly confirm the successful functionalization of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles with AP-Pd groups.

TGA curve of a Fe3O4/SiO2, b Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP, and c Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

EDX images of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

To confirm the presence of Pd metal in Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd, EDS and ICP-OES analyses were performed, as shown in Fig. 6. The EDS spectrum of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd confirmed the existence of O, C, Si, N, Fe, and Pd elements within the catalyst. To quantify the exact amount of Pd, ICP-OES analysis was utilized. Additionally, the extent of palladium leaching from Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd was investigated through ICP-OES analysis, and hot filtration experiments. The ICP-OES results indicated palladium concentrations of 1.6 × 10⁻³ mol g⁻¹ in the fresh catalyst and 1.5 × 10⁻³ mol g⁻¹ in the reused catalyst. These findings demonstrate that metal leaching in this catalyst is minimal (Fig. 6).

The particle size and morphology of the synthesized catalyst were examined through FE-SEM analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The results showed nanometer-sized particles with an almost uniform distribution. The Pd species are visible as distinct dots uniformly spread throughout the matrix. These observations suggest that the catalyst is formed of granular nanoparticles, with sizes varying from 22 to 45 nm (Fig. 7).

SEM images of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis validated the mesoporous structure of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd, as illustrated in Fig. 8. The BET analysis revealed a surface area of 12.48 m²/g, while the BJH method measured a total pore volume of 0.06 cm³/g and an average pore diameter of 4.31 nm, confirming its porous characteristics (Fig. 8).

N2-adsorption isotherms of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

Figure 9 presents the magnetic remanence curves for Fe3O4 and Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd nanoparticles. The saturation magnetization (Ms) values for these nanoparticles were approximately 48 and 34 emu g⁻¹, respectively. Despite these values, the magnetization was sufficient to allow effective magnetic separation. Consequently, the catalyst could be conveniently retrieved from the reaction medium using a permanent magnet after completing the reaction (Fig. 9).

Catalytic study

Following the successful synthesis and characterization of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd MNPs, their catalytic performance was evaluated in the Suzuki reaction. The model reaction chosen for optimization involved coupling iodobenzene (1 mmol) with phenylboronic acid (1 mmol) under varying conditions to determine the optimal reaction parameters. Factors such as catalyst amount, solvent choice, base type, and reaction temperature were systematically studied (outlined in Scheme 2), with the findings summarized in Table 1. The initial investigation focused on the influence of catalyst quantity on reaction efficiency. It was observed that the highest yield was achieved using 0.02 g of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd MNPs, while no reaction occurred in the absence of the catalyst even after 24 h. Subsequent testing examined the effects of solvent, base, and temperature on the reaction outcome. The results demonstrated that water served as the ideal solvent, with Na2CO3 identified as the most effective base, achieving the best carbon–carbon coupling product under reflux conditions. Overall, the optimal parameters for the Suzuki reaction were established as follows: 0.02 g of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd catalyst, 1.2 mmol of Na2CO3 as the base, and water as the solvent under reflux conditions.

After fine-tuning the reaction conditions, the catalytic performance of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) was assessed in Suzuki C-C cross-coupling reactions involving a wide variety of commercially available aryl halides and phenylboronic acid as the phenylating agent, as illustrated in Scheme 2. The results, summarized in Table 2, demonstrate yields ranging from moderate to excellent for the target products. Experimental findings indicate that ortho-, meta-, and para-substituted aryl halides, including aryl iodides, bromides, and chlorides featuring both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, yielded their respective biphenyl derivatives with good to excellent efficiency. A notable observation was that aryl halides possessing electron-donating groups on the aromatic ring required longer reaction times relative to those with electron-withdrawing groups. This is likely because aryl halides, which serve as electrophilic partners in Suzuki coupling, are more effectively activated by electron-withdrawing substituents in ortho or para positions. These groups enhance the reactivity during the oxidative addition step compared to electron-donating substituents. Furthermore, aryl iodides and bromides demonstrated higher reactivity in Suzuki C–C cross-coupling with phenylboronic acid than aryl chlorides, which proved less reactive. The electrophilic partner’s reactivity followed the trend: I > Br > Cl, with chlorides exhibiting minimal reactivity due to their reduced propensity for oxidative addition. In this study, incorporating BCR as an electron-rich ligand on the Pd catalyst, combined with Na2CO3 as a base, facilitated the activation and cleavage of chloride groups, enabling the successful coupling of aryl chloride electrophiles. To validate the chemoselectivity of this novel catalytic system, 1-bromo-4-chlorobenzene was employed in the reaction. The bromide group displayed greater reactivity than the chloride group under these conditions, as shown in Table 2 (entry 10). This selective behavior offers a significant advantage by leaving an active halide group intact in the product for potential further functionalization.

The catalytic cyclic mechanism of the Suzuki reaction mediated by Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd is illustrated in Fig. 10, drawing on previously established pathways. This Suzuki cross-coupling reaction follows three essential steps: oxidative addition, transmetalation, and reductive elimination, as represented in the diagram. Initially, the process commences with the oxidative addition of aryl halides to the Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd complex, leading to the formation of intermediate 1, which serves as a catalytically active Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd species. Since oxidative addition represents the most demanding phase of the catalytic cycle, the presence of oxygen-based electron-donating groups on the AP ligand plays a crucial role in enhancing the activity of the Pd catalyst. These groups aid in breaking the Ar-X bond, subsequently forming the Pd-Ar and Pd-X bonds. In the subsequent step, an organoborane compound reacts with intermediate 1 in the presence of a base, undergoing transmetalation to produce intermediate 2. The final stage involves reductive elimination, which generates the desired product while simultaneously restoring the original Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd complex, allowing it to participate in successive catalytic cycles (Fig. 10).

Catalyst leaching study

To examine the leaching of palladium in the reaction mixture and evaluate the heterogeneity of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd, a hot filtration test was performed during the synthesis of butyl cinnamate via the coupling of iodobenzene and phenylboronic acid. In the initial run, a 63% product yield was obtained at the reaction’s midpoint. The experiment was then repeated, and at the same midpoint, the catalyst was removed, with the filtered solution continuing to react for an additional 30 min. At this point, the yield showed a slight increase to 65%. However, no further progress in the reaction was observed after the hot filtration step, indicating minimal palladium leaching.

Catalyst recyclability

Recovering and reusing nanocatalysts is an essential attribute. To assess this capability, we examined the performance of the recycled catalyst in the model reaction conducted under optimized conditions. The results showed that the reused catalyst maintained steady efficiency, allowing it to facilitate biphenyl synthesis up to five cycles without significant degradation in its original catalytic activity, as depicted in Fig. 11.

Recyclability of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd.

The FT-IR spectrum analysis of the recycled nanocatalyst indicates the absence of significant changes following the recovery process (Fig. 12). This stability in the spectral data provides clear evidence of the structural robustness and integrity of the Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd composite material, as illustrated in Fig. 12.

Conclusion

Magnetic nanoparticles were synthesized through a fast and cost-efficient method. Notably, a palladium catalyst (AP-Pd) was immobilized on their surface for the first time. Comprehensive characterization of the catalyst was performed using various analytical techniques, including SEM, EDS, TGA, FT-IR, XRD, and VSM. The catalytic activity of Fe3O4/n-Pr/AP-Pd MNPs was assessed in C–C coupling reactions carried out in water under eco-friendly conditions. This approach demonstrated compatibility with aryl halides containing both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups, highlighting its adaptability to a broad range of substrates. The catalyst delivered high product yields and TOF values, indicating exceptional efficiency and selectivity for biphenyl synthesis. Its stability and heterogeneous attributes were validated through hot filtration experiments.
