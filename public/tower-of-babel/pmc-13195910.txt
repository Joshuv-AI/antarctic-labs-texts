Development and validation of a Knowledge, Belief, and Behaviour Questionnaire on Sarcopenia for Older Adults (KBBQ-SOA): a study protocol based on the COSMIN guidelines
Authors: Ya Shi, Ying Ye, Emma Stanmore, Can Gu, Chris Todd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe lack of a standardized and theoretically grounded instrument to assess psychosocial determinants of sarcopenia prevention represents a critical gap in public health. This protocol describes the development and validation of the Knowledge, Belief, and Behaviour Questionnaire on Sarcopenia for Older Adults (KBBQ-SOA).MethodsGuided by the Consensus-based Standards for the selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) and informed by the Knowledge-Belief-Behaviour framework, the study will be conducted in four sequential stages: (1) item generation and construct clarification through literature review, expert consultation, and semi-structured interviews; (2) content validation using a Delphi expert panel and cognitive interviews; (3) psychometric evaluation in a multi-site sample of 300–500 community-dwelling older adults across four diverse regions of China; and (4) assessment of interpretability and feasibility, including a preliminary evaluation of cross-cultural applicability.OutcomesThis paper presents the study design and methodology; no results are reported. Background

Sarcopenia, first defined in 1988 as an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and function [53], has undergone substantial conceptual refinement over the past three decades. Early definitions primarily focused on reduced muscle mass [5, 29]. Since 2010, several international groups, including the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP), the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH), and the Sarcopenia Definition and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC), have proposed operational definitions incorporating muscle strength and physical performance, although diagnostic thresholds have varied across populations and methods [7, 10, 19, 59]. Despite these advances, sarcopenia was long regarded as a “condition” rather than a disease, with inconsistent recognition in disease classification systems of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [3, 27, 55]. In 2024, the Global Leadership Initiative in Sarcopenia (GLIS) reached a consensus defining sarcopenia as an age-related generalised muscle disease characterized by reduced muscle mass and strength, with impaired physical performance as an outcome [31]. This definition may facilitate greater conceptual alignment and standardization in the field.

Sarcopenia is highly prevalent among older adults, with rates increasing with age and varying across diagnostic criteria and populations. Notably, “possible” or “probable” sarcopenia defined primarily by low muscle strength [11, 20] has been reported in over 40% of community-dwelling older adults in some settings [13, 47, 51, 68]. Multiple factors, including genetics, aging, malnutrition, physical inactivity, and chronic diseases, contribute to its development [9, 38, 67, 70]. If left unaddressed, sarcopenia is associated with adverse outcomes such as falls, fractures, functional decline, disability, long-term care dependency, and increased mortality [25, 34, 37, 52, 65], as well as substantial healthcare and economic burdens [2, 66, 69]. These findings highlight the importance of early identification and prevention.

Despite its clinical importance, public awareness of sarcopenia remains limited. Studies consistently report low knowledge of its definition, risk factors, and prevention strategies. In a Dutch cohort, 91% of older adults were unfamiliar with the term sarcopenia, though most expressed willingness to engage in treatment (76%) or preventive measures (71%) once informed [61]. Similarly, Chiu et al. [14] found that older adults at risk of sarcopenia had poorer knowledge across all domains, particularly regarding screening and diagnosis, followed by lifestyle influences and adverse health outcomes. In China, sarcopenia awareness (36.0%) was the lowest among common geriatric syndromes, trailing falls (62.0%), dementia (55.1%), malnutrition (54.7%), and frailty (40.5%) in a multicentre study of 6,653 participants [64]. Furthermore, Wang et al. [62] used structural equation modelling to show that sarcopenia awareness was strongly shaped by psychosocial and health-related factors, including physical health perception, protein intake, exercise frequency, smoking status, self-efficacy, religious beliefs, and social support. Collectively, these findings indicate that insufficient awareness may hinder effective prevention and management.

A key barrier underlying this low awareness is the lack of standardized and validated instruments to assess sarcopenia-related knowledge and related psychosocial factors. Existing tools are limited in scope, focusing predominantly on knowledge while neglecting the beliefs and behaviours that drive preventive action. Several knowledge-specific questionnaires have been developed, including the Sarcopenia Knowledge Questionnaire [35], a 10-item Chinese sarcopenia knowledge scale [36], and a 21-item sarcopenia knowledge quiz [57]. However, these instruments lack a strong theoretical foundation and comprehensive validation, limiting their utility for research and intervention design.

Behavioural theories such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) posit that knowledge alone is insufficient to drive behaviour change [1, 6, 16, 30]; rather, modifying key beliefs is essential for fostering behavioural intentions and sustained change [46]. In a recent feasibility study of a social media-based intervention [56], our team assessed knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours using a self-developed knowledge questionnaire, the Self-Efficacy for Managing Chronic Disease Scale (SEMCD-6), and the Self-Management Behaviour for Chronic Disease Scale (SMB-CDS). We identified two key limitations in the latter two scales. First, the SEMCD-6 uses generic terms (e.g., “chronic disease”) and focuses on symptom control, whereas sarcopenia is often asymptomatic and requires specific behaviours such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake. Second, the SMB-CDS emphasizes general aerobic activities over strength or balance training and neglects nutritional self-management—both central to sarcopenia prevention. These limitations compromise accurate evaluation of intervention effects on sarcopenia-specific beliefs and behaviours.

These limitations highlight the need for a targeted assessment tool grounded in the knowledge–belief–behaviour (KBB) framework specific to sarcopenia prevention. Although Fan et al. [24] recently developed a knowledge–attitudes–practice (KAP) questionnaire for sarcopenia among older adults in China, this instrument has three critical shortcomings. First, theoretically, the KAP framework measures generalized “attitudes” rather than specific beliefs (e.g., self-efficacy, perceived barriers) theorized by HBM and TPB as primary mediators of behaviour change. Second, methodologically, its development lacked rigorous standards; validation was limited to expert review without reliability testing, leaving its psychometric properties unknown. Third, the instrument was developed in a single urban setting, raising concerns about its generalizability to China’s diverse older adult population. These shortcomings underscore the need for a more robust and broadly applicable tool.

The present study aims to address these gaps through the development and validation of the Knowledge, Belief, and Behaviour Questionnaire on Sarcopenia for Older Adults (KBBQ-SOA), guided by the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines [43]. The KBBQ-SOA introduces three corresponding innovations. First, grounded in an integrated KBB framework and operationalized using HBM and TPB constructs, it will assess not only knowledge but also motivational beliefs (e.g., self-efficacy, perceived barriers) and preventive behaviours (e.g., resistance exercise, protein intake) essential for behaviour change. Second, its development and validation will strictly adhere to COSMIN guidelines, ensuring robust evidence on content validity, structural validity, reliability, and measurement error—meeting contemporary psychometric standards. Third, to enhance generalizability, the KBBQ-SOA will be validated across four diverse Chinese regions (Changsha, Yangzhou, Zhaotong, Urumqi), capturing heterogeneity in socioeconomic status, diet, physical activity, and ethnicity. By filling these gaps, the KBBQ-SOA is expected to provide a theoretically robust, psychometrically sound, and broadly applicable tool to assess sarcopenia-related knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours, ultimately supporting the design and evaluation of targeted interventions. The detailed methodology may also serve as a blueprint for culturally adapted sarcopenia assessment tools in other populations.

Methods/design

The methodological choices described below were guided by the COSMIN framework, which provides a consensus-based standard for evaluating the quality of health measurement instruments [43]. Where multiple analytic options existed, selections were made based on the following principles: (a) alignment with the theoretical framework (KBB, HBM, TPB); (b) adherence to contemporary psychometric standards; (c) appropriateness for the target population (older adults with sarcopenia); and (d) practical feasibility in a multi-site study context. Each key design choice is justified in the corresponding subsection below, with reference to methodological literature where applicable. Based on these principles, the study will be conducted in four sequential stages, each building upon the outcomes of the preceding one to ensure methodological rigour and comprehensiveness (Fig. 1). This research will be completed within two years.

Research process

Research process

Stage 1: Item generation and construct clarification

Stage 1 will focus on item generation and construct clarification, structured into five sequential parts (Fig. 2). First, the research scope and constructs will be defined through problem formulation and team discussions. Second, a scoping review will identify existing tools and evidence, generating a preliminary conceptual map and item bank. Third, semi-structured interviews with community-dwelling older adults will capture experiential perspectives. Fourth, expert consultation will refine the conceptual framework and item pool. Finally, findings from the review, interviews, and expert consultation will be integrated to generate the initial KBBQ-SOA version. Throughout this stage, adherence to the COSMIN guidelines for content validity will be maintained: relevance through expert input, comprehensiveness through scoping review, and comprehensibility through older adult interviews.

Five-step process of stage 1 for developing the KBBQ-SOA

Stage 2: Content validation and item refinement

Stage 2 will focus on content validation and item refinement through a four-part process (Fig. 3). First, a multidisciplinary expert panel will be established to conduct a two-round modified Delphi process [40, 49], providing quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback on the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the preliminary item pool. Second, cognitive interviews [63] with community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia will be undertaken to evaluate comprehensibility, cultural appropriateness, and acceptability of the items. Third, findings from the Delphi survey and cognitive interviews will be integrated, and iterative revisions will be made to refine the instrument. Finally, a pre-final version of the KBBQ-SOA will be prepared, accompanied by documentation of all refinement decisions, ready for psychometric testing in Stage 3. This stage will also be conducted in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines for content validity, addressing the relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility of the instrument.

Four-step process of stage 2 for developing the KBBQ-SOA

Stage 3: Psychometric evaluation

Stage 3 will focus on the psychometric evaluation of the KBBQ-SOA through four sequential and interrelated parts. First, pilot testing and item analysis will be undertaken to assess feasibility, response distribution, and preliminary item performance. Second, reliability testing will be conducted to examine internal consistency using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω and to establish test–retest reliability through intraclass correlation coefficients. Third, construct validity will be evaluated by combining exploratory factor analysis to explore dimensionality and confirmatory factor analysis to test the hypothesized three-domain structure, supplemented by assessments of convergent and discriminant validity. Finally, findings across these analyses will be synthesized to finalize the instrument, resulting in a validated KBBQ-SOA with robust psychometric properties. This stage will be carried out in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines, which emphasize rigorous evaluation of reliability, structural validity, and construct validity in the development of health measurement instruments.

Study design

A cross-sectional study will be conducted to evaluate the psychometric properties of the KBBQ-SOA among community-dwelling older adults. The study will include pilot testing, reliability assessment, and validity testing, following the COSMIN guidelines for measurement property evaluation.

Study setting

This study will employ a multi-site recruitment strategy across four geographically and culturally distinct regions of China to ensure a diverse and representative sample. Participants will be recruited from Changsha (central China, representing urban interior cities), Yangzhou (eastern China, representing developed coastal areas), Zhaotong (southwestern China, representing mountainous and less-developed regions), and Urumqi (northwestern China, representing ethnic minority and high-plateau areas). These sites were purposively selected to capture heterogeneity in socioeconomic status, dietary habits, physical activity patterns, and ethnic composition, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the KBBQ-SOA across the broader Chinese population of older adults. Within each city, recruitment will be conducted from three types of settings: urban residential communities, general hospitals (geriatrics, rehabilitation, and endocrinology departments), and senior care institutions (including community day-care and residential nursing homes). This multi-setting approach further ensures inclusion of older adults with varying health statuses and living arrangements.

Standardization and quality control across sites

To ensure data quality and comparability across the four study sites, a rigorous standardization protocol will be implemented. All research assistants will undergo centralized training covering eligibility criteria (AWGS 2019), instrument administration, physical assessment procedures, and ethical protocols; only those demonstrating competency will collect data. A detailed Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) manual will specify scripts, questionnaire order, query responses, and missing data protocols. Throughout data collection, regular virtual meetings and periodic quality audits of completed questionnaires will monitor adherence and address site-specific challenges. These procedures aim to minimize inter-site variability, ensuring that observed differences reflect true population heterogeneity rather than methodological discrepancies.

Participant screening and data collection procedure

Within the communities, recruitment will be conducted in collaboration with community health centers and neighborhood committees through health talks and on-site screening. In hospitals, potential participants will be identified by their attending physicians in the relevant departments and referred to the research team. In senior care institutions, staff will assist in identifying eligible residents. At all sites, potential participants will undergo a standardized eligibility screening based on the criteria below. Those who provide informed consent will subsequently complete the study questionnaires in a quiet, private room on the premises to ensure data quality and confidentiality.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals will be excluded from participation if they meet any of the following criteria:

Presence of mild or severe cognitive impairment, defined as a score of < 24 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [45], or a known diagnosis of dementia. This decision is based on the nature of the KBBQ-SOA as a self-report instrument assessing knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours—all of which require sufficient cognitive capacity for comprehension, recall, and introspective judgment. Including individuals with cognitive impairment could introduce systematic measurement error, as their responses may not accurately reflect their underlying knowledge, beliefs, or behavioural intentions due to difficulties in understanding item content or retrieving relevant information. This approach aligns with COSMIN guidance, which emphasizes that the target population for validation should reflect the population for whom the instrument is intended to be used—in this case, older adults capable of independently reporting their knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours. Sample size

The target sample size of 300–500 participants was determined based on multiple considerations. First, COSMIN guidelines recommend a minimum of 5–10 participants per item for factor analysis [22, 26, 44]. With an anticipated final instrument of approximately 25–35 items, a sample of 300 ensures adequate power for detecting stable factor structures, whereas 500 allows for more robust estimations and accommodates potential attrition. Second, this range exceeds the recommended minimum of 200 cases for both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses [15, 41], thereby supporting the planned split-sample approach (see below). Third, a sample of this size enables detection of small-to-moderate effect sizes in known-groups validity testing with adequate statistical power (α = 0.05, β = 0.20).

For the pilot study, a sample of 30–50 participants was chosen as this range is sufficient to identify ambiguous wording, response format issues, and administration problems without placing undue burden on participants [4, 48]. Consistent with guidelines on instrument revision [18, 58], pilot participants may be included in the main psychometric dataset if only minor wording changes are made; this decision balances methodological rigor with recruitment efficiency. If substantial modifications are required, pilot participants will be excluded and a new sample recruited to avoid bias.

To assess structural validity, the full sample will be randomly split into two independent subsamples of approximately equal size. This approach addresses the common limitation of overfitting in exploratory factor analysis (EFA) by allowing subsequent confirmation of the factor structure in a separate sample (confirmatory factor analysis, CFA), thereby providing stronger evidence of structural validity than using a single sample for both purposes [23].

For test–retest reliability, a purposive subsample of 50 participants was selected to complete the questionnaire after a 2-week interval. This sample size exceeds the minimum of 30 recommended for reliability studies [60] and accounts for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, ensuring at least 40 complete pairs for intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimation. The 2-week interval was chosen to balance the need to minimize recall bias (shorter interval) while avoiding true change in knowledge, beliefs, or behaviours (longer interval), consistent with COSMIN guidance [43].

Data collection procedures

Data collection procedures

Data will be collected by trained research assistants through face-to-face administration of the KBBQ-SOA in quiet settings, including community centers, outpatient clinics, and nursing homes. Sociodemographic and health-related data (e.g., age, sex, education, sarcopenia risk) will also be recorded. The questionnaire will take approximately 10–15 min to complete, and assistance will be provided for participants with visual or literacy difficulties, without influencing their responses.

Statistical analysis

Psychometric analyses will be conducted in accordance with COSMIN guidelines [43, 50]. Structural validity will be examined using a split-sample approach. The full dataset will be randomly divided into two independent subsamples of approximately equal size. In the first subsample, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) will be conducted using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation. Principal axis factoring was chosen over principal component analysis because it focuses on shared variance among items and is therefore more appropriate for identifying latent constructs underlying item responses [23]. Oblique rotation (e.g., promax) was selected because the three hypothesized domains (knowledge, beliefs, behaviours) are expected to be correlated, making orthogonal rotation (which forces uncorrelated factors) theoretically inappropriate [17].

In the second subsample, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) will be performed to test the factor structure identified in the EFA, using robust maximum likelihood estimation to account for potential non-normality in item responses. Model fit will be evaluated using multiple indices: χ²/df < 3, CFI and TLI 0.90, RMSEA 0.08, and SRMR 0.08 [28]. This combination of indices was chosen because it provides complementary information: χ²/df assesses absolute fit, CFI/TLI compare the model to a null model (incremental fit), and RMSEA/SRMR evaluate parsimony-corrected and absolute misfit, respectively [32].

Internal consistency will be assessed using both Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω. Cronbach’s α is reported to facilitate comparison with existing instruments, as it remains the most commonly used reliability coefficient in health research. However, α assumes tau-equivalence (i.e., all items have equal factor loadings), which is rarely met in practice; therefore, McDonald’s ω, which does not require this assumption, will be calculated as a more robust indicator of composite reliability [21, 42]. Values 0.70 for both coefficients will be considered acceptable [60].

Test–retest reliability will be assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with a two-way mixed-effects model and absolute agreement definition. The two-way mixed model was selected because it accounts for systematic differences between the two time points and treats participants as random effects and time as a fixed effect— an approach appropriate for test–retest designs where the same raters (time points) are used for all participants [33]. Absolute agreement was chosen over consistency because we are interested in whether participants’ scores remain stable in absolute terms, not just whether they follow a consistent pattern. An ICC 0.70 will be considered acceptable for group-level comparisons [60].

Measurement error will be estimated through the standard error of measurement (SEM) and smallest detectable change (SDC), and Bland–Altman plots will be used to visualise agreement over time [8]. Missing data patterns will first be examined. If data are deemed Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) or Missing at Random (MAR) with a missing rate > 5%, multiple imputation will be employed. Otherwise, listwise deletion will be applied for MCAR data with a missing rate 5% [54]. Known-groups validity will be tested by comparing KBBQ-SOA scores across predefined subgroups, such as using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for binary subgroups (e.g., sarcopenia risk vs. non-risk), and one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis H tests for subgroups with three or more levels (e.g., education level). Together, these analyses will provide evidence of the structural validity, internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and measurement error of the KBBQ-SOA, ensuring its robustness for use in both research and clinical practice.

Stage 4: Interpretability and feasibility testing

Stage 4 will evaluate the interpretability, feasibility, and potential cross-cultural utility of the KBBQ-SOA, ensuring its robustness and readiness for real-world application. First, interpretability will be assessed through an analysis of score distributions, floor and ceiling effects to provide contextual meaning for the scores. Second, feasibility will be rigorously assessed by quantifying completion time, item-level missing data, and participant-reported burden. Third, the conceptual and semantic clarity of the final instrument will be evaluated through a structured international expert review; this step is designed to assess an aspect of cross-cultural validity and signal the instrument’s potential for future transnational comparative studies, without conducting a formal adaptation. Finally, all findings will be integrated to establish scoring guidelines and practical recommendations. This stage aligns with COSMIN guidelines, which highlight the importance of interpretability and cross-cultural validity.

Interpretability assessment

The interpretability assessment will involve a secondary analysis of the cross-sectional dataset (N = 300–500) collected during Stage 3. The interpretability of the KBBQ-SOA scores will be established by analyzing their distribution and assessing floor and ceiling effects. Descriptive statistics—including the mean, standard deviation, range, skewness, and kurtosis—will be calculated for each domain and the total score, with the shape of the distributions being visually inspected using histograms and box plots. Simultaneously, floor and ceiling effects will be evaluated by determining the proportion of participants achieving the lowest and highest possible scores; an effect will be considered significant if more than 15% of the sample reaches these extremes [39].

Feasibility assessment

The feasibility assessment will require primary data collection from a new, independent sample of participants (n = 30–40), who will complete the final KBBQ-SOA version resulting from Stage 3 analyses. Feasibility will be quantitatively and qualitatively assessed through several key metrics: completion time will be recorded for each participant, item-level missing response rates will be calculated with any item exceeding a 5% missing threshold flagged for review [60], and participant feedback will be collected via a short, structured form evaluating perceived difficulty, clarity of items and instructions, and overall burden.

Potential cross-cultural utility

The potential cross-cultural utility of the KBBQ-SOA will be evaluated through a structured review by a panel of international experts in gerontology, sarcopenia, and psychometrics. These experts, recruited from non-Chinese cultural and linguistic regions, will independently assess the final instrument using a standardized protocol. They will evaluate the conceptual relevance of each item and the comprehensiveness of the domains within their own cultural contexts and will also analyze the semantic clarity and potential for misinterpretation of the Chinese-to-English translated items. Their qualitative feedback and quantitative ratings will be synthesized to identify any items or concepts that may be culturally specific or problematic for broader application. This process does not constitute a formal cross-cultural adaptation but serves to assess the instrument’s cross-cultural validity and signal its potential for future transnational research and adaptation.

Discussion

This protocol describes the development and validation of the KBBQ-SOA in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines. Its primary strengths include: (1) theoretical integration of the KBB framework with HBM and TPB constructs, enabling assessment of key psychological mediators (e.g., self-efficacy, perceived barriers) beyond mere knowledge; (2) methodological rigor ensured by COSMIN-guided development and validation; (3) enhanced generalizability through multi-site recruitment across four diverse Chinese regions and multiple setting types (communities, hospitals, care institutions); and (4) forward-looking assessment of cross-cultural utility through international expert review, positioning the instrument for future transnational research.

Despite the rigorous design, several potential limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design for psychometric testing in Stage 3, while standard for establishing reliability and structural validity, precludes the evaluation of longitudinal measurement invariance or responsiveness to change. Future studies will be needed to evaluate how the KBBQ-SOA scores change over time or in response to interventions. Second, although the recruitment has been expanded to four geographically and culturally diverse cities (Changsha, Yangzhou, Zhaotong, and Urumqi) to improve sample representativeness, this selection may still not fully capture the heterogeneity of all older adults in China, particularly those from Northeast or South China regions with unique cultural and socioeconomic contexts. Therefore, subsequent validation studies in additional regions are recommended to further consolidate the instrument’s applicability across the entire Chinese population. Notably, despite the geographical expansion, the implementation of centralized training and standardized operating procedures across all four sites strengthens the internal validity of the study and supports the pooling of data for psychometric analysis, thereby ensuring the robustness of the psychometric properties estimated in this multi-site sample. Third, the feasibility assessment in Stage 4 relies on a relatively small independent sample. While adequate for estimating completion time and initial feedback, this sample size may not capture the full spectrum of usability issues. Fourth, the exclusion of older adults with cognitive impairment (MMSE < 24) limits the generalizability of the KBBQ-SOA to this substantial subpopulation. This decision was made deliberately to ensure the validity and reliability of the instrument in its initial validation phase, as cognitive impairment could introduce confounding measurement error. However, given that sarcopenia and cognitive impairment often co-occur in older populations, future studies should specifically validate the KBBQ-SOA, or develop an adapted version (e.g., with simplified wording, proxy-report format, or interviewer administration), in cognitively impaired older adults to extend its clinical utility. Finally, the instrument’s validity is contingent upon the accuracy of self-reported knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours, which may be subject to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies.

Upon successful validation, the KBBQ-SOA will provide researchers, clinicians, and public health practitioners with a much-needed tool to quantitatively assess the needs and gaps in sarcopenia prevention among older adults. It can serve as a needs assessment tool to identify target areas for community health education programs, a baseline and outcome measure for evaluating the effectiveness of behavioural interventions aimed at preventing sarcopenia, and a screening tool in clinical settings to identify individuals with poor knowledge or low self-efficacy who may benefit from targeted counselling. By providing a standardized and validated instrument, this study has the potential to advance the field of sarcopenia prevention from a focus solely on physical intervention to a more holistic approach that addresses the critical cognitive and psychosocial determinants of health behaviour.

Conclusion

This protocol describes the planned development and validation of the KBBQ-SOA, a novel questionnaire designed to assess knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours regarding sarcopenia prevention among community-dwelling older adults. Grounded in established health behaviour theories and guided by the stringent COSMIN methodology, the KBBQ-SOA is poised to become a psychometrically sound and practically useful instrument. The successful execution of this protocol will fill a significant void in the current assessment landscape, enabling a more nuanced understanding of the factors influencing preventive behaviours against sarcopenia. Ultimately, it is expected that the KBBQ-SOA will contribute to the development of more effective, targeted strategies to combat this debilitating condition, thereby promoting healthy aging and reducing the associated personal and societal burdens.
