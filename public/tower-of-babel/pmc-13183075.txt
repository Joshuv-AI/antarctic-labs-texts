Prediction of postoperative mortality risk in hepatocellular carcinoma based on biomarkers and neural network models
Authors: Yingchen Li, Linan Yin, Bowen Liu, Yan Liu, Dongfeng He, Xuesong Liu, Ruibao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality. Postoperative survival is influenced by various factors. Traditional prognostic assessment methods have limitations and often fail to fully reflect individual patient differences. This study aims to analyze physiological, biochemical, and clinical characteristics of HCC patients to explore their association with mortality risk and construct a neural network-based HCC mortality scoring system, offering a novel approach to personalized prognostic evaluation. This retrospective study included 327 HCC patients admitted to a tertiary hospital between June 2020 and June 2022. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationship between biomarkers (e.g., lymphocytes, neutrophils, alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) and clinical features (e.g., number of lesions, imaging stages, microvascular invasion) with mortality risk. A neural network model was then constructed to develop an HCC mortality scoring system, and its predictive performance was assessed. Both univariate and multivariate analyses identified significant predictors of postoperative mortality risk, including lymphocytes (HR = 1.06, P < .001), ALT (HR = 0.98, P = .005), AST (HR = 1.03, P = .003), and the number of lesions (≥3, HR = 11.87, P < .001). The neural network model based on these factors demonstrated good performance in predicting survival among non-deceased patients (precision 78%, recall 84%), but its performance for the mortality category was suboptimal. Biomarkers such as lymphocytes, ALT, and AST, as well as clinical features including the number of lesions and imaging stages, are significant predictors of postoperative mortality risk in HCC patients. This study is the first to develop an HCC mortality scoring system using a neural network model, providing scientific support for personalized prognostic evaluation and precise treatment of HCC.

1. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common and deadly malignancies worldwide, particularly in Asia and Africa.[1,2] The high incidence of liver cancer is closely associated with chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis, and infections by hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus.[3] Despite significant advances in modern medicine in early diagnosis and treatment techniques, such as imaging screening, surgical resection, and targeted therapy, the prognosis of HCC remains poor because it is often diagnosed at advanced stages or in the presence of severe liver dysfunction. The high mortality rate remains a major challenge in global public health.[4–6]

Prognostic assessment of liver cancer is crucial for guiding treatment and improving patient survival. Currently, commonly used prognostic tools include the Barcelona liver cancer staging system, Child-Pugh score,[7] and TNM staging system.[8] These traditional methods mainly focus on tumor staging, liver function status, and overall clinical presentation.[9–11] However, these tools have certain limitations, as they cannot fully reflect the biological characteristics of the tumor and individual patient differences.[12,13] In recent years, with the rapid development of molecular biology and precision medicine, blood biomarkers such as lymphocytes, neutrophils, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) have gradually attracted research attention as potential prognostic markers for HCC.[14,15] These biomarkers may provide supplementary information in the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of liver cancer.

However, a single biomarker is insufficient for comprehensive prognostic prediction. The combination of multiple biomarkers and clinical features, combined with machine learning methods, could provide a more accurate tool for HCC prognostic evaluation. In recent years, artificial intelligence technologies, particularly neural network models,[16] have rapidly developed in the medical field, showing unique advantages in analyzing multidimensional data and uncovering latent patterns. This offers new possibilities for predicting the prognosis of complex diseases. This study aims to systematically explore the relationship between physiological, biochemical indicators, and clinical features with mortality risk in HCC patients through retrospective analysis and to construct a neural network-based HCC mortality scoring system to provide new insights and support for personalized prognostic evaluation of HCC patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Clinical data

Patients diagnosed with liver cancer who visited the hepatobiliary surgery department of the hospital between June 2020 and June 2022 were enrolled in the experimental group. The control group consisted of patients who visited the hospital but were not diagnosed with liver cancer, as confirmed by imaging and pathological examinations.

2.1.1. Inclusion criteria

Liver cancer group: Patients diagnosed with HCC and planned for surgical resection. Age: 18 to 80 years; gender: no limitation.

Control group: Patients confirmed to not have liver cancer through imaging and pathological examinations. Age: 18 to 80 years; gender: no limitation.

2.1.2. Exclusion criteria

Patients with a history of liver surgery (e.g., liver transplantation, liver resection);Patients with concurrent other malignant tumors; Patients with severe liver dysfunction (e.g., Child-Pugh grade C) or other severe comorbidities (e.g., heart disease, respiratory diseases); Patients currently using or who have used medications affecting liver function within 2 weeks prior to sampling (e.g., certain antibiotics, antiviral drugs); Patients with allergic reactions to anesthetics or surgical drugs (e.g., isoflurane, propofol); and Patients with severe coagulation disorders or bleeding tendencies.

2.2. Data collection

General information: This includes both dependent and independent variables. The dependent variables include liver cancer mortality and recurrence, recording whether the patient died or experienced a recurrence during follow-up due to liver cancer-related factors. Independent variables include clinical basic information (gender, age, height, weight, surgery date, and HBV infection status), laboratory examination indicators (lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, etc), pathological indicators (histological grade, presence of satellite lesions, number of lesions), and CNLC.

Methodology: First, patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected to ensure sample representativeness. Subsequently, all patients underwent detailed clinical evaluations, including medical history collection, physical examination, imaging, and laboratory tests to ensure data accuracy. Personalized surgical plans were developed based on the patient’s liver cancer stage and liver function status, and regular follow-ups were conducted postoperatively to monitor recovery and tumor recurrence.

2.3. Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. First, the time status was divided into 3 groups: pre-hospitalization (labeled as 1), pre-surgery (labeled as 2), and post-surgery (labeled as 3). Differences among these 3 groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Then, based on pre-hospitalization data, the samples were divided into 2 groups: death (labeled as 1) and survival (labeled as 0). For normally distributed continuous variables, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and a t-test was used to explore differences between the 2 groups. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range [M P25, P75)] were used to describe the data, and non-parametric tests were applied for group comparisons. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the correlation between patient mortality and clinical features as well as biomarkers. Relevant variables were selected, and continuous variables were divided into 2 groups based on the mean, followed by univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. A forest plot was created for a visual presentation of the results. Subsequently, Cox regression analysis and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis were performed. To further investigate the data, a neural network model was constructed to predict the liver cancer mortality scoring system. In all statistical analyses, a P-value ≤ .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

3. Results

3.1. Univariate ANOVA analysis of patient status at different time points

This analysis explored the trends and significant differences in various physiological and biochemical indicators of patients at pre-hospitalization, pre-surgery, and post-surgery stages.

Lymphocytes showed a decrease in count pre-surgery (from 1.96 ± 4.71 before hospitalization to 1.06 ± 1.21 pre-surgery), but slightly increased post-surgery (1.91 ± 7.98), with the overall change not being significant (P = .56). Monocytes significantly increased from 3.26 ± 2.22 before hospitalization to 7.66 ± 7.27 pre-surgery (P < .001), and then decreased to 4.91 ± 3.93 post-surgery. Neutrophils increased from 0.47 ± 0.22 before hospitalization to 0.77 ± 0.49 pre-surgery (P < .001) and then increased further to 0.93 ± 1.07 post-surgery. Both monocytes and neutrophils showed a significant increase pre-surgery (P < .001), with monocytes decreasing post-surgery but remaining elevated, and neutrophils continuing to rise.

Red blood cells decreased significantly from 142.82 ± 16.62 before hospitalization to 116.24 ± 19.93 pre-surgery (P < .001), with a slight recovery post-surgery to 117.90 ± 18.34. Platelets showed a decrease followed by an increase: they were 116.91 ± 47.25 pre-surgery and increased to 176.16 ± 80.16 post-surgery. The red blood cell count significantly decreased pre-surgery (P < .001).

ALT and AST levels significantly increased pre-surgery (P < .001), and while they decreased post-surgery, they remained higher than pre-hospitalization levels. Total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, and indirect bilirubin also showed significant increases pre-surgery (P < .001), with a decreasing trend post-surgery. Total protein, albumin, and globulin significantly decreased pre-surgery (P < .001), with a slight recovery post-surgery but still lower than pre-hospitalization levels. The albumin-globulin ratio showed no significant difference across the 3 stages (P = .658). Prealbumin significantly decreased pre-surgery (P < .001), with a slight recovery post-surgery but still lower than before hospitalization. Glucose levels showed no significant differences across the 3 stages (P = .965) (Table 1).

3.3. Pearson correlation analysis of patient mortality and baseline characteristics

Based on Pearson correlation analysis, lymphocyte count was significantly correlated with patient mortality status (P = .043). The correlation coefficient was positive (R = 0.112), indicating a positive correlation between lymphocyte count and the risk of patient mortality. The correlation between survival time and patient mortality status was negative (r = −0.085), suggesting that as survival time increases, the likelihood of death seems to decrease. The correlation coefficient for weight and patient mortality status was 0.068 (P = .222), which is close to the statistical significance threshold. A decrease in weight may reflect worsening patient condition, which is associated with poor prognosis. Although the P-value for total bilirubin did not reach statistical significance (P = .16), the correlation coefficient was negative (R = 0.078), suggesting that elevated total bilirubin levels may be associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients. The correlation coefficient for the number of lesions and patient mortality status was 0.026 (P = .638). Although it did not reach statistical significance, patients with multiple lesions generally had higher mortality rates, and an increase in the number of lesions may reflect poor prognosis of the tumor (Table 3).

3.5. Multivariate cox regression survival analysis

According to the results of the multivariate Cox regression analysis, for each unit increase in lymphocyte count, the risk of related events increased by approximately 6% (HR = 1.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.03 to 1.09), with this result showing high statistical significance (P < .001). This suggests that an increase in lymphocyte count may indicate a worse prognosis in certain disease states. Conversely, for each unit increase in ALT level, the risk of related events decreased by approximately 2.2% (HR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.96 to 0.99), which was statistically significant (P < .05), indicating a potential protective effect of alanine. AST showed that for each unit increase in its level, the risk of related events increased by approximately 3% (HR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01 to 1.04), with statistical significance (P < .05).

Neutrophil count revealed that for each unit increase, the risk of related events increased by approximately 399% (HR = 4.99, 95% CI = 1.10 to 22.74), with statistical significance (P = .036). The presence of satellite lesions was associated with a 75% decrease in the risk of related events (HR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.09 to 0.69), which was statistically significant (P = .008). Microvascular invasion (MVI) was associated with an approximately 119% increase in the risk of related events (HR = 2.19, 95% CI = 1.15 to 4.19), with statistical significance (P = .018). The number of lesions (≥3) was associated with an approximately 1087% increase in the risk of related events (HR = 11.87, 95% CI = 3.29–42.78), with highly significant statistical results (P < .001). For CNLC (III-A), each unit increase in the stage was associated with a 256% increase in the risk of related events (HR = 3.56, 95% CI = 1.06–12.00), which was statistically significant (P = .04), indicating that these factors may be important risk factors (Table 4).

3.6. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

The patients were grouped based on the mean values of lymphocyte count, ALT, AST, and neutrophil count. Group 1 included patients with values below the mean, and Group 2 included those with values above the mean. Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed significant changes in survival rates for each group when survival time approached 40 months, suggesting that these biomarkers may have significant value in early prognostic assessment.

Specifically, in the lymphocyte group, the survival rate for patients with lymphocyte counts below the mean was 50%, while for those with counts above the mean, the survival rate was 60% (Fig. 2A), indicating that higher lymphocyte levels may be associated with a better prognosis. The result for the ALT group was even more significant, with a survival rate of only 40% for patients with ALT levels below the mean, and 70% for those above the mean (Fig. 2B), suggesting that ALT may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker. The AST group showed a similar trend (Fig. 2C), further supporting the importance of biomarkers in tumor prognosis assessment. In contrast, the survival rate for neutrophils was about 50% in both the below-mean and above-mean groups (Fig. 2D), reflecting the complex role of neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis curve. The x-axis represents survival time (in months), and the y-axis represents survival rate. The curve illustrates the impact of different clinical features (including lymphocyte count, alanine aminotransferase levels, aspartate aminotransferase levels, neutrophil count, number of lesions, number of satellite lesions, microvascular invasion status, and imaging staging) on patient survival rates.

In addition to biomarkers, clinical features such as the number of lesions, presence of satellite lesions, MVI, and CNLC also significantly impacted patient prognosis. For patients with 3 or more lesions, the survival rate at 60 months was approximately 50% (Fig. 2E), indicating that an increasing number of lesions is associated with poor prognosis. Conversely, the presence of satellite lesions appeared to have a positive impact on survival rate, with patients without satellite lesions having a survival rate of about 50%, and those with satellite lesions having a survival rate of 60% (Fig. 2F). The presence of microvascular invasion significantly lowered the survival rate, with patients with MVI having a survival rate of about 40%, while those without MVI had a survival rate of 57% (Fig. 2G), highlighting the importance of microvascular invasion in liver cancer progression. Finally, for imaging stage IIIA, the survival rate at 60 months was close to 60% (Fig. 2H), further confirming the critical role of CNLC in patient prognosis assessment.

3.7. Construction of the neural network model for HCC mortality scoring system

3.7.1. Model evaluation

The confusion matrix of the model showed that the number of true positives was 64, and the number of true negatives was 5, with a total sample size of 99. The overall accuracy of the model was 70% (Fig. 3A). However, when addressing the issue of class imbalance, the model performed well in predicting non-deceased patients, with a precision of 78% and a recall rate of 84%, demonstrating strong identification capability for this category. In contrast, for predicting mortality, the model’s precision was 29% and recall rate was 22%, indicating weak predictive ability and failure to effectively identify this minority class (Fig. 3B). An analysis of the raw data suggests that this performance discrepancy may arise from class imbalance, as the number of non-deceased samples greatly exceeded the number of deceased samples.

4. Discussion

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from 327 HCC patients, systematically exploring the relationship between physiological, biochemical indicators, clinical characteristics, and liver cancer mortality risk. The findings show that biomarkers such as lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, ALT, AST, as well as factors such as the number of lesions, CNLC, and microvascular invasion, significantly affect postoperative mortality risk in HCC patients. These findings provide important evidence for prognostic assessment and personalized management of liver cancer patients.

Lymphocyte count was significantly associated with mortality risk (R = 0.112, P = .043), reflecting the important impact of immune status on the progression of liver cancer. A decline in immune function may increase tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. Previous studies have indicated that immune dysfunction in patients with chronic hepatitis or cirrhosis is related to immune escape within the tumor microenvironment.[17,18] Additionally, ALT levels were negatively correlated with mortality risk (HR = 0.98, P = .005), suggesting that patients with better liver function tend to have a better prognosis. Related studies have found that liver function reserve capacity is an important factor influencing treatment outcomes in liver cancer patients.[9,19] In contrast, increased AST levels (HR = 1.03, P = .003) reflect more severe liver dysfunction and inflammatory responses, which may accelerate tumor progression and lead to poor prognosis. The increase in neutrophil count significantly raised the risk of mortality (HR = 4.99, P = .036). Existing literature suggests that cytokines released by neutrophils during inflammation may promote tumor invasion and metastasis.[20,21] Therefore, these biomarkers not only serve as potential predictive tools but may also become future therapeutic targets.

In terms of clinical characteristics, this study found that the number of lesions is an important factor affecting the prognosis of liver cancer patients. Patients with 3 or more lesions had a significantly higher mortality risk (HR = 11.87, P < .001). Multiple lesions suggest a higher tumor burden, which may be accompanied by extensive liver metastasis, thus lowering the patient’s survival rate. This finding is consistent with previous literature, further confirming that the number of lesions is a key indicator for assessing patient prognosis.[22,23]

CNLC, as an important tool for liver cancer staging, was also validated for its prognostic value in this study. Patients with stage IIIA had a significantly higher risk of mortality (HR = 3.56, P = .04). Advanced staging is usually associated with increased tumor invasiveness and greater difficulty in surgical resection, which may lower the patient’s long-term survival rate.[24,25] MVI was also identified as an important risk factor in this study (HR = 2.19, P = .018). The presence of MVI suggests a higher degree of tumor malignancy, which may promote the intra-vascular spread of tumor cells, significantly increasing the postoperative recurrence rate.[26,27] In contrast, patients with satellite lesions had a lower mortality risk (HR = 0.25, P = .008), suggesting that these patients may have relatively localized tumor progression, leading to a better prognosis.

This study is the first to attempt constructing a mortality scoring system for liver cancer patients using a neural network model. The model performed well in predicting non-deceased patients (with a precision of 78% and recall of 84%), demonstrating its potential in handling complex clinical data. However, the model’s predictive performance for the mortality category was low (with a precision of 29%), primarily due to the class imbalance in the data. Future research could improve the model’s overall predictive ability by increasing sample size and optimizing the algorithm (e.g., by introducing class weighting mechanisms). Although the current model’s predictive performance has not yet reached clinical application standards, it shows advantages in uncovering latent patterns in multidimensional data. This machine learning-based predictive approach, combined with traditional multivariate statistical analysis, provides an innovative idea for the prognostic evaluation of complex diseases.

5. Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed the clinical data of HCC patients and found that biomarkers such as lymphocytes, ALT, and AST, as well as clinical features including the number of lesions, CNLC, and microvascular invasion, have significant predictive value for postoperative mortality risk. This study not only validated key risk factors but also, for the first time, constructed a liver cancer mortality scoring system using a neural network model, demonstrating the advantages of integrating multidimensional data. This provides new insights for personalized management and prognostic evaluation. These findings offer strong support for precision medicine in liver cancer. In the future, combining multicenter data and richer biomarkers is expected to further optimize the model’s predictive ability, providing scientific evidence for the improvement of HCC diagnosis and treatment strategies.
