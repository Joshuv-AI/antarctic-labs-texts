Current Status of Cellular and Gene-Based Therapies for Congenital Metabolic Disorders: A Review
Authors: Angelika Masiarz, Pola Kosteczko, Magdalena Mazur, Patrycja Pelczar, Żaneta Kimber-Trojnar, Bożena Leszczyńska-Gorzelak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital metabolic disorders often lead to irreversible organ damage beginning during fetal life or shortly after birth. Advances in prenatal diagnostics have enabled earlier identification of these conditions, creating opportunities for prenatal therapeutic intervention. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that prenatal cell therapies can result in donor cell engraftment, enzyme production, and partial correction of metabolic defects in several disease models. The fetal environment may support immune tolerance and enhance treatment effectiveness. Limited clinical experience suggests that these approaches are feasible and may be beneficial in selected conditions, including lysosomal storage diseases and infantile-onset Pompe disease. In utero gene therapy has also shown long-term metabolic correction in animal models. However, important challenges remain, including limited engraftment, immune responses, vector safety, ethical concerns, and regulatory barriers. Prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies are promising but remain experimental. Current evidence is strongest for biological rationale and preclinical proof of concept; clinical benefit has not yet been established for most disorders. Further clinical studies are needed to determine their safety, efficacy, and future role in the treatment of congenital metabolic disorders. This article reviews the current status of prenatal fetal cellular and gene-based therapeutic approaches for inborn errors of metabolism.

Introduction

Definition of Prenatal Cellular and Gene-Based Therapies

In utero therapy includes treatments performed during fetal life to prevent, modify, or treat genetic and metabolic disorders before birth, before irreversible organ damage occurs. This approach exploits several unique features of the fetal environment, such as relative immune tolerance, rapid tissue growth, and early access to developing organs. These characteristics may improve cell engraftment and support long-term therapeutic gene expression [1,2]. Prenatal treatment strategies for metabolic disorders include stem cell transplantation, progenitor cell therapy, enzyme replacement, and gene-based approaches that target the underlying genetic defect [2,3]. Among cell-based therapies, pluripotent stem cells have attracted particular interest because they can self-renew indefinitely and differentiate into all 3 germ layers. Embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells can generate specialized cell types with metabolic functions, including hepatocytes and phagocytic cells. These cells may be useful for treating multiple inherited metabolic disorders [2,4,5]. Additionally, pluripotent cells can be genetically corrected before transplantation, allowing disease-causing variants to be repaired prior to treatment [5,6].

Importance of Metabolic Disorders During the Prenatal Period

Inborn errors of metabolism are a group of inherited disorders caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in metabolic pathways. These defects lead to accumulation of toxic substances or deficiencies in essential metabolic products, disrupting normal cellular function [7]. Many inherited metabolic disorders, including lysosomal storage diseases, urea cycle disorders, and other monogenic diseases, begin during fetal life or shortly after birth. Enzyme deficiency or the accumulation of toxic metabolites disrupts normal tissue development and causes progressive damage, particularly in the liver, brain, and skeletal muscles [8,9]. Alpha-mannosidosis is a notable example. This lysosomal storage disorder is associated with neurodegeneration and developmental abnormalities that may begin before birth [10]. Improvements in prenatal diagnosis, including molecular testing and fetal ultrasonography, now allow many of these conditions to be identified during pregnancy [1,4]. Early diagnosis creates an opportunity for prenatal treatment prior to development of irreversible tissue damage. Early intervention may slow disease progression and improve both short- and long-term neurological and developmental outcomes [9,11].

Translational Potential of Prenatal Cellular and Gene-Based Therapies

Prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies offer a promising approach for treating congenital metabolic disorders. Pluripotent stem cells can differentiate into specialized cell types and replace dysfunctional cells in organs such as the liver, hematopoietic system, and nervous system, thereby helping to restore enzyme activity [5,12]. These cells can also be genetically modified using tools such as CRISPR-Cas9 before transplantation, allowing correction of disease-causing mutations [6,13]. The fetal environment may further improve treatment outcomes. Stem cell transplantation before birth can potentially promote immune tolerance, reduce the risk of graft rejection, and decrease the need for immunosuppressive therapy after birth [9,14]. Donor cells may integrate into developing tissues more efficiently before birth than after birth, increasing the likelihood of long-term metabolic correction. Even low levels of donor-cell engraftment may provide clinical benefit through cross-correction, particularly in lysosomal storage disorders [5,10,15]. This effect is supported by the relatively tolerant fetal immune system, which is characterized by central immune tolerance, limited antigen presentation, and increased regulatory T-cell activity [14].

This review provides an integrated overview of prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies for the major groups of congenital metabolic disorders. We compare these approaches with respect to their mechanisms of action, disease suitability, intervention timing, potential for long-term metabolic correction, and major barriers to clinical translation. Particular attention is given to disorders with prenatal or early neonatal onset, low enzyme replacement thresholds, and the potential to benefit from cross-correction. We also discuss the main challenges that currently limit clinical translation and highlight future directions for the development of prenatal therapies to manage congenital metabolic disorders.

Congenital Metabolic Disorders as a Target for In Utero Therapy

Characteristics of Congenital Metabolic Disorders

Congenital metabolic disorders are highly heterogeneous in their genetic basis and clinical presentation. This diversity led to the development of the International Classification of Inherited Metabolic Disorders (ICIMD) in 2021, which divides these conditions into 24 subgroups [16]. Inborn errors of metabolism are usually caused by inherited or de novo pathogenic variants that disrupt specific metabolic pathways. Such disruption results in the accumulation of toxic substrates and deficiencies in downstream products [16,17]. The ICIMD also includes disorders caused by somatic mutations, as well as conditions that either can be identified through biochemical testing or present with a characteristic metabolic phenotype. Additionally, some pathway abnormalities without clear clinical manifestations are included. Mitochondrial and nuclear DNA-related disorders are both represented in this classification [16].

Inherited Metabolic Diseases With Potential Indications for Prenatal Cellular and Gene-Based Therapies

The suitability of congenital metabolic disorders for prenatal therapeutic intervention depends on several factors, including the timing of disease onset, the risk of irreversible prenatal organ damage, the accessibility of affected tissues, and the potential for therapeutic benefit from partial metabolic correction. Disorders characterized by early systemic manifestations, cross-correction potential, or severe neonatal presentation may be particularly suitable candidates for prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies.

Gaucher disease is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the gene encoding β-glucosidase (glucocerebrosidase), which result in the accumulation of glucocerebroside in macrophages and formation of characteristic Gaucher cells. The acute neuronopathic form of the disease is severe and often leads to death within the first 2 years of life [18–20].

Pompe disease is caused by a deficiency in acid alpha-glucosidase. Glycogen accumulates in lysosomes, leading to cardiomyopathy, muscle weakness, and early death if left untreated [21]. Hurler syndrome results from a deficiency in alpha-L-iduronidase; it is a rapidly progressive disease with multisystem involvement and a life expectancy of approximately 5 years without treatment [22].

Lysosomal storage disorders are considered attractive candidates for prenatal therapeutic approaches because relatively low levels of enzymatic activity may provide clinical benefit through cross-correction mechanisms. Additionally, several lysosomal storage disorders begin during fetal development, supporting the biological rationale for early intervention to limit progressive neurological injury. Urea cycle disorders (UCDs) result from defects in enzymes responsible for ammonia detoxification in the liver. The urea cycle includes 6 enzymes and 2 transporters [23,24]. Ammonia accumulation leads to metabolic imbalance and neurotoxicity, which can result in cerebral edema, demyelination, and impaired brain function. Clinically, UCDs present with lethargy, seizures, coma, feeding difficulties, and developmental delay. If untreated, they are often fatal [24]. Major UCDs include deficiencies in carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, N-acetylglutamate synthase, ornithine transcarbamylase, argininosuccinate synthase 1, argininosuccinate lyase, and arginase [23].

Because severe neonatal hyperammonemia can rapidly lead to irreversible neurological injury, selected UCDs may represent potential targets for early prenatal therapeutic intervention. However, the feasibility of durable metabolic correction remains uncertain and may depend on the degree of hepatic engraftment or the efficiency of gene delivery.

Fatty acid β-oxidation disorders affect mitochondrial energy metabolism. This process involves 4 main enzymes and transport systems, including carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT) and carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase [25]. CPT I deficiency may cause hypoglycemia, liver dysfunction, and hyperammonemia. Transport defects can also lead to neurological symptoms and liver disease. In contrast, CPT II deficiency typically presents later in life during periods of metabolic stress, along with recurrent rhabdomyolysis and severe myalgia [26]. Severe carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase deficiency presents in the neonatal period with cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and neurological damage [27]. Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiencies are classified according to fatty acid chain length. The most common is medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency, which may present with seizures, coma, or sudden death in early childhood [25]. Symptoms of β-oxidation defects are frequently exacerbated by fasting, infection, or physiological stress [28]. Less common disorders include enoyl-CoA hydratase deficiency and 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency [28,29].

Overall, congenital metabolic disorders substantially differ in their biological and clinical suitability for prenatal therapeutic intervention. Disorders associated with severe prenatal or early neonatal manifestations, progressive neurodegeneration, and low therapeutic thresholds for enzymatic correction appear to be the most promising targets for prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies. In contrast, disorders requiring extensive tissue replacement or predominantly presenting later in life may be less amenable to current prenatal therapeutic strategies.

Justification for Prenatal Intervention

Hyperammonemia is a key feature of urea cycle disorders and often develops soon after birth [24]. Newborns who are initially asymptomatic often begin to exhibit clinical manifestations within 36 to 48 hours after birth [23]. Elevated ammonia levels can cause irreversible brain injury, seizures, coma, and death [24]. Other metabolic disorders also carry a high risk of permanent organ damage, even with early postnatal diagnosis [25,27].

Current treatments have important limitations. Most UCDs lack a definitive cure; N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency has an effective treatment. Patients exhibit a lifelong risk of metabolic crises despite treatment [24,30]. Nevertheless, prenatal diagnostic methods continue to improve. Many disorders, including Gaucher disease, Hurler syndrome, and Pompe disease, can now be detected before birth. Earlier therapeutic intervention is generally associated with improved outcomes in several metabolic disorders, providing a rationale for investigation of prenatal therapeutic strategies [20,21,31].

Current Treatment Options and Their Limitations

Hyperammonemic encephalopathy secondary to urea cycle defects may be treated with liver transplantation. Early transplantation has been shown to improve survival and reduce baseline ammonia concentrations, thereby lowering the risk of recurrent metabolic crises. However, outcomes remain suboptimal in patients with carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency or ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, particularly in those presenting with initial ammonia levels above 360 μmol/L, due to persistent neurodevelopmental impairment. Moreover, delayed transplantation does not reverse pre-existing neurological injury [24]. Additional limitations include the need for lifelong immunosuppression and the shortage of suitable donors.

Another therapeutic strategy is postnatal gene therapy for metabolic pathway defects, including liver-directed approaches for UCDs. Although the liver is an attractive target because of its high perfusion and relative tolerogenicity, this approach does not address established neurological damage. Furthermore, hepatic fibrosis, particularly in argininosuccinate lyase deficiency, may reduce therapeutic efficacy. To date, clinical studies have primarily involved adults with late-onset phenotypes; data remain limited for severe neonatal and infantile presentations [30].

Gene therapy targeting extrahepatic tissues (eg, muscle and nervous tissue) faces additional challenges, such as vector clearance by the host immune system and the risk of severe systemic immune reactions, including anaphylaxis and disseminated intravascular coagulation [21].

Enzyme replacement therapy comprises administering an exogenous form of the deficient enzyme and is currently used to treat disorders such as Gaucher disease, Hurler syndrome, and Pompe disease. However, enzyme replacement therapy has important limitations, including reduced enzyme stability relative to endogenous proteins, diminished quality of life due to repeated lifelong infusions, high treatment costs, and an inability to cross the blood-brain barrier [19,21,32]. Rare complications include hypersensitivity reactions, allergic reactions, and treatment discontinuation because of poor venous access [32]. A gradual decline in therapeutic efficacy with prolonged treatment has also been reported [21]. An alternative option for selected disorders is substrate reduction therapy, although its use may be limited by adverse effects and the risk of complications [32].

In Utero Cell Therapies: The State of Experimental Research

Animal Models of In Utero Cell Therapies

Animal models are central to the development of in utero cell therapies. They allow evaluation of feasibility, engraftment, immune tolerance, and therapeutic efficacy. Most studies to date have used hematopoietic, mesenchymal, or monocytic cells.

Rodent models provided the first proof-of-concept data. A notable example is the Gunn rat model of Crigler-Najjar syndrome. In this model, placental stem cells were injected into the fetal liver under ultrasound guidance. The procedure proved feasible; the transplanted cells engrafted, survived, and persisted after birth. Functional integration was observed, including restoration of deficient hepatic enzymatic activity [33]. These findings support the ability of transplanted cells to integrate into the fetal environment and restore metabolic function.

Early studies also identified key determinants of therapeutic outcome. The timing of intervention, route of administration, and target organ all influence engraftment and efficacy. Donor cells were shown to persist postnatally and mediate partial metabolic correction without requiring immunosuppression. These findings support the possibility of sustained postnatal therapeutic effects in preclinical models.

Large-animal models play a critical role in clinical translation. The fetal sheep model was the first to demonstrate durable allogeneic engraftment after in utero transplantation and remains widely used. It enables detailed analysis of engraftment kinetics, biodistribution, and long-term integration in a setting that closely resembles human pregnancy. The feline model of alpha-mannosidosis provides further insight into disease-specific applications. In this model, monocytic cells are transplanted in utero via intracardiac and intraperitoneal routes, enabling systemic distribution. Donor cells express functional enzyme, and even partial chimerism results in therapeutic benefit via cross-correction [34]. This mechanism is considered central to lysosomal storage disorders and underlies many current cell-based therapeutic strategies.

Emerging approaches include the use of pluripotent stem cells, which offer broader differentiation potential and the ability to generate disease-specific cell types. However, their use in prenatal settings remains largely experimental and requires further validation in relevant models.

Preclinical and Clinical Studies in Metabolic Diseases

Most studies of in utero cell therapy have focused on severe, early-onset metabolic disorders, including lysosomal storage diseases, urea cycle defects, and inherited hepatic enzyme deficiencies. In these conditions, organ damage often begins before birth, supporting the rationale for prenatal intervention.

In both rodent and large-animal models, prenatal cell delivery has resulted in donor-cell engraftment, enzyme expression, and partial metabolic correction [35–37]. Such findings provide a foundation for potential future clinical applications. More recent work has expanded the range of cell sources to include mesenchymal stromal cells, placental-derived cells, and human amniotic epithelial cells [37,38]. These cell populations are characterized by low immunogenicity and differentiation potential, making them suitable candidates for prenatal use.

The fetal immune environment represents a major advantage of in utero therapy. It promotes immune tolerance and reduces the risk of rejection [39], potentially allowing long-term engraftment without requiring immunosuppression.

Clinical data remain limited. Most available studies are early-stage and primarily focus on feasibility and safety, rather than definitive efficacy. To date, no large randomized controlled trials have evaluated in utero cell therapy for metabolic diseases. Some translational strategies are emerging. The PEARL clinical trial is a phase 1 study evaluating prenatal enzyme replacement therapy for selected lysosomal storage disorders [40,41]. Treatment is administered between 18 and 35 weeks of gestation through ultrasound-guided umbilical vein injection. Outcomes include survival, growth, neurodevelopment, and organ function. A notable clinical case has been reported concerning infantile-onset Pompe disease. Prenatal therapy, followed by standard postnatal treatment, resulted in normal cardiac function and appropriate development at 13 months of age [42]. Although this was not a cell-based in utero therapy, it illustrates the potential value of early prenatal intervention. In the context of future cell-based approaches, pluripotent stem cells represent a promising direction. Their ability to generate multiple cell types and support sustained enzyme production makes them attractive candidates. However, their clinical application in prenatal settings has not yet been established; reliable conclusions cannot be drawn from isolated clinical observations alone. Efficacy and Safety Results

Most evidence regarding in utero cell therapy is derived from animal studies; IUSCT serves as the primary approach. These studies provide important insights into factors that determine therapeutic success.

In a murine model of mucopolysaccharidosis type VII, donor-derived cells were detected in multiple tissues, including the brain. Engraftment was correlated with enzyme activity. Although enzyme levels were relatively low, improvements in skeletal structure and reproductive function were observed [43]. These findings suggest that partial restoration of enzyme activity is sufficient to achieve measurable phenotypic improvement in preclinical models. Subsequent studies demonstrated that IUSCT can result in microglial engraftment in the brain without conditioning. Cross-correction was observed in the liver; the overall disease phenotype improved across multiple tissues. Reduced inflammation was noted in regions adjacent to donor-derived microglia [44].

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of IUSCT continue to reduce its effectiveness. First, fetal hematopoietic organs, particularly the liver, are already densely populated with endogenous hematopoietic stem cells. Consequently, transplanted cells must compete for limited niche space, hindering long-term engraftment [45]. Second, cell dose is a critical determinant of therapeutic success, although higher doses may increase toxicity risk [46]. Third, immune-related factors remain important. Whereas the fetal immune system is immature, maternal antibodies and inflammatory signals can cross the placenta and interfere with graft acceptance or lead to rejection [47]. Finally, the procedure must be performed within a narrow gestational window, when the fetal liver is the primary hematopoietic site. Interventions performed too early or too late may reduce cell homing, survival, and engraftment [48]. Clinical data, although limited, provide useful context. A 2023 analysis identified 66 IUSCT procedures performed in 52 fetuses. Procedure-related fetal mortality was 3%, and the overall complication rate was 4.5% [4]. Engraftment outcomes varied across studies and disease models.

Maternal safety is especially important, given that prenatal interventions involve both the pregnant individual and the fetus. Ultrasound-guided administration of therapeutic agents, such as enzymes or recombinant proteins, is likely associated with risks similar to those of fetal blood transfusion. According to the International Fetal Transplantation and Immunology Society, fetal transfusions have a complication rate of 1.2% per procedure and a fetal loss rate of 0.6% [49]. Maternal sensitization after exposure to fetal or donor antigens may also occur and can increase the risk of hemolytic disease in future pregnancies. The use of Rho(D) immune globulin in Rhesus-negative patients reduces this risk to approximately 0.35% [50]. Overall, current data suggest that most procedural risks are related to the therapeutic agents used, rather than to the minimally invasive IUSCT procedure itself [49].

Future Directions

Integration of In Utero Cell Therapies With In Utero Gene Therapy

Despite progress in IUSCT, its clinical application remains limited. Importantly, advances in in utero gene therapy (IUGT) are expanding the range of potential prenatal interventions. These approaches aim to modify disease before birth, but their clinical value has not been fully established. To better understand the potential role of in utero therapies, it is useful to compare cell-based and gene-based approaches. Although these strategies involve distinct mechanisms, both seek to modify disease before birth and limit early organ damage. Such a comparison helps identify the major strengths and limitations of each approach, including differences in engraftment, durability of effect, and safety profile. In some cases, combining elements of both therapies may offer additional benefits; this concept remains experimental and requires further investigation.

In utero cell therapy involves transplantation of donor stem or progenitor cells into the fetal circulation or developing organs. The goal is to achieve engraftment and enzyme production via direct cell replacement or cross-correction. This approach is most relevant for hematopoietic and immune disorders, in which even partial donor chimerism may provide clinical benefit. The immature fetal immune system may support tolerance and allow engraftment without myeloablation or long-term immunosuppression. Nevertheless, in practice, donor cells must compete with endogenous fetal hematopoietic stem cells for limited niches. Additionally, maternal immune factors can affect graft acceptance. Consequently, consistent and durable engraftment has been achieved in only a limited number of conditions, including severe combined immunodeficiency [1,2,51].

In contrast, IUGT targets the underlying genetic defect directly by delivering a functional gene or editing the disease-causing locus in fetal cells. Such targeting enables endogenous production of the missing protein or enzyme. Unlike cell therapy, this approach does not require donor-cell engraftment. It also can focus on multiple organs, including the liver, lungs, skeletal muscle, heart, and central nervous system [52–54]. The fetal environment offers several advantages for gene therapy. Lower body mass allows a higher vector dose per kilogram. Furthermore, fetal tissues contain highly proliferative cells, which may improve the efficiency of gene transfer and genome editing. This is particularly important when utilizing approaches based on homology-directed repair. Some animal studies also indicate that in utero gene transfer may induce immune tolerance to the transgene product.

However, clinical experience in humans remains limited. Immune responses to viral vectors, particularly adeno-associated virus, remain a major concern [51,55]. Preclinical studies have shown that in utero CRISPR-based editing of metabolic genes (eg, Pcsk9 and Hpd) can achieve long-term correction in mouse models of dyslipidemia and hereditary tyrosinemia type I [6]. Gene therapy offers the potential for long-term correction after a single intervention and treatment before irreversible damage occurs [56]. Persistent challenges include delivery efficiency, off-target effects, immunogenicity, and ethical concerns related to treating both the fetus and the pregnant individual [57]. Overall, IUSCT and IUGT should be viewed as complementary—not competing—approaches. In some cases, combinations of these strategies may provide additional benefits. For example, donor cells could be genetically corrected ex vivo and then transplanted before birth. This approach might improve engraftment and reduce the need for systemic vector delivery. Translation Barriers—From Preclinical Research to Clinical Practice

Scientific research drives medical progress, but many barriers can slow the translation of new therapies into clinical practice. Such barriers include organizational complexity, stringent regulations, lengthy approval timelines, high costs, and communication challenges among multidisciplinary teams. There is also a shortage of experienced clinical trial coordinators. The impact of these factors may differ among public institutions, private centers, and industry-sponsored research [58].

Regulatory requirements are especially demanding in the field of advanced therapies. Although these requirements are essential for patient safety, they can substantially delay clinical translation. For example, extensive preclinical data are often required, although animal models do not always accurately predict human fetal disease. Additional requirements, including toxicology studies, long-term follow-up, and stringent manufacturing standards, can further prolong development timelines [59]. Other challenges include scalable good-manufacturing-practice-compliant manufacturing and the lack of standardized long-term follow-up frameworks for prenatally treated patients.

Prenatal therapies face additional challenges including ethical issues related to balancing maternal and fetal risks, as well as the acquisition of informed consent in time-sensitive situations. Clinical implementation is further complicated by the rarity of many congenital metabolic disorders, which limits patient recruitment and reduces the feasibility of large prospective studies. Furthermore, treatment requires highly specialized centers and multidisciplinary teams.

Coordinated efforts are needed to address these barriers. Improvements in regulatory processes, clinical trial infrastructure, funding, and manufacturing are warranted. International collaboration will also be important. Collectively, these measures may facilitate the translation of prenatal cellular and gene-based therapies into clinical practice while maintaining appropriate safety and scientific standards.

Conclusions

In utero cell-based therapies represent a promising but experimental strategy for treating congenital metabolic disorders, particularly those with early onset and rapid progression. The fetal environment provides a unique opportunity for intervention, given that its relative immune tolerance and ongoing organ development may support donor-cell engraftment and enable treatment before irreversible damage occurs.

Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated the feasibility of in utero cell transplantation, showing evidence of engraftment, enzyme production, and partial metabolic correction across a range of disease models. In some conditions, even low levels of donor chimerism may be sufficient to achieve therapeutic benefit via cross-correction. However, outcomes remain variable and are influenced by factors such as intervention timing, cell type and dose, administration route, and competition with endogenous fetal cells.

Despite encouraging experimental data, clinical translation remains limited. Available human studies have primarily focused on safety and feasibility; robust data concerning long-term efficacy remain insufficient. Additionally, consistent and durable engraftment has been achieved in only a limited number of conditions, highlighting the need to further optimize therapeutic strategies.

Key challenges include technical limitations related to cell delivery, variable engraftment efficiency, and the effects of maternal and fetal immune interactions. Ethical and regulatory considerations also play an important role in prenatal therapy development. Advances in related fields, including IUGT, may offer complementary approaches and help overcome some of these limitations.

Overall, in utero cell-based therapies hold substantial potential, but their role in clinical practice is not yet fully defined. Further well-designed preclinical and clinical studies are needed to establish their safety, efficacy, and optimal application in the treatment of congenital metabolic disorders.
