Elastic rigid rod in an expanding universe
Simão Correia, José Natário and Jorge Drumond Silva

Abstract
. We study the motion of a rigid elastic rod, initially set in its relaxed state along a spacelike geodesic, in an expanding Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker universe. This leads to an initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for a nonlinear wave equation whose nonlinearity depends on a parameter κ ≥ 0 \kappa\geq 0 , related to the ratio between the rod’s length and the cosmological scale. We show that if κ \kappa is small enough then the solution to the IBVP is global in time and bounded, meaning that the rod’s length oscillates around its initial value. For greater values of κ \kappa , however, the solution to the IBVP blows up in finite coordinate time, indicating that the rod is infinitely stretched by the cosmological expansion. This supports the widely held belief that sufficiently small bound systems do not follow the Hubble flow, whereas larger systems may do so. Similar conclusions apply to the tethered galaxy version of this problem, where the rod is used to connect two point masses (which results in nonlinear boundary conditions for the IBVP).

This setup gives rise to an initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for a nonlinear wave equation, where the nonlinearity depends on a dimensionless parameter

Similar results are obtained in the “tethered galaxy” version of the problem, where the elastic rod connects two point masses. Although this variation gives rise to nonlinear boundary conditions in the IBVP, which require a more intricate analysis to establish an existence and uniqueness result, the qualitative behavior of the solutions remains essentially unchanged.

1.
FLRW universes

Arguably, the simplest models of expanding universes are given by the FLRW metrics,

(1)

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)h_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\,.

These metrics are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Sigma
, where
(\Sigma,h)
is the simply connected Riemannian manifold of constant curvature
0
,
1
or
-1
, that is,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with the standard metric. The lines of constant spatial coordinates
(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
are timelike geodesics, typically interpreted as representing galaxies moving away from each other; this motion, known as the Hubble flow, is governed by the scale factor function
a(t)>0
, which we assume to be strictly increasing and unbounded. If the galaxies are modeled as a pressureless fluid of uniform density
\rho=\rho(t)
then the Einstein equations for (
1
) imply

(2)

\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}=-\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}\,,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho a^{3}\right)=0\,,

where
\Lambda
is the cosmological constant. Notice that our hypotheses on
a(t)
require that
\Lambda\geq 0
.

If
\gamma\subset\Sigma
is the image of a geodesic then
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\gamma
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Sigma
, whose induced metric can be put in the form

(3)

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)dx^{2}\,,

with Gauss curvature

(4)

K=\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}=-\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}

(see Appendix
A
). It follows from equations (
2
) and our hypotheses on
a(t)
that
K(t)
is a nondecreasing function with

(5)

\lim_{t\to\infty}K(t)=\frac{\Lambda}{3}\geq 0\,.

Now consider a group of galaxies initially arranged along a geodesic segment of
\Sigma
. If, instead of allowing them to evolve as non-interacting test particles, we bind them together into an elastic medium, then we can expect their motion to deviate from the Hubble flow. Nevertheless, tidal forces arising from the nonvanishing curvature may try to pull the galaxies apart, straining the elastic medium. Whether this effect is sufficient to overcome the elastic restoring forces depends on the details of the elastic model, which we discuss in the next section.

2.
Elastic rod

Since we are interested in the motion of a one-dimensional elastic body (an elastic rod) along a spatial geodesic direction, we may restrict ourselves to a two-dimensional spacetime
(M,g)
(namely the totally geodesic two-dimensional spacetime submanifold along which the motion takes place). The elastic rod can then be modeled by a scalar field
\lambda:M\to\mathbb{R}
with nonvanishing spacelike gradient, whose level sets correspond to the worldlines of the particles making up the rod (see
[
20
]
for more details). If we choose
\lambda
to be the proper length along the undeformed rod, then the local compression factor is given by the positive function
n
defined via

(6)

n^{2}={|\operatorname{grad}\lambda|}^{2}=({\nabla}^{\alpha}\lambda)({\nabla}_{\alpha}\lambda)\,.

Because we are considering a two-dimensional spacetime, the energy-momentum tensor of the elastic rod necessarily takes the form of a perfect fluid:

(7)

T_{\alpha\beta}=(\mu+p)u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+pg_{\alpha\beta}\,,

where
\mu
is the energy density (per unit length),
p
is the pressure (actually a force in this two-dimensional setting) and
u
is the unit tangent covector to the rod particle’s worldlines.
For simplicity, we assume that the rod is rigid, meaning that its speed of sound equals the speed of light:

(8)

\frac{dp}{d\mu}=1\Rightarrow\mu=\mu_{0}+p\,,

where
\mu_{0}
is the (constant) density in the relaxed state. Under this assumption, as shown in
[
20
]
, the energy density and the pressure are given by

(9)

\mu=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\left(n^{2}+1\right)\,,\qquad p=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\left(n^{2}-1\right)\,,

and so the rod’s energy-momentum tensor becomes

(10)

T_{\alpha\beta}=\mu_{0}\left[{\nabla}_{\alpha}\lambda\,{\nabla}_{\beta}\lambda-\frac{1}{2}({\nabla}^{\mu}\lambda\,{\nabla}_{\mu}\lambda)g_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\alpha\beta}\right]\,,

which is, up to a term with zero divergence, the energy-momentum tensor of a massless scalar field
\lambda
. Consequently, one can readily show that
\lambda
must satisfy the wave equation:

(11)

\Box\lambda=0\,.

Generically, the motion of a finite rod leads to a free boundary problem with nonlinear boundary conditions for this equation.
Such problems are highly nontrivial, and a judicious choice of comoving coordinates, for which where the boundary remains fixed, is typically the most effective approach to study them. In this simple two-dimensional setting, a natural comoving coordinate is the function
\lambda
itself, since the free boundary, corresponding to the endpoints of the rod, is given by level sets of this function. As shown in
[
20
]
, one can always choose a conjugate time function
\tau:M\to\mathbb{R}
, also satisfying the wave equation, such that the metric in the (harmonic) coordinates
(\tau,\lambda)
is written as

(12)

ds^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}(\tau,\lambda)}\left(-d\tau^{2}+d\lambda^{2}\right)\,.

Conversely, if the metric can be expressed in the form (
12
) then
\lambda
is a solution of the wave equation. Therefore, a method for solving the problem of the rod’s motion is simply to find a suitable harmonic coordinate system.

3.
Initial boundary value problem

To solve the free boundary problem we perform a change of variable, setting

(13)

n(\tau,\lambda)=e^{u(\tau,\lambda)}\,,

so that the Gauss curvature of the two-dimensional metric (
12
) is given by

(14)

K=e^{2u}\left(-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime}\right)\,,

where
\dot{}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}
and
{}^{\prime}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}
(see Appendix
A
). We assume that the curvature function satisfies the monotonicity condition

(15)

\dot{K}\geq 0\,,

which holds for the expanding FLRW models discussed in Section
1
, as well as for many other expanding cosmological spacetimes.

Assuming that the rod is initially relaxed and free of internal motions, we are led to the following IBVP:

(16)

\begin{cases}-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime}=Ke^{-2u}\\
u(0,\lambda)=\dot{u}(0,\lambda)=0,\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
u(\tau,0)=u(\tau,1)=0,\quad\tau\geq 0\end{cases}\,,

where we normalize the length of the rod to unity.

Consider the energy functional

(17)

H(\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{u}^{2}+u^{\prime 2}-Ke^{-2u}\right)d\lambda\,.

Differentiating with respect to time yields

\displaystyle\dot{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(2\dot{u}\ddot{u}+2u^{\prime}\dot{u}^{\prime}+2Ke^{-2u}\dot{u}-\dot{K}e^{-2u}\right)d\lambda=\int_{0}^{1}\left(2\dot{u}u^{\prime\prime}+2u^{\prime}\dot{u}^{\prime}-\dot{K}e^{-2u}\right)d\lambda

(18)

\displaystyle=\Big[2\dot{u}u^{\prime}\Big]_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\dot{K}e^{-2u}d\lambda\leq 0\,.

Hence,
H(\tau)
is a nonincreasing function of
\tau
. This monotonicity property allows us to establish a global existence and boundedness result, applicable to the expanding FLRW models discussed in Section
1
in the case when
\Lambda=0
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\dot{K}\geq 0
and
K\leq 0
then the solution to the IBVP (
16
) is global in time towards the future and bounded.

Proof.

According to the blow-up alternative in Theorem
B.1
(see Appendix
B
), if
\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
remains bounded for
\tau>0
then the unique solution to the IBVP can be extended globally in time towards the future. This norm is controlled by the
L^{2}
norm of the spatial derivative, since

(19)

u(\tau,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}u^{\prime}(\tau,s)ds\Rightarrow\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|u^{\prime}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|u^{\prime}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\,,

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Consequently,

(20)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq H(\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\leq H(0)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\,.

If
K\leq 0
we then have

(21)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\leq H(0)\,.

∎

Regarding the expanding FLRW models discussed in Section
1
, this result states that if the cosmological constant vanishes then the rod never follows the Hubble flow. The next result implies that this is still true in the case when
\Lambda>0
if
K
is small enough.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
\dot{K}\geq 0
and
0\leq K\leq\kappa<1/e^{2}
then the solution to the IBVP (
16
) is global in time towards the future and bounded.

Proof.

Since
K\geq 0
, we have

(22)

H(0)=-\int_{0}^{1}K(0,\lambda)d\lambda\leq 0\,,

and so we obtain from (
20
)

(23)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\leq\kappa e^{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\,.

Consider the function

(24)

f(x)=x^{2}-\kappa e^{2x}\,,

which satisfies
1
1

1

It can easily be shown that
f
becomes positive in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
if and only if
\kappa<1/e^{2}
.

(25)

f(0)=-\kappa<0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad f(1)=1-\kappa e^{2}>0\,.

From (
23
) we have

(26)

f(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq 0\,,

and since
\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}=0
we conclude that
\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}<1
for
\tau>0
.
∎

The condition
K\geq 0
corresponds, in the context of the expanding FLRW models discussed in Section
1
, to the regime in which the cosmological constant dominates the expansion – that is, when
\Lambda>4\pi\rho
. In this case,
\kappa
effectively measures the dimensionless ratio between the square of the rod’s length and the square of the asymptotic cosmological radius
R=\sqrt{3/\Lambda}
, and so small
\kappa
corresponds to a rod whose length is much shorter by the cosmological scale.

When the curvature becomes sufficiently large, on the other hand, we obtain the following finite-time blow-up result.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\dot{K}\geq 0
and
K\geq\kappa>\pi^{2}
then the solution to the IBVP (
16
) blows up before
\tau=1/2
.

Proof.

The proof consists in showing that under these hypotheses there is an ODE mechanism driving the blow-up. Consider the future Cauchy development of the initial data for the IBVP (
16
),

(27)

\mathcal{D}=\left\{(\tau,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0\leq\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}-\left|\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right\}

(see Figure
1
), and the vector fields

(28)

L^{-}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad L^{+}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\,,

whose integral curves are of the form

(29)

\tau\mapsto(\tau,\lambda_{0}-\tau)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\tau\mapsto(\tau,\lambda_{0}+\tau)

(with

(30)

L^{-}L^{+}u=-Ke^{-2u}\,,

L^{-}L^{+}u\leq-\kappa e^{-2u}\,.

From the initial conditions in (

It is easy to show (by induction) that the sequences

are decreasing for each

Note that

and

(and similarly for

). From (

are smooth real-valued functions on some interval of the form

, and identically

(\tau_{*},\frac{1}{2})

the function

satisfies the ODE Cauchy problem

(40)

\begin{cases}\ddot{v}=-\kappa e^{-2v}\\
v(0)=\dot{v}(0)=0\end{cases}\,,

whose solution is

(41)

v(\tau)=\log\cos\left(\sqrt{\kappa}\tau\right)\,.

This shows that

(42)

\tau_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}<\frac{1}{2}\,.

Since from (

) we have

(43)

u\leq v(\tau)\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{D}\,,

we conclude that

blows up before
\tau=1/2
.
∎

Concerning the expanding FLRW models discussed in Section
1
, this result states that if the initial length of the rod is comparable to the cosmological scale then the rod is infinitely stretched by the Hubble flow.

4.
The constant curvature case

In the constant curvature case, where the FLRW model is the de Sitter universe with cosmological radius
R=1/\sqrt{K}
, there is a simple geometric interpretation of the blowup in Theorem
3.3
: the condition
K>\pi^{2}
is simply the condition that
R<1/\pi
, meaning that the diameter of the region bounded by the cosmological horizon (measured along a spacelike geodesic) is
\pi R<1
. Since we have set the rod’s length to
1

\mathscr{I^{+}}
(see Figure
2

\tau

If

\cosh\left(\alpha\sqrt{K}\Delta\tau\right)=\frac{\sin\left(\sqrt{K}\,\frac{1-\Delta}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\sqrt{K}\,\frac{2\delta+\Delta}{2}\right)}+\frac{\sqrt{K}\delta\cos\left(\sqrt{K}\,\frac{2\delta+\Delta}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\sqrt{K}\,\frac{1-\Delta}{2}\right)}\end{cases}\,,

and let us define the new variables

\begin{cases}x=\sqrt{K}\,\frac{2\delta+\Delta}{2}\\

From (

(68)

\begin{cases}\alpha=\frac{\sinh z\cos x}{\sin y}\\
\cosh z=\frac{\cos y}{\cos x}+\frac{\sqrt{K}\delta\cos x}{\sin y}\end{cases}\,.

Since

(69)

\sqrt{K}\delta=x-y+\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{K}}{2}\,,

\frac{\sqrt{K}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}+x-y-\sin y\left(\frac{\cosh z}{\cos x}-\frac{\cos y}{\cos^{2}x}\right)\,.

\delta>0\\

∎

Remark 4.3

It is interesting to note that the linearized version of the IBVP

and

5.
The tethered galaxy problem

We now consider the tethered galaxy problem, where the rod is used to connect two galaxies, modelled as point masses with rest mass
m>0
(note that this is not exactly the same problem as that discussed in
[
10
]
, where a tether initially keeps the galaxies at a fixed spatial distance and then is cut). This can be seen as the relativistic analogue of a system of two masses connected by a spring.

The proper acceleration of the galaxies is (see Appendix
A
)

(79)

A=-u^{\prime}e^{2u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\,,

while the force on the galaxy at
\lambda=1
is

(80)

F=p\,e^{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\left(e^{2u}-1\right)e^{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\,.

Therefore, at
\lambda=1
we obtain the nonlinear boundary condition

(81)

F=mA\Leftrightarrow u^{\prime}=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{m}\sinh u\,.

At
\lambda=0
the force is reversed, and so we obtain the same boundary condition but with a plus sign. If again we assume that the rod is initially unstretched and not moving, we are led to the following IBVP:

(82)

\begin{cases}-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime}=Ke^{-2u}\\
u(0,\lambda)=\dot{u}(0,\lambda)=0,\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
u^{\prime}(\tau,0)=\sigma\sinh u(\tau,0),\quad\,\,\,\,\tau\geq 0\\
u^{\prime}(\tau,1)=-\sigma\sinh u(\tau,1),\quad\tau\geq 0\end{cases}\,,

where
3
3

3

Note that the IBVP (
16
) studied in the previous sections corresponds to the limit of this IBVP when
m\to 0
, that is, when
\sigma\to+\infty
.

(83)

\sigma=\frac{\mu_{0}}{m}\,.

Consider the modified energy

(84)

H(\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{u}^{2}+u^{\prime 2}-Ke^{-2u}\right)d\lambda+2\sigma\Big[\cosh u(\tau,0)+\cosh u(\tau,1)\Big]\,.

Still under the assumption
\dot{K}\geq 0
, we have (recall (
18
))

\displaystyle\dot{H}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Big[2\dot{u}u^{\prime}\Big]_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\dot{K}e^{-2u}d\lambda+2\sigma\Big[\dot{u}(\tau,0)\sinh u(\tau,0)+\dot{u}(\tau,1)\sinh u(\tau,1)\Big]

(85)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\dot{K}e^{-2u}d\lambda\leq 0\,,

that is,
H(\tau)
is decreasing. This allows us to prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\dot{K}\geq 0
and either
K\leq 0
or
0\leq K\leq\kappa
, for sufficiently small
\kappa>0
, then the solution to the IBVP (
82
) is global in time towards the future and bounded.

Proof.

According to the blow-up alternative in Theorem
B.1
(see Appendix
B
), if
\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
remains bounded for
\tau>0
then the unique solution to the IBVP can be extended globally in time towards the future. Since

(86)

u(\tau,\lambda)=u(\tau,0)+\int_{0}^{\lambda}u^{\prime}(\tau,s)ds\,,

we have

(87)

\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq|u(\tau,0)|+\|u^{\prime}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\cosh u(\tau,0)+\|u^{\prime}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\,,

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
|x|<\cosh x
. The same inequality with
\cosh u(\tau,1)
replacing
\cosh u(\tau,0)
is obviously true, and so

(88)

\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big[\cosh u(\tau,0)+\cosh u(\tau,1)\Big]+\|u^{\prime}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\,.

Consequently, we have

(89)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(H(\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)+\left(H(\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Since the function

(90)

f(x)=\frac{1}{4\sigma}x+x^{\frac{1}{2}}

is clearly increasing for
x\geq 0
, we have

(91)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(H(0)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)+\left(H(0)+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

If
K\leq 0
, we have

(92)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}H(0)+H(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

and so
u
is bounded. If
0\leq K\leq\kappa
, we have

(93)

H(0)=-\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda+4\sigma\leq 4\sigma\,,

and so

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(4\sigma+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)+\left(4\sigma+\int_{0}^{1}Ke^{-2u}d\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(4\sigma+\kappa e^{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)+\left(4\sigma+\kappa e^{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Consider the function

(95)

g(x)=x-\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(4\sigma+\kappa e^{2x}\right)-\left(4\sigma+\kappa e^{2x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

which satisfies

(96)

g(0)=-\frac{1}{4\sigma}\left(4\sigma+\kappa\right)-\left(4\sigma+\kappa\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<0\,.

Given

(97)

x_{0}>2\sqrt{\sigma}+1\,,

we have

(98)

\lim_{\kappa\to 0}g(x_{0})=x_{0}-1-2\sqrt{\sigma}>0\,,

and so
g(x_{0})>0
for sufficiently small
\kappa>0
. Since from (
94

(99)

g(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}})\leq 0\,,

and given that
\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}=0
, we conclude that, for
\kappa>0
sufficiently small,
\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}<x_{0}
for
\tau>0
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Note that the result in Theorem
5.1
is weaker than the corresponding result in Theorem
3.2
. Physically, this difference arises because it is easier for the expansion to stretch the rod when there are masses attached to its endpoints, as the tidal forces on these masses add to the body forces stretching the rod.

Since the blow-up argument in Theorem
3.3
is completely independent of the boundary conditions, we also have the following result.

Theorem 5.3
.

If
\dot{K}\geq 0
and
K\geq\kappa>\pi^{2}
then the solution to the IBVP (
82
) blows up before
\tau=1/2
.

In the constant curvature case, we also have the following result:

Theorem 5.4
.

If
K>0
is constant then the solution to the IBVP (
82
) blows up for some
K<\pi^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
applies in this case if we can establish that
u(\tau,0)\leq 0
for
\tau\in[0,1]
. Now from the expression of the exact solution in region
\mathcal{D}
we have
L^{+}u\leq 0
in this region, and by integrating
L^{-}L^{+}u=-Ke^{-2u}
from the boundary of
\mathcal{D}
into the region
\mathcal{B}
we obtain
L^{+}u\leq 0
in this region. Therefore we have

(100)

\dot{u}(\tau,0)+\sigma\sinh u(\tau,0)=\dot{u}(\tau,0)+u^{\prime}(\tau,0)=L^{+}(\tau,0)\leq 0

for
\tau\in[0,1]
. Since
u(0,0)=0
, we conclude from the usual comparison theorem for ODEs that
u(\tau,0)\leq z(\tau)
, where
z(\tau)
is the solution to the ODE Cauchy problem

(101)

\begin{cases}\dot{z}=-\sigma\sinh z\\
z(0)=0\end{cases}\,,

that is,
z(\tau)\equiv 0
.
∎

In other words, the qualitative behavior of the tethered galaxy system in response to the cosmological expansion is exactly the same as that of the rod alone. Taken together, these two examples provide mathematically rigorous support for the widely held view that sufficiently small bound systems do not follow the Hubble flow, whereas larger systems may do so.

Appendix A
Geometry calculations

The metric (
3
) can be written as

(102)

ds^{2}=\left(\omega^{0}\right)^{2}+\left(\omega^{1}\right)^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad\omega^{0}=dt\,,\quad\omega^{1}=a(t)dx\,.

From

(103)

\begin{cases}d\omega^{0}=0=-\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}\wedge\omega^{1}\\
d\omega^{1}=\dot{a}dt\wedge dx=-\omega^{1}_{\,\,\,\,0}\wedge\omega^{0}\end{cases}

we obtain the connection form

(104)

\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=\omega^{1}_{\,\,\,\,0}=\dot{a}\,dx\,,

and consequently the curvature form

(105)

\Omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=d\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=\ddot{a}\,dt\wedge dx=\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}\,\omega^{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\,,

so that the Gauss curvature is

(106)

K=\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}\,.

The metric (
12
), on the other hand, can be written as

(107)

ds^{2}=\left(\omega^{0}\right)^{2}+\left(\omega^{1}\right)^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad\omega^{0}=e^{-u}d\tau\,,\quad\omega^{1}=e^{-u}d\lambda\,.

From

(108)

\begin{cases}d\omega^{0}=-u^{\prime}e^{-u}d\lambda\wedge d\tau=-\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}\wedge\omega^{1}\\
d\omega^{1}=-\dot{u}e^{-u}d\tau\wedge d\lambda=-\omega^{1}_{\,\,\,\,0}\wedge\omega^{0}\end{cases}

we obtain the connection form

(109)

\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=\omega^{1}_{\,\,\,\,0}=-u^{\prime}d\tau-\dot{u}\,d\lambda\,,

and consequently the curvature form

(110)

\Omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=d\omega^{0}_{\,\,\,\,1}=-u^{\prime\prime}d\lambda\wedge d\tau-\ddot{u}\,d\tau\wedge d\lambda=e^{2u}(-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime})\,\omega^{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\,,

so that the Gauss curvature is

(111)

K=e^{2u}(-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime})\,.

Finally, note that if

(112)

E_{0}=e^{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\,,\qquad E_{1}=e^{u}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}

is the orthonormal frame dual to
\{\omega^{0},\omega^{1}\}
then the covariant acceleration of the observers at rest with respect to the rod is

(113)

\nabla_{E_{0}}E_{0}=\omega^{1}_{\,\,\,\,0}(E_{0})E_{1}=-u^{\prime}e^{u}E_{1}\,.

Appendix B
Existence and uniqueness results

In this appendix we prove an existence and uniqueness result for classical solutions of the IBVPs considered in this work.

Theorem B.1
.

The IBVPs (
16
) and (
82
) admit unique
C^{2}
solutions on maximal time intervals
[0,T)
, where either
T=+\infty
or
\limsup_{t{\scriptscriptstyle\nearrow}T}\|u(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty
.

Proof.

We present only the proof for the more complicated case of the IBVP (
82
); the proof for the simpler IBVP (
16
) is a trivial adaptation.

Let us consider first the IBVP

(114)

\begin{cases}-\ddot{u}+u^{\prime\prime}=Ke^{-2u}\\
u(\tau_{0},\lambda)=f(\lambda),\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
\dot{u}(\tau_{0},\lambda)=g(\lambda),\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
u^{\prime}(\tau,0)=\sigma\sinh u(\tau,0),\quad\,\,\,\,\tau\in[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]\\
u^{\prime}(\tau,1)=-\sigma\sinh u(\tau,1),\quad\tau\in[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]\end{cases}\,,

where we assume that
\Delta\tau=\tau_{1}-\tau_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
and that
f\in C^{2}([0,1])
and
g\in C^{1}([0,1])
satisfy the compatibility conditions

(115)

\begin{cases}f^{\prime}(0)=\sigma\sinh f(0)\\
f^{\prime}(1)=-\sigma\sinh f(1)\end{cases}

and

(116)

\begin{cases}g^{\prime}(0)=\sigma g(0)\cosh f(0)\\
g^{\prime}(1)=-\sigma g(1)\cosh f(1)\end{cases}\,.

To solve this problem, we introduce the new variables

(117)

v=L^{+}u\,,\qquad w=L^{-}u\,,

where
L^{+}
and
L^{-}
are the vector fields defined in (
28
), and study the first order IBVP

(118)

\begin{cases}\dot{u}=\frac{1}{2}(v+w)\\
L^{-}v=-Ke^{-2u}\\
L^{+}w=-Ke^{-2u}\\
u(\tau_{0},\lambda)=f(\lambda),\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
v(\tau_{0},\lambda)=g(\lambda)+f^{\prime}(\lambda),\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
w(\tau_{0},\lambda)=g(\lambda)-f^{\prime}(\lambda),\quad\quad\lambda\in[0,1]\\
v(\tau,0)-w(\tau,0)=2\sigma\sinh u(\tau,0),\quad\,\,\,\,\tau\in[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]\\
w(\tau,1)-v(\tau,1)=2\sigma\sinh u(\tau,1),\quad\tau\in[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]\end{cases}\,,

which we write as the following system of integral equations:

(119)

\begin{cases}u(\tau,\lambda)=f(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\left[v(s,\lambda)+w(s,\lambda)\right]ds\\
v(\tau,\lambda)=g(\lambda+\tau-\tau_{0})+f^{\prime}(\lambda+\tau-\tau_{0})-\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}K(s,\lambda+\tau-s)e^{-2u(s,\lambda+\tau-s)}ds\,,\quad\tau+\lambda\leq 1+\tau_{0}\\

w(\tau,\lambda)=v_{L}(\tau,\lambda)-2\sigma\sinh u_{L}(\tau,\lambda)-\int_{\tau-\lambda}^{\tau}K(s,\lambda-\tau+s)e^{-2u(s,\lambda-\tau+s)}ds\,,\quad\tau-\lambda\geq\tau_{0}\\
u_{L}(\tau,\lambda)=f(0)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau-\lambda}\left[v(s,0)+w(s,0)\right]ds\\
u_{R}(\tau,\lambda)=f(1)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau+\lambda-1}\left[v(s,1)+w(s,1)\right]ds\\

is the time integral of its time derivative, and the next four equations give

and

by integrating the equation along the characteristics, starting either at the initial time or at the boundaries; the functions
