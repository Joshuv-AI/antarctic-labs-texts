Buddy Wire–Supported Distal Access Catheter Advancement Technique (B-DAT) for Difficult Vertebral Artery Access: A Technical Note
Authors: Kosuke Umeda, Hikaru Wakabayashi, Shoko Fujii, Satoru Takahashi, Kyohei Fujita, Hirotaka Sagawa, Sakyo Hirai, Kazutaka Sumita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndovascular treatment (EVT) of posterior circulation lesions can be technically challenging when access to the vertebral artery (VA) is difficult because of tortuosity, stenosis, or a narrow caliber. Stable advancement of the distal access catheter (DAC) is often required for safe and effective EVT; however, selective VA cannulation with a guiding catheter (GC) may increase the risk of vascular injury or compromise flow in an unfavorable anatomy. We developed a buddy wire–supported DAC advancement technique (B-DAT) to facilitate direct DAC advancement into the VA. We evaluated the feasibility and safety of B-DAT in contemporary posterior circulation EVT practice, building on the modified buddy wire concept.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive posterior circulation EVT cases performed at our hospital between April 2020 and September 2025, and identified cases in which B-DAT was used. In B-DAT, the GC is positioned in the subclavian artery and stabilized using a stiff buddy wire advanced into the ipsilateral brachial artery, enabling direct advancement of the DAC into the VA. The patient characteristics, VA anatomy, procedural details, technical success, and procedure-related complications were assessed.ResultsB-DAT was used in 10 of the 122 posterior circulation EVT cases. Indications included intracranial aneurysm embolization, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting, mechanical thrombectomy with adjunctive treatment, and transarterial embolization for arteriovenous malformations. Direct DAC advancement was successful in all cases, and all planned procedures were completed without procedure-related complications.ConclusionB-DAT is a feasible and practical approach for facilitating direct DAC advancement in cases of difficult VA access during posterior circulation EVT.

Introduction

In recent years, endovascular treatment (EVT) has become the mainstay of treatment for a broad spectrum of posterior circulation lesions, including intracranial aneurysms, vertebrobasilar large-vessel occlusion, and arteriovenous malformations (AVMs).1–4) Although the increasing use of distal access catheters (DACs) has improved the feasibility and safety of EVT, safe and stable guiding catheter (GC) placement remains challenging in a subset of posterior circulation cases owing to anatomical variations in the vertebral artery (VA), such as tortuosity, stenosis, and a narrow caliber. In such cases, repeated or forceful VA cannulation may increase the risk of dissection, flow stagnation, vasospasm, and thromboembolism, complicating lesion access.

To overcome these access-related difficulties, Nishino et al. described a modified buddy wire (MBW) technique for posterior circulation aneurysms, stabilizing the GC in the subclavian artery using a stiff buddy wire advanced into the brachial artery to improve proximal stability.5) In their workflow, a microcatheter was directly introduced through the GC without using a DAC, and coil embolization was successfully completed in all cases; however, they noted potential limitations in supporting stiffer devices such as balloons or stent-mounted catheters. In the current EVT practice, DACs are commonly used to enhance distal support; thus, the performance of the MBW technique in this setting has not been fully established.

In this technical note, we adapted the MBW concept to direct DAC advancement into the challenging VA anatomy, which we refer to as the buddy wire–supported DAC advancement technique (B-DAT), and evaluated the feasibility and safety of B-DAT in posterior circulation EVT.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a single-center retrospective review of consecutive cases of posterior circulation EVT performed between April 2020 and September 2025. Among these, we included all cases in which B-DAT was used when selective VA cannulation with a GC was considered difficult or risky based on the VA anatomy. Clinical information and procedural details were obtained from electronic medical records and procedural reports. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Science Tokyo (approval number: M2020-102-01) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting followed the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Data collection and assessment

For each case, we collected the patient demographics (age and sex), diagnosis, procedure, accessed VA side, reasons for using B-DAT, VA origin diameter, and details of the access system, including the GC or guiding sheath (GS), DAC, and buddy wire. When used, details of the adjunctive devices (stents and balloons) were also recorded. The reasons for using B-DAT included tortuosity, stenosis, and a narrow caliber of the target VA. These categories were defined based on the angiographic appearance of the VA origin, where stable placement of the planned GC in the VA was considered difficult. We assessed the technical success and procedure-related complications. Technical success was defined as the successful completion of the planned procedure. Procedure-related complications were recorded as intraprocedural adverse events, regardless of whether they were attributable to B-DAT.

B-DAT

B-DAT is illustrated in Fig. 1. In this technique, the GC is positioned within the subclavian artery and is not selectively advanced into the VA. We primarily used a stiff 0.014-inch peripheral guidewire (Agosal; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) and, in 2 cases, a 0.025–0.035-inch guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). The buddy wire was advanced into the ipsilateral brachial artery, ideally beyond the elbow joint into the forearm, to form a stable reversed U-shaped configuration. Its recoil stabilized the GC against the subclavian artery wall, thereby providing proximal support.

Schematic of B-DAT. In B-DAT, the GC is positioned in the subclavian artery without selective cannulation of the VA. A stiff 0.014-inch buddy wire is advanced through the GC into the ipsilateral brachial artery and maintained to provide proximal support and stabilize the GC. With the proximal system stabilized, a DAC is advanced into the VA to obtain distal support for subsequent device navigation and treatment.

B-DAT, buddy wire–supported distal access catheter advancement technique; DAC, distal access catheter; GC, guiding catheter; VA, vertebral artery

A DAC was then introduced directly into the VA using a microcatheter and microguidewire and positioned so as not to compromise the VA flow to obtain sufficient distal support for subsequent device navigation and treatment delivery. The GC and DAC were selected at the operator’s discretion. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with appropriate antiplatelet therapy and intraprocedural anticoagulation according to the institutional protocol.

Results

During the study period, B-DAT was applied in 10 of the 122 posterior circulation EVT cases. The patient characteristics and procedural details are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 73 years, and 6 patients (60%) were female. The indications were coil embolization for intracranial aneurysms (6 cases, 5 unruptured and 1 ruptured), symptomatic atherosclerotic stenosis treated with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting (PTAS) (2 cases), acute basilar artery (BA) occlusion treated with mechanical thrombectomy and PTAS (1 case), and transarterial embolization for a ruptured AVM (1 case). In all cases, B-DAT was planned based on preprocedural assessment and was not used as a bailout maneuver during the procedure. The angiographic findings at the origin of the VA in all cases are summarized in Fig. 2. The left VA was assessed in 8 cases (80%). The reasons for using B-DAT were tortuosity in 7 cases, stenosis in 4 cases, and a narrow caliber in 2 cases (some cases had more than 1 contributing factor). The median VA origin diameter was 2.65 mm (range, 1.5–3.5 mm). A stiff 0.014-inch guidewire (Agosal; Asahi Intecc) was used in 8 cases, whereas a 0.025–0.035-inch guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo) was used in 2 cases. The DACs used were Vecta 71 (Stryker, Fremont, CA, USA) in 4 cases, Catalyst 5 (Stryker) in 3 cases, TACTICS (Technocrat Corporation, Aichi, Japan) in 2 cases, and GuidePost (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) in 1 case. Technical success was achieved in all the cases. No procedure-related complications occurred.

A 9-Fr, 40-cm sheath was inserted into the right femoral artery, and a 7-Fr GS (Shuttle; Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was positioned in the left subclavian artery. A buddy wire (Agosal) was advanced into the distal left brachial artery (Fig. 3A and 3B). The DAC (Vecta 71) was then advanced to the V4 segment (Fig. 3C). A balloon catheter (Scepter XC; Terumo) was first navigated and positioned near the aneurysm neck on standby in case of intraoperative rupture. Subsequently, a microcatheter (Excelsior SL-10; Stryker) was advanced into the aneurysm sac and coil embolization was performed. Twenty coils were deployed to achieve complete occlusion of the aneurysm. The DAC remained stable throughout the procedure (Fig. 3D), and no intraprocedural complications were observed.

Discussion

In this technical note, we demonstrated that B-DAT is feasible and useful for posterior circulation EVT in current practice, where DACs are commonly used. In our series, all planned procedures were completed with successful DAC navigation directly into the VA using B-DAT, and no procedure-related complications were observed. These findings suggest that this approach is a practical option when stable VA cannulation with a GC is considered difficult or risky and is often encountered in posterior circulation EVT.

Anatomical variation of the VA is common and can directly affect access to the posterior circulation. VA caliber laterality is frequent: both VAs are similar in 61.5% of cases; the left VA is dominant in 21.2%, and the right VA is dominant in 17.3%; VA hypoplasia has been reported in the left (14.4%), right (20.2%), and bilateral (4.8%).6) In addition, the left VA arises directly from the aortic arch in approximately 5% of individuals.6–8) Cervical VA tortuosity is also common; a systematic review estimated tortuosity in the V1–V2 segments to be 26.6%.7) Importantly, VA tortuosity is not just a technical issue; in acute vertebrobasilar occlusion treated with mechanical thrombectomy, greater VA tortuosity is associated with worse functional outcomes.9,10) Taken together, these variations can make stable GC placement in the VA challenging and should be recognized as a common cause of difficult posterior circulation access.

Nishino et al. first reported the MBW technique in 3 cases of posterior circulation aneurysms.5) In their method, a GC was kept in the subclavian artery, and a stiff 0.018-inch buddy wire was advanced sufficiently distally into the brachial artery to form a reversed U-shape; its recoil helped stabilize the GC against the subclavian artery wall. A microcatheter was then introduced into the VA directly and positioned within the aneurysm, and coil embolization was completed in all cases; however, the authors noted that support for stiffer devices, such as balloons or stent-mounted catheters, was not established. In addition, their original workflow did not use a DAC, which was reasonable, given that DAC use was not routine at that time. In contrast, in our series, a DAC could be advanced directly into the VA, even in a challenging anatomy, without procedure-related complications. This can be attributed to 2 factors. First, advances in DAC design—graded shafts, thin-walled large-bore lumens, and hydrophilic coatings—have improved conformability and trackability, helping reduce vessel wall stress in tortuous or narrow-caliber VAs while still accommodating larger-bore DACs. Second, to preserve system compatibility within the GC for the direct delivery of a large-bore DAC and to minimize interference by the buddy wire, we primarily used a stiff 0.014-inch peripheral guidewire (Agosal; Asahi Intecc), which is smaller than the 0.018-inch wire used in the original MBW method; nevertheless, it provided sufficient proximal support in our cases, including those requiring large-bore DACs such as the Vecta 71 (Stryker), with no clinically relevant interference observed in any device combination used in this series. A thicker guidewire was used in 2 cases early in our experience. Collectively, B-DAT represents an extension of the originally reported MBW concept to the current “DAC era.”

B-DAT may reduce access-related risks, such as arterial dissection, flow stagnation, vasospasm, and thromboembolic events, and offers an alternative strategy when posterior circulation access is difficult, compared with conventional selective VA cannulation with a GC. A comparison of the options is presented in Table 2. Snare-assisted stabilization and pull-through techniques,11,12) both of which require additional brachial or radial access, can provide strong proximal support by fixing or tensioning the GC in the subclavian artery; however, they increase procedural invasiveness and complexity. The brachial and radial approaches can also be useful when femoral access is not feasible.13,14) Compared with the femoral approach, they are generally less invasive and have a lower risk of puncture-site complications15); however, the smaller caliber of the access arteries often limits the system profile. Additionally, catheterization can be challenging with a sharp subclavian-to-VA takeoff angle.16,17) Recent transradial strategies, such as direct DAC navigation without a GC and radial-specific guide catheters, including the Rist radial access system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), have been reported,16,18,19) but their performance and generalizability have not been fully established. Direct cervical VA puncture, a more invasive bailout option, has been reported20) but is not widely applicable. In this context, B-DAT has several practical advantages. While B-DAT was planned preprocedurally in all cases in the present series, it is a simple maneuver that can be implemented quickly without extra puncture, applied to routine systems, and even adopted intraprocedurally when standard VA cannulation is judged to be difficult or risky—without any additional preparation. Although no single quantitative threshold exists for selecting B-DAT, this flexibility may represent a practical advantage of the technique.

This study had some limitations. This was a single-center retrospective study with a small number of cases and mixed indications and devices; cases in which B-DAT was considered but not performed could not be systematically identified. There was no control group and the assessment was descriptive. In addition, B-DAT is not applicable to all patients, such as those with unfavorable femoral access conditions or VA origin anatomy, including those with a direct origin from the aortic arch. Larger studies are required to confirm the role of B-DAT and compare it with other support techniques.

Conclusion

B-DAT is a simple and useful method for enhancing the proximal support for direct DAC advancement into the VA when selective VA cannulation with a GC is considered difficult or risky. Given that challenging VA anatomy is often encountered, B-DAT should be considered a practical option for posterior circulation EVT.
