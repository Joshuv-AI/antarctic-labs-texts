Toward more practical hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: clinical implications of fibrosis-based risk stratification
Authors: Hae Lim Lee, Minjong Lee
Published: 2026

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents a heterogeneous substrate for hepatocarcinogenesis. Although fibrosis remains the major determinant of liver-related outcomes, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk in MASLD is further modified by metabolic dysfunction, chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility, which may act independently and synergistically (1). Moreover, despite the high prevalence of MASLD, only a minority of non-cirrhotic patients progress to HCC, making it particularly challenging to identify those at sufficient risk to benefit from surveillance (2).

Current guidelines stratify HCC surveillance recommendations in MASLD broadly according to fibrosis stage. Surveillance is not recommended for patients with F0–F2 fibrosis, given the low annual HCC incidence and uncertain cost-effectiveness. Of note, surveillance may be considered on an individual basis for patients with F3 according to the 2024 European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guideline, though evidence remains limited (3). By contrast, surveillance is recommended for patients with MASLD-related cirrhosis, consistent with both American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidance and EASL guideline (3,4). However, it is limited how non-invasive fibrosis tests should be translated into practical surveillance thresholds.

The study by Lai et al. addressed this gap by repurposing the fibrosis-oriented non-invasive testing pathway recommended in current MASLD guidelines to inform HCC surveillance (5). In this multinational study of 12,950 patients with a median follow-up of 47.4 months, patients with a low fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) score had a very low HCC risk, with an annual incidence of 0.07% and a 5-year cumulative incidence of 0.3%, supporting interval reassessment rather than immediate further work-up. At the other end of the spectrum, FIB-4 ≥3.25 identified a subgroup with an annual HCC incidence exceeding 1.0%. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) further refined this framework: as a stand-alone test, LSM ≥20 kPa crossed the 1% annual incidence threshold, while in a two-step approach restricted to patients with increased FIB-4, LSM ≥10 kPa identified a risk-enriched subgroup with an annual HCC incidence of ≥0.2%, and LSM ≥15 kPa identified a subgroup in whom regular HCC surveillance may be justified. Taken together, this approach provides a clinically accessible tool for surveillance-oriented risk stratification in MASLD.

However, a surveillance strategy centered on fibrosis markers may not fully capture the spectrum of MASLD-related HCC risk. Tan et al. reported that 13% of patients with MASLD-related HCC overall and 26% of those without known cirrhosis had a low FIB-4 at the time of HCC diagnosis (6). Additionally, Rabbat et al. reported that F3–F4 fibrosis was present in 6.4% of patients with low FIB-4 and LSM, 13.7% of those with high FIB-4 and low LSM, and 30.6% of those with low FIB-4 and high LSM, highlighting potential discordance between non-invasive markers in identifying advanced fibrosis (7). Together, these findings suggest that FIB-4/LSM-based thresholds alone may be insufficient for identifying all patients at clinically relevant risk.

Several risk prediction models have attempted to address this issue. The Agile 3+ and Agile 4 scores were originally developed to identify advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, respectively, by combining LSM with routine clinical and laboratory variables. A subsequent study suggested that single or serial Agile scores accurately predicted liver-related events in MASLD, although their prognostic utility appears stronger for overall liver-related events than for HCC alone (8). The recently proposed MASLD-HCC score, in contrast, incorporates age, sex, platelet count, obesity, and diabetes without directly incorporating FIB-4 or LSM, thereby highlighting the importance of metabolic and host factors in HCC risk estimation. The high-risk group defined by the MASLD-HCC score had an annual HCC incidence of 0.24% (9).

Furthermore, fibrosis should not be viewed as a static trait, as it may regress with lifestyle modification or progress over time. In the ESSENCE trial, 22.4% of patients in the placebo group showed fibrosis improvement over 72 weeks of follow-up (10). Similarly, in a large study of 5,080,410 patients with steatotic liver disease, improvement in MASLD, defined as the presence of MASLD at the first health screening and its absence at the second screening, was associated with a significantly lower incidence of HCC compared with persistent MASLD (11). These observations highlight the dynamic nature of steatosis and fibrosis and, in turn, the inherent limitation of risk models that rely solely on variables measured at a single baseline time point, which may not adequately capture longitudinal changes in disease activity and fibrosis burden.

The study by Lai et al. also underscores subgroup-specific differences in surveillance eligibility. Across all FIB-4 and LSM strata, female patients had annual HCC incidence rates below the conventional surveillance threshold, typically regarded as 1.0–1.5% per year (3,12). Consistent with this observation, previous studies have suggested that women have a lower overall risk of HCC than men across the MASLD spectrum (13). However, this should not be interpreted as indicating uniformly negligible risk in women, because hepatocarcinogenesis in female patients with MASLD may still be driven by factors beyond fibrosis alone (14). Similar caution is needed in patients with obesity. In this subgroup, LSM may not always reflect fibrosis burden alone, because obesity can influence both the measurement itself and the biological risk of hepatocarcinogenesis (9,15).

A remaining challenge is the reliable implementation of fibrosis-based risk stratification in real-world settings beyond hepatology clinics. In primary care and diabetes clinics, FIB-4 is not routinely calculated, and access to LSM remains limited. Addressing this implementation gap will require simple, scalable, and structured interdisciplinary referral pathways that connect primary care providers and diabetologists with hepatologists.
