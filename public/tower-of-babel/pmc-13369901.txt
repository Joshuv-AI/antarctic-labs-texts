How semantic context and temporal dynamics affect egocentric and allocentric spatial representations
Authors: Gennaro Ruggiero, Francesco Ruotolo, Michela Possenti, Renato Orti, Francesca D’Olimpio, Tina Iachini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPouring water from a bottle into a glass requires representing their relative spatial positions (spatial information) and knowing that such objects are found together, or one by one (temporal dynamics), in typical contexts, such as a kitchen (semantic information).While spatial information is encoded using egocentric (body-centered) or allocentric (object-centered) reference frames, semantic knowledge is organized in context frames, which help predict object–context associations. This study investigates whether daily-like semantic contexts and temporal dynamics affect egocentric and allocentric spatial representations. Participants were immersed in semantically meaningless (abstract) or meaningful (bathroom, kitchen) virtual environments. They memorized triads of objects presented either simultaneously (static condition) or sequentially (dynamic condition) and then provided egocentric or allocentric spatial judgments. Results revealed an improvement of allocentric accuracy in meaningful rather than meaningless environments, whereas no differences emerged for egocentric spatial judgments. Crucially, this semantic benefit for allocentric encoding emerged only in the dynamic condition, where spatial information had to be integrated over time. These findings show that semantic context selectively facilitates allocentric spatial representations, particularly when perceptual input unfolds dynamically. The results support the interaction between semantic and spatial memory to shape how we perceive the surrounding world.Keywords: Egocentric, Allocentric, Frames of reference, Contextual semantic knowledge, Spatial-semantic bindingSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Everyday actions rely on the combined use of at least two types of information: knowledge about the typical context in which objects are usually found (semantic information) and knowledge about the location of these objects in space (spatial information). For example, pouring water from a bottle into a glass requires not only understanding that both objects are commonly found in a kitchen, but also representing the position of the bottle relative to one’s hand or to the glass.

Spatial information can be encoded according to two distinct reference frames: an egocentric frame, which is body-centered and encodes locations relative to the observer (e.g., “the bottle is to my left”), and an allocentric frame, which is object-centered and encodes spatial relations among elements in the scene (e.g., “the glass is to the right of the bottle”)1–3. Egocentric and allocentric representations cooperate in many daily functions4–6. However, egocentric processing, primarily supported by fronto-parietal regions, plays a central role in guiding motor actions7, whereas allocentric processing, mainly linked to medio-temporal and posterior medial brain structures, gives an important contribution to recognition and scene representation8–15. Several studies have shown a consistent advantage of egocentric over allocentric encoding16–22. This is often attributed to the egocentric frame being the body’s primary interface with the environment, directly supporting real-time interaction23,24. Allocentric encoding, in contrast, requires detaching from a self-centered perspective and constructing spatial relations among external elements, resulting in an effortful process that demands greater cognitive resources25,6.

However, most of this evidence derives from experiments using abstract or geometric stimuli (e.g., bars, dots) in simplified laboratory settings (e.g.26, which lack the semantic richness of real-life contexts. Recent theories of scene perception have emphasized how semantic and spatial regularities in real-world environments guide perception and memory27–30. Specifically, the concept of scene grammar refers to our knowledge of how objects are typically arranged in specific environments (e.g., sink near stove in a kitchen) and this knowledge allows us to predict which objects should appear and where in the context, thereby facilitating recognition and spatial encoding31,32. These contextual expectations reduce ambiguity and allow the brain to encode scenes more efficiently.

So far, previous studies have shown that spatial memory is enhanced when the position of objects conforms to familiar scene arrangements33, and that semantic predictions about object identity and location are activated at early stages of scene perception27,34. In line with this, a growing body of research points to a close link between semantic and spatial memory systems, supported by shared structures in the medial temporal lobe, including the hippocampus and parahippocampal cortex35–37. Importantly, Ruotolo and colleagues38 showed that semantic relations between objects selectively modulate the engagement of neural systems underlying spatial representations, providing neurofunctional evidence for an interaction between semantic structure and spatial coding.

From a behavioral perspective, Karimpur and colleagues39 showed that reaching movements were biased toward landmark objects that were both spatially shifted and semantically related to the target, suggesting that semantic congruency enhances allocentric processing. However, this paradigm did not embed objects within full environmental contexts, limiting its generalizability to everyday settings. More recently, Baltaretu and colleagues40 showed that, in ecological virtual scenes, structurally stable anchor objects implicitly guide the allocentric encoding of local objects. However, their paradigm did not include a direct comparison between egocentric and allocentric reference frames.

Beyond this limitation, an additional and critical aspect of everyday spatial experience concerns the temporal structure of object encounters. Indeed, everyday objects may be found all together (static condition) or one at a time (dynamic condition). Dynamic situations are known to increase memory demands41,42, but they may also activate predictive mechanisms. For instance, seeing a sink in a bathroom may prompt the expectation of subsequently encountering a toothbrush or soap. These anticipatory processes, favored by context-based schemas27,29,33, might affect the step-by-step construction of spatial representations.

Despite the relevance of these mechanisms, no study has yet systematically investigated how the semantic context and the temporal structure of environmental stimuli influence egocentric and allocentric spatial representations. Addressing this gap is crucial for understanding how spatial cognition operates in real-world environments.

To this aim, a modified version of the Ego-Allo Task16 was developed using an immersive virtual reality (IVR) setup. The task involved triads of everyday objects presented in meaningful contexts (e.g., an oil bottle in a kitchen, a toothbrush-holder in a bathroom) or triads of geometric objects in a meaningless context (e.g., a cube in an empty room). Participants were asked to memorize each triad and then to provide judgements of relative distances using an egocentric (i.e., identifying the object closest to themselves) or allocentric (i.e., identifying the object closest to another object in the triad) reference frame. The Ego-Allo Task has previously been used successfully to distinguish egocentric and allocentric spatial representations across various populations, including healthy adults and older individuals19–21, as well as clinical populations with neurological43 and sensory deficits44,45. Additionally, in the present study, triads were presented under two temporal conditions: a Static condition, in which all three objects appeared simultaneously, and a Dynamic condition, in which objects were presented one after the other.

According to the literature reviewed above, if semantic information enhances spatial memory, we expected better spatial performance in meaningful contexts compared to a semantically meaningless (abstract) environment. More specifically, given the stronger reliance of allocentric processing on relational and context-based encoding, this facilitation was expected to be more pronounced for allocentric than for egocentric spatial representations.

Crucially, because sequential presentation may engage predictive mechanisms over time, we hypothesized that allocentric facilitation would emerge primarily under dynamic presentation, when spatial information must be integrated step-by-step within a coherent semantic context.

Finally, although two distinct meaningful environments (Kitchen and Bathroom) were included, we did not formulate a priori hypotheses about differences between them. Both contexts were designed to provide semantically coherent and ecologically valid object–context associations; therefore, we expected the semantic facilitation effect to generalize across meaningful environments rather than to be context-specific.

Methods

Participants

The sample size was determined through an a priori power analysis with the G*Power software, version 3.1.9.746, considering the following values: Cohen’s effect size d = 0.42 (based on a previous study of Ruotolo and colleagues21, α = 0.05, Power (1 − β) = 0.90, N. of groups = 1. The minimum sample size to reach an actual power of 0.90 was 39.

A total of forty healthy participants aged 20–34 years old (19 males, Mage = 25.33; SDage = 3.01) were recruited. All participants were right-handed, as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory47(EHI score > 0.5), had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and did not report any neurological, motor or sensory disorders. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The procedures were conducted in accordance with the requirements of the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki and of the local Ethics Committee that approved the study (34\2024).

Virtual environments and stimuli

Three virtual environments were created by means of the free 3D modelling software ‘’SketchUp Make’’ (Trimble, USA), consisting in a meaningless empty room (henceforth, “Abstract”) and in two meaningful contexts (namely, “Bathroom” and “Kitchen”). The three virtual environments measured 6 × 4 × 3 m. A 120 × 100 × 80 cm table was placed in the centre of each room. The participant’s main view was located approximately 0.3 m from the edge of the table. In Fig. 1a reproduction of the three virtual environments is shown (Fig. 1A–C).

Concerning the stimuli, a virtual version of the Ego-Allo Task16 was devised and administered to the participants using the Unity graphic engine software (version 2022.3.21f1, Unity Technologies; https://unity.com). For each room, six 3D semantically congruent objects were selected and modelled by means of the same software. Specifically, for the Abstract room, six geometrical 3D objects (i.e., cone, cube, sphere, cylinder, pyramid and parallelepiped) were created. Similarly, for Bathroom and Kitchen, six 3D daily-used objects per room (i.e., plunger, napkin box, toothbrush-holder, rubber duck, hairspray, perfume for the Bathroom; saltshaker, oil bottle, glass, mug, kitchen roll, milk box for the Kitchen) were selected and created. All the objects were created with a small (6 × 6 cm) or large (8 × 8 cm) base. In Fig. 2 an example of the triads in their relative contexts is shown (Fig. 2A–C).

Stimuli: the figure depicts the triads of objects presented in their typical context. (A) The Abstract room with 3D geometrical objects; (B) the Bathroom with a napkin box, a perfume and a plunger; (C) the Kitchen with a saltshaker, a glass and a milk box. The daily-used objects were selected via a pilot study in which each object was evaluated based on six parameters: (i) Familiarity (“On a scale from 0 to 6 where 0 indicates ‘not at all familiar’ and 6 indicates ‘very familiar’, how familiar is this object to you?”); (ii) Manipulation (“On a scale from 0 to 6 where 0 indicates ‘not manipulable’ and 6 indicates ‘manipulable’, how manipulable do you consider this object to be?”); (iii) Kitchen-relatedness (“On a scale from 0 to 6 where 0 indicates ‘not at all likely’ and 6 indicates ‘very likely’, how likely do you think it is to find this object in a kitchen?”); (iv) Bathroom-relatedness (“On a scale from 0 to 6 where 0 indicates ‘not at all likely’ and 6 indicates ‘very likely’, how likely do you think it is to find this object in a bathroom?”); (v) Visual complexity (“On a scale from 0 to 6 where 0 indicates ‘not very complex’ and 6 indicates ‘very complex’, how visually complex do you consider this object to be?”) and (vi) Object recognition (“Write down the name of the object”, 1 = recognized, 0 = not recognized). Finally, based on the results of the pilot study, six objects were selected for each room. All the selected objects were easily recognisable, familiar, manipulable, kitchen- or bathroom-related and low-visually-complex (see Appendix 1 for details).

The selected objects were then arranged in thirty-six triads according to the following criteria: (i) each triad was placed in correspondence of the participants’ midsagittal plane; (ii) inter-object distances were clearly perceivable; (iii) the amount of metric difficulty was the same for all judgements. For instance, in the Abstract condition, if a triad consisted in a pyramid (allocentric target), a cone and a parallelepiped, in which cone and parallelepiped were the objects nearest to the participants, metric distances could be the following: pyramid-cone = 21 cm, pyramid-parallelepiped = 13 cm, cone-participant = 15 cm and parallelepiped-participant = 7 cm. In this way, the metric difference for the egocentric judgement (15 − 7 = 8 cm) was equal to the metric difference for the allocentric judgement (21 − 13 = 8 cm). Setting

The experiment took place in a soundproof room of the Laboratory of Cognitive Science and Immersive Virtual Reality (CS-IVR) at the University of Campania “L. Vanvitelli”. The virtual reality experimental setup consisted of an HTC Vive VR Pro (HTC Corporation, Taoyuan City, Taiwan) head mounted display (HMD) equipped with two Organic Light Emitting Diode displays (OLED) for stereoscopic depth – resolution of 1440 × 1600 pixel per eye (global resolution: 2880 × 1600), 110° field of view, 90 Hz refresh rate, two SteamVR base stations (Lighthouse) for 6 degrees of freedom (6-DOF). Additionally, the relative HTC VR Vive Controller was used by participants to provide motor responses.

Procedure

Training phase

First, participants were given written instructions, which were then revised orally by the experimenter. This was followed by a naming phase, in which all objects used during the experiment were presented one by one, and participants were asked to name them aloud. If participants missed the name of an object, it was provided by the experimenter. To ensure that participants recognized the objects, their intended use and context were required. Then, the experimenter helped participants to wear the HMD and to grasp the HTC VR Vive Controller with their dominant hand. Subsequently, participants were immersed in one of the three virtual environments and then a training phase (5 min) began to allow participants to familiarize themselves with the task. When participants reported to be confident with the task, the experimental session began.

Familiarization phase

The experimental session started with a familiarization phase (30 s). In this phase, participants were encouraged to look around with explorative movements of the head and identify the room in which they were located. Based on the condition, participants could be immersed in the Abstract room, in the Kitchen or in the Bathroom. Participants familiarized with each room only one time. After the end of the familiarization phase, the learning phase began.

Learning phase

In this phase, triads of objects were presented to participants and they were asked to memorize them and their positions as accurately as possible. Based on the condition, objects could appear in two different ways. Specifically, in the Static condition, the objects of the triad appeared simultaneously, as in the original version of the Ego-Allo task16. On the contrary, to better reflect everyday situations in which objects can be encountered one after another (e.g., a waiter setting the table for guests), a variation in the Dynamic condition was introduced. In this case, objects were placed on the table one at a time by a virtual human. The amount of time for the learning phase was the same in both conditions: in the Static condition, objects were presented simultaneously for 2.4 s, whereas in the Dynamic condition, objects were presented sequentially, with each appearing for 0.8 s and disappearing before the onset of the next one, resulting in a total presentation of 2.4 s (0.8 s × 3). Thus, during the Dynamic condition the three objects were never visible simultaneously. In the Dynamic condition, each object on which the spatial judgement was required appeared as first, second or third object in an equal number of times. After a delay of 5 s, the testing phase began (see Fig. 4 for a schematic representation of the experimental flow).

The figure shows an example of trial in the Static (A) and Dynamic (B) conditions. Each trial started with the presentation of the triad: in the Static condition (A), all the objects of the triads appeared simultaneously on a table, whereas in the Dynamic condition (B), the objects were placed one at a time by a virtual human. Then, after a 5 s delay, one spatial judgement was required by means of a question in a short form. The question consisted of two subsequent images, each lasting 0.5 s. Finally, participants provided a spatial judgement through a motor response within a free time window.

Testing phase

Each triad was presented in a random order, and for each trial participants were asked to provide a single spatial judgement based on either an egocentric or an allocentric reference frame. An example of egocentric question was: “Was object X the closest to you?”, whereas an example of an allocentric question was: “Was object X the closest to object Y?”. After a fixation cross lasting 0.5 s, the question appeared in a short form, with two subsequent images each lasting 0.5 s. In the case of an egocentric judgement, an image of a silhouetted man was presented first, followed by an image of the object to be judged. Conversely, for allocentric judgement, an image of the target object appeared first, followed by the image of the object on which the judgement was to be provided. Participants provided a binary response (“Yes” or “No”), pressing the corresponding buttons of the HTC Vive Controller within a free time window. The correspondence between the response and the buttons of HTC Vive Controller was counterbalanced across the participants.

A within-subjects study was designed, so all participants were assigned to all experimental conditions. A block design was employed. For each condition (i.e., Static and Dynamic), three blocks were presented, in each of which participants were immersed in one of the three virtual rooms (i.e., Abstract room, Kitchen or Bathroom). Each block consisted of twelve trials, half requiring an egocentric and half an allocentric spatial judgement, for a total of thirty-six trials presented in a random order in each of the Static and Dynamic conditions. Each trial lasted about 10 s, for a total of around 5 min per block. The whole experimental session lasted approximately 30 min. The order of presentation of static and dynamic conditions and the rooms was counterbalanced across the participants. Accuracy (0–1) was collected as a behavioral measure.

Data analysis

Because the Static and Dynamic conditions differed in their mode of stimulus presentation (simultaneous vs. sequential) and in the cognitive demands required for spatial encoding, we conducted two separate Repeated Measures ANOVAs (one for the Static condition and one for the Dynamic condition). Each analysis included Room (Abstract, Bathroom, Kitchen) as a three-level within-subjects factor and Frame of Reference (FoR: Egocentric, Allocentric) as a two-level within-subjects factor, with mean accuracy as the dependent variable. Mean accuracy was computed as the proportion of correct responses (0 = incorrect, 1 = correct). For the separate condition-specific analyses, accuracy was calculated by dividing the number of correct responses by 6 (maximum score per FoR × Room combination).

To directly examine differences between the two temporal conditions, an additional Repeated Measures ANOVA was performed including Condition (Static, Dynamic) and Frame of Reference (Egocentric, Allocentric) as within-subjects factors. In this case, mean accuracy was calculated by dividing the number of correct responses by 18 (maximum score per Condition × FoR combination).

For completeness and transparency, a combined 2 × 2 × 3 Repeated Measures ANOVA (Condition × FoR × Room) is also reported in Appendix 2.

In all the above analyses, the Tukey HSD test was used for post hoc comparisons and the magnitude of effect size was expressed by η2p.

Discussion

This study examined how the semantic context of everyday environments modulates the egocentric and allocentric representations of objects’ position, and whether this effect interacts with the temporal structure of the task. Using an immersive virtual reality task, participants judged relative distances between objects embedded in meaningful (Kitchen, Bathroom) or meaningless (Abstract) scenes, either using an egocentric (self-centered) or allocentric (object-centered) reference frame. Critically, the triads of objects were presented either simultaneously (Static condition) or sequentially (Dynamic condition), allowing us to assess how semantic context and temporal dynamics affect spatial memory.

As predicted, results revealed a clear difference between the two presentation modes. In the Static condition, participants were significantly more accurate when making egocentric than allocentric spatial judegments, regardless of the context. This is consistent with previous research demonstrating the primacy of egocentric over allocentric coding23,24. Contrary to our expectations, spatial performances did not differ significantly between meaningless and meaningful environments. One possible explanation is that being objects and their spatial relationships visible at once, participants focused directly on the spatial layout itself, making the surrounding context less relevant. In other words, the availability of all spatial information at once may have reduced the need to engage with contextual cues, which remained in the background (see48,49.

Interestingly, the Dynamic condition revealed a significant interaction between spatial reference frames and semantic contexts, with a significant improvement in allocentric performance in meaningful environments. This finding confirms our central hypothesis that context-induced semantic information affects spatial memory, with an evident improvement in allocentric representations, which rely on scene-level integration and context-driven relational binding29–32,37–39. The fact that allocentric improvement occurred only under dynamic conditions suggests that the underlying mechanism is anticipatory in nature. In fact, the semantic context was more useful when the information was presented sequentially and participants had to construct the spatial configuration step-by-step. In this case, the context became a useful support for relational encoding, activating expectations about the identity and position of objects that might appear later. Thus, unlike the Static condition, the context played an active functional role in guiding spatial encoding. We interpret this in terms of predictive semantic processing: as objects appeared one by one, participants in meaningful contexts could use scene grammar and prior expectations27,30 to anticipate spatial relationships and chunk information efficiently50.

However, it should be noted that the Dynamic condition was more difficult overall. In this condition, spatial information had to be maintained and integrated over time, rather than simply maintained, thus requiring higher cognitive resources (e.g.51–53. Within this more demanding setting, the predictive mechanisms triggered by the semantic context may have supported allocentric spatial representations. By contrast, the reduced egocentric accuracy in the Dynamic compared to Static condition may reflect the vulnerability of body-centered representations to temporal decay and updating costs.

The fact that the allocentric facilitation did not emerge in the Abstract environment indicates that sequential presentation alone does not favor allocentric representations; rather, it is the semantic meaning of the context that plays a critical role. At the same time, the allocentric advantage observed when moving from abstract to meaningful contexts does not exclude a possible contribution of egocentric processing. The egocentric system is known to support spatio-temporal integration of events, as in navigation and dynamic updating processes54; see also4. Thus, the allocentric facilitation observed in meaningful compared to abstract contexts under dynamic presentation may arise from an interaction between predictive semantic mechanisms and the ongoing integration supported by egocentric representations. Importantly, if the observed facilitation were merely driven by object concreteness per se (e.g.59,60, we would not expect a selective modulation of allocentric encoding. Instead, the present pattern of results suggests a differential sensitivity of spatial reference frames to scene meaning and temporal anticipation.

Finally, consistent with our hypotheses, no significant differences emerged between the Bathroom and Kitchen environments. This finding supports the robustness and generalizability of the semantic effect on allocentric spatial representations, indicating that it reflects a context-level mechanism rather than an effect tied to a specific setting or object set. However, future studies should explore this further by verifying the effect of incongruence between context and objects and evaluating the effect of different temporal parameters on reference frames.

From a practical standpoint, these findings introduce a novel and ecologically valid paradigm for investigating spatial cognition in immersive virtual settings. This approach opens new avenues for applications in neurorehabilitation, education, and human–machine interaction, where the interplay of semantic context and dynamic presentation can be harnessed to enhance spatial memory, particularly in populations with cognitive or perceptual impairments. In neurorehabilitation, VR-based training could expose patients to progressively unfolding, familiar environments to stimulate allocentric encoding, which is often impaired in cases of hippocampal damage or hemispatial neglect. In educational contexts, dynamic virtual scenarios grounded in semantic coherence, such as scientific labs or historical reconstructions, may enhance learning by fostering relational spatial representations. In assistive technologies, context-aware navigation systems can leverage semantic predictability and sequential guidance to improve wayfinding and orientation, especially for aging populations or users with cognitive decline.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the semantic richness of everyday environments enhances spatial memory in a selective and context-dependent manner. Specifically, semantic information facilitated allocentric spatial representations, presumably through anticipatory mechanisms active when spatial information unfolded dynamically. These results support recent theories on the link between semantic and spatial memory35–37. They also highlight the importance of temporal structure in enabling semantic effects to emerge.

Additionally, a significant Frame of Reference (FoR) x Room was found: F(2, 78) = 5.298, p = 0.007, η2p = 0.121. The Tukey post hoc showed that the egocentric-based spatial judgements were more accurate than the allocentric ones in the Abstract room (p < 0.001) and in the Kitchen (p < 0.05).

Finally, a significant Frame of Reference (FoR) x Room x Condition was found: F(2, 78) = 4.454, p = 0.015, η2p = 0.103. The Tukey post hoc showed that the egocentric-based spatial judgements were more accurate than the allocentric ones in the Abstract room (p < 0.001). Moreover, allocentric-based spatial judgements were more accurate in the Kitchen and in the Bathroom than in the Abstract room (at least p < 0.01).
