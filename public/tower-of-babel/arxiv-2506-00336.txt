Structured Column Subset Selection for Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design
Hugo Díaz, Arvind K. Saibaba, Srinivas Eswar, Vishwas Rao, Zichao Wendy Di

Abstract
We consider optimal experimental design (OED) for Bayesian inverse problems, where the experimental design variables have a certain multiway structure. Given d d different experimental variables with m i m_{i} choices per design variable 1 ≤ i ≤ d 1\leq i\leq d , the goal is to select k i ≤ m i k_{i}\leq m_{i} experiments per design variable. Previous work has related OED to the column subset selection problem by mapping the design variables to the columns of a matrix 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} . However, this approach is applicable only to the case d = 1 d=1 in which the columns can be selected independently. We develop an extension to the case where the design variables have a multi-way structure. Our approach is to map the matrix 𝑨 \boldsymbol{A} to a tensor and perform column subset selection on mode unfoldings of the tensor. We develop an algorithmic framework with three different algorithmic templates, and randomized variants of these algorithms. We analyze the computational cost of all the proposed algorithms and also develop greedy versions to facilitate comparisons. Numerical experiments on four different applications—time-dependent inverse problems, seismic tomography, X-ray tomography, and flow reconstruction—demonstrate the effectiveness and scalability of our methods for structured experimental design in Bayesian inverse problems.

Introduction and Motivation

Optimal experimental design (OED) aims to enhance inference and decision-making by strategically selecting experiments that maximize a given statistical criterion while respecting physical and budgetary constraints. These techniques are essential in various fields, including nuclear physics
ComptonScattering
, medical imaging
Dale1999
;
fingerprinting
, and geophysical exploration
seidler2016optimal
;
geophysicalapplications
. For a comprehensive review, see
Huan_2024
.

We consider OED in the context of Bayesian inverse problems, where unknown parameters are estimated by combining prior knowledge with data. This approach models parameters, observations, and noise as random variables, with the solution given by a posterior distribution, that is, the conditional distribution of parameters given observations
Dashti2017
.
This probabilistic framework enables uncertainty quantification and guides data collection through OED
alexanderian2021optimal
;
eswar2023optimal
.
When the relationship between parameters and data is linear and the noise follows a Gaussian distribution, the posterior distribution remains Gaussian, simplifying inference.
In this setting, many common OED criteria admit closed-form expressions, making it possible to evaluate and optimize designs efficiently.

More broadly, OED can be formulated using alternative optimality criteria, depending on the inference objectives and the structure of the problem
(
alexanderian2021optimal
, Section 4)
. For example, A-optimality seeks to reduce the average posterior variance across all parameters, treating all directions in parameter space equally. In contrast, C-optimality targets the variance in a specific linear combination of parameters, focusing the design on improving estimates of a particular quantity of interest.
In this article, we focus on OED based on the D-optimality criterion, which is related to the determinant of the posterior covariance matrix. Equivalently, it can also be interpreted as the expected information gain (EIG) from the prior to the posterior distribution of the parameters of interest
alexanderian2021optimal
;
Chaloner
.

Structured Column Subset Selection:

In this paper, we will follow the approach in
eswar2023optimal
and view OED through the lens of column subset selection.
The column subset selection problem (CSSP) was originally formulated to identify a subset of columns that best preserve the spectral properties of a matrix
martinsson2020randomized
. In the context of OED, we consider the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, which represents a transformed version of the forward model that incorporates prior knowledge and measurement noise
eswar2023optimal
. Details on the construction of this matrix are given in Section
2.2
. The choice of which columns of
\boldsymbol{A}
to select determines which design variables–such as sensor locations, experimental conditions, or control parameters–will be used for inference.
CSSP can be adapted to experimental design by interpreting the columns of a specific matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
as candidate designs.
In this context, selecting a subset of columns corresponds to determining design variables to be included in the experiment to maximize informativeness.

In many real-world applications, however, selection is subject to structural constraints that prevent choosing columns independently.
To capture such constraints, our formulation enforces selection at the level of columns. To make this idea more concrete, we consider the case of time-dependent inverse problems, where there are
m_{\text{cs}}
candidate sensor locations and each sensor can collect
m_{t}
measurements in time. The goal is to choose a subset of the sensors
K
(out of
m_{\text{cs}})
at which to place the sensors.
Specifically, the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
is partitioned as

\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{A}_{m_{\text{cs}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},

where
M=m_{\text{cs}}\cdot m_{\text{t}}
and each block
\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m_{\text{t}}}
for
1\leq j\leq m_{\text{cs}}
contains
m_{\text{t}}
columns.
The goal of OED is to select
K\leq m_{\text{cs}}
representative blocks corresponding to the sensors that are placed. Naïvely applying column subset selection to select
K\cdot m_{\text{t}}
columns does not guarantee that exactly
K
unique sensors will be selected; it may be possible that the algorithm determines a few columns corresponding to all the sensors. In other examples, in medical imaging, sensor arrays must be selected as a whole to maintain spatial coherence; and in geophysical monitoring, sensors are deployed in fixed grid patterns, restricting individual placement. Addressing these constraints requires structured selection methods that enforce group-wise selection while retaining the benefits of CSSP.
More generally, if selection involves
d
categorical variables, each with
m_{j}
possible choices in each category, the problem reduces to selecting
k_{j}
elements per category for
1\leq j\leq d
. We call this problem a structured column subset selection problem; if
d=1
, this formulation recovers the standard CSSP. This is the central focus of the paper.

Challenges and Our Approach:

It is well known that CSSP, which is a special case of structured column subset selection, is NP-hard (see, e.g.,
civril2009selecting
). Enforcing structured selections further restricts the feasible set of solutions.
Exhaustive search is impractical, and standard greedy heuristics currently do not incorporate structured dependencies.

To address the challenges imposed by the constraints, we propose a structured generalization of CSSP that enforces selection at the level of column blocks, ensuring that structural dependencies are maintained. Our approach leverages tensor decompositions and randomized methods to efficiently capture correlations among design variables while reducing computational costs. By integrating structured selection into CSSP, our methods provide a scalable and effective framework for experimental design in complex inverse problems.

Related Work:

While a lot of existing work has focused on CSSP (i.e., for
d=1
), we could not find many works that tackle the structured column subset selection. Eswar et al.
eswar2023optimal
developed approximation algorithms for OED using CSSP algorithms. Our work builds on this idea by extending it to structured data that can be formulated as a tensor, allowing for structured selection across multiple modes of a tensor. A specific generalization for time-dependent problems was considered in
alexanderian2021optimal
. To our knowledge, none of the approaches in the literature directly and systematically tackle the issue of structured column subset selection, which is the focus of this paper. For this reason, we had to develop greedy versions of the proposed algorithms, to facilitate comparisons between algorithms.

However, some works are tangentially related to our goals. In tensor decompositions, subset selection has been used to obtain interpolative decompositions, as in
saibaba2016hoid
;
Minster
. Tensor-based sensor placement was also considered in recent work
Tensor-based_flow
but is different from the proposed approaches in the objective function used. In optimization, subset selection has been enforced across groups of variables using the notion of group sparsity (see, e.g.,
huang2010benefit
). The notion of clustering different modes of a tensor is the main idea behind a technique called tensor co-clustering
spectral_co-clustering
. This framework also shares conceptual similarities with sparse reconstruction techniques in MRI, where prior information is leveraged to recover images from undersampled data
Lustig
;
Yang2016SparseMRI
.

2.1
Tensor Background

We provide a brief introduction to tensors, focusing on the concepts relevant to this paper; for a more comprehensive treatment, see
kolda2009tensor
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\dots\times n_{P}}
be an order-
P
tensor with entries
x_{i_{1},\dots,i_{P}}
, where
1\leq i_{\ell}\leq n_{\ell}
and
1\leq\ell\leq P
.
Tensors can be unfolded into matrices in different ways, a useful feature for performing linear algebraic operations.

Mode Unfolding

A mode-
j
unfolding (matricization), denoted
\boldsymbol{X}_{(j)}
, reshapes the tensor to form a matrix of size
{n_{j}\times\prod_{\ell=1,\ell\neq j}^{P}n_{\ell}}
.
Tensors can be multiplied with matrices using mode products, which are defined via mode unfoldings.
For example, given a
{t\times n_{j}}
matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
, the mode product along mode
j
, denoted,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{j}\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\dots\times n_{j-1}\times t\times n_{j+1}\times\cdots\times n_{P}},

can be computed as
\boldsymbol{Y}_{(j)}=\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{X}_{(j)}
.
If
\boldsymbol{S}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are matrices of compatible sizes, mode products satisfy the associativity property
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{i}\boldsymbol{S}\times_{j}\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{j}\boldsymbol{B}\times_{i}\boldsymbol{S}
. For the same mode,
i=j
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{i}\boldsymbol{S}\times_{i}\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{i}\boldsymbol{BS}
.

Given two matrices
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
(
m,n,p,q
all positive integers, but with no constraints on the size), then the Kronecker product between the matrices, denoted
\boldsymbol{S}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}
, is defined as

\boldsymbol{S}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}s_{11}\boldsymbol{B}&\cdots&s_{1n}\boldsymbol{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}\boldsymbol{B}&\cdots&s_{mn}\boldsymbol{B}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(mp)\times(nq)}.

Note that

\left(\boldsymbol{S}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}\right)^{\top}=\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(nq)\times(mp)}.

Relation between Mode and Kronecker Products

Kronecker products are closely related to mode products as follows: Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{j}\times n_{j}}
for
1\leq j\leq P
be a sequence of matrices such that the tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\bigtimes_{j=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{S}_{j}
is well-defined. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}_{(j)}=\boldsymbol{S}_{j}\boldsymbol{X}_{(j)}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{P}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{j+1}\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{j-1}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\right)^{\top}\qquad 1\leq j\leq P.

(1)

Tucker Representation and HOSVD

Given a tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{P}}
and a target rank
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{P})
, the Tucker decomposition approximates
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\bigtimes_{\ell=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{P}}
is the core tensor and
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}\times r_{\ell}}
are factor matrices with orthonormal columns.

The higher-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD)
de_lathauwer_multilinear_2000
generalizes the matrix SVD to tensors by computing the Tucker factor matrices from the truncated SVD:
\boldsymbol{X}_{(j)}\approx\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}\boldsymbol{V}_{j}^{\top}
,
where
\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
is a matrix whose columns consist of the leading
r_{j}
singular vectors. The core tensor is then computed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\bigtimes_{\ell=1}^{P}\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}^{\top}.

(3)

Sequentially Truncated HOSVD (ST-HOSVD)

ST-HOSVD
vannieuwenhoven_new_2012
;
Minster
refines HOSVD by computing truncated factor matrices sequentially, mode by mode.
Rather than optimizing each factor matrix
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}
from the full tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
, it prioritizes preserving the interaction between the core tensor and the factor matrices, as captured in (
3
).
ST-HOSVD
updates the core tensor after each step, ensuring that subsequent truncations are applied to increasingly compressed representations.
The algorithm proceeds as follows: Initialize the core tensor as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
. For each mode
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,P\}
, compute the truncated SVD of the mode-
\ell
unfolding of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell-1)}
,

\boldsymbol{G}^{(\ell-1)}_{(\ell)}\approx\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{r_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{r_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{V}_{r_{\ell}}^{\top},

retain the top
r_{\ell}
left singular vectors
\boldsymbol{U}_{\ell}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{r_{\ell}}
, and update the core tensor as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell-1)}\bigtimes_{j=1}^{\ell}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{\top}\qquad 1\leq\ell\leq P.

2.2
Bayesian Inverse Problems and Optimal Experimental Design

Bayesian inverse problems aim to estimate unknown parameters by integrating prior knowledge with noisy observations within a statistical framework. The solution yields a probability distribution on the unknown parameters, which facilitates uncertainty quantification
alexanderian2021optimal
;
Dashti2017
;
Huan_2024
.
More specifically, a linear inverse problem is formulated as recovering an unknown parameter
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from observations
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, modeled as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{Fu}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the parameter-to-observable map and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{R})
accounts for measurement and model errors. A key challenge in inverse problems is that they are ill-posed, since there may be insufficient observations (i.e.,
m\leq n
).
In order to address these issues, a Bayesian formulation incorporates prior knowledge of the parameter, modeled as
\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{u}_{pr},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}})
, leading to the likelihood:

\pi_{\textrm{like}}(\boldsymbol{d}|\boldsymbol{u})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{Fu}-\boldsymbol{d}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}}\right).

With Bayes’ rule, the posterior distribution’s density
\pi_{\textrm{post}}(\boldsymbol{u}|\boldsymbol{d})
satisfies

\displaystyle\pi_{\textrm{post}}(\boldsymbol{u}|\boldsymbol{d})=\frac{\pi_{\textrm{like}}(\boldsymbol{d}|\boldsymbol{u})\pi_{\textrm{pr}}(\boldsymbol{u})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{d})}\>\propto\>\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{Fu}-\boldsymbol{d}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}}-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\textrm{pr}}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{-1}}\right).

(5)

Since this problem is linear and the special choice of prior and noise model, the posterior distribution is also Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\textrm{post}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{post}})
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{post}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\boldsymbol{F},~\mbox{ and }~\boldsymbol{u}_{\textrm{post}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textrm{post}}\left(\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{\textrm{pr}}\right);

(6)

see
(
alexanderian2021optimal
, Section 3.3)
.
We now cast optimal sensor placement as an OED problem, where a statistical criterion measures optimality.

Optimality Criterion:

We adopt the expected information gain (EIG) as the optimality criterion for sensor selection
alexanderian2021optimal
;
eswar2023optimal
, which, in finite-dimensional settings, is equivalent to the D-optimality criterion.
This criterion quantifies the reduction in uncertainty by measuring the volume of the posterior uncertainty ellipsoid, expressed through the determinant of the posterior covariance matrix
(
alexanderian2021optimal
, Section 4.1)
. It is widely used in Bayesian OED to assess the informativeness of selected observations
Bakker2020ExperimentalDF
;
eswar2023optimal
;
TractablGlobal
;
GOOED
.
The EIG measures the expected reduction in uncertainty from the prior
\pi_{\text{pr}}
to the posterior
\pi_{\text{post}}
after observing data. In the Gaussian case, it has a simple analytic form
(
alexanderian2021optimal
, Section 4.1)
, and, up to a multiplicative factor
1/2
, it is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{EIG}}:=-\mathrm{logdet}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{post}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{-1})=\mathrm{logdet}\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

Following
(
eswar2023optimal
, Section 3)
, we assume a diagonal noise covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{R}=\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{I}
, meaning each column of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
corresponds to an individual design variable. In contrast, a non-diagonal
\boldsymbol{R}
results in linear combinations of the design variables. This suggests defining the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\sigma^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{R}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{pr}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}.

(7)

Substituting this into the EIG expression, we get

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{EIG}}=\mathrm{logdet}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})=\mathrm{logdet}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A})=\mathrm{logdet}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}).

(8)

The second equality follows from Sylvester’s determinant lemma. Here,
\boldsymbol{I}
denotes the identity matrix of appropriate size, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries are the squared singular values of
\boldsymbol{A}
, with at most
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A})
nonzero singular values. We also use the notation
\Psi(\boldsymbol{A})=\mathrm{logdet}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{AA}^{\top})
, so that
\phi_{\text{EIG}}=\Psi(\boldsymbol{A})
. In the sequel, we show how the design enters the design criterion.

2.4
Column Subset Selection Problem

The column subset selection problem aims to identify a subset of columns from a given matrix that preserves key spectral properties
mahoney2009cur
;
martinsson2020randomized
. This problem is fundamental in dimensionality reduction and low-rank approximations.
The discussion below corresponds to the case

.

Golub–Klema–Stewart Method (GKS)

In this work we focus on the GKS method
(
MatrixComputations
, Section 5.5.7)
, a two-stage approach for selecting
K
informative columns (
K\leq M
) from a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
. The GKS method provides a way to extract a well-conditioned subset of columns that retains essential spectral characteristics of
\boldsymbol{A}

First, a rank-
K
approximation of
\boldsymbol{A}
is obtained via truncated SVD:

\boldsymbol{A}\approx\boldsymbol{U}_{K}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{K}\boldsymbol{V}_{K}^{\top},

where

\boldsymbol{U}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
,
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}

In the second stage, a column-pivoted QR (CPQR) factorization of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{K}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\boldsymbol{V}_{K}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{Q}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}&\boldsymbol{R}_{12}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{11}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{V}_{12}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where

We summarize the CSSP algorithm based on the GKS method in Algorithm
1
, which consists of computing a truncated SVD followed by a column-pivoted QR factorization on the top right singular vectors.

Computational Complexity of the GKS Method

This method requires a truncated SVD on
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, which has a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\min(N,M)^{2}\cdot\max(N,M))
flops if a thin SVD is performed.
Additionally, it involves a column-pivoted QR factorization on a short and wide matrix
\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M}
, with a complexity of
2MK^{2}-2K^{3}/3
flops
(
eswar2023optimal
, Section 3.3)
. Thus, the dominant computational cost arises from the truncated SVD, motivating the need for more suitable alternatives for large-scale problems. To address this computational cost, we use randomized approaches in Section
.

3
Algorithms for Structured OED

This section introduces efficient and scalable approaches for OED with structured column selection. The proposed methods combine tensor decompositions, CSSP techniques, randomized sketching, and adjoint-free variants.
Since mode unfolding arranges data row-wise, the problem reduces to row subset selection. In numerical linear algebra, however, column selection is the primary focus.
To leverage standard tensor and matrix libraries, we first compute the Tucker-like factors for the target modes and then apply a column selection algorithm to their transposes. In Section
3.1
, we give the overview of our approach and discuss the three algoritmic templates in Section
3.2
. In Section
, we give the Sketch-First randomized approach and, finally, in Section
, we discuss the computational costs of all the proposed algorithms. The algorithmic templates can be adapted to give greedy variants of the algorithms, which we discuss in Appendix
, including a detailed comparison with the proposed algorithms.

3.1
Overview of Our Approach

We consider tensors derived from the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, which corresponds to a weighted forward operator as defined in (
8
). We reshape the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
to obtain the tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}\times N}
, where
d
represents the number of categories of selection variables. The matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
can be recovered from the tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{X}_{(d+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\quad M=\prod_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}.

(17)

The structure of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
enables structured selection across multiple experimental variables.
For each mode
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we select
k_{j}
indices, represented by the tuple
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
, where
k_{j}\leq m_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq d
. The total number of selected design variables is

K=\prod_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}.
The following discussion generalizes the approach for
d=1
in Section
2.3
.

Structured Selection (several design variables)

Given a positive integer
t
, denote
[t]=\{1,\dots,t\}
. Given
d
categorical variables, the columns of
\boldsymbol{A}
can be identified with the set
[m_{1}]\times\dots\times[m_{d}]
.
We must choose
k_{j}
indices out of
[m_{j}]
indices for
1\leq j\leq d
. We denote the sets
\mathcal{S}^{(j)}=\{i_{1}^{(j)},\dots,i_{k_{j}}^{(j)}\}
so that the selection operator is of the form
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{(1)}\times\dots\times\mathcal{S}^{(d)}
. The corresponding selection operator is

\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{S}_{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{1},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{S}_{j}=\boldsymbol{I}(:,\mathcal{S}^{(j)}),\>1\leq j\leq d.

The corresponding selected columns of
\boldsymbol{A}
are denoted
\boldsymbol{AS}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
, where
K=\prod_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}
.
We can also compute the subsampled tensor as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{\top}\dots\times_{d}\boldsymbol{S}_{d}^{\top}.

With this notation
\boldsymbol{AS}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{X}}_{(d+1)}=\boldsymbol{X}_{(d+1)}(\boldsymbol{S}_{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{1})
,
where
\boldsymbol{S}=(\boldsymbol{S}_{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{1})
. The main objective of this article is to find a selection operator
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{S}_{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{S}_{1}
that maximizes (
13
). Since this problem is NP-hard, prior work
eswar2023optimal
has proposed approximation methods based on CSSP (i.e.,
d=1
), which selects a subset of columns that approximately preserve the main spectral properties of
\boldsymbol{A}
, to identify the most informative design variables.
We extend this approach to the more general case
d>1
.

The following methods adapt strategies for computing the Tucker factors, treating the number of selected variables per mode as the target rank. We first discuss the methods and then discuss the computational cost of each method.

3.2
Algorithmic Templates for Structured OED

We begin by presenting a framework for selecting indices in each mode, ensuring a structured selection, with three different methods.
Each method outputs
d
selection matrices
(\boldsymbol{S}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{S}_{d})
.
The methods differ in how they incorporate information from other design variables (modes).
While the proposed framework is compatible with any CSSP algorithm, we primarily employ the GKS-based approach detailed in
Algorithm

1
. In order to emphasize the fact that any CSSP algorithm can be used (hence, an algorithmic template), the proposed algorithms refer to a function
\boldsymbol{S}\leftarrow\text{CSSP}(\boldsymbol{M},k)
, which takes in a matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and returns a selection operator
\boldsymbol{S}
corresponding to
k\leq n
important columns
\boldsymbol{MS}
.

3.2.2
SeqSelect: Sequential Mode Selection

In this approach, we start with the same as in IndSelect and obtain the selection operator
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}
from
\boldsymbol{X}_{(1)}^{\top}
. However, in the second step, rather than working with
\boldsymbol{X}_{(2)}^{\top}
, we form the core tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times m_{2}\dots\times m_{d}\times N}
and perform mode subset selection on
\boldsymbol{G}_{(2)}
. We can thus proceed sequentially.

At core
j
(for
1\leq j\leq d
), we can consider the core tensor

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(j-1)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\bigtimes_{i=1}^{j-1}\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\dots\times k_{j-1}\times m_{j}\dots\times m_{d}\times N},

(19)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
, and we perform subset selection on
\boldsymbol{G}^{(j-1)}_{(j)}
to obtain
\boldsymbol{S}_{j}
. Thus, this method incorporates the selection performed in the previous steps. It is similar to the ST-HOSVD approach for computing low-rank Tucker decompositions. Similar to ST-HOSVD, we allow for a different order of processing the modes; however, unlike ST-HOSVD, we focus only on reducing the core tensor and do not consider the factor matrices. This approach is summarized in Algorithm

3.2.3
IterSelect: Iterative Mode Selection

IterSelect begins with a random initialization of the selection operators defined by
(\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{(0)},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}_{d}^{(0)}).
Then the method performs a sweep through all the modes to sequentially determine the selection operator. At iteration
t
, we consider mode
j
(
1\leq j\leq d
). We have the selection operator defined by

\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}_{j-1}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{S}_{j}^{(t-1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}_{d}^{(t-1)}\right).

To obtain the selection operator
\boldsymbol{S}_{j}^{(t)}
, we form the intermediate tensor

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{(t,j)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\bigtimes_{i=1}^{j-1}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{(t)}\right)^{\top}\bigtimes_{i=j+1}^{d}\left(\boldsymbol{S}_{i}^{(t-1)}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times\dots\times k_{j-1}\times m_{j}\times k_{j+1}\times\dots\times k_{d}\times N},

(20)

for
t\geq 0
and
1\leq j\leq d
. At the end of each sweep through the modes,
the method checks for improvement in the design criterion
\phi_{\text{EIG}}
.
If the improvement is below a specified tolerance or if EIG decreases, the algorithm terminates and returns the last selection matrices.

HOOI

method for Tucker approximation and is summarized in Algorithm
