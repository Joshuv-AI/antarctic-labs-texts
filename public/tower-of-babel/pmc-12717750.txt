Empowering safe medication practices: translation and validation of the Malay inpatient medication-safety involvement scale (M-IMSIS)
Authors: Mohd Hanif Mohd Nawawi, Mohd Ismail Ibrahim, Najib Majdi Yaacob, Maizun Mohd Zain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient engagement in medication safety is a recognized public health priority that reduces preventable harm and improves clinical outcomes. Despite its importance, Malaysia lacks a validated Malay-language instrument to assess inpatient involvement in medication safety, limiting efforts to tailor interventions and monitor engagement.ObjectiveThis study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically validate the Inpatient Medication Safety Involvement Scale (IMSIS) for use among Malay-speaking adult inpatients.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 100 adult inpatients across four wards of a tertiary hospital in Kelantan, Malaysia. The eight-item IMSIS was translated using ISPOR cross-cultural adaptation guidelines, including forward–back translation, expert review, and cognitive debriefing. Content and face validity indices were calculated. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) assessed construct validity, and internal consistency was evaluated using Raykov’s rho.ResultsThe Malay IMSIS demonstrated excellent content (S-CVI = 0.94) and face (S-FVI = 0.87) validity. All standardized loadings were ≥0.60, and composite reliability was high (Raykov’s ρ = 0.90). The instrument showed good interpretability, with higher scores reflecting greater medication-safety engagement.ConclusionThe Malay version of the IMSIS (M-IMSIS) is a brief, culturally appropriate, and psychometrically sound tool for assessing inpatient involvement in medication safety. Background

Medication safety is increasingly recognised as a critical public health concern worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that unsafe medication practices lead to avoidable costs of approximately US $42 billion annually and contribute to nearly half of all preventable harm in healthcare systems [1]. Adverse drug events (ADEs) are particularly prevalent in acute care settings. A 2022 systematic review found a wide variation in ADE-related hospitalisations globally, ranging from 10 to 383 per 100,000 individuals, and reported as many as eight associated deaths per 100,000 people each year [2].

Malaysia faces similar challenges. A recent multicentre observational study revealed that 68% of medication doses administered in neonatal intensive care units contained at least one error [3]. Additional research has highlighted issues such as inappropriate prescribing, insufficient monitoring, and limited patient understanding of medications all of which contribute to increased medication-related harm. These findings underscore the urgent need for effective strategies to enhance medication safety in Malaysian healthcare settings.

One promising approach is improving patient engagement in medication safety. Active involvement of patients such as verifying prescriptions, asking clarifying questions, reporting concerns, and self-administering medications where appropriate has been shown to reduce the incidence of medication errors [4–6]. The concept of patient safety engagement includes patients’ proactive participation in treatment discussions, medication verification, and communication with healthcare providers [7]. This aligns with the WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030, which calls for patients and families to be “full partners” in the prevention of healthcare harm [8].

Recent evidence, including randomized trials and systematic reviews, has demonstrated that interventions such as interactive education, motivational interviewing, and app-based follow-ups can improve medication adherence and reduce discrepancies. These interventions also enhance patient self-efficacy, a key factor influencing patients’ willingness to ask questions, double-check medications, and adhere to therapies [5, 9, 10].

Despite increasing global attention to this issue, Malaysia lacks a validated instrument in the Malay language to measure patient engagement in medication safety within hospital settings. This gap is especially critical in a multicultural and multilingual nation where language-appropriate tools are essential to ensure inclusivity and effectiveness. Furthermore, the country’s relatively low levels of health literacy, with only 6.6% of adults having adequate health literacy [11], heighten the need for simple and validated tools to assess and support patient participation in safety behaviours.

To address this gap, we focused on the Inpatient Medication Safety Involvement Scale (IMSIS), an eight-item instrument originally developed to measure patient engagement in hospital medication safety. The theoretical foundation of this instrument is grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Patient Activation Theory. According to TPB, an individual’s behaviour is influenced by their intention to act, which is shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control [12]. This framework supports the role of beliefs and confidence in predicting safety-related behaviours, such as medication verification and question-asking. Meanwhile, Patient Activation Theory describes a staged process through which individuals gain the knowledge, skills, and confidence to actively manage their health [13, 14]. The IMSIS reflects these progressive competencies by assessing a range of behaviours from passive awareness to active self-management. Together, these theories provide a robust conceptual underpinning for the scale’s focus on intention, self-efficacy, and proactive engagement in medication safety. Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study from December 2024 till end of January 2025 at a tertiary teaching hospital in Kelantan, Malaysia. The study followed a multi-phase process of translating, culturally adapting, and evaluating the IMSIS among patients admitted to medical, surgical, orthopedic, and obstetrics–gynecology wards. Eligible participants were adults aged 18–60 years, proficient in Malay, medically stable, and without a self-reported history of psychiatric disorders. Eligibility screening was carried out by the Chief Nurses of the selected wards, with no formal diagnostic assessment performed. A roster of admitted patients was prepared with the assistance of ward administrative staff, and participants were then selected using a stratified random sampling method. Written informed consent was obtained from all eligible patients prior to completion of the IMSIS questionnaire.

The IMSIS instrument

The IMSIS is a self-administered tool designed to evaluate the extent of patient involvement in medication safety within hospital settings. The IMSIS consists of 8 items, each measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), resulting in a total mean score between 1 and 5. Higher mean scores reflect greater patient engagement in medication safety activities, such as consistently verifying medication labels, confirming instructions and actively communicating with healthcare providers about their medication regimen. Previous research has validated the IMSIS in Western contexts, demonstrating its effectiveness in assessing patient involvement in medication safety [15].

Translation and cultural adaptation

Translation and cultural adaptation followed Wild et al.’s ten-step methodology to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence in the Malaysian context [16]. Permission to translate the IMSIS was obtained from the original scale developers prior to initiating the process.

Preparation

During the preparation phase, the research team selected qualified translators and established a detailed timeline for the study. One translator was a public health medicine specialist with expertise in medication safety constructs, while the other had formal training in linguistics from Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM). Both translators were thoroughly briefed on the study objectives, key terminologies related to the IMSIS and the deadlines for each translation stage.

Forward translation

The initial step involved two independent translators converting the IMSIS from English into Malay. Translator 1, a healthcare professional, emphasized maintaining the conceptual integrity of medication safety, whereas Translator 2, a language educator, focused on ensuring each item’s clarity and grammatical accuracy. Both translators documented any ambiguous or potentially confusing terms encountered during translation.

Reconciliation

After the forward translations, the research team reviewed and merged the two Malay versions into a single draft. A panel comprising experts in clinical care, medical education, hospital quality management and other relevant fields (as detailed in Table 1) reviewed the consolidated draft to ensure contextual appropriateness and linguistic coherence for Malay-speaking patients in Malaysia. The panel refined any unclear language to enhance cultural sensitivity and content validity, conducting an initial assessment of item clarity and face validity.

Back translation

Two additional translators, who were proficient in both Malay and English but unfamiliar with the original IMSIS, independently translated the reconciled Malay draft back into English. This back-translation step was critical for identifying any discrepancies or deviations from the original meaning, ensuring that the translated version retained the content, intent and tone of the IMSIS. This phase was allocated approximately two weeks to allow for meticulous attention to linguistic details.

Back translation review

Upon completion of the back translations, the review committee compared these English versions with the original IMSIS to identify any conceptual or contextual inconsistencies. The panel discussed the fidelity of each item in preserving the original scale’s meaning and made necessary adjustments to the Malay translation to achieve maximum equivalence.

Harmonization

Harmonization involved a thorough comparison of the reconciled Malay translation with the back-translated English versions and the original IMSIS. The objective was to resolve any remaining discrepancies and finalize a version of the M-IMSIS ready for pretesting. This quality control step ensured that the scale accurately reflected the core constructs of medication safety while remaining comprehensible to Malay-speaking patients.

Cognitive debriefing

A convenience sample of 10 participants who met the study’s inclusion criteria (adult inpatients, fluent in Malay, no psychiatric illness) completed the prefinal M-IMSIS for cognitive debriefing. Participants were asked to highlight any words or phrases that were confusing or difficult to interpret. Their feedback was instrumental in refining the scale to ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness.

Review of cognitive debriefing results and finalization

The research team reviewed the feedback from the cognitive debriefing sessions and discussed any recommended changes. Consensus was reached on the necessary modifications incorporated into the translated IMSIS. These refinements enhanced the clarity and relevance of the scale, culminating in the final Malay-IMSIS.

Proofreading

The finalized M-IMSIS underwent a detailed proofreading process to identify and correct any typographical errors or linguistic inconsistencies. The expert panel conducted a final review to confirm that the items accurately represented the original IMSISs intent while maintaining cultural coherence within the Malay context.

Final report

A comprehensive final report documenting the entire translation and adaptation process was compiled. This report detailed each stage, justified all modifications based on expert and participant feedback and provided a transparent account of the methodology, serving as a reference for future validation studies.

Pretesting

Pretesting of the M-IMSIS was conducted at the same teaching hospital to evaluate the instrument’s functionality and clarity. A random sample of 30 patients, selected from the same wards but excluded from the cognitive debriefing phase, participated in this stage. After receiving a verbal explanation of the study objectives, participants gave written informed consent and completed the questionnaire. The self-administered M-IMSIS was distributed and collected in person, ensuring a private and comfortable environment for completion. This approach allowed for immediate clarification of any uncertainties and the collection of insightful feedback regarding the comprehensibility and cultural relevance of each item. The pretesting insights facilitated final adjustments to the M-IMSIS, ensuring that all items were appropriately framed and easily understood by the target population.

Validity assessment

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated by having ten experts from public health and clinical care independently assess each item using a four-point relevance scale. Items with scores of 3 or 4 were considered relevant; I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave were calculated using standard thresholds (≥0.78 and ≥ 0.90, respectively). Microsoft Excel was employed for all calculations, selected for its accessibility and alignment with practices from previous local content validity research [17, 18].

Face validity

Item clarity was assessed by thirty patients using a four-point scale. Only items rated as either “clear” or “very clear,” corresponding to scores of 3 or 4, were deemed valid. Subsequently, the Item-Level Face Validity Index (I-FVI) and Scale-Level Face Validity Index (S-FVI/Ave) were computed, applying standard thresholds of 0.80 and 0.83, respectively. Participants’ feedback guided minor revisions aimed at enhancing item clarity.

Data analysis

The M-IMSIS was administered as a paper-based, self-administered questionnaire. Prior to distribution, the research team briefed the Chief Nurses of the selected wards on the study objectives and the procedure for answering the questionnaire to ensure consistent explanation to participants. The Chief Nurses then distributed the questionnaire to eligible and consented patients in their respective wards, who were given 24 hours to complete it. The completed forms were collected the following day and checked for completeness before being handed over to the research team. Data entry was performed by a trained researcher, with random cross-checks conducted between the hardcopy responses and the electronic dataset to ensure accuracy. The finalized dataset was first processed and analyzed using SPSS (version 29) to generate descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, to describe participants’ demographic and clinical profiles. The dataset was then imported into R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), where the lavaan and semTools packages were used to perform confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and to estimate composite reliability (Raykov’s rho) [19]. The final recruited sample comprised 100 participants, corresponding to the recommended 10:1 participant-to-item ratio for psychometric validation studies [20–24]. Recent simulation-based research indicates that with standardized loadings ≥0.60, samples of ≈ 100 can achieve sufficient power for CFA and provide stable parameter estimates [25, 26].

The internal consistency and structure of the IMSIS were evaluated using CFA, with model fit determined by several indices: chi-square goodness-of-fit (χ2, p > 0.05), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.95), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.95), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08, with upper bound of 90% CI ≤ 0.08) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08), based on recommendations by Schreiber JB (2017) [27]. Further refinement of the CFA model relied on standardized residuals (SRs) and modification indices (MIs), where items with factor loadings of ≥ 0.30 were retained [28]. Additionally, parameters demonstrating SRs ≥ 2.58 or MIs ≥ 3.84 underwent detailed evaluation for possible theoretical adjustments [29]. Convergent validity, discriminant validity, and test–retest reliability were not assessed in this study and are recommended for future validation work.

To detect potential multicollinearity, correlations among latent variables exceeding 0.85 were examined critically. Alternative model specifications were evaluated by comparing Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, with lower values reflecting superior fit [29], thereby guiding the selection of the most robust model. Composite reliability was quantified using Raykov’s rho, adopting a threshold of ≥ 0.70 to indicate acceptable internal consistency [28, 30–32]. Recent Monte-Carlo simulations suggest that with loadings ≥ 0.60, an 8-indicator single-factor CFA achieves > 80% power at n ≈ 100 [25].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was secured from the Research Ethics Committee of the participating institutions (Reference No. NMRR ID-24–02809-XTS) and the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM/JEPeM/KK/24080655). Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of their participation and their right to withdraw at any point without any repercussions. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study and strict confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process, with access limited to the principal investigator and supervising researchers. All findings and publications derived from this research were presented without disclosing any identifiable participant information.

Patient and public involvement

Patients participated in this study by assessing face validity and contributing to the CFA. Their insights informed revisions aimed at enhancing item clarity, phrasing accuracy and contextual appropriateness. Nonetheless, patients did not take part in designing the research, determining outcome measures, conducting data analyses, or interpreting findings. Following study completion, results will be shared with participants and the broader hospital community through institutional briefings and academic presentations, ensuring transparency and recognizing patient contributions. Patients were not involved in preparing this manuscript.

Results

Content validity

The M-IMSIS demonstrated strong content validity overall, with the computed Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave) reaching 0.94. This exceeded the widely accepted threshold of 0.83, underscoring the expert panel’s agreement on the instrument’s validity [18, 33]. At the individual level, the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) ranged between 0.80 and 1.00, comfortably surpassing the recommended minimum of 0.78 [33, 34] as detailed in Table 2. Specifically, item 7 (“I have kept and administered my medicines while in hospital”) recorded the lowest I-CVI score at 0.80, while all other items attained values of 0.90 or greater. Experts provided feedback suggesting minor revisions, primarily recommending simplification of technical terminology to enhance cultural appropriateness and clarity. These suggestions were subsequently integrated into the final questionnaire.

Face validity

The M-IMSIS showed strong face validity, achieving a high average Scale-level Face Validity Index (S-FVI/Ave) of 0.87, surpassing the recommended benchmark of 0.83 [18, 33]. Each item was individually recorded with an Item-level Face Validity Index (I-FVI) ranging from 0.80 to 1.00, clearly meeting or exceeding the accepted standard of 0.80 as presented in Table 3. Participants consistently described the questionnaire items as either “clear” or “very clear,” confirming the suitability and comprehensibility of the instrument for the target respondents. Such favourable scores imply that the participants effectively understood the meaning of the items and could confidently provide meaningful responses [17, 35].

Socio-demographic characteristics of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) participants

One hundred inpatient adults from four tertiary-care wards (medical, surgical, orthopaedic and obstetrics–gynaecology) completed the M-IMSIS during the CFA phase as illustrated in Table 4. Participants were predominantly Malay (99%) and female (56%) with a mean age of 37.7 ± 11.8 years. Nearly three-quarters were married (72%) and educational attainment was skewed toward secondary school or higher, with 60% holding a college diploma or university degree, while only 12% had not completed secondary schooling. Employment status was heterogeneous: 38% were government employees, 18% self-employed, 11% worked in the private sector and 11% were unemployed; the remainder comprised housewives, students and retirees. Hospitalisation patterns were short-to-moderate in duration (median length of stay = 3 days), with 42% discharged within two days. Although one-third reported at least one chronic illness, only 19% had been hospitalised more than twice in the previous year. Almost all respondents perceived strong social support and reported feeling either “very” or “somewhat” comfortable raising medication-safety questions with staff, suggesting a sample receptive to engagement initiatives.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results

Initial testing of a single-factor model (Model 1) produced marginal fit (χ2 (20) = 69.9, CFI = 0.883, TLI = 0.836, RMSEA = 0.158, SRMR = 0.091). Modification-index inspection highlighted a theoretically defensible correlated residual between Items 7 (“kept my own medicines”) and 8 (“brought my medicines to the ward”). Allowing this covariance yielded Model 2, which fit the data well (χ2 (17) = 27.5, CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.959, RMSEA = 0.079, SRMR = 0.042) and represented a significant improvement over Model 1 (Δχ2 (3) = 42.4, p < 0.001). The correlated uniqueness of 0.181 suggests that even after the common factor is extracted, patients who bring their medications are also likely to self-administer them, an overlap consistent with clinical practice. Factor loadings

Table 7 and Figure 1 present the final one-factor CFA model for the M-IMSIS, in which all eight items load significantly on the latent construct of medication-safety involvement. Standardised loadings range from 0.605 (IMSIS 1) to 0.859 (IMSIS 6); none falls below the 0.40 guideline. IMSIS 5 (“I have verified my medicines before taking them”) and IMSIS 6 (“I have asked staff if my medicines are correct”) are the strongest indicators, each accounting for roughly 73% of its observed variance (λ = 0.852 and 0.859, respectively). IMSIS 2, 3 and 4 also show robust associations (λ = 0.743–0.781), explaining 55–61% of their variance, whereas IMSIS 1 (0.605), IMSIS 7 (0.644) and IMSIS 8 (0.676) exhibit moderate but acceptable relationships with the factor. Corresponding error variances span 0.262 (IMSIS 6) to 0.634 (IMSIS 1), indicating satisfactory measurement precision overall. A modest residual correlation between IMSIS 7 and IMSIS 8 (r = 0.181) reflects shared content related to self-administration behaviours. Collectively, these results support the one-dimensionality, reliability and construct validity of the scale.

Raykov’s rho

Composite reliability for the one-factor solution, computed via Raykov’s ρ, was 0.901. This exceeds the conventional 0.70 criterion and the more stringent 0.80 benchmark for research tools, confirming excellent internal consistency. The high reliability aligns with prior work on the English IMSIS and suggests that the Malay translation preserves item cohesion while minimising redundant error.

95% Confidence Intervals (CIs)

The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the standardised loadings were uniformly positive, substantiating the statistical significance of each indicator. The most precise estimates were observed for the strongest items of IMSIS 5 (0.763, 0.941) and IMSIS 6 (0.729, 0.989), reflecting narrow intervals around high point estimates. Even the lowest-loading item (IMSIS 1) maintained a CI of 0.425, 0.784, comfortably above zero, reinforcing its substantive contribution. Wider intervals for IMSIS7 (0.388, 0.900) and IMSIS8 (0.441, 0.911) parallel their higher residual variance yet still confirm reliable positive associations with the latent factor. These findings indicate that the eight-item M-IMSIS is a coherent, internally consistent and psychometrically sound instrument for gauging inpatient engagement in medication-safety behaviours, while also highlighting Item 7s relatively lower precision and the conceptual overlap between Items 7 and 8 that may warrant refinement in future iterations.

Discussion

This study is the first to provide a rigorously translated and psychometrically evaluated M-IMSIS. The one-factor model with a theoretically defensible correlated residual for Items 7 and 8 fitted the data well and composite reliability (Raykov’s ρ = 0.90) exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.80. These indices are comparable to, or stronger than, those reported for recent East-Asian translations of patient-engagement instruments such as the Traditional-Chinese Medication Understanding and Use Self-Efficacy Scale, which achieved ω = 0.91 in a sample more than twice our size [36]. The psychometric performance of the M-IMSIS is also consistent with the original IMSIS validation, which reported a stable one-factor structure and high internal consistency [15]. Similar findings have been observed in other translation efforts, such as the Malay-language gastrointestinal symptom questionnaire [35], which likewise demonstrated strong content and face validity indices. These parallels reinforce the robustness of our translation process and support the suitability of the M-IMSIS for routine use in Malaysian healthcare settings.

In terms of sample size, our CFA was conducted with 100 participants, which aligns with the commonly applied 10:1 participant-to-item ratio and recent simulation-based recommendations. Monte Carlo studies suggest that with factor loadings above 0.60, samples of around 100 can yield adequate power and stable estimates. While larger samples (≥200) are typically recommended for invariance testing across subgroups, our sample is comparable to or larger than those reported in several other translation studies, which have validated instruments with similar or even smaller sample sizes while still producing acceptable fit indices [35, 36]. This supports the adequacy of our sample size for the current stage of scale validation, though we acknowledge that future studies with larger and more diverse samples will be important for testing measurement invariance and predictive validity.

Together with excellent content and face validity, the results confirm that the eight-item structure remains conceptually intact after cultural adaptation and is suitable for routine use in Malaysian hospitals. Consequently, the M-IMSIS offers clinicians, psychologists and health-communication specialists a succinct metric for tailoring activation-focused interventions and auditing safety culture across wards. The M-IMSIS enables hospital administrators to benchmark patient involvement in medication safety and design targeted interventions based on engagement gaps. The M-IMSIS also aligns with international safety imperatives. The Joint Commission’s 2024 National Patient Safety Goals emphasise patient identification, labelling and verification of medicines and medication reconciliation, behaviours captured directly by IMSIS Items 1, 5 and 6 [37]. By offering a brief, validated metric in Malay, the current study provides frontline teams with a practical way to monitor the patient-participation pillar of WHOs Medication Without Harm challenge and the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) 2024–2025 best-practice bundle, which urges hospitals to embed barcode checks and robust reconciliation at every transition [38]. In this context, the strong psychometric performance supports its utility in safety surveillance, audits and research on behavioural determinants of medication safety in low- and middle-income countries.

At the item level, the strongest loadings arose from proactive communication (Items 5–6), mirroring evidence that interventions such as motivational interviewing and structured medication education have the greatest impact on safety behaviours among chronically ill inpatients [5]. By contrast, self-administration (Item 7) showed higher error variance and shared residual variance with Item 8, suggesting that Malaysian policies regulating bedside self-administration may still be ambiguous. Future quality-improvement work can exploit these differential item parameters to target coaching toward lower-loading behaviours, particularly bringing medicines from home and safe self-administration, where Malaysian patients appear less confident. The observed loadings (0.605–0.859) and derived communalities (0.366–0.738) exceed simulation-based thresholds ( > 0.50 for loadings; ≥ 0.30–0.35 for communalities) required for accurate parameter recovery with samples of ≈ 100 [25, 26].

Acceptance of bedside self-administration varies cross-culturally and Malaysia’s relatively high ‘power-distance’ orientation may partly explain the lower loadings observed for the two self-administration items. In high power-distance settings, patients socialised to defer to professional authority can perceive self-management requests as challenging established hierarchies, leading to hesitation or passive compliance [39]. Sociological work in Malaysian hospitals likewise shows that patients often wait for explicit staff permission before questioning prescriptions or handling medicines, a behaviour rooted in respect for medical authority and the desire to avoid appearing disrespectful [40]. A culturally sensitive implementation strategy should therefore include explicit reassurance, from nurses and pharmacists, that self-administration is not a breach of etiquette but an endorsed safety practice.

The M-IMSIS offers direct practical applications for healthcare providers and decision-makers. At the clinical level, individual scores can help identify patients who require more intensive medication counselling or motivational support. At the ward level, aggregated results can be incorporated into safety audits and used to highlight specific behaviours such as medication reconciliation or self-administration that may require targeted interventions. For hospital administrators, the M-IMSIS provides a benchmarking tool for patient engagement in medication safety, which can inform policy development, staff training, and the allocation of resources to strengthen patient-centred safety practices. At the system level, adoption of the M-IMSIS can support national quality and safety strategies, aligning local practice with international frameworks such as WHOs Medication Without Harm challenge and The Joint Commission’s National Patient Safety Goals.

Beyond its psychometric rigour, the M-IMSIS is readily actionable across multiple domains of practice. Psychologists can integrate the scale into cognitive-behavioural and motivational-interviewing programmes, using baseline scores to identify patients who need more intensive coaching and tracking gains in activation over time, an approach associated with improved medication adherence and overall quality of care [41, 42]. Item-level findings likewise help health-communication specialists fine-tune infographics, bedside posters and teach-back scripts so that safety messages resonate with specific literacy levels and concerns. In routine practice, displaying M-IMSIS scores in the electronic chart allows clinicians to reference the patient’s activation status during medication discussions, thereby aligning treatment choices with each individual’s desired degree of involvement and strengthening shared-decision-making processes [43, 44]. For researchers, the scale supplies a theory-aligned behavioural endpoint, making it possible to examine whether improvements in activation mediate reductions in administration errors and other preventable harms.

Strengths and limitations

This study followed a rigorous, industry-standard translation pathway, beginning with dual independent forward translations, a reconciled synthesis, blind back-translations, expert harmonisation and cognitive de-briefing interviews. The procedure mirrors the ISPOR 10-step “Principles of Good Practice” [45] and the Beaton et al. cross-cultural adaptation framework [46]. Both are gold standards for ensuring semantic, experiential and conceptual equivalence of patient-reported outcome measures. Importantly, our multidisciplinary expert panels deliberately covered clinical care, medical education, hospital quality management and other relevant fields, thereby capturing distinct professional and lay perspectives that reduce construct under-representation and improve content validity.

Psychometric testing was equally comprehensive. Model-fit indices (CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.94, RMSEA < 0.06) met widely accepted thresholds and internal-consistency estimates = 0.90; composite reliability = 0.90 exceeded the ≥ 0.80 benchmark recommended for applied research. By sampling across four high-volume adult wards (medical, surgical, orthopaedic, obstetrics-gynaecology), the study maximised clinical heterogeneity and upheld ecological validity; patients ranged in age, length of stay and diagnostic mix, reflecting the everyday contexts in which the scale will be used. No missing data were reported, further strengthening confidence in the parameter estimates.

This validation was conducted in a single tertiary hospital where almost all respondents were Malay (99%), limiting the generalisability of findings to more linguistically and culturally diverse settings. Potential differences that might arise in Mandarin-, Tamil-, or English-speaking wards therefore remain unexplored. Although the sample size of 100 participants was adequate for CFA based on current simulation evidence, larger samples (≥200–300) are typically recommended to enable more robust testing of measurement invariance across subgroups and to strengthen predictive validity. Replication in larger and more heterogeneous populations is thus essential.

The cross-sectional, self-report design also imposed several constraints. First, test–retest reliability and sensitivity-to-change analyses could not be undertaken. Second, criterion validity was not assessed, as responses were not triangulated with objective outcomes such as barcode-recorded administration errors or discrepancies identified during medication reconciliation. Furthermore, allowing a correlated residual between the two self-administration items suggests potential overlap in content or the presence of an autonomy-related subdimension that requires further qualitative clarification. The absence of differential item functioning (DIF) testing also means that systematic biases across demographic or contextual factors such as sex, age, health literacy, or ward type cannot be excluded.

Finally, the present study did not examine associations between M-IMSIS scores and actual patient outcomes, such as medication adherence, reconciliation discrepancies, or documented medication errors. Future research should investigate these relationships to establish the predictive validity of the M-IMSIS and determine its practical value in informing and improving medication safety practices.

Directions for future research

Future work should first extend the M-IMSIS across Malaysia’s multilingual landscape. Translating and culturally adapting the scale into Mandarin, Tamil and selected Indigenous languages, followed by scalar-invariance testing, would allow meaningful nationwide benchmarking and ensure that linguistic nuances do not mask differences in medication-safety engagement. Once a multilingual suite is in place, researchers can examine the scale’s predictive value by linking baseline scores to barcode-verified medication-error logs, adverse-drug-event–related readmissions and associated cost data, thereby clarifying whether higher engagement indeed translates into measurable clinical and economic gains.

A second priority is to establish responsiveness. Randomised trials that embed the IMSIS as a primary endpoint, whether evaluating digital “5 Moments” dashboards, motivational-interviewing pathways or pharmacist-led discharge coaching, should calculate minimal important differences using anchor-based approaches. Complementing these trials, longitudinal cohort studies that track IMSIS trajectories from admission through 30 days post-discharge would illuminate how patient engagement evolves across care transitions, a key objective of the WHO Patient-Safety Action Plan 2021–2030. Finally, advanced psychometric work such as Rasch or bifactor modelling can refine the item hierarchy, probe local dependency and detect misfitting items, particularly within the self-administration domain identified here as potentially multidimensional. Together, these strands of research will establish the IMSIS as a robust tool for quality monitoring, intervention evaluation and policy benchmarking throughout Malaysia’s diverse hospital system.

Conclusion

The M-IMSIS fills a critical measurement gap, offering a concise, culturally adapted and psychometrically validated tool for assessing inpatient participation in medication safety. Its high reliability and solid construct validity parallel or surpass contemporary instruments and align closely with international safety frameworks such as the WHO Medication Without Harm challenge and the Joint Commission’s 2024 NPSGs. While external validation and longitudinal research are required, hospitals can immediately deploy the M-IMSIS to identify low-engagement behaviours, monitor quality-improvement initiatives and advance Malaysia’s contribution to the global patient-safety agenda. The M-IMSIS can serve as a model for similar adaptation efforts in other multilingual Southeast Asian contexts, thereby contributing to regional patient-safety improvements.
