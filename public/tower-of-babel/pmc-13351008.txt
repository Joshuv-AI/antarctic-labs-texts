Chiral LiPS–LiTFSI dimers enabling biomimetic electrocatalysis in lithium||sulfur batteries
Authors: Yangyang Dong, Dong Cai, Hongtian Ning, Meiling Shu, Haijin Ni, Shuo Yang, Xuemei Zhou, Huagui Nie, Haoran Tu, Chenghua Sun, Songbai Han, Zhi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lithium||sulfur batteries promise high energy density but are plagued by sluggish polysulfide conversion under lean-electrolyte conditions. Here we show the formation of chiral LiPS-LiTFSI dimers, fundamentally reshaping the intrinsic chemistry of polysulfides. Building on this finding, we design biomimetic MOF catalysts (MOF-L-His-Cu) in which L-histidine-Cu centers selectively recognize the chiral dimers, accelerating sulfur redox via enzyme-like kinetics. Such MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst achieves 1.84-fold higher activity than its D-enantiomer, enabling lithium||sulfur pouch cells with specific energy of 408 Wh kg−1 (based on the mass of the whole pouch cell deducting the package) at high sulfur loading (12 mg cm−2) and low electrolyte/sulfur ratio (3 μL mg−1). Our findings suggest that chiral coordination could be a previously overlooked factor in lithium||sulfur chemistry. This insight may inform the design of selective electrocatalysts for next-generation energy storage, representing a potential step forward in the field.

Introduction

Development of lithium||sulfur (Li||S) batteries has become one of the hottest research areas in high-performance energy storage due to their attractive high specific energy density (~2600 Wh kg−1)1–3. While the formation of soluble lithium polysulfide (LiPS) intermediates is key to realizing the high energy capacity of the Li||S chemistry, these dissolved species simultaneously act as a primary cause of cell failure4–6. Especially, in the practical application of Li||S batteries (low-electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio and high-sulfur-loading), highly concentrated LiPS dramatically increase the viscosity of the electrolyte, resulting in limited ion diffusion capability and sluggish sulfur redox reaction (SRR) kinetics7,8. Slow kinetics exacerbates the polarization and deteriorates the rate performances9. More critically, the absence of a detailed understanding of LiPS–particularly their intrinsic states, electronic structures, and solvation behavior–has impeded precise control over their conversion and deposition processes10,11. It caused that most these batteries reported to date exhibit unsatisfactory electrochemical performance. Therefore, effective strategies to gain a fundamental understanding of LiPS and regulate their conversion kinetics at lean electrolyte conditions are urgently needed.

To address these challenges, Peled et al. investigated the redox processes of LiPS on a glassy carbon electrode in tetrahydrofuran (THF)12. This groundbreaking work has spurred further research into analyzing the solvation structure of LiPS. On this basis, Zhang et al. further investigated the solvation structure of the cationic LiPS and reveal that Li salt anions are involved in the solvation structure of LiPS and significantly influence their intrinsic reaction kinetics13. However, a comprehensive understanding of the coordination structure between LiPS and LiTFSI, especially regarding how LiPS, interact with the electrolyte components and form specific dimer complexes, remain elusive. Further exploration is requested to uncover the underlying coordination chemistry and unlock full potential of Li||S batteries.

Chiral sulfur-containing molecules are ubiquitous in natural products and play crucial roles in asymmetric catalysis and pharmaceutical synthesis14–16. Among them, sulfonimidate ester–a class of chiral compounds featuring hexacoordinate sulfur(VI) stereocenters–exhibit broad utility in chemical and biological applications17,18. Notably, we found that the structures of the Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers are consistent with that of sulfonimidate, with potential a chiral hexavalent sulfur center. On this basis, designing a tailored chiral catalyst, which can achieve selective recognition of chiral sulfur substrates and simultaneously boost the lean-electrolyte sulfur redox activity, is the key bridge between lab-scale demonstration and industrial commercialization of the high-energy Li||S batteries.

Natural enzymes orchestrate precise selectivity via the spatial and chiral organization of amino acids within their active sites19–24. This principle inspires the design of artificial enzyme mimics for electrocatalysis25,26. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), with programmable porosity and tunable coordination microenvironments, serve as ideal scaffolds for such biomimicry27,28. Integrating chiral amino acid moieties as molecular “recognition arms” into MOFs allows for the construction of artificial enzymes with higher analog accuracy. This strategy offers a promising avenue to develop efficient chiral electrocatalysts, elucidate LiPS coordination, and tackle key challenges in lean-electrolyte Li||S batteries.

Herein, we have successfully incorporated chiral histidine and the Cu metal center into the MOF-808 ring to construct the chiral biomimetic catalysts (MOF-L(D)-His-Cu) for advanced Li||S batteries. Through a series of in-situ and post-mortem spectroscopy and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations, it was found that the chiral matching between MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst and L-Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrate (L-substrate) leads to enhanced interfacial kinetics and promote LiPS conversion. The catalytic activity was quantified by Michaelis–Menten equations and MOF-L-His-Cu showed the highest catalytic efficiency for LiPS conversions. As a result, the positive electrode with MOF-L-His-Cu biomimetic catalyst delivers a high initial capacity of 1581 mAh g−1 at 0.34 A g−1, with a low capacity decay rate of 0.017% per cycle at 8.38 A g−1 over 2600 cycles. Moreover, the Li||S pouch cell with mere 1.0 wt% MOF-L-His-Cu exhibits a high specific energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 under a sulfur loading of 12.0 mg cm−2 and the E/S ratio of 3.0 μL mg−1. This work demonstrates chiral LiPS coordination structures and presents the design of chiral biomimetic catalysts with favorable selectivity toward chiral dimer substrates.

Results

Structural characterizations of chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers

The interaction between LiTFSI and LiPS is investigated to probe the coordination chemistry of LiPS, with Li2S6 selected as the model LiPS. Figure 1a shows the optimized structure of Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers by Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, where the S=O group of LiTFSI interacts with the Li atom of Li2S6 through Li∙∙∙O bonds (Li-bonds). The optimized coordinates for chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers are provided in Supplementary Data 1. Notably, the structures of the Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers are consistent with that of sulfonimidate ester, with a chiral hexavalent sulfur center. On this basis, we further investigated the ground state energy of two different chiral substrate dimers to explore their stability in Li||S system. The calculated values are the calculated binding energy for the whole structure with respect to free atoms. The results confirm that compared to D-Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrate (D-substrate), L-Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrate (L-substrate) exhibits lower ground state energy (−94.26 eV) (Supplementary Table 1). The population ratio between the two conformers was estimated via Boltzmann distribution theory to be ~3.0 × 10−3. Figure 1b exhibits the 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of the LiTFSI with or without the addition of Li2S6 in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) solvent under room temperature. The 19F NMR signal of the TFSI− anion exhibits a significant downfield shift in the presence of Li2S6. This shift indicates a reduction in LiTFSI ion pairing due to competitive anionic coordination upon its incorporation into the Li2S6 structure13. The 7Li NMR spectra of the Li2S6-LiTFSI system exhibit a pronounced shift, indicating that S62− competes with TFSI− for Li+ coordination sites upon the introduction of Li2S6 (Fig. 1c). This competition alters the lithium coordination environment, thereby reducing the shielding effect induced by the strong electron-withdrawing groups. To further investigate the coordination environment of LiPS in electrolyte, Raman spectra of LiTFSI solution with the addition of Li2S6 were collected (Fig. 1d). For the blank LiTFSI solution, the Raman signal in the wavenumber region of 730–750 cm−1 is assigned to the vibrational modes of TFSI− anions. This signal can be deconvoluted into two components, including uncoordinated TFSI− at 739 cm−1 (free TFSI−), and TFSI− coordinated with Li+ located at 742 cm−1 (CIP-TFSI−). With the addition of Li2S6, a special vibrational mode of TFSI− emerges at 746 cm−1, indicating bonding interaction between TFSI− and LiPS13. Most notably, as the concentration increases, the interaction intensity of Li2S6-LiTFSI increases significantly. To further investigate the chirality of Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers, we conducted optical rotation tests. Specifically, the polarimeter measures the rotation of plane-polarized light caused by an optically active substance, with the angle of rotation indicating the sample’s chiral properties (Supplementary Fig. S1)29. As illustrated in Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 2, Li2S6 and LiTFSI substrate solution did not exhibit significant optical signals. By contrast, the mixed solution of the two substrates exhibits a clear left polarized light signal. Since L-substrate possesses a lower ground-state energy, the mixed solution preferentially displays a left-handed photoresponse during chiral dimer racemization. Moreover, there was a noticeable increase in the left polarized photoresponsive signal corresponding to the concentration of Li2S6-LiTFSI solution. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy measures the differential absorption of left- and right-circularly polarized light and is used to probe the secondary structure and conformational changes of chiral molecules in solution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, a weak positive dissymmetry factor (g‑factor) signal was observed. These results confirm LiPS and LiTFSI form chiral dimers through Li-bond interactions, which were consistent with the DFT calculation results. Subsequently, similar trends are also observed in Na2S6-NaTFSI and K2S6-KTFSI solutions, suggesting that the universality of chiral dimers in alkali metal batteries (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Tables 3, 4).

Synthesis and characterization of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu

Inspired by the finely evolved structures of natural enzymes, the key amino acid residues within their catalytic pockets play a crucial role in enabling specific substrate recognition and preferential orientation, which collectively lead to remarkable catalytic selectivity30. Moreover, both the spatial arrangement and the stereochemical configuration of chiral amino acids govern the stereoselectivity and enantioselectivity of enzymatic reactions. Consequently, incorporating functional groups as recognition elements into confined nanospaces offers a promising strategy to achieve selective control in LiPS catalysis. To replicate the catalytic microenvironment of catechol oxidase, the chiral biomimetic catalysts, designated MOF-L(D)-Histidine-Copper (MOF-L(D)-His-Cu), were synthesized via post-synthetic modification of MOF-808 (Fig. 2a). Specifically, MOF-808 was selected as the structural backbone to simulate the folded peptide chains that naturally position amino acid residues. On this scaffold, chiral histidine moieties and Cu metal centers were sequentially introduced into the MOF cavities without compromising structural integrity, thereby creating an enzyme-like catalytic environment and introducing steric hindrance units. The microstructures of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu biomimetic catalysts were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), CD microscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT−IR), X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron (XPS) measurements. SEM images reveal that the as-prepared MOF-L-His-Cu exhibits an octahedral shape (~700 nm), closely resembling that of pristine MOF-808 and MOF-L-His (Supplementary Fig. S4)31. TEM and Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images (Supplementary Figs. S5, 6) further reveal a homogeneous distribution of zirconium (Zr), copper (Cu), oxygen (O), carbon (C), and nitrogen (N) throughout the MOF support, confirming the successful incorporation of His and Cu. More precisely, inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) analysis revealed that the contents of Zr and Cu in MOF-L-His-Cu were 3.2 wt% and 7.9 wt%, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). Furthermore, the His motif-induced chiral generation was investigated by CD spectrum. Accordingly, the obtained MOF-L(D)-His-Cu display obvious opposite mirror CD signals with the peak in the wavelength range of 200−240 nm (Fig. 2b), indicating the introduction of chirality into MOF-808 exoskeleton from the chelated L(D)-His-binding Cu centers. Interestingly, when two chiral catalysts are physically mixed in equal amounts, the CD signal is zero due to the perfect cancellation of their positive and negative signals, resulting in a racemic mixture (Supplementary Fig. S7). The XRD pattern of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu matches that of pristine MOF-808, suggesting that the incorporation of His-coordinated Cu units does not compromise the crystallinity of the MOF host (Supplementary Fig. S8). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements reveal that MOF-L(D)-His-Cu possesses a high specific surface area of 530.3 m2 g−1 with a predominantly microporous structure, which is beneficial for the efficient adsorption of sulfur species (Supplementary Fig. S9).

The structural features and local coordination environment of the fabricated catalysts were characterized by FT−IR and XPS. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S10, the FT−IR spectrum reveals an out-of-plane bending vibration of the N−H bond in the imidazole moiety at 821 cm−1 following the incorporation of His into the MOF framework. After introducing Cu active sites, this characteristic signal weakens considerably, which is attributed to coordination between Cu and N atoms. Further insight into the coordination environment was obtained via XPS measurements32,33. From the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Cu 2p shown in Supplementary Fig. S11a, two dominant peaks are located at 930.8 and 950.6 eV for CuI, which belong to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 electrons of Cu(I), respectively. After functionalization of MOF-L-His, the MOF-L-His-Cu sample exhibits additional peaks at higher binding energies−933.0 and 952.6 eV−which are characteristic of Cu(II) species. Moreover, the Cu(I) signals in MOF-L-His-Cu shift toward higher binding energies relative to those in the unmodified MOF, indicating enhanced electron transfer between the Cu centers and the MOF-L-His framework. Additionally, compared to MOF-His, MOF-L(D)-His-Cu shows a marked increase in metal−N content and a significant decrease in pyrrolic−N content (Supplementary Fig. S11b), confirming coordination between Cu and pyrrolic nitrogen. The O 1s peak position shifts negatively upon introducing Cu species into MOF-L(D)-His, demonstrating the formation of Cu−O bonds (Supplementary Fig. S11c).

For various catalytic applications of MOFs, stability, mainly including chemical, thermal and electrolyte stability, is one of the major concerns. The electrochemical stability of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu was verified by symmetric cell tests. We carried out CV trials at room temperature within a potential window of −1.5–1.5 V at a sweep rate of 10 mV s−1. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S12a–e, the CV curves exhibit a pair of rectangular-like redox peaks, indicative of pseudocapacitive behavior for MOF-L(D)-His-Cu. The overlapping CV profiles in subsequent cycles confirm the electrochemical stability and reversibility of the material. Furthermore, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S13, the XRD pattern reveals no significant change in crystal structure after heat treatment and electrolyte immersion, demonstrating favorable thermal and electrolyte stability for MOF-L(D)-His-Cu.

The enzymatic performance of the MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst in Li||S batteries was investigated in-situ using UV–vis spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. S14). For the MOF-L-His-Cu electrode, characteristic absorption peaks appear at approximately 492, 475, 420, and 617 nm, corresponding to S82−, S62−, S42−, and S32−/S3*− species, respectively. Notably, as the discharge depth increases, the MOF-L-His-Cu electrode exhibits a much more rapid decline in the intensities of S82−, S62−, and S42−, along with a sharper rise in the S32−/S3*− signal, compared to the other two electrodes (Supplementary Figs. S15, 16). These observations suggest that the generated S32−/S3*− anions accelerate the conversion of LiPSs34. On this basis, the enzymatic activity of MOF-L-His-Cu was further quantified by determining its steady-state kinetic constants (Fig. 2c). The Michaelis–Menten (MM) model, a widely used framework in biochemistry, was employed for this analysis35. The Michaelis constant (Km) represents the substrate concentration at half-saturation and reflects the binding affinity between the enzyme and substrate–lower Km values indicate stronger binding. As shown in Fig. 2d, the positive electrode with MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst exhibits the Km value of 5.0 × 10–3 mM, which is notably lower than that of MOF-D-His-Cu (9.2 × 10–3 mM). In contrast, the CNT-modified positive electrode exhibits kinetics that do not conform to the MM model, indicating a lack of intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. S17). These results demonstrate that the positive electrode with MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst possesses favorable biomimetic enzyme reaction kinetics, enhancing substrate adsorption and improving catalytic reaction rate and efficiency.

The kinetic behaviors of MOF-L-His-Cu, MOF-D-His-Cu, and CNT for LiPS reduction were investigated by combining the measurement results of linear cyclic voltammetry (LSV) and rotating disc electrode (RDE), strictly following the protocols developed for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR)36. The testing device is shown in Supplementary Fig. S18. LSV curves were obtained to determine half-wave potential, diffusion-limited current density (JD), Tafel slope (η) and turnover frequency (TOF). The half-wave potential in LSV is a potential at which the polarographic current is half that of the JD. Whereas the JD is the current at which the surface concentration of active species decreases to zero. Both of these two currents can act as a good descriptor of the reaction kinetics. As shown in Fig. 2e, the MOF-L-His-Cu displays an optimal activity with the high JD (−5.9 mA cm−2), as well as the most positive half-wave potential at 2.14 V. By contrast, MOF-D-His-Cu and CNT demonstrate rather low JD (−4.8 and −2.1 mA cm−2) and negative half-wave potential (2.11 V and 1.99 V, respectively), suggesting enhanced sulfur conversion with the introduction of the MOF-L-His-Cu. Notably, the η for MOF-L-His-Cu (63.8 mV dec−1) is much lower than that of MOF-D-His-Cu (88.8 mV dec−1) and CNT (197.6 mV dec−1), further confirming the considerably accelerated reaction kinetics and higher electrocatalytic activity (Supplementary Figs. S19, 20). The electron transfer number (n) and kinetic-limited current density (Jk, i.e., the current density under negligible mass-transport limitations) for the SRR were determined using the Koutecky–Levich equation across different rotation speeds37. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S21, the plot of J−1 versus ω−1/2 for both MOF-L-His-Cu and MOF-D-His-Cu exhibits good linearity across potentials ranging from 1.9 to 1.6 V. Notably, MOF-L-His-Cu achieves a higher electron transfer number (~15.2) and a higher Jk (~61.2 mA cm−2), indicating that this biomimetic catalyst promotes more complete sulfur reduction and faster conversion of LiPSs into insoluble products (Supplementary Fig. S22). To further rationalize catalytic activity, the TOF was calculated, which reflects the number of reactions occurring per active site per unit time. Assuming Cu atoms as the active sites for the SRR, the TOF was derived based on the normalized Cu content. MOF-L-His-Cu exhibits a TOF of 0.33 s−1, slightly higher than that of MOF-D-His-Cu (0.31 s−1)(Fig. 2f). In addition, MOF-L-His-Cu demonstrates a higher mass activity of 7.65 A mg−1. Based on the above analysis, these results confirm that the MOF-L-His-Cu biomimetic catalyst effectively lowers the energy barriers for LiPS conversion and Li2S nucleation, thereby accelerating the overall electrochemical reaction kinetics.

Compatibility of chiral MOF-L(D)-His-Cu catalysts with chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrates in Li||S batteries

The compatibility between chiral catalysts and substrates is the core of achieving efficient and highly selective catalytic reactions38. Generally, suitable catalysts can further stabilize specific transition states of substrates, reduce activation energy, and accelerate reactions. To further explore the relationship between MOF-L(D)-His-Cu and Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers, their binding energies were evaluated by DFT simulations. The optimized coordinates of the MOF-L(D)-His-Cu catalyst after adsorption of the chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrates are provided in Supplementary Data 3. As shown in Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Table 6, the optimized geometric configurations for the adsorption of L-substrate and D-substrate gave binding energies (BE) of −426.95 and −425.62 eV on MOF-L-His-Cu (L-catalyst), and −421.01 and −420.89 eV on MOF-D-His-Cu (D-catalyst), respectively. This indicated that the dynamic conformational matching between the L-catalyst and L-substrate significantly enhanced the adsorption capability for LiPS, mainly through Cu–S bonds. This is further confirmed by the smaller atomic spacing (2.18 Å) between Cu and S atoms in L-catalyst/L-substrate system (Supplementary Table 7). Therefore, the strong electron stretching effect between Cu and S atoms induced a significant change in the bond length of Cu–N bonds. Notably, during catalyst-substrate dynamic binding, proton transfer occurs at the catalytic center. Molecular orbital calculations serve as a crucial theoretical approach for evaluating catalyst-substrate compatibility39,40. In terms of redox kinetics, the electron transfer between Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO)/Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO) of catalysts and HOMO/LUMO of sulfur species plays a crucial role. At the atomic scale, the LUMO of active center acts as an electron–acceptor to facilitate the S–S breakage, accelerating the reduction kinetics41. As presented in Fig. 3f, the large energy gap between the LUMO of MOF-D-His-Cu and HOMO of L(D)-substrates creates a high energy barrier for electron transfer from MOF-D-His-Cu to L(D)-substrates. This significantly reduces the reduction kinetics of the sulfur species during the discharging process. In contrast, the elevated LUMO in MOF-L-His-Cu effectively narrowed the energy gap between active center and HOMO orbital of L(D)-substrates (ΔE = 0.27/0.11 eV), which effectively facilitates the electron transfer between both orbitals, thereby enabling faster reduction kinetic (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, when symmetry and energy level requirements are satisfied, the spatial overlap extent of molecular orbitals becomes the determining factor for the dominant interaction mode42. Unfortunately, in L-catalyst/D-substrate system, only a few sulfur atomic orbitals of the substrate’s HOMO exhibit effective overlap with the LUMO of the catalytic center. In L-catalyst/L-substrate system, the HOMO of the substrate is primarily localized at the polysulfide terminal. This spatial distribution enables significant orbital overlap with the LUMO of the catalytic center, facilitating effective electron transfer. Consequently, chiral dynamic matching refines the spatial conformation and orientation of the adsorbed Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers, which in turn determines the thermodynamics (adsorption energy) and kinetics (adsorption rate) of the Cu–S bond formation process. Importantly, this effect does not imply that the local Cu–S bond itself is chiral; rather, the chiral environment governs the probability, geometry, and efficiency of Cu–S bond formation. Building on this, we further analyzed the coordination environment between Cu atoms in the L-catalyst and S atoms in L(D)-substrates using radial distribution function (RDF). Compared to the D-substrate, the L-catalyst matched with the L-substrate exhibited a significantly enhanced characteristic peak at a shorter atomic distance (~2.33 Å) (Fig. 3g), indicating strong electron cloud overlap between the Cu atoms and the S atoms. This interaction forms a lower-energy, more stable chemical bond. This finding was further supported by coordination number analysis, which revealed an increase in the coordination number of S atoms from the L-substrate around the Cu atoms of the L-catalyst starting at 2.18 Å (Fig. 3h). The strong short-range interaction significantly polarizes and activates the Li2S6 molecule, weakening its S–S bond and substantially reducing the reaction energy barrier.

Dynamic structural evolutions of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu and chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimers during SRR have been further clarified by AIMD studies. The optimized coordinates from AIMD simulations of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu interacting with chiral Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrates are provided in Supplementary Data 4. As shown in Fig. 3i, j and Supplementary Fig. S23, both D-catalyst/L-substrate and D-catalyst/D-substrate systems underwent rapid structural degradation (e.g., ligand dissociation and active-site distortion) within 3.0 ps, leading to a decrement in turnover frequency. Differently, L-catalyst exhibited optimal stereochemical alignment with L-substrate, preserving its coordination geometry and enabling stable catalytic cycling.

Investigation of sulfur conversion mechanism mediated by MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst

The surface adsorption of LiPS represents the critical initial step in the heterogeneous electrocatalysis of sulfur conversion reactions43. To elucidate the intrinsic chemical interactions between MOF-L-His-Cu and LiPS, XPS measurements of MOF-L-His-Cu were implemented before and after adsorption of Li2S6. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. S24, the S 2p spectrum is deconvoluted into peaks corresponding to terminal sulfur (ST), bridged sulfur (SB), thiosulfate and polythionate, respectively. The increase in ST and SB content indicates that LiPS is anchored on the surface of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu after the adsorption experiment. Most notably, upon adsorbing Li2S6, the proportion of ST in MOF-L-His-Cu increased significantly. This indicates a strong electronic interaction between MOF-L-His-Cu and Li2S6, which facilitates rapid polysulfide conversion and suppresses the shuttle effect. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S25, the MOF-L-His-Cu cell shows the lowest shuttle current value (4.8 μA) compared with the control samples, which corroborates the favorable LiPS trapping ability of MOF-L-His-Cu in working Li||S batteries.

Based on this good prerequisite, the LiPS conversion kinetics, including liquid–liquid and liquid–solid conversions, were deeply investigated on MOF-L-His-Cu positive electrode by comparing with other positive electrodes. The catalytic kinetics regarding the initial liquid–liquid conversion process between dissolved LiPS (Li2Sn, 4 ≤ n ≤ 8) was evaluated via symmetric cell techniques using identical pairs of electrodes (CNT/MOF-L-His-Cu, CNT/MOF-D-His-Cu, CNT/MOF-L-His, CNT/MOF-D-His, CNT/MOF-808 or CNT) and Li2S6 electrolyte. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S26, the electrode with MOF-L-His-Cu catalyst displays stronger response currents (1.36 mA), demonstrating the reliable electrocatalytic activity of MOF-L-His-Cu toward LiPS conversion. Besides, a potentiostatic nucleation/dissolution experiment affords vital evidence on the kinetic promotion for liquid–solid transition pertaining to Li2S deposition/dissociation44. Therefore, the reduction of short-chain LiPS on various cells to form Li2S was monitored by the potentiostatic Li2S nucleation tests at 2.04 V. Through fitting i–t curves (Supplementary Fig. S27), we ranked the capacity that corresponds to Li2S deposition as MOF-L-His-Cu (187.6 mAh g−1) > MOF-D-His-Cu (143.7 mAh g−1) > CNT (64.4 mAh g−1) with a response time of 519, 937, and 1489 s, respectively. By contrast, the MOF-L-His-Cu cell shows much faster responsivity, higher current density, and larger Li2S precipitation capacity, pointing to accelerated Li2S deposition kinetics on MOF-L-His-Cu surface. To further evaluate the electron/ion transport during sulfur conversion, we used electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests to determine the potential barrier for electron/ion transport. The high mass loading of active materials and low usage of electrolyte are essential requirements for the practical application of batteries50. However, a low E/S ratio signifies a high concentration of LiPS, which often results in severe shuttle effects and sluggish sulfur conversion. As shown in Fig. 5e, even at a high sulfur areal loading of 7.6 mg cm−2 and an E/S ratio of 5.5 μL mg−1, the thick MOF-L-His-Cu electrode still afforded a capacity of 850 mAh g−1 after 65 cycles, whereas the cells with CNT and MOF-D-His-Cu positive electrodes demonstrate significantly decayed capacities. Remarkably, the electrochemical performance of the MOF-L-His-Cu positive electrode is competitive compared with several recent reports (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Table. 15), highlighting the commercial feasibility of MOF-L-His-Cu biomimetic catalyst for Li||S batteries51–56.

Discussion

In summary, this study introduces chiral LiPS coordination structures and chiral biomimetic catalysts (MOF-L(D)-His-Cu) with favorable selectivity toward chiral dimer substrates. In-situ and post-mortem experimental results together with AIMD simulations unveil that the chiral matching between MOF-L-His-Cu catalysts and L-Li2S6-LiTFSI dimer substrates leads to improve interfacial kinetics and to promote LiPS conversion. As a result, the Li||S batteries catalyzed by MOF-L-His-Cu exhibit favorable electrochemical performance: the batteries deliver a high initial discharge capacity of 1581 mAh g−1 at 0.34 A g−1 and a low capacity decay rate of 0.017% per cycle at 8.38 A g−1 over 2600 cycles. Moreover, the Li||S pouch cell with mere 1.0 wt% MOF-L-His-Cu exhibits a high specific energy density of 408 Wh kg−1 under a sulfur loading of 12.0 mg cm−2 and the E/S ratio of 3.0 μL mg−1. This work enlightens understanding of coordination structures between LiPS and LiTFSI, and also provides a light on the design of biomimetic catalysts for Li||S battery kinetics regulation at lean electrolyte condition.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

The following chemicals and solvents are purchased and used without further purification. Zirconyl chloride octahydrate (ZrOCl2·8H2O, ≥99.9%, metals basis, Macklin), 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (H3BTC, ≥98%, Aladdin), L-histidine (>99%, Aladdin), D-histidine (>99%, Aladdin), Cuprous iodide (CuI, ≥99.999% metals basis, Aladdin), N,N’-dimethylformamide (DMF, >99.9%, GC, Aladdin), formic acid (FA, >99%, Aladdin), acetone (>99%, GC, Aladdin), acetonitrile (≥99.9%, HPLC, Aladdin), carbon disulfide (CS2, >99.9%, Aladdin), thanol (EtOH, AR, H2O < 0.3%, Aladdin), 1,3-dioxolane (DOL, >99.8%, Aladdin), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME, >99.5%, GC, Aladdin), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.9%, Aladdin), trichloromethane (CHCl3, Aladdin), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Sigma-Aldrich), bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, >99.9%, Aladdin), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, >99.99%, metals basis, Aladdin), sublimed sulfur (>99.99%, Aladdin), Lithium sulfide (Li2S, >99.98%, metals basis, Aladdin), multiwalled carbon nanotube (CNTs, >95%, inside diameter, 3–5 nm, Aladdin), Nmethyl pyrrolidone (NMP, >99.9%, Aladdin), tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME, >99.5%, Aladdin) and Distilled water.

Synthesis of MOF-808 composites

H3BTC (210 mg) and ZrOCl2·8H2O (970 mg) were dissolved in a mixture of anhydrous DMF (30 mL) and FA (30 mL)64,65. Then, the mixture was placed in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h. The resultant white precipitate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm and washed with DMF and acetone three times. Finally, the product was dried overnight at 50 °C.

Synthesis of MOF-L(D)-His composites

L(D)-histidine (745 mg) was dissolved in water (8 mL) and the mixture was placed in an oven at 85 °C to prepare a saturated solution. MOF-808 (160 mg) was ultrasonically suspended in an L-histidine-saturated solution and kept at 85 °C oven overnight. MOF-L(D)-His was centrifuged to remove the supernatant when the reaction mixture was cooled to about 50 °C (before the recrystallization of L-histidine). The resultant white product was centrifuged and washed with water and acetone three times. Finally, MOF-L(D)-His was dried overnight at 50 °C.

Synthesis of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu composites

CuI (47.8 mg) was dissolved in acetonitrile (7.5 mL) while MOF-L(D)-His (75 mg) was dissolved in acetonitrile (1.9 mL). The mixture was stirred for 24 h at 65 °C. The resultant green product was washed with acetonitrile five times and dried overnight at 50 °C.

Preparation of MOF-L-His-Cu positive electrode sheets

The sulfur powder was mixed with commercial CNTs at a mass ratio of 4:1 by ground milling. The obtained mixture was stirred in CS2 for 12 h, followed by heating at 155 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven, which allowed sulfur to immerse into the CNTs and form CNTs-S composites. The 80 wt% of above CNTs-S composite, 14 wt% CNT conductive additive, 5 wt% PVDF and 1 wt% MOF-L-His-Cu were then mixed in NMP solvent to produce homogeneous slurry and cast onto an Al foil with a scraper blade, followed by drying at 60 °C in vacuum for 12 h. MOF-D-His-Cu, MOF-L-His, MOF-D-His and MOF-808 positive electrodes can be prepared by a similar method. Finally, the obtained positive electrode was punched into a disk of 14 mm in diameter to prepare the CNT-S@MOF-L-His-Cu, CNT-S@MOF-D-His-Cu, CNT-S@MOF-L-His, CNT-S@MOF-D-His, CNT-S@MOF-808 and CNT-S (abbreviated as MOF-L-His-Cu, MOF-D-His-Cu, MOF-L-His, MOF-D-His, MOF-808 and CNT) positive electrodes. The average sulfur loading of the positive electrode was ~1.4 mg cm−2 for the normal electrochemical measurements. The sulfur loading electrodes of 7.6 mg cm−2 were also prepared for the high sulfur mass loading experiments. For the high sulfur mass loading experiments, the commercial polyacrylonitrile copolymer binder (LA133) was substituted for the PVDF binder.

Electrochemical evaluation of the Li||S coin cells

The CR2025 coin cells were assembled using MOF-L-His-Cu, MOF-D-His-Cu, MOF-L-His, MOF-D-His, MOF-808 or CNT positive electrode, Li negative electrode (≥99.9%, China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., diameter 14.3 mm), Celgard 2500 separator (25 μm thickness, 55% porosity) and traditional electrolyte (1.0 M LiTFSI in DME:DOL = 1:1 (v:v) with 1% LiNO3) in an Ar-filled glovebox (<0.1 ppm O2 and H2O, Universal, MIKROUNA). The E/S usage used was controlled at ~15 μL mg−1 for 1.4 mg cm−2 and 5.5 μL mg−1 for high sulfur loading electrodes. Cyclic voltammetry (CV, potential range: 1.6−2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+), sweep rate: 0.1 mV s−1) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, frequency range: 0.01 Hz−200 KHz, AC voltage amplitude: 5 mV) measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) at an ambient temperature (25 °C). The rate, cycling and galvanostatic charge/discharge curves were marked with a Neware CT-4008T-5V20mA-164 system between 1.6 and 2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+) under various C-rates (1C = 1675 mAh g−1) at room temperature. The capacity of Li||S battery was calculated according to the sulfur mass of the positive electrode.

Assembly and evaluation of the Li||S pouch cells

The potential applications of MOF-L(D)-His-Cu were evaluated in Li||S pouch cells (4.3 cm × 5.5 cm in dimension). The cell fabrication process was carried out in a dry room by assembling the positive electrode slurry on Al current collector (Canrd), the Li negative electrode and the Celgard 2500 separator into Al-laminated films (Canrd), followed by the injection of the electrolyte (E/S ratio = 3 μL mg−1) and final encapsulation in an argon-filled glovebox. The Al and Ni tabs (Canrd) were welded together with the positive electrodes and negative electrodes, respectively, and introduced for outward connection. The cell was fabricated using a sandwich-like structural design with a sulfur content of approximately 90 wt% and a sulfur areal loading 12 mg cm−2. Electrochemical measurements for pouch cells were conducted similarly to that of coin cells.

The n can therefore be derived from the slope of the linear plot of JLDC−1 versus ω−1/2 (K–L plot), according to the relation n = 1/(0.62KFCD2/3υ−1/6).

Li2S nucleation tests

A 0.25 M Li2S8 solution was prepared by dispersing sulfur and Li2S (7:1 molar ratio) in TEGDME at 50 °C for 24 h with vigorous stirring. The MOF-L-His-Cu, MOF-D-His-Cu and CNT electrodes for Li2S nucleation study were prepared in a similar way to the symmetric cell experiments. The 2025 coin cells were assembled with MOF-L-His-Cu, MOF-D-His-Cu or CNT as the positive electrode, Li foil as the negative electrode, Celgard 2500 membrane as the separator, Li2S8 solution and TEGDME solution with the identical usage of 40 μL as the catholyte and anolyte, respectively. For Li2S nucleation measurements, the cells were galvanostatically discharged to 2.06 V (vs Li/Li+) at 0.112 mA and subsequently held at 2.05 V potentiostatically for nucleating Li2S until the current dropped below 10−5 A.

In-situ neutron diffraction experiments

In-situ neutron diffraction experiments were conducted at the Multi-Physics Instrument (MPI) of the China Spallation Neutron Source (CSNS) in Dongguan, Guangdong, China. This facility supports a wide range of materials research and provides specialized environments for operando structural studies of cycling batteries. Prior to the measurements, MOF-L-His-Cu and MOF-D-His-Cu cells were mounted onto the scattering cavity using a sample holder equipped with an insulated clamp fixture. The distance from the upper end of the holder to the cell center was approximately 80 mm. Electrical wires were wound around the holder and covered with neutron-shielding materials to prevent extraneous signals. One end of each wire was connected to the cell, and the other end to an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E)66. After completing the equipment and cell setup, a series of tests was performed. Rietveld refinements were carried out using GSAS-II, with a manually selected background function. Peak profiles were modeled using a Pseudo-Voigt function, with a shape mixing parameter (α) of 0.018632925054274437. The Gaussian broadening parameters were beta-0 (0.025096116404318136), beta-1 (0.00016284673859025408), and beta-q (−2.8253010335140135e-05); the Lorentzian broadening parameters were sig-0 (−122.69432012873185), sig-1 (699.1480722445735), sig-2 (25.93950751783386), and sig-q (11.487725288563462). Instrumental parameters, including sample-to-detector distances (X: 0.3909999085274728, Y: 0.355954390775463, Z: 0.0) and a zero offset of 40.178806508677475, were also refined.

In-situ UV–vis spectroscopy measurements

The UV–vis spectroscopy was conducted on a Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–vis spectrometer at different states during charging and discharging. The spectro-electrochemical cell was a 1 cm path-length quartz cuvette with the MOF-L-His-Cu coated glassy carbon electrode. A platinum wire was used as the counter electrode. All the potentials are referenced to Ag/AgCl. The 0.25 × 10−3 M Li2S8 solutions were used as the electrolyte, which was prepared by mixing sulfur and Li2S powders (mass ratio: 7:1) into DMSO solvent for 24 h with vigorous stirring. The cell was assembled in an Ar-filled glovebox. All spectra were background-subtracted for the DMSO solution contribution (marked as a blank and automatically subtracted from subsequent spectra) before the initiation of any electrochemical reactions. The UV–vis absorbance was normalized by making the absorbance at 2.8 V as 1. For comparison, the working electrode coated with MOF-D-His-Cu or CNT was also measured in the same way.

In-situ FT–IR spectroscopy measurement

In-situ FT–IR spectra were recorded using a Thermo Fisher iS50 spectrometer. The MOF-L-His-Cu slurry was coated onto a carbon paper film, which served as the positive electrode. A lithium sheet and a porous Celgard 2500 membrane were used as the negative electrode and separator, respectively, to assemble a Li||S cell. Based on the Lambert-Beer law, the attenuation of infrared light passing through a thin electrolyte layer at a 45° angle before reaching the electrode surface was estimated to be approximately 0.3. Prior to data collection, the battery was subjected to two charge-discharge cycles within a potential range of 1.6–2.8 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. Subsequently, spectral measurements were performed with an accumulation time of 10 s, recorded at intervals of 0.1 V25.

Theoretical calculation

Spin-polarized density functional theory calculations were carried out under the scheme of generalized gradient approximation67, with the use of PBE functional68 and double numerical polarized (DNP) basis, as embedded in DMol3 package69,70. A global orbital cutoff with a radius of 4.4 Å was employed, under which all structures have been fully relaxed with total energy and atomic force converged to 10−6 Ha and 0.005 Ha/Å (1 Ha ≈ 27.2114 eV). DNP basis has been extensively tested, whose size is comparable to the Gaussian basis 6-311 + G** sets71. During the calculations, van der Waals interaction has been taken into account using the DFT-D dispersion approach (Tkatchenko-Scheffler scheme72) as embedded in DMol3.
