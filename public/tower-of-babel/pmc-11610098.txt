A structural equation modelling to explore the determinants of mental health disorders among reproductive-aged women in Nepal: a nation-wide cross-sectional survey
Authors: Md. Ashfikur Rahman, Mortuja Mahamud Tohan, Sayeeda Zaman, Md. Amirul Islam, Md. Sazedur Rahman, Md. Hasan Howlader, Satyajit Kundu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSimilar to other countries, reproductive-age women in Nepal frequently encounter diverse mental disorders, yet they receive inadequate screening and attention. This research seeks to address this gap by conducting a comprehensive examination of the prevalence and factors associated with mental disorders of reproductive-age women in Nepal.MethodsThis study used the nationally representative Nepal Demographic and Health Survey 2022 dataset to examine the association between mental disorders and potential confounders. Screening tools for Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) were employed, and descriptive and inferential statistics, including linear regression and structural equation modeling, were utilized in the analysis.ResultsThe study found that 22% of Nepalese women aged 15 to 49 reported anxiety symptoms, while 5% reported depressive symptoms. The Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) reveals that anxiety had the most significant positive effect on depression (β = 0.683, p < 0.001), followed by emotional violence (β = 0.139, p < 0.001). Marital status (β = 0.072, p < 0.001) and the presence of genital discharge (β = 0.084, p < 0.001) or ulcer (β = 0.058, p < 0.001) in the last 12 months were also associated with higher levels of depression. Conversely, self-rated health status (β=-0.121, p < 0.001) was negatively associated with depression, indicating that better-perceived health was linked to lower levels of depression.ConclusionAlthough the prevalence of anxiety symptoms is higher than that of depression, there is a positive relationship between these two. Consequently, it is crucial to address the identified factors contributing to anxiety and depression in this population group through behavioral and policy interventions to enhance the mental well-being of reproductive-age women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06249-2.

Background

Nowadays, mental disorders have become an unavoidable crisis, seeping into every corner of our lives, from our workplaces to our sleep. It is often considered the “hidden epidemic” of the 21st century, while mental health is recognized as a universal human right for all individuals [1]. In 2019, 970.1 million people worldwide were affected by mental health issues, and according to a comprehensive study, it is anticipated that half of the world’s population may experience a mental disorder at some point in their lifetime [2, 3]. Gender is a significant factor that influences the prevalence of mental disorders, where women are more likely to develop those conditions compared to men [4]. Particularly, the impact of mental health on reproductive-age women is profound and multifaceted [5]. However, in light of the growing significance of this matter, women’s mental and reproductive health have been explicitly embraced as essential elements of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 [6].

Women are noticeably at least two times more vulnerable to depression and anxiety than men, both of which negatively impact their overall well-being and functioning [5]. World Health Organization (WHO) reported that the global prevalence of depression and anxiety among women is approximately 5.1% and 4.6%, respectively [7]. Mental illness among women in the reproductive age group is a severe public health concern since they have to perform multifactorial gender and social roles, from childbearing and child-rearing to managing the family [8]. In addition, biological factors, specifically reproductive life events such as menstruation, pregnancy, the postpartum period, and menopause—have an adverse impact on the mental well-being of women [5, 8].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated that mental disorders accounted for around 11.47% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) among women of reproductive age worldwide [9].

Postpartum Depression affects approximately 10–20% of women globally, with estimates ranging from 13% in high-income countries to 19.8% in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [10]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that approximately 20% of pregnant and postpartum women in low- and middle-income countries experience generalized anxiety disorder, with 8.3% experiencing posttraumatic stress disorder [11].

Another systematic review revealed that the rates of antenatal depression range from 15 to 65%, which often leads to serious health consequences for both the mother and child, including low birth weight and premature delivery [12]. Another review involving 565 studies from 80 different countries reported that approximately 17.22% of women experience postpartum depression (PPD) [13]. Besides that, women undergoing infertility, menopausal transition, and Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) exhibit a substantially higher prevalence of depression and other mental disorders [14, 15]. Many social, economic, and cultural circumstances govern women’s mental health. The factors associated with antenatal depression include poor social status, poverty, lack of control over sexual and reproductive lives, intimate partner violence, sexual or domestic violence, childhood abuse, forced marriage, weak family support systems, poor physical health including poor nutritional status, lack of access to education, lack of decision-making power including family planning to socioeconomic choices, lack of access to antenatal care (ANC), or living with sexually transmissible diseases including HIV often contribute to suicidal tendencies among women [5, 8, 13, 16].

Several studies have identified that the severity of mental disorders in women of reproductive age is more prominent in low- and middle-income countries [5, 12–15]. Despite the fact, this burning issue often remains undiagnosed, unaddressed, and stigmatized in many countries, including Nepal [17]. A cross-sectional study conducted in the rural Saptari district in Nepal reported a high prevalence of maternal anxiety and depression [18]. Furthermore, a hospital-based study found a substantial level of depressive symptoms among post-partum mothers [19]. According to another study, more than one out of four mothers has been diagnosed with depressive symptoms [20]. Notably, the primary cause of death among women of reproductive age is suicide in Nepal [21]. However, no study has been deep dive into the contributing factors associated with mental illness, specifically anxiety and depression among women of reproductive age. Most studies relied on small sample sizes; hence, the findings are insufficient for macro-level decision-making. Since the mental health of women is often a neglected issue, it poses a tremendous threat to the physical and psychological health and well-being of women. Remarkably, there is a need for a well-designed investigation in order to better understand the significance of this problem. Thus, this study set out to determine the prevalence and factors associated with depression among women of reproductive age in Nepal. Therefore, this study is expected to contribute to the broader public health agenda by providing evidence-based analysis and synthesizing critical factors.

Methods

Data source and sampling

The study used the Nepal Demographic and Health Survey 2022 (NDHS 2022) data, which is a periodical survey carried out across the country from Jan 05 to June 22, 2022, in order to evaluate health indicators and to offer a detailed overview of population, maternal, and child health issues along with mental health status [22]. Covering 14,280 households from 476 Primary Sampling Units (PSU), this study utilized two-stage stratified sampling where all women from all the households were eligible for interviewed whereas half of the households were chosen for the males; as a result, males from every second household were qualified to be interviewed. By the end, 4,913 men and 14,845 women (representing 97% and 95% of the overall response rates, respectively) had successfully completed interviews. In this study, a total of 7,442 women’s data were analyzed who participated in the mental health questions. Details of the sampling strategy and other measurements have been found elsewhere (NDHS 2022) [22]. This de-identified dataset is publicly available for academic and specific research purposes upon request. Before proceeding with the survey, all the DHS survey protocols obtained ethical approval from both the ICF Institutional Review Board and the country-specific review board.

Measurements

Outcome variables

Two widely-used instruments for screening for mental disorders were included in the 2022 NDHS’s mental health module. The Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) were employed for assessing anxiety and depression, respectively, with both scales having been previously validated in the Nepalese context and demonstrating good reliability [23]. PHQ-9 consists of 9 questions that align with the DSM-IV criteria for major depressive disorder. Each item asks about symptoms experienced over the past two weeks, and respondents rate them using a Likert scale, with options ranging from “not at all” to “nearly every day.” PHQ-9 score of 0–4 is considered minimal symptoms or no symptoms, while a score of 5–9 is considered mild, 10–14 is considered moderate, 15–19 is considered moderately severe, and 20–27 is considered severe [24]. GAD-7 has 7 items focusing on generalized anxiety symptoms, again using a Likert scale to assess frequency over the past two weeks. GAD-7 score of 0–5 is considered mild, while a score of 6–14 is considered moderate and 15–21 is considered severe [25] while a [24] Both tools used a Likert scale to show how bad the symptoms are. Answers of “not at all” (never), “several days” (rarely), “more than half the days” (often), and “nearly every day” (always) are given numbers 1, 2 and 3. When you add up the scores of all the items, you get the total number. The sum of the scores for each individual item yields the final score. In the 2022 NDHS, using the validated Water Glass Pictorial Scale was used to rate the intensity of the symptoms. An empty glass means “never,” a glass that is only 1/4 full means “rarely,” a glass that is half full means “often,” and a glass that is three-fourths full means “always.” This picture chart helped us understand how respondents felt and put the severity of symptoms they described into context, added that, there was no diagnostic testing done, these tools are screening instruments rather than diagnostic tests. This means that, although the scores can indicate mild, moderate, or severe levels of symptoms, they do not confirm an official diagnosis of depression or anxiety. Clinical diagnoses typically require further evaluation from healthcare professionals, including diagnostic interviews or medical assessments, which were not conducted as part of this survey. Cronbach alpha of the PHQ-9 was 0.8875, while GAD-7 was 0.8126 in this sample.

Predictor variables

To identify the frequently used common predictor factors that are linked to outcome variables, we conducted a review of the literature. A few biological and socioeconomic variables were selected based on what was available in the dataset and relevant research. Age, region, education, wealth status, residence and marital status, and self-rated health status (classified as 1 for bad or very bad and 0 for very good, good or moderate) which were used by several previous studies and found significant connection to the mental health condition of women [5, 8, 13, 16]. While some biological and behavioral factors such as total lifetime number of sex partners [26], ever had a terminated pregnancy [27], genital discharge in the last 12 months [28], experienced emotional violence, smoked cigarettes, frequency of husband/partner being drunk [29], visited a health facility in last 12 months, had genital sore/ulcer in last 12 months [30] were considered for this study purpose as those were widely used to predict mental health of women by different previous studies.

Statistical analysis

Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used for this study. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the background characteristics of the respondents while inferential statistics were used to determine the predictors variables against outcome measurements. Since the outcome were scored/continuous data we used linear regression methods along with structural equation method (SEM). Univariate and multiple linear regression was carried out to select the predictors for final model. For the data analysis STATA MP.16 and AMOS.24 were used.

Assessing the SEM algorithms model

Structural equation modeling (SEM) is designed with the two-phase model (i) measurement model and (ii) structural model. The measurement model depicts the inner relationships between latent variables and observed variables, and the latter, along with the structural model, is used to investigate loadings and estimate indicators [24–26]. The conceptual framework model was run using AMOS.24, and the structural framework used in the hypothesis testing segment is shown in Fig. 1. AMOS.24 consists of a series of standard metrics, such as loading indicators, composite reliability, average variance extracted (AVE), path coefficients, inner structural correlations, latent variable scores, t values, etc. A structural procedure has been introduced to investigate loading and eliminating indications (loadings < 0.70) was adopted [27]. In the context of the study, we utilized a reflexive measurement framework. In line with the majority of other behavioral research [28, 29], a set of assessment criterion that can quantify the credibility and viability of the SEM model with recessive criterion was proposed.

Analysis of variance of anxiety and depression among the observed groups

Among the respondents, it was observed in Table 2 that older respondents, specifically those aged 35–49, exhibited the highest levels of anxiety (m-GAD score = 3.697; p = < 0.001) and depression (m-PHQ score = 2.886; p = < 0.001) compared to other age groups. Additionally, individuals who were widowed, divorced, or separated reported the highest levels of anxiety (m-GAD score = 5.348; p = < 0.001) and depression (m-PHQ score = 4.931; p = < 0.001), while unmarried individuals demonstrated the lowest levels of both anxiety and depression. Economic status also played a role, with poor respondents scoring significantly higher in both anxiety (m-GAD score = 3.562; p = < 0.001) and depression (m-PHQ score = 2.901; p = < 0.001) compared to the other two economic groups, while rich individuals scored the lowest (m-GAD score = 3.103; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 2.387; p = < 0.001).

Furthermore, respondents who reported an inability to refuse sex (m-GAD score = 4.134; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 3.327; p = < 0.001) or those with multiple partners (m-GAD score = 4.812; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 3.921; p = < 0.001) scored significantly higher in both depression and anxiety scales compared to their counterparts. Women who had terminated a pregnancy (m-GAD score = 4.317; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 3.489; p = < 0.001), smoked cigarettes (m-GAD score = 4.829; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 4.131; p = < 0.001), experienced emotional violence (m-GAD score = 6.254; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 5.799; p = < 0.001), visited a health facility (m-GAD score = 3.567; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 2.889; p = < 0.001), or had genital sores or ulcers in the last 12 months (m-GAD score = 5.310; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 4.741; p = < 0.001) also scored significantly higher in both anxiety and depression scales. Additionally, females who perceived their health as bad (m-GAD score = 6.087; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 5.498; p = < 0.001) or reported their husbands getting drunk (m-GAD score = 4.205; p = < 0.001; m-PHQ score = 3.464; p = < 0.001) scored higher in both scales.

In our study utilizing a Structural Equation Model (SEM) to measure depression among reproductive-aged women in Nepal, we employed standardized beta coefficients (β) to elucidate both direct and indirect associations among observed variables. The model demonstrated a good fit with the following goodness of fit indices: CMIN/df = 21.750; CFI = 0.914; TLI = 0.987; RMSEA = 0.037; PCLOSE = 0.003.

Significant variables from the linear regression table were initially incorporated into the model. However, variables exhibiting insignificant direct or indirect effects on the dependent variables were systematically removed. The resulting nine-factor model aimed to predict general depression among reproductive-aged women in Nepal. Figure 1 shows that, among the factors, anxiety exhibited the most substantial direct positive effect on respondents’ depression (β = 0.65; p = < 0.001). Emotional violence (β = 0.09; p = < 0.001) demonstrated a significant direct positive effect on depression, with a significant indirect positive effect through anxiety and genital ulcer, indicating that respondents who experienced emotional violence scored higher on the depression scale and it also significantly affected the relationship of anxiety and genital ulcer to depression. Smoking (β = 0.02; p = 0.149) did not exhibit a direct significant effect on depression but had a direct significant effect (β = 0.04; p = 0.003) on anxiety, with a significant positive indirect effect on depression, suggesting that while smoking may not directly affect depression, it passively increases it. Similarly, pregnancy termination (β = 0.00; p = 0.241) did not show a significant direct effect on depression but had an indirect effect on depression and a significant positive effect (β = 0.05; p = 0.009) on anxiety, indicating it also passively increases depression among reproductive-aged women in Nepal.

Self-rated health status had a significant direct negative effect on depression (β= -0.08; p = < 0.001) and anxiety (β= -0.20; p = < 0.001). Moreover, it had an indirect negative effect on depression and anxiety, influencing respondents’ frequency of visiting health facilities and experiencing genital ulcer or discharge in the last 12 months, indicating that poor self-rated health status both directly and indirectly increases depression and anxiety. Genital ulcer and discharge both exhibited significant direct positive effects on both depression and anxiety, with an indirect effect on depression through anxiety, meaning that respondents who faced genital discharge or ulcer in the last 12 months are more likely to experience depression. Visiting a health facility in the last 12 months did not have any significant indirect effect but had a direct positive effect on both anxiety (β = 0.03; p = 0.005) and depression (β= -0.02; p = 0.026).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to discern the factors associated with depression among reproductive-aged women in Nepal, constricting a comprehensive model to elucidate the pervasive contributors to depression. The analysis incorporates socioeconomic factors, anxiety, and habitual aspects to develop a holistic understanding. This research identifies a robust negative relationship between self-rated health status and depression. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) further supports the notion that those perceiving their health as poor are more likely to experience depression. This aligns with the conclusions of Millán-Calenti et al. (2012) and Miller (2019) who found that reproductive-aged women with poorer self-rated health were more prone to depression [31, 32].

Mohit et al. (2011) highlight the interconnectedness of physical illness and depression, asserting that one in three individuals with physical ailments experiences depression [33]. Furthermore, an individual’s self-rated health status has an indirect influence on depression and can impact their likelihood of seeking medical care for conditions such as genital discharge or ulcers. Doherty & Gaughran (2014) highlights the interconnectedness of physical health and mental well-being, suggesting that deteriorating physical health can contribute to various health complications, potentially exacerbating depression symptoms [34].

Factors such as the inability to self-care, prolonged treatment regimens, persistent pain, frustration, fatigue, and the associated uncertainties and anxieties about health conditions might contribute to heightened depressive symptoms among individuals grappling with physical illnesses. Moreover, living with a physical disease can exacerbate depression, citing feelings of hopelessness, sleep disturbances, and the overarching uncertainty associated with health issues as exacerbating factors.

Genital discharge or sores in the last 12 months are identified as having a positive effect on depression, as indicated in the structural equation model (SEM). Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, with Patel (2005) and Winter & Stephenson (2013) noting that women may experience anxiety due to these conditions [35, 36]. Genital sores, causing continuous pain, can contribute to greater depression. Psychological factors play a significant role in this phenomenon, as these conditions can be uncomfortable, and painful. This raises concerns about hygiene, infection, and sexual health.

Moreover, genital discharge or sores not only directly impact physical health but also have indirect effects on mental well-being, particularly depression. Chokka & Hankey (2018) posit that sexual dysfunction resulting from such conditions can induce severe anxiety, especially among reproductive-aged women, due to concerns about further health deterioration [37]. This anxiety can exacerbate depressive symptoms. Additionally, these conditions can strain relationships, causing fear of rejection, intimacy issues, and communication breakdowns, all of which contribute to heightened anxiety and subsequent depression [38]. Additionally, the possibility of underlying medical conditions, such as sexually transmitted infections (STIs) or chronic skin conditions, may contribute to increased depression among women.

The study further underscores the significant impact of emotional violence from partners on depression, as revealed through Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). This finding aligns with a body of literature that consistently demonstrates a strong correlation between emotional violence and depression among women [39–43]. Dutton et al. (2006) found that depression prevalence among victims of emotional violence is double that of the general population [44]. Bonomi et al. (2006) further observed that women who experience emotional violence are 2.06 times more likely to suffer from minor depression and 1.75 times more likely to develop severe depression [41]. Additionally, the study highlights the role of emotional violence in exacerbating anxiety, which in turn intensifies depression. Collins (2013) argued that emotional violence, characterized by manipulative and unpredictable behavior, creates an atmosphere of fear and instability, triggering anxiety as victims are constantly on alert for potential threats [45]. This psychological strain contributes to heightened depression, with emotional abuse often manifesting in verbal attacks, belittlement, gaslighting, and manipulation. These abusive behaviors erode self-esteem and confidence, potentially leading to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and further increasing depression.

In a similar vein, the study supports existing research showing that smoking among women is linked to increased depression [46, 47]. Flensborg-Madsen et al. (2011) highlighted the association between smoking and temporary stress relief, but noted that withdrawal symptoms, such as irritability and anxiety, may worsen depressive symptoms [48]. Chronic smoking and nicotine dependence can contribute to a cycle of increased depression [49]. The study also suggests that smoking elevates anxiety, which subsequently exacerbates depression. Roth et al. (2001) argue that women of reproductive age are particularly sensitive to the health risks of smoking, such as fertility issues and cancer, leading to heightened anxiety and its compounding effect on depression [50].

The study also suggests that while pregnancy termination may not directly impact depression, it significantly contributes to anxiety, which in turn influences depression [51]. Marčinko et al. (2011) asserted that pregnancy termination substantially raises anxiety levels, potentially leading to long-term depression [52]. González-Ramos (2021) noted that complex emotions, such as guilt, anxiety, and relief, are common following pregnancy termination, particularly when done for medical reasons [27]. While Andersson et al. (2014) argued that many women do not regret terminating pregnancies, relying on rational thinking over difficult emotions [53], other studies emphasize the strong positive relationship between pregnancy termination and anxiety [54–56]. In South Asia, cultural beliefs and societal stigma surrounding pregnancy termination further exacerbate anxiety and depression by triggering feelings of shame, isolation, and judgment.

A key finding of the study is the strong association between anxiety and depression, with anxiety emerging as the strongest predictor of depression among reproductive-aged women in Nepal. This is consistent with previous studies that have shown elevated depressive symptoms in individuals with anxiety disorders [49, 57]. Gdańska et al. (2017) also supported this connection, reporting that approximately half of individuals with anxiety disorders also experience depression [58]. Pawlusk et al. (2017) further emphasized the neurobiological factors common to both conditions, such as imbalances in serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine, which regulate mood, stress response, and emotional stability [59]. Negative thought patterns, including rumination and catastrophizing, associated with anxiety can erode self-esteem, fuel hopelessness, and precipitate depression. Moreover, anxiety-induced social withdrawal can reduce access to support systems, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms .

Policy recommendations

The findings of this study indicate the importance of prioritizing the integration of mental health services into existing reproductive health programs in Nepal. This can be achieved by ensure that healthcare providers receive training in recognizing and addressing mental health issues during routine reproductive health check-ups especially among the women have any history of having genital ulcer or discharge. This study also highlights the implication of strengthening legal frameworks against emotional violence within family and societal relationships in order to reduce the mental health symptoms in women. This may be attainable through implementing educational programs in communities to raise awareness about emotional abuse, its consequences, and available support services. Furthermore, training of healthcare professionals could be effective to identify signs of anxiety and provide timely interventions, including counseling and referral to specialized mental health services.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths and weaknesses of this study are accredited. The strength that lies in this study is the generalizability of the findings for Nepal since this survey covered nationally representative data covering a wide geographic area. While limitations are, some biological and behavioral variables were not collected based on clinical diagnose so readers need to be aware of reading the results. Even, the outcome measurements were collected before two weeks preceding the survey based on their self-report so it might be a recall biased problem.

Furthermore, there are some concrete methodological limitations that should be acknowledged and future research on this is warranted. First, although many of the predictor variables in our model are dichotomous, we opted to use Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) rather than Weighted Least Squares (WLS) for our SEM analysis. MLE, especially the robust version used (MLR), is known to perform adequately with both continuous and dichotomous variables under certain conditions, such as adequate sample size. However, we acknowledge that alternative estimation techniques, such as WLSMV, may be more appropriate in cases where categorical data dominate, and the sample size is smaller or more specific. Future research might benefit from exploring different estimation methods to compare results.

Second, while we included mediating variables in the model and examined both direct and indirect effects, we did not perform a formal mediation analysis using bootstrap estimates. Instead, we relied on path analysis within the SEM framework to test the relationships between the variables, which adequately captures indirect effects. Although bootstrapping may provide additional insights into the significance of indirect pathways, the decision to forgo this approach was based on our research objectives and the theoretical grounding of the model. Researchers interested in a more detailed examination of mediation could further explore these indirect effects using bootstrapped estimates in future studies.

Finally, the complexity of our model, which includes multiple mediators, introduces the possibility of a large number of indirect effects. While we aimed to balance methodological rigor and clarity of results, we acknowledge that further studies could explore these pathways in greater depth. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the intricate relationships among the variables but may require more advanced methods to handle the complexity introduced by multiple mediators.

Conclusion

In terms of socioeconomic, behavioral, and biological characteristics, this study revealed a significant issue with women’s mental health difficulties in Nepal. Because mental disorders are highly frequent among women in their reproductive years in Nepal, immediate, targeted efforts are required to screen and diagnose these patients in order to give care. This study may provide evidence to key stakeholders in designing programs to improve the mental health status of women in Nepal and other comparable countries.
