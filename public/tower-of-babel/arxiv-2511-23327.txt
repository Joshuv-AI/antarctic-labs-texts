The 2-switch-degree of a graph
Adrián Pastine, Victor N. Schvöllner

Abstract
In this work, we delve into the study of the 2-switch-degree of a graph G G , which is nothing more than the degree of G G as a vertex of the realization graph 𝒢 ⁡ ( s ) \mathcal{G}(s) associated with the degree sequence s s of G G . We explore the characteristics of active and inactive vertices, the basic properties of the degree, explicit formulas for its computation, and its behavior in specific families of graphs, such as trees and unicyclic graphs.

In this case, we say that

induced by

. The set

of all neighbors of a vertex

of
v
in
G
. Clearly,

. Two edges

that do not share a common vertex are said to be

(

). The number of edges incident to a vertex

in

, denoted by

. It is clear that

. If

is said to be

; if

, then

is a

; and if

(i.e.,

is

In order to properly define the 2-switch-degree of a graph, we have to refresh earlier some preliminary and well-known concepts like degree sequence, 2-switch and realization graph.

We usually write
V(G)=[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for some
n
. Then, the degrees of the vertices in
G
can be arranged as an
n
-tuple

(d_{i})_{i=1}^{n}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}),

forming what is called the

of

, denoted by

or simply by

is clear from the context. For example, if

, then

. If we label the vertices of

so that

, we can rewrite

where
\{n_{i}:i\in[k]\}\subseteq[n]

and

This form is specially useful for unlabeled graphs, where only the multiplicities
\alpha_{i}
of the degrees

matter. Two degree sequences

. On the other hand, two degree sequences
d_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots d_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}

\{(d_{i},\alpha_{i}):i\in[n]\}=\{(\delta_{i},\beta_{i}):i\in[n]\}.

In the mathematical literature, a

[

and

[

]
, page 5, or
[

\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}:\mathfrak{G}_{n}\rightarrow\mathfrak{G}_{n}

(1).

\tau(G)={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}G=(G-\{ab,cd\})+\{ac,bd\},

(1)

if
\{ab,cd\}\subseteq E(G),ab\cap cd=\varnothing
and
\{ac,bd\}\subseteq E(\overline{G})
;

(2).

\tau(G)=G,

otherwise.

. The matrix

represent the edges to be added. We say that

is a permutation matrix, it is clear that

is not the identity in

for all

. It follows from the definition that the 2-switch preserves the degree sequence, i.e.:

(2)

for every 2-switch

A direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
is that
\mathcal{G}(s)
is connected. In recent years, the problem of determining whether a given induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(s)
is connected or not has been extensively studied. In this direction, many advances have already been made. For example, it is known that
\mathcal{F}(s)
, i.e., the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(s)
induced by forests, is connected. An analogous result also holds for unicyclic graphs, connected graphs, and pseudoforests (the latter are graphs whose components are trees or unicyclic graphs). To delve deeper into these topics, the following papers can be consulted:
[
10
,
11
,
13
]
. The most important feature of a connected
\mathcal{H}\preceq\mathcal{G}(s)
is that it ensures the possibility of transforming, via 2-switches, a graph
G\in V(\mathcal{H})
into another graph
G^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
in such a way that every intermediate graph in the transition also belongs to
V(\mathcal{H})
. Let
s=(d_{v})_{v=1}^{n}
be a degree sequence. The sequence

\bar{s}=(n-1-d_{v})_{v=1}^{n}

is called the
dual sequence
of
s
. Being
s=s(G)
for some
G
, it is clear that
\bar{s}=s(\overline{G})
. Below, we cite a remarkable result about
\mathcal{G}(\overline{s})
; we also include our proof for completeness.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
1
]
, page 218)
.

\mathcal{G}(s)\approx\mathcal{G}(\overline{s}).

Proof.

We need to exhibit a graph isomorphism between
\mathcal{G}(s)
and
\mathcal{G}(\overline{s})
. Let
\varphi:\mathcal{G}(s)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(\overline{s})
be the function defined by
\varphi(X)=\overline{X}
and let
G,H\in\mathcal{G}(s)
,
G\neq H
. If
H=\tau(G)
for some 2-switch
\tau
, then it is easy to see that

\overline{H}=\tau^{-1}(\overline{G}).

Therefore,
\{\varphi(H),\varphi(G)\}\in E(\mathcal{G}(\overline{s}))
, which shows that
\varphi
is a homomorphism. Clearly,
\varphi
is bijective, and its inverse
\phi:\mathcal{G}(\overline{s})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(s)
is the function defined by
\phi(Y)=\overline{Y}
. Let
A,B\in\mathcal{G}(\overline{s})
, with
A\neq B
. If
B=\theta(A)
for some 2-switch
\theta
, we know that
\overline{B}=\theta^{-1}(\overline{A})
, i.e.,
\{\phi(B),\phi(A)\}\in E(\mathcal{G}(s))
. Thus,
\phi
is also a homomorphism, and hence we conclude that
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

Let
G
be a graph with degree sequence
s
. The
2-switch-degree
of
G
(or simply, the degree), denoted by
\deg(G)
, is the degree of
G
viewed as a vertex of
\mathcal{G}(s)
, i.e., the number of neighbors of the vertex
G
in the realization graph associated with
s
. In other words,
\deg(G)
is the number of active 2-switches in
G
. In this paper, we focus on the study of this parameter. The notion of 2-switch-degree captures a graph’s flexibility under local transformations and is closely tied to other combinatorial and structural properties.

A key family of graphs for the study of realization graphs is that of split graphs. A
split graph

is a graph
G
whose set of vertices can be partitioned into two sets
K
and
I
, such that every pair or vertices in
K
induce a complete graph, and the vertices in
I
induce a graph without edges. In such a case, we say that the pair
(K,I)
is a
bipartition
for
G
. For graphs in general, a subset of vertices that induce a complete graph is called a
clique
and a set of vertices that induce a graph without edges is called an
independent set
. We call
clique vertices
the elements of
K
, and
independent vertices
the elements of
I
. Two bipartitions
(K,I)
and
(K^{\prime},I^{\prime})
for a split graph
S
are equal if
K=K^{\prime}
and
I=I^{\prime}
. The notation
(S,K,I)
means that we are considering the split graph
S
together with a bipartition
(K,I)
for it. Split graphs have been characterized as those that do not contain a
C_{4}
(the cycle on
4
vertices),
C_{5}
(the cycle on
5
vertices), or
2K_{2}
(a pair of disjoint edges) as induced subgraphs (see
[
9
]
). If
S\in V(\mathcal{G}(s))
and
S
is split, then all the vertices of
\mathcal{G}(s)
are also split (
[
14
]
, page 205).

The concept of realization graph has received increasing attention over the years. The study of these objects was formalized in
[
7
]
, and subsequent work (
[
1
]
) showed that realization graphs of split graphs are isomorphic to interchange graphs of certain matrices. Tyshkevich introduced in
[
14
]
a decomposition of graphs into indecomposable factors which are all split graphs, except, at most, for one of them. Building on this, Barrus proved in
[
2
]
that realization graphs of decomposable graphs can be expressed as the Cartesian product of the realization graphs of the factors, and more recently, cliques in realization graphs and degree sequences whose realization graphs are complete graphs were characterized in
[
3
]
.

Our study is organized into five sections, covering the characterization of active and inactive vertices, fundamental properties of the degree, explicit formulas for computation, and its behavior in special families of graphs.

We introduce the concepts of
active
and
inactive
vertices. Inactive vertices, which were implicitly observed by Barrus and West in
[
4
]
, remain invariant under 2-switches.
Section

2
formalizes these notions and characterizes active vertices in terms of their neighborhoods and degrees. In graphs of diameter at least 4, all vertices are active. This naturally partitions any graph
G
into an
active part
and a
inactive part
, a division that is consistent across all members of
\mathcal{G}(s)
. In
Section

3
, we focus on the subgraph
G^{*}
induced by the active vertices of
G
.

Section

4
presents fundamental properties of the 2-switch-degree.
In
Section

5
, we provide explicit formulas for the 2-switch-degree in terms of certain combinatorial invariants. Remarkably, we establish connections with the first and second Zagreb indices from Chemical Graph Theory.
Finally,
Section

6
studies the degree of trees and unicyclic graphs.

Active vertices

Let
G
be a graph, and let

Q_{G}=\{H\preceq G:|H|=4\},\quad Q_{G}^{*}=\{H\in Q_{G}:\deg(H)\neq 0\}.

We say that a vertex
v\in G
is
active
in
G
if there exists an
H\in Q_{G}^{*}
containing
v
. It is easy to deduce from
Figure

1
that

Q_{G}^{*}=\{H\preceq G:H\approx X\in\{P_{4},C_{4},2K_{2}\}\}.

We say that
v
is
inactive
in
G
when it is not active in
G
, i.e., if
\deg(H)=0
for every
H\in Q_{G}
containing
v
. We denote by
\act(G)
the set of all active vertices of
G
. In other words, a vertex
v
is active in
G
if there exists some 2-switch that activates it in
G
. Clearly,
V(G)-\act(G)
is the set of all inactive vertices of
G
.

It is important to note that a vertex
v
is active in
G
if and only if
v
is active in
\overline{G}
. Indeed, if
\tau
is a 2-switch that activates
v
in
G
, then
\tau^{-1}
activates
v
in
\overline{G}
. Thus,

\act(G)=\act(\overline{G}).

From this, it immediately follows that universal vertices are inactive, since they are isolated in the complement.

A key fact about active and inactive vertices is that they are invariant under
2
-switch. I.e., a vertex
a
is active in
G
if and only if it is active in
\tau(G)
for every
2
-switch
\tau
. The proof of this fact is case by case and divided in the next several lemmas.

.

We have to analyze two cases: 1)

(1).

If

Lemma 2.5
.

If
G
is a graph and
\tau
is a 2-switch, then
\act(G)=\act(\tau(G))
.

Proof.

If
\tau(G)=G
, then there is nothing to prove. Therefore, assume
\tau(G)\neq G
. Let
a\in\act(G)
. If
\tau
activates
a
in
G
, recall that
\tau^{-1}
activates
a
in
\tau(G)
, and so

. If
\tau
does not activate

in
G
, consider any

containing

. Let
V_{\tau}
be the set of four vertices activated by
\tau
in
G
. Then, we have two cases: 1)
|V(H)\cap V_{\tau}|\leq 1
; 2)
|V(H)\cap V_{\tau}|\in\{2,3\}

We say that a graph

is

if

\act(G)=V(G).

Otherwise, we simply say that

is

(or non-active). In particular, if

, we say that

is

. It is clear that

when

is active. Some examples of active graphs are

(i.e., the graph

. Also, all disconnected graphs without isolated vertices are clearly active. Inactive graphs, on the other hand, are precisely the graphs of degree 0, i.e., threshold graphs (see

.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let

The

of a vertex

and any other vertex in

In the following corollary, we show that in a graph without isolated vertices, inactive vertices have eccentricity 1 or 2.

Proposition 2.9

Let

be a graph without isolated vertices. If

, then

, for every
v\in V(G)
. In other words:

ecc_{G}(x)\in\{1,2\}.

Proof.

If
v
is a vertex at distance

from

(in
G
), we can find an induced path of length

connecting
x
to
v
in
G
. If

, then there exist vertices

and

such that the 2-switch
\binom{x\,y}{z\,v}
is active in

. This means

.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
G
be a graph without isolated vertices. If
G
is not active, then
\diam(G)\leq 3
. Moreover, this bound is sharp.

Proof.

By

2.8
, we know

is connected. Consider a vertex

. By the triangle inequality and

, for any pair of vertices

, we have (in

) that

By

, equality holds if and only if

for some

. But in this case,

would contain an induced path of the form

Proposition 2.12
.

If

Proof.

(1).

(2).

(3).

(4).

Let

and

be split graphs. Then,

is balanced.

Let

be a bipartition for

is balanced but

is not. By definition of

. If

, then

, which is impossible (recall that

). If

is balanced. If

. However,

The

. If

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and let

be the set of all vertices activated by some 2-switch in the sequence
\theta=(\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. If

and

, then

\theta(G-W)=\theta(G)-W.

Proof.

Let

, that is,
\theta={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
. Since
W\cap V_{\theta}=\varnothing
, it is clear that
\theta
is active in
G
if and only if it is active in

. If
\theta
is active in

, then we have

\theta(G-W)=((G-W)-\{ab,cd\})+\{ac,bd\}.

Since
V_{\theta}=\{a,b,c,d\}
and

, it follows that

(G-W)-\{ab,cd\}=(G-\{ab,cd\})-W.

For the same reason, it is easy to see that

((G-\{ab,cd\})-W)+\{ac,bd\}=

((G-\{ab,cd\})+\{ac,bd\})-W=\theta(G)-W.

The rest of the proof is carried out easily by induction on

, since it suffices to recycle the case

by replacing

with
\tau_{1}\ldots\tau_{k-1}(G)
and
\theta
with
\tau_{k}
.

Let

we define the

\ext(X,V^{\prime},E^{\prime})=(V\cup V^{\prime},E\cup E^{\prime}).

Clearly,

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
\theta
be a sequence of 2-switches. If

and

\ext(\theta(G^{*}),W,E^{\prime})=\theta(\ext(G^{*},W,E^{\prime})).

Proof.

The first thing to note is that

. Hence, it is clear that
\ext(G^{*},W,E^{\prime})=G
. Secondly, since each 2-switch in

(remember that

is an invariant associated with

, by

2.6
). With this in mind, and applying

, we obtain

\ext(\theta(G^{*}),W,E^{\prime})=\ext(\theta(G)-W,W,E^{\prime})=

(V,E(\theta(G)-W)\dot{\cup}E^{\prime}).

Since

we conclude that
\ext(\theta(G^{*}),W,E^{\prime})=\theta(G)

X_{0}
with any other vertex of
\mathcal{G}(s)
, the set
E_{0}
does not change. Now suppose that
G^{*}=H^{*}
. Then,

G=\ext(G^{*},W,E_{0})=\ext(H^{*},W,E_{0})=H,

which shows that
\psi
is injective. Since
V(\mathcal{X})^{*}=\psi(V(\mathcal{X}))
,
\psi
is obviously surjective. Therefore,
\psi
is a bijection.

Let
G^{*},H^{*}\in V(\mathcal{X})^{*}
,
G^{*}\neq H^{*}
. According to
Theorem

1.1
, there exists a sequence
\theta
of 2-switches such that
H=\theta(G)
, because
G,H\in V(\mathcal{G}(s))
and
G\neq H
. Then, applying
Theorem

2.6
and
Lemma

3.1
for

, we deduce that

\theta(G^{*})=\theta(G-W)=\theta(G)-W=H-W=H^{*},

which means that
s(G^{*})=s^{*}=s(H^{*})
. In other words,
V(\mathcal{X})^{*}\subseteq V(\mathcal{G}(s^{*}))
. We define the
active space

\mathcal{X}^{*}
associated with
\mathcal{X}
as the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(s^{*})
induced by
V(\mathcal{X})^{*}
. Thanks to

3.1
and

2.6
, it is clear that

is a homomorphism between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. On the other hand, observe that the function
\zeta:V(\mathcal{X}^{*})\rightarrow V(\mathcal{X})

, is the inverse of

, and it is also a homomorphism by

. Then,

is an isomorphism.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\mathcal{X}\preceq\mathcal{G}(s)

Proof.

It follows from the previous discussion.

4
Basic properties of the degree

At this point, it should come as no surprise that the number of 2-switches acting on a certain graph
G
can be obtained by summing the degree of all induced subgraphs of order 4 in
G
. In other words,

\deg(G)=\sum_{H\in Q_{G}}\deg(H),

where
\deg(H)=2
if
H\approx 2K_{2}
or
C_{4}
,
\deg(H)=1
if
H\approx P_{4}
, and
\deg(H)=0
in the remaining cases (see
Figure

1
). If
X
is one of the 11 graphs in
Figure

1
, we define

Q_{G}(X)=\{H\in Q_{G}:H\approx X\}.

Then, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every graph
G
,

\deg(G)=2|Q_{G}(2K_{2})|+2|Q_{G}(C_{4})|+|Q_{G}(P_{4})|.

(3)

In particular, if
G
is a split graph, then
\deg(G)=|Q_{G}(P_{4})|
.

Proof.

Equality (
3
) follows from the previous discussion. When
G
is split, we know it does not contain induced subgraphs isomorphic to
C_{4}
or
2K_{2}
. Therefore,
|Q_{G}(2K_{2})|=|Q_{G}(C_{4})|=0
.
∎

From (
3
) we observe that

\deg(G)\equiv|Q_{G}(P_{4})|\pmod{2},

that is, the degree of
G
has the same parity as the number of induced
P_{4}
’s in
G
. In particular, every graph of odd degree contains at least one induced
P_{4}
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any graph
G
,

\deg(G)=\deg(\overline{G}).

Proof.

Let
G
be a vertex of
\mathcal{G}(s)
. Then,
\overline{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\bar{s})
. By
Theorem

1.2
, we have
\mathcal{G}(s)\approx\mathcal{G}(\bar{s})
via the isomorphism
G\mapsto\overline{G}
, and therefore
\deg(G)=\deg(\overline{G})
.
∎

It is worth mentioning that
Corollary

4.2
can also be proven using the fact that
H\preceq G
if and only if
\overline{H}\preceq\overline{G}
. This, together with the fact that
\overline{P_{4}}=P_{4}
and
\overline{C_{4}}=2K_{2}
, implies that
|Q_{G}(P_{4})|=|Q_{\overline{G}}(P_{4})|
,
|Q_{G}(C_{4})|=|Q_{\overline{G}}(2K_{2})|
, and
|Q_{G}(2K_{2})|=|Q_{\overline{G}}(C_{4})|
.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
H\preceq G
, then

\deg(H)\leq\deg(G).

Proof.

This is true because
Q^{*}_{H}\subseteq Q^{*}_{G}
.
∎

Proposition

4.3
is not generally true if the subgraph is not induced. For example,
P_{4}\subseteq K_{4}
but
\deg(P_{4})=1
and
\deg(K_{4})=0
.
Proposition

4.3
can be easily generalized as follows.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\{H_{i}:i\in[k]\}
be a collection of induced subgraphs of
G
such that
|E(H_{i})\cap E(H_{j})|\leq 1
for
i\neq j
. Then,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\deg(H_{i})\leq\deg(G).

Proof.

If
\tau
were an active 2-switch on both
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
for certain distinct
i,j
, then we would have
|E(H_{i})\cap E(H_{j})|\geq 2
, contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore, each active 2-switch in
H_{i}
is inactive in
H_{j}
, for every
j\neq i
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

If
G^{\prime}\preceq G^{*}
and
\deg(G^{\prime})=\deg(G)>0
, then
G^{\prime}=G^{*}
. In other words,
G^{*}
is the smallest induced subgraph (in terms of order) of
G
with the same degree as
G
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G^{\prime}\preceq G^{*}
and
\deg(G^{\prime})=\deg(G)\geq 1
, but
G^{\prime}\neq G^{*}
. If
v\in V(G^{*})-V(G^{\prime})
, then there is at least one 2-switch
\tau
that acts on
G
activating
v
. However,
\tau
is clearly inactive on
G^{\prime}
, and therefore
\deg(G^{\prime})\leq\deg(G)-1
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The next result states that computing the degree of a graph essentially reduces to computing the degree of its components.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph such that
\kappa(G)=k\geq 2
. If
G_{i}
is a component of
G
, then

\deg(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\deg(G_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}2\rVert G_{i}\rVert\rVert G_{j}\rVert.

(4)

Proof.

Suppose
k=2
. Since
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are disjoint induced subgraphs of
G
, the degree of
G
is at least
\deg(G_{1})+\deg(G_{2})
, by
Proposition

4.4
. The remaining 2-switches that transform
G
are those that replace edges from different components. More specifically: if
ab\in G_{1}
and
cd\in G_{2}
, then
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
d&c\end{pmatrix}
are inactive in
G
. Therefore,
\deg(G)=\deg(G_{1})+\deg(G_{2})+2\|G_{1}\|\|G_{2}\|
. The rest of the proof follows easily by induction on
k
.
∎

If
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
are disjoint, then we denote by
G_{1}+G_{2}
the graph whose vertex set is
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
and whose edge set is

E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup\{xy:x\in G_{1},y\in G_{2}\}.

This binary operation is well known and is called the
join
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. If
V_{2}=\{v\}
(i.e.,
G_{2}\approx K_{1}
), we can write
G_{1}+G_{2}
as
G_{1}+v
. The join operation is clearly commutative and associative. Also,
G_{1},G_{2}\preceq G_{1}+G_{2}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any graph
G
and any
n\geq 1
,

\deg(G+K_{n})=\deg(G).

(5)

Proof.

Since
G
and
K_{n}
are disjoint induced subgraphs of
G+K_{n}
, we have that
\deg(G)+\deg(K_{n})=\deg(G)\leq\deg(G+K_{n})
, by
Proposition

4.4
. If
\tau
is a 2-switch between
ab\in G
and
cd\in K_{n}
, then
\tau
is inactive in
G+K_{n}
because
a,b,c
, and
d
form a clique there. If
a,b\in V(G)
and
c,d\in V(K_{n})
, then every 2-switch involving these 4 vertices is inactive in
G+K_{n}
, because
a,b,c,d
induce a
K_{4}
or a
D_{4}
(see
Figure

1
) in
G+K_{n}
.
∎

By taking
G+K_{n}
, we can thus obtain a graph with a certain fixed degree (that of
G
), but with arbitrarily large order and size.

Theorem 4.8
.

If
(S,I,K)
is a split graph and
G
is a graph, then

\deg(S\circ G)=\deg(S)+\deg(G).

(6)

Proof.

Since
S
and
G
are disjoint induced subgraphs of
S\circ G
, we have that
\deg(S\circ G)\geq\deg(S)+\deg(G)
, by
Proposition

4.4
. Thanks to
Lemma

4.7
, to obtain the equality (
6
) we only need to focus on an arbitrary pair of disjoint edges of the form
\{ik,ab\}
, where
\{ik,ab\}\subseteq E(S\circ G)
,
i\in I,k\in K
and
ab\notin E(S)
. Since
H=\langle a,b,i,k\rangle_{S\circ G}
contains the triangle
kabk
, we have that
\deg(H)=0
. Thus, equality (
6
) is finally proven.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

S\circ G
is active if and only if
S
and
G
are active.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
H=S\circ G
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Since
S,G\preceq H
, it is clear that every active vertex in
S
or in
G
remains active in
H
. Since
V(H)=V(S)\cup V(G)
by the definition of
\circ
, we conclude that
H
is active.

(
\Rightarrow
) If
x\in V(S)
is active in
H
, then by applying
Theorem

4.8
we obtain that

\deg(S)+\deg(G)=\deg(H)>\deg(H-x)=\deg(S-x)+\deg(G).

Hence,
\deg(S)>\deg(S-x)
, which shows that
x
is active in
S
. If
x\in V(G)
is active in
H
, we repeat the argument and complete the proof.
∎

5
Explicit formulas

If
G
is a graph, we define

dpe(G)=|\{\{e,f\}:e,f\in E(G),e\cap f=\varnothing\}|.

In other words,
dpe(G)
is the number of unordered pairs of disjoint edges in
G
. The abbreviation “
dpe
” stands for “
disjoint pairs of edges
”.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
G
is a graph with degree sequence
s=(d_{v})_{v=1}^{n}
, then:

dpe(G)=\binom{\|G\|}{2}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\binom{d_{v}}{2}=\binom{\|G\|+1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2},

where
|s|^{2}=d_{1}^{2}+\ldots+d_{n}^{2}
and
\binom{i}{j}=0
if
i<j
. In particular,
dpe(G)
is constant on
\mathcal{G}(s)
, i.e., it is an invariant associated with
s
.

Proof.

We can obtain
dpe(G)
by computing the number of pairs of non-disjoint edges in
G
and then subtracting this number from
\binom{\|G\|}{2}
, i.e., the total number of edge pairs in
G
. Since there are
d_{v}
edges incident to each
v\in V(G)
, we get
\binom{d_{v}}{2}
pairs of edges sharing
v
, and hence
\sum_{v=1}^{n}\binom{d_{v}}{2}
edge pairs with a common vertex.

The second equality follows from expanding the binomial coefficients and applying simple manipulations.
∎

Motivated by
Proposition

5.1
, we may use the notation
dpe(s)
instead of
dpe(G)
whenever
s=s(G)
. Moreover, note that
dpe(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx 2K_{2}\}|
. The next proposition gives a formula expressing the
dpe
of a graph in terms of the
dpe
of its connected components.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
G
is a graph with
k
components
G_{i}
, then

dpe(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}dpe(G_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\|G_{i}\|\|G_{j}\|.

Proof.

Let
k=2
, i.e.,
G=G_{1}\dot{\cup}G_{2}
. If
e_{1}\in E(G_{1})
and
e_{2}\in E(G_{2})
, clearly
e_{1}\cap e_{2}=\varnothing
and there are
\|G_{2}\|
pairs of the form
\{e,e_{2}\}
for each
e\in E(G_{1})
. Then,

dpe(G)=dpe(G_{1})+dpe(G_{2})+\|G_{1}\|\|G_{2}\|.

The rest follows easily by induction on
k
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

For any graph
G
of order
n
and size
m\geq 0
,

\deg(G)\leq 2dpe(G)\leq m(m-1),

and equality holds if and only if
G\approx(mK_{2})\dot{\cup}\overline{K}_{n-2m}
.

Proof.

For each pair of disjoint edges in
G
, there are at most two 2-switches that replace them. Hence,

\deg(G)\leq 2dpe(G)=m(m-1)-\sum_{v=1}^{n}(d_{v}^{2}-d_{v})\leq m(m-1).

Clearly, equality is achieved precisely when
d_{v}\in\{0,1\}
for each vertex
v\in G
. Under this condition, we must have
d_{u}=d_{v}=1
for every
uv\in E(G)
, and the remaining
n-2m
vertices of
G
are isolated.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
G
be a graph. If
p_{4}(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx P_{4}\}|
and
k_{3}(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx K_{3}\}|
, then

p_{4}(G)+3k_{3}(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1).

(7)

Proof.

For each edge
uv\in G
, we have
d_{u}-1
options to attach an edge
ux\neq uv
at
u
, and
d_{v}-1
options to attach an edge
vy\neq uv
at
v
. If
G
has no triangles, there are exactly
(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1)
different
P_{4}
’s for each edge
uv
in
G
. If
G
has triangles, then

(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1)=|P_{4}^{*}(uv)|+|K_{3}(uv)|,

(8)

where
P_{4}^{*}(uv)
is the set of all
P_{4}
’s in
G
with
uv
as the central edge, and
K_{3}(uv)
is the set of all triangles in
G
containing
uv
. Since

\{H\subseteq G:H\approx P_{4}\}=\dot{\bigcup}_{e\in E(G)}P_{4}^{*}(e),

it is clear that
\sum_{e\in E(G)}|P_{4}^{*}(e)|=p_{4}(G)
. On the other hand, let
M
be the multiset given by the union, over
e\in E(G)
, of all
K_{3}(e)
. Since each triangle
T\in\{H\subseteq G:H\approx K_{3}\}
appears in
M
with multiplicity 3, it follows that
\sum_{e\in E(G)}|K_{3}(e)|=3k_{3}(G)
. Finally, (
7
) follows from (
8
) by summing over all
uv\in E(G)
.
∎

We denote by
c_{4}(G)
and
k_{4}(G)
the number of cycles and cliques of order 4 in
G
, respectively. More precisely:

c_{4}(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx C_{4}\}|,

k_{4}(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx K_{4}\}|=|Q_{G}(K_{4})|.

Theorem 5.5
.

If
G
is a vertex of
\mathcal{G}(s)
, then

\deg(G)=2dpe(s)+2c_{4}(G)-p_{4}(G)-4k_{4}(G).

(9)

Letting
\|G\|=m
, (
9
) can be rewritten as:

\deg(G)=m(m+1)-|s|^{2}+2c_{4}(G)+3k_{3}(G)-4k_{4}(G)-\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1).

Proof.

First, recall that
dpe(G)=|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx 2K_{2}\}|
. If
Y\preceq G
and
|Y|=4
, observe that

|\{H\subseteq G:H\approx Y\}|=\sum_{i=1}^{11}|Q_{G}(X_{i})|\cdot|\{H\subseteq X_{i}:H\approx Y\}|,

(10)

where the sum in (
10
) ranges over the 11 graphs
X_{i}
in
Figure

1
. To simplify notation, we write
|X|
instead of
|Q_{G}(X)|
and use
p_{4},c_{4},k_{4},dpe
instead of
p_{4}(G),c_{4}(G),k_{4}(G),dpe(s)
. Applying formula (
10
) for
Y\in\{C_{4},P_{4},2K_{2}\}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle c_{4}

\displaystyle=|D_{4}|+|C_{4}|+3k_{4},

\displaystyle p_{4}

\displaystyle=4|C_{4}|+|P_{4}|+2|U_{4}|+6|D_{4}|+8k_{4},

\displaystyle dpe

\displaystyle=2|C_{4}|+|P_{4}|+|2K_{2}|+|U_{4}|+2|D_{4}|+3k_{4}.

Solving for
|C_{4}|
,
|2K_{2}|
, and
|P_{4}|
from these, and applying
Theorem

4.1
, we find:

\displaystyle\deg(G)

\displaystyle=2(c_{4}-|D_{4}|-3k_{4})+

\displaystyle\quad 2(dpe-2|C_{4}|-|U_{4}|-|P_{4}|)+

\displaystyle\quad p_{4}-4|C_{4}|-2|U_{4}|-6|D_{4}|-8k_{4}

\displaystyle=2dpe+2c_{4}+p_{4}-12|D_{4}|-20k_{4}-4|U_{4}|-2(4|C_{4}|+|P_{4}|)

\displaystyle=2dpe+2c_{4}-p_{4}-4k_{4}.

∎

Theorem

5.5
suggests that the difficulty of computing the 2-switch-degree of
G
essentially lies in determining the number of 4-cliques in
G
, i.e.,
k_{4}(G)
. This is because it is relatively “easy” to compute
c_{4}(G)
and
k_{3}(G)
(from which
p_{4}(G)
depends). Indeed, it is well known that these can be obtained from the
adjacency matrix

A
of
G
, an
n\times n
matrix (where
n=|G|
), with entry
[A]_{ij}=1
if
ij\in E(G)
and
0
otherwise. In fact:

k_{3}(G)=\frac{1}{6}\,\text{tr}(A^{3}),

where
\text{tr}(\ast)
is the trace, and

c_{4}(G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\binom{[A^{2}]_{ij}}{2}.

We also have:

\text{tr}(A^{4})=8c_{4}(G)+2\|G\|+4\sum_{v=1}^{n}\binom{d_{v}}{2}=8c_{4}(G)-2\|G\|+2|s|^{2},

c_{4}(G)=\frac{1}{8}\text{tr}(A^{4})+\frac{1}{4}\|G\|-\frac{1}{4}|s|^{2}=\frac{1}{8}\text{tr}(A^{4})+\frac{1}{2}dpe(s)-\frac{1}{4}\|G\|^{2}.

No general closed formulas (like the above) are currently known for
k_{4}
. In fact, the problem of counting cliques of a given size in a graph is generally of extreme computational complexity, as it is NP-complete. However, for certain families of graphs it might be “easy” to determine the degree of their members (or the degree might even be constant in such families), allowing
k_{4}
to be computed easily via formula (
9
).

As immediate corollaries of
Theorem

5.5
, we can derive simplified formulas for specific graph classes. For example, if
G
is triangle-free (i.e.,
G
contains no 3-cycles), then
k_{3}(G)=0=k_{4}(G)
. If
G
is
C_{4}
-free, then
c_{4}(G)=0=k_{4}(G)
. The
girth
of a graph
G
, denoted by
g(G)
, is the length of the shortest cycle contained in
G
. If
G
is acyclic,
g(G)
is defined as
\infty
. Note that
g(G)\geq 5
if and only if
k_{3}(G)=0=c_{4}(G)
.

Corollary 5.6
.

If
G\in\mathcal{G}(s)
and
g(G)\geq 5
, then

\deg(G)=2dpe(s)-p_{4}(G).

(11)

Letting
\|G\|=m
, formula (
11
) can be rewritten as

\deg(G)=m(m+1)-|s|^{2}-\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1).

Proof.

It follows from the previous discussion.
∎

As an application of
Corollary

5.6
, we can compute the degree of paths and cycles, since
g(P_{n})=\infty
and
g(C_{n})=n
.

Paths of order 1 or 2 clearly have degree 0. For
n\geq 3
,
s=s(P_{n})=2^{n-2}1^{2}
. Since
dpe(s)=\binom{n-2}{2}
and
p_{4}(P_{n})=n-3
, we get

\deg(P_{n})=(n-3)^{2}.

(12)

For each
n\geq 3
,
s=s(C_{n})=2^{n}
and
dpe(s)=\binom{n}{2}-n
. Clearly,
\deg(C_{3})=0
and
\deg(C_{4})=2
. If
n\geq 5
, then
p_{4}(C_{n})=n
and therefore,

\deg(C_{n})=n(n-4).

(13)

Note that
\deg(C_{n})\geq\deg(P_{n})
for all
n\geq 3
. We can apply formulas (
12
) and (
13
) to obtain a lower bound on the degree of a graph
G
that contains some path
P
or cycle
C
as an induced subgraph. Indeed, from
Proposition

4.3
we have
\deg(G)\geq\deg(P),\deg(C)
.

Let
G
be a graph with degree sequence
s=(d_{v})_{i=1}^{n}
. The numbers

\zeta_{1}(G)=\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{v}^{2}=|s|^{2},

\zeta_{2}(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}d_{u}d_{v}

are called the
first
and
second Zagreb indices
of
G
, respectively. These are well-known parameters in Chemical Graph Theory and widely studied in the literature. These so-called topological invariants are often used to quantify structural characteristics of chemical compounds. Both
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
were first introduced in 1972 by Gutman and Trinajstić (see
[
8
]
) and have been used to relate molecular properties such as chemical stability and
\pi
-electron energy. The latter refers to the electrons involved in
\pi
bonds, a type of covalent bond formed by the lateral overlap of
\pi
atomic orbitals. There is a relationship between the total energy of
\pi
electrons and the Zagreb indices, since the latter capture aspects of molecular connectivity and branching that influence electron delocalization. For example, in certain studies, it has been found that greater branching (reflected in higher Zagreb index values) can lead to lower stability and thus higher total
\pi
-electron energy. Therefore, Zagreb indices can be useful tools for estimating and understanding how molecular structure affects
\pi
-electron energy.

It is a curious fact that these chemical indices arise naturally in our context. Indeed, since
\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}+d_{v})=\zeta_{1}(G)
, we have

\sum_{uv\in E(G)}(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1)=\zeta_{2}(G)-\zeta_{1}(G)+\|G\|.

(14)

We know that the left-hand sum in (
14
) (which equals
p_{4}(G)+3k_{3}(G)
by
Proposition

5.4
) is part of the formula in
Theorem

5.5
and
Corollary

5.6
for computing
\deg(G)
. Hence, we see a link between the 2-switch and the Zagreb indices. More specifically, the next theorem tells us how the 2-switch-degree is tied to
\zeta_{2}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

If
G
is a graph, then

\deg(G)+\zeta_{2}(G)=\|G\|^{2}+2c_{4}(G)+3k_{3}(G)-4k_{4}(G).

Proof.

It follows from the previous discussion.
∎

When
g(G)\geq 5
, we obtain a remarkable corollary of
Theorem

5.7
: the sum of the 2-switch-degree and the second Zagreb index equals the square of the size.

Corollary 5.8
.

If
g(G)\geq 5
, then

\deg(G)+\zeta_{2}(G)=\|G\|^{2}.

(15)

Proof.

It follows immediately from
Theorem

5.7
, since
c_{4}(G)=k_{3}(G)=k_{4}(G)=0
when
g(G)\geq 5
.
∎

Now suppose we are studying extremal values of the second Zagreb index (and which graphs attain them) in a family of graphs with fixed size. Equality (
15
) may be very useful in this case, as it tells us that minimizing (maximizing)
\zeta_{2}
is equivalent to maximizing (minimizing) the degree.

6
Degree of trees and unicyclic graphs

We denote by
\mathcal{F}(s)
the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(s)
induced by vertices of
\mathcal{G}(s)
that are forests. Since all members of
V(\mathcal{F}(s))
have the same number of connected components, it follows that if one of them is a tree, then all the others are trees as well. A 2-switch
\tau
on a forest
F\in V(\mathcal{F}(s))
is called an
f-switch
on
F
if
\tau(F)\in V(\mathcal{F}(s))
. When
F
is a tree, we can use the term
t-switch
instead of f-switch. The
f-degree
of a forest
F
, denoted by
\deg_{f}(F)
, is the degree of the vertex
F
in the graph
\mathcal{F}(s)
. Equivalently,
\deg_{f}(F)
is the number of f-switches that act on
F
. Clearly,
\deg_{f}(F)\leq\deg(F)
, because
\mathcal{F}(s)\preceq\mathcal{G}(s)
.

The following result provides a formula to compute the f-degree of a tree.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
T
be a tree in
\mathcal{F}(s)
, where
s=(d_{v})_{v=1}^{n}
. Then,

\deg_{f}(T)=dpe(s)=\binom{n-1}{2}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\binom{d_{v}}{2}=\binom{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2},

where
\binom{i}{j}=0
if
i<j
.

Proof.

Consider the 2-switches
\tau=\binom{a\ b}{c\ d}
and
\tau^{\prime}=\binom{a\ b}{d\ c}
. Assuming that
ab\ldots cd
is the path connecting
a
to
d
in
T
, we observe that:

1.

\tau
is a t-switch on
T
;

2.

if
bc\notin E(T)
, then
\tau^{\prime}
disconnects
T
, and therefore is not a t-switch on
T
;

3.

if
bc\in E(T)
, then
\tau^{\prime}
is inactive on
T
.

With this in mind, it is now easy to see that we can perform exactly one t-switch in
T
for each unordered pair of disjoint edges of
T
. Then,
\deg_{f}(T)=dpe(s)
, and we apply
Proposition

5.1
.
∎

It is quite surprising that the f-degree of a tree
T\in V(\mathcal{F}(s))
is an invariant associated with
s
.
Theorem

6.1
has a significant impact on the global structure of
\mathcal{F}(s)
. Indeed, we see that every vertex of this graph has the same degree, namely
dpe(s)
. Moreover, we already know that
\mathcal{F}(s)
is connected (
[
11
]
, page 9). Hence, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.2
.

If
s
is the degree sequence of a tree, then
\mathcal{F}(s)
is a connected and
k
-regular graph, where
k=dpe(s)
.

Proof.

It follows from the preceding discussion.
∎

Despite what we have just observed for
\deg_{f}(T)
, the next corollary shows that
\deg(T)
is not generally an invariant associated with
s(T)
.

Corollary 6.3
.

If
T
is a tree of order
n
, then

\deg(T)=2\deg_{f}(T)-\sum_{uv\in E(T)}(d_{u}-1)(d_{v}-1)=(n-1)^{2}-\zeta_{2}(T).

(16)

Proof.

The first equality is an immediate consequence of
Theorems

5.5
and
6.1
, where
k_{3}(T)=c_{4}(T)=k_{4}(T)=0
and
dpe(T)=\deg_{f}(T)
. The second equality follows from
Corollary

5.6
.
∎

If
T
is a tree in
\mathcal{G}(s)
, note that
\deg(T)-\deg_{f}(T)
is the number of disconnected neighbors of
T
in
\mathcal{G}(s)
.

We denote by
\mathcal{U}(s)
the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(s)
induced by unicyclic graphs with degree sequence
s
. It is worth noting that
\mathcal{U}(s)
is connected (
[
10
]
, page 8). A 2-switch
\tau
on a unicyclic graph
U\in V(\mathcal{U}(s))
is said to be a
u-switch
on
U
if
\tau(U)\in V(\mathcal{U}(s))
. The
u-degree
of a unicyclic graph
U
, denoted by
\deg_{u}(U)
, is the degree of the vertex
U
in the graph
\mathcal{U}(s)
. Equivalently,
\deg_{u}(U)
is the number of u-switches acting on
U
. Clearly,
\deg_{u}(U)\leq\deg(U)
, because
\mathcal{U}(s)\preceq\mathcal{G}(s)
.

In a unicyclic graph
U
, we can always identify the following two subgraphs: the unique cycle
C
of
U
and the forest

F=U-E(C)-\{v\in V(C):\deg_{U}(v)=2\}.

Then,
E(U)=E(C)\dot{\cup}E(F)
and
|V(F)\cap V(C)|
is the number of components of
F
. If
s(U)=2^{n}
, then
U\approx C_{n}
and
F=K_{0}
. But if
d_{v}\neq 2
for some
v\in V(U)
, then
E(F)\neq\varnothing
. Note that
C
is an induced subgraph of
U
, but
F
, in general, is not.

Corollary 6.4
.

If
U\in\mathcal{U}(s)
is a unicyclic graph of order
n
with a cycle of length
c
, then

\deg(U)=\begin{cases}n^{2}-\zeta_{2}(U)+3,&\text{if }c=3\\
n^{2}-\zeta_{2}(U)+2,&\text{if }c=4\\
n^{2}-\zeta_{2}(U),&\text{if }c\geq 5\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

It follows immediately from
Theorem

5.7
.
∎

Theorem 6.5

(
[
10
]
, page 5)
.

Let
\tau
be a 2-switch acting on the edges
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
of a unicyclic graph
U
. If
C
and
F
are, respectively, the cycle and the forest of
U
, then we have the following:

1.

if
e_{1},e_{2}\in F
and
e\in E(C)
, then
\tau
is a u-switch on
U
if and only if it is a t-switch on
U-e
;

2.

if
e_{1}\in C
and
e_{2}\in F
, then
\tau
is a u-switch on
U
;

3.

if
e_{1},e_{2}\in C
, then
\tau
is a u-switch on
U
if and only if
\tau(C)\approx C
.

Theorem 6.6
.

If
U
is a unicyclic graph with cycle
C
and forest
F
, then

\displaystyle\deg_{u}(U)=

\displaystyle\deg(U)-\deg(C)+dpe(C)-dpe(F)+p_{4}(F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\deg(U)-\deg(C)-\deg(F)+dpe(C)+dpe(F).

Proof.

We can compute the required formulas by computing the number
\bar{u}(U)
of u-switches that do not preserve the unicyclic structure of
U
, since
\deg_{u}(U)=\deg(U)-\bar{u}(U)
. Using
Theorem

6.5
, it follows that these 2-switches fall into two disjoint categories:

(1).

those between edges in
F
that are not t-switches on
U-e
, for any
e\in E(C)
;

(2).

those between edges in
C
that split
C
into two disjoint cycles.

If
F
has
k
components
T_{i}
, then there are
\deg(T_{i})-\deg_{f}(T_{i})
2-switches of type (1) acting on each
T_{i}
. Moreover, there is exactly one 2-switch of type (1) for each unordered pair of edges lying in different components of
F
. Using
Corollary

5.6
, we deduce that

\deg(T_{i})-\deg_{f}(T_{i})=dpe(T_{i})-p_{4}(T_{i}).

Furthermore, it is clear that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{4}(T_{i})=p_{4}(F).

Finally, since

dpe(F)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}dpe(T_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\|T_{i}\|\|T_{j}\|

by
Proposition

5.2
, we find that there are

dpe(F)-p_{4}(F)

(17)

active 2-switches of type (1) in
U
. Using
Corollary

5.6
, we see that (
17
) is equal to
\deg(F)-dpe(F)
.

To determine the number of 2-switches of type (2), first note that
\deg_{u}(C)=dpe(C)
. Then, there are

\deg(C)-dpe(C)

active u-switches of type (2) in
U
, and therefore,

\bar{u}(U)=\deg(C)-dpe(C)+dpe(F)-p_{4}(F).

∎

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
U\in\mathcal{U}(s)
be a unicyclic graph of order
n
with cycle
C
and forest
F
. If
c=|C|
, then we have:

\deg_{u}(U)=\begin{cases}2dpe(s)+dpe(F)-\deg(F)-p_{4}(U),&\text{if }c=3\\
2dpe(s)+dpe(F)-\deg(F)-p_{4}(U)+2,&\text{if }c=4\\
2dpe(s)+dpe(F)-\deg(F)-p_{4}(U)-\frac{c}{2}(c-5),&\text{if }c\geq 5\\
\end{cases}

where

p_{4}(U)=\begin{cases}\zeta_{2}(U)-|s|^{2}+n-3,&\text{if }c=3\\
\zeta_{2}(U)-|s|^{2}+n,&\text{if }c\geq 4\\
\end{cases}

Contrary to
\mathcal{F}(s)

is not regular in general. To see this, consider the unicyclic graph

obtained by identifying a vertex of a

with a leaf of a

. We see that

and
