On the spectral stability of the periodic capillary-gravity waves
Changzhen Sun, Erik Wahlén

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the spectral stability of periodic traveling waves in the two dimensional gravity-capillary water wave problem. We derive a stability criterion based on an index function, whose sign determines the spectral stability of the waves. This result aligns with earlier formal analyses by Djordjević & Redekopp [ 15 ] and Ablowitz & Segur [ 1 ] , which employed the nonlinear Schrödinger approximation in the modulational regime. In particular, we show that instability is excluded near spectral crossings away from the origin when the surface tension is positive and the inverse square of the Froude number α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) , \alpha\in(0,1), which results from the fact that the corresponding Krein signatures are identical. Numerical studies further reveal the existence of a decreasing curve β = β ⁡ ( α ) : ( 0 , 1 ) → ℝ + , \beta=\beta(\alpha):(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}, such that the periodic waves are spectrally stable for all β > β ⁡ ( α ) \beta>\beta(\alpha) . These findings highlight the stabilizing effect of surface tension on periodic capillary-gravity waves.

The equations of motions are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}U+U\cdot\nabla U+\nabla P+ge_{z}=0,\\[3.0pt]

The systems

\displaystyle U_{2}|_{z=0}=0,

where

As the velocity

Theorem 1.1
.

\beta>0

Through straightforward calculations (see Appendix

[

who derived a criterion for modulational stability using the Evans function approach and investigated spectral stability near the first two crossings away from the origin through numerical studies.
However, on the one hand, when applied to water waves, the Evans function approach tends to be much more computationally intensive. On the other hand, the Evans function approach reduces the analysis of stability near high-frequency crossings to determining the sign of a certain index function. This contrasts with Kato’s perturbation theory, where stability can be characterized by the non-vanishing of an index function, which facilitates the spectral analysis near high-frequency crossings.
We therefore approach the problem using Kato’s perturbation theory, which not only yields a simple criterion for modulational (in-)stability but also provides a more precise description of the spectrum near the origin. Moreover, we rigorously prove that no instability arises from
any
spectral crossings away from the origin when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III,

thereby establishing global spectral stability beyond the modulational regime.

1.1.
Background on the stability of Stokes waves

The stability of Stokes waves—periodic traveling wave solutions of the gravity water wave equations—has been an important theme in the study of water waves.
A key phenomenon in this context is modulational instability, where long-wavelength perturbations can cause the wave train to destabilize. This effect, commonly referred to as the Benjamin–Feir instability, was first observed by Benjamin and Feir through experiments and formal calculations in the 1960s
[
5
]
.
A rigorous mathematical foundation was laid by Bridges and Mielke
[
10
]
, who analyzed the modulational stability of Stokes waves in finite depth using a Hamiltonian spatial dynamics framework.
Recently, Nguyen and Strauss
[
26
]

proved modulational instability of deep-water Stokes waves, a situation where the
spatial dynamical reduction cannot be employed.
In
[
19
]
, Hur and Yang re-examined the spectral instability of finite-depth Stokes waves using the Evans function approach. They identified a criterion for modulational instability and also detected instability at the next spectral crossing away from the origin. In a series of paper
[
8
,
9
,
7
,
6
]
, Berti et al. investigated the stability of Stokes waves based on
Bloch-Floquet theory by employing the
analytic perturbation theory of Kato
[
23
]
. We also refer to
[
12
]
for a formal perturbative analysis and numerical simulations concerning the high-frequency instabilities of Stokes waves.
Recently, the stability of one-dimensional Stokes waves as solutions to the three-dimensional water-wave system—known as transverse stability—has attracted considerable research interest. See
[
14
,
13
]
for studies on purely transverse perturbations (i.e., co-periodic perturbations along the longitudinal direction), and
[
20
]
for analyses involving both longitudinal and transverse perturbations.

The study of capillary-gravity Stokes waves—periodic solutions of the capillary-gravity water-wave system—is also interesting but has received relatively limited mathematical attention. Apart from the formal analyses presented in
[
15
,
1
]
, the only existing mathematical proof to date is due to Hur and Yang
[
18
]
, who employed the Evans function approach in the spirit of their earlier work on gravity waves. In this work, we aim to re-examine the problem by using the Kato’s perturbation theory, which not only enables us to find the criterion for the modulational stability, but also enables us to rule out any potential instability near other spectral crossings away from the origin when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III.
As a result, we obtain a global spectral stability result.

1.2.
Strategy of the proof

In a broad sense, our work falls within the scope of stability studies of periodic waves for Hamiltonian systems, for which we can refer to

[
28
,
3
,
16
,
21
,
11
,
27
,
2
]
.

Since the operator (
1.13
) has periodic coefficients, one can apply the Bloch-Floquet transform to reduce the spectral analysis of

L^{\varepsilon}
on the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})-
based space
Y
to the spectral study of a family of operators
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
parametrized by the Floquet parameter
\xi\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]

on the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{2\pi})-
based
space
Y_{\mathrm{per}}.
Since each
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
has compact resolvent, the spectrum of
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
is composed by a family of discrete eigenvalues with finite multiplicities. Moreover, when
\varepsilon=0,
the spectrum of each
L_{\xi}^{0}
can be computed via the Fourier transform and lies entirely on the imaginary axis.
Inherited from the Hamiltonian structure (
1.15
) the spectrum of each
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
remains symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis. As a result, any potential unstable spectrum of
L^{\varepsilon}
can only bifurcate from the multiple eigenvalues of
L_{\xi}^{0}
(which we will refer to as the crossing spectrum) for certain values of the Floquet parameter
\xi
. It is shown in Lemma
2.1
that the multiple eigenvalues consist of

•

Zero eigenvalue of
L_{0}^{0}
with multiplicity four,

•

Countably many other spectral crossings
away from the origin with multiplicity two (arising from the same branch of the spectral curve
when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III
).

The task is then to study how the spectrum bifurcates from these crossing points as
\varepsilon

becomes non-zero but remains small. The general strategy is to construct a finite-dimensional representation matrix whose eigenvalues coincides with the spectrum of
L^{\varepsilon}
near these crossing points. This is achieved by constructing appropriate bases and dual bases for the associated eigenspaces. The latter is carried out by employing Kato’s perturbation theory, starting from the bases at
\varepsilon=0
or
\xi=0,
which can be obtained via the
Fourier transform or by exploiting the structure of the background waves.

Let us first explain how to examine the spectral behavior near the crossing points away from zero, where the corresponding eigenspaces are two-dimensional. Assume the crossing spectrum
\lambda_{0}
happens with Floquet parameter
\xi_{0}.
When

|(\varepsilon,\xi-\xi_{0})|
is small,
we can construct the basis
(q_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),q_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot))
by extending the basis
(q_{1}^{0}(\xi,\cdot),q_{2}^{0}(\xi,\cdot))
that is obtained by Fourier transform. Thanks to the Hamiltonian symmetry

(L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})^{*}=-J^{-1}L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}J,

one can construct the dual basis
\{\tilde{q}_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\tilde{q}_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\}
from the linear combinations of
\{\mathrm{i}Jq_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\mathrm{i}Jq_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\}.
It results from the fact that

q_{1}^{0}(\xi,\cdot),q_{2}^{0}(\xi,\cdot)
always involves different Fourier modes that the dual basis can be chosen as

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)=\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{j}^{-1}\,J{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\qquad\mathrm{with}\,\,\,\omega_{j}(\xi)=\langle\mathrm{i}\,J{q}_{j}^{0}(\xi,\cdot),{q}_{j}^{0}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle,\,\,j=1,2.

One important property in the current situation is that the associated Krein signatures—that is, the signs of
\alpha_{1}(\xi),\alpha_{2}(\xi)-
are always the same. This fact, combined with the relation
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=JA_{\xi}^{\varepsilon},
where
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}

is a symmetric operator, allows us to deduce that the representation matrix takes the form

(1.20)

\displaystyle D_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\,\left(\langle\tilde{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}q_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle_{L_{per}^{2}}\right)_{1\leq j,\ell\leq 2}=\mathrm{i}P_{\xi}\tilde{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}P_{\xi}^{-1}

where
P_{\xi}
is an invertible matrix and

\tilde{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
is a real symmetric matrix.
It follows from (
1.20
) that the eigenvalues of
D_{\xi}^{\varepsilon},
hence the spectrum of
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
near
\lambda_{0},
must stay on the imaginary axis. Consequently, no instability arises from any of the crossing spectra away from zero. This aligns with the classical understanding in Hamiltonian PDE theory that the collision of purely imaginary eigenvalues with identical Krein signatures typically does not result in spectral instability, see
[
22
]
.
Let us remark that such a procedure for rigorously proving stability in this setting was also observed in an earlier work by the first author and collaborators, in the context of periodic waves for the Euler–Poisson system
[
27
]
. In that case, however, the Krein signatures are always opposite, leading instead to a conclusion of instability.

We are now in a position to outline the strategy for analyzing the spectrum of
L^{\varepsilon}
near zero, which corresponds to the modulational stability of periodic waves. Since zero is a spectral crossing of

L_{0}^{0}
with multiplicity four, the resulting matrix will be
4\times 4.

To construct the four bases, one can again start with the eigenfunctions of
L_{\xi}^{0}

and extend them to the case
\varepsilon\neq 0.
However, this approach requires computing the expansions of
these basis functions in terms of the amplitude
\varepsilon
(at least to the second order), making it computationally demanding. Therefore, we instead begin with the basis of
L_{0}^{\varepsilon},
whose spectrum near the origin consists solely of
\{0\}.
The associated eigenfunctions can be obtained using the profile equations and the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction (see Proposition
5.2
), and are then extended to form a basis for
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}

with small nonzero
\xi
by the Kato’s perturbation theory. The dual bases are then found from linear combinations of
\{\mathrm{i}Jq_{1}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\mathrm{i}Jq_{2}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\mathrm{i}Jq_{3}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\mathrm{i}Jq_{4}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\}.

With these in hand, we find the rather explicit expression of the representation matrix

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\,\left(\langle\tilde{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}q_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle_{L_{per}^{2}}\right)_{1\leq j,\ell\leq 4}\,.

To compute the eigenvalues of this matrix, we further exploit the symmetries and parity properties of the wave profiles to derive useful information about its entries. The first expression we obtain is

(1.21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\,&*&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\,\varepsilon&*\,\varepsilon\\
*\,(\kappa\xi)^{2}&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi&*(\kappa\xi)^{2}\varepsilon&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\,\varepsilon\\
*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\,\varepsilon&*\,\varepsilon&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi&*\\
*(\kappa\xi)^{2}\varepsilon&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\,\varepsilon&*(\kappa\xi)^{2}&*\,\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi\end{pmatrix}

where
*
denotes certain
real
constants depending smoothly on
\xi,\varepsilon.
Motivated by this expression, we anticipate that the eigenvalues of

\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
are proportional to
\mathrm{i}\kappa\xi.
This observation prompts the following transformation:

(1.22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\,\kappa\,\xi}\mathrm{P}_{\xi}\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{P}_{\xi}^{-1}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{M}_{11}&\varepsilon\mathrm{M}_{12}\\
\varepsilon\mathrm{M}_{21}&\mathrm{M}_{22}\end{pmatrix}(\varepsilon,\xi)\qquad

where
\mathrm{P}_{\xi}=\text{diag}\,(\mathrm{i}\,\kappa\xi,1,\mathrm{i}\,\kappa\xi,1)
and
\mathrm{M}_{j\ell}\,(1\leq j,\ell\leq 2)
are
2\times 2
matrices with real coefficients which are smooth in
\xi
and
\varepsilon.
As is readily seen,
\sigma(\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})=\mathrm{i}\,\kappa\xi\,\sigma(\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}),
and the problem thus reduces to analyzing the eigenvalues of the real matrix

\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon},
which represents the crucial part of the analysis. Since the off-diagonal
2\times 2
blocks are of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon),
one can expect the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
to be close to that of
\mathrm{M}_{11}
and
\mathrm{M}_{22}.
A direct calculation shows that the two eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{22}
remain real, provided
(\varepsilon,\xi)
is sufficiently small. The eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{11},

on the other hand, possess nonzero imaginary parts when
|\xi|
is much smaller than
\varepsilon
and their size is of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon).
Consequently, the presence of non-vanishing off-diagonal terms can potentially render all eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
real.
The idea to get the full description of the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
is to introduce some transformation to make one of the off-diagonal

2\times 2
block
\varepsilon\mathrm{M}_{12}
or
\varepsilon\mathrm{M}_{21}
vanish, whose existence is ensured as long as the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{11}
and
\mathrm{M}_{22}
are well-separated (i.e.
e_{*}\neq\sqrt{\alpha}
), which is the case when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III,

see Proposition
5.14
and
Remark (
5.15
). More precisely, we first perform a transformation to make the block
\mathrm{M}_{22}
diagonal and then find another transformation to make
\varepsilon\mathrm{M}_{12}
vanish, which is proven to exist by applying the Inverse Function Theorem. Finally, we arrive at the transformed matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{M}_{11}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{Q}\mathrm{G}^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{21}&0\\
\varepsilon\mathrm{G}^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{21}&\begin{pmatrix}X_{2}^{+}&\\
&X_{2}^{-}\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{G}^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{21}\mathrm{Q}\end{pmatrix}

where
X_{2}^{+},X_{2}^{-}
are two positive real eigenvalues of
\mathrm{M}_{22},

G
and
Q
are two
2\times 2
real matrices. It is now clear that the eigenvalues of the above matrix consist of the real values
X_{2}^{\pm}+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2})

and the two eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathrm{M}_{11}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{Q}\mathrm{G}^{-1}\mathrm{M}_{21}.
These latter two are purely real when the quantity
w_{1}^{\prime\prime}(0)C(\alpha,\beta),
defined in (
5.34
), is positive and they acquire nonzero imaginary parts
of order
{\mathcal{O}}(|\varepsilon\xi|)
when
|\xi|\ll|\varepsilon|.

Our proof of modulational stability differs from
[
8
]
, where Kato’s perturbation method is
employed to study the stability of gravity Stokes waves.
In
[
8
]
, the construction of the basis begins with the operator
L_{0}^{0}
which is then extended to a basis for
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
for small nonzero values of
\xi
and
\varepsilon.
Since the Kato extension operator preserves the symplectic inner product, the dual basis can be conveniently constructed via

(1.23)

\tilde{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)=\mathrm{i}Jq_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),\,\qquad j=1,2,3,4.

The subsequent spectral analysis proceeds by performing three steps of Bloch diagonalization on the resulting representation matrix. In contrast, our method directly exploits the structure of the background waves to construct a basis for
L_{0}^{\varepsilon}
which we then extend to a basis for
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
for small
\xi\neq 0.
This approach simplifies the computation by avoiding the need to explicitly compute the Kato extension in terms of the amplitude parameter. The trade-off, however, is that our initial basis
\{q_{j}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\}
does not satisfy perfect orthogonality with respect to the symplectic inner product, and therefore, the dual basis cannot be expressed simply as in (
1.23
).
Instead, it must be constructed as a linear combination of
\{Jq_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\}_{j=1,2,3,4}.
Fortunately, we find that the dual basis
\tilde{q}_{j}(\xi,\cdot)
is a small perturbation of
Jq_{j}^{\varepsilon},
see (
5.5
),(
5.9
),
which suffices for our purposes. With the bases and dual bases in hand, we derive the representation matrix as in expression (
1.21
). By observing that the eigenvalues of the diagonal blocks
\mathrm{M}_{11}
and
\mathrm{M}_{22}
are uniformly separated, we can find directly a similarity transformation to make one of the off-diagonal blocks vanish and thus facilitate the subsequent spectral study.

2.
Spectral preliminaries

2.1.
Bloch transform

To analyze the spectrum of operators with periodic coefficient, it is expedient to introduce Bloch symbols, associated with the (Floquet-)Bloch transform.

In a similar spirit to the inverse Fourier transform, the inverse Bloch transform provides a way to reconstruct a function from its Bloch components. For any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\hat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, it holds that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{ix\xi}\check{f}(\xi,x)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

where the direct Bloch transform of
f
,
{\mathcal{B}}(f)=\check{f}
, is defined as

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}(f)(\xi,x)=\check{f}(\xi,x):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mathrm{i}\,j\,x}\hat{f}(\xi+j).

Note that by design, for each
\xi
,
\check{f}(\xi,\cdot)
is periodic of period
2\pi
, so that
f
is written as an integral of functions
g_{\xi}\,=\,e^{i\xi\,\cdot}\check{f}(\xi,\cdot)
satisfying
g_{\xi}(x+2\pi)=e^{i\xi}\,g_{\xi}(x)
. Such functions are known as Bloch waves, the number
e^{i\xi}
being a Floquet multiplier, and
\xi
is classically called Floquet exponent.

With the help of the inverse Bloch transform (
2.1
), one can turn an operator with periodic coefficients acting on a function over
\mathbb{R}
into a family of operators acting on periodic functions. Explicitly, it holds that,

(2.3)

\displaystyle L^{\varepsilon}(u)(x)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{ix\xi}L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}(\check{u}(\xi,\cdot))(x)\mathrm{d}\xi

where each
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}:=L^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)
acts on
Y_{\mathrm{per}}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
with domain
H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
. The main gain when replacing the direct analysis of
L^{\varepsilon}
with those of
L_{\xi}^{\delta}
is that each
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
has compact resolvent and thus their spectrum is composed of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity. A key related observation is that

\displaystyle\sigma(L^{\varepsilon}|_{Y})=\bigcup_{\xi\in[-1/2,1/2]}\sigma(L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}).

It is also important to notice that the Hamiltonian symmetry is inherited by
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}:

(2.4)

\displaystyle(L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})^{*}=-JL_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}J^{-1},

and thus its spectrum is again symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis.

2.2.
Study of the constant operator
L_{\xi}^{0}

By definition,

\displaystyle L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}k_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)(d_{\varepsilon}\cdot)&G_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]\cdot\\[5.0pt]
P_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]-w_{\varepsilon}&k_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)\end{array}\right)\,,

where

(2.5)

\displaystyle G_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]=e^{\mathrm{i}\,x\cdot\xi}G[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]e^{-\mathrm{i}\,x\cdot\xi};\quad P_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]=e^{\mathrm{i}\,x\cdot\xi}P[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]e^{-\mathrm{i}\,x\cdot\xi}

are operators acting on periodic functions.
Specifically, when
\varepsilon=0
,

\displaystyle L_{\xi}^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\kappa(\partial_{x}+i\xi)&|\kappa(\partial_{x}+i\xi)|\tah(|\kappa(\partial_{x}+i\xi)|)\\[5.0pt]
\beta\kappa^{2}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)^{2}-\alpha&\kappa(\partial_{x}+i\xi)\end{array}\right)\,.

Being an operator with constant coefficients, its spectrum can be found explicitly by using the Fourier transform:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sigma(L_{\xi}^{0})=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(\xi);\qquad\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(\xi)=\mathrm{i}\bigg(\kappa(j+\xi)\pm\sqrt{\kappa(j+\xi)\tah(\kappa(j+\xi))\big(\alpha+\beta\kappa^{2}(j+\xi)^{2}\big)}\big)\bigg).

The spectrum of
L_{\xi}^{0}
is thus contained in the imaginary axis. By the Hamiltonian symmetry (
2.4
),
the simple eigenvalue of
L_{\xi}^{0}
remain in the imaginary axis for small
\varepsilon>0
and possible unstable spectrum bifurcates only from multiple eigenvalues of
L_{\xi}^{0}
when
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small. Therefore, we need to find all the crossing spectrum of
L_{\xi}^{0}
with
\xi\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}].

Lemma 2.1

(Crossing of
L_{\xi}^{0}
)
.

Let

(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III,
where

\rm I,III
are defined in (

The following facts hold:

(1). [Crossing at the origin].

The origin is an eigenvalue of

of algebraic multiplicity four:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{-1}^{+}(0)=\lambda_{1}^{-}(0)=\lambda_{0}^{+}(0)=\lambda_{0}^{-}(0)=0.

(2). [Crossing away from the origin].
There are
countably many other spectral crossings of multiplicity two, which occur when

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{j_{\ell}}^{+}(\xi_{\ell})=\lambda_{j^{\prime}_{\ell}}^{+}(\xi_{\ell}):=i\sigma_{\ell};\qquad\lambda_{-j_{\ell}}^{-}(-\xi_{\ell})=\lambda_{-j_{\ell}^{\prime}}^{-}(-\xi_{\ell}):=-i\sigma_{\ell}\qquad

Let

.
It can be shown that, when

has only one critical point

for

and

\sigma_{-}(k)\geq 0
for

One gets the corresponding property for
\sigma_{+}(k)
from the relation

.
This proves the crossing (

) and that
\lambda^{\pm}_{j}(\xi)\neq 0
for all other

. Note that

resulting in the eigenvector

and the generalized eigenvector

To study the crossings away from the origin, note that for any

and

From the concavity

On the other hand, if

Moreover, from the sign and monotonicity properties of

3.
Exclusion of unstable spectrum with large imaginary part

In this section, we aim to show that the spectrum of

L^{\varepsilon}
lies on the imaginary axis when their imaginary part is large enough:

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}>0
and a constant
M=M(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1})\in(1,+\infty)
such that for any
|\varepsilon|\lesssim\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1},
any
\xi\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],
it holds that

\displaystyle\sigma(L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})\,\cap\{\lambda|\,|{\rm Im}\,\lambda|\geq M\}\subset\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Let
L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=JA_{\xi}^{\varepsilon},
with the symmetric operator
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
defined by

\displaystyle A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-P_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]+w_{\varepsilon}&-d_{\varepsilon}k_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)\\[5.0pt]
k_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}+i\xi)(d_{\varepsilon}\cdot)&G_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]\,\cdot\end{array}\right)\,,

where
P_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}],\,G_{\xi}[\zeta_{\varepsilon}]
are defined in (
2.5
). Since
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
is compact, it suffices to prove the desired result for
\xi
lying in a small interval
(\xi_{0}-\delta,\xi_{0}+\delta)
, where
\xi_{0}\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
and
\delta>0
is small enough.

The proof relies on the fact that the symmetric operator
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
has finite negative eigenvalues for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
that we will show now. When
\varepsilon=0,
the eigenvalues of
A_{\xi}^{0}
can be computed via Fourier transform

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}^{\pm}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg({(\alpha+\beta\eta_{j}^{2})+|\eta_{j}|\tah(|\eta_{j}|)}\pm\sqrt{\big((\alpha+\beta\eta_{j}^{2})-|\eta_{j}|\tah(|\eta_{j}|)\big)^{2}+4\eta_{j}^{2}}\bigg)\in\mathbb{R}

where
\eta_{j}:=\kappa(j+\xi).
It is easily verified that
\Lambda_{j}^{+}(\xi)>0
for any
j\in\mathbb{Z},\xi\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],
and
\Lambda_{j}^{-}(\xi)<0
if and only if

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta\eta_{j}^{2}<\frac{\eta_{j}}{\tah(\eta_{j})}\,.

Consequently, for each
\xi\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],
there are only finitely many
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\Lambda_{j}^{-}(\xi)<0.
For convenience, we label the real eigenvalues of
A_{\xi}^{0}
by

(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}(\xi))_{j=-j_{0}(\xi)}^{+\infty}

where

j_{0}(\xi)\in(0,+\infty),\qquad\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell}(\xi)\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell+1}(\xi),

and
\tilde{\sigma}_{0}(\xi)\geq 0
is the smallest nonnegative eigenvalue of
A_{\xi}^{0}.
It is also worth noticing that the multiplicity of each eigenvalue is at most four. Take a
k_{0}\geq 5
such that
\tilde{\sigma}_{k_{0}+1}(\xi)-\tilde{\sigma}_{k_{0}}(\xi)>0
for any
\xi\in(\xi_{0}-\delta,\xi_{0}+\delta).

We can find a ball
B_{R}
around the origin with radius large enough such that

\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell}\in B_{R}
for any
\ell\in(-j_{0}(\xi),k_{0})
and any
\xi\in(\xi_{0}-\delta,\xi_{0}+\delta)
,
and
\partial B_{R}\in\rho(A_{\xi}^{0};(L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}})^{2}).
Moreover, the uniform resolvent estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{|\xi-\xi_{0}|\leq\delta}\sup_{\lambda\in B_{R}}\|(\lambda I-A_{\xi}^{0})^{-1}\|_{B((L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}})^{2})}<+\infty

holds.
Since

\displaystyle\|A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\xi}^{0}\|_{B({\rm Dom}(A_{\xi}^{0}),\,(L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}})^{2})}\lesssim\varepsilon,

there exists
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2},
such that for any

|\varepsilon|\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2},\lambda\in\rho(A_{\xi}^{0},Y_{\mathrm{per}}).

Then we define the projector

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}:=\oint_{B_{R}}(\lambda I-L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

Since
\|\tilde{\Pi}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\Pi}_{\xi}^{0}\|\lesssim\varepsilon,
we know that

B_{R}
contains the same number of eigenvalues of
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
and
A_{\xi}^{0},
and thus
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
also has finitely many negative eigenvalues.

Since
A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}
is self-adjoint on
(L_{\mathrm{per}}^{2}([0,2\pi]))^{2}
and has compact resolvent, there is a corresponding orthonormal eigenbasis
\{\theta_{j}(\xi,\cdot)\}
of
(L_{\mathrm{per}}^{2}([0,2\pi]))^{2}

Furthermore, for any

\displaystyle\langle u^{\perp},A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}u^{\perp}\rangle\geq c_{0}\|u^{\perp}\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}^{2},\qquad\forall\,\xi\in(\xi_{0}-\delta,\xi_{0}+\delta).

Now for
\lambda

{\rm Re}\,\lambda>0

(3.1)

\displaystyle(\lambda I-JA_{\xi}^{\varepsilon})u=f.

Writing

\displaystyle u=\sum_{j=-j_{0}}^{k_{0}}\gamma_{j}\theta_{j}+u^{\perp}

and taking real part of the

we find that

\displaystyle c_{0}\,{\rm Re}\,\lambda\|u^{\perp}\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}^{2}\leq{\rm Re}\,\lambda\langle u^{\perp},A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}u^{\perp}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-{\rm Re}\,\lambda\sum_{j=-j_{0}}^{k_{0}}|\gamma_{j}|^{2}\langle\theta_{j},A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\theta_{j}\rangle+{\rm Re}\,\langle f,A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}u\rangle

where we have used the fact that
\theta_{j}

It thus follows from Young’s inequality that

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\lambda\,\|u^{\perp}\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}^{2}\leq C\bigg({\rm Re}\,\lambda\sum_{j=-j_{0}}^{k_{0}}|\gamma_{j}|^{2}+({\rm Re}\,\lambda)^{-1}\|f\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}^{2}\bigg)\,.

On the other hand, we take the inner product of

(3.3)

\displaystyle|\lambda|\sum_{j=-j_{0}}^{k_{0}}|\gamma_{j}|\lesssim\|u\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}+\|f\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}.

Combining (

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}\leq\|u^{\perp}\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}+\sum_{j}|\gamma_{j}|\|\theta_{j}\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j}|\gamma_{j}|+({\rm Re}\,\lambda)^{-1}\|f\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\lambda|^{-1}\|u\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}+({\rm Re}\,\lambda)^{-1}\|f\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}\,.

Consequently, as long as

(and hence

\displaystyle\|u\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}\lesssim({\rm Re}\,\lambda)^{-1}\|f\|_{Y_{\mathrm{per}}}\,,

so that

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\rm I\cup III

Let
\xi_{0}>0

5.2.2.
Some useful properties of the entries

We now aim to gather some useful properties of the entries, from the symmetries of the system and the parity of the basis.

Lemma 5.9
.

For
|\xi|\leq\xi_{0},\,\varepsilon\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{2},

let

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}=\big(d_{j\ell}(\varepsilon,\xi)\big)_{1\leq j,\ell\leq 4}\,.

\displaystyle d_{j\ell}\in\mathrm{i}\,\mathbb{R},\,\quad\forall\,j,\ell\in\{1,3\}\textrm{ or }\{2,4\}\,,

In particular, it holds that
\mathrm{for}\,\,j,\ell\in\{1,3\}
or

\displaystyle d_{j\ell}(\varepsilon,\xi)=\mathrm{i}\,\kappa\,\xi\,m_{j\ell}(\varepsilon,\xi)

and for

\displaystyle d_{j\ell}=-\kappa^{2}\xi^{2}m_{j\ell}(\varepsilon,\xi).

Here

and

Proof.

By the construction (see Proposition
5.2
),

\displaystyle q_{1}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),\,q_{3}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{odd}\\
\mathrm{even}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\,q_{2}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),\,q_{4}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{even}\\
\mathrm{odd}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}q_{j}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=(-1)^{j}\,q_{j}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\,,

where reversibility operator
{\mathcal{I}}
is defined in (
E.3

), this property is preserved under the action of the extension operator so that

The other piece of information is that

which is readily checked from the parity of the background waves.

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\,\overline{\langle q_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),{\mathcal{I}}A_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}q_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle}

\langle Jq_{j}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot),L_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}q_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}(\xi,\cdot)\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\quad\mathrm{for}\,\,j+\ell\,\,\mathrm{even};\,\,\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\,\,\mathrm{for}\,j+\ell\,\,\mathrm{odd}.

The property (
5.12
) then follows from

\tilde{q}_{j}^{\varepsilon}

in (
5.5
).

Next, since

which, together with (
5.12
), leads to (
5.13

Moreover, when

and
\ell\in\{1,3\},
by using

\displaystyle=\big\langle Jq_{j}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),L_{1}^{\varepsilon}q_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\big\rangle,

Proof of Theorem
