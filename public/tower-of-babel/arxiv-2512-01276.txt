Samplability makes learning easier
Guy Blanc Stanford Caleb Koch Stanford Jane Lange MIT Carmen Strassle Stanford Li-Yang Tan Stanford

Abstract
The standard definition of PAC learning (Valiant 1984) requires learners to succeed under all distributions—even ones that are intractable to sample from. This stands in contrast to samplable PAC learning (Blum, Furst, Kearns, and Lipton 1993), where learners only have to succeed under samplable distributions. We study this distinction and show that samplable PAC substantially expands the power of efficient learners. We first construct a concept class that requires exponential sample complexity in standard PAC but is learnable with polynomial sample complexity in samplable PAC. We then lift this statistical separation to the computational setting and obtain a separation relative to a random oracle. Our proofs center around a new complexity primitive, explicit evasive sets , that we introduce and study. These are sets for which membership is easy to determine but are extremely hard to sample from. Our results extend to the online setting to similarly show how its landscape changes when the adversary is assumed to be efficient instead of computationally unbounded.



1
Introduction

In the PAC model
[
Val84
]
, the learner is given labeled data
(\bm{x},f(\bm{x}))
where
\bm{x}
is drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
. The functions
f
is promised to belong to a known, often simple, concept class, but no assumptions are made about
\mathcal{D}
. Notably, the learner is required to succeed even under distributions that are intractable to sample from. These are distributions
\mathcal{D}
for which any generator
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{Unif})=\mathcal{D}
must have superpolynomial circuit size. Consequently, making even a single draw
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
takes superpolynomial time.

This is arguably an overly stringent requirement. PAC learners are expected to be efficient and yet the distribution, which can be viewed as an adversary in this context, is allowed to be computationally unbounded. This stacks the odds in favor of the adversary and is one reason why efficient PAC learning algorithms have been hard to come by even for simple concept classes. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{D}
is intractable to sample from then it alone renders the entire learning process inefficient, regardless of the efficiency of the learner. If the PAC model is to capture efficient end-to-end learning, from data collection to hypothesis generation, we may as well consider only samplable distributions. Relatedly, if one believes in the strong Church-Turing thesis, then all distributions occurring in practice are samplable. Quoting
[
Imp95
]
, “Presumably, real life is not so adversarial that it would solve intractable problems just to give us a hard time.”

Samplable PAC.

It is therefore natural to consider a variant of the PAC model, samplable PAC, where the distribution is assumed to be samplable but is otherwise still unknown and arbitrary. This strikes the balance of imposing just enough structure on the distribution to place the learner and adversary on equal footing, while still allowing for enough expressivity to capture the complexities of real-world learning.

The samplable PAC model was first considered by Blum, Furst, Kearns, and Lipton
[
BFKL93
]
. The focus of their paper was not on the distinction between samplable and standard PAC. Rather, they showed that efficient learning in samplable PAC is tightly connected to the existence of fundamental cryptographic primitives. As their main result, they proved that if one-way functions do not exist, then every concept class is
average-case
learnable in samplable PAC.

1

1

This is a further relaxation of samplable PAC where there is an additional distribution, this one over target functions, and the learner is only required to succeed with respect to a random target function drawn from this distribution. We do not consider this variant in our work.

1.1
This work

We study the distinction between samplable and standard PAC.
We are interested in formalizing the extent to which the assumption of samplability—a seemingly mild and reasonable assumption—expands the power of efficient learners. In measuring efficiency, we focus on the two most basic resources in learning: samples and runtime.

Statistical separation.

The sample complexity of learning in standard PAC is fairly well-understood, in large part due to an elegant characterization in terms of VC dimension
[
VC71
,
BEHW89
]
—a foundational result now called “The Fundamental Theorem of PAC Learning”
[
SSBD14
]
. A VC dimension lower bound is generally viewed as an information-theoretic no-go in terms of efficient learnability: If a learning task cannot be learned with a reasonable number of samples, that trivially implies that it cannot be learned in a reasonable amount of time either.

Our first result is as follows:

Theorem 1

(See
Theorem

5
for the formal version)
.

There is a concept class with exponential VC dimension—and hence requires exponential sample complexity in standard PAC—but is learnable with polynomial sample complexity, and in fact even in polynomial time, in samplable PAC.

This shows that VC dimension lower bounds can be overly pessimistic. A learning task with large VC dimension may nevertheless be efficiently learnable if one assumes that these samples are generated according to a reasonable distribution. Put differently, the VC dimension lower bound may be witnessed by an extremely complicated shattering set, one that is intractable to sample from and hence arguably will not arise in the real world—indeed, this intuition is the starting point for our proof of
Theorem

1
.

Computational separation.

The concept class in
Theorem

1
has exponential circuit complexity, necessarily so since the class of size-
s
circuits has VC dimension
\tilde{O}(s)
. It is natural to then ask about samplable vs. standard PAC learning of classes of polynomial-size circuits. Such classes are of interest because functions that arise in practice can be assumed to be efficiently computable, and relatedly, the barriers to their efficient learnability are solely computational in nature and cannot rely on information-theoretic impossibility.

As already observed in
[
Val84
]
, if
\mathsf{RP}=\mathsf{NP}
then every concept class of polynomial-size circuits is efficiently learnable in polynomial time in standard PAC. So short of proving
\mathsf{RP}\neq\mathsf{NP}
, any computational separation will have to either rely on complexity assumptions or be relativized. Implicit in the work of Xiao
[
Xia10
]
is a separation relative to a
specific
oracle. We discuss
[
Xia10
]
’s result in
Section

3
, mentioning for now that this is an oracle relative to which one-way functions do
not
exist. This therefore should not be viewed as evidence as to whether such a separation exists in the unrelativized world—and
[
Xia10
]
did not claim it as such—since presumably we believe that one-way functions do exist in the unrelativized world.

Our second result gives a computational separation conditioned on two complexity assumptions, a standard one (ironically, the existence of one-way functions) and a new one that we introduce (the existence of explicit “evasive sets”,
2.6
):

Theorem 2

(See
Theorem

7

Assume the existence of one-way functions and explicit evasive sets. There is a concept class of polynomial-size circuits that requires superpolynomial time to learn in standard PAC, but is learnable in polynomial time in samplable PAC.

The definition of an explicit evasive set is rather technical and we defer it to
Section

2

that is

2.2
).

Random oracle separation.

Proving the existence of explicit evasive sets unconditionally is likely difficult: Like in the case of one-way functions, doing so will imply
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
(

Corollary 1.1

(See
Theorem

9

The assumptions of

As is standard in complexity theory, we view

Remark 1.2

(Evasive sets and uniform generation)
.

The existence of explicit evasive sets is related to, but differs from, the hardness of

[

]

and is asked to sample
\mathrm{Unif}(C^{-1}(1))
(either exactly or approximately). The existence of explicit evasive sets implies the hardness of uniform generation but not vice versa: It could be the case that every polynomial-size circuit
C

does
have a corresponding polynomial-size circuit that generates

Online learning.

Another well-studied model of supervised learning is the online mistake bound model
[
Lit88
]
. Learning in this model proceeds in rounds. In each round, the adversary presents the learner with an unlabeled instance
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. The learner responds with its prediction
y\in\{0,1\}
and is then told whether that is correct (i.e. whether
y=f(x)
). The goal of the learner is to minimize its total number of mistakes.

Here again the standard definition allows the adversary to be computationally unbounded—it can take superpolynomial time to produce the test instance in each round. Yet, efficient online learners are expected to be efficient even against such adversaries. For the same reasons as in the PAC setting, it is therefore natural to consider the variant where the adversary is also assumed to be efficient. In
Section

9
we show how our techniques can be extended to the online setting to similarly show how the complexity of online learning—both in terms of mistake bounds and the runtime of learners—can depend on the power of the adversary.

The connection to PAC learning

Proof overview of
Theorem

1
.

We first describe how
Lemma

2.5
yields a statistical separation of samplable PAC from standard PAC. For a set
H\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
and function

to denote the following restriction of

f_{H}(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)&\text{if $x\in H$}\\

For a concept class

to denote the restriction of

to

\mathcal{C}_{H}=\{f_{H}\colon f\in\mathcal{C}\}.

(1)

Now consider
\mathcal{A}_{H}

\mathcal{A}

On the other hand, we are able to exploit the evasiveness of

to design an efficient algorithm for learning
\mathcal{A}_{H}
in samplable PAC:

Lemma 2.7

(Evasiveness implies efficient learners)
.

Let
H\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a set that
(\varepsilon,k)
-evades size-
s
distributions. Then for any concept class
\mathcal{C}
there is an algorithm for learning
\mathcal{C}_{H}
to error

under all size-
s

The intuition for

is simple. If

(\varepsilon,k)

Proof overview of
Theorem

2
.

In the proof of

Consider the class

Distribution-specific learning.

Given the apparent difficulty of designing efficient algorithms in standard PAC, there has been a large body of work on
distribution-specific
learning. Here the data is promised to be drawn from a specific distribution, e.g. the uniform distribution. The main downside is that this is a stylized assumption that limits the practical relevance of the model: We want our algorithms to succeed for as broad a class of distributions as possible, not just a specific one. In the case of the uniform distribution in particular, it does not capture much of the richness real-world distributions that stem from correlations among features.

Samplable PAC can be viewed as a middle ground that simultaneously corrects for the overly stringent requirements of standard PAC and the overly strong assumptions of distribution-specific PAC.

Lifting uniform-distribution learners.

In the same spirit of bridging this gap between standard PAC and distribution-specific PAC, recent works
[
BLMT23
,
BLST25
]
show how uniform-distribution PAC learners can be generically “lifted” to also succeed under various non-uniform yet still structured classes of distributions.

Compared to these works, our work attempts to bridge the gap “from the opposite direction”. While these lifters scale
up
the distribution-specific model, the samplable PAC model scales
down
the distribution-free model (i.e. standard PAC).

Computable PAC and online learning.

Another recent line of work
[
AABD+20
,
AABD+21
,
Ste22
,
DRKRS23
]
studies the distinction between standard PAC and a variant known as
computable
PAC where learners are restricted to be computable. Among other results, these works show that there are classes with finite VC dimension that are not learnable in computable PAC. See also
[
HBD23
,
DRKS25
]
for the online analogue.

The focus of our work is on statistical and computational complexity in settings where computability is not an issue, rather than the distinction between computable and uncomputable learners.

Samplable distributions in average-case complexity.

Outside of learning theory, samplable distributions are central to the study of average-case complexity
[
Lev86
,
BDCG89
]
. While
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
rules out the possibility of efficient algorithms that solve
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problems on all instances, average-case complexity is concerned with the possibility of efficient algorithms that solve most instances generated by a samplable distribution (i.e. the possibility that
\mathsf{NP}
is hard in the worst case but easy on average). As in samplable PAC, in this context samplable distributions are taken as the formalization of distributions that actually occur in practice.

distributions

Figure 3
:
An illustration of how samplable distributions relate to real-world distributions.

Samplability is generally viewed as a baseline requirement for real-world distributions, not a characterization. See
Figure

3
. Samplable PAC is therefore only a first cut at refining the standard PAC model.

We see two takeaways from this. First, as alluded to in the introduction, lower bounds in standard PAC—be they statistical or computational—may be overly pessimistic: While certain learning tasks may have hard instances, these hard instances themselves may be hard to find, and arguably will not occur in practice. Second, this calls for a better understanding of the actual structure of real-world distributions beyond just samplability, which can then be leveraged in the design of learning algorithms. This falls within the overall agenda of going beyond the worst-case analysis of algorithms
[
Rou21
]
. Our techniques suggest the possibility of deeper connections to the complexity of sampling and uniform generation.

A concrete open problem is that of characterizing the sample complexity of learning in samplable PAC. Sample complexity in standard PAC is characterized by VC dimension—what is the corresponding characterization for samplable PAC? More generally, is there a characterization that takes both function and distribution complexity into account?

5.5
Pseudorandom function families

Definition 5.12

(Pseudorandom function families (PRFFs) secure against non-uniform adversaries)
.

A pseudorandom function family
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{s}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}\}_{s\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
is a collection of functions such that the following holds.

(i)

There exists a polynomial-time algorithm that given
s,x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
computes
f_{s}(x)
.

(ii)

For all polynomial-time oracle algorithms with advice
A
and a negligible function
\sigma(n)
, we have

\begin{split}\left|\begin{aligned} &\quad\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{s}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[A(1^{n})\text{ outputs }1\text{ when given oracle access to }f_{\bm{s}}]\\
&\quad\qquad-\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{{\bm{f}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}}[A(1^{n})\text{ outputs }1\text{ when given oracle access to }{\bm{f}}]\quad\end{aligned}\right|<\sigma(n)\end{split}

where
{\bm{f}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a uniformly random function.

A classic result in cryptography shows that one way functions (OWFs) secure against non-uniform adversaries imply the existence of PRFFs secure against non-uniform adversaries
[
GGM86
]
. Since we only consider non-uniform adversaries, when we write OWFs, we mean those that give rise to the PRFFs in
Definition

5.12
.

6.1
Proof of
Lemma

2.5

In this section, we will find an
H
that evades all samplable distributions. This

will form the basis for the concept class

that will be learnable in samplable PAC.

We prove
Lemma

2.5
using the probabilistic method. We first show that any
fixed
distribution, regardless of samplability, hits a random
\bm{H}
with small probability (
6.1
). Taking a union bound over all samplable distributions, we derive the existence of an
H
that evades all of them.

Claim 6.1

(Distributions are unlikely to hit
\bm{H}
)
.

Let
\delta>0
,
\varepsilon\geq 2\delta
, and
k\in\mathds{N}
. Let
\bm{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a random subset such that each
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is included in
\bm{H}
independently with probability
\delta
. Then for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\text{$\mathcal{D}$ \emph{$(\varepsilon,k)$-hits} $\bm{H}$}]\leq e^{-\varepsilon k/6}.

Proof.

Let
T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
consist of
x
such that
\mathcal{D}(x)\geq\frac{1}{k}
. Notice that
|T|\leq k
. So if
\mathcal{D}

(\varepsilon,k)
hits
H
, then by definition
\mathcal{D}(H\setminus T)\geq\varepsilon
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\text{$\mathcal{D}$ \emph{$(\varepsilon,k)$-\emph{hits}} $\bm{H}$}]\leq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus T)\geq\varepsilon]

so it suffices to bound this second probability. Unpacking this notation, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus T)=\sum_{x}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}[x\in\bm{H}\setminus T]

is the sum of independent random variables. Let’s name these variables
\bm{Z}_{x}=\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}[x\in\bm{H}\setminus T]
. By the definition of
T
, each variable is bounded
\bm{Z}_{x}\in[0,1/k]
, and we can also bound the expected value of their sum

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus T)]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{H}}\bigg[\sum_{x}\bm{Z}_{x}\bigg]\leq\sum_{x}\mathcal{D}(x)\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[x\in\bm{H}\setminus T]\leq\delta

by the density of
\bm{H}
. Rescaling,
k\bm{Z}_{x}
are random variables bounded in
[0,1]
with expected sum at most
\delta k
. We can therefore apply the Chernoff bound given in
5.1
:

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus T)\geq\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}\bigg[\sum_{x}k\bm{Z}_{x}\geq k\varepsilon\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}\bigg[\sum_{x}k\bm{Z}_{x}\geq(1+(\varepsilon-\delta)/\delta)\delta k\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}}{\delta^{2}(2+(\varepsilon-\delta)/\delta)}\delta k\bigg)

(
5.1
with
\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{\delta}
)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}}{\varepsilon+\delta}k\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\varepsilon/2)^{2}}{3\varepsilon/2}k\bigg)

(
\delta\leq\varepsilon/2
)

\displaystyle=e^{-\varepsilon k/6},

which proves the claim. ∎

(Discretizing

)

,

(\varepsilon,O(k/\varepsilon))

Let

(\varepsilon,O(k/2^{-i}))
-hits

Proof.

Let
\delta^{\prime}=2\delta
and
\bm{H}

By

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}\Big[\exists\text{ $s\in\mathds{N}$ such that $\mathrm{Bad}\Big(s,\varepsilon,k^{\star},\bm{H}\Big)$}\Big]\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}e^{-c_{2}(s+n)\log s}.

.

Let
\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(A)
denote
A
’s expected error over
\mathcal{D}
. Since the target function is constant 0 outside
H
, this learner can only err on
x
if
x\in H
and
x\not\in S
. Therefore, its expected error is at most

\displaystyle\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(A)\leq\sum_{x\in H}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[x\notin\bm{S}].

Let
H^{*}\subseteq H
be the
k
heaviest elements in
H
. Formally,
H^{*}
is the set maximizing
\mathcal{D}(H^{*})
subject to the constraints
|H^{*}|\leq k
and
H^{*}\subseteq H
. Then, we can split the above sum in two by considering elements in
H^{*}
and not in
H^{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in H\setminus H^{*}}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[x\notin\bm{S}]+\sum_{x\in H^{*}}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[x\notin\bm{S}]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\sum_{x\in H^{*}}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[x\notin\bm{S}]

(Definition of
(\varepsilon,k)
-missing)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\sum_{x\in H^{*}}\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot(1-\mathcal{D}(x))^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\sum_{x\in H^{*}}\mathcal{D}(x)e^{-\mathcal{D}(x)m}.

By taking the first derivative of each term in the sum with respect to
\mathcal{D}(x)
and setting it equal to 0, we can see that the above sum is maximized if each
\mathcal{D}(x)=1/m
, at which point this simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(A)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\sum_{x\in H^{*}}\frac{1}{em}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\frac{k}{em}.

(Size of
T
)

Setting
m=O(k/\varepsilon)
achieves expected error
O(\varepsilon)
. Applying a Markov bound completes the proof.
∎

(\varepsilon,O((s\log s)/\varepsilon))
-evades all size-
s

and

Pseudorandom construction of

for

.

5.10
provides a straightforward way of randomly constructing a subset

: set

generates a
\delta

-biased

-dense with high probability (

Claim 7.2

(Fixed distribution hits a pseudorandomly generated
\bm{H}
with small probability)
.

Let
\delta<1/2
and let
\bm{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be
\delta
-biased
d
-wise independent subset. Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then,

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{H}}[\mathcal{D}\text{ }(\varepsilon,k)\textnormal{-hits }\bm{H}]\leq e^{-k\varepsilon/6}

for any
\varepsilon\geq 2\delta
and
k\leq\min\{d/(2\delta),2^{n}/2\}
.

Proof.

We mostly follow the proof of
6.1
but will use concentration for random variables with bounded independence (
5.11
) rather than a standard Chernoff bound. Let
H^{*}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be the set of
x
such that
\mathcal{D}(x)\geq 1/k
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution there are at most
k
such
x
:
|H^{*}|\leq k
. If
\mathcal{D}

(\varepsilon,k)
-hits
\bm{H}
, then
\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus H^{*})\geq\varepsilon
.

Let
\overline{H^{*}}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus H^{*}
denote the complement of
H^{*}
. For each
x\in\overline{H^{*}}
, we define a scaled indicator random variable
\bm{Z}_{x}\coloneqq k\cdot\mathcal{D}(x)\cdot\mathds{1}[x\in\bm{H}]
so that
\bm{Z}_{x}\in[0,1]
and

\sum_{x\in\overline{H^{*}}}\bm{Z}_{x}=k\cdot\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus H^{*}).

Furthermore, we compute the mean of the sum as

\displaystyle\mu\coloneqq\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\Bigg[\sum_{x\in\overline{H^{*}}}\bm{Z}_{x}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=k\cdot\sum_{x\in\overline{H^{*}}}\mathcal{D}(x)\operatorname{{Pr}}[x\in\bm{H}]

(Definition of
\bm{Z}_{x}
)

\displaystyle=d\delta\sum_{x\in\overline{H^{*}}}\mathcal{D}(x)\leq k\delta.

(Assumption on
\bm{H}
and
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution)

Using our assumptions that
k\leq 2^{n}/2
and
\delta<1/2
, it is straightforward to verify that

\frac{\mu}{1-\mu/|\overline{H^{*}}|}\leq 2k\delta

and therefore we have enough independence to apply
5.11
. Let
\gamma
be such that
k\varepsilon\coloneqq(1+\gamma)\mu
. Notice by our assumption that
\varepsilon\geq 2\delta
, we have
k\varepsilon\geq 2\delta k\geq 2\mu
and so
\gamma\geq 1
. We calculate

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus H^{*})\geq\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[k\mathcal{D}(\bm{H}\setminus H^{*})\geq k\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}\Bigg[\,\sum_{x\in\overline{H^{*}}}\bm{Z}_{x}\geq k\varepsilon\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{\gamma}}{(1+\gamma)^{(1+\gamma)}}\right)^{\mu}

(
5.11
)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}-\ln(1+\gamma)\right)k\varepsilon\right)

(
\mu=k\varepsilon/(1+\gamma)
)

\displaystyle<\exp(-k\varepsilon/6)

(
\gamma\geq 1
)

where in the last line we used the fact that the function
\gamma/(1+\gamma)-\ln(1+\gamma)
for
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
has its maximum at
\gamma=1
and
1/2-\ln(2)<-1/6
.
∎

Claim 7.3
.

Let
\bm{Z}
have the same mean and variance as a binomial distribution with mean
\mu\geq 1
. Then
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{Z}>\mu/2]\geq 1/5
.

Proof.

We apply Cantelli’s inequality (sometimes referred to as one-sided Chebyshev’s inequality): For any
t\geq 0

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{Z}\leq\mu-t]\leq\frac{\operatorname{{Var}}[\bm{Z}]}{\operatorname{{Var}}[\bm{Z}]+t^{2}}.

Using the upper bound
\operatorname{{Var}}[\bm{Z}]\leq\mu
and plugging in
t=\mu/2
,

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{Z}\leq\mu/2]\leq\frac{\mu}{\mu+(\mu/2)^{2}}=\frac{4}{4+\mu}\leq\frac{4}{5}.\qed

Using the fact that the mean and variance of
\sum_{i\in[m]}\bm{z}_{i}
are the same as if
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{m}
are fully independent or pairwise independent, we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
\bm{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be a random subset such that the random variables
\{\mathds{1}[x\in\bm{H}]\}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
are
\delta
-biased,
d
-wise independent for
d\geq 2
and
\delta\geq 2^{-n}
. Then,
|\bm{H}|>\delta 2^{n-1}
with probability at least
1/5
.

We can now prove the main lemma of this subsection.

Proof of
Lemma

.

Let

(2)

\bm{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{x}^{(m)}

is marginally uniform)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{km}{2^{n}}.

)

\displaystyle<\exp(-k\varepsilon/6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\Big[|\bm{H}|

Lemma 8.8

(Breaking PRFF security using efficient learners)
.

For any function family
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{f_{s}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}\}_{s\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\begin{split}\left|\begin{aligned} &\quad\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{s}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[A\text{ outputs }1\text{ when given oracle access to }f_{\bm{s}}]\\
&\quad\qquad-\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{{\bm{f}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}}[A\text{ outputs }1\text{ when given oracle access to }{\bm{f}}]\quad\end{aligned}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon-\frac{\alpha}{2},\end{split}

is a fully random function.

Proof.

Let

On the other hand, if instead we set the label to

for a fully random function

, then as long as

is not contained in

will be independent of the sample

Hence, there exists an explicit choice of

Proposition 9.10
