1.
Introduction

Combinatorial neural codes provide a discrete model for patterns of neural activity. A code

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
records which subsets of
n
neurons can be simultaneously active across stimuli.
Beyond being a list of observed firing patterns, a code implicitly encodes constraints among receptive
fields and, more broadly, structural information about an underlying stimulus representation that is
shared across realizations. Extracting such realization invariant structure is subtle, and algebra
provides a systematic way to do so. In particular, Curto et al.
[
2
]

introduced
neural ideals
as an algebraic encoding of essential receptive-field relations; see also

[
3
,
5
,
10
]
and the references therein.

To a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
one associates a neural ideal
J_{\mathcal{C}}\subset k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. The ideal

J_{\mathcal{C}}
can be studied via its
canonical form

CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
, a distinguished generating set
consisting of the minimal pseudo-monomials encoding minimal receptive-field relations; this set
generates
J_{\mathcal{C}}
(not necessarily minimally). Since neural ideals are typically not graded, we pass
to a graded setting by polarizing the canonical form generators, following
[
6
]
: replacing each
factor
(1-x_{i})
by a new variable
y_{i}
produces a squarefree monomial ideal

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})\ \subset k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}],

the
polarized neural ideal
of
\mathcal{C}
. This is the main algebraic object we study in this paper.
Our goal is to organize neural codes using homological data of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
. We focus on the graded
invariants
\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
and
\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
, namely the projective dimension and
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

Because
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is squarefree, it is the Stanley–Reisner ideal of a simplicial complex
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
,
which we call the
polar complex
(following the terminology of
[
6
]
). In particular, the
multigraded Betti numbers of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
, and hence
\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
and
\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
, are controlled
by the topology of induced subcomplexes of
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
via Hochster’s formula. This
correspondence forms the foundation of our homological study of neural codes.

Several authors have associated simplicial complexes to neural codes in order to study geometric and
combinatorial properties complementary to the homological questions pursued here. For example,

[
7
]
introduces a polar complex
\Gamma(\mathcal{C})
and uses
shellability to analyze stable hyperplane codes, while
[
9
]
defines the factor
complex
\Delta_{\cap}(\mathcal{C})
to study interval structure and (max-)intersection-complete codes. Neither

\Gamma(\mathcal{C})
nor
\Delta_{\cap}(\mathcal{C})
agrees in general with the polar complex
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
considered in
this paper.

Although
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is a squarefree monomial ideal in
2n
variables, it is far from arbitrary: it
satisfies the ambient constraint that no minimal generator is divisible by
x_{i}y_{i}
. Homological
properties of squarefree monomial ideals satisfying this condition have been examined recently
in
[
1
]
. Our approach differs from
[
1
]
in that we repeatedly
exploit additional structure coming from the fact that
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
arises by polarizing canonical
form generators, a hypothesis not imposed there.

A guiding theme of this work is that extremal and boundary behavior for
\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
and

\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))
is controlled by elementary combinatorial features of the
Hamming cube
in which
the code
\mathcal{C}
lives. Throughout, by the

we mean the
n

Our first results provide general constraints from the topology of

We prove that for every nonempty code

A recurring phenomenon is that Hamming-cube geometry controls both invariants at their extremes.
The smallest nonzero regularity,
\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))=1

At the

. We exploit this interplay
between fixing coordinates, taking complements of coordinate subcubes, and antipodality throughout the
paper, and these results support the broader theme that the homological invariants of

provide a useful organizing lens for neural codes.

Beyond extremal classifications, we give explicit constructions realizing large regions of the

2.
Preliminaries

This section collects background and notation used throughout the paper. We follow the algebraic
framework of
[
2
]
, which introduces the neural ideal and its canonical form as a minimal
algebraic encoding of receptive-field (RF) relations. We then recall polarization of neural ideals
and the associated
polar complex
from
[
6
]
.

Set-Up 2.1
.

Fix a field
k
. For
n\geq 1
set
R:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and

S:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
, both standard
\mathbb{Z}
-graded with

\deg(x_{i})=\deg(y_{i})=1
. We write
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and identify
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}=\{0,1\}^{n}\subset k^{n}
.

2.1.
Neural codes and realizations

Many of the codes we study arise from a family of receptive fields
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
in a
stimulus space
X
. The resulting code records which firing patterns occur and, equivalently, which
set-theoretic containments among intersections
\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}
and unions
\bigcup_{j\in\tau}U_{j}

are forced by the absence of certain patterns.

Definition 2.2
.

A
neural code
on
n
neurons is a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
whose elements

\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{n})
are called
codewords
. The
i
th coordinate
\mathbf{c}_{i}
indicates
whether neuron
i
fires (
\mathbf{c}_{i}=1
) or does not fire (
\mathbf{c}_{i}=0
).

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X\neq\emptyset
and
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
with
U_{i}\subseteq X
.
The
code of
\mathcal{U}
is

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U}):=\left\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\left(\bigcap_{\mathbf{c}_{i}=1}U_{i}\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\mathbf{c}_{j}=0}U_{j}\right)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

If
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U})
for some
\mathcal{U}
, we say
\mathcal{U}
is a
realization
of
\mathcal{C}
.

(a)

Collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},U_{4}\}

(b)

Collection
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\{U_{1}^{\prime},U_{2}^{\prime},U_{3}^{\prime},U_{4}^{\prime}\}

Figure 2
.
Two collections of receptive fields in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Example 2.4
.

For the collections
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
in
Figure

2
, the associated codes are

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle=\{0000,1000,0100,0001,1100,1110,0101,0111\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\{0000,1000,1100,1101,1110\}.

Although a code
\mathcal{C}
can have many realizations, the code determines a collection of RF relations
that must hold in
every
realization. A basic family of such relations are containments of the form

\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in\tau}U_{j},\qquad\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n].

2.2.
Neural ideals and RF relations

Neural ideals provide an algebraic way to record RF containments. We work in the polynomial ring

R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a neural code. For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
define its
characteristic
pseudo-monomial

\rho_{v}:=\prod_{v_{i}=1}x_{i}\prod_{v_{j}=0}(1-x_{j})\in R.

The
neural ideal
of
\mathcal{C}
is

J_{\mathcal{C}}:=\langle\rho_{v}:v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}\rangle\subseteq R.

Remark 2.6
.

For each
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the pseudo-monomial
\rho_{v}
satisfies
\rho_{v}(v)=1
and
\rho_{v}(w)=0

for all
w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
w\neq v
. Consequently, the zero set of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is

V(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ f(w)=0\ \text{for all}\ f\in J_{\mathcal{C}}\}=\mathcal{C}.

See also
[
2
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Notation 2.7
.

For
\sigma\subseteq[n]
, write

U_{\sigma}:=\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x_{\sigma}:=\prod_{i\in\sigma}x_{i}.

The following theorem explains how pseudo-monomials encode RF containments.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
2
]

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
be a realization in a stimulus space
X
.
Fix
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
with
\sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset
. Then

x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}\iff\bigcap_{i\in\sigma}U_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in\tau}U_{j}.

Notation 2.9
.

We use the conventions

\bigcap_{i\in\emptyset}U_{i}=X\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigcup_{j\in\emptyset}U_{j}=\emptyset.

2.3.
Canonical form, and Type 1–3 relations

Neural ideals are generated by many characteristic pseudo-monomials, and it is useful to extract a
generating set capturing the essential RF relations. In
[
2
]
, Curto et al. introduced the

canonical form
of a neural ideal and organized its relations into three types.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
I\subseteq R
be an ideal.

•

A
pseudo-monomial
is a polynomial of the form

x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j}),\qquad\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n],\ \sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset.

•

A pseudo-monomial
f\in I
is
minimal
if no proper divisor of
f
is contained in
I
.

•

The
canonical form

CF(I)
is the set of all minimal pseudo-monomials in
I
.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
2
, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

For any neural code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the neural ideal
J_{\mathcal{C}}
is generated by its canonical form:

J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})\rangle.

Moreover,
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
decomposes as a disjoint union of minimal relations of three types:

•

Type 1:
monomials
x_{\sigma}
, encoding
U_{\sigma}=\emptyset
;

•

Type 2:
pseudo-monomials
x_{\sigma}\prod_{i\in\tau}(1-x_{i})
, encoding

U_{\sigma}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\tau}U_{i}
;

•

Type 3:
pseudo-monomials
\prod_{i\in\tau}(1-x_{i})
, encoding

X\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\tau}U_{i}
.

Remark 2.12
.

If
f=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
, then the corresponding containment

U_{\sigma}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in\tau}U_{j}
holds, but it fails if
\sigma
or
\tau
is replaced by a
proper subset. Equivalently,
f
is minimal among pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
under divisibility.
See
[
2
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Remark 2.13
.

The canonical form
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is a canonical generating set by pseudo-monomials: its elements
are minimal under divisibility among pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, and
J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})\rangle
.
However, this does
not
imply that
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is a minimal generating set of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
as an ideal
in the usual sense, since
J_{\mathcal{C}}
may admit a generating set with fewer elements
once arbitrary polynomials (not necessarily pseudo-monomials) are allowed.

2.4.
Polarization and the polar complex

Neural ideals need not be homogeneous.
To access graded invariants (graded Betti numbers, regularity, projective dimension), one replaces
each factor
(1-x_{i})
by a new variable
y_{i}
, producing a squarefree monomial ideal.

Definition 2.14

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
f=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})
be a pseudo-monomial with
\sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset
.
Its
polarization
is the squarefree monomial

{\mathcal{P}}(f):=x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}\in S.

Then the
polarized neural ideal
is

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}):=\langle{\mathcal{P}}(f):f\in J_{\mathcal{C}}\text{ is a pseudo-monomial }\rangle\subseteq S.

Theorem 2.15
.

[
6
, Theorem 3.2]

Let
J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\rangle
be a neural ideal in canonical form, i.e.
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\}=CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
. Then

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\langle{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{P}}(f_{k})\rangle\subseteq S.

We say that the polarized neural ideal
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is in canonical form if it is written as

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\langle{\mathcal{P}}(f_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{P}}(f_{k})\rangle.

Remark 2.16
.

It is shown in
[
6
, Theorem 3.5 and Corollary 4.1]
that polarization preserves the algebraic
structure of the neural ideal in a precise sense: one can recover a free resolution of
J_{\mathcal{C}}
from a
minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
by substituting
y_{i}\mapsto(1-x_{i})
in the presentation matrices.

Since
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
is a squarefree monomial ideal, it corresponds to a simplicial complex via
Stanley–Reisner theory.

Notation 2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. We write
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
for the simplicial complex whose Stanley–Reisner ideal is
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})\subseteq S
.
We call
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
the
polar complex
of
\mathcal{C}
as in
[
6
]
.

Definition 2.18
.

A squarefree monomial
m\in S
is
transversal
if
x_{i}y_{i}\nmid m
for all
i\in[n]
.
Equivalently, for each
i
, the monomial
m
contains at most one of
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
.

For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
\sigma(v)=\{i\in[n]:v_{i}=1\}
, define the associated transversal monomial

m_{v}:=x_{\sigma(v)}y_{[n]\setminus\sigma(v)}=\prod_{i\in\sigma(v)}x_{i}\prod_{i\in[n]\setminus\sigma(v)}y_{i}.

2.5.
Graded Betti numbers, projective dimension, and regularity

Let
S
be a standard
\mathbb{Z}
-graded polynomial ring,
and
M
be a finitely generated
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
S
-module. A
minimal graded free resolution

of
M
has the form

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{\ell,j}(M)}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}S(-j)^{\beta_{0,j}(M)}\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow 0,

where the integers
\beta_{i,j}(M)
are the
graded Betti numbers
.
Equivalently,

\beta_{i,j}(M)=\dim_{k}\Tor_{i}^{S}(M,k)_{j}.

The
projective dimension
and
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
of
M
are defined by

\pd(M):=\max\{i:\beta_{i,j}(M)\neq 0\},\qquad\reg(M):=\max\{j-i:\beta_{i,j}(M)\neq 0\}.

Throughout the paper, if
I\subseteq S
is a homogeneous ideal, we regard
I
as a graded
S
-module
and apply these definitions to
M=I
.

In this paper, we compute graded Betti numbers of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
using Hochster’s formula.

Theorem 2.19

(Hochster’s formula)
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on the vertex set
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
with
Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta}\subseteq k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
. Then for all
i,j
,

\beta_{i,j}(I_{\Delta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq V\\
|W|=j\end{subarray}}\dim_{k}\widetilde{H}_{j-i-2}(\Delta_{W};k),

where
\Delta_{W}
is the induced subcomplex on
W
and
\widetilde{H}_{\ast}
denotes reduced simplicial
homology.

We repeatedly use the following standard fact about adjoining new variables.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated graded
S
-module,
and let
z
be a new variable of degree
1
. Set
S^{\prime}:=S[z]
and
M^{\prime}:=M\otimes_{S}S^{\prime}
.
Then

\pd_{S^{\prime}}(M^{\prime})=\pd_{S}(M)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\reg_{S^{\prime}}(M^{\prime})=\reg_{S}(M).

Moreover, for any proper homogeneous ideal
J\subsetneq S
, writing
JS^{\prime}\subseteq S^{\prime}
for its extension,
one has

\pd_{S^{\prime}}(JS^{\prime}+(z))=\pd_{S}(J)+1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\reg_{S^{\prime}}(JS^{\prime}+(z))=\reg_{S}(J).

3.
Basic operations on codes

This section records conventions and two elementary operations on neural codes that will be used
throughout the paper. The first adjoins a
free neuron
(a new coordinate that can be chosen
independently), and the second adjoins a
constant neuron
(a new coordinate fixed to
0
or
1
).
On the algebraic side, these correspond to extension by new variables, and (in the constant
case) adding a new linear generator. In particular, these operations allow us to vary the number of
neurons while controlling
(\pd,\reg)
.

Set-Up 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a neural code on
n
neurons. Write
J_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq R
for its neural ideal,
and let

I={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})\subseteq S

denote the polarized neural ideal. When no confusion can arise, we write
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
for the
polar complex of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e. the Stanley–Reisner complex of
I
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with polarized neural ideal
I={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
and polar complex
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
.

(a)

No minimal generator of
I
is divisible by
x_{i}y_{i}
.

(b)

If
x_{i},y_{i}\in I
, then depolarization gives
x_{i},1-x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}

.
Thus

\mathcal{C}=V(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\emptyset

(c)

The ideal

In particular,

(d)

. Hence

, and therefore

for all

.

Remark 3.3
.

Two degenerate cases will be used implicitly.
We use the notation

to denote the list of all codewords on

(a)

(b)

Remark 3.4

When

Throughout the paper we assume

Definition 3.5

Free and constant neurons

Let

4.
Polar complex and its connectedness

In this section we study the polar complex
\Delta
and connectivity properties of its induced subcomplexes. These connectivity statements will be used repeatedly to force vanishing of certain homology groups in Hochster’s formula. In particular, we prove that
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
is connected and that every induced subcomplex on sufficiently many vertices is connected.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code with its polar complex
\Delta
. Then
|V(\Delta)|\geq n
. In particular, if
|V(\Delta)|=n
, then
\mathcal{C}
consists of a single word and
\Delta
is a simplex.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[n]
. We claim that at least one of
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
is a vertex of
\Delta
.
Indeed, if neither
x_{i}
nor
y_{i}
were a vertex, then both
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
would lie in
I
.
By
Remark

3.2
(2), this forces
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, contradicting the hypothesis.
Thus
|V(\Delta)|\geq n
.

Now assume
|V(\Delta)|=n
. Then for each
i
exactly one of
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
is a vertex of
\Delta
,
so the other variable lies in
I
. Depolarizing, we obtain that for each
i
either
x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}

or
(1-x_{i})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. Since every polynomial in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
vanishes on
\mathcal{C}
, it follows that for each
i

the
i
th coordinate is constant across
\mathcal{C}
(equal to
0
in the first case and
1
in the second).
Therefore
\mathcal{C}
contains at most one word, and since
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
it consists of exactly one word.

Finally, when
|V(\Delta)|=n
the ideal
I
contains precisely one of
x_{i},y_{i}
for each
i
, hence is
generated by
n
variables. Equivalently,
\Delta
is the simplex on its vertex set.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code with its polar complex
\Delta
such that
|V(\Delta)|\geq n+1
.
If
W\subseteq V(\Delta)
satisfies
|W|\geq n+1
, then the induced subcomplex
\Delta_{W}
is connected.

Proof.

Since there are only
n
indices, the inequality
|W|\geq n+1
implies that
W
contains both vertices
from at least one pair
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
; that is, there exists
i\in[n]
such that
x_{i},y_{i}\in W
.
Because
x_{i},y_{i}\in V(\Delta)
, we have
x_{i},y_{i}\notin I
.
Moreover, no polar canonical form generator involves both
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
, so
x_{i}y_{i}\notin I
.
Hence
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
is an edge of
\Delta
, and therefore also an edge of the induced subcomplex
\Delta_{W}
.

Suppose for contradiction that
\Delta_{W}
is disconnected, and let
\Gamma
be the connected component
of
\Delta_{W}
containing the edge
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
. Choose a vertex
u\in W\setminus V(\Gamma)
.
Then
u
is not adjacent in
\Delta_{W}
to either
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
. So
\{u,x_{i}\}
and
\{u,y_{i}\}
are
nonfaces of
\Delta_{W}
, hence also nonfaces of
\Delta
.
Since
u,x_{i},y_{i}\in V(\Delta)
, the singletons are faces, so these missing edges are minimal nonfaces.
Therefore the quadratic monomials
ux_{i}
and
uy_{i}
lie in
I
.

First assume
u=x_{j}
for some
j
. Then
x_{j}x_{i}\in I
and
x_{j}y_{i}\in I
.
Depolarizing
y_{i}\mapsto(1-x_{i})
gives
x_{j}x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
and
x_{j}(1-x_{i})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence

x_{j}=x_{j}x_{i}+x_{j}(1-x_{i})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}.

Since
x_{j}
is a pseudo-monomial and
1\notin J_{\mathcal{C}}
, it is minimal in
J_{\mathcal{C}}

with respect to divisibility. Hence
x_{j}\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
and
x_{j}\in I
, contradicting
x_{j}\in V(\Delta)
.

Now assume
u=y_{j}
for some
j
. Then
y_{j}x_{i}\in I
and
y_{j}y_{i}\in I
.
Depolarizing gives
x_{i}(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
and
(1-x_{i})(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence

(1-x_{j})=x_{i}(1-x_{j})+(1-x_{i})(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}.

Again
(1-x_{j})
is minimal among pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
. Therefore

(1-x_{j})\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
and
y_{j}\in I
, contradicting
y_{j}\in V(\Delta)
.

Therefore no such
u
exists, and
\Delta_{W}
is connected.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code. Then its polar complex
\Delta
is connected.

Proof.

It follows from
Lemma

4.1
that
|V(\Delta)|\geq n
and
\Delta
is connected when
|V(\Delta)|=n
.
If
|V(\Delta)|\geq n+1
, Lemma
4.2
applied to
W=V(\Delta)

shows that
\Delta
is connected.
∎

5.
Maximum Projective Dimension

In this section we study the projective dimension of polarized neural ideals. Our first goal is to provide an upper bound on

\pd(I)
in terms of
n
, and our second goal is to determine whether this bound is sharp.

Remark 5.1
.

Since
I\subseteq S
is an ideal in a polynomial ring in
2n
variables, Hilbert–Syzygy Theorem gives the crude bound

\pd(I)\leq 2n
. If
I\neq 0
(namely,
\mathcal{C}\neq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
), then

\depth(I)\geq 1
. By Auslander–Buchsbaum,

\pd(I)\leq 2n-1.

We can improve the upper bound as follows.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code. Then

\pd(I)\leq 2n-3.

Proof.

Recall that
V(\Delta)\subseteq V
where
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
.
By Hochster’s formula, if
\beta_{i,j}(I)\neq 0
then there exists
W\subseteq V(\Delta)
with
|W|=j

such that
\widetilde{H}_{j-i-2}(\Delta_{W};k)\neq 0
.

Since
|V(\Delta)|\leq 2n
, we have
j\leq 2n
. If
i\geq 2n-2
, then
j-i-2\leq 0
.
Moreover, for
|W|\geq 1
the induced complex
\Delta_{W}
is nonempty. So
\widetilde{H}_{t}(\Delta_{W};k)=0

for all
t<0
. Hence the only potentially nonzero case is when
(i,j)=(2n-2,2n)
.

If
|V(\Delta)|<2n
, then no subset
W\subseteq V(\Delta)
has
|W|=2n
, so
\beta_{2n-2,2n}(I)=0
.
If instead
|V(\Delta)|=2n
, then the only such subset is
W=V(\Delta)
and

\beta_{2n-2,2n}(I)=\dim_{k}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta;k)
.
By Corollary
4.3
,
\Delta
is connected. So
\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta;k)=0
.

Therefore
\beta_{i,j}(I)=0
for all
i\geq 2n-2
. Hence
\pd(I)\leq 2n-3
.
∎

We next relate the extremal projective dimension to regularity: attaining the upper bound

\pd(I)=2n-3
forces a lower bound on
\reg(I)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
\pd(I)=2n-3
, then
\beta_{2n-3,2n}(I)\neq 0
. In particular,
\reg(I)\geq 3
in this case.

Proof.

Since
\pd(I)=2n-3
, there exists
j
with

\beta_{2n-3,j}(I)\neq 0
. By Hochster’s formula, there is a subset
W\subseteq V(\Delta)
with
|W|=j

such that
\widetilde{H}_{j-2n+1}(\Delta_{W};k)\neq 0
.

Because
W\subseteq V(\Delta)
, the induced complex
\Delta_{W}
is nonempty. Hence

\widetilde{H}_{t}(\Delta_{W};k)=0
for
t<0
. Therefore
j-2n+1\geq 0
, so
j\geq 2n-1
.
On the other hand
j\leq|V(\Delta)|\leq 2n
. Thus either
j=2n-1
or
j=2n
.

If
j=2n-1
, then Hochster’s formula forces
\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta_{W};k)\neq 0
, so
\Delta_{W}
is disconnected.
But
|W|=2n-1\geq n+1
, and
Lemma

4.2
asserts that every induced subcomplex on at least
n+1

vertices is connected, a contradiction. Hence
j\neq 2n-1
. Therefore
j=2n
and

\beta_{2n-3,2n}(I)\neq 0.

Consequently,
\reg(I)\geq j-(2n-3)=3
.
∎

Next, we identify classes of neural codes achieving the upper bound from
Theorem

5.2
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{00\cdots 0,11\cdots 1\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Then

\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}))=2n-3\qquad\text{and}\qquad\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}))=3.

We call
\mathcal{C}_{0}

“all-or-nothing code”

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{000,111\}
for

Write
J=J_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
and

.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{0^{n},1^{n}\}

and
at least one coordinate equal to

T=\{i\in[n]:v_{i}=1\}

a_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i},&i\notin T,\\
y_{i},&i\in T,\end{cases}\qquad b_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i},&i\notin T,\\
x_{i},&i\in T.\end{cases}

Then, we have
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{v}})=\langle a_{i}b_{j}:i\neq j\rangle
since

a_{i}b_{j}=\begin{cases}x_{i}y_{j},&i\notin T,\ j\notin T,\\
x_{i}x_{j},&i\notin T,\ j\in T,\\
y_{i}y_{j},&i\in T,\ j\notin T,\\
y_{i}x_{j},&i\in T,\ j\in T.\end{cases}

Let
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
and
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
. Then
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{v}})
is the edge ideal of the bipartite graph on
A\sqcup B
with all edges except the
matching edges
\{a_{i},b_{i}\}
, i.e. the same graph as in
Theorem

5.4
after relabeling
vertices. Therefore
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{v}})
has the same graded Betti numbers as
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{0}})
, and the stated
values of
\pd
and
\reg
follow from
Theorem

5.4
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

There are
2^{n-1}
distinct antipodal pair codes
\{v,\overline{v}\}
on
n
neurons.
Thus
Corollary

provides exponentially many examples of neural codes
whose polarized neural ideals attain the maximal projective dimension

while having regularity

.

6.
Maximum Regularity

In this section we study the regularity of polarized neural ideals. Our goal is to describe the range of possible values of
\reg(I)
, with particular emphasis on establishing a sharp upper bound and characterizing the neural codes for which this bound is attained.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a neural code on
n
neurons.
Then

\reg(I)\leq 2n-1.

Proof.

Recall from Remark
3.2
(b) that
j\leq 2n
when
\beta_{i,j}(I)\neq 0
. So
j-i\leq 2n-1
when
i\geq 1
.
For
i=0
, Remark
3.2
(c) gives
\beta_{0,j}(I)=0
for
j>n
.
Hence no nonzero Betti number can occur with
j-i>2n-1
. Therefore
\reg(I)\leq 2n-1
.
∎

The next proposition pins down where the extremal value
\reg(I)=2n-1
must appear in the Betti table.

Proposition 6.2
.

If
\reg(I)=2n-1
, then
\beta_{1,2n}(I)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Assume
\reg(I)=2n-1
. Then there exist
i,j
with
\beta_{i,j}(I)\neq 0
and
j-i=2n-1
.
By Remark
3.2
(b), we have
j\leq 2n
, so
i\leq 1
.
If
i=0
, then
j=2n-1>n
, contradicting Remark
3.2
(c).
Thus
(i,j)=(1,2n)
and
\beta_{1,2n}(I)\neq 0
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

If
\reg(I)=2n-1
, then
\mathcal{C}
omits an antipodal pair
\{v,\overline{v}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Proof.

Assume
\reg(I)=2n-1
. By
Proposition

6.2
we have

\beta_{1,2n}(I)\neq 0
. Hence there exists a squarefree monomial
u
of degree
2n
with

\beta_{1,u}(I)\neq 0
. So,
u=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}y_{1}\cdots y_{n}
. For monomial ideals, a multidegree
u
occurring in homological degree
1

is the least common multiple of two minimal generators. Thus there are minimal generators
m_{1},m_{2}\in I

with
u=\lcm(m_{1},m_{2})
.

Recall that every minimal generator of
I
is transversal
(no generator is divisible by
x_{i}y_{i}
). Therefore for each
i\in[n]
, exactly one of
x_{i},y_{i}

divides
m_{1}
, and
m_{2}
contains the other. In particular
\deg(m_{1})=\deg(m_{2})=n
, and there is

\sigma\subseteq[n]
such that

m_{1}=x_{\sigma}y_{[n]\setminus\sigma},\qquad m_{2}=x_{[n]\setminus\sigma}y_{\sigma}.

Let
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be defined by
v_{i}=1
iff
i\in\sigma
. Then
m_{1}={\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{v})
and

m_{2}={\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\overline{v}})
. Since
\rho_{w}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
exactly when
w\notin\mathcal{C}
, it follows that

v,\overline{v}\notin\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and write
\sigma=\{i:v_{i}=1\}
.
Suppose
\rho_{v}=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\notin\sigma}(1-x_{j})
is a minimal pseudo-monomial in
J_{\mathcal{C}}

(i.e. no proper divisor of
\rho_{v}
lies in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
).
Then
v\notin\mathcal{C}
, and every codeword at Hamming distance
1
from
v
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Since
\rho_{v}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\rho_{v}(v)=1
, we have
v\notin V(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}
.

Fix an index
t\in[n]
. Define

f_{t}:=\begin{cases}\rho_{v}/x_{t},&\text{if }t\in\sigma(v_{t}=1),\\[2.0pt]
\rho_{v}/(1-x_{t}),&\text{if }t\notin\sigma(v_{t}=0).\end{cases}

Then
f_{t}
is a proper divisor of
\rho_{v}
and by minimality
f_{t}\notin J_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Observe that
f_{t}\notin J_{\mathcal{C}}

implies that there exists
c^{(t)}\in\mathcal{C}
on which
f_{t}
evaluates to
1
which means
c
satisfies the coordinate
constraints encoded by
f_{t}
. Equivalently,
c^{(t)}_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i\neq t
. Since
v\notin\mathcal{C}
, we have
c^{(t)}\neq v
,
and therefore
c^{(t)}
differs from
v
exactly in the
t
-th coordinate. Hence
c^{(t)}
is obtained from
v
by flipping the
t
-th bit. Thus
c^{(t)}
is a Hamming neighbor of
v
that lies in
\mathcal{C}
.
As
t
was arbitrary, every Hamming neighbor of
v
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose
\reg(I)=2n-1
. Then
\mathcal{C}
omits an antipodal pair
\{v,\overline{v}\}
,
and every Hamming neighbor of
v
and of
\overline{v}
lies in
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

It follows from
Lemma

6.3
and its proof that
\mathcal{C}
omits an antipodal pair
\{v,\overline{v}\}
and

{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{v}),{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\overline{v}})
are minimal generators of
I
.
In particular,
\rho_{v}
and
\rho_{\overline{v}}
are minimal pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Otherwise some
f\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
would properly divide
\rho_{v}
(respectively
\rho_{\overline{v}}
),
and then
{\mathcal{P}}(f)
would properly divide
{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{v})
(respectively
{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\overline{v}})
),
contradicting minimality in
I
.
Then applying
Lemma

6.4
to
\rho_{v}
and
\rho_{\overline{v}}
completes the proof.
∎

We now show that the upper bound in
Lemma

6.1
is sharp, and we classify all codes achieving it.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\reg(I)=2n-1
;

(2)

I
is minimally generated by two complementary degree-
n
monomials

I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}},x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}\rangle

for some
\sigma\subseteq[n]
;

(3)

\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{v,\overline{v}\}
for some
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

In particular, if
\reg(I)=2n-1
then
\pd(I)=1
.

Figure 4
.
Realization of
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}\setminus\{001,110\}

Proof.

Set
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
:
Assume
I=\langle m_{1},m_{2}\rangle
with
m_{1}=x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}}
and
m_{2}=x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}
.
Then
\gcd(m_{1},m_{2})=1
and the Taylor resolution of
I
is minimal.
Hence
\reg(I)=\max\{n,2n-1\}=2n-1
and
\pd(I)=1
.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
:
Assume
\reg(I)=2n-1
. By
Proposition

6.2
we have
\beta_{1,2n}(I)\neq 0
, and by

Lemma

6.3
there exist complementary minimal generators

m_{1}=x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}},\qquad m_{2}=x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}

for some
\sigma\subseteq[n]
. Let
A=\supp(m_{1})
and
B=\supp(m_{2})
. Then
V=A\sqcup B
and

|A|=|B|=n
. In particular,
V(\Delta)=V
for the Stanley–Reisner complex
\Delta
with
I=I_{\Delta}
.
Since
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are minimal generators,
A
and
B
are minimal nonfaces of
\Delta
.
Thus no face of
\Delta
can contain all of
A
or all of
B
. Equivalently,

\Delta\subseteq\Delta_{0}:=\{F\subseteq V:A\nsubseteq F,B\nsubseteq F\}.

Let
\Delta_{A}
and
\Delta_{B}
be the full simplices on
A
and
B
. Then

\Delta_{0}=\partial(\Delta_{A})*\partial(\Delta_{B}),

the join of the boundary spheres of
\Delta_{A}
and
\Delta_{B}
. Its facets are exactly

F_{a,b}:=V\setminus\{a,b\}
with
a\in A
and
b\in B
.
In particular,
\Delta_{0}
is a simplicial
(2n-3)
-sphere, so

\widetilde{H}_{2n-3}(\Delta_{0};k)\cong k
.

Claim 6.7
.

Every
(2n-3)
-cycle in
\Delta_{0}
(with coefficients in
k
) is a scalar multiple of

\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}F_{a,b}
. In particular, any nonzero
(2n-3)
-cycle in
\Delta_{0}

has nonzero coefficient on every facet
F_{a,b}
.

Proof of Claim
6.7
.

Fix an orientation of the simplicial sphere
\Delta_{0}
, so that each ridge is contained in two
facets with opposite induced orientations. Write a
(2n-3)
-chain as

z=\sum_{a\in A,b\in B}c_{a,b}F_{a,b}
.
Fix
b\in B
and distinct
a,a^{\prime}\in A
. The ridge
R=V\setminus\{a,a^{\prime},b\}
lies in exactly two facets,

F_{a,b}
and
F_{a^{\prime},b}
, and their induced orientations on
R
are opposite. Hence the coefficient
of
R
in
\partial z
is
\pm(c_{a,b}-c_{a^{\prime},b})
. If
\partial z=0
, then
c_{a,b}=c_{a^{\prime},b}
.
Thus for fixed
b
, the coefficient
c_{a,b}
is independent of
a
. A symmetric argument (fix
a

and vary
b
) shows the coefficients are also independent of
b
. Therefore all
c_{a,b}
are equal
to a common scalar
\lambda
, and the claim follows.
∎

If
\Delta\neq\Delta_{0}
, then
\Delta
omits some facet
F_{a,b}
of
\Delta_{0}
.
Let
z
be any
(2n-3)
-cycle in
\Delta
. Viewing
z
as a
(2n-3)
-chain in
\Delta_{0}

(by assigning coefficient
0
to every facet of
\Delta_{0}\setminus\Delta
), we obtain a

(2n-3)
-cycle in
\Delta_{0}
whose coefficient on
F_{a,b}
is
0
. By
Claim
6.7
, this forces
z=0
. Hence

\widetilde{H}_{2n-3}(\Delta;k)=0
.
Applying Hochster’s formula at
(i,j)=(1,2n)
,
and using that the only subset
W\subseteq V
with
|W|=2n
is
W=V
, we obtain

\beta_{1,2n}(I)=\dim_{k}\widetilde{H}_{2n-3}(\Delta;k)=0,

contradicting
\beta_{1,2n}(I)\neq 0
. Therefore
\Delta=\Delta_{0}
. So
A
and
B
are the only minimal
nonfaces of
\Delta
. Equivalently,
I
has exactly the two minimal generators
m_{1},m_{2}
, proving (2).

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
:
Assume
I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}},x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}\rangle
minimally and write

m_{1}=x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}}
,
m_{2}=x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}
.
By definition of polarization and canonical form,
I=\langle{\mathcal{P}}(f):f\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})\rangle
, and each

{\mathcal{P}}(f)
is a transversal squarefree monomial of degree at most
n
.
Since
m_{1},m_{2}
are the only minimal monomial generators of
I
, every
{\mathcal{P}}(f)
is divisible by
m_{1}

or
m_{2}
. The degree bound
\deg{\mathcal{P}}(f)\leq n=\deg(m_{i})
forces
{\mathcal{P}}(f)\in\{m_{1},m_{2}\}
.
Hence
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{\rho_{v},\rho_{\overline{v}}\}
, where
v_{i}=1
iff
i\in\sigma
.

Now let
w\notin\mathcal{C}
. Then
\rho_{w}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. Among the pseudo-monomial divisors of
\rho_{w}
that lie in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
,
choose one minimal under divisibility. This divisor lies in
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
, so it is
\rho_{v}
or
\rho_{\overline{v}}
,
and in particular it divides
\rho_{w}
. Since characteristic pseudo-monomials contain exactly one factor from

\{x_{i},1-x_{i}\}
in each coordinate, divisibility
\rho_{u}\mid\rho_{w}
forces
u=w
. Therefore

w\in\{v,\overline{v}\}
, and
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{v,\overline{v}\}
.

(3)\Rightarrow(2)
:
If
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{v,\overline{v}\}
, then
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{\rho_{v},\rho_{\overline{v}}\}
,
and polarization gives

I=\langle{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{v}),{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\overline{v}})\rangle=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\sigma^{c}},x_{\sigma^{c}}y_{\sigma}\rangle,

where
\sigma=\{i:v_{i}=1\}
.

The final statement
\pd(I)=1
follows from
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
.
∎

7.
Small projective dimension:
\pd(I)\leq 1

In this section we study polarized neural ideals of small projective dimension. We first classify the codes

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
for which
\pd(I)=0
, and then give a family of codes with
\pd(I)=1
whose regularity
ranges across
[1,2n-1]
.

7.1.
Classification of neural codes with projective dimension
0

We begin by classifying neural codes
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
whose polarized neural ideal
I
has projective
dimension
0
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a neural code.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\pd(I)=0
;

(2)

I
is principal;

(3)

there exist disjoint subsets
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
with
\sigma\cup\tau\neq\emptyset
such that

I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}\rangle;

(4)

there exist disjoint
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
with
\sigma\cup\tau\neq\emptyset
such that
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus Q(\sigma,\tau)
where

Q(\sigma,\tau):=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ v_{i}=1\ (i\in\sigma),\ v_{j}=0\ (j\in\tau)\}.

Moreover, writing
r:=|\sigma|+|\tau|
, we have
1\leq r\leq n
and
\reg(I)=r
.

Proof.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
:
A finitely generated graded
S
–module has projective dimension
0
if and only if it is free, i.e.
I\cong S(-d)
for some
d
which is equivalent to
I
is principal.

(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
:
Assume
I=\langle m\rangle
is principal.
Then the unique minimal monomial generator
m
is transversal in the sense of
Definition

2.18
(squarefree and
x_{i}y_{i}\nmid m
for all
i\in[n]
).
Writing

\sigma:=\{i:x_{i}\mid m\},\qquad\tau:=\{j:y_{j}\mid m\},

we have
m=x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}
with
\sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset
.
The implication
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
is immediate.

(3)\Rightarrow(4)
:
If
I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}\rangle
, then depolarizing gives

J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle f\rangle\qquad\text{where}\qquad f=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j}).

For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
we have

f(v)=\prod_{i\in\sigma}v_{i}\cdot\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-v_{j})\in\{0,1\}.

So
f(v)=1
if and only if
v\in Q(\sigma,\tau)
and
f(v)=0
otherwise. Since
V(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}
,
we obtain

\mathcal{C}=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ f(v)=0\}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus Q(\sigma,\tau).

(4)\Rightarrow(3)
:
Assume
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus Q(\sigma,\tau)
and set
f=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})
.
If
v\in Q(\sigma,\tau)
, then
\rho_{v}
is divisible by
f
. Hence
J_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\langle f\rangle
.
Conversely,

\sum_{v\in Q(\sigma,\tau)}\rho_{v}=x_{\sigma}\prod_{j\in\tau}(1-x_{j})\prod_{\ell\notin\sigma\cup\tau}\bigl(x_{\ell}+(1-x_{\ell})\bigr)=f.

So
f\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
and therefore
J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle f\rangle
.
Moreover,
f
is minimal under divisibility among pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
(dropping any factor yields a
pseudo-monomial that does not vanish on some codeword). Hence
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\{f\}
and
J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle f\rangle
is in canonical form.
Polarizing gives
I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}\rangle
.

Finally, if
I=\langle x_{\sigma}y_{\tau}\rangle
is principal generated in degree

r:=|\sigma|+|\tau|
, then its minimal free resolution is
0\to S(-r)\to I\to 0
, so
\reg(I)=r
.
Since
\sigma\cap\tau=\varnothing
and
I\neq 0
, we have
1\leq r\leq n
.
∎

7.2.
The
\pd(I)=1
line

We now turn our attention to the line
\pd(I)=1
. In the family we construct, the polarized neural ideal is generated by two
squarefree monomials, and hence has a resolution with a single first syzygy. In this situation

\reg
is determined by the degree of the least common multiple of the two generators, which admits
a direct interpretation in terms of the Hamming distance between the corresponding missing words.
Using the free-neuron extension from
Lemma

3.6
, we realize every pair

(1,r)
with
r\in[1,2n-1]
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Fix
n\geq 3
and let
r
be an integer with
1\leq r\leq 2n-1
.
There exists a neural code
\mathcal{C}_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that, with

I_{r}={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{r}})
, we have

(\pd(I_{r}),\reg(I_{r}))=(1,r).

Moreover, if
r\geq 3
, the code
\mathcal{C}_{r}
may be chosen to be nondegenerate.

Proof.

For
r=1
,
set

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ c_{1}=0,\ c_{2}=1\}.

Then
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{1}})=\langle x_{1},\ y_{2}\rangle
and
(\pd,\reg)=(1,1)
.

For
r=2
, set

\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ c_{1}=0\text{ and not }(c_{2}=c_{3}=1)\}.

Then
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{2}})=\langle x_{1},\ x_{2}x_{3}\rangle
. Hence
(\pd,\reg)=(1,2)
. (One can also realize
(1,2)
with a nondegenerate simplicial code; see

Section

8.2
for related constructions.)

Now assume
r\geq 3
. Choose integers

m:=\Big\lceil\frac{r+1}{2}\Big\rceil\leq n,\qquad d:=r-m+1,

so that
2\leq d\leq m
and
m+d-1=r
. Pick distinct
v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
with
d_{H}(v,w)=d
and define

\mathcal{C}^{(m)}:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\setminus\{v,w\}.

Since the noncodewords of
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
are exactly
v
and
w
, we have

J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}}=\langle\rho_{v},\rho_{w}\rangle
.
We claim that
\rho_{v}
and
\rho_{w}
are precisely the elements of
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}})
.
Indeed,
d_{H}(v,w)\geq 2
implies that for each of
v
and
w
there is a word of Hamming distance
1

from it that is still a codeword (e.g. flip a single coordinate of
v
; the result is neither
v
nor

w
since
w
differs from
v
in at least two coordinates). Consequently, if
f
is a proper
pseudo-monomial divisor of
\rho_{v}
(so
\deg(f)<m
), then
f
does not vanish on that neighboring
codeword, and hence
f\notin J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}}
. Thus
\rho_{v}
is minimal under divisibility among
pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}}
, and similarly for
\rho_{w}
. Therefore
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}})=\{\rho_{v},\rho_{w}\}
. It follows that

I_{(m)}:={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}^{(m)}})=\langle m_{v},m_{w}\rangle,

where
m_{v}=x_{\sigma(v)}y_{[m]\setminus\sigma(v)}
and

m_{w}=x_{\sigma(w)}y_{[m]\setminus\sigma(w)}
are transversal monomials of degree
m
.
Since
m_{v}
and
m_{w}
have the same degree and are distinct, neither divides the other, so
I_{(m)}

is minimally generated by these two monomials. Hence
\pd(I_{(m)})=1
, and the minimal resolution of a
two-generated monomial ideal gives

\reg(I_{(m)})=\deg\lcm(m_{v},m_{w})-1.

Moreover,
\deg\lcm(m_{v},m_{w})=m+d_{H}(v,w)=m+d
: for each coordinate where
v
and
w
agree,

\lcm(m_{v},m_{w})
contains exactly one of
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
, while for each coordinate where they differ it
contains both. Therefore
\reg(I_{(m)})=(m+d)-1=r
.

Finally, define
\mathcal{C}_{r}:=\mathcal{C}^{(m)}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and set
I_{r}={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{r}})
.
By repeated application of
Lemma

3.6
, adjoining free coordinates preserves projective dimension
and regularity. So
\pd(I_{r})=1
and
\reg(I_{r})=r
.

For nondegeneracy, observe that when
m\geq 2
deleting two words from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
cannot fix any coordinate:
each coordinate takes both values on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, and removing only two words leaves at least one codeword with

0
and at least one with
1
in every coordinate. Hence
\mathcal{C}^{(m)}
is nondegenerate, and so is its free
extension
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

The hypothesis
n\geq 3
in
Proposition

7.2
is necessary only because the stated range

1\leq r\leq 2n-1
includes
r=2
when
n=2
. In our construction for
n\geq 3
we delete two words

v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
with

, which forces
\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\setminus\{v,w\}}))=m+d_{H}(v,w)-1
.
For
n=2
the condition

forces
m=2
, and then
d_{H}(v,w)\geq 2
implies

. So the

\reg(I)=3
(deleting an antipodal pair) but cannot produce

where

. Attempting to
obtain

would require deleting Hamming neighbors (

hence a linear generator in

. So

by

.
Thus
(\pd(I),\reg(I))=(1,2)
does not occur for

.

8.
Small regularity:
\reg(I)\leq 3

In this section we study polarized neural ideals in the low-regularity regime
\reg(I)\leq 3
.
We treat the cases
\reg(I)=1,2,3
in turn. For
\reg(I)=2
we use Hochster’s formula together with a
connectedness criterion for induced subcomplexes of the polar complex to obtain the sharp bound

\pd(I)\leq n-2
for every nonzero proper polarized neural ideal, and we construct families realizing each value
in this range. Along the line
\reg(I)=3
we exhibit a family realizing every projective dimension in

[0,2n-3]
.

8.1.
Classification of neural codes with regularity
1

Assume
\reg(I)=1
. Then
I
has a
1
–linear resolution, hence is generated by variables. For polar
neural ideals, these generators record fixed coordinates:
x_{i}\in I
(respectively
y_{i}\in I
) means that
the
i
th coordinate is identically
0
(respectively
1
) on
\mathcal{C}
. This forces
\mathcal{C}
to be a coordinate
subcube of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
obtained by fixing some coordinates to
0
and some disjoint coordinates to
1
.
The next theorem makes this correspondence precise and computes
\pd(I)
from the number of fixed
coordinates.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\reg(I)=1
;

(2)

I
is generated by variables and, after relabeling,

I=\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in\tau\rangle\qquad\text{for some disjoint }\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n];

(3)

\mathcal{C}
is a subcube of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
obtained by fixing some coordinates to
0
and some disjoint coordinates to
1
, i.e.

\mathcal{C}=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:v_{i}=0\ \forall i\in\sigma,\ \ v_{j}=1\ \forall j\in\tau\}

for some disjoint
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
.

Moreover, if these conditions hold and
t:=|\sigma|+|\tau|
, then

\pd(I)=t-1\leq n-1,

and
\pd(I)=n-1
if and only if
t=n
, equivalently if and only if
\mathcal{C}
consists of a single codeword.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
:

If
\reg(I)=1
, then every minimal monomial generator of
I
has degree
1
. Hence
I
is generated by variables.
Since
I
is proper, it cannot contain both
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
for any
i
by
Remark

3.2
(2).
Therefore

I=\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in\tau\rangle

for some disjoint
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
:

Conversely, any ideal generated by variables has a
1
–linear resolution and
\reg(I)=1
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
:

Assume
I=\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in\tau\rangle
with
\sigma\cap\tau=\varnothing
, and set

Q:=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:v_{i}=0\ \forall i\in\sigma,\ v_{j}=1\ \forall j\in\tau\}.

Since
x_{i}\in I
, then
x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. So
x_{i}(c)=c_{i}=0
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
.
Similarly,
y_{j}\in I
implies that
(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
. So
(1-x_{j})(c)=1-c_{j}=0
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
.
Thus
\mathcal{C}\subseteq Q
.

To prove equality, suppose
\mathcal{C}\subsetneq Q
and choose
u\in Q\setminus\mathcal{C}
.
Then
u
is a noncodeword, so
\rho_{u}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Choose a minimal pseudo-monomial
f\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
dividing
\rho_{u}
. By definition

f\in CF(J_{\mathcal{C}})
.

We claim that
\deg(f)\geq 2
.
Indeed, since
u\in Q
, for each
i\in\sigma
one has
u_{i}=0
, so
\rho_{u}
is divisible by
(1-x_{i})
but
not by
x_{i}
, and for each
j\in\tau
one has
u_{j}=1
, so
\rho_{u}
is divisible by
x_{j}
but not by
(1-x_{j})
.
Thus none of the linear pseudo-monomials
x_{i}
(
i\in\sigma
) or
(1-x_{j})
(
j\in\tau
) divides
\rho_{u}
.
If
k\notin\sigma\cup\tau
, then neither
x_{k}
nor
(1-x_{k})
lies in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
, since otherwise the
k
th
coordinate would be fixed on
Q
, contradicting the description of
Q
.
Hence no degree-
1
pseudo-monomial in
J_{\mathcal{C}}
divides
\rho_{u}
. Therefore
\deg(f)\geq 2
.

Notice that
{\mathcal{P}}(f)\in I
.
On the other hand, none of the variable generators of
I
in (2) divides
{\mathcal{P}}(f)
: the same divisibility
check as above shows that neither
x_{i}
(
i\in\sigma
) nor
y_{j}
(
j\in\tau
) divides
{\mathcal{P}}(f)
.
This contradicts the assumption that
I
is generated by
\{x_{i}:i\in\sigma\}\cup\{y_{j}:j\in\tau\}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{C}=Q
.

(3)\Rightarrow(2)
:

Assume
\mathcal{C}
as in (3), with
\sigma
and
\tau
the sets of coordinates fixed to
0
and
1
.
Every noncodeword violates at least one fixed coordinate, so each characteristic pseudo-monomial
\rho_{v}
is divisible by
some
x_{i}
(
i\in\sigma
) or some
(1-x_{j})
(
j\in\tau
). Hence

J_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma,\ (1-x_{j}):j\in\tau\rangle.

Conversely, for
i\in\sigma
we have

\sum_{v:v_{i}=1}\rho_{v}=x_{i}\prod_{k\neq i}\bigl(x_{k}+(1-x_{k})\bigr)=x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}},

and for
j\in\tau
,

\sum_{v:v_{j}=0}\rho_{v}=(1-x_{j})\prod_{k\neq j}\bigl(x_{k}+(1-x_{k})\bigr)=1-x_{j}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}.

Thus

J_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma\rangle+\langle(1-x_{j}):j\in\tau\rangle,

and polarizing gives

I=\langle x_{i}:i\in\sigma\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in\tau\rangle.

Finally, if
I
is generated by
t=|\sigma|+|\tau|
variables, then the Taylor on these variables
yields a minimal free resolution of
I
of
length
t-1
. Therefore
\pd(I)=t-1\leq n-1
. Moreover,
t=n
if and only if every coordinate is fixed,
equivalently if and only if
\mathcal{C}
consists of a single codeword.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

Coordinate subcubes control two boundary behaviors of the
\pd(I)
and
\reg(I)
, depending on
whether the code is itself a subcube or the complement of one. By
Theorem

8.1
,

\reg(I)=1
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is obtained by fixing some coordinates to
0
and some disjoint
coordinates to
1
; equivalently, in the notation of
Theorem

7.1
,

\mathcal{C}=Q(\tau,\sigma)
for some disjoint
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
, and then
I
is generated by variables.
By
Theorem

7.1
,
\pd(I)=0
if and only if
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus Q(\sigma,\tau)
for
some nonempty disjoint
\sigma,\tau
, and then
I
is principal.
In this sense, subcubes (and their complements) play for the
(\reg,\pd)
boundary the same organizing role
that antipodal pairs play for the extremal corners described in

Theorems

6.6
and
5.6
.

8.2.
The
\reg(I)=2
line

We now consider the case
\reg(I)=2
. By Hochster’s formula, the Betti table is supported on the linear
and quadratic strands, so contributions arise only from induced subcomplexes of the polar complex that
are empty or disconnected. The connectedness threshold in
Lemma

4.2
is the key input
that controls when such disconnectedness can occur.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a nonempty neural code. If
\reg(I)=2
, then

\pd(I)\ \leq\ n-2.

Proof.

Set
F:=\{t\in[n]:x_{t}\in I\text{ or }y_{t}\in I\}
and
f:=|F|
. For a polarized neural ideal we cannot have both
x_{t}
and
y_{t}
in
I
by
Remark

3.2
(2). So
f\leq n
.

Let
S^{\prime}:=\Bbbk[\{x_{i},y_{i}:i\in[n]\setminus F\}]

and decompose

I=L+I^{\prime},\qquad L:=\langle x_{t}:x_{t}\in I\rangle+\langle y_{t}:y_{t}\in I\rangle,\qquad I^{\prime}\subseteq S^{\prime},

where
I^{\prime}
is generated by the remaining minimal generators of
I
. Since
\reg(I)=2
, we have
I^{\prime}\neq 0
.

It follows from
Lemma

2.20
that

\reg_{S^{\prime}}(I^{\prime})=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pd_{S}(I)=\pd_{S^{\prime}}(I^{\prime})+f.

Because
I^{\prime}
has no linear generators and
\reg_{S^{\prime}}(I^{\prime})=2
, all minimal generators of
I^{\prime}
have degree
2

and
I^{\prime}
has a
2
–linear resolution; equivalently, the only possible nonzero Betti numbers of
I^{\prime}
lie on
the quadratic strand
j-i=2
.

Let
\Delta^{\prime}
denote the polar complex associated to
I^{\prime}
(equivalently, the polar complex of the restricted code obtained from
\mathcal{C}
by deleting the coordinates in
F
; indeed, each coordinate in
F
is constant on
\mathcal{C}
.). This restricted code is nondegenerate, since
I^{\prime}

contains no variables. By Hochster’s formula, if
\beta_{a,a+2}(I^{\prime})\neq 0
, then there exists

W\subseteq V(\Delta^{\prime})
with
|W|=a+2
such that

\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta^{\prime}_{W};\Bbbk)\neq 0,

i.e.
\Delta^{\prime}_{W}
is disconnected. Applying
Lemma

4.2
to the nondegenerate code on

n-f
neurons shows that
\Delta^{\prime}_{W}
is connected whenever
|W|\geq(n-f)+1
. Hence any
W
witnessing
disconnection must satisfy
|W|\leq n-f
. So
a\leq(n-f)-2
. It follows that

\pd_{S}(I)=\pd_{S^{\prime}}(I^{\prime})+f\leq((n-f)-2)+f=n-2,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

Proposition

8.3
explains what we observe along the
\reg(I)=2
lines in
Figure

1
. In particular, this explains why
(\pd(I),\reg(I))=(2,2)
for
n=3
and
(\pd(I),\reg(I))=(3,2)
for
n=4
cannot occur.

Proposition

8.3
describes the allowable range of projective dimensions along the line
\reg=2
. We next give an explicit family of codes realizing every value
0\leq\pd(I)\leq n-2
on this line.

Proposition 8.5
.

Fix
n\geq 2
. For every integer
p
with
p\in[0,n-2]
, there exists a neural code

\mathcal{C}_{n,p}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that for
I_{n,p}:={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}})\subseteq S
we have

(\pd(I_{n,p}),\reg(I_{n,p}))=(p,2).

Proof.

Fix
p\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
and define

\mathcal{C}_{n,p}:=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\ v_{1}=\cdots=v_{p}=0\ \text{ and not }(v_{p+1}=v_{p+2}=1)\}.

Recall that
J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
is generated by the characteristic pseudo-monomials of
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{n,p}

\rho_{v}=\prod_{i:v_{i}=1}x_{i}\prod_{j:v_{j}=0}(1-x_{j}),

and
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}})
consists of the pseudo-monomials in
J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
that are minimal under divisibility.

We first show that
x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
for
i=1,\dots,p
. Indeed, every codeword in
\mathcal{C}_{n,p}
satisfies
v_{i}=0
. So every word with
i
th coordinate
1
is a noncodeword. Summing their characteristic pseudo-monomials gives

\sum_{v:v_{i}=1}\rho_{v}=x_{i}\prod_{k\neq i}\bigl(x_{k}+(1-x_{k})\bigr)=x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}.

Moreover, each
x_{i}
is minimal.

Next we show
x_{p+1}x_{p+2}\in J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{n,p}
contains no word with
v_{p+1}=v_{p+2}=1
,
every word with
v_{p+1}=v_{p+2}=1
is a noncodeword, and summing their characteristic pseudo-monomials yields

\sum_{v:v_{p+1}=v_{p+2}=1}\rho_{v}=x_{p+1}x_{p+2}\prod_{k\notin\{p+1,p+2\}}\bigl(x_{k}+(1-x_{k})\bigr)=x_{p+1}x_{p+2}\in J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}.

This pseudo-monomial is minimal because
\mathcal{C}_{n,p}
contains words with
(v_{p+1},v_{p+2})=(1,0)
and with
(0,1)
,
so neither
x_{p+1}
nor
x_{p+2}
lies in
J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
.

Now let
v\notin\mathcal{C}_{n,p}
. Then either
v_{i}=1
for some
i\leq p
, in which case
\rho_{v}
is divisible by
x_{i}
,
or else
v_{1}=\cdots=v_{p}=0
and
v_{p+1}=v_{p+2}=1
, in which case
\rho_{v}
is divisible by
x_{p+1}x_{p+2}
.
Thus every
\rho_{v}
is divisible by one of
x_{1},\dots,x_{p},x_{p+1}x_{p+2}
. So these pseudo-monomials generate
J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}}
, and by the
minimality statements above they are exactly the canonical form. Thus

I_{n,p}={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{n,p}})=\bigl\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{p},\ x_{p+1}x_{p+2}\bigr\rangle.

Let
G(I_{n,p})=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{p},u\}
where
u:=x_{p+1}x_{p+2}
. For any subset
A\subseteq G(I_{n,p})
,

\lcm(A)=\begin{cases}\prod_{x_{i}\in A}x_{i},&u\notin A,\\[4.0pt]
x_{p+1}x_{p+2}\prod_{x_{i}\in A}x_{i},&u\in A.\end{cases}

In particular, distinct subsets yield distinct least common multiples, so the Taylor resolution of
I_{n,p}

is minimal and has length
p
. Therefore

\pd(I_{n,p})=p
.

To compute regularity, for a subset
A
of size
s
the corresponding Taylor basis element lies in homological
degree
s-1
. If
u\notin A
, then
\deg(\lcm(A))=s
; if
u\in A
, then

\deg(\lcm(A))=s+1
. Hence
\reg(I_{n,p})=2
.
∎

8.3.
The
\reg(I)=3
line

In this subsection we work along the line
\reg(I)=3
by building a flexible family of examples.
We start with the all-or-nothing code on
m
coordinates; by
Theorem

5.4
its polar ideal
satisfies
\reg(I)=3
and
\pd(I)=2m-3
. We then fix
t
additional coordinates, which adjoins
t
linear
generators in variables disjoint from the all-or-nothing part. This operation preserves regularity while
adjusting projective dimension in a controlled way.

Construction 1
.

Fix
n\geq 3
. Choose a partition

[n]=A\sqcup B\sqcup F\qquad\text{with}\qquad|A|=m,\ |B|=t,\ |F|=n-m-t,

where
2\leq m\leq n
and
0\leq t\leq n-m
.
Further split
B=B_{0}\sqcup B_{1}
(coordinates fixed to
0
and to
1
).

Define a code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by requiring:

•

on
A
, only the two patterns
0^{A}
and
1^{A}
occur;

•

on
B_{0}
we impose
v_{i}=0
and on
B_{1}
we impose
v_{j}=1
;

•

on
F
all bits are free.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{C}=\Big(\{0^{A},1^{A}\}\times\{0\}^{B_{0}}\times\{1\}^{B_{1}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{F}\Big)\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

Remark 8.6
.

When
t>0
this construction has fixed coordinates (hence the code is degenerate in our
sense), but this is harmless for the homological invariants we compute below.

Proposition 8.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be as in Construction
1
.
Then

I=\big\langle x_{i}y_{j}:i,j\in A,\ i\neq j\big\rangle+\langle x_{i}:i\in B_{0}\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in B_{1}\rangle,

and

(\pd(I),\reg(I))=((2m-3)+t,3).

Proof.

Write
S=S_{A}\otimes_{k}S_{B}\otimes_{k}S_{F}
, where

S_{A}=k[x_{i},y_{i}:i\in A],\qquad S_{B}=k[x_{i},y_{i}:i\in B],\qquad S_{F}=k[x_{i},y_{i}:i\in F].

Set

I_{A}:=\big\langle x_{i}y_{j}:i,j\in A,\ i\neq j\big\rangle\subseteq S_{A},\qquad L:=\langle x_{i}:i\in B_{0}\rangle+\langle y_{j}:j\in B_{1}\rangle\subseteq S_{B}.

The variables appearing in
I_{A}
and in
L
are disjoint, and
L
is generated by
t
variables.

We first identify the generators of
I
. Since
\mathcal{C}
restricts on
A
to the two words
0^{A}
and
1^{A}
,
no codeword has
v_{i}=1
and
v_{j}=0
for distinct
i,j\in A
. Thus
x_{i}(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
; it is minimal
because both values occur in each coordinate of
A
. Polarizing gives
x_{i}y_{j}\in I
for all
i\neq j
in
A
.
On
B_{0}
every codeword has
v_{i}=0
, so
x_{i}\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
minimally and hence
x_{i}\in I
for
i\in B_{0}
.
Similarly, on
B_{1}
every codeword has
v_{j}=1
, so
(1-x_{j})\in J_{\mathcal{C}}
minimally and polarizes to
y_{j}\in I
.

Conversely, any noncodeword either violates a fixed coordinate in
B
(forcing divisibility by a generator of
L
)
or differs from both
0^{A}
and
1^{A}
on
A
, in which case it contains a
1/0
mismatch among coordinates of
A

and is divisible by some

with

. Hence these are exactly the minimal generators, and

Since
I_{A}
is the all-or-nothing ideal on

coordinates,
Theorem

5.4
gives

(\pd(I_{A}),\reg(I_{A}))=(2m-3,3).

Adjoining the
t
generators of

one at a time,
Lemma

2.20
increases projective dimension
by
1
at each step and leaves regularity unchanged. Therefore

(\pd(I),\reg(I))=((2m-3)+t,3).\qed

Proposition
8.7
reduces the problem of realizing projective dimensions on the line

\reg=3
to choosing parameters
(m,t)
with

. We now show that this is possible for every

.

Theorem 8.8
.

Fix
n\geq 3
. For every integer

, there exists a neural code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

such that

(\pd(I),\reg(I))=(p,3).

Proof.

If
p=0
, choose disjoint
\sigma,\tau\subseteq[n]
with

and set

. Then

is principal of degree

. So
\pd(I)=0
and
\reg(I)=3
.

Now assume
1\leq p\leq 2n-3
.
If
1\leq p\leq n-1
, apply Construction

with

and

.
Then Proposition

gives

\pd(I)=(2\cdot 2-3)+(p-1)=p\qquad\text{and}\qquad\reg(I)=3.

9.
Constructions of neural codes below the line
p+r\leq n

In this section we construct nondegenerate neural codes whose polarized neural ideals realize every pair

(p,r)=(\pd(I),\reg(I))
in the region
p+r\leq n
, with
0\leq p\leq n-1
and
1\leq r\leq n
.
Our main input is that simplicial codes have neural ideals generated purely by Type 1 relations, so the
polar ideal is a Stanley–Reisner ideal
[
2
, Lemma 4.4]
. Indeed, any squarefree monomial ideal only in the
x
-variables are polarized neural ideals generated by Type 1 relations. By focusing on simplicial complexes, we use
Auslander–Buchsbaum and Cohen–Macaulayness to control projective dimension and Hochster’s formula to detect
regularity via top homology. Finally, adding free neurons extends constructions from
m=p+r
to arbitrary

n
without changing
\pd
or
\reg
.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on
[m]
and set

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}:=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}:\supp(c)\in\Delta\}.

Then the neural ideal is the Stanley–Reisner ideal,

J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}}=I_{\Delta}\subseteq k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}],

and hence

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}})=I_{\Delta}\cdot T,\qquad T:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}].

Moreover, the induced subcomplex of the polar complex
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}}
on the
x
–vertices

\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
is
\Delta
.

Proof.

By construction,
\supp(\mathcal{C}_{\Delta})=\Delta
. Since
\Delta
is a simplicial complex, it was shown in
[
2
]

that the canonical form
CF(J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}})
has no Type 2 or Type 3 relations and that
J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}}

is generated by the Type 1 relations, i.e. by the squarefree monomials corresponding to minimal nonfaces of

\Delta

[
2
, Lemma 4.4]
. Thus
J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}}=I_{\Delta}
in
k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
, and polarization yields

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}})=I_{\Delta}\cdot T
.

Finally, since
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}})
is generated by monomials in the
x
–variables, the induced subcomplex of

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}}
on
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
has faces exactly those
F\subseteq[m]
for which
x_{F}\notin I_{\Delta}
,
namely
F\in\Delta
. Hence
(\Delta_{\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}})|_{\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}}=\Delta
.
∎

We now realize all pairs
(\pd(I),\reg(I))
in the region
p+r\leq n
. Since the cases of
\reg=1,2,3

were handled in
Sections

8.1
,
8.2
and
8.3
, throughout we assume
r\geq 4
.

Theorem 9.2
.

Fix integers
n\geq 4
,
p\in[0,n-1]
, and
r\in[4,n]
with
p+r\leq n
.
Then there exists a nondegenerate neural code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that

(\pd(I),\reg(I))=(p,r).

Proof.

Put
m:=p+r
, so
m\leq n
and
m\geq r
. Choose an
(r-2)
–dimensional simplicial sphere
\Delta
on
vertex set
[m]
; for instance, one may take a stacked
(r-2)
–sphere with
m
vertices.

Define the simplicial code

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}=\{c\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}:\supp(c)\in\Delta\}.

Since
\emptyset\in\Delta
, we have
0\cdots 0\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Moreover, because
\Delta
has vertex set
[m]

(no ghost vertices), each singleton
\{i\}
lies in
\Delta
, so the word with support
\{i\}
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
Hence for every
i\in[m]
there are codewords in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with
i
th coordinate equal to
0
and to
1
, and

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is nondegenerate. Now add
n-m
free coordinates and set

\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

This preserves nondegeneracy, since each of the last
n-m
coordinates attains both values in
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
S_{m}:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
, let
T:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]
. By Lemma
9.1
,

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}})=I_{\Delta}\cdot T.

Iterating
Lemma

3.6
to account for the
n-m
free coordinates yields

I={\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}})\cdot S=I_{\Delta}\cdot S.

In particular, adjoining these extra variables does not change projective dimension or regularity, so it
suffices to compute
\pd(I_{\Delta})
and
\reg(I_{\Delta})
over
S_{m}
.

Because
\Delta
is a simplicial sphere, the Stanley–Reisner ring
S_{m}/I_{\Delta}
is Cohen–Macaulay
(by Reisner’s criterion; see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 5.1.9]
), and

\dim(S_{m}/I_{\Delta})=\dim\Delta+1=r-1.

Hence
\depth(S_{m}/I_{\Delta})=r-1
, and Auslander–Buchsbaum gives

\pd_{S_{m}}(I_{\Delta})+1=\pd_{S_{m}}(S_{m}/I_{\Delta})=m-\depth(S_{m}/I_{\Delta})=m-(r-1)=p+1.

It follows that
\pd_{S_{m}}(I_{\Delta})=p
.

For regularity, apply Hochster’s formula to
I_{\Delta}
. Since
\Delta
is an
(r-2)
–sphere on
m
vertices,

\widetilde{H}_{r-2}(\Delta;k)\cong k
. Taking
W=V(\Delta)
(so
|W|=m
and
\Delta_{W}=\Delta
) yields

\beta_{p,m}(I_{\Delta})=\dim_{k}\widetilde{H}_{m-p-2}(\Delta;k)=\dim_{k}\widetilde{H}_{r-2}(\Delta;k)=1,

and therefore
\reg(I_{\Delta})\geq m-p=r
.
Conversely, if
\beta_{i,j}(I_{\Delta})\neq 0
, then Hochster’s formula gives

\widetilde{H}_{j-i-2}(\Delta_{W};k)\neq 0
for some
W
with
|W|=j
. Hence

j-i-2\leq\dim(\Delta_{W})\leq\dim(\Delta)=r-2,

so
j-i\leq r
for every nonzero graded Betti number. Thus
\reg(I_{\Delta})\leq r
, and we conclude that

\reg(I_{\Delta})=r
.

Finally, since
I=I_{\Delta}\cdot S
is obtained from
I_{\Delta}
by adjoining variables,
Lemma

3.6

implies that
\pd
and
\reg
are preserved. Therefore
\pd(I)=p
and
\reg(I)=r
.
∎

Example 9.3
.

Let
n=8
,
p=3
,
r=4
. Here
m=p+r=7
and
r-2=2
, so we need a
2
–sphere
\Delta
on vertices
[7]
. We construct
\Delta
as a stacked
2
-sphere on
[7]
by starting from the tetrahedron boundary

\partial\Delta_{3}
on
\{1,2,3,4\}
and successively stacking on the facets
123
,
124
, and
134
,
introducing the new vertices
5,6,7
in turn. Then its facets (triangles) are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\Delta)=\{

A direct check shows that the minimal nonfaces of

27,36,45,56,57,67,123,124,134.

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{7}

By Lemma

, the polarized neural ideal is
generated by these minimal nonfaces (now viewed in
k[x_{1},\dots,x_{8},y_{1},\dots,y_{8}]

{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})=\langle x_{2}x_{7},x_{3}x_{6},x_{4}x_{5},x_{5}x_{6},x_{5}x_{7},x_{6}x_{7},x_{1}x_{2}x_{3},x_{1}x_{2}x_{4},x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}\rangle.

Equivalently (as a description of the code),
\mathcal{C}

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{8}

not

10.
Conclusion and Further directions

The results above suggest an emerging dictionary between elementary features of the Hamming cube and
the homological data of the polarized neural ideal
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
. Throughout, we translate the
combinatorics of
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
into the topology of induced subcomplexes of the polar complex

\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}
via Hochster’s formula. Several sharp boundary phenomena arise from particularly rigid
cube structure: coordinate subcubes and complements of coordinate subcubes control

\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))=1
and
\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))=0
, while antipodality controls the extremal corners

\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))=2n-1
and
\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))=2n-3
. These patterns raise the prospect that the achievable
region of
(\pd,\reg)
values, even finer invariants such as the shape of the Betti table, may
be describable using a small amount of combinatorial data, and that additional geometric restrictions
on
\mathcal{C}
(e.g. convexity or hyperplane structure) may dramatically constrain what can occur. We
conclude our study by collecting several questions motivated by this viewpoint.

Question 10.1
.

To what extent is the pair
(\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})),\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})))
controlled by Hamming-cube geometry of

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
? Are the boundary classifications (subcubes/co-subcubes and antipodal configurations)
shadows of a broader cube-theoretic organization of the entire achievable
(\pd,\reg)
region, or do
genuinely new behaviors appear away from the boundary?

Question 10.2
.

For fixed
n
, determine the set

\mathcal{R}_{n}:=\bigl\{(\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})),\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))):\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\bigr\}.

Question 10.3
.

Give a combinatorial description of the multigraded Betti numbers of
{\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})
in terms of
Hamming-cube structure of
\mathcal{C}
(or of its complement
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}
).

Question 10.4
.

For a class
\mathfrak{F}
of codes (e.g. convex codes, stable hyperplane codes), determine

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathfrak{F}):=\{(\pd({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}})),\reg({\mathcal{P}}(J_{\mathcal{C}}))):\mathcal{C}\in\mathfrak{F},\ \mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\}.

How does
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathfrak{F})
compare to
\mathcal{R}_{n}
?
