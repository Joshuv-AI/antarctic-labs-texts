Proteome organ aging and cardiometabolic risk in a population at risk for heart failure
Authors: Kaiyong Qu, Elisa Dal Canto, Anne-Mar L. N. van Ommen, Sabrina Abou Kamar, Arco J. Teske, Maarten Jan Cramer, Marianne C. Verhaar, Dirk J. Duncker, Frans H. Rutten, Isabella Kardys, Roxana Menken, Leonard Hofstra, Igor I. Tulevski, G. Aernout Somsen, N. Charlotte Onland-Moret, Ernest Diez Benavente, Hester M. den Ruijter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiological aging varies across individuals and tissues, influencing chronic diseases, including heart failure (HF). Emerging proteome techniques enable quantification of organ-specific aging acceleration (OAA), but whether OAA relates to HF severity and differs by sex remains unclear. We aim to assess the sex-related association between OAA of heart, artery and kidneys and HF severity, and to investigate relevant cardiometabolic risk factors of organ aging.MethodsIn 556 participants from the HELPFul cohort, we estimated predicted biological age for heart, artery, and kidneys using plasma proteomics and calculated OAA as the deviation from chronological age. Associations between OAA and HF stage, echocardiographic parameters, and cardiometabolic risk factors were evaluated using regression models. Composite indices, including triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI), c-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index and triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio were assessed for associations with advanced OAA.ResultsMean age was 63 ± 9 years; 65% were women. Patients were classified as HF stage A (35%), B (29%) and C/D (36%). Heart OAA was significantly associated with advanced HF (Stage C/D) in both sexes (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.23 in women; OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.32 in men), while artery OAA was linked to HF only in women (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.18). Multi-organ aging (≥ 2 organs with advanced OAA) conferred over three-fold higher odds of being in Stage C/D. Heart OAA correlated with impaired cardiac structure and function, particularly reduced ejection fraction in men and increased left ventricular mass index in both sexes. Diabetes emerged as the most relevant factor of artery and kidney OAA. TyG-BMI was significantly associated with advanced kidney OAA, only in women (z-scored OR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.45 to 2.45).ConclusionsProteome-derived organ aging correlates with HF severity, with possible sex-related patterns. Diabetes and higher TyG-BMI are associated with faster organ aging, which may reflect shared aging mechanisms between metabolic dysfunction and HF.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02980-6.

Research insights

What is currently known about this topic?

Biological aging varies by organ and predicts risk for chronic diseases including heart failure (HF).

What is the key research question?

Does organ-specific aging acceleration (OAA) relate to HF severity and cardiometabolic risk factors and differ by sex?

What is new?

Proteome-derived heart OAA correlates with HF severity in both sexes. Artery OAA relates to HF only in women, suggesting a possible sex-related vascular role. Cardiometabolic risk factors including diabetes and TyG-BMI correlate with artery and kidney OAA, which are more evident in women.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Organ aging clocks may tailor HF care, highlighting focus on managing artery aging and diabetes and insulin resistance, especially in women.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by high morbidity and mortality [1]. It poses a significant burden on global health, with its prevalence and impact expected to increase alongside population growth and increasing life-expectancy [2]. Advanced age is a major risk factor for HF development [3], with prevalence increasing markedly with age-from 1.3% in individuals aged 45–54 years to 16.1% in those over 74 years [4, 5]. Additionally, HF phenotypes vary by sex [6]. HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is more prevalent in women while HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) occurs more often in men. Although aging has traditionally been considered a non-modifiable cardiovascular risk factor, recent animal studies examining molecular changes that occur with aging have shown that age-related deterioration varies substantially across organs and between individuals [7]. While chronologic age progresses at a predictable rate, biological age is more variable. The processes governing biological age are hypothesized to be common drivers of chronic progressive disease pathogenesis in older adults. In other words, “biological aging” of human organ systems reflects the interplay of age, risk factors, lifestyle and genetic risk. Various measures have been developed to assess biological age such as telomere length and epigenetic clocks [8]. Only recently, a comprehensive analysis of the human plasma proteome has enabled estimation of organ-specific biological age for 11 distinct organs—including the heart, artery, and kidneys—based on circulating protein signatures that reflect underlying tissue-specific processes [9]. These organ-level aging metrics were shown to predict clinical outcomes: Accelerated aging of specific organs is associated with a 20–50% increase in mortality risk, and diseases affecting individual organs are linked to more rapid aging of those same organs. Individuals with accelerated heart aging exhibit a 2.5-fold higher risk of developing HF, while accelerated aging of the brain and vasculature predicts Alzheimer’s disease progression independently of plasma pTau-181 [10]—the current leading blood-based biomarker for AD. However, it remains unclear to what extent accelerated aging in specific organs correlates with disease severity across different HF stages and whether this association differs by sex. Additionally, metabolic risk factors, such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, and insulin resistance, increase the risk of HF development, but appear to confer disproportionately greater risk in women compared to men, for reasons that are still poorly understood. In parallel, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced guidelines on cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, highlighting the interdependence of these organ systems [11]. Population-based data showed that women experienced greater mortality risk across CKM stages [12]. Organ-specific biological aging may provide mechanistic insights into this sex-based disparity. In this context, we focused organ-age analysis on the heart, artery, and kidneys, given their well-established roles in the development and progression of HF. While cardiac changes are intrinsic to HF, arterial remodeling—including increased stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, and extracellular matrix alterations—raises left ventricular afterload and contributes to impaired relaxation and diastolic dysfunction [13]. Similarly, renal structural deterioration, marked by nephron loss, tubular injury, and interstitial fibrosis, impairs sodium and fluid homeostasis, exacerbating volume overload and congestion [14]. To address this, we quantified organ aging acceleration (OAA) in key organs (heart, artery, and kidneys) among women and men across various HF stages and examined associations with HF stages by sex. Additionally, we evaluated potential clinical determinants of OAA.

Box: key definitions

Chronological age: The actual age of an individual.

Organ age: The predicted biological age of a specific organ, estimated using plasma proteome based on organ-specific protein signatures.

Conventional age: The predicted biological age derived from plasma proteome using the full set of proteins, without reference to organ specificity.

Organ aging acceleration: OAA, the deviation between predicted organ age and chronological age, quantified as the vertical distance from the LOWESS curve fitted between these two measures. Advanced OAA was defined as the top 25% of OAA values within the cohort, and youthful OAA as the bottom 25%.

Methods

Study population

We included 556 participants from the HELPFul (“Discovery of biomarkers for the presence and progression of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and HEart faiLure with Preserved ejection Fraction in patients at risk for cardiovascular disease) [15]. The HELPFul registry received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Review Committee (METC) of the UMC Utrecht, with METC protocol number 16-290/M-X and CCMO number NL57077.041.16. It was registered under the Dutch Trial Register number NTR6016, with registration on 18 August 2016. The HELPFul study was a cohort study including 880 participants ≥ 45 years with increased cardiovascular risk profile, who were referred by the general practitioner to the CCN (Cardiology Centers Netherlands, Location Utrecht Galgenwaard) for cardiac evaluation [15]. Participants were categorized according to AHA clinical classification as HF stage A, B or C/D [16]. A comparison between the overall HELPFul population and the study population showed no significant differences in key demographic and clinical variables except that the study population had lower HDL cholesterol levels (2.14 mmol/ml in the overall population vs 1.60 mmol/ml in the study population) (Supplementary Table 1). The participant selection flow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Assessment of clinical parameters

Approximately 70 mL of venous blood was collected on the day of the baseline assessment. Plasma total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), C-reactive protein (CRP), blood glucose, and serum creatinine concentrations were determined as previously described [15]. Kidney function was estimated according to the 2021 CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation yielding an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2) [17]. Information on medical history, smoking status, and medication use were obtained in a structured interview at the baseline visit. Height and weight were assessed while standing without shoes but with light clothing on. Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by height squared (m2). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) were measured at rest, during the study visit according to a standardized protocol using a Microlife WatchBP and arterial hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg and/or use of anti-hypertensive medication. In addition, we calculated three composite metabolic indices. The C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) was calculated as: CTI = 0.412 × ln [CRP (mg/L)] + ln [TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2]. The triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI) was calculated as: ln [TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2] × BMI (kg/m2). The triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL ratio) was calculated by dividing TG (mg/dL) by HDL cholesterol (mg/dL).

Echocardiography

Participants underwent rest echocardiography, performed on a General Electric Vivid E6 or E7 ultrasound device (General Electric Medical Systems, Horten, Norway) using a standardized protocol involving 2-dimensional (2D), M-mode, Doppler and tissue Doppler (TDI) in accordance with current recommendations [18, 19]. A comprehensive analysis of morphological and functional markers of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) was carried out by trained sonographers. In particular: Pulsed-wave TDI E′ velocities were measured at the lateral and septal mitral anulus and the average E’ velocity was computed to calculate the E/e’ ratio. Left ventricular mass index (LVMI) was calculated from LV linear dimensions according to the formula validated by Devereux and indexed to body surface area (BSA) [18]. The left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was assessed quantitatively (Teichholz or Simpson's).

Plasma proteome and organ-specific aging model application

Venous blood collected from participants was processed and frozen following analogous protocols. Samples were aliquoted and frozen at − 80 °C within two hours from the moment of venipuncture. Plasma (ethylenediamine tetra acetic acid, EDTA) samples were sent (frozen and on dry ice with temperature monitoring) to SomaLogic (Boulder, Colorado) for SomaScan® V4 assay measurement, as described previously [20]. Plasma protein levels were measured, generating relative abundances for 5284 SOMAmer reagents. Standard SomaLogic normalization and quality control were applied, including within- and across-plate normalization using pooled reference standards and an adaptive maximum likelihood procedure. Organ age was calculated for each individual based on SomaScan® V4 proteome data using the Python package "organage" (https://github.com/hamiltonoh/organage), as previously described [9]. Briefly, organ-specific aging models were developed in a prior study by mapping plasma proteins to human organ transcriptomes using RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project [21]. Genes were considered ‘organ-enriched’ if their expression in one organ was at least fourfold higher than in any other organ, following the Human Protein Atlas definition [22]. This annotation was transferred to the plasma proteome, and a total of 856 organ-enriched proteins (out of 4778 curated human proteins measured by SomaScan®) were identified. Using these protein sets, the original study trained a bagged ensemble of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) models for 11 major organs (including heart, kidney, artery, etc.) to predict chronological age based on organ-specific plasma protein signatures, using data from 1398 healthy individuals in the Knight-ADRC cohort [23]. These pre-trained models were made available through the organage package and were directly applied to our dataset to estimate predicted organ ages.

In this study, we focused on aging profiles of the artery, heart, and kidney, due to their known relevance to heart failure. We also applied the conventional aging model, which was trained using all 4, 778 proteins regardless of organ specificity, to represent a global plasma proteome aging signature. Supplementary Table 7 from Oh et al. [9] contains the full list of proteins and coefficients for each organ-specific model. The predicted organ ages showed consistent correlation with chronological age in our cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2), confirming model generalizability across datasets. OAA were calculated for each organ model following the same procedure described by the original authors [9]. A locally weighted regression (LOWESS) curve was fitted between chronological and predicted age using the Python statsmodels [24] package, with a smoothing span of two-thirds, the default tricube kernel, and five robustness iterations to reduce the influence of outliers. Local regression was used instead of linear regression to capture nonlinear relationships between plasma protein profiles and aging. OAA were then defined as the vertical residuals from this LOWESS curve, rather than as the direct difference between predicted and chronological age, to account for cohort-specific differences. In this way, OAA represents a relative measure within each cohort, consistent with standard practice in aging clock research [25]. The ± 2 SD cut-off of OAA used in the original study was defined as the "extreme" ager. We did not apply this approach due to the limited number of extreme cases in our cohort (n = 14). Instead, we analyzed "advanced" OAA, defined as the top 25% OAA values within the cohort. The same approach was applied to define youthful OAA as the bottom 25%.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. For group comparisons, continuous variables were compared using either the independent-samples t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two groups, and either analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across more than two groups, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, based on expected cell counts. Baseline characteristics were summarized according to AHA functional HF stages (A, B, and C/D). Missing data were described in Supplementary Table 2 and imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (10 imputations and 20 iterations per imputation). Statistical models were fitted separately within each imputed dataset, and the parameter estimates were subsequently pooled across imputations using Rubin's rules. All analyses were conducted in the total population and stratified by sex. All regression models were adjusted for chronological age and sex in the total population, and for chronological age only in the sex-stratified analyses. First, we assessed the association between OAA—including artery, heart, kidney, and conventional—and HF stage (A vs. B vs. C/D) using multinomial logistic regression models. Similar analyses were performed with the number of organs exhibiting advanced or youthful OAA as the determinant excluding conventional OAA from organ counts. Second, we used linear regression models to examine the association between OAA and selected echocardiographic markers, including functional parameters (E/e′ and EF) and structural measures (LVMI). Third, we investigated the association between cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors and OAA as the outcome using multivariable linear regression models. Each risk factor was first examined individually in models adjusted for age and sex, before entering all risk factors simultaneously into a multivariable model. Fourth, we examined the associations of CTI, TyG-BMI, and TG/HDL ratio with the presence of advanced OAA using multivariable logistic regression models. For determinants showing significant associations with advanced OAA, restricted cubic spline analyses were subsequently conducted to explore potential nonlinear relationships. To assess sex differences in all associations, interaction terms between sex and each determinant were added to the models. Likelihood ratio tests were performed to compare models with and without interaction terms to evaluate whether inclusion of the interaction significantly improved model fit. For all models, standard diagnostic procedures were applied to check key assumptions, including linearity of continuous predictors, homoscedasticity of residuals, and absence of multicollinearity. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied, separately within each analytical section. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided threshold of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 556 participants were included in the HELPFul study, with 195 (35%) classified as HF stage A, 162 (29%) as stage B, and 199 (36%) as stage C/D according to AHA functional heart failure classification (Table 1). The mean age of the overall cohort was 63 (± 9) years, with older ages in higher HF stages (Stage A: 58 years; Stage B: 65 years; Stage C/D: 66 years; P < 0.001). Women constituted 65% of the study population, and their proportion was higher in advancing HF stage (P < 0.001). Participants with more advanced HF stages tended to have a higher BMI, and a greater prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. The use of antihypertensive medication and statins was also higher with increased HF severity. In contrast, the proportion of current smokers remained relatively consistent across the groups. Coronary artery disease and atrial fibrillation were more frequently observed in participants with stage C/D HF. Laboratory measurements showed broadly comparable levels of total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, fasting plasma glucose, and serum creatinine across HF stages. However, LDL cholesterol was modestly lower and HbA1c levels were higher among those with more advanced HF. Renal function, as estimated by eGFR, declined with increasing HF severity, and NT-proBNP levels rose substantially across stages, consistent with the clinical trajectory of disease progression. Among the derived metabolic indices, both CTI and TyG-BMI demonstrated upward trends with HF stage, although these did not reach statistical significance. No notable differences were observed in the TG/HDL ratio. Structural and diastolic abnormalities progressively worsened with advancing HF stage (Supplementary Table 3). Descriptive analysis of organ aging profiles in the study participants

Across HF stages, only heart OAA showed significant differences, with Stage C/D (1.0 ± 3.9 years) being higher than Stage A (− 0.2 ± 2.8 years) and Stage B (− 0.1 ± 2.8 years, P < 0.001). Artery OAA (P = 0.061), kidney OAA (P = 0.771), and conventional OAA (P = 0.100) were broadly comparable across stages, though artery and conventional OAA were slightly higher in Stage C/D (Table 1). Chronological age was comparable between women and men (Fig. 1), with a similar age distribution observed across sexes. However, the organ age predicted by the plasma proteins demonstrated sex-related trends. Women had significantly higher predicted artery age compared to men, whereas men exhibited older predicted kidney age across all HF stages. No significant sex differences were observed for heart or conventional predicted age. When examining OAA, distinct sex-related trends emerged across HF stages (Fig. 2). Women consistently exhibited higher artery OAA than men across all HF stages, while men had greater kidney OAA. Heart and conventional OAA showed no clear sex-related trends. The amount burden of organ-specific aging also differed by HF stage. The proportion of individuals with no advanced OAA was lower in more advanced HF stages, whereas the proportion with ≥ 2 organs showing advanced OAA was higher in Stage C/D compared with Stage A, suggesting a cumulative pattern of multi-organ aging associated with greater HF severity. Correlations between OAA estimates across different organ systems are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Conventional OAA was significantly correlated with all three organ-specific OAA measures, most strongly with heart OAA (r = 0.41, P < 0.001). A weak correlation was also observed between artery and heart OAA (r = 0.22, P < 0.001). Associations between OAA and heart failure stages

Multinomial logistic regression analyses revealed significant associations between several OAA measures and HF stage severity (Fig. 3, Table 2). In the total population, each 1-year increase in higher heart OAA was associated with increased odds of HF stage C/D compared to stage A (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.22, FDR = 0.005), while associations for artery, kidney, and conventional OAA were not statistically significant. No significant associations were observed for any OAA measure with stage B. Sex-stratified analyses demonstrated distinct trends. In women, both heart OAA (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.23, FDR = 0.040) and artery OAA (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.18, FDR = 0.040) were significantly associated with higher odds of HF stage C/D, whereas in men, only heart OAA showed a significant association (OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.32, FDR = 0.027). No significant sex interactions were observed for any organ system (all P interaction > 0.05). A line graph portrayed the relationship between log odds of HF stage C/D and OAA, shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. We next examined the cumulative burden of organ aging using the number of organs with advanced/youthful OAA (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4). Compared with participants without any advanced OAA, those with ≥ 2 had substantially higher odds of HF stage C/D (OR = 3.08, 95% CI 1.64 to 5.79, FDR = 0.002). Having only one advanced OAA organ was also associated with higher odds of HF stage C/D, though effect sizes were smaller. No significant associations were observed for stage B. Additionally, no associations were observed between the number of youthful organs and HF stage in either the total population or sex subgroups. Associations between OAA and echocardiographic markers

In linear regression analyses, higher heart OAA was significantly associated with adverse structural and functional echocardiographic markers in the total population (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, each 1-year increase in heart OAA was associated with a higher LVMI (β = 1.11, 95% CI 0.66 to 1.55, FDR < 0.001) and lower EF (β = − 0.23, 95% CI − 0.45 to − 0.01, P = 0.039, FDR = 0.106). Conventional OAA was also associated with increased LVMI (β = 0.56, 95% CI 0.10 to 1.03, FDR = 0.041). No significant associations were observed for E/e′ ratio. Sex-stratified analyses revealed notable differences. In women, heart OAA remained positively associated with LVMI (β = 0.58, 95% CI 0.07 to 1.10, P = 0.026, FDR = 0.052), but no associations were observed with EF or other organ systems. In men, heart OAA was strongly associated with both increased LVMI (β = 1.75, 95% CI 0.93 to 2.58, FDR < 0.001) and reduced EF (β = − 0.52, 95% CI − 0.86 to − 0.18, FDR = 0.037). Additionally, conventional OAA in men was significantly associated with higher LVMI (β = 1.28, 95% CI 0.31 to 2.24, FDR = 0.037). Interaction tests supported a potential sex difference for the association between heart OAA and EF (P for interaction = 0.010), and LVMI (P for interaction = 0.010) but not for E/e′.

Associations between cardiometabolic risk factors and OAA

In multivariable linear regression analyses including all clinical risk factors simultaneously (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 6), diabetes emerged as the most consistent determinant of OAA. In the total population, diabetes was significantly associated with higher artery (β = 2.17, 95% CI 1.04 to 3.31, FDR = 0.003) and kidney OAA (β = 0.87, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.49, FDR = 0.036). Sex-stratified analyses revealed that in women, diabetes was associated with greater artery (β = 1.83, 95% CI 0.22 to 3.45, P = 0.026, FDR = 0.158) and kidney OAA (β = 1.19, 95% CI 0.31 to 2.07, FDR = 0.036). In men, a similar relationship between diabetes and artery OAA was observed (β = 2.36, 95% CI 0.79 to 3.93, FDR = 0.030), while kidney associations were not significant. No significant sex interactions were detected. Other findings included positive associations between BMI and kidney OAA in the total population, and in women only. Discussion

In this study, we quantified OAA in the heart, artery, and kidneys and examined its relationship with HF severity and risk factors. We found that heart OAA was significantly associated with advanced HF stages, particularly stage C/D, in the overall cohort. Importantly, possible sex-related patterns emerged: women with HF had both elevated heart and artery OAA, whereas in men only heart OAA was linked to HF severity, although the interaction test was not statistically significant. We also observed that individuals with multi-organ advanced OAA (≥ 2 organs) had over three-fold higher odds of HF stage C/D compared to those without any OAA. These findings extend emerging evidence that biological aging of organs relates to chronic disease risk. Prior work using proteome age metrics showed that about 20% of healthy adults exhibit OAA in one or more organs, conferring higher mortality and disease risk [9]. Individuals with heart OAA have a ~ 2.5-fold greater risk of developing HF, consistent with our observation that heart OAA is linked to clinical HF status. Accelerated aging appears to be organ-specific: diseases of a given organ tend to coincide with faster aging of that organ. Our results build on this concept by demonstrating that such proteome-based organ “age gaps” are not only predictive of future HF but also reflect current HF severity, and that accumulating aging deficits across multiple organs markedly correlates with more advanced HF stage.

Protein signatures underlying cardiovascular-related organ aging

The proteins with the strongest weights in the heart aging model include pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NPPB), a negative regulator of blood pressure that increases in response to heart failure, and troponin T (TNNT2), a heart muscle protein involved in contraction and an established clinical marker of heart damage [26, 27]. The severity of HF is therefore expected to be related to increased heart OAA reflected by these proteins. In the artery aging model, TNFRSF11B has the highest weight, encoding osteoprotegerin which is produced in the vascular system. Elevated serum osteoprotegerin levels are often observed in patients with type 2 diabetes and are correlated with the severity of coronary artery disease and the presence of atherosclerosis and other vascular calcifications [28, 29]. This may account for the observed association between diabetes and artery OAA in our study. In the analysis examining artery OAA and HF severity, a significant association was observed only among women, which will be further discussed below. In the kidney aging model, renin (REN), a kidney enzyme known to regulate blood pressure via the renin-angiotensin pathway [30], ranks first in the feature importance of the model. The alterations in the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system are known to contribute to the underlying pathophysiology of insulin resistance in humans [31], which is consistent with our observation that increased TyG-BMI is associated with advanced kidney OAA.

Sex-related trends in OAA and heart failure

We found possible sex-related patterns in how organ biological aging relates to HF. Among women, both heart and artery OAA were significantly associated with advanced HF, whereas in men only heart OAA showed a significant association. Although formal interaction tests by sex were not statistically significant, these observations suggest a possible stronger relationship between vascular aging and HF in women. This aligns with known sex differences in HF pathophysiology. Women—who are more prone to HFpEF—often exhibit more pronounced vascular abnormalities, including arterial stiffening and endothelial dysfunction [32]. Indeed, women with HFpEF have been shown to have significantly higher arterial stiffness than men, ~ 10% higher augmentation index and higher pulse pressure, which is associated with worse diastolic function during exercise [32]. The presence of stiffer arteries increases left ventricular afterload and impairs relaxation, contributing to HFpEF in older women. Our finding that artery OAA correlates with HF severity in women but not men is consistent with this concept, suggesting that in women, vascular aging may be an important factor associated with HF severity alongside cardiac aging. In fact, sex differences in vascular phenotypes emerge as early as childhood and follow distinct trajectories [33], including differences in blood pressure patterns, which contribute to sex-specific variation in cardiovascular events later in life [34, 35]. Conversely, in men, who more often develop HFrEF, the disease may follow myocardial infarction and be predominantly driven by cardiac aging/damage alone. In addition, we observed that heart OAA was associated with adverse cardiac remodeling in a sex-related manner with significant interactions. In men, each 1-year increase in heart OAA was significantly associated with higher LVMI and lower EF, indicating a greater degree of hypertrophy and systolic dysfunction in men with biologically older hearts. In women, heart OAA was linked to higher LVMI as well, but not significantly to EF. This suggests that in women, cardiac aging may manifest more as concentric remodeling with preserved EF, whereas in men it contributes to overt systolic impairment. Thus, heart OAA appears to be linked to sex-related HF phenotypes—predominantly diastolic dysfunction in women and combined systolic dysfunction in men.

Diabetes in OAA

Among the cardiometabolic risk factors evaluated, diabetes mellitus emerged as the most prominent determinant of OAA in multiple organs. In our multivariable analyses, diabetes was independently associated with greater artery OAA and kidney OAA, even after accounting for other risk factors. This is consistent with evidence indicating that diabetes is essentially a state of vascular aging. Chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance lead to endothelial dysfunction [36], increased oxidative stress, and glycation of proteins, which age the vasculature and kidneys at an accelerated rate [37]. Our findings reinforce this: diabetic individuals had biologically “older” arteries and kidneys than their non-diabetic counterparts of the same age. In addition, we found that in women, diabetes correlated with artery and kidney OAA, whereas in men only artery OAA was significant. This trend mirrors epidemiological data that diabetes confers a greater relative cardiovascular risk in women than in men[38]. For example, women with diabetes experience a higher HF risk compared to men with diabetes, and women lose the cardioprotection conferred by estrogens during pre-menopause once diabetes is present [39]. Our results suggest one biological basis for this phenomenon: women with diabetes may suffer more from OAA of critical organs, which in turn elevates their HF risk disproportionately. This sex-related trend could be due to women’s hormonal differences, or to the fact that women with diabetes often have more prolonged exposure before diagnosis and are less aggressively treated in some cases [40]. Regardless, diabetes appears to make the organs age faster, and especially so in women, underscoring the importance of risk factor management in women. Beyond diabetes, we also found that other modifiable factors like obesity and smoking were associated with OAA, particularly in the kidneys. Higher BMI and current smoking status were linked to greater kidney OAA in the total population, suggesting that general lifestyle-related damage accumulates in the kidneys. Smoking promotes oxidative stress and arteriosclerosis in renal microvessels, while obesity drives glomerular hyperfiltration and inflammation—both accelerating kidney “wear and tear” akin to aging [41, 42]. In contrast, hypertension and dyslipidemia were not independent predictors of OAA in our multivariable models, possibly because in our study population these conditions were well managed.

TyG-BMI and renal OAA: the role of insulin resistance

We studied composite metabolic indices and found that the TyG-BMI showed a significant association with kidney OAA. Participants with higher TyG-BMI, a marker of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome [43], had significantly higher odds of exhibiting advanced kidney OAA.

There was a significant sex interaction, indicating the relationship between insulin resistance and renal aging is stronger in women. Indeed, in women this association was particularly robust, with one standard deviation increase in TyG-BMI nearly doubling the odds of advanced kidney OAA. These results reinforce the link between metabolic dysfunction and kidney health. Previous prospective data have similarly shown that an elevated TyG-BMI is an independent risk factor for the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD): individuals in the highest TyG-BMI tertile had over fourfold higher odds of incident chronic kidney disease compared to those in the lowest tertile [44]. Notably, the TyG-BMI–CKD link seemed more pronounced in women and in younger individuals, matching our observations in an aging context. The biological mechanisms behind this likely involve insulin resistance-driven glomerular hyperfiltration, increased visceral adiposity releasing pro-inflammatory adipokines, and dyslipidemia causing lipid deposition in the kidney—all contributing to structural and functional degeneration of the kidneys, often termed “metabolic kidney disease”. Our finding of a linear relationship between TyG-BMI and kidney aging supported by spline analysis suggests there may be no safe threshold—even moderate metabolic derangement steadily ages the kidneys faster. Other metabolic indices, the TG-HDL ratio and CTI were not significantly associated with organ aging in our cohort. TyG-BMI may be a more sensitive integrative marker capturing both insulin resistance and adiposity, which are key drivers of renal microvascular damage. Clinically, this underscores the importance of managing insulin resistance, through diet, exercise, and possibly metformin or GLP-1 agonists to preserve kidney health. Given that renal aging and dysfunction feed into HF by exacerbating volume overload and hypertension, addressing metabolic health could mitigate one facet of HF progression.

Our findings have several implications and may help guide future work. First, they suggest that measuring organ-specific biological age provides information about HF severity beyond chronological age alone. Patients with HF who appear similar in chronological age may have very different biological ages of their cardiovascular organs, which could explain variation in disease progression and outcomes. Second, our demonstration that having two or more organs with advanced OAA multiplies HF risk supports the concept of a synergistic multi-organ aging burden in HF. Our work underscores the importance of a multi-organ approach in HF management. It could also shed light on whether slowing organ-specific aging translates into improved HF outcomes, which would however require longitudinal studies and possibly intervention trials targeting aging mechanisms. In addition, we used the SomaScan platform for our study, which may capture distinct proteins aging profiles compared with other platforms. Reconstruction of the organ-specific proteome clock in UK Biobank showed moderate consistency between Olink and SomaScan platforms, likely due to technical differences and non-overlapping protein coverage between platforms [45]. Standardization across proteome technologies will be important for future work to enhance comparability. This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and elucidate the causal pathways between organ aging and HF progression. Moreover, although adjustments were made for confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as physical activity and diet remains likely. Furthermore, the HELPFul cohort comprises individuals referred by GP for cardiac evaluation due to elevated cardiovascular risk. This potentially limits the generalizability of our findings to broader, community-based populations. Another important limitation relates to sample size, both for the total cohort of 556 individuals, and the sex-stratified analyses. We used FDR adjustment to reduce the risk of false positives. Nevertheless, the modest sample size increases the likelihood of false negatives. In the sex-stratified analyses, certain associations were statistically significant in one sex but not in the other; however, formal interaction tests were largely non-significant, likely reflecting limited power. These findings should therefore be viewed as exploratory trends rather than definitive sex-specific outcomes. Together, these limitations highlight the importance of replication in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate that OAA of cardiovascular-related organs is closely linked to HF severity, with possible sex-related patterns. Women with HF exhibit a greater burden of both artery and heart OAA, whereas HF in men relates to heart OAA. Metabolic dysfunction, including diabetes and higher TyG-BMI, is associated with accelerated cardiovascular-related organ aging, supporting a cardiometabolic continuum underlying cardiovascular disease pathogenesis.
