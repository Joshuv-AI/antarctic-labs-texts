Gut microbiota analysis and LC-MS-based metabolomics to investigate AMPK/NF-κB regulated by Clostridium butyricum in the treatment of acute pancreatitis
Authors: Biyan Wen, Yaoxing Huang, Guiqing Deng, Qingqing Yan, Lin Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) is an inflammatory condition with potentially life-threatening complications. This study investigates the therapeutic potential of Clostridium butyricum for modulating the inflammatory cascade through the AMPK/NF-κB signaling pathway, focusing on inflammation induced by AP. LC-MS analysis of serum samples from AP patients highlighted the regulation of lipid metabolism and inflammation, and found that metabolites involved in the inhibition of NF-κB phosphorylation and the AMPK activation pathway were downregulated. We hypothesized that pre-administration of Clostridium butyricum and its culture supernatant could mitigate AP-induced damage by modulating the AMPK/NF-κB pathway.MethodsLipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cell inflammation models. LPS combined with CAE induced acute pancreatitis in mice. We divided mice into four groups: Con, AP, AP + C.Buty (AP with Clostridium butyricum treatment), and AP + CFS (AP with culture supernatant treatment). Analyses were performed using WB, RT-qPCR, Elisa, flow cytometry, IHC, and HE, respectively.ResultsOur study shows that CFS can reduce the apoptosis of LPS-induced cellular inflammation and reduce the release of LPS-induced cytoinflammatory factors through the AMPK/NF-κB pathway in vitro. In vivo, Clostridium butyricum and its supernatant significantly reduced inflammatory markers, and corrected histopathological alterations in AP mice. Gut microbiota analysis further supported these results, showing that Clostridium butyricum and its supernatant could restore the balance of intestinal flora disrupted by AP.ConclusionsMechanistically, our results indicated that the therapeutic effects of Clostridium butyricum are mediated through the activation of AMPK, leading to the inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Clostridium butyricum and its culture supernatant exert a protective effect against AP-induced damage by modulating the AMPK/NF-κB signaling pathway. Future studies will further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of Clostridium butyricum in AP and explore its clinical applicability in human subjects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05764-w.Keywords: Acute Pancreatitis, Clostridium butyricum, AMPK/NF-κB signaling, Inflammation, Gut microbiota, LC-MS

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common inflammatory disease that can be life threatening in severe cases. AP leads to damage to the intestinal barrier, which can result in the penetration of pathogenic substances, such as pathogenic bacteria and their derivatives, into the systemic circulation. Gram-negative bacteria are a group of bacteria that often cause health problems and are resistant to antibiotics. Translocation of pathogenic substances explains the presence of large amounts of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the plasma of patients with severe pancreatitis. LPS is a major component of the cell wall of gram-negative bacteria. It is usually present in the blood at very low levels, and when many gram-negative bacteria enter the bloodstream, LPS is elevated in the blood, binding to toll like receptor 4 (TLR4) on cells, leading to myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88)-mediated activation of the inflammatory cytokine cascade. MyD88 is involved in TLR-triggered inflammation, and the cascade after LPS stimulates TLRs leads to upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors, including interleukin 6 (IL-6) and interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β) [1–3]. During AP, the release of a large number of inflammatory factors can further promote the activation of associated proteases, which in turn accelerate the necrosis of pancreatic acinar cells [4–6].

Under normal intestinal physiology, commensal flora interacts well with the host immune system [7]. Certain gut microbes have been shown to inhibit inflammation. Studies have shown that Clostridium butyricum and Bacillus subtilis can repair damaged structures of the TJS and increase the expression levels of occludinand ZO-1, thereby regulating intestinal mucosal barrier function and alleviating DSS-induced colitis [8, 9]. Studies have been conducted on the gavage of mouse models of IBD with a culture supernatant of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and it was found that the supernatant acts on the differentiation stage of Th17 cells by inhibiting Th17/IL-17 signaling to produce anti-inflammatory effects [10]. The study of Quevrin et al. also found that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can secrete a 15 kDa protein that inhibits the function of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway [11], which confirms that the secreted protein in the culture supernatant of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can alleviate colon inflammation in dinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (DNBS) model mice to a certain extent. The effect of intestinal bacteria or bacterial secretion products, which have a better anti-inflammatory effect during the course of the disease, is worth exploring.

Inflammation is known to reduce AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity through multiple mechanisms, and signaling activation of AMPK can improve inflammation by downregulating NF-κB function through multiple signaling targets [12–16]. Activation of AMPK has been shown to have a potential therapeutic effect in animal models of colitis and pneumonia [17] Banskota, S et al., in a study for the treatment of colitis, found that AMPKβ1 deletion promotes NF-κB nuclear translocation, thereby upregulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [18]. Multiple studies have shown that activation of AMPK can inhibit the activation of the lps-induced NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing the production of NF-κB-dependent pro-inflammatory cytokines in RAW 264.7 cells [19–21].

In our study, we observed a significant decrease in the abundance of Clostridium butyricum in animal models of AP. Furthermore, prior administration of Clostridium butyricum and butyrate partially mitigated the damage induced by AP and reduced the release of inflammatory factors in these models. Clostridium butyricum is a component of the normal intestinal flora in humans and has been shown to play a crucial role in maintaining the balance of intestinal microbiota under physiological conditions [22]. Additionally, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) serves as a key regulator of glycolipid metabolism. Clostridium butyricum has the potential to alleviate obesity, insulin resistance, and fat inflammation associated with high-fat diets by modulating intestinal inflammation and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity. It is particularly interesting to investigate whether Clostridium butyricum or its secreted products can activate AMPK in the context of AP.

Combined with the findings of Quevrain et al., anti-inflammatory proteins have been shown to exert anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, and other effects in the culture supernatant of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [11]. We hypothesize that Clostridium butyricum and its culture supernatant products can inhibit damage to AP inflammation by activating AMPK to modulate the NF-κB pathway in a model of AP mimicking LPS translocation.

Materials and methods

Human subjects

This study enrolled 58 patients (Atlanta criteria revised in 2012) who received AP treatment at Guangzhou First People’s Hospital between June 2020 and June 2022. In addition, 31 healthy controls (no relevant medical history) were included in the study. Participant stool samples were stored at -80 °C. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of South China University of Technology.

Chemicals and reagents

The Clostridium butyricum was obtained from Shanghai Qingdayingke Medical Technology Co., Ltd. Reinforced clostridial medium (Beijing Land Bridge Technology Co., Ltd). LPS (Sigma, SMB00704), cerulein (APEBIO, B8465), and total protein extraction kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were used. Compound C (Dorsomorphin, BML-275,MCE). AMPK-α (Affinity, AF6423), Phospho-AMPK alpha (Affinity, AF3423). TLR4 (Immunoway, YT0744), MyD88 (Cell-Signaling, #4283), NF-κB p65 (Cell-Signaling, #8242), p-NF-κB p65 (Cell-Signaling, #3033), COX-2 (Signalway, #33345-2), β-actin (Affinty, AF7018). IL-6 (Affinty, DF6087), IL-1β (Affinty, AF5103), p-NF-κB p65 (Affinty, AF2006), ZO-1 (Affinty, AF5145), Occludin (Affinty, DF7504). HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit (Danvers, MA, USA). IL-1β (cat.no. MM-0040M1, MEIMIAN), IL-6 (MM-0163M1, MEIMIAN). Anti-Human/Mouse CD11b-FITC (eBioscience™, 35-0112-U100), Anti-Mouse CD14-FITC (eBioscience™, 11-00141-82), Flow apoptosis kit (Annexin-V-APC/7-AAD, AP105-100-kit).

Clostridium butyricum and Cell-free culture supernatant preparation

The Clostridium butyricum was cultured in Reinforced Clostridial Medium at 37 °C for 30 h in an anaerobic environment. After that, Clostridium butyricum were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in saline. The bacterial suspension was calibrated to 3 × 108 CFU/mL by turbidimetry. Cell-free culture supernatant (CFS) was obtained from cultures of Clostridium butyricum. After incubating Clostridium butyricum in an anaerobic environment at 37 °C for 72 h, the supernatant was collected via centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant components were then concentrated using ultrafiltration centrifuge tubes.

Cells

Cell lines were purchased from Guangzhou elgbio Co., Ltd. CCK-8 experiments were performed to determine the cytotoxicity of the CFS. Briefly, after stable passages, cells were evenly distributed in a 96-well plate, and after reaching 40% confluence, they were treated with different concentrations of CFS. After 24, 48, and 72 h, the medium was changed, CCK-8 was added, and cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader and the corresponding cell viability was calculated. After determining the therapeutic concentration range, cells were stably passaged evenly into a 6-well plate, and after reaching 60% confluence, they were treated with different concentrations of supernatant for 24 h. Cells and cell supernatants were collected after 3 h of incubation with LPS.

Flow cytometry

Mouse plasma was collected, and Fc receptor-binding inhibitors were added to block non-specific Fc receptor-mediated non-specific binding. The fluorescent direct standard antibody was added to the cells according to the amount used, mixed well, incubated in the dark for 30 min, and the red blood cell lysate was added. After lysis, the pellet was collected and resuspended in a centrifuge to assess fluorescence.

The cell pellet was collected after enzyme digestion, washed three times with PBS, stained with the Flow Apoptosis Kit, and fluorescence was assessed. The data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. The cells were then blocked for 1 h with 5% BSA, incubated with the antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then incubated with secondary antibodies the next day. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. The stained cells were imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using a total protein extraction kit and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA method. In brief, 20 µg protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA in TBST buffer for 90 min and then incubated with the following antibodies (1:1000 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. After extensive rinsing with TBST, the blots were incubated with secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody at 23 ± 2 °C for 90 min, and the immunoreactive bands were imaged using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP. Protein bands were quantified by densitometry using the Image Lab software.

ELISA assay

The levels of IL-1β and IL-6 were measured by ELISA kits. The levels of triglycerides, serum amylase, and total cholesterol in mouse serum samples were detected using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., LTD).

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Cells were lysed using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for total RNA extraction. Total RNA (5 µg) was reverse transcribed was carried out using TransScript® Uni One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Transgen), and qPCR assays were performed in StepOnePlus Real Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgen). The levels were detected, and the relative mRNA levels were normalized to β-actin using the ΔΔCt method. The primers were as follows:

IL-1β, forward, GAAATGCCACCTTTTGACAGTG,

IL-1β, reverse, TGGATGCTCTCATCAGGACAG,

IL-6, forward, CTGCAAGAGACTTCCATCCAG,

IL-6, reverse, AGTGGTATAGACAGGTCTGTTGG,

β-actin, forward, AGCGAGCATCCCCCAAAGTT,

β-actin, reverse, GGGCACGAAGGCTCATCATT.

Histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry analysis

Tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm. The sections were stained with H&E to distinguish the morphological structures of the normal and pathological tissues. The tissue sections were adhered to slides, dewaxed, and hydrated for antigen repair. Paraffin sections were treated with hydrogen peroxide (3 m/v) for 30 min. Nonspecific staining blockers were added and incubated at 23 ± 2 °C for 15 min. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:400 dilution). A biotin-labeled goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG secondary antibody was then added, followed by incubation at 23 ± 2 °C for 15 min. The slides were then incubated with peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin at 23 ± 2 °C for 15 min. Sections were stained with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Image-Pro Plus counted the IOD and Area values, and the AOD (IOD/Area) was used as the strength score of immune reactivity.

Animals

C57 mice (♂, 3–5 weeks old, weighing 16–22 g) were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co. LTD. The rats were maintained on a 12-hourlight/12-hour-dark cycle at 22 °C, given a standard laboratory diet and water. All rats were divided into four groups, the control group (Con, 100 µL 0.9% saline solution, n = 9), the model group (AP, was established via the injection of caerulein, 50 µg/kg for 7 days, 12 h before anesthesia, LPS was injected at a dose of 10 mg/kg, n = 9), the Clostridium butyricum group (AP + C.Buty, before AP modeling, every two days by gastric 3 × 108 CFU/mL Clostridium butyricum, 100 µL per mouse, n = 9), the Cell-free culture supernatant group (AP + CFS, before AP modeling, once every two days by gastric 500 µg/mL Clostridium butyricum supernatant, 100 µL per mouse, n = 9). The blood samples, pancreatic tissues and ileum tissue of the above animals were collected to analysis.

Gut microbiota analysis

DNA was extracted from fecal samples using a QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, MD, USA) and quantified using Nanodrop. DNA sequencing was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). Amplify the V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using specific primers. The PCR amplification recovered products were quantified by fluorescence using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit and the Microplate reader (BioTek, FLx800). Sequencing libraries were prepared using an Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit. After quality inspection of the library at the Agilent Bioanalyzer using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit, the gradient of each qualified on-machine sequencing library was diluted, mixed in proportion according to the required sequencing amount, and denatured into single strands using NaOH for on-machine sequencing.

LC-MS analysis

Sample were extracted using a 400 µL methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) solution. The mixture then sonicated at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5 °C. Proteins are precipitated and dried for pretreatment before loading. The instrument platform for LC-MS analysis is UHPLC-Q Exactive system of Thermo Fisher Scientific. 2 µL of sample was separated by HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm) and then entered into mass spectrometry detection. After the mass spectrometry detection is completed, the raw data of LC/MS is preprocessed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software. The metabolites were searched and identified, and the main database was the HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/) and Majorbio Database. Differential metabolites among two groups were summarized, and mapped into their biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis based on database search (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/).

LC-QE analysis

Protein peptide samples were digested using endonuclease (Trypsin) and then analyzed using LCMSMS (nanoLC-QE). Finally, the LCMSMS data were analyzed using MASCOT to obtain qualitative identification information for the protein peptide molecule of interest.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software version 7. Differences between three or more groups were analyzed by One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s tests. p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All data were repeated independently at least three times.

Composition of microbial diversity

There were differences in the microbial community composition of the Con and AP groups in the corresponding dimensions (Fig. 1A), indicating that AP changed the microbial community composition of the human intestine. Under the premise of sufficient sequencing volume in this study, the Chao 1 and observed species indices of the AP group were reduced compared with those of the Con group (Fig. 1B). The threshold filter condition p < 0.05 was used as the Welch’s t-test, and the correspondence analysis of “species-gene-phenotype” was carried out. The results showed that gram-negative bacteria and potential pathogenic bacteria were increased in the AP group (Fig. 1C). We further examined the intestinal flora structure at the level of the phylum, with Bacteroides, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria being the dominant flora in the samples (Fig. 1D). As the figure shown that the proportion of Proteobacteria in the AP group is significantly higher than that in the Con group. Amplification of the Proteobacteria phylum causing endotoxemia and inflammatory necrosis, is considered a characteristic of intestinal dysbacteriosis, with inflammation being common amplification factors. The intestinal flora in the human body regulates the synthesis of host fat by affecting the absorption and metabolism of intestinal energy. Based on the PICRUSt function prediction, the KEGG functional abundance of the samples showed that the genes of lipid metabolism, cell growth and death in the AP group should have higher (enrichment) ability (Fig. 1E). Abnormal lipid metabolism and cell necrosis are typical features of AP. The ratio of Bacteroidetes to Firmicutes is presumed to be a potential biomarker for disease or health. The F/B ratio was significantly lower in the AP group than in the control group (Fig. 1F). Clostridium butyricum levels in the AP group were significantly lower than those in the Con group (Fig. 1G).

Effects of CFS on LPS-induced inflammation

Elevated levels of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are known to cause abnormalities in the Toll-like receptors (TLRs) signaling pathway. TLR4 is the well-established classical receptor for LPS. The upregulation of TLR4 triggers the activation of the MyD88 cascade and NF-κB pathway. In order to demonstrate the correlation between LPS and the clinical manifestations of acute pancreatitis (AP), we utilized immunohistochemistry (IHC) to assess the expression of TLR4 in a cohort of human tissue chips from AP patients (Fig. 3A). The findings revealed a significant increase in TLR4 expression levels in the acute pancreatitis group compared to the control group. This observation further reinforces the role of LPS as an inflammatory activator in our subsequent experiments. To further investigate the effects of CFS on immune cell function, we examined whether CFS stimulation affected RAW264.7 cells. The p-p65 expression of RAW264.7 cells were reached the peak level after 20 µg/mL of LPS treatment for 3 h and then gradually decreased (Fig. 3B). At a 24-hour exposure time and concentrations ranging from 32 to 128 μg/mL, CFS demonstrated effects on RAW264.7 cells that varied from non-cytotoxic to cytotoxic (Fig. 3C). At the same time, RAW264.7 immune the morphology of the cells changed under LPS activation, while CFS reversed the morphological changes in these cells (Fig. 3D). AMPK phosphorylation levels were down-regulated by LPS, but after treatment with CFS, p-AMPK levels were increased and NF-KB phosphorylation was inhibited (Fig. 3E). Western blotting confirmed that 32 µg/mL CFS downregulated the LPS-induced levels of phosphorylation of NF-κB (Fig. 3E). Moreover, we found that the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in the supernatant of the model group were significantly higher than those in the control group, whereas CFS treatment inhibited the increase in the levels of these inflammatory factors, as revealed by ELISA (Fig. 3F). Correspondingly, our RT-qPCR analysis showed that LPS upregulated the level of IL-1β, a proinflammatory mediator in RAW264.7 cells, compared with that in the control group. However, its effect on IL-6 transcript levels was not as expected (Fig. 3G). Based on the above results, we concluded that CFS may inhibit the activation of macrophages and regulate inflammation.

Effects of CFS intervention on AR42J and MPC83 cells

We tested p-p65 expression in AR42J and MPC83 cells treated with LPS for different time periods. The results indicated that p-p65 expression of AR42J and MPC83 cells were reached the peak level after 10 µg/mL of LPS treatment for 3 h and then gradually decreased (Fig. 4A-a and B-a). To sum up, we chose 10 µg/mL LPS treatment for 3 h as the action condition for subsequent inflammation induction. The CCK-8 test results showed that the cytotoxicity of CFS increased in a concentration-dependent manner. Depending on the dose, the effects of CFS on tested cells ranged from noncytotoxic to cytotoxic (Fig. 4A-b and B-b). Therefore, we selected three concentration gradients (CFS-L: 16 µg/mL, CFS-M: 64 µg/mL, CFS-H: 256 µg/mL) as the therapeutic concentrations for subsequent experiments. An increase in cell necrosis leads to the release of more inflammatory factors. Our flow cytometry analysis showed that compared with the control group, inflammation-induced model group had significantly increased early and late apoptosis. Conversely, the level of apoptosis showed a downward trend after CFS intervention (Fig. 4A-c and B-c). This suggested that CFS could reduce cell necrosis during inflammation to some extent. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the levels of COX-2 (the classic marker of inflammation) in AR42J and MPC83 cells in the CFS-treated group were lower than those in the LPS-induced model group (Fig. 4C and D), but CFS failed to reduce the expression of MyD88. This suggests that CFS inhibits the expression of inflammatory cytokines through other pathways.

Clostridium butyricum and CFS attenuate the inflammatory response in LPS/CAE-induced AP mouse model

The construction process of the AP mouse model is shown in Fig. 6A. The survival rate of the model was between 60% and 70% (Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared to the Con group, the mice in the AP group that received LPS/CAE alone experienced weight loss during the model induction period (Fig. 6B). Notably, the AP + C. Buty and AP + CFS groups did not show a trend of weight loss during LPS/CAE induction.Staining showed that the acinar cells in the control group were normal, and the pancreatic tissue cells were arranged tightly and in an orderly manner. However, the cells induced by LPS/CAE (Lipopolysaccharide/Cerulein) in the model group showed pathological changes such as inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, irregular cell outline, and loose arrangement. In the Clostridium butyricum suspension and cell-free culture supernatant intervention groups, the appearance of pancreatic tissue acinar cells was significantly normal compared with that in the AP group. Most of the cells had regular outlines, and pathological changes such as necrosis and damage were significantly reduced (Fig. 6C).

The results of serum-related diagnostic indices of AP showed that the levels of triglycerides, serum amylase, and total cholesterol in the model group were higher than those in the Con group (p < 0.001), while the results showed a downward trend in the C. Buty and CFS intervention groups (Fig. 6D). Macrophages are widely involved in immune responses. Commonly used macrophage markers include CD11b and CD14. We found that the levels of CD11b and CD14 in the AP group were higher than those in the Con group. Conversely, the levels of CD11b and CD14 in the serum of mice in the AP + C.Buty group were relatively lower than those in the AP group. Subsequent detection of inflammatory factors associated with serum showed that IL-6 and IL-1β expression levels in the AP group were elevated (p < 0.01) compared to the Con group. However, the expression level of IL-1β did not change significantly in the AP + CFS group. In summary, C.Buty was able to slow LPS/CAE-induced acute inflammation in mouse models. The therapeutic effect of C.Buty has also been reflected in the detection of serum diagnostic markers for acute pancreatitis (Fig. 6E).

As shown in Fig. 6F, phosphorylation levels of AMPK were down-regulated after inflammation induction, but increased after Clostridium butyricum or CFS treatment. The expression levels of MyD88 and p-p65 were higher in the AP group than Con group. The expression levels of p-p65 were decreased in the group pretreated with Clostridium butyricum or its supernatant, while the expression levels of MyD88 were no significant difference. Subsequent detection of inflammatory factors associated with pancreatic tissue showed that IL-6 and IL-1β expression levels in the AP group were elevated (p < 0.01) compared to the Con group. IL-6 and IL-1β protein expression levels in the AP + C.Buty groups were lower than the AP group. However, the expression level of IL-1β did not change significantly in the AP + CFS group. As shown in Fig. 6G, western blotting detection with pancreatic tissue showed that Clostridium butyricum increased the expression level of p-AMPK (p < 0.05). MyD88 and p-p65 expression levels in the AP group were elevated (p < 0.001) compared to the Con group. The expression levels of p-p65 (p < 0.01) were decreased in the group pretreated with Clostridium butyricum. However, there was no significant difference in the expression level of MyD88, p-AMPK and p-p65 in the supernatant group. IHC and WB results suggest that the effect of Clostridium butyricum in reduced AP inflammation may be by participating in the AMPK/NF-κB signaling pathway to slow the inflammatory response in mouse of AP. RT-qPCR was used to detect the transcription levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in pancreatic tissues (Fig. 6H).

Compound C attenuated the protective effect of Clostridium butyricum on AP mice

Since the effect of CFS in vivo did not achieve the desired effect, in this part of the experiment, we inhibited AMPK on the AP model pretreated by Clostridium butyricum to verify that Clostridium butyricum plays a protective role through AMPK (Fig. 8A). As shown in the Fig. 8B, Clostridium butyricum effectively reduced the levels of TG, TC and AMY in the serum of mice, but Compound C attenuated the reduction effect of Clostridium butyricum on the above components. The HE results were consistent with the above, and the pancreatic histiocytes after AP induction were loosely arranged and irregularly contoured, and vacuoles were present in all groups (Fig. 8C). IHC results (Fig. 8D) showed that Compound C attenuated the effect of Clostridium butyricum on upregulating p-AMPK, and correspondingly, the levels of IL-6 and IL-1β increased. Western blotting (Fig. 8E) of pancreatic tissue were consistent with the results of IHC, and Compound C down-regulated the level of p-AMPK. The up-regulation of p-p65 further indicates that the protective effect of Clostridium butyricum on the AP model is related to AMPK. More experiments are needed to verify the specific mechanism of action. RT-qPCR was used to detect the transcription levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in pancreatic tissues (Fig. 8F).

A limitation of the current study is that we have not been able to isolate Clostridium butyricum strains from healthy humans and scale up culture for experiments. We identified the relevant components from the culture supernatant from this study to further speculate the effect of Clostridium butyricum on AP. Discussion

The occurrence of inflammation stimulated by LPS and caerulein can largely mimic the pathogenesis of AP. Caerulein is a gastric regulatory molecule similar in function and composition to cholecystokinin, which induces pancreatitis by dysregulating digestive enzyme secretion, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and pancreatic edema. The IHC results (Supplementary Fig. 1) further support the idea that changes in gram-negative bacteria lead to LPS translocation in this study. However, this study have not attempted to isolate Proteobacteria in this study. It would be interesting if used the human isolates and tested whether they activate the TLR4/MyD88 axis for regulating the inflammatory response. After confirming that Clostridium butyricum and CFS have no obvious toxic effects on healthy mice (Supplementary Fig. 3), we conducted our studies from the LPS-mediated TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB classical inflammatory pathway. In vivo experiments, consistent with in vitro studies, Clostridium butyricum inhibited the p65 phosphorylation and production of pro-inflammatory factors in pancreatic tissue. Simultaneously, both Clostridium butyricum and CFS upregulated the expression levels of ZO-1 and occludin proteins (Supplementary Fig. 4), correcting histopathological changes and intestinal flora disorders during AP.

Dysregulation of the proportion of Proteobacteria can cause inflammation and changes in the gut microbiota [23], and the results of our animal experiments are consistent with the results of 16 s in clinical samples, and the proportion of Proteobacteria in the AP group increases. Clostridium butyricum or CFS intervention can reverse this alteration, altering the microbiota disturbance to levels close to those of the control group. Local containment and maintenance of microbial ecosystems in the host are great challenges for the mammalian innate immune system [24]. Many inflammatory diseases, including metabolic, autoimmune, and autoinflammatory diseases, are affected by changes in the crosstalk between innate immunity and the microbiome [25, 26]. It has been used in many fields to protect intestinal barrier function by modulating the structure of the intestinal flora, thereby inhibiting related signaling pathways and alleviating disease damage. Supplementation with bifidobacteria can increase antioxidant levels [27], lower cholesterol [28], relieve the body’s inflammatory response [29], and treat gastrointestinal diseases. Lachnospiraceae is involved in the production of short-chain fatty acids and maintenance of intestinal immune homeostasis [30, 31]. Lactic acid bacterial supplementation may reduce pancreatic sepsis in patients with AP [32]. Although many studies have shown that Clostridium butyricum is effective in mitigating inflammatory damage, most have focused on probiotic supplementation to maintain intestinal barrier homeostasis. The association of gut microbial metabolites with pathogenesis has been increasingly reported [33]. Intestinal microbe-derived nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) can alleviate the severity of AP by activating the SIRT3-PRDX5 pathway. NMN exerts its protective effects by converting into NAD+, which further reduces mitochondrial protein acetylation and improves mitochondrial function. Patients with hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis (HTGP) exhibit a downregulated abundance of intestinal Bacteroides uniformis (B.uniformis), leading to a decrease in the level of its metabolite taurine. Taurine exerts protective effects on HTGP by inhibiting the NF-κB and IL-17 signaling pathways and reducing the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in the pancreas. Metabolites of gram-positive bacteria in the intestine can significantly inhibit the secretion of cytoinflammatory factors induced by Matrix metalloproteinases. There is also an immunoregulatory mechanism between SCFAs and a common matrix metalloproteinase, and the administration of SCFAs alone or related gut microbial metabolites to maintain intestinal homeostasis and mitigate disease damage has become a promising preventive and therapeutic strategy [34].

Multiple experimental models of pancreatitis have shown that NF-κB activation correlates with the severity of AP in mice, with NF-κB being activated early in acinar cells and inducing the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory genes during AP [35]. In this study, we found that the NF-κB pathway was activated in the acinar cell inflammation panel. However, CFS inhibited the expression of p-NF-κB p65 and pro-inflammatory factors during acinar expression. In vivo experiments further showed that Clostridium butyricum or CFS inhibited the expression of NF-κB pathway-related proteins in pancreatic tissues. However, this inhibition does not pass through the MyD88 pathway. Mechanistically, CFS can also activate activated protein kinase (AMPK) and has shown good anti-inflammatory effects in vivo and in vitro. Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key kinase in the lipid metabolism pathway. AMPK coordinates lipid metabolism through transcriptional regulation of de novo fat synthesis and fatty acid oxidation factors. Activation of AMPK Thr172 phosphorylation reduces adipogenesis or decreases adipose activity, such as fatty acid synthase and sterol regulatory element-binding proteins [36]. AMPK can inhibit the activation of NF-κB and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors in a variety of cell types [37–42]. Inflammatory stimulation and fatty acid-rich diets can reduce the expression and activity of AMPK in adipose tissue and macrophages in mice, and trigger the expression of inflammatory factors [43]. Therefore, AMPK (Thr172) phosphorylation is an effective way to attenuate NF-κB inflammatory activation.

AMPK activity is inversely correlated with obesity/inflammation [44, 45]. The activity of AMPK is regulated by the availability of nutrients such as carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids. Levels of p-AMPK are altered in a rat model of caerulein and L-arginine-induced pancreatitis, with higher levels of pancreatic expression in the mild pancreatitis group compared to the induced severe model [46, 47]. Nutritional overnutrition, inflammation and certain anabolic hormones can inhibit the activity of AMPK, further affecting the normal metabolism and apoptosis of cells and aggravating the inflammatory response. Therefore, we assessed the levels of metabolites associated with lipid metabolism-inflammation through the testing of clinical serum samples, further clarifying the potential therapeutic role of Clostridium butyricum or CFS in influencing AP by compensating for the AMPK pathway. It has been reported that the deoxycholic acid (DCA), quinic acid (QA), or phenylalanine synthesis pathways involved in the inhibition of NF-κB phosphorylation have all been down-regulated in the AP group [48–50]. Among them, palmitoleic acid, which has been clearly associated with the activation of AMPK phosphorylation, was significantly reduced in the AP group [51]. The activation of AMPK triggers peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPAR-α), and PPAR-α can inhibit the expression of pro-inflammatory genes by inactivating NF-κB [52], while linoleic acid and its synthesis pathway associated with the activation of PPAR signaling pathway are down-regulated in the AP group. These results suggest that the exacerbation of inflammation in AP is associated with a decrease in the AMPK activation pathway. Therefore, compensation for the AMPK activation pathway may be an effective strategy to ameliorate AP. However, the current mechanism basis for the regulation of AP by AMPK/NF-κB is still insufficient. Further consideration of the number and source of clinical acute pancreatitis samples is necessary to enhance confidence in the mechanistic predictions. Notably, the analysis of this study revealed that pancreatic cancer metabolites were downregulated in the acute pancreatitis (AP) group. A cohort study conducted in Sweden, which included 25,000 patients with acute pancreatitis, reported no association between acute pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer after more than 10 years of follow-up [53]. This finding contradicts some studies that have suggested a risk of pancreatic cancer in patients with pancreatitis. Several studies conducted in Europe and the United States, which did not report varying follow-up durations, have indicated that acute pancreatitis may indeed be a risk factor for pancreatic cancer [54, 55]. In a nationwide population-based matched cohort study in Denmark, an association was identified between the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis and the long-term risk of developing pancreatic cancer [56]. This underscores the necessity of establishing a long-term and comprehensive follow-up system to ensure a high-precision study population, while also addressing enrollment conditions and classifications, particularly concerning confounding factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption that may obscure the association between acute pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer.

The change in intracellular S-AMP concentration is not only controlled by the level of cellular metabolism, but also related to exogenous supplementation. In the transaminogenesis of protein catabolism in organisms, the amino acids generated by decomposition are synthesized by Adss to synthesize adenyly-based succinic acid, and adenylylate-based succinic acid is further cleaved to generate AMP, and AMP activates AMPK to regulate related biological functions in the body. Morbid obesity is a risk factor for severe AP [57, 58]. During AP, lipid accumulation can enhance the activation of NF-κB-induced inflammatory responses. There are three main pathways of fat catabolism, enzymatic hydrolysis of fat, degradation or conversion of glycerol into key substances for sugar metabolism, and β-oxidative decomposition of fatty acids. β-oxidation is the most important pathway for fatty acid oxidation, and deficiency of 3-hydroxyyl-CoA dehydrogenase leads to fatty acid oxidation disorders [59]. Damage to mitochondrial fatty acids β-oxidized enzymes/transporters can alter the lipid profile of patients, and alterations in lipid profile can affect the function of cells/organs [60–62]. Lipids are important key biomolecules in human physiology. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism and profiling has been implicated in the pathophysiology of a variety of diseases. We believe that exploring the regulatory mechanism of Clostridium butyricum from the perspective of fatty acid metabolism needs to be further explored in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Clostridium butyricum can mitigate the inflammatory damage associated with pancreatitis in mice via the AMPK/NF-κB pathway. These findings underscore the potential of microbial components and their metabolites to modulate inflammatory pathways and restore intestinal homeostasis. However, the supernatant product of Clostridium butyricum did not yield the anticipated results in in vivo experiments, indicating the need for further optimization of the dosing method or the selection of human isolates for culture. In summary, Clostridium butyricum may alleviate the inflammatory processes mediated by NF-κB by compensating for the dysregulation of AMPK phosphorylation in acute pancreatitis (AP) (Fig. 9), which could facilitate the exploration of potential treatments for AP.
