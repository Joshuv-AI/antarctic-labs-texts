Periplocin targets low density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 and protects against osteoarthritis
Authors: Jincheng Liu, Qingyang Fu, Chunlin Li, Kun Gao, Mengsi Sun, Mingyu Xu, Junfei Chen, Xu Zhai, Wencan Zhang, Le Li, Wanlong Xu, Yang Liu, Xinhui Wu, Haipeng Si, Kaidi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is recognized as the most common chronic joint disease, characterized primarily by the degeneration of articular cartilage. Through screening a natural product library, we identified periplocin as a candidate agent capable of promoting chondrocyte anabolism. Periplocin, a cardiotonic steroid isolated from Periploca forrestii, has been proven to be involved in antiinflammatory responses. However, the role and mechanism of periplocin in OA have not been confirmed. This study aims to explore the biological role and potential of periplocin in chondrocyte metabolism and OA treatment. A drug library was screened to identify candidate components that promote chondrocyte anabolism. In vitro experiments showed that periplocin could promote chondrocyte anabolism, enhance chondrogenic differentiation, and inhibit inflammation-induced chondrocyte catabolism. Using a surgically induced destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) OA mice model, combined with histological staining, micro-computed tomography, and behavioral assessments, we confirmed that periplocin alleviated cartilage degeneration, suppressed osteophyte formation, improved motor function, and relieved OA-related pain in DMM mice. Molecular docking, Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) assay, Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA), and Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation identified LRP4 as the key target of periplocin, with Asp812 serving as its critical binding site. With the help of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to knock out low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4) in C28I2 and generate chondrocyte-specific Lrp4 knockout mice, the dependence of periplocin-mediated regulation of chondrocyte function on LRP4 was verified. This study not only provides new insights into understanding the chondroprotective effect of periplocin targeting LRP4 and its underlying mechanism but also offers experimental evidence for the use of periplocin as a potential disease-modifying OA drug (DMOAD) in OA treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00982-0.Keywords: Periplocin, Osteoarthritis, Chondrocyte, Catabolism, LRP4, MAPK

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), the most prevalent joint disease, represents a primary cause of disability in the elderly population [1]. OA imposes a substantial economic burden, as healthcare expenditures associated with the disease are estimated to constitute 1–2.5% of the gross national product across numerous high-income nations [2].

It is now widely recognized that OA is not an isolated cartilage disorder, but a progressive whole-joint disease involving pathological alterations of all intra-articular and periarticular tissues [3]. Beyond the well-documented progressive degradation of articular cartilage, which is regarded as the hallmark and central pathological event of OA [4, 5], the disease is characterized by concurrent meniscal degeneration [6], aberrant subchondral bone remodeling and sclerosis [7], chronic synovial membrane inflammation [8], and progressive biochemical and biomechanical changes of the infrapatellar fat pad [9], all of which collectively drive the initiation and progression of OA.

Physiologically, articular cartilage serves as the load-bearing structure of the synovial joint, exhibiting unique viscoelastic and shock-absorbing biomechanical properties to withstand cyclic compressive and shear forces during joint movement [10]. The homeostatic function and metabolic phenotype of chondrocytes are tightly regulated by physiological mechanical cues, which maintain the dynamic balance between ECM synthesis and degradation [11]. In the context of OA, progressive disruption of the type II collagen fibril network and loss of aggrecan from the cartilage matrix lead to a marked deterioration of the tissue’s biomechanical properties, including reduced compressive modulus, impaired energy dissipation capacity, and increased tissue fragility [12, 13]. Meanwhile, aberrant mechanical loading (including excessive joint loading, traumatic injury, or altered joint biomechanics due to meniscal damage) disrupts chondrocyte mechanotransduction, driving a phenotypic shift from a homeostatic state to a catabolic and proinflammatory state [14, 15]. This biomechanical disturbance further exacerbates ECM degradation, forming a vicious cycle between mechanical property deterioration, chondrocyte metabolic imbalance, and progressive cartilage destruction [16, 17].

Articular cartilage is an avascular and nonself-regenerating tissue, whose structural integrity depends on a highly specialized extracellular matrix (ECM). This matrix is sustained through finely regulated feedback mechanisms orchestrated by sparsely distributed chondrocytes, which synthesize key ECM constituents such as type II collagen and aggrecan [5, 18]. Mounting evidence suggests that OA is not merely a passive “wear and tear” disorder, but rather an active dysregulation of joint tissue homeostasis, characterized by an imbalance between destructive and reparative processes [19, 20]. Therefore, alongside well-established catabolic events, including elevated metalloproteinase activity and cartilage erosion, attention is increasingly directed toward anabolic pathways and adaptive ECM remodeling in OA [20, 21]. Furthermore, emerging studies indicate the presence of stem/progenitor cells within articular cartilage that contribute to tissue homeostasis and repair, these cells are thought to play a role in matrix remodeling under pathological conditions [22, 23]. The development of OA may result from an imbalance between the anabolism and catabolism of the cartilage matrix, and this also serves as a core entry point for understanding the pathogenesis of OA and identifying therapeutic targets.

Despite decades of research, a critical unmet medical need persists, no disease-modifying OA drugs (DMOADs) have been approved to safely arrest or reverse OA progression [24]. Clinical management remains focused on symptomatic relief, functional improvement, and minimizing treatment-related adverse effects [25]. Consequently, the development of effective disease-modifying agents constitutes a critical and unmet medical need. The discovery of novel, cost-effective, and safe pharmacological candidates is therefore of considerable therapeutic interest. Guided by this objective, we screened a drug library and identified periplocin as a compound demonstrating potential efficacy against OA in preliminary assessments.

Natural products, particularly those derived from traditional medicinal plants, have long been a rich source of novel therapeutic agents owing to their favorable safety profiles, cost-effectiveness, and evolutionary conservation of biological activity [26–28]. Periplocin, a cardiac glycoside isolated from the root bark of Periploca cortex (a traditional Chinese medicine [TCM] known as “Xiangjiapi”), is one such natural product with a long history of medicinal use. In TCM theory, Periploca cortex has been traditionally prescribed to treat edema, and palpitations, leveraging its antiinflammatory, diuretic, and cardiotonic properties [29]. As a cardiac glycoside, periplocin exerts its cardiotonic effects by inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, thereby increasing intracellular calcium levels and enhancing myocardial contractility [30]. This mechanism underlies its role as a key active component of Qili Qiangxin Capsules. As a TCM formulation approved by the China National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for the treatment of chronic heart failure, Qili Qiangxin Capsules has demonstrated significant efficacy in improving cardiac function and reducing hospitalization rates, with a well-documented safety profile [31]. Besides its cardioprotective effects, accumulating studies have revealed the pleiotropic biological functions of periplocin in various pathophysiological processes, it promotes wound healing via Na+/K+-ATPase-mediated activation of the Src/ERK and PI3K/Akt pathways [32], it ameliorates age-related meibomian gland dysfunction in mice through upregulation of Na+/K+-ATPase via the SRC pathway, with an antiaging effect comparable to that of classic antiaging drugs [33, 34], recent studies have also shown that periplocin exerts an antiosteoporotic effect by inhibiting osteoclast differentiation [35]. Although periplocin potently retards the progression of a range of diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, inflammatory disorders, aging-related diseases, and bone metabolic diseases, no studies have investigated the effects and underlying mechanisms of periplocin on osteoarthritis to date.

Therefore, the aims of this study were to systematically explore the regulatory effect of periplocin on chondrocyte metabolic homeostasis and its chondroprotective effect in OA, to identify the direct molecular target of periplocin in chondrocytes, and to elucidate the downstream signaling mechanism underlying its pharmacological action. This study will provide new insights into the pharmacological effects of periplocin and experimental evidence for its development as a potential disease-modifying OA drug (DMOAD).

Methods

Cell culture and treatment

The C28I2 human chondrocyte cell line (#BFN60803901) was purchased from Qingqi (Shanghai, China) Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. The C28I2 human chondrocyte cell line was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 50 units per milliliter (U/mL) of penicillin, and 0.05 mg per milliliter (mg/mL) of streptomycin. The C3H/10T1/2 cell line (#CL-0325) was purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China. C3H10T1/2 mesenchymal stem cells were maintained in the Eagle’s Basal medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum to a final concentration of 10% and 50 U/mL penicillin and 0.05 mg/mL streptomycin. For primary mouse chondrocytes, these cells were isolated from neonatal mice aged 4–7 days. First, the neonatal mice were euthanized, and their body surfaces were sterilized using 75% ethanol. Next, microsurgical tools were employed to open the capsular tissue of the mice’s knee joints and excise the white, translucent articular cartilage. The harvested cartilage tissue was then digested overnight using 0.25% collagenase II. On the following day, any remaining tissue debris was removed by filtration, and the resulting chondrocytes were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum.

C28I2 human chondrocyte cell line was used for drug screening, dose-gradient efficacy screening, target validation, and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) transcriptomic analysis. This cell line, with a stable chondrocyte phenotype and uniform genetic background, is suitable for high-throughput screening and molecular mechanism experiments with high requirements for repeatability. Primary mouse chondrocytes were used for parallel verification of the regulatory effect of periplocin on chondrocyte anabolism and catabolism under inflammatory stimulation, as well as the cytotoxicity and safety evaluation of periplocin, to cross-validate the reliability of the experimental results from the C28I2 cell line.

Periplocin (purity > 99%, #HY-N1381) and Istaroxime hydrochloride (purity > 99%, #HY-15718A) were obtained from MedChemExpress and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). In cell experiments, the drug in each treatment group was dissolved and prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the control group was supplemented with an equal volume of DMSO, which was set as the vehicle control group at a drug concentration of 0 μM.

To further investigate whether periplocin regulates the biological process of chondrocytes via Na+/K+-ATPase, C28I2 human chondrocytes were treated with 10 μM periplocin in the presence or absence of 0.1 μM Istaroxime hydrochloride for 24 h. After the intervention, total RNA and total protein of C28I2 cells were extracted respectively, to detect the changes of anabolic and catabolic related markers of chondrocytes.

Drug screening

A drug library screen was performed using the mRNA expression level of Type II collagen in C28I2 human chondrocytes as the readout. Primary screen: 200 compounds (MedChemExpress, USA) were added to each well of 24-well plates seeded with C28I2 cells at a final concentration of 10 μM, with DMSO set as the control. Total RNA was extracted to detect the expression level of Type II collagen. Secondary screen: 15 compounds that could induce Type II collagen expression in C28I2 cells from the primary screen were selected for the secondary screen. C28I2 cells were treated with candidate compounds at gradient concentrations (0.5 μM, 1 μM, 10 μM) for 24 h. Tertiary screen: five compounds that induced Type II collagen expression in a dose-dependent manner from the secondary screen were selected for the tertiary screen. C28I2 cells were treated with Amikacin (hydrate), edotreotide, 8-chloroadenosine, periplocin, and oxytocin, respectively. The expression levels of anabolic marker genes were detected by qRT-PCR.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent (#15596026, Invitrogen, USA) was employed for the extraction of total RNA from both mouse cells and the cancellous bone tissue located at the distal femur. Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction assays were carried out in accordance with the experimental procedures detailed in a prior study [36]. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal reference gene, and the relative expression levels of target genes were normalized using the comparative CT (ΔΔCT) method. Information regarding the specific primers utilized in this experiment is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Chondrogenesis

C3H10T1/2 cells were cultured in micromass form and seeded at the center of 24-well plates. The plates were then placed in an incubator set at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the cells were allowed to incubate for 3 h. Once the cells formed aggregates, chondrogenic medium was supplemented into each well. Subsequently, chondrogenic induction was performed, and the induction process was maintained for either 7 days or 14 days. After the specified induction periods, the cells were subjected to Alcian blue staining (#G1560, Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

C28I2 cell or primary mice chondrocytes suspension was seeded in 96-well-culture plates (BIOFIL, Guangzhou, China) at a density of 105 cells/mL containing 100 μL of complete medium and incubated for 24 h. Cells were treated with various concentrations of periplocin (0, 0.1, 1, 5, and 10 μM) (#HY-N1381, MedChemExpress, USA). Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 (#E-CK-A362, Elabscience, China) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to assess cell proliferation. Subsequently, optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm (Bio-Rad, USA).

Chondrocyte micromass culture and Alcian blue staining

C3H/10T1/2 cells were resuspended in growth medium, and the viable cell density was adjusted to 2.5 × 107 cells/mL. Subsequently, micromass inoculation was performed: 20 μL of the above cell suspension was dropped into a single well of a 24-well plate (#TCP010024, Jet Biofil, China) to establish the micromass system. Cells were first allowed to adhere by static incubation for 3 h in the absence of medium, after which growth medium was gently added to the wells, followed by continued static incubation for 24 h. During the induction culture period, complete medium replacement was performed with fresh chondrogenic induction medium every 3 days, with care taken to avoid touching or disturbing the cell micromasses during medium change. The total induction cycle was 14 days. After the completion of induction, the cell micromasses were collected for subsequent Alcian blue staining. The chondrogenic induction medium (#HUXXC-90041, OriCell, China) was purchased from Cyagen.

Experimental post-traumatic OA in mice

All animal studies were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Shandong University (approval no. 24016, date: 24 April 2024). We confirm that all methods used in this study are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal experiments. Every aspect of the experiment design, including ethical approval, animal care, randomization, blinding, and statistical analysis, as been documented and presented in a manner consistent with the guides to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and the ethical use of animals in research. C57BL/6 mice (male, 3 months old, 6 mice per group) were purchased from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed under the same specific pathogen-free standard environmental conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle), with free access to food and allowed to move freely. Destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery was performed to induce post-traumatic OA, following the methods described in prior research [37].

Drug administration

The animal experimental groups included the sham, DMM, DMM (low-dose periplocin, 1 mg/kg), and DMM (high-dose periplocin, 5 mg/kg) groups, each having six male C57BL/6 J mice (8-week-old, weighing 20 ± 0.10 g). In the treatment group receiving different doses of periplocin (1 mg/kg or 5 mg/kg), the compound was injected intraperitoneally at appropriate doses, and the DMM group was given the same dose of normal saline mixed with DMSO to eliminate the influence of the solvent. The mice in each group underwent the above-mentioned procedure every other day for 12 consecutive weeks [35]. After the mice were sacrificed, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and bilateral knee joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde; paraffin embedding, sectioning, Safranin O/Fast Green staining, and immunohistochemistry staining were then performed.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) scanning, histology and immunohistochemistry

Mice knee joints were collected 12 weeks after surgery. After being anesthetized, the mice were euthanized. Subsequently, knee joints were dissected out and preserved in 75% ethanol. These specimens were then scanned and analyzed using a micro-CT instrument (PerkinElmer, USA). The scanning process was set with the following parameters: 90 kV, 88 μA, and 7 μm/pixel isotropic resolution. The acquired scan data were reconstructed and quantitatively analyzed using Analyze 12.0 software (PerkinElmer, USA). The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the medial compartment of the knee joint (the primary lesion area of the DMM model), and the core quantitative parameter evaluated via micro-CT imaging was the number of osteophytes (osteophyte number, OP. N), and the total number of osteophytes in the ROI of each knee joint was counted for subsequent statistical analysis.

Mice were euthanized at the experimental end point, and bilateral knee joints were harvested. The surrounding adherent muscle and soft tissues were carefully trimmed under a dissecting microscope while preserving the intact anatomical structure of the knee joint, followed by immediate fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h at room temperature. After fixation, knee joint specimens were transferred into 10% EDTA decalcification solution (pH 7.4) and placed in a constant temperature shaker for continuous decalcification for 3 weeks at 25–30 °C with a rotation speed of 110–120 r/min, with the decalcification solution refreshed every 3 days to ensure sufficient decalcification. Upon completion of decalcification, specimens were rinsed with running deionized water for 4 h to remove residual decalcification solution, then processed through sequential gradient ethanol dehydration, xylene clearance, and paraffin infiltration in a fully automatic tissue dehydrator. The processed specimens were embedded in molten paraffin using an automatic embedding machine, with the sagittal plane of the knee joint set as the standardized sectioning plane; after complete solidification of paraffin at room temperature, the embedded blocks were cut into consecutive 5 μm-thick sagittal sections using a rotary microtome. The obtained sections were floated on a 45 °C water bath to achieve full flattening, then mounted onto adhesive glass slides and baked in a slide drying oven at 60 °C for 2 h to ensure firm tissue adhesion, with the prepared slides stored at room temperature for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical staining.

For histological assessment of articular cartilage degeneration, paraffin sections were subjected to Safranin O/Fast Green staining following a standardized protocol. Briefly, slides were first baked in a constant temperature oven at 60 °C for 1 h until complete melting of paraffin, then fully deparaffinized by sequential immersion in two changes of xylene for 15 min each, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (100% ethanol for 2 min, 95% ethanol for 2 min, 80% ethanol for 2 min, 75% ethanol for 2 min) and three 5-min washes with PBS. Rehydrated sections were incubated with freshly prepared Weigert’s iron hematoxylin solution for 4 min for nuclear staining, rinsed once with deionized water, treated with acidic differentiation solution for 10 s, and then rinsed thoroughly with running deionized water for 5 min. Subsequently, sections were immersed in Fast Green staining solution for 5 min, rinsed with weak acid solution for 15 s, and then stained with Safranin O solution for 5 min to visualize cartilage proteoglycans. After staining, sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100% ethanol for 2 s each), cleared in two changes of xylene for 1 min each, mounted with neutral balsam, and air-dried at room temperature for microscopic observation. Stained sections were imaged under an optical microscope, and the severity of OA-like cartilage lesions was evaluated by two independent observers blinded to experimental group information using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) histological scoring system [38].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed to detect the in situ protein expression of target molecules in articular cartilage, with the baking, deparaffinization, and rehydration steps identical to those described for Safranin O/Fast Green staining. After rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed by immersing slides in sodium citrate antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0), which was preheated in a microwave oven for 10 min and then maintained at a sub-boiling state under low microwave power for an additional 15 min, with strict prevention of section drying during the procedure. Slides were naturally cooled to room temperature and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, then incubated with endogenous peroxidase inhibitor in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by three additional 5-min PBS washes. Nonspecific antigen binding was blocked by covering sections with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking solution and incubating in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 60 min. After blocking, the solution was discarded, and sections were incubated with diluted primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber, including anti-COL2A1 (#AF0135, 1:200, Affinity Biosciences, China, RRID: AB_2833318), anti-ACAN (#DF7561, 1:200, Affinity Biosciences, China, RRID: AB_2841055), anti-MMP13 (#AF5355, 1:200, Affinity Biosciences, China, RRID: AB_2837840), and anti-ADAMTS5 (#DF13288, 1:200, Affinity Biosciences, China, RRID: N/A). Following overnight incubation, unbound primary antibodies were removed by three 5-min washes with PBS, and sections were incubated with the corresponding Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) HRP secondary antibody (#S0001, 1:500, Affinity Biosciences, RRID: AB_2839429) in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 60 min. After secondary antibody incubation, slides were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each, and fresh 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution (prepared by mixing DAB solution A and B at a volume ratio of 20:1) was applied to the sections. Chromogenic development was monitored in real time under a microscope, and the reaction was terminated by rinsing with deionized water once distinct brown positive staining signals were observed. Sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin solution at room temperature for 1 min, rinsed thoroughly with running tap water, treated with hydrochloric acid–ethanol differentiation solution for 15 s, rinsed again with running water, and immersed in weak alkaline solution for blueing to restore nuclear staining. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted with neutral balsam, and air-dried following the same procedure as Safranin O/Fast Green staining. Stained sections were imaged under an optical microscope, and the integrated optical density (IOD) of the positive staining area within the articular cartilage region was quantified using Image J software (version 1.54, National Institutes of Health, USA) for semi-quantitative analysis of target protein expression levels.

Behavioral assessment

Mechanical allodynia of the hind paw was assessed using a set of calibrated von Frey filaments (Shanghai Yuyan Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Before the test, each mouse was placed in a transparent acrylic chamber on a wire mesh grid and allowed to acclimate to the testing environment for 15 min until exploratory behaviors ceased. Each filament was applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the hind paw with a steady force for 3–5 s; the filaments used in this test covered a force range of 0.04–2.0 g, with the test initiated at 0.4 g. The 50% paw withdrawal threshold was determined using the classic up-down iterative method, as reported in previous studies [37]. A positive withdrawal response was defined as the animal displaying any nociceptive behavior (brisk paw withdrawal, paw licking, or paw shaking) either during the stimulus application or immediately after filament removal. All tests were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental group assignment of the animals.

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-related behavior of mice were evaluated via the open field test (OFT). The test was performed in a sound-attenuated, temperature-, and humidity-controlled behavioral testing room (23 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity) during the light phase of the 12 h light/dark cycle (9:00–12:00 a.m.) to avoid interference from circadian rhythm fluctuations. Mice were transferred to the behavioral testing room 24 h prior to the test for acclimation to the environment, to minimize stress-induced behavioral deviations. The OFT was conducted using a square polyvinyl chloride (PVC) open field chamber with dimensions of 40 × 40 × 40 cm. The chamber was equipped with an overhead high-resolution digital camera (Basler acA1920-155um, Basler AG, Ahrensburg, Germany), which was connected to the SMART 3.0 Video Tracking System (version 3.0, Panlab SL, Harvard Apparatus, Barcelona, Spain) for real-time video capture, movement tracking, and automated quantitative analysis. Between each test session, the entire chamber was thoroughly sanitized with 75% (v/v) ethanol and completely air-dried to eliminate residual olfactory cues from the previous animal. For each test, individual mice were gently placed in the central area of the open field chamber (with the head facing the chamber wall) and allowed to explore freely without disturbance for 3 min. The movement trajectory and behavioral events of each mouse were continuously recorded in real time by the camera system. The following core behavioral parameters were automatically quantified via the SMART 3.0 tracking software: number of rearing (up-righting) events, total moving distance, mean moving speed, and number of zone transitions (defined as the number of times the mouse crossed between the central zone (20 × 20 cm central area of the chamber) and the peripheral zone).

Objective assessments of mice’s footprints, locomotor performance, and physical coordination were carried out using the CatWalk XT automated gait analysis system (version 10.6, Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, the Netherlands), which consists of a transparent glass walkway plate, an enclosed dark test corridor, a high-speed digital video camera mounted below the walkway, and the supporting CatWalk XT analysis software. Before formal testing, mice were trained to traverse the walkway at a consistent speed for 3 consecutive days to ensure familiarity with the testing environment. During the test, the footprints of each mouse were recorded in real time by the camera below the glass plate as they walked freely along the corridor, and the following gait parameters were recorded and analyzed via the CatWalk XT software: Max contact area: Maximum area of a paw that comes into contact with the glass plate during a single step; Print Area: The actual area occupied by the paw print left after a single foot comes into contact with the system detection plane; Mean Intensity: The average pressure level of a single foot in contact with the system’s detection plane; Duty Cycle: The percentage of the contact time between a single foot and the system’s detection plane relative to its complete gait cycle.

All behavioral tests were conducted in a sound-attenuated, temperature-controlled room with consistent light conditions to avoid environmental interference with the test results.

Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (#ST506, Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 1 × protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#P1045, Beyotime, China). The protein concentration of each sample was determined via Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay to ensure equal loading. For western blotting analysis, the polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) Gel Fast Preparation Kit (#PG112, Epizyme Biotech, China) was utilized to prepare sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels, with the specific experimental procedures following a previously reported protocol [39]. Briefly, equal quantities of total protein (30 μg per well) were separated via polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. These membranes were blocked with protein free rapid blocking buffer (#PS108, EpiZyme, Shanghai, China) for 10 min before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including: anti-iNOS (#18985-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China, RRID: AB_2782960), anti-ADAMST4 (#11865-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China, RRID: AB_2877799), anti-MMP13 (#AF5355, 1:1000, Affinity Biosciences, USA, RRID: AB_2837840), anti-LRP4 (#DF9610, 1:1000, Affinity Biosciences, China, RRID: AB_2842806), anti-TRAF6 (#AF5376, 1:1000, Affinity Biosciences, USA, RRID: AB_2810280), anti-p-ERK (#28733-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China, RRID: AB_2881202), anti-ERK (#4695 T, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, RRID: AB_390779), anti-p-JNK (#4668 T, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, RRID: AB_823588), anti-JNK (#9252 T, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, RRID: AB_2250373), anti-p-P38 (#28796-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, China, RRID: AB_2918205), anti-P38 (#AF6456, 1:1000, Affinity Biosciences, USA, RRID: AB_2835277). After washing, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies. Ultimately, the antibody–antigen complexes were visualized using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China).

Lentivirus-based overexpression and knockout experiments

Using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, LRP4 knockout lentivirus was acquired from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. For viral transfection, C28I2 cells at 50% confluency were incubated with the lentivirus along with polybrene (at a final concentration of 8 μg/mL). After 12 h, the culture medium was replaced with complete medium. At 72 h post-transfection, lentivirus-positive cells were selected using puromycin. The expression levels of the target genes were confirmed by western blotting and qRT-PCR prior to conducting subsequent experiments.

RNA-Seq transcriptomics and bioinformatics analyses

Total RNA was isolated from C28I2 using TRIzol reagent (#15596026, Invitrogen, USA), after which RNA sequencing was conducted. To evaluate RNA quantity and purity, a Bioanalyzer 2100 instrument and the RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (#5067-1511, Agilent, CA, USA) were employed, and high-quality RNA samples with a RNA Integrity Number greater than 7.0 were selected for sequencing library construction. For library preparation, mRNA was enriched from total RNA using oligo(dT)-coated magnetic beads to remove rRNA, followed by chemical fragmentation (10 min at 94 °C) in fragmentation buffer; first-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamer primers and M-mLV reverse transcriptase, second-strand cDNA with DNA polymerase I and RNase H, and the resulting cDNA fragments were end-repaired, adenylated at 3’ ends, ligated to Illumina index adapters, size-selected (200–300 bp) using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and amplified by 12 cycles of PCR, with library quality verified via the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and quantification via the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosciences, Wilmington, MA, USA).

Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technologies, Hangzhou, China) with paired-end 150 bp (PE150) reads, generating ~6 Gb of raw data per sample; raw reads were processed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove adapters and low-quality reads (Phred score < 20, length < 50 bp), clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.10a), and gene-level counts were quantified with featureCounts (v2.0.3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.34.0) in R 4.4.2, with normalization via the median of ratios method, and DEGs defined as those with |fold change|> 2 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Biological interpretation of DEGs was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler (v4.8.1). Enrichment significance was assessed via the hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction, requiring FDR < 0.05. Subsequently, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of DEGs was constructed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (https://cn.string-db.org/), and visualized with Cytoscape 3.7.2 [40]. We have completed the full deposition of all RNA-seq raw data, processed gene count matrices, and related experimental metadata generated in this study into the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public database. The dataset has been assigned the unique permanent accession number GSE325883.

Drug affinity responsive target stability experiment

C28I2 cells were washed, then covered with cell lysate and lysed on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C, 2 μL of periplocin and 2 μL of DMSO (each added separately) were introduced into 100 μL of the lysate, and the mixture was shaken for a minimum of 60 min to ensure thorough mixing. Next, each portion of the protein solution was aliquoted into individual centrifuge tubes. Protease E (#P8360, Solarbio, China) was diluted to four different ratios (1:200, 1:400, 1:600, and 1:800), where the ratio was calculated on the basis of the volume of protease relative to the amount of protein in each tube, and the solution was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to facilitate protein digestion. Following the digestion step, 2 μL of protease inhibitor was added to each tube; the tubes were immediately transferred to ice and kept for 5 min to stop the digestion reaction. Finally, the results of this process were confirmed using western blotting.

CETSA assay

C28I2 cells were exposed to DMSO, and 10 μM periplocin for 1 h in a cell culture incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After treatment, the cells were harvested and evenly distributed into sterile tubes. The aliquoted cells were digested at various temperatures for 3 min each. Subsequently, the samples underwent three cycles of freeze–thawing to achieve lysis, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 g. The resulting supernatants were collected for western blotting analysis. In the isothermal dose–response experiment, cells were treated with periplocin at concentrations ranging from 0.001 μM to 100 μM under constant temperature conditions (no temperature changes were applied). All remaining experimental procedures were performed as described in previous reports [41].

Molecular docking

The protein structures of human TLR2, TLR4, IL-1R1, TNFR1, TNFR2, LRP4, and P2X7 were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The detailed UniProt accession numbers and complete protein code information of all target proteins used for molecular docking are fully listed in Supplementary Table S2. The three-dimensional (3D) structure of periplocin was generated using the ChemDraw Professional & Chem3D (version 20.0, PerkinElmer, USA), and energy minimization was performed using the MMFF94 force field to obtain the stable conformation of the ligand.

Prior to docking, both protein and ligand structures were preprocessed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.7: polar hydrogen atoms and Gasteiger partial charges were added to the protein structures, nonpolar hydrogen atoms were merged, and the protein backbone was set as rigid; Gasteiger charges were assigned to the periplocin ligand, and all rotatable bonds of the small molecule were defined. Molecular docking was then performed using AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 [42].

As blind docking was employed, the grid box was set to encompass the entire protein structure with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å, to allow for unbiased exploration of potential binding sites. The core docking parameters were set as follows: exhaustiveness = 32, number of generated binding modes = 20, energy range = 4 kcal/mol, with other parameters set to the software default values.

Following successful docking simulations, the binding affinities of periplocin to each target protein were evaluated on the basis of the lowest binding free energy scores. The most stable binding conformations with the lowest binding energy were subsequently visualized and analyzed using PyMOL 2.5.0.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation

To investigate the key amino acid residues involved in the interaction between periplocin and LRP4, MD simulations of the protein–ligand complex were performed using GROMACS 2024.5 [43]. As the AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structure of LRP4 contains extended, low-confidence loop regions at both the N- and C-termini, and the molecular docking results indicated that periplocin primarily binds to the β-propeller domain; these terminal regions were truncated prior to simulation to reduce computational cost and improve stability. A more accurate geometry optimization and partial charge calculation for periplocin was carried out using Gaussian 09. Topology and parameter files for the ligand were generated using a combination of Multiwfn and Sobtop tools, while PDB2GMX was employed to prepare the LRP4 input files [44]. The MD simulations were conducted using the TIP3P water model. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the system, which was then subjected to energy minimization using the conjugate gradient algorithm. To ensure homogeneous distribution of solvent and ions, a 100-ps equilibration was performed under isothermal–isobaric (NPT) conditions at 298.15 K, using the V-rescale thermostat and Berendsen barostat. A 100-ns production MD simulation was subsequently run, with all bond lengths constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a grid spacing of 1.0 Å. The integration time step was set to 2 fs, and simulation data (coordinates and energies) were recorded every 10 ps. The resulting trajectory was used for downstream structural and energetic analyses.

Genotyping

The Acan-CreERT2 transgenic line was acquired from Cyagen (USA). CRISPR-Cas9 technology was utilized by the same provider to generate Lrp4flox/+ mice. Intercrossing of Lrp4flox/+ heterozygotes produced homozygous Lrp4flox/flox animals. These were subsequently bred with Acan-CreERT2 mice to obtain Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4flox/flox dual-transgenic progeny. Experimental groups consisted of 12-week-old female mice carrying both genotypes. To induce conditional knockout, animals received intraperitoneal tamoxifen injections (MCE, #HY-13757A, USA; 1 mg daily for 5 consecutive days), generating Lrp4 conditional knockout (Lrp4-cKO) mice.

Genomic DNA isolation involved collection of 1-mm tail biopsies. Tissue samples were processed using the M5 Animal Tissue Direct PCR Kit (Mei5bio, #MF280, China) following manufacturer specifications, with subsequent DNA amplification. Electrophoresis separation was performed on PCR products using 2% agarose gels prepared in 1 × TAE buffer containing GelRed (5 μL per 100 mL). Imaging and documentation employed the Bio-Rad GelDoc Go System (USA). Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Plasmid construction and transfection

Consistent with our previous research method [37], the Flag-tagged LRP4 plasmid was constructed according to previously reported protocols. In brief, complementary DNA (cDNA) of LRP4 (R&D Systems, Cat. No. RDC2653) was used as the template, and PCR amplification was performed with PCR primers containing Hind III and Sal I restriction sites (Forward: ATCGAAGCTTAGGCGTCAGTGGGGAGC; Reverse: ACCTGGCTCTCTGAGGAGTCGACATAAT). Both the PCR product and pCMV-tag2b plasmid (Agilent, Cat. No. 211172) were digested with Hind III (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. R0104) and Sal I (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. R0138) overnight at 37 °C. The digested fragments were ligated overnight at 16 °C using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. M0202), and the ligation product was verified by DNA sequencing at Pumeigen Company.

Using the constructed Flag‑tagged LRP4 plasmid as the template, LRP4 site‑directed mutants were generated using the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (#C214, Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The specific mutation sites are as follows: Valine 809 (GTG) changed to Alanine (GCG), Aspartic acid 812 (GAT) changed to Alanine (GCT), Valine 810 (GTC) changed to Phenylalanine (TTC), Glycine 847 (GGC) changed to Aspartic acid (GAC), Valine 1072 (GTG) changed to Phenylalanine (TTC), Methionine 1061 (ATG) changed to Isoleucine (ATC), Methionine 1047 (ATG) changed to Isoleucine (ATC), Glycine 1046 (GGC) changed to Aspartic acid (GAC), Isoleucine 1034 (ATC) changed to Threonine (ACC), Leucine 1110 (CTG) changed to Proline (CCG).

C28I2 chondrocytes at the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6‑well plates at an appropriate density and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator until reaching 60–70% confluence. Flag‑LRP4 negative control, Flag‑LRP4, Flag‑LRP4 mutant negative control, and Flag‑LRP4 mutant plasmids were separately diluted and mixed with LipofectamineTM 3000 Transfection Reagent in Opti‑MEM medium at the recommended ratio. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form liposome complexes, which were then added to the cells with gentle mixing. After 6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with normal complete medium, and the cells were further cultured. Cells were harvested at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection for subsequent RNA and protein extraction and analysis, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were implemented using GraphPad Prism (v9.0.0; GraphPad, USA). Between-group comparisons utilized two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. Multiple group comparisons employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data are presented throughout as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. Identification of periplocin as a potential chondroprotective agent

To screen for small-molecule drugs capable of inducing the expression of the messenger RNA of collagen type II alpha 1 chain (COL2A1), the major component of the chondrocyte extracellular matrix, 200 existing drugs from a drug library were first screened. Briefly, C28I2 chondrocytes were treated with the drugs respectively for 24 h, and then the expression of COL2A1 following different drug treatments was detected using qRT-PCR. After the first round of screening, 15 drugs that effectively increased COL2A1 expression were identified (Fig. 1A). These 15 candidate drugs underwent a second round of screening, where C28I2 chondrocytes were treated with each drug respectively for 24 h. The heatmap showed that only five candidates could induce COL2A1 expression significantly and in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). To further evaluate their potential anabolic effects, we treated C28I2 cells with these five drugs and then detected the expression of various genes related to chondrocyte differentiation and metabolism. Among the five drugs analyzed, only periplocin strongly induced the expression of anabolic marker genes, including COL2A1, aggrecan (ACAN), and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) (Fig. 1C). Owing to the ability of periplocin to increase COL2A1 expression in C28I2 chondrocytes, we initially aimed to verify whether this drug was capable of inducing chondrogenesis. We treated C3H10T1/2 mesenchymal stem cells in micromass culture with periplocin for 7 or 14 days. As shown in Fig. 1D, Alcian blue staining confirmed that chondrocyte differentiation was enhanced in the periplocin-treated group compared with the control group.

The chemical structure of periplocin is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A. Since the safety of periplocin was our primary consideration, we designed tests to ensure its safety in both cells and mice. Results from the CCK-8 assay indicated that there was no significant difference in the impact of periplocin on cell proliferation. The results showed that within the selected drug concentration gradient, there was no significant damage to the visceral organs. Furthermore, consecutive treatment with periplocin at different doses did not result in a significant difference in the body weight of mice (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Overall, the results indicate that periplocin has no significant adverse effects on cells or mice within a certain concentration range.

Periplocin regulated chondrocyte metabolism

Next, we investigated whether periplocin could upregulate the expression of anabolic marker genes in mice and human chondrocytes. We found that periplocin dose-dependently stimulated the expression of COL2A1, ACAN, and COMP in both mice and human chondrocytes (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we further determined whether periplocin could also inhibit the catabolism of chondrocytes activated by proinflammatory cytokines. After C28I2 chondrocytes or mouse primary chondrocytes were incubated with proinflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) or interleukin-1β (IL-1β)) for 24 h, qRT-PCR and western blotting were used to detect the levels of catabolic markers in chondrocytes (Fig. 2B). The results showed that in C28I2 chondrocytes, periplocin significantly reduced the TNF-α-induced mRNA levels of catabolic markers, including matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13), A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin 1 Motif 4 (ADAMTS4), and matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP3) (Fig. 2C). The TNF-α-induced increases in the protein levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), ADAMTS4, and MMP13 were reversed by periplocin in C28I2 chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2A). A similar trend to the aforementioned results was observed in mice primary chondrocytes. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of catabolic markers in the periplocin-treated group were significantly downregulated, including nitric oxide synthase 2 (Nos2), a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (Adamts5), Mmp13, and Mmp3 (Fig. 2D). Additionally, the reduced protein levels of iNOS, ADAMTS5, and MMP13 further confirmed that periplocin inhibited the catabolism of chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Furthermore, the results of qRT-PCR and western blotting showed that periplocin significantly reduced the expression of IL-1β-induced catabolic markers in C28I2 chondrocytes (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. S2C). In mice primary chondrocytes, the results demonstrated that the mRNA and protein levels of catabolic markers in the periplocin-treated group were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S2D).

Periplocin protected against OA in a surgically induced model in vivo

To investigate the effect of periplocin on the progression of OA, we performed DMM surgery on 12-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice. Starting from 3 days after the surgery, the mice were treated with or without periplocin (intraperitoneal injection at doses of 1 mg/kg or 5 mg/kg). We conducted behavioral analysis and histological analysis on the mice at 12 weeks after DMM surgery, respectively, to evaluate knee joint damage (Fig. 3A). As expected, severe cartilage degeneration was observed 12 weeks after DMM surgery. Results of Safranin O/fast green staining of mice knee joints showed that periplocin significantly alleviated cartilage degradation in DMM-operated mice (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, periplocin significantly reduced the OA Research Society International (OARSI) score in DMM-operated mice (Fig. 3C). Micro-CT results showed that osteophyte formation in mice significantly increased 12 weeks after DMM surgery, and this effect was reversed by periplocin (Fig. 3D). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that at 12 weeks post-DMM surgery, the levels of COL2A1 and ACAN in cartilage were significantly reduced, while the levels of catabolic markers (MMP13 and ADAMTS5) were increased, and this change was notably reversed by periplocin (Fig. 3E). In conclusion, the aforementioned results indicated that periplocin effectively inhibited the progression of OA in a mice model of injury-induced OA.

LRP4 was a novel potential target of periplocin in chondrocyte

Periplocin can exert its effects by targeting Na+/K+-ATPase [32]. Therefore, we further explored whether periplocin could regulate the biological processes of chondrocytes through Na+/K+-ATPase. Therefore, in C28I2 cells, the activity of Na+/K+-ATPase was inhibited by Istaroxime hydrochloride. Surprisingly, the inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase activity had no effect on either the drug’s promotional effect on anabolism or its inhibitory effect on catabolism (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). This finding indicated that periplocin may possess targets other than Na+/K+-ATPase for regulating the homeostasis of chondrocytes. Previous studies have shown that membrane proteins play important roles in regulating chondrocyte metabolism [37, 45–49]. Based on this, we further explored the biological relationship between membrane proteins and periplocin.

Molecular docking is a widely used, efficient method for estimating the binding affinity between proteins and small molecules. In this study, docking simulations were employed to rapidly assess the binding energy of periplocin with the selected membrane-associated proteins, enabling a preliminary screening of its potential cellular targets. The docking results indicated that periplocin was capable of binding to all the proteins evaluated (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Among these, LRP4 exhibited the lowest binding energy with periplocin, significantly lower than that of the other proteins, suggesting a higher binding affinity (Supplementary Fig. S4B, C). Structural comparisons of the resulting protein–ligand complexes revealed that, unlike with other proteins where periplocin binds primarily to surface regions, the compound interacts with LRP4 within a well-defined binding pocket located between its two β-propeller domains. This mode of interaction implies a more stable and specific binding of periplocin to LRP4. To further clarify the relationship between periplocin and LRP4 in chondrocytes, we performed a DARTS assay. The results showed that periplocin could protect LRP4 from enzymatic digestion (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we performed a CETSA to confirm the changes in the thermal denaturation temperature of the target protein under different drug doses. Compared with the control group, periplocin prevented the denaturation of LRP4 at different temperatures, particularly at 49 °C (Fig. 5B). The melting curve showed that in the presence of periplocin, a significant change occurred in the melting temperature (Tm), and the Tm of the control group and the periplocin group were 42.3 °C and 46.06 °C, respectively. We also performed CETSA at 49 °C using different drug concentrations. The results showed that periplocin prevented LRP4 denaturation in a dose-dependent manner, with an EC50 of 8.508 × 10−2 (Fig. 5C).

To further elucidate the key amino acid residues involved in the binding of periplocin to LRP4, 100 ns MD simulations were performed for both the periplocin-LRP4 complex and the apo-LRP4 system, using the molecular docking results as the initial conformation. Structural stability was first assessed by calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of each system relative to its initial structure throughout the simulation. As shown in Fig. 5D(a), both systems exhibited considerable RMSD fluctuations during the initial phase (0–20 ns), which gradually stabilized after approximately 50 ns, indicating that the systems had reached equilibrium. Notably, the RMSD of periplocin fluctuated within 0.2 nm during the final 50 ns, suggesting a stable binding conformation with LRP4. Additionally, the number of hydrogen bonds between periplocin and LRP4 stabilized after 25 ns, with an average of approximately four hydrogen bonds, further supporting the structural stability of the complex (Fig. 5D(b)). To evaluate local flexibility of the protein, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values were calculated for each residue of LRP4 (Fig. 5D(c), (d)). In the unbound state, significant fluctuations were observed at both termini, especially in the region spanning residues 1400–1600, where the RMSF reached a pronounced peak. Upon binding with periplocin, although the overall RMSF profile remained similar, the amplitude of fluctuations—particularly in the 1400–1600 region—was markedly reduced, suggesting that periplocin binding confers local stabilization to the LRP4 structure. To further validate these findings, the radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) were calculated to assess structural compactness and solvent exposure, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5D(e,f), periplocin binding led to a notable reduction in Rg and a slight decrease in SASA, indicating a more compact molecular conformation, consistent with the observed decrease in RMSF. Collectively, these results suggest that the periplocin–LRP4 complex exhibits high structural stability throughout the simulation, supporting its use as a reliable model for subsequent interaction analyses. The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) results showed that periplocin bound rapidly to LRP4 when periplocin at different concentrations was passed through, with a binding KD value of 34.86 μM, and dissociated rapidly from LRP4 after the flow of periplocin was stopped (Fig. 5E). As illustrated in Fig. 5F(a), periplocin was positioned within the interdomain cleft between the two β-propeller domains of LRP4, with its hydrophobic moiety deeply embedded in the cavity. This hydrophobic region forms stable interactions with nonpolar residues including Leu1110, Ile1034, Gly1046, Met1047, Met1061, and Val1072. Meanwhile, the polar sugar ring of periplocin establishes five stable hydrogen bonds with surface residues Val809 and Asp812 (Fig. 5F(b,d)). These combined hydrophobic and polar interactions contribute to the strong and stable binding of periplocin to LRP4.

Next, we mutated the aforementioned sites of LRP4 and performed DARTS to identify the key site(s) for the binding of LRP4 to the drug (Fig. 5G). The results showed that the protective effect of periplocin on the Asp812 mutant of LRP4 was mostly lost, indicating that Asp812 is a key amino acid for the drug to target LRP4 (Fig. 5H).

Periplocin regulated the metabolism of chondrocytes by binding to LRP4

To determine the role of LRP4 in the effect of periplocin on chondrocyte metabolism, we employed the CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing strategy to efficiently knockout the Lrp4 gene (Fig. 6A). Based on the results of qRT-PCR and western blotting, we selected the KO LRP4-2 C28I2 cells for subsequent functional experiments (Fig. 6B). Notably, in chondrocytes with LRP4 gene knockout, the dose-dependent effect of periplocin on inducing anabolic markers was abrogated, resulting in unchanged expression levels of anabolic marker genes such as COL2A1, ACAN, and COMP following drug treatment (Fig. 6C). Next, we re-expressed wild-type or Asp812-mutated LRP4 in LRP4-knockout C28I2 chondrocytes. The wild-type LRP4 expression vector restored the periplocin-induced increase in anabolic markers, whereas the Asp812-mutated LRP4 did not (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, knockout of the LRP4 gene reversed the inhibitory effect of periplocin on chondrocyte catabolism. Results from qRT-PCR and western blotting showed that after LRP4 gene knockout in chondrocytes, the levels of catabolic markers iNOS, ADAMTS4, and MMP13 were increased (Fig. 6E, F). The wild-type LRP4 expression vector restored the periplocin-induced decrease in catabolic markers, whereas the Asp812-mutated LRP4 did not elicit such a change (Fig. 6G, H). In summary, the above findings indicated that periplocin promotes chondrocyte anabolism and inhibited catabolism in an LRP4-dependent manner (Fig. 6I), and Asp812 played a critical role in the drug-mediated regulation of chondrocyte metabolism.

LRP4 deficiency abolished the protective effect of periplocin on DMM mice

The aforementioned in vivo experiments confirmed that periplocin could ameliorate the progression of OA in DMM mice. Given that LRP4 was the target protein of periplocin in regulating chondrocyte metabolism, we constructed chondrocyte-specific Lrp4-deficient mice to further verify the regulatory effect of periplocin on LRP4 in chondrocytes. We crossed Lrp4fl/fl mice with Acan-CreERT2 mice to generate conditional Lrp4 knockout mice (Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4fl/fl), and the results of PCR confirmed that these conditional Lrp4 knockout mice could specifically delete Lrp4 in chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S5A–C).

To assess the unintended background recombination of the tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase system, we collected knee joint samples from 12-week-old, littermate Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4flox/flox mice, Lrp4flox/flox mice, and wild-type C57BL/6 mice that did not receive tamoxifen injection. We performed Micro-CT scanning, three-dimensional reconstruction, and quantitative analysis of the knee joints from the above mice. The results showed that there were no obvious pathological alterations in the bony architecture, joint space, and subchondral bone morphology of the knee joints among the three groups, and no spontaneous OA-related phenotypes (such as osteophyte formation and subchondral bone sclerosis) were observed (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Furthermore, we detected the mRNA expression level of Lrp4 in articular cartilage via qRT-PCR. The results revealed no statistically significant difference in Lrp4 mRNA levels in the knee articular cartilage among the three groups without tamoxifen induction, confirming that no significant nonspecific Lrp4 knockout caused by leaky expression of Cre recombinase occurred in this model in the absence of the inducer (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

We conducted behavioral analysis and histological analysis on the Lrp4 knockout mice at 12 weeks after DMM surgery, respectively, to evaluate knee joint damage (Fig. 7A). Results of Safranin O/fast green staining of mice knee joints showed that periplocin treatment did not alleviate cartilage degradation in Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4fl/fl DMM mice (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, there was no significant statistical difference in the OARSI score between the Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4fl/fl DMM group and the periplocin group (Fig. 7C). Micro-CT results showed that the formation of osteophytes was significantly increased in Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4fl/fl DMM mice at 12 weeks after DMM surgery, and this effect was not reversed by periplocin (Fig. 7D). Immunohistochemical staining showed that at 12 weeks after DMM surgery, the levels of COL2A1 and ACAN in the cartilage tissue of Acan-CreERT2; Lrp4fl/fl DMM mice were significantly decreased, while the levels of catabolic markers (MMP13 and ADAMTS5) were increased, and this change was not improved by periplocin treatment (Fig. 7E). The above results indicated that the deletion of LRP4 abolished the therapeutic effect of periplocin on the traumatic OA mice model, further confirming that LRP4 was a key target of periplocin in chondrocyte metabolism.

Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) was required for periplocin/LRP4 to regulate chondrocyte metabolism

To elucidate the downstream molecular mechanisms by which periplocin mediates chondrocyte metabolism under TNFα-induced inflammatory conditions, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on control cells, TNFα-treated cells, and TNFα-treated cells followed by periplocin intervention (Fig. 9A). Volcano plot analysis revealed that TNFα treatment significantly upregulated the expression of 392 genes and downregulated 322 genes (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses demonstrated that TNFα primarily activated the interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling pathway and the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, confirming the successful induction of an inflammatory state in C28I2 chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S7B, C).

Volcano plot analysis comparing periplocin-treated versus untreated TNFα-stimulated cells identified 3133 significantly upregulated and 3538 significantly downregulated genes (Fig. 9B). A Venn diagram was used to identify 270 common DEGs across the two comparisons (Supplementary Fig. S7D). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these common DEGs revealed a significant enrichment in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig.s S7E and S9C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that periplocin treatment significantly regulated the activity of the MAPK pathway (Fig. 9D). Heatmap analysis showed that the expression of key MAPK pathway-related genes was reduced in response to periplocin (Fig. 9E).

Next, we re-expressed wild-type LRP4 in LRP4-knockout C28I2 chondrocytes. The wild-type LRP4 expression vector restored the inhibitory effect of periplocin on the MAPK signaling pathway, as evidenced by decreased phosphorylation levels of ERK, P38 and JNK (Fig. 9F). These findings indicate that the inhibition of chondrocyte catabolism by periplocin via the MAPK signaling pathway is LRP4-dependent. PPI network analysis identified TRAF6 as a central hub molecule (Fig. 9G). Therefore, we further explored the role of TRAF6 in chondrocyte metabolism mediated by periplocin. First, we transfected C28I2 chondrocytes with TRAF6 plasmid. qRT-PCR and western blotting results showed that the TRAF6 expression increased notably after transfection (Supplementary Fig. S7F). Additionally, we found that TRAF6 overexpression could reverse the inhibitory effect of periplocin on chondrocyte catabolism (Fig. 9H and Supplementary Fig. S7G). Results from western blotting and qRT-PCR showed that periplocin could downregulate the expression of TRAF6, while knockout of LRP4 decreased this inhibiting effect (Fig. 9I and Supplementary Fig. S7H). Furthermore, the wild-type LRP4 expression vector restored the inhibitory effect of periplocin on TRAF6, whereas expression of LRP4 with an Asp812 site mutation in LRP4-knockout C28I2 cells failed to restore this inhibitory effect of periplocin on TRAF6 (Fig. 9J and Supplementary Fig. S7I). In summary, these findings indicated that the inhibition of chondrocyte catabolism by periplocin is LRP4-dependent, and TRAF6, as a key molecule in the MAPK signaling pathway, played a critical role in the regulation of chondrocyte metabolism.

Discussion

The maintenance of articular cartilage homeostasis relies on a dynamic balance between chondrocyte-driven anabolism and catabolism, which ensures the structural integrity and functional resilience of synovial joints [19]. Chondrocytes, the sole cell type in articular cartilage, undergo differentiation and metabolic adaptation in response to mechanical stress and inflammatory cues [14]. Excessive activation of catabolic pathways, driven by proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, disrupts this equilibrium by upregulating matrix-degrading enzymes and suppressing anabolic factors, ultimately leading to cartilage degradation and the progression of OA [50]. Despite advances in symptomatic management, the lack of disease-modifying therapies targeting chondrocyte metabolic dysregulation remains a critical gap in OA treatment [51].

In the present study, we obtained several key findings. (1) Through three rounds of qRT-PCR screening of a drug library, we identified periplocin as a candidate drug that potentially promotes chondrocyte extracellular matrix anabolism. (2) In vitro and in vivo experiments have shown that administration of periplocin can effectively inhibit chondrocyte catabolism and alleviate their pain sensitivity. (3) By integrating DARTS, CETSA, MD simulation, and site-directed mutagenesis analysis, we for the first time identified the Asp812 residue of LRP4 as the crucial amino acid for LRP4–periplocin binding. We also confirmed that the regulatory effect of periplocin on chondrocytes depends on the presence of LRP4. (4) Based on chondrocyte-specific Lrp4 knockout mice, the results showed that after establishing a DMM-induced OA model in these knockout mice, administration of periplocin failed to reverse the progression of OA. This further confirms that LRP4 is a key target for periplocin to inhibit the progression of OA. These findings suggest that targeting LRP4 by periplocin may facilitate the development of potential therapeutic strategies for OA.

The inherent biocompatibility and potential multi-target regulatory properties of compounds make them important sources for drug discovery. Through screening of a drug library, we identified periplocin as a candidate chondroprotective agent. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that periplocin exerts potent antiinflammatory effects in models of autoimmune diseases and cancer [52–54]; however, the present study is the first to show that it can effectively protect against the OA. In vitro experiments revealed that periplocin not only inhibits chondrocyte catabolism but also promotes extracellular matrix anabolism. In vivo data further confirmed its therapeutic potential, in DMM mice, periplocin not only reduced articular cartilage damage but also restored pain-related behaviors. This stands in sharp contrast to conventional treatments, as currently used symptomatic agents have limited efficacy in inhibiting cartilage degeneration [55]. By simultaneously achieving structural protection and symptom relief, periplocin fills a critical unmet need in OA management.

Most of the biological activities exhibited by cardenolides stem from their capacity to inhibit the membrane-bound Na+/K+-ATPase. Notably, such inhibition has been demonstrated to induce cell proliferation, autophagy, and even apoptosis, not only in cardiomyocytes, but also across a range of other cell lines [37]. The inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase activity in chondrocytes by Istaroxime hydrochloride did not affect the role of periplocin in regulating the metabolism of chondrocyte. This indicated that periplocin might regulate the dynamic balance of chondrocyte through a completely distinct target. Therefore, by combining DARTS, CETSA, MD simulation, and site-directed mutagenesis, we identified LRP4 as the target, with Asp812 being the key amino acid involved in the interaction with periplocin. Through knockout and re-expression of LRP4 in chondrocytes, we demonstrated that LRP4 was indispensable for periplocin to inhibit extracellular matrix degradation by regulating the MAPK pathway.

LRP4 is a member of the low-density lipoprotein receptor family. LRP4 binds neuronal aggregation proteins at neuromuscular junctions and forms complexes with muscle-specific kinases to form neuromuscular synapses [56]. In recent years, a growing body of research has reported the role of LRP4 in bone metabolic diseases [37, 57]. Our previous studies have shown that digoxin acts on LRP4 to protect cartilage, thereby ameliorating OA [37]. Molecular docking analysis revealed that among multiple key membrane proteins, the binding between LRP4 and periplocin was the most stable. Therefore, we systematically investigated the interaction mechanism between LRP4 and periplocin by integrating MD simulations with SPR technology. Kinetic simulations and site-directed mutagenesis experiments validated the binding mode of periplocin with LRP4, revealing its critical binding site and the molecular interaction mechanism. The results demonstrated that periplocin can form a stable solid-state complex with the LRP4 protein, and the amino acid residue Asp812 is the key binding site.

Functional experiments further showed that mutation of the LRP4 Asp812 site completely abolished the ability of periplocin to promote chondrocyte anabolism and inhibit catabolism. Furthermore, we generated chondrocyte-specific Lrp4 knockout mice, which provided in vivo experimental evidence that periplocin affected the progression of OA by regulating LRP4. Experiments showed that in knockout mice with DMM-induced OA, despite periplocin treatment, there was no improvement in cartilage erosion and pain sensitivity. This stands in sharp contrast to the therapeutic effects observed in wild-type mice. The in vivo loss-of-function experiment strongly confirms that periplocin exerts its antiosteoarthritic effects via LRP4.

TRAF6 is a key adaptor protein in the TRAF family, acts as a central node in transducing signals from multiple cell-surface receptors, including tumor necrosis factor receptors (TNFRs) and interleukin-1 receptors (IL-1Rs), which are critical for regulating inflammatory responses, cell survival, and metabolic homeostasis [58, 59]. Previous studies have established TRAF6 as a key driver of chondrocyte catabolism. Upon stimulation by proinflammatory cytokines, TRAF6 is recruited to activated receptor complexes, where it initiates downstream inflammatory signaling cascades and activates the MAPK pathway [60, 61]. Our study demonstrated that periplocin could influence chondrocyte metabolism by regulating TRAF6. Additionally, we found that periplocin mediated LRP4 could reduce TRAF6 expression in chondrocyte. Therefore, we proposed that periplocin-mediated LRP4 might regulate the MAPK signaling pathway by affecting the expression of TRAF6, thereby determining the metabolic fate of chondrocytes. This discovery reveals that TRAF6 may act as a key molecule to participate in the process by which periplocin/LRP4 regulates chondrocyte metabolism.

The present study also has several limitations. Although we validated the chondroprotective effect of periplocin in the C28I2 human chondrocyte cell line and primary mouse chondrocytes, we have not yet verified the regulatory effect of periplocin on chondrocyte metabolic homeostasis in primary chondrocytes isolated from normal and patients with OA, and the results will be integrated into our subsequent research. In the present study, we did not select rapidly progressive severe OA models for validation, including the post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) model induced by anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) transection and the OA model induced by direct mechanical injury. Consequently, we were unable to comprehensively evaluate the interventional efficacy of periplocin in OA across different pathological subtypes and progression stages. In follow-up studies, we will systematically supplement in vivo validation experiments using different mouse models of OA, to further clarify the interventional effects and molecular mechanisms of periplocin in various types of OA, improve the relevant research data, and provide more comprehensive and robust experimental support for the subsequent clinical translation of this compound. In addition, LRP family proteins are single-transmembrane receptors with highly conserved intracellular domains, which can participate in the precise regulation of multiple signaling pathways through protein–protein interactions [62]. Previous studies have confirmed that LRP5, the core coreceptor of the Wnt pathway, can bind to TRAF6 upon Wnt3a stimulation [63]. This finding provides a theoretical basis for the potential interaction and functional regulation between TRAF6 and LRP4, a homologous member of the LRP family. Although the present study indirectly demonstrated that LRP4 can regulate TRAF6 function to maintain chondrocyte metabolic homeostasis through gene knockout, site-directed mutagenesis experiments, and analyses of changes in downstream TRAF6 expression levels and MAPK signaling pathway activity, direct experimental evidence supporting the interaction mechanism between LRP4 and TRAF6 is still lacking. In subsequent studies, we will further verify whether a direct interaction exists between LRP4 and TRAF6 and reveal its precise molecular mechanism. Additionally, in vivo property analysis and pharmacokinetic studies of periplocin in patients with OA, including parameters such as dose dependency, administration frequency and route, and treatment duration, still need to be further verified using preclinical animal models and human clinical trials.

Conclusions

Functional studies, encompassing a spectrum of cell-based assays, as well as an in vivo animal model provided robust support for the potential clinical application of periplocin as a novel therapeutic agent for patients with OA. Furthermore, an integrated approach combining molecular docking, DARTS, CETSA, MD simulation, and site-directed mutagenesis has successfully identified LRP4 as the key target for the chondroprotective effects of periplocin. Collectively, this study not only yields novel insights into the pharmacological actions and underlying mechanisms of periplocin in OA pathogenesis but also paves the way for expanding its clinical utility as a promising DMOAD (Fig. 10).

Periplocin targets low density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 and protects against osteoarthritis.
