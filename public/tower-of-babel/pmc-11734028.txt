Urgent Cardiac Surgery for Patient With Floating Thrombus in the Left Ventricle
Authors: Olena K. Gogayeva, Oleksandr O. Nudchenko, Yevhenii V. Aksonov, Anatolii V. Rudenko
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 65-year-old patient was admitted to the Institute with complaints of shortness of breath, palpitation, and limb edema. Comorbidities were type 2 diabetes mellitus, gout, obesity. Echo: left ventricular ejection fraction 22%, left ventricular aneurysm (LVA), floating thrombus 5.5 × 2.35 cm, pulmonary hypertension. Ultrasound screening of the braсhiocephalic arteries revealed thrombotic occlusion of the right internal carotid artery that was confirmed by computed tomography study. Coronary angio: multivessel disease with left anterior descending artery occlusion. EuroSCORE II risk: 14.88%. Operation: coronary artery bypass graft with resection of LVA with thrombectomy (duration 6 hours, aortic cross-clamp 79 minutes, perfusion time 152 minutes). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10. At the 5-month follow-up, the patient had no complaints, left ventricular ejection fraction 42%. This clinical case demonstrates successful surgical treatment of the patient with LVA with floating thrombus and thrombotic occlusion of the right internal carotid artery. The key to success in the treatment is careful planning of perioperative management by the heart team and stability of hemodynamic parameters.

One of the life-threatening complications of acute myocardial infarction (MI) is left ventricular aneurysm (LVA). History of Presentation

A 65-year-old patient was admitted to the Institute with complaints of shortness of breath during physical activity, palpitation, and limb edema.

Past Medical History

Past medical history included cardiac asthma 3 weeks ago, an MI 11 years ago (2013), and arterial hypertension for >20 years (maximum blood pressure [BP] 200/120 mm Hg, average BP 135/80 mm Hg). Comorbidities included type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity I stage (body mass index 32.7 kg/m2), metabolic syndrome (waist circumference 124 cm), and gout.

Physical Examination

On admission, BP 160/90 mm Hg in the right arm, 150/90 mm Hg in the left arm, heart rate (HR) 80 beats/min, SaO2 96%. Heart auscultation: heart tones are rhythmic, aortosclerosis, moderate mitral insufficiency with increasing of systolic noise on the left side, moderate tricuspid insufficiency and accentuated second tone over the pulmonary artery. Lung auscultation: decreased breath sounds over the left lower lung field. Hepatomegaly +2 cm, limbs edema.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (ECG) on admission: sinus rhythm, HR 91 beats/min, PQ 0.16 seconds”. Chronic coronary insufficiency: postinfarction scar of the anterior-septal-apical area of the left ventricle (LV). Ultrasound screening of the braсhiocephalic arteries revealed thrombotic occlusion (Figure 2, Video 2) of the right internal carotid artery (RICA) that was confirmed by computed tomography study.

Coronary angiography found multivessel disease: occlusion of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and subocclusion of the diagonal artery, arteria intermedia, and 1 obtuse marginal artery (OM) (Video 3).

Despite the RICA occlusion, the patient had no neurological deficit and was consulted by a neurologist and neurosurgeon. There were no absolute contraindications for cardiac surgery.

EuroSCORE II risk was 14.88%.

Differential Diagnosis

Taking into account anamnesis of acute MI, postinfarction scar with ECG signs of LVA, severe decrease of contractility of LV due to the widespread postinfarction scar, LVA on echo study, and multivessel disease with LAD occlusion on coronary angiography, we had no doubts of ischemic genesis of thrombosed LVA. Occlusion of the RICA is embolic complication of left ventricle thrombus (LVT), which was described as moving with a threat of separation on echo study.

Management

After heart team discussion, we performed an urgent operation: coronary artery bypass graft with resection of LVA with thrombectomy on-pump. The LV was opened with an 8-cm incision, detected and removed thrombi from the LV cavity (Figure 3), plastic of LV with double-row seam. All coronary arteries were calcified. Coronary artery bypass graft (venous grafts) to 1 OM (Ø 2.1 mm), LAD (Ø 2.4 mm), a.intermedia (Ø 1.9 mm). Flowmetry: 1 OM blood flow rate 64.2 mL/min, resistance 1.89; LAD flow rate 27.1 mL/min, resistance 2.44, a.intermedia blood flow rate 192 mL/min, resistance 0.68. Outcome

The postoperative period was uneventful and systolic BP was maintained at 130 mm Hg. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 with positive dynamic (ejection fraction 25%, EDV 221 mL) (Video 4) without complaints and neurological deficit (signs of partial recanalization of the right internal carotid artery) (Video 5).

Follow-Up

During follow-up 5 months after the operation, the patient lost 15 kg and had no complaints. BP 130/80 mm Hg on both arms, Ps 87 per minute, SaO2 98%. ECG: sinus rhythm, HR 87 beats/min, PQ 0.16 seconds, chronic coronary insufficiency: postinfarction scar of anterior-septal-apical area of the LV (Figure 4). Echo study revealed an increase in LV ejection fraction up to 42%, stroke volume 76 mL, and decrease of EDV 186 mL, end-systolic volume 110 mL, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure 42 mm Hg (Video 6). Mild mitral insufficiency and mild tricuspid insufficiency. Thrombotic occlusion of the RICA remained (Video 7). The patient continues therapy for chronic heart failure in accordance with current recommendations.Figure 4Electrocardiogram 5 Months After the Operation

Discussion

Incidence of LVT can be as high as 15% in patients with ST-segment elevation MI, and up to 25% in patients experiencing an anterior MI. The risk of embolic events from a LVT post-MI ranges from 6.1% to 86% and seems to be greatest in the first 3 months after MI. LVT embolizations more frequently appear in patients of age >68 years, protrusion in the cavity of LV, mobility of the LV walls, area of the thrombus, and recurrence of the thrombosis after anticoagulation therapy, in the case of calcification and laminated thrombus risk of embolization is low. But up to 40% of embolic events can occur when thrombi are neither mobile nor protuberant.1 Leow et al2 showed that the median time from LVT diagnosis to cerebrovascular event was 20.5 days. Analysis of the stroke type showed that 76.5% of patients had cardioembolic disease, followed by 14.7% small vessel disease and 8.8% large artery atherosclerosis.2 Acute ischemic stroke occurred in 11.8% of patients with an LVT who received anticoagulation as compared with 44.1% in those who were not on anticoagulants. Treatment of LVT with vitamin K antagonists for a minimum of 3 to 6 months requires individual bleeding risk calculation. There is no randomized controlled trial that proves the effect of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in LVT, but in small randomized and observational studies and case reports DOACs have been found to be comparable with vitamin K antagonists regarding the safety and efficacy of LVT treatment.3 At the same time, Robinson et al4 demonstrated higher stroke and systemic embolism rates (HR: 2.6–2.7) with DOAC use for an LVT. The recurrence rate of LVT after 6 months of anticoagulation has been reported as high as 18.5%.5 LV thrombectomy is an option for patients with high embolic risk undergoing other open-heart surgery, but the opinion of Massussi et al6 is that the high mortality rate of this approach outweighs the benefits of performing surgery solely for the indication of LVT. According to the American Heart Association, there are insufficient data to recommend surgical excision without other indications for surgery for the treatment of LVT, because of the risks of surgery and lack of supportive data.7 We found only 1 study with data about emergency operation of LVT due to high risk of embolization.8 Our more than 20 years’ experience of coronary artery bypass grafting with resection of postinfarction LVA (mortality rate <1%) allows us to recommend surgical treatment for this category of patients.9, 10, 11, 12

Conclusions

This clinical case demonstrates successful surgical treatment of the patient with LVA with floating thrombus and thrombotic occlusion of RICA. The key to success in the treatment is careful planning of perioperative management by the heart team and stability of hemodynamic parameters.
