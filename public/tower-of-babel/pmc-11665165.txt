Effectiveness of Extended-Release Bupropion and Duloxetine on Pelvic Pain and Sexual Function among Patients with Chronic Prostatitis/Chronic Pelvic Pain
Authors: Shokufe Jaafari, Mitra Molaeinezhad, Mohammad J. Tarrahi, Mohsen R. Moosaei, Katayoun Sahfiei, Hamid Afshar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to compare the efficacy of the two antidepressants, duloxetine and bupropion, on pelvic pain in patients with chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS).Materials and Methods:This single‐blind clinical trial was conducted on 68 CP/CPPS patients. The patients with CP/CPPS received either an extended-release formulation of 150 mg/day bupropion (bupropion group) or duloxetine 30 mg/day for 12 weeks (duloxetine group). The pain, depression (using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)), quality of life (QoL), and sexual dysfunction scores were evaluated and compared before the intervention and after 4, 12, and 16 weeks of intervention in both groups.Results:The pain score in the duloxetine group was significantly lower than the bupropion group after 12 and 16 weeks of intervention (P value <0.05). The mean HADS and QoL scores at baseline and after 4, 12, and 16 weeks of intervention had insignificant differences between the two groups (P value >0.05) but improved in both groups. After 4 and 12 weeks of intervention, sexual dysfunction scores in the duloxetine group were higher than those in the bupropion group (P value <0.05).Conclusions:Depression and anxiety scores were significantly reduced in both groups. Sexual function was improved in the bupropion group compared with the duloxetine group, while pain scores improved significantly in the duloxetine group. Therefore, proposing the appropriate treatment based on the experienced complications of CP/CPPS is the key element of the management of these patients; however, further studies are required to compare the long-term effects of these two regimens on CP/CPPS patients’ concerns.

INTRODUCTION

According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) prostatitis classification system, prostatitis is divided into four categories: acute bacterial, chronic bacterial, chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS), and asymptomatic inflammatory.[1]

This type of CP/CPPS is more common in younger and middle-aged men. It is the most common urological disease in males aged <50 years, with a prevalence of 2.5–16%.[2]

It manifests as chronic pain in a variety of areas, such as perineum, penis, testicles, and pelvic floor muscles. In addition, CP/CPPS patients may complain of burning urination, painful ejaculation, and slow urine flow. CP/CPPS can severely impair the quality of life (QoL) of these patients.[23] Additionally, it often impacts sexual function, leading to negative behavioral and social consequences. The exact cause of this disease is not yet completely understood, and thus, it is incredibly difficult to treat the patients diagnosed and there is no definitive treatment for CP/CPPS. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), alpha-blockers, antibiotics, and gabapentin can be used to alleviate CP/CPPS-induced symptoms, but most of them have not been effective in treatment.[45] Today, antidepressants, such as doxepin, amitriptyline, desipramine, and duloxetine, can be used for pain relief in various patients and are considered to have satisfactory outcomes. These sedative medications exert their effects by altering the neurotransmitter receptors or ion channels.[67] Duloxetine is a selective serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) proven effective in treating depression, diabetic neuropathy, chronic musculoskeletal pain, and fibromyalgia. It can also help alleviate pain in patients with CP/CPPS.[89] However, sexual dysfunction is one of the most common side effects of prescribed antidepressants, such as duloxetine. Therefore, long-term duloxetine use in CP/CPPS patients can reduce their QoL and also cause sexual dysfunction.[10] For this reason, researchers have focused on other antidepressants, such as bupropion, which appears to act through norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibition, with no effect on serotonin. Several studies have indicated that this drug can reduce chronic neuropathic pain.[11] According to some studies, it not only has low side effects but also improves sexual function.[11]

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the efficacy of the two antidepressants, duloxetine and bupropion, on pelvic pain reduction and improvement of both sexual function and QoL in patients with CP/CPPS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single-blind clinical trial was conducted from November 2019 to February 2020 on men with CP/CPPS referred from the urology clinic of Noor Hospital affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences to the Khorshid Psychosexual Clinic. From an initial sample of 78 patients, 68 male patients, who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria based on a clinical interview by a psychiatrist based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), were randomized to participate in the trial, after obtaining written informed consent [Figure 1].

Consort flowchart

Male patients with CP/CPPS were considered eligible in case they met the following criteria: being married, being 30–50 years old, having complaints of pelvic pain for at least 3 months, not using antibiotics and alpha-blockers, lack of urinary tract infection, urethral stricture, prior pelvic surgery, previous or accompanying history of urinary tract cancer, lack of underlying diseases, such as liver or kidney disease, no risk of major depressive disorders, psychosis or mania, nonuse of drugs affecting the sexual function and interacting with duloxetine or bupropion (including antidepressants’ interactions, alpha-blockers, and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor drugs, such as finasteride), no alcohol consumption, and not using sedative medicines or opioids or stimulants. The study exclusion criteria were specified as follows: drug intolerance due to its complications, failure to follow-up treatment, and participant's decision to stop continuing the study at any time.

Intervention method

After obtaining the ethics code (ethical code: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1398.350) from the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, obtaining the clinical trial code (approval code: IRCT20191205045621N1), and getting written consent from all participants, they were randomly divided into two groups of 34 patients using the random block method with two blocks.

The two groups are the group receiving bupropion (group A) and the group receiving duloxetine (group B). Patients were treated appropriately in cases of any complications resulting from the study. Information about dropped-out patients was recorded and reported, and the patients were followed up by telephone calls during the study. Group A received bupropion sustained release (SR) of 150 mg/day (made by Dr. Abidi Pharmaceutical Laboratory Co., Tehran, Iran), and since the end of the first week, the weekly dose has been increased based on symptom improvement. If the patients tolerated it, the dose was increased to a maximum of 300 mg daily (two 150 mg tablets). The participants in group B received duloxetine 30 mg once a day, and one week after treatment, the dose was increased to 60 mg daily based on the patients’ tolerance and symptom improvement. After the 12-week duration of the intervention, the drugs were gradually discontinued for 2 weeks.

Due to the different forms of the two drugs, it was not possible to blind the researcher, but the patients and the data analyst were not aware of the types of the two groups.

Data collection tools

First, the baseline characteristics of the patients (i.e. age and duration of disease) were recorded. The 12-item Short-Form Survey (SF-12) questionnaire was used to assess QoL, and the Short Form of the McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) were applied to assess the pain in the patients. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to detect anxiety and depressive disorders in the patients. The Arizona Sexual Experiences Scale (ASEX) was employed to assess their sexual function. The questionnaires were completed by the subjects at baseline and after 4, 12, and 16 weeks of intervention in follow-up sessions.

SF-MPQ and VAS

SF-MPQ was first developed in 1971 by Melzack.[12] This scale is a widely used self-reporting measure of pain to evaluate and measure a person experiencing significant pain over time and to determine the effectiveness of any intervention. SF-MPQ is comprised of 15 words (11 sensory and four affective) from the original MPQ.[13] VAS is a segmented numeric scale, a horizontal bar or line in which a respondent selects a whole number (0–10 integers) that best reflects the intensity of their pain.[14] A higher score means more pain perception. The revised Persian version of the SF-MPQ-2 has shown acceptable reliability and validity in a study by Tanhaee et al. (2012).[15] The validity of this questionnaire is found to be above 0.85.[1617]

SF-12

This 12-item scale was used to assess QoL. Higher scores indicate better QoL. The reliability and validity of this questionnaire are found to be above 0.70.[1819]

HADS

The HADS questionnaire measures depression in seven questions and anxiety in seven other questions. The total score of HADS ranges from 0 to 21, and higher scores indicate more severe anxiety and depressive disorders. The reliability and validity of this questionnaire have been confirmed by previous studies.[2021]

ASEX

ASEX is a five-item self-report questionnaire, designed to measure the major components of sex performance, including sexual arousal, sexual drive, ability to reach an orgasm, and orgasmic satisfaction. The total score ranges from 5 to 30, and higher scores indicate worse sexual function. The Persian version of the ASEX demonstrates high validity.[2223]

Statistical analysis

Finally, the collected data were entered into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. The mean, standard deviation (SD), frequency, and percentage were used to display the data. The results of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test showed that the data are normally distributed. Levene's test confirmed the homogeneity of the variance. For statistical analysis, an independent-samples t-test was applied to compare the mean of age, disease duration, and initial measurements between the two groups and an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test was used to compare the mean of variables with the control of pretest variables. Also, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the changes in the mean of the variables for each of the two groups during the intervention. A P value lower than 0.05 was assumed to be significant.

DISCUSSION

The main purpose of the current study was to investigate the effect of bupropion and duloxetine on pain, QoL, and sexual dysfunction scores in CP/CPPS patients. Our results suggest that duloxetine was more effective in reducing pelvic pain in patients with CP/CPPS than bupropion, but no significant difference was observed between the two groups after 16 weeks of intervention. Our results were in accordance with a study conducted by Baltenberger et al.,[24] which showed that duloxetine, as one of the antidepressants, should be used as a first-line agent in the treatment of neuropathic pain. Also, due to the more complications, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are suggested to be considered as second-line treatment. The effectiveness of other treatment options, such as bupropion, is very limited.[24]

Our findings were also consistent with other studies, suggesting that duloxetine is effective in reducing pelvic pain in patients with CP/CPPS. For example, the results of a study by Zhang et al.[25] indicated that duloxetine significantly reduced pelvic pain in patients with CP/CPPS compared with sertraline. Also, another study showed that duloxetine reduced pelvic pain and improved QoL in these patients compared with the controls.[9]

The findings of the current study about the favorable effects of duloxetine in reducing the symptoms of CPPS are most likely related to the mechanism of action of duloxetine and the dual inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake at spinal and/or supraspinal levels.[26] It can also control voiding dysfunctions and urinary incontinence and affect Onuf's nucleus in the sacral spinal cord.[2627]

However, some studies have demonstrated that bupropion can be effective in reducing neuropathic pain. These studies have shown that bupropion SR (150–300 mg daily) is effective and well tolerated for the treatment of neuropathic pain.[2829] The results of the above studies have also shown the efficacy of these two antidepressants in reducing pain in CP/CPPS patients, which is in accordance with our results. However, it seems that the efficacy of bupropion and duloxetine in reducing depression and improving sexual function and QoL in patients with CP/CPPS should also be investigated to achieve the appropriate drug selection for pain control in these patients.

We also found that duloxetine significantly reduced depression in patients with CP/CPPS after 16 weeks, while bupropion was more effective in reducing anxiety. In general, bupropion and duloxetine significantly decreased the mean HADS score (including depression and anxiety) in these patients, but an insignificant difference was observed between the two groups. Consistent with our results, previous studies have shown the same effect of these two drugs on the drug-specific response or remission in patients with resistant depression who did not respond to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), so that the risk of depression was reduced by more than half.[3031] In addition, the findings of a systematic review by Patel et al.[32] revealed that bupropion use as a sole or co-prescribed antidepressant can be considered, particularly if weight gain or sexual dysfunction is, or is likely to be, the significant problems.

Similarly, our study also showed that bupropion use was more effective in improving the sexual function of CP/CPPS patients after 4 weeks compared with duloxetine. Although duloxetine can cause sexual dysfunction in CP/CPPS patients, continuing the treatment might be useful for the improvement of their sexual function after 16 weeks.

Our results might suggest an improvement in QoL, which was associated with duloxetine and bupropion use in both groups during the time of intervention, though no difference was found. It can be expected that using these two drugs might be effective in improving QoL among CP/CPPS patients due to a suggested close association between the reduction in pain, depression, improvement in sexual function, and QoL. However, our study duration was not long enough to show these associations.

Most of the prescribed antidepressants are associated with sexual dysfunctions.[33] Nonetheless, duloxetine is one of the antidepressants suggested to reduce sexual dysfunction in patients as compared with other drugs, such as paroxetine, nefazodone, and citalopram.[343536] It seems that long-term use of duloxetine may change the severity of these complications.[37]

However, numerous studies have shown the favorable effect of bupropion in reducing sexual dysfunction in patients who were either switched from their previous SSRI/SNRI treatment to bupropion monotherapy or add-on regimen.[323839] Based on a survey of psychiatrists and the results of other studies, bupropion can also be used to treat SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction or in the treatment of hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD).[40]

According to previous studies, males with CP/CPPS appear more likely to experience sexual dysfunction compared with the general population,[41] which deteriorates with the duration of the disease.[42] Our results might suggest that choosing the appropriate medication that favorably could influence the severity of sexual dysfunction and improve QoL in CP/CPPS seems to be the best treatment approach. Our study results also indicated that adding bupropion or duloxetine to the treatment protocol of CP/CPPS can lead to the improvement of depression, anxiety symptoms, and pain, which could favorably lead to better sexual function and QoL among these patients.

Therefore, one of the strengths of this study was that it could help treat these patients with the aim of reducing their pelvic pain and other complaints, such as depression, anxiety, and sexual dysfunction, and consequently improving their QoL.

The current study had a number of limitations. First, the number of patients in both groups was limited. Second, the duration of treatment was limited so the failure of long-term follow-up is a point of limitation in our study. Therefore, it is recommended to do future studies with a larger sample size and a longer follow-up duration to achieve more definitive results.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite the limitations, our results supported that depression and anxiety scores were significantly reduced with bupropion and duloxetine use after 16 weeks of intervention. Pain scores significantly improved in the duloxetine group with the progression of the intervention until 16 weeks in comparison with the bupropion group. However, sexual function was improved in the bupropion group compared with the duloxetine group after 4 and 12 weeks of intervention. Therefore, proposing the appropriate treatment based on the experienced complications of CP/CPPS is the key element of the management of these patients; however, further studies are required to compare the long-term effects of these two regimens on CP/CPPS patients’ concerns.
