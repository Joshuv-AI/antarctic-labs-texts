Towards Tight Bounds for Estimating Degree Distribution in Streaming and Query Models
Arijit Bishnu, Debarshi Chanda, Gopinath Mishra

Abstract
The degree distribution of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) , | V | = n \left|{V}\right|=n , | E | = m \left|{E}\right|=m is one of the most fundamental objects of study in the analysis of graphs as it embodies relationship among entities. In particular, an important derived distribution from degree distribution is the complementary cumulative degree histogram (ccdh). The ccdh is a fundamental summary of graph structure, capturing, for each threshold d d , the number of vertices with degree at least d d . For approximating ccdh, we consider the ( ε 𝒟 , ε ℛ ) (\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}) -BiCriteria Multiplicative Approximation, which allows for controlled multiplicative slack in both the domain and the range. There are only two works in sublinear models prior to us that considered ccdh – one in streaming setup [Simpson et al. (ICDM 2015)] and the other in property testing setup with query models [Eden et al. (WWW 2018)]. However, the exact complexity of the problem, including lower bounds, was not known and had been posed as an open problem in WOLA 2019 [Sublinear.info, Problem 98]. In this work, we first design an algorithm that can approximate ccdh if a suitable vertex sample and an edge sample can be obtained and thus, the algorithm is independent of any sublinear model. Next, we show that in the streaming and query models such samples can be obtained, and (almost) settle the complexity of the problem across both the sublinear models. These massive graphs are ubiquitous wherever there are entities and relationships among those entities like in web-pages and hyperlinks between web-pages, IP addresses and connection links between addresses, social network entitites and their friendships, etc.

Given complete access to the graph in the RAM model, degree distribution can be solved in linear time and space. In massive graph scenario, such an access is almost ruled out. Problem Setup

In this section, we formally define the problem of computing the complementary cumulative degree histogram, the models of computation used and the results.

2.1
Problem Details

We consider simple graphs
G=\left({V,E}\right)
, with
\left|{V}\right|=n
, and
\left|{E}\right|=m
. For any vertex
v\in V
, we denote
Neigh(v)=\left\{{u~|~\left({u,v}\right)\in E}\right\}
to be the set of neighbors of the vertex
v
, and
d_{v}=\left|{Neigh(v)}\right|
to be the degree of the vertex
v
. The degree distribution of a graph with
n
vertices is the sequence of numbers
n_{0}
,
n_{1}
,
\ldots
,
n_{n}
, where
n_{d}=\left|{\left\{{v\in V~|~d_{v}=d}\right\}}\right|
is the number of vertices in
G
with degree
d
. Let
V_{a}
be the set of active vertices, i.e., vertices with degree at least
1
, and denote
n_{a}=\left|{V_{a}}\right|
. Based on the findings in
Clauset et al. 2009
, both the prior works in streaming
Simpson et al. 2015
and query
Eden et al. 2018
use the notion of
Complementary Cumulative Degree Histogram
(ccdh) instead of
degree histogram
because ccdh is more immune to noise and is also monotonically decreasing
Clauset et al. 2009
. We also follow suit. The notion of ccdh formally defined below, is based on the number of vertices of degree at least
d
.

Definition 1

(Complementary Cumulative Degree Histogram)
.

Given a graph
G
, let
{C}:{\left[{n}\right]}\rightarrow{\left[{n}\right]}
be the complementary cumulative degree histogram, defined as:

\displaystyle C(d)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sum_{r\geq d}n_{r}=\left|{\left\{{v\in V~|~d_{v}\geq d}\right\}}\right|

\widehat{C}(d)
will denote the approximation of
C(d)
.
Ideally, a ccdh value (or approximate ccdh) for a degree
d
should be specified as
C(d)
(or
\widehat{C}(d)
), but at times we would drop the dependence on
d
and just write
C
(or
\widehat{C}
). We want to approximate ccdh in terms of the following bi-criteria multiplicative approximation guarantee defined in
Eden et al. 2018
:

Definition 2

(
(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}})
-BiCriteria Multiplicative Approximation)
.

Given a monotonically decreasing function
{f}:{\mathcal{D}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}
, with
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we define an approximation
\hat{f}
to be
(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}})
-BiCriteria Multiplicative Approximation if:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{D},(1-\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}})f((1+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}})x)\leq\hat{f}(x)\leq(1+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}})f((1-\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}})x)

The bi-criteria formulation allows for two approximation factors,
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}
that are for the domain and range of the function, respectively. The monotonicity condition on the underlying function is to ensure that
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}}
-based domain relaxation is meaningful. A similar criteria can also be defined for monotonically increasing functions by reversing the signs of the domain relaxation. For simplicity, we denote this criterion as
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA henceforth.

Note that this criterion, while
weaker
than multiplicative approximation, is
stronger
than standard distributional norms. The choice of this distance captures the closeness of the graph structure properly. In contrast to standard norms like
\ell_{1}
-distance, it does not allow us to discount small mass segments entirely which may contain high degree vertices critical to the graph structure. A simple motivating example is to consider a matching and a star graph, which would be close in
\ell_{1}
distance, but is not an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA of each other.

We also introduce the
h
-index formally:

Definition 3

(h-index)
.

Given a graph, we define its h-index, denoted
h
, as:

\displaystyle h=\max\left\{{d|\left|{\left\{{v\in V|d_{v}\geq d}\right\}}\right|\geq d,{d\in\left[{n}\right]}}\right\}

Essentially,
h
-index is the maximum degree
d
such that the number of vertices with degree greater than or equal to
d
is at least
d
. The
h
-index for a graph indicates the number of vertices that have a high degree. Notice that
h\geq\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}
. Thus,
h
-index is indicative of the density of the graph.

We assume
c
to be a large enough universal constant in this work. By high probability, we mean the probability to be at least
1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{n^{c}}}
. We use
\widetilde{O}
notation to hide polynomial dependencies on
\log n
.

2.2
The Computing Models

2.2.1
Streaming Model

In the streaming model, or the sublinear-space setting, we consider the edge-arrival model of graph streaming. In the insertion-only variant of the model, we are presented with a stream of edges
\left\{{\sigma_{i}}\right\}_{i\in[m]}
of a graph
G
, where each edge arrives in an arbitrary order. In the turnstile variant of the model, deletion of edges over the stream is allowed over and above addition. Our objective is to output
\widehat{C}
, an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA to the true ccdh
C
of the graph
G
, using as small space as possible.

2.2.2
Query Model

In the sublinear-time setting,
G
can be accessed with the following queries:

•

\texttt{Degree}{(v)}
: Given a vertex
v\in V
, return
d_{v}
.

•

\texttt{Neighbor}(v,i)
: Given a vertex
v\in V
and an index
i\in\left[{n}\right]
, return the
i
-th vertex
u\in Neigh(v)
, if it exists; otherwise, return
\perp

•

\texttt{EdgeExist}(u,v)
: Given two vertices
u,v\in V
, return
1
if
(u,v)\in E
, and
0
, otherwise.

•

Random Edge
: Returns an edge
e\in E
uniformly at random.

The
Degree
,
Neighbor
, and
EdgeExist
queries, collectively known as local queries are well known in the query model
Goldreich 2010
. The
Random Edge
query has been considered in more recent works
Aliakbarpour et al. 2018
;
Assadi et al. 2019
and are similar to the edge sampling framework for graph sampling
Ribeiro and Towsley 2012
;
Stumpf and Wiuf 2005
;
Leskovec and Faloutsos 2006
. This query is well motivated for large graphs as they are generally stored as edge files
Leskovec and Krevl 2014
. We consider both non-adaptive and adaptive models for the graph property testing
Goldreich and Wigderson 2022
. In the non-adaptive model, all the queries made by the algorithm are pre-determined based on the input parameters, and in particular, does not depend on the subsequent queries. In the adaptive model, a query depends on the prior sequence of queries.
The objective in both cases is to output
\widehat{C}
, an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA of the true ccdh
C
using as minimum queries as possible.

3
Technical Overview

In this section, we provide a high level overview of the main technical aspects of this work.

3.1
Upper Bound

Let us think of the ccdh distribution in two parts —
head
containing the lower values of degree, and
tail
containing the higher values of degree. A few observations on the properties of ccdh are in order:

Property-1:

Because of the monotonically non-increasing nature of ccdh, the head has high values for ccdh, while the tail has low values.

Property-2:

The tail of the ccdh consists of vertices with relatively higher degrees.

We divide the ccdh distribution into these two parts via two thresholds. We define the head of the ccdh distribution to be those values of degree
d
that have the corresponding
C\left({d}\right)
values larger than a threshold
\tau_{C}
. In contrast, we define the tail to be the values of degree
d
that are higher than a threshold
\tau_{d}
.

Higher value of ccdh for a degree
d
indicates that given a random sample of vertices, the number of vertices with degree greater than or equal to
d
is relatively high. Thus, we can obtain a good approximation of the ccdh values for the head parts through an appropriately sized random sample of vertices. If we were measuring the approximation in terms of some
\ell_{p}
norm, that would have been sufficient to give us a good approximation. However, it does not satisfy the
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA guarantee we are aiming for.

The approximation error we have for the head is a multiplicative approximation. We have not yet leveraged the domain approximation relaxation. To obtain a domain approximation for the ccdh, the goal would be to obtain an approximation on the degrees of the contributing vertices. Because of Property-2 stated above, if we obtain a random sample of edges from the graph, the number of edges with tail vertices as one of the endpoints should be relatively high. We leverage this fact to obtain an approximation of the degrees of the high degree vertices through an appropriately sized random sample of edges. However, this is not sufficient to ensure a domain-wise approximation as the degrees of the vertices with low degrees might be overestimated. To address this issue, we show that the vertices with degrees much lower than the degree threshold
\tau_{d}
for tail vertices, does not have an approximate degree higher than the same threshold.

Finally, to establish our result, we combine the range-wise approximation for the head, and the domain-wise approximation for the tail to obtain an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA for the entire ccdh spectrum. We show that setting both of the thresholds,
\tau_{C}
, and
\tau_{d}
to a constant factor of the h-index
h
covers all values of
d
. We then devise techniques to implement these ideas in the streaming model as well as the query model to obtain our final algorithms.

In the adaptive and multi-pass streaming scenarios, we can sample active vertices directly through a random sample of edges. We leverage this idea to obtain a better algorithm for sparse graphs in these settings as only active vertices contribute meaningfully to ccdh.

3.2
Lower Bounds

For both streaming and query model lower bounds, we establish a reduction to approximate ccdh from the problem of set disjointness in communication complexity. For the streaming model, we appropriately generate edges from the inputs of Alice and Bob separately. For the query model, we use the framework proposed by
Eden and Rosenbaum 2018
to construct an appropriate embedding.

The graph constructed for the reduction in both models has a broadly similar structure. For the general graph case, we establish the lower bound by constructing simple gadgets for each string element with the overall graph having h-index
1
. For the
h
-index sensitive case, we use a graph construction similar to the gadgets with each vertex in the gadget is replaced by a set of vertices . The details are in Section
6
. In the query model, we establish a way to simulate
Random Edge
even when the degree of vertices does not remain same across all graph instances.

6.1
Communication Problem

In this section, we introduce communication complexity briefly, with a particular focus on the set-disjointness problem. We show our lower bounds in both streaming and query model using reductions from set-disjointness .

In the two-party communication complexity setting, there are two parties: Alice and Bob. They wish to compute a function
{f}:{\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{M}\times\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{M}}\rightarrow{\left\{{0,1}\right\}}
. Alice is given an input string
x\in\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{M}
. Bob is given
y\in\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{M}
. While the parties know the function
f
, Alice has no knowledge of
y
, and Bob has no knowledge of
x
. They communicate bits according to a pre-specified protocol. The goal is to compute the value
f(x,y)
. In the randomized setting, the parties have access to a shared source of randomness. This is a common random string of arbitrary length which is often referred to as public randomness. A randomized protocol is said to compute
f
if, for every input pair
(x,y)
, the correct value
f(x,y)
is the output with probability at least
2/3
. The (randomized) communication complexity of the function
f
is the minimum number of bits exchanged in the worst case, over all inputs, by any such (randomized) protocol that computes
f
. This is over all randomized protocols with access to public randomness that computes
f
. For more detailed exposition on communication complexity, the reader may refer to
Kushilevitz and Nisan 1996
;
Rao and Yehudayoff 2020
. Now, we formally define the function of set-disjointness (denoted
DISJ

Definition 15

(Set-Disjointness(
DISJ

.

Given two strings

\texttt{DISJ}_{M}
function as taking the value
\texttt{DISJ}_{M}(x,y)=1
if

\texttt{DISJ}_{M}(x,y)=0
if
\sum_{i\in[M]}x_{i}y_{i}\neq 0

In simpler terms, the strings denote membership of elements in a set. Given
M

denotes the presence of the
i

evaluates to

if the corresponding sets of

We specifically consider the promise version of disjointness,

Lemma 16

An algorithm that constructs an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA of ccdh for any
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}}<\frac{1}{2}
for all graphs will be able to distinguish between these two cases. Now, we discuss how to implement the queries in the communication model:

•

Degree
:
Given a vertex
j=5i+k
,
0\leq k\leq 4
, checking
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
suffices to know the degree. Therefore, we need at most
2
bits of communication to implement the
Degree
query.

•

Random Edge
:
Each edge in the graph has an one-to-one correspondence with each element in the two strings. Choose one index out of the
[2M]
indices at random and communicate that bit to obtain the corresponding edge. Hence, we need exactly
1
bit of communication to implement the
Random Edge
given access to shared random string to Alice and Bob.

•

EdgeExist
:
Each vertex-pair has at most one possible way of having an edge, defined by corresponding
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
. Communicating this bit suffices to decide whether the given vertex-pair contain an edge or not. Thus, communicating
1
bit suffices to implement
EdgeExist
query.

•

Neighbor
:
Each vertex have one-possible neighbour, simulate
EdgeExist
for that vertex. Return it if there, otherwise return null. Hence, communicating
1
bit suffices to implement the
Neighbor
query.

As described above, all the queries can be implemented using
O\left({{1}}\right)
bits of communication. Thus, any algorithm that computes an
\left({\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)
-BMA of ccdh for any
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{D}}<\frac{1}{2}
requires to make
\Omega\left({{M}}\right)
queries. Given,
n=5M
, we have the stated lower bound.
∎
