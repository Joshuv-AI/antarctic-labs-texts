MRI markers of brain fluid dynamics link reduced blood–brain barrier water exchange rate to disease severity in CADASIL
Authors: Yunqing Ying, Rong Zhou, Jianqi Li, Qi Yang, Weijun Tang, Xingfeng Shao, Xin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReduced water exchange rate (kw) across the blood–brain barrier has been linked to disease severity in cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL), but the mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. We investigated whether kw is associated with indirect MRI-based markers of brain fluid dynamics and whether these markers statistically explain the relationship between kw and disease severity.Patients and MethodsSeventy genetically confirmed CADASIL patients underwent clinical, cognitive and multimodal MRI assessments. Whole-brain kw was measured using diffusion-prepared pseudo-continuous arterial spin labelling. Brain fluid dynamics were assessed using indirect MRI-derived markers, including white matter free water (WM FW) fraction, diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, choroid plexus (CP) volume and enlarged perivascular space burden.ResultsLower whole-brain kw was associated with higher WM FW fraction (β = −0.419, P-corrected = .012), lower DTI-ALPS index (β = 0.431, P-corrected = .028) and larger CP volume (β = −0.510, corrected P < .001). Lower kw was also associated with smaller brain volume and slower processing speed. Magnetic resonance imaging markers of brain fluid dynamics were related to lacune count, cerebral microbleed count, WM hyperintensity volume and brain volume. Exploratory mediation analyses identified significant indirect associations between kw and brain volume through WM FW fraction, DTI-ALPS index and CP volume.ConclusionsThese cross-sectional findings indicate that lower kw is associated with MRI measures consistent with altered brain fluid dynamics and with greater disease burden in CADASIL. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to determine the temporal and biological relationships among these measures.Keywords: blood–brain barrier, brain fluid dynamics, CADASIL, MRI, water exchange rate

Introduction

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is the most common monogenic cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) and is caused by pathogenic variants in NOTCH3.1 Its pathological hallmark is the accumulation of mutant NOTCH3 extracellular domain and granular osmiophilic material (GOM) within the walls of small arteries and arterioles.2 These changes are accompanied by vascular smooth muscle cell degeneration, arteriolar wall thickening and fibrosis and impaired vascular reactivity, ultimately contributing to brain lesions.2 In addition to arteriolar wall pathology, abnormalities of the neurovascular unit, including endothelial cells, basement membranes, perivascular astrocytic endfeet and aquaporin-4 (AQP4) polarisation, may contribute to altered blood–brain barrier (BBB) function, which is also considered to play a vital role in the pathogenesis of CADASIL.3

The BBB is a dynamic and selective interface that regulates molecular exchange between the bloodstream and the central nervous system. Beyond solute transport and barrier integrity, water exchange across the capillary wall may be relevant to the coupling between the vascular compartment, interstitial fluid and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).4 The BBB water exchange rate (kw), measured using diffusion-prepared pseudo-continuous arterial spin labelling (DP-pCASL), has emerged as a promising indicator of BBB dysfunction.4 Recent studies have shown that kw is significantly reduced in CADASIL and is associated with greater disease severity.5–7 Nevertheless, kw should not be interpreted as a direct measure of BBB permeability or of a single cellular component of the BBB. Rather, it may reflect the integrated effects of neurovascular-unit alterations on transcapillary water exchange.

The mechanisms linking lower kw to CADASIL progression remain unclear. A plausible, but unproven, link is altered brain fluid transport. Perivascular spaces (PVSs) provide anatomical pathways for CSF–interstitial fluid exchange, while astrocytic endfeet and polarised AQP4 channels may facilitate water movement at the vascular–parenchymal interface.8 Pathological studies have reported altered AQP4 distribution and enlarged PVS in CADASIL.9 In addition, NOTCH3-related arteriolar fibrosis and loss of vascular smooth muscle cells may reduce arterial pulsatility, a proposed driver of perivascular fluid transport. Thus, impaired BBB water exchange and altered brain fluid dynamics may represent related consequences of small-vessel and neurovascular-unit dysfunction (Figure 1).

The structural foundation of blood–brain barrier water exchange and brain fluid dynamics. BBB is composed of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, surrounded by a basement membrane and perivascular astrocytic endfeet enriched with AQP4 water channels. The brain fluid dynamics comprise of 3 main components: CSF influx along periarterial spaces, CSF–ISF exchange within the brain parenchyma and ISF efflux along perivenous spaces. Magnetic resonance imaging markers potentially related to aspects of brain fluid dynamics include increased CP volume, expanded PVS, elevated extracellular FW and reduced DTI-ALPS index. While direct measuring techniques using fluorescent tracers or contrast agents have demonstrated brain fluid movement, that is glymphatic system, in animal studies,10 their application in humans is invasive and remains limited. Several non-invasive MRI measures have therefore been proposed as indirect surrogates associated with aspects of brain fluid dynamics, including white matter (WM) free water (FW) fraction, diffusion tensor imaging along perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, choroid plexus (CP) volume and PVS volumes.11–13 Importantly, none of these measures directly quantifies glymphatic transport or CSF–interstitial fluid flux. Choroid plexus is a key regulator in CSF production and larger CP volume has been associated with reduced CSF turnover and impaired glymphatic clearance. Increased FW fraction may reflect fluid outflow blockages and greater extracellular water content, while enlarged PVS may be associated with impaired perivascular fluid transport. The DTI-ALPS index assumes that it measures the diffusivity capacity of the perivenous space surrounding the deep medullary vein around the lateral ventricles.14 All these MRI markers have been associated with SVD severity.13,15,16

To address this gap, we investigated the associations between kw and a panel of MRI-derived, indirect markers associated with brain fluid dynamics in genetically confirmed CADASIL. We further examined whether these markers statistically mediated the associations between kw and imaging or clinical measures of disease severity. Rather than establishing a direct measure of glymphatic function or causal pathways, this study aimed to characterise the in vivo relationships among BBB water exchange, MRI correlates of brain fluid dynamics and disease burden in CADASIL.

Patients and methods

Study population

Patients with CADASIL were recruited between May 2021 and May 2025 from the outpatient clinics and inpatient wards of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy patients were included based on a confirmed genetic diagnosis of a typical cysteine mutation in the NOTCH3 gene and age ≥ 18 years. Demographic and clinical data collected included age, gender, years of education and vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and smoking history). Functional disability was assessed using the mRS, with scores ≥ 2 indicating significant disability.

Neuropsychological assessment

A standardised cognitive battery was administered to assess neuropsychological function. In this study, the analysis focused on processing speed, a primary area of cognitive impairment in CADASIL patients.17 The mean z-scores of Trail Making Test A (TMT-A), Stroop Color-Word Test (SCWT) cards I and II, Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) and forward Digit Span Test (DST) were calculated to assess processing speed.18 Test scores were inverted when necessary to ensure that higher scores reflected better cognitive performance.

MRI acquisitions

Brain MR scans were conducted using a Siemens 3 T Prisma MRI system (Erlangen, Germany) with a 64-channel head coil. The water exchange rate across BBB was measured using DP-pCASL as previously reported.5 Other sequences included T1-weighted (T1w) 3D magnetisation prepared rapid gradient echo, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), T2-weighted sampling perfection with application-optimised contrast using different flip angle evolutions, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Detailed parameters of the sequences are provided in Table S1.

Imaging analysis

SVD MRI markers

The SVD MRI markers were defined following the 2023 updated version of Standards for ReportIng Vascular changes on nEuroiamging (STRIVE) guidelines.19 The lacunes on T1 and FLAIR images and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) on SWI images were counted manually by 2 well-trained raters, followed by a consensus meeting. Intra-rater and inter-rater reliability were excellent, that is the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were 0.98 for lacunes and 0.97 for CMBs. White matter hyperintensity (WMH) masks were segmented on FLAIR images using the lesion segmentation toolbox and then manually refined by a neurologist. White matter hyperintensity volumes were normalised to the total intracranial volume (TIV). Brain volumes were determined by the ratio between the sum of grey matter (GM) and WM volumes and TIV volume, served as a marker of brain atrophy.

Whole brain kw

Diffusion-prepared-pCASL data were postprocessed offline using the LOFT BBB Toolbox (http://www.loft-lab.org/index-5.html), following previously described procedures.5 In brief, BBB kw was calculated based on the ratio between intra- and extra-vascular compartments of ASL signals at PLD of 1800 ms, after adjusting for arterial transit time and tissue T1. Next, T1w images were registered to the DP-pCASL M0 images20 and the whole brain average kw was calculated based on the overlap between T1w and kw map masks. In this study, we only calculated whole brain average kw because brain fluid dynamics is a brain-wide network and the signal-to-noise ratio constraints with DP-pCASL imaging in regional analysis such as WM.

Free water

Each participant’s raw diffusion data were corrected for susceptibility-induced field distortions with FSL’s topup using the participant’s reverse phase-encoding diffusion scan. Data were skull-stripped, and participant motion and eddy currents were corrected with FSL’s eddy.

Then, the signal of each voxel was fit based on a 2-compartment model, resulting in a FW map and FW-corrected tensor map using Python.21 To minimise partial volume effects from CSF, FW fraction values above the 80% threshold were set to 0. The mean WM FW fraction was extracted from the WM mask generated from T1w images using the CAT12 toolbox in SPM12.

DTI-ALPS index

The DTI-ALPS index was calculated using DTI-Studio (https://www.mristudio.org), based on established protocols.14,22 After pre-processing, diffusivities maps [Dxx, Dyy, Dzz] and colour-coded fractional anisotropy (FA) maps were processed on the software. In each hemisphere, 2 regions of interest (ROIs), with a circle of diameter of 5 mm, were labelled in the association fibres and the projection fibres on the colour-FA map, at the site where the orientation of the deep medullary veins is perpendicular to the ventricle body. Then, the diffusivity values along the x-axis (Dx), y-axis (Dy) and z-axis (Dz) directions of the ROIs on both projection fibres and association fibres were documented as Dxxassoc, Dyyassoc, Dzzassoc, Dxxproj, Dyyproj and Dzzproj, respectively. Diffusion tensor imaging along perivascular space index was calculated by the formula [(Dxxproj + Dxxassoc)/(Dyyproj + Dzzassoc)] for each hemisphere of the brain. The global DTI-ALPS index was calculated as the mean of the left and right hemispheres. The radial asymmetry (λ2/λ3) in DTI has impact on the interpretation of DTI-ALPS index.23 λ2/λ3 of projection fibres (λ2/λ3proj) and association fibres (λ2/λ3assoc) were calculated by the ratio of the mean Dxx to the mean Dyy in the ROIs of bilateral projection fibres and the ratio of the mean Dxx to the mean Dzz in the ROIs of bilateral association fibres, respectively.

PVS rating

Perivascular space was assessed visually on T2-weighted images using a previously established semiquantitative rating approach. We initially explored automated PVS segmentation on pre-processed 3-dimensional T1w images using a Frangi filter-based method. However, visual inspection of the segmentation outputs indicated suboptimal reliability in the present CADASIL cohort. In particular, small lacunes in the basal ganglia could not always be reliably distinguished from PVS, while extensive deep WMHs interfered with PVS identification on T1w images. Given these disease-specific sources of misclassification, automated volumetric measurements were not used for the primary analyses. Enlarged PVS in the basal ganglia (BG) and centrum semiovale (CSO) were rated separately. The rating criteria were as follows: PVS enlargement severity was rated 0 when there were < 10 PVS, rated 1 when there were 10–20 PVS and rated 2 when there were > 20 PVS. The burden of PVS was rated by 2 well-trained raters followed by a consensus meeting. The ICC was 0.79 for BG-PVS and 0.64 for CSO-PVS. We calculated the total PVS score as the sum of BG-PVS and CSO-PVS score to represent the burden of PVS.

Choroid plexus volume

T1-weighted images were segmented automatically using FreeSurfer version 7.4.1. We calculated the mean volumes of left and right choroid plexus and corrected them for TIV.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data were reported as mean ± SD for normally distributed variables and as median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %).

To examine associations between whole brain kw and MRI-based metrics of brain fluid dynamics, general linear models were applied with kw and covariates (age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and smoking history) as independent variables, and each of the 4 MRI indicators of brain fluid movement as dependent variables. General linear models were also applied to assess the relationships of kw and MRI indicators of brain fluid movement with disease severity, adjusting for the same covariates plus education level for processing speed. λ2/λ3proj and λ2/λ3assoc were residualised against ALPS index to remove their co-linear effects and added in the general linear models for the analysis of DTI-ALPS index. To control for potential confounding effects of SVD on processing speed and mRS, SVD imaging markers were additionally included in the general linear regression models. Standardised regression coefficients (β) with 95% CIs were reported for continuous outcomes, and odds ratios with 95% CIs were reported for binary outcomes. Missing data were not imputed. To assess potential informative missingness in processing speed measurements, clinical and imaging characteristics were compared between participants with and without available processing speed data.

Prespecified exploratory mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro (version 4.2) for SPSS to examine whether the associations between kw and disease severity were statistically shared with MRI markers of brain fluid dynamics. Because all variables were measured cross-sectionally, these models were not intended to establish temporal ordering or causal mediation. Indirect effects were estimated using bootstrap CIs. The proportion mediated was calculated using the unstandardised indirect and total effects from each mediation model as (indirect effect/total effect) × 100%. Significantly associated MRI indicators of brain fluid dynamics were tested as mediators between kw and disease severity, adjusting for age, gender and vascular risk factors.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0, with a significance level set at a 2-tailed P < .05. For analyses examining the associations between kw and 4 MRI measures related to brain fluid dynamics, Bonferroni correction was applied across 4 comparisons. For analyses of disease-severity measures, each clinical or imaging outcome was considered a separate hypothesis family, within which 5 MRI markers were tested; accordingly, Bonferroni correction was performed for 5 comparisons within each outcome-specific family.

Results

Subject characteristics

Patient demographics, vascular risk factors, imaging features and cognitive performance are provided in Table 1. A total of 70 CADASIL patients were included, with a mean age of 46.4 years (SD 11.4); 50% of subjects were male. Among MRI-based brain fluid dynamics indicators, FW and DTI-ALPS data were unavailable for 5 subjects due to incomplete DTI scans. For cognitive assessments, 15 subjects lacked processing speed measurements due to severe cognitive decline, which prevented completion of the tests. Subjects without processing speed measurements had a greater overall disease burden than those with available measurements, including more severe SVD imaging findings, higher mRS scores and higher NOTCH3-SVD severity spectrum scores (Table S3).

Associations between whole brain kw and MRI indicators of brain fluid dynamics

Lower whole brain kw was significantly associated with a higher WM FW fraction (β = −0.419; 95% CI, −0.697, −0.141; P-corrected = .012; Figure 3A), lower DTI-ALPS index (β = 0.431; 95% CI, 0.143, 0.719; P-corrected = .028, Figure 3B) and larger CP volume (β = −0.510; 95% CI, −0.721, −0.298; P-corrected < .001; Figure 3C) after adjusting for age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and smoking history. No significant association was found between whole brain kw and PVS score (β = 0.024; 95% CI, −0.235, 0.283; P-corrected = 1.000; Figure 3D).

Associations between whole brain kw, MRI indicators of brain fluid dynamics and disease severity

Figure 4 shows the results of the whole brain kw and brain fluid dynamics with disease severity, including SVD imaging markers, processing speed and mRS. For imaging markers, whole brain kw was positively associated with brain volume (β = 0.426; 95% CI, 0.225, 0.628; P-corrected = .001) after adjusting for covariates and Bonferroni correction. For cognition, lower whole brain kw was correlated with poorer processing speed (β = 0.367; 95% CI, 0.217, 0.517; P-corrected < .001), which remained significant (β = 0.203; 95% CI, 0.066, 0.339; P-corrected = .036) after further adjustment for lacune count, CMB count, WMH volume and brain volume (Table S2). For disability, there was no significant link between kw and the risk of mRS ≥ 2 after Bonferroni correction (Figure 4).

Magnetic resonance imaging indicators of brain fluid dynamics were also linked with disease severity. For SVD imaging markers, higher WM FW fraction correlated with higher lacune and CMB counts and smaller brain volume, lower DTI-ALPS index was associated with higher lacune count, higher WMH burden and smaller brain volume, and larger CP volume correlated with higher lacune and CMB counts and lower brain volume. Discussion

This study investigated the associations between kw and MRI markers related to brain fluid dynamics and explored whether these markers were statistically associated with the relationship between kw and disease severity. We found that lower whole brain kw was associated with a higher FW fraction, larger CP volume and lower DTI-ALPS index, indicating more disrupted brain fluid dynamics. Magnetic resonance imaging markers related to brain fluid dynamics were statistically associated with the relationship between kw and disease severity.

In this study, we observed significant associations between whole brain kw and MRI non-invasive measurements of brain fluid dynamics, supporting this proposal. The recent investigation in older adults20,24 and APOE ɛ4 carriers25 found that kw was correlated with the Aβ42 concentration levels in CSF and iron deposition in the brain, supporting its associations with less brain fluid turnover and brain clearance function. There are a few explanations for the relationship between kw and MRI measurements of brain fluid dynamics observed in CADASIL. Firstly, perivascular AQP4 on the BBB is an important protein to facilitate water exchange on the BBB. Aquaporin-4 knockout mice and large nonhuman primates with AQP4 inhibitor (TGN-020) studies both demonstrated reduced water exchange rate using MRI scan.26,27 However, direct quantification of AQP4 expression on the BBB is challenging. Although positron emission tomography provides high sensitivity for in vivo imaging of transport mechanisms, specific radiotracers for aquaporins are still in development.28,29 Secondly, the deposition of mutant NOTCH3 proteins is the hallmark of CADASIL and contributes to arteriolar wall thickening and fibrosis,1 which may impair arterial pulsatility. Arterial pulsation is one of the key driving forces of the perivascular CSF drainage and brain fluid flow.

Our results showed that WM FW fraction, DTI-ALPS index and CP volume all correlated with disease severity. Previous studies have shown that DTI-ALPS index30 and FW fraction31 were strongly associated with clinical status or SVD imaging burden in CADASIL patients. This study is the first to report an association between CP volume and disease severity in CADASIL. It is worth noting that the interpretation of DTI-ALPS requires caution. Although proposed as a marker of glymphatic function, recent studies suggest that it is influenced by local WM microstructure and shows limited correspondence with tracer-based measures of glymphatic clearance. Diffusion tensor imaging along perivascular space should therefore be considered an indirect diffusion-based marker potentially related to perivascular fluid dynamics rather than a specific measure of glymphatic function. We adjusted for radial asymmetry (λ2/λ3), but residual microstructural effects cannot be excluded.

A post-mortem study reported an increased PVS area in the WM of the temporal pole in CADASIL.32 Notably, PVS burden was not significantly associated with whole-brain kw or disease severity in the current study. This may be considered from 2 complementary perspectives: biological differences between these imaging measures and methodological limitations in PVS assessment. First, from a biological perspective, whereas kw reflects trans-BBB water exchange, visible PVS burden primarily captures a structural consequence that may arise from multiple processes, including arterial stiffening, protein aggregation and the stagnation of fluid drainage. Thus, functional disturbances in brain fluid exchange may not necessarily translate into proportional PVS enlargement. The differing associations observed across WM FW fraction, DTI-ALPS index, CP volume and PVS burden further support a multidimensional view of these MRI markers rather than a single unitary measure of brain fluid dynamics. Second, from a methodological perspective, the semi-quantitative visual rating used in our study may have limited sensitivity compared with volumetric approaches, which may partly explain the lack of significant associations involving PVS burden. Although automated volumetric quantification may provide greater sensitivity, its application in CADASIL remains challenging because extensive WMH and lacunes may interfere with accurate PVS segmentation. Future studies combining quantitative PVS measurements with more direct or dynamic assessments of brain fluid transport are needed to clarify the relationship between PVS morphology and fluid dynamics in CADASIL.

The observed indirect associations between kw, MRI markers of brain fluid dynamics and disease severity may provide a framework for considering their interrelationships in CADASIL. However, these mediation findings should be interpreted cautiously because all variables were measured cross-sectionally. Although significant indirect associations were identified, the temporal ordering of kw, MRI markers of brain fluid dynamics and disease severity cannot be established, and the analyses therefore do not demonstrate a causal mediation pathway. From a biological perspective, impaired brain fluid transport has been proposed to contribute to SVD through reduced clearance of inflammatory mediators and consequent neuroinflammation.33,34 Additionally, the mutant NOTCH3 protein deposited in the vessel wall is a prominent pathological feature of CADASIL. The impairment of glymphatic clearance might predict the increased GOM deposition in CADASIL. Besides, the extracellular fluid accumulation may contribute to WM damage in CADASIL as Yu et al. reported.35 These observations provide biological plausibility for an association between altered brain fluid dynamics and disease burden, but do not establish the direction or mechanism of these relationships.

The strength of this study lies in the comprehensive assessment of BBB water exchange rate and non-invasive MRI measurements of brain fluid dynamics in CADASIL patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, animal models and autopsy studies are needed to directly confirm that reduced perivascular AQP4 expression and deposition of mutant NOTCH3 protein lead to lower kw in CADASIL and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking kw and brain fluid dynamics. Second, our single-shell diffusion approach for FW fraction calculation has limitations. Single-shell acquisitions, although feasible, are heavily influenced by initialisation parameters and may not distinguish well between FW and mean diffusivity modifications. Future studies using multi-shell diffusion imaging may enhance FW metric reliability. Third, our analysis is limited by inherent limitations of a cross-sectional study design, resulting in difficulties in inferring causal relationships. The mediation findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, the study was conducted in a single CADASIL cohort without an independent external validation cohort, which may limit the generalisability and robustness of the observed associations. Replication in independent cohorts, preferably with longitudinal follow-up, will be important to confirm these findings and determine their prognostic relevance. Fifth, processing-speed data were unavailable in 15 participants with more severe cognitive impairment, indicating informative missingness. Complete-case analyses may therefore underrepresent patients with more advanced CADASIL and limit the generalisability of the observed kw–processing speed association. Finally, although regional kw analyses may provide important information on the spatial heterogeneity of BBB water exchange abnormalities in CADASIL, the spatial resolution of the DP-pCASL sequence used in this study did not permit reliable quantification in relatively small regions such as the anterior temporal poles and external capsules.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that lower kw may be associated with altered brain fluid dynamics in CADASIL subjects, and brain fluid dynamics appears to contribute to the disease severity. These findings should be validated in animal models and longitudinal studies to better understand disease mechanisms.
