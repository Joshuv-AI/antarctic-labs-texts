From ROS scavenging to boosted osseointegration: cerium-containing mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles functionalized implants in diabetes
Authors: Xue Jiang, Jianxu Wei, Xinxin Ding, Kai Zheng, Tian Zhou, Junyu Shi, Hongchang Lai, Shujiao Qian, Xiaomeng Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) around titanium implants under diabetic conditions causes persistent inflammation, leading to poor osseointegration and even implant failure. Surface modification is an effective way to promote ROS clearance, alleviate inflammation, and stimulate bone formation. In this study, a multifunctional coating is fabricated by introducing cerium (Ce)-containing mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles (Ce-MBGNs) onto the titanium surface via an electrophoretic deposition method. The incorporation of Ce-MBGNs remarkably improves surface hydrophilicity by increasing the surface areas. The bioactive ions are appropriately released, thereby promoting mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and differentiation under diabetic conditions. The conversion between Ce(III) and Ce(IV) endows Ce-MBGNs coating with antioxidative nanoenzymes properties to scavenge diabetes-induced ROS, resulting in macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory phenotype. The therapeutic effect of Ce-MBGNs-modified titanium implants is also verified in diabetic rats by inhibiting inflammatory responses and accelerating early osseointegration. Taken together, the findings reveal that the ROS-scavenging and immunomodulation activity of the Ce-MBGNs coating contributes to enhanced osseointegration, and provides a novel implant surface for diabetic patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02865-y.

Introduction

Titanium (Ti) is commonly utilized in intrabony implants for repairing bone fractures and replacing missing teeth. However, implant survival is affected by undesirable conditions such as diabetes mellitus (DM), which is characterized by hyperglycemia. Clinical studies have demonstrated that diabetic patients are more prone to peri-implantitis and implant failure than non-diabetes [1–3]. Although the definite etiology remains unclear, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) stimulated by hyperglycemia [4–6], including superoxide radicals (O2.−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radicals (·OH) have been found to promote macrophage polarization towards the pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype. Moreover, its transition to the anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype is impaired, resulting in prolonged inflammation and delayed bone healing [7]. Furthermore, ROS also directly hinder osteoblast differentiation, ultimately compromising titanium osseointegration [8].

Considering the deleterious effects of excessive ROS, several studies have introduced bioactive particles and antioxidants capable of eliminating ROS onto titanium surfaces [9–12]. Cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles as nanoenzymes have shown their great potential in various biomedical applications due to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The conversion from Ce(III) to Ce(IV) endows CeO2 with superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity, which catalyzes the dismutation of O2.− into oxygen (O2) and H2O2. In addition, the reverse conversion mimics the catalase (CAT) activity, which transitions H2O2 into O2. This redox-based cycle allows CeO2 to act as a bottomless antioxidase, exerting superior antioxidant activity [13–16]. Although CeO2 nanoparticles modified Ti alloy have been reported to exert SOD-like activity [17], CeO2 nanoparticles may lead to undesired biotoxicity and inflammatory responses due to their low degradability and their monofunctionality limits their application in bone healing for chronic inflammatory disease conditions such as diabetes.

Mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles (MBGNs), which have a highly ordered mesoporous channel structure and a large specific surface area, are biodegradable and highly biocompatible. MBGNs have been widely used as bioactive fillers and carriers in biomedical applications [18]. Ce-containing MBGNs (Ce-MBGNs) have been reported to possess excellent biocompatibility along with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory performances [19–21]. Biologically active ions (silicon and calcium ions) can be released upon degradation of Ce-MBGNs, which further improves osteogenesis [19]. Thus, the excessive ROS generation and compromised osseointegration of Ti implants induced by hyperglycemia would be significantly alleviated by introducing Ce-MBGNs, which possess superior abilities to scavenge ROS, thwart inflammation, and promote osteogenesis. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies on the modification of Ti implant surfaces with Ce-MBGNs, let alone their application in a diabetic setting.

Electrophoretic deposition (EPD) is a versatile nano-engineering technique that can be utilized to form coatings on Ti substrates with favorable feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and efficiency. Typically, EPD involves charging of nanoparticles in an electrolyte, which is followed by the deposition of charged nanoparticles driven by an electric field. And the desired coatings can be fabricated by controlling the EPD parameters [22]. This electric-field-driven deposition has been demonstrated superior stability and simplicity compared to alternative methods such as physical vapor deposition or chemical vapor deposition, which is also applicable to a wide range of nanoparticles [23]. Herein, EPD can be applied to fabricate Ce-MBGNs coatings with customized nanotopographies on Ti implants.

This study aimed to design a multifunctional coating for Ti implants with a sandblasted, large-grit, and acid-etched surface (SLA, the most commonly used surface in clinic) to enhance its ROS removal, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic properties under diabetic conditions. As shown in Scheme 1, Ce-MBGNs were obtained by incorporating Ce into MBGNs via postimpregnation method. The optimal ratio of Ce was determined based on the previous study [19]. Ce-MBGN@SLA was then synthesized by introducing Ce-MBGNs onto SLA surfaces by EPD. The surface characterization, biocompatibility, and multi-functions of Ce-MBGN@SLA were systematically evaluated under diabetic conditions. Moreover, the potential mechanisms underlying the anti-inflammatory effects of Ce-MBGN@SLA were further investigated, and the osseointegration of the novel Ce-MBGN@SLA surface was validated in diabetic rats.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis and applications of Ce-MBGN@SLA under diabetic conditions

Materials and methods

Preparation of MBGNs and Ce-MBGNs

The MBGNs were synthesized following the previous work [19]. The anti-inflammatory and osteogenic properties of MBGNs doped with different concentrations of Ce ions have been investigated in our previous studies. The results showed that Ce-MBGNs prepared with 0.2 M Ce were more effective, and thus Ce-MBGNs prepared under the same procedure were used in this study [19].

Preparation of MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA

MBGNs and Ce-MBGNs were loaded onto the surface of SLA by the EPD method. SLA disks (15 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness) and implants (3.3 mm in diameter and 10 mm in length) were used in the present study. 0.74 g of magnesium nitrate was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water to achieve a magnesium nitrate solution. Subsequently, 0.15 g of MBGNs or Ce-MBGNs was dissolved in a solution comprised of 0.375 mL of magnesium nitrate solution and 30 mL of 2-propanol. After stirring with ultrasound for 3 min, the homogeneous solution was obtained. The EPD of MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA was performed with the prepared solution at 20 V for 2 min using a DC source (Zhaoxin, China). Finally, specimens were washed twice with deionized water and then air dried.

Physicochemical characterization

The hydrodynamic diameter of nanoparticles was measured by a dynamic light scattering instrument (Malvern, UK). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Scientific, USA), transmission electron microscope (TEM) (JEOL, Japan), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Zeiss, Germany), and focused ion beam-SEM (FIB-SEM) (Zeiss, Germany) were utilized to characterize the surface chemical elements and morphology of specimens. The water contact angle of specimens was investigated by a drop shape analysis method (Dataphsics, Germany). The surface roughness was detected by Optical Profiler (Bruker, Germany).

ROS scavenging properties

Nanoparticles were dispersed in deionized water at a concentration of 5 mg mL−1 for ROS scavenging capacity assay. The Inhibition and produce superoxide anion assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to detect the O2.− scavenging ability of nanoparticles. The kit could mimic the reaction system of xanthine and xanthine oxidase in the body to produce O2.−. The addition of an electron transfer substance and gress’s colorant could give the reaction system a purplish-red color, the absorbance could be measured at 550 nm. The absorbance of the sample would be lower than that of the control when the sample could scavenge O2.−. Hydroxyl Free Radical assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) was used to determine the ·OH scavenging ability. The kit could utilize the Fenton reaction to generate ·OH, which, when given an electron acceptor, would be colored with griess reagent to form a red substance, and the amount of ·OH was reflected by detecting its absorbance at 550 nm. Nanoparticles were incubated with 0.8 M H2O2 for 24 h. Residual H2O2 was assayed by the Hydrogen Peroxide assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). H2O2 could interact with molybdic acid to form a complex, and the amount of H2O2 could be calculated by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm.

Release behavior

Each sample was incubated with 2.5 mL of deionized water. After incubation for 0.5, 2, 4, 7, 14, and 28 days, the concentrations of Si, Ca, and Ce ions in the supernatant were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent, USA).

Cell culture

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were obtained from the bone marrow of 3-week-old Sprague-Dawly (SD) rats and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) complete media. BMSCs of passages 3–5 were used for the experiments. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the bone marrow of 5-week-old C57BL/6J mice and cultured in Roswell park memorial institute (RPMI) 1640 complete media supplemented with supernatants of L929 cells. Considering the different stimulation durations for BMSCs and BMDMs, 25 mM glucose and 0.2 mM sodium palmitate (Kunchuang, China) were used to mimic diabetic stimulation (labeled as DM) for BMSCs, whereas the concentration of sodium palmitate used for BMDMs was 0.4 mM.

Cell viability

BMDMs and BMSCs were cultured on SLA, MBGN@SLA, and Ce-MBGN@SLA for 24 h and 72 h, respectively. Subsequently, the CCK8 kit (Dojindo, Japan) and LIVE/DEAD staining kit (BestBio, China) were utilized to detect the viability of cells. In addition, the adhesion and morphology of BMSCs grown on specimens were visualized by FITC-phalloidin staining (Yeasen, China) after stimulation for 72 h. Photos of cells were taken by a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Assessment of gene expression

Trizol reagent (Takara, Japan) was utilized to collect cellular RNA. Then, cDNA was obtained by the PrimeScript RT Reagent kit (Takara, Japan). The RT-qPCR reaction was carried out with TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan). The primer sequences of target genes are listed in Table S1.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity detection

BMSCs were seeded and stimulated on SLA, MBGN@SLA, and Ce-MBGN@SLA for 7 and 14 days, and then the cells were assayed for ALP expression using an ALP assay kit (Beyotime, China) following the protocols of manufacturers. To facilitate the detection of ALP staining of BMSCs, BMSCs were co-cultured with Ti disks through transwell chambers for 7 and 14 days, and then the cells were stained for ALP using the BCIP/NBT ALP Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Intracellular ROS assay

The ROS levels in BMDMs were detected using the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, BMDMs were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA probe for 20 min at 37 °C. Then cells were washed three times using serum-free cell culture medium [24]. Finally, cells were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) or analyzed by flow cytometry (Agilent, USA).

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity analysis

After culturing for 24 h, the intracellular GPx content in BMDMs was determined by Cellular Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit with NADPH (Beyotime, China) according to the protocols of manufacturers.

Immunofluorescence

The treated BMDMs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with QuickBlock™ (Beyotime, China) for 20 min. Anti-CD86 (Absin, China), anti-CD206 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and anti-Nrf2 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were used as primary antibodies. Images were obtained by a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) or a confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Treated BMDMs were collected and rinsed three times with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Cells were incubated with FITC anti-mouse F4/80 (Biolegend, USA), PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD206 (Biolegend, USA) and APC anti-mouse CD86 (Biolegend, USA) for 40 min at 4 °C in the dark. After rinsing three times with PBS, the cells were detected by flow cytometry (Agilent, USA), and the CD86 and CD206 expression of BMDMs was expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity.

Western blot

The treated BMDMs were scraped in lysis buffer for 30 min and the collected proteins were denatured for 10 min at 100 °C. Anti-Keap1 (Proteintech, USA), anti-Nrf2 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and anti-β-Actin (Beyotime, China) were used as primary antibodies.

Animal surgery

Diabetic rats were established by feeding 6-week-old SD rats with a high-fat diet (Medicience, China) for 4 weeks and injecting streptozotocin (20 mg/kg) continuously for 4 days. Then, diabetic rats were assigned into three groups: DM + SLA group, DM + MBGN@SLA group, and DM + Ce-MBGN@SLA group. After anesthesia, the hind limb was shaved and disinfected with chlorhexidine. The pilot hole was drilled through the middle of the intercondylar notch of the distal femur, followed by the insertions with different implants. All rats were treated with antibiotics for 3 days after surgery. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ninth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

Micro-CT analysis

Four weeks after surgery, the rats were sacrificed and the femurs were scanned by micro-CT (Bruker, USA). Bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular numbers (Tb.N) were analyzed by CTAn (Bruker, Belgium).

Histological evaluation

The specimens of 3 days and 4 weeks post-surgery were fixed for 2 days and then decalcified for 4 weeks before being embedded and sectioned. To evaluate the early immune response and antioxidant modulation, samples of 3 days post-surgery were incubated with anti-CD206 (Abcam, UK), anti-CD68 (Servicebio, China) and anti-keap1 (Proteintech, USA) antibodies. To analyze new bone formation, Masson’s trichrome, hematoxylin-eosin (HE), and anti-OPN (Proteintech, USA) immunohistochemical staining was conducted on the specimens at 4 weeks after implantation.

Statistical analyses

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc test. When there were only two groups, the t test was used for statistical analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01.

Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization

In this work, Ce-MBGN@SLA was prepared by loading Ce-MBGNs onto the SLA surface. Firstly, Ce-MBGNs were synthesized according to the method previously reported [19], and then Ce-MBGNs were uniformly deposited on the SLA surface using the EPD method. The hydrodynamic diameter of MBGNs was 282.2 ± 13.57 nm and was 316.63 ± 6.91 nm for Ce-MBGNs, showing an increase due to the nanoparticle cerium deposition (Fig. S1). Ce-MBGNs possessed a uniform mesoporous structure (Fig. 1A). It was shown that nanomaterials with mesoporous structure were favorable for better loading capacity and interaction with biomolecules, resulting in better bioactivity [25, 26]. The results of energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images showed the constituent elements of Ce-MBGNs including Ce, Si and Ca (Fig. 1B). As displayed in Fig. 1C, the nanoparticles were introduced onto SLA surface, revealing no significant differences in particle morphology between MBGNs and Ce-MBGNs. The incorporation of nanoparticles retained the micron surface of SLA while forming a certain nano-raised structure, which significantly increased the surface areas. Si, Ca, and Ce elements were found on the Ce-MBGN@SLA surface following the introduction of Ce-MBGNs (Fig. 1D). Compared with SLA, the XPS results of Ce-MBGN@SLA showed Ca, Si and Ce elements. This also validated the successful loading of Ce-MBGNs on the SLA surface (Fig. 1E, F). High-resolution XPS spectra further indicated that Ce existed with different valences, and the quantitative analysis revealed that the ratio of Ce(IV)/Ce(III) was 1.90 (Fig. 1G). Ceria exerted its antioxidant effect by eliminating excess intracellular ROS, which was achieved by the valence switch between Ce(III) and Ce(IV). The SOD-like properties of Ce(III) enabled the conversion of O2.− into H2O2, while Ce(IV) showed CAT-like properties by reacting with H2O2, producing O2 and Ce(III) [15]. In this study, Ce-MBGNs exhibited excellent scavenging performance against O2.− (Fig. 1H) and H2O2 (Fig. 1J), which was in accordance with previous studies [27, 28]. Ceria also has been reported to convert ·OH to O2 [29] or H2O [30]. However, the results showed that Ce-MBGNs and MBGNs had similar efficiency in ·OH elimination (Fig. 1I), suggesting that Ce incorporation did not enhance ·OH scavenging properties. The corresponding curves for O2.− and ·OH scavenging by nanoparticles were shown in Figs. S2 and S3, respectively.

Hydrophilic implants could accelerate early bone formation by promoting the adsorption of platelets and blood proteins, alleviating the inflammation response, and enhancing mesenchymal stem cell adhesion and differentiation [31, 32]. Surfaces with water contact angles greater than 90° were defined as hydrophobic and less than 90° as hydrophilic [33]. The average contact angle of SLA, MBGN@SLA, and Ce-MBGN@SLA was 112.12 ± 8.66°, 19.20 ± 0.67°, and 20.80 ± 2.57°, respectively. These findings demonstrated that the MBGNs and Ce-MBGNs coating dramatically improved the wettability of SLA surfaces (Fig. 2A, B), favoring early bone formation. The elevated hydrophilicity might be partially attributed to the increased surface areas of nano-structured MBGNs and Ce-MBGNs. The surface roughness of the SLA was 2.296 ± 0.461 μm, whereas the surface roughness of the MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA decreased slightly to 1.629 ± 0.014 μm and 1.688 ± 0.099 μm, respectively (Fig. 2C, D). This moderate rough surface (Sa within 1–2 μm) has been reported to better promote osseointegration compared to smoother (Sa < 1 μm) or rougher (Sa > 2 μm) surfaces [34].

The release behavior of MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA was measured by ICP-MS. Ce-MBGN@SLA rapidly released Ce ions within the first 4 days. Under diabetic conditions, excessive M1 macrophages accumulate around the implant within 4 days after implant placement, leading to a severe inflammatory microenvironment [35, 36]. Ce ions released during this period could relieve inflammation and promote regeneration. Additionally, the Si and Ca ions were almost completely released from MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA within 7 days, which could promote osteoblast differentiation (Fig. 2E). The release properties of Ce-MBGN@SLA could facilitate early osseointegration, which is pivotal for implant success in diabetes.

In conclusion, Ce-MBGN@SLA, with excellent hydrophilicity and moderate roughness for osseointegration, had been successfully constructed. Ce could mimic antioxidant enzymes to scavenge ROS, and the bioactive ions were released in the desired manner. These properties synergistically endowed Ce-MBGN@SLA with anti-inflammatory properties and promote bone formation under diabetic conditions.

Cytocompatibility and osteogenic evaluation under diabetic conditions in vitro

The viability of BMDMs and BMSCs seeded on different samples was determined using CCK8 assay. The survival rate of cells cultured with DM culture medium on different samples all exceeded 80% of the control group, indicating that the biosafety of MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA complied with the international standard ISO 10993-5 (Fig. 3A). The best viability of BMSCs was observed in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group after 3 days of stimulation with DM medium, which was also consistent with the results of live/dead staining (Fig. 3B). In addition, the morphology of BMSCs on samples was investigated by staining with FITC-phalloidin (Fig. 3C). Compared to the DM + SLA group, BMSCs in the DM + MBGN@SLA and DM + Ce-MBGN@SLA groups were more elongated and outstretched. The results demonstrated the superior biocompatibility of MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA, which could be attributed to their favorable hydrophilicity, as well as the early release of bioactive ions.

Moreover, osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs is critical for osseointegration under diabetic conditions. The early osteogenic potential was investigated by evaluating the mRNA expression of osteogenic markers, including Osterix, Runx2, and Opn. The results revealed a significant upregulation after 7 days in the DM + Ce-MBGN@SLA group compared to other groups (Fig. 3D). ALP staining was conducted after BMSCs were co-cultured with different samples through transwells for 7 and 14 days. Notably, compared with the Ctrl + SLA group, there was no significant decrease in ALP activity in the DM + SLA group after 7 days of incubation, whereas ALP activity was significantly weakened when incubated for 14 days (Fig. 3E, F). Quantitative and qualitative detection of ALP in BMSCs after 7 and 14 days of induction consistently showed that ALP activity was significantly higher in the DM + Ce-MBGN@SLA group compared to the DM + SLA group (Fig. 3E, F).

Overall, the diabetic niche did not significantly affect osteogenic potential in the early stage, while the prolonged involvement impaired the late osteogenesis [37, 38]. The early release of bioactive ions from the Ce-MBGN@SLA surface (Fig. 2E) significantly facilitated early osteogenesis, whereas the niche with accumulated bioactive ions markedly ameliorated the impairment of late osteogenic properties.

Anti-inflammatory properties under diabetic conditions in vitro

In addition to directly affecting osteoblast differentiation, DM indirectly impairs osteoblast differentiation by hindering the transition of macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype [39, 40]. To investigate the immunoregulatory capacity of the MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA, BMDMs were seeded on different samples and cultured with DM medium for 24 h. Subsequently, the protein expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) was evaluated. The flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining results consistently revealed that DM upregulated the expression of CD86 while downregulating the expression of CD206, indicating that DM promoted macrophage polarization toward pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. In contrast, the MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA could downregulate CD86 and increase CD206 levels in macrophages under DM conditions (Fig. 4B–E).

In addition to the above findings regarding macrophage polarization status, the mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory-related markers were further examined. DM increased the expression of Il1β, Il6, and Inos, while the MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA significantly suppressed this upward trend (Fig. 4A). This finding is consistent with the prior study indicating that Ce-MBGN@SLA could downregulate the pro-inflammatory gene levels [19]. Collectively, these results indicated that the Ce-MBGN@SLA effectively reprogramed M1 macrophages to M2 phenotype, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory properties.

Antioxidant properties under diabetic conditions in vitro

The potential mechanisms by which Ce-MBGN@SLA exerted anti-inflammatory effects under diabetic conditions were further explored. Generally, intracellular ROS are maintained at low levels by the balanced redox systems. However, DM disrupts the antioxidant system, leading to ROS accumulation and promoting M1 macrophage polarization, thereby exacerbating inflammation [41]. On the one hand, Ce-MBGNs could act as nanoenzymes mimicking SOD and CAT as well as ·OH scavengers to rapidly scavenge ROS after exposure (Fig. 1H–J). On the other hand, ceria has been shown to activate the expression of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, which synergistically scavenge intracellular ROS [42, 43]. DM significantly triggered the excessive generation of ROS and Ce-MBGN@SLA dramatically reversed this process after 4 h of incubation (Fig. 5A). Notably, the expression of genes encoding endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including Gpx, copper–zinc SOD (Sod1), and extracellular SOD (Sod3), showed almost no difference between the SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA groups (Fig. 5A, C). These results indicated that the nanoenzyme activities might play a pivotal role in scavenging ROS during this period. After 8 h of stimulation, Ce-MBGN@SLA upregulated the expression of manganese SOD (Sod2) and Sod3, synergically enhancing ROS scavenging properties (Fig. 5A, C). After 24 h of stimulation, DM decreased the transcriptional levels of Gpx1 and Sod1 while significantly increasing those of Sod2 and Sod3, indicating the disrupted antioxidative system. In the Ce-MBGN@SLA group, the level of genes encoding SOD was not further upregulated, probably because ROS had been significantly reduced to the level of the control group after 8 h of induction and thus additional antioxidant enzymes were no longer required (Fig. 5A, C). Simultaneously, the mRNA and protein expressions of GPx, which could catalyze H2O2 similar to CAT [44], were upregulated by Ce-MBGN@SLA, further restoring the endogenous antioxidant system to normal (Fig. 5C, D). Moreover, Ce-MBGN@SLA markedly reduced DM-induced intracellular ROS compared to MBGN@SLA (Fig. 5A, B), consistent with the superior O2.− and H2O2 scavenging capacities (Fig. 1H, J) as well as modulate the expressions of endogenous antioxidant enzymes (Fig. 5C).

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key harmonizer of the intracellular antioxidative system. Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), a negative regulator of Nrf2, binds to Nrf2 and promotes its proteasomal degradation. As the self-antioxidant defense system of cellular, upon exposure to ROS, Keap1 is modified and dysfunctional, resulting in nuclear translocation of Nrf2, thereby activating the transcription of antioxidative enzymes to combat oxidative stress [45, 46]. However, chronic hyperglycemia stimulation exceeds the regulatory capacity of the cell, leading to the dysregulation of the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway [47–49]. After 8 h of stimulation, DM did not seem to affect Nrf2 expression. Whereas Ce-MBGN@SLA significantly upregulated the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 (Fig. 5E), which may explain the increased antioxidant gene expression (Fig. 5C). After incubation for 24 h, upregulated expressions of Keap1 and Nrf2 were observed under DM, while Ce-MBGN@SLA decreased the level of Keap1, indicating that Ce-MBGN@SLA attenuated DM-induced dysregulation of the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway, thereby restoring the balance of intracellular antioxidative system (Fig. 5F).

Bone formation and histological evaluation in diabetic rats

Encouraged by the anti-inflammatory and osteogenetic properties of Ce-MBGN@SLA in vitro, the therapeutic efficacy of Ce-MBGN@SLA surface was investigated in a diabetic rat model. Implants were placed in the femur of diabetic rats for 4 weeks. Especially under diabetic conditions, implants should be firmly integrated with the surrounding bone, and early bone formation is critical for implant osseointegration and long-term success. Reconstructed micro-CT images suggested that the amount of new bone around Ce-MBGN@SLA implants far surpassed that of SLA and MBGN@SLA implants (Fig. 6A, B). Micro-CT analysis reflected higher BV/TV, BMD, and Tb.N in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group in comparison with the SLA group. Tb.Th also showed a similar trend (Fig. 6C). Thus, both the quality and quantity of bone tissues around the implant suggested that Ce-MBGN modification enhanced the early osteogenic capacity in diabetic rats. Notably, bone formation parameters including BV/TV, BMD, Tb.Th were not statistically different between MBGN@SLA and Ce-MBGN@SLA groups. However, it could be seen that all the parameters in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group showed an upward trend compared to the MBGN@SLA group. The process of osseointegration usually involves four events i.e., clot formation, granulation tissue formation, bone formation and bone remodeling. During bone formation, trabeculae are first formed to connect the bone to the implant surface, followed by thickening of the trabeculae which further affects the BMD and BV/TV [50]. Therefore, Tb.N is critical in bone formation, which was statically significantly elevated in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group compared to the MBGN@SLA group.

To further demonstrate bone formation and osseointegration at the tissue, cellular, and molecular levels, histological section staining was performed. Compared to SLA implants, the results of Toluidine Blue-stained histological sections showed more newly formed bone tissue tightly connected to the Ce-MBGN@SLA implants (Fig. 7A), in line with the results of HE and Masson’s trichrome (Fig. 7B). Inflammatory cell infiltration around the implant was observed in the SLA group, confirming that DM caused chronic inflammation and was partly responsible for significantly reduced bone regeneration [51]. In the Ce-MBGN@SLA group, the peri-implant inflammatory cells were significantly reduced while being replaced by regular and dense bone tissue. Meanwhile, bone tissue grew into the implant threads, indicating favorable early osseointegration (Fig. 7B). To verify osteogenic differentiation capacity at the molecular level, OPN, a marker of mid-osteogenic differentiation involved in bone matrix mineralization [52], was detected by immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 7C). The results showed higher levels of OPN-positive staining in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group than in the SLA group. The above results demonstrated that Ce-MBGN@SLA facilitated osteogenic protein expression and enhanced bone formation, which ultimately promoted osseointegration in diabetic rats. In addition, Ce-MBGN@SLA had better osteogenic activity than MBGN@SLA, which may be ascribed to the Ce ions released by the Ce-MBGN@SLA.

Inflammation occurs at the early stage after implant insertion and is more severe in diabetic conditions, during which macrophages are key immune cells. Macrophage transition from M1 to M2 is critical for osteoblast recruitment and differentiation [39, 40]. In vitro studies demonstrated that Ce-MBGN@SLA modulated the inflammatory response to facilitate the transition of macrophages to a reparative phenotype. To further validate the mechanism by which Ce-MBGN@SLA promotes osteogenesis in vivo, histological staining of tissue sections was performed. Three days after implantation, CD206 expression was detected by immunohistofluorescence labeling in bone tissue sections. CD68 staining was used to label macrophages. The number of CD68+CD206+ (M2) macrophages was significantly increased in the Ce-MBGN@SLA group (Fig. 7D), which was favorable for tissue repair and bone formation. To further determine whether keap1 was relevant to the M2 polarization in vivo, the protein expression of keap1 was investigated. Keap1 expression around SLA implants was significantly upregulated in diabetic rats, while Ce-MBGN@SLA suppressed this trend (Fig. 7E). The results were in line with prior research indicating that higher keap1 protein levels in diabetic mice and knockdown of keap1 restored the osteogenetic properties [48]. These results implied that the Ce-MBGN@SLA could relieve inflammation by modulating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype via keap1 signaling in the early phase after implantation.

Conclusion

In this study, Ce-MBGNs-modified SLA surfaces were developed by introducing Ce-MBGNs onto SLA surfaces via the EPD method. The Ce-MBGNs coating greatly improved surface hydrophilicity, which promoted cell adhesion. Ce ions were piggybacked with MBGNs, which endowed Ce-MBGN@SLA with a controlled release of bioactive ions. Under diabetic conditions, the anti-inflammatory properties were achieved on the one hand by mimicking SOD and CAT enzymes to eliminate excessive ROS through the conversion of Ce(III) and Ce(IV), and on the other hand by restoring the homeostasis of the antioxidative system via the modulation of Keap1/Nrf2 pathway. The hydrophilicity of Ce-MBGN@SLA and the release of bioactive ions synergistically promoted osteoblast differentiation under diabetic conditions, thereby accelerating early osseointegration, which is critical for implant success in diabetic patients. In a diabetic rat implant model, Ce-MBGN@SLA significantly reduced inflammation and enhanced osseointegration. Thereupon, Ce-MBGN@SLA is a promising implant for enhancing osseointegration in diabetic patients.
