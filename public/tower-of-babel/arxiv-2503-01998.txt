Statistical mechanics of a cold tracer in a hot bath
Amer Al-Hiyasat, Sunghan Ro, Julien Tailleur

Abstract
We study the dynamics of a zero-temperature particle interacting linearly with a bath of hot Brownian particles. Starting with the most general model of a linearly-coupled bath, we eliminate the bath degrees of freedom exactly to map the tracer dynamics onto a generalized Langevin equation, allowing for an arbitrary external potential on the tracer. We apply this result to determine the fate of a tracer connected by springs to N N identical bath particles or inserted within a harmonic chain of hot particles. In the former “fully-connected” case, we find the tracer to transition between an effective equilibrium regime at large N N and an FDT-violating regime at finite N N , while in the latter “loop” model the tracer never satisfies an FDT. We then study the fully-connected model perturbatively for large but finite N N , demonstrating signatures of irreversibility such as ratchet currents, non-Boltzmann statistics, and positive entropy production. Finally, we specialize to harmonic external potentials on the tracer, allowing us to exactly solve the dynamics of both the tracer and the bath for an arbitrary linear model. We apply our findings to show that a cold tracer in a hot lattice suppresses the fluctuations of the lattice in a long-ranged manner, and we generalize this result to linear elastic field theories.

II.1
Fully-connected model

We consider the fully-connected model of Fig.
1
(a), which can be interpreted as a mean-field model of a tracer immersed in a cold fluid containing colloidal particles at an enhanced temperature. We first apply the formalism introduced above to construct the generalized Langevin equation describing the dynamics of this system (see Ref.
75
for related results in the context of multi-temperature systems).

For simplicity, we consider a noninteracting bath, though our results are general and apply in the presence of an interaction potential between the bath particles, as shown in Sec.
III
. For this model, the potential energy takes the form

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{k}{2}(x_{i}-x)^{2}+U(x)\;.

(11)

Matching Eq. (
11
) to Eq. (
2
) leads to
A_{ij}=k\delta_{ij}
,

, and
\tilde{U}(x)=U(x)+Nkx^{2}/2
. Since we would like a generalized Langevin equation involving
U
and not

, we note that Eq. (
9
) is invariant under the combined transformation
\tilde{U}(x)\rightarrow\tilde{U}(x)+bx^{2}/2
and

, we can thus eliminate the term

to obtain a generalized Langevin equation like Eq. (

\displaystyle K(s)

\displaystyle=2\mu^{-1}\delta(s)+Nke^{-k|s|},

(12a)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=TNke^{-k|s|}.

(12b)

Generically, the first term of Eq. (

) breaks the FDT,

III

There are, however, some limiting cases of interest in which an FDT is recovered. For example, if the springs are very stiff,

to show that the kernels (

\displaystyle K(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2(\mu^{-1}+N)\delta(s),

(13a)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2TN\delta(s).

(13b)

This implies an equilibrium dynamics at an effective temperature
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=T\mu N/(1+\mu N)
. In this limit, tracer and bath particles behave as a single rigid body, with a drag coefficient
\mu^{-1}+N
and a temperature lowered from

by the cold tracer.

It is of experimental interest, particularly in the study of colloids in active enzyme solutions

;
81
, to study the behavior of a tracer that interacts with very many bath particles at once.
To characterize this large-

9

\displaystyle K(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mu N}\delta(s)+Nke^{-Nk|s|},

(14a)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=TNke^{-Nk|s|}.

(14b)

If

is made large while keeping the other parameters
\mathcal{O}(N^{0})
, the rescaled kernels become white

K(s)\rightarrow 2\delta(s),\quad G(s)\rightarrow 2T\delta(s),\quad(N\gg 1).

(15)

We thus have an equilibrium dynamics at temperature

for large
N
. Although there continues to be a heat flux from the

to the
T=0

N

In experiments, increasing the number of hot particles that interact with the tracer can be achieved by increasing either the particle density or the tracer radius. In a realistic fluid, the latter also reduces the tracer mobility, which calls for a study of the joint limit
N\rightarrow\infty,\mu\rightarrow 0
. The result here depends on how quickly one sends
\mu
to zero; letting
\mu
take the large-

form
\mu\sim\tilde{\mu}N^{-\alpha}
with
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\alpha>0
, we find that if
\alpha>1
, the tracer no longer equilibrates: it ceases to fluctuate and follows a zero-temperature gradient descent in
U(x)
. If
\alpha<1
, the tracer equilibrates at temperature
T
for large
N
. Interestingly, in the marginal case
\alpha=1
, the tracer equilibrates at a tunable temperature:

T_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{1+\tilde{\mu}}T.

(16)

For a spherical tracer of radius
R
interacting with hot Brownian colloids in a fluid, the Stokes formula predicts
\mu\propto R^{-1}
.
Since the number of colloids interacting with the tracer scales with its surface area,
N\propto R^{2}
, we have
\alpha=1/2
and thus expect an asymptotically large tracer to equilibrate at temperature
T
.

Another regime of interest, which has drawn attention in the context of Hamiltonian systems
17
;
10
, is the so-called weak-coupling limit, in which the number of bath particles is large but the coupling to the tracer is vanishingly weak. To explore this regime, we let
k\sim\tilde{k}N^{-\alpha}
. When
\alpha<1
, we recover Eq. (
15
) upon rescaling time by

. When
\alpha>1
, Eqs. (
12

K(s)=2\mu^{-1}\delta(s)
and

, implying a zero-temperature overdamped dynamics for the tracer. If
\alpha=1
, the friction and noise kernels (
12
) do not satisfy an FDT, and the tracer dynamics remain out of equilibrium as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Before concluding this section, we mention here that by an appropriate scaling of parameters, one can obtain from this fully-connected model a known model of active particles. Specifically, if, for some number
\nu>0
, the parameters are scaled as

k\sim\tilde{k}N^{-1-\nu},\quad\mu\sim\tilde{\mu}N^{-1-\nu},\quad T\sim\tilde{T}N^{\nu}.

(17)

Then upon rescaling

and taking

large, we find

\displaystyle K(s)

\displaystyle=2\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\delta(s)

(18a)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=2\tilde{T}\tilde{k}e^{-\tilde{k}|s|}

(18b)

When observed on (unrescaled) time scales

, the tracer thus behaves as an Active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Particle (AOUP), which is one of the standard models of self-propelled motion. Although this scaling may appear artificial, it establishes that the heat flux between the hot and cold reservoirs can be harnessed to produce an active dynamics that is known to display signatures of irreversibility. In section
III
, this connection to active matter will be shown to hold generically at finite
N,c
and
T
, although the dynamics then constitute a novel type of active particle that is different from the AOUP.

III.1
Perturbative computation of the stationary measure

We define a rescaled velocity variable

\varepsilon\equiv N^{-1}

The Fokker-Planck equation for the joint probability density of

\partial_{t}\Psi(x,{p})=\mathcal{L}\Psi(x,{p})

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{k}}\left(U^{\prime}\partial_{p}-p\partial_{x}\right)+\left[1\!+\!\varepsilon\left(\frac{U^{\prime\prime}}{k}\!+\!1\right)\right]\partial_{p}p+T\partial_{p}^{2}.

(31)

Our objective is to solve the steady-state equation

III.2

The marginal distribution of the tracer position

\displaystyle P(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int dp\Psi(x,p)\propto e^{-U_{\mathrm{eff}}/T},

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)

The first few

\displaystyle U_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{U^{\prime 2}}{2k}+U-\frac{TU^{\prime\prime}}{2k},

\displaystyle U_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{2k^{2}}U^{\prime\prime}(2k+U^{\prime\prime}),

(40b)

\displaystyle U_{3}=

III.3
Ratchet currents and irreversibility

If the confining walls are replaced by periodic boundaries, the asymmetric obstacle generates a steady ratchet current
J\equiv\langle\dot{x}\rangle
, whose leading contribution appears at
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
:

J=\langle\dot{x}\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{k}\frac{L\int_{0}^{L}dz\,U^{(3)}(z)U^{\prime 2}(z)}{2\int_{0}^{L}dz\,e^{\frac{-U(z)}{T}}\int_{0}^{L}dz\,e^{\frac{U(z)}{T}}},

(43)

where
\langle\dot{x}\rangle
is computed as described in Appendix
B.2
. The fact that the density rectification is present at
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})
while the current appears only at order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
reflects the fact that the effective mobility of the tracer vanishes as
1/N
for large
N
(Eq.
12
).

The emergence of a steady-state current (
43
) is a direct manifestation of time-reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking. The degree to which TRS is broken can be quantified by the entropy production, which is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the probability measure of trajectories of a specified length to
the measure of time-reversed trajectories of the same length. According to Eqs. (
30
), the probability density of any path
\{x(s):s\in[0,t]\}
can be written as
49

\mathcal{P}\left[\{x(s\leq t)\}\right]\propto e^{-\mathcal{S}(t)},

(44)

where the dynamical action
\mathcal{S}(t)
is given by,

\mathcal{S}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\frac{\left[k^{-1}\dot{p}+U^{\prime}+\left(k^{-1}U^{\prime\prime}+N+1\right)p\right]^{2}}{4TN},

(45)

and the stochastic integral is to be interpreted in the Stratonovich sense. The steady-state entropy production rate is then defined as
40
;
60
:

\sigma\equiv\!\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\!\log\frac{\mathcal{P}\left\{x(s\leq t)\right\}}{\mathcal{P}\left\{x^{\mathrm{R}}(s\leq t)\right\}}\!\right\rangle=\!\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\!\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}(t)-\mathcal{S}(t)\right\rangle,

(46)

where the time-reversed action
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}
is obtained by replacing
\partial_{t}\rightarrow-\partial_{t}
and
p\rightarrow-p
. We then have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}(t)-\mathcal{S}(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}ds\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{k^{2}}\dot{p}pU^{\prime\prime}+\frac{(1+\varepsilon)}{k}\dot{p}p\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\varepsilon}{k}pU^{\prime}U^{\prime\prime}+(1+\varepsilon)pU^{\prime}\right\}.

(47)

All but the first term of the integrand are total derivatives and therefore integrate to a finite contribution that is not extensive in
t
. Since these do not contribute to Eq. (
46
), we find

\sigma=-\frac{\varepsilon}{Tk^{2}}\langle\dot{p}pU^{\prime\prime}\rangle\;.

(48)

In the steady-state, all expectation values are stationary, so that
\partial_{t}\langle p^{2}U^{\prime\prime}\rangle=\left\langle 2p\dot{p}U^{\prime\prime}+p^{3}U^{\prime\prime\prime}\right\rangle=0
. This leads to

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon}{2Tk^{2}}\langle p^{3}U^{\prime\prime\prime}\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon^{3}T}{2k^{2}}\left\langle U^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)^{2}\right\rangle_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(49)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
denotes an expectation value with respect to the Boltzmann measure
e^{-U/T}
, and where the final equality follows from the procedure outlined in Appendix
B.2
. Irreversibility thus emerges at order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})
.

We note that the deviation from the Boltzmann weight scales as
1/N
(Eq.
40
), while the entropy production and ratchet current vanish much faster than this, respectively as
1/N^{3}
and
1/N^{4}
. This implies the existence of an intermediate effective equilibrium regime at large but finite
N
, in which the steady-state distribution is non-Boltzmann, but standard manifestations of TRS breaking cannot be detected.

III.4
Beyond linear coupling and zero-temperature tracers

It is natural to ask whether the predictions of our perturbation theory extend beyond the setting of a linearly-coupled zero-temperature tracer. We first argue analytically for the universality of the infinite-density equilibrium limit, under the condition that a generalized Langevin dynamics for the tracer exists. IV
Exactly-solvable linear models at the microscopic scale

So far, we have studied Eq. (
1

V
and an arbitrary external potential
U

, then the joint distribution of the tracer and bath particles will be Gaussian and can be computed exactly for any
N
. Although this restriction precludes any TRS-breaking phenomena like ratchet currents, it allows for a non-perturbative characterization of the tracer dynamics and, more importantly, of the effect of the tracer on the bath.

It will prove convenient to combine the tracer and bath degrees of freedom into a single vector
{\mathbf{x}}=(x,x_{1},\dots,x_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}

, so that the total potential energy can be written

\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A{\mathbf{x}}

(51)

where
A
is a positive-definite symmetric matrix. Equation (

\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}=-MA{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\Gamma}(t),

(52)

where
M
is the diagonal matrix whose

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{i}(t)\Gamma_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2M_{ij}k_{B}T\delta(t-t^{\prime})(1-\delta_{0i}).

Equation (

is a Gaussian random vector characterized completely by its first two moments. The solution to (

(54)

(57)

denotes an expectation value with respect to the Boltzmann measure

\Delta_{ij}\equiv T^{-1}\left[\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}-\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle\right].

(58)

\Delta_{ij}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}dsG_{i0}(s)G_{j0}(s)\mu_{0}

(61)

where summation over repeated indices is implied. This can be written

\Delta_{ij}=2\mu_{0}\sum_{n,\,m=0}^{N}\frac{v_{i}^{(n)}u_{0}^{(n)}v_{j}^{(m)}u_{0}^{(m)}}{\lambda_{n}+\lambda_{m}},

where

The most general model in which the tracer is linearly coupled to

\end{cases}

It is straightforward to verify that

A_{0j}^{-1}=\frac{c+(k-c)\delta_{j0}}{k_{0}k-c^{2}N},\quad A^{-1}_{pq}=\frac{\delta_{pq}}{k}+\frac{c^{2}}{k(k_{0}k-c^{2}N)},

(69)

for
p,q\neq 0
. To interpret these results, we note that the ratio
R_{ii}=\langle x_{i}^{2}\rangle/\langle x_{i}^{2}\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}
can be thought of as a dimensionless effective temperature for particle
i
, and can be determined from Eq. (
64
) as

R_{00}=\frac{c^{2}N}{k^{2}+kk_{0}},\quad R_{jj}=1-\frac{ck^{2}}{(k+k_{0})\left[kk_{0}-c^{2}(N-1)\right]},

(70)

for
j\neq 0
. To study the large
N
limits of these results, we must make specific choices for
k
,
c
and
k_{0}
as functions of
N
. We first consider the model depicted in Fig.
1
(a) with
V_{b}=0
and
U(x)=ux^{2}/2
. This corresponds to
c=k
and
k_{0}=u+kN
. We furthermore allow for weak coupling, in which
k
is made to vanish with
N
as
k\sim\tilde{k}N^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. We then find

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{00}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\alpha<1,\\
\dfrac{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}+u},&\alpha=1,\\
0,&\alpha>1.\end{cases}

(71a)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{jj}

\displaystyle=1,\qquad j>0.

(71b)

Eq. (
71a
) confirms the result of Sec.
II
that the tracer equilibrates at temperature
T
for
\alpha<1
and follows a zero-temperature dynamics for
\alpha>1
. For the marginal case
\alpha=1
, the linearity of the model forces the tracer to equilibrate at some temperature, but the result above tells us that this temperature can be tuned between zero and
T
by varying
\tilde{k}
and
u
. Eq. (
71b
) shows that in such a fully-connected model, the bath variables remain equilibrated at temperature
T
for
N\gg 1
, regardless of the status of the tracer.

For completeness, we mention an alternative choice for the parameters
c
and
k_{0}
: In their study of the thermalization of Hamiltonian dynamical systems, Caldeira and Leggett considered an interaction similar to Eq. (
65
), but with an arbitrary potential on the tracer and an additional so-called “counter-term” of the form
c^{2}Nx_{0}^{2}/2k
that keeps the system mechanically stable as
N\rightarrow\infty
. If we choose a quadratic external potential
ux_{0}^{2}/2
, then this corresponds exactly to Eq. (
65
) but with
k_{0}=u+c^{2}N/k
. Allowing for weak coupling
c\sim\tilde{c}N^{-\alpha}
, we find

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{00}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\alpha<1/2,\\
\dfrac{\tilde{c}^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{2}+k(k+u)},&\alpha=1/2,\\
0,&\alpha>1/2.\end{cases}

(72a)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{jj}

\displaystyle=1,\qquad j>0.

(72b)

The Caldeira-Leggett model thus exhibits a similar transition as
\alpha
is increased, with equilibrium dynamics at temperature
T
for
\alpha<1/2
, a tunable temperature for
\alpha=1/2
, and zero-temperature behavior for
\alpha>1/2
.

IV.2
The loop model

We now return to the loop model of Fig.
1
(b). We first confirm that the model never equilibrates and then characterize the effect of the tracer on the bath. Interestingly, we find the latter to be long-ranged, leading to a suppression of fluctuations in the bath that decays as a power law with the distance to the tracer.

Setting
U=0
for simplicity, the interaction energy is

\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N}(x_{i+1}-x_{i})^{2},

(73)

where
x_{N+1}\equiv x_{0}
and the spring stiffness has been set to unity without loss of generality. We furthermore set
\mu_{i}\penalty\ =\penalty\ 1
for all
i
, so that the matrix
Z\equiv MA
is given by

Z_{ij}=2\delta_{ij}-\delta_{|i-j|,1}-\left(\delta_{i0}\delta_{jN}+\delta_{iN}\delta_{j0}\right).

(74)

This is a symmetric circulant matrix that is diagonalized in a discrete Fourier basis. The orthonormal eigenvectors are, for
k\in(0,\dots,N)
,

v_{j}^{(k)}=\frac{w^{jk}}{\sqrt{N+1}}

(75)

where
w
is the
(N+1)
st root of unity

w\equiv\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{N+1}\right).

(76)

The corresponding eigenvalues are

\lambda_{k}=2-w^{k}-w^{Nk}.

(77)

There is a zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
corresponding to global translations, which dominate the fluctuations of
x_{i}
.
To circumvent this, we change to relative displacements by defining
\tilde{\Delta}_{i\neq j}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv T^{-1}\left[\left\langle(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\right\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}-\left\langle(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\Delta_{ii}+\Delta_{jj}-2\Delta_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(N\!+\!1)^{2}}\!\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{N}\frac{w^{i(k+\ell)}+w^{j(k+\ell)}-2w^{ik+j\ell}}{4-w^{k}-w^{\ell}-w^{Nk}-w^{N\ell}}\;.

(78)

Introducing the continuous variables
x\equiv 2\pi k/N,y\equiv 2\pi\ell/N
, the large
N
limit of
\tilde{\Delta}
can be expressed as an integral:

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\!\tilde{\Delta}_{nm}=\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{dxdy}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\,\frac{\left(e^{inx}-e^{imx}\right)\left(e^{iny}-e^{imy}\right)}{2-\cos(x)-\cos(y)},

(79)

where we have explicitly symmetrized in
n
and
m
so that the integrand is analytic at the origin. Note that, in taking the infinite loop limit to go from Eq. (
78
) to Eq. (
79
), we lose periodicity under
n\to n+N+1
. Equation (
79
) is thus quantifying the fluctuations between two points on the same side of the loop at finite distances
n
and
m
from the tracer.

It is instructive to consider
\tilde{\Delta}_{m,m+1}
, which measures the deviation of the mean squared length of the
m
th spring from its equilibrium value, normalized by temperature. This can be written, for large
N
, as

\tilde{\Delta}_{m,m+1}=\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{dz}{\pi^{2}}\cos\left[(2m\!-\!1)z\right]\left[2z\sec(z)-\pi\tan(z)\right],

(80)

which follows from Eq. (
79
) after making the variable change
2z\equiv x+y,2w\equiv x-y
, integrating over
w
, and using the integrality of
m
to simplify the result. It can be furthermore shown that, for integer
m
, the integral in Eq. (
80
) is equal to

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{m,m+1}=\frac{1}{\pi}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{2}{2m+1}\right.+2\pi(-1)^{m}

\displaystyle+\psi\left.\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{m}{2}\right)-\psi\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{m}{2}\right)\right],

(81)

where
\psi
denotes the digamma function. We note that we can extend Eq. (
IV.2
) to negative values of
m
to describe particles on the left of the tracer, using
\tilde{\Delta}_{-m-1,-m}\equiv\tilde{\Delta}_{m,m+1}
.
To quantify the degree to which the cold tracer suppresses the fluctuations of the bath, we may assign an effective temperature to the
m
th particle by averaging the fluctuations of its two neighboring springs

T_{\mathrm{eff}}(m)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left\langle(x_{m}-x_{m-1})^{2}+(x_{m+1}-x_{m})^{2}\right\rangle.

(82)

In equilibrium, the above is equal to
T
for all
m
by the equipartition theorem. Adding the cold tracer at
m=0
will reduce
T_{\mathrm{eff}}(m)
from its equilibrium value in accord with Eq. (
IV.2
). Defining
\Delta T(m)=T-T_{\mathrm{eff}}(m)
, we find, using the identity
\psi(z+1)=\psi(z)+z^{-1}
, that

\Delta T(0)=\frac{\pi-2}{\pi}T,\qquad\Delta T(m\neq 0)=\frac{2T}{\pi}\frac{1}{4m^{2}-1}\;.

(83)

We see from Eq. (
83
) that the effect of the cold tracer decays as
m^{-2}
for
m\gg 1
and is therefore long-ranged. This means that the single cold particle cools down the entire loop.

Equations (
IV.2
) and (
83
) are exact for the one-dimensional chain depicted in Fig.
1
(b). In section
V
, we present an alternative, field-theoretic derivation of this
m^{-2}
scaling that allows generalization to higher-coordination lattices.

V
Field theory of a cold particle in a hot lattice

In this section, we consider a generalized version of the loop model of Fig.
1
(b), in which the one-dimensional chain is replaced by a
d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice. For generality, the particle coordinates
\{\vec{x}_{i}\}
are allowed to be
n
-dimensional, which allows for a study of spring networks that are confined to lower dimensions
(n<d)
, or embedded in higher-dimensional spaces
(n>d)
— think about a two-dimensional (2D) sheet confined to a 1D tube or embedded in a 3D space. We first consider a microscopic model that can be coarse-grained exactly for direct comparison to the predictions of section
IV
, and then study a more general continuum elastic theory.

V.1
From micro to macro: an exactly solvable case

We index the particles by
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
m_{k}
labels the particle’s coordinate along the lattice direction
k\in 1,\dots,d
. The corresponding particle position is denoted
\vec{x}_{\mathbf{m}}
. The number of particles along each lattice dimension is denoted
L
, so that
N=L^{d}
.
The most direct generalization of the loop model, as defined by Eq. (
73
), is to let the
\mathbf{m}
th particle evolve according to

\dot{\vec{x}}_{\mathbf{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\sigma(\mathbf{m})}(\vec{x}_{\mathbf{p}}-\vec{x}_{\mathbf{m}})+\sqrt{2T}\vec{\eta}_{\mathbf{m}}(t),

(84)

where
\sigma(\mathbf{m})
denotes the nearest neighbors of
\mathbf{m}
, and
\vec{\eta}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has correlations

\langle\eta^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{m}}(t)\eta^{\beta}_{\mathbf{p}}(t)\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{p}}(1-\delta_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{0}})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;.

(85)

The noise
\eta_{\mathbf{m}}(t)
thus acts on all particles except the cold tracer at
\vec{x}_{\mathbf{0}}
.

To coarse-grain this model into a field theory, we define a continuous variable
{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a displacement field
\vec{u}(\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
via

r_{k}\equiv L^{-1}m_{k},\qquad\vec{u}\left(L^{-1}\mathbf{m}\right)\equiv L^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\vec{x}_{\mathbf{m}}.

(86)

The choice to scale
u
as
L^{\frac{d-2}{2}}
will prove to keep
u\in\mathcal{O}(1)
as
L
is made large. Upon a diffusive rescaling of time
t\rightarrow t/L^{2}
, the dynamics of
\vec{u}({\bf r},t)
read,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{u}=

\displaystyle L^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big[\vec{u}({\bf r}+L^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i})+\vec{u}({\bf r}-L^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i})-2\vec{u}({\bf r})\big]

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2TL^{d}}\vec{\eta}_{L{\bf r}}(t),

(87)

where
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
is the unit vector along lattice direction
i
. Taking
L
large, we find

\partial_{t}\vec{u}({\bf r},t)=\nabla^{2}\vec{u}+\vec{\Lambda}({\bf r},t),

(88)

where the correlations of
\vec{\Lambda}
are obtained from Eq. (
85
) using
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}L^{d}\delta_{L{\bf r},L{\bf r}^{\prime}}=\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})
:

\langle\Lambda_{i}({\bf r},t)\Lambda_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2T\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\left(1-L^{-d}\delta({\bf r})\right).

(89)

In Fourier space, Eq.
88
reads
\partial_{t}\vec{u}({\bf q},t)=-q^{2}\vec{u}+\vec{\Lambda}({\bf q},t)
,
where
\vec{u}({\bf q},t)\equiv\int d^{d}{\bf r}e^{-i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf r}}\vec{u}({\bf r},t)
, and the Fourier-space noise correlations can be shown to equal

\langle\Lambda_{i}({\bf q},t)\Lambda_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2T\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\!\left[(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\bf q}\!+\!{\bf q}^{\prime})\!-\!L^{-d}\right].

(90)

The long-time solution is

\vec{u}({\bf q},t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}dse^{-q^{2}(t-s)}\vec{\Lambda}({\bf q},s),

(91)

from which it follows that

\langle u_{i}({\bf q},t)u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t)\rangle=T\delta_{ij}\left[(2\pi)^{d}\frac{\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})}{q^{2}}-\frac{2L^{-d}}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}\right].

(92)

We are here interested in the fluctuations of the springs connecting the particles and not in the absolute displacements, leading us to consider

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u_{i}({\bf r},t)\cdot\nabla u_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{d}{\bf q}d^{d}{\bf q}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}

\displaystyle e^{i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf r}+i{\bf q}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}^{\prime}}{\bf q}\cdot{\bf q}^{\prime}\langle u_{i}({\bf q},t)u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t)\rangle

(93)

The first term of Eq. (
92
) contributes a term
T\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})
to the above. The contribution of the second term involves the integral

\int\frac{d^{d}{\bf q}d^{d}{\bf q}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\frac{e^{i{\bf q}\cdot{\bf r}+i{\bf q}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}^{\prime}}{\bf q}\cdot{\bf q}^{\prime}}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}=\nabla_{\bf r}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}\int\frac{d^{2d}\tilde{{\bf q}}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\frac{e^{i\tilde{{\bf q}}\cdot\tilde{{\bf r}}}}{\tilde{q}^{2}},

(94)

where
\tilde{{\bf r}}^{T}\equiv({\bf r}^{T},{\bf r}^{\prime T})
, and similarly for
\tilde{{\bf q}}
. The integral on the right hand side is a
2d
-dimensional Coulomb potential
32
evaluating to

\int\frac{d^{2d}\tilde{{\bf q}}}{(2\pi)^{2d}}\frac{e^{i\tilde{{\bf q}}\cdot\tilde{{\bf r}}}}{\tilde{q}^{2}}=\begin{cases}-\log(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2})/4\pi,&d=1,\\
\frac{(d-2)!}{4\pi^{d}}(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2})^{1-d},&d\geq 2.\end{cases}

(95)

It then follows that, for all
d
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u_{i}({\bf r},t)\cdot\nabla u_{j}

\displaystyle({\bf r}^{\prime},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=T\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\left[\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})-\frac{2d!}{\pi^{d}L^{d}}\frac{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf r}^{\prime}}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2})^{1+d}}\right].

(96)

The first term above is the equilibrium result that would be obtained if all particles were at temperature
T
. The second term represents the suppression of fluctuations due to the cold particle at
r=0
, which decays as
r^{-2d}
at large distances.

We can connect back to the microscopic model by generalizing the definition in Eq. (
82
) to arbitrary
d
and
n
:

T_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathbf{m})\equiv\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\sigma(\mathbf{m})}\left\langle(\vec{x}_{\mathbf{p}}-\vec{x}_{\mathbf{m}})^{2}\right\rangle\;.

(97)

From the definitions of Eq. (
86
), we have

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathbf{m})\!=\!\!\!\!\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\sigma(\mathbf{m})}\!\!\!\!\frac{\big\langle\!\left[\vec{u}(L^{-1}\mathbf{p})-\vec{u}(L^{-1}\mathbf{m})\right]^{2}\!\big\rangle}{2nL^{d-2}}=\frac{\big\langle\!\left[\nabla\vec{u}({\bf r},t)\right]^{2}\!\big\rangle}{nL^{d}}

(98)

Using
\delta(0)=L^{d}
, it can then be shown that the temperature suppression satisfies

\Delta T(\mathbf{m})\equiv T-T_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathbf{m})=\frac{d!}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{T}{m^{2d}}.

(99)

Note that for
d=n=1
, this is identical to the result of the exact calculation, Eq. (
83
), for
m\gg 1
. We conclude that the effect of a cold particle within a hot,
d
-dimensional hypercubic lattice decays with the distance to the tracer as
m^{-2d}
.

V.2
Phenomenological theory of a cold inclusion in a hot elastic medium

The model defined in Eq. (
84
) represents a lattice of points (or displacements)
\vec{x}_{\mathbf{m}}
connected by springs of zero rest length.
It does not resist shear or compression at zero temperature and thus does not reflect the elastic properties of most realistic materials.
To extend our results to such materials, we use a standard phenomenological linear elastic theory.
We let
\mathbf{u}({\bf r},t)
denote the displacement of a material particle from its equilibrium position at
{\bf r}
and consider the Hamiltonian
36

\mathcal{H}=\int d^{d}{\bf r}\left[\mu(u_{ik})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda(u_{ii})^{2}\right]\;,

(100)

where
u_{ij}({\bf r})\equiv(\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i})/2
is the symmetrized strain tensor, and
\mu
and
\lambda
are the two Lamé coefficients. To describe the evolution of a gel-like material (or of a solid coupled to a substrate
58
) we use an overdamped dynamics for the lattice

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}({\bf r},t)=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\mathbf{u}({\bf r})}+\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)\;,

(101)

where we have chosen the units of time such that the field has unit mobility, and the correlations of
\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)
are given by Eq. (
89
) as before. We define
\kappa\equiv(\mu+\lambda)/\mu
and set
\mu=1
, which is equivalent to a rescaling of time and temperature as
t\rightarrow\mu t,T\rightarrow T/\mu
. The dynamics of
\mathbf{u}
then read

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}({\bf r},t)=\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}+\kappa\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf r},t)\;.

(102)

The second term of Eq. (
102
), although allowed by symmetry, did not appear in the coarse-grained description (Eq.
88
) of the simpler model defined in Eq. (
84
). The solution to Eq. (
102
) is obtained in Fourier space as

\mathbf{u}({\bf q},t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}ds\exp\left[-(q^{2}\mathbbm{1}+\kappa{\bf q}\otimes{\bf q})(t-s)\right]\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf q},s),

(103)

where
({\bf q}\otimes{\bf q})_{ij}\equiv q_{i}q_{j}
. To diagonalize the exponential, we split
\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf q},s)
into longitudinal and transverse components:

\mathbf{\Lambda}({\bf q},t)=\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\parallel}({\bf q},t)+\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\perp}({\bf q},t).

(104)

where
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\parallel}\equiv(\hat{{\bf q}}\cdot\mathbf{\Lambda})\hat{{\bf q}}
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\perp}\equiv\mathbf{\Lambda}-\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\parallel}
. The former is an eigenvector of
{\bf q}\otimes{\bf q}
with eigenvalue
q^{2}
, while the latter has eigenvalue zero. We thus have

\mathbf{u}=\int_{-\infty}^{t}dse^{-q^{2}(t-s)}\left[e^{-\kappa q^{2}(t-s)}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\parallel}({\bf q},s)+\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\perp}({\bf q},s)\right].

(105)

The two-point correlator
\left\langle u_{i}({\bf q},t)u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t)\right\rangle
is computed in Appendix
D
. A simple scaling analysis then shows that the influence of the cold tracer on the fluctuations of the strain tensor should decay as
1/r^{2d}
, similar to the
\kappa=0
case studied in Sec.
V.1
. To demonstrate this, we compute the effect of the cold tracer on local compressions and expansions of the material, which are quantified by the trace of the strain tensor
u_{ii}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}
. As shown in Appendix
D
, this leads to a form very similar to Eq. (
96
):

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})({\bf r}^{\prime},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{1+\kappa}

\displaystyle\left[\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})-\frac{2d!}{\pi^{d}L^{d}}\frac{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf r}^{\prime}}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2})^{1+d}}\right].

(106)

The effect of the cold tracer is thus long-ranged and decays as
r^{-2d}
, as before.

We note that the long-ranged effect of the cold inclusion is a purely nonequilibrium phenomenon. Indeed, if instead of cooling the inclusion at the origin we impose a localized strain through an external potential, the effect is local: As shown in Appendix
D
, the last term in Eq. (
106
) proportional to
{{\bf r}\cdot{\bf r}^{\prime}}/{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2})^{1+d}}
is then replaced by one proportional to
\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})
.

Finally, it might be tempting to predict the local effective temperature of the gel using the heat equation with a cold sink at the origin. We note, however, that the presence of hot and cold heat reservoirs makes energy a non-conserved field, so that drawing predictions from the heat equation is not justified

8
. Indeed, it is easy to check that
T_{\rm eff}({\bf r})=T-ar^{-2d}
is not a solution to
\nabla^{2}T_{\rm eff}({\bf r})=0
.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a general theory of the dynamics of a cold tracer particle within a bath of linearly-coupled hot Brownian particles.
We first constructed the generalized Langevin equation of the tracer for the most general linearly-coupled bath, allowing for an arbitrary external potential acting on the tracer (Sec.
II
).
We applied this result to two specific models: first, a fully-connected model in which the tracer interacts harmonically with
N
hot particles at once, and second, a model in which the cold tracer is inserted within a harmonic loop of hot particles.
The former can be seen as a mean-field model of a tracer in a fluid
2
, and was found to transition from an FDT-violating dynamics at finite
N
to an equilibrium dynamics at large
N
.
The loop model, which can be thought to describe a gel, was found to violate the FDT irrespectively of the size of the loop.
Our results thus suggest that a cold tracer in a fluid of hot colloids will equilibrate only if the density of colloids or radius of the tracer is sufficiently large, while a cold particle in a hot gel will never equilibrate.

In Sec.
III
, we studied the emergence of nonequilibrium features in the fully connected model perturbatively for large but finite
N
. We found that the tracer departs from the Boltzmann distribution at leading order in
N^{-1}
and, at higher orders, displays signatures of irreversibility such as entropy production, ratchet currents and long-ranged density rectification by asymmetric obstacles. These findings establish analytically a connection between the dynamics of a cold tracer in a hot bath and those of an active particle
2
;
59
.

Prior to this work and its companion Letter
2
, existing results on the behavior of a cold tracer in a hot bath pertained only to free or harmonically trapped tracers, focusing on such quantities as the long-time diffusivity and drag coefficients of the tracer
28
;
75
;
29
;
23
. These results suggested the cold tracer to be amenable to an equilibrium description with an effective temperature, in spite of the theoretical possibility of a nonequilibrium dynamics driven by the steady heat flux
73
;
82
;
38
. Our work demonstrates conclusively that the dynamics of the tracer cannot be adequately described by equilibrium physics outside the large-density limit.

To characterize the impact of the tracer on the bath itself, we considered in Sec
IV
the case of a linear bath coupled to a harmonically trapped tracer, which is exactly solvable. We applied this to study the suppression of bath fluctuations by the cold tracer in the loop model and found it to be long-ranged, decaying with the inverse square of the distance to the tracer. We generalized this result to higher-dimensional lattices and elastic media in Sec
V
, finding that the damping of fluctuations decays with the distance to the cold particle as
r^{-2d}
. A cold inclusion in a hot gel will thus cool down the entire gel.

Beyond their theoretical interest, the models introduced in this article may be of experimental relevance because they describe the dynamics of a passive tracer within any bath whose fluctuations are enhanced by an external energy source.
The external drive could arise, for example, by irradiation of the bath particles
74
, or due to their coupling to an out-of-equilibrium chemical reaction as in the case of active enzyme solutions
22
;
48
;
61
;
15
;
81
.
For a passive colloid in an active enzyme solution, it can be argued that, thanks to scale separation, a micron-sized tracer will interact with many bath particles at once, so that the large-
N
regime may be within reach. We note that, when the tracer is embedded in a momentum-conserving fluid, hydrodynamics may play an important role
37
;
16
;
47
;
69
, for instance leading to the emergence of long-time tails
71
. We believe that our results should be robust to these effects since our fully-connected and short-ranged models agree very well, despite the latter having an additional conserved density field.
The existence of additional hydrodynamic modes thus does not seem to significantly impact our predictions.
Beyond the infinite
N
limit, our predictions on the scaling of the steady current with the bath density could be verified, for example, using asymmetric corrugated substrates.
As for our results on cold inclusions in hot elastic media, we believe that active solids with locally tunable activity constitute an ideal experimental platform to measure the predicted long-ranged correlations
42
;
5
.

to
d
dimensions, and furthermore allow for a nonzero tracer temperature
T_{0}>0
.
We let
{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the tracer position. As before, we pack all bath degrees of freedom into a vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
; if there are
N
bath particles in
d
dimensions, then
M=dN
. The most general linear coupling can be expressed in the form

\mathcal{H}({\bf r},\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}+{\bf r}^{T}C\mathbf{x}+\tilde{U}({\bf r}),

(107)

where
A
and
C
are
M\times M
and
d\times M
matrices, respectively. The equations of motion are then

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf r}}

\displaystyle=-\mu\nabla\tilde{U}-\mu C\mathbf{x}+\sqrt{2\mu T_{0}}\bm{\zeta}(t),

(108)

\displaystyle\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle=-A{\mathbf{x}}-C^{T}{\bf r}+\sqrt{2T}\bm{\eta}(t),

(109)

where
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are independent centered Gaussian white noises with correlations
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\zeta_{i}(t)\zeta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. As in the one-dimensional case, we integrate the bath dynamics to obtain a closed equation for the tracer

\dot{{\bf r}}=-\mu\nabla\tilde{U}+\mu\int_{-\infty}^{t}ds\,Ce^{-A(t-s)}C^{T}{\bf r}(s)+\mu\bm{\xi}(t),

(110)

where
\bm{\xi}(t)\equiv\sqrt{2\mu T_{0}}\bm{\zeta}(t)+\sqrt{2T}\int^{t}ds\,Ce^{A(t-s)}\bm{\eta}(s)
. This can be recast into a generalized Langevin equation using the same manipulations as in Sec.
II
. The result is:

\int_{-\infty}^{t}dsK(t-s)\dot{{\bf r}}(s)=-\nabla\tilde{U}({\bf r}(t))+\bm{\xi}(t),

(111)

where

\displaystyle K(s)

\displaystyle\equiv 2\mu^{-1}\delta(s)\mathbb{1}+CA^{-1}\left(e^{-A|s|}-1\right)C^{T},

(112)

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\bm{\xi}(t+s)\otimes\bm{\xi}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=2\mu^{-1}T_{0}\delta(s)\mathbb{1}+TCA^{-1}e^{-A|s|}C^{T}.

(113)

When
T_{0}=T
, we have an FDT upon shifting the potential as:

U({\bf r})=\tilde{U}({\bf r})-\frac{1}{2}{\bf r}^{T}CA^{-1}C^{T}{\bf r}.

(114)

It can then be verified that the analysis of the fully-connected model in Sec.
II.1
generalizes without modification to higher dimensions: the friction and noise kernels are isotropic and given by Eqs. (
12
). If the tracer temperature is nonzero, this simply contributes a term
2\mu^{-1}T_{0}\delta(s)
to Eq. (
12b
). Since this term is
\mathcal{O}(N^{0})
, the large-
N
results are unmodified.

Appendix B
Perturbation theory for the fully-connected model

B.1
Proof that
H_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n+1

Here, we prove that the expansion coefficients
\{H_{n}\}
of the effective Hamiltonian in Eq. (
33
) are polynomials in
p
of degree
n+1
, so that
h^{(n)}_{m}=0
for
m>n+1
. The proof is by induction in
n
with Eq. (
35
) as the
n=1
base case. Suppose the statement is true for all
H_{2},\dots,H_{n-1}
. Substituting
m=n+2+k
into Eq. (
38
) with
k
a nonnegative integer, we obtain

T(n+3+k)(n+4+k)h_{n+4+k}^{(n)}-(n+2+k)h_{n+2+k}^{(n)}\\
=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n+2+k}(n+k-\ell+3)(\ell+1)\sum_{q=1}^{n-1}h_{n+k-\ell+3}^{(q)}h_{\ell+1}^{(n-q)}\;.

(115)

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists nonzero terms in the sum on the RHS of Eq. (
115
). By the induction hypothesis, such terms must satisfy both
n+k-\ell+2\leq q
and
\ell\leq n-q
. Adding these inequalities leads to a contradiction, implying that the RHS of Eq. (
115
) is zero, and hence that

h^{(n)}_{n+4+k}=\frac{n+2+k}{T(n+3+k)(n+4+k)}h^{(n)}_{n+2+k}.

(116)

We now partition the power series for
H_{n}
as

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n+1}h^{(n)}_{m}p^{m}+p^{n+2}G_{n}(x,p),

(117)

\displaystyle G_{n}(x,p)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h^{(n)}_{n+2+k}(x)p^{k}.

(118)

Using Eq. (
116
), we rewrite
G_{n}(x,p)
as

G_{n}(x,p)=h_{n+2}^{(n)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\ell}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2T}\right)^{\ell}+ph_{n+3}^{(n)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}b_{\ell}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2T}\right)^{\ell},

(119)

where we have separated the sum into its even and odd parts, and where
\{a_{\ell},b_{\ell}\}
are defined recursively by
a_{0}=b_{0}=1
and

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\ell+1}}{a_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{n+2}{2}+\ell\right)}{\left(\frac{n+3}{2}+\ell\right)\left(\frac{n+4}{2}+\ell\right)},

(120)

\displaystyle\frac{b_{\ell+1}}{b_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{n+3}{2}+\ell\right)}{\left(\frac{n+4}{2}+\ell\right)\left(\frac{n+5}{2}+\ell\right)}\;.

(121)

Since these are rational functions of
\ell
, we identify the sums in (
119
) as generalized hypergeometric series
3

G_{n}(x,p)=h_{n+2}^{(n)}\,{}_{2}F_{2}\left[\frac{n+2}{2},1;\frac{n+3}{2},\frac{n+4}{2};\frac{p^{2}}{2T}\right]\\

(122)

The leading terms in a large-
p
expansion of the above are

G_{n}(x,p)=e^{\frac{p^{2}}{2T}}\frac{T^{\frac{n+3}{2}}}{p^{n+3}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Big[(n+2)(n+1)!!\,h^{(n)}_{n+2}\\
+\sqrt{T}(n+3)(n+2)!!\,h^{(n)}_{n+3}+\mathcal{O}(p^{-1})\Big].

(123)

We see that
G_{n}
(and hence
H_{n}
) grows exponentially in

for large

\Psi(x,p)\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}/T}
is not normalizable in
p
unless
G_{n}=0
. This implies that

, so that by Eq. (
116

h^{(n)}_{m}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad m>n+1.

(124)

We conclude that
H_{n}
is a polynomial in
p
of degree
n+1
.

dimensions, so long as the asymmetric external potential is unidirectional.

In the general case, more work is required to compute the correction to the Boltzmann distribution and the entropy production rate. The
d
-dimensional generalization of Eq. (
III.1
) is

\displaystyle\left\{\varepsilon\sqrt{k}\left(\nabla U\!\cdot\!\nabla_{\bf p}-{\bf p}\!\cdot\!\nabla\right)+\left[\varepsilon({\bf p}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\nabla U+k(1\!+\!\varepsilon){\bf p}\right]\cdot\nabla_{\bf p}\right.

\displaystyle+kT\nabla_{\bf p}^{2}\Big\}\mathcal{H}({\bf r},{\bf p})=k\left(\nabla_{\bf p}\mathcal{H}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon T\nabla^{2}U+Tdk(1+\varepsilon)

(167)

As before, we expand
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}H_{n}({\bf r},{\bf p})
, and obtain the following recursion relation for
n\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\left[T\nabla_{{\bf p}}^{2}-{\bf p}\cdot\nabla_{\bf p}\right]H_{n}+\bigg\{\!\left[{\bf p}+\left(k^{-1/2}+\frac{{\bf p}\cdot\nabla}{k}\right)\nabla U\right]\!\cdot\!\nabla_{\bf p}

\displaystyle-k^{-1/2}{\bf p}\cdot\nabla\bigg\}H_{n-1}=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\left(\nabla_{\bf p}H_{m}\right)\left(\nabla_{\bf p}H_{n-m}\right)

(168)

The polynomial expansion of
H_{n}
in Eq.
37
now has tensorial coefficients:

H_{n}({\bf r},{\bf p})=\sum_{k}h^{(n,k)}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k}}({\bf r})\,\,p_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{\alpha_{k}}.

(169)

The corresponding generalization of Eq.(
38
) is cumbersome. We instead work out the first few orders by brute-force substitution. The results are listed below, setting
k=1
for brevity:

\displaystyle H_{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}}{2}+U,

(170)

\displaystyle H_{1}=

\displaystyle U+\frac{\left(\nabla U\right)^{2}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}T\nabla^{2}U+\frac{1}{2}\left({\bf p}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}U+\frac{p^{2}}{2},

(171)

\displaystyle H_{2}=

\displaystyle f_{2}({\bf r})+\frac{T}{2}({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)\nabla^{2}U-\frac{1}{6}({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)^{3}U,

(172)

\displaystyle H_{3}=

\displaystyle f_{3}({\bf r})-{\bf p}\cdot\nabla f_{2}-\frac{T}{2}({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)\nabla^{2}U+\frac{1}{6}({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)^{3}U,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}(\nabla U)\cdot({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)^{2}\nabla U+\frac{1}{24}({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)^{4}U

\displaystyle+Tp_{\alpha}\!\left[(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\lambda}U)(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}U)\!-\!\frac{3}{2}(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}U)(\partial_{\beta}\nabla^{2}U)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}p_{\gamma}(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\lambda}U)(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{\lambda}U).

(173)

with
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
undetermined at this order. Using these results, we compute the marginal distribution of the tracer position as
p({\bf r})\propto e^{-U_{\mathrm{eff}}({\bf r})/T}
, where

U_{\mathrm{eff}}({\bf r})=U+\varepsilon\left[\frac{\left(\nabla U\right)^{2}}{2}-T\nabla^{2}U+U\right]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

(174)

Finally, we generalize the result on the entropy production rate. The dynamical action of Eq. (
45
) reads

S=\int^{t}_{0}ds\frac{\left[\dot{{\bf p}}+\nabla U+({\bf p}\cdot\nabla)\nabla U+(N+1){\bf p}\right]}{4TN}

(175)

Using similar manipulations as in Eqs. (
III.3
)-(
49
), we obtain

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\left\langle\left({\bf p}\cdot\nabla\right)^{3}U\right\rangle.

(176)

To compute this, we expand as before

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\left\langle\left({\bf p}\cdot\nabla\right)^{3}U\left[1+\varepsilon A_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}A_{2}\right]\right\rangle_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(177)

where
A_{1}({\bf r},{\bf p})=H_{1}/T
and
A_{2}({\bf r},{\bf p})=H_{1}^{2}/2T^{2}-H_{2}/T
. Upon evaluating the Gaussian averages over
{\bf p}
, we obtain

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon^{3}T}{2}\left\langle\left(\nabla^{2}U\right)^{2}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(178)

This scales as
N^{-3}
, proving that the time-reversible yet non-Boltzmann regime extends to higher dimensions.

Appendix C
Weakly non-linear external potentials

Here, we show how the general formalism of Sec.
IV
, and in particular Eqs. (
62
) and (
64
), can be extended to weakly anharmonic potentials acting on the tracer. We thus consider a potential energy:

\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A{\mathbf{x}}+\varepsilon Q({\mathbf{x}}),

(179)

where
\varepsilon
is taken to be small. The equations of motion (
52
) become
\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}=-Z{\mathbf{x}}-M\nabla H+\mathbf{\Gamma}
, so their solution read

{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}dsG(t-s)M\nabla Q({\mathbf{x}}(s)),

(180)

where
{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
is a solution to the linear theory with
\varepsilon=0
, and
G
is the unperturbed Green’s function given in Eq. (
55
). To leading order in
\varepsilon
, the value of any two-point correlator is then given by

\displaystyle\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle\!=\!\langle x_{i}x_{k}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\!-\!\varepsilon\!\int_{0}^{t}\!ds\mu_{k}\left[G_{jk}(t\!-\!s)\langle x_{i}(t)\partial_{k}Q({\mathbf{x}}(s))\rangle_{0}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-G_{ik}(t\!-\!s)\langle x_{j}(t)\partial_{k}Q({\mathbf{x}}(s))\rangle_{0}\right],

(181)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
denotes an expectation value with respect to the Gaussian measure of the
\varepsilon=0
linear theory. For simplicity, we focus on the case where the nonlinearity acts only on the tracer. In the notation of Eqs. (
1
), this means that
V
is quadratic but
U
is not:
\varepsilon Q(\mathbf{x})=U(x_{0})
. We pick a quartic form for
U
:

U(x_{0})=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}x_{0}^{4}.

(182)

It then follows from Eq. (
181
) and Wick’s theorem that

\displaystyle\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle=\langle x_{i}x_{k}\rangle_{0}-3\varepsilon\!\int_{0}^{t}\!ds\mu_{0}\left[G_{j0}(t\!-\!s)\langle x_{i}(t)x_{0}(s)\rangle_{0}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-G_{i0}(t\!-\!s)\langle x_{j}(t)x_{0}(s)\rangle_{0}\right]\left\langle x_{0}(s)^{2}\right\rangle,

(183)

To proceed, we note that in the linear theory, unequal-time correlators are related to equal-time correlators through the (noiseless) Green’s function
54
. In particular, defining the matrix
K_{ij}(s,t)=\langle x_{i}(t+s)x_{j}(t)\rangle_{0}
, it is straightforward to verify that
K(s,t)=G(s)K(0,t)
. Using this fact, Eq. (
183
) can be shown to imply the following form for the steady-state two-point correlator,

\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle=\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle_{0}-3\epsilon\langle x_{0}^{2}\rangle_{0}\langle x_{k}x_{0}\rangle_{0}B_{ijk}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(184)

where

B_{ijk}\equiv\mu_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\left[G_{j0}(s)G_{ik}(s)+G_{i0}(s)G_{jk}(s)\right].

(185)

As done in section
IV
, we may diagonalize
G
and express
B_{ijk}
explicitly in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
Z
. We find

B_{ijk}=\mu_{0}\sum_{n,m=0}^{N}\frac{u_{0}^{(n)}u_{k}^{(m)}}{\lambda_{n}+\lambda_{m}}\left[v_{j}^{(n)}v_{i}^{(m)}+v_{i}^{(n)}v_{j}^{(m)}\right].

(186)

where we remind that
\{\mathbf{v}^{n}\}
and
\{\mathbf{u}^{n}\}
are respectively the right and left eigenvectors of
Z
.

Upon computing Eq. (
184
) for a particular model, we would like to compare the result to what it would be in an equilibrium ensemble. The equilibrium expectation value of any observable
f({\mathbf{x}})
can be computed perturbatively in
\varepsilon
following standard procedures

\displaystyle\langle f({\mathbf{x}})\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int d{\mathbf{x}}f({\mathbf{x}})\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2T}{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A{\mathbf{x}}-T^{-1}U(x_{0})\right]}{\int d{\mathbf{x}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2T}{\mathbf{x}}^{T}A{\mathbf{x}}-T^{-1}U(x_{0})\right]}

\displaystyle=\langle f\rangle_{0}^{\mathrm{eq}}-T^{-1}\left(\langle fU\rangle_{0}^{\mathrm{eq}}-\langle f\rangle_{0}^{\mathrm{eq}}\langle U\rangle_{0}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(187)

using which it can be shown that,

\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}=TA^{-1}_{ij}-3\epsilon T^{2}A^{-1}_{i0}A^{-1}_{j0}A^{-1}_{00}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(188)

This allows us to write an explicit expression for the ratio of equilibrium and nonequilibrium correlators, as was done in the linear case:

\displaystyle R_{ij}\equiv\frac{\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle}{\langle x_{i}x_{j}\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}}=R_{ij}^{(0)}\left\{1+\varepsilon\left[\frac{A^{-1}_{i0}A^{-1}_{j0}A^{-1}_{00}}{A^{-1}_{00}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.-\frac{\big(A^{-1}_{00}-\Delta^{(0)}_{00}\big)\big(A^{-1}_{k0}-\Delta^{(0)}_{k0}\big)B_{ijk}}{A^{-1}_{ij}-\Delta_{ij}^{(0)}}\right]\right\},

(189)

where
R^{(0)}
is the
\varepsilon=0
value of
R
given by Eq. (
64
) and
\Delta^{(0)}
is defined by Eq. (
62
).

As an application of this perturbation theory, it can be shown that for the fully-connected models of section
IV.1
, in the cases where
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{ij}^{(0)}=1
, we also have
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}R_{ij}=1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
according to Eq. (
189
). This not only confirms the conclusion of Section
II
that the tracer equilibrates in arbitrary potentials when
N\rightarrow\infty
, but also allows the finite-
N
corrections to be computed exactly to leading order in the nonlinearity.

Appendix D
Two-point correlator for an overdamped elastic medium with a cold inclusion

We list here the correlator
\langle u_{i}({\bf q},t)u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t)\rangle
computed from Eqs. (
105
) and (
89
). First, Eq. (
89
) directly leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda^{\parallel}_{i}({\bf q},s)\Lambda^{\parallel}_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},s^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{i}q_{j}^{\prime}{\bf q}\cdot{\bf q}^{\prime}}{q^{2}q^{\prime 2}}g({\bf q},{\bf q}^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})

(190)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Lambda^{\parallel}_{i}({\bf q},s)\Lambda_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},s^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{q^{2}}g({\bf q},{\bf q}^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})

(191)

where

g({\bf q},{\bf q}^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})\equiv 2T\delta(s-s^{\prime})\left[(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})-L^{-d}\right]

(192)

Using this, long but straightforward algebra leads to:

\displaystyle\langle u_{i}({\bf q},t)u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime},t)\rangle=T(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\bf q}+

\displaystyle{\bf q}^{\prime})\bigg[\frac{\delta_{ij}}{q^{2}}-\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{q^{4}}\bigg]

\displaystyle-2TL^{-d}H_{ij},

(193)

where the influence of the cold tracer is contained within the tensor
H
, defined as

\displaystyle H_{ij}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{ij}}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}+\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{q^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(1+\kappa)q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}-\frac{1}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{q_{i}^{\prime}q_{j}^{\prime}}{q^{\prime 2}}\left[\frac{1}{q^{2}+(1+\kappa)q^{\prime 2}}-\frac{1}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{q_{i}q_{j}^{\prime}{\bf q}\cdot{\bf q}^{\prime}}{q^{2}q^{\prime 2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}+\frac{1}{(1+\kappa)(q^{2}+q^{\prime 2})}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+\kappa)q^{2}+q^{\prime 2}}-\frac{1}{q^{2}+(1+\kappa)q^{\prime 2}}\bigg].

(194)

Equation (
D
) can be used to write a corresponding correlation function for the strain tensor
2u_{ij}({\bf q})=-i[q_{i}u_{j}({\bf q})+q_{j}u_{i}({\bf q})]
. Since
H\sim 1/q^{2}
, the contribution of the tracer to the real-space correlation function of the strain tensor will scale as
1/x^{-2d}
, so that the cold tracer has a long-ranged effect on the fluctuations of the elastic medium.

As a demonstration, we compute the correlation function of the trace of the strain tensor
u_{ii}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}
, which describes local compressive deformations of the material. We find

-\frac{1+\kappa}{T}\langle q_{i}u_{i}({\bf q})q_{j}^{\prime}u_{j}({\bf q}^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})+\frac{2{\bf q}\cdot{\bf q}^{\prime}}{L^{d}(q^{2}+q^{\prime 2})}

(195)

The inverse transform of this was computed in Sec.
V.1
, Eqs.
93
and
96
, leading to the result given in Eq. (
106
).

Appendix E
Localized inclusions in equilibrium elastic media

Here, we revisit the models discussed in Sec.
V
and consider a different perturbation: rather than locally cooling the central lattice node at
\mathbf{r}=0
, we instead stretch or compress the springs attached to this node through an external potential. If all springs are stretched (compressed) equally, then this is equivalent to pinning the divergence of the displacement field
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}|_{\mathbf{r}=0}
to some positive (negative) value
g
. We let this pinning be harmonic with stiffness
c
and thus modify the Hamiltonian of Eq. (
100
) as follows:

\mathcal{H}=\int d^{d}\mathbf{r}\left[\mu(u_{ik})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda(u_{ii})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}c\delta(\mathbf{r})(u_{ii}-g)^{2}\right]\;.

(196)

where we have used
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\equiv u_{ii}
. As this change does not drive the system out of equilibrium, the stationary distribution of the strain field is the Boltzmann distribution
\mathcal{P}[u]\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}[u]/T}
. Notably, the Hamiltonian does not couple the fluctuations of the strain tensor at different locations, so that
u_{ij}(0)
is independent from any
u_{ij}(\mathbf{r}\neq 0)
. By separating out the traceless part of the strain tensor, we may write a Hamiltonian for
u_{ii}
:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\int d^{d}{\bf r}\left[\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{2}(u_{ii})^{2}+\frac{c}{2}\delta({\bf r})(u_{ii}-g)^{2}\right].

The Gaussian correlators may now be computed by inverting the kernel of the quadratic form above, yielding

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})(r)\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{cg}{\lambda+2\mu+cL^{d}}\delta({\bf r})

(197)

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})({\bf r})(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})({\bf r}^{\prime})\rangle_{c}=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{\lambda+2\mu}\Big[\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle-\frac{c}{\lambda+2\mu+cL^{d}}\delta({\bf r})\delta({\bf r}^{\prime})\Big].

(198)

Comparing Eq. (
198
) to Eq. (
106
) shows that the long-ranged suppression of fluctuations has been replaced by a purely local effect: Away from
{\bf r}=0
, the correlations are entirely insensitive to the localized potential, showing that local perturbations have purely local effects in equilibrium elastic media. Had there been higher-order gradient terms in Eq. (
196
), we would have found that the effect of the inclusion decays exponentially with a finite correlation length. The long-ranged influence of the local inclusion is thus a fundamentally nonequilibrium effect, with analogies in diffusive and active matter systems
24
.
