Trajectories of post-discharge cognitive function and associated risk factors in ICU survivors: a secondary analysis
Authors: Jiyeon Kang, Gee Young Suh, Chi Ryang Chung, Young-Shin Cho, Jiwon Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment is a significant concern among intensive care unit (ICU) survivors, yet post-discharge cognitive trajectories remain understudied. This study aimed to explore cognitive function trajectories and associated factors in ICU survivors after hospital discharge. This secondary analysis used data from a prospective post-ICU cohort established in Busan, South Korea. A total of 329 ICU survivors who completed cognitive assessments at both early and late follow-up were included. Cognitive function was measured using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. Approximately 40% of ICU survivors experienced mild cognitive impairment (MCI) within 12 months. Participants were classified into four groups based on the trajectory of cognitive impairment status: Normal, Recovered, Delayed Onset, and Persistent. Compared to the Normal Group, older age and comorbidities were significantly associated with the Recovered MCI Group. In the Delayed Onset MCI Group, older age, admission through emergency department, and trauma-related admission were determinants. In the Persistent MCI Group, factors included older age, lower education level, comorbidities, and discharge to an extended care facility. Cognitive trajectories may vary among ICU survivors after discharge. Based on our findings, a better understanding of post-discharge cognitive trajectories may help guide long-term monitoring and individualized rehabilitation strategies to improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32872-4.Keywords: Cognitive dysfunction, Intensive care unit, Post-intensive care syndrome, Secondary data analysis, SurvivorsSubject terms: Risk factors, Epidemiology

Introduction

As mortality rates decline despite the increasing age and disease severity among critical care patients, interest in their post-discharge health outcomes has grown. Long-term medical resource utilization and mortality rates of patients discharged from the intensive care unit (ICU) are significantly higher than those of patients discharged from general wards1. These adverse health outcomes in ICU survivors may be linked to post-intensive care syndrome (PICS). PICS involves new or worsening mental, cognitive, and physical impairments resulting from ICU admission and treatment2. Approximately half of the survivors experience various combinations of these impairments2,3.

Among the three domains of PICS, cognitive impairment serves as a major barrier to returning to work and resuming family and social roles, thereby leading to a persistent decline in quality of life among ICU survivors4. It encompasses problems in global cognition, including executive function, memory, and attention5. The Bringing to Light the Risk Factors and Incidence of Neuropsychological Dysfunction in ICU Survivors (BRAIN-ICU) study revealed that, 3 months after discharge, approximately 40% of patients exhibited cognitive impairment comparable to that seen in moderate traumatic brain injury, with delirium identified as a significant risk factor6. Additionally, a prospective cohort study in Korea3 found that 25% of patients met the criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) 3 months post-discharge, with older age, lower education level, and discharge to a long-term care facility as contributing risk factor.

Research on the recovery trajectory of critical illness indicates that survivors’ functional impairments rapidly improve during the early post-discharge period, within the first 3–6 months, and gradually return to baseline over subsequent years, lasting well beyond 12 months7–9. However, cognitive impairments present a more challenging recovery; it has been reported that 30–80% of survivors do not regain their pre-ICU cognitive level10,11, with impairments persisting for up to nine years12. Furthermore, cognitive impairments can adversely affect other areas of PICS, including physical disabilities, mental health issues, and health-related quality of life4,13.

Despite the high incidence and long-term persistence of cognitive impairment among ICU survivors, research in this area is limited, especially compared with studies on the physical and mental domains14. Moreover, little is known about trajectory in cognitive function after discharge. While the extent of cognitive limitations and recovery in survivors varies based on individual characteristics and circumstances15,16, there is a notable scarcity of studies that identify the factors influencing this trajectory. Consequently, this study aimed to explore the trajectory in cognitive function and the factors related to this trajectory after hospital discharge in critically ill patients through a secondary analysis of multicenter cohort data from Korea.

Methods

Design

This secondary analysis used data from a prospective cohort study on PICS in ICU survivors. The primary study aimed to identify the incidence and risk factors for PICS subtypes, including physical, cognitive, and mental health impairments, assessed 3 months after hospital discharge3. In this secondary analysis, we aimed to examine the trajectories of cognitive function and their associated factors.

Setting and sample

The primary study was conducted from June 2019 to July 2022 in 19 ICUs in four university hospitals in Busan, Korea. The participants included patients aged 18 years or older who had been admitted to the ICU for more than 24 hours. Patients who had been admitted to the ICU within the past year, those with a clinically diagnosed physical, mental, or cognitive disability prior to ICU admission and those who were unable to communicate were excluded. During the COVID-19 pandemic, patients with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection were managed exclusively in designated cohort isolation units and were not admitted to general ICUs in Korea. Therefore, no COVID-19 patients were included in this study.

Prior to conducting the analysis, we also verified that the data size was adequate. The minimum sample size for logistic regression can be calculated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow method17, which involves dividing the number of predictors by the incidence rate of the dependent variable and then multiplying the result by 10. In the current study, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 325, based on 13 predictors and an incidence rate of cognitive impairment of 40% in ICU survivors6. We analyzed data from 329 participants, thus meeting the minimum sample size.

In this study, we aimed to identify cognitive function trajectory groups of ICU survivors by categorizing the assessment timeline from the original cohort dataset into early and late post-discharge periods. Based on previous literature defining the early post-discharge period as within 6 months after critical illness7–9, cognitive assessments conducted at 3 or 6 months in the original cohort were classified as representing the early post-discharge period. Assessments collected at 12 months were classified as representing the late post-discharge period.

Measures: cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)18 during face-to-face interviews and the MoCA-Blind19 for telephone interviews when necessary. The MoCA consists of 32 items that assess short-term memory, visuospatial abilities, executive functions, attention, concentration, working memory, language, and orientation to time and place. Scores range from 0 to 30. The MoCA-Blind consists of 24 items, excluding those that require visual ability. It has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity for detecting cognitive impairment when visual items cannot be assessed or when administered by telephone19. The total score ranges from 0 to 22. According to the official information provided on the MoCA website20, MoCA-Blind scores were proportionally converted to a 30-point scale (converted score = [MoCA-Blind score × 30] ÷ 22) to allow for direct comparison with MoCA results. A cutoff score of 23 was applied to both MoCA and converted MoCA-Blind scores to define MCI18.

During the early post-discharge period, 37.7% of participants were assessed using the face-to-face MoCA and 62.3% with the MoCA-Blind, whereas during the late post-discharge period, 0.6% and 99.4% were assessed with each version, respectively. We classified participants into four groups according to the trajectory of MCI status between the early and late post-discharge periods. The first group, the Normal Group, included participants who had no MCI in either the early or late post-discharge periods. The second, the Recovered MCI Group, comprised participants who had MCI in the early post-discharge period but not in the late period. The third, the Delayed Onset MCI Group, included those with no MCI in the early period but who developed MCI in the late period. Lastly, the Persistent MCI Group consisted of participants with MCI in both the early and late post-discharge periods.

Procedure

We used the following deidentified demographic and clinical data from the primary study, along with participants’ MoCA scores: age, gender, employment, education, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACE II) score or Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS III) at admission, Functional Comorbidity Index (FCI), awareness of ICU admission, ICU admission route, reason for ICU admission, mechanical ventilator use, delirium, ICU length of stay, and discharge place.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 27.0. As this was a secondary analysis, we used the variable categorizations defined in the original cohort study3, including both cutoff-based classifications and predefined nominal categories Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile range were calculated for the MoCA scores and demographic and clinical characteristics. Disease severity was standardized and analyzed using either APACHE II or SAPS III scores, depending on the hospital. Changes in MoCA scores over time were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. Group comparisons were conducted according to variable type. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test. For continuous variables, Welch’s ANOVA was applied to account for unequal variances and group size imbalances among the four trajectory groups. The determinants of each MCI group compared with the normal group were examined using multinomial logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Youngsan University (YSUIRB-202408-HR-161-02; 19 August 2024). As this study involved a secondary analysis of anonymized data, the need to obtain informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Youngsan University.

Results

A total of 891 patients were enrolled in the original cohort study and we analyzed data from 329 participants who completed both the early and late post-discharge cognitive period (Fig. 1). The remaining participants were lost to follow-up due to inability to contact, withdrawal, or death. Participants who were withdrawn tended to be older or have higher illness severity than those who completed both assessments (Supplementary Table S1).

Participants’ characteristics

The participants’ characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The average age was 57.99 ± 13.23 years and 58.7% were men. The majority (72.2%) of the participants had at least a high school diploma. Approximately half (50.6%) were employed, and half (49.5%) were admitted through the emergency department. The average disease severity scores were 10.68 ± 6.18 (APACHE II) and 32.86 ± 16.23 (SAPS III). Most participants (78.4%) had 0–1 comorbid disease, and most (79.6%) had awareness of their ICU admission. Participants who required mechanical ventilation during their ICU stay accounted for 16.7%, and 11.2% experienced delirium. The mean ICU stay was 3.89 ± 4.17 days, and 84.8% of the participants were discharged to their home.

Related factors of cognitive trajectory group

The results of the multinomial logistic regression analysis, which compared the determinants of each MCI group to those of the Normal Group by including variables that were significant in the univariate analysis, are presented in Table 4. For the Recovered MCI Group, the determinants compared to the Normal Group included age ≥ 65 years (OR = 5.36, 95% CI = 2.01–14.24, p = 0.001) and FCI ≥ 2 (OR = 2.71, 95% CI = 1.03–7.12, p = 0.043). The determinants for the Delayed Onset MCI Group were age ≥ 65 years (OR = 2.49, 95% CI = 1.15–5.42, p = 0.021), admission through the emergency department (OR = 2.46, 95% CI = 1.20–5.05, p = 0.014), and trauma-related admission (OR = 3.64, 95% CI = 1.29–10.29, p = 0.015). For the Persistent MCI Group, the determinants included age ≥ 65 years (OR = 10.77, 95% CI = 4.55–25.50, p < 0.001), education less than high school graduate (OR = 4.25, 95% CI = 1.90–9.50, p < 0.001), FCI ≥ 2 (OR = 2.53, 95% CI = 1.10–5.84, p = 0.029), and discharge to an extended care facility (OR = 4.18, 95% CI = 1.48–11.81, p = 0.007).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of post-discharge cognitive trajectories, approximately 40% of the ICU survivors experienced MCI within 12 months, and their cognitive trajectories were classified into four distinct groups: Normal group, Recovered MCI Group, Delayed Onset MCI Group, and Persistent MCI Group. These MCI groups were associated with factors such as older age, comorbidities, admission through the emergency department, trauma-related admissions, education less than a high school diploma, and discharge to an extended care facility.

In the current study, about 40% of participants experienced MCI within one year after ICU discharge, which is comparable to that observed in older adults in their 80s21. Given that the average age of our participants was 58 years, this finding suggests that ICU survivors are at high risk of cognitive impairment. Furthermore, 31% of our participants experienced MCI 12 months after discharge, which is consistent with the 24–34% incidence rate reported in previous studies at the same time point6,22. These findings, along with those of earlier studies, imply that cognitive impairment in ICU survivors can persist for more than one year.

We observed that older age and comorbidities were key determinants in the MCI groups, with age being particularly associated with all three MCI groups. Older age and comorbidities are well-established risk factors for cognitive impairment23. As the global population ages, the age of patients admitted to ICUs has also increased24, and the number of comorbidities tends to increase with age25. This trend may further increase the risk of developing cognitive impairment among ICU survivors. Health care providers should be particularly vigilant when screening survivors for cognitive impairment, recognizing that age and comorbidities are major risk factors.

In this study, emergency and trauma-related admissions were identified as determinants for the Delayed Onset MCI Group. Hall et al.26 reported that the incidence of cognitive impairment in trauma patients was 37% 1 month after injury, increasing to 45% at 3 months. Another study27 reported that 55% of trauma patients experienced cognitive impairment 12 months after discharge. Taken together, previous studies and our findings suggest that trauma survivors may be at risk for delayed cognitive deterioration. Among our study participants, trauma-related admissions primarily involved musculoskeletal injuries and these patients were more often discharged to extended care facilities, suggesting greater functional limitations at discharge3. Prior research suggests that prolonged immobility during or after critical illness may contribute to PICS and negatively affect long-term cognitive recovery28. Other studies have shown that early mobilization is associated with better cognitive outcomes, indicating that restricted movement may increase vulnerability to later cognitive decline29. This may help contextualize the delayed onset of cognitive impairment observed in the trauma group in our study. However, because our dataset did not include detailed information on injury sites or post-discharge mobility, we were unable to directly examine these relationships. Future research should include more detailed assessments of trauma-related factors and post-discharge functional status to clarify how these elements affect cognitive trajectories and contribute to delayed cognitive decline in trauma survivors.

We observed that lower levels of education and discharge to places other than back to one’s home may increase the risk of belonging to the Persistent MCI Group. Education is recognized as a major risk factor for cognitive impairment, along with age and comorbidities30. Although education is an unmodifiable factor, discharge place represents a setting where clinical intervention may be feasible. Discharge to an extended care facility often indicates that patients have ongoing functional dependency or additional needs3 and may also reflect socioeconomic vulnerability or limited access to rehabilitation resources compared with those discharged home. However, despite these significant needs, PICS-related interventions remain concentrated in acute care settings or post-ICU clinics, with little provision within long-term care facilities31. Given that extended care facilities play an increasingly important role in the recovery pathway of ICU survivors and were a major determinant of the Persistent MCI group in our study, assessing cognitive function and implementing appropriate interventions during the early post-discharge recovery phase in these settings is essential. Enhancing staff education and awareness regarding cognitive assessment and rehabilitation within extended care facilities may contribute to improving long-term cognitive outcomes among ICU survivors.

This study is significant in that it identified four cognitive trajectories and their associated factors among ICU survivors after discharge. Based on these trajectories, we propose several hypotheses regarding each MCI group. The presence of a Recovered MCI group suggests that MCI may be reversible in certain clinical contexts, particularly among patients admitted for postoperative care or neurological conditions, indicating that cognitive improvement may occur as the underlying condition resolves. In the Delayed MCI group, a higher proportion of trauma-related admissions was observed, raising the hypothesis that trauma-specific factors may contribute to delayed cognitive impairment and underscoring the need for longitudinal studies focused on this population. Lastly, Persistent MCI was more common among patients discharged to extended care facilities, a pattern that may reflect broader clinical or social vulnerabilities and highlights the importance of monitoring and supporting cognitive health after ICU discharge.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a secondary analysis using existing cohort data, our ability to fully examine all factors influencing changes in cognitive function was limited. Although individuals with clinically diagnosed cognitive impairment prior to ICU admission were excluded, routine screening for pre-existing mild cognitive impairment or IQ estimation was not performed, leaving open the possibility of unmeasured baseline deficits. Information on new neurological events between 3 and 12 months post-discharge was also unavailable which may have affected cognitive changes. In addition, because follow-up assessments were conducted using either the face-to-face MoCA or the MoCA-Blind depending on pandemic-related constraints, mode-related differences between the two instruments may have introduced some measurement bias despite the use of recommended score conversion. Second, of the 891 patients initially enrolled in the primary cohort, only 329 were included in the final analysis due to death, withdrawal, or loss to follow-up. Participants lost to follow-up were generally older and exhibited greater illness severity, which may have led to an underestimation of the prevalence of cognitive impairment. Third, the four cognitive trajectory groups differed in sample size. Although appropriate statistical approaches were applied to address variance heterogeneity, these imbalances may still have influenced statistical power. Fourth, patients with acute neurological conditions or trauma were included because the aim of this study was to characterize diverse cognitive trajectories among ICU survivors. However, their inclusion may have influenced the observed results. Nevertheless, trauma-related admissions were identified as an important determinant of the Delayed MCI group, underscoring the need for future studies on trauma-related mechanisms of cognitive decline.

Conclusions

We observed that approximately 40% of ICU survivors experienced MCI within 12 months after discharge, and their cognitive trajectory groups could be divided into the Normal Group, Recovered MCI Group, Delayed Onset MCI Group, and Persistent MCI Group depending on whether MCI occurred in the early and/or late post-discharge periods. These MCI groups were associated with factors such as older age, comorbidities, admission through the emergency department, trauma-related admissions, education less than a high school diploma, and discharge to an extended care facility. There is a need for proactive cognitive screening of patients with known risk factors as well as for research and intervention strategies targeting cognitive impairment in ICU patients admitted for emergency and trauma care and those discharged to extended care facilities.
