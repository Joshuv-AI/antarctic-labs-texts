Changes in spectrum of respiratory pathogens infections among hospitalized patients post COVID-19 pandemic in Jinan, Shandong Province
Authors: Lulu Li, Yunlei He, Ping Li, Xuelei Cao, Lili Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe patterns of respiratory pathogens have undergone significant changes, underscoring the critical need for accurate pathogen identification to guide targeted treatment. This study aimed to investigate the positive rates of common respiratory pathogens over the past two years using the multiplex molecular testing in clinical settings. This study evaluated respiratory pathogens in 7861 hospitalized patients at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from March 2022 to February 2024. Pathogen detection was performed using multiplex RT-PCR combined with capillary electrophoresis technology, targeting influenza A virus (Flu-A), influenza A virus H1N1 (H1N1), influenza A virus H3N2 (H3N2), influenza B virus (Flu-B), parainfluenza virus (PIV), adenovirus (ADV), human Metapneumovirus (HMPV), human Coronavirus (HCOV), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), parainfluenza virus (PIV), Boca virus (Boca), human Rhinovirus (HRV), and Chlamydia (Ch). A comparative analysis of epidemiological patterns was conducted between the period during and after implementation of COVID-19 control policies. The overall pathogen detection rate was significantly higher at 65.79% (3786/5754) post COVID-19 pandemic compared to 30.36% (640/2107) during COVID-19 pandemic. With the relaxation of COVID-19 control policies, the positive rates of viruses such as Flu-A, Flu-B, PIV, RSV, and HCOV increased significantly. In contrast, there were no significant differences in the positive rates of HRV and Boca between the two periods. Notably, the positive rate of RSV was significantly higher across all age groups in the post-pandemic COVID-19 period, expect among individuals aged 14–18 and 19–40 years. MP was identified as the predominant pathogen in patients aged 4–13 years. Seasonal variation was evident in the detection rates of respiratory pathogens, ranging from 18.27% in 2022 to 77.79% in 2023. Flu-A H1N1 subtype infections were detected throughout spring, autumn and winter of 2023, peaking in spring and declining gradually in subsequent months. Additionally, MP infections fist emerged in May 2023 and increased monthly, reaching the peak in October of the same year. The findings illustrate the progressive re-emergence and considerable epidemiological importance of multiple respiratory pathogens among hospitalized patients across different age groups and seasons during the two-year surveillance period. Utilizing a multiplex molecular approach for respiratory pathogen surveillance enables accurate assessment of prevalence trends, especially in hospitalized patients with more severe clinical presentations, which can help clinicians to take necessary measures in the management of prevention, control, and precise therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29616-9.Keywords: Respiratory pathogens, Multiplex RT-PCR, Detection, COVID-19Subject terms: Infectious diseases, Virology

Introduction

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) impose a significant burden on global health, significantly affecting mortality and morbidity worldwide1. The majority of RTIs are caused by viruses and certain atypical respiratory pathogens, which are susceptible to seasonal and circulate with highly transmission2,3. Despite a decline in lifestyle and awareness of prediction in relation to respiratory pathogens since the advent of COVID-19, the spectrum of respiratory pathogens infections among hospitalized patients post the COVID-19 pandemic has been changed dramatically4–6. To recognize the prevalence of respiratory pathogens variation is important for the prediction and clinical management of RTIs.

Although there are numerous types of pathogens causing respiratory infections, epidemiological investigations have shown that the most common pathogens of respiratory infections include viruses, such as influenza viruses, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), adenovirus (ADV), human coronavirus (HCOV), mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), and so on7,8. The similar symptoms such as common cold can be caused by various pathogens with differing levels of severity. Some cases are mild discomfort and self-limiting, while others involving severe or recurrent respiratory infections may lead to long-term complications or death1,9. Several studies have shown that the spectrum of RTIs pathogens has been shifted before and after COVID-19 pandemic. Variation in the prevalence of respiratory pathogens among countries and regions has been demonstrated in epidemiological studies, attributed to disparities in geographical location, humidity levels, and other influencing factors10–12. Indeed, the prevalence of RTIs pathogens showed a relatively-stable trend before COVID-19. For instance, the prevalence of influenza A virus (Flu-A) and influenza B virus (Flu-B) may exhibited with seasonal patterns each year13,14. Usually, after the prevalence of Flu-A in the first influenza season, Flu-B will be the dominant strain in the next year. During the global epidemic of COVID-19, the incidence of respiratory infections decreased significantly owing to the implementation of prevention and control measures. A range of non-pharmaceutical interventions were implemented to curb the spread of COVID−19, including mask usage, proper hand hygiene, isolation of infected individuals, and vaccination. The successful implementation of these stringent interventions effectively reduced the transmission of COVID−19 and concurrently impacted the prevalence of other respiratory pathogens15. The decreased exposure to common pathogens may result in the development of an immunity gap or immunity debt, making individuals more vulnerable to future infections by respiratory pathogens16,17. After the relaxation of interventions in several countries, there have been documented instances of resurgence of respiratory pathogens in different geographical areas18,19. In China, since the adjustment of COVID-19 control policies in December 2022, the epidemiological characteristics of respiratory pathogens have undergone alterations, marked by the sudden resurgence of influenza viruses and other pathogens. This situation has presented complex challenges to clinical management. Timely identification of pathogens is of crucial significance in the early clinical diagnosis and treatment.

With the rapid development of clinical laboratory technology, the molecular detection with high sensitivity and specificity for rapid detection of respiratory viruses performed significant advantages during the epidemic season of RTIs. This study investigated respiratory pathogens among 7861 hospitalized patients at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from March 2022 to February 2024 using a multiplex reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) combined with capillary electrophoresis technology. A comparative analysis was conducted to examine changes in pathogen epidemiology during and after the implementation of COVID-19 control policies. The study aims to elucidate shifts in the respiratory tract infection (RTI) pathogen spectrum, which may provide valuable insights for guiding rational treatment strategies and informing future prevention efforts against RTIs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted at Qilu hospital of Shandong University, a comprehensive medical center in Jinan, Shandong Province, from March 2022 to February 2024, with approval from the hospital’s ethics committee. A total of 7861 inpatients were enrolled to comprehensively evaluate the positive rate of common respiratory pathogens during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on the local epidemiological timeline of COVID-19 in Jinan, the study period was divided into two cohorts: the during-COVID cohort (March 2022 to February 2023) and the post-COVID cohort (March 2023 to February 2024). The age range of the patients were classified as < 1 year old, 1–3 years old, 4–13 years old, 14–18 years old, 19–40 years old, 41–59 years old, and ≥ 60 years groups. The throat swabs or nasopharyngeal swabs were collected for further detection of the common RTIs pathogens. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Respiratory tract pathogens detection

All specimens were immediately processed or stored at −20 °C freezers for less than 24 h. The extraction was performed using the Nucleic Acid Extraction or Purification Kit (HEALTH GENETECH, Ningbo, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The 13 respiratory pathogen multiplex kit (HEALTH GENETECH, Ningbo, China) was used to detect the common pathogens including Flu-A virus with H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes, Flu-B, PIV, ADV, HMPV, HCOV, MP, RSV, parainfluenza virus (PIV), Boca virus (Boca), human Rhinovirus (HRV) and Chlamydia (Ch) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The different pathogens were first amplified for single-step RT-PCR amplification in a single amplification tube (LongGene, Hangzhou, China). The Amplification procedure is as following: (1) 25℃, 5 min, 1 cycle; (2) 50℃, 15 min, 1 cycle; (3) 95℃, 2 min, 1 cycle; (4) 94℃, 30 s; (5) 65℃→60℃, 30 s;(6) 72℃, 60 s(4–6: 6 cycles); (7) 94℃, 30 s; (8) 60℃, 30 s; (9) 72℃, 60 s(7–9: 29 cycles); (10) 72℃10 min, 1 cycle; (11) 4℃,1 cycle. Then the amplified products of different lengths were further separated and analyzed by capillary electrophoresis in the Applied Biosystems 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, USA).

Statistical analysis

The categorical variables were represented as n (%). Fisher’s exact test or χ2 were used to calculate the significance between groups when appropriate. All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism v.8.0.1 software, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Asterisks denote statistically significant differences, with *** P < 0.001, ** P < 0.01, and * P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic data and patient characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the during-COVID cohort consisted of 2107 patients, including 1294 male. Among these, 640 respiratory pathogen strains were detected, yielding a total positive rate of 30.36%. In comparison, the post-COVID cohort (March 2023 to February 2024) included 5754 patients, with 3786 pathogen strains identified, corresponding to a significantly higher positive detection rate of 65.79% (P < 0.0001). All the patients were grouped by age. In the during-COVID cohort, the proportion of people aged < 1 year, and 1–3 years was 13.76% and 19.84%, respectively, which was significantly higher than the corresponding 9.49%, and 16.02% in the post-COVID cohort. It is worth noting that in the during-COVID cohort, there were fewer people in the age groups of 19–40 years old (2.14% vs. 5.06%, p < 0.0001), 41–59 years old (5.36% vs. 8.46%, p < 0.0001) and ≥ 60 years old (13.15% vs. 17.07%, p < 0.0001) than in the post-COVID cohort. Additionally, when age was analyzed as a continuous variable (Table S1), no significant differences were observed between the two cohorts.

Differences in the pathogen spectrum among patients affected by COVIVD-19 control measures

During the period of stricter COVID-19 control measures, the overall positive rate was significantly lower, and the top three pathogens identified were HRV (8.78%, 185/2107), Flu-A (3.32%, 70/2107), and PIV (3.04%, 64/2107) in the during-COVID cohort. Nevertheless, with the gradually ease of COVID-19 control policies, the predominant pathogen spectrum has been changed and comprised influenza virus (10.22%, 588/5754), followed by RSV (9.51, 547/5754), HRV (9.35, 538/5754) and MP (6.50, 374/5754). The positive rates of PIV (4.57% vs. 3.04%), RSV (9.51% vs. 2.04%), Flu-A (10.22% vs. 3.32%), Flu-B (2.05% vs. 0.38%), ADV (5.47% vs. 2.28%), HCOV (3.89% vs. 1.61%) in post-COVID cohort were significantly higher than those in the during-COVID cohort (all P < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences in the positive rates of HRV (9.35% vs. 8.78%), HMPV (3.35% vs. 2.75%) and Boca (1.49% vs. 1.04%) between the two cohorts (all P > 0.05), as depicted in Table 1. In addition, there were significant differences in the positive rates of mixed infection of two of viruses (2.37%vs6.67%, P < 0.0001), mixed infection of three of viruses (0.14%vs0.78%, P = 0.002). However, there was no significant difference in the positive rates of mixed infection of four of viruses (0%vs0.05%, P > 0.05).

The different pathogen spectrum of mixed infection

Among all cases during COVID-19 pandemic, 53 cases of mixed infection were identified with at least two pathogens, including 50 patients with two pathogens, 3 patients with three pathogens. The top ten combinations were PIV and HRV (13.21%, 7/53), followed by MP + HRV (11.32%, 6/53), ADV + HRV (11.32%, 6/53), Boca + HRV (5.66%, 3/53), H3N2 FluA + HCOV (5.66%, 3/53), HMPV + HRV(3.77%, 2/53), HMPV + RSV(3.77%, 2/53), HMPV + HCOV(3.77%, 2/53), PIV + HCOV(3.77%, 2/53), and H3N2 FluA + ADV(1.89%,1/53) (Fig. 1A). After COVID-19 pandemic, the mixed infections were observed in 432 cases, including 384 patients with two pathogens, 45 patients with three pathogens, and 3 patients with four pathogens. Of the top ten mixed infection patterns in the post-COVID cohort, HRV infection was the most common pathogen in mixed infections, and the higher mixed infections were MP + HRV (10.19%, 44/432), PIV + HRV (6.48%, 28/432), RSV + HRV (5.79%, 25/432), ADV + HRV (5.32%, 23/432), MP + ADV (4.63%, 20/432), HRV + HCOV (3.47%, 15/432), MP + RSV (3.24%, 14/432), RSV + HCOV(3.01%, 13/432), H3N2 FluA + ADV(2.78%, 12/432), and HMPV + HRV(2.78%, 12/432) (Fig. 1B).

Detection of respiratory pathogens in patients of different genders

As showed in Fig. 2, the positive rate of pathogen infection was 23.37% (190/813) in female and 24.96% (323/1294) in male during March 2022 and Feb 2023. The most common respiratory pathogen in the patients of different genders was HRV. The results showed that the positive rates of HRV and HCOV in male were significantly higher than those in female (9.81% vs. 7.13%, p = 0.034, 2.16% vs. 0.74%, p = 0.012). However, during the period of March 2023 and Feb 2024, the positive rate of pathogen infection was 47.31% (1194/2524) in female and 48.76% (1575/3230) in male, and a significantly higher detection was only observed in Boca (p = 0.019), while there were no significant differences in positive rates for other pathogens. Noticeably, Flu-A was the predominantly pathogen in both male and female (10.46%, 338/3230; 9.90%, 250/2524) after COVID-19 pandemic.

Differences in respiratory pathogen spectrum among patients of different age groups

In this study, patients were categorized by age. We found that the respiratory pathogen spectrums in different age groups have also been changed significantly after COVID-19 pandemic in 2023 (Fig. 3). The positive detection rates of RSV reached as high as 29.12% (159/546) and 17.57% (162/922), respectively, in the group of < 1 year old and 1–3 years old in 2023, which were significantly surpassing those observed (5.52%, 16/290; 5.26%, 22/418) in 2022. No positive detections of RSV were found in the adult and elder patients with the age > 40 years old in 2022. Nevertheless, it reached with the positive rate of 7.39% (36/487) and 5.8% (57/982) in the groups of 41–59 years old and > 60 years old in 2023, compared with zero infection of RSV in the two groups in 2022. HRV was found with the highest incidence in most groups except for the group of 19–40 years old during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2022. However, among all the different age groups, HRV was not the predominant pathogen any more in 2023, expect in the group of 14–18 years old. Moreover, among the patients with 4–13 years old, MP (15.00%, 322/2146) was found to be the predominant pathogen, followed by HRV (12.30%, 264/2146), Flu (9.55%, 205/2146) and ADV (9.23%,198/2146) in 2023. In the group of 14–18 years old, the positive rate of Flu-A (7.11%, 27/380) was higher than that in 2022 (1.48%, 2/135). To be noted, the positive detection rates of influenza virus increased significantly, and emerged as the predominant pathogen in the patients of adult and elder. The positive rate of Flu was 12.08, 13.55 and 15.38, respectively, in the group of 19–40 years old, 41–59 years old and ≥ 60 years old. Besides, RSV and HCOV were also the major pathogens in patients of the three groups.

Respiratory pathogen spectrum in different seasons

The positive rates of respiratory pathogens varied over the different seasons from 18.27% (2022) to 77.79% (2023). In detail, the positive rates of respiratory pathogens were 29.38% (141/480) in spring of 2022, 29.19% (141/483) in summer of 2022, 38.74% (282/728) in autumn of 2022 and 18.27% (76/416) in winter of 2022, respectively. However, after the COVID-19 pandemic, the total positive rate of respiratory pathogens was significantly higher. The positive rates of respiratory pathogens were 52.34% (671/1282) in spring of 2023, 51.02% (499/978) in summer of 2023, 77.79% (963/1238) in autumn of 2023 and 73.27% (1653/2256) in winter of 2023, respectively (Fig. 4).

Seasonal distribution of positive detected pathogens in spring (A), summer (B), autumn (C) and winter (D) during 2022 and 2023.

In spring, the positive rates of Flu-B, MP, PIV, HMPV, and HRV were higher in 2022 than those in 2023. However, Flu-A including H3N2 and H1N1 subtypes exhibited prevalence in 2023 compared to the zero infection in 2022. In summer, the positive rates of MP, PIV, and RSV were much higher, with notably no detection rate of Flu-A in 2023. In autumn, the positive rates of H3N2 + Flu-A, MP, ADV, PIV, HRV and HCOV in 2023 were higher than those in 2022. In winter, the positive rates of Flu-B, H3N2, MP, ADV, HMPV, HRV, RSV, and HCOV were all much higher in 2023 compared with those in 2022. In addition, the positive rate of HRV was the highest in spring (9.79%, 47/480), summer (11.18%, 54/483) and autumn (10.03%, 73/728) of 2022, which changed with Flu-A (16.85%, 216/1282), PIV (13.29%, 130/978) and MP (14.46%, 179/1238) as the predominant prevalence in spring, summer, and autumn of 2023, respectively. Among all the positive detected pathogens, the positive rate of Ch showed no significant differences throughout all seasons in this study.

Monthly distribution of respiratory pathogens

To further explore the epidemic features of most common viruses, we studied the temporal peaks of virus detection and found them varied among different months (Fig. 5). For example, Flu-A demonstrated predominant prevalence firstly in June 2022, with the highest positive rate of H3N2 subtype from August to October in 2022, and then reached peak with H1N1 subtype in March 2023, following with the highest positive rate of H3N2 subtype in December 2023. It is worth noting that as the number of Flu-A cases decreases, there was an increase trend in Flu-B. On the other hand, FluB exhibited significant higher positive rate in March and April in 2022 compared with that the same month in 2023. However, it was particularly notable with the high detection rate of Flu-B from December 2023 to February 2024. Conversely, the prevalence trend appeared relatively sporadic in other months. The positive rate of MP showed no significant changes throughout all months in 2022, while it increased significantly in June 2023 and reached the temporal peak in October 2023. ADV was not detected in September 2022, and the peak was in January 2023. It also showed the similar changes in the epidemic trend of PIV, which both peaked in July and August each year. Compared with the monthly positive rates of HMPV in 2022, we found the peak was in July 2023 and exhibited lower detection in other months. The detection rate of Boca increased rapidly from November, and reached the highest in December 2022 (Fig. S1). However, the prevalent trend of RSV was reversed after the relaxation of the epidemic control policies. The highest positive detection of RSV occurred in May 2023 and was significantly higher than that in the same period in 2022. The similar trend was found in the positive rate of HCOV, with the peak in August 2022 and June 2023, respectively. In addition, there were no significant differences in the overall prevalence trend of HRV and Ch.

Discussion

RTIs impose a significant burden for both morbidity and mortality to the global healthcare systems. With the sweeping of COVID-19 in recent years, the transmission of respiratory pathogens has been effectively limited. However, more attention began to be paid to the etiological patterns of respiratory pathogens especially since the relaxation of COVID-19 strict control measures20,21. The current study described the dynamic epidemiological profiles of multiple respiratory pathogens using multiple PCR combined with capillary electrophoresis during and after the COVI-19 pandemic in Jinan. We also carried out the comprehensive analysis to assess the epidemic trend of different pathogens among 7861 samples in the groups of COVID pandemic period (March 2022 - February 2023) and after COVID pandemic period (March 2023 - February 2024).

In the current study, a total of 7861 patients were included between March 2022 and February 2024. During the period of March 2022 to February 2023, the prevention and control measures were first strictly with the use of medical masks, and negative nucleic acid testing of COVID-19. Although the increasing number of people were infected with COVID-19 with the reopening of COVID-19 control measure at the end of 2022, most of them were still restricted with the outdoor movements, and stayed at home until recovering. We found that the total positive rate of respiratory pathogens in the post-COVID cohort was significantly higher than that in the during-COVID cohort. To be noted, there were no significant differences in the positive rates of HRV and Boca between the two groups, while the positive rates of viruses including Flu-A, Flu-B, PIV, RSV, and HCOV were significantly higher after COVID-19 pandemic. The current study was similar with the previous study, and compared with HRV and Boca, the other viruses such as RSV might be more susceptible to the impact of control measures such as hand washing22,23. Moreover, with the gradually relaxation of COVID-19 control policies, the predominant pathogen spectrum has also been changed greatly. In the previous studies, the predominant pathogen identified was FluA in 2023, compared to 2021 and 202221. As shown in our results, the predominant pathogen was Flu instead of HRV, and more cases were found to be infected with mixed respiratory pathogens. Actually, the activities of masks wearing, hand washing, less gathering helped to reduce the transmission of respiratory pathogens in 2022. Cases of the respiratory virus such as RSV and influenza A have increased after COVID-19 pandemic. The rise of these viral infectious diseases may be associated or even inter-dependent with acute, latent or recurrent infection with COVID-19 infection24. The previous study has shown that non-pharmacological interventions could reduce the spread of COVID-19 and other respiratory pathogens17. After the policy of COVID-19 relaxation, the increased social interactions and poorer prevention awareness might result in the resurgence of respiratory pathogen infections. For example, RSV hospitalizations practically disappeared in 2020. In Italy, there has been a decrease in RSV infections, which was linked to the implementation of rigorous non-pharmacological public health interventions in 2020, aimed at combating COVID-19 infection25. Another study also indicated that there were no cases of RSV respiratory infections during November 2020 to February 2021. Nevertheless, there was a resumption of cases of RSV infections in conjunction with an increase in the number affected by COVID-19 since September 2021. Zhao et al. also showed that the infection rates of RSV returned in 2023 either exceeded or were comparable to those in 2021 and 202221,26. The significant rise in cases of RSV also occurred among young children with the reopening of schools. Leung et al.27 found an immunity debt of influenza in the USA and England postpandemic COVID-19 because of the removal of non-pharmaceutical interventions such as lockdowns and face mask mandates, which might inadvertently increase people’s risk of future infections. The absence of significant difference in the positive detection rates of respiratory pathogens between genders was consistent with the previous study28.

Mixed infections were commonly found in RTIs and the patterns of the predominant mixed infections differed. We found that the top three combinations were PIV and HRV, followed by MP with HRV, and ADV with HRV during-COVID cohort, while the higher mixed infections in post-COVID cohort were MP with HRV, PIV with HRV, and RSV with HRV. HRV has the highest prevalence and is the main pathogen of mixed infection29. And the detection rates of MP and HRV were higher in coinfections than in monoinfection30. In our previous study, we found that HRV was frequently detected in single infections and also in coinfections with other pathogens during the years 2020 to 2021 under the strict policies of COVID-19 prevention31. In the current study, we also found that HRV infection was the most common pathogen in mixed infections in both during- and post-COVID cohorts, with different patterns of combination. As a prevalent pathogen in mixed respiratory infections, HRV was characterized by its ability to co-infection with diverse pathogens, affecting individuals across age groups and posing significant health risks. It is also possible that the process of interference of one virus in a co-infection paradigm may counteract, or perhaps even aid viral interference of the other co-infecting virus. Alternatively, viral camouflage may result an escape from immune surveillance32.It is also not common that one viral infection may protect the organism against another with a viral interference process mediated by γ-interferon-modulated immune surveillance33. It is likely that in situations of co-infection by two or more viruses, one viral particle may aid, or favor the escape of another. The in-depth research into the mechanisms, epidemiological patterns, and prevention strategies for virus infections may be crucial for reducing the impact of respiratory diseases.

We further explored the age- and season-specific epidemiological characteristic of respiratory pathogens. We found the positive rates of RSV were significantly higher post-pandemic COVID-19 in all age groups expect for the groups of 14–18 and 19–40 years old. In children under 1 year, there were no differences in positive rates of the other pathogens in different groups, but we found a significantly higher risk of RSV infection. In addition, there was also a sudden increase of RSV infection in the groups of 41–59 years old and > 60 years old. The possible reason might be the predominant infection of RSV occurred in children and the elder34. Moreover, among the patients with 4–13 years old, MP was found to be the predominant pathogen. Similar with the previous studies, the positive rate of MP infection was increased in school-age children after COVID-19 pandemic35–37. As the epidemic prevention measures was relaxed, the positive rates of respiratory pathogens varied over the different seasons, which not only was increased in numbers, but also exhibited unusual seasonal patterns. RSV usually appeared with obvious seasonality in October and lasted until May of the next year. During the winter of 2022, the RSV detection in during-COVID cohort followed a conventional seasonal pattern. However, it showed widespread off-season peaks of RSV infection in summer and autumn, and a small peak in winter in the observation group. Manca Luštrek et al.38 found that influenza and RSV infections were significantly higher with the gradual lifting of COVID-19 prevention and control measures. Foley DA et al.39 found that after Australia opened its interstate borders, the number of children infected with RSV was gradually increased, exceeding the previous seasonal peak media. Zhu et al.40 found that local Flu-A infections peaked in the 48th week of 2023, while Flu-B infections peaked in the 1 st week of 2024. In our study, we found that Flu-A H1N1 subtype infection occurred in all three seasons of spring, autumn and winter around the year of 2023, with the peak in spring. And it showed the infection trend of Flu was H1N1 subtype in spring, H3N2 in autumn and winter accompanied by the appearance of Flu-B in winter in 2023. Specifically, the infection of Flu-A reached its peak in March 2023 and then decreased month by month. Besides, we also found the positive rate of MP appeared in May 2023 and increased monthly, which reached the peak in October 2023, which was similar with the changes of respiratory infection patterns in previous studies41,42.

There were still some limitations in the current study. Firstly, the epidemic patterns of respiratory pathogens may be affected by climate, population flow and distribution of basic diseases in different regions. The present single-center investigation was restricted to hospitalized patients with relatively severe conditions. The limitation in generalizability was inevitable; the findings of this study might over-represent severe infections when compared with community-level epidemiology and are unable to precisely mirror the actual prevalence, pathogen composition, or clinical features of mild respiratory infections in communities. Secondly, the results might be affected by the set of pathogen category, assay methods, and study population. For example, the multiple comparisons across different pathogens, age groups and time points may increase the risk of statistical error. In future study, the multiple-center study with community populations on the epidemiology of respiratory pathogens needed to be performed for a better understand of the epidemiological patterns and characteristics of respiratory pathogens. In addition, the reasonable multiple test correction methods, or expanding sample size in different groups can be performed to reduce the statistical error risk in future, so as to enhance the reliability and application value of the conclusions.

Conclusions

The current study provided a comprehensive perspective on respiratory infections over a two-year period at a large comprehensive medical center in Jinan, Shandong Province. We compared the spectrum changes of respiratory pathogens and elucidated the gradual resurgence and notable epidemiological significance of various respiratory pathogens in different age groups and seasons among the hospitalized patients.The continuous surveillance of respiratory pathogens and epidemiological characteristics of patients with RTIs may offer a precise evaluation of pathogen prevalence, especially for the hospitalized patients with relatively severe conditions, which can help clinicians to take necessary measures to improve the clinical management of prevention, control, and precise therapy. In addition, the in-depth research into a superinfection theory or the viral interferences, epidemiological patterns, and prevention strategies for virus infections may be crucial for reducing the impact of respiratory diseases.
