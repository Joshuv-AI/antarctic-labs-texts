Celastrol induces apoptosis of myelodysplastic syndrome cells through a ROS-dependent pro-apoptotic endoplasmic-reticulum stress pathway
Authors: Fuxiang Zheng, Wenjun Shao, Li Liu, Chaoying Yang, Dingyu Hu, Xiaoling Su, Xuan Li, Pengfei Cao, Jing Liu, Yunlong Xu, Long Liang, Jun He, Ji Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) represent clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorders with inherent propensity for leukemic transformation. Emerging evidence implicates dysregulated Endoplasmic-Reticulum stress (ER-stress) in sustaining malignant clones through adaptive suppression of pro-apoptotic PERK/CHOP signaling. Celastrol serves as a natural compound with documented ER-stress modulating activity in solid tumors. However, the beneficial effect and molecular mechanisms of celastrol on MDS are unknown.ObjectiveThis investigation aims to determine whether celastrol inhibits MDS tumor cell proliferation, and molecular mechanisms of celastrol on MDS involved in the terminal pro-apoptotic ER-stress branches PERK/CHOP axis.ResultsCelastrol demonstrated cytotoxic effects against MDS cell lines (IC50 1.09 µM-2.02 µM) and primary patient-derived bone marrow mononuclear cells (n = 3, mean apoptosis rate: 42.6%). Flow cytometry results showed that celastrol can induced MDS-L and SKM-1 cell apoptosis. And celastrol treatment can elevate apoptotic protein expression, including cleaved PAPR1, rH2AX and cleaved caspase 3. Celastrol can induce ROS production, and the NAC pretreatment can eliminate the apoptosis-induced effect of celastrol. Our Bulk RNA-seq result of SKM-1 cell and the bone marrow sequencing results of patients with high-risk MDS (GSE19429) suggested that celastrol can activate the terminal pro-apoptotic ER-stress branches PERK/CHOP axis. In consistent, electron microscope screen found celastrol can induce ER Swelling and thickening in a ROS-dependent manner. In vitro experiments, celastrol can increase the eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP protein expression in a ROS-dependent manner. Molecular docking (ΔG -10.9 kcal/mol) and CETSA suggested celastrol might bind to BIP protein. In vivo, celastrol monotherapy can reduce tumor burden in xenograft models. Moreover, in MDS-L cell, celastrol can synergistically enhance the effect of venetoclax, as a BCL2 inhibitor inducing tumor cell apoptosis.ConclusionThese results indicate that celastrol induces apoptosis in MDS cells and can activate the pro-apoptotic terminal ER-stress response in a ROS-dependent manner. Together, our findings suggest that ER stress activation plays a role in celastrol‑induced apoptosis in MDS cells, supporting further investigation of celastrol as a candidate for MDS therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04344-w.Keywords: Natural product, Celastrol, Myelodysplastic syndromes, Reactive oxygen species (ROS), Apoptosis

Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are a group of heterogeneous diseases primarily affecting the elderly population and characterized by complex pathogenesis. Patients with higher-risk MDS have a median survival of less than three years [1, 2]. For those with higher-risk MDS, hypomethylating agents such as azacitidine, decitabine, or the combination of decitabine and azacitidine are considered first-line therapy. However, less than 50% of patients respond to these treatments [1, 3, 4]. Increased genetic instability and the accumulation of additional mutations are now recognized as mechanisms explaining the elevated risk of leukemic transformation in MDS patients [2]. The MDS cell of origin is a hematopoietic stem cell that proliferates and escapes apoptosis [1, 5–8]. Clonal marrow cells taken from individuals with early-stage MDS tend to apoptosis, while those from individuals with advanced-stage MDS are capable of avoiding pro-apoptotic cues.

The ER-stress is triggered by misfolded protein accumulation, activating both adaptive survival signaling and pro-apoptotic pathways [9]. The ER-stress plays a key role in hematopoietic homeostasis and hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) self-renewal [10, 11]. Persistent ER-stress activation in leukemic cells drives pro-survival signaling and chemotherapy resistance, [12, 13]. High-risk MDS have less apoptosis [14], which is also consistent with our recently published research [15]. These observations suggest that targeting ER-stress homeostasis, particularly through pro-apoptotic pathway activation, may provide novel therapeutic strategies for MDS. However, the mode of action of ER-stress homeostasis in MDS is unknown.

Celastrol is the main bioactive molecular isolated from the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Tripterygium wilfordii [16], has been used in Chinese medicine for centuries. Studies have demonstrated that celastrol possesses potential cytotoxicity against various solid tumor [16–23]. However, the antitumor effect and mechanism of celastrol in MDS cells is not understood. In this study, we observed that celastrol inhibited the proliferation and induced apoptosis of MDS cells both in vivo and vitro. The effects of celastrol on activating pro-apoptotic ER-stress were observed. Interestingly, celastrol showed a synergistic effect with Venetoclax, an inhibitor of the apoptosis regulator B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2). Thus, this research provides a new foundation for considering celastrol as a potential drug for the treatment of MDS.

Results

Celastrol induces MDS cells apoptosis

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of celastrol in MDS cells, cell viability was assessed using the CCK8 assay. The MDS cell lines derived from MDS patients, MDS-L and SKM-1 were selected and treated with increasing concentrations and extended exposure times of celastrol. The results showed that celastrol inhibited both cancer cell lines with comparable IC50 values of 1.90µM −2.02 µM at 24 h, 1.49 µM −1.32 µM at 48 h, and 1.34 µM −1.09 µM at 72 h (Fig. 1 A). Celastrol significantly inhibited the activity of SKM-1 and MDS-L cells, with IC50 values of approximately 2 µM at 24 h. Colony formation assays showed reduced growth ability in MDS-L and SKM-1 cell lines under celastrol treatment (Fig. 1B). In summary, these results demonstrate that celastrol effectively suppresses the viability and proliferation of MDS cells.

Next, we explored the death manner of SKM-1 and MDS-L cells under celastrol treatment. We treated MDS cells with celastrol and analyzed whether the reduction in cell survival was associated with the activation of apoptosis, ferroptosis, or autophagy. As shown in Fig. 1 C, only the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK significantly reversed the cell viability reduction induced by celastrol, and Z-VAD-FMK restored cell viability of celastrol-treated cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1D). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that 24 h of exposure of cells to celastrol resulted in a dose-dependent increase of both early and late apoptotic cells, both in SKM-1 cell and MDS-L cell (Fig. 1E). Western blot results also showed that celastrol effectively induced the cleavage of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) and caspase 3, and high expression of DNA damage marker γH2AX (Fig. 1 F in SKM-1 and in MDS-L cells). These results indicate that celastrol can induce apoptosis in SKM-1 and MDS-L cell cells.

Celastrol suppresses cell viability and induce apoptosis in BMMCs derived from MDS patients

To evaluate the effect of celastrol on bone marrow-derived mononuclear cells (BMMC) from MDS patients and healthy donors, we conducted CCK8 assays to measure cell viability and flow cytometry to detect apoptosis levels. The Clinical and genetic characteristics of five MDS patients is presented at Supplemental Table 1. BMMC cells were sorted and treated with celastrol (0.5 µM-2 µM). The results showed that celastrol significantly inhibited the viability of MDS patients’ cells at concentrations of 1 µM-2 µM (Fig. 2 A, right), but had no obvious toxic effect on healthy donors’ cells as measured by the CCK8 assay (Fig. 2 A, left). Importantly, flow cytometry analysis showed that 24 h of exposure of cells to celastrol resulted in a dose-dependent increase of both early and late apoptotic cells (apoptosis rate: ˜46% in celastrol 2 µM) (Fig. 2B), but had no significant toxic effect on healthy donors’ BMMC (apoptosis rate: under 10%) (Fig. 2 C). These results suggest that celastrol inhibits the cell viability and induces apoptosis of BMMC cells derived from MDS patients.

The sequence results had been uploaded to Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (GSE276645). Heat map showed the enriched difference gene between different treatment of celastrol and DMSO (Fig. 3 A). ER unfolded protein response and apoptotic associated genes were upregulated (Fig. 3 A). Gene Ontology analysis showed differenced pathways. PERK-mediated unfolded protein response and pro-apoptotic ER-stress were activated upon celastrol treatment (Fig. 3B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis analysis (GSEA) revealed that pro-apoptosis ER stress pathway was activated under celastrol treatment (Fig. 3 C). Then, MDS cell lines (SKM-1 and MDS-L) were treated with celastrol (2 µM) for indicated time point. Western blots results showed that PERK pro-apoptosis pathway involved proteins, including Phosphorylated eIF2A (peIF2A), ATF4 and CHOP, as well as apoptotic proteins were upregulated (Fig. 3D). While PERK and BIP were not significantly changed, the individual protein level is not directly associated with pathway activation, which would be discussed following. These results suggested that celastrol treatment activated the PERK pro-apoptotic UPR pathway. Furthermore, we reanalyzed a MDS patient dataset GSE19429 download from GEO [24]. The forth-three high-risk MDS patient (MDS-IB2) and seventeen healthy controls’ CD34-positived HSC RNA transcriptome sequence results were chosen and conducted to GSEA analysis. The results showed that, compared to healthy donor, the PERK mediated apoptotic pathway and cell to unfolded protein response were inhibited in high-risk MDS patients (Fig. 3E and F). These results are consistent with previous findings showing that a smaller proportion of cells in high-risk MDS patients undergo apoptosis [14]. Together, these results suggest that celastrol can activate the pro-aoptotic ER-stress pathway in MDS cells.

Celastrol induces MDS cell apoptosis in a ROS-dependent manner

Over-production of ROS may lead to programmed apoptosis [25]. Promoting apoptosis by increasing ROS in tumor cells is mechanisms of action for many chemotherapeutic agents [16–18, 26]. To determine if celastrol-triggered MDS apoptosis is due to ROS generation, we examined the role of ROS in celastrol-induced apoptosis in MDS cells. The antioxidant N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) is a ROS scavenger and was then used to determine the key role of ROS in celastrol-induced apoptosis in MDS cells. SKM-1 cells were exposed to 2 µM celastrol, cell viability and ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA staining and fluorescence microscopy to analyze DCFH-DA positive cells. The results show that NAC could rescue the cell viability inhibition induced by celastrol treatment (Fig. 4 A) and celastrol significantly increased cellular ROS levels (Fig. 4B). Pretreatment with 5 mM NAC prevented celastrol-induced apoptosis in both MDS cell lines (Fig. 4 C in SKM-1 cells and 4D in MDS-L cells). Flow cytometry analysis results showed that NAC pretreatment significantly reduced celastrol-induced apoptosis in MDS cells (Fig. 4 C and D). Western blot results also suggested that NAC pretreatment reversed the expression of apoptosis-related proteins induced by celastrol in both two MDS cell lines (Fig. 4E). Additionally, NAC pretreatment significantly restored cell viability in celastrol-treated MDS cells, as determined by trypan blue staining in both SKM-1 and MDS-L cells (Fig. 4 F). These results suggest that ROS contributes to celastrol-induced apoptosis in MDS cells.

Celastrol activates pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathway in MDS cell in a ROS-dependent manner

ROS was an ER-stress inducer and could induced apoptosis [27]. In ER-stress associated pathways, the BIP/PERK/ATF4/CHOP branch is conducted to induce cell apoptosis [9]. Thus, we speculated that celastrol may activate pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathway. To test this hypothesis, MDS-L and SKM-1 cells were treated with celastrol. The proteins eIF2A, phosphorylated eIF2A (peIF2A), ATF4, and CHOP, which are involved in the PERK pro-apoptotic pathway, were detected using western blots (Fig. 5 A). The results showed that celastrol stimulated the production of these pro-apoptotic proteins in a dose-dependent manner in SKM-1 and MDS-L cells (Fig. 5 A). After NAC pretreatment for four hours, this induction was prevented (Fig. 5 A). After celastrol treatment, obvious dilatation of the ER lumen was observed under the Transmission Electron microscope, which can be reversed by NAC pretreatment (Fig. 5B). To investigate the involvement of the BIP/PERK pathway, MDS cells were co-treated with both celastrol and salubrinal, which prevents ER stress by inhibiting eIF2A dephosphorylation [28]. Addition of salubrinal preferentially increased MDS cell survival after celastrol treatment (Fig. 5 C). Moreover, MDS cells were treated with both celastrol and the PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 [29]. Like salubrinal, GSK2606414 recovered the MDS cell viability (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that the pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathways PERK/peIF2A/ATF4 may be involved in the inhibition of viability in celastrol-treated MDS cells.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress is activated by the dissociation of BIP from the luminal portion of the endoplasmic reticulum integral membrane protein PERK, which is the initial event necessary to induce the observed stress response [30]. Given that BIP plays a key role in the upstream regulation of the PERK/ATF4/CHOP pathway, molecular docking analysis was conducted, and the results showed that celastrol binds to BIP at arginine 97 and glutamic acid 256 through hydrogen bonds, and at arginine 289 through pi-cation interaction, with a binding energy of −10.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 5E). We then conducted a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), which quantifies changes in the thermal denaturation temperature of a target protein under various conditions, including varying temperatures [31]. Celastrol increased the resistance of BIP in SKM-1 cells to heat-induced denaturation from 54℃ to 78℃ compared to DMSO (Fig. 5 F). These results indicate that celastrol might bind to BIP protein. Although the protein expression levels of BIP and PERK were not affected by celastrol treatment (Fig. 3D, top two panels). Taken together, these findings suggest that celastrol treatment can activate the pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathway and may contribute to MDS cell apoptosis, which may involve direct binding to the BIP protein.

Celastrol exhibits anti-tumor activity in a xenograft model of MDS cells

To further evaluate the efficacy of celastrol in vivo, we established subcutaneous tumor xenograft models using SKM-1 cells following the methods previously published by our team [15, 32]. After the tumor volume increased to 50–100 mm3, the mice were divided into three groups, including celastrol (1 mg/kg)-treated group, celastrol (2 mg/kg)-treated group and the control group [18, 27]. Celastrol or vehicle was administered intraperitoneally into mice once every two days for 11 days. The model diagram of animal experiment process is shown in the Fig. 6A. The mean tumor volumes and weights in the celastrol groups (1 mg/kg or 2 mg/kg) were smaller than those in the control group (Fig. 6B, C and D). Importantly, the body weight of mice did not significantly decrease (Fig. 6B). Additionally, HE staining of vital organs (heart, liver, kidney) did not show apparent toxicities (Fig. 6 F), indicating that celastrol is relatively safe in vivo. As shown in Fig. 6E, western blot results of SKM-1 cell-bearing tumor tissues indicated that celastrol injection increased the expression of the apoptotic protein cleaved caspase 3 and the pro-apoptotic ER stress protein CHOP. Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues revealed that celastrol treatment increased the expression of apoptotic proteins cleaved caspase 3 and CHOP, and inhibited the expression of the cell proliferation protein Ki-67 (Fig. 6G). Altogether, these results suggest that celastrol can suppress MDS development in vivo.

Celastrol exhibits synergistic effects with Venetoclax

Clinically, hypomethylating agents such as decitabine have been shown to improve the survival of MDS patients. Venetoclax is an orally bioavailable small molecule BCL-2 inhibitor that shows powerful therapeutic potential in patients with relapsed and high-risk MDS [4]. However, many patients develop drug resistance [33], showing do not respond to HMAs and Venetoclax or fail after several courses of application. Combination of HMAs or venetoclax with other medicines might be an effective method [4], which also consistent with our recently research, and the results showed that a molecular drug RSL3 showed synergistic effect with Decitabine in inhibiting MDS cell viability [15]. Given the advantage of celastrol in inhibiting MDS cell proliferation, we tested whether there is a synergistic effect of celastrol with Decitabine and Venetoclax by measuring cell viability using the CCK8 assay. Through single and combination treatments of celastrol with Decitabine or Venetoclax, we found that both combinations synergistically inhibited the growth of SKM-1 cells after 48 h of treatment (Fig. 7 A and B). The experimental combination indexes (CIs) < 1 demonstrate drug synergy between celastrol and Decitabine or Venetoclax in SKM-1 cells (right panels in Fig. 7 A and B). Venetoclax showed lower CIs than Decitabin in combination therapy with celastrol. These results suggest that celastrol may have the potential to be combined with Venetoclax, warranting further investigation in the context of relapsed or high-risk MDS.

Discussion

Although hypomethylating agents, such as Decitabine, are included in the first-line treatment of MDS clinically, approximately 50% of high-risk MDS patients show resistance and have no obvious response to the therapy [1]. Venetoclax is an orally bioavailable small molecule BCL-2 inhibitor that shows powerful therapeutic potential in patients with relapsed and high-risk MDS [4]. However, many patients develop drug resistance [33]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapies, including single agents or drug combinations, to treat MDS. In this study, our findings suggest that celastrol, a small molecule natural compound, can induce MDS cell apoptosis, which may involve activation of the ER-stress PERK pathway. Mechanistically, celastrol may bind to BIP and interfere with the interaction between BIP and PERK. Celastrol exhibited synergistic effects with Decitabine and Venetoclax. Our research suggests that celastrol may represent a promising candidate drug for MDS treatment, warranting further investigation.

Broad interference with growth signals by natural compounds with pleiotropic actions may offer an opportunity to target high-risk MDS [7, 10, 19, 34, 35]. Celastrol a triterpenoid compound, is extracted from Tripterygium wilfordii, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb [19]. Recent studies have shown that celastrol exhibits growth-limiting activities in various human cancers [19, 36, 37]. Although changes in the activity of stress pathways such as JNK and nuclear factor-κB have been observed with celastrol treatment [17, 27], the effects and mechanisms of celastrol on MDS, is unknown. In this study, we found that celastrol inhibited MDS cell viability and induced apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo. Importantly, celastrol suppressed cell viability and induced apoptosis of BMMC cells from MDS patients, but not healthy donor. Our results suggested celastrol as a potential drug against MDS.

Activation of the apoptosis pathway is considered a major mechanism by which chemotherapeutic agents eliminate cancer cells [30]. Clonal marrow cells taken from individuals with early-stage MDS exhibit a tendency towards apoptosis, while those from individuals with advanced-stage MDS are capable of avoiding pro-apoptotic cues [38]. High-risk MDS have less apoptosis [14]. To determine the mechanisms by which celastrol induces cell viability inhibition, various cell death inhibitors, including autophagy inhibitior CQ, were tested, and results showed that only the pan-apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK could restore cell viability. In our study, the important effects of celastrol on MDS was to induce MDS cells to undergo apoptosis. However, combination of autophagy inhibitor CQ with celastrol lead to severer cell viability inhibition compared to celastrol treatment individually. While celastrol have been reported to activate autophagy pathways in breast cancer [39], autophagy may function to resist celastrol-induced cell death in our study (data not shown). Thus, interaction model between autophagy and apoptosis in celastrol-treatment MDS cell require further exploration.

ROS plays a pivotal role as signaling mediators, regulating cell survival and death in response to diverse stimuli [25, 40]. In clinical practice, 50–80% of MDS patients experience iron overload. This condition elevates ROS levels and triggers apoptosis in bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells, which can be partially mitigated by antioxidant and iron chelator treatments [41]. Consistent with our finding, celastrol increased ROS level and antioxidant NAC mitigated the growth inhibitory activity and apoptosis induced by celastrol. Furthermore, we found that ROS might involve in activating pro-apoptotic ER-stress branch. However, the detailed molecular mechanism of how ROS regulate ER stress in our model remains unclear and will require further investigation in future studies.

Maintaining ER-stress homeostasis during transformation of HSCs to leukemia, such as high-risk MDS progressing to AML, is important [10, 11]. While LSCs play critical roles in leukemia relapse and drug resistance, hampering complete cure of the disease, ER-stress is hijacked by LSCs to enable their survival during leukemogenesis and leukemia progression as well as to promote resistance to therapy [42, 43]. Clinical evidence and in vitro studies link adaptive ER-stress activation to drug resistance in multiple cancer types [9, 42]. While adaptive ER-stress acts as a key cytoprotective mechanism for cancer cells, supporting their rapid growth in adverse conditions, prolonged or unresolved ER stress can trigger pro-apoptotic signaling pathways, resulting in cell death. Activating pro-apoptotic branches of the ER stress response, like the PERK pathway, could boost the effectiveness of drugs that target other pathways activating the ER-stress as a protective response and overcome the drug resistance [44]. Promisingly, our research demonstrated that celastrol activated the PERK ER-stress pathway, leading to apoptosis in MDS cells. Thus, the detail of mechanisms of how celastrol activated ER-stress pathway need further investigation.

Hypomethylated drugs (HMAs), including Azacytidine and Decitabine are the most commonly used treatments for MDS patients. However, patients with high-risk MDS and AML frequently exhibit significant drug resistance and high relapse rates [2]. While Azacytidine induced ER-stress, reflecting in increasing BIP protein and PERK expression [44], ER stress activation might contribute to resistance against HMAs [45]. It has been reported that activating pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathways through inhibiting molecular chaperone DNAjC10 resulted in remarkably increased Azacytidine sensitivity in AML cells [44]. These researches suggested that activating pro-apoptotic ER-stress pathways might be a potential therapy way to overcoming drug resistance. Interestingly, we found that celastrol activates pro-apoptotic branch PERK/peIF2A/ATF4/CHOP of ER stress, which was reported involved in regulating chemotherapy resistance in multiple types of cancers [31, 46–48]. Importantly, we found that celastrol showed synergized effects with Venetoclax, which suggested that celastrol might be a potential drug to combination with venetoclax for patients with relapsed and high-risk MDS. Whether celastrol downregulates MCL1 and thereby sensitizes MDS cells to venetoclax remains to be investigated. Future studies will be required to determine the relative contribution of MCL1 downregulation to the synergistic effect observed with venetoclax.

We acknowledge that the present study did not investigate whether celastrol treatment induces or selects for specific genetic mutations in MDS cells. While celastrol is primarily known as an apoptosis‑inducing agent that acts through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and endoplasmic reticulum stress, ROS‑mediated DNA damage has been implicated in the acquisition of mutations in genes such as TP53 in various cancer models. However, to the best of our knowledge, no systematic profiling of celastrol‑induced mutations has been reported, and celastrol is generally not considered a classical mutagen. Given that the current study focused on short‑term apoptotic effects, we did not evaluate the long‑term genomic consequences of celastrol exposure. Future studies employing whole‑genome or whole‑exome sequencing of celastrol‑treated cells – especially after chronic or sub‑lethal exposure are warranted to determine whether celastrol selects for specific resistance‑conferring mutations. Such investigations would also provide insights into the long‑term safety of celastrol in potential therapeutic setting.

In conclusion, our study revealed that natural moclecular celastrol is a promising compound to inhibit MDS cell growth. Mechanically, celastrol activate pro-apoptosis ER-stress pathway in a ROS-dependent manner. Importantly, we found that celastrol showed synergized effect with Decitabine and Venetoclax in decreasing MDS cell viability. Thus, celastrol may serve as a promising chemotherapeutic strategy for MDS patients.

Limitations of work

First, the majority of mechanistic experiments were performed using the MDS‑L and SKM‑1 cell lines, which are well‑characterized MDS‑derived cell lines but have undergone progression toward acute myeloid leukemia. Although we validated key findings in a small cohort of five primary MDS patient samples (as shown in Supplemental Table 1), the limited sample size and the relatively low‑risk profile of these patients (all IPSS‑R low risk) may not fully capture the heterogeneity of the MDS population. Future studies with larger cohorts encompassing diverse risk categories are warranted to confirm the clinical relevance of our observations. Second, our study revealed a stronger synergistic effect between celastrol and the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax. Although we have established that ROS contributes to this synergy, the potential contribution of celastrol‑mediated downregulation of anti‑apoptotic proteins such as MCL1 was not addressed in the current work. Given that MCL1 is a key mediator of venetoclax resistance, future studies are planned to systematically examine whether celastrol sensitizes MDS cells to venetoclax through MCL1 downregulation.

We acknowledge that our in vivo toxicity evaluation was limited to a short‑term xenograft model using a specific dosing regimen (1‑2 mg/kg once every two days for 11 days). Under these conditions, no significant toxicity was observed in body weight or heart/liver/kidney histopathology. However, potential toxicities at higher doses, longer durations, or in other models cannot be excluded. Comprehensive toxicological studies are required prior to clinical translation.

Methods and materials

Cell lines and cultivation

Human MDS cell lines (SKM-1 and MDS-L) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, USA). SKM-1 and MDS-L cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Life Technologies Inc., Gibco BRL, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Zeta-life, USA). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell authentication was performed using STR profiling. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. Clinical samples were obtained with informed consent from the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of South China (Hengyang, China). Sample collection was approved by the Protection of Human Subjects Committee of the respective hospitals. MDS primary samples were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum and 100U/ml IL-3, SCF, and FLT3.

CCK-8 assay and colony formation assay

The cell viability was detected by CCK-8 kit (Procell, China). The MDS cell line (SKM-1 and MDS-L cell) were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/ml. after treatment of celastrol at indicated concentrations and time points, 10% of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and continued to incubation for another two hours, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm through a microplate reader (BioTek ELX800, USA).

For colony formation assays, cells (3 × 103/well) were seeded in 12-well culture plates with three wells in each group. The cells were resuspended in 0.33% agar and nutrition was performed twice a week by adding two drops of MDS cell cultures to the medium. After incubation for 14days at 37 °C, images of the 12-well plate were taken under a microscope then scanned, and counted the colony numbers with ImageJ.

Isolation of primary human bone marrow-derived mononuclear (BMMC) cells

All experiments were approved by the Human Experiment Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of South China (Hengyang, China). Mononuclear cells from 5 mL of bone marrow aspirate were separated using density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque buffer (ρ = 1.077 g/mL). The collected BMMC cells were subjected to different treatments and assessed for cell viability and apoptosis levels.

Cell apoptosis assay and detection of ROS production in cells

The ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) was utilized to measure ROS levels. Cells were treated with the ROS-sensitive dye DCFH-DA, and the percentages of ROS-positive cells were calculated using a fluorescence microscope. To assess apoptosis levels, the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit I and Propidium Iodide (PI) were purchased from Beyotime (China). Human MDS cells were plated in 6-well dishes in complete medium and then treated with indicated concentrations of celastrol and DMSO (control) for specified time points to evaluate the effect of celastrol on programmed cell death. In some assays, cells were pretreated with 5 mM NAC for 60 min. Cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Apoptosis Detection Kit. Cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI, then analyzed for fluorescence using a FACSCali-bur (USA). FlowJo software was used to analyze the data.

Tumorigenicity assays in vivo

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (Shenzhen, China) and all experiments were performed in accordance with the approved protocol and other relevant guidelines and regulations. All female NOD-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/NjuCrl (NCG) mice, aged 4–6 weeks, were purchased from Jiangsu Gem Pharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). The NCG mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the flanks with 8 × 10^6 SKM-1 cells in 100 µL of Opti-MEM medium. When the tumors became measurable, the mice were randomized into three groups (n = 5 per group) and treated with vehicle (10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 75% Tween-80, and 45% normal saline, control group), celastrol (1 mg/kg), or celastrol (2 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection every two days. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every two days. Mice were euthanized at the conclusion of the experiment. Tumors were quickly excised, weighed, and photographed, with each sample categorized by group. Tumor tissues were divided into three portions: lysed for western blotting, fixed in 10% formaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical staining were performed according to standard protocols. Images were acquired using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Japan).

Molecular docking analysis

The workflow of molecular docking analysis begins by downloading pharmacologically active compound structures of celastrol (PubChem CID: 122724) from the pubmed database and converting them to.pdbqt format using AutoDockTools. The BIP protein structure (PDB ID: 3LDB) is retrieved from the PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/), and water molecules along with the original ligand are removed with PyMOL. The BIP protein is then prepared by adding hydrogens via AutoDockTools-1.5.6. Using the same software, the active pocket parameters are determined to define the binding site for docking. Subsequently, molecular docking is performed with vina. Finally, the docking outcomes are visualized in PyMOL.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

For CETSA, experiments were performed according to the previously published protocols with minor modifications [49]. CETSA could directly detect the binding affinity between drugs and target proteins in cells or tissues. Briefly, SKM-1 cells were plated in 10-cm dishes and grown to 80%–90% confluence. The cells were treated with 2 µM celastrol or DMSO for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were collected, divided into eight equel parts, washed twice with DPBS, suspended in DPBS containing protease inhibitors, and heated at 54℃, 56℃, 59℃, 63℃, 68℃, 72℃, 75℃, and 78℃ for 3 min each using Gradient PCR. Following five cycles of freezing and thawing, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min and analyzed by western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as average ± SD of three independently performed assays. The diversity in statistics between diverse groups was analyzed via the Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism 9 (USA). p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
