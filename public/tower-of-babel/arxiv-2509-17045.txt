Boundary Feller-Dynkin processes associated with Laguerre processes and Pickrell diffusions
ALEXANDER I. BUFETOV, YOSUKE KAWAMOTO

Abstract
. In this paper, we construct a Feller-Dynkin boundary process by applying the method of intertwiners to the coherent family, introduced in our previous work, of Laguerre processes with a fixed parameter. The corresponding boundary process is computed explicitly, and it turns out to be a deterministic dynamical system. Since the Pickrell diffusions that leave the Pickrell measure invariant are coherent with respect to the same projective system, we also obtain the stochastic boundary process associated with the coherent family of Pickrell diffusions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The method of intertwiners

The aim of this paper is to give an explicit construction of boundary Feller-Dynkin processes associated with the projective system introduced in
[
12
]
, by applying the method of intertwiners.
Infinite-dimensional dynamics can be constructed in many examples
[
7
,
8
,
29
,
13
,
3
]
, and the boundary process is sometimes a stochastic infinite-dimensional diffusion
[
28
]
.
Sometimes, however, a surprising phenomenon occurs, and in transition to infinite dimension the stochasticity disappears: the limit diffusion turns out to be a deterministic dynamical system
[
3
]
.
In this paper, we construct a projective system and verify that the resulting infinite-dimensional system is indeed deterministic, see (
1.6
) below.

We begin with a brief overview of this approach.
A detailed rigorous formulation is provided in Section
2
.
A projective system
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of a sequence of spaces
E^{N}
and Markov kernels
L_{N}^{N+1}
from
E^{N+1}
to
E^{N}
.
The boundary of a projective system is again a pair consisting of a space and a Markov kernel.
The boundary
E^{\infty}
is a projective limit of the spaces
\{E^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in a suitable sense.
Let
\{T_{t}^{N}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov semigroup associated with a Markov process
\mathbf{X}^{N}
on
E^{N}
.
If the intertwining relation

\displaystyle T_{t}^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}=L_{N}^{N+1}T_{t}^{N}\text{ for any }t\geq 0

holds for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then we say that the family of Markov processes
\{\mathbf{X}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is coherent (or consistent) with respect to
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Under certain additional conditions, a coherent family
\{\mathbf{X}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
induces a corresponding Markov process
\mathbf{X}^{\infty}
on the boundary
E^{\infty}
.
Furthermore, if the kernels
L_{N}^{N+1}
are Feller and the Markov processes
\{\mathbf{X}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are Feller-Dynkin, then the boundary process
X^{\infty}
is also Feller-Dynkin.

The method of intertwiners was introduced by Borodin and Olshanski
[
7
]
.
They constructed a Feller-Dynkin process on the boundary of the Gelfand-Tsetlin graph, which describes the branching of irreducible representations of the chain of unitary groups.
This approach was further applied to branching graphs associated with other groups by Borodin and Olshanski
[
8
,
29
]
and by Cuenca
[
13
]
.
In their frameworks, the state spaces of the projective systems are discrete.
Assiotis
[
3
]
first applied the method of intertwiners to a continuous setting.
Let us explain the framework by Assiotis.

Let
\mathring{W}^{N}=\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,;\,x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}\}
be the Weyl chamber and
W^{N}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,;\,x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N}\}
be its closure.
For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N+1}\in W^{N+1}
, we introduce the set

\displaystyle W^{N,N+1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{y}\in W^{N}\,;\,x_{1}\leq y_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq y_{N}\leq x_{N+1}\}

For
\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in W^{N}
, denote

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(y_{j}-y_{i}),

the Vandermonde determinant.

In the particular case
\mathbf{x}\in\mathring{W}^{N+1}
, we define the probability measure
\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
on
W^{N}
by the formula

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}(\mathbf{x},d\mathbf{y})=N!\cdot\frac{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y})}{\Delta_{N+1}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbf{1}_{W^{N,N+1}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}.

The kernel
\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}
can be extended to a Feller kernel
W^{N+1}\dashrightarrow W^{N}

[
3
, Lemma 2.5]
.
Thus, we have a projective system
\{W^{N},\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
The Dyson Brownian motions for the inverse temperature
\beta=2
, and its Ornstein-Uhlenbeck counterparts, form consistent families with respect to this projective system
[
4
, Section 3.8]
,
[
35
, Section 3]
(see also
[
17
,
32
]
for general
\beta
).
Furthermore, the family of Markov processes leaving the Hua-Pickrell measures invariant is also coherent
[
3
]
.
Therefore, these coherent systems give rise to the associated boundary processes by a framework established in
[
3
]
.

The Laguerre processes are also intertwined by
\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}

[
1
,
4
]
, but in this case, the parameter of the process varies with
N
(shifted intertwining relation).
As a result, the Laguerre processes with fixed parameter are not coherent families with respect to
\{W^{N},\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
In
[
12
]
, we introduced a new kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
by which the Laguerre processes with fixed parameter are intertwined.
Let
W_{\geq}^{N}=W^{N}\cap[0,\infty)^{N}
denote the closure of the non-negative Weyl chamber.
Let
\alpha>-1
be a real number.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N+1}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\,;\,0<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N+1}\}
, we define
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
as a probability measure on
W_{\geq}^{N}
given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}(\mathbf{x},d\mathbf{y})=N!(\alpha+1)_{N}\frac{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y})}{\Delta_{N+1}(\mathbf{x})}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\bigg(\mathbf{1}_{[x_{k-1},x_{k+1}]}(y_{k})\int_{x_{k}\vee y_{k}}^{x_{k+1}\wedge y_{k+1}}\frac{y_{k}^{\alpha}}{z^{\alpha+1}}dz\bigg)d\mathbf{y}.

Here, we use the symbols
x_{0}=0
and
y_{N+1}=\infty
for notational convenience.
We also use the symbol
(x)_{n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)
to denote the shifted factorial.
Since the definition of (
1.2
) is valid for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N+1}
, the kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
is extended to a Feller kernel
W_{\geq}^{N+1}\dashrightarrow W_{\geq}^{N}

[
12
, Lemma 5, Proposition 8]
.

In this paper, we apply the method of intertwiners to the projective system
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.
As a result, the boundary process corresponding to the Laguerre processes are obtained.
The boundary process turns out to be deterministic, and we find it explicitly: see (
1.6
) below.
Furthermore, we introduce diffusion processes leaving the Pickrell ensemble, the radial parts distribution of the Pickrell measure, invariant.
These dynamics are also intertwined by
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
, and determines a boundary processes.

To state our main results, we give an informal description of a boundary of the projective system.
A precise definition will be given after the main results.
A boundary of the projective system
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Omega=\{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\gamma)\,;\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,\,\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{i}\leq\gamma\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbb{R}

endowed with product topology.
By the definition of boundary, there exists a kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}:\Omega\dashrightarrow W_{\geq}^{N}
such that, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N+1}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}.

1.2.
The boundary Laguerre process

We first state our result on the Laguerre processes.
For
\alpha>-1
, we consider the
N
-dimensional stochastic differential equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{N,i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2X_{t}^{N,i}}dB_{t}^{i}+\Big(-X_{t}^{N,i}+\alpha+N+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{X_{t}^{N,i}+X_{t}^{N,j}}{X_{t}^{N,i}-X_{t}^{N,j}}\Big)dt

for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
Here,
(B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
denotes the standard
N
-dimensional Brownian motion.
For
\alpha>-1
, the equation (
1.4
) has a unique strong solution for any starting point
\mathbf{x}\in W_{\geq}^{N}

[
19
, Theorem 2.2]
.
We call the unique strong solution
\mathbf{X}^{N}=(X^{N,1},\ldots,X^{N,N})
the Laguerre (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) process of parameter
\alpha
.
Let
\{T_{\alpha,t}^{N}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov semigroup associated with
\mathbf{X}^{N}
.

Note that the equation (
1.4
) is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck analogue of the non-colliding squared Bessel processes, which have been extensively studied in the context of random matrix theory (see
[
21
]
for example).
In fact, the non-colliding squared Bessel process describes the evolution of the squared singular value of a rectangular matrix whose entries are independent complex Brownian motions
[
15
,
25
]
(see also
[
9
]
for the real-valued case).

Define
C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
as the space of continuous functions on
W_{\geq}^{N}
vanishing at infinity.
We established the following intertwining relation.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
12
, Theorem 1]

Assume that
\alpha>-1
.
Then, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, and
t\geq 0
, we have the intertwining relation

(1.5)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha,t}^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}f

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}f.

Remark 1.2
.

The intertwining relation (
1.5
) corresponds to the case of inverse temperature
\beta=2
.
This relation was extended to general
\beta\geq 1

[
22
]
.

From Proposition
1.1
, the family of Laguerre processes of parameter
\alpha
is coherent with respect to
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Therefore, there exists a corresponding boundary process as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a unique (
\alpha
-independent) Feller-Dynkin semigroup
\{T_{t}^{\Omega}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\Omega
such that, for any
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, and
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}f=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}f

and the boundary process is given by the formula (
1.6
).
Let
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),\gamma(t))
denote the process associated with
\{T_{t}^{\Omega}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Then we have

(1.6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}(0)e^{-t},\quad\gamma(t)=1+(\gamma(0)-1)e^{-t}.

The boundary Laguerre process is deterministic and is given explicitly.
Our formulas can be compaed with those by Assiotis
[
3
, Section 5.2]
.
Actually, the boundary processes associated with the Dyson model and its Ornstein-Uhlenbeck counterpart are also deterministic and given explicitly.

1.3.
The boundary Pickrell process

In addition to the Laguerre process, we identify another stochastic process with a rich integrable structure, called the Pickrell diffusion.
Actually, the family of Pickrell diffusions is also coherent with respect to the projective system
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
As stated in Section
1.5
, this diffusion is closely related to the Pickrell measures.

For
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
, we consider the
N
-dimensional stochastic differential equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{N,i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2X_{t}^{N,i}(1+X_{t}^{N,i})}dB_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big((2-2N-\mathfrak{s})X_{t}^{N,i}+\alpha+1+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{2X_{t}^{N,i}(1+X_{t}^{N,i})}{X_{t}^{N,i}-X_{t}^{N,j}}\Big)dt

for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
The well-posedness of this equation will be verified in Lemma
4.2
.
Let
\mathbf{X}^{N}=(X^{N,1},\ldots,X^{N,N})
be the unique strong solution to (
1.7
), and
\{T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}\}_{t\geq 0}
be its semigroup.
We call
\mathbf{X}^{N}
the Pickrell diffusion.
The Pickrell diffusions are intertwined by
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
.
Then, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, and
t\geq 0
, we have the intertwining relation

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}f=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}f.

It is important to note that the intertwining relations of the Pickrell diffusions are closely related to the
\beta
-corner Jacobi processes introduced in
[
5
]
(see Remark
4.10
).

Theorem
1.4
shows that the family of Pickrell diffusions is coherent with respect to
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Therefore, there exists an associated boundary process.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, there exists a unique (
\alpha
-independent) Feller-Dynkin semigroup
\{T_{\mathfrak{s},t}^{\Omega}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\Omega
such that, for any
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, and
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},t}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}f=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}f.

Furthermore, if
\mathfrak{s}>-1
, the unique invariant probability measure for
\{T_{\mathfrak{s},t}^{\Omega}\}_{t\geq 0}
is
m_{\mathfrak{s}}
, which will be defined in Section
1.5
.

1.4.
Ergodic decomposition for
\mathbb{U}(\infty)\times\mathbb{U}(\infty)
-invariant probability measures

Let
M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
be the space of
m\times n
matrices with complex entries, and for brevity write
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})=M_{n,n}(\mathbb{C})
.
We introduce the following subsets
H_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{U}(n)\subset M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
:
H_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the space of Hermitian matrices of size
n
, and
\mathbb{U}(n)
is the space of unitary matrices of size
n
.
Let
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
be the projective limit
\varprojlim M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, the space of
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
matrices.
Let
M_{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
denote the inductive limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, the space of
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
matrices with finitely many non-zero entries.

Let
\mathbb{U}(\infty)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{U}(N)
denote the the inductive limit unitary group.
We say that a probability measure on
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
is
\mathbb{U}(\infty)\times\mathbb{U}(\infty)
-invariant if it is invariant under the actions of multiplication by
\mathbb{U}(\infty)
on the left and right.
We write
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{inv}}
for the set of all
\mathbb{U}(\infty)\times\mathbb{U}(\infty)
-invariant probability measures on
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
.
These invariant probability measures admit an ergodic decomposition, cf.
[
10
]
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{erg}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{inv}}
be the set of all ergodic
\mathbb{U}(\infty)\times\mathbb{U}(\infty)
-invariant probability measures.

For a probability measure
P
on
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
, we define the characteristic function of
P
as the function on
M_{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
given by the formula

\displaystyle F_{P}(A)=\int_{M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})}e^{i\mathfrak{Re}\mathrm{Tr}(AX^{*})}P(dX).

Note that, if
P\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{inv}}
, its characteristic function
F_{P}
is uniquely determined by
F_{P}(A)
for all diagonal matrices
A\in M_{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
.

The set
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{erg}}
can be characterised by
\Omega
, defined by (
1.3
).
For
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\gamma)\in\Omega
, we write
\overline{\gamma}(\omega)=\gamma-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{i}
.
We define a function
F_{\omega}
on
\mathbb{R}
by

(1.8)

\displaystyle F_{\omega}(\lambda)=e^{-4\overline{\gamma}(\omega)\lambda^{2}}\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{1+4\alpha_{i}\lambda^{2}}.

The next characterisation was first proved in
[
30
]
(see also
[
31
]
).

Lemma 1.6

(
[
30
]
)
.

There exists a bijective correspondence between
\Omega
and
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{erg}}
.
Then, we write
P_{\omega}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{erg}}
as the ergodic probability measure corresponding to
\omega\in\Omega
.
Furthermore, the characteristic function of
P_{\omega}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\text{erg}}
is given by, for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})}e^{i\mathfrak{Re}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n},0,\ldots)X^{*})}P_{\omega}(dX)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}F_{\omega}(r_{j}).

Based on Lemma
1.6
, we will prove in Proposition
3.7
that
\Omega
is a boundary of
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Therefore, a coherence family of processes with respect to
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
leads to a boundary process on
\Omega
.
We next introduce a Markov kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}:\Omega\dashrightarrow W_{\geq}^{N}
as follows.

Define the map
\mathfrak{rad}_{n}:M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})\to W_{\geq}^{n}
by the formula

\displaystyle\mathfrak{rad}_{n}(X)=(\lambda_{1}(X),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(X)),

where
(\lambda_{i}(X))_{i=1}^{n}
denotes the eigenvalues of
X^{*}X
arranged in non-decreasing order, which is referred as the radial part of
X
.
For a random matrix
X\in M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
, let a probability measure
P_{\mathfrak{rad}}^{n}[X]
on
W_{\geq}^{n}
be the distribution of the radial part of
X
.

For
m_{1}\geq m_{2},n_{1}\geq n_{2}
, let
\pi_{m_{2},n_{2}}^{m_{1},n_{1}}:M_{m_{1},n_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m_{2},n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})
be the natural projection sending an
m_{1}\times n_{1}
matrix to its upper left
m_{2}\times n_{2}
corner.
We employ the expression
\pi_{m_{2},n_{2}}^{m_{1}}
in place of
\pi_{m_{2},n_{2}}^{m_{1},n_{1}}
if
m_{1}=n_{1}
, and use a similar symbol for
m_{2}=n_{2}
.
Furthermore, let
\pi_{m,n}^{\infty}:M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
be the natural projection sending each
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
matrix to its upper left
m\times n
corner.
We also use the symbol
\pi_{n}^{\infty}:=\pi_{n,n}^{\infty}
.

For
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
, the boundary Markov kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}:\Omega\dashrightarrow W_{\geq}^{N}
is given by the formula

(1.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}(\omega,\cdot)=(\mathfrak{rad}_{N}\circ\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{\infty})_{*}P_{\omega}.

Remark 1.7
.

(1)

Regarding the projective system
\{W^{N},\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, the explicit form of the boundary kernel to
W^{N}
is given
[
3
, Equation (8)]
.
It would be of interest to derive an explicit expression of
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}
.

(2)

Because the construction of the boundary relies on the matrix structure, parameters
\alpha
in Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
are restricted to non-negative integers, although the intertwining relations in Proposition
1.1
and Theorem
1.4
are established for any
\alpha>-1
.

1.5.
The Pickrell measures

Let
dX
be the Lebesgue measure on
M_{M,N}(\mathbb{C})
.
The Pickrell measure on
M_{M,N}(\mathbb{C})
is defined by

(1.10)

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M,N}(dX)\propto\det(1+X^{*}X)^{-M-N-\mathfrak{s}}\,dX

for a real number
\mathfrak{s}
.
Remark that this measure is finite if and only if
\mathfrak{s}>-1
.
Hereafter, when
\mathfrak{s}>-1
holds, we regard
P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M,N}
as a probability measure on
M_{M,N}(\mathbb{C})
by normalising it with an appropriate constant.

For
\mathfrak{s}>-1
and
\alpha>-1
, we consider the probability measure on
W_{\geq}^{N}
given by the formula

\displaystyle m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}(d\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}}\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{\alpha}(1+x_{k})^{-2N-\alpha-\mathfrak{s}}\,d\mathbf{x},

where
Z_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
is the normalise constant.
If
\alpha
is a non-negative integer, then the probability
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
is identical to the radial part of a random matrix according to the Pickrell measure
(\mathfrak{rad}_{N})_{*}P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{N+\alpha,N}
.
Actually, the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure
dX
on
M_{N+\alpha,N}(\mathbb{C})
under the map
\mathfrak{rad}_{N}
is given by

(1.11)

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{rad}_{N})_{*}dX=c_{N,\alpha}\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{\alpha}\,d\mathbf{x}.

for a positive constant
c_{N,\alpha}
depending on
N
and
\alpha
.
Thus, we call
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
the Pickrell ensemble.
Remark that, for
\mathfrak{s}>-1
and
\alpha>-1
, one can directly check that the Pirkcell diffusion given by (
1.7
) is reversible with respect to the Pickrell ensemble
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
.
Hereafter in this subsection, we assume
\mathfrak{s}>-1
.

The Pickrell measures have a consistency in the sense that

\displaystyle(\pi_{M_{2},N_{2}}^{M_{1},N_{1}})_{*}P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M_{1},N_{1}}=P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M_{2},N_{2}}

from
[
11
, Proposition C.1]
.
Thereby, the Kolmogorov extension theorem yields a probability measure
P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}
on
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\displaystyle(\pi_{M,N}^{\infty})_{*}P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}=P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M,N}.

We also call
P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}
the Pickrell measure.

Clearly,
P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}^{M,N}
is
\mathbb{U}(M)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant under the actions by the unitary group of multiplication on the left and right.
Hence,
P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}
is also by the unitary group
\mathbb{U}(\infty)\times\mathbb{U}(\infty)
.
Therefore, there exists a unique probability measure
m_{\mathfrak{s}}
on
\Omega
such that

can be decomposed as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{Pic},\mathfrak{s}}=\int_{\Omega}P_{\omega}dm_{\mathfrak{s}}(\omega).

It is important to point out that

is identified with the modified Bessel random point field

in the following sense

We define the Bessel kernel, introduced in

]
, given by the formula

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{Be},\mathfrak{s}}(x,y)=\frac{\sqrt{x}J_{\mathfrak{s}+1}(\sqrt{x})J_{\mathfrak{s}}(\sqrt{y})-\sqrt{y}J_{\mathfrak{s}+1}(\sqrt{y})J_{\mathfrak{s}}(\sqrt{x})}{2(x-y)},

where

denotes the Bessel function with parameter

The Bessel random point field

is the determinantal random point field associated with the Bessel kernel

(more precisely, see

The modified Bessel random point field

Setting

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}=\{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\gamma)\in\Omega\,;\,\alpha_{i}>0,\,\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{i}=\gamma\},

2.
Basic concepts of Feller boundaries and boundary processes

In this section we recall the nesessary background on the method of intertwiners by Borodin and Olshanski

2.1.
Feller kernels and semigroups

We first recall concepts of kernels and semigroups on the Euclidean spaces (see
[
7
, Section 2]
for a more comprehensive discussion).
Let
E
and
E^{\prime}
be Borel subsets of Euclidean spaces.
Consider a function
L(x,A)
, where
x\in E
and
A
is a Borel subset of
E^{\prime}
.
We say that
L
is a Markov kernel from
E
to
E^{\prime}
, denoted by
L:E\dashrightarrow E^{\prime}
, if the following two conditions hold:

•

L(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure on
E^{\prime}
for any
x\in E
.

•

L(\cdot,A)
is a Borel function on
E
for any Borel subset
A\subset E^{\prime}
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(E)
denote the Banach spaces of
\mathbb{R}
-valued bounded Borel functions with the sup-norm on
E
.
Then, a Markov kernel
L:E\dashrightarrow E^{\prime}
defines a linear operator
\mathcal{B}(E^{\prime})\to\mathcal{B}(E)
by
(Lf)(x)=\int_{E^{\prime}}L(x,dy)f(y)
.
Let
C_{\infty}(E)
be the set of all continuous functions on
E
vanishing at infinity.
We say a Markov kernel
L:E\dashrightarrow E^{\prime}
is Feller if the induced map
L:\mathcal{B}(E^{\prime})\to\mathcal{B}(E)
satisfies
L(C_{\infty}(E^{\prime}))\subset C_{\infty}(E)
.

Following
[
7
]
, we use the symbol
\mathcal{M}_{p}(E)
to denote the space of all probability measures on
E
equipped with the weak topology.
For
m\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(E)
, we write
mL(\cdot):=\int_{E}L(x,\cdot)m(dx)\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{\prime})
.
Thus, a Markov kernel
L:E\dashrightarrow E^{\prime}
induces a linear map
\mathcal{M}_{p}(E)\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{\prime})
.

A Markov semigroup on
E
is, by definition, a family of Markov kernels
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
E
such that
T_{0}=1
and
T_{t}T_{s}=T_{t+s}
.
This semigroup is said to be Feller-Dynkin if the following two conditions hold:

•

The induced map
T_{t}:\mathcal{B}(E)\to\mathcal{B}(E)
satisfies
T_{t}(C_{\infty}(E))\subset C_{\infty}(E)
for any
t\geq 0
.

•

The function
t\to T_{t}
is strongly continuous in the sense that
\lim_{t\to 0}T_{t}f=f
in
C_{\infty}(E)
holds for any
f\in C_{\infty}(E)
.

Note that Feller-Dynkin semigroups are sometimes simply called Feller semigroups.
To avoid confusion, we distinguish between these two in this paper.
For us, the Feller property means just the property of mapping the space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity into itself; and we speak of the Feller-Dynkin property if, additionally, the strong continuity holds.

2.2.
Feller boundary

A projective system
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is, by definition, a sequence of Borel spaces
\{E^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and Markov kernels
L_{N}^{N+1}:E^{N+1}\dashrightarrow E^{N}
.
The family of induced maps
L_{N}^{N+1}:\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N+1})\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})
forms a chain on the space
\prod_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})
with the product topology.
Define the set of all coherent probability measures by

\displaystyle\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})=\Big\{\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})\,;\,m^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}=m^{N}\text{ for all }N\Big\}.

Definition 2.1
.

A set
E^{\infty}
is called a boundary of a projective system
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
if there exists a kernel
L_{N}^{\infty}:E^{\infty}\dashrightarrow E^{N}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following (i) and (ii) hold:

(i)

L_{N+1}^{\infty}L_{N}^{N+1}=L_{N}^{\infty}
.

(ii)

The induced map
L^{\infty}:\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{\infty})\to\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})
defined by
L^{\infty}(P)=\{PL_{N}^{\infty}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Borel isomorphism.

Additionally, if
\{L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{L_{N}^{\infty}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are Feller kernels, then
E^{\infty}
is called a Feller boundary.

A boundary exists and is unique up to a Borel isomorphism
[
36
, Theorem 4.1.3]
.
This boundary coincides with the set of extremal coherent probability measures.
Actually, from the proof of
[
36
, Theorem 4.1.3]
, we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle E^{\infty}=\mathrm{Ex}\big(\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})\big),

where
\mathrm{Ex}
denotes the set of extreme points.

2.3.
Intertwining relations and boundary processes

A coherent family of Markov processes produces a boundary process as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
7
, Proposition 2.4]

For each
N
, let
T^{N}:=\{T_{t}^{N}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov semigroup on
E_{N}
.
Assume that, the family of Markov semigroups
\{T^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is coherent with respect to the projective system
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in the following sense: for
t\geq 0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle T_{t}^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}=L_{N}^{N+1}T_{t}^{N}.

Then, there exists a unique Markov semigroup
T^{\infty}:=\{T_{t}^{\infty}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
E^{\infty}
such that, for
t\geq 0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle T_{t}^{\infty}L_{N}^{\infty}=L_{N}^{\infty}T_{t}^{N}.

Furthermore, if
E^{\infty}
is a Feller boundary and
T^{N}
is a Feller-Dynkin semigroup for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then the semigroup
T^{\infty}
is also Feller-Dynkin.

By Definition
2.1
, for any coherent family
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})
, there exists a unique element
m^{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{\infty})
such that

\displaystyle m^{\infty}L_{N}^{\infty}=m^{N}.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
7
, Section 2.8]

Suppose that the family of Markov semigroups
\{T^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is coherent with respect to
\{E^{N},L_{N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
m^{N}
be an invariant probability measure of
T^{N}
.
If
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{N})
, the corresponding measure
m^{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(E^{\infty})
is invariant for
T^{\infty}
.
Moreover, if
m^{N}
is a unique invariant probability of
T^{N}
, then so is
m^{\infty}
for
T^{\infty}
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the second statement in Lemma
2.3
, we do not have to assume that
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is coherent.
In fact, the coherence follows from the assumption.
Actually, we have
m^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}T_{t}^{N}=m^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}
from (
2.2
).
Therefore, we obtain
m^{N+1}L_{N}^{N+1}=m^{N}
from the uniqueness.

In this particular case, the kernels

We say that a random matrix

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}(\mathbf{z},\cdot)=P_{\mathfrak{rad}}^{N}[\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1}(V_{N+\alpha+1})\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{z_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{z_{N}})]

\displaystyle P_{\mathfrak{rad}}^{N}[X_{N+\alpha+1,N}]\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}=P_{\mathfrak{rad}}^{N}[\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1,N}(X_{N+\alpha+1,N})]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}(\mathbf{x},\cdot)=P_{\mathfrak{rad}}^{N}\big[\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(V_{N+\alpha+1}D_{N+\alpha+1,N+1}U_{N+1})\big]

Lemma 3.3

For any

Let

Lemma 3.4

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}f(\omega)=c_{N,\alpha}\int_{W_{\geq}^{N}}\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{\alpha}\Big)F_{\omega}(x_{1})\cdots F_{\omega}(x_{N})\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x},

Lemma 3.5

It remains to prove that

\displaystyle c_{N,\alpha}\int_{W_{\geq}^{N}(R)}\Big|\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{\alpha}\Big)F_{\omega}(x_{1})\cdots F_{\omega}(x_{N})\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x})}\Big|\,d\mathbf{x}<\varepsilon,

.
Thus, Lemma

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}f(\omega)|

\displaystyle<\varepsilon+c_{N,\alpha}\int_{W_{\geq}^{N}\cap[0,R]^{N}}\Big|\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}^{\alpha}\Big)F_{\omega}(x_{1})\cdots F_{\omega}(x_{N})\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x})}\Big|d\mathbf{x}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+c_{N,\alpha}\Big(\int_{0}^{R}|x^{\alpha}F_{\omega}(x)|dx\Big)^{N}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in W_{\geq}^{N}\cap[0,R]^{N}}|\Delta_{N}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\overline{\widehat{f}(\mathbf{x})}|.

We now suppose that
\omega\to\infty
, which is reduced to the following three cases by the definition of

Therefore, combining this with (

\displaystyle\cdots\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N+1})\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N})\to\cdots\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{2})\to\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{1}).

Define the set of all coherent probability measures with respect to this chain by

\displaystyle\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})=\big\{\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N})\,;\,m^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=m^{N}\big\}.

The projective system
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
has the boundary
W_{\geq,\alpha}^{\infty}
given by

\displaystyle W_{\geq,\alpha}^{\infty}=\mathrm{Ex}\big(\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})\big)

from (
2.1
).
The boundaries seem to depend on
\alpha
at this stage, but we shall show that these all are identical to
\Omega
and independent of
\alpha
.

For any
P\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{inv}}
, we have

\displaystyle\big((\mathfrak{rad}_{N+1}\circ\pi_{N+\alpha+1,N+1}^{\infty})_{*}P\big)\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=(\mathfrak{rad}_{N}\circ\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{\infty})_{*}P

by (
3.6
).
Hence,
\Phi_{\alpha}(P):=\{(\mathfrak{rad}_{N}\circ\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{\infty})_{*}P\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
defines a map from
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{inv}}
to
\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The map
\Phi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{inv}}\to\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
is a bijection.

Proof.

We first note that, a
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant probability measure on
M_{N+\alpha,N}(\mathbb{C})
is characterized by its radial parts distribution
m^{N}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N})
as follows.
For
m^{N}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, let
V_{N+\alpha},U_{N},D_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})
be independent random matrices such that
V_{N+\alpha}\in\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha),U_{N}\in\mathbb{U}(N)
are Haar distributed and

\displaystyle D_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{x_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{x_{N}})\\
\mathbf{0}_{\alpha\times N}\end{bmatrix}\in M_{N+\alpha,N}(\mathbb{C}),

where the distribution of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is given by
m^{N}
.
We set
X_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N}):=V_{N+\alpha}D_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})U_{N}
.
By construction,
X_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})
leads to a
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant probability measure on
M_{N+\alpha,N}(\mathbb{C})
.
Furthermore, by a similar reason as in
[
14
, Lemma 2.4]
(see also
[
12
, Equation (30)]
), a random matrix which has radial parts law
m^{N}
is identical to
V_{N+\alpha}D_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})U_{N}
in law.
Thus, a
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant probability is determined by its radial parts distribution.

For
m^{N+1}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(W_{\geq}^{N+1})
, let
X_{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(m^{N+1})
be as above.
Clearly, the random matrix

\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(X_{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(m^{N+1}))

is
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant.
Furthermore, by (
3.6
), its radial parts has law
m^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
.
Therefore, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(X_{N+\alpha+1,N+1}(m^{N+1}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{law}}}{{=}}X_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}).

For
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, let
P^{N+\alpha,N}
be the distribution of the random matrix
X_{N+\alpha,N}(m^{N})
.
Then, from (
3.12
) and the consistency
m^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=m^{N}
, the sequence
\{P^{N+\alpha,N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the consistency

\displaystyle(\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{N+\alpha+1,N+1})_{*}P^{N+\alpha+1,N+1}=P^{N+\alpha,N}.

Hence, by the Kolmogorov extension theorem, there exists a probability measure
P
on
M_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\displaystyle(\pi_{N+\alpha,N}^{\infty})_{*}P=P^{N+\alpha,N}.

Since
P^{N+\alpha,N}
is
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant, it holds that
P\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\mathrm{inv}}
.
By construction, we have
\Phi_{\alpha}(P)=\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, which implies that
\Phi_{\alpha}
is a surjection.

For the same reason as above that the
\mathbb{U}(N+\alpha)\times\mathbb{U}(N)
-invariant distribution on
M_{N+\alpha,N}(\mathbb{C})
is characterised by its radial parts law, it is easy to see that
\Phi_{\alpha}
is injective.
Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

From (
3.8
), we can define a map
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\Omega}:\Omega\to\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
by

\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\Omega}(\omega):=\{\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}(\omega,d\mathbf{x})\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Furthermore, we define an induced map
\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}:\mathcal{M}_{p}(\Omega)\to\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}(m)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\alpha}\Big(\int P_{\omega}dm(\omega)\Big).

The map
\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
is an affine bijection from Lemma
1.6
and Lemma
3.6
.
Remark that
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
gives a bijection between
\Omega
and
\mathrm{Ex}\big(\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})\big)
.

We now prove that
\Omega
is a Feller boundary, following the arguments in
[
3
, Section 2.2]
and
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Proposition 3.7
.

For any
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
, the Feller boundary of
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is given by
\Omega
.
In particular, the boundary is independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.5
, the map
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathrm{Ex}\big(\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})\big)
is continuous, which implies that
\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
is Borel.
To show that the inverse of
\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
is also Borel, we can use a result in
[
26
]
.
Actually, since
\Omega
is a standard Borel space, so does
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, from
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
, we conclude that
\tilde{\Lambda}_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
is a Borel isomorphism.
Combining this with (
3.8
), we prove that
\Omega
is a boundary.
The Feller assertion comes from Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.5
.
∎

The method of intertwiners yields boundary processes on
\Omega
as follows.

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.
Assume that a family of Markov semigroups
\{T_{\alpha}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
on
W_{\geq}^{N}
satisfies, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
,

(3.13)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha,t}^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}.

Then, there exists a unique Markov semigroup
T_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
on
\Omega
such that

(3.14)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha,t}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}.

Furthermore, if
T_{\alpha}^{N}
is Feller-Dynkin, so does
T_{\alpha}^{\Omega}
.
Additionally, if

(3.15)

\displaystyle T_{\alpha+1,t}^{N}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}

holds, then
T_{\alpha+1,t}^{\Omega}=T_{\alpha,t}^{\Omega}
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from Lemma
2.2
.
Using (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), we obtain

T_{\alpha+1,t}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha+1,N}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}=\Lambda_{\alpha+1,N}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}

, which implies
T_{\alpha+1,t}^{\Omega}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{\Omega}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}
from (
3.7
).
It follows that
T_{\alpha+1,t}^{\Omega}=T_{\alpha,t}^{\Omega}
by uniqueness.
Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

4.
Construction of the boundary Feller-Dynkin processes associated with Pickrell diffusions

4.1.
Pickrell diffusions and the Karlin-MacGregor semigroups

For
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
, we consider a diffusion on
[0,\infty)
associated with the generator

\displaystyle L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,x}^{(N)}:=L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}:=x(1+x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\{(2-2N-\mathfrak{s})x+(\alpha+1)\}\frac{d}{dx}

with the following boundary conditions (see, for example,
[
16
]
for a detailed discussion of boundary conditions): the point
\infty
is a natural boundary, and the origin is an entrance boundary for
\alpha\geq 0
and a regular boundary for
-1<\alpha<0
, in which case we impose the reflecting boundary condition.
The stochastic differential equation associated with
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
is

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\sqrt{2X_{t}(1+X_{t})}dB_{t}+\{(2-2N-\mathfrak{s})X_{t}+(\alpha+1)\}dt.

Lemma 4.1
.

The Vandermonde determinant
\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})
is an eigenfunction of the second-order operator
\sum_{i=1}^{N}L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,x_{i}}^{(N)}
with eigenvalue

\lambda_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N}=\frac{N(N-1)(-4N+2-3\mathfrak{s})}{6}.

Proof.

We can check this lemma by a direct computation
[
24
]
.
∎

Let
p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}(x,y)
be the transition density of the diffusion associated with
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
.
For
(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N}\times W_{\geq}^{N}
, consider the Karlin-McGregor transition density of
N
particle
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
-diffusions h-transformed by
\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\mathbf{p}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=e^{-\lambda_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N}t}\frac{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y})}{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})}\det_{i,j=1}^{N}[p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}(x_{i},y_{j})],

which gives the transition density of non-colliding systems of
N
particles
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
-diffusions.

By the same computation for h-transformation as in
[
25
]
, the stochastic differential equation of
N
particle
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
-diffusions h-transformed by
\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})
is given by (
1.7
).
Hence, the density
\mathbf{p}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}
corresponds with the transition density of the solution to the equation (
1.7
) under the uniqueness of solutions, which will be proved in Lemma
4.2
.
Thus, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}(\mathbf{x},d\mathbf{y})=e^{-\lambda_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N}t}\frac{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y})}{\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})}\det_{i,j=1}^{N}[p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}(x_{i},y_{j})]d\mathbf{y}.

We now establish the well-posedness of a solution to the stochastic differential equation (
1.7
).
We observe that the equation (
1.7
) becomes

(4.1)

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{N,i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2X_{t}^{N,i}(1+X_{t}^{N,i})}dB_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(-\mathfrak{s}X_{t}^{N,i}+N+\alpha+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{2X_{t}^{N,i}X_{t}^{N,j}+X_{t}^{N,i}+X_{t}^{N,j}}{X_{t}^{N,i}-X_{t}^{N,j}}\Big)dt

by using

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{2x_{i}(1+x_{i})}{x_{i}-x_{j}}=\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{2x_{i}x_{j}+x_{i}+x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}+(N-1)(2x_{i}+1).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
.
Then, for any starting point
\mathbf{x}\in W_{\geq}^{N}
, the stochastic differential equation (
1.7
) has a unique strong solution, and the solution satisfies the non-explosion and non-colliding property.

Proof.

We first introduce a sufficient condition for the strong uniqueness
[
19
, Theorem 2.2]
.
Set
I=[0,\infty)
.
For continuous functions
\sigma,b:I\to\mathbb{R}
and a continuous non-negative function
H:I^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider the following stochastic differential equation of
N
-particles on
I
: for
i=1,\ldots,N
,

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=\sigma(X_{t}^{i})dB_{t}^{i}+\Big(b(X_{t}^{i})+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{H(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})}{X_{t}^{i}-X_{t}^{j}}\Big)dt.

Then, this equation has a unique strong solution and the non-explosion and non-colliding property hold if the following conditions (C1)–(C2), (A1)–(A4) hold on
I
:

(C1)

There exists a function
\rho:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
\int_{0^{+}}\rho^{-1}(x)dx=\infty
and that
|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|^{2}\leq\rho(|x-y|)
.
Moreover,
b
is Lipschitz continuous.

(C2)

There exists a constant
c>0
such that
\sigma(x)^{2}+b(x)x\leq c(1+x^{2})
and
H(x,y)\leq c(1+xy)
.

(A1)

For
0\leq w<x<y<z
, it holds that
H(w,z)(y-x)\leq H(x,y)(z-w)
.

(A2)

There exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that

\sigma^{2}(x)+\sigma^{2}(y)\leq c(x-y)^{2}+4H(x,y).

(A3)

There exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that for any
0\leq x<y<z
,

\displaystyle H(x,y)(y-x)+H(y,z)(z-y)\leq c(z-y)(z-x)(y-x)+H(x,z)(z-x).

(A4)

The set
G:=\{x\,;\,\sigma^{2}(x)+H(x,x)=0\}
consists of isolated points.
Furthermore, for any
x\in G
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N-2}\in I
, we have

\displaystyle b(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N-2}\frac{H(x,y_{j})}{x-y_{j}}\mathbf{1}_{I\setminus\{x\}}(y_{j})\neq 0.

We specialise this result to the equation (
4.1
) by taking

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\sqrt{2x(1+x)},\quad b(x)=-\mathfrak{s}x+N+\alpha,\quad H(x,y)=2xy+x+y.

Then, it remains to show (C1)–(C2), (A1)–(A4) to prove this lemma.

Conditions (C1), (C2), (A2) hold trivially.
Let
f(x,y)=H(x,y)/(y-x)
for
x<y
.
A straightforward calculation then shows that
\partial_{x}f(x,y)\geq 0
and
\partial_{y}f(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x,y\geq 0
.
It follows that
f(w,z)\leq f(x,y)
for
w<x<y<z
, which implies that the condition (A1) holds.
The condition (A3) is satisfied for
c\geq 2
because

\displaystyle H(x,z)(z-x)-H(x,y)(y-x)-H(y,z)(z-y)=-2(z-x)(y-x)(z-y).

Since
G=\{0\}
, we see (A4) from
\alpha>-1
.
Thus, we have shown all conditions, and therefore the proof of this lemma is completed.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
.
Then, the semigroup
T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
is Feller-Dynkin, that is, for any
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
we have the following:

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})\text{ for any }t>0,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}f=f.

Proof.

We prove this lemma using matrix processes, following the technique in
[
2
, Proposition 1.3]
.
Let
H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C})
be the space of all non-negative definite Hermitian matrices of size
N
.
Let
\mathfrak{eval}(\mathsf{X})\in W_{\geq}^{N}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{X}\in H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C})
arranged in non-decreasing order.

Consider the matrix valued stochastic differential equation

(4.2)

\displaystyle d\mathsf{X}_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{X}_{t}}{2}}d\mathsf{W}_{t}\sqrt{\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{X}_{t}}+\sqrt{\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{X}_{t}}d\mathsf{W}_{t}^{*}\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{X}_{t}}{2}}+(-\mathfrak{s}\mathsf{X}_{t}+(N+\alpha)\mathsf{I})dt,

where
\mathsf{W}_{t}
be the
N\times N
complex Brownian matrix and
\mathsf{I}
is the identity matrix of size
N
.
Note that the coefficients of (
4.2
) have no singularities, and global Lipschitz functions on
H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C})
.
Therefore, (
4.2
) has a unique strong solution
\mathsf{X}
for any starting point
\mathsf{X}_{0}\in H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C})
.
Its eigenvalue process
\mathfrak{eval}(\mathsf{X})
satisfies the stochastic differential equation (
4.1
)
[
18
, Theorem 4]
.
Hence, for any
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}f(\mathbf{x})=\mathsf{T}_{t}(f\circ\mathfrak{eval})(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{x})).

Let
\mathsf{T}:=\{\mathsf{T}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the semigroup associated with
\mathsf{X}
.
From the global Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients, the semigroup
\mathsf{T}
is Feller-Dynkin.
Thus, for any
F\in C_{\infty}(H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C}))
, we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{t}F\in C_{\infty}(H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C})),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\mathsf{T}_{t}F=F.

Observe that
f\circ\mathfrak{eval}\in C_{\infty}(H_{N,\geq}(\mathbb{C}))
for any
f\in C_{\infty}(W_{\geq}^{N})
.
Therefore, the Feller-Dynkin property of
T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}
follows from (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) with (
4.3
).

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{s}>-1
and
\alpha>-1
.
Then, the Pickrell ensemble
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
is the unique invariant probability measure of
\{T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

This follows by the same proof as of
[
3
, Proposition 4.4]
.
More precisely, from the fact that
p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}
is symmetric with respect to the speed measure
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
and the equation

\int_{W_{\geq}^{N}}\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{x})\det_{i,j=1}^{N}[p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}(y_{i},x_{j})]d\mathbf{x}=e^{\lambda_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N}t}\Delta_{N}(\mathbf{y}),

we have
m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}=m_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N}
by a straightforward computation.
Furthermore, we have
T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N}(\mathbf{x},A)>0
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N}
and a Borel set
A\subset\mathring{W}_{\geq}^{N}
with positive Lebesgue measure from
[
20
, Theorem 4]
.
Therefore, the uniqueness follows by the same argument as in
[
3
, Proposition 4.4]
.
∎

4.2.
Dual operators and h-transformations

To establish the intertwining relation of the Pickrell diffusion, we employ the same strategy as in
[
12
]
.
Key equations in this technique are two h-transformations, which we prepare in this subsection.

We consider the operator
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
When
\alpha\leq-1
, the origin is an exit boundary.
Let
\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
be the Siegmund dual operator of
L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
, that is,

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}=x(1+x)\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\{(2N+\mathfrak{s})x-\alpha\}\frac{d}{dx}.

Here, the point
\infty
is a natural boundary; the origin is an exit boundary for
\alpha\geq 0
, a regular absorbing boundary for
-1<\alpha<0
, and an entrance boundary for
\alpha\leq-1
.
The speed measure for
\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}(x)=x^{-(\alpha+1)}(1+x)^{2N+\mathfrak{s}+\alpha-1}.

Let
\hat{p}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}
be the transition density associated with
\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}
.
We need two formulas of Doob’s h-transform.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, the following (i) and (ii) hold, where we set

\displaystyle c_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N+1}=-2N-\mathfrak{s},\qquad d_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N+1}=-\alpha(2N+\mathfrak{s}+\alpha-1).

(i)

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle e^{-c_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N+1}t}\hat{p}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N+1)}(x,y)\frac{(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)}(y))^{-1}}{(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)}(x))^{-1}}=p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha+1,t}^{(N)}(x,y).

(ii)

For
\alpha>-1
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle e^{-d_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N+1}t}p_{\mathfrak{s}+2\alpha-2,-\alpha,t}^{(N+1)}(x,y)\frac{y^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha}}=p_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{(N)}(x,y).

Proof.

The proof is carried out by straightforward computations.
Actually, we see that
(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)}(x))^{-1}=x^{\alpha+1}(1+x)^{-2N-\mathfrak{s}-\alpha-1}
is a positive eigenfunction of
\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)}
with eigenvalue
c_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N+1}
.
Then, (
4.6
) follows from

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)}\circ(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)})^{-1}=c_{\mathfrak{s}}^{N+1}(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)})^{-1}+(\hat{m}_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N+1)})^{-1}L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha+1}^{(N)}.

Furthermore, note that
L_{\mathfrak{s}+2\alpha-2,-\alpha}^{(N+1)}x^{\alpha}=d_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N+1}x^{\alpha}
.
Then, (
4.7
) comes from

\displaystyle L_{\mathfrak{s}+2\alpha-2,-\alpha}^{(N+1)}\circ x^{\alpha}=d_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{N+1}x^{\alpha}+x^{\alpha}L_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha}^{(N)}.

∎

Hereafter, to simplify notation, we write the characteristic functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N,\mathbf{x}}^{+}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{1}_{W^{N,N+1}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y}),\qquad\mathbf{1}_{N,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{1}_{W_{\geq}^{N,N}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y}).

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1
, the following equations hold:

(4.8)

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha>-1

Then, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha,t}^{N+1}\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}f=\Lambda_{N}^{N+1}T_{\mathfrak{s},\alpha+1,t}^{N}f.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show (
4.10

We first consider the case where

Multiplying both sides in (

and (

), we see that the right hand side becomes

because of the Feller property (
3.2
) and the Feller-Dynkin property established in Lemma

.
Thus we complete the proof.
∎

5.
Approximation of boundary Feller-Dynkin processes

Define an embedding map
\mathfrak{r}_{N}:W_{\geq}^{N}\to\Omega
by
\mathfrak{r}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=(\alpha(\mathbf{x}),\gamma(\mathbf{x}))
, where

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{x_{N+1-i}}{N^{2}}&\text{ for }i\leq N,\\
0&\text{ for }i\geq N+1,\end{cases}\qquad\gamma(\mathbf{x})=\frac{x_{1}+\ldots+x_{N}}{N^{2}}

for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1}\ldots,x_{N})\in W_{\geq}^{N}
.

The boundary probability measure
m\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\Omega)
is approximated by the corresponding coherent measures in the following sense:

Lemma 5.1
.

[
6
,
11
]

For
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, let
m\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\Omega)
be the corresponding measure.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}(\mathfrak{r}_{N})_{*}m^{N}=m\quad\text{ in distribution}.

As a dynamical version of this result, we obtain the following:

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\{m^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
, let
m\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\Omega)
be the corresponding measure.
Suppose that a family of Feller-Dynkin processes
\{\mathbf{X}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is coherent with respect to
\{W_{\geq}^{N},\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, and let
\mathbf{X}
be its boundary Feller-Dynkin process on
\Omega
.
Assume that
\mathfrak{r}_{N}(\mathbf{X}_{0}^{N})=m^{N}
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}=m
in distribution.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
fixed, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathfrak{r}_{N}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})=\mathbf{X}_{t}\quad\text{ in distribution}.

Proof.

This statement can be proved by the same argument as in
[
3
, Proposition 5.4]
.
Actually, from the equality

\displaystyle m^{N+1}T_{t}^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}=m^{N+1}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}T_{t}^{N}=m^{N}T_{t}^{N},

it follows that
\{m^{N}T_{t}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\in\varprojlim\mathcal{M}_{p,\alpha}(W_{\geq}^{N})
.
Furthermore, the corresponding measure of this coherent family is
mT_{t}^{\Omega}
.
Therefore, by applying Lemma
5.1
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

The existence of the boundary Feller-Dynkin process
\{T_{\alpha,t}^{\Omega}\}_{t\geq 0}
results from Proposition
3.8
with Proposition
1.1
.
Here, the
\alpha
-independence follows from the shifted intertwining relation
T_{\alpha+1,t}^{N}\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}=\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N}T_{\alpha,t}^{N}
, that was proved in
[
12
, Lemma 14]
.

In order to obtain the formula (
1.6
), by a straightforward computation, we observe that
\mathfrak{r}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
satisfies the stochastic differential equation

(5.1)

\displaystyle d\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})}}{N}dB_{t}^{i}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big(-N^{2}\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})+\alpha+N

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})+\alpha_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})}{\alpha_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})-\alpha_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})}\Big)dt,

(5.2)

\displaystyle d\gamma(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N})}}{N}dB_{t}+\frac{N(1-\gamma(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N}))+\alpha}{N}dt,

where
B_{t}^{i},B_{t}
are standard Brownian motions.
Applying Lemma
5.2
, we have
\lim_{N\to\infty}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N}),\gamma(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N}))=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(t),\gamma(t))
in distribution for fixed
t
.
Therefore, taking
N\to\infty
in (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha_{i}(t)}{dt}=-\alpha_{i}(t),\qquad\frac{d\gamma(t)}{dt}=-\gamma(t)+1,

which completes the proof.
∎

6.
Discrete kernels that converge to
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}

In this section, we show that our kernel
\Lambda_{\alpha,N}^{N+1}
is obtained from branching formula of multivariate Jacobi polynomials.

6.1.
Branching formula for multivariate Jacobi polynomials

Fix two parameters
\alpha>-1
and
\beta>-1
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}_{n}(x;\alpha,\beta)
be the classical Jacobi polynomials, which is expressed in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{n}(x;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\begin{matrix}-n,n+\alpha+\beta+1\\
\alpha+1\end{matrix};\frac{1-x}{2}\Big).

These polynomials are orthogonal on
[-1,1]
with the weight function
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
.
The value at
x=1
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{n}(1;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)}

and the leading coefficient in
\mathfrak{p}_{n}(x;\alpha,\beta)
is

\displaystyle k_{n}:=2^{-n}\frac{\Gamma(2n+2\sigma)}{\Gamma(n+2\sigma)\Gamma(n+1)},

where we set
\sigma=(\alpha+\beta+1)/2
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition of natural numbers and
l(\lambda)
be its length.
Define the multivariate Jacobi polynomial indexed by a partition
\lambda
with
l(\lambda)\leq n
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};\alpha,\beta):=\frac{\det_{i,j=1}^{n}[\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda_{i}+n-i}(x_{j};\alpha,\beta)]}{\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{x})}.

Let
1_{n}
denote the
n
-dimensional vector whose components are all equal to
1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}(1_{n};\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i)(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}+2n-i-j+2\sigma)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+1)\Gamma(n-i+\alpha+1)\Gamma(i)}.

Proof.

From
[
27
, Proposition 7.1]
and
[
27
, (2.12)]
with a direct computation, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}(1_{n};\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{\lambda_{i}+n-i}}{2^{|\lambda|}}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2},

where
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{l(\lambda)}\lambda_{i}
and

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i}{j-i}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}+2n-i-j+2\sigma}{2n-i-j+2\sigma},

\displaystyle\Pi_{2}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{\Gamma(2\lambda_{i}+2n-2i+2\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(2\lambda_{i}+2n-2i+2\sigma)}\frac{\Gamma(2n-2i+2\sigma)}{\Gamma(2n-2i+2\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+2\sigma)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}\frac{\Gamma(n-i+\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(n-i+2\sigma)}.

A straightforward computation with the duplication formula
\Gamma(2z)/\Gamma(z)=2^{2z-1}\Gamma(z+\frac{1}{2})/\sqrt{\pi}
yields

(6.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{\lambda_{i}+n-i}}{2^{|\lambda|}}\Pi_{2}=2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+1)\Gamma(n-i+\alpha+1)}\frac{\Gamma(2n-2i+2\sigma)}{\Gamma(n-i+2\sigma)}.

Noting that

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{1}{2n-i-j+2\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(2n-2i+2\sigma)}{\Gamma(n-i+2\sigma)}=1,

we prove this lemma from (
6.1
) and (
6.2
).
∎

The Jacobi polynomial
\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}
satisfies a two-step branching formula.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any partition
\lambda
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq n
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},1;\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{\mu\prec\lambda}\sum_{\nu\prec\mu\cup 0}\frac{c_{\nu,n-1}}{c_{\lambda,n}}A_{\mu,\nu}\mathfrak{P}_{\nu}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};\alpha,\beta).

Here,

\displaystyle A_{\mu,\nu}

\displaystyle:=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}B(\mu_{i}+n-i-1,\nu_{i}+n-i-1),

\displaystyle B(m,l)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(2m+\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(m+\beta+1)m!(2l+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(l+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(l+\alpha+1)}{2\Gamma(m+\alpha+\beta+2)\Gamma(m+\alpha+2)\Gamma(l+\beta+1)l!},

\displaystyle c_{\lambda,n}

\displaystyle:=\Gamma(\alpha+1)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+n-i+\alpha+1)}.

Furthermore,
\mu\prec\lambda
means

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geq\mu_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-1}\geq\mu_{n-1}\geq\lambda_{n},

and
\nu\prec\mu\cup 0
means

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\geq\nu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n-1}\geq\nu_{n-1}\geq 0.

Proof.

The proof is directly adapted from that of
[
27
, Proposition 7.5]
.
∎

6.2.
Convergence of discrete kernels

We set
W_{d,\geq}^{N}:=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in(\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N})^{N}\,;\,\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}\}
.
Define a kernel
L_{N}^{N+1}:W_{d,\geq}^{N+1}\dashrightarrow W_{d,\geq}^{N}
by, for
\lambda\in W_{d,\geq}^{N+1}
,

\displaystyle L_{N,\alpha,\beta}^{N+1}(\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{\mu\,;\,\nu\prec\mu\cup 0,\mu\prec\lambda}\frac{c_{\nu,N}}{c_{\lambda,N+1}}A_{\mu,\nu}\frac{\mathfrak{P}_{\nu}(1_{N};\alpha,\beta)}{\mathfrak{P}_{\lambda}(1_{N+1};\alpha,\beta)}.

Here, for
\nu=(\nu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{N})\in W_{d,\geq}^{N}
, we interpret
c_{\nu,N}
as
c_{(\nu_{N},\ldots,\nu_{1}),N}
, and other symbols are understood in the same way.
Since
\sum_{\nu\in W_{d,\geq}^{N}}L_{N}^{N+1}(\lambda,\nu)=1
from (
6.3
), the kernel
L_{N}^{N+1}
is Markov.

Lemma 6.3
.

For
\lambda\in W_{d,\geq}^{N+1}
, let
Z_{\lambda}
be a random variable distributed as
L_{N,\alpha,\beta}^{N+1}(\lambda,\cdot)
.
Then, the distribution of
\kappa^{-1}Z_{\kappa\lambda}
converges weakly to the probability measure given by

(6.4)
