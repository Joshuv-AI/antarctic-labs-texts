Comparing Delivery Modes and Caesarean Section Indications Between Turkish Nationals and Syrian Refugees: A 7-Year, Large-Scale Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Emrah Göçer, Harika Göçer, Mehmet Genco, Merve Genco, Cemal Ünlü, Hüseyin Aksoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare delivery modes, caesarean section (CS) rates, and CS indications between Turkish nationals and Syrian refugee women at a tertiary referral centre in Türkiye, and to evaluate temporal trends.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 62,586 women delivering at Kayseri City Hospital between January 2019 and June 2025. Participants were categorised as Turkish nationals or Syrian refugees. Total CS rates, primary CS rates, annual delivery trends, and CS indications were compared using chi-square and independent-samples t-tests (p<0.05).ResultsOf the total population, 71% were Turkish nationals and 29% Syrian refugees. The overall CS rate was significantly higher among Turkish women (48% vs. 29%, p<0.001). Primary CS rates were also higher in Turkish women (17% vs. 9%, p<0.001), while vaginal delivery was more frequent among Syrian women (71% vs. 52%). Turkish women were significantly older on average; gestational age, fetal sex, and birth weight were broadly comparable between groups. In both populations, repeat caesarean section or previous uterine surgery was the most common CS indication. Among primary CS cases, fetal presentation and positional anomalies were the leading indications. Primary CS rates showed a significant temporal decline in both groups across the study period.ConclusionSyrian refugee women demonstrated markedly lower total and primary CS rates compared with Turkish nationals. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective single-centre design and the absence of key confounders, including parity and body mass index. Prospective studies incorporating multivariable analyses are warranted to elucidate the determinants of these disparities.

Introduction

Caesarean section is a surgical procedure involving an incision into the abdomen to deliver a baby when vaginal delivery is risky or impossible, aiming to protect the health of the mother and/or baby. CS is one of the most frequently performed surgical methods in modern obstetric practice, playing a life-saving role in preserving maternal and neonatal health. While performed according to medical indications, it reduces maternal and infant mortality-morbidity; however, when unnecessarily applied, it can increase short- and long-term health risks.1

The World Health Organization (WHO) states that a caesarean section rate of 10–15% at the population level is optimal for maternal and neonatal health outcomes. There is no evidence to suggest that caesarean rates exceeding this threshold are associated with further reductions in mortality rates2. Over the past three decades, caesarean section rates have demonstrated a marked increase on a global scale. The global average, which stood at approximately 7% in the 1990s, rose to nearly 21% by 2021, and is projected to reach 28–29% by the year 2030.3 Regional disparities are striking: while rates remain below 7% in sub-Saharan Africa, they exceed 50% in Latin America, the Caribbean, and certain Asian countries.4

Caesarean indications include medical reasons such as fetal distress, failure to progress in labor, placenta previa, cephalopelvic disproportion, and previous caesarean history, as well as non-medical factors like maternal request, fear of childbirth, and socio-cultural preferences.5 This increasing global trend, to which both medical and non-medical factors contribute at varying rates, requires careful evaluation from health policy and clinical practice perspectives.

This global trend is similarly observed in Turkey. The caesarean section rate in Turkey significantly exceeds the limits recommended by the WHO. A study using Ministry of Health data reported this rate as 60.5% in 2022, while another study for the 2018–2023 period generally reported it as 57.55%.6,7 This increasing trend is influenced by countries’ demographic structures and differences in access to health services.

Following the civil war that began in Syria in 2011, Turkey adopted an “open door” policy, hosting millions of Syrian refugees. Within this scope, Syrian individuals granted temporary protection status can benefit from various public services, including health and education, free of charge.8 Turkey also provides long-term accommodation and support services to refugees from many different countries, aiming to increase their social integration.9

According to data from the Turkish Directorate of Migration Management, approximately 3 million Syrians under temporary protection live in cities, and about 257,000 reside in temporary accommodation centers.8 UNHCR also reports that there are 222,000 refugees or asylum seekers from other countries, primarily Iraq, Afghanistan, and Iran, not covered by this scope in Turkey.10

Kayseri City Hospital serves as a regional health center, providing services not only to Kayseri but also to surrounding provinces. As of May 2025, it is estimated that approximately 100,000 Syrian refugees live in Kayseri and its vicinity.8 One of the limited studies conducted in Turkey on this topic is by Hansu et al, which examined 74,864 births in Kahramanmaraş between 2013 and 2021. This study found the general caesarean rate to be 56% in Turkish women and 32% in Syrian refugee women.11 Furthermore, among women with a history of multiple caesarean sections, Syrian refugee women have been reported to have significantly higher rates of emergency caesarean delivery compared to Turkish nationals, with a greater prevalence of more than two prior caesarean sections in the refugee cohort — suggesting that emergent rather than elective obstetric interventions may predominate in this population.12 These findings highlight the importance of investigating birth mode preferences and health service utilization habits of the migrant population.

Although previous studies have consistently reported lower caesarean section rates among Syrian refugee women compared to Turkish nationals, the specific distribution of CS indications, the emergency versus elective pathway distinction, and temporal trends in primary CS rates remain insufficiently characterized in high-volume tertiary care settings. We hypothesized that Syrian refugee women would exhibit lower total and primary CS rates than Turkish women, and that the distribution of CS indications — particularly the proportion of emergency versus elective procedures — would differ significantly between the two populations.

Methods

This study included a total of 62,586 pregnant women who gave birth at Kayseri City Hospital between January 1, 2019, and June 30, 2025. Data were obtained through a retrospective review of the hospital’s information management system. Inclusion criteria were as follows: singleton pregnancies resulting in live births at or beyond 22 weeks of gestation, delivered at Kayseri City Hospital during the study period, with nationality recorded as either Turkish or Syrian. Exclusion criteria included multiple gestations (twin, triplet, or higher-order pregnancies), cases with major fetal structural anomalies, and intrauterine fetal death prior to the onset of labor. Women whose nationality could not be verified through the hospital information system were also excluded from the analysis. Parity and body mass index (BMI) data were not available within the hospital information system and were therefore not included in the analysis. This retrospective study was approved by the Kayseri City Hospital Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/432). As this study involved retrospective review of routinely collected clinical data and all data were analyzed anonymously, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The pregnant women included in the study were divided into two groups: Syrian refugee women and Turkish women. The total caesarean delivery rate and primary caesarean delivery rate were calculated separately for both groups. Primary caesarean delivery rates for Syrian refugee women and Turkish women were compared by year; additionally, caesarean delivery indications in both groups were identified and compared.

The obtained data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software. Quantitative variables were reported as mean and percentage, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Caesarean delivery rates over the years were analyzed; the chi-square test was used to compare caesarean indications between the groups. A significance level of p<0.05 was accepted for all statistical evaluations.

Statistical Method

SPSS version 26.0 statistical package program was used for data evaluation, working within 95% confidence limits. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency and percentage for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to check for normality of data distribution. The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. For comparing continuous variables of two independent groups, the independent samples t-test was used for variables meeting normality assumptions. Graphs were prepared using SPSS IBM and Microsoft Excel package programs. As this was a retrospective cohort study utilising all eligible deliveries recorded during the study period, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Given the large sample size, statistically significant p-values should be interpreted alongside effect sizes and clinical relevance rather than in isolation. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for continuous variables to assess clinical relevance (maternal age: d=0.33; gestational age: d=0.00; birth weight: d=0.01).Of the total 62,586 participants in the study, 71% were Turkish and 29% were Syrian nationals.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the modes of delivery among Turkish and Syrian pregnant women residing in a region with a high concentration of Syrian refugees in Turkey, and to evaluate the potential effects of medical and sociocultural factors on birth outcomes. The findings are broadly consistent with national and international studies in the literature.

Caesarean section rates have increased markedly worldwide over the past three decades. The global average, which stood at approximately 7% in the 1990s, has risen to 21% today and is projected to reach 28–29% by 2030.1–3 It has been reported that not only medical necessities but also socioeconomic, legal, and cultural factors contribute to this increase.4,5 A similar trend is observed in Turkey, where the national caesarean section rate for 2022 was reported as 60.5%.6,7 In our study, the overall caesarean section rate among Turkish women was 48% and the primary caesarean section rate was 17%, whereas among Syrian women these rates were 29% and 9%, respectively (p<0.001). These rates are consistent with similar studies conducted in different regions of Turkey. Hansu et al reported rates of 56% among Turkish women and 32% among Syrian refugee women, while Kıyak et al reported rates of 33.4% and 23.9%, respectively.11,13 In this context, a recent large-scale study by Güner and Karabudak, encompassing 68,944 deliveries from a high-volume tertiary care centre in Türkiye, reported an overall caesarean section rate of 55.0%, with previous uterine surgery being the most common indication (56.2%), followed by foetal distress (24.3%). These findings are consistent with the caesarean section indication profile observed among Turkish women in our study, further confirming that repeat caesarean section remains the predominant indication in tertiary care settings across Türkiye.14 These findings suggest that caesarean section rates in Turkey are shaped not only by obstetric necessities but also by sociocultural trends and differences in healthcare delivery.

The increase in caesarean section rates in Turkey is closely associated with socioeconomic conditions, healthcare system dynamics, and physician behaviour. According to data from the Turkey Demographic and Health Survey, the caesarean section rate rose from 14.3% in 1993 to 51.9% in 2013, an increase that has been linked to advanced maternal age, higher educational attainment, urbanisation, and the frequency of deliveries in private hospitals.15 Another analysis based on the Robson classification demonstrated that caesarean section rates in private and university hospitals were higher than those in public hospitals, accounting for a significant share of the national increase.16 Furthermore, individual factors influencing women’s birth preferences — including fear of childbirth, the desire to plan the process, and the perception of caesarean section as a “safer” option — reinforce this trend.17

Legal liability and malpractice concerns among physicians also play an important role in the rising caesarean section rates. Studies conducted among obstetricians in Turkey indicate that the direct attribution of physician responsibility in the event of intrapartum complications has led to an increase in defensive medical practices.18,19 Similarly, international studies have demonstrated that physicians tend to prefer caesarean section over vaginal delivery due to fear of litigation.20 This suggests that the increase in caesarean section rates is associated not only with medical or sociocultural factors, but also with legal and professional dynamics.

The overall caesarean section rate among Syrian women was found to be 29%, with a primary caesarean section rate of 9%. A multicentre analysis conducted in the Arab region reported that hospital-based caesarean section rates in Syria ranged from 23% to 30% in the pre-crisis period, rising to 45% during the civil war.21,22 A more recent study conducted by Abdulrazzak et al at the University Hospitals of Aleppo and Damascus reported rates of 41.7% and 38.5%, respectively.23 The differences between the groups may be attributable to the younger age of Syrian women, their correspondingly lower parity, cultural birth preferences, and structural barriers to accessing healthcare services. The language, legal status, access, and continuity of care issues that limit refugee women’s utilisation of antenatal care services have been previously highlighted.24,25 The fact that Syrian women were younger (25±6 vs. 27±6, p<0.001) and of lower parity compared to Turkish women may partially explain the difference in caesarean section rates. These findings are also consistent with meta-analyses demonstrating an increased risk of obstetric complications and caesarean section with advancing age.26 However, Cohen’s d effect size analysis revealed that differences in gestational age (d=0.00) and birth weight (d=0.01) between groups were negligible, confirming that statistical significance in this large sample does not necessarily reflect clinical relevance. When all these variables are considered together, it is thought that the lower caesarean section rates observed among Syrian women are largely associated with demographic differences and the structural conditions governing access to healthcare services.

Systematic reviews conducted among migrant and refugee women in Europe indicate that caesarean section rates are generally lower than those of native women, which may be related to cultural factors, birth preferences, and healthcare-seeking behaviours.27 A study conducted in Germany found that the caesarean section rate among Syrian refugee women was lower than that of native women (36% vs. 43.9%), attributing this finding to the “healthy migrant effect”.28 Since migrating individuals are generally younger, healthier, and have a lower obstetric risk profile, this may contribute to favourable birth outcomes. However, the sustainability of this effect is closely related to the level of integration into the host country’s healthcare system and the availability of social support mechanisms. Under conditions of inadequate integration, the initially observed advantage may diminish over time. Studies conducted in Turkey have shown that perinatal health indicators of Syrian refugee women are similar to those of Turkish women in some regions; however, antenatal care utilisation remains lower among the refugee population.29

It is noteworthy that despite high caesarean section rates in Syrian hospitals, this rate remains at the level of global averages among Syrian women living in Turkey. This finding, together with the healthy migrant effect, suggests that a certain degree of progress has been achieved in the integration of Syrian women into the Turkish healthcare system. However, our findings indicate that lower caesarean section rates may be associated not only with cultural preferences, but also with structural factors such as late presentation to the healthcare system, inadequate prenatal follow-up, and a lower likelihood of intrapartum intervention. Furthermore, the fact that Kayseri City Hospital serves as a regional referral centre and that complicated cases are referred to this centre from surrounding provinces may partially explain the higher caesarean section rates observed among Turkish women.

Regarding caesarean section indications, the most frequent reason was “previous caesarean section/history of uterine surgery.” Among primary caesarean sections, fetal distress, malpresentation, and cephalopelvic disproportion were the leading indications, with hypertensive complications also being noteworthy. This distribution is consistent with national and international literature.13,28,29 Some studies have reported higher rates of emergency intervention among migrant women,30 suggesting that this may be related to the timing of presentation and continuity of care. The multilayered barriers to healthcare access among forcibly displaced women — including language, cultural norms, trust, and access to rights — have been systematically documented.31 Qualitative studies conducted in Syria prior to the conflict have also highlighted inequalities in access to antenatal care.32 It is further recognised that the demand for caesarean section is not solely an individual “rational choice,” but is influenced by fear of childbirth, previous experiences, and professional guidance.33

These findings demonstrate that access to maternity services for refugee women is not solely a matter of health policy, but also a fundamental human rights issue. Qualitative studies conducted in Turkey have revealed that language barriers, legal status uncertainty, and lack of trust in the healthcare system significantly limit the utilisation of antenatal care services.34,35 Reports by the World Health Organization have further highlighted that refugee women encounter cultural insensitivity and structural inequalities throughout prenatal and postnatal care processes.36 The view that access to healthcare constitutes a fundamental human right indicates that this issue must be addressed not only from a clinical perspective, but also in terms of its ethical and social dimensions.37 Consequently, the development of migrant health policies should take into account not only medical indicators, but also societal and ethical considerations.

In conclusion, caesarean section rates among Syrian refugee pregnant women were found to be significantly lower than those among Turkish women. The mode of delivery is associated not only with clinical indicators, but also with cultural, socioeconomic, and structural factors. The higher rates of vaginal delivery observed among Syrian women may be explained by younger age, parity differences, cultural birth preferences, and less interventionist midwife–patient communication. Furthermore, the late presentation of refugee women to the antenatal care system may indirectly influence intervention rates by increasing the tendency towards spontaneous progression during labour. Enhancing cultural sensitivity in migrant health services, reducing language and communication barriers, improving the accessibility of antenatal care, and restructuring maternity services in a manner that addresses the specific needs of refugee women are considered priority requirements.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgement. First, parity and body mass index (BMI) data were not available for extraction from the hospital information system and could therefore not be included in the analysis. Given that Syrian refugee women are known to have higher parity at younger ages, and that maternal obesity is a primary physiological driver of dysfunctional labor and macrosomia, the absence of these variables represents a source of residual confounding. The observed difference in primary caesarean section rates (17% vs 9%) may therefore partly reflect differences in parity distribution rather than a true clinical disparity. Second, as the study relied on retrospectively coded hospital records, coding errors and misclassification of caesarean indications cannot be independently verified, and the retrospective single-centre design limits the generalisability of findings to other settings. Third, language and intake barriers may have contributed to miscoding or incomplete documentation of CS indications in the Syrian cohort. Fourth, the marked decline in primary caesarean section rates observed in 2024–2025 could not be attributed to specific administrative, clinical, or policy drivers, as no corresponding institutional audit or policy data were available within the scope of this retrospective study. Future prospective studies incorporating parity, BMI, and multivariable regression modelling are needed to more rigorously characterise the determinants of caesarean delivery in refugee populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large-scale retrospective study demonstrates statistically significant differences in caesarean section rates between Turkish and Syrian refugee women, with Syrian women exhibiting lower total and primary caesarean section rates (29% vs 48% and 9% vs 17%, respectively; p<0.001). While these differences are consistent with the existing literature, the retrospective design and absence of parity and BMI data preclude definitive causal interpretation. The observed disparities likely reflect a combination of younger maternal age, cultural birth preferences, and structural barriers to healthcare access rather than a true clinical disparity.

Future prospective studies incorporating parity, BMI, and multivariable regression modelling, alongside qualitative research, are needed to more rigorously characterise the determinants of caesarean delivery in refugee populations. Enhancing cultural sensitivity in migrant health services, reducing language barriers, and improving antenatal care accessibility remain priority requirements for equitable obstetric care.
