Analysis of bacterial pathogen spectrum and epidemiological characteristics of pediatric lower respiratory tract infections: a large sample study based on bronchoalveolar lavage
Authors: Lijuan Yin, Rui Yang, Xi Chen, Zhou Fu, Xiaoyan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) is a common disease that endangers the health of children. Our study aims to investigate the bacterial etiology of LRTI and explore the risk factors associated with severe pneumonia among hospitalized children in Chongqing, China.MethodsThe bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BLAF) samples of 2,962 hospitalized pediatric patients diagnosed with LRTI from January to December 2024 were collected and subjected to detection of respiratory bacterial pathogens using a kit based on on-chip loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP). The distributions of the bacterial pathogens in different age groups, genders and seasons were analyzed. Logistic regression analyses were applied to determine the factors associated with the severity of pneumonia in children.ResultsOf the 2,962 pediatric patients, 1,520 (51.32%) cases tested positive for at least one respiratory bacterial pathogen. Among the bacterial pathogens, Haemophilus influenzae (24.75%) was the most prevalent, followed by Streptococcus pneumoniae (19.75%), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus (11.31%) and Staphylococcus aureus (9.08%), which were the main pathogens detected in the children enrolled. Age-related analyses indicated higher detection rates of Haemophilus influenzae (28.55%) in toddlers, Streptococcus pneumoniae (29.00%) in preschool children, and Staphylococcus aureus (17.37%) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus (21.09%) in infants. No significant difference in infection rates was identified between boys and girls. Moreover, diverse seasonal peaks were observed for the main pathogens. Co-infections were detected in 428 (14.45%) cases, with double infections constituting the majority (79.67%). In addition, logistic regression analyses revealed that male gender, elevated body temperature, high level of NEU and infection with Streptococcus pneumoniae might be risk factors for severe pneumonia of the children with LRTI.ConclusionOur findings revealed the epidemiological patterns of respiratory bacterial pathogens and the potential risk factors for severe pneumonia in children with LRTI, providing theoretical basis for clinical prevention and specific therapies.Keywords: LRTI, Pediatric patients, Respiratory pathogens, Pneumonia

Background

Lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) is one of the most common pediatric diseases that threatens the health of children and has become the predominant cause of childhood mortality [1]. LRTI refers to inflammation of the lower respiratory tract caused by various pathogens, covering a spectrum of severity from acute bronchitis to severe pneumonia, with clinical manifestations of cough, fever, production of sputum, shortness of breath, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, etc [2, 3]. While improved nutrition, LRTI-specific interventions, and vaccines have substantially reduced LRTI-caused mortality of children under 5 years old in recent decades, pneumonia remains the most common cause of life lost in this age group worldwide [4]. It has been documented that LRTI accounted for approximately 13.9% (0.74 million) of all deaths in children under 5 years old in 2019 globally [5]. As a preventable and treatable disease, it is essential to better understand the etiology of LRTI to develop targeted preventive interventions and provide effective treatment.

The respiratory pathogens responsible for LRTI are diverse and complex. The most common bacterial pathogens of LRTI are Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) and Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), which usually caused typical pneumonia [6, 7]. Among these bacteria, S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae are top two leading cause of childhood pneumonia [8]. In addition, S. aureus, which has no available vaccine and can develop resistance to multiple antibiotics, is a clinically important cause of complicated pneumonia, resulting in significantly poor outcomes [9]. In recent years, several studies have assessed the bacterial profiles of hospitalized pediatric patients with LRTI in some local regions of China [10, 11]. However, the prevalence of respiratory bacterial pathogens was substantially affected by many factors such as age, gender, climate, environment, medical level and geographical economy [12]. Research on the etiology and epidemiology of LRTI in pediatric patients in developing countries such as China remains inadequate.

In the present study, we analyzed the epidemiological characteristics of common respiratory bacterial pathogens among hospitalized children who were diagnosed with LRTI in Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from January to December 2024. Moreover, we assessed the risk factors for severe pneumonia in children with LRTI, which might suggest potential factors associated with the development of severe pneumonia.

Methods

Patients

A total of 2,962 hospitalized children diagnosed with LRTI in Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from January to December 2024 were enrolled in this retrospective study. Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged younger than 18 years old; (2) Diagnosed with LRTI by the pediatrician; (3) With complete clinical information; (4) Clinical samples were collected for pathogen detection. Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of congenital airway malformation; (2) Diagnosed with chronic lung disease; (3) Immunodeficiency; (4) Underlying diseases of vital organs (e.g., brain, heart, liver, and kidneys); (5) Presence of tumor, and other systemic disorders. Written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (File No.2025 − 227).

Clinical specimens

Clinical specimens were collected from the hospitalized children by trained nurses for respiratory pathogenic bacteria detection. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed with sterile normal saline that has been pre-warmed to body temperature. The volumes of BAL were adjusted to body weight using 3 mL/kg of normal saline. In children weighing less than 20 kg, the total volume was divided into three equal fractions, while for those over 20 kg, it was delivered in sequential 20 mL portions. Each fraction was instilled through the suction channel of the flexible fibreoptic bronchoscope with a syringe, followed by an air bolus to clear the dead space. The BLAF recovery was achieved by mechanical suction at 3.33–13.3 kPa (25–100 mmHg) into a trap [13]. The samples were kept at 4 ℃ and transported to the laboratory. Clinical data, such as age, gender, body temperature, white blood cell (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP) and neutrophil count (NEU) of the children were obtained from the electronic hospital record system.

Detection of respiratory pathogens

The clinical samples were first subjected to nucleic acid extraction using a nucleic acid extraction kit (CapitalBio Technology, Beijing, China), and followed by pathogen detection, including S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, methicillin-resistant staphylococcus (MRS), K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, H. influenzae and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (S. maltophilia), with an on-chip LAMP kit (CapitalBio Technology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each chip of this kit was pre-loaded with one positive control, nine negative controls, and one positive internal control, which are human specific primers. According to the result interpretation criteria, the positive control must test positive, while all nine negative controls are required to yield negative results. The positive internal control is expected to be positive, however, a negative result may occur in cases of low human genomic DNA content in the sample, indicating an insufficient number of human cells and necessitating repeat testing with a newly collected specimen. This kit has undergone clinical trials on a total of 2084 sputum samples across five hospitals. The positive and negative agreement rates with sequencing methods were greater than 96% and 99%, respectively, and the overall agreement rate was more than 99%. Among them, the consistency rate between the kit results and culture method of 733 clinical samples was 67.4%. The limit of detection (LOD) for the assay was 500 copies/reaction.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.0. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and compared between groups using the Chi-square test, which is appropriate for assessing associations between categorical variables. To identify independent risk factors associated with the severity of pneumonia (mild/moderate or severe pneumonia), univariate and multivariate logistic regression was employed, as this method is designed to model the relationship between multiple variables and a binary outcome. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of the enrolled pediatric patients

From January to December 2024, a total of 2,962 pediatric patients with LRTI were enrolled in our study. As depicted in Table 1, the ratio of boys to girls was 1.24:1. The average age of the children was 3.86 ± 3.47 years, ranging from 1 month to 18 years old. Most of the children were younger than 6 years old, accounting for 78.76% of all cases. Regarding the disease spectrum, 2,195 (74.11%) children were diagnosed with mild or moderate pneumonia, the other 767 (25.89%) cases were confirmed as severe pneumonia.

Overall detection of respiratory bacterial pathogens in children with LRTI

Among the 2,962 children enrolled, 1,520 (51.32%) cases were tested positive for at least one of the respiratory bacterial pathogens. The most prevalent pathogen was H. influenzae, with a detection rate of 24.75% (n = 733), followed by S. pneumoniae (19.75%, n = 585), MRS (11.31%, n = 335), S. aureus (9.08%, n = 269), A. baumannii (1.32%, n = 39), S. maltophilia (1.11%, n = 33), K. pneumoniae (1.05%, n = 31) and P. aeruginosa (1.01%, n = 30). It suggests that H. influenzae, S. pneumoniae, MRS and S. aureus are the main respiratory bacterial pathogens in children with LRTI in the present study.

Distribution of the respiratory bacterial pathogens by age and gender

As shown in Table 2, the prevalence of the respiratory bacterial pathogens in children of different age groups was significantly different. The overall positive rate of the infant (< 1 year), toddler (1–3 years), preschool (3–6 years) and school-age (≥ 6 years) groups were 56.08%, 48.86%, 53.52% and 44.99%, respectively, which exhibited a downward trend with advancing age on the whole (χ2 = 20.685, P < 0.001). Among the pathogens, S. pneumoniae had the highest detection rate in preschool group (29.00%), followed by school-age children (19.71%), toddler (17.47%) and infant group (12.28%) (χ2 = 75.210, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). H. influenzae was the most common pathogen in the toddler group (28.55%), followed by preschool children (25.24%), infant (23.70%) and school-age group (21.14%) (χ2 = 11.835, P = 0.008) (Fig. 1). In addition, S. aureus, and MRS were detected much more frequently in infant group than the other groups (χ2 = 101.605, P < 0.001, χ2 = 109.847, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Similarly, the detection rates of K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii were slightly but consistently higher in the infant group compared with other age cohorts (χ2 = 24.122, P < 0.001, χ2 = 12.605, P = 0.006). However, no significant differences were observed in the positive rates of P. aeruginosa and S. maltophilia in the different age groups (χ2 = 4.454, P = 0.216, χ2 = 6.961, P = 0.073). Moreover, we conducted a gender-specific analysis of the detection rates of the 8 respiratory bacterial pathogens, which revealed no statistically significant differences between boys and girls.

Distribution of the respiratory bacterial pathogens by season

As depicted in Table 3, the overall positive rates of the respiratory bacterial pathogens were 55.13% in spring (March to May), 49.57% in summer (June to August), 47.66% in autumn (September to November) and 51.24% in winter (December to February), indicating significantly higher prevalence in colder seasons (winter and spring) than warmer seasons (summer and autumn) (χ2 = 9.298, P = 0.026). In general, the prevalence of each individual respiratory pathogen showed specific seasonality. In the present study, the outbreaks of the main pathogens also had distinct seasonal variation (Table 3; Fig. 2). H. influenzae had much higher positive rates in spring (29.69%) and winter (32.06%) than in summer (20.69%) and autumn (13.49%) (χ2 = 76.634, P < 0.001). S. pneumoniae was detected more frequently throughout spring (20.85%), summer (19.95%) and autumn (22.66%), with a decline observed in winter (15.67%) (χ2 = 10.883, P = 0.012). The prevalence of S. aureus in summer (10.16%) and autumn (13.31%) was significantly higher than that in spring (7.42%) and winter (6.59%) (χ2 = 21.365, P < 0.001). P. aeruginosa displayed a similar seasonality with S. aureus, although its total positive rate was only 1.01% (χ2 = 10.807, P = 0.013). Additionally, MRS was detected more often in spring (12.26%) and summer (13.51%) than in autumn (8.27%) and winter (9.22%) (χ2 = 13.662, P = 0.003). However, we did not find any distinct seasonal variations in A. Baumannii, K. pneumoniae and S. maltophilia.

Co-infections of the respiratory bacterial pathogens

Co-infections of multiple respiratory pathogens in pediatric patients with LRTI are quite common. Among the 1,520 positive cases, 1,092 (71.84%) were just infected with single one bacterial pathogen, and the other 428 (28.16%) were co-infected with multiple pathogens. Among the co-infection cases, 341 (79.67%) were co-infected with two pathogens and 87 (20.33%) were co-infected with 3 to 5 pathogens. The co-infection patterns in different age groups and seasons were shown in Table 4. The infant group showed highest co-infection rate (14.52%) of two bacteria, followed by school-age (12.08%), preschool (11.53%) and toddler group (7.54%) (χ2 = 18.234, P < 0.001). A similar trend in co-infection rate of more than two bacterial pathogens was detected in different age groups (χ2 = 28.894, P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in both co-infection types in different seasons (χ2 = 3.745, P < 0.29, χ2 = 1.599, P < 0.66).

Risk factors for severe pneumonia of pediatric patients with LRTI

To understand the risk factors that influence the severity of pneumonia in children with LRTI, the parameters such as gender, age, body temperature, WBC, CRP, NEU, single respiratory bacterial infection and mixed infection were incorporated into logistic regression analyses. As depicted in Table 5, the univariate logistic regression analysis indicated that gender, body temperature, WBC, CRP, NEU, S. pneumoniae, A. baumannii and S. maltophilia infection were associated with the severity of pneumonia in children (P < 0.1). Since age is a critically important factor, we included it, along with other statistically significant variables from the univariate analysis, in the further multivariate regression analysis, finding that male gender (HR = 1.263, P = 0.021), high body temperature (HR = 1.168, P = 0.015), high level of NEU (HR = 1.054, P = 0.002) and infection with S. pneumoniae (HR = 1.051, P = 0.048) might be risk factors of severe pneumonia in children.

Discussion

The incomplete development of the respiratory tract and low immune resistance in children make them more susceptible for LRTI than other age groups. LRTI seriously affects the health of children and has been considered as a worldwide public health problem [14]. Environmental changes and the wide application of antibiotics have led to shifts in the epidemiological profile of respiratory pathogens causing LRTI. In recent years, several studies have investigated the prevalence of various respiratory pathogens among children in some regions of China, such as Shanghai [15], Beijing [16], Shenzhen [17], Nanjing [18] and Chengdu [19]. However, China is a very large country with distinct climates, geography, customs and socioeconomic status in different regions. And few studies with large-scale samples have been reported on the respiratory bacterial profiles of LRTI in children in Chongqing, China. The present study revealed the prevalence, age distribution, seasonal variation and co-infection patterns of common bacterial pathogens in children diagnosed with LRTI from January to December 2024 in Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, which is the top three children’s specialized hospitals in China, serving as an important diagnosis and treatment center for critical care of respiratory infections with about 40% of patients from non-local area.

In our study, 51.32% (1,520/2,962) of the children were detected positive for at least one of the respiratory bacterial pathogens. The former large-sample study performed from 2013 to 2015 in Shanghai revealed that the respiratory pathogens were detected in 58.94% (5,996/10,123) of the children with LRTI, which is roughly comparable to our overall findings [20]. Another more recent research conducted from January 2023 to July 2024 in Hanzhong, China, suggested that the bacterial pathogens were identified in 37.53% (2,050/5,463) of the pediatric participates with LRTI [21]. It was quite different from both our data and the earlier Shanghai report [20]. The obvious discrepancy may be attributed not only to the differences in diagnostic methods, but also to the regional and temporal factors, which highlighted the variability of pathogen prevalence. Moreover, we found that H. influenzae (24.75%) and S. pneumoniae (19.75%) emerged as the most prevalent pathogens in children with LRTI. Our finding was closely aligned with global epidemiological data, which recognized these bacteria as primary causes of childhood pneumonia [22].

The age-specific analyses revealed an inverse correlation between age and the detection rate of respiratory bacterial pathogens, a trend likely due to the progressive maturation of both the respiratory tract and the immune system of the children. However, there was a slight increase in infection rate in preschool children, which might be owing to the attendance in kindergarten and increased social interactions of preschool children, leading to their exposure to various respiratory pathogens. Previous study has demonstrated that daycare attendance was significantly correlated with increased odds of respiratory infections and pneumonia [23]. Moreover, the prevalence of S. pneumoniae was gradually elevated among infant, toddler and preschool children but declined in school-age children, which could be attributed to the progressively higher exposure rates to S. pneumoniae in the younger groups and the gradual maturation of the respiratory system and development of acquired immunity in those school-age children. The infection rate of H. influenzae was the highest in toddler group. This may be attributed to the gradual waning of maternally derived protective antibodies in toddlers. Concurrently, S. aureus and MRS exhibited particularly high detection rates in infants, which may be owing to their immature immunity that unable to resist the pathogens effectively, along with close contact with their caregivers, raising the risk of exposure to these pathogens [24]. This epidemiological pattern was consistent with the findings from a regional study conducted in Beijing, China [25]. The convergence between our results and the Beijing study strengthens the validity of this observed trend across different geographic populations. The other pathogens, including K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii and S. maltophilia, were also more frequently detected in infants than in other age groups, although the number of positive cases for each individual pathogen was relatively low. Our findings revealed the age-specific infection patterns of respiratory bacterial pathogens in children, which might be helpful for the formulating of targeted prevention and treatment approaches for children of different age groups.

Generally, respiratory bacterial pathogens showed specific seasonal characteristics, since there are distinct temperatures and humidity in different seasons, which were critical factors for the survival and transmission of pathogens [26]. The overall pathogen detection rate in the present study was higher in winter and spring than in summer and autumn. This seasonality is likely attributed to cold weather, which may both decrease the local resistance of respiratory mucosa in children and enhance the survival and transmission of pathogens. In addition, we found that H. influenzae was the most prevalent in spring and winter, which was consistent with the previous regional studies in Chaoshan and Wuhan, China [27, 28]. This consistency of epidemiological observations across three distinct geographical regions strengthened the validity of this seasonal pattern of H. influenzae. Moreover, our data revealed that the detection rate of S. pneumoniae was evidently declined in winter compared with other seasons. This finding contrasts with the study from Hohhot, Inner Mongolia, China, which reported higher detection rates of S. pneumoniae in winter [15]. The colder and drier climate of Inner Mongolia may create environmental conditions that favor winter transmission of S. pneumoniae. Additionally, regional variations in vaccination coverage and usage of antibiotics may also contribute to these divergent epidemiological patterns [29]. Furthermore, the detection rate of S. aureus peaked in autumn, and MRS was detected more frequent in spring and summer. The other pathogens did not exhibit significant seasonality. Our findings disclosed the dynamic seasonality of respiratory bacterial pathogens infections, which could guide the prevention and control strategies.

Numerous studies have shown that LRTIs in children are caused by more than one respiratory pathogen [30–32]. In the current study, co-infections with multiple pathogens were detected in 28.16% of the children, which was roughly similar with the result (22.30%) of the study performed in southern Sichuan, China [33]. Among the co-infection cases, the vast majority (79.67%) of them were infected with 2 pathogens. Moreover, the detection rates of co-infection with 2 pathogens was highest in infants, followed by school-age and prechool groups, which might be due to the incomplete development of the respiratory tract and lower immune risistance in infants, as well as more opportunities exposure to various pathogens among school-age and preschool children. A similar pattern was also observed for co-infection with more than 2 pathogens. Therefore, mixed infection of respiratory pathogens cannot be ignored in children with LRTI. In consideration of the large proportion and high complexity of multiple infection of pathogens, further study with larger sample size and longer study periods is needed.

LRTI caused pneumonia, especially severe pneumonia, was the main cause of death in children under 5 years old [2]. In our study, we performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify the factors that associated with the severity of pneumonia, finding that male gender, high body temperature, high level of NEU and infection with S. pneumoniae might be risk factors of severe pneumonia in children. As established, we have already addressed the significance of age in LRTI. Despite the lack of statistical significance in both univariate and multivariable analyses, we emphasize that age must remain a fundamental clinical consideration in assessing disease severity. Existing evidences suggested that males are more susceptible to LRTIs than females, with generally more severe course and higher mortality rates observed in males, particularly in cases of community-acquired pneumonia, which supported our data [34, 35]. Elevated body temperature and high level of WBC, NEU and CRP have been considered as non-specific biomarkers for infection and inflammation [36]. In our study, WBC and CRP showed significant associations with severe pneumonia in the univariate analysis, but lost their independent predictive significance in the multivariate model after adjusting for other variables. This finding did not negate their clinical value, but indicated that they were better characterized as indicators of systemic inflammation rather than as independent drivers of pneumonia progression. Given the ease of measurement and low cost, their value as tools for initial assessment and dynamic monitoring in clinical practice should not be ignored. A similar situation was observed for A. baumannii and S. maltophilia. These 2 bacteria were typical opportunistic pathogen whose detection and pathogenicity are strongly linked to a compromised patient baseline, such as prolonged hospitalization or underlying disease. The multivariate analysis suggested that the underlying health status of patient might be the primary cause of pneumonia severity, rather than the infection with these two bacteria itself. The detection of these 2 bacteria might be an indicator for identifying high-risk and vulnerable populations. Furthermore, the possibility of limited statistical power due to their low detection rates should also be considered. Among bacterial LRTIs, S. pneumoniae has been confirmed to be the most prevalent pathogen and the leading cause of LRTI-related deaths in children [37]. Although our study identified it as a risk factor for severe pneumonia, it should be noted that in our multivariate analysis, the P-value for S. pneumoniae approached the 0.05 threshold, indicating a risk of Type I error.

Several limitations of the current study should be considered. First, this is a single-center investigation in the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University with only hospitalized children, limiting the broader applicability, the findings would be more convincing if the data were derived from multiple centers with larger sample size and longer study periods. Second, the prevalence of common viral pathogens was absent, which might lead to the underestimate of the viral burden and co-infections of bacteria and viruses. Third, our study lacked the precise data on antibiotic usage prior to sample collection. The antibiotic treatment could have suppressed the growth of bacteria, leading to reduced pathogen detection rates and an underestimation of the true prevalence of the bacterial pathogens. Future multi-center studies using more comprehensive molecular diagnostic methods, including virus detection, and detailed documentation of antibiotic use will help validate and expand our findings.

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings gave insights into the prevalence of common respiratory bacterial pathogens in children with LRTI in Chongqing, China. The study uncovered the variations of pathogen prevalence among different age groups and seasons, and the risk factors for severe pneumonia in children with LRTI, highlighting the variability of pathogen prevalence and providing valuable information for the targeted prevention and effective treatments for LRTI.
