Enhanced wheat crop leaf disease classification using multi-level contrast enhancement and modified vision transformers
Authors: Irfan Haider, Muhammad Nazir, Sajid Ali Khan, Suliman Aladhadh, Muhammad Ramzan, Mohamed I. Habib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The integration of advanced tools and techniques has significantly boosted agricultural productivity. Wheat crops, which are vital for global food security, are often susceptible to various bacterial and viral diseases, considerably impacting both yield and quality. Efficient disease detection is crucial for effective treatment and yield optimization. This study presents an innovative approach that combines a multi-level contrast enhancement framework with a novel transformer-based architecture for the rapid and precise diagnosis of wheat crop leaf diseases. By employing contrast enhancement techniques, we enhance the visual quality of wheat crop images, facilitating improved feature extraction. Incorporating Vision Transformers (ViTs) enhances computational efficiency, enables multi-scale feature extraction, and reduces dimensionality. We implement and evaluate our proposed models on two publicly available wheat datasets, utilizing three variants of ViT: a modified ViT with seven-block transformers, a pre-trained ViT-16-Tiny, and a modified ViT with seven transformer blocks and skip connections. Our results demonstrate classification accuracies of 98.90% for the modified seven-block ViT, 97.50% for the ViT-16-Tiny model, and 97.90% for the ViT seven-block with skip connections. A comparative analysis with state-of-the-art techniques reveals that our proposed techniques outperform existing methods in terms of accuracy, precision, sensitivity, False Negative Rate, and the total number of learnable parameters.

Introduction

The growth of agriculture is not only meant to feed the population, but it also refers to the economic development of any country1. As the world’s population is growing rapidly and cultivation land is also getting scarce, it has become a worrisome task for governments to balance the demand and supply of food for the masses2. Therefore, this era requires the production of high-quality food products for everyone3. Crops are vulnerable to multiple problems, including critical weather conditions, various bacterial and viral diseases, and pest attacks4,5. These problems badly affect crop production quantity and quality6. Diseases are a common problem in all crops and must be addressed promptly to ensure crop yield7. Wheat is the most crucial cash crop due to its demand and usage8. Numerous diseases affect wheat production, such as yellow rust, brown rust, and septoria9. There are various methods for detecting diseases. The most basic approach involves traditional techniques, where crop knowledge is utilized, and the disease in crop leaves is identified by visually interpreting the symptoms10. However, these methods are prone to error due to a lack of field knowledge or misclassification11. These traditional methods are also laborious and time-consuming. Instead, there is a need for automated and computerized systems capable of solving the problem of multi-class disease classification12.

Early classification of wheat diseases is essential for enhancing agricultural practices and ensuring food security13. Old models, which use statistics or machine learning, do not effectively manage complex patterns within the data. Moreover, deep learning has experienced remarkable growth in recent years, with significant advancements in transformer-based models that have achieved great success in natural language processing and computer vision, due to their expressive abilities to traverse sequential- and spatial data formats14,15. Deep learning has proven to be highly productive across many domains, thereby demonstrating its utility for automated analysis16. In computer vision applications alone, new convolutional and transformer-based models have pushed the state of the art in tasks such as object detection, image recognition, Disease Detection in Sugarcane Leaves, and medical image analysis17,18. For example, models are achieving higher levels of accuracy in Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks with deep learning techniques, providing a foundation for this improvement in machine translation, sentiment analysis, and large-scale language understanding19. Beyond these types of applications, automated aided developments in domains such as precision agriculture, autonomous driving, and industrial automation signal the general robustness and versatility of deep learning methods20. Numerous studies in the literature have demonstrated the efficiency of deep learning models in agriculture21–25. With these proven capabilities, we develop on this knowledge by implementing deep learning to classify wheat leaf disease using a multi-level contrast adjustment and a modified ViT architecture.

Motivated by the above successes, this study proposes applying a modified transformer model to classify wheat diseases from agricultural datasets. This research aims to demonstrate that transformer-based models can achieve better classification accuracy and computational efficiency, thereby providing a more validated and agnostic method for classifying wheat diseases.

This work is significant as its outcome may lead to a revolutionary change in agricultural forecasting, with enhanced precision and scalability of models for wheat forecasting, resulting in better decisions for crop management and resource allocation to improve productivity, thereby contributing to global food security.

Computer Vision (CV) and Machine Learning (ML)-based methods have demonstrated promising results in recent years, enabling the precise detection and classification of diseases. These methods follow a complete cycle, starting with image pre-processing, then extracting deep features, and concluding with classification. Pre-processing of sample images tends to enhance the Region of Interest (RoI) or improve the overall visual quality of the image. Then comes the deep feature extraction step, which involves deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) models for extracting useful features that can be used in the later stages of disease classification. Therefore, this paper presents a multi-level image enhancement technique, followed by the implementation of three variants of ViT models for feature extraction. Related work

Deep learning (DL) has been heavily utilized in the classification of plant diseases, particularly in wheat, due to its significant role in global food security. Numerous studies have employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs), transfer learning, and transformer-based methods to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of disease detection methods.

Some works have principally focused on CNNs of various kinds (modified CNNs) and ResNet. In26, Ruby et al. presented a modified ResNet50 network for disease classification in the wheat leaf dataset. An efficient and timely disease detection and classification method is necessary to accurately identify diseases in wheat leaves, thereby preventing a decline in wheat production quantity and quality. A DL-based approach addresses this challenging task. A Collaborative Generative Adversarial Network (CGAN) is inculcated to discriminate between the same sample input images. A modified ResNet50 architecture is used for model training, which incorporates convolutional, batch normalization, and Leaky ReLU activation functions as modifications. These layers act as feature extractors and discriminators. After experimentation, the highest accuracy rate of 98.44% is obtained on the publicly available wheat leaf dataset. Saraswat et al.27 described a DL and ensemble-based technique for precise disease detection and classification of multi-class wheat diseases. Wheat is a vital crop and is considered a crucial component of global food security. It is also susceptible to various viral and bacterial diseases, which can impact the yield of this crop. It is essential to devise a methodology that promptly saves the crop from disease attacks and ensures its supply. DL is a power tool for automated disease detection and classification. Therefore, the authors of27 have considered five different classes of wheat disease for experimentation and validation purposes. Experimentations show that the described model yields the highest accuracy rate of 98.08% on the wheat leaf dataset compared to the traditional DCNN method. Likewise, Verma et al.28 presented a lightweight CNN (Convolutional Neural Network) model for disease classification in multiple crops, including corn, rice, and wheat. A lightweight CNN model is developed for validation on less-resourced hardware devices and implemented on various crop datasets. After experimentation, an accuracy rate of 97.50% is observed on the wheat dataset. The presented CNN model extracts salient wheat features by incorporating multiple-sized filters at the same level.

While these approaches illustrate the promise of CNNs, they remain limited by their inherent reliance on convolutional kernels, which only exploit local features and struggle to utilize long-distance dependencies.

To overcome the limitations of utilizing CNNs, researchers have turned to ViTs and hybrid models. Zhang et al.29 presented an architecture based on transformers used for image anomaly detection. Previously, models utilized the concepts of autoencoders and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to reconstruct missing parts in an image, which helps extract local features but suffers from problems when applied to issues such as anomaly detection in images. To solve this problem, a transformer-based model is built, which easily extracts long-term dependencies and global features to detect image anomalies. This model is implemented on publicly available datasets for validation, and after experimentation, a 3.1% increase in detection capability is obtained. ViT is used to efficiently extract useful deep features, as Lin et al.30 utilized patch-based methods to preserve the semantic information in an image. The traditional patch-based transformers are less reliable in extracting information on the interrelation between different patches of an image. A novel Efficient Attention Pyramid Transformer (EAPT) is presented to interlink multiple patches of an image and extract correlations. To achieve this, a new Encode-Decode Communication (EDC) block is added to determine the communication information between image patches, thereby obtaining global communication information. Extensive experiments have been conducted to validate this model, demonstrating its performance in tasks such as classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation. Li et al.31 presented a Dual Self-Supervised Multi-Operator Transformation (DSMT-Net) for disease diagnosis from endoscopic images. Pancreatic cancer is the worst kind of cancer, which requires endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) imaging techniques to analyze it. DL-based techniques sometimes misclassify due to the availability of less labeled data and inter-variability issues. To address these problems, DSMT-Net has been developed, which utilizes a multi-operator transformation approach to precisely extract the region of interest from ultrasound images. The model was experimented on 3500 sample images, performing better than many SOTA DL models. Segmentation plays a vital role in accurate disease classification, as demonstrated by Li et al.32, who presented a segmentation-based model for medical image segmentation and classification. The MA-UNet model is presented to enhance the segmentation process, which is comprised of two-stage encoder blocks and multi-scale attention blocks. The model is experimented on three publicly available datasets, achieving better results.

Transfer learning and hybrid approaches are also playing a vital role in accurate disease classification in the agriculture sector, as Nanda et al.33 presented an automated model for the classification of Rust disease in wheat crops using DL techniques. Agriculture plays a vital role in boosting the country’s economy and fulfilling the food needs of every citizen26. Wheat crops are heavily consumed worldwide, but they are also vulnerable to various diseases, including yellow rust, leaf rust, and stem rust, which can degrade both the quantity and quality of the crop. Therefore, there is a need for an automated system that facilitates accurate disease identification and classification, thereby preventing the decline in crop production. Image processing and ML-based techniques are used in the described methodology to detect and classify diseases in wheat crops. Ni et al.34 proposed AppleNet, a CNN-based model with hybrid attention and BiLSTM for apple leaf disease classification. The model was trained and tested on samples from multiple public datasets, resulting in a 94.66% accuracy rate. This surpasses the accuracy of ResNet18 and other state-of-the-art models. Ablation studies also showed that the modifications were effective. This supports the efficacy of a tailored CNN architecture for plant disease-specific classification; however, it still has limitations in modeling long-range dependencies, which motivates the exploration of transformer-based models, as they are more suitable for modeling global contextual relationships within agricultural images.

Wang et al.35 used the concept of goal-aware transformers for virtual table tennis applications. This model proposes a novel transformer-based architecture, Opponent Temporal Goal Aware Transformer (TOGA-Net), to capture the correlation between local tensors. This model is evaluated on OpenTTGames and Google Table Tennis Robot datasets, and the validation results demonstrate the outperformance of the proposed model. Similar to26, the ResNet-50 model is employed for deep feature extraction, yielding an accuracy rate of 98.50% after experimentation on the wheat leaf dataset. Avanija et al.36 applied different Transfer Learning (TL) models for disease classification in wheat crops. To tackle this problem regarding the misclassification of diseases in wheat crops, different pre-trained DCNN models, such as Visual Geometry Group-16 (VGG-16), are used. The Visual Geometry Group-19 (VGG-19), Residual Network (ResNet), and other models are tested on a wheat dataset to detect multiple diseases, where VGG-19 achieves the highest accuracy of 95.05%, outperforming the others. ViT is used for generalized paintings, as Lee et al.37 presented a model based on ViT using the batch-based technique for painting. A model is presented to produce a generalized painting using patches and a ViT. By using this technique, missing parts in the painting could be reconstructed, and the model is given the name PIPformer, which achieved better performance in terms of PSNR, RMSE, and SSIM.

According to the reviewed literature, it is evident that ML-based methodologies achieve high classification accuracies and precision rates. However, certain challenges arise during the data acquisition process. For instance, due to limited data availability and imbalanced classes in datasets, the sample images are often of low quality and contain noise, which reduces the training accuracy of DCNN models and thereby leads to misclassification during the testing phase38. Additionally, conventional CNN models rely on convolutional kernels, which effectively extract local-level features from input data but struggle to capture global correlations between features39. In contrast, ViTs utilize the self-attention mechanism to identify global dependencies among all image patches, regardless of spatial distance.

To overcome these limitations, this study presents a methodology that incorporates dual contrast enhancement for image quality improvement, augmentative data for class balancing, and enhanced ViTs that represent local and global data differently. This method directly addresses the limitations of both conventional CNNs and ViTs, providing a novel framework for reliable and scalable multi-class wheat disease recognition. This methodology utilizes modified ViTs for training, employs data augmentation to mitigate class imbalance, and incorporates dual contrast enhancement techniques to enhance the visual quality of input images.

Although a lot of good research has been done on classifying plant diseases, most methods struggle with two main things: (1) a lack of robust pre-processing techniques tailored for challenging conditions in field images, and (2) an underutilization of advanced architectures like ViT for this specific task. To address these limitations, this study proposes a novel framework integrating a multi-level contrast enhancement technique with an ensemble of customized ViT models. The detailed architecture of this proposed model is presented in the following section.

Proposed model

This section presents the proposed methodology for classifying diseases in publicly available wheat leaf images from Kaggle. Then, a multi-level contrast enhancement technique is presented, which involves two levels of contrast adjustments as needed. The enhanced images are then processed by three ViT variants: a standard ViT-16-Tiny, a modified ViT with seven transformer layers, and a further modified ViT with seven transformer layers and skip connections. The dataset is split into 70% for training and 30% for testing to validate the model’s performance. After feature extraction, Neural Network (NN) and support vector machine (SVM)- based classifiers are utilized to evaluate the performance of the proposed model. Datasets

For experimentation, four publicly available datasets are utilized: wheat leaves40, wheat diseases41, cotton leaf diseases42, and cotton plant disease43. The data was prepared for training by splitting the combined dataset into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing. The split is stratified to keep the same class ratios in each subset. The wheat dataset comprises 4,086 RGB sample images with a resolution of 256 × 256, featuring three classes related to the wheat leaves dataset: healthy, septoria, and stripe rust, as well as three classes related to the wheat disease dataset: brown rust, healthy, and yellow rust. Different augmentation techniques resolve the class imbalance problem, including image rotation at various angles and flipping. The cotton dataset comprises eight classes and 4107 images. Classes involve aphids, armyworm, bacterial blight, curl virus, fusarium wilt, healthy, powdery mildew, and target spot. Contrast enhancement

Contrast enhancement is a process that aims to improve the visibility of an image by adjusting contrast. It is a necessary process that is used in multiple applications like medical imaging44, remote sensing45, and other CV-based tasks46. In our model, we first use the Naive contrast stretching algorithm to enhance the low-contrast input images. Then, the reduced haze technique minimizes the effects of fog and pollution on the environment before feeding them to the proposed network-level fused model for training and testing purposes.

Naive contrast stretching

The first stage of the multi-level contrast enhancement framework proposed herein is a straightforward contrast stretching operation, referred to sometimes as a naive approach. This step is then followed by mapping each pixel’s intensity values to maximally enhance the visual features of the image47. The process begins by finding the maximum and minimum intensities of an image. The Naive contrast stretching algorithm used for contrast enhancement involves techniques that update each pixel’s intensity levels to improve an image’s overall visual features48. The process consists of reading a specific image and determining the maximum and minimum pixel intensity values within it49. Subsequently, a mapping operation is applied, where the original pixels are scaled to a new range of [0, 255] in an 8-bit image. The pixel values of the original image are updated with those of a new one, and an enhanced image is obtained as output, as shown in Fig. 3. This paper applies multi-scale contrast enhancement to preprocess the data, such as the Naive contrast stretching and the reduce haze technique. This enhancement highlights various subtle features in the wheat crop dataset to enhance its utility for subsequent analysis.

Multi-level contrast enhancement is the first phase of our work, which adjusts the contrast of the dataset at different scales to extract essential features that would otherwise go unnoticed due to confounding factors. This technique becomes particularly useful in complex agricultural datasets, where variations in soil texture, vegetation, and environmental conditions can mask critical patterns. The contrast enhancement method we adopted is based on histogram equalization, which enhances the global contrast of the data to improve the classification of wheat features into specific classes, such as growth stages or yields. Although contrast stretching enhances the global intensity level distribution, it may still fail to recover fine structure in the presence of haze and/or low-light conditions. Therefore, we do a second processing with the Re-Haze to enhance contrast at a local, structural level. Using both contrast stretching and Re-Haze ensures global intensity normalization and recovery of fine structure, resulting in images that provide greater clarity for downstream disease classification.

To mitigate the effects of atmospheric interference, we also incorporate haze reduction techniques to enhance the quality of image-based data. The common problem of haze often occurs in remote sensing images of agricultural fields, as it obscures key details that may be crucial for precise characterization and analysis. The haze reduction technique is based on a particular dark channel prior method (or an appropriate alternative method), which restores true colors and image clarity, thereby ensuring that the features used for classification are as visible as possible.

The main objective of the ViT model is to learn the mapping of image patch patterns to their semantic labels. The ViT model architecture is based on a vanilla Transformer37 with the encoder-decoder architecture that can produce output (or predictions) in parallel from sequentially ordered data without using recurrent networks. The key differentiator of transformer models is the utilization of the self-attention mechanism that learns the relationships among the elements of long-range sequential data. The strength of ViT lies in its attention-based mechanism, which enables ViTs to learn different regions of an image and subsequently concatenate information learned from entire image sequences. The ViT model features a generic architecture comprising multiple layers, including an embedding layer, an encoder, and a classification layer. The created patches of images are taken as input to the encoder, but are first linearly mapped into a k-dimensional vector space with the learned embedded matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F$$\end{document}. The embedded matrices are concatenated with learnable classification tokens \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{class},$$\end{document}, which are used to perform the classification tasks. The transformer treats the embedded patches as an unordered collection of patches based on the ordering of the patches. To preserve the spatial information of the patch sequences, the data of the patch positions is encoded and attached to the representation of the patches. The resulting sequence of the embedded patches with the token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{o,}\:$$\end{document}is given by Eq. 3:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x_o}=\left[ {{w_{class}};~{a_1}F;{a_2}F; \ldots ;{a_m}F} \right]+{F_{pos}};F \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{\left( {{p^2}d} \right) \times k}},{F_{pos}} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{\left( {m+1} \right) \times k}}$$\end{document}

According to55, 1D and 2D positional encoding schemes produce similar results. Therefore, the 1-D positional encoder maintains the information regarding flattening patch positions.

The resultant patch sequence of embedded images \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{o}$$\end{document} is fed to the transformer encoder. The architecture of the encoder consists of identical layers I, where each layer has two further sub-components: the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) block and a fully connected feed-forward dense block, also known as a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The MLP block comprises two dense layers and a GeLU activation function. Both sub-components of the transformer encoder utilize skip connections, which are followed by a Layer Normalization (LN) layer, as shown in Eqs. 4 and 5.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{f}^{\prime }=MSA\left( {LN\left( {{x_{f - 1}}} \right)} \right)+{x_{f - 1}},~f=1, \ldots ,I$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x_f}=MLP\left( {LN\left( {x_{f}^{\prime }} \right)} \right)+x_{f}^{\prime },~f=1, \ldots ,I$$\end{document}

In the encoder architecture, the first element of the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{I}^{o}$$\end{document} is taken from the last layer and fed to the classifier for label classification, as shown in Eq. 6.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b=LN\left( {x_{I}^{o}} \right)$$\end{document}

MSA is a central part of the transformer encoder, determining the importance of the individual image patches relative to other embedded patches in the created sequence. This MSA block consists of four layers: a linear layer, a self-attention layer, a concatenation layer that combines the outputs of multiple attention classifier heads, and a final linear layer. Attention at a higher level can be defined as the attention weights, which are the sum of all the weight values of sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x$$\end{document}. The head of the Self-Attention (SA) block computes the query-key-values by dot product to learn the weights19. In the sequence of image patches, three values are generated, namely C (query), E (key), and Y (value), which are obtained by multiplying the values of three already learned matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{M}_{CEY}$$\end{document}. A dot product is calculated between the vector elements of C and E to find a relation between the sequence elements. The resultant product gives the importance of the respective image patch, which is then scaled and fed to the softmax layer. The computation of the dot product performed through SA is identical to the standard dot product calculations, but in SA, the key dimension factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{E}$$\end{document} is also computed for scaling. Ultimately, the values of the embedded patch vectors are multiplied by the output of the softmax layer to obtain weights with high attention scores. Results and discussion

In this section, the results of the proposed methodology are presented. For training all three models, hyperparameters were manually tuned, with a learning rate of 0.00025, a momentum of 0.725, 50 epochs, a mini-batch size of 64, and SGD used as the optimizer. The entire experiment was conducted using MATLAB R2023b on a desktop computer with the following configuration: 128 GB of RAM, a 512 GB SSD, and a 12 GB NVIDIA RTX 3060 graphics card.

Results of vit-16-tiny

The ViT-16-Tiny model comprises twelve transformer blocks, 144 layers, 168 connections, and 86 million learnable parameters, as shown in Table 3. The model’s architecture begins with an image input layer of size 384 × 384, which is then passed to the next patch embedding layer to transform the input image into a sequence of embedded patches. Then comes the re-flatten layer, which transforms the patch embeddings back into a spatial format. Before the first transformer block starts, there is an addition layer, which is a sum of embedding, concatenation, and a position-embedded layer. The embedding concatenation layer acts as an adder, combining embeddings from various sources, including position embeddings. Following the addition, the first transformer block is introduced, comprising a dropout layer, a normalization layer, a self-attention layer, and another dropout layer. The addition layer is used to concatenate the output of the main dropout layer with the dropout layer 1. Then comes a sequence of layers, starting with the normalization layer 2, followed by convolution 1, the GeLU activation function, the dropout layer 2, convolution 2, and another dropout layer. Again, an additional layer concatenates the output from the second dropout with the direct skip connection. In this way, the architecture of twelve-block transformers is completed with the same specifications and sequence of layers as the first transformer block. Finally, the normalization layer is followed by the indexing layer, which is used for extracting specific embeddings or patches. Then comes the fully connected softmax and classification layer. The complete architecture of ViT is given in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5Structure of the ViT-16-Tiny model.

Structure of the ViT-16-Tiny model.

In this work, we utilized ViTs as feature extractors, capturing deep features from them, and then classified these features using a set of ten existing, conventional machine learning classifiers, including SVM and Neural Networks. Although it is common practice to perform end-to-end training with an MLP classification, we opted for a two-stage approach for several reasons. First, agricultural disease datasets are small and have a degree of class imbalance, which can influence the generalization capacity of end-to-end deep models. The decoupling of feature extraction and classification made it possible for classifiers that are more appropriate or sensitive to small or medium-sized datasets to utilize the robust deep features presented by ViTs. Second, through the use of multiple classifiers, we could have wider comparative studies and demonstrate that ViTs extract features that are robust to different classification paradigms. Lastly, the design of using a feature extractor followed by a lightweight classifier provides flexibility in deployment, as the ViT-based feature extractor can serve as a backbone in various agricultural systems tailored to different real-world application needs.

To achieve an optimal balance between model complexity and performance, we selected seven transformer encoder blocks. In early experiments, we found this number of layers to be the best balance in terms of final accuracy, using computational resources more effectively than other optimizers’ more shallow (fewer blocks) and deeper (more blocks) variations. We also replace the more traditional skip connections, commonly found in conventional transformers, with convolutional layers. We made this change because we wanted to isolate some aspects of local spatial dependencies that the skip connections found in ViT do not provide. Convolutional layers enhance the locality of features, while transformer layers capture long-range dependencies, resulting in a hybrid architecture that offers greater efficiency and higher accuracy for disease classification tasks.

Discussion of results

A discussion has been conducted in this section. This article utilizes three different variants of the ViT. The first variant of ViT is a pre-trained ViT-16-Tiny with twelve transformers. Pre-trained ViT achieved the highest accuracy rate of 97.50% with a prediction time of 26.56 s on the Wide Neural Network classifier, as shown in Table 3. ViT-16-Tiny has 144 layers with 168 connections and a total of 86 million learnable parameters, as given in Table 3. The second proposed variant of ViT comprises seven transformers with skip connections. After experimentation, the model achieves a 97.90% accuracy rate with a prediction time of 8.28 s. The seven-transformer block model comprises 88 layers, 102 connections, and 51.4 million learnable parameters. The third proposed model used for data training is a modified version of ViT with seven transformers. Instead of skip connections, the convolution and resize layers are used in every skip connection. The proposed modified ViT gave the highest accuracy rate of 98.90% with a prediction time of 8.09 s on the cubic SVM classifier, as shown in Table 5. The proposed model comprises 102 layers, 116 connections, and 53.9 million learnable parameters. The parameter table and the performance measure table for each model indicate that the proposed modified ViT outperforms other models in terms of classification accuracy, time, precision, sensitivity, and total number of learnables. The proposed and pre-trained models are trained using 50 epochs. Figure 11 illustrates the validation accuracy at each epoch, showing that the proposed modified ViT model achieves a validation accuracy of 99.27%. A validation accuracy of 97.87% is achieved on ViT with seven transformers and skip connections. 87.68% validation accuracy is obtained by utilizing a pre-trained ViT-16-Tiny model.

After analyzing the results and comparing them with SOTA models, it is observed that the proposed modified ViT performed better in terms of precision and depth. This was possible due to the insertion of a convolutional layer instead of a skip connection in every encoder block; the convolutional layer is responsible for extracting complex local patterns, such as textures and edges, which overcomes the problem of spatial bias in ViT with skip connections. The proposed model is more generalized due to its regularized learning strategy, which also reduces the problem of overfitting. Adding a convolutional layer also enhances the model’s learning capabilities by allowing it to extract low-level features. Hence, it has been demonstrated that a hybrid technique combining ViT with a convolutional layer can enhance the model’s robustness and adaptability. ViT with skip connections is prone to vanishing gradient problems, whereas a convolutional layer can effectively reduce this issue by propagating features across layers. Alongside the advantages of ViT, there are a few limitations, including the requirement for a substantial amount of labeled data to outperform CNN models. Due to the self-attention mechanism of ViT, computational complexity also increases, which can result in the overlook of local patterns and a potential lack of spatial information.

Efficiency and computational cost

In addition to examining accuracy, we also evaluated the efficiency of the proposed model in terms of the computational cost it entails. All experiments were run on an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU (12 GB of memory). On average, the training time required per epoch is ~ 5 min, and the model converged after 45 epochs, resulting in a total training time of ~ 3.8 h. The inference time per image was ~ 10ms, indicating that the proposed model is adequate for real-time classification.

Transformer-based architectures, when compared to a CNN-based baseline, require more parameters and training time. The advantage of a transformer-based architecture is that, although there is an increment in training overhead, the richer feature representation enables better performance and increased accuracy while learning to classify. For example, while CNNs train and consume GPU resources faster than the proposed transformer, their performance plateaus with lower accuracy. The modified transformer architecture achieved greater generalization and robustness; however, to be fair, it did come at a higher cost, particularly in cross-dataset evaluations (e.g., cotton).

These observations summarize the trade-off between efficiency and performance. CNN architectures remain computationally lightweight, but there are added advantages in performance when using transformers in complex agricultural disease classifications. Overall, we determined that a transformer-based architecture offers a good trade-off between accuracy and scalability for efficient agricultural disease classification.

Dataset expansion and robustness analysis

The diversity and representativeness of the training data usually limit the efficacy of deep learning models for plant disease classification. This study concentrated on wheat leaf disease datasets. We applied significant augmentation and multi-scale contrast enhancement techniques to simulate diverse field conditions and enhance generalization. The augmentation also enables the model to handle variations in illumination, texture, or noise more effectively, which are commonly found in real-world agricultural fields. For generalization, two other crop datasets, namely cotton leaf datasets, are used. After experimentation, the good classification results obtained with a smaller number of parameters demonstrate the generalizability and robustness of the proposed models.

We also recognize the advantage of further enhancing the applicability of the proposed model by utilizing more heterogeneous wheat disease datasets, particularly those collected from field settings under critical climatic and geographic conditions. Lastly, domain adaptation and few-shot learning methods may be suitable approaches to overcome dataset weaknesses and reduce reliance on a large amount of labeled data. Our future research will focus on enhancing the scalability and robustness of the model in broader agricultural domains.

Conclusion and future work

The paper focuses on implementing different ViTs variants for disease detection and classification in wheat crops. It also includes a hybrid technique for enhancing the visual quality of input data, which involves a Naïve algorithm and a reduced haze technique to improve the image’s contrast, making the Region of Interest (RoI), i.e., the diseased part, more prominent. Later on, three ViT models with different specifications are deployed on the enhanced dataset. The pre-trained ViT-16-Tiny achieved the highest accuracy rate of 97.5% using the WNN classifier. ViT-7 with skip connections yielded an accuracy rate of 97.9% on the WNN classifier, and the modified ViT-7 achieved an accuracy rate of 98.90% on the MNN classifier. Experimentations have shown that modified ViT is a lightweight model with seven transformers, a depth of 102, and 53.9 million total parameters, but it gave better accuracy than other SOTA models. The model will be reduced in terms of the number of learnable parameters and deployed on multiple crop datasets, such as cotton, corn, and fruits, to validate its performance. It will also be fine-tuned to accommodate different data types.
