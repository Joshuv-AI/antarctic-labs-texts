Awareness, Perception, and Knowledge about Oral Microbiota among College of Dentistry Students in Gulf Medical University/United Arab Emirates
Authors: Sura A. A. F. Al Bayati, Sarah A. Salim, Youser H. Al Maged, Zahraa M. Jawad, Fawz A. Ismael
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:The human oral microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining oral and systemic health. Dysbiosis has been implicated in diseases such as periodontitis, diabetes, cardiovascular conditions, and cancers.Aims:This study aimed to evaluate awareness, perception, and knowledge of oral microbiota and its systemic implications among clinical dental students at Gulf Medical University (GMU), UAE.Settings and Design:A descriptive cross-sectional study involving 150 clinical dental students from the third, fourth, and fifth years of the Bachelor of Dental Surgery program.Methods and Materials:A validated, anonymous questionnaire (24 multiple-choice items) was distributed electronically. The survey assessed participants’ demographics, oral hygiene practices, perception of oral microbiota, and its relation to systemic health.Statistical Analysis Used:Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency and percentages) in Microsoft Excel.Results:Among respondents, 84% recognized the oral cavity as a habitat for diverse microbes, and 88% agreed that microbial imbalance leads to oral diseases. Awareness of the oral microbiota’s systemic links was high (87.3%), particularly with diabetes (70.7%) and cardiovascular diseases (48.7%). However, only 19.3% linked oral microbiota monitoring with early cancer detection. While 72% brushed twice daily, only 34.7% used probiotics.Conclusion:GMU dental students demonstrated substantial awareness of the oral microbiota’s role in oral and systemic health. However, gaps exist in advanced knowledge areas such as diagnostic microbiota testing and therapeutic interventions, indicating a need for curriculum enhancement.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the study of human microbiota has undergone a transformative shift, with increasing emphasis on the role of microbial communities in health and disease.[12] The oral cavity, a complex anatomical environment, supports diverse microenvironments that influence microbial distribution, biofilm formation, and host-microbe interactions.[3] Evidence suggests that the oral microbiota, highly diverse community of over 700 microbial species, may also play a pivotal role in systemic health.[345]

Emerging research links dysbiosis of the oral microbiome with a wide range of chronic systemic conditions, including nephritis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), cancers, cardiovascular diseases, Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, preterm birth, and lung cancer,[346789101112] in addition to the prediction of treatment effectiveness for several malignancies.[4] These associations underscore the importance of recognizing the oral microbiota as a diagnostic and therapeutic target. Furthermore, changes in the oral microbiome are often gradual and detectable, making it a promising candidate for early disease risk screening.[13]

Oral microbiota dysbiosis is increasingly linked to soft tissue oral diseases such as oral candidiasis, lichen planus, leukoplakia, and oral cancer, dental caries, and periodontitis, where microbial imbalance disrupts mucosal integrity and immune regulation.[1415] This imbalance is characterized by a loss of beneficial microbes and overgrowth of pathobionts, which impairs epithelial barriers and alters immunological responses, contributing to disease onset and progression.[151617]

Despite these developments, awareness and knowledge among healthcare professionals, especially in dental education, remain limited. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by exploring the multifaceted roles of oral microbiota in health and systemic disease, contributing to a growing understanding of its clinical and educational significance.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design, sample size, and questionnaire

A cross-sectional study was done among university students who agreed to participate in the study and signed an informed consent. The participants were both genders, from several nationalities, studying in the third, fourth, and fifth-year Bachelor of Dental Surgery Program in Gulf Medical University (GMU). Sample size and sampling were conveniently determined as 150 participants. Data was gathered via a Google sheet. The questionnaire consisted of 24 questions (multiple-choice questions) prepared for dental students of different ages and genders to obtain real-time awareness, perception, and knowledge about oral microbiota. The first part of the questionnaire is about the participant’s demographic information. The second part was directed toward the oral hygiene practices of the participants, the third part includes awareness and perception of oral microbiota and health outcome, and the fourth part is their knowledge about oral microbiota and systemic diseases. The questionnaire was validated by two dental consultants. Participants were asked to fill in the study anonymously. The responses for each question have been described in actual numbers and percentages.

Data collection

Data was gathered via a Google sheet after the study was approved by the institutional research board of the GMU on February 17, 2025, Ref. no. IRB-COD-STD-88-Feb-2025. The research objectives were explained to the participants, and informed written consent was obtained from each participant before including the participant in the study.

Participants were asked to fill in the questionnaire anonymously. The responses for each question have been described in actual numbers and percentages.

RESULTS

A total of 150 clinical dental students from GMU, spanning the third, fourth, and fifth years of study, participated in this research. The cohort comprised 60% female (n = 90) and 40% male (n = 60) students, reflecting the prevailing gender distribution within the university’s dental program. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 30 years. The majority were fourth-year students (50%, n = 75), followed by third-year (30%, n = 45) and fifth-year students (20%, n = 30), allowing for a representative perspective across various stages of clinical education.

In terms of awareness and perception related to the oral microbiota, 84% of participants recognized the oral cavity as a site of diverse microbial communities, while 88% acknowledged that microbial imbalance (dysbiosis) contributes to oral diseases. Furthermore, 87.3% understood the impact of oral microbiota on systemic health, and 70.7% were specifically aware of its association with conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. Additionally, 86.7% believed that dietary habits influence oral microbial composition, and 71.3% supported incorporating oral microbiota testing into routine dental check-ups, as summarized in Table 1.

Awareness and perception of oral microbiota

With respect to oral hygiene practices and their perceived impact on oral microbiota, 72% of students reported brushing their teeth twice daily, whereas a small minority of 3.3% admitted to brushing infrequently. Mouthwash was incorporated into the oral hygiene routine by 58% of participants, while 42% did not report its regular use. Flossing practices demonstrated moderate adherence, with 56.6% engaging in regular flossing and 43.3% indicating they did not floss. Notably, only 34.7% of students had used probiotics for oral health purposes, highlighting limited exposure to emerging microbiota-modulating interventions.

Dietary behavior revealed that 44.7% of respondents occasionally consumed sugary or acidic foods, whereas 13.3% reported rare intake, reflecting a degree of dietary awareness. Furthermore, a significant majority of 89.9% recognized the importance of regular dental check-ups in supporting a balanced oral microbiota, while 10.7% did not consider such visits essential. Oral hygiene practices and its impact on oral microbiota

Most participants, 86.7%, acknowledged that poor oral hygiene could negatively affect overall health. In relation to specific systemic conditions, 51.3% of respondents identified a potential link between poor oral hygiene and cancer, while 48.7% recognized its association with cardiovascular diseases. However, awareness of connections to rheumatoid arthritis 20.7% and Alzheimer’s disease 12.7% was comparatively lower. Additionally, 64% of participants were aware that oral microbiota imbalance might contribute to the development of oral cancers.

Lifestyle factors were also widely recognized as influential. Notably, 78% of respondents reported that psychological stress and insufficient sleep could adversely affect oral microbial balance, and 88% believed that smoking or tobacco use significantly disrupts oral microbiota. Furthermore, nearly all students, 94.7%, expressed concern that antimicrobial resistance could complicate the treatment of microbiota-related infections. Awareness of emerging interventions was moderate, with 67.3% of participants reporting familiarity with oral microbiota transplants as a potential treatment modality for oral diseases, as outlined in Table 3.

Oral microbiota and health outcomes

Most participants, 67.3%, demonstrated awareness of the association between oral microbiota dysbiosis and systemic conditions such as IBD and rheumatoid arthritis. A greater proportion, 87.3%, acknowledged that maintaining a balanced oral microbiota may reduce the risk of systemic infections and inflammatory responses. However, awareness of the potential role of oral microbiota monitoring in the early detection of oral and systemic cancers was limited, with only 19.3% recognizing this possibility. Slightly more than half of the respondents, 58%, reported having encountered information regarding the oral microbiota–cancer link through media sources or healthcare professionals. Encouragingly, 80.7% believed that oral microbiota-targeted interventions could contribute to cancer prevention or therapy. Furthermore, 89.3% agreed that addressing oral microbiota imbalances should be integrated into general healthcare planning, indicating strong recognition of its significance in overall health management [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

The oral microbiota comprises diverse, dynamic microbial communities that play a crucial role in maintaining oral health. However, when this balance is disrupted, due to factors such as inflammation or high sugar intake, it can contribute to diseases like dental caries and periodontitis. These conditions arise from dysbiosis, where the normal microbial equilibrium is disturbed. As highlighted by Lamont et al.,[17] recent advances in microbiome research have deepened our understanding of microbial interactions and are guiding the development of novel treatment strategies for oral diseases. In our study, most dental students at GMU demonstrated a strong understanding of the role of oral microbiota in both oral and systemic health, likely due to their clinical exposure and academic training in microbiology. This aligns with Lamont et al.[17] who emphasizes the consequences of microbial imbalance in the oral cavity. Similarly, 87.3% of students acknowledged that oral microbiota impacts general health, and 70.7% were aware of its association with conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. These findings are consistent with previous literature, including Peng et al.,[18] who noted that the oral microbiota reflects interactions between the body and its environment and can serve as an early indicator of disease.

Regarding the relationship between oral hygiene and systemic conditions, many participants in our study recognized that poor oral hygiene may contribute to illnesses like cancer and heart disease. This awareness is reinforced by Peng et al.,[18] who reported strong links between oral microbiota and several systemic diseases, including IBD, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer’s disease. Their work supports the growing call for integrating oral-systemic health knowledge into healthcare education, enabling future dental professionals to better appreciate the broader implications of oral hygiene.

When evaluating students’ oral hygiene habits, the majority reported brushing twice daily 72%, with over half using mouthwash 58%, and flossing regularly 56.6%. These findings reflect a baseline awareness among dental students regarding preventive practices. However, only 34.7% reported using oral probiotics, highlighting a knowledge gap about newer interventions. This supports the need to enhance education on advanced oral care strategies. Chhalliyil et al.[19] similarly demonstrated that different oral cleaning methods, including brushing, tongue cleaning, and gum and tooth rubbing with index finger and tongue cleaning and water swishing (GIFTS) method, significantly influenced the oral microbiome, underscoring the importance of consistent and effective hygiene in microbial management and disease prevention.

Further evidence confirms the connection between oral microbiota and systemic diseases through their role in modulating inflammation. Notably, oral microbes are linked to conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, Alzheimer’s, IBD, and rheumatoid arthritis. The fungus Candida albicans—normally regulated by the oral microbiome—can become opportunistic in cases of immune suppression or microbial imbalance. When combined with certain bacteria, C. albicans can disturb microbial balance and cause tissue damage. Although its exact role is not yet fully understood, its contribution to dysbiosis and systemic disease progression is increasingly recognized.[1518]

CONCLUSION

This study revealed that most dental students possessed a solid understanding of the oral microbiota and its relationship to both oral and systemic health. Participants also recognized the critical influence of oral hygiene in maintaining microbial balance and preventing disease. However, certain gaps remain, particularly in awareness of emerging topics like probiotics and the broader implications of microbiota-related dysbiosis.
