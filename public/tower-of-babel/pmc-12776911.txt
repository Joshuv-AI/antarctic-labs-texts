Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimSepsis is a major health problem that causes the death of the majority of those affected. Early determination of mortality is essential to provide timely and adequate interventions. We investigated the efficacy of various prognostic indicators for the prediction of mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) sepsis patients.Patients and methodsSepsis patients admitted to the ICU of our hospital between March 15, 2023 and January 01, 2024, were included in this study. These were divided into two groups: Survivors and nonsurvivors. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data of the groups were compared and their efficacy in predicting mortality was investigated.ResultsOf the 115 patients in our study, 58 (50.4%) were in the nonsurvivor group and 57 (49.6%) were in the survivor group. Highlights

Sepsis is a serious clinical condition with a high mortality rate. It is important to predict mortality in sepsis patients for optimal management. Our study revealed that combined use of prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and urea-albumin ratio (UAR) is beneficial in the prediction of mortality in sepsis patients.

Introduction

Sepsis represents a complex clinical syndrome characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction triggered by an imbalanced host response to infection.1 Sepsis and septic shock continue to pose major global health threats, contributing to substantial mortality despite advances in modern medicine.2 Owing to its high incidence and lethality, early recognition and intervention remain essential to improving survival rates.3 Identification of patients with high mortality risk and accurate prediction of outcomes in the early stages of sepsis are essential for timely and effective interventions to be performed in these patients.4

Despite notable advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, sepsis still exhibits unacceptably high mortality and unpredictable disease progression. Variability in host immune response, comorbidities, and pathophysiological mechanisms lead to diverse clinical trajectories and outcomes in sepsis. Consequently, recognizing and characterizing prognostic markers associated with mortality is fundamental for optimizing management and reducing sepsis-related deaths.4

Prognostic indicators used to predict disease course in sepsis patients will positively affect patient management and contribute to the reduction of mortality. Several established scoring systems, including sequential organ failure assessment score (SOFA), quick sequential organ failure assessment score (qSOFA), and acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE-II), are routinely employed to estimate disease severity and mortality risk in sepsis. However, they may not give adequate information in some patients because they are not effective in the evaluation of the nutritional status of the patients. Hence, there is an increasing need for novel mortality predictors that integrate nutritional and inflammatory parameters in sepsis assessment.

The PNI and UAR are novel prognostic indicators which were found to be beneficial for predicting negative outcomes in critical health conditions such as sepsis. These indicators jointly mirror the patient's nutritional reserve and systemic inflammatory burden; maintaining optimal nutritional status may thus mitigate mortality risk in sepsis.5,6

The present study sought to evaluate several laboratory-derived prognostic markers and to determine whether combining PNI and UAR could enhance mortality prediction in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with sepsis.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This prospective study included patients who were diagnosed with sepsis and treated in the ICU of our institution between March 15, 2023 and January 1, 2024. Participants were categorized into two subgroups based on their survival status: Survivors and nonsurvivors. The study evaluated the predictive capacity of multiple prognostic markers for ICU mortality.

Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised adult ICU patients diagnosed with sepsis as defined by the sepsis—III consensus during the specified study period.1

Power Analysis

The statistical power analysis was conducted by G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). A sensitivity analysis was conducted for a two-sided independent-samples t-test with α = 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80. Given the available sample sizes (n1 = 57, n2 = 58), the minimum detectable effect size was d = 0.53.

Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were performed using MedCalc software (demo version 19.0.7, MedCalc Software bvba, Ostend, Belgium). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test assessed the normality of data distribution. Homogenously distributed variables were reported as mean (M) ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Nonhomogenously distributed parameters were summarized as median (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages and evaluated with the Chi-square test. Bivariate Spearman's correlation analysis test was used to determine the direction and strength of the relationship between prognostic indicators and mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was applied to assess the discriminative power of each prognostic marker for mortality prediction. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each indicator, and the standard error and 95% confidence interval (CI) were determined. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated in accordance with the ROC curve. Independent mortality predictors were identified through multivariate logistic regression modeling. Variables were selected for multivariate logistic regression analysis with the enter method. Model 1 included UAR alone, model 2 included PNI alone, and model 3 included PNI and UAR together in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were calculated for each independent variable. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used for multicollinearity evaluation. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05.

Ethical Standards

The present study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Bolu Abant Izzet Baysal University Faculty of Medicine (Date: March 14, 2023, Decision Number: 2023/53). Informed consents were taken from the patients or their legal guardians.

Results

A total of 164 ICU patients meeting sepsis-III diagnostic criteria were initially screened during the study period. After applying the predefined exclusion criteria, 44 patients were removed from the analysis. In addition, 5 patients who were still hospitalized in the ICU at the end of the study were also excluded. Consequently, 115 eligible patients were included in the final evaluation. Patient ages varied between 21 and 95 years, with an M ± SD of 77.6 ± 11.8. The cohort consisted of 57 females (49.6%) and 58 males (50.4%). The median ICU stay was 9 days (IQR: 4–17). The most common comorbidities in the patients enrolled in the study were hypertension (HT) (67%), cardiovascular disease (CVD) (52%), diabetes mellitus (DM) (41%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (22%), and chronic renal failure (CRF) (20%). The number of patients without any disease diagnosis was 13 (11%).

When the infectious conditions of the patients included in the study that caused hospitalization in the ICU were analyzed, the foci of infection in order of frequency were as follows: 43.4% respiratory system infection, 20% urinary system infection, 4.3% gastrointestinal system infection, 4.3% skin-soft tissue infection, 2.6% catheter infection, 0.86% cardiac infection, and 26.9% multiple infection foci. The most frequently isolated causative pathogens were Gram-negative bacteria (24.3%) and polymicrobial pathogens (24.3%). This was followed by Gram-positive bacteria (9.5%), fungal (9%), and viral (3%) pathogens, respectively. No pathogen could be isolated in 31.3% of the patients. Vasopressors were required in 42% of the patients, and invasive mechanical ventilation was required in 56.5%.

We classified our study population into two subgroups as deceased and survivors according to ICU mortality. Fifty-seven patients were in the survived group and 58 were in the deceased group. No significant intergroup differences were found in age, sex distribution, ICU stay duration, comorbidities, or vasopressor use (p > 0.05). However, CRF comorbidity was found to be more common in the deceased patient group compared to the surviving patient group (p < 0.05). Invasive mechanical ventilation was required significantly more often among nonsurvivors than survivors (p < 0.05).

When the prognostic indicators that may be related with mortality were analyzed, the indicators with significant differences were lactate/albumin ratio, UAR, lactate dehydrogenase-albumin ratio (LAR), D-dimer-albumin ratio (DAR), and PNI (p < 0.05 for all). On the other hand, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of glucose/potassium ratio, fibrinogen-albumin ratio (FAR), C-reactive protein-albumin ratio (CAR), procalcitonin-albumin ratio (PAR), systemic-inflammatory index (SII), multi-inflammatory index (MII), platelet mass index (PMI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), mean platelet volume-to-platelet ratio (MPR), and mean platelet volume-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) (p > 0.05 for all). Discussion

In this investigation evaluating mortality-associated parameters among ICU patients with sepsis, both the PNI and the UAR were identified as valuable predictors of patient outcomes. Moreover, integrating PNI and UAR within a combined analytical model improved predictive accuracy compared with their separate evaluation. These results indicate that composite models incorporating multiple prognostic markers may provide a more robust assessment of mortality risk in sepsis.

Scoring tools designed for the diagnosis and clinical assessment of sepsis play a pivotal role in guiding therapeutic decision-making. The SOFA score remains a benchmark for evaluating organ dysfunction and defining sepsis, whereas the qSOFA serves as a simpler and more pragmatic bedside alternative, particularly suited for emergency settings.1 Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that qSOFA demonstrates limited sensitivity but moderate specificity in mortality prediction.7 Similarly, a prospective cohort study by Askim et al. confirmed the inadequate sensitivity of qSOFA in forecasting short-term mortality (7–30 days) among emergency department sepsis patients.8 Consistent with previous findings, our analysis showed that qSOFA failed to reach statistical significance in distinguishing survivors from nonsurvivors (p = 0.601). Collectively, these data emphasize that qSOFA alone may not be sufficient either for sepsis diagnosis or for accurate mortality risk estimation. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that the mortality rate increased by 1.8–3.3% with a one-point increase in the mean SOFA score.9 In the present cohort, higher SOFA scores correlated significantly with mortality risk (p = 0.001). However, in clinical practice, the SOFA score can be cumbersome to compute in emergency or ICU environments because of its complexity and the numerous variables it requires. Conversely, while qSOFA offers greater practicality and rapid calculation, it may underestimate mortality risk in certain patient subgroups. Therefore, the identification of simple, easily obtainable, and reliable mortality predictors remains a clinical priority in sepsis research.

The PNI serves as a composite biomarker integrating serum albumin concentration and lymphocyte count to reflect both nutritional reserve and immune competence. Previous reports consistently demonstrate that elevated PNI levels are linked with lower mortality risk among patients with sepsis. Conversely, decreased PNI values reflect malnutrition and systemic inflammation that compromise immune function, leading to higher mortality susceptibility.6 Wu et al. reported that a PNI threshold ≥29.3 independently predicted 30 days survival and reduced the likelihood of death among sepsis patients. In their study, the AUC obtained for PNI was calculated as 0.6436 (95% CI: 0.6204–0.6625).10 Xie et al., in a large-scale cohort involving 1,238 sepsis patients, identified PNI as a promising biomarker for forecasting sepsis-related acute kidney injury.11 Shimayoma and colleagues reported that PNI yielded an AUC of 0.730 in predicting 28 days mortality, serving as an independent prognostic determinant with 70% sensitivity and 80% specificity.12 In agreement with these findings, PNI exhibited the strongest diagnostic performance in our dataset (AUC = 0.704; optimal cut-off ≤37.4; sensitivity 86.2%; specificity 49.1%). Multivariate logistic regression analysis yielded an OR of 0.8867 (95% CI: 0.8297–0.9476; p < 0.001), confirming PNI as an independent mortality predictor. Taken together, our observations support prior evidence that PNI effectively predicts sepsis-related mortality and can serve as a practical adjunct in clinical prognostication.

Blood urea nitrogen to serum albumin ratio (BAR) and UAR are the other novel prognostic parameters which were found useful in sepsis patients. UAR is a mathematical derivative of BAR, so these parameters are similar. In a retrospective cohort study by Cai et al.13 involving 13,464 patients with sepsis admitted to ICUs, a correlation was found between BAR and in-hospital mortality. High BAR or UAR values indicate a poor prognosis. Protein catabolism rate increases in sepsis patients, which is usually complicated by acute kidney injury. Thus, sepsis patients usually have high blood urea levels. In sepsis patients, renal blood flow significantly decreases leading to renal dysfunction, which is often associated with impaired functioning of the immune system.13 Wang et al. retrospectively analyzed 7,656 patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU and showed that high BAR values were associated with an increased risk of mortality.14 In another study involving 10,578 patients with sepsis, Min et al. found that high BAR values were an independent factor associated with mortality in the ICU.15 In a study including 222 patients with septic shock, Pereira et al. found that UAR had a sensitivity of 47%, specificity of 66%, a positive predictive value of 67%, and a negative predictive value of 45% according to the cut-off point ≥47.25 (AUC = 0.617).16 In our study, UAR ranked second to PNI in predictive strength (AUC = 0.690; cut-off >58.33; sensitivity 36.2%; specificity 93.0%). The strong specificity and diagnostic performance of UAR observed in our cohort indicate its potential as a clinically meaningful mortality biomarker.

In the study of Wu et al., the AUC obtained for PNI for predicting 30 days mortality in sepsis patients was calculated as 0.6436 (95% CI: 0.6204–0.6625).10 In another study, Shimayoma et al. found that PNI has an AUC value of 0.730 for predicting 28 days mortality in sepsis patients with a sensitivity of 70% and specificity of 80%.12 Similar to previous studies, we found an AUC value of 0.704 for PNI for the prediction of in-hospital mortality (95% CI: 0.612–0.786). In the study of Pereira et al., they calculated an AUC level of 0.617 (95% CI: 0.541–0.693; p = 0.003) for UAR at the cut-off of ≥47.25 for the prediction of ICU mortality development.16 In agreement with this study, we found an AUC of 0.690 (95% CI: 0.597–0.773) for UAR for the prediction of ICU mortality. Small differences in AUC and UAR levels between previous studies and our study could be related to different patient numbers in the studies.

Multiple regression modeling demonstrated that the combined PNI–UAR model (model 3) outperformed single-parameter models, offering superior mortality discrimination. According to the results of ROC analysis, the AUC of model 3 was higher than the other two models, indicating that model 3 was more accurate and sensitive in predicting mortality. These findings highlight the synergistic contribution of nutritional depletion and inflammatory activation in determining sepsis prognosis. Continuous monitoring of PNI and UAR may thus support early therapeutic adjustments and more accurate prognostic evaluation in clinical settings.

Although our study was prospective, the fact that it was conducted in a limited time period and with a relatively small number of cases was one of the important limitations. In addition, the fact that it was a single-center study can be considered as another handicap. Despite all these, we think that our study is important because it is the most comprehensive study investigating prognostic indicators associated with mortality in our province and one of the few studies in the literature comparing many prognostic indicators.

In ICUs, sepsis and septic shock are among the important health concerns causing high morbidity and mortality. In recent years, although a better understanding of the pathophysiology of sepsis and revision of diagnostic criteria have led to earlier recognition of the disease, mortality rates are still unacceptably high. Therefore, there is a need for practical and economic indicators to determine the prognosis of patients admitted to the ICU and to determine early diagnosis and effective treatment strategies. The discovery of new biomarkers that can be used in the management of sepsis and septic shock will be an important step to improve patient survival.

There are some limitations in our study. First, this study was conducted in a single-center, and its sample size was small. Second, external validation is lacking and there may be some potential selection bias. Thus, our findings cannot reflect the whole population. Validating the combined PNI–UAR model in multicentric cohorts and perhaps integrating it with SOFA or another parameter should be conducted by large multicenter, prospective, and long-term studies with a larger number of patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we suggest using PNI and UAR indicators together which may be a useful tool in the evaluation of mortality risk in sepsis patients hospitalized in the ICU and may contribute to better management of these patients.
