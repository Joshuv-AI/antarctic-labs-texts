Characterization of carcinogenesis resistance in Anatolian blind mole-rat mesenchymal stem cells and assessment of transcriptomic signatures
Authors: Nese Unver, Ece Tavukcuoglu, Betul Budak, Onurcan Sezginer, Suleyman Can Ozturk, Saniye Elvan Ozturk, Ekim Z Taskiran, Teoman Kankilic, Kazim Yalcin Arga, Gunes Esendagli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) exhibit dual roles in tumor progression and therapy resistance. However, the blind mole-rat (BMR, Nannospalax xanthodon) exhibits extraordinary natural resistance to spontaneous and induced carcinogenesis, making its MSCs a subject of significant therapeutic interest. This study investigates BMR MSCs’ and fibroblasts’ resistance to the carcinogen N-methyl-N-nitrosourea (MNU).MethodsWe examined the cellular behavior and transcriptomic expression differences of bone marrow-derived MSCs and also fibroblasts isolated and characterized from blind mole-rats after exposure to the chemical carcinogen MNU. Similar analyses were also performed on mouse, rat, and human-derived MSCs, and expression disparities between species were evaluated.ResultsWhile MNU treatment significantly induced apoptosis and reduced cell viability in human and mouse MSCs, BMR MSCs maintained stable proliferation kinetics, viability, and multilineage differentiation potential. Transcriptomic profiling revealed species-specific gene expression patterns, highlighting unique adaptive responses in the BMR. In BMR fibroblasts, MNU treatment suppressed PI3K/Akt signaling and modulated mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, whereas BMR MSCs demonstrated a flexible signaling architecture that preserved cellular programming and p53-mediated transcriptional regulation. Cross-species analysis integrated with TCGA datasets identified LAMTOR3 as a critical p53-dependent tumor-protective node.ConclusionThe ability of BMR MSCs to maintain the p53-LAMTOR3 axis following MNU treatment, alongside the suppression of pro-tumoral SMAD signaling, suggests a robust evolutionary strategy to inhibit malignant transformation. These findings provide novel insights into the genomic integrity of the BMR and identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for enhancing cancer resistance.

The blind mole-rat (Nannospalax xanthodon) represents a unique natural model for understanding cancer resistance. We show that its mesenchymal stem cells maintain cellular integrity and functional properties following carcinogen exposure. Preservation of the p53-LAMTOR3 axis and suppression of pro-tumoral SMAD signaling emerge as key features of this response. These findings identify a novel molecular basis of cancer resistance and highlight LAMTOR3 as a potential mediator of this phenotype.

Introduction

The blind mole-rat (BMR) serves as an extraordinary model organism for studying cancer resistance. Adapted to extreme subterranean conditions characterized by severe hypoxia and hypercapnia, these rodents possess unique physiological stress tolerance. Despite their exceptional longevity, spontaneous tumors are virtually nonexistent in their natural populations. This suggests that despite the age-related accumulation of somatic mutations, which is a primary risk factor for malignancy in humans, BMRs have evolved robust, multifaceted tumor-suppressive pathways.1 Tumor suppression in the BMR is mediated by distinct modifications to the p53 pathway, a unique extracellular matrix (ECM) composition, and a finely tuned balance between apoptosis and cellular senescence. For example, stress signals that typically induce apoptosis in human fibroblasts instead trigger a massive concerted necrosis or senescence-like mechanism in BMR cells, effectively preventing malignant transformation.2 Additionally, the constitutive overproduction of high-molecular-weight hyaluronan confers resistance within the extracellular milieu and augments antitumor immune responses.3 These mechanisms collectively protect blind mole-rats from oxidative stress and hypoxia-induced genomic instability.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) preferentially migrate toward the tumor microenvironment (TME), where they secrete paracrine factors that stimulate tumor progression. Furthermore, MSCs contribute to tumor recurrence and the development of chemoresistance by supporting the maintenance and expansion of the cancer stem cell (CSC) niche.4 BMR-derived MSCs phenotypically express conventional surface markers, including CD73, CD90, and CD105, while lacking the hematopoietic markers CD34, CD45, and CD11b. Notably, BMR MSCs exhibit superior oxidative stress responses and enhanced DNA repair capabilities compared to other mammalian species, thereby preserving superior genomic integrity.5,6 While maintaining tri-lineage differentiation potential (osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic), these cells also display potent immunomodulatory properties, regulating inflammatory cascades through the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. Characterizing these MSCs is essential to understanding the BMR’s natural cancer resistance and may provide a foundation for novel therapeutic strategies in gerontology and oncology.7,8

In most mammals, MSCs are vulnerable to oncogenic transformation; malignant transformation of bone marrow-derived MSCs is recognized as a significant source of CSCs.9 This transition is often linked to dysregulation in self-renewal and signaling pathways governing proliferation and differentiation. Mesenchymal stem cells exhibiting pro-tumoral phenotypes and aberrant proliferation are primary targets during carcinogenesis due to their inherent plasticity.10 However, it remains to be elucidated whether BMR MSCs, which are isolated from an organism whose fibroblasts and stromal cells are famously resistant to transformation, share this resistant phenotype. Comparative analysis is, therefore, vital to identify the specific cellular checkpoints that prevent BMR MSCs from transitioning into a pro-tumorigenic state.

Given the well-established use of MNU as a DNA-alkylating carcinogen for the induction of experimental mammary tumors,11,12 identifying p53-associated, MNU-responsive genes may provide insight into the molecular basis of cancer resistance in Nannospalax xanthodon. Accordingly, BLM, TXNRD1, LAMTOR3, ELOC, and E2F8, identified through our comparative transcriptomic analysis, were further evaluated in a human breast carcinoma cohort to assess their translational relevance.

In this study, bone marrow-derived MSCs were isolated from N. xanthodon, a BMR species endemic to Türkiye,13 to establish primary cell cultures. Following immunophenotypic and functional characterization, transcriptome profiling was conducted on BMR fibroblasts and MSCs under control conditions and exposure to the chemical carcinogen MNU. These analyses aimed to delineate the molecular signatures of cellular transformation, apoptosis, and proliferation. Through cross-species comparative genomics, we demonstrate a unique genetic signature that governs the BMR’s cellular resilience to chemical oncogenesis.

Methods

Specimen collection and cytogenetic characterization

Specimens of the Anatolian BMR (N. xanthodon) were collected from Ankara province, Türkiye. Taxonomic identification was confirmed through karyotype and morphological analyses (mean body weight: 191.4 g; mean body length: 21 cm) were performed (Figure S1). Living individuals with a diploid (2n = 60, ♀) chromosome number14 were transferred to the Hacettepe University Laboratory Animals Research and Application Center (HUDHAM).

All procedures were conducted under the requisite ethical approvals (Republic of Türkiye, Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, Decision: E-21264211-288.04-4576330; Hacettepe University Animal Experiments Ethics Committee, Decision: 2022/11-04). Karyotype analyses were performed using the colchicine-hypotonic citrate technique following the protocol described by Ford and Hamerton.15

Isolation, expansion, and multilineage differentiation of different species

Bone marrow-derived MSCs were isolated via aspiration from the femurs and tibiae of the same individuals. Human bone marrow-derived MSCs (PCS-500-012) were purchased from American Type Culture Collections. Adherent cells were selected through plastic adherence and expanded in vitro. Bone marrow-derived MSCs from different species were propagated in DMEM LG (Sartorius, 01-050-1A) with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Pen/Strep media at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, under standard sterile conditions. Human MSC cell lines used in this study have been authenticated within the past 3 years using short tandem repeat profiling in accordance with standard quality control procedures. In addition, all experiments were performed exclusively with mycoplasma-free cell cultures, regularly tested using validated detection methods to ensure experimental integrity. Osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic differentiation assays were performed. Carcinogen resistance assays were conducted using mesenchymal stem cells featuring the most passages at passage 5 (P5).

To confirm their identity as MSCs, cells were subjected to tri-lineage differentiation assays. For osteogenic differentiation, the cells were cultured in an osteogenic induction medium (Biological Industries, BI05-442-1B) and verified by Alizarin Red S (Sigma, TMS-008-C) staining of mineralized deposits. For chondrogenic differentiation, the cells were cultured in chondrogenic induction medium (StemPro, A10071-01) and verified by Alcian Blue (Abcam, ab150662) staining of sulfated glycosaminoglycans. For adipogenic differentiation, the cells were cultured in adipogenic induction medium (Biological Industries, BI05-330-1B) and verified by Oil Red O (Abcam, ab150678) staining of intracellular lipid droplets. Surface marker expression was further validated by flow cytometry for the positive stemness markers CD73 (Bioss, bs-4834R) and CD90.2 (Biolegend, 105312). After characterization, the MSCs were used for downstream transcriptomic analyses.

Isolation and characterization of dermal fibroblasts derived from N. xanthodon

Fibroblast cultures were derived from axillary skin tissue biopsies. Tissues were subjected to enzymatic digestion using Liberase (Roche Diagnostics). Isolated dermal fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Sartorius, 01-170-1A) with 15% FBS media and maintained until passages 2-4. Morphological verification was conducted via hematoxylin and eosin and Trichrome staining (Abcam, ab150686). Immunophenotypic characterization was performed via flow cytometry using antibodies against Vimentin (Abcam, ab92547) and CD90.2 (BioLegend, 105312).

Cytotoxicity assay and IC20 determination

To determine the nontoxic dose range for the alkylating agent MNU, 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Serva, 20395.02)16 was performed on N. xanthodon fibroblasts, BMR MSCs, and human, mouse, and rat-MSCs. Cells were seeded at 104 cells well−1 in 96-well plates, and cytotoxicity was measured 24, 48, and 72 h after MNU administration. After MTT incubation (4 h), the reaction was stopped with dimethylformamide/sodium dodecyl sulfate (DMF/SDS) solution, the plates were incubated for 16 h, and absorbance was read at 570 nm. Analyses were performed in triplicate to calculate IC20 values, and MNU-IC20 doses specific to each cell type were used in transcriptomic experiments.

Cell viability analysis

Viability was analyzed by flow cytometry with propidium iodide (PI) and Annexin V dyes (BioLegend, Annexin V-PI kit 640914) to determine cell death and viability after 48 h of MNU incubation with N. xanthodon mesenchymal stem cells, dermal fibroblasts, human mesenchymal stem cells, mouse mesenchymal stem cells, and rat mesenchymal stem cells.

Cell proliferation analysis

Cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dye (BioLegend, 423801; 5 μM) and cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h. At the end of the culture period, proliferation indexes were calculated by dividing the CFSE median fluorescent intensity (MFI) values at 24 h by the MFI values at 24, 48, and 72 h.

Transcriptomic analyses

The responses of N. xanthodon MSCs and fibroblasts to MNU were compared to those of human, mouse, and rat MSCs using RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. Following MNU treatment, RNA isolation (Norgen, 51800) was carried out. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the QIAseq Stranded RNA Library Kit and sequenced on a NextSeq 500/550 platform using the High Output Kit v2.5 (300 cycles).

Gene expression quantification

Sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC. N. xanthodon reads were pseudoaligned to the reference transcriptome of the closely related Nannospalax galili, while human reads were pseudoaligned to the GRCh38, mouse C57BL/6, and rat GRCr8 reference transcriptomes using Kallisto (v0.46.2). Transcript abundances were quantified as transcripts per million, and transcript-level estimates were imported into R using Tximport to generate a count matrix. Gene-level counts were obtained by summarizing transcripts using a transcript-to-gene mapping (tx2gene) derived from the corresponding GTF annotation file.

Differential gene expression analysis

Binary differential expression comparisons were performed for each RNA-Seq dataset by contrasting MNU-treated samples against their matched untreated controls. Specifically, analyses included human MSCs (control N = 2; MNU-treated N = 2), N. xanthodon fibroblasts (control N = 2; MNU-treated N = 2), N. xanthodon MSCs (control N = 4; MNU-treated N = 4), C57BL/6 mouse MSCs (control N = 2; MNU-treated N = 2), and Sprague-Dawley rat MSCs (control N = 2; MNU-treated N = 2). Differential gene expression analysis was performed on Tximport-derived gene-level count data using the DESeq2 R package (v1.46.0), comparing MNU-treated samples against their corresponding controls for each dataset. Due to the time-intensive establishment and characterization of N. xanthodon primary cell cultures and the inherent heterogeneity of MSC populations, only well-characterized cultures exhibiting stable MSC properties and sustained proliferative capacity were included in the analyses. Consequently, the sample size was limited, while rigorous statistical analyses ensured the robustness of the findings. Consequently, the sample size was limited; however, to ensure the robustness of the findings, we applied a conservative DESeq2 workflow in which size factors and genewise dispersions were estimated prior to model fitting, and Wald tests were used for significance testing. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined using an adjusted P < .05 for the human dataset. In the remaining dataset, because the number of DEGs was very limited after multiple testing correction, P < .05 was used as the cutoff for statistical significance.

Pathway enrichment analyses

Pathways and biological processes associated with upregulated and downregulated DEGs were identified using Metascape. Enrichment was evaluated using curated resources including KEGG Pathway, GO Biological Process, Reactome, CORUM, WikiPathways, and PANTHER. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and adjusted P < .01 were considered significant.

Cross-species transcriptomic comparisons

Cross-species comparisons were performed using both one-to-one (1:1) ortholog mapping and pathway-level analyses. Unambiguous 1:1 orthologs across human, mouse, rat, and Nannospalax were retrieved from Ensembl BioMart (via the biomaRt R package) and harmonized using Ensembl gene IDs; where necessary, gene symbols were mapped to Ensembl IDs prior to filtering. Gene-level overlaps and concordance were assessed on this shared 1:1 ortholog set, while pathway enrichment was performed independently within each species and compared across species using adjusted P-values.

The Cancer Genome Atlas data analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) contains data on chromosomal gain or loss, tumor typing, disease status, mRNA expression, mutation count, and information about mutant genes. Common pathways showing increased expression in N. xanthodon MSCs and human MSCs were examined, revealing shared genes associated with transcriptional regulation. These markers, the BLM, TXNRD1, LAMTOR3, ELOC, and E2F8 genes, were evaluated in the TCGA-BRCA RNA-seq dataset comprising 1081 breast invasive carcinoma patients using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter tool (https://kmplot.com/). Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level, and overall survival was assessed by Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Tumor immune estimation resource assessment

The Gene_Mutation module was applied to compare differential gene expression between samples stratified by TP53 mutation status and the expression of 5 selected genes, each analyzed individually in breast invasive carcinoma (N = 1010). Statistical significance was determined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with P < .05 considered significant.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from at least 3 independent experiments were presented as mean ± SD or SE. Statistical analysis of the data obtained (ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square and/or Student’s t-tests) was performed using the GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0.

No significant changes in proliferation were observed in N. xanthodon MSCs or N. xanthodon fibroblasts after MNU exposure (Figure 2C). To determine whether the observed responses were species-specific or conserved across different organisms, we next evaluated the effects of MNU in MSCs derived from 4 distinct species. After 48 h of MNU treatment, IC20 values were 2.85 mM for human MSCs, 1.3 mM for mouse MSCs, and 2 mM for rat MSCs. Exposure to 8 mM MNU revealed significant interspecies differences in dead cell percentages, particularly between human and rat MSCs and between mouse and rat MSCs (P < .05) (Figure 2D).

Mesenchymal stem cells from all species were assessed for viability 48 h after MNU treatment. Apoptotic responses were species-specific: early apoptosis increased in mouse MSCs (P < .05), whereas late apoptosis increased in human MSCs (P < .001). In contrast, Nannospalax-derived MSCs showed no remarkable alterations in early or late apoptosis (Figure 2E). Furthermore, MNU treatment had no significant effect on any species’ proliferation index.

Notably, when compared to the other species, Nannospalax MSCs consistently showed the lowest proliferation index across all groups. This could be due to an evolutionarily conserved mechanism that supports genomic stability and resistance to unrestrained growth of cells in Nannospalax (Figure 2F).

Discussion

A paradigm for comprehending the carcinogenesis process is MNU, a molecule that adds alkyl groups to DNA. It results in O6-methylguanine alterations, which lead to mistakes in DNA replication, and eventually accumulates mutations in oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes.41,42 In animal models, for example, in rats, a single dose of intraperitoneal MNU results in the development of breast tumors.43,44

N-methyl-N-nitrosourea-based carcinogenesis models continue to play a major role in translational cancer research and are not restricted by conventional experiments. Understanding the mechanisms behind these processes is aided by comparative research on various species that concentrate on the molecular fundamentals of cancer resistance. The results show a clear connection between metabolic genes, DNA repair, and cellular stress responses.45

Investigating S. galili’s adaptation to low oxygen levels, it turns out that this species demonstrated notable resistance to the development of cancer despite extreme environmental circumstances. In addition, pro-inflammatory genes were expressed at low levels, while DNA repair and cell death processes were active in the organs of S. galili.7 These findings suggest that longevity and cancer resistance are caused by the interplay of inheritable and environmental variables.

In our study, MNU treatment increased apoptosis in mouse and human MSCs; however, no significant change was observed in either fibroblasts or MSCs of the N. xanthodon. At 48 h, mouse MSCs exhibited predominantly increased early apoptosis, nevertheless human MSCs showed a significant increase in late apoptotic cell percentages. The lack of an apoptotic response in N. xanthodon MSCs shows that cells may respond differently to MNU. This difference may stem from a stronger capacity for DNA damage repair or apoptotic response mechanisms; furthermore, genetic and epigenetic diversity may lead to different activation of processes such as DNA repair, cell cycle control, and stress response in N. xanthodon. In addition, metabolic rates, tolerance levels to oxidative stress, and adaptation capacity to culture conditions may also contribute to differences in responses. Nannospalax xanthodon MSCs are more resistant than other cell types, as evidenced by the deficiency of notable changes in proliferation kinetics or apoptotic responses after MNU treatment. This highlights species-specific response diversity and requires further investigation into the underlying biological mechanisms.

In N. xanthodon fibroblasts, alterations in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, response to TGF-β stimulation, and VEGFA/VEGFR2-related genes suggest MNU-dependent adaptive responses in energy metabolism and signal transduction. In N. xanthodon-derived MSCs, MNU exposure modulated transcriptional programs associated with stem cell proliferation, epithelial differentiation, cell cycle regulation, and p53-mediated signaling, without altering the overall proliferation rate. These findings suggest that MNU primarily influences cellular programming rather than proliferative capacity. In mice, changes in cell polarity, aerobic respiration, and electron transport chain pathways indicate possible disruptions in metabolic and structural homeostasis, while in rats, alterations in gene expression associated with oxidative phosphorylation and IL-1 response demonstrate modulation of different stress responses and inflammatory processes across species. These findings reveal that MNU exerts multifaceted effects on proliferation, differentiation, and stress responses by modulating metabolic and transcriptional pathways depending on both cell type and species.

N-methyl-N-nitrosourea treatment suppresses the PI3K/Akt pathways in N. xanthodon fibroblasts, thereby restricting their growth and survival signaling. This may be due to the high reliance of fibroblasts on PI3K/Akt pathways for tissue repair and metabolic activities. On the other hand, the lack of significant changes in this pathway in N. xanthodon mesenchymal stem cells suggests that stem cells possess more flexible and adaptive mechanisms in the face of stress, maintaining signaling through alternative pathways. This indicates that the effect of MNU depends not only on the cell itself but also on the cell type and its functional requirements.

A conserved genotoxic stress response with distinct prognostic implications in human cancer has been identified through a comparative pathway analysis of MNU-treated human and N. xanthodon MSCs. Increased expression of TXNRD1, ELOC (TCEB1), and E2F8 was linked to a poor clinical outcome, while LAMTOR3 expression was linked to a favorable prognosis, according to integration with TCGA datasets. Only LAMTOR3 was markedly downregulated in TP53-mutant invasive breast cancers, despite the fact that all 4 genes were regulated by p53. This suggests a selective reliance on intact p53 signaling. LAMTOR3 may serve as a p53-dependent tumor-protective node, which is compromised upon p53 inactivation, whereas TXNRD1, ELOC, and E2F8 may persist through p53-independent oncogenic pathways. Given the extraordinary cancer resistance of N. xanthodon, the maintenance of p53-LAMTOR3 signaling after genotoxic damage may constitute a vital mechanism that inhibits malignant progression.

In carcinogenic environments, the behavior of MSCs becomes complex and is shaped by genetics and signaling pathways. Specifically, the p53 and SMAD signaling pathways are critical mechanisms determining the risk of cancer development in MSCs and how they interact with their environment. The observation of a decrease in proliferation rate, while early and late apoptosis rates remained unchanged, indicates that the cell population maintained its viability, but its growth rate slowed. This suggests that the self-renewal capacity may have been functionally affected. In contrast, when considering N. xanthodon MSCs, the lack of significant changes in proliferation and early/late apoptosis rates, together with the successful multipotent differentiation experiments, indicate that the studied cell population retained its multipotent characteristics.

The p53 protein acts as a “guardian of the genome”46 with essential functions such as cell cycle control, DNA repair, and apoptosis. In N. xanthodon MSCs, p53 activation is triggered as a protective response against DNA damage caused by carcinogenic agents. This response includes processes such as cell cycle arrest, initiation of DNA repair, and triggering of apoptosis in the case of irreparable damage.47,48 In this way, MSCs gain strong protection against direct cancerization.49 In this species, a change similar to mutations seen in human cancers has been found in the DNA binding region of the p53 gene.50 This mutation reduces p53’s ability to activate apoptosis target genes while preserving its cell cycle regulation function.51 This adaptation facilitates Nannospalax’s survival against environmental challenges such as hypoxia and oxidative stress. Specifically, p53’s ability to activate different cellular death pathways, such as apoptosis and autophagy, plays a significant role in this species’ resistance to cancer development.52 The SMAD signaling pathway determines the differentiation potential and functional orientation of MSCs. Decreased SMAD activity pushes MSCs into a more passive and progenitor state, meaning the cells cannot fully differentiate. This can lead to MSCs assuming stromal or immune regulatory roles.

Inhibition of SMAD signaling can prevent the transformation of MSCs into cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby eliminating their pro-tumoral effects in the tumor microenvironment.53 In carcinogenic environments, MSCs indirectly influence the tumor microenvironment rather than directly becoming cancerous. When p53 is active, MSCs do not undergo transformation; however, due to the suppression of SMAD signaling, their tissue repair capacity may decrease, they may secrete immunosuppressive factors and indirectly support the growth of tumor cells. This situation may cause MSCs to transform into tumor-supporting stromal cells instead of directly becoming cancerous.54

However, some limitations of the present study should be addressed. Isolation of primary cells from N. xanthodon is technically demanding, time-consuming, and subject to intrinsic MSC heterogeneity, limiting the number of biological samples available for analysis. In addition, our findings are primarily based on in vitro models and bioinformatic analyses and therefore require validation in larger biological cohorts and functional studies. Although DNA repair pathways are recognized as important contributors to cancer resistance, analyses of DNA repair-related genes were beyond the scope of the present study. Future studies integrating transcriptomic and functional evaluation of DNA repair pathways will help further elucidate the molecular basis of cancer resistance in N. xanthodon.

Conclusion

Considering the functions of MSCs in the carcinogenic microenvironment, inhibition of cancer through p53, blockage of cell differentiation through SMAD reduction, and their role in indirectly supporting the tumor by adapting to the niche in which they interact are all possible outcomes. Therefore, the obtained gene expression patterns will help us understand the transformation-resistant structure of N. xanthodon-derived MSCs and how they adapt to the microenvironment, as well as contribute to the identification of new biomarkers in cancer development.
