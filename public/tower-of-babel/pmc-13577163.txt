Dengue Seroprevalence Among Blood Donors in the Lazio Region (Italy): Is There an Iceberg Beneath the Surface?
Authors: Martina Spaziante, Lugi Rosa, Francesca Colavita, Ilaria Mussetto, Gabriella De Carli, Valentina Vantaggio, Alessandro Agresta, Sabrina Coen, Silvia Cammisa, Alessandra D'Abramo, Gaetano Maffongelli, Stefania Vaglio, Stefano Iaboni, Ida Casorelli, Luca Mele, Luciana Teofili, Paola Scognamiglio, Andrea Siddu, Emanuele Nicastri, Fabrizio Maggi, Enrico Girardi, Deborah Ilaria Gadaleta, Deborah Ilaria Gadaleta, Diletta D'Epiro, Enrico Duranti, Manuela Cornacchia, Tecla Maria Fontana, Maria Laura Foddai, Francesca Frangiolini, Giuseppina Antonia Natale, Mafalda De Rienzo, Francesco Vairo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuring 2023, dengue virus (DENV) autochthonous circulation was detected in Lazio, Italy. To investigate the magnitude of the phenomenon, anti‐DENV serological status of blood donors residing in previously affected Rome municipalities was evaluated. Between December 2024 and April 2025, blood donors consecutively accessing to the seven recruited transfusion medicine units in the Lazio region (Italy) were screened for enrollment. Anti‐DENV IgG antibodies were assessed using a commercial ELISA assay (EUROIMMUN, Germany), and positive results were confirmed by virus neutralization (VNT). A total of 1297 consecutive blood donors were enrolled. None reported having a previous diagnosis of dengue. Six came from endemic countries. Initial ELISA screening identified 22 samples (18 positive and 4 equivocal) that were further investigated by VNT. Anti‐DENV neutralizing antibodies were confirmed in 7 donors, with a 0.54% prevalence rate (7/1297; 95% CI 0.14–0.94). Only 1 out of the 7 donors with confirmed presence of anti‐DENV IgG denied a history of travels to endemic areas. The prevalence of donors with possible local transmission was 0.08% (95% CI: 0.01–0.44). The study suggests that undetected viral circulation in the Lazio region is very unlikely, and the rate of mild or asymptomatic infections may be lower than that reported in endemic countries.

Introduction

Dengue virus (DENV) is a single positive‐stranded RNA virus of the family Flaviviridae, genus Orthoflavivirus, transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, and responsible for DENV fever, an arboviral infection endemic in more than 100 countries worldwide [1].

Climate change, along with social and demographic trends such as urbanization, population growth, migration, and human mobility, is exacerbating the global DENV burden, both by increasing the incidence of the disease in endemic countries and by spreading the virus to previously not affected temperate areas [2]. Recently, driven by an increasing number of international travels and sustained by the spread of the competent vector Aedes albopictus in Southern Europe, autochthonous DENV virus circulation was detected in the Mediterranean basin, particularly in France, Spain, and Italy [3, 4, 5].

In Italy, the first autochthonous outbreak of DENV fever was identified in Veneto (Northern Italy) in 2020 [6]. Between August and September 2023, an autochthonous outbreak was detected in Lombardy (Northern Italy); during the same period, three autochthonous DENV transmission events occurred in Lazio (Central Italy) [7, 8]. During the 2024 vector season, the national surveillance system recorded 213 confirmed autochthonous DENV cases in 6 Italian regions in Northern and Central Italy (Abruzzo, Emilia‐Romagna, Lombardy, Marche, Tuscany, and Veneto), while no cases were detected in Lazio [5].

Given that dengue infection is frequently asymptomatic or only mildly symptomatic (9), a proportion of infections may remain undetected by routine surveillance systems, which primarily capture clinically evident and laboratory‐confirmed cases. Following the autochthonous DENV outbreak reported in the Lazio region in 2023, assessing the possible extent of unrecognized transmission became relevant, particularly in a nonendemic setting where clinical suspicion may be limited. In this context, we conducted a multicenter, prospective study to assess the extent of viral circulation during the 2023 outbreak, and investigate potential undetected local transmission, in the Lazio region (Central Italy) during 2024. This study involved serological analysis of IgG DENV status among blood donors residing in the area affected by the 2023 outbreak.

Materials and Methods

All blood donors referring between December 1, 2024 and April 30, 2025 to seven transfusion medicine units of public hospitals located within the municipality of Rome—specifically four centers in Local Health Unit (LHU) Roma 1, one in LHU Roma 2, and two in LHU Roma 3—were consecutively screened for enrollment in the study.

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Lazio Region Ethics Committee (Approval N° 38‐2024 of the Lazio Region, Ethics Committee Area 4, March 20, 2024). All study participants provided signed informed consent prior to enrollment. For each blood donor, demographic (age, nationality) and epidemiological information (vaccination status against other Flaviviruses, any travel abroad to DENV‐endemic areas in the last year, previous diagnosis of DENV or other arboviruses in the enrolled subjects, their family members or cohabitants) were collected at the time of sample collection. For confirmed positive subjects, follow‐up visits were scheduled to investigate any stays in DENV‐endemic areas more than 1 year prior to study enrollment.

Inclusion criteria were established as: no personal history of dengue vaccination and having been continuously domiciled in the territory of the municipalities of Rome affected by the 2023 DENV outbreak in the 12 months preceding the enrollment date. Furthermore, study participants were required to meet the physical eligibility criteria established by current national regulations, be in good general health, and report no history of high‐risk behaviors. Temporary donor deferral criteria established by current national legislation were applied. Therefore, individuals who met any temporary deferral condition, including travel to areas at risk of tropical diseases during the applicable deferral period, were temporarily ineligible for blood donation and were not enrolled in the study at the time of donation.

Blood samples were collected concomitantly with the blood donation which had place in different transfusion units. Blood samples were collected in vacuum blood collection tubes containing a serum separator gel to obtain serum and were transported to the Regional Reference Laboratory at the National Institute for Infectious Diseases “L. Spallanzani” under refrigerated conditions (2°C–8°C); serum was separated by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 20 min, aliquoted and stored at −20°C until analysis in order to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles and preserve sample integrity. All serological analyses were performed at the Regional Reference Laboratory of the National Institute for Infectious Diseases “L. Spallanzani.”

Due to the lack of reliable dengue seroprevalence data in urban Italian settings, as well as the intrinsic variability of the blood donor population over time, a nonprobability convenience sampling was applied with a target number of at least 1000 participants.

Serological Investigation

DENV (types 1–4) IgG were tested using a commercial ELISA kit (Euroimmun, Germany) and processed on the EUROIMMUN Analyzer I‐2P, following the manufacturer's instructions. Antibody reactivity was calculated by the Analyzer software and expressed semi‐quantitatively in relative units (RU): IgG titers > 22 RU were considered positive, 16–22 RU equivocal, and < 16 RU negative; equivocal results were retested and classified as positive if persistent.

Serological tests for anti‐DENV IgG are known to be influenced by cross‐reactivity with other orthoflaviviruses [9]. Therefore, DENV ELISA‐positive sera were confirmed by virus neutralization test (VNT), as previously described [10], and tested against viral strains of DENV‐2 and closely related orthoflaviviruses, including Yellow Fever Virus (YFV), Zika virus (ZIKV), Usutu virus (USUV), and West Nile Virus (WNV). Neutralizing antibody titers (nAbs) were defined as the highest serum dilution inhibiting ≥ 90% cytopathic effect; titers < 1:10 were considered negative. Positive and negative neutralization controls were included in each experiment.

Results

Between December 1, 2024 and April 30, 2025, 1297 blood donors attending the transfusion medicine units of seven public hospitals located in the municipality of Rome were consecutively enrolled in the study.

The demographic and epidemiological characteristics of the study cohort, as well as factors associated with DENV seroprevalence, are described in Tables 1 and 2. Among all variables analyzed, only origin from dengue‐endemic countries and prior flavivirus vaccination were significantly associated with IgG positivity (p < 0.001 and p = 0.016, respectively). The latter—predominantly yellow fever vaccination—likely reflects a history of travel to endemic areas, thus serving as an indirect marker of exposure risk.

Of the 1297 serum samples screened by ELISA, 18 yielded positive results and 4 were equivocal, resulting in a total of 22 (1.7%) specimens that were subsequently tested by VNT. As expected, cross‐reactivity against different orthoflaviviruses was observed. As shown in Figure 1, based on nAb titers, neutralizing anti‐DENV response was confirmed in 7 (31.8%) out of 22 donors, while in 6 sera the highest nAb titer was observed against other orthoflaviviruses (anti‐YFV in 2, anti‐ZIKV in 1, and anti‐WNV in 3); no nAbs against USUV were detected. No orthoflavivirus nAbs were detected in the remaining 9 donors, 3 of whom were detected as equivocal by ELISA.

After confirmation tests, a 0.54% overall prevalence rate was observed (7/1297; 95% CI 0.14–0.94).

Among the seven donors with confirmed anti‐DENV IgG, five reported travel to endemic areas more than a year prior to enrollment (2 to Philippines, 1 to Mexico and Thailand, 1 to Sri Lanka, 1 to Venezuela). One donor reported exclusively domestic travel to Italian regions with documented autochthonous circulation during the 2024 vector season (Abruzzo and Lombardy), denying travel to tropical areas. Notably, this donor was a 21‐year‐old Italian individual recruited at a blood transfusion center within the RM1 Local Health Authority, which accounted for the highest number of dengue cases in 2023.

The seventh seropositive donor could not be contacted despite repeated attempts; therefore, information on travel history to DENV‐endemic areas was unavailable and was treated as missing data. Accordingly, this individual was excluded from the estimation of possible local dengue transmission.

Based on the remaining confirmed cases, the observed prevalence of donors with confirmed anti‐DENV IgG and no documented travel to DENV‐endemic areas was 0.08% (95% CI: 0.01–0.44), indicating a very low prevalence of possible local transmission in the study population.

Discussion

Over the past 5 years, the number of DENV cases has steadily increased worldwide, and the disease is spreading to new areas [11, 12]. Indeed, a significant increase in both imported and autochthonous DENV cases has recently been recorded in Italy [2, 6, 8, 13]. In the period from August to November 2023, the first autochthonous outbreak of DENV fever was registered in Lazio (Central Italy) with 3 different clusters identified and a total of 48 cases (DENV‐1: 45 cases, DENV‐2: 1 case, DENV‐3: 2 cases) [8, 14]. A recent cross‐sectional study conducted in the Lazio region from July to November 2023 (when the outbreak was ongoing) enrolling 475 people living with HIV observed a DENV IgG seroprevalence in 37 individuals (7.79%) (95% CI 5.54–10.6). Notably, among the Italian participants in the study, three individuals (2.9%) who had not traveled outside Italy in the past 12 months showed seroconversion during the 2023 outbreak, providing further evidence for autochthonous DENV transmission in the country [15, 16].

The present study represents the first serological analysis of DENV in healthy blood donors carried out in Italy following the local DENV outbreak that occurred in 2023. The results obtained within our study cohort do not suggest at the present time a significant, undetected autochthonous DENV circulation in our region. In fact, out of the seven participants who tested positive for anti‐DENV nAbs, only one reported no documented history of travel to DENV‐endemic countries, corresponding to a 0.08% seroprevalence rate in the overall study population. The only donor with confirmed anti‐DENV IgG and no documented travel to DENV‐endemic areas was a young adult recruited within the RM1 Local Health Authority, an area that reported dengue transmission during the 2023 outbreak. Although this finding is consistent with the possibility of local exposure, the presence of only a single case precludes any evaluation of demographic or clinical factors associated with DENV seropositivity. Therefore, no reliable conclusions regarding potential risk factors can be drawn from our data.

The expansion of the boundaries of disease‐affected areas, even outside tropical regions, is mainly driven by global warming, which affects the lifecycle of A. albopictus, the main competent vector in Europe for several arboviruses including dengue. Climatic changes impact on the vector homodynamicity (i.e., the ability to breed all‐year‐round), which is a driver of expansion, and a primary condition for an Aedes‐borne disease to become endemic in temperate areas. In fact, data from a 4‐year monitoring network set in Central Italy combined with weather records and three global climate models for the next three 20‐year periods (from 2021 to 2080), demonstrated that by the end of the century the January minimum temperature will increase enough to allow an all‐year‐round A. albopictus oviposition. Consequently, the Aedes‐borne diseases possibly will become endemic in Southern Europe, worsening the public health burden [17].

Given the low prevalence rate observed in our study despite evidence of local viral circulation in 2023, it is plausible to suggest that pauci‐ and asymptomatic infections—which represent the majority of cases in endemic regions—may be less prevalent in our region. It may reflect enhanced case ascertainment driven by increased diagnostic suspicion among first‐line healthcare workers during a DENV outbreak in a nonendemic setting, thereby reducing the likelihood of undetected subclinical infections. Furthermore, in endemic areas, DENV has historically been a disease that disproportionately affects children, which, compared to the general population, tend to have milder or no symptoms. Among infants, the rate of asymptomatic infections may rise to 85%–90% [18]. Changing population demographics and increasing travel to and from the tropics have contributed to a growing incidence in adults [19, 20]. DENV fever occurring in adults, and particularly in older adults, as it may be the case of autochthonous cases in temperate areas, may result in different clinical presentations, posing complex and unprecedented challenges to case management.

This study presents some limitations. First, the donor population may not accurately reflect the demographic features of the general population. In addition, the analyzed sample may not be sufficiently large to detect low‐level dengue circulation within the area. Furthermore, DENV exposure was assessed through anti‐DENV IgG detection; since IgG antibodies may persist for years after infection, seropositivity does not allow accurate determination of the timing of infection and cannot establish whether exposure occurred recently from the sample collection or at an earlier time point. Another limitation of the study is the inability to perform a precise a priori sample size calculation, owing to the absence of robust dengue seroprevalence estimates for urban Italian populations and the inherent fluctuations in the composition of the blood donor population over time; moreover, the study was interrupted with the onset of the vector season, which imposed operational and public health constraints; however, a second phase is currently ongoing to strengthen the robustness of the findings.

Finally, as the study population was limited to blood donors, who represent a selected group of healthy individuals within specific age ranges, the results may not be fully generalizable to the general population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the very low prevalence of anti‐DENV IgG observed in this study suggests that any undetected autochthonous transmission following the 2023 outbreak in the Lazio region is likely to be limited in magnitude; in our region, the main driver of autochthonous DENV infections is likely still the (increasing) incidence of cases imported from endemic areas. However, due to global warming, which significantly impacts homodynamicity, viral circulation may no longer experience a breakdown due to winter temperatures in the near future.

Interestingly, study findings may suggest that the clinical presentation of the disease in temperate areas may differ from that described in endemic areas.

These considerations underline the importance of referring to DENV (and other vector‐borne diseases) as a major public health concern, deserving of strengthened surveillance and control measures for these infections.
