Gigahertz-frequency acousto-optic phase modulation of visible light in a CMOS-fabricated photonic circuit
Authors: Jacob M. Freedman, Matthew J. Storey, Daniel Dominguez, Andrew Leenheer, Sebastian Magri, Nils T. Otterstrom, Matt Eichenfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optical phase modulators operating at visible wavelengths are essential components for photonic technologies such as those for quantum control, communications, and laser ranging. However, they remain challenging to implement in scalable, integrated platforms capable of handling high optical powers. Here we present a visible-light, gigahertz-frequency acousto-optic phase modulator, fabricated on a 200-mm wafer in a volume CMOS foundry, that supports greater than 500 mW of optical power at 730 nm. The device combines a piezoelectric transducer and a photonic waveguide within a single, wavelength-scale structure that confines both a propagating optical mode and an electrically excitable breathing-mode mechanical resonance. By tuning the device’s geometry to optimize the optomechanical interaction, we achieve modulation depths up to 4.85 rad with 80 mW of applied microwave power at 2.31 GHz in a 2-mm-long device. This corresponds to resonant modulation figures of merit of Vπ = 1.32V and Vπ ⋅ L = 0.26V cm. To our knowledge, this is the lowest Vπ ever demonstrated in any acousto-optic phase modulator and represents a 15-fold reduction in Vπ and a 100-fold reduction in required microwave power relative to state-of-the-art modulators with high visible-wavelength power handling commonly employed in quantum control systems.

Introduction

Bulk acousto-optic technology has enabled high-extinction pulse carving, single-sideband frequency shifting, beam deflection, and tunable filtering1. However, the devices that provide these critical functions typically consume high power and operate at low frequencies. Recently, on-chip acousto-optics platforms have yielded fast optical switches2, microwave-to-optical transducers3–5, isolators6–8, beam deflectors9, and narrow-band filters10. These devices offer increasingly compact and energy-efficient versions of their traditionally large, high-power-consumption bulk analogs and facilitate higher frequency operation in the gigahertz range1,3,11. Also, because non-vanishing photoelastic coefficients exist regardless of a material’s crystal symmetries, acousto-optic devices can be implemented in a significantly broader class of materials than their electro-optic counterparts12. This flexibility allows for the use of very large scale integration-compatible materials such as silicon nitride (SiNx) that have desirable properties including wide transparency window13, low propagation loss14, high power-handling capability15, and compatibility with complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS) fabrication processes16.

Demonstrations of high-frequency, integrated electro-optic17–20 and acousto-optic21–28 phase and frequency modulators with excellent performance have been implemented in a variety of material platforms. Yet to date, these operate with telecom-band light or else rely on materials that are both incompatible with CMOS foundry processes and incapable of supporting high optical power (see comparison in Supplementary Material Section 1)29. For instance, many of these modulators are based on lithium niobate, for which photorefractive effects set stringent limits on maximum optical power density, especially at visible wavelengths30 (Supplementary Material Section 2.2).

These limitations to power handling and mass manufacturability hinder the scalability of on-chip optical signal processors, which could otherwise allow for simultaneous control of many channels derived from a single, high-power laser. For example, trapped-ion and neutral-atom quantum computers will require thousands or even millions of channels with independent frequency control for each qubit31,32, and gate operations typically require tens of milliwatts of laser power incident on the qubit site33. This means integrated photonic control chips will need to handle watt-level power.

For these reasons and more13, many demonstrations of integrated components for quantum control have been implemented in platforms based on SiNx, which has high power handling at visible wavelengths and is CMOS-compatible. These demonstrations include multi-channel light-delivery systems34,35, chip-to-qubit free-space couplers with focusing diffraction gratings36, polarization rotators37, and reconfigurable Mach-Zehnder interferometers2. The addition of on-chip optical frequency control to this growing library of components would serve as a crucial resource for generating individually addressable, channelized optical signals for Raman gates, cooling beams, and state measurements, all of which require visible-light operation and gigahertz frequency shifts31.

In this work, we present the design and experimental realization of a CMOS-fabricated, resonant acousto-optic phase modulator implemented in a piezo-optomechanical aluminum nitride-SiNx platform. We obtain a strong optomechanical interaction by confining a propagating optical mode and a breathing-mode mechanical resonance to a single, wavelength-scale structure. A piezoelectric actuator embedded within the structure, combined with this optomechanical coupling, enables efficient electromechanically driven acousto-optic phase modulation. Our device harnesses this phase modulation to generate gigahertz-frequency sidebands on a 730-nm laser input. At a 2.31-GHz modulation frequency, we achieve a resonant figure of merit of Vπ = 1.32 V with a 2-mm-long device (Vπ ⋅ L = 0.26V cm). To the best of our knowledge, this is the lowest Vπ ever demonstrated with an integrated acousto-optic modulator, which is significant because applications like sideband generation typically require large modulation depths, and scaling shorter devices with low Vπ ⋅ L to longer lengths entails substantial challenges (Supplementary Material Section 1.2). We demonstrate optical power handling in our platform’s waveguides that exceeds 500 mW (Supplementary Material Section 2.1), and we operate the device with up to 19 dBm of applied microwave power, at which point the modulation depth reaches 4.85 rad. The modulator has a bandwidth, which we define as the frequency range over which the modulation depth is maintained at least half of its maximum value, of 24 MHz around the center frequency of 2.31 GHz. Our device is uniquely suited for scaling optical systems operating with high power at visible wavelengths, such as those for underwater communication38, visible-light laser ranging (LiDAR)39, and quantum control31,40. This is because it employs SiNx waveguides known for their power handling at visible wavelengths, is fabricated on a 200-mm wafer in a CMOS foundry, has a 15x decrease in Vπ and a 100x reduction in required microwave power compared to the state-of-the-art (Supplementary Material Section 1), and is built in a process in which many functions such as high-speed switches2 and tunable narrow-band filters41 already exist. It further lays the foundation for a visible-light, acousto-optics platform capable of supporting a variety of components such as isolators and single-sideband frequency modulators.

Results

Device platform and working principle

Our acousto-optic modulator comprises a photonic waveguide situated directly above a piezoelectric actuator. A SiNx core enclosed by SiO2 cladding forms the waveguide, and the actuator leverages the dominant d33 and d31 piezoelectric coefficients of an aluminum nitride (AlN) film by sandwiching it between two electrode layers (Fig. 1)42. The devices are fabricated in a volume CMOS foundry on a 200-mm wafer, which is then diced into around 120 individual chips (one of these chips is photographed in Fig. 1g, and a subset of the phase modulators it contains are micrographed in Fig. 1f). We utilize etched trenches and a release layer to physically isolate the structure from the wafer substrate and surrounding photonic and piezoelectric layers (Fig. 1e). This inhibits the principal mechanism by which mechanical energy can escape the device and consequently allows sharp mechanical resonances to exist. The AlN’s thickness of 450nm is chosen to maximize its piezoelectric response while remaining amenable to etching43, and the 710-nm bottom cladding is designed to optimize the tradeoff between strain-overlap with the optical mode and metal-induced propagation losses41. We obtain resonantly enhanced optomechanical coupling by confining a breathing-mode mechanical resonance and a propagating optical mode to the same wavelength-scale structure. The coupling results from the photoelastic effect, which describes strain-induced changes to a material’s refractive index, and the moving-boundary effect, which accounts for alterations to a waveguide’s cross section by mechanical displacements at the core-cladding interface44,45. These mechanically driven effects modify the optical mode’s effective refractive index by an amount Δneff. The optical mode then accumulates a phase shift of Δϕ = ωΔneffL/c after propagating through a length, L, over which this index-modification is present, where ω is the angular frequency of the optical mode and c is the speed of light in vacuum.

We electrically excite resonantly enhanced phase shifts by applying a sinusoidal microwave signal to a device’s piezoelectric transducer at a mechanical resonance frequency, Ω/2π. Device performance, measured in modulation depth imparted per volt of applied microwave signal, ultimately depends on the entire piezo-optomechanical transduction chain. First, microwave power is transferred from driving electronics to the on-chip device, where the effective voltage drop across the device depends on its electrical impedance matching with the microwave transmission line. Second, the piezoelectric transducer converts some fraction of the delivered microwave power to mechanical energy, the efficiency of which depends on the electromechanical coupling coefficient, k246. Third, as discussed above, the optomechanical coupling between the two modes in question determines the change to the optical propagation constant per unit excited mechanical displacement. Finally, the quality factor, Q, of the excited mechanical mode increases the displacement and strain build-up in the structure and consequently determines the degree to which the modulation is resonantly enhanced.

Experimental results

We characterize the fabricated devices with the setup shown schematically in Fig. 2c. A laser source is coupled to a fiber interferometer whose upper arm contains the chip on which the devices are fabricated and whose lower arm contains a commercially available acousto-optic frequency shifter (AOFS). In the upper arm, light from the fiber is grating-coupled to and from a waveguide that routes an optical mode through a particular phase modulator device. Meanwhile, light in the lower arm is passed through the AOFS, which shifts its frequency by an amount Δ/2 π = 125 MHz. To precisely determine the modulation depth, we extract it from the ratio of the measured optical power of two distinct sidebands at the microwave frequencies mΩ + Δ and nΩ + Δ (details of this method and its verification found in Supplementary Material Section 5.2). The calculated modulation depth is plotted in Fig. 3a along with the microwave reflection coefficient, S11, measured by the VNA. Note that this modulation-depth curve is akin to a typical electro-optic S21 curve. Dips in the S11 curve indicate those frequencies at which mechanical resonances are transduced, and they are labeled with their associated quality factors (which are calculated by a fit detailed in Supplementary Material Section 5.3). As expected, the locations of these resonant mechanical dips closely agree with the frequencies at which peaks in modulation depth occur. However, the presence of a strongly excited mechanical mode does not necessarily yield a large modulation depth because the degree to which a given mechanical mode induces changes to the effective refractive index is mediated by both the mechanical quality factor and the strength of the optomechanical coupling. This is evidenced by drive frequencies at which large dips in S11 appear, yet correspond only to a weak or nonexistent rise in modulation depth. We also observe that the regions of resonantly enhanced modulation depth consist of doublet peaks (see inset of Fig. 3a). This is because the device’s continuous translational symmetry is broken by the periodically placed nanopillar supports along its length (as depicted in Fig. 1e). For the 2.31 GHz resonance, we observe a linear dependence between the modulation depth and voltage amplitude, V, of the applied microwave signal until V ≈ 1.91 V (Fig. 3c), past which the device malfunctions. We suspect that the ultimate malfunctioning is caused by dielectric breakdown of the AlN (Supplementary Material Section 5.2)53. Although a modulation depth of nearly 5rad is sufficient for purposes such as sideband generation, some applications benefit from stronger modulation54, which future work could achieve by elongating the device to reduce Vπ.

Using Vπ = 1.32 V, obtained from a fit to the data in Fig. 3c, to convert from drive power to modulation depth, we theoretically calculate the conversion efficiency to each of the sidebands of a pure phase modulation process and compare this against our experimentally obtained values in Fig. 3b. Excellent agreement is observed until a drive power of 19 dBm, beyond which the device ceases to function. The theoretical calculation predicts slightly higher conversion efficiencies than those observed for the sidebands, but this is explained by frequency-dependent loss in the microwave cables connecting the photoreceiver to the RFSA (Fig. 2c).

We extract a mechanical quality factor of Q = 228 from a fit to the modulation depth data at the 2.31 GHz resonance, implying a fast switching time of 31.4 ns. We perform loss measurements on a ring resonator that has nearly the same cross-section as our modulator and which is fabricated on the same die as it. These measurements indicate the on-chip propagation losses are 0.55 dB cm−1 (Supplementary Material Section 7). The coupling loss is 9.61 dB per grating coupler, which can be improved by optimizing the grating-coupler design or by employing edge couplers. We also measure the on-resonance impedance of the device to be 149 Ω, which corresponds to 74.3% microwave power coupling. In future work, impedance matching networks could be used to improve the microwave coupling efficiency55. We further check the stability of the device when driven with P0 = 15 mW of incident microwave power on the resonance at 2.31 GHz, and we find that the average modulation depth remains relatively constant over a two-hour period (Supplementary Material Section 8). We note that during this experiment, the electrical power delivered to the device, which we define here as (1−S11)P0 = 11.15 mW, was constant over the measurement.

Operation at other mechanical frequencies and optical wavelengths

The ability to accurately and precisely design our device to operate at desired, application-specific modulation frequencies would allow for its deployment in a variety of systems. For example, hyperfine-ground-state and Zeeman-split qubits have transition frequencies that depend on the atomic species or applied magnetic field, typically lying between 1 GHz and 13 GHz31,56. Consequently, optical control systems require modulation across this same range. Our device’s mechanical modes determine its operating frequencies, and they primarily depend on material properties, layer thicknesses, and layer widths. The latter of these provides a straightforward knob by which to tune the mechanical resonance frequency because the layer widths are lithographically controlled, and modifying them therefore avoids adjusting any finely tuned fabrication steps.

To demonstrate this capability, we perform piezoelectrically coupled mechanical simulations of our device and track the mechanical resonance frequencies of several mode families as the width of the device is swept from 500 nm to 1.5 μm in steps of 25 nm. We also fabricate and characterize the resonance frequencies of devices with four distinct widths: 800 nm, 950 nm, 1.10 μm, and 1.25 μm (Supplementary Material Section 6.3). Figure 4a shows these measured resonance frequencies superimposed onto the bands we extract from the simulations. The top oxide thickness is the only free parameter in our model because it is the least precisely controlled in the material stack. All other parameters use standard material parameters reported in the literature and the as-designed geometric parameters. The average amount by which the experimentally observed resonance frequencies differ from those predicted by simulation is 37 MHz. Although this amount is larger than the bandwidth of the modulator presented herein, we show in Supplementary Material Section 9 we can control the bandwidth by lithographically modifying the release structure of the devices. This enables us to adjust the expected yield of devices given the degree of precision our model and fabrication provide. To investigate the latter, we collect statistics on several fabricated devices from different dies and wafers and find the standard deviation in resonance frequency is 18 MHz and the standard deviation in Vπ is 0.10 V (Supplementary Material Section 10).

We further show through FEM simulations of the mechanical and optical modes, combined with calculations of the optomechanical coupling integrals (Eqs. (2) and (3)), that our modulator can perform efficiently over the entire visible wavelength range and into the near-infrared. Figure 4b shows the results of these simulations for two mechanical modes calibrated to the device performance experimentally measured at 730 nm (see Supplementary Material Section 6.1 for details). In practice, our fabricated devices are limited by the bandwidth of their grating couplers, so we experimentally confirm the simulations over the 12-nm range over which they efficiently operate (Fig. 4c). For applications requiring broadband optical operation, edge couplers with higher optical bandwidth could be employed57.

Discussion

We have demonstrated a visible-light, gigahertz-frequency acousto-optic modulator fabricated in a wafer-scale CMOS process. Our 2-mm-long device produces resonantly enhanced modulation depths reaching 4.85 rad at a frequency of 2.31 GHz when driven with 80 mW of microwave power. This corresponds to resonant modulation figures of merit of Vπ= 1.32 V and Vπ ⋅ L = 0.26 V cm. These metrics indicate significantly superior performance to state-of-the-art resonant, visible-light phase modulators with high power handling, which typically consume watt-level powers to produce modulation depths of order 1rad58,59.

Atomic and ionic species have optical transitions within the ultraviolet to near infrared and microwave transitions in the gigahertz range (selected species listed in Fig. 5a)31,32,56,60. Qubits are often encoded in the microwave levels, and gates are applied with Raman beams, which have a carrier frequency detuned from the optical transition and which exhibit amplitude modulation at the microwave transition frequency48. In Fig. 4b and c, experimentally supported simulations show our modulators can operate at wavelengths spanning from 400 nm to 1000 nm. We further demonstrate in Fig. 4a that by tuning the width of the waveguide structure, our device can be designed to operate at precisely controlled mechanical resonance frequencies, and in Supplementary Material Section 6.2, we present simulations that show there exist strongly optomechanically coupled mechanical resonances over the frequency range spanning 1 GHz to 5 GHz. In this range of operation, our acousto-optic phase modulator will enable the generation of gigahertz frequency shifts applicable to a variety of qubit species. By integrating this work with devices that have been demonstrated in the same piezo-optomechanical platform, we envision a photonic control chip that generates and delivers Raman beams to atomic and ionic qubits (shown schematically in Fig. 5b). The chip will distribute a single laser input to several channels, each of which can be individually amplitude-modulated by Mach-Zehnder modulators with sub-microsecond switching times2,61, frequency-modulated by the devices presented in this work, and spectrally filtered by tunable ring resonators with quality factors exceeding 1.5 million41,62.

The latter component is vital because the light incident on the qubits must be amplitude modulated at the qubit transition frequency, yet the pure phase-modulation process that generates the sidebands contributes zero amplitude modulation. Fortunately, conversion from phase to amplitude modulation can be achieved by ring-resonator-enabled narrow-band filtering of the carrier or sideband signals. Filtering the carrier signal produces amplitude modulation at twice the modulation frequency, which is advantageous for qubits like 133Cs or 137Ba+ that have relatively large hyperfine ground state splitting frequencies (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the splitter tree and Mach-Zehnder modulator layers can be combined into a binary tree mesh that performs both switching and reconfigurable power routing, as was previously demonstrated in the same platform as the devices presented herein63.

When densely channelizing the phase modulators from this work (as depicted in Fig. 5b), acoustic crosstalk becomes a concern. Although our chip is not designed to characterize this, measurements indicate the crosstalk in modulation depth between 270-μm-spaced devices is suppressed by 16.5 dB. This corresponds to 29 dB of suppression in first-order sideband power when operating to maximize conversion efficiency (Supplementary Material Section 12). Since no effort was made to mitigate crosstalk, future qubit control chips could likely significantly improve upon this value by employing various mitigation strategies. For example, future chips could utilize acoustic scatterers or absorbers fabricated between modulators, photonic crystals designed to prevent acoustic waves from propagating between channels, staggered modulator locations, or simply a reduction in the size and density of the nanopillar supports that radiate and couple mechanical energy between devices. For the lattermost strategy, the minimum size at which nanopillars can be reliably fabricated and the minimum density at which they effectively prevent the released structure from collapsing are still unknown in our platform and perhaps significantly smaller and less dense than those utilized in this work (Supplementary Material Section 9).

In summary, by enabling the high optical power handling typically achieved with bulk devices and simultaneously maintaining modulation efficiencies competitive with state-of-the-art integrated technology, our work is poised to uniquely provide large-scale, channelized frequency control at visible wavelengths and gigahertz frequencies for cooling, state preparation, gate operations, and readout. Although the transparency window of SiNx precludes operation deep into the ultraviolet, future work can utilize an alumina-based platform, in which we have already demonstrated piezo-optomechanical switches and tunable microresonators, to extend operation to this regime64. Additionally, our device is suited for other visible-wavelength applications requiring high optical power, for example, lidar systems for ranging distant targets65 or underwater communication systems in which the limited available bandwidth diminishes the need for ultra-fast modulation66. We can further expand on our acousto-optics platform to leverage mechanical waves that copropagate with the optical mode to excite intermodal Brillouin scattering processes, enabling nonmagnetic isolation67 and single-sideband frequency modulation. The latter of these capabilities is also immediately implementable using the phase modulators of this work, arranged in a dual parallel Mach-Zehnder modulator configuration19.

Methods

Device fabrication

The devices are fabricated on 200-mm wafers at Sandia National Laboratories. The process begins with a SiO2 film on a silicon substrate. First, aluminum is deposited and patterned to form a metal routing layer. Next, amorphous silicon (a-Si) and tungsten are deposited and patterned to form a release layer and vertical electrical connections, respectively. Then, the bottom electrode, AlN, and top electrode are sequentially deposited and patterned to form the piezoelectric actuation layers. After this, the optical waveguiding layers are deposited and patterned, which include the lower SiO2 cladding, SiNx core, and top SiO2 cladding. With all these layers formed, patterned trenches are etched through all layers until the a-Si is reached. This serves the dual purpose of accessing the a-Si in order to perform the release etch while also generating the approximately micron-wide structures that comprise the phase modulators. Finally, the a-Si is selectively removed through these trenches with a xenon-difluoride etch. A figure depicting this process and more details, including layer thicknesses, is provided in Supplementary Material Section 11.

Measurement details

In order to characterize the phase modulation, the device is driven with a sinusoidal electrical signal produced by a VNA (Keysight P9374A) and delivered to the chip through a radio-frequency probe (FormFactor ACP40-GSG). The VNA also records the microwave reflection coefficient of the device, from which we obtain its equivalent electrical impedance, and the frequencies at which mechanical modes are efficiently excited. We then use laser light at a 730-nm wavelength (Sacher Lasertechnik LION series) to read out the on-chip phase modulation using a heterodyne interferometer (Fig. 2c). We use a high-speed photoreceiver (Newport 1554-A) to convert the optical output to microwave signals that are recorded with an RFSA (Rhode and Schwarz FSV3013). From these data, we extract the ratio, Rmn, of the optical power in the mth and nth sidebands of the phase-modulation comb (Fig. 3d).
