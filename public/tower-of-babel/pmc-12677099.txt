Ginsenoside Rg1 alleviated testicular and sperm damage in rats exposed to bisphenol A
Authors: Fatemeh Abdi, Hamed Shoorei, Ramji Kumar Bhandari, Bahareh Amin, Marzieh Kafami, Samad Nazemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBisphenol-A (BPA), a global environmental contaminant, is a well-known endocrine disruptor that can impair male reproductive function. The purpose of the study was to investigate the effects of ginsenoside Rg1 on alleviating BPA-induced testicular dysfunction and poor sperm quality.Adult male rats were divided into four groups: control, BPA, BPA + Rg1, and Rg1. Rats were injected intraperitoneally daily with BPA (50 mg/kg) and Rg1 (20 mg/kg) for 8 weeks. The histopathology of testicular tissues, sperm quality, mRNA and protein expression of Bax and Bcl-2, serum levels of testosterone, FSH, and LH, and the activity of antioxidant markers (MDA, SOD, and GPx) were determined.BPA exposure resulted in a significant reduction in the thickness and diameter of seminiferous tubules, a decrease in both mRNA and protein expression of Bax, a reduction in serum levels of testosterone, LH, and FSH, a reduction in SOD and GPx activity, and an elevation in MDA levels. Additionally, BPA exposure resulted in increased DNA fragmentation, chromatin decondensation, and abnormalities in sperm samples.All end points examined above were significantly reversed by Rg1 co-treatment, suggesting that the administration of ginsenoside Rg1 can provide protective effects against BPA-induced testicular impairments.Keywords: Bisphenol A, Antioxidant, Ginsenoside Rg1, Testis, Sperm

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA), chemically identified as 2,2-bis(4-hydroxyphenyl) propane, is an industrial chemical that mostly used in the synthesis of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins, with two unsaturated phenolic rings. BPA is recognized as an estrogenic endocrine disruptor [1,2]. BPA is used in the production of consumer products, mainly disposable plastics, dental devices, sealants, sports equipment, and certain food containers. BPA can enter the human body through absorption, ingestion, and inhalation [3].

A wide range of pathophysiological conditions, including developmental anomalies, neurological disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and reproductive toxicity, are linked to BPA exposure, since epidemiological studies found measurable amounts of BPA in the urine or blood of people with those pathological conditions [4,5]. BPA can predominantly induce severe testicular damage through mechanisms involving overproduction of reactive oxidative species (ROS), particularly superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide, but also impair endogenous antioxidant defenses [6].

In testicular tissue, BPA-induced ROS disrupts spermatogenesis, leading to diminished sperm quality and dysfunction of germ cells [7]. BPA can disrupt testicular steroidogenesis also by binding to estrogen-related receptor gamma (ERR-γ) as well as estrogen receptors alpha and beta (ERα and ERβ) [1]. BPA can regulate the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, thereby negatively affecting normal steroidogenesis. This disruption is likely mediated by reductions in LH, FSH, and testosterone levels, which contribute to aberrant spermatogenesis [8].

Studies also suggests that BPA exposure has negative impact on steroidogenesis in mammals. BPA exposure downregulated the expression of critical genes involved in steroidogenesis, including CYP11A1, CYP17A1, 17β-HSD, 3β-HSD, and StAR in the testicular tissues of both rats and mice [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. The integrity of the blood-testis barrier (BTB) was impacted by BPA through altering the structural normalcy of testicular tissues and resulting in sperm abnormalities [12,13]. BPA significantly decreased the expression of essential genes integral to BTB integrity, such as Occludin and Nectin-3, in male Wistar rats in a dose-dependent manner [14]. Adult male Wistar rats treated with oral BPA exposure (50 mg/kg for 2 weeks) exhibited impairments in spermatogenesis, with spermatocytes being most adversely impacted among all germ cells [15]. In C57BL/6 mice, oral administration of BPA (0–250 μg/kg for 8 weeks) resulted in a significant downregulation of the CatSper gene and an apparent reduction in sperm motility [16].

In recent years, plant-based drugs have been widely used for ameliorating male reproductive toxicity because of their cost-effectiveness and availability [17]. Panax ginseng is a well-known and prominent herbal medicine in various Eastern countries. The therapeutic benefits of ginseng arise from its active compounds, ginsenosides—particularly Rg1. Rg1 is known for its well-known pharmacological actions, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties. In mice, Rg1 administration can improve sperm quality and ameliorate d-galactose-induced testicular aging by downregulating the p19/p53/p21 signaling pathway [18]. In male C57BL/6J mice, Rg1 (20 mg/kg for 1 month) can ameliorate testicular damage induced by cranial irradiation [19]. The protection of germ cells involved the suppression of germ cell apoptosis via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, resulting in increased sperm count and a reduction in morphological abnormalities [19].

It is not clearly understood if BPA-induced testicular abnormalities can be minimized by applying Rg1 as a protectant. The present study investigated the alleviating effects of Rg1 against BPA-induced testicular injury and examined sperm parameters, hormone production, germ cell apoptosis, and biochemical parameters.

Method and materials

Animals and exposure

In the present study, 32 adult male Wistar rats (8 weeks old, 250–300 g) were obtained from the Animal House at Birjand University of Medical Sciences and placed in standard cages. Experimental rats were raised under normal dark and light periods (12/12 h), a normal diet, and a controlled environment with a standard rat laboratory diet. The Ethical Committee of Sabzevar University of Medical Sciences, Razavi Khorasan Province, Iran, approved the use of animals and protocol for handling and care of animals under the ethical code (IR.MEDSAB.AEC.1402.002). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals, from a random selection, were equally divided into 4 groups:

1)Control Group: Rats were daily treated with an injectable normal saline (normal saline + 1 % DMSO).

Control Group: Rats were daily treated with an injectable normal saline (normal saline + 1 % DMSO).

2)Bisphenol A Group (BPA): To induce testicular injury, rats were daily treated with BPA (50 mg/kg) [20].

Bisphenol A Group (BPA): To induce testicular injury, rats were daily treated with BPA (50 mg/kg) [20].

3)Co-treatment Group (BPA + Rg1): Rats were daily treated with BPA (50 mg/kg) and Rg1 (20 mg/kg).

Co-treatment Group (BPA + Rg1): Rats were daily treated with BPA (50 mg/kg) and Rg1 (20 mg/kg).

4)Rg1 Group: Rats were daily treated with Rg1 (20 mg/kg) [18].

Rg1 Group: Rats were daily treated with Rg1 (20 mg/kg) [18].

The rats were treated for 8 weeks, which is equivalent to the complete spermatogenesis duration in rats. In human studies, it has been reported that BPA, in a dose-dependent manner in a range from 30 pg/mL to 24.2 μg/L, could change semen quality and alter hormone levels in men [21]. Moreover, in animal studies, researchers have emphasized that higher doses of BPA have more toxic effects on the male reproductive function [22]. In our study, both BPA and Rg1 (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, BPA CAS Number: 80-05-7, Rg1 CAS Number: 22427-39-0) were dissolved in injectable normal saline (normal saline + 1 % DMSO), and both were injected simultaneously (in group 3: Rg1 in the left side and BPA in the right side of the rat's body).

At the end of the study, all rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine/xylazine (10/1 mg.kg-1) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany, CAS Number: 61763-23-3). Blood samples (3–4 mL) were collected from the heart. Following euthanization by cervical dislocation, rats were dissected, and epididymides (caudal parts) and testes were collected for histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses.

Histopathological evaluation

For the histopathological examination, the right testis was fixed in a 10 % formaldehyde solution for 48 h, followed by paraffin embedding. In each paraffin block of testicular tissue, three distinct regions (i.e., upper pole, lower pole, and middle part of the testicles) were serially sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Several slides (n = 20 for each group) were randomly selected for histopathological evaluations using a light microscope. Two independent evaluators (pathologists) were assigned for blind examination of testicular pathologies. The quantitative analysis of testicular injury was performed following the methodologies described in prior studies and by focusing on spermatogenesis as assessed by the mean Johnson's score (MJS), ranging from 1 to 10 for each seminiferous tubule [23,24]. On each slide, the mean seminiferous tubule diameter (MSTD) and the height (thickness) of the germinal epithelium (HST) were calculated in twelve randomly selected seminiferous tubules using ImageJ software (USA).

Biochemical analysis

In testicular tissues, oxidative stress markers were examined following established protocols outlined in our previous study [23]. Using commercial assay kits (Ransod and Ransel, Randox Com, UK, Cat No: RS505 and SD126), the levels of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were quantified using the ELISA method. Testicular issues were weighed, then PBS (0.05 M, PH = 7.4) containing 0.1 mM EDTA was added. After that, all tissues were homogenized (1:9 w/v) and centrifuged for 10–15 min at 2500 rpm, and the supernatants were isolated. GPx activity was measured based on the oxidation of GSH by cumene hydroperoxide, which is catalyzed by GPx, and reduced NADPH to NADP, while SOD activity was measured based on superoxide radicals generated by xanthine–xanthine oxidase reaction and a reaction with trazolium salt (INT) to form a red formazan dye. The absorbance for GPx and SOD was recorded at 340 and 505 nm, respectively. To determine malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, testicular tissue samples were homogenized in a potassium chloride solution (5.1 % w/v) at a dilution of 1:10 (w/v). Subsequently, the concentrations of MDA in the homogenized tissues were measured using the standard TBARS assay, which is a widely recognized method for quantifying lipid peroxidation. The absorbance for MDA was recorded using a spectrophotometer at 450 nM.

Hormone analysis

After centrifuging blood samples at 1500 rpm for 10–15 min, serum samples were collected and stored at −80 °C. Then, serum levels of testosterone (T, with a detection range of 0.1–25 ng/mL and a sensitivity of 0.024 ng/mL), follicle stimulating hormone (FSH, with a detection range of 3.13–200 ng/mL and a sensitivity of 1.88 ng/mL), and luteinizing hormone (LH, with a detection range of 1.56–100 ng/mL and a sensitivity of 0.94 ng/mL) were determined by ELISA using the manufacturer's instructions (Demeditec Diagnostics Company, Germany and Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., USA). In this regard, 10–100 μL of serum was used per well, and the absorbance was measured at 405 and 450 nm. The catalog numbers for testosterone, FSH, and LH kits are DEV9911, E-EL-R0391, and E-EL-R0026, respectively.

Sperm preparation and spermatozoa parameters

Following the dissection of the scrotum, the testicles and epididymides were removed. Sperm was analyzed following the WHO guidelines [25]. The caudal part of the epididymis was washed with normal saline and placed in a Petri dish containing 5 mL of Ham's F10 medium (Sigma, Germany, MDL number: MFCD00217607). The Petri dishes were incubated at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 20–30 min to facilitate the migration of spermatozoa into the medium. Following careful pipetting of semen solution, 10 μL of solution was added to 1 mL of Ham's F10 medium in a microtube. 10 μL of solution was transferred onto a clean Neubauer chamber (HBG, Germany) following swim-up under an optical microscope at 200 × magnification in four 16-well squares for sperm counting (sperm counts/mm3). The Petri dish containing sperm solution was placed under optical microscope (200 × magnification), and both progressive and non-progressive motile sperm were counted in five visual fields and calculated as a percentage [26].

Sperm staining: Eosin-Nigrosin, aniline blue, and Acridine Orange/ethidium bromide

The assessment of sperm viability was conducted using individual rat sperm samples. Each sample, containing 20 μL, was placed on a slide, and an equal volume of Eosin-Nigrosin (EN) solution was added to create a smear. After allowing the smear to dry at room temperature, stained spermatozoa were classified under a light microscope as those that appeared red were identified as dead, while those that remained unstained (white) were deemed viable [24]. Chromatin condensation is a critical indicator of the transition from histones to protamines during spermiogenesis. Therefore, to assess the percentage of spermatozoa with chromatin decondensation, Aniline blue (AB) staining was utilized. For sample preparation, air-dried smears were fixed for 30 min in a mixture of phosphate buffer (0.2 M) and glutaraldehyde solution (3 %). Following fixation, slides were stained for 5 min with aqueous AB (5 %), then spermatozoa were classified under a light microscope as immature abnormal spermatozoa (dark blue) and normal spermatozoa (pale blue) [24].

Acridine Orange (AO) and Ethidium Bromide (EB) staining (Sigma, CAS Numbers: 65-61-2 and 1239-45-8) techniques were used to evaluate DNA fragmentation in spermatozoa according to the method described by Ammar et al. (2019) [27]. Sperm samples (20 μL) were smeared on each slide and air-dried at room temperature for 10 min. The Carnoy's solution (3:1 ratio of methanol and glacial acetic acid) was poured onto each slide to fix smears. Fifteen minutes later, smears were hydrated with PBS for 1 min and immediately stained with a drop of mixed solution (1:1) of AO and EB for 1–2 min at 37 °C in the dark. The slides were washed with PBS, and several images were captured under a fluorescent microscope. Then, the captured images were evaluated and spermatozoa classified as normal (green fluorescence) and apoptotic (bright orange to red fluorescence) [24].

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA from testicular tissues was isolated using a TRIzol reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, Cat No: 1218355). Following RNA extraction, the samples were treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No: 18047019) to eliminate any genomic DNA contamination. The ratio of the absorbance at 260 and 280 nm was calculated to determine the RNA concentration of the samples. The final concentration (0.7 μg/mL) was adjusted using RNase-free water. After that, cDNA was generated using a commercial kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No: 11756050). The reaction of RT-qPCR was performed in a 48-well plate using 10 μL of SYBR Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No: A35379), 2 μL of primers (1:1), 1 μL of cDNA, and 7 μL DNase/RNase-free water. The primer sequences used in this study were previously documented [24], with the specific forward and reverse primer sequences for genes (Table 1). To normalize the expression levels of the target genes, β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the Pfaffl method.

Immunofluorescence analysis

To analyze protein expression of the examined genes, immunofluorescence staining was performed using an immunoperoxidase kit (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Germany, Cat No: sc-493, sc-783, and sc-25778, for Bax, Bcl-2, and GAPDH). The procedure included: inactivating endogenous peroxidase with 3 % H2O2, antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer, enhancing cell permeability with 0.3 % Triton, and incubating with primary antibodies against Bax, Bcl-2, and GAPDH overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were treated with a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody and stained with propidium iodide for nuclei visualization. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Negative controls received only secondary antibodies. Mean densities of Bax and Bcl-2 were quantified using ImageJ software, as in our previous study [23].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test, was conducted to compare differences among groups. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences were considered statistically significant at a p-value of less than 0.05, corresponding to a 95 % confidence level.

Results

Histological evaluations

The morphology of seminiferous tubule epithelium, spermatogenic cells, and spermatozoa appeared normal in both the control and Rg1 groups (Fig. 1). In contrast, irregularities in germ cells, seminiferous tubule atrophy, degeneration, and detachment of the epithelium were observed in the BPA-exposed rats, while Rg1 co-treatment mitigated these negative effects. Additional parameters, including HST, MSTD, and MJS (Table 2), were found to be decreased in the BPA-exposed rats (P < 0.001). In the Rg1-BPA-treated group, HST, MSTD, and MJS showed a notable increase relative to the BPA group (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the control and Rg1 groups for these criteria (P > 0.05).

Antioxidant status evaluations

To evaluate the ability of Rg1 in reducing BPA-induced ROS within testicular tissue, a number of biochemical parameters—including GPx, SOD, and MDA—were determined (Table 3). The activities of both SOD and GPx in testicular tissues were significantly decreased (P < 0.001) in the BPA group, while treatment with Rg1 resulted in a significant recovery of these enzymatic activities when compared to the BPA group (P < 0.001). In contrast, MDA levels in the testicular tissues of BPA-exposed rats were significantly elevated compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Importantly, the Rg1 + BPA group showed a significant decrease in MDA levels compared to the BPA group (P < 0.001). All the biochemical parameters mentioned above showed no significant variations between the control and Rg1-treated rats (P > 0.05).

RT-qPCR analysis

RT-qPCR was performed to investigate the effects of Rg1 and BPA on the mRNA levels of pro-apoptotic gene Bax and anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 in the testis. As shown in Table 4, mRNA levels of the Bax gene were significantly increased in the BPA-exposed group compared to the control group (P < 0.001). In the Rg1 + BPA group, a substantial reduction in Bax expression was found compared to the BPA-only group (P = 0.002). Furthermore, the mRNA levels of Bcl-2 were significantly diminished in the BPA-exposed rats compared to the control group (P = 0.003). Rg1 administration led to an increase in Bcl-2 mRNA levels when compared to the BPA group (P = 0.041). Notably, no significant differences were observed between the untreated and Rg1-treated groups regarding the expression of either gene. An analysis of the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio revealed a notable rise in this ratio within the BPA group compared to the control group (P < 0.001). When the Rg1-treated group was compared to the BPA group, a significant drop was noted (P < 0.001). No significant differences in this ratio were found between the control and Rg1 groups.

Immunofluorescence for Bax and Bcl-2 proteins

To assess the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, immunofluorescence staining was performed (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The fluorescent intensity of the Bax protein (Fig. 2) was significantly increased in the BPA-exposed group compared to the control group (P < 0.03), suggesting a rise in testicular cell death. On the other hand, in the BPA-exposed rats treated with Rg1, a remarkable reduction in Bax intensity was observed relative to the BPA-only group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected between the control and Rg1 groups. The fluorescence intensity of Bcl-2 protein was significantly decreased in the BPA-treated group compared to the control group (P < 0.03) (Fig. 3). The Rg1+BPA-treated group, however, showed higher Bcl-2 intensity compared to the BPA group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found between the control and Rg1 groups.

Hormonal analysis

Table 5 provides a comparative analysis of serum levels of LH, FSH, and T. In the BPA-treated group, when compared to the control group, significantly decreased levels of LH, FSH, and T were observed (P < 0.001). However, in rats subjected to both BPA and Rg1 treatment, a notable rise in all hormones (FSH, LH, and T) was observed in comparison to the BPA-only group (P = 0.028 for LH, P < 0.001 for FSH, and P = 0.047 for T). Furthermore, no significant differences were found in the levels of LH, FSH, and testosterone when comparing the control group with the Rg1-treated group.

Sperm parameters, sperm chromatin decondensation, and sperm apoptosis

From a functional perspective, as summarized in Table 6, BPA exposure resulted in a significant reduction in the count, motility, and viability of sperm within the caudal epididymis when compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Additionally, BPA exposure was associated with an increased incidence of sperm with abnormal morphology (P < 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 4 (first row). In contrast, treatment with Rg1 alleviated the testicular damage induced by BPA, leading to a significant enhancement in sperm count, motility, and viability (P < 0.001), as well as a marked decrease in the proportion of sperm with abnormal morphology (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the control group and the Rg1-treated group regarding sperm count, motility, and morphology (P > 0.05).

As shown in Table 6 and Fig. 4 (second row), the BPA group showed a significant increase in the proportion of AB + spermatozoa compared to the control group (P < 0.001). In contrast, the Rg1 + BPA group demonstrated a reduction in the number of AB + spermatozoa relative to the BPA group (P = 0.002). No significant differences were found when comparing the control and Rg1 groups (P > 0.05). AO/EB fluorescent staining (Fig. 4, third row) was used to evaluate the incidence of DNA damage in sperm which showed a significant increase in the proportion of sperm exhibiting DNA damage in the BPA group compared to the control group (P < 0.001). However, administration of Rg1 in the Rg1 + BPA group led to a significant reduction in the number of apoptotic sperm when compared to the BPA group (P < 0.001). No significant differences were noted between the control and Rg1 groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

BPA induces testicular cell damage in mammals. It is important to develop a mitigation strategy to protect males from testicular damage caused by BPA, as BPA was detected in the urine or blood of the majority of human subjects examined [21].

The present study evaluated the efficacy of Rg1 in alleviating testicular injury induced by BPA exposure by co-treating rats with BPA and Rg1 for 8 weeks spanning the full spermatogenic cycle in males.

The histopathological findings revealed that BPA exposure resulted in testicular degeneration, including abnormalities in the structure of seminiferous tubules and spermatogenic cells. Testicular parameters, such as mean Johnson's scores, mean seminiferous tubule diameter, and the height of seminiferous epithelium, were significantly reduced. In contrast, the administration of Rg1 in BPA-exposed rats mitigated these negative consequences. Furthermore, the testes of rats that received Rg1 alone showed normal histological structures, indicating that Rg1 did not have harmful impacts on the testis.

Several studies involving mice or rats indicated that exposure to a wide range of BPA doses (0.5–200 mg/kg) could lead to severe testicular damage, including mitochondrial dysfunction, iron accumulation, histological abnormalities, and damage to junctional proteins [[28], [29], [30]]. Huang et al. (2024) demonstrated that Rg1 therapy (20 mg/kg for 2 weeks) can ameliorate the structural damage to testicular tissue in C57BL/6 male mice subjected to heat stress [31]. Additionally, one study in C57BL/6 mice conducted by Wang et al. (2023) found that Rg1 was able to effectively decrease d-galactose-induced testicular dysfunction by enhancing the testicular weight and testicular volume and improving the morphology of testicular shape [32].

The present biochemical analyses indicated that BPA exposure resulted in elevated levels of MDA and decreased levels of SOD and GPx in testicular tissues compared to the control group. In mice, the BPA-induced oxidative damage is mostly due to lipid peroxidation within the mitochondria, which is associated with the increased iron influx and ROS overproduction through Fenton reactions resulting from mitochondrial membrane impairment [29]. MDA has been recognized as a dependable indicator for assessing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, as well as a sign of damage to testicular tissues. Thus, ROS overproduction caused by BPA exposure can contribute to a significant reduction in the levels of endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and GPx. In a study, Rg1 administration could preserve the activity of SOD and the GSH/GSSG ratio, whereas d-galactose-induced excessive ROS production and 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) levels enhanced spermatogonium apoptosis, leading to testicular injury [32]. Rg1 ameliorated heat stress-induced testicular injury by enhancing the activities of CAT and SOD, and reducing MDA production in testicular tissues [31]. The ROS generated in the testicular cells by BPA exposure was neutralized by Rg1 treatment.

In the testes of BPA-exposed rats, ROS induce apoptosis and cell death by triggering damage to both DNA and proteins. This process is mediated by the regulation of phosphorylation within the Bcl-2 protein family, disrupting the equilibrium between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins, such as Bax and Bcl-2. Li et al. (2009) demonstrated that BPA can induce germ cell apoptosis by activating intrinsic apoptotic pathways, including caspase-3, as well as extrinsic apoptotic pathways, such as the Fas/FasL signaling cascade, in the testes of mice [33].

In the present study, BPA significantly elevated the mRNA expression of Bax and the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio compared to the control group, which is further supported by the immunofluorescence staining of Bax and Bcl-2. Rg1 treatment enhanced both mRNA and protein expression of the anti-apoptotic gene, Bcl-2. In a study involving pubertal mice exposed to BPA (480 mg/kg), a significant elevation in Bax expression, cytosolic release of cytochrome c from mitochondria, and levels of active caspases-3/8/9 were observed [34]. BPA caused a marked upregulation of ferroptosis-related genes, including GPX4, FTH1, COX2, and ACSL4 in the mouse testis [29].

Rg1 injection (40 mg/kg) mitigated ROS effects and diminished the rate of cell death in spermatogonia in C57BL/6 mice exposed to d-galactose by modulating the Akt/GSK-3β/NRF2 axis and activating the Akt/Bad pathway [32]. Zhou et al. (2025) indicated that Rg1 (20 mg/kg) could reduce the elevated ratio of Bax/Bcl-2 and the activity of caspase-3 via modulating the SCF/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway; therefore, it was able to inhibit the apoptosis of testicular cells following exposure to a single dose of 5 Gy X-ray irradiation [19]. Taken together, the present results suggest that Rg1 may reduce the testicular severity caused by DNA damage.

Another part of our results showed that BPA exposure decreased sperm quality, including count, motility, and viability, while increasing sperm morphological abnormalities, chromatin decondensation, and sperm apoptosis. Rg1 administration, however, exerted its positive effects on spermatogenesis and also improved sperm quality. Acridine orange/ethidium bromide staining is used to show whether BPA increased DNA damage in sperm cells or not. When DNA is damaged, the stain is orange or red, while in normal conditions, sperm are green. Ullah et al. (2017) found that an analogue of BPA, bisphenol-S (BPS), could elevate DNA damage in sperm cells [35]. Moreover, several investigations have shown that the overproduction of ROS is positively correlated with a reduction in sperm count and motility [36,37]. Zhang et al. (2025) found that prenatal exposure of BPA (50 mg/kg) from embryonic day 0.5–18.5 dramatically reduced the number of Leydig cells and increased apoptosis, leading to sperm quality defects [38]. Wisniewski et al. (2015) reported that BPA (5 or 25 mg/kg, daily administration from postnatal day 50–105) reduced sperm production, sperm reserves in the epididymis, and sperm transit time from the epididymis, while increasing the number of sperm abnormalities [39]. Similar results have also been reported for mice that were exposed to BPA or BPB [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Serval studies on human samples showed that BPA (0.3–354 ng/mL) found in urinary was associated with abnormal sperm parameters, higher sperm DNA damage, and lower sperm concentration compared to non-exposed men [44,45], while other articles revealed that it (0.5–14.9 ng/mL) may be associated with lower sperm DNA fragmentation or had no association with sperm quality parameters [[46], [47], [48]]. Another study showed that BPA just can decrease sperm progressive motility [47].

Wang et al. (2018) found Rg1 (20 mg/kg/day, for 28 days) could significantly improve sperm morphology in D-gal-induced aging mice [18]. Another study found that treatment with Korean red ginseng, containing ginsenoside phytochemicals such as Rg1, could increase sperm motility and sperm count in rats that received cyclophosphamide and gamma-irradiation [49]. Any defects in the head, tail, and midpiece of sperm cells have been considered as a primary factor of male sterility, while poor sperm quality has been associated with a low rate of fertilization and an increased number of miscarriages post-fertilization [50]. Therefore, treatment with antioxidant supplements such as Rg1 may improve sperm quality and fertilization. This claim should be examined in human studies.

The last part of our data showed that BPA could significantly decrease serum levels of testosterone, LH, and FSH, while Rg1 therapy restored the levels of these hormones.

In normal spermatogenesis, both Leydig and Sertoli cells are required to produce testosterone and to support the viability of stem cells through their interactions with LH and FSH, separately. By reducing the number of Leydig cells or causing Sertoli cell damage via the activation of apoptotic pathways such as Fas, FasL, and caspase-3, BPA could reduce serum concentrations of testosterone, LH, and FSH, in a dose-dependent manner [33,39,51,52]. The reduction of LH and FSH may also be resulted from the downregulation of FSHR and LGCGR expression, which were found in adult male zebrafish that received BPA (500 ng/L) for about 2 months [53]. Moreover, the reduced testosterone synthesis found in an investigation conducted by Feng et al. (2012) when the injection of bisphenol AF dramatically affected the expression of genes involved in cholesterol and steroid biosynthesis [54]. Zhao et al. (2025) revealed that a one-week dose of BPA (20 mg/kg) could elevate the mRNA expression of Apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1), disrupting cholesterol transport, and resulting in testosterone synthesis inhibition [55]. In addition, BPA by targeting the JNK/c-Jun signaling pathway or decreasing cAMP production in Leydig cells can alter the normal function of these cells, leading to testosterone production reduction [56,57]. Exposure to BPA, BPS, and BPF could also alter the production of testosterone by inhibiting the mRNA expression of essential genes involved in steroidogenesis including CYP17A1, 3β-HSD, and StAR [58]. In human studies, Hanaoka et al. (2002) found that urinary BPA concentration (0–11.2 ng/mL) had no impact on LH concentration but decreased FSH [59], while other studies showed that BPA was not associated with FSH, LH, or T levels [48,60]. These studies suggested that the effect of BPA on serum hormone levels involved in male reproductive function is controversial.

On the other hand, an in vitro study reported that ginseng extracts could sustain normal production of testosterone in TM3 Leydig treated with BPA [61]. In mice exposed to cranial radiation, Rg1 therapy by affecting hormonal synthesis from the HPG axis could elevate the levels of GnRH and FSH through inhibiting the SCF/C-Kit signaling pathway [19]. Xu et al. (2020) found that Rg1 could increase levels of testosterone and LH, while decreasing FSH levels in C57BL/6J males with reproductive dysfunction induced by dinbutyl phthalate (DBP) [62]. Therefore, to find the exact mechanism by which how Rg1 can increase the secretions of LH, FSH, and testosterone, we suggest further studies to investigate the function of the HPG axis and genes involved in steroidogenesis.

Conclusion

Our current research showed that Rg1 therapy effectively mitigated testicular dysfunction and sperm injury in BPA-exposed rats. Rg1, as an antioxidant source, could diminish the production of MDA and the indicator of cell death, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, whereas markedly increasing the expression of Bcl-2 and activities of SOD and GPx in testicular tissues. In addition, treatment with Rg1 by elevating serum levels of LH, FSH, and testosterone could alleviate the impaired spermatogenesis. Moreover, it can successfully improve both sperm quality and quantity by enhancing the viability of sperm and reducing the abnormal morphology, chromatin decondensation, and DNA fragmentation of sperm cells. For further studies, our recommendation is that to clarify the precise role of BPA and Rg1 in spermatogenesis, other important signaling pathways, especially those involved in steroidogenesis and apoptosis, should be explored.

Study limitations

As we had financial constraints, we could not investigate the effects of both BPA and Rg1 on genes involved in apoptosis and steroidogenesis to find their impacts on germ cell death and testosterone synthesis. Also, the effects of other dosages of BPA and Rg1 on testicular function were not investigated owing to financial problems.
