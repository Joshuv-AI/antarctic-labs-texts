Families of Morse functions for manifolds with boundary
Maciej Borodzik, Weronika Buczyńska

Abstract
. We study 1-parameter families of Morse functions for manifolds with boundary. We list all degeneracies that may occur in generic 1-parameter families.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
primary: 57Q60.

1.
Introduction

Morse theory for manifolds with boundary was developed by Hajduk
[
Haj81
]
and Jankowski–Rubinsztein
[
JR72
]

in the 1970s, and rediscovered by
Kronheimer and Mrowka

[
KM07
]
. Despite formal similarities to the classical case of closed manifolds, the theory is more complex.
The analog of Morse–Smale–Witten chain complex for manifolds with boundary involves non-trivial terms coming from
critical points on the boundary; see
[
Haj81
,
KM07
]
.
In
[
BNR16
]
it was proved that a critical point in the interior can be pushed into the boundary, where it splits into two boundary critical points. This phenomenon, which we will refer to as
boundary splitting
was observed first by
[
Haj81
]
. In
[
BM25
]
, an opposite phenomenon was studied: given two boundary critical points such that there is a single trajectory of a gradient-like vector field connecting them, one can collide these critical points and push them into the interior. We refer to
[
BM25
]
for a precise statement.

Families of Morse functions on closed manifolds were extensively studied by Cerf
[
Cer70
]
. However, even one-parameter families of Morse functions on manifolds with boundary have not been described in the literature. A natural question arises: Is it true that a typical one-parameter family of functions consists of Morse functions and finitely many occurrences of births/deaths and boundary splittings?

In this paper, we answer this question negatively. We study 1-parameter families of smooth functions on manifolds with boundary and investigate non-Morse singularities that appear generically (there is a subtlety in what we mean by a generic
1
-parameter family, see Remark
2.4
). Among them, as expected, there are birth/deaths of pairs of critical points in the interior, births/deaths of pairs of boundary critical points. However, there is an extra phenomenon: an interior critical point collides with a boundary critical point, and then the critical point on the boundary changes its stability. Surprisingly, this phenomenon – unlike boundary splitting – is a codimension 1 behavior. We call it a
collision
and describe in detail in Subsection
3.4
.

Our main theorem describes the full list of bifurcations that can occur in generic one-parameter families. It is stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N
be a smooth compact manifold with boundary.
Suppose
F_{0},F_{1}\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
be two Morse functions.
Assume
F_{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
is a path connecting these functions. Then, up to perturbing
F_{\sigma}
rel
F_{0},F_{1}
, we can assume that

•

there exists a finitely many points
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}
such that
F_{\sigma}
is Morse if
\sigma
is different from
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}
;

•

at each of
\sigma_{i}
, there is precisely one non-Morse critical pint. The change between
F_{\sigma_{i}-\varepsilon}
and
F_{\sigma_{i}+\varepsilon}
(with
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small) is one of the following:

–

birth/death of critical points in the interior;

–

birth/death of critical points on the boundary;

–

a collision of a boundary and interior critical point.

We refer to Subsection
3.4
for a detailed description of these three events. As an example,
we will describe the boundary splitting
as a codimension 2 behavior and show how it can be presented as a sequence of codimension 1 events, see Subsection
4.2
.

The main idea of the proof is to pass from a function on
N
to a function on its double and consider it as a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant function. Then, we study codimension 1 singularities of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant functions via equivariant Thom transversality theorem
as in
[
Wal85
]
.

The structure of the paper is the following. In Section
2
,
we recall basic properties of Morse functions for manifolds with boundary. We notice that there are essentially three non-equivalent notions of a Morse function for manifolds with boundary. We give a translation between the two of the three notions.
Finally, we recall equivariant jet theory of
[
Wal85
]
. It gives a framework for stating and proving genericity functions. Section
3
develops theory of families of equivariant Morse functions. Theorem
1.1
is proved in Section
4
. As an example, we show how boundary splitting can be decomposed into a birth and a collision in Subsection
4.2
.

2.
Boundary Morse functions

2.1.
Definitions

Given a closed manifold
X
, a function
F\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is called
Morse
, if for
any
p\in\operatorname{Crit}(F)
,
\det D^{2}F(p)\neq 0
.
Hereafter,
\operatorname{Crit}(F)
is the set of critical points of a function. If
DF(p)=0
, then the condition
\det D^{2}F(p)\neq 0

is independent on the choice of coordinates.

Following
[
BNR16
]
we extend this definition for manifolds with boundary.
Let
N
be a smooth manifold with boundary.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
F\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is
boundary Morse
if

(BM-1)

for any
p\in N
, if
DF(p)=0
, then
\det D^{2}F(p)\neq 0
;

(BM-2)

for any
p\in\partial N
, with
f:=F|_{\partial N}
, if
Df(p)=0
, then
DF(p)=0
.

For future reference, we recall the boundary Morse lemma, see
[
BNR16
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
p\in\partial N
is a critical point of a boundary Morse function. Then, there are local coordinates near
p
,

x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}
, and choices of signs
\epsilon_{x},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1}
, such that
\partial N=\{x=0\}
and

(2.1)

F(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})=F(p)+\epsilon_{x}x^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\epsilon_{i}y_{i}^{2}.

We pause for the moment to clarify different variants of Definition
2.1
.
Hajduk’s
m
-functions satisfy
(BM-1)
, but a critical point of
f
is assumed
not to be
a critical point of
F
,
so the opposite of
(BM-2)
is assumed. That is,
m
-functions share more properties of embedded Morse functions, see
[
BP16
]
.
In particular, a boundary Morse lemma for
m
-function should have term
x^{2}
replaced by
x
, as in the case of embedded Morse
functions, compare
[
BP16
, Lemma 2.16]
.

We will see below,
Hajduk’s
m
-function are more natural in the sense that the set of
m
-functions in open-dense, unlike the set of boundary Morse functions.
As a drawback,
analogs of gradient-like vector fields in that case have to use embedded gradient-like vector fields, see
[
BP16
, Section 3]
.
The latter
are not linearizable at critical points on
\partial N
. This leads to a more complicated local behavior of the trajectories of gradient-like vector fields for
m
-functions.

In
[
KM07
, Section 2.4]
, Kronheimer and Mrowka use another definition, namely they consider the double
M
of
N
and look at functions
on
N
that are restrictions of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
equivariant functions on
M
.
This approach is suitable for studying degenerations of Morse functions, via equivariant jet transversality theorem. As the main drawback we note that not all ‘regular’ functions on
N
extend to smooth symmetric functions on
M
. So the definitions seems rather restrictive.

Definition
2.1
combines drawbacks and highlights of the two other approaches. Namely, studying gradient-like vector fields
is the easiest, and the conditions of Definition
2.1
are less restrictive than those in
[
KM07
]
. On the other
hand, as we shall see in Proposition
2.3
, functions satisfying
(BM-2)
are not open-dense in the space of all Morse functions. This means that
studying families of Morse functions via transversality theory is substantially harder.

In Subsection
2.3
, we explain how to pass from a boundary Morse function of Definition
2.1
to
a Morse function in the sense of
[
KM07
]
. The passage will be done in a precise way, in particular not modifying the function near
boundary critical points. We will then study deformations of equivariant functions leading to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We conclude Subsection
2.1
with the following observation. For a manifold with boundary
N
, denote
\mathcal{F}(N)=C^{\infty}(N;\mathbb{R})
, and let
\mathcal{B}(N)\subset\mathcal{F}(N)

be the subspace of boundary Morse functions.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\partial N\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{B}(N)
is neither open nor dense in
\mathcal{F}(N)
.

Proof.

The culprit is the specific condition
(BM-2)
. Indeed, if
p\in\partial N
is such that
DF(p)=0
, then

(BM-2)
imposes that
\partial_{\vec{n}}F(p)=0
, where
\vec{n}\in T_{p}N\setminus\{0\}
is normal to
\partial N
. This is a closed
condition, and it
leads to failure of both openness and density.

To see that failure, we construct a function that satisfies
(BM-1)
, but not
(BM-2)
, and show
that no function in its neighborhood satisfies
(BM-2)
.
Suppose
F\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is a boundary Morse function and
p\in\partial N
is a critical point. Choose
local coordinates
(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
near
p
, so that
\partial N=\{x=0\}
and
F
has form
(
2.1
). Choose a smooth function
\eta\colon N\to[0,1]

near
p
, equal to
1
in a neighborhood of
p
and supported in a set where the local coordinates are defined.
Let
\widetilde{F}=F+\varepsilon x\eta
. Then,
\widetilde{F}
fails to
(BM-2)
, indeed,
there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
\frac{\partial\widetilde{F}}{\partial x}\geq\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
on
U
.

Suppose
F^{\prime}
is close to
\widetilde{F}
, that is
\operatorname{dist}_{C^{2}}(F^{\prime},\widetilde{F})<\delta
for
\delta>0
sufficiently small. In particular, if
\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
, then

\frac{\partial F^{\prime}}{\partial x}>\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
everywhere on
U
. Next,
\operatorname{dist}_{C^{2}}(F^{\prime}|_{\partial N},F|_{\partial N})<\delta
. The function
F|_{\partial N}

is Morse in the ordinary sense, which is an open condition in the
C^{2}
-norm. If
\delta
is sufficiently small,
then
F^{\prime}|_{\partial N}
must have a Morse critical point in
U\cap\partial N
.
Hence,
F^{\prime}
is not Morse, because it does not satisfy
(BM-2)
.

This shows that an open neighborhood of
F^{\prime}
consists of non-Morse functions, leading to the failure of density. Also, by taking

\widetilde{F}_{n}=F+\frac{1}{n}x\eta
, we obtain a sequence of functions failing to
(BM-2)
converging to the Morse function
F
.
That is,
\mathcal{B}(N)
is not open.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Failure to openness and density of boundary Morse functions makes it hard to define precisely what is a
generic path of boundary Morse
functions
, Formally one would have to consider e.g. generic functions withing the closure of boundary Morse functions in the space of all functions. We do not find applications for this rather delicate notion. In the present paper, we consider mostly paths of functions
on
N
that are restrictions of paths of equivariant functions on the double of
N
.

2.2.
Index of a critical point

Suppose
F\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is a Morse function for a manifold with boundary.
Assume
p
is a critical point of
F
.

The index of a critical point
p
,
\operatorname{ind}p
is by definition the dimension
of the negative subspace of
D^{2}F(p)
: the index of local mininum is equal to
0
,
the index of a local maximum is equal to
\dim N
.

If
p\in\partial N
is a critical point, we can also consider the restriction
f:=F|_{\partial N}
and consider

\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p
to be the dimension of the negative subspace of
D^{2}f(p)
. Informally,

\operatorname{ind}p=\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p
if the function
N
is increasing when going from
p
to
the interior, while
\operatorname{ind}p=\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p+1
if
F
is decreasing in the normal direction.

Definition 2.5
.

A critical point
p\in\partial N
is called
boundary stable
if

\operatorname{ind}p=\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p+1
. It is called
boundary unstable
if
\operatorname{ind}p=\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p
; see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Boundary stable (left) and boundary unstable (right) critical points. We have sketched the flow of the gradient of
the corresponding Morse function.

In
[
KM07
,
BNR16
]
, boundary stability was defined using gradient vector fields. In that setting, a
critical point
p
is boundary stable if the unstable manifold of
p
is contained
in
\partial N
. Here, unstable manifold is with respect to vector field
\nabla F

(
[
KM07
]
uses
-\nabla F
). The two definitions are equivalent. In fact,
if
\operatorname{ind}p=\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p
, then the negative definite part of
D^{2}F(p)
is
contained in
T_{p}\partial N
. This implies that the tangent space to the stable
manifold of
\nabla F
at
p
is contained in
T_{p}\partial N
. As
[
KM07
,
BNR16
]
it was assumed that
\nabla F
is tangent to
\partial N
, it quickly follows that
W^{s}(p)
is contained in
\partial N
, so the critical point is boundary unstable.

The advantage of Definition
2.5
is that it does not require introducing any auxilliary objects such as a Riemannian metric or a gradient-like vector field.

2.3.
Doublable functions

We now realize our plan to compare boundary Morse functions of Definition
2.1
with
the approach of
[
KM07
]
.
Let
N
be a manifold with boundary. The
double
of
M:=\mathcal{D}(N)
is the boundary connected sum of a manifold with a copy itself,
\mathcal{D}(N)=N\cup_{\partial N}N
. To form a double, we fix an identification
\tau
of
N
with its copy. This identification leads to an involution
\tau\colon M\to M
.
With that choice,
M
has a structure of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
manifold. The fixed point
set
M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
is
\partial N
.

Any continuous function
P\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
can be extended to a function
\Phi\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
by declaring that
\Phi(z)=\Phi(\tau z)=P(z)
,
for all
z\in N
. The extension
\Phi
is continuous, but not necessarily smooth.

Definition 2.6
.

A function
P\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is called
doublable
if the extension
\Phi
of
P
to
M
is a smooth function.

We define
\mathcal{P}(N)
to be the set of doublable functions.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose
F\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is a boundary Morse function. Then, there exists a function
P\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties.

(F-1)

P
is doublable;

(F-2)

\operatorname{dist}_{C^{0}}(F,P)
can be as small as we please, moreover
F
agrees with
P
away from an arbitrarily small
neighborhood of
\partial N
;

(F-3)

P
has the same set of critical points as
F
;

(F-4)

P
agrees with
F
in a neighborhood of these critical points;

(F-5)

there exists a family
F_{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
of boundary Morse functions, such that

F_{0}=F
,
F_{1}=P
and
F_{\sigma}

is independent of
\sigma
near any critical point of
F
.

Proof.

By the collar theorem, there exists a neighborhood
V
of
\partial N
such that
V
can be identified
with
\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
.
We write
\theta\colon V\to\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
.

For each critical point
p\in\partial N
of
F
we fix a neighborhood
U_{p}
on which
F

has local coordinates as in boundary Morse Lemma
2.2
.
We assume the identification
\theta
takes
U_{p}\cap V
to

\left(U_{p}\cap\partial N\right)\times[0,\varepsilon]
via
\theta(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})=(0,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})\times\{x\}
.

This specific property can be achieved by constructing
\theta
as follows: choose a Riemannian metric
and for each
z\in N
consider the nearest point
z^{\prime}\in\partial N
on the boundary (with the smallest distance).
By the implicit function theorem this assignment is well-defined on a neighborhood
V
of
\partial N
(the
neighborhood depends on the metric). We define
\theta(z)=(z^{\prime},\operatorname{dist}(z,z^{\prime}))
.

Having fixed the identification, we use Hadamard Lemma to write

F(z,x)=F(z,0)+xG(z,x).

for
(z,x)\in\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
, where
G
is a smooth function.
Now choose a bump function
\eta\colon N\to[0,1]
equal to
0
away from
\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon/2]
.
Our aim is to set

(2.2)

P(z,x)=F(z,0)+(1-\eta)xG(z,x).

That is,
P(z,x)=F(z,x)
on
\partial N\times[\varepsilon/2,\varepsilon)
and setting
P
equal to
F

away from
V
leads to a smooth function.
The choice of
\eta
has to be made carefully, since we want
P=F
near critical points of
F
.

For a critical point
p\in\partial N
, let
U^{\prime}_{p}
be a neighborhood such that
\overline{U^{\prime}_{p}}\subset U_{p}

and
U^{\prime}_{p}
has product structure,

U^{\prime}_{p}=(U^{\prime}_{p}\cap\partial N)\times[0,\delta)
, where
\delta>\varepsilon

(we can first choose
\delta
and then
\varepsilon
).
Define
\eta
to be
\eta_{0}\times\rho
, where
\eta_{0}\colon\partial N\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\rho\colon[0,\varepsilon)\to[0,1]
are such that

(N-1)

\rho\equiv 1
on
[0,\varepsilon/4]
;

(N-2)

\rho\equiv 0
away from
[0,\varepsilon/2]
;

(N-3)

\eta_{0}\equiv 1
away from
\bigcup U_{p}
;

(N-4)

\eta_{0}\equiv 0
on
\bigcup U^{\prime}_{p}
.

Furthermore, we can assume that
|D\eta|<C_{\eta}\varepsilon^{-1}
for some constant

C_{\eta}>0
. That constant does not depend on
\varepsilon
, but can depend on
U_{p}
and
U^{\prime}_{p}
, in general
it might depend on the distance between boundaries of
U_{p}
and
U^{\prime}_{p}
. Now we define
P
via (
2.2
),
and check all the required properties.

Checking
(F-1)
.

A smooth function
G\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is doublable if its restriction to
\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
satisfies

\frac{\partial^{k}G}{\partial x^{k}}(z,0)=0
for all
z\in\partial N
and for all odd
k\geq 1
.
Our
P
satisfies this condition for all
k
odd.
Indeed, if
z\notin\bigcup U_{p}
, then
P(z,x)=P(z,0)
for all
x
sufficiently small.

Suppose
z\in U_{p}
. The specific form of
\eta=\eta_{0}\rho
together with
(N-1)
,
implies that
\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial x^{k}}\eta(z,0)=0
for all
x
. Furthermore, by boundary Morse lemma,

xG(z,x)=\pm x^{2}
,
where the sign depends on boundary stability of the critical point
p
.
It is routine to check that
\frac{\partial^{k}P}{\partial x^{k}}(z,0)=0

for all
k\neq 0,2
.

Checking
(F-2)
.
Notice that
\operatorname{dist}_{C^{0}(N;\mathbb{R})}(F,P)\leq\sup t|G(z,t)|
, where
the supremum is taken over the
(z,t)\in\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
.
To show that this can be made as small as we please, notice
that by the mean value theorem applied to
x\mapsto F(z,x)

G(z,x)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}(z,\zeta_{x})
for some
\zeta_{x}\in(0,x)
. This means that
||G(z,t)||\leq||F(z,t)||_{C^{1}}
.
Hence, the distance between
F
and
P
in the
C^{0}
-norm is bounded by
\varepsilon||F||_{C^{1}}
.

Checking
(F-3)
and
(F-4)
. The number of critical points of
F
is finite, hence,
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, the region
\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)
contains
only critical points of
F
on
\partial N
. From this we conclude that there is a global constant
c

such that
||DF(z,0)||>c
for all
z\in\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)\setminus\bigcup U^{\prime}_{p}
. Suppose
||DG||<C
.
On choosing
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon C<c
, we achieve that the derivative of
P

in the
z
-directions is non-zero. That is,
P
has no critical points on
\partial N\times[0,\varepsilon)

away from
\bigcup U^{\prime}_{p}
, but on each
U^{\prime}_{p}
,
P=F
.
Given
(F-3)
, item
(F-4)
is trivial, because
P=F
near critical points of
F
.

Checking
(F-5)
.
We define
F_{\sigma}(z,x)=F(z,0)+(1-\sigma\eta(z,x))xG(z,x)
. The proof that
F_{\sigma}
has no critical
points other than
F
follows the same pattern as the proof of
(F-3)
above.
Also,
F_{\sigma}=F
on each of the
U^{\prime}_{p}
,
F_{0}=F

and
F_{1}=P
.
∎

2.4.
Equivariant Morse theory

Morse theory for functions on manifolds with group actions was studied by Wassermann
[
Was69
, Section 4]
.
We quote a few results, restricted to our specific situation of
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
\dim M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}=\dim M-1
. General results can be found in
[
Was69
]
.
Throughout the subsection, we assume that
M
is a smooth closed manifold, acted upon by
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
We denote by
\tau\colon M\to M
the involution.

Definition 2.8
.

Suppose
\Phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is an equivariant function.
We say
f
is
equivariant Morse
if for any critical point
p
of
\Phi
,
D^{2}\Phi(p)
is non-degenerate.

The definition is analogous to all previous definitions of Morse functions.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose
P\colon N\to\mathbb{R}
is a doublable function. Let
M
be the double of
N
and
\Phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
be
the extension of
P
to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-invariant function on
M
.
Then
P
is boundary Morse if and only if the function
\Phi
is equivariant Morse.

Proof.

The proof is a routine checking of the axioms, we leave them to the reader.
∎

There is an equivariant version of Morse lemma.

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose
\Phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is an equivariant Morse function. There exists
local coordinates
(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})
near
p
and a choice of signs

\epsilon_{x},\epsilon_{y_{1}}\dots,\epsilon_{y_{n-1}}\in\{-1,1\}
such that

\Phi(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})=\Phi(p)+\epsilon_{x}x^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\epsilon_{y_{j}}y_{j}^{2}.

•

if
\tau p\neq p
, then
\tau
takes
x
to
x
and
y_{j}
to
y_{j}
,

•

if
\tau p=p
, then
\tau
takes
x
to
-x
and
y_{j}
to
y_{j}
.

The above lemma follows from a more general statement, see
[
Was69
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Wassermann proves also the following result; see
[
Was69
, Lemma 4.8]

Lemma 2.11
.

Equivariant Morse functions are open-dense in the space
C^{\infty}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(M;\mathbb{R})
of smooth equivariant functions.

Corollary 2.12
.

The space of doublable boundary Morse functions is open-dense in the space
\mathcal{P}(N)
of smooth doublable functions.

2.5.
Equivariant jet spaces

Equivariant jet spaces are considered in
[
Wal85
]
. There,
G
is a compact Lie group.
We recall the general setting and then specialize to
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Suppose
V,W

3.
Cerf theory for manifolds with boundary

3.1.
Decomposing space of equivariant functions

Suppose
M
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-manifold obtained by doubling
N
a compact manifold with boundary.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
\Phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant function,
p\in M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
. Then
the matrix of second derivatives
D^{2}\Phi(p)
has block structure,

D^{2}\Phi(p)=A\oplus B
, where
A
is the matrix of derivatives with respect to
y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}

and
B
is the
1\times 1
matrix with entry
\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x^{2}}(p)
.

Proof.

The statement is equivalent to
\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x\partial y_{i}}(p)=0
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
.
To see this, consider the function
H_{i}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x\partial y_{i}}
. As
\Phi
is
\tau
-invariant and

\tau x=-x
, while
\tau y_{i}=y_{i}
, we note that
H_{i}(\tau z)=-H_{i}(z)
.
Now
p
is fixed by
\tau
, so
H_{i}(p)=0
as desired.
∎

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the space of all smooth equivariant functions from
M
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Inside
\mathcal{F}
we will specify several subspaces. Our convention is that the superscript refers to codimension of
a given condition. The tilde over a subspace indicates that the space is given by a condition, but the relevant space
will be defined as a relatively open set in the tilded space.

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{0}
is the subspace of functions
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\det D^{2}\Phi(p)\neq 0
for any point
p\in M
such that
D\Phi(p)=0
;

•

The space
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}
is the subspace of
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying

\det D^{2}\Phi(p)\neq 0
for all but one
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-orbit of critical points
however, there exists a single orbit
p_{0},\tau p_{0}\in M
with
D\Phi(p_{0})=0
such that
\dim\ker D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})=1
;

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{1}
is the space of
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\det D^{2}\Phi(p)=0
for more than
1
orbit of critical points of
\Phi
;

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
is the space of
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
such that there exists an orbit of critical points
p,\tau p
with
D\Phi(p)=0
and
\dim\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)>1
.

Note that the spaces
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
are not mutually disjoint.

We decompose the space
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}
into two subspaces

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{1}
— when the point
p_{0}
is the interior point of
N

•

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
— when
p_{0}
is on the boudary.

We describe
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{1}
in more detail.
Let us fix a vector
v\in T_{p_{0}}M
such that
v\in\ker D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})
. We declare

•

\Phi\in\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1}
if
D^{3}\Phi(p_{0})
evaluated at
(v,v,v)
is non-zero;

•

\Phi\in\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{3}
if
D^{3}\Phi(p_{0})
evaluated at
(v,v,v)
is zero.

If
p_{0}\in\partial N
, we need to specify the position of the vector
v
generating
\ker D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})
with respect
to the
\tau
-action.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\Phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is an equivariant function such that
\dim\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)=1
for
a point
p\in\partial N=M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
. Let
v\in T_{p}M
be a vector
spanning
\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)
. Then either
\tau v=v
or
\tau v=-v
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, there is a decomposition
T_{p}M=T_{p}M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\oplus V

with a one-dimensional
V
, such that
D^{2}\Phi(p)
has a compatible block structure.
Here,
\tau
fixes
T_{p}M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
and acts by multiplication by
-1
on
V
.

Write
A
and
B
for the two blocks. The dimension of the kernel of a block sum of matrices is the sum
of dimensions of the kernels of the summands, so either
\dim\ker A=1
and
\dim\ker B=0
,
or vice versa. The first possibility means that
\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)\subset T_{p}M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
, so with
v
spanning this kernel,

\tau v=v
. The other
possibility is that
\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)\subset V
, leading to
\tau v=-v
.
∎

Lemma
3.2
allows us to specify two subspaces of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
. We keep using the notation
v
for a vector generating
\ker D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})
.

•

The space
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,1}
is the space of functions in
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
such
p_{0}\in M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
and
\tau v=v
;

•

The space
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
is the space of functions in
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
such that
p_{0}\in M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
and
\tau v=-v
.

The spaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
describe different kinds of degeneracies of functions.
We set
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,1}=\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1}\cup\mathcal{F}^{2}_{3}
, where

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1}
is defined by the condition
D^{3}\Phi(p_{0})(v,v,v)\neq 0
.

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{3}
is defined by the condition that
D^{3}\Phi(p_{0})(v,v,v)=0
.

In local coordinates
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},x)
, if
D^{2}\Phi(p)
is diagonal and
\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial y_{n-1}^{2}}(p_{0})=0
,
the two spaces are distinguished by vanishing or not of
\frac{\partial^{3}\Phi}{\partial y_{n-1}^{3}}(p_{0})
.

While it is tempting to copy these conditions to the case
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
, we hit the following problem:
by equivariance,
\frac{\partial^{3}\Phi}{\partial x^{3}}(p_{0})=0
.
Merely studying
\frac{\partial^{4}\Phi}{\partial x^{4}}(p_{0})
is not enough: in the presence of
terms like
\frac{\partial^{3}\Phi}{\partial x^{2}\partial y_{i}}
, the fourth derivative might
depend on the choice of coordinates. Therefore, we make the following definition.

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose
\Phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
and
p_{0}
is the critical point with
\det D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})=0
.
The
quasi-homogeneous quadratic form

B\Phi(p_{0})
of
\Phi

is given by

B\Phi(a,b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1})=\alpha a^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}ab_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{ij}b_{i}b_{j},

where
\alpha=\frac{\partial^{4}\Phi}{\partial x^{4}}(p_{0})
,
\alpha_{i}=\frac{\partial^{3}\Phi}{\partial x^{2}\partial y_{i}}(p_{0})
,

\alpha_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(p_{0})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\Phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
, then vanishing of
\det(B\Phi)
is independent of coordinate changes.

Proof.

The statement and the proof are similar to the well-known fact that the quadratic form

D^{2}\Phi(p)
is well-behaved under the change of local coordinates as long as
D\Phi(p)=0
.
For the proof, let us assign weight
2
to
y_{i}
and weight
1
to
x
. If
\Phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
,
we see that
B\Phi(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})
is the least degree term of the Taylor
expansion of
\Phi
.
Define the matrix
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
with

\gamma_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}
if
i,j\leq n-1
,
\gamma_{in}=\gamma_{ni}=\alpha_{i}
,

\gamma_{nn}=\alpha
. With
\vec{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},x^{2})
, we have

B\Phi(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})=\vec{y}\Gamma\vec{y}^{T}.

Suppose
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{y}_{n-1})
are other coordinates
transformed equivariantly by
y_{i}=\sum a_{ij}\widetilde{y}_{j}+a_{j}\widetilde{x}^{2}+\dots
,

x=a\widetilde{x}+\dots
with higher order terms meaning higher order terms
with respect to the weighted degree. Define
C
as the
n\times n
matrix of the derivatives
of the change of variables, that is
C=\{c_{ij}\}
, where
c_{ij}=a_{ij}

if
i,j\leq n-1
,
c_{nj}=0
,
c_{jn}=a_{j}
for
j\leq n-1
,
c_{nn}=a
.
Also, define
E
to be the matrix as
C
, but with
a^{2}
on the
(n,n)
-place.
We write
C^{\prime}
for the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
minor of
C
obtained by deleting the last row and the last column.
It is straightforward to see that
\det C=a\det C^{\prime}
,
\det E=c^{2}\det C^{\prime}

so
C
is non-degenerate if and only if
E
is.

It can be verified that

B\Phi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{y}_{n-1})=\vec{\widetilde{y}}E\Gamma E^{T}\vec{\widetilde{y}}^{T},

where
\vec{\widetilde{y}}=(\widetilde{y}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{y}_{n-1},\widetilde{x}^{2})
.
∎

Given Lemma
3.4
, we specify two subspaces of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
:

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
is defined by
\Phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
for which
B\Phi
is non-degenerate;

•

The space
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{5}
is defined by
\Phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2,2}
for which
B\Phi
has non-trivial kernel.

3.2.
Counting dimensions

We are now in position to apply Equivariant Transversality Theorem
2.14
to restrict potential singularities
of a function. To state the result, we introduce one more quantity.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
W
is a submanifold in the jet space
\mathfrak{I}_{G}^{k,s}(M;\mathbb{R})
.
Suppose
\mathcal{F}_{W}
is the subset of equivariant functions whose degree
k
multijet extension misses
W
.

(a)

If
\operatorname{codim}W>s\dim M
, then
\mathcal{F}_{W}
is residual in
\mathcal{F}
;

(b)

If
\operatorname{codim}W=1+s\dim M
, and
\Phi_{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
, is such that
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1}
miss
\mathcal{F}_{W}
,
then
\Phi_{\sigma}
can be perturbed rel
0,1
to a path that hits
\mathcal{F}_{W}
transversally finitely many times;

(c)

If
\operatorname{codim}W>1+s\dim M
, and
\Phi_{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
, is such that
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1}
miss
\mathcal{F}_{W}
,
then
\Phi_{\sigma}
can be perturbed rel
0,1
to a path that misses
\mathcal{F}_{W}
.

Proof.

To prove (a), it is enough to show that if
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
is such that
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}\Phi
is transverse to
W

at a point
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}\Phi(z_{1},\dots,z_{s})
,
then
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}\Phi(z_{1},\dots,z_{s})
misses
W
. The domain of the map
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}
is
M_{0}^{s}
, which is
of dimension
s\dim M
.
Hence, by transversality, the image of
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}
misses
W
.

An analogous parameter-counting argument works for (b) and (c), however
a formal proof of (b) and (c) requires a technical step. It is well-known to the experts, so we provide only a sketch.
Let
\widetilde{M}=M\times[0,1]
with the
G
-action induced from
the action on
M
and a trivial action on the interval. We define
\widetilde{M}^{s}_{0}
to be the set of pairs
of points
(z_{1},\sigma_{1}),\dots,(z_{s},\sigma_{s})
such that
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
if
i\neq j
, but
\sigma_{1}=\dots=\sigma_{s}
.
Then,
\dim\widetilde{M}=1+s\dim M
.

The space
\mathfrak{I}_{G}^{k,s}(\widetilde{M};\mathbb{R})
fibers over
\mathfrak{I}_{G}^{k,s}(M;\mathbb{R})
. This can be seen locally: take the Taylor expansion
of a function on
M\times[0,1]
and set all terms corresponding to
[0,1]
-variable to
0
.
Let
\widetilde{W}
be the preimage of
W
under the map.
It is a codimension
\operatorname{codim}W
submanifold of
\mathfrak{I}_{G}^{k,s}(\widetilde{M};\mathbb{R})
.
Define
\widetilde{W}_{0}=\widetilde{W}\cap\mathfrak{a}^{-1}(\widetilde{M}^{s}_{0})
.
Now,
\operatorname{codim}\widetilde{W}_{0}=s-1+\operatorname{codim}W
, because equality of the
\sigma_{i}
coordinates gives

s-1
conditions.

Given this notation, a family
\Phi_{\sigma}
as in items (b) and (c) induces a map
\widetilde{\Phi}\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathbb{R}

via
\widetilde{\Phi}(z,\sigma)=\Phi_{\sigma}(z)
. This map has its multijet extension

\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}_{G}\widetilde{\Phi}\colon\widetilde{M}^{s}\to\mathfrak{I}_{G}^{k,s}(\widetilde{M};\mathbb{R}).

Its image is of dimension
\dim\widetilde{M}^{s}=s+s\dim M
. If
s-1+\operatorname{codim}W=s+s\dim M
,
then
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}_{G}\widetilde{\Phi}
intersects

\widetilde{W}_{0}
at finitely many times, each such intersection corresponds to
a parameter
\sigma
for which
\mathfrak{j}^{k,s}\Phi_{\sigma}
hits
W
.

If
\dim\widetilde{M}^{s}<\operatorname{codim}\widetilde{W}_{0}
, then a residual set of maps
\widetilde{\Phi}\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathbb{R}
has
multijet extension missing
\widetilde{W}_{0}
. This
implies that
\Phi_{\sigma}
can be perturbed rel boundary to a path that misses
W
.
∎

As a corollary, we have the following result.

Proposition 3.6
.

(a)

The set of equivariant maps in
\mathcal{F}^{0}
is residual;

(b)

Any path
\Phi_{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in[0,1]
such that
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1}\in\mathcal{F}^{0}
can be perturbed to a path that

–

\Phi_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{F}^{0}
for all but finitely many
\sigma
,

–

\Phi_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1}\cup\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
for finitely many
\sigma
,

–

\Phi_{\sigma}
misses
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{3}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{4}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{5}
.

Proof.

The proof relies on a parameter counting argument and Lemma
3.5
.

First note that the complement of
\mathcal{F}^{0}
is the sum of spaces

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{1}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
.
A case-by-case analysis reveals that
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
are defined by a codimension

conditions, while

\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
are defined by codimension
2
conditions.

For
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{1}
this is done as follows. Choose a point
p_{0}\notin M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
and local coordinates

y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
near it. The conditions defining
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{1}
at that point (that is the intersection
of the manifold
W\subset I^{2,1}_{G}(M;\mathbb{R})
with
\mathfrak{b}^{-1}(p_{0})
) are

•

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_{i}}(p_{0})=0
for
i=1,\dots,n
;

•

\det D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})=0
.

Therefore, the manifold
W\subset I^{2,1}_{G}(M;\mathbb{R})
is of codimension
n+1
.

For
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
, we choose
p_{0}\in M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
and local coordinates
(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})

where
\tau x=-x
and
\tau y_{i}=y_{i}
.
Then the conditions at
p_{0}
defining
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
are

•

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_{i}}(p_{0})=0
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

•

\det D^{2}\Phi(p_{0})=0
,

•

.

This means that the subspace
W\subset\mathfrak{I}^{2,1}_{G}(M;\mathbb{R})
defining

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{1}_{2}
fibers via
\mathfrak{b}
over
M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
with fiber of codimension
n
. Hence, it is of codimension
n+1
.

Note that in the latter case
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}(p_{0})
is satisfied automatically and
does not increase the codimenision of the subspace.

The parameter counting for other subspaces is analogous.
∎

3.3.
Local forms for the degenerations

We provide a normal form of a function in
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
.
The proofs are classical, however we present sketches of proofs to assert that
the changes of variables can be made equivariant.

We begin with a general lemma.
It can be found in
[
Mil63
, proof of Lemma 2.1]
, as
a step in proving
Morse Lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose
H
is a smooth function in variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
. Suppose
DH(p)=0
and
D^{2}H(p)
is diagonal
and the term
c=\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial u_{1}^{2}}(p)\neq 0
. Then, there exists a change of variables

(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\to(w_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n})
, local near
p
, such that

H(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=cw_{1}^{2}+\sum_{2\leq j\leq k\leq n}u_{j}u_{k}H^{\prime}_{jk}(w,u_{2},\dots,u_{n}).

Moreover, if
\tau
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
via
\tau u_{i}=\epsilon_{i}u_{i}
, and
H
is
\tau
-invariant,
then the change of variables can be made
\tau
-equivariant.

Proof.

We will say that a function
L\colon M\to\mathbb{R}

is
\epsilon
-equviariant if
L\circ\tau=\epsilon L
. With this terminology, we assume that
H(p)=0
,

p
has coordinates
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
, and we write

H(z)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dH}{dt}(tz)dt
, leading to:

H(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}H_{j}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}).

Here the functions
H_{j}
are
\epsilon_{j}
-equivariant by construction.
Moreover
H_{j}(0,0,\dots,0)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{j}}(p)=0
. That is, we may apply Hadamard Lemma again to obtain

H(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\sum_{j\leq k}u_{j}u_{k}H_{jk}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),

where now
H_{jk}
are
\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}
-equivariant.

We use the fact that
H_{jk}(p)=\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial u_{j}\partial u_{k}}
, vanishing if
j\neq k
. Set now

w=\sqrt{\frac{1}{c}H_{11}}\left(u_{1}+\sum_{j>1}u_{j}\frac{H_{1j}}{H_{11}}\right).

Then,
\frac{\partial w}{\partial u_{1}}(p)=1
. Moreover
w
is
\epsilon_{1}
-equivariant. Hence, map
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\mapsto(w,u_{2},\dots,u_{n})

is an equivariant change of variables as in the claim.
∎

From the above result, we obtain the following corollary.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
H
is a smooth function in variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
. Suppose
DH(p)=0
and
D^{2}H(p)
is diagonal
with diagonal terms
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
, and all but the last term are non-zero. Then, there is a
\tau
-equivariant
base change
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\mapsto(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
so that

H(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})=c_{1}w_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{n-1}w^{2}_{n-1}+w_{n}^{3}H^{\prime}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}).

Proof.

Apply Lemma
3.7
so as to obtain coordinates
u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}
and a function
H^{\prime}
such that

H(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime})=c_{1}{u_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}+\dots+c_{n-1}{u_{n-1}^{\prime}}^{2}+{u_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}H^{\prime}(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}).

We have that
H_{1}(0,\dots,0)=0
, so by Hadamard lemma, we obtain

H_{1}(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime})=u_{1}^{\prime}H^{\prime}_{1}(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime})+u_{2}^{\prime}H_{2}(u_{2}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime})+\dots+u_{n}^{\prime}H^{\prime}_{n}(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}).

We now apply the coordinate change

w_{i}=u^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2c_{i}}{u^{\prime}_{n}}^{2}H^{\prime}_{i}.

This is a local diffeomorphism. Moreover,
\tau
acts on
{u^{\prime}_{n}}^{2}H^{\prime}_{i}
in the same way as on
w_{i}
, so the change is
\tau
-equivariant.
Its effect is to trade the term
{u_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}u^{\prime}_{i}H^{\prime}_{i}
in
H
for a term starting with
{u^{\prime}_{n}}^{4}
.
With this change of variables, we eventually obtain:

H(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})=c_{1}w_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{n-1}w_{n-1}^{2}+w_{n}^{3}H^{\prime\prime}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}).

∎

With the above lemmas, we describe the normal form of a function in
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose
G
is an equivariant function on
M
,
the point
p_{0}\notin M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
is a critical point of
G
, such that
DG(p_{0})=0
and
\dim\ker D^{2}G(p_{0})=1
.
Assume also, that there exists a vector
v\in\ker D^{2}G(p_{0})
satisfying
D^{3}G(p_{0})(v,v,v)\neq 0
.
Then, there exists a local coordinate system
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
near
p_{0}

such that

G(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\epsilon_{i}y_{i}^{2}+y_{n}^{3}+G(p_{0}),

where
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1}\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

The proof is classical and follows the lines of the proof of classical Morse
Lemma as in
[
Mil63
, Lemma 2.1]
. First, choose coordinates
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}

such that
D^{2}G(p)
is diagonal,
\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial y_{n}^{2}}(p)=0
. It
follows from the assumptions that
\frac{\partial^{3}G}{\partial y_{n}^{3}}(p)\neq 0
. We are in
the situation of Lemma
3.8
; as
p\notin M^{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}
, we consider trivial
\tau
-action near
p
. By
Lemma
3.8
, there exists a change of variables such that

G(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{n-1}y_{n-1}^{2}+y_{n}^{3}G^{\prime}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})+G(p_{0}).

Here,
c_{i}
are the diagonal terms of the matrix
D^{2}G(p)
. We can
make them equal to
\pm 1
, by replacing
y_{i}
with
\sqrt{|c_{i}|}y_{i}
.

Note that
G^{\prime}(0,\dots,0)\neq 0
, because it is equal, up to a constant factor,
to the third derivative
\frac{\partial^{3}G}{\partial y_{n}^{3}}(p)
. Hence, a change of variables
y_{n}\mapsto y_{n}\sqrt[3]{G^{\prime}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})}
is a local diffeomorphism. After this change,

has the desired form.
∎

Next, we describe the local form for

. The argument is very similar
to the proof of Proposition

.

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose

3.5.
Versal deformations

In Subsection
3.4
, we described local deformations
of singularities
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
. We now prove the following statement.

Proposition 3.15
.

Suppose
\Phi_{\sigma}
is a family of equivariant Morse functions on
M
.
Assume that
\Phi_{\sigma}
hits
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}

at a parameter
\sigma_{0}
. Then, there exists a local coordinate system
near
p_{0}
, possibly depending on
\sigma
, the non-Morse critical point of
f_{\sigma_{0}}
, and a function

\lambda(\sigma)
with
\lambda(\sigma_{0})=0
such that
in these local coordinates
\Phi_{\sigma}=\Psi_{\sigma}\circ G_{\lambda(\sigma)}
,
where
\Psi_{\sigma}
is a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
for all
\sigma
near
\sigma_{0}
. Moreover,

\Phi_{\sigma}
is transverse (as a path) to
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
,
if
\frac{d\lambda(\sigma)}{d\sigma}(\sigma_{0})\neq 0
.

Proof.

We use standard arguments, used e.g. in
[
Wal08
]
and based on versality theory of
[
AGZV12
, Section 8]
.
The statement is by definition equivalent to saying that
G_{\lambda}

constructed in Subsection
3.4
is versal. Indeed, versality means precisely that every deformation
can be induced from the given one.

In order to show that the deformation is versal, it is enough
to show that it is infinitezimally versal by Versality Theorem
[
AGZV12
, Theorem 8.3]
.
1
1

1

The proof
in
[
AGZV12
]
is given in a non-equivariant setting, but it is easily adapted
to the case with a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action.

Versality is usually discussed locally, i.e. via germs of maps, rather than for global spaces.
Consider the space
\mathcal{O}
of germs at
p_{0}
of smooth equivariant functions from
M
to
\mathbb{R}
taking
value
0
at
0
. On this space two groups of diffeomorphisms act.
First one is the group of reparametrizations (germs of self-diffeomorphisms of
M
preserving
p_{0}
),
the other is the group of self-diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
preserving
0
.
For any
F_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
, let
\mathcal{G}_{F_{0}}
be the orbit of
F_{0}
under the action of the product of the two groups (in
[
AGZV12
]
this is called the orbit of RL-equivalence).

A deformation
F_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
, where
\Lambda
is an open subset of some
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(
\Lambda
is the parameter space)
defines a (germ of a map)
\mathcal{T}\colon\Lambda\to\mathcal{O}
. We say that
F_{\lambda}
is infinitezimally versal, if
\mathcal{T}
is transverse to
\mathcal{G}_{F_{0}}
.

Infinitezimal versality of each of the three deformations of Subsection
3.4
can be readily verified using

[
AGZV12
, Section 8.2, Theorem]
. We present a more conceptual approach.

Suppose
G_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
is as in Definition
3.12
.
It deforms the
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1}

. Assume

By direct inspection of Definition
3.12
, the map
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times M\to\mathfrak{I}^{3,1}_{G}(M;\mathbb{R})

given by
(\lambda,z)\mapsto\mathfrak{j}^{3,1}_{G}G_{\lambda}(z)
is transverse to
W
at
\lambda=0
.
This, however, is not yet the same as transversality of
\mathcal{T}
to the orbit
\mathcal{G}_{G_{0}}
required by the condition for infinitezimal versality.

Consider the space
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{O}

of all germs

such that

\mathfrak{j}^{3,1}_{G}G_{\lambda}

.

The key result from Proposition

∎

We remark that if the normal form depends on continuous parameters (i.e. there are so-called

4.2.
Moving critical points to the boundary

As an application, we describe the splitting of an interior critical point described in
[

In
[
BNR16
]
, the process was explained as a deformation of a

To simplify the notation, we work with

,

.
The higher dimensional case involves adding quadratic terms to the function
\Phi
and to its deformation,
which does not substantially differ from the two-dimensional case.

On
M

x,y
, with
\tau x=-x

\tau y=y

This function has

On the other hand,
\Phi_{\lambda}|_{N}

.

We make the following observation.

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\Phi
belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
.

Proof.

The space
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
has been defined by the condition that there exists
a critical point

\dim\ker D^{2}\Phi(p)>1
. And this is the case
for
\Phi

(0,0)
all the quadratic terms vanish, so
\dim\ker D^{2}\Phi(0,0)=2
.
∎

As
\Phi
belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{\geq 2}_{2}
, one cannot expect that the deformation
\Phi_{\lambda}

to be versal (the expectation is that the base of a versal deformation has dimension at least

).
Consider

\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(x,y)=y^{3}-x^{2}y+\lambda y+\mu x^{2}.

We study this deformation and show how we can pass from
\Phi_{-1,0}
to
\Phi_{1,0}

by hitting only
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1},\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
strata.

To this end, we study critical points of
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}
. Note that

(4.1)

D\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(x,y)=(2x(\mu-y),3y^{2}-x^{2}+\lambda).

It is also convenient to compute the second derivative.

(4.2)

D^{2}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}2(\mu-y)&-2x\\
-2x&6y\end{pmatrix}.

To study critical points of
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}
, following (
4.1
), we consider two cases
x=0
and
y=\mu
. It might be helpful to consult Figure
5
.

Figure 5.
The behavior of
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}
.
The derivative
\frac{\partial\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}}{\partial\mu}
vanishes on
x=0
and on one of the horizontal lines, whose height is
\mu
.
The derivative
\frac{\partial\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}}{\partial\lambda}
vanishes on one of the hyperbolas (or a degenerate hyperbola)
depending on the parameter
\lambda
. Critical points of
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}
are read off as the intersection points of the two vanishing sets. Transverse intersection
corresponds to Morse-type critical points.

Case 1.
Suppose
x=0
. Assume first
\lambda<0
.
There are two critical points
p_{\pm}=(0,\pm\sqrt{-\lambda/3})
.
As
x=0
, by (
4.2
), the second derivative is diagonal.
The indices
\operatorname{ind}_{\partial}p_{\pm}
of the two critical points are
0
and
1
depending
on whether
y>0
or
y<0
. The second derivative with respect to
x
is
2(\mu-y)
.
That is, for
\mu\neq\pm\sqrt{-\lambda/3}
, both
p_{+}
and
p_{-}
are non-degenerate, i.e. they are Morse critical points.

If
\lambda=-3\mu^{2}
, then the kernel either of
D^{2}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(p_{+})
(if
\mu>0
) or of
D^{\Phi}_{\lambda,\mu}(p_{-})
(if
\mu<0
)
is one-dimensional, but tangent to
x=0
. By direct inspection
B\Phi
is non-degenerate.
With these parameters
\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,2}
.

If
\lambda=0
but
\mu\neq 0
, then
p_{+}=p_{-}=(0,0)
and so
D^{2}\Phi
has one-dimensional kernel, spanned
by
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
. The derivative
\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial y^{3}}\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}\equiv 6\neq 0
,
so
(0,0)
corresponds to
\mathcal{F}^{1}_{2,1}
stratum.

For
\lambda>0
, there are no critical points with
x=0
.

Case 2.
Suppose
y=\mu
. Then, the critical points have

x^{2}=\lambda+3\mu^{2}
. For
\lambda>-3\mu^{2}
, we have a pair of critical
points
q_{\pm}=(\pm\sqrt{\lambda+3\mu^{2}},\mu)
that are swapped under the
\tau
-action. So on
N
, we have one interior critical point. If
\lambda=-3\mu^{2}
,
q_{+}=q_{-}
,
x=0
and we are in the situation
of Case 1.

In Figure
5
, the critical points for
y=\mu
correspond to intersections of a green, dashed line with a horizontal purple line.

From this discussion it follows that in the space of parameters we can specify
the following four regions (see Figure
6
):

•

\lambda>0
corresponds to a single critical point in the interior.

•

\lambda<0
but
\lambda>-3\mu^{2}
has a pair of critical points
on the boundary (of the same stability) and an interior critical point. This case accounts for two
regions with
\mu>0
and
\mu<0
.

•

\lambda<-3\mu^{2}
, we have two critical points on the boundary that
have opposite stability.

When starting from parameter
\lambda=1,\mu=0
, and going counterclockwise, we hit the vertical line
\lambda=0
.
This creates a cancelling pair of critical points on the boundary, both of which are boundary unstable. Next, we hit the parabola
\lambda=-3\mu^{2}
. At that point, a collision occurs. The interior critical point hits the upper boundary critical point changing its stability. We reach

\lambda=-1,\mu=0
parameter.

When going clockwise, we hit the line
\lambda=0
first, but this time with parameter
\mu<0
. A cancelling pair of critical points is
created, but this time they are boundary stable. Then, on hitting the parabola, a collision occurs, changing the stability of the
lower
boundary critical point.
