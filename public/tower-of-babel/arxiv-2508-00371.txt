A Riemannian viewpoint on the Amari–Čencov α \alpha -connections and Proudman–Johnson equations
Martin Bauer, Alice Le Brigant, Cy Maor

Abstract
. We give a new geometric interpretation of the Amari–Čencov α \alpha -connections ∇ ( α ) \nabla^{(\alpha)} from information geometry: On the space of densities Dens + ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M) , we show that there exist Riemannian metrics G α G^{\alpha} , which we call α \alpha -Fisher–Rao metrics, whose Levi-Civita connections are ∇ ( α ) \nabla^{(\alpha)} . With the exception of α = 0 \alpha=0 (the Fisher–Rao metric), these metrics are non-invariant to the action of the diffeomorphism group Diff ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Diff}(M) , even though the connections are invariant. This gives a new way of interpreting the geodesics of the ∇ ( α ) \nabla^{(\alpha)} as energy-minimizing curves. On the space of probability densities Prob ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Prob}(M) , we show that the same phenomenon holds for α ∈ { − 1 , 0 , 1 } \alpha\in\{-1,0,1\} and that the α \alpha -connections are not metric otherwise. We show that ∇ ( α ) \nabla^{(\alpha)} -geodesics on this space can be interpreted as radial projections of straight lines on appropriate hyper-surfaces, and use this geometric picture to obtain geodesic convexity for any α ∈ ℝ \alpha\in\mathbb{R} . In addition, we prove analogous results for appropriate metrics and connections on Diff ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Diff}(M) , which, for the case M = ℝ M=\mathbb{R} , imply that the generalized Proudman–Johnson equations on the real line are the Euler–Arnold equations of non-right invariant metrics. Finally, in the finite-dimensional case, we show that ∇ ( α ) \nabla^{(\alpha)} can be metric or non-metric depending on the considered statistical model.



1.
Introduction and main results

Information geometry is concerned with the study of spaces of probability densities as differentiable manifolds.
Two of the most important structures in information geometry are the Fisher–Rao metric
[
31
,
16
]
and the Amari–Čencov
\alpha
-connections
[
13
,
2
,
17
,
28
]
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(the case
\alpha=0
corresponding to the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher–Rao metric).
The first is the unique invariant metric
[
13
,
7
]
while the second is the family of all invariant torsionless affine connections
[
4
]
.
Here, invariance has one of two meanings: in the finite-dimensional setting of parametric statistical models, it refers to invariance with respect to sufficient statistics, while in the non-parametric, infinite-dimensional setting of all densities, the meaning can be alternatively understood as invariance under diffeomorphic change of the support
[
4
,
7
]
.

These invariant structures have numerous applications in various fields, ranging from statistical inference and learning
[
1
]
to, more recently, optimal transport
[
3
]
, quantum field theory
[
26
]
, Markov chains
[
34
]
, image analysis
[
8
]
and machine learning: generative neural networks
[
14
]
, natural language processing
[
33
]
and reinforcement learning
[
27
]
.

Another motivation to study infinite-dimensional information geometry stems from its link to geometric hydrodynamics
[
19
,
18
,
22
]
, where fluid motion is described by geodesics on the space of (volume preserving) diffeomorphisms of the fluid domain. Indeed, given a compact manifold
M
with a volume form
\lambda
, the map
\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{*}\lambda
maps the diffeomorphism group
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
to the space
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
of probability densities, thus indentifying
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
with the quotient space
\operatorname{Diff}(M)/\operatorname{Diff}_{\lambda}(M)
of diffeomorphisms modulo diffeomorphisms that preserves a reference volume form
\lambda
.
(In non-compact cases like
M=\mathbb{R}
, this yields the space
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
of all positive densities with appropriate decay conditions.)
Thus, one can pullback the
Fisher–Rao metric and the
\alpha
-connections to obtain right-invariant metrics and connections on
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
.
This was studied in
[
18
,
23
]
for the Fisher–Rao metric, and in
[
22
,
9
]
for the
\alpha
-connections.
See
[
19
]
for a recent overview.
Interestingly, for
M=S^{1}
and
M=\mathbb{R}
, the geodesic equations that arise on
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
are well-known equations from hydrodynamics: the Hunter–Saxton equation is the geodesic equation of the Fisher–Rao metric
[
18
]
, and the generalized Proudman–Johnson equations are the geodesic equations of the
\alpha
-connections
[
22
]
.
The geodesic equations for a general manifold
M
are thus the higher-dimensional version of these equations, and the study of the geometry of the
\alpha
-connections is a way to gain insight on their solutions.

Recently, the authors of the present paper took a step in that direction by studying the geometry of the
\alpha
-connections on two different spaces: the space of positive densities
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
and the space of probability densities
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
. They showed in
[
9
]
that on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, the
\alpha
-connections for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
define the same geodesics as a family of invariant Finsler metrics, the
L^{p}
-Fisher–Rao metrics, where
p=2/(1-\alpha)
. On
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, it was shown that the
\alpha
-connections are the pullback by the
p
-root map
\mu\mapsto(\mu/\lambda)^{1/p}
of the connection on the
L^{p}
-sphere in the space of functions, obtained by a radial projection of the trivial connection.
In both cases, this geometric perspective led to explicit solutions of the geodesic equations: On
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, these are pullbacks by the
p
-root map of straight lines, and on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, they are pullbacks of projections of straight lines on the
L^{p}
-sphere.
This is analogous to the analysis of the non-periodic
[
6
]
and periodic
[
21
]
Hunter–Saxton equation, respectively.
On
\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{R})
this analysis led to solutions of the non-periodic generalized Proudman–Johnson equation (or equivalently, the
r
-Hunter–Saxton equation)
[
15
,
10
]
, and for a general closed manifold
M
, similar analysis was used to estimate the diameter of
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
with respect to right-invariant Sobolev metrics
[
11
]
.

In this paper, we further explore the geometry associated to the
\alpha
-connections on spaces of densities and probability densities, this time from a Riemannian perspective, and its implications for some well-known equations in hydrodynamics.

1.1.
Main results and future directions

We now describe the main contributions of this article, as well as open questions that arise from our results:

(1)

On the space of positive densities
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, we present a family of Riemannian metrics
G^{\alpha}
, which we call the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metrics
, and show that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the
\alpha
-connection
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection of
G^{\alpha}
(Theorem
3.3
).
With the exception of
\alpha=0
, corresponding to the Fisher–Rao metric, all these metrics are non-invariant to the action of
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
, but their connections are.
Note that this characterization of the
\alpha
-connections as a Levi-Civita connection holds for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, unlike the one of being associated with invariant Finsler metrics
[
9
]
, which only holds for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
.

(2)

Applying this characterization to the case
M=\mathbb{R}
, and pulling it back to
\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{R})
(with appropriate decay conditions at infinity),
we obtain that the non-periodic, generalized Proudman–Johnson equations are the Euler–Arnold equations of non-invariant metrics.
To the best of our knowledge, the only other case of an invariant equation that arises as geodesic equation of a non-invariant metric on a group of diffeomorphisms is Burgers’ equation, which is the geodesic equation of the non-invariant
L^{2}
-metric related to optimal mass transport
[
30
]
; in future work it would be of interest to obtain a complete characterization of all instances for this phenomenon, both on the groups of diffeomorphisms and on general finite and infinite dimensional Lie groups.

(3)

On the space of probability densities
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, we show that the Levi-Civita connection of the restriction of
G^{\alpha}
to
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
coincides with the
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
only for
\alpha\in\{0,-1\}
.
For
\alpha=1
, the
\alpha
-connection is the Levi-Civita connection of a variant of the
1
-Fisher–Rao metric, and for
\alpha\notin\{-1,0,1\}
, it is not a metric connection with respect to any Riemannian metric (Theorem
4.2
).
For finite-dimensional parametric statistical models, we show in Proposition
4.16
that a similar phenomenon holds for two-dimensional exponential families; it would be interesting to know whether this result holds in any dimension. We also show that other statistical models can present a different behavior, namely we give an example of a statistical model such that all
\alpha
-connections are metric (Example
4.13
).

(4)

Finally, we show that the geometric picture presented in
[
9
]
of the geodesics of the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, as pullbacks of projections of straight lines on an appropriate surface, holds for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(Theorem
4.9
).
The difference from the case
\alpha\in(-1,1)
presented in
[
9
]
is that the surfaces projected upon are not parts of spheres but can be non-convex and unbounded.
We use this geometric picture to show that, for all
\alpha
,
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is uniquely geodesically convex with respect to
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
, i.e., that for any two elements in the space there exists a unique
\alpha
-geodesic connecting them.
In the case of
M=S^{1}
, which corresponds to the periodic generalized Proudman–Johnson equations, this geometric picture might lead to a geometric explanation of the different blowup behavior of the equations for various values of
\alpha
(see
[
32
,
20
]
), as well as to establish similar results for the higher-dimensional case; as this paper focuses on the geometric picture, we intend to investigate this direction in a separate future work.

Structure of the paper

Section
2
gives some background on the spaces of densities considered in the paper, as well as the Fisher–Rao metric and the
\alpha
-connections. Section
3
is devoted to the case of positive densities: we introduce the
\alpha

\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

2.
Background: Spaces of densities, the Fisher–Rao metric and Amari–Čencov
connections

2.1.
The space of densities as an infinite dimensional manifold

Throughout this article, unless otherwise mentioned, we let
M
be a closed, oriented,
n
-dimensional manifold.
We denote by
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
the space of smooth positive densities and by
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
the subspace of smooth probability densities, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{\mu\in\Omega^{n}(M):\mu>0\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prob}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M):\int\mu=1\}.

Here,
\Omega^{n}(M)
is the space of all
n
-forms on
M
, and the positivity means the positivity of the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{\mu}{\lambda}
of
\mu
with respect to some volume form
\lambda
on
M
(say, of some Riemannian metric).
Since
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
is an open subset of the Fréchet space
\Omega^{n}(M)
it carries the structure of a Fréchet manifold with tangent space
T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}(M)=\Omega^{n}(M)
.
Similarly, as a linear subspace of a Fréchet manifold, the space of probability densities is a Fréchet manifold, where the tangent space is given by

\displaystyle T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M)=\left\{a\in\Omega^{n}(M):\int a=0\right\}.

On both the space of densities and probability densities
we can consider the pushforward action of the diffeomorphism group
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
. On
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
it is given by

(1)

\operatorname{Diff}(M)\times\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)\ni(\varphi,\mu)\mapsto\varphi_{*}\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

and, since the pushforward by a diffeomorphism is volume preserving, this action restricts to an action on the space of probability densities.
By a result of Moser
[
25
]
the action on the space of probability densities is transitive, which allows us to identify the space of probability densities with the quotient

\operatorname{Prob}(M)\equiv\operatorname{Diff}(M)/\operatorname{Diff}_{\lambda}(M),

where
\operatorname{Diff}_{\lambda}(M)
is the group of volume preserving diffeomorphisms of some fixed probability density
\lambda
.
Thus, constructions (metrics, connections, geodesics) on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
can be pulled back to
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
via the map
\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{*}\lambda
.

For
a\in\Omega^{n}(M)
and
\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, we denote by
\frac{a}{\mu}
the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
a
with respect to
\mu
. Given a fixed (probability) density
\lambda\in\operatorname{Prob}(M)
the map
\mu\mapsto\frac{\mu}{\lambda}
allows us to identify
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
with positive smooth functions on
M
, and
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
with the positive smooth functions that integrate to one.

2.2.
The Fisher–Rao metric

Next we introduce the central object of information geometry: the Fisher–Rao metric, a Riemannian metric defined on the space of (probability) densities. It is an infinite-dimensional version of the historical Fisher–Rao metric, introduced by Rao
[
31
]
on parametric families of probability distributions using the Fisher information, in the sense that it induces Rao’s metric on the corresponding submanifolds.

Definition 2.1

(Fisher–Rao metric)
.

Given
\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
and
a,b\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
the Fisher–Rao metric on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
is given by

(2)

\displaystyle G_{\mu}(a,b)=\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu\;.

Via restriction
G
induces a Riemannian metric on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, which we denote by the same letter.

The Fisher–Rao metric is invariant under the action of the diffeomorphism group, i.e.,

G_{\varphi_{*}\mu}(\varphi_{*}a,\varphi_{*}b)=G_{\mu}(a,b)

for all (probability) densities
\mu
, tangent vectors
a,b
and diffeomorphisms
\varphi
.
Čencov’s theorem states that, on the space of probability densities, it is indeed the unique Riemannian metric with this property
1
1

1

In the setting of the present article this requires that
\operatorname{dim}(M)>1
, see
[
7
]
; assuming invariance under the larger set of all sufficient statistics, as used, e.g., in
[
4
]
, this condition is not needed.
, cf.
[
13
,
4
,
7
]
.

2.3.
The Amari–Čencov
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

Now we introduce the
\alpha
-connections on the space
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, a family of affine connections that are dual with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric, and such that the
0
-connection is the Levi-Civita connection. We will follow the presentation in
[
9
]
. In the finite-dimensional case, i.e., when
M
is a finite set, the definitions below coincide with the classical ones, see, e.g.,
[
2
,
4
]
.

Definition 2.2

(
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
)
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the
\alpha
-connection
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
is given by

(3)

\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}b=Db.a-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\frac{a}{\mu}b,\quad a,b\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M).

Here
Db.a|_{\mu}:=D_{\mu}b(a_{\mu})
denotes the directional derivative of the vector field
b
in the direction given by
a_{\mu}
.

The easiest way to read this definition (and similar formulae below) is to consider again the identification of densities and positive functions via
\mu\mapsto\mu/\lambda
.
The
\alpha=-1
and
\alpha=1
connections are called the mixture and exponential connections, respectively. In the following lemma we will collect several key properties for the
\alpha
-connections on the space
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
.

Lemma 2.3

(Properties of
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
)
.

Let
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
be as defined in (
3
). We have:

(a)

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is invariant under the action (
1
) of
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
.

(b)

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is torsionless.

(c)

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
and
\nabla^{(-\alpha)}
are dual connections on
(\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M),G)
, i.e.,

D(G(a,b)).c=G(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{c}a,b)+G(a,\nabla^{(-\alpha)}_{c}b).

For
\alpha=0
we obtain that
\nabla^{(0)}
is self-dual (i.e., metric) and thus coincides with the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher–Rao metric.

(d)

For
\alpha\in(-1,1)
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}

can be interpreted as the connection associated to the
\alpha
-divergence
D^{(\alpha)}
, i.e.,

G(\nabla_{a}^{(\alpha)}b,c)=-\left.\partial_{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}D^{(\alpha)}(\mu||\nu)[b,c])[a]\right|_{\nu=\mu},

where
D^{(\alpha)}:\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)\times\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
is defined via

(4)

D^{(\alpha)}(\mu||\nu)=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\int_{M}\nu+\frac{2}{1+\alpha}\int_{M}\mu-\frac{4}{(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)}\int_{M}\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\left(\frac{\nu}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\lambda.

(e)

For
\alpha\in(-1,1)
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
can be identified with the Chern connection of an invariant Finsler metric on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, called the
L^{p}
-Fisher–Rao metric, where
p=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}
.

(f)

For
\alpha\in(-1,1)
and any initial conditions
\mu_{0}\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
and
a\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, the unique
\alpha
-connection geodesic
\mu:[0,T)\to\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

defined on its maximal interval of existence
[0,T)
is given by

\mu(t)=\left(\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}+t\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\frac{a}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\lambda.

The geodesic
\mu(t)
exists for all time
t
, i.e.,
T=\infty
, if and only if
\frac{a}{\lambda}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in M
.
Thus
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is geodesically incomplete since there exist geodesics that leave
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
in finite time.

Proof.

Properties

)

(

)

)

2.4.
The Amari–Čencov
\alpha
-connections on

Next we introduce the
\alpha
-connections on the space of probability densities, which we will denote by

.

(
\alpha

)
.

, the

\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}

Lemma 2.5

(Properties of
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
)
.

(a)

(b)

(d)

3.
The
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric and the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

In this section we will present the first main result of this article: namely, we introduce a new family of Riemannian metrics on the space
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, which we will construct in such a way that the induced Levi-Civita connections coincide with the
\alpha
-connections. As a by-product of our construction we will extend in Theorem
3.5
the explicit formula for geodesics from Lemma
2.3
to arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, in Section
3.2
we will extend our construction to the space of densities on the non-compact manifold
M=\mathbb{R}
, which will allow us to find an interpretation of the non-periodic, generalized Proudman–Johnson equation as an Euler-Arnold equation.

3.1.
A Riemannian interpretation for the
\alpha
-connections

We start by defining the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric:

Definition 3.1

(
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\lambda
be a fixed background density on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
. Given
\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
and
a,b\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
we define the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric via:

(6)

\displaystyle G^{\alpha}_{\mu}(a,b)=\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-1}\frac{a}{\lambda}\frac{b}{\lambda}\lambda\;.

Note that
\alpha=0
corresponds to the standard Fisher–Rao metric as defined in (
2
), i.e.,
G^{0}=G
.

Remark 3.2

(Invariance of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric)
.

Note that
\frac{\mu}{\lambda}
is a strictly positive function and thus the above indeed defines a smooth Riemannian metric, i.e., it is positive definite.
However, the metric is
not
invariant under the action (
1
) of
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
unless
\alpha=0
, for which it is equal to the Fisher–Rao metric.
To see the non-invariance for
\alpha\neq 0
we just note the dependance on the background density
\lambda
, which is an obstruction to invariance with respect to the full diffeomorphism group.
These metrics are, however, invariant under the action of
\operatorname{Diff}_{\lambda}(M)
, the group of all
\lambda
-preserving diffeomorphisms.
This can be easily seen by applying a
\lambda
-preserving diffeomorphism
\varphi
, using that
\lambda=\varphi^{*}\lambda
and then applying a change of variables by
\varphi
in the integral.
Note that this is analogous to the situation of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein metric in optimal transport, which corresponds to a
\operatorname{Diff}_{\lambda}(M)
-invariant metric on
\operatorname{Diff}(M)
, that is not invariant under the action of the full diffeomorphism group.

Next, we show that the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric satisfies the desired property, i.e., we show that the covariant derivative associated to the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
is equal to the Amari–Čencov
\alpha
-connection.

Theorem 3.3

(Levi-Civita connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
)
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the Levi-Civita connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric on the space of densities
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
is given by

\nabla_{a}b=Db.a-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu,

which coincides with the
\alpha
-connection as introduced in Definition
2.2
.

Proof.

The proof follows by direct calculation: we have
\nabla_{a}b=Db.a+\Gamma(a,b)
, where the Christoffel map
\Gamma(a,b)
can be computed using

G^{\alpha}_{\mu}(c,\Gamma_{\mu}(a,b))=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,a}G^{\alpha}\right)(b,c)+\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,b}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,c)-\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,c}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,b),

where
D_{\mu,a}G^{\alpha}
denotes the variation of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric in direction
a
. We calculate

\displaystyle\left(D_{\mu,a}G^{\alpha}\right)(b,c)=D_{\mu,a}\left(\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-1}\frac{b}{\lambda}\frac{c}{\lambda}\lambda\right)=-\int(\alpha+1)\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-2}\frac{a}{\lambda}\frac{b}{\lambda}\frac{c}{\lambda}\lambda\;.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,a}G^{\alpha}\right)(b,c)+\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,b}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,c)-\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,c}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,b)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{1}{2}\int(\alpha+1)\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-2}\frac{a}{\lambda}\frac{b}{\lambda}\frac{c}{\lambda}\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}\int(\alpha+1)\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-1}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\lambda}\frac{c}{\lambda}\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad=G\left(-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\frac{a}{\mu}b,c\right).

Consequently we obtain the desired formula for the Christoffel symbols.

∎

This interpretation of the
\alpha
-connections as Levi-Civita connections of a Riemannian metric leads to a new interpretation of the geodesics of the
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
as energy-minimizing curves, implying in particular that the corresponding Riemannian energy is conserved along geodesics:

Corollary 3.4

(Conservation of the Riemannian Energy)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\mu:[0,1]\to\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
be a geodesic of the
G^{\alpha}
-metric (of the
\alpha
-connection, equivalently). Then the (instantaneous) Riemannian
G^{\alpha}
-energy

\displaystyle E(\mu)=G^{\alpha}_{\mu}(\mu_{t},\mu_{t})=\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-1}\frac{\mu_{t}}{\lambda}\frac{\mu_{t}}{\lambda}\lambda\

is constant in time.

Next we show that the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric can be obtained as the pullback of the flat
L^{2}
-metric on the space of smooth functions, thereby leading to explicit formulas for geodesics on the space of densities:

Theorem 3.5
.

For fixed
\lambda
we consider the mapping
\Phi_{\alpha}:\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)\to C^{\infty}(M)
, defined via

(7)

\Phi_{\alpha}:\begin{cases}\mu&\mapsto\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right),\qquad\qquad\,\alpha=1\\
\mu&\mapsto\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}},\qquad\alpha\neq 1.\end{cases}

Then the pullback via
\Phi_{\alpha}
of the
L^{2}(\lambda)
Riemannian metric on
C^{\infty}(M)
is the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric. Furthermore, for any
\alpha\neq 1
the image
\Phi_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M))
is the space of all smooth, positive functions, whereas for
\alpha=1
it coincides with all of
C^{\infty}(M)
.
In particular,
G^{\alpha}
is flat for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Note that the maps
\Phi_{\alpha}
as defined here differ from their analogues in
[
9
]
by the factor
\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}
.

Proof.

To calculate the pullback metric we only need to calculate the variation of
\Phi_{\alpha}
at
\mu\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
in direction
a\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
. We have, for
\alpha\neq 1
:

T_{\mu}\Phi_{\alpha}(a)=\operatorname{sgn}(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}}\frac{a}{\lambda},

and thus the desired formula follows by observing that

G^{\alpha}_{\mu}(a,b)=\int T_{\mu}\Phi_{\alpha}(a).T_{\mu}\Phi_{\alpha}(b)\lambda.

The calculation for
\alpha=1
is similar.
∎

Using that geodesics in
C^{\infty}(M)
w.r.t. the
L^{2}(\lambda)
-metric are straight lines, see e.g.
[
12
]
, a direct consequence of the above results are the following explicit formulas for geodesics of all
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
; for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
this formula was already shown in
[
9
]
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Given any initial conditions
\mu_{0}\in\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
and
a\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, the unique
\alpha
-connection geodesic
\mu:[0,T)\to\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

defined on its maximal interval of existence
[0,T)
is given by

\mu(t)=\begin{cases}\operatorname{exp}\left(t\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{-1}\frac{a}{\lambda}\right)\lambda,\qquad\alpha=1\\
\left(\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}+t\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\frac{a}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\lambda,\qquad\alpha\neq 1.\end{cases}

For
\alpha\neq 1
the geodesic
\mu(t)
exists for all time
t
, i.e.,
T=\infty
, if and only if
\frac{a}{\lambda}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in M
.
Thus for
\alpha\neq 1
,
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is geodesically incomplete since there exist geodesics that leave
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
in finite time. For
\alpha=1
all geodesics exist for all time
t
and thus
\nabla^{(1)}
is geodesically complete.

3.2.
The Proudman–Johnson equations and the non-invariant
\dot{H}^{1}
-metric on
\operatorname{Diff}(\mathbb{R})

We conclude this section by describing an analogous construction on the group of diffeomorphisms on the real line, which turns out to be related to the family of inviscid, generalized Proudman–Johnson equations with parameter
\alpha
(henceforth gPJ equation), given by

u_{txx}+(2-\alpha)u_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx}=0,

where
u
is a function on the real line. The interest in these equations can be found in the area of mathematical hydrodynamics, as they contain several prominent PDEs as special cases: the original Proudman–Johnson equation, in which
\alpha=3
, corresponds to axisymmetric Navier–Stokes equations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; the generalized equations, which were first proposed in
[
29
]
, contain several other important special cases, in particular the Hunter–Saxton equation for
\alpha=0
, the
\mu
-Burgers equation
\alpha=-1
, and self-similar axisymmetric Navier–Stokes equations in higher dimensions.
See
[
35
]
for further information about the equation and its motivation.

The relation of these equations to the
\alpha
-connections studied here has been first described by Lenells and Misiołek in the periodic setting
[
22
]
: namely they considered the mapping
\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{*}d\theta
, which maps the diffeomorphism group of the circle to the space of probability densities on
S^{1}
. They showed that the pullback of the geodesic equations of the
\alpha
-connections via this mapping yields the periodic gPJ equations with parameter
\alpha
.
The resulting connection reduces to a Levi-Civita connection of the homogenous
W^{1,2}
-Riemannian metric for
\alpha=0
, thus recasting the geometric interpretation of the Hunter–Saxton equation as an Euler–Arnold equation, as described in
[
21
]
. In the non-periodic setting we have shown in
[
10
]
, that one can interpret the gPJ equations as Euler-Arnold equations of a Finsler-metric if
\alpha\in(-1,1)
.

In this section, we will consider the pullback of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric to show that one can indeed interpret the whole family of gPJ equations as Euler-Arnold equations, albeit for a non-invariant Riemannian metric.
We note that the results in this section do not follow directly from the above calculations on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
as we previously assumed that the manifold
M
is compact, whereas here we treat the case
M=\mathbb{R}
.

We start by introducing an appropriate group of diffeomorphisms on the real line, where we have to pay specific attention to the decay conditions towards infinity: we let
\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
denote the group of diffeomorphisms that converge to the identity as
x\to-\infty
. More precisely, we define

\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{\varphi=x+f(x):f^{\prime}\in W^{\infty,1}(\mathbb{R}),\;f^{\prime}>-1,\text{ and }\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=0\right\}\;,

where
W^{\infty,1}(\mathbb{R})=\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}W^{k,1}(\mathbb{R})
is the intersection of all Sobolev spaces.
The space
\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
has been first considered in
[
6
]
as a modeling space for the Hunter-Saxton equation on the real line and it has been shown to be an infinite-dimensional Lie group with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}:=\left\{u:u^{\prime}\in W^{\infty,1}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }\lim_{x\to-\infty}u(x)=0\right\}\;.

We are now ready to define the
\alpha\dot{H}^{1}
Riemannian metric, which will yield the desired geometric description for the gPJ equations:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\varphi\in\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
and
u\circ\varphi,v\circ\varphi\in T_{\varphi}\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
with
u,v\in C_{1}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. The
\alpha\dot{H}^{1}
-metric is given by

\displaystyle G^{\alpha\dot{H}^{1}}_{\varphi}(u\circ\varphi,v\circ\varphi)=\int\varphi_{x}^{-\alpha-1}(u\circ\varphi)_{x}.(v\circ\varphi)_{x}\;\lambda=\int\varphi_{x}^{-\alpha+1}(u_{x}\circ\varphi).(v_{x}\circ\varphi)\;\lambda,

where
\lambda
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.
Note that the decay conditions on
\varphi,u
and
v
guarantee the well-posedness of the above definition.

Using the change of coordinate formula it follows that the metric
G^{\alpha\dot{H}^{1}}
is right-invariant if and only if
\alpha=0
.

Next we will show that the
\alpha\dot{H}^{1}
-metric can be obtained as a pullback of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric.
To this end, we first need to introduce an appropriate space of densities on the real line, which we will define as the image of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
under the map
\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{*}d\theta
. We obtain the space:

\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{h\lambda:(h-1)\in W^{\infty,1}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }h>0\right\}\;.

It follows that
\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
is an infinite-dimensional manifold with tangent space

T_{\mu}\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{r\lambda:r\in W^{\infty,1}(\mathbb{R})\right\}\;.

Using these definitions, it follows that the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric, as defined in (
6
) for densities on compact manifolds, can be extended to
\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, and by direct calculation we obtain the following result:

Lemma 3.8
.

Consider the map

\Theta:\begin{cases}\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})&\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\\
\varphi&\mapsto\varphi_{*}\lambda.\end{cases}

The pullback of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric via
\Theta
is equal to the
\alpha\dot{H}^{1}
-metric on
\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., for all
u\circ\varphi,v\circ\varphi\in T_{\varphi}\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
we have

G^{\alpha\dot{H}^{1}}_{\varphi}(u\circ\varphi,v\circ\varphi)=(\Theta^{*}G^{\alpha})_{\varphi}(u\circ\varphi,v\circ\varphi)\;.

Proof.

This follows by direct calculation.
∎

We are now prepared to present the main result of this subsection, which, given the above results, should come as no surprise.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The generalized Proudman–Johnson equations with parameter
\alpha
are the Euler-Arnold equations of the (non-invariant) Riemannian metric
G^{\alpha\dot{H}^{1}}
on
\widetilde{\operatorname{Diff}}(\mathbb{R})
. Consequently, the generalized-Proudman–Johnson equations are globally well-posed on
\mathfrak{g}
if and only if
\alpha=1
.

Proof.

The proof of this result follows from Lemma
3.8
and the fact that the geodesic equations of the
\alpha
-connections on
\widetilde{\operatorname{Dens}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
are equivalent to the gPJ equation.
∎

4.
The
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric and the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)

4.1.
Metricity of the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)

Here we consider the restriction of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric to a Riemannian metric on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
.
We will, however, show that, in general, the Levi-Civita connection of this Riemannian metric does not coincide with the Amari-C̆encov
\alpha
-connection on this space.
The difference stems from the fact that the
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is the orthogonal projection of the
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric
[
9
]
, while the induced connection of the restriction of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric to
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is the orthogonal projection of the
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
with respect to the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric.
These two only coincide for
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=-1
. Indeed, the main result of this section shows that the Amari-C̆encov
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian metric if and only if
\alpha\in\{-1,0,1\}
: for
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=-1
this Riemannian metric is the restriction of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric, and for
\alpha=1
it is a slight variant of the restricition of the
1
-Fisher–Rao metric. We start by calculating the Levi-Civita
connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric:

Lemma 4.1

(Levi-Civita connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
)
.

The Levi-Civita connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric on the space of densities
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is given by

\tilde{\nabla}_{a}b=Db.a-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\left(\frac{a}{\mu}b-\frac{\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu}{\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\lambda}\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\lambda\right),

which coincides with the
\alpha
-connection as defined in Lemma
2.2
if and only if
\alpha\in\{0,-1\}
.

Proof.

To calculate the induced connection on the submanifold
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, we only need to calculate the orthogonal projection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric.
For every
\mu\in\operatorname{Prob}(M)
the tangent space
T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M)
at
\mu
is given by all
\delta\mu
such that
\int\delta\mu=0
(independently of
\alpha
).
Let
a\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
. We claim that any element in the orthogonal complement can be written as
a=C\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\lambda
with
C\in\mathbb{R}
being a constant. It is easy to see that such
a
is indeed orthogonal to all
\delta\mu\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M)
as

G^{\alpha}_{\mu}(a,\delta\mu)=\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\alpha-1}C\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda}\frac{\delta\mu}{\lambda}\lambda=\int\delta\mu=0\;.

It remains to show that this really spans all of the complement, but this is clear since for any
a\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)

a-C\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\lambda\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M),\,\text{ where }\,\,C=\frac{\int a}{\int\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{\alpha+1}\lambda}.

Note that if
\alpha=0
, then
C=\int a
as the denominator integrates to one.
∎

Next we formulate the main result of this section, which shows that the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
cannot be viewed as Levi-Civita connections of a Riemannian metric if
\alpha\notin\{-1,0,1\}
. Note that the Riemannian interpretation for
\alpha\in\{-1,0\}
follows directly from the above theorem, but that the case
\alpha=1
needs a slight adaption of the construction of the corresponding Riemannian metric.

Theorem 4.2
.

The
\alpha
-connection
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian metric if and only if
\alpha\in\{-1,0,1\}
. More precisely we have:

•

If
\alpha\in\{-1,0\}
then
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection
of the restriction of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric to
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
.

•

If
\alpha=1
then
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection
of the Riemannian metric

(8)

\tilde{G}^{1}_{\mu}(a,b)=\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda-\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\lambda\int\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda.

•

If
\alpha\notin\{-1,0,1\}
then
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is a non-metric affine connection.

Remark 4.3

(Interpreting the Riemannian metric for
\alpha=1
as a pullback metric)
.

As mentioned above, the metric for
\alpha=1
does not coincide with the (restriction) of the
1
-FR metric as introduced in the previous section. Instead of the pullback via the map
\Phi_{1}
, one has to slightly adapt the mapping and define
\tilde{\Phi}_{1}:\operatorname{Prob}(M)\to C^{\infty}(M)
via

\tilde{\Phi}_{1}(\mu)=\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)-\int\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)\lambda.

Using this definition, the pullback of the flat
L^{2}
-metric on
C^{\infty}(M)
is exactly the metric
\tilde{G}^{1}
as defined above.
Note that the image of
\tilde{\Phi}_{1}
is the linear space of all zero average functions, whose geodesics with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric are simply straight lines and exist for all time; thus, the geodesics of
\tilde{G}^{1}
(i.e., of
\overline{\nabla}^{(1)}
) are the pullback of these straight lines, and the space is geodesically complete.
This map and the characterization of the geodesics (though not the Riemannian metric (
8
)) were studied in
[
22
]
.

To prove Theorem
4.2
we first need to compute the Riemann curvature tensor of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
a,b,c\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M)
the Riemann curvature tensor of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is given by:

R^{(\alpha)}(a,b)c=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}\left(G(b,c)a-G(a,c)b\right),

where
G
is the Fisher–Rao metric.

Remark 4.5
.

This implies the well-known facts that the curvature of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is flat for
\alpha=\pm 1
, that
R^{(\alpha)}=R^{(-\alpha)}
and that the sectional curvature is
1/4
for the Fisher–Rao metric, cf.
[
5
, Appendix B]
.

Proof.

Let
\tau=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
. The connection
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
can be obtained from
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
as

\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}b=\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}b+\tau G_{\mu}(a,b)\mu,\quad\text{where}\quad\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}b=Db.a-\tau\frac{a}{\mu}b,

so that

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{b}c

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}\left(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{b}c+\tau G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}\left(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{b}c+\tau G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu\right)+\tau G_{\mu}\left(a,\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{b}c+\tau G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu\right)\mu

\displaystyle=\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{b}c+\tau D\left(G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu\right).a-\tau^{2}\frac{a}{\mu}G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\tau^{2}G_{\mu}(a,\mu)G_{\mu}(b,c)+\tau G_{\mu}(a,Dc.b)-\tau^{2}G_{\mu}(a,\frac{b}{\mu}c)\right)\mu.

Using the fact that
G_{\mu}(a,\mu)=0
for any
a
tangent to
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, and

D\left(G_{\mu}(b,c)\mu\right).a=G_{\mu}(Db.a,c)\mu+G_{\mu}(b,Dc.a)\mu-G_{\mu}\left(a,\frac{b}{\mu}c\right)\mu+G_{\mu}(b,c)a,

we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{b}c

\displaystyle=\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{a}\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{b}c+\tau\left(G_{\mu}(a,Dc.b)+G_{\mu}(b,Dc.a)+G_{\mu}(c,Db.a)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\left(1-\tau\right)G_{\mu}(b,c)a-\tau\left(1+\tau\right)G_{\mu}(a,\frac{b}{\mu}c)\mu.

Using the fact that
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
has zero curvature on
\operatorname{Dens}_{+}(M)
, the curvature tensor of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is given by

\displaystyle R^{(\alpha)}(a,b)c

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{b}c-\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{b}\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}c-\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{[a,b]}c

\displaystyle=\tau G_{\mu}(c,Db.a-Da.b)\mu+\tau\left(1-\tau\right)(G_{\mu}(b,c)a-G_{\mu}(a,c)b)-\tau G_{\mu}([a,b],c)\mu,

which yields the result as the first and third terms on the righthand side cancel.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

For
\alpha\in\{-1,0\}
we have shown in Lemma
2.2
that
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection of the
\alpha
-Fisher–Rao metric. Next we will show the statement for
\alpha=1
. To calculate the Christoffel symbols we proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
. We have:

\displaystyle\left(D_{\mu,a}\tilde{G}^{1}\right)(b,c)

\displaystyle=D_{\mu,a}\left(\int_{M}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda-\int_{M}\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda\int\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=-2\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda+\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda\int\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda+\int_{M}\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda

Thus we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,a}\tilde{G}^{1}\right)(b,c)+\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,b}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,c)-\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{\mu,c}G^{\alpha}\right)(a,b)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda+\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\lambda\int\frac{c}{\mu}\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad=\tilde{G}^{1}\left(\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu-\left(\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu\right)\mu,c\right).

Thus we read off that

\Gamma_{\mu}(a,b)=\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu-\left(\int_{M}\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\mu\right)\mu.

To see that these really are the Christoffel symbols it remains to note that
\Gamma_{\mu}(a,b)\in T_{\mu}\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, which follows since they integrate to zero.

It remains to prove that
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is non-metric for
\alpha\notin\{-1,0,1\}
. This part of the proof is inspired by a post of Robert Bryant on MathOverflow
2
2

2

https://mathoverflow.net/questions/54434/when-can-a-connection-induce-a-riemannian-metric-for-which-it-is-the-levi-civita
.
Let us assume that there exists a Riemannian metric
\bar{G}^{\alpha}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
such that
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
is compatible with
\bar{G}^{\alpha}
, i.e.,

(9)

a\bar{G}^{\alpha}(b,c)-\bar{G}^{\alpha}(\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}b,c)-\bar{G}^{\alpha}(b,\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}c)=0,

for all
a,b,c
vector fields on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
. Then we would also have:

\bar{G}^{\alpha}(R^{(\alpha)}(a,b)c,c)=0,

where
R^{(\alpha)}
is the Riemann curvature tensor of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
. From Lemma
4.4
, this condition can be rewritten

G_{\mu}(b,c)\bar{G}^{\alpha}_{\mu}(a,c)=G_{\mu}(a,c)\bar{G}^{\alpha}_{\mu}(b,c),

implying that

(10)

\bar{G}_{\mu}^{\alpha}(a,b)=h(\mu)G_{\mu}(a,b),

for some conformal factor
h(\mu)=e^{\theta(\mu)}
. The terms in the compatibility condition (
9
) can then be computed using (
10
) and the definition of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
:

\displaystyle a\bar{G}^{\alpha}(b,c)

\displaystyle=G_{\mu}(b,c)Dh.a+h(\mu)\left(G_{\mu}(Db.a,c)+G_{\mu}(Dc.a,b)-\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\mu\right),

\displaystyle\bar{G}^{\alpha}(\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}b,c)

\displaystyle=h(\mu)\left(G_{\mu}(Db.a,c)-\tau\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\mu\right),

\displaystyle\bar{G}^{\alpha}(\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}_{a}c,b)

\displaystyle=h(\mu)\left(G_{\mu}(Dc.a,b)-\tau\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\mu\right),

where
\tau=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
.
Using the fact that
Dh.a=h(\mu)D\theta.a
, we obtain that equation (
9
) is written

G_{\mu}(b,c)D\theta.a+\left(2\tau-1\right)\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\mu=0,\quad\forall a,b,c\in T\operatorname{Prob}(M).

Now we show that for every
a\in T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
, there exist
b,c\in T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
such that the second term of the LHS in the above equation vanishes while
G_{\mu}(b,c)
is not zero. This will imply that
\theta
is constant, so that
\bar{G}^{\alpha}
is in fact a constant multiple of the Fisher–Rao metric
G
, yielding a contradiction for all values of
\alpha
except for
\alpha=0
(i.e.,
\tau=1/2
), the only case where
G
is compatible with the
\alpha
-connection.

To show this last step, fix
a\in T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
and consider
b\in T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
that is also in the orthogonal of
a
with respect to
G
. Then
\frac{a}{\mu}b
belongs to
T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
. Denote

c=b-G_{\mu}\left(b,\frac{a}{\mu}b\right)G_{\mu}\left(\frac{a}{\mu}b,\frac{a}{\mu}b\right)^{-1}\frac{a}{\mu}b.

Then
c
also belongs to
T\operatorname{Prob}(M)
and is, by construction,
G
-orthogonal to
\frac{a}{\mu}b
, so that

\int\frac{a}{\mu}\frac{b}{\mu}\frac{c}{\mu}\mu=0.

On the other hand

G_{\mu}(b,c)=G_{\mu}(b,b)-G_{\mu}\left(b,\frac{a}{\mu}b\right)^{2}G_{\mu}\left(\frac{a}{\mu}b,\frac{a}{\mu}b\right)^{-1}

is strictly positive since
\frac{a}{\mu}
is not constant, because
a
must integrate to zero.
∎

4.2.
The geometry of the
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)

In this section we aim to use the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
as defined in (
7
) for understanding the geometry of the
\alpha
-connection
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
;
for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
similar calculations were done in
[
9
]
; here we note how they extend to any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

The main idea in this section is to pull back an appropriate natural connection on a submanifold of
C^{\infty}(M)
. For this connection, the geodesic equation has a simpler structure than for
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
, and we can obtain a closed formula for its solution, up to a solution of an ODE. In the following we will exclude the case
\alpha=1
, which is slightly different and was treated in Remark
4.3
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\alpha\neq 1
and let
\Phi_{\alpha}
as in (
7
).
Then

S_{\alpha}:=\Phi_{\alpha}(\operatorname{Prob}(M))=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}(M)~:~f>0\text{ and }\,\int_{M}f^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\,\lambda=\left(\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\right\}

is a smooth submanifold of
C^{\infty}(M)
, whose tangent space is

T_{f}S_{\alpha}=\left\{\xi\in C^{\infty}(M)~:~\int_{M}f^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\xi\,\lambda=0\right\}.

Furthermore, the inverse
\Phi^{-1}_{\alpha}:S_{\alpha}\to\operatorname{Prob}(M)
is given by

\Phi^{-1}_{\alpha}(f)=\left(\frac{|1-\alpha|}{2}f\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the formula for
\Phi_{\alpha}
: the first condition follows from the fact that elements of
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
are positive densities, whereas the second condition follows from the total mass constraint. The remaining statements directly follow.
∎

Definition 4.7

(Radial projection and the
\alpha
-connection on
S_{\alpha}
)
.

Let
\alpha\neq 1
. The radial (
\alpha
-)projection
\pi^{\alpha}:TC^{\infty}(M)|_{S_{\alpha}}\to TS_{\alpha}
is the projection with respect to the splitting

T_{f}C^{\infty}(M)=T_{f}S_{\alpha}\oplus\operatorname{span}\{f\}
, that is,

\pi_{f}^{\alpha}(\xi)=\xi-\left(\frac{|1-\alpha|}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\left(\int_{M}f^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\xi\,\lambda\right)f.

This notion allows us define a new
\alpha
-connection on
S_{\alpha}
:
let
\nabla
be the trivial connection on
C^{\infty}(M)
.
We then define the
\alpha
-connection
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
on
S_{\alpha}
by

(\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}_{\xi}\eta)_{f}=\pi^{\alpha}_{f}(\nabla_{\xi}\eta).

The following proposition shows that the pullback of the
\alpha
-connection
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
yields a multiple of the Amari-C̆encov
\alpha
-connection on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
:

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\alpha\neq 1
. Up to a constant depending on the footpoint, the pullback of
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
to
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
via the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
is the Amari-C̆encov
\alpha
-connection
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
; more precisely we have

\Phi^{*}\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}|_{\mu}=\operatorname{sgn}(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}|_{\mu}.

In particular, the geodesic equations of
\Phi^{*}\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
and
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
are the same.

Proof.

The proof follows by direct calculation, cf.
[
9
, Theorem 3.12 and Theorem 4.10]
where the case
\alpha\in(-1,1)
was treated.
∎

By definition the geodesic equation of the connection
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
is given by

\pi^{\alpha}_{\gamma}(\ddot{\gamma})=0
, that is,
\ddot{\gamma}\in\operatorname{span}(\gamma)
, i.e., the geodesic equations can be written as

\begin{cases}\ddot{\gamma}\,\parallel\,\gamma\\
\int_{M}\gamma^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\,\lambda=\left(\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}\right)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}.\end{cases}

Given the simplicity of the geodesic equation of
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
, one can guess that its solutions are projections of straight lines to
S_{\alpha}
.
For
\alpha\in(-1,1)
this has been shown to be true, up to time reparametrization, cf.
[
9
, Theorem 4.11]
.
The following theorem states that this is true for any
\alpha\neq 1
, and extends this to treat both the initial and boundary value geodesic problems:

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\neq 1
. Let
f\in S_{\alpha}
and let
\xi\in C^{\infty}(M)
,
\xi\neq f
.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval containing
0
, and suppose
\tau:I\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the ODE

\begin{split}\ddot{\tau}(t)&=2\frac{\int_{M}(f+\tau(t)\xi)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\xi\,\lambda}{\int_{M}(f+\tau(t)\xi)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\,\lambda}\dot{\tau}(t)^{2},\\
\tau(0)&=0,\\
\dot{\tau}(0)&=1.\end{split}

Then
\gamma:I\to S_{\alpha}
, defined by

\gamma(t)=\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}\frac{f+\tau(t)\xi}{\left(\int_{M}(f+\tau(t)\xi)^{\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\,\lambda\right)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}},

is a geodesic of
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
emanating from
\gamma(0)=f
.
For
\xi\in T_{f}S_{\alpha}
, this is the unique geodesic with initial conditions
\gamma(0)=f
,
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\xi
.
For
\bar{f}\in S_{\alpha}
and
\xi=\bar{f}-f
, this is the unique geodesic from
f
to
\bar{f}
.

Corollary 4.10

(Geodesics of
\alpha
-connections on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
)
.

The geodesics of
\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}
on
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
are obtained by pulling back the geodesics of
\tilde{\nabla}^{\alpha}
via the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
.
In particular, this gives the general solution of the geodesic equation, as well as showing that
(\operatorname{Prob}(M),\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)})
is uniquely-geodesically-convex — any two probability densities can be connected by a unique
\alpha
-geodesic.

3

3

We note that for finite dimensional parametric models, a similar equation for the geodesic connecting two probability measures is given in

(see also
[

Yet, to best of our knowledge, this formula appears without a proof that
\tau

\tau
.

Proof.

The fact that
\gamma(t)
is a geodesic is identical to the proof of
[

.
A straightforward calculation shows that

\dot{\gamma}(0)=\xi-\left(\frac{2}{|1-\alpha|}\right)^{-\frac{2}{1-\alpha}}\left(\int_{M}f^{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\xi\,\lambda\right)f=\pi^{\alpha}_{f}(\xi),

and in particular
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\xi
if
\xi\in T_{f}S_{\alpha}

The fact that, in this case,

\overline{\nabla}^{(\alpha)}

[

.
This establishes that all the geodesics are obtained by projecting straight lines onto

In order to prove its existence, we need to show that for some

.
Note that

Remark 4.11

(Longtime existence of
\alpha
-geodesics and relation to the periodic generalized Proudman–Johnson equations.)
.

It follows from the above discussion that geodesics can blowup in one of two cases: either if

is a convex (it is part of the

-sphere), incomplete sub-manifold (it is shown that
\tau>t
for

, hence for every

one hits the boundary of

.

In Section

4.3.
Finite dimensional statistical models

In this final section, we investigate the Riemannian property of the
\alpha
-connections in the case of finite-dimensional submanifolds of
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that describe parametric statistical models.
Consider a parametric family of probability densities
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}=\{p(x,\theta)\,dx:\theta\in\Theta\}
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the sample variable,
dx
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the parameter.
The Fisher–Rao metric on
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
is obtained by restricting the metric from
\operatorname{Prob}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the
\alpha
-connections are obtained by projecting the connections from the larger space.
Below we recall the explicit expressions, when
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}
is identified with the parameter space
\Theta
.

Definition 4.12

(Fisher–Rao metric and
\alpha
-connections on
\Theta
)
.

The Fisher–Rao metric on the parameter space
\Theta
is defined for any
1\leq i,j\leq d
by

G_{ij}(\theta):=E_{\theta}\left[\partial_{i}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{j}\ell(X,\theta)\right]

where
\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{i}}
,
\ell(x,\theta)=\log p(x,\theta)
is the log-density and
E_{\theta}
denotes expectation with respect to the density
p(\cdot,\theta)
. The
\alpha
-connection
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
has Christoffel symbols defined for any
1\leq i,j,k\leq d
by

\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k}(\theta):=G_{\theta}(\nabla^{(\alpha)}_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j},\partial_{k}):=E_{\theta}\left[\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ell(X,\theta)+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\partial_{i}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{j}\ell(X,\theta)\right)\partial_{k}\ell(X,\theta)\right].

Note that here and below, the Christoffel symbols of the first kind
\Gamma_{ij,k}
are obtained from the Christoffel symbols of the second kind
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
by lowering the index with respect to the Fisher–Rao metric, even when the connection is not metric with respect to it.

The following example shows that in finite dimensions, we can get a different behavior than the one described in Theorem
4.2
in infinite dimensions. For this particular model, taken from
[
2
]
, all
\alpha
-connections are flat and thus Riemannian.

Example 4.13
.

Consider a probability measure
q
on
\mathbb{R}
,
d
i.i.d. random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}\sim q
, and the distribution of the random vector
X=Y+\theta
obtained by translating
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
in the direction of a given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}=\{p(x,\theta)=q^{(d)}(x-\theta),\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}\quad\text{where}\quad q^{(d)}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}q(x_{i})\quad\forall x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}).

Then the Fisher–Rao metric is given by

\displaystyle G_{ij}(\theta)

\displaystyle=E_{X\sim p(\cdot,\theta)}\left[(\log q)^{\prime}(X_{i}-\theta_{i})(\log q)^{\prime}(X_{j}-\theta_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}r\quad\text{where}\quad r=E_{Y\sim q}\left[(\log q)^{\prime}(Y)^{2}\right],

where we have used the fact that
E_{q}\left[(\log q)^{\prime}(Y)\right]=0
. Notice that
r
is independent of
\theta
and thus the Fisher–Rao metric on
\mathcal{M}
is Euclidean. Similarly, the Christoffel symbols of the
\alpha
-connection are given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\alpha)}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-E_{Y\sim q^{(d)}}\left[\left(\delta_{ij}(\log q)^{\prime\prime}(Y_{i})+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}(\log q)^{\prime}(Y_{i})(\log q)^{\prime}(Y_{j})\right)(\log q)^{\prime}(Y_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-E_{Y\sim q}\left[\left((\log q)^{\prime\prime}(Y)+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}(\log q)^{\prime}(Y)^{2}\right)(\log q)^{\prime}(Y)\right]\quad\text{if}\quad i=j=k\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\alpha)}
is zero unless
i=j=k
, for which it is equal to a constant that does not depend on
\theta
. Dropping the
\alpha
exponent in the Christoffel symbols for the sake of simplicity, we obtain for the curvature tensor

\displaystyle{R^{(\alpha)}}_{ijk}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\Gamma_{jk}^{\ell}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\Gamma_{ik}^{\ell}+\Gamma_{im}^{\ell}\Gamma_{jk}^{m}-\Gamma_{jm}^{\ell}\Gamma_{ik}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(\Gamma_{im,\ell}\Gamma_{jk,m}-\Gamma_{jm,\ell}\Gamma_{ik,m}\right)=0,

so that the
\alpha
-connection is flat on the model
\mathcal{M}
identified with its parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any value of
\alpha
. Thus it admits a local parallel frame field, which is global since the manifold is simply connected. By declaring this frame field to be orthonormal one defines a metric with respect to which the connection is metric. Since
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is torsionless, it is the Levi-Civita connection of this metric.
Thus the
\alpha
-connections on
\mathcal{M}
are Riemannian connections for all
\alpha
, unlike in the infinite-dimensional setting.

By contrast, for other standard examples, namely exponential families with a two-dimensional parameter space, we get the same picture as in the non parametric setting: the
\alpha
-connections are non metric except for
\alpha\in\{\pm 1,0\}
. Note that the case
\alpha=\pm 1
is not trivial since the definition of the
\alpha
-connections on these statistical models are obtained by projecting the connections from the larger space by the FR metric, which is not the metric with respect to which the connection is metric.

Definition 4.14

(Exponential family)
.

A parametric model is an exponential family with natural parameter
\theta
if the density is of the form

(11)

p(x,\theta)=\exp(h(x)+\theta\cdot\varphi(x)-F(\theta))

for certain functions
h
,
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
F
on
\Theta
.

The following geometric properties of exponential families are well-known.

Lemma 4.15

(Geometry of exponential families)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}=\{p(x,\theta)\,dx:\theta\in\Theta\}
be an exponential family, i.e., such that
p
is of the form (
11
). Then for
1\leq i,j,k,\ell\leq d
:

•

The Fisher–Rao metric is the hessian of the log-partition function
F

(12)

G_{ij}(\theta)=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}F(\theta).

•

The Christoffel symbols of the
\alpha
-connections are given by

(13)

\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k}=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}F_{ijk}.

•

The curvature tensor is written

(14)

{R^{(\alpha)}}_{ijk}^{\ell}=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}\left(F_{ikm}F_{jrs}-F_{jkm}F_{irs}\right)

Here we have used the notation
F_{ijk}:=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}F
.

Proof.

The expression for
G_{ij}
follows from straightforward computation. Since the hessian of the log-density is independent of the sample variable and
\partial_{k}\ell(X,\theta)
has zero expectation, we get

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{ij,k}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}F(\theta)E_{\theta}\big[\partial_{k}\ell(X,\theta)\big]+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}E_{\theta}\big[\partial_{i}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{j}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{k}\ell(X,\theta)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}E_{\theta}\big[\partial_{i}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{j}\ell(X,\theta)\partial_{k}\ell(X,\theta)\big].

Denoting
\partial_{i}f=f_{i}
, we notice that, since
\ell_{ij}=f_{ij}/f-f_{i}f_{j}/f^{2}
and
\ell_{ij}
is constant in the sample variable,

\displaystyle E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{ijk}\big]

\displaystyle=E_{\theta}\left[\partial_{i}\left(\frac{f_{jk}}{f}-\frac{f_{j}f_{k}}{f^{2}}\right)\right]=E_{\theta}\left[\frac{f_{ijk}}{f}-\frac{f_{jk}f_{i}}{f^{2}}-\frac{f_{ki}f_{j}}{f^{2}}-\frac{f_{ij}f_{k}}{f^{2}}+2\frac{f_{i}f_{j}f_{k}}{f^{3}}\right]

\displaystyle=0-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{jk}\ell_{i}\big]-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{ki}\ell_{j}\big]-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{ij}\ell_{k}\big]-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{i}\ell_{j}\ell_{j}\big]

\displaystyle=-\ell_{jk}E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{i}\big]-\ell_{ki}E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{j}\big]-\ell_{ij}E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{k}\big]-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{i}\ell_{j}\ell_{j}\big]=-E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{i}\ell_{j}\ell_{j}\big],

and since
E_{\theta}\big[\ell_{ijk}\big]=-F_{ijk}
we obtain
\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(\alpha)}=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}F_{ijm}
. Then the curvature tensor defined by
R^{(\alpha)}(\partial_{i},\partial_{j})\partial_{k}=:{R^{(\alpha)}}_{ijk}^{\ell}\partial_{\ell}
is written

\displaystyle{R^{(\alpha)}}_{ijk}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}\Gamma_{jk}^{\ell}-\partial_{j}\Gamma_{ik}^{\ell}+\Gamma_{im}^{\ell}\Gamma_{jk}^{m}-\Gamma_{jm}^{\ell}\Gamma_{ik}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left(F_{ijkm}G^{m\ell}+F_{jkm}\partial_{i}G^{m\ell}-F_{ijkm}G^{m\ell}+F_{ikm}\partial_{j}G^{m\ell}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}\left(F_{jkr}g^{rm}F_{ims}G^{s\ell}-F_{ikr}G^{rm}F_{jms}G^{s\ell}\right).

Then using the fact that
\partial_{i}G^{mr}G_{rs}=-G^{mr}\partial_{i}G_{rs}
and so
\partial_{i}G^{m\ell}=-G^{mr}\partial_{i}G_{rs}G^{s\ell}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{R^{(\alpha)}}_{ijk}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\left(F_{jkm}F_{irs}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}+F_{ikm}F_{jrs}G^{mr}G^{s\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{4}\left(F_{jkr}F_{ims}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}-F_{ikr}F_{jms}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{4}\bigg(-2F_{jkm}F_{irs}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}+F_{ikm}F_{jrs}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-\alpha)F_{jkm}F_{irs}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}-(1-\alpha)F_{ikm}F_{jrs}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{4}G^{rm}G^{s\ell}\left(F_{ikm}F_{jrs}-F_{jkm}F_{irs}\right)

as expected.
∎

The following lemma summarizes the Riemannian or non-Riemannian properties of
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
on a two-dimensional exponential family.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta}=\{p(x,\theta)\,dx:\theta\in\Theta\}
be an exponential family, i.e., such that
f
is of the form (
11
). Assume that the Fisher–Rao metric on
\Theta
is not flat and
\dim\Theta=2
. Then

•

If
\alpha=1
, then
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection of the Euclidean metric on
\Theta
.

•

If
\alpha=0
, then
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher–Rao metric.

•

If
\alpha=-1
, then
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is the Levi-Civita connection of the metric
G^{(-1)}(\theta)=(\mathrm{Hess}_{\theta}F)^{2}
.

•

If
\alpha\notin\{1,0,-1\}
then
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
is a non metric affine connection.

Remark 4.17
.

Note that in the infinite-dimensional setting, it is the
(-1)
-connection that is the trivial connection while here for finite-dimensional submanifolds corresponding to exponential models, it is the
1
-connection. On the finite-dimensional version of
\operatorname{Prob}(M)
defined by the submanifold of probability measures on a finite set (a mixture model, see
[
2
]
), it is the
(-1)
-connection that is trivial, just like in the infinite-dimensional setting. This is the reason why the
1
and
(-1)
-connections are called the exponential and mixture connections, respectively.
