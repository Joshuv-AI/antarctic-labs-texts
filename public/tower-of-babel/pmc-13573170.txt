Choline chloride-hexanoic acid deep eutectic solvent: structure, stability and aggregation behaviour in light rare earth extraction
Authors: Nanda Kishore Mohanta, Sujata Mishra, Puspalata Rajesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) formed by mixing a hydrogen bond donor (HBD) and hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) in specific molar ratios have emerged as green solvents because of their advantageous physicochemical properties, biodegradability and low toxicity. In the present work, a hydrophobic DES (HDES) composed of choline chloride (ChCl) and hexanoic acid (HexA) in a molar ratio of 1 : 4 was prepared and characterised by FTIR and 1H NMR spectral studies. Physico-chemical property measurements showed a gradual decrease in density and refractive index as the temperature increased from 298.15 K to 328.15 K. The conductivity and dielectric constant values increased in the same temperature range. The 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was further used for the solvent extraction of lanthanum (La), cerium (Ce), praseodymium (Pr), and neodymium (Nd) from aqueous nitrate solutions. The effects of various parameters, such as pH, HNO3 molarity, shaking time, DES/aqueous phase volume ratio, and temperature, on the efficiency of light rare-earth extraction were investigated. Good extraction efficiencies of 83.03%, 88.30%, 91.20% and 78.78% were obtained for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii), respectively, from 3 M HNO3 at a DES/aqueous phase ratio of 3 : 1. The radiation stability of the DES and its subsequent effect on the extraction behaviour of LREEs were examined. The aggregation behaviour of DESs before and after irradiation was also investigated using dynamic light scattering.

Introduction

Rapid technological advancements have significantly increased the demand for rare-earth elements (REEs), stimulating the exploration of alternative and sustainable sources for their recovery.1 Their distinctive and remarkable magnetic, optical, and catalytic properties, arising from unique electronic configurations, make them special for emerging technologies.2 Rare-earth elements are crucial, high-value materials principally used for the production of electronics, powerful permanent magnets, green energy technologies, and defence systems.3 The increasing demand for REEs, together with concerns regarding the depletion of primary resources and the growing generation of REE-containing wastes, has intensified interest in the recovery of these elements from both primary and secondary resources. Recycling such wastes not only reduces the environmental burden associated with REE-containing waste streams but also contributes to the conservation of primary rare-earth resources. Thus, the approach of recovering these REE metals from secondary resources falls within the purview of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).4 Several technologies have been investigated for the recovery and separation of REEs, including precipitation, adsorption, ion exchange, chromatography, membrane separation, electrochemical processes, biosorption, and solvent extraction.5,6 Among these, solvent extraction is one of the most established and widely applied techniques for the separation and purification of metal ions because of its high selectivity, efficient mass transfer, operational flexibility and suitability for large-scale processing.7–9 In particular, solvent extraction has demonstrated considerable potential for the separation of light rare-earth elements (LREEs), where differences in the chemical affinity of REE ions toward suitable extractants can be exploited for selective separation. However, traditional solvents used in solvent extraction often pose environmental and safety concerns and also adversely affect equipment during prolonged use. It also presents several challenges, including greater operating costs. To make these separation processes safer and cleaner, the use of green solvents for REE separation is emerging nowadays.10 An effective green solvent should ideally possess low volatility, low toxicity and non-flammability. Additional properties that are critical are moderate viscosity, appropriate hydrophobic characteristics, and high density, which lead to better separation efficiency and extraction performance.11,12 Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) are a group of neoteric promising solvents formed by combining a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD) in a suitable molar ratio.13 Depending on their classification, DESs comprise either a variety of cations and anions (Types I–IV)14 or exclusively non-ionic molecular components (Type V).15 They display low melting points, attributed to the presence of large, structurally asymmetric ions or highly dissimilar molecular complexes that lower the system's lattice or cohesive energy. Because of their beneficial properties, such as ease of synthesis, low toxicity, biodegradability, non-volatility, and tunable properties for specific applications, these solvents have recently gained considerable attention as an alternative to conventional organic solvents in various fields.16,17 The ability of a DES to accept protons or donate electrons for hydrogen bond formation is largely determined by the properties of the anionic segment of the HBA.18

Among various DESs, choline chloride (ChCl)-based DESs have received considerable attention because of their relatively low cost, ease of preparation, low vapour pressure and eco-friendly nature. ChCl-based DESs show an extensive liquid range, non-flammability, low vapour pressure, biocompatibility, and biodegradability.19 Acidic DESs formed by mixing ChCl and carboxylic acids are used for metal oxide dissolution.20 In hydrometallurgy, leaching and solvent extraction are sequential processes. Leaching acts as the bulk dissolution stage, whereas solvent extraction serves as the precise separation stage.21 ChCl-carboxylic acid-based DESs have been mostly used for the leaching of REEs from different sources. The ChCl : lactic acid DES was found to exhibit good leaching efficiency to recover REEs from NdFeB magnet wastes. It was found that a high temperature and an appropriate water content in the DES media yielded a leaching efficiency over 85% for Nd and Pr.22 Using ChCl : urea : malonic acid (MA) (1 : 1 : 0.5) and ChCl : MA (1 : 1), the leaching of REEs followed the order La < Ce < Nd < Sm, showing an overall increase in the extraction efficiency with the increasing atomic number, except for yttrium. In comparison, DESs based on citric acid exhibited lower selectivity, with the leaching sequence not corresponding to the increasing atomic number.19 Amongst all REEs, Nd displayed the maximum dissolution relative to other metals. Among the LREEs, La exhibited a slightly higher solubility than that of Ce; however, both exhibited low overall dissolution, indicating limited selectivity of the tested DESs.23 These studies clearly demonstrate the potential of DESs as leaching media for REE recovery from secondary resources. However, DES-assisted leaching and DES-based solvent extraction represent fundamentally different approaches. Although DESs have been increasingly investigated for the leaching of REEs from ores, magnets and other secondary resources, their application as extraction media for the selective solvent extraction of LREEs from aqueous solutions remains comparatively limited. In particular, systematic investigations correlating the physicochemical properties, molecular interactions, and structural characteristics of ChCl-based DESs with their LREE extraction behaviour are still scarce.

Considering these limitations, the present investigation focuses on the synthesis and spectroscopic characterization of the ChCl : HexA (1 : 4) DES. Detailed physicochemical investigations, including density, viscosity, dielectric constant, refractive index, and electrical conductivity, were conducted on the as-synthesized DES. To analyze the aggregation characteristics of DESs, dynamic light scattering studies were carried out. The radiation stability of the DES was investigated to judge its suitability for use in the nuclear fuel reprocessing industry. The solvent extraction of LREEs such as La, Ce, Pr, and Nd from a nitrate solution was carried out under varying pH values, HNO3 concentrations, shaking times, phase volume ratios, and temperatures. Efforts were also made to evaluate the LREE extraction performance using the irradiated DESs.

Materials and methods

This work used only analytical-grade chemicals and reagents. These were used without any further purification. Lanthanum(iii) nitrate hexahydrate, cerium(iii) nitrate hexahydrate, praseodymium(iii) nitrate hexahydrate, and neodymium(iii) nitrate hexahydrate (each with 99.99% purity), choline chloride (99%) and hexanoic acid (>99%) were obtained from Merck life science private limited, Mumbai, India. Millipore water was used throughout the experiments for solution preparation. The details of all reagents used throughout this study are presented in Table S1 of the SI.

Synthesis of the DES

The 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA hydrophobic DES was prepared by combining desired amounts of ChCl with HexA in a molar ratio of 1 : 4 by the heating and stirring method (Fig. S1 of the SI). The mixture was heated to 333.15 K with constant stirring at 500 rpm using a thermostat magnetic stirrer. The process was continued until a transparent and homogeneous liquid (DES) was formed. Then, it was cooled to room temperature and stored in an airtight bottle in a moisture-free atmosphere (desiccator). The water content of the synthesized DES was 1.00 wt% in its pure form. This was measured by Karl Fischer titration using a Systronics Auto Universal Titrator 354. After shaking the DES with distilled water for 1 hour at room temperature and keeping it in contact with water for more than 24 h, the water content increased to 1.55 wt%. According to Van Osch et al.,24 DESs containing less than 2.55 wt% water can be classified as hydrophobic. Therefore, the synthesized DES retained its hydrophobic character even after prolonged contact with water.

Instruments

The prepared 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and 1H NMR (proton nuclear magnetic resonance) spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 MHz NanoBay instrument, using 32 scans with a relaxation delay of 2 seconds. The samples were prepared in CDCl3. FT-IR analysis was carried out using a JASCO ATR PRO ONE FTIR-4600 Type A spectrometer in the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The aggregation behaviour of the DES was evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 instrument (Malvern, UK). For the DLS measurements, approximately 1–2 mL of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was transferred into a thoroughly cleaned quartz cuvette with a path length of 10 mm. The measurements were performed to evaluate the average aggregate size and particle size distribution. In several instances, peaks attributable to larger particle sizes were observed, possibly due to a few large aggregates, dust particles, or experimental artifacts. To minimize such interference, all samples were filtered prior to analysis using Millex-GV 0.22 µm disposable PVDF membrane syringe filters (Aldrich). An avalanche photodiode (APD) detector was used to measure the scattered light intensity as a function of time using a non-invasive backscatter technique with a backscattered angle of 173°. All experiments were conducted in the manual mode at a constant temperature of 298.15 K and at temperatures ranging from 298.15 K to 328.15 K. To ensure reproducibility, each measurement was repeated at least six times, with each measurement consisting of 20 runs of 10 s duration, without any delay between consecutive runs. The LREE concentrations before and after the extraction process were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (SYSTRONICS model 2202 TS, India) by the Arsenazo(III) method.25 When individual metals were taken into study, Multi-LREEs analysis was performed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-AES Ultima 2, Horiba Jobin Yvon, France). Gamma irradiation to different absorbed doses was carried out using a Gamma Chamber 5000 obtained from the Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology (BRIT), Department of Atomic Energy, Mumbai, India. The dose rate was 1.127 kGy h−1 as measured using Fricke dosimetry.26 The weights of the chemicals were measured using the SAFFRON digital balance of model SES 224C with an accuracy of ±0.0001 g. The density of DES was determined using the relative mass measurement method. An Ostwald viscometer was used to measure the viscosity of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES. The Dielectric permittivity kit (for liquids) from Mittal Enterprises, New Delhi, was employed to measure the dielectric constant of the DES. The uncertainty in the measurement was ±0.004. An Abbe Refractometer, model MA-01, supplied by Mittal Enterprises, was used to determine the refractive index of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES with an accuracy of ±0.0002. An X-ray diffraction study was performed using a Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan D/max 2400 diffractometer. All measurements were performed in triplicate to minimise the measurement error.

Methods

Solvent extraction

For the solvent extraction experiment, the aqueous phase containing the requisite quantity of nitrate salts of LREEs in nitric acid or at a desired pH (La, Ce, Pr and Nd) and DES phases with 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA were contacted and stirred in a mechanical shaker under different experimental conditions of shaking time, HNO3 molarity, pH and DES/aqueous phase volume ratios. The concentration of LREEs was chosen by mimicking their amounts present in simulated high-level liquid waste (SHLLW) (La: 342 mg L−1, Ce: 684 mg L−1, Pr: 340 mg L−1 and Nd: 1120 mg L−1).27 Afterwards, the phases were allowed to settle down and subsequently centrifuged. The concentrations of LREE ions in the aqueous phase before extraction and in the raffinate were determined. Results and discussion

Spectral analysis

The formation of a hydrogen bonding network that accounts for the eutectic behaviour was confirmed by characterization techniques such as FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy. The hallmark of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES is the extensive hydrogen bonding network between the HBA (ChCl) and the HBD (hexanoic acid). In the FTIR spectra, the O–H stretching band of the pure HBD broadens, shifts to lower wavenumbers, and decreases in intensity, indicating the existence of H-bonding. In NMR, characteristic peak shifts upon the formation of the DES are observed owing to changes in the electronic environment caused by hydrogen bonding.

FTIR spectroscopy

The FTIR spectroscopy analysis of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES confirmed the formation of hydrogen-bond interactions between the chloride ion of ChCl and the hydroxyl group of HexA. As observed in Fig. 2, the –OH stretching vibration peak of pure HexA seen at 2940 cm−1 shifted to 2942 cm−1 for the DES with a substantial decrease in intensity, confirming the formation of a hydrogen bonding interaction. 1H NMR spectroscopy

Fig. 3 shows the 1H NMR spectra of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES constituents, that is, ChCl, HexA, and 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES. The –OH proton shows a downfield shift from 10.81 ppm for HexA to 11.10 ppm for the DES due to strong hydrogen bonding networks with ChCl. The alpha and beta –CH2 proton peaks at 2.34 ppm and 1.64 ppm in HexA remain unchanged because the aliphatic protons neighbouring the CO group are shielded from major DES interactions. In ChCl, the methyl group bound to nitrogen (N–CH3), observed at 3.5 ppm, becomes sharper and shifts to 3.4 ppm in the DES due to increased molecular mobility and altered electrostatic shielding from nearby HexA. The methylene group adjacent to the nitrogen (CH2–N) peak at 4.09 ppm for ChCl shows an upfield shift to 3.73 ppm for the DES due to a strong coordination between the choline head group and the Cl− ion, which markedly alters the local shielding. The peak at 4.25 ppm, assigned to methylene protons adjacent to the –OH group in ChCl, showed an upfield shift to 4.1 ppm and was better resolved in the DES.

Physico-chemical properties

Detailed knowledge of DESs' physicochemical characteristics, such as density, polarity, and viscosity, is essential for their possible industrial use as solvents in analytical applications like solvent extraction. These characteristics are influenced by the selection of HBAs and HBDs, their temperature, molar ratios, and the synthesis method.28 As previously reported, 1 : 1 and 1 : 2 ChCl : HexA DESs show high density and viscosity values.29 Table S2 in the SI represents the comparison of density and viscosity values with the present 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES. The density and viscosity values decease with the increase in the molar ratio of hexanoic acid in the DESs. Higher values of these properties in the case of 1 : 1 and 1 : 2 ChCl : HexA DESs make them unsuitable for the purpose of solvent extraction. To determine the optimum molar ratio, ChCl : HexA HDESs at various molar ratios (1 : 3, 1 : 4, and 1 : 5) were synthesized by the heating and stirring method. As demonstrated in Fig. S2 of the SI, a molar ratio of 1 : 3 results in a turbid, unstable suspension containing unreacted components. Conversely, increasing the hexanoic acid (HBD) concentration to 1 : 4 and 1 : 5 molar ratios productively yields entirely homogeneous, stable, transparent, hydrophobic liquid networks. This confirms that the critical threshold for attaining a fully coordinated DES is encountered within the 1 : 4 to 1 : 5 ranges, extending its flexibility for use. The excess uncomplexed acid in the 1 : 5 ChCl : HexA HDES may undergo esterification degradation with the alcohol group of choline, and therefore, 1 : 4 ratio was considered for extraction purpose. The measurements of density, viscosity, dielectric constant, refractive index, and electrical conductivity of the synthesized 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES were carried out at 10 K intervals in the temperature range of 298.15 K to 328.15 K.

Density

Density is an important property of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES, which assesses their solvation ability and is highly relevant to the solvent extraction biphasic system.30 The temperature, structural packing and molecular organization affect the density values of the DES.31 The density measurement of the synthesized 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was performed in the temperature range of 298.15 K to 328.15 K. The density was found to decrease as the temperature increased. At 298.15 K, the density was 0.973 g cm−3, which decreased to 0.960 g cm−3 at 328.15 K. The decrease in density with the increasing temperature is due to thermal expansion and increased molecular motion at higher temperatures, causing a decrease in mass per unit volume. Utilizing the relationship between the density and the temperature, the thermal expansion coefficient (α) was determined by means of eqn (S4) (SI). For the DES, both Vm and So (eqn (S5) and (S6) and Table S3 in the SI) showed a consistent increase with the increase in temperature, which can be attributed to thermal expansion and greater molecular mobility at higher temperatures. In contrast, Upot (eqn (S7) in the SI) decreased gradually as the temperature increased, indicating a reduction in intermolecular interactions. The molecular volume is a critical thermodynamic property that depends on the packing efficiency of the DES and determines the available free molecular volume for molecular mobility in the system, and is very important in mass transfer processes like solvent extraction. These values help elucidate the structure, packing efficiency, and available free space in the DES.

Viscosity

In separation technology, the viscosity of the solvent is of interest due to its strong influence on mass transport properties in the biphasic system. Several factors affect the viscosity of DESs, including the chemical nature of their components, their molar masses, and their molar ratios.32 The dynamic viscosity of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was measured in the temperature range of 298.15 to 328.15 K. The viscosity was found to decrease from 25.408 to 15.636 mPa s with the increase in temperature from 298.15 to 328.15 K. This decrease has been explained by the increase in molecular mobility at high temperatures, which reduces intermolecular interactions. The activation energy of viscous flow was determined using the Arrhenius equation from a plot of ln η vs. 1000/T (eqn (S8) and Fig. S3 in the SI). The calculated activation energy value for the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was 13.206 kJ mol−1.

Conductivity

In DESs, the conductivity mainly depends on the nature of the ionic species and their mobility. Typically, most DESs have low conductivity at room temperature due to strong intermolecular forces and restricted ion mobility.33 For the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES system, the electrical conductivity was found to increase from 2.397 mS cm−1 at 298.15 K to 9.496 mS cm−1 at 328.15 K. The observed increase in conductivity with temperature is mainly due to the decreased viscosity and weakened intermolecular interactions at higher temperatures. The dependence of the activation energy of the system was obtained from the linear plot of ln κ0 against 1000/T (eqn (S9) and Fig. S4 in the SI). The value of Ea was determined from the slope to be 38.383 kJ mol−1.

Dielectric constant

Dielectric constant is a very important physical property related to a solvent's polarity and its response to an external electric field. It plays an important role in ion solvation, intermolecular interactions and solvent properties in electrochemical and extraction applications in the DES.34 The dielectric constant of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES increased gradually from 4.970 at 298.15 K to 10.384 at 328.15 K. As the temperature increases, the viscosity of the DES decreases, making it easier for dipolar species to orient properly in the electric field. This results in higher polarization and more efficient charge distribution in the system. The molar polarization (Pm) of the investigated system was evaluated from experimentally measured dielectric constant values at different temperatures using the Clausius–Mossotti relation (eqn (S10) and Fig. S5 in the SI). The molar polarization, namely, electronic and ionic polarization, is independent of the temperature, whereas the orientation polarization of dipolar molecules depends on temperature.35 For this investigated DES system, the value increased from 70.749 at 298.15 K to 96.208 at 328.15 K. The plot of molar polarization versus temperature (Fig. S4 in the SI) showed a linear relationship, suggesting that orientation polarization is significant in the DES's polarization behaviour. The observed variation may be related to changes in dipolar alignment and molecular association as the temperature increases.

Refractive index

Refractive index is a very important physical property which can be used to test purity and to identify a substance. The refractive index values of DESs have been reported to decrease linearly with the increasing temperature.36 The refractive index of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was measured in a varying range of temperature from 298.15 to 328.15 K. The refractive index values showed a slight but continuous decrease with the increasing temperature (Fig. S6(a) in the SI), changing from 1.438 at 298.15 K to 1.435 at 328.15 K. The increase in temperature causes thermal expansion, reducing the close packing of molecules and lowering their density, thereby allowing light to travel faster and decreasing the refractive index. The molar refraction is a derived property associated with the polarizability and provides useful information about the molecular structure, intermolecular interactions, and optical behaviour of the system. Molar refraction (eqn (S11) and Fig. S6(b) in the SI) shows a slight increase with the increasing temperature, which may be attributed to the disruption of intermolecular interactions like hydrogen bonding present in the DES at higher temperatures.

Radiation stability

Gamma irradiation of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was carried out using a Gamma Chamber. The DES samples were exposed to gamma radiation at doses of 5, 25, 75, and 100 kGy to evaluate the influence of gamma radiation on the structural stability of the DES. High irradiation doses were selected to examine the radiation tolerance and possible degradation behaviour of this DES under extreme conditions. The radiation stability of the DES was investigated by recording FTIR, 1H NMR, UV-visible spectra, and DLS measurements of the DES after irradiation. The FTIR spectra of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES and γ-irradiated DES at different absorbed doses (5–100 kGy) were recorded. Fig. 4 reveals that the O–H stretching band at 2950–3000 cm−1 shows slow changes in peak intensity after increasing the radiation dose. DESs are highly cooperative supramolecular networks bound together by extensive hydrogen bonding, and upon absorbing radiation, their vibrational dynamics change. Similarly, the peaks of the CO stretching vibration around 1710 cm−1, the CH2 bending vibration around 1450 cm−1, and the C–N stretching vibration at slightly lower wavenumbers, which displayed changes in peak intensity, may be due to the liberation of free, uncomplexed HBDs/HBAs and subsequent structural distortion. No significant functional group shifts as peak positions remain fixed across all doses. No new peaks appear, confirming that no macro-degradation of the DES occurs, as detected by FTIR spectroscopy. Earlier studies on the DES showed no new species formation from FTIR or UV studies in the irradiated (100 kGy dose) DES, except the formation of a few gaseous products such as H2, CH4, CO2 and CO.37

FT-IR spectra of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES and gamma-irradiated DES exposed to different radiation doses of 5, 25, 75, and 100 kGy.

The 1HNMR spectra (Fig. 5) revealed that after irradiation (100 kGy), the –OH peak shifted upfield from 11.2 ppm to approximately 10.7 ppm, with slight broadening. This shift directly indicates that the radiation induces the restructuring or weakening of the intermolecular hydrogen bonds between HexA and ChCl. The symmetry of three equivalent methyl groups on the choline cation is broken after irradiation, as peak 6 shifts significantly upfield to around 2.9 ppm, separating from peaks 7 and 8, which remain at 3.4 ppm. This indicates a localized change in the chemical environment of the choline head group, in which one methyl group becomes distinctly shielded, compared to the other two groups. The chemical shifts and splitting patterns of the aliphatic protons for HexA (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and the ethylene backbone of choline (9, 10) remain virtually unchanged. No major new peaks or fragmentation degradation products are observed, indicating that the DES components are chemically stable under irradiation and only undergo spatial/conformational rearrangement within their non-covalent network.

1H NMR spectra of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES and gamma-irradiated DES exposed to a radiation dose of 100 kGy.

The effect of gamma irradiation on the structural stability of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES was studied by recording the UV-Vis absorption spectra in the range of 290–400 nm. The spectra obtained are displayed in Fig. 6. All samples exhibited broad absorption bands in the UV region, with absorbance decreasing gradually with the increasing wavelength.38 The characteristic broad absorption band of the DES was observed at λmax 304 nm with a moderate absorption intensity. After gamma irradiation, the absorbance drops slightly compared to the non-irradiated DES. The primary absorbance band remains centred around 305–310 nm. The absorption edge extends further toward 400 nm at higher doses. A large hyperchromic shift was observed at high doses (75–100 kGy). The 100 kGy-irradiated DES exhibited the highest absorbance across the entire wavelength range, with a broader absorption profile extending toward higher wavelengths. These observations complement the findings of FTIR and NMR investigations.

UV-Vis spectra of the unirradiated and gamma-irradiated 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DESs at different radiation doses (5, 25, 75, and 100 kGy).

The gamma irradiation of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES induces dose-dependent conformational and spatial reorganization of its non-covalent network instead of covalent degradation.39 High-dose exposure considerably weakens and restructures the intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network, as evinced by an upfield shift of the acid –OH proton in the NMR spectra of HexA and higher hydroxyl or carbonyl stretching peak intensities in the FTIR spectra. Moreover, radiation disrupts the local molecular symmetry around the choline head group, triggering a distinct desymmetrization of its methyl protons (NMR) and an increase in the intensity of the C–N band (FTIR). The broadening of the UV-Vis absorption band above 25 kGy points to enhanced electronic interactions, evidencing that the DES matrix accommodates high radiation doses through structural restructuring rather than covalent cleavage.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was also performed to examine the microstructural behaviour and aggregation characteristics of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES system. The aggregates in the DES phase are in constant Brownian motion, and this causes fluctuations in the scattered light intensity.40 The Stokes–Einstein expression (eqn (1)) can be used to estimate the average size and size distribution of the aggregates existing in the DES phase by examining the intensity variations at a fixed scattering angle.1where dh represents the hydrodynamic diameter of the aggregates, kB is the Boltzmann constant, D is the translational diffusion coefficient, T denotes the absolute temperature, and η corresponds to the dynamic viscosity of the dispersing medium. The intensity distribution data were considered for analysis because they most closely represent the actual measured scattering signal. The average particle size values were derived from the intensity-based particle distribution, which is more affected by the contribution of scattering from large-sized particles.41 The change in particle size distribution and the structural homogeneity of the DES system were evaluated by determining the hydrodynamic diameter and the polydispersity index (PDI), and the results obtained are summarized in Table S4 in the SI.

The obtained DLS results indicated a slight decrease in hydrodynamic diameter with the increasing irradiation dose. The unirradiated DES sample had an average diameter of 15.41 nm and a PDI of 0.49, which decreased gradually to 11.04 nm and 0.377, respectively. The polydispersity index (PDI) values are in the acceptable range. With the increasing irradiation dose, the DES particles become more uniform in size, as evidenced by lower PDI values. The number density distribution in hydrodynamic diameter as a function of irradiation dose is shown in Fig. 7. The maximum particle size was ∼1.757 nm in unirradiated DES, which gradually decreased to 1.498 nm, 1.076 nm, and 0.7131 nm at 5, 25, and 100 kGy, respectively. The hydrodynamic size of the nanoscale cluster formed by Ethaline (ChCl : ethylene glycol = 1 : 2) with phenol was reported to be ∼1.59 nm.42 This difference in the aggregation pattern of the DES system particle sizes and PDI with radiation is due to the change in the intermolecular interactions. The changes are mainly due to radiation-induced changes in hydrogen bonding and molecular packing in the DES structure.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) number distribution curves, showing a change in the number percentage of particles of different sizes in the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES with gamma-irradiation doses at 298.15 K.

There are a few studies in the literature on the radiation effects on DESs using dynamic light scattering. Based on density functional theory (DFT), choline acetate and malonic acid (1 : 2) are reported to be the most radiation and chemically stable DESs among a selected series of DESs.37

Thermal stability

The thermal stability of the DES depends on the HBA and HBD combination, their molar ratio, and the operating temperature. The stronger the hydrogen bonding, the higher the stability. ChCl : tartaric acid is reported as the most thermally stable DES among the 1 : 1 ChCl:carboxylic acid combinations at 393.15 K.43 The effect of temperature on the hydrodynamic size was measured over the temperature range of 298.15–328.15 K, and is given in Fig. 8. The results showed a decrease in hydrodynamic diameter with the increasing temperature. The DES samples showed an average diameter of 1.757 nm and a PDI of 0.491, which decreased to 0.703 nm and 0.246, respectively. The details of the size distribution with PDI values for 298.15–328.15 K are given in Table S5 in the SI. The PDI values also decreased with the increasing temperature, indicating greater uniformity in the size distribution. In DLS measurements, as shown in eqn (1), the calculated hydrodynamic diameter depends directly on the temperature, diffusion coefficient, and viscosity. As the temperature increases, the viscosity of the DES decreases, and hence, the diffusion increases. The refractive index also decreases for many DESs as the temperature increases.44 This can be the reason for the decrease in DES particle size with the increase in temperature.45,46

Solvent extraction studies

Variation in the HNO3 concentration and aqueous pH

The extraction efficiencies of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii), and Nd(iii) were highly sensitive to the pH and nitric acid concentration in the aqueous feed solution. The HNO3 medium offers numerous benefits in terms of selectivity, pH control, corrosion resistance, and compatibility with downstream processes compared with HCl and H2SO4 media for LREE extraction.47 The HNO3 concentration plays a very important role in tuning the extraction efficiency by creating an acidic environment, which facilitates DES solubility, promotes good phase separation, and drives the speciation of LREE ions. For the assessment of these effects, extraction experiments were conducted at varying concentrations of nitric acid (0.5–3 M), with a shaking time of 5 min and a DES/aq. phase ratio of 1 : 1. The extraction efficiency of LREEs was gradually enhanced with the increase in the acidity of aqueous phase. The extraction efficiencies for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) were found to be 30%, 32.26%, 61.78% and 21.57%, respectively, at 0.5 M HNO3. With the increase in nitric acid concentration to 3 M, the extraction efficiencies reached 44.26%, 43.12%, 64.33%, and 43.93% for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii), and Nd(iii), respectively, as indicated in Fig. 9(a). The UV-Vis spectra of the LREE(III)-loaded DES are shown in Fig. S7 in the SI, indicating a redshift (λmax shifts to longer wavelengths) with the increasing acid molarity. This increase in extraction with the increasing HNO3 concentration may be because of the availability of more LREE(III) nitrate species for extraction into the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES phase. The extraction of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii), and Nd(iii) was also studied by varying the aqueous pH from 0.5 to 3, with a shaking time of 5 min and a DES : Aqueous phase volume ratio of 1 : 1. The extraction efficiency of REEs shows a very slow increase with the increasing pH of the metal solution. At a certain pH, the extraction efficiency of REEs should decrease. The extraction efficiencies at pH 1.5 were 18.60%, 31.33%, 53.76% and 22.61%, respectively, for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii). The pH of the aqueous solution was increased from 1.5 to 2.5. At pH 2.5, the extraction efficiencies increased to 32.73% for La, 34.11% for Ce, 57.21% for Pr, and 28.19% for Nd. However, when the pH was further increased to 3, a slight decrease in extraction efficiency was observed for all REEs, as shown in Fig. 9(b). The extraction was observed to be higher with HNO3 than with the pH. Further studies were conducted in a nitric acid medium.

Effect of (a) nitric acid concentration and (b) pH on the extraction efficiency (% E) of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) using the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES.

Variation in shaking time

Phase mixing in metal extraction involves bringing an aqueous phase containing a metal ion into intimate contact with an immiscible solvent, preferably an extractant, to allow the metal ion to transfer into the solvent by forming a complex through various interactions. The mixing of the aqueous nitrate LREE(III) feed and the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES phase (1 : 1 phase volume ratio) was performed to achieve better mass transfer and extraction efficiency. The effect of shaking time on the extraction efficiency of LREEs was investigated at a constant HNO3 concentration (3 M) by varying the shaking time from 1 to 10 min. The extraction efficiency of LREEs increased with the shaking time from 1 to 5 minutes and then decreased. Fig. S8 in the SI shows a redshift with the increasing shaking time. The extraction efficiency of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) increases from 40%, 39%, 59.99%, and 39.51% to 44.26%, 43.12%, 64.33%, and 43.93%, respectively, when the time changes from 1 to 5 min and afterwards decreases, as shown in Fig. 10. Hence, it was clear that the extraction reached equilibrium at 5 min. Therefore, further experiments were conducted with a shaking time of 5 min.

Effect of shaking time on the extraction of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) using the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES.

Variation in the DES/aqueous phase volume ratio

The extraction efficiency of REEs was studied using a 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES at different DES-to-aqueous phase volume ratios, with the nitric acid concentration in the feed held constant at 3 M and the shaking time set to 5 min. The extraction efficiency of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) was enhanced with the phase ratio from 1 : 1 to 3 : 1. The extraction efficiencies of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) were increased from 44.26%, 43.12%, 64.33% and 43.93% at 1 : 1 phase ratio to 83.03%, 88.30%, 91.20% and 78.78%, respectively, at 3 : 1 phase ratio. This observation indicates that a higher volume of the DES phase provides a higher concentration of the DES, allowing it to bind to LREE(III) ions more effectively, thereby improving extraction, as shown in Fig. 11. The UV-vis spectral studies are also presented in Fig. S9 in the SI, showing a redshift with the increasing DES/aqueous phase volume ratio. This ensures the participation of a 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES in the extracted complex.

Effect of the DES/aqueous phase ratio on the extraction of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) using the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES.

Variation in temperature

Temperature plays an important role in the solvent extraction of rare-earth elements. The effect of temperature on the extraction behaviour was investigated by conducting experiments at temperatures ranging from 298.15 K to 328.15 K with an interval of 10 K, while the other experimental parameters, including HNO3 concentration (3 M), shaking time (5 min), and DES/aqueous phase ratio (1 : 1), were kept constant. The extraction efficiency of LREEs increased with the increasing temperature, indicating the endothermic nature of the extraction process. The extraction efficiencies of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii), and Nd(iii) increased from 44.26%, 43.12%, 64.33%, and 43.93% at 298.15 K to 49.55%, 49%, 65.51%, and 48.05%, respectively, at 328.15 K. The UV-Vis spectral studies are presented in Fig. S10 in the SI, showing a redshift (λmax becomes longer) upon increase in temperature. The improved extraction at high temperatures could be attributed to a reduction in the viscosity of the DES system, which facilitates better mass transfer and, in turn, faster diffusion of metal ions from the aqueous phase to the DES phase. Furthermore, higher temperatures might promote metal-DES interactions and facilitate the transport of complexes into the DES phase. The thermodynamic parameters for the extraction of LREEs by the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES were determined from the van't Hoff plots of log D against 1000/T (Fig. 12). The values of standard enthalpy change (ΔH°) and standard entropy change (ΔS°) were calculated from the slope and intercept values. The ΔH° values obtained for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) were 6.5464, 7.4972, 0.9994, and 5.6388 kJ mol−1, respectively, while the corresponding ΔS° values were 20.1142, 22.5246, 8.2141, and 16.4397 J mol−1 K−1. The positive ΔH° values indicate that the extraction process was endothermic, consistent with the observed increase in extraction efficiency with the increasing temperature. Furthermore, the positive ΔS° values suggested an increase in randomness during the extraction process, which may be attributed to the formation of inner-sphere complexes between the LREE(III) ions and the DES components.

van't Hoff plots of log D versus 1000/T for the extraction of La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) using the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES.

Effect of the radiation dose and aggregation on the extraction of LREE(III)

The effect of gamma irradiation on the extraction performance of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES toward LREE(III) ions was studied at various absorbed doses of 5, 25, 75 and 100 kGy. The study aimed to assess the suitability of the DES for the extraction processes under radioactive conditions and its reusability in reprocessing. The results showed that upon using the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES (irradiated with 100 kGy), the extraction efficiency of LREEs gradually decreased: La(iii)(83.03% to 65.65%), Ce(iii)(88.3% to 67.84%), Pr(iii)(91.2% to 67.84%) and Nd(iii)(78.78% to 68.98%). This could be due to radiation-induced disruption of the hydrogen-bonding network of the DES.

DLS analysis was carried out to investigate the particle size distribution and aggregation behaviour of DES loaded with all four LREE(III) ions. The intensity (%) distribution graph of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES before and after extraction at room temperature and 328.15 K is given in Fig. 13. The average particle size (Z-average) of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES before extraction at 298.15 K was 15.41 nm due to hydrogen-bonding interactions between ChCl and hexanoic acid. After LREE(III) loading, the DLS profile showed a noticeable increase in the intensity percentage from larger aggregates and a decrease in the intensity from smaller particles, which is shown in Fig. 13. The Z-average particle size increased to 291.8 nm and 1109 nm at 298.15 K and 328.15 K, respectively, indicating that these LREE(III)-DES coordination complexes largely form aggregates in the DES phase. The average size distribution and the polydispersity index (PDI) values for the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES before and after metal ion extraction are given in Table S5 in the SI. These observations confirm successful interaction and encapsulation of all four types of LREE ions within the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES network and demonstrate its suitability for metal extraction applications. La, Ce, Pr and Nd loadings increased the viscosity of the DES phase, implying that the species formed (LREE-DES complexes) is larger or more aggregated than the free DES. The formation of metal-extractant complexes often leads to their aggregation into larger reverse micelles, increasing the overall hydrodynamic radius of these species in the DES phase, particularly at higher multiple rare-earth loadings.48

Extraction mechanism and separation feasibility

The DES structure shows the donor sites and selectivity for lanthanides. Furthermore, developing an effective DES-based extraction method for LREEs depends heavily on the nature of the DES and the rare earths, as well as the extraction operating conditions. From the above discussion, it is very clear that the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES exhibits good potential to extract La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) from 3 M HNO3. The improved extraction of LREEs at higher nitric acid concentrations may be attributed to enhanced phase separation and increased solubility of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES, thereby increasing metal-DES complexation. The oxygen atom of the carbonyl group (CO) of HexA of the DES forms a coordinate bond with the LREE ions, and the presence of nitrate ions in the aqueous feed is counted for neutralising the positive charges on the LREE ions. The FTIR spectrum (Fig. 14) illustrates how an infrared spectrum changes from a free functional group in the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES to a LREE-coordinated complex as apparent from the shifting of CO (1710.55 cm−1 to 1714 cm−1) and O–H (2942.84 cm−1 to 2994 cm−1) peaks. The 1H NMR spectral data (Fig. 15) provide molecular-level evidence of structural rearrangements in the DES upon coordination with LREE ions. The hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail of the fatty acid does not participate directly in chemical bonding with the metal ions, and hence, remains largely unaltered in chemical shifts. The upfield shifting of the –OH peak from 11.10 ppm to 10.57 ppm in LREE-loaded DES confirms participation of the carboxylic acid functional group in complex formation. The alkyl chain protons of HexA (peaks 1–5) are structurally distant from the LREE centre and do not experience line broadening. The alkyl-chain proton signals of HexA appear relatively intense and sharp, whereas the ChCl signals are broadened and less intense. Consequently, the spectrum scale is dominated by the sharp HexA peaks, causing the broadened ChCl peaks to appear diminished in the overall spectrum.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) studies of the pure 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES and LREE-loaded DES were carried out after extraction. Both the pure DES and the LREE-loaded DES exhibit a single, broad hump centered on 2θ = 19.58° and 19.64°, respectively. There are no sharp, distinct Bragg peaks in either pattern. This shows that both samples are entirely amorphous liquids, deficient of any long-range crystalline structural order. When trivalent LREEs are loaded into the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES, the pattern retains its broad, amorphous profile. This shows that the LREEs are completely complexed within the DES network. The LREE-loaded DES shows a slightly higher peak intensity. This is typically due to the introduction of LREEs, which have a much higher electron density and cause stronger X-ray scattering than the ChCl and HexA molecules making up the pure 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES. There is a minor shoulder around 2θ = 13.10° in the pure DES that becomes smoother upon LREEs' loading. This indicates a minor rearrangement in the short-range molecular or ionic packing (the local coordination environment) of the DES when it complexes with the metal ions.

A schematic and structural presentation of the LREE(III)-DES complex formation is demonstrated in Fig. 16. The trivalent rare-earth centre experiences complete charge neutralization through coordination with three monodentate nitrate anions. The primary coordination sphere is established by the direct coordination of three neutral HexA molecules of DESs, which bind to the LREE(III) centre via the lone pairs of their carbonyl oxygen (CO) atoms, forming a stable 6-coordinate inner-sphere complex. Importantly, the ChCl component of the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES is responsible for the outer-sphere stabilization to the extracted complex. The chloride anions (Cl−) participate in an intricate hydrogen-bonding network with the acidic hydroxyl groups of HexA. The chloride network is structurally balanced by the large quaternary choline cations (Ch+) that hover on the edge through electrostatic interactions. The outer-sphere Ch+ and Cl− matrix and the inner-sphere HexA ligands work together to drive the thermodynamic feasibility of the extraction process. This mechanism is well illustrated in the schematic outline (Fig. 17), which shows that extraction proceeds via a neutral solvation-coordinated pathway aided by outer-sphere supramolecular interactions.

Pr, a mid-light rare-earth element, has a relatively high charge density and a smaller ionic radius, enabling the formation of stronger, more stable coordination complexes with the DES. Conclusions

Most reported studies on the recovery of REEs using DESs have used them in the leaching process rather than as extraction agents. Hydrophobic DESs (HDESs) have shown promising results as extractants for metal separation; however, their potential for REE recovery remains largely unexplored. The NMR and FTIR spectral characterization confirmed the formation of H-boding between ChCl and HexA forming the 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA DES. Physicochemical property determination showed that the density and refractive index decreased gradually from 0.973 g cm−3 to 0.953 g cm−3 and from 1.438 to 1.435, respectively, as the temperature increased from 298.15 K to 328.15 K. The conductivity and dielectric constant values increased from 2.397 mS cm−1 to 9.496 mS cm−1 and from 4.970 to 10.384, respectively, in the same temperature range. The radiation stability studies confirmed the chemical stability of the DES framework up to 100 kGy, indicating the reusability of the DES in a radiation environment. The key novelty of the present work lies in the synthesis and characterization of 1 : 4 ChCl : HexA hydrophobic deep eutectic solvents (HDESs), which exhibited appreciable extraction efficiencies for La(iii), Ce(iii), Pr(iii) and Nd(iii) from 3 M HNO3, achieving extraction percentages of 83.03%, 88.30%, 91.20% and 78.78%, respectively, at a DES-to-aqueous phase ratio of 3 : 1. An increase in temperature, DES/aqueous phase ratio, and HNO3 concentration had a positive impact on the extraction efficiency. The extraction is enthalpy-driven, with the formation of LREE(III)-DES inner-sphere complexes increasing the standard entropy change. Furthermore, the highest separation factor, 5.968, was obtained for the Pr/Nd pair. The light-scattering studies provided insights into the aggregation behaviour of the DES and the alterations in its microstructure upon LREE loading during extraction. The significant conclusions may be beneficial for the use of ChCl-carboxylic acid-based DESs in the solvent extraction of LREEs from HLLW generated by the spent nuclear fuel in the nuclear industry.
