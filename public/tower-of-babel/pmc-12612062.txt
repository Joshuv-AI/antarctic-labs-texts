Analysis of tunneling conductivity for MXene polymer system by the network of interphase: parametric examinations and experimental validation
Authors: Zahra Hadi, Jafar Khademzadeh Yeganeh, Yasser Zare, Muhammad Naqvi, Kyong Yop Rhee, Soo-Jin Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis investigation introduces a pioneering procedure to assess the effective conductivity of composites incorporating MXene nanosheets. The proposed model takes into account a variety of factors as the dimensions and volumetric fraction of MXene, the onset of percolation, tunneling characteristics, network fraction, the total resistance of the nanocomposite, and the interphase thickness. The forecasts generated by the proposed model are in strong accordance with the empirical conductivity measurements collected from a range of laboratory samples. Moreover, a comprehensive analysis has been carried out to explore how the predicted conductivity correlates with various parameters, thereby validating the efficiency of the suggested method. The electrical conductivity reaches 1.26 S/m, when the MXene nanoparticle has a minimum thickness of 1 nm at the maximum contact diameter of 20 nm. However, as the thickness of the MXene nanoparticle increases and the contact diameter diminishes, the nanocomposite conductivity reduces. Notably, when the MXene nanoparticle thickness exceeds 2 nm or the contact diameter falls below 8 nm, the conductivity sharply declines to zero, turning the nanocomposite into an insulator.Keywords: Polymer nanocomposites, MXene, Contact resistance, Interphase, Percolation onset, Effective conductivitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

MXenes, recognized as a category of two-dimensional nanomaterials, are being spotlighted as potential augmenting elements for composite materials1–7. They are birthed through a technique known as targeted etching of MAX compounds, which are a peculiar type of stratified materials, constituted of carbides or nitrides8–10. The MAX phases are distinguished by a combination of beneficial properties inherent in metals and ceramics, such as extraordinary elastic rigidity, proficient heat transfer, and excellent electrical conductivity. The unique amalgamation of traits earmarks MXenes as potential contenders for a multitude of applications within the sphere of materials science11–13. The incorporation of functional groups within their structure enables MXenes to display impressive mechanical properties, self-lubrication features, and excellent heat transfer capabilities14,15. Recent studies have reported exceptionally high electrical conductivity values for Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene, exceeding 20,000 S/cm under optimized conditions16,17. This remarkable conductivity, combined with its two-dimensional morphology and tunable surface chemistry, makes MXene a highly promising candidate for constructing efficient conductive networks in polymer nanocomposites. Figure 1 highlights a selection of representative applications of MXene materials across diverse technological fields, including biomedical engineering, energy storage, environmental remediation, and electronic device fabrication18–22. These examples underscore the multifunctional nature of MXenes and demonstrate their suitability for integration into advanced polymer-based systems23.

Schematic representation of MXene-based multifunctional applications.

In the contemporary scientific arena, MXene/polymer composites and their wide applications have become a hotbed of research interest24,25. In a landmark study, Ling and his team24 have successfully engineered a novel adaptable and exceptionally conductive polymer/MXene nanocomposite, marking the advent of a groundbreaking era of potentialities. This remarkable advancement has sparked a surge of curiosity in exploring various MXene-based materials for developing a wide range of nanocomposites. Such materials are extremely valued due to the excellent properties, including remarkable strength, high modulus, and significant thermal/electrical conductivity25,26. In another comprehensive piece of research, Gao et al.25 have conducted a comprehensive review of the recent progress in the domain of MXene/polymer membranes. Specifically, George and his colleagues27 have explored the potential applications of MXene/polymer composites in the biomedical field. With the inherent hydrophilicity of MXene, along with its impressive thermal and electrical conductivity, MXene is especially suitable for use in electronic applications28,29. Furthermore, the distinctive shape of MXene nanoparticles results in an elevated aspect ratio, which lowers their percolation threshold and significantly enhances their electrical conductivity30. Thanks to its remarkable properties, MXene emerges as an ideal candidate for the creation of MXene-based composites.

Electrons have the ability to move between layers of MXene through the contact space31,32. Interestingly, a minor separation among particles may improve the conductivity, a phenomenon due to the tunneling phenomenon. Therefore, the tunnel conductivity of the nanocomposite is largely determined by the contact resistance, which is associated with factors such as the size of the filler, the distance between contacts, the type of polymer, and the contact area33–35. Moreover, the interphase, the juncture that differentiates the distinct phases of polymer and filler, is a crucial determinant in shaping the electrical properties of composites36–41. The refinement of interphase properties can result in an increase in the conductivity, since it can establish and swells the network rising the electron transferring. Hence, a deep exploration and comprehension of the interphase role in conductivity can be instrumental in the design and enhancement of composite materials.

In the domain of MXene–polymer nanocomposites, numerous studies have sought to describe their structural and electrical characteristics42–45. Yet a comprehensive and predictive model for effective electrical conductivity—one that unifies the key governing mechanisms—remains lacking. While properties such as the percolation threshold, the extent and architecture of the MXene conductive network, and the combined effects of intrinsic, contact, and tunneling resistances are known to critically influence conductivity, prior models often treat these variables in isolation or in a purely empirical fashion. In this work, we present a physics-based, systematically validated framework that explicitly integrates filler concentration and morphology (lateral size and thickness), interphase thickness and its impact on the percolation threshold, and total interparticle resistance encompassing both interphase and tunneling contributions. This unified treatment enables accurate prediction across low-loading, near-threshold, and post-percolation regimes, offering mechanistic transparency for materials design. The robustness of the proposed model is demonstrated through validation against experimental datasets from multiple polymer matrices, confirming its generalizability. Furthermore, we employ contour mapping and exhaustive parametric analyses to reveal the relative influence of morphological and interfacial factors, thereby identifying practical pathways—through interphase engineering, nanosheet size optimization, and junction resistance minimization—to achieve high conductivity at minimal filler loadings. By bridging theoretical rigor with experimental evidence, this study fills a critical gap in the literature and establishes a new, versatile tool for the rational design of high-performance MXene/polymer nanocomposites.

Analysis of parameters

Electrical conductivity of nanocomposites is influenced by a multitude of parameters. By conducting the parametric studies, we can not only corroborate the established model, but also assess the potency of every individual parameter regarding their impact on the conductivity of MXene nanocomposites. To illustrate the interplay between two parameters affecting conductivity, while keeping other parameters at their mean levels, we utilize graphical representations in the form of contour and three-dimensional (3D) plots. In all computations, the mean and fixed values for the variables are considered as ρ = 100 Ω m, D = 500 nm, λ = 5 nm, d = 10 nm, ϕf = 0.04, t = 2.5 nm, ti = 8 nm and σf = 1.5 × 106 S/m8.

As depicted in Fig. 4, the parameters f and ϕf are crucial in determining the conductivity of nanocomposites. Both f and ϕf exert a direct influence on the conductivity of these materials. The optimal observed conductivity, quantified as 0.038 S/m, is attained when f is 0.9 and ϕf is 0.08. However, a reduction in the values of f along with ϕf results in a corresponding reduction in conductivity. For instance, when ϕf is 0.06 and f is 0.6, the conductivity diminishes to 0.007 S/m. Furthermore, for values of ϕf less than 0.04 or f less than 0.5, the conductivity experiences a significant decrease, eventually stabilizing, and the nanocomposite begins to exhibit insulating properties. Therefore, it is inferred that a significant percentage of network and a substantial volume fraction of MXene are effective in enhancing the conductivity of the nanocomposite. On the contrary, nanocomposites characterized by a low percentage of network and a small MXene volume fraction demonstrate markedly weak conductivity.

The different extents of computed conductivity for varying degrees of f and ϕf: (a) three-dimensional as well as (b) two-dimensional representations.

f value represents the contribution of nanosheets to the conductive network. An increased f suggests the development of a more extensive and denser network within the nanocomposite, resulting in superior conductivity. It’s crucial to note that f is affected by the active amount of nanoparticles inside the nanocomposite as well as the percolation threshold. In general, slender and big nanosheets, combined with substantial interphase, establish optimal conditions for efficient filler distribution and percolation threshold. This leads to elevated f parameters. Furthermore, a substantial quantity of contacts amongst MXene nanosheets can lead to the development of network within the composite.

A rise in the volumetric fraction results in a growth in the interphase volume within the composite material, as indicated in Eq. (5). In a similar manner, a rise in the actual MXene volume fraction, as indicated in Eq. (6), promotes the size of the network (Eq. 7), resulting in improved conductivity. Consequently, the maximum volumetric filler value yields the highest conductivity. Nevertheless, before percolation beginning, increasing the concentration of MXene nanosheets has a minimal effect on conductivity, as it only marginally changes the network performance. Numerous experimental studies have similarly observed a relationship among nanocomposite conductivity and the volumetric fraction of MXene57–60,63. This suggests that the amount of MXene can substantially manage the conductivity of MXene samples. Therefore, the advanced model logically explains how f and ϕf influence the conductivity of the nanocomposite.

The data indicates that the top conductivity, measured at 1.26 S/m, is attained when d is 20 nm and t is 1 nm. Furthermore, as the thickness of the MXene nanoparticle increases, the conductivity decreases until it stabilizes. This stabilization is particularly noticeable when t exceeds 2 nm or d falls below 8 nm, where the conductivity reaches its minimum value of 0. Therefore, the conductivity of nanocomposites is proportionally linked to the contact size as well as inversely linked to the thickness of the MXene nanoparticle. Thus, maximizing the contact size and minimizing the thickness of the MXene nanoparticle results in the optimal conductivity.

As the diameter of the contact area grows, the resistance at the interface between the polymer layer and MXene sheets decreases. In essence, a larger contact diameter amplifies the impact of superconductive MXene layers at the points of contact. Accordingly, a larger contact diameter results in a weaker resistance, primarily improving the effective conductivity. In contrast, a smaller contact diameter worsens the function of MXene layers at the contact zone weakening the conductivity. Consequently, the sophisticated model logically describes the effect of the contact diameter and the thickness of MXene on the nanocomposite conductivity.

The inverse relationship between MXene nanosheet thickness and the effective conductivity of the nanocomposite arises from several interrelated physical mechanisms. As the thickness of the nanosheets increases at a constant filler volume fraction, the number of dispersed nanosheets within the polymer matrix decreases, leading to a reduction in the total interfacial area and the density of conductive contacts. This results in fewer electron tunneling junctions and longer average tunneling distances, thereby increasing the overall resistance of the system64,65. Additionally, thinner MXene nanosheets exhibit higher aspect ratios and larger surface-to-volume ratios, which not only enhance the probability of forming interconnected conductive pathways but also contribute to a lower percolation threshold. Their extended lateral dimensions facilitate more efficient spatial distribution and overlap, enabling the formation of a percolated network at reduced filler loadings. These combined effects explain the observed decline in conductivity with increasing nanosheet thickness.

The optimal electrical conductivity, 0.19 S/m, is achieved when the total resistance is at its lowest level (R = 109 Ω) and the interphase thickness is at its maximum (ti = 16 nm). As R increases and the interphase thickness decreases, the conductivity also decreases. For example, when R = 2 Ω and ti = 14 nm, the conductivity drops to 0.09 S/m. Moreover, the weakest electrical conductivity, 0, occurs when ti is less than 9 nm. As the interphase gets thinner, the conductivity gradually reduces to zero, turning the nanocomposite into an insulator. Therefore, R negatively influences the conductivity, while the thickness of the interphase has a direct impact. This suggests that to achieve higher conductivity, R should decrease and ti should increase.

A substantial level of total resistance can greatly reduce the mobile charges in the nanocomposites, thereby primarily diminishing the conductivity. So, to enhance the conductivity of nanocomposites, the total resistance needs to be lowered. Given this understanding, the current model precisely forecasts the impact of R on the conductivity of the nanocomposite.

Equation (6) indicates that having a substantial interphase boosts the concentration efficiency of MXene in the nanocomposite. Also, when the interphase is thicker, the percolation threshold becomes smaller (as shown in Eq. 4). This occurs due to the development of network by the interphase regions inside the nanocomposite. Essentially, a thick interphase surrounding the nanosheets aids to expanding the network within the nanocomposite, as the nanosheets contribute to the conductive network. This means that a substantial interphase can enhance the role of nanomaterials within the composite conductivity through creating a conductive network and expanding the interconnected region. Therefore, the nanocomposite electrical conductivity is closely linked to the thickness of the interphase, as demonstrated by the proposed model.

The percolation threshold of nanoparticles is crucial in determining the required concentration for network. It also establishes the proportion of nanoparticles that contribute to the network, as outlined in Eq. (7). A lower percolation threshold suggests that a minimal quantity of nanoparticles can establish a conductive network, resulting in elevated conductivity in the nanocomposite. Essentially, a lower enhances the properties of the network and the conductivity of the nanocomposite. Hence, an inverse correlation among the percolation threshold and electrical conductivity is plausible.

An inverse correlation among effective conductivity and the contact distance is shown. Contact regions, primarily composed of insulative polymer layers, observe an increase in thickness with the expansion of contact distance. This leads to an intensification of resistance in contact areas, mainly due to the growth of the insulative phase. A larger contact distance, encompassing a greater amount of insulative materials, impedes electron transfer from the contact area, resulting in a significant reduction in effective conductivity34. Without a doubt, the advanced model clearly illustrates the influence of the percolation threshold and interaction distance on the efficient conductivity.

The pinnacle of conductivity, quantified as 0.026 S/m, is attained when D is 1000 nm and ρ is 5 Ω m. This observation underscores that the most superior electrical conductivity is achieved at the smallest ρ value and the largest D range. However, an increase in ρ coupled with a decrease in D leads to a reduction in conductivity. This is particularly evident when D is 500 nm and ρ is 35 Ω m, where the conductivity diminishes to 0.002 S/m. These observations highlight the direct influence of D and the inverse influence of ρ on the effective conductivity. So, enhancing ρ and reducing D results in a decrease in conductivity, while decreasing ρ and increasing D leads to the growth of conductivity.

The clear influence of D on conductivity is understandable, given that larger nanosheets reduce the percolation threshold (as per Eq. 4), leading to changes in the density as well as size within the filler network. The larger nanosheets can create extensive network within the sample, thereby facilitating electron transfer (refer to Eq. 7). In contrast, shorter nanosheets enhance the percolation threshold leading to a decrease in the density and size of the network structure. Consequently, the conductivity of the nanocomposites is closely linked to the size of the MXene particles, as demonstrated in our proposed model.

The tunnel resistance plays a pivotal role in modifying the contact resistance. The contact areas, which consist of MXene sheets and an insulating polymer matrix, are crucial to this process. By employing MXene sheets, which possess high conductivity, the specific resistance of the polymer-based insulating layer becomes the main contributor to the contact resistance34. Hence, it makes sense to note an inverse relationship between effective conductivity and ρ in the formulated mathematical model, as an escalation in ρ causes a growth in contact resistance. It’s noteworthy that at extremely high values, the electrical conductivity undergoes only minor changes. This is because the specific resistance of the polymer-based insulating layer becomes the primary contributor to the contact resistance. Consequently, even with a high ρ, the total conductivity of the nanocomposite remains relatively stable. Given these insights, our current model is adept at accurately predicting the role of ρ in determining the nanocomposite conductivity.

Conclusions

This paper proposed an approach for determining the effective conductivity of MXene-based system, considering the dimensions and volumetric fraction of MXene, proportion of networked MXene, interphase depth, percolation onset, total resistance of the system as well as the tunneling features. An increased value of contact diameter with a thin MXene yields a desirable level of conductivity. On the other hand, high tunneling distance and great percolation onset cannot improve in the conductivity. The peak electrical conductivity of 1.26 S/m was recorded with MXene nanoparticles when the thickness was at least 1 nm and the contact diameter was up to 20 nm. Nevertheless, increasing the thickness of MXene beyond 2 nm and reducing the contact diameter to fewer than 8 nm result in an insulative example. The optimal electrical conductivity, measured as 0.19 S/m, is achieved when the total resistance is 109 Ω and the interphase thickness is 16 nm. However, an interphase thickness less than 10 nm results in a significant reduction of electrical conductivity to zero. Additionally, the maximum network fraction of 0.9 and the highest filler content of 8 vol.% yield a conductivity of 0.038 S/m. In contrast, lower network percentage than 0.5 or smaller volume fraction of MXene than 0.04 cannot grow the conductivity of samples.
