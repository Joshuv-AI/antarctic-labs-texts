Tracking U.S. Liquefied Natural Gas Supply Chain Greenhouse Gas Emission Intensity through Direct Measurements
Authors: Yuanrui Zhu, Gregory B. Ross, Jenna Brown, Olga Khaliukova, William Daniels, Jiayang Wang, Selina Roman-White, Fiji C. George, Daniel Zimmerle, Dorit Hammerling, Arvind P. Ravikumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent regulatory and voluntary initiatives to estimate supply chain greenhouse gas (GHG) emission intensity of liquefied natural gas (LNG) have emphasized the use of direct measurements as activity-based national inventories tend to systematically underestimate GHG emission intensities. In this work, we demonstrate how measurement data can be integrated into a life cycle assessment (LCA) framework for assessing the GHG emissions of LNG supply chains by synthesizing results from a three year measurement campaign over 54 sites across production, midstream, and liquefaction stages. Measurement-informed GHG emission intensity ranges from 13.8 to 17.2 g of CO2 equiv/MJ of LNG produced, about 19–39% higher than those derived from an activity-based inventory assessment. We also observe large variation in the contribution of each stage to the total supply chain emission intensity. Critically, we find that stages downstream of gas production account for up to 73% of the production to liquefaction GHG emission intensity of LNG. Thus, relying on aggregate production-only emission intensities as the basis to assess the emission impact of the LNG is likely to underestimate emissions, leading to potentially ineffective public or corporate policies. Finally, we find that supply-chain-specific GHG intensities can significantly differ from basin-level, representative GHG intensities; thus, assessments of the GHG intensity of LNG can benefit from being tied to individual transactions and specific gas pathways where data allow.

Introduction

Global trade in liquefied natural gas (LNG) has seen significant growth in recent years. Worldwide liquefaction capacity is expected to reach 968 million tonnes per annum (mtpa) by 2028, driven by rising demand in Europe and Asia. − However, as LNG production and trade expand, concerns about its environmental impact have also grown, with supply chain methane emissions becoming a focus issue in Europe. Methane, the main component in natural gas, is a potent greenhouse gas and excessive methane emissions across production, transportation, and liquefaction of natural gas can reduce the climate benefit of LNG over more carbon-intensive fuels. −

Regulatory measures and voluntary initiatives to address methane emissions from the oil and gas industry have emerged globally, with a focus on employing measurement-based approaches to track and mitigate emissions. − In the U.S., the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) finalized methane regulations that allow for the use of new technologies to detect superemitters. − More recently, the EPA also updated the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP) guidelines to make better use of empirical data and improve the accuracy of methane emissions estimates compared to prior activity-based bottom-up inventory approaches. The European Union (EU) announced new methane regulations that require importers to seek monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) of supply chain GHG emissions to support a methane intensity standard for new supply contracts post 2030. Beyond regulations, several voluntary initiatives such as the Oil and Gas Methane Partnership (OGMP 2.0), the Measurement, Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification (MMRV) framework, and the Coalition for LNG Emission Abatement toward Net-zero (CLEAN) initiative further aim to address GHG emissions along the supply chain. ,,

Multiple field measurements across every stage of the LNG supply chain have revealed that official inventory estimates underestimate methane emissions. − This underestimation has been observed not just in the U.S. but also in other parts of the world. ,− Measurements from upstream oil and gas production indicated that activity-based methane emissions inventory underestimated methane emissions by a factor of 1.5. , Similarly, most midstream facilities reported lower inventory methane estimates compared to top-down measurements. In addition, the first direct measurements at U.S. liquefaction terminals demonstrated that the reported GHGRP methane emissions are 74–88% lower than measurement-based estimates. Despite these measurements at individual facilities and supply chain stages, no study has quantified the impact of this underestimation in the context of life cycle GHG emissions from U.S. LNG supply chains. Furthermore, the range of GHG emission intensities observed across different gas-producing basins suggests that a geospatial, measurement-informed supply chain assessment of LNG will not only be more accurate but necessary to effectively quantify the climate impacts of U.S LNG.

Field campaigns across upstream, midstream, and downstream stages of the LNG supply chain provide an opportunity to integrate direct measurements of methane emissions into a geospatial, measurement-informed LCA framework. − ,,− ,,,, Multiple LCA studies have quantified the GHG emissions from U.S. LNG supply chains with natural gas sourced from North America and delivered globally. − However, most of these studies rely on activity-based inventory methods and data used in national inventories, which have been shown to underestimate methane emissions. − Two recent LCA studies from coauthors incorporate limited measurement data collected using different methods and technologies into an LCA framework to provide a more accurate emission estimate. , However, these studies have three limitations. First, measurement data used in these studies were not comprehensive and were supplemented with inventory data, thus potentially underestimating supply chain GHG impacts. Second, measurement data from different stages are drawn from various sources with different measurement methods, technologies, and protocols, introducing potential bias. Third, the lack of upstream operational data to contextualize these measurements within an LCA introduces additional uncertainty to the emission estimates.

In this work, we present the first direct integration of facility-level measurement data into an LCA framework to track the GHG emission intensity of U.S. LNG supply chains. Using multiscale measurements of carbon dioxide and methane across 54 facilities from three U.S. LNG supply chains originating from the Marcellus, Haynesville, and Permian basins to two liquefaction terminals in the Gulf Coast, we demonstrate the need for supply chain-specific assessments to quantify GHG intensity of LNG. The specific LNG supply chains assessed in this study refer to the Cheniere-specific LNG supply chains defined using Cheniere’s gas-pathing algorithms, which trace gas delivered to each liquefaction terminal back to the upstream and midstream assets that physically supply that gas. Our work represents two key advances: it provides a practical framework for regulations to incorporate GHG emissions measurement data into conventional LNG supply chain assessments and it demonstrates how systematic underestimation in official inventory estimates can translate to underestimation in supply chain GHG emission intensities.

Methods

We first analyze the measurement-informed GHG emission intensity across three U.S. LNG supply chains sourcing from three major gas-producing basins. We compare the measurement-informed and operator-estimated, inventory-based emission intensity and reveal the extent and source of discrepancy between them. We also analyze the contribution of methane emissions across each stage of the LNG supply chain and identify key opportunities for emission mitigation. We finally introduce data quality indicators and evaluate data reliability and representativeness of both methane and carbon dioxide emission estimates at different stages of the LNG supply chain.

Measurements and Inventory Data Source

Facility-level measurement data from each stage of the LNG supply chain used in the LCA model were collected as part of the quantification, monitoring, reporting, and verification (QMRV) campaign and described in the peer-reviewed literature. ,,, The measurement data collected from a facility-level aerial-LIDAR-based survey (Bridger Photonics, Inc.) were used in this study for detecting and quantifying methane emissions across different stages of LNG supply chain. Carbon dioxide emissions were either measured at the facility level by ChampionX employing a mass balance approach or at the source level using conventional methods, such as stack tests of exhaust emissions. The Bridger measured emissions represent the average across multiple snapshot measurements, which may introduce uncertainty caused by uncaptured low-probability, high consequence emission events. To assess the influence of measurement variability, this study provided a sensitivity analysis to evaluate how variations in measured emissions affect supply chain emission estimates.

In addition to measurement data, the operator used activity-based methods to calculate GHG emissions for each site during each measurement survey, referred to as operator-estimated inventory (OEI). The OEI expands on emissions reported through the U.S. EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP) by incorporating sources excluded from the GHGRP, such as emissions from smaller facilities below reporting threshold, portable sources, emergency generators and other equipment. Thus, the OEI is comparable to, but more comprehensive than, the GHGRP and includes both methane and carbon dioxide emissions, incorporating data from stack tests, ground-based leak detection and repair (LDAR) records, fuel gas flow rates, and other information essential for calculating a comprehensive whole-site emissions inventory.

In the measurement-informed LCA model, we prioritize the use of measurement data and supplement it with OEI estimates when direct measurements are not available. The majority of methane emissions are informed by Bridger Photonics, Inc. (Bridger) measurements with limited use of calculated emissions for sources where calculations have proven more accurate than direct measurements. Bridger’s LiDAR technology requires sufficient methane column density along the laser path to register a statistically significant absorption signal. As a result, emissions from turbine exhaust stacks, where methane is diluted due to strong vertical momentum and rapid mixing, often fall below the system’s detection capability. Small fugitive components pose a similar challenge. Many of these sources emit below Bridger typical minimum detection limit (3 kg/h with 90% probability of detection), making them unlikely to be observed. In addition, episodic events, such as compressor blowdowns, may not occur during the same time period as Bridger snapshot measurements. For these emission sources, OEI calculations provide more accurate and complete quantification than direct measurement. For carbon dioxide emissions, emission measurements from mass-balance aerial surveys and on-site stack tests are prioritized, and the OEI is used as a supplement when those measurements are not available.

LNG Supply Chain Design

Figure shows the three virtual indicative supply chains included in this study. Two indicative supply chains pathways source from the Marcellus and Haynesville basins and deliver to the Sabine Pass Liquefaction (SPL) terminal. The other supply chain illustrates a gas pathway from the Permian basin and delivers it to the Corpus Christi Liquefaction (CCL) terminal. As of November 2024, the Marcellus, Permian, and Haynesville are the three largest natural gas-producing basins in the U.S., accounting for 32, 22, and 16% of U.S. total shale gas production, respectively. In addition, the SPL and CCL facilities together accounted for 52% of U.S. LNG exports in 2024.

Three LNG supply chains with natural gas sourced from Marcellus, Haynesville, and Permian basins and delivered to Sabine Pass Liquefaction (SPL) and Corpus Christi Liquefaction (CCL) terminals. The different colored points in this figure represent various types of sites in the natural gas supply chains, including production sites, gathering and boosting (G&B) sites, processing plant, transmission stations, storage site, and liquefaction terminals. The thick dotted lines represent the portion of the supply chain that is transported directly from production basins to liquefaction terminals, while the thin dotted lines represent the supply chains that is transported from production basins to storage sites and ultimately to the liquefaction terminals.

The LNG supply chains in our LCA model are informed by the 54 sites directly surveyed during the QMRV campaign. The details of the QMRV measurement data can be found in section S1. We recognize that the direct measurements at these facilities may not be statistically representative of all facilities within that stage of the supply chain, however, this unique data set of high-granularity direct measurements paired with granular operator bottom-up data helps highlight the importance of incorporating measurements within supply chain assessments. Also, while the measured facilities are a part of Cheniere’s supply chain and may not be representative of all U.S. LNG supply chains, they are highly relevant to the supply chains under this study. Therefore, the results in this study reflect the Cheniere-specific LNG supply chains made of contractors participating in the QMRV campaign and are not intended to represent basin- or national-level emission averages. In addition, this study is designed to characterize the average emission profile represented by the observed measurement snapshots collected during the measurement campaign period, rather than fully continuous time-averaged emission rates representative of all operating conditions throughout the measurement campaign. A key goal of this study is to demonstrate that representative, basin-level GHG intensities can significantly differ from supply chain-specific GHG intensity of LNG. Assessing the emissions performance of LNG supply chains requires individualized LCA models that account for measurement data from specific facilities.

To create indicative gas transmission pathways, we employ key transmission activity data from Roman-White et al., along with public data, to develop QMRV supplier average gas transmission pathways for the three distinct LNG supply chains. In each basin, the natural gas produced from several production sites is initially collected at surrounding gathering and boosting (G&B) sites and then transported to the nearby processing plant for further separation (see section S1). The processed gas is then pressurized for pipeline transportation to liquefaction terminals. The majority of the natural gas is directly transported to liquefaction terminals, while on average 14% of natural gas is transported to storage sites for temporary storage and ultimately transported to liquefaction terminals.

Incorporating emissions measurements in the transmission stage poses two challenges. One, the operational state of each transmission station generally follows demand and can vary over time; this means that measurements at any single point in time may not be representative of operations each year. Second, not all transmission compressor stations of Cheniere specific supply chain were directly measured as part of the QMRV program across the three supply chains. Thus, we develop a statistical approach to estimate emissions in the transmission stage from selected measurements. We classify the measurement surveys on transmission stations into six categories according to the type of compressors (reciprocating and centrifugal) and their operating mode (operating, non-operating depressurized, non-operating pressurized) and calculate the measurement-informed emission intensities in each category. The transmission stage emission is estimated by multiplying the emission intensity of each station type by the corresponding annual hours spent in each operating mode and summing across all categories. The operating hours of compressors are calculated based on individual compressor information provided by the EPA. More details can be found in the section S3.3.

Measurement-Informed LCA Model

A measurement-informed LCA model is developed to estimate the emission intensity of the three LNG supply chains. The model framework builds on the recent development of a geospatial, measurement-informed LCA model that incorporated regional measurement data to differentiate LNG supply chains. The measurement-informed LCA model in the current study uses the QMRV supplier average gas transmission pathways to estimate GHG emission intensities of LNG supply chains. It does not incorporate complexities of each supplier-specific pathway as was done in Roman-White et al., since the focus of this study is to reveal the underestimation of OEI-based estimate by synthesizing collected emission measurement data into the LCA framework. Since emissions from preproduction activities and pipeline fugitive and venting events are not included in direct measurements, those emissions are calculated based on values reported in the peer-reviewed literature, which are based on 2018 GHGRP data. The functional unit of our LCA model is 1 MJ (HHV) of LNG liquefied at the liquefaction terminal. We quantify emissions of methane and carbon dioxide in terms of CO2 equivalents using 100 year global warming potentials (GWP) based on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) sixth assessment (AR6) report (GWP of methane: 29.8).

The GHG emissions at each stage are calculated by first calculating the energy-normalized, measurement-informed emission intensity for that stage (including all co-products) and multiplying by the energy content of the NG product stream (section S1) consistent with prior studies. ,, Such allocation is done for the production, G&B, and processing stages. We employ an energy-based and product-assigned allocation method in our study. For each emission source, we first identify all coproducts associated with this source and then allocate GHG emissions to all associated coproducts based on their energy content (see section S1). The emission intensities of all emission sources across all stages of the three LNG supply chains are provided in Tables S3–S47.

Data Quality Assessment

In this study, we introduce two numerical data quality indicators to assess the quality and accuracy of the data used in the measurement-informed supply chain emission intensity calculation. The quality assessment framework is based on the work of the measurement, monitoring, reporting, and verification (MMRV) international working group comprised of over 16 major LNG importing and exporting countries and codeveloped by the U.S. Department of Energy. These indicators, data reliability and data representativeness, are scored on a 1 to 5 scale, with lower numerical scores representing higher quality data. The data sources with the highest level of data reliability are verified data based on direct site- or source-level measurements. The data representativeness is a composite indicator of four subcategories, including temporal correlation, geographic correlation, technology correlation, and data collection method, indicating the extent to which the data sources are representative of the characteristics of emissions within the scope of the supply chain (see section S8). Results of the data quality assessment for the Marcellus-SPL supply chain are presented in section , and the results for other supply chains are described in section S8. The data reliability and data representativeness are calculated for both methane and carbon dioxide across all stages of each LNG supply chain.

Results

Measurement-Informed GHG Emissions of LNG Supply Chains

Overall, measurement-informed GHG emission intensity of the Marcellus-SPL LNG supply chain is comparable to the Permian-CCL supply chain and 25% more emission-intensive than the Haynesville-SPL supply chain. This is a result driven by differences in transmission stage emissions across the three LNG supply chains. Gas transmission from production basins to liquefaction terminals is a complex process with multiple pipeline companies and associated compressor stations along the gas pathway. In the absence of supplier-specific details, we employ QMRV supplier average transmission length in our model. The average transportation distance in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain is 1148 miles, which is 2.4 times longer than the Permian-CCL supply chain and 3.9 times longer than the Haynesville-SPL supply chain. The average number of transmission stations along the Marcellus-SPL supply chain is over 4 times larger than the Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains. The longer natural gas transportation distance and the larger number of transmission stations in the Marcellus-SPL result in higher emissions (see section S3.3) compared to other supply chains. Consequently, emission intensity associated with the transmission stage is 6.4, 1.3, and 1.5 g of CO2 equiv/MJ for the Marcellus-SPL, Haynesville-SPL, and Permian-CCL supply chains, respectively.

In contrast to the transmission stage, upstream emission intensity, consisting of production and G&B stages, follow general trends established in the peer-reviewed literature across these basins. Upstream emissions in the Permian basin are higher primarily due to oil-dominated operations in this basin, which involves greater associated gas handling, venting, and flaring compared to gas-focused basins such as Marcellus and Haynesville. Practices such as tank flashing, pneumatic controllers, and intermittent flaring contributes to elevated GHG emissions in the Permian basin. This pattern of higher emissions is consistent with substantial peer-reviewed literature from this region. ,− For example, Sherwin et al. compiled over one million aerial measurements across six U.S. regions and reported that gas-normalized methane emission rate in the Permian basin is 9.63%, roughly an order of magnitude higher than in other basins. In this study, Marcellus has the lowest upstream GHG emission intensity of 4.6 g of CO2 equiv/MJ of LNG, followed by Haynesville with an upstream GHG intensity of 6.2 g of CO2 equiv/MJ. The higher production emissions in the Haynesville basin are related to the more frequent and high-volume tank emission events in that basin. The Permian basin has the highest upstream GHG emission intensity of 8.1 g of CO2 equiv/MJ. These emissions have been allocated to various coproducts in each basin and represent emissions associated with the natural gas product stream.

For the QMRV measurement-informed inventories, despite Marcellus exhibiting the lowest upstream production GHG emission intensity, the Marcellus-SPL supply chain has the highest GHG emission intensity of all three supply chains in this work. We find stages downstream of the gas production and G&B in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain account for 73% of the whole supply chain GHG emission intensity of LNG, with transmission contributing 37%. Therefore, we conclude that production-stage GHG emission intensity is not necessarily representative of the full supply chain emission intensity, highlighting the importance of considering emissions across all stages when evaluating the climate impacts of natural gas systems.

Additionally, we find gas transmission pathways have a significant impact on the lifecycle GHG emissions. Figure b shows the range in supply chain GHG emission intensity based on the minimum and maximum transmission distances or number of transmission stations across all gas pathways between the production basin and the liquefaction terminal as estimated by Cheniere’s gas pathing algorithm in Roman-White et al. for data year 2022. Under these scenarios, the life cycle GHG emissions of the Marcellus-SPL, Haynesville-SPL, and Permian-CCL supply chains vary by +24/–9, +15/–8, and +15/–5%, respectively, across all Cheniere supplier specific gas pathways from each basin. The Marcellus-SPL supply chain, which exhibits the highest GHG emission intensity under the QMRV average transmission pathway, could potentially have lower emissions than both Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains when using a pathway with the minimum number of transmission stations. In general, transmission stage emissions are typically dominated by carbon dioxide resulting from energy of fuel gas combustion at compressors required to move gas over long distances, rather than by fugitive or vented methane. However, the QMRV data revealed unusually high methane emissions from the sampled transmission stations. In this case, gas pathways with more transmission stations result not only in higher combustion-related carbon dioxide emissions, but also in larger fugitive and vented methane emissions from those transmission stations. Thus, differentiating LNG supply chains based on GHG emission intensity in the U.S. requires not only measurements across all stages of the supply chain but also an accurate understanding of the specific gas pathway.

Measurement-informed GHG emission intensity is similar across the storage and liquefaction stages for the three supply chains, while it varies at the processing stage. Because 14% of gas, on average, is routed to storage across all supply chains, the associated emission intensities are similar. Although natural gas from different LNG supply chains is sent to different liquefaction terminals, the GHG emission intensities at two liquefaction terminals are comparable. By contrast, the disparity of measurement-informed GHG emission intensity at the processing stage is associated with the different percentages of natural gas from the gathering stage sent to the processing plant. In the Permian-CCL supply chain, all natural gas gathered is sent to the processing plants to remove impurities, whereas, in the Marcellus-SPL and Haynesville-SPL supply chains, only 59 and 70% of the natural gas from the G&B site is sent to a processing plant, respectively, resulting in lower emission intensity.

Figure a also shows a comparison between measurement-informed and OEI-based GHG supply chain emission intensities. In general, measurement-informed GHG emission intensity is 19–39% higher than OEI-based supply chain GHG intensity estimates. Although OEI underestimates emissions across all stages, not all stages equally contribute to total supply chain emission intensity. For example, in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain, while OEI-based GHG intensities underestimate emissions by 83% at the storage stage, the emission contribution of this stage is only 3%. On the other hand, the transmission stage OEI in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain underestimates emissions only by 28% compared to measurement-informed inventory, but its contribution to the total emissions is 37%.

Figure presents the OEI underestimation rate compared to the measurement-informed estimate and the contribution to total measurement-informed GHG emissions for each stage of the three LNG supply chains. The stage-level OEI underestimation ranges from 2 to 80% across the studied scenarios, and the contribution of each stage to total supply chain emissions ranges from 3 to 37%. We assume 20% as a reasonable threshold for assessing significance of both OEI underestimation rates and GHG emission contribution. This threshold is not intended to represent formal statistical significance, but rather reflects the approximate magnitude of practical uncertainty associated with best available measurement technologies when averaged over a large number of observations. Differences below this threshold are considered difficult to distinguish from measurement uncertainty, whereas differences exceeding this threshold are interpreted as indicative of potentially meaningful deviations. It is also intended to align with policy implementation considerations and may evolve over time as policies change or measurement practices improve. This threshold divides the figure into four regions. These four regions provide a guide for directing efforts toward improving emissions estimation accuracy, mitigation priorities, and points of verification in a differentiated gas framework: (1) lower left, corresponding to low underestimation and low contribution, represents a lower relative priority region, (2) top left, corresponding to low underestimation and high contribution, represents a region with primarily a mitigation focus, (3) lower right, corresponding to high underestimation and low contribution, represents a region with primarily an estimation focus, and (4) top right, corresponding to high underestimation and high contribution, represents a region with both an estimation and mitigation focus. The “lower relative priority region” term indicates that stages falling into this region have limited influence on total emissions and are generally well estimated by operator reports. When resources are constrained, priority should therefore be directed toward stages located in the other regions, where either emissions contributions or underestimation rates are substantially higher. It is important to note that, in this context, mitigation refers to potential emission reduction opportunities evaluated based on each stage contribution to total CO2 equiv emission, without implying the technical feasibility or cost-effectiveness of implementing those reductions, which may vary widely across supply chains. The focus of this study is to develop a framework for prioritizing stages for improved emission estimation or mitigation efforts, rather than to propose specific mitigation measures. Each supply chain stage can be classified into one of these four regions, helping identify key stages that contribute to overall supply chain emission intensity as well as areas of focus for improving estimation or emissions mitigation.

Relationship between the OEI underestimation rate and the contribution of different supply chain stages to total GHG emission intensity across the three LNG supply chains. The x axis represents the percentage of underestimation by the OEI-based estimate compared to the measurement-informed estimate. The y axis represents the proportion of GHG emissions accounted for by each stage in the LCA boundary from production through liquefaction.

The transmission stages in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain and the production and G&B stages in the Haynesville-SPL supply chain fall in the “mitigation estimation” region, where all stages have large contributions to total emissions and high OEI underestimation rates. The “estimation only” region is dominated by processing and storage stages from all three LNG supply chains and by transmission stages in the Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains, which exhibit high OEI underestimation rates but a limited impact on the life cycle GHG emission estimate due to their small share of total emissions. By contrast, the OEI underestimation rates for liquefaction stages across the three LNG supply chains are all lower than 10%, indicating that the OEI-based estimates are comparable to measurement-informed estimates at liquefaction terminals. This result reinforces the findings of the QMRV measurement study conducted at U.S. liquefaction terminals. While this analysis demonstrates significant variation in the relative importance of emissions estimation and mitigation across different supply chains, such variation can also be observed between regions when there are multiple pathways for gas to travel between production regions and the liquefaction terminal.

Methane vs Carbon Dioxide Emissions

Figure shows measurement-informed methane and carbon dioxide emission intensity for each LNG supply chain. Overall, the ratio of life cycle methane emissions to total GHG emissions is similar across three supply chains, ranging from 30 to 37%. When comparing the different stages of the supply chain, methane emissions are significant for production, processing, and storage, but make up less than 5% of total emissions for liquefaction. For the production stage, the more frequent and larger methane emission events observed at the Haynesville and Permian basin production sites result in higher methane emission intensity in these two basins compared to the Marcellus basin. For the midstream stage, the processing plant and storage site exhibited higher measured methane emission intensity than other midstream sites. At the transmission stage, although total GHG emissions vary significantly across the three supply chains, the methane share of stage-level GHG emissions remains similar, at approximately 42–44%. The similarity is driven by the consistent activity factors across the supply chains, including the operating hours of each compressor mode, the number of compressors installed at each transmission station, and the percentages of different types of compressors stations. Total GHG emission intensity from the liquefaction stage is dominated by carbon dioxide, accounting for over 95%. The variability in the contribution of methane to total GHG emission intensity across the three supply chains can be attributed to two major differences: the higher number of reciprocating engine-based transmission stations in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain and the larger number of high-emitting tank-related methane emissions events in the Haynesville and Permian basins compared to Marcellus basin. Regulators and market participants should expect similar variations across global supply chains as differences in the efficiency of extracting and transporting natural gas and gas composition drive significant intensity variation. This further underscores the critical need for measurement-informed, supply chain-specific GHG emission intensities.

Data Quality Assessment

Using the framework developed by the international working group on MMRV, we assess supply chain stage-specific data quality using data reliability and data representativeness metrics. Each metric is scored on a 1–5 scale, with 1 representing the highest data quality indicator. Figure shows data reliability and data representativeness values across different stages of the Marcellus-SPL supply chain for both methane and carbon dioxide. We focus on the Marcellus-SPL supply chain in the main text. The data quality scores for Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains can be found in Tables S81 and S82. We make several critical observations.

Data quality indicator scores, data reliability and data representativeness, across each stage of the Marcellus-SPL supply chain for both (a) methane and (b) carbon dioxide emissions. The x axis refers to methane and carbon dioxide emission intensity of each stage in units of gram methane or carbon dioxide emissions per MJ of LNG produced at the liquefaction terminal. Scores for the Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains are provided in Tables S81 and S82. The data quality indicators are scored on a 1–5 scale, with lower numerical scores representing higher quality data.

Methane emissions from the transmission stage are not only a major contributor to total emission intensity, accounting for 43% of the supply chain methane emission intensity, but are also calculated based on data that are less reliable and representative. Although measured transmission stations include both reciprocating engines and centrifugal turbines, the majority of the measured stations are located in the southern US, making them less representative of emissions across all stations in the Marcellus-SPL supply chain. Additionally, the activity factor, the total number of compressor operating hours for all transmission stations, is calculated by regional statistical data across individual compressor stations, contributing to lower data reliability compared to a scenario where facility-specific operating hours for each station are available. Similarly, the high data representativeness value for the processing stage is also related to the poor geographic representation of using measurements at a processing plant in the Permian basin to represent processing plant emissions in the Marcellus basin.

From the perspective of carbon dioxide emissions, data reliability and representativeness values for the production stage are high, which indicate low data quality for CO2 emissions at this stage. This is because emissions from preproduction activities, such as drilling, hydraulic fracturing, and wastewater disposal, account for 58% of production stage total carbon dioxide emissions in the Marcellus basin, and those emissions are calculated based on generic emission factors from literature. However, the total carbon dioxide emission intensity from production is small, leading to a relatively minor impact on the data quality of the life cycle GHG emission estimate. The high data reliability and representativeness values for the production stage can also be observed in the Haynesville-SPL and Permian-CCL supply chains (see section S8).

Discussion

Regulatory policies and voluntary initiatives around the world increasingly require the use of measurements for supply chain emission accounting, given the widespread underestimation by activity-based inventory estimates. Advances in measurement approaches and LCA models now enable us to incorporate measurement data into supply chain assessments. In this study, we synthesize measurement results from the upstream, midstream, and downstream stages of the LNG supply chain within a geospatial LCA model to estimate a measurement-informed GHG emission intensity across three indicative U.S. LNG supply chains. Importantly, the development of measurement-informed inventory models that enable the translation of snapshot measurement data into lifecycle inventory inputs is a key precursor to developing measurement-informed supply chain assessments. Unlike conventional LCA studies that rely on lifecycle inventories built from official inventory estimates, our study incorporates site-specific GHG emissions data, providing a more accurate and dynamic approach to differentiating supply chains. We highlight three key implications of our work relevant to the GHG emissions impact of global trade in LNG.

First, our results highlight that a natural gas producing basin with a lower GHG intensity does not necessarily result in a natural gas supply chain with lower GHG intensity because of the significant contribution from downstream supply chain stages. For example, natural gas from the lower intensity Marcellus basin may result in higher overall emission intensity than natural gas from the higher intensity Permian basin because of the longer transmission distance. Thus, regulations that are benchmarked to methane intensity (e.g., the EU methane intensity standard) will need to account for emissions across all stages of the supply chains rather than solely focusing on production basin emission intensities in the exporting country. While technologies like satellites are effective in providing regional methane emission estimates for production basins around the world, their ability to quantify emissions along long transmission supply chains is limited, especially in certain gas-exporting countries. Although high-sensitivity, source-resolved satellites can provide emission estimates for some facilities at known locations, they remain limited in identifying leaks along long-distance transmission pipelines and detecting emissions from every individual transmission station, particularly those powered by electric-driven compressors with relatively low emissions. These constrains underscore the need for more regionalized studies and incorporating total transmission distance in the life cycle assessment. As we show in this study, the transmission stage can be a significant contributor to supply chain emissions and can more than offset lower upstream emissions. Furthermore, the specific gas transmission pathway can result in a 33% difference in emission within a single production basin to the liquefaction terminal supply chain. Relying exclusively on satellite technology to differentiate global gas supply chains risks underestimating the total life cycle impact and overemphasizing the contribution of the production and G&B stage emissions.

Second, recent field measurements across the LNG supply chain have demonstrated that official inventories are underestimating methane emissions. However, those studies are limited to specific stages of the supply chain and few studies present the overall extent of underestimation within an LCA framework. Without such a systems-level perspective, capital-intensive decisions on emissions monitoring and mitigation may not be optimally deployed to reduce the overall emission intensity of the supply chain. We show that the OEI underestimates LNG supply chain emission by 19–39% compared to measurement-informed estimates. Critically, the impact of OEI underestimation is not equally significant across all stages of the supply chain. Using the twin levers of OEI underestimation and contribution to total emission intensity, we identify four regimes relevant to stakeholders: lower relative priority, mitigation only, estimation only, and mitigation and estimation. Whether our focus for a given stage of the supply chain should be on improving emission estimates, advancing mitigation, or both varies by the specifics of the supply chain, the accuracy of bottom-up inventory estimation methods, and measurement data. The results presented in this study pertain only to the specific LNG supply chains assessed here and can significantly differ from basin-level representative GHG intensities. Thus, such assessments of GHG intensity of LNG should be unique to individual transaction in LNG and should not be generalized to a regional- or basin-level emission mitigation strategies. The trade-off between the accuracy of a given stage’s emissions estimates and that stage’s contribution to total supply chain emission intensity should be evaluated on a supply chain-specific basis to identify key points of verification in a differentiated gas market.

Third, qualitative metrics to assess the reliability and representativeness of emissions data within a supply chain framework can help convey complex information on aggregate uncertainty to industry and government stakeholders. While quantitative, multidimensional uncertainty estimates for supply chain GHG emission intensity that combine uncertainties from measurements, extrapolation, activity data, or supply chain tracing are ideal, they are not yet available and require additional scientific research. In their absence, qualitative metrics can provide easy-to-interpret guidance to market participants, regulators, and investors. However, developing reliable qualitative criteria and corresponding scores rests on a comprehensive understanding of current challenges with supply chain emission intensity. For example, carbon dioxide emissions at different supply chain stages such as compressor stations or liquefaction terminals can be accurately estimated based on fuel consumption measured through simple flow meters. Here, requiring direct measurements of carbon dioxide, which often have higher uncertainty than fuel flow measurements, as a proxy for high data quality would be a mistake. Thus, a comprehensive data quality metric approach should account for the nature of the emission source and the best approach to estimate emissions, recognizing that the best approach may not be direct measurements.

Measurement-informed emission accounting methods generally capture more emissions than inventory-based approaches and provide more accurate emission estimate. However, their broader implementation in the oil and gas sector faces several challenges. Direct measurements are costly and resource-intensive, making comprehensive coverage across thousands of facilities difficult. Measurement technologies also vary in their detection limits and sensitivities, which can lead to inconsistencies in reported inventories. Moreover, many technologies measure emissions at facility or regional level and struggle with attributing emissions to specific sources, introducing uncertainty when reconciling with bottom-up inventory estimates. Finally, although OGMP 2.0 offers guidance on incorporating measurement data into emission accounting, there is no universally accepted protocol for how to construct measurement-informed inventories. This lack of standardized protocols limits comparability across studies and restricts the large-scale use of measurement-informed emission accounting methods.

As carbon accounting becomes a key metric in the global and domestic trade of natural gas, practical, consistent, and timely approaches for developing supply chain intensity estimates are needed. Our study demonstrates one such practical approach in using direct measurements to inform the development of accurate LNG supply chain emission intensity estimates. Critically, we demonstrate why measurements improve supply chain emission intensities compared to activity-based inventory estimates, explain the importance of assessing each supply chain as unique pathways from production through liquefaction, and identify how geospatial supply chain analysis can help stakeholders identify optimal opportunities to reduce emissions. While establishing this approach took several years of planning and execution, the availability of measurement, reporting, and verification protocols and supply chain analysis tools can ensure that similar analyses can be scaled up in a time scale relevant to global markets.
