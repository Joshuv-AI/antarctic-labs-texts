Perceptions of robot-assisted arthroplasty among patients and the public
Authors: Scott R. Morrison, Nick D. Clement, Jon V. Clarke, Christopher Gee, Swati Chopra, Andrew J. Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThe use of robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) in total hip and knee arthroplasty is increasing, offering potential benefits in implant positioning accuracy. However, patient and public perceptions, including concerns and preferences, remain poorly understood. This scoping review aimed to explore the literature on patient and public perceptions of RAS in orthopaedics, identify common themes, assess knowledge gaps, and inform future research priorities.MethodsA five-stage scoping review was undertaken according to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews. Searches were performed across PubMed, Directory of Open Access Journals, Scopus, and ProQuest Central; Google Scholar and reference lists were also searched. Articles were collated and evaluated against predetermined criteria. Data from included studies were collated to facilitate descriptive and thematic analysis.ResultsA total of 16 studies were included, primarily cross-sectional surveys. Four major themes were identified: interest in RAS; understanding regarding RAS; concerns regarding RAS; and influences on preferences and perception. Patients often overestimated the prevalence of use and the benefits of RAS, and based their preferences on marketing or limited information. Trust in surgeons remained a strong influence on patient attitudes.ConclusionDespite growing patient enthusiasm for RAS, significant gaps in public understanding and perception persist. Many patients held inaccurate beliefs about RAS capabilities and benefits. Future research should focus on creating validated educational tools for patients about RAS, expanding studies across diverse populations, and clarifying the value of RAS. Aligning public perception with clinical and non-clinical evidence is essential for informed consent and ethical surgical innovation.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(6):733–743.

Introduction

The number of total hip (THA), total knee (TKA), and unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) procedures increases each year.1 This number is expected to continue to increase as populations age and the incidence of arthritis increases.2

Arthroplasty is an effective procedure in end-stage hip and knee osteoarthritis; despite this, a proportion of patients remain dissatisfied following their joint arthroplasty, sometimes without evident cause.3,4 Numerous factors have been proposed as influencing patient satisfaction, such as significant preoperative pain or poor soft-tissue balancing.5,6 Accuracy of bone resection and implant positioning are considered to be important influences on satisfaction, as well as long-term revision risk.7-10 The development of orthopaedic robots, allowing for robot-assisted surgery (RAS), has made implant positioning more precise relative to manual procedures.9,11,12 This has led to a growing number of studies designed to assess if this improved precision translates to better clinical outcomes relative to manual arthroplasty.13-15

Despite growing clinical evidence for RAS, little is known regarding these systems. Research in this area is required urgently in order to guide clinical practice, by allowing clinicians to better identify and meet the needs of patients, and to facilitate effective and fully informed clinical decision-making. Furthermore, it is important to understand public perception of RAS in order to inform researchers regarding patient priorities when designing and conducting research that evaluates the efficacy and outcomes associated with new arthroplasty techniques.

Patient and public involvement in research has grown in popularity over recent years, and holds value in guiding research priorities and the development of effective research studies.16,17 Patient involvement allows more meaningful research to take place. In the context of RAS systems, patient involvement can help clinicians to better understand what matters to patients, and what outcomes make the most difference to them, and should therefore hold the most weight in relevant research. Patient involvement can also help highlight areas of misunderstanding, or lack of education, which can be targeted appropriately to ensure fully informed shared decision-making. It is vital to understand patient perceptions; as patients become more informed and engaged with their own healthcare, we must ensure they are provided with adequate information to navigate their healthcare choices with complete understanding.

As RAS pushes orthopaedics into a new frontier, we must actively involve patients in the research that supports this shift. If we do not engage with or understand patient perceptions, concerns, and desires, then work in this area will never be able to effectively address and answer the questions that matter most to our patients.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the evidence relating to patient and public understanding and opinion of RAS in arthroplasty. The secondary aim was to identify current knowledge gaps in the literature and develop priorities for further research. A structured scoping review study design was considered the most appropriate methodology, based on the broad nature of the research questions and the need to map existing literature themes.18

Methods

Protocol

The study adhered to an established five-stage scoping review methodology following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), and was conducted in accordance with current best practice.19 This study was exempt from research ethics committee approval. The draft protocol was revised and the final version of the protocol (version 1.0) is available upon request from the corresponding author (SRM).

Eligibility criteria

All studies referencing patient or public perception, interest, or concerns regarding robot-assisted arthroplasty were considered. In line with scoping review methodology, any literature on the topic may be assessed, however we limited our assessment to peer-reviewed studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, qualitative and quantitative studies, as well as unpublished research work or theses.20 Demographic details of participants in the reviewed studies were not restricted to meet any specific eligibility criteria. A publication timeframe limit was not set. Only English-language studies, or studies published in another language with an accurate English translation, were reviewed. Exclusion criteria were: studies relating to trauma, reports without objective data or empirical findings, and studies either not relevant to public opinion on RAS, or not relevant to orthopaedics.

Information sources and search strategy

A number of searches were carried out to best inform appropriate search terminology. The search strategy was developed a priori to capture the widest breadth of articles, utilizing Boolean search logic. The search strategy was applied to the following databases: PubMed, Directory of Open Access Journals, Scopus, and ProQuest Central. Google Scholar was used to search for relevant grey literature articles. Reference lists of included studies were screened for additional relevant articles.

Study selection

Following a search of databases, all identified articles were collated using a dedicated collaborative review platform (Nested Knowledge, USA) and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened by two reviewers (SRM, SC) independently and assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-text articles were retrieved for all potentially relevant sources, and a detailed assessment against the inclusion and exclusion criteria was conducted for every paper by two reviewers (SRM, SC). No disagreements occurred; thus, no third reviewer discussions were necessary. The results of this search process were reported using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews flowchart of the search and screening process and included articles. DOAJ, Directory of Open Access Journals; RAS, robotic-assisted surgery.

Data extraction

The search results were incorporated into data extraction tables by the lead reviewer (SRM). A secondary reviewer (SC) extracted data into identical tables, ensuring accuracy of extraction, and agreement between reviewers. There were no disagreements. Data extraction categories included: author, year of publication, source of publication, location of study, study aims, interventions assessed, study design, study population and sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, follow-up period, outcomes assessed, key findings, and detailed quantitative findings.

Methodological quality appraisal

Methodological quality and risk of bias were not appraised in the included articles, consistent with guidance on scoping review conduct.21,22

Result synthesis

Data were collated and summarized using descriptive and thematic analysis. Descriptive analysis allowed a description of studies reviewed, including the number of studies, study designs used, populations analyzed, and source of the works. Thematic analysis was carried out using an inductive model of data analysis.23 This allowed themes to emerge organically during the review process based on recurring patterns and insights from the reviewers and discussion with the wider research team.

Results

Descriptive analysis

A flow diagram following PRISMA-ScR conventions describes the output from the search and selection process (Figure 1). The initial search identified 455 eligible articles. Of these, 104 were duplicates and were removed. The abstracts of 351 articles were screened, with 336 not meeting the inclusion criteria. A full-text review was conducted on 15 eligible articles; none were excluded. Citation review highlighted one further paper for review, which was included. Data extraction was conducted from 16 included articles.

Discussion

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis identified four key themes: patient and public interest in orthopaedic RAS (n = 6); patient and public understanding regarding RAS (n = 13); concerns regarding RAS (n = 8); and influences on patient and public preferences and perception (n = 4). Most studies addressed multiple themes. A study infographic summarizing the findings of the analysis is represented in Figure 2.

Infographic helping to summarize review findings and identified research gaps. RAS, robotic-assisted surgery.

Patient and public interest in orthopaedic RAS

Across all studies, patient interest in RAS was consistently high, although definitions and measures of interest varied. Chang et al27 reported that 77.8% of patients would explore RAS if they required arthroplasty, with other studies noting that many had heard of it. Pagani et al25 found 75.5% were familiar with robotic surgery terminology, and 63.8% had seen related media or advertising, with around one-third of patients preferring RAS to conventional surgery. Brinkman et al36 observed an exponential rise in Google search volumes for robotic joint arthroplasty since 2011, with Gao et al37 expecting searches to double by 2030, reflecting growing awareness. Pinci et al26 reported that 53.1% preferred RAS, though only 44.2% were familiar with it prior to the survey. Dandamudi et al28 noted that 69.1% were interested but unsure of the benefits.

There certainly appears to be patient awareness and interest in RAS, but these studies help to highlight an unmet need for patient information and knowledge to allow this interest to be fully explored. It should also be cautioned that a large portion of patients in certain studies had seen advertising or media regarding these technologies, and that this should not be the principal way patients are informed regarding use of RAS.

Patient and public understanding of orthopaedic RAS

Despite clear interest in orthopaedic RAS, the depth of understanding among patients was often limited. Abdelaal et al24 highlighted that when questioned about their overall knowledge of RAS, 49.3% of patients rated it as poor or very poor. Khalik et al29 echoed this, noting that as many as 71% of patients were unfamiliar with RAS.

Several studies, including those by Abdelaal et al,24 Pinci et al,26 Chang et al,27 Lavelle et al,33 and Jassim et al35 reported widespread misconceptions that robots operate autonomously, with up to 70% believing that robots could perform half or more of a procedure without surgeon input. This misconception is difficult to fully explain, but may be related to the use of the term ‘robot’ and the associated pop culture references to this, as well as media exposure. This highlights a vital need for the degree of autonomy to be discussed with patients at the time of consent, in order to ensure full understanding.

Multiple studies, including Abdelaal et al24 and Chang et al27 noted that patients tend to overestimate the scale of RAS usage. Chang et al27 found a common belief among patients that 30% to 50% of academic institutions use RAS for arthroplasty. Jassim et al35 noted that three-quarters of patients believe 10% or more of orthopaedic procedures in the NHS are performed with robot assistance, with this perceived figure being even higher in the private sector. This overestimation of the scale of use is not inherently problematic, but could play into patient decision-making or raise concerns about equity of care, especially based on the fact that patients perceived larger academic units, or hospitals with fee-paying patients, to utilize RAS more commonly than other centres.

Abdelaal et al,24 Pagani et al,25 Pinci et al,26 Khalik et al,29 and Pierce et al32 found that many patients believe RAS guarantees better outcomes. Along with Chang et al27 and Jassim et al,35 these studies show that patients associate RAS with more precise implant positioning, less pain, fewer complications, and faster recovery. Additional preconceptions included shorter operating times, less scarring, and reduced surgeon workload. The fact that some of these studies relate to patients already consented for RAS surgery, or patients who have already undergone a RAS procedure, highlights that patients may not always be accurately informed regarding their decision for RAS surgery. These preconceptions are not yet able to be proven as true or false, and the current degree of evidence for RAS should be discussed clearly with patients at the time of consent to ensure informed decision-making.

The sentiment towards RAS was positive but cautious. Pinci et al26 and Hölgyesi et al31 both reported that over 50% of patients would prefer to undergo RAS over a manual arthroplasty procedure. However, not all studies reflected a preference for RAS. Chang et al27 found that 31.9% of participants viewed RAS as superior to manual surgery. Dandamudi et al28 reported even lower rates, with 17.8% favouring RAS over conventional arthroplasty and 69.1% interested but undecided, suggesting that patients may not oppose RAS, but simply require more information. Abdelaal et al24 noted mixed opinions, again, with some hesitant due to unfamiliarity. Pierce et al32 showed that 92% of preoperative RAS-THA and 83% of RAS-TKA patients would recommend RAS over conventional arthroplasty, however these patients were already consented for RAS arthroplasty and may well have been biased from this. Pagani et al25 reported that 69.4% believed in better results, 64.2% in fewer complications, and 54.6% in faster recovery. Pinci et al26 found that 54.7% expected better outcomes and 53.1% anticipated faster recovery. Abdelaal et al24 found that patients most often selected “greater precision”, “reduced error risk”, and “better implant alignment” as the top expected benefits. Some of these views may be derived from marketing materials, peer anecdotes, or assumptions, rather than physician-led education, as highlighted by both Chang et al27 and Pagani et al.25 While preference rates varied widely, most studies appear to indicate that indecision reflected limited understanding rather than any true aversion, highlighting again the importance of improved patient education.

Patient concerns regarding orthopaedic RAS

Certain repeated concerns were observed. Pagani et al25 and Pinci et al26 reported concerns held by some patients regarding surgeon inexperience with robotic systems. Pagani et al25 quantified this, with 28.7% of patients concerned that surgeons lacked sufficient experience with robots. Kelenc et al39 also noted patients’ concerns around being early in a surgeon’s learning curve. Honest discussion between surgeons and patients regarding their level of experience with new technology is important to address this.

Robot malfunction was noted as a significant patient concern by Abdelaal et al,24 Khalik et al,29 and Pierce et al,34 the last of which found as many as 88% of patients to be worried about potential malfunction of the robot. A number of these concerns may be related to the erroneous perception that robots operate somewhat autonomously, however, as with any consenting process, the risk of inaccurate bone cuts or other malfunctions should be covered in detail to best inform patients.

Pinci et al26 and Guevara-Serra et al30 highlight radiation exposure as a patient concern. Guevara-Serra et al30 reported that 34.8% of patients would rather avoid a CT scan – once aware of the radiation exposure involved – if they could have an arthroplasty procedure safely without undergoing one. Interestingly, while this study found that a relatively large group of patients prefer the idea of RAS, legitimate concerns were expressed about the requirement for preoperative CT with certain systems. Although only based on two smaller studies, this does raise the possible importance from a patient perspective of imageless RAS systems in orthopaedics, and may be able to help guide future technology development and investment. It also highlights the importance of comprehensive discussion on the radiation exposure related to image-based RAS systems when consenting patients.

Influences on patient and public preferences and perceptions regarding orthopaedic RAS

The studies reviewed had relatively wide demographic backgrounds. Patients who expressed higher levels of awareness and interest tended to be younger (aged < 50 years), more educated (college-level or above), and digitally literate.

Across studies by Pagani et al,25 Chang et al,27 and Hölgyesi et al,31 it was seen that younger, educated, digitally literate, more affluent patients were more receptive to RAS, with those from urban populations and those with prior surgical experience also more open to the idea. This raises some concerns about the possibility of health inequity; it should be ensured that all patients have access to similar opportunities within the same healthcare setting regardless of demographic profile.

Trust in the surgeon remained a stronger factor in decision-making than trust in the technology alone. Abdelaal et al24 showed that 98% of patients wanted a surgeon taking an active role in intraoperative decisions. It does seem that patient perceptions of the intended benefits of RAS have a significant impact on their decision-making, with Pagani et al25 showing that about half (48.8%) of patients would prefer a low-volume surgeon using robotic technology over a high-volume surgeon using a conventional technique.

Knowledge gaps and research priorities

As many studies were cross-sectional surveys, a larger study using a validated questionnaire across diverse populations and health systems would yield more reliable, comparable results. This would clarify whether the optimistic perceptions observed in some studies reflect broader trends or are limited to certain systems. Long-term patient-reported outcomes comparing RAS and manual procedures would better inform the public of the potential benefits of orthopaedic RAS, and allow them to make evidence-based decisions regarding their care.

Patients should be at the centre of the decision-making process; what they really think is of significant importance to care, and the general direction of medical development. Research into patients’ understanding and preferences in relation to RAS, once they have been appropriately informed, would be very useful. This further work would aid in decisions on service development and investment, while also allowing further research on RAS interventions to better consider what is important to investigate for patients. Further studies relating to the influence of socioeconomic and cultural factors on perception and acceptance of RAS in orthopaedics would allow an optimized approach to shared decision-making and patient information provision.

This review has several limitations. As a scoping review, no formal appraisal of methodological rigour or risk of bias was undertaken, and thus the strength of conclusions is limited by the quality of the primary studies. The majority of included studies were cross-sectional, primarily surveys, which may be subject to self-reporting, recall, and non-response bias. In addition, many surveys were not validated instruments, which may affect the reliability and comparability of findings. These surveys were also carried out at various timepoints and represent patient groups that may or may not have been influenced by clinician discussions, prior surgery, or media exposure.

While efforts were made to capture a comprehensive dataset, including grey literature, language restrictions limited the review to English-language sources. This may have excluded relevant studies in other languages. Most included studies originated from North America, particularly the USA and Canada, with relatively few from the UK or other healthcare systems. This raises concerns about the generalizability of findings to international populations, especially in systems with different funding structures, patient demographic details, and public attitudes towards technological interventions.

Lastly, thematic analysis was conducted inductively based on the data. Although multiple reviewers were involved to enhance rigour, thematic identification remains interpretive and subject to reviewer bias.

The review also has strengths, such as its adherence to a robust scoping review methodology, which has allowed a broad overview of the literature to highlight key evidence gaps for future research as well as broad points of understanding to reflect on in current practice. This review is also timely, at a point where the volume of research on RAS and general RAS use in orthopaedics is significantly rising.

In conclusion, this review revealed a growing interest among patients and the public in RAS for joint arthroplasty, but this interest often coexisted with significant misunderstandings and concerns. While attitudes are generally favourable towards RAS, frequently associating it with improved outcomes and surgical precision, these perceptions are not always grounded in clinical data or accurate knowledge. Misconceptions regarding the level of robotic autonomy, expected outcomes, and the risks associated with RAS are widespread, and many patients appear to rely on marketing or anecdotal information rather than surgeon-led education. There remains a lack of high-quality studies focused specifically on patient understanding, preferences, and informed decision-making in this area. Moreover, few studies explore how socioeconomic, cultural, or healthcare system differences influence patient perceptions of robotic technology. Future research should aim to clarify the benefits and limitations of RAS from patients’ perspectives, develop validated educational tools, and expand the geographical and demographic scope of studies in this area. Ultimately, improving patient education and aligning public perception with current evidence will be essential to support informed consent and the ethical integration of robotic technology into orthopaedic practice.

Take home message

- Patient interest in robot-assisted arthroplasty is high, but misconceptions about robot autonomy and significantly improved outcomes are common, highlighting a gap between perception and evidence.

- Closing this gap is critical for safe, informed, and patient-centred innovation.
