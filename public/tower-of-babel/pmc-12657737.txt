Response to correspondence on ‘Deep learning-based quantification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes as a prognostic indicator in nasopharyngeal carcinoma: multicohort findings’
Authors: T Lu, J Li
Published: 2025

We sincerely thank the commentators for their thoughtful and constructive remarks on our recent article entitled ‘Deep learning-based quantification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes as a prognostic indicator in nasopharyngeal carcinoma: multicohort findings’ published in ESMO Open.1 Their insights are highly valuable, and we are pleased to address the points raised.

As noted in our article, previous large-scale retrospective studies reported an overall treatment failure risk of 20%-30%, with most 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates <80%.2, 3, 4 In our study, the 5-year DFS in the training and validation cohorts was 73.7% and 78.8%, respectively, which fall well within previously reported ranges and therefore do not support the impression of ‘somewhat elevated disease progression’. We also recognize that in retrospective studies—particularly those in computational pathology—selection bias is difficult to eliminate entirely. For example, substandard pathological sections (e.g. air bubbles, blurred or folded tissue) must necessarily be excluded, which may skew the sample. Regarding our validation protocol, accuracy was assessed using 40 image patches from 20 patients as a proof-of-concept step. While this approach demonstrated feasibility, we agree that future work should employ larger, stratified sampling across disease stages and incorporate multi-observer annotations to further improve reliability. We chose to directly apply a pretrained convolutional neural network model (Lymphocyte Maps)5 from the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) to leverage an established architecture overfitting in a relatively modest dataset. Once strong performance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) was observed, we judged that the incremental benefit of further fine tuning would be limited. Our primary goal was to establish the independent prognostic value of deep-learning-derived tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte quantification (TILDL) in NPC, rather than optimizing the model architecture per se.

We also agree that TILDL may outperform immunohistochemistry (IHC)-derived subsets because it captures a broader immune context and may implicitly encode spatial features beyond single-marker quantification. In our study, IHC for CD20+, CD3+, and CD8+ cells was included primarily to validate the reliability of DL quantification, not to develop combined prognostic models. Given that only 220 patients underwent IHC testing, the sample size was insufficient to perform robust multivariable analyses with interaction terms. Nonetheless, we concur that exploring potential interactions is a valuable direction, and we have already initiated a prospective observational study (NCT06763640) to validate the prognostic role of TILDL and assess its complementarity with IHC-based markers. Regarding programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, it was not uniformly available across our retrospective cohorts. More importantly, in NPC, the tumor microenvironment is typically immune rich, resulting in a very high rate of PD-L1 positivity,6,7 which limits its discriminative prognostic value compared with other cancers. This supports our focus on TIL-based markers. Looking ahead, we agree that integrating multimodal data—including genomic features, multiplex IHC, and image-omic fusion methods—within larger prospective immunotherapy cohorts will be essential to fully validate and extend the clinical utility of DL-based TIL quantification.

In conclusion, we thank the commentators once again for their constructive feedback. Their suggestions will help refine our ongoing efforts in computational pathology for NPC and ultimately facilitate clinical translation.
