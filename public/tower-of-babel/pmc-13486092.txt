Gut microbiome composition and cellulolytic bacteria associated with the carpenter bee Xylocopa frontalis
Authors: Ana Carolina Costa Santos, Joberth Lee Corrêa, Darlan Sedinário de Lima, Aline Carla Alves Silva Andrade, Camila Nonato Junqueira, Anderson Rodrigues dos Santos, Solange Cristina Augusto, Ana Maria Bonetti, Carlos Ueira-Vieira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractCarpenter bees of the genus Xylocopa interact extensively with woody substrates during nest construction, suggesting that associated microorganisms may contribute to the degradation of plant-derived polymers. Despite their ecological relevance, the gut microbiome and functional potential of Xylocopa species remain poorly characterized. In this study, we investigated the bacterial and fungal gut microbiome of Xylocopa frontalis using 16S rRNA and ITS amplicon sequencing, complemented by culture-dependent approaches. The gut microbiome was dominated by bacterial taxa commonly associated with bees, including Bombiscardovia, Bifidobacterium, and Frischella, whereas fungal communities were more variable and included genera such as Aspergillus and Cladosporium. Several taxa were consistently detected across samples, indicating recurrent community members; however, a consistent core microbiome was not clearly observed. We established a collection of cultivable microorganisms, including a bacterial isolate capable of utilizing cellulose as a carbon source, as demonstrated by plate assays. Phylogenetic and genomic analyses identified this isolate as Bacillus velezensis strain Xf, which harbors genes associated with cellulose and hemicellulose degradation. These findings suggest the potential for lignocellulose-related metabolism in the gut microbiome. Together, our results provide a combined culture-independent and culture-dependent characterization of the X. frontalis gut microbiome and identify microorganisms with relevant functional traits. Although based on a limited sample size, this study expands current knowledge of microbial associations in carpenter bees and establishes a foundation for future investigations into their functional roles.Key points• Gut microbiome of Xylocopa frontalis was characterized by amplicon sequencing• Bacillus velezensis strain Xf showed cellulolytic activity in vitro• Gut-associated microbes showed genes linked to lignocellulose degradationSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13911-0.Keywords: Carpenter bees, Gut microbiome, Cellulolytic bacteria, Amplicon sequencing, Lignocellulose degradation

Introduction

Bees of the genus Xylocopa, commonly known as carpenter bees, comprise approximately 400 species distributed across tropical and subtropical regions (Michener 2007; Melo and Martins 2025). These bees are among the largest members of the superfamily Apoidea and are characterized by robust mandibles adapted for excavating wood, as well as strong flight capabilities that enable long-distance foraging (Gerling et al. 1989; Junqueira et al. 2012). Most Xylocopa species construct their nests by burrowing into dry or decaying plant substrates, such as dead trunks and branches, or by occupying pre-existing cavities, including artificial trap-nests (Hurd 1958; Camillo and Garofalo 1982; Gerling et al. 1989; Silva et al. 2019). Nest construction involves both mechanical excavation and the deposition of glandular secretions that line galleries, contributing to structural integrity, microclimatic regulation, and antimicrobial protection (Gerling et al. 1989).

The gut microbiota of bees plays essential roles in host physiology, including nutrient acquisition, detoxification of plant-derived compounds, protection against pathogens, and maintenance of homeostasis (Melo 2020; Motta and Moran 2024; Maurice-Lira et al. 2024). In eusocial bees, these microbial communities are typically simple, stable, and maintained through social transmission. In contrast, solitary bees generally harbor more variable microbiomes that are strongly influenced by environmental exposure (Kwong et al. 2017; Voulgari-Kokota et al. 2019). As most Xylocopa species are solitary, their gut microbiome is expected to reflect a stronger environmental component, although occasional nest sharing may facilitate limited microbial exchange (Camillo and Garofalo 1982).

Recent studies have reported that Xylocopa species harbor distinct gut microbial communities, often including bacterial lineages also found in honeybees and bumble bees, although their composition and relative abundance vary across species and ecological contexts (Holley et al. 2022; Handy et al. 2023). However, the functional capabilities of these microbial communities, particularly in relation to lignocellulose-associated processes, remain poorly characterized.

Given the strong ecological association of carpenter bees with woody substrates, their microbiota is likely to be involved in interactions with plant-derived polymers present in nesting substrates. In other wood-associated insects, such as termites and xylophagous beetles, the degradation of lignocellulosic compounds—including cellulose and hemicellulose—is largely mediated by microbial symbionts (Geib et al. 2008; Brune 2014). In this context, the gut microbiome of Xylocopa may represent a reservoir of microorganisms with lignocellulose-degrading capabilities. In this system, such microbial functions are more likely associated with interactions between the bee and its nesting environment rather than direct nutritional contributions to the host, although their ecological roles remain to be clarified.

Despite these advances, the gut microbiome of Xylocopa frontalis remains poorly characterized, particularly with respect to its functional capabilities. In this context, the present study aimed to characterize the bacterial and fungal gut microbiome of X. frontalis using 16S rRNA and ITS amplicon sequencing, and to explore its functional potential through the isolation and screening of cultivable microorganisms capable of utilizing cellulose as a carbon source. By integrating culture-independent and culture-dependent approaches, this study provides a combined taxonomic and functional perspective on the microbial diversity associated with carpenter bees and identifies microorganisms with potential roles in lignocellulose-related processes.

Material and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Females of X. frontalis (n = 4) were collected while visiting flowers at the Ecological Station of Panga, a protected reserve belonging to the Federal University of Uberlândia (UFU), located in Uberlândia, Minas Gerais, Brazil. The reserve encompasses an area of 403.85 ha (19°10′ S, 48°24′ W), with an average altitude of 800 m, and is situated within the urban area of Uberlândia (Cardoso et al. 2010). Bee collection was conducted in accordance with Brazilian environmental legislation and under authorization issued by the Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation (ICMBio/SISBIO) (authorization number 14165-1) and SISGEN A1F87EA. Three specimens were immediately frozen after collection, thawed, and then surface-sterilized by washing with 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by brief air drying. The bees were dissected under sterile conditions using ethanol-sterilized forceps. The last abdominal segment was carefully removed along with the entire gut, which was transferred to a sterile tube containing 1 mL of sterile saline solution. Three samples were processed for DNA extraction (one individual was reserved for culture-based analyses), using the DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions for bacterial and fungal DNA. DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis stained with ethidium bromide. Additionally, one fresh specimen was processed for culture-based isolation of gut microorganisms.

Amplicon sequencing and data analysis

For bacterial community analysis, 30 ng of DNA were used as input for library preparation following the Illumina “16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation” protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) (Klindworth et al. 2013). The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 341 F and 806R, and amplicons were purified, indexed, pooled, and sequenced in paired-end mode (2 × 250 bp) on the MiSeq platform (Klindworth et al. 2013). Raw reads were processed using the DADA2 package in R (v4.0), with parameters trimLeft = (c (17,21) and truncLen = (c(250,230) to remove primers and low-quality bases (Callahan et al. 2016; Santos et al. 2023). Sequence variants were inferred, chimeras removed, and taxonomy assigned using the SILVA 138 database, with a minimum identity threshold of 99% (Quast et al. 2012).

For fungal community profiling, DNA libraries were prepared according to the Illumina Fungal Metagenomic Sequencing Demonstrated Protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The ITS1 region was amplified using a two-step PCR workflow with ITS-specific primers (White et al. 1990), followed by the addition of dual-index adapters and library pooling using the Nextera XT v2 indexing kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed in paired-end mode (2 × 250 bp) on a MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Data processing followed the “DADA2 ITS Pipeline Tutorial,” with primer removal (via Cutadapt) and filtering of reads shorter than 50 bp (Martin 2011), followed by Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) inference and taxonomic classification using the UNITE ITS database, with a minimum identity threshold of 99% (Martin 2011; Callahan et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2021; Santos et al. 2023).

Alpha and beta diversity metrics were calculated following the workflow described in the “DADA2 Pipeline Tutorial,” using standard methods for microbial community diversity and ordination analyses (Santos et al. 2023).

Microorganism isolation from X. frontalis gut and biotechnology prospection

One hundred microliters (100 µL) of gut homogenate in saline solution from X. frontalis specimens were used to prepare two suspensions by inoculating 100 µL of the sample into 10 mL of Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and 10 mL of 15GF broth (Santos et al. 2023). The suspensions were incubated at 37 ± 0.5 °C for 24 h, after which serial dilutions were prepared up to 10⁻4. Aliquots of 100 µL from appropriate dilutions were spread onto different culture media to isolate a broad diversity of microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi. Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) and Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose Agar (YPD) supplemented with chloramphenicol (100 mg/L), as well as Sabouraud Dextrose Agar, were used for the isolation of fungi and yeasts. Bacterial isolation was performed using Nutrient Agar, MacConkey Agar, Mannitol Agar, de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar, and M17 agar. In addition, Luria–Bertani (LB) agar supplemented with 1% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was used to screen for microorganisms with potential cellulolytic activity. Plates were incubated at 28–30 °C for 48 h to 7 days, depending on the culture medium and microbial growth. Colonies with distinct morphologies were purified by repeated subculturing, maintained on solid media under refrigeration, and preserved in appropriate liquid media supplemented with 20% (v/v) glycerol at − 80 °C for long-term storage (Santos et al. 2023).

Colony morphology was evaluated by culturing the bacterial isolate on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24–48 h. Morphological characteristics were recorded based on visual inspection of well-isolated colonies. For microscopic characterization, Gram staining was performed using the standard protocol (Beveridge 2001). After rinsing with distilled water and air-drying, stained cells were examined under a light microscope using a 100 × oil immersion objective. Cell morphology and Gram reaction were determined based on staining behavior and cellular shape.

To evaluate cellulase production, isolated bacteria were grown on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates supplemented with 1% (w/v) carboxymethylcellulose (CMC). Bacteria were inoculated in triplicate onto Petri dishes containing LB-CMC and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cellulolytic activity was revealed by flooding the plates with 0.25% (w/v) Congo red and allowing staining for 15 min, as described for screening polysaccharide-degrading enzymes (Islam and Roy 2018). Excess dye was then removed by washing the plates with 1 M NaCl, which increases the contrast between stained and degraded regions. The formation of orange to pale halos around the colonies—corresponding to areas where CMC was hydrolyzed—was considered indicative of cellulase activity. To quantify enzymatic activity, the Enzymatic Index (EI) was calculated by dividing the average diameter of the hydrolysis halo by the average diameter of the corresponding bacterial colony, both measured with a digital caliper (Hankin and Anagnostakis 1975; Florencio et al. 2012; Liguori et al. 2021).

Bacteria genome sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted as described above. The sample was sequenced on the Illumina platform (library had an insert size of 300 bp with a paired-end sequencing length of 150 bp) at the Life Sciences Core Facility (LaCTAD) from the State University of Campinas (UNICAMP, Campinas, Brazil).

Following sequencing, raw reads were subjected to quality control using SOAPnuke v2.1.9 (Chen et al. 2018), which was employed to remove adapter sequences and filter out low-quality reads. Contigs shorter than 150 bp or with an N content ≥ 0.1% were excluded from downstream analyses. The quality score format for the processed reads was configured to the Phred+33 scale.

Genome assembly was performed using SPAdes v3.15.4, and the resulting contigs were aligned against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTN (Prjibelski et al. 2020). Taxonomic assignment was subsequently conducted using an in-house species identification pipeline and the genome assembly (Ueira‐Vieira et al. 2025). For functional annotation, the genome annotation pipeline PANNOTATOR (version 1.0) was employed (Santos et al. 2013).

Genome annotation and screening for cellulose-degrading enzymes

All predicted protein-coding genes were screened to identify those potentially involved in cellulose and plant cell-wall polysaccharide degradation.

Protein sequences for all candidate loci were extracted from annotated coding sequences (CDS) features and translated using the standard bacterial genetic code. Full-length open reading frames (ORFs) were retained, including N-terminal signal peptides and precursor motifs whenever available. The translated amino acid sequences were used for subsequent functional validation as described below.

Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) classification was inferred based on canonical catalytic and binding domains. Candidate proteins were evaluated against established CAZy families known to participate in cellulose and hemicellulose depolymerization, including glycoside hydrolase (GH), carbohydrate esterase (CE), and carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) families (e.g., GH1, GH3, GH5, GH6, GH7, GH9, GH12, GH30, GH43, CBM2, CBM3, CE1) (Cantarel et al. 2009; Lombard et al. 2014).

The protein families (Pfam) and InterPro signatures associated with glycosyl hydrolases, glucosidases, xylanases, or polysaccharide-binding modules were also used when available in the genome annotations. Genes were considered high-confidence cellulolytic candidates if their annotated product names aligned with known cellulose-active enzymes and/or if they contained CAZy-related conserved domains (e.g., GH catalytic folds, CBM carbohydrate-anchoring motifs) (Finn et al. 2016; Blum et al. 2021; Mistry et al. 2021).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R using the phyloseq, vegan, and ggplot2 packages (Santos et al. 2023). Prior to analysis, the data were filtered to remove taxa annotated as “Mitochondria,” “Chloroplast,” and “Unassigned.”

Bacterial community sequencing

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed a total of 161 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) identified across the three gut samples of X. frontalis (Supplementary Table S1). Among these, 15 ASVs were shared across all three samples. In total, 58 ASVs were detected in sample Xf1, 91 in Xf2, and 56 in Xf3 (Fig. 1A).

Only two ASVs remained unclassified at the phylum level, indicating that most sequences were taxonomically assigned at this level (Fig. 1A). Bacterial ASVs were assigned to seven phyla, with Actinobacteria being the most dominant across all samples (Fig. 1B).

A total of 33 bacterial genera were identified, with the most abundant taxa shown in Fig. 2. Bombiscardovia was the predominant genus across all individuals, followed by Bifidobacterium, Neokomagataea, and Atopobium. Several genera were shared across the three samples, including Akkermansia, Atopobium, Azorhizophilus, Bifidobacterium, Bombiscardovia, Faecalibacterium, Frischella, Methylobacterium, and Neokomagataea. Notably, 49% of the ASVs could not be classified at the genus level, indicating the presence of poorly characterized taxa.

A set of 15 bacterial ASVs was shared across all samples (Supplementary Table S1). These ASVs were taxonomically assigned to the following genera: Atopobium (n = 1), Azorhizophilus (n = 1), Bifidobacterium (n = 2), Bombiscardovia (n = 3), Frischella (n = 1), and Methylobacterium (n = 1). An additional four ASVs were assigned only at the family level—Bifidobacteriaceae, Atopobiaceae, Rhizobiaceae, and Lachnospiraceae—and could not be resolved to a defined genus. The remaining two ASVs were classified only at the domain level (Bacteria), indicating limited taxonomic resolution for these sequences.

Fungi community sequencing

Sequencing of the ITS region identified a total of 215 fungal ASVs across the three gut samples of X. frontalis (Supplementary Table S2). Only three ASVs were shared across all samples, indicating low overlap among individuals (Fig. 3A). Fifty-nine percent of the ASVs were classified at the phylum level, grouped into three major fungal phyla—Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Mortierellomycota. Among them, Ascomycota was the most prevalent and consistently detected across all individuals (Fig. 3B).

Microorganism isolation and biotechnology prospection

A total of 31 microorganisms were isolated from the gut of X. frontalis using different media, including 26 bacteria and 5 yeasts. Phenotypic characterization of Bacillus velezensis strain Xf revealed morphological features consistent with those of the genus Bacillus. When grown on Luria–Bertani medium, strain Xf exhibited abundant growth, forming opaque, cream-colored colonies with irregular shapes, rough surfaces, and well-defined margins (Fig. 7A), a colonial pattern commonly reported for Bacillus species. Gram staining analysis classified strain Xf as Gram-positive, as confirmed by microscopic observation of cells retaining the crystal violet stain (Fig. 7B).

Phenotypic characterization of B. velezensis strain Xf. A Colony morphology of strain Xf grown on LB agar, showing opaque, irregular colonies with rough surfaces and well-defined margins. B Gram staining of strain Xf, revealing Gram-positive cells retaining the crystal violet stain. C Cellulolytic activity on carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) agar was revealed by Congo red staining, showing clear hydrolysis halos surrounding the colonies

The cellulolytic activity of the isolate Xf-CMC-03 was measured by determining colony diameter and the hydrolysis halos on LB–CMC plates (Fig. 7C). Colony diameters were 3.58, 4.43, and 3.83 mm, with a mean diameter of 3.95 mm across triplicates. Hydrolysis halo diameters were 6.82, 8.28, and 7.75 mm, resulting in a mean halo diameter of 7.62 mm. The enzymatic index (EI), calculated as the ratio between the mean halo diameter and the mean colony diameter, was 1.93. According to standard criteria, isolates with EI ≥ 2.0 are classified as strong cellulase producers. Although cellulolytic activity was observed, the EI 1.93 was slightly below the commonly used threshold to classify strong cellulase producers.

Genome characterization

Genomic characterization further corroborated the identification of strain Xf as a member of the B. velezensis species complex. The assembled genome, designated B. velezensis strain Xf, has a total length of 3,950,764 bp (Fig. 8). The overall GC content was 46.52%, which falls within the expected range for the genus Bacillus, supporting its classification within the B. velezensis species complex.

A total of 3966 protein-coding sequences (CDSs) were identified, with an average gene length of 886 bp. The genome exhibits a high coding density (88.9%), corresponding to approximately one gene per 996 bp, indicative of a compact genome with limited non-coding DNA. Only 11 pseudogenes were detected, suggesting a low level of gene decay and overall genomic stability.

In addition, the genome harbors 14 rRNA genes and 79 tRNA genes, providing a complete and functionally robust translational apparatus.

Annotation of genes encoding proteins putatively involved in cellulose degradation

Genome mining of the B. velezensis strain Xf genome identified five coding sequences annotated as potentially involved in cellulolytic or hemicellulolytic processes (Table 2). These included genes for one endonuclease (eglS), one endoglucanase (eglS), one β-glucanase (bglS), and two hemicellulose-degrading enzymes, an endo-1,4-xylanase/feruloyl esterase, and a bifunctional xylanase/deacetylase. Additionally, a gene encoding a cellulose synthase regulatory protein (bcsR) was identified, which may be associated with cellulose-related metabolic pathways. These functional assignments are based on genome annotation and domain prediction and therefore represent putative activities (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

Despite the extraordinary biological diversity of the Neotropical region, microbiomes associated with native solitary bee species remain comparatively underexplored. In this study, we investigated X. frontalis, a large-bodied carpenter bee that represents an ecologically important yet microbiologically understudied taxon (Michener 2007). Its characteristic nesting behavior in woody substrates provides a well-defined ecological context for investigating gut-associated microorganisms with potential lignocellulose-degrading capabilities. In this system, such microbial functions are more likely associated with interactions at the interface between the bee and its lignocellulosic nesting substrate than with direct nutritional contributions to the host. This study provides the first integrated characterization of the gut microbiome of X. frontalis, contributing to the understanding of bee–microbe associations in solitary bees.

The composition of bee-associated microbiomes is known to vary in response to multiple factors, including diet, environmental microbial reservoirs, and nest substrate characteristics (Engel and Moran 2013; Kwong and Moran 2016). In the present study, differences between bacterial and fungal communities were observed; however, given the limited sample size (n = 3), these patterns should be interpreted as preliminary observations rather than as evidence of distinct ecological dynamics or transmission routes. Differences in richness and diversity indices, as well as variation observed in NMDS analyses, indicate heterogeneity among samples. Although based on a limited sample size, these observations suggest variability in microbiome composition among individuals of X. frontalis.

The bacterial community identified in X. frontalis was dominated by members of the phylum Actinobacteria, with recurrent detection of genera such as Bombiscardovia, Bifidobacterium, and Atopobium. A direct comparison with Xylocopa tenuiscapa from Thailand further highlights variation in microbiome composition among species within the genus. The gut microbiome of X. tenuiscapa is dominated by Firmicutes, particularly Lactobacillales, along with Proteobacteria such as Gilliamella and members of Enterobacteriaceae (Subta et al. 2020). In contrast, X. frontalis harbors a community characterized by a predominance of Actinobacteria and the consistent presence of genera such as Bombiscardovia, Bifidobacterium, Atopobium, Methylobacterium, and Frischella. These differences may reflect ecological, environmental, or host-related factors among species.

Beyond taxonomic profiling, this study combined culture-based isolation with functional screening and genome analysis to explore the metabolic potential of gut-associated microorganisms. Among the isolated strains, one bacterial isolate exhibited detectable cellulolytic activity in qualitative assays on carboxymethyl cellulose-containing medium. Genomic sequencing and taxonomic assignment identified this isolate as B. velezensis strain Xf. Genome annotation revealed genes associated with cellulose and hemicellulose degradation, including a secreted endoglucanase (eglS), a β-glucanase (bglS), and enzymes involved in xylan degradation and deacetylation (Chen et al. 2023). Several of these genes encode proteins with signal peptides and domain architectures characteristic of extracellular glycoside hydrolases, supporting their potential involvement in lignocellulose transformation processes.

These findings support the presence of lignocellulose-related metabolic potential; these functions remain to be experimentally validated. Although qualitative assays indicated cellulolytic activity, quantitative analyses will be required to determine enzymatic efficiency and substrate specificity (Kim et al. 2012; Dobrzyński et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). In addition to B. velezensis, several microorganisms detected across samples, including Aspergillus, Neocucurbitaria, Azorhizophilus, Bombiscardovia, and Bifidobacterium, have been previously reported to produce enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism (Baldrian 2006; Sánchez 2009; Kellner et al. 2014; Viswanath et al. 2014; Al-Fakih and Almaqtri 2019; El-Hawary et al. 2020). Together, these findings indicate the presence of microorganisms with known lignocellulolytic capabilities.

From a functional perspective, the data suggest that gut-associated microorganisms may contribute to interactions between carpenter bees and woody substrates. Similar functional associations have been reported in termites (Beránková et al. 2024) and wood-boring beetles (Scully et al. 2013), where microbial communities mediate the transformation of plant-derived polymers. While comparable roles remain to be demonstrated in Xylocopa, no direct evidence of a nutritional contribution to the host was observed in this study.

Finally, it is important to note that ITS regions are highly variable and fungal reference databases remain incomplete, which likely contributes to the high proportion of unclassified fungal ASVs observed. This limitation highlights the need for expanded fungal genomic resources and complementary culture-based approaches. Overall, this study provides a combined taxonomic and functional characterization of the gut microbiome of X. frontalis and identifies microorganisms with putative functional traits based on taxonomic and genomic inference. These results expand current knowledge of insect-associated microbiomes and highlight carpenter bees as potential reservoirs of metabolically versatile microorganisms associated with lignocellulose-related processes, with relevance for functional and applied microbiology.

Conclusion

This study provides an initial characterization of the gut microbiome of X. frontalis, integrating taxonomic profiling, genomic analyses, and microbial isolation. A key finding was the identification of a cellulolytic Bacillus velezensis strain Xf, harboring genes associated with cellulose and hemicellulose degradation. In addition, the detection of microbial taxa with known lignocellulolytic capabilities suggests the presence of microorganisms with potential functional relevance.

Although based on a limited number of samples, this study establishes a foundation for future investigations into microbial diversity and function in Xylocopa species and contributes to the broader understanding of microbiomes associated with solitary bees.
