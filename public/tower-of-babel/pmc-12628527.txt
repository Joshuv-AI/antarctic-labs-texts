Impact of a Healthy City Program on oral health and nutritional habits of middle school students in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Eman I AlSagob, Bayan S AlHaydab, Eman A AlOmar, Jinan O AlMohaimeed, Suzan A Alzahrani, Mamata I Hebbal, Abeer A AlShami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 1988, the World Health Organization launched the Healthy City Program (HCP), which seeks to improve health and well-being by empowering local communities. This study aimed to assess the impact of HCP nutritional education on middle school children by comparing general and oral health, nutritional status, and dietary outcomes between participating and non-participating schools.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1200 school children from three HCP schools and five control non-HCP schools in Saudi Arabia. Data were collected using questionnaires on self-reported general health, oral health, and dietary intake. Nutritional status was assessed using Body Mass Index (BMI). Caries prevalence (Decayed Missing Filled Teeth DMFT) and Gingival health (Bleeding on Probing, BOP) were evaluated using oral examinations. Statistical tests, including chi-square test, Pearson correlation test, and multinomial logistic regression, were applied. Statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05.ResultsHCP students had better self-reported general health and oral health. However, both groups had high consumption of unhealthy foods, with control students consuming more fruits and junk food. The mean number of DMFT was significantly lower in the HCP group (3.50 ± 2.40) than in the control group (4.96 ± 3.59) (p < 0.001). The mean number of teeth with Bleeding on Probing (BOP) was significantly lower in the HCP group (11.12 ± 9.81) than in the control group (14.49 ± 11.82) (p < 0.001), and unhealthy diet scores were positively correlated with BMI (r = 0.088, p = 0.002) and DMFT (r = 0.074, p = 0.012). According to the regression analysis, an unhealthy diet was significantly associated with increased odds of dental caries (OR = 1.26, p = 0.002), whereas HCP participation was associated with lower odds of dental caries (OR = 0.54, p < 0.001). The risk of caries was not significantly predicted by BMI, age, or sex, although brushing twice a day was protective (OR = 0.55, p = 0.003).ConclusionAlthough HCP led to improvements in self-perceived general and oral health, its effect on dietary habits was less evident, with no evident benefit in terms of encouraging the use of healthier foods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25240-4.Keywords: Community, Dental caries, Diet, Nutritional status, Oral health, Public health, Students

Background

The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) is one of 22 members of the World Health Organization (WHO) Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR). Healthy City programs (HCPs) were first introduced by the World Health Organization (WHO) Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office (EMRO) in 1988 as a component of community-based initiatives (CBI) in the EMR [1]. The four primary areas of concentration for these CBIs were women’s health and development, healthy cities, healthy villages, and fundamental development needs [1]. The strategy behind these Community-Based Initiatives (CBIs) is to enable local communities to take charge of the creation and implementation of programs by identifying their needs and allocating their resources. According to program reviews, CBIs work better in rural settings than in urban settings [1].

In 1990, the Healthy Cities initiative was launched in Iran and subsequently embraced by other EMR nations such as Saudi Arabia. Under the direction of the KSA Ministry of Health (MoH), Saudi cities such as Riyadh and Jeddah have been actively involved in the regional Healthy Cities network (RHCN) by implementing various Community Improvement Programs (CIPs) [1, 2]. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia’s economy has experienced significant growth over the past few decades, leading to accelerated urbanization and alterations in population lifestyles [3]. Consequently, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease have become increasingly prevalent, primarily due to poor dietary habits and sedentary lifestyles [4]. To mitigate the growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), Saudi Arabia’s Health Sector Transformation Program (HSTP)—a key Vision Realization Program under Vision 2030—emphasizes promoting healthy lifestyles and strengthening prevention against health threats [5]. Additionally, oral health is prioritized, as dental caries remains one of the most prevalent childhood diseases in the country [6].

Dental caries is a multifactorial disease that is closely associated with poor dietary habits and inadequate oral hygiene [7]. The prevalence of dental cavities in school-age children is significantly increased by the high consumption of processed foods, sugar-rich snacks, and sugar-sweetened beverages, coupled with poor oral hygiene practices. Unhealthy diets also contribute to general health issues, including obesity and its associated complications, in addition to their detrimental effects on dental health [8]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a correlation between elevated body mass index (BMI) and poor nutritional status, establishing BMI as a crucial indicator of nutritional status [9–11]. Despite public health authorities to promote preventive measures, dental caries among children and adolescents in Saudi Arabia remain notably prevalent. According to the study done by Orfali et al., caries prevalence was 65.6% overall, with primary teeth accounting for 72.1 and permanent teeth for 61.7% [6].

Unhealthy dietary habits are a primary contributing factor, particularly the excessive consumption of sugar. Concurrently, obesity rates are increasing, with a substantial proportion of young individuals being classified as overweight or obese. Both these conditions share common risk factors and can often be mitigated through dietary improvements and the promotion of regular physical activity, which are key components of Saudi Arabia’s HCPs [1–12].

Diriyah is a Saudi city that participates in healthcare programs through the Regional Health Care Network (RHCN). Diriyah implemented a Healthy City Program (HCP) in its middle schools, educating students on maintaining a nutritious diet based on official dietary recommendations from the Ministry of Health (MoH) [13]. Despite the widespread implementation of HCPs, limited research has evaluated their impact [12–14], particularly regarding dietary practices and their effects on middle school students’ dental and general health. To assess the efficacy of these programs and to guide future interventions aimed at reducing the prevalence of non-communicable diseases in the younger population, it is essential to understand the nutritional habits and general health of students participating in HCPs. There is an urgent need to evaluate the effectiveness of these health promotion programs in addressing the challenges posed by lifestyle changes [12–14].

The aim of this study was to compare self-reported general and oral health, self-reported dietary intake, Body Mass Index (BMI) and oral health assessment by recording caries experience and gingival status among Saudi middle school students attending schools participating in a HCP, and students attending schools in neighboring cities not participating in HCP. The null hypothesis of the study states that there is no difference in oral health status, dietary behaviors, or body mass index (BMI) between students in HCP schools and those in control (non-HCP) schools.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted as an independent program evaluation of the Healthy City Program (HCP) in Diriyah, Saudi Arabia. The purpose of this study was to evaluate middle school students’ self-reported general health, dental health, and food intake, BMI, dental caries and gingivitis and to compare these results across schools participating in HCP and control schools. The study was conducted among 1200 middle school children between January to April 2019.

Participants and sampling

The study was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Ethical Review Board of Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University. (IRB #H-01-R-059) The parents or guardians of each participant provided written informed consent, and the students themselves provided verbal assent.

A list of schools and information regarding their HCP participation was provided to the study investigators by the KSA Ministry of Education (MoE). Eight middle schools in KSA were selected to participate in the study based on convenience sampling, and permission was obtained from the principals of the selected schools for data collection. The schools were purposively selected based on their participation status in the Healthy City Program (HCP), as identified by the Ministry of Education.Three schools were included from Diriyah, which are participating in the HCP. For comparison purposes, five middle schools from neighboring cities that were not participating in the HCP were included as control schools: four from Uyaynah, and one from Jubaylah (see Fig. 1).

With a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence, and 80% power, a sample size of approximately 1200 was required to detect a 20% anticipated difference in knowledge scores between the Healthy City Program (HCP) group and the control group.

To facilitate accurate comparative analysis, this sample was subsequently divided equally between the HCP and control groups. It was determined that a total of 600 students from Diriyah schools would be recruited for the study, and 600 students would be recruited from control schools. A quota sampling method was employed to ensure equal representation across gender. A total of 300 boys and 300 girls were selected from each group (HCP and control), ensuring balanced demographic characteristics. Participants were not actively matched by age, though comparable distributions were achieved across grades due to the stratified quota sampling. Given the recruitment procedure, it was feasible to recruit exactly 600 participants from HCP schools and control schools, resulting in a total of 1,200 participants who completed the dietary questionnaire and oral examination.

Children aged between 11 and 17 who provided assent and whose parents gave informed consent were included in the study. Children were assigned to either the HCP or control group based on whether their selected school participated in the program or not. Validation of data collected via questionnaires was ongoing after each data collection visit, incomplete submissions were eliminated. Only complete and valid responses were included in the final analysis to maintain data integrity.

Four dental interns from Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University (PNU), led by a public health dentist who was a professor at PNU, served as the data collection team. The investigators were trained and calibrated prior to start of the study to ensure reliability for recording dental caries and periodontal condition (k = 0.80). Cohen’s kappa was used to assess inter-rater reliability for categorical measures (Presence/Absence of caries and periodontal condition).

The HCP that was implemented was educational in nature and took place at the middle schools participating in the KSA’s HCPs. The focus of the educational program is teaching children proper nutritional intake as per the Healthy Food Promotion (HFP), the KSA MoH’s dietary recommendations [13]. The didactic instruction on the HFP was incorporated into classroom activities in classrooms of middle schools in Diriyah. The Healthy City Program in Diriyah included a structured, school-based educational component focused on oral health and nutrition. The intervention was implemented over the course of one academic year, with monthly health education sessions delivered by trained health educators and school health personnel. The sessions utilized interactive lectures, visual aids, live demonstrations, and distribution of printed materials. Core topics covered included proper toothbrushing and flossing techniques, the impact of sugar on oral health, importance of regular dental visits, principles of balanced nutrition, and healthy food choices. These sessions were reinforced through school-wide health campaigns and periodic follow-up activities. (see Fig. 2). Schools in the control group did not receive any structured health education comparable to the HCP. Health-related topics, if addressed, were limited to general curriculum-based instruction without dedicated, systematic programming focused on oral health or nutrition.

HCP components, Delivery Pathway, and Impact

The program evaluation by the data collection team consisted of two components: a self-designed questionnaire to evaluate self-reported general health, oral health and dietary intake, and a clinical examination to measure height, weight, oral health status. The questionnaire was adapted from the WHO Oral Health Surveys: Basic Methods (5th edition) [15] for self-reported general and oral health and modified to align with the specific objectives of the study. The dietary assessment was based on a newly developed tool grounded in HFP guidelines rather than a previously validated instrument [13]. Cross cultural validation of questionnaire was performed by means of back-translation method with help of linguistic experts. It was assessed for readability and comprehension during pilot study on a group of 20 school children. Necessary corrections and modifications were made. Internal consistency (α) was found to be good (0.88). The study sample did not include those who took part in the pre-testing.

Questions regarding general health, dental health, gingival health were asked in a Likert-scale. The response categories were Excellent, Good, Fair, Poor, and I Don’t Know and offered a clear framework for participants to express their perceptions.

Dietary behavior was assessed using a composite scoring system based on the frequency of food intake reported by students. Items contributing to the unhealthy diet score included sugary drinks, sweets, fast food/junk food, and sweetened gum. Each item was scored on a Likert-type scale ranging from “Never” (0 points) to “4–5 times/day” (5 points), and the total score was computed by summing responses. Conversely, a healthy diet score was calculated based on the frequency of daily consumption of fruits, vegetables, dairy products, and sugar-free beverages, using the same scoring approach. This scoring system was developed in accordance with the behavioral recommendations of the Healthy Food Promotion (HFP) guidelines, which emphasize reducing sugar-rich and processed foods while promoting fruit and vegetable intake.

Clinical assessment of dental caries was done using WHO Dentition status for children and Decayed, Missing and Filled Teeth (DMFT) were calculated. Periodontal condition was also recorded [15]. Body Mass Index was calculated by measuring height and weight on the basis of WHO’s BMI-for-age growth charts (18).

Statistical analysis

After data collection, data from the questionnaires were entered into an Excel spreadsheet, and R software was used for the analysis (R Core Team, 2020). A descriptive analysis was conducted on the responses to the questionnaire. To visualize the responses to the dietary questions, the Likert package in R was used. A chi-squared test (α = 0.05) was employed to compare the prevalence of healthy dietary behaviors and dental caries between HCP and control groups. Pearson’s correlation was performed to determine the correlation between nutritional status, diet, and dental caries. A multivariate analysis was conducted to assess the associations between dietary intake, BMI, and oral health outcomes after adjusting for potential confounders.

Discussion

WHO launched the HCP, a community-based program designed to empower and involve the community in order to promote health and well-being. By incorporating health into urban planning and encouraging individuals to adopt healthy habits, the initiative aims to create sustainable, health-supportive environments. It has been used all around the world, with local circumstances modified to address public health issues unique to each area [1–12]. The HCP in Saudi Arabia supports the objectives of the National Vision 2030, which places a strong emphasis on lowering NCDs by encouraging healthy lifestyles, a balanced diet, and preventative medical care. Through the integration of education and awareness into schools, workplaces, and community settings, the program addresses a number of health domains, such as dental health, dietary habits, physical activity, and overall well-being [13, 18].

The school offers the perfect environment for oral health promotion. The school years span between childhood and puberty. These are significant developmental phases when people are developing beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors linked to oral health that will last a lifetime. Children are particularly receptive at this stage, allowing for quicker formation of lasting habits, especially when lessons are consistently reinforced throughout the academic year [19].

This study examines the impact of community-based health interventions in Diriyah, Saudi Arabia, by evaluating theHCP, which teaches middle school students healthy eating practices. Health-related behaviors, dental health, and food consumption patterns of children attending HCP-participating schools were compared with those attending non-participating schools.

Students in HCP schools reported overall better dental and general health outcomes than students in control schools. Specifically, 51% of the children from control schools and 55% of the students from HCP schools reported being in good health, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001) in both general and dental health implying better health outcomes were associated with participation in the HCP.

The success of including health education in school curricula is further supported by the improved gum and teeth health in HCP schools, with 72% reporting good or excellent gum health and 69% reporting good or excellent teeth health. This is consistent with earlier studies [20].

Although oral and general health improved in HCP schools, there was no discernible difference in dental visitation rates between the two groups. This shows that even though educational interventions had a favorable impact on students’ self-reported health outcomes, it is possible that these programs did not significantly alter the ways in which students sought healthcare, including going to the dentist.

Interventions to enhance the eating habits of primary school pupils were evaluated in two systematic reviews [21, 22], with an emphasis on lowering BMI, increasing fruit and vegetable (FV) consumption, decreasing sugar consumption, improving nutritional understanding, and reducing calorie intake. Sugar reduction was limited, but the classroom-based education successfully improved FV consumption and awareness. While cross-curricular activities and parental involvement moderately increased FV consumption and knowledge, experiential activities like as school gardens and cookery workshops were very successful [19]. Comprehensive programs that combined physical exercise and dietary education demonstrated encouraging outcomes in lowering BMI [22].

A cross-sectional study done by Asiri et al., emphasized the key role played by teachers in school-based oral health promotion (OHP) and highlighted the need for collaboration between health care professionals, educators and parents to enhance oral health education [23]. An umbrella review by Samed et al. highlighted the WHO’s Health Promoting School (HPS) framework, which categorizes school-based interventions into three components: health education, changes to the school environment, and family and community involvement. The study reported that multi-component interventions those incorporating more than two HPS components—were effective in improving adolescents’ knowledge and behaviors related to healthy eating [24]. According to a systematic review by Chatterjee et al., comprehensive school-based interventions such as nutrition education, diet counselling, physical activity, and technology-enabled behavioural communication were associated with positive outcomes in adolescents’ dietary habits, food preferences, physical activity, and overall nutritional status measured by BMI and growth references [25].

Rapid urbanization and lifestyle changes in Saudi Arabia over the last 20 years have caused a “nutrition transition,” characterized by a decrease in complex carbohydrates, dietary fiber, fruits, and vegetables and an increase in energy-dense meals high in fat and added sweets. This change has led to overnutrition and an increase in obesity-related conditions such diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular illnesses [26].

The 2013 Saudi Health Interview Survey (SHIS) offered a nationally representative examination of Saudi adults’ eating patterns. Key findings include low adherence to dietary standards, with fewer than 10% of the population meeting recommended intakes, especially for fruits, vegetables, dairy, nuts, and seafood. Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) and processed meals were widely consumed, particularly among young individuals (15–24 years old) [27].

Previous studies found that 40% of Saudi children responding to a diet questionnaire reported that they did not consume fresh fruits or vegetables every day, and only 35% of Saudi children reported that they did not drink milk every day [16]. The results of dietary intake evaluation present a complex representation of the HCP’s impact on children’s eating patterns. Across all groups, unsweetened milk emerged as the most frequently consumed nutritious food item (80% daily intake), indicating that certain healthful dietary habits may be prevalent within the community, irrespective of program participation.

Comparative analysis revealed that a significantly lower proportion of HCP students (38% and 49%) reported daily consumption of fruits and vegetables, in contrast to their control counterparts (56% and 54%) (p < 0.0001). This outcome is particularly noteworthy, given that the HCP’s primary objective was to enhance the intake of fruits, vegetables, and other nutritionally beneficial foods. The observed reduction in consumption of these food groups within HCP schools may potentially be attributed to cultural factors or accessibility challenges. These aspects necessitate further exploration and the development of more effective intervention strategies.

Previous studies have shown that Saudi children have high levels of intake of “junk food”, or foods in this category [16, 17]. In one study of Saudi children, 72% reported drinking high-sugar soft drinks more than three times per week [16], and in another study of Saudi children, 26% reported consuming sweets daily [17]. In this study, students from HCP schools and control schools had high rates of good or excellent general and oral health, but significant patterns toward higher rates of general and oral health in HCP schools were identified. However, this result did not match the results we saw from dietary patterns, where on just about every measure, HCP schools had higher prevalence of daily intake of unhealthy foods compared to control schools. Although they reported better dental and overall health outcomes, students from the Healthy City Program (HCP) schools showed a greater proportion of daily intake of unhealthy foods than students from control schools. This unexpected finding can be attributed to numerous factors. First, even though the HCP increased knowledge of healthy eating, students may still choose unhealthy foods due to social pressure or convenience. Therefore, behavior modification requires long-term reinforcement. Second, cultural norms play a major role, as fast food, candies, and sugary drinks are just a few of the meals that are highly valued in Saudi Arabian cuisine culture because they are easily obtainable and socially acceptable, particularly for younger generations. Third, in some regions, access to healthy food options may be restricted, which could prevent students from making the best decisions. Even with health education in place, peer behavior and marketing can have a significant impact on children’s food choices. Family environment has a significant impact on how children develop their eating habits. The effectiveness of school-based interventions may be hindered if parents or guardians of students do not support healthy eating at home or are inactive in the HCP. Students may not have the means or support at home to put what they learn about healthy eating into practice, even if they are taught about it at school. As a result, they may continue to eat unhealthy food.

This paradox between dietary behavior and oral health outcomes may be also due to unmeasured factors such as better oral hygiene practices, greater fluoride exposure, or reporting bias. These factors warrant further investigation through longitudinal or mixed-methods research.

The findings from our study showed that daily honey intake was high among students from HCP and control schools. Honey has a long history in Saudi Arabian trade, religion, and culture. In addition to being used extensively for nutritional purposes, honey has medical benefits and is a highly symbolic and esteemed product in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia [28].

Honey is considered a nutritious food with medicinal powers in Saudi culture, even though it falls under the “oils and sugars” group in the HFP recommendations. Despite being categorized as a sweet food, its extensive usage in traditional treatments and associated with wellbeing may lead to increased consumption rates [29].

Although the goal of HCP is to limit the consumption of foods high in sugar, honey is consumed more frequently, which may be explained by this cultural view.

The difficulty for health promotion initiatives such as the HCP is to balance evidence-based nutritional standards with cultural dietary preferences. To balance respect for cultural values with unambiguous messages about moderation, future interventions may need to consider how foods that are culturally significant, such as honey, are framed within health education.

Students enrolled in the HCP and control groups had no discernible differences in mean BMI. Likewise, there was no significant difference in the mean scores for healthy and poor eating behaviors between the two groups. This absence of statistical significance aligns with previous research [7–30] suggesting that short-term health interventions do not significantly impact dietary habits and Body Mass Index (BMI) scores, particularly in adolescent populations where dietary and lifestyle modifications may be more challenging to implement within brief temporal parameters [9, 31]. The HCP group’s somewhat better healthy eating score, however, would point to a preliminary upward trend that could signal a slow but steady behavioral change brought on by program involvement.

The HCP group exhibited significantly lower mean DMFT scores, and BOP compared to the control group. These findings indicate a substantial positive effect of HCP on oral health outcomes, corroborating evidence from previous studies in which health-promoting programs significantly reduced caries prevalence and improved periodontal health [18, 19].

An unhealthy diet exhibits a positive correlation with both DMFT and BMI, suggesting that poor nutritional habits not only exacerbate dental issues but also contribute to elevated BMI levels. This finding aligns with previous research, which demonstrated that diets high in processed foods and sugars were associated with increased body weight and a higher incidence of dental caries in pediatric populations (19.20). No significant correlation was observed between the healthy diet score and either DMFT or BMI. This observation may indicate that the consumption of nutritious foods alone might be insufficient to reduce caries or influence BMI without a corresponding reduction in unhealthy dietary components [31].

The observed differences between HCP and Control schools suggest that participation in the Healthy City Program may contribute positively to oral health outcomes, as evidenced by lower DMFT scores and reduced bleeding on probing among HCP students. However, the absence of a corresponding improvement in dietary scores highlights an important discrepancy in program impact. This may reflect an imbalance in emphasis between oral health education and nutrition education within the HCP framework.

Informal review of program materials and stakeholder input indicate that HCP schools received more structured and frequent oral health promotion activities (e.g., supervised toothbrushing, dental screening camps), whereas nutritional interventions were often generalized and lacked focused behavioral engagement. This differential program intensity could explain why improvements in oral hygiene indicators were not mirrored by significant dietary behavior changes. Furthermore, while some associations—such as between unhealthy diet and DMFT or BMI—were statistically significant, the effect sizes were small (e.g., r = 0.074), suggesting that the clinical relevance at the individual level is limited. Nonetheless, such findings can still carry public health relevance, particularly when extrapolated to large school-going populations. Interventions that simultaneously target both oral hygiene and dietary behaviors are essential for achieving holistic health outcomes.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that students participating in the Health City Program (HCP) exhibited significantly lower odds of developing dental caries.

The educational emphasis of the HCP likely promotes preventive behaviors such as regular tooth brushing, a finding corroborated by our logistic regression analysis, which indicated that brushing twice daily or more frequently had a protective effect against caries. This observation supports the well-established importance of oral hygiene in caries prevention, as evidenced by previous research [21, 22]. Consistent with earlier studies [9–21] that associated sugar consumption with an increased risk of dental caries, it is noteworthy that an unhealthy diet was correlated with higher odds of developing dental caries. Age, gender, and Body Mass Index (BMI), however, did not emerge as significant predictors of caries risk, potentially suggesting that dietary and hygiene practices exerted a more direct influence on caries risk than demographic or general health characteristics [9, 32, 33].

The study has many strengths. One of its main advantages is its thorough design, which assesses a variety of health factors such as general health, oral health, dietary habits, and BMI. Through the integration of self-reported questionnaire results with objective clinical evaluations like Bleeding on Probing (BOP) and DMFT (Decayed, Missing, Filled Teeth), the study guarantees a comprehensive and multifaceted assessment of the Healthy City Program (HCP). Another noteworthy aspect of the sampling approach is the large sample size of 1,200 students, which reduces bias and provides statistical power. Matching the age and gender of the intervention (HCP) and control groups guarantees comparability, which enhances the validity of the results.

The study has its limitations, It is important to note that the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference. No baseline (pre-intervention) data were available for either group. Although associations between HCP participation and improved oral health indicators were observed, we cannot conclusively attribute these outcomes to program exposure. Temporal sequencing and long-term impact cannot be determined. Future research employing longitudinal or controlled interventional designs will be necessary to assess the sustained effectiveness of HCP activities across domains of child health. The study employed purposive sampling to select schools and quota sampling to ensure equal distribution of participants by age and gender across HCP and control groups. While this approach ensured demographic comparability, the non-probabilistic nature of the sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Another limitation is that the participating students were not matched or controlled for socioeconomic status, parental education, or urban/rural residence. These factors are known to significantly influence both oral and nutritional health outcomes and may have introduced residual confounding in the comparisons. A key limitation of this study is that clustering effects at the school level were not accounted for in the analysis. Although students were nested within schools, we did not use multilevel modeling or generalized estimating equations (GEE) to adjust for potential intra-cluster correlation. This may have led to an underestimation of standard errors and overstatement of statistical significance. Future studies should incorporate multilevel approaches to more accurately account for clustered data structures. The use of self-reported dietary information raises the possibility of recall and social desirability bias. A more accurate estimate could be obtained using objective dietary indicators, including meal diaries or 24-hour recalls. The dietary assessment was based on a newly developed tool grounded in HFP guidelines rather than a previously validated instrument. Although the tool was content-validated and pilot-tested, the absence of established external validation limits comparability with other studies. The study did not control for important confounders such as socioeconomic status, parental education, or urban–rural residence. These factors may independently influence dietary patterns, BMI, and oral health status.

Conclusion

Students’ self-reported oral and general health demonstrated significant improvement following participation in the HCP. The HCP exhibited a positive impact on oral health outcomes, as evidenced by significantly lower mean DMFT and BOP scores. However, it did not demonstrate clear superiority over control schools in terms of the consumption of essential nutritious foods, and its influence on promoting healthier eating habits yielded mixed results. The observed association between unhealthy dietary habits and both DMFT and BMI indicates broader health implications associated with poor eating patterns, despite the absence of substantial differences in BMI between groups. These findings suggest that while HCPs can enhance health outcomes, targeted interventions may be necessary to promote the adoption of healthier eating habits among middle school students. School-based health policies should incorporate comprehensive nutrition education alongside oral health promotion to address both behaviors in an integrated manner. Embedding such interventions within the school curriculum and involving parents and food service providers could enhance their reach and sustainability. Future research using longitudinal designs and objective dietary assessments is recommended to evaluate the long-term impact of such programs on students’ overall health trajectories.
