Health workers’ perspectives on barriers and opportunities to optimising quality improvement implementation in urban health facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi
Authors: Wiza Kumwenda, Angela M Bengtson, Shaphil Wallie, Jimmy Ba Villiera, Agatha K Bula, Edith Ngoma, Mina Hosseinipour, Victor Mwapasa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEliminating vertical HIV transmission requires high-quality Option B+ services, which provide lifelong antiretroviral therapy to pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV. In Malawi, coverage has expanded, but quality remains suboptimal, contributing to challenges in sustaining engagement in care. Quality improvement (QI) approaches can strengthen service delivery and evidence-based practices, with healthcare workers (HCWs) central to implementation. This study explores barriers and opportunities HCWs face in implementing QI in Option B+ within high-volume urban facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological qualitative study was conducted between March and June 2021 in five health facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi. Semistructured in-depth interviews were conducted with 30 purposively selected HCWs (4–8 per facility) involved in delivering Option B+ services. Thematic analysis was used, with data coded deductively and inductively. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research was applied to map the identified themes into relevant domains and constructs.ResultsQI practices among HCWs were hindered by resource constraints, lack of incentives/reimbursement for QI activities conducted beyond working hours, poor communication between QI teams and implementers and resistance or negative attitudes towards QI. Opportunities to enhance QI included increasing meeting frequency, providing mentorship, peer-to-peer learning through exchange visits and securing funding from development partners.ConclusionImproving QI utilisation in Option B+ requires mobilising resources and having a structured feedback and mentorship mechanisms. Targeted incentives/reimbursements for HCWs and peer-to-peer learning through exchange visits between facilities can further enhance QI. Therefore, a blended QI approach incorporating these recommendations is needed to better support HCWs in delivering evidence-based interventions, strengthen the health system and may improve engagement outcomes.Keywords: Quality improvement, Healthcare quality improvement, Maternal Health Services, Qualitative research, Implementation science

Introduction

Malawi has made notable progress towards eliminating vertical transmission of HIV (EVT), largely driven by widespread availability of Option B+ services.1 These services offer lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) to pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV (PWLHIV), regardless of disease stage.1 It also assures integrated HIV testing and counselling within antenatal care, prophylaxis for HIV-exposed infants and follow-up of the mother–infant pair for up to 2 years or until the child’s HIV status is confirmed.2 These interventions are expected to reduce vertical transmission rates to 2%–5% or lower.3 However, the country narrowly missed the target in 2023, mainly due to the disengagement of PWLHIV in Option B+. Addressing this disengagement requires strategies that enhance the quality of Option B+ implementation and, in turn, support the attainment of the Sustainable Development Goal of EVT by 2030.4 5

Quality is a fundamental pillar of an effective health system,6 7 encompassing dimensions such as safety, effectiveness, patient-centred care, timeliness, integration, efficiency and equity.8 9 However, in resource-limited settings, health service quality remains suboptimal.10,12 This is reflected in poor patient retention and insufficient coordination across care platforms, including those delivering Option B+ services.12 Quality improvement (QI) approaches offer a structured pathway to enhance service quality across all levels of the health system.13 They can also facilitate the adoption of evidence-based practices,14 15 such as monitoring the engagement of PWLHIV in the cascade of Option B+.

Recognising QI as a critical strategy for health system strengthening, Malawi established a Quality Management Directorate in 2017 and deployed QI focal persons at all health system levels, including health facilities.16 Despite these initiatives, QI implementation in Option B+ services remains underexplored. This study investigates the barriers and opportunities influencing QI utilisation among healthcare workers (HCWs) providing Option B+ services in high-volume urban facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi.

Methods

Design, setting and participants

This analysis is part of a descriptive, phenomenological qualitative study, which explored HCWs’ practices in monitoring women’s engagement in Option B+ across five high-volume urban health facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi. The study was conducted between March and June 2021. A detailed description of the full study procedures is published elsewhere.17

Briefly, the five study sites serve a combined catchment population of over 700 000 people, including more than 50 000 pregnant women and approximately 2000 women living with HIV served annually.18,20 Option B+ services are delivered at multiple service points within each facility, including antenatal clinics, maternity clinics, HIV testing services, ART clinics and under-5 clinics. These facilities experience high rates of loss to follow-up and suboptimal monitoring of engagement in HIV services.21 22

A total of 30 HCWs participated in the parent study, including nurses, clinicians, data personnel and health surveillance assistants/counsellors working in clinics providing Option B+services. The same participants served as informants for this analysis, with the focus on their interview responses to questions about perceived barriers and opportunities for implementing QI initiatives.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected through semistructured, in-depth interviews using a pilot-tested interview guide that included a section on QI, which explored HCWs’ perspectives on QI workflow, challenges and opportunities. Interviews were conducted by the first author (WK) and a trained bilingual research assistant (EN), in either English or Chichewa based on the participant’s preference. All interviews were audio-recorded with written informed consent from participants.

Facility QI workflow

Each facility typically had either a designated QI focal person or a Quality Improvement Support Team (QIST), depending on its size, to oversee QI activities. The QIST, led by the focal person, generally comprised departmental heads and was responsible for supporting or directly leading Work Improvement Teams, also referred to as the lead QI teams. HCWs either volunteered or were assigned to these teams to implement service-specific QI initiatives, including those related to Option B+.

QI projects were commonly initiated through one of three pathways: (1) routine supervisory visits by district or national-level teams; (2) initiatives led by development partners providing technical support to the Ministry of Health or (3) internal facility-led data reviews. The QI approach used for each project was typically determined by the initiating body or based on methods already familiar to facility staff. Common QI methods included, but were not limited to, PDSA cycles, Lean/Six Sigma, performance benchmarking, clinic audits and the Model for Improvement.

Tools used to document QI projects varied depending on the approach employed and ranged from structured QI journals aligned with specific methodologies to unstructured notebooks. The lead QI team was responsible for developing, monitoring and documenting QI projects. Progress was reviewed in QI meetings, although there was no standard schedule for their frequency or timing.

Guiding conceptual framework

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)23 was used as an organising framework to categorise reported challenges and opportunities to QI practice among HCWs. This meta-theory framework systematically identifies multilevel factors that affect the implementation of a programme innovation. It consists of the following five domains (table 1): intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, individual characteristics and the implementation process.23 24

Data analysis

Thematic content analysis25 was employed using deductive and inductive approaches, supported by NVivo and Microsoft Excel. First, the researchers (WK and JBV) read the transcripts repeatedly to familiarise themselves with the content. Second, they conducted deductive coding based on interview guides, and then the codebook was refined inductively to ensure inclusion, identifying emerging themes, which were done in NVivo. Thereafter, barriers and facilitators were mapped into relevant CFIR constructs and domains using Excel. The research team then interpreted the frequency and emphasis of participants’ statements to reflect their relative influence on QI implementation. Data coding and interpretation were conducted independently by the first author and a clinician (male), a master-trained clinician who was not involved in data collection or cleaning. Differences in interpretation were resolved through discussion. A senior qualitative expert (female)—a director of nursing and qualitative research unit with a doctoral degree—reviewed and validated the final CFIR-based codes, themes and interpretations.

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, proportions, means with SD and/or medians with IQRs) were used to summarise participant characteristics.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Barriers and opportunities to QI practice in Option B+

The analysis identified 11 of the 39 CFIR constructs influencing QI practices in Option B+ across four domains—four as barriers, three as facilitators and four as both (see table 3). Key barriers were resource constraints, restrictive implementation climate and inadequate QI meeting attendance. Opportunities highlighted by HCWs included capacity building through mentorship and peer exchange, leadership support in protecting time and mobilising resources and leveraging development partner support.

Inner setting

Availability of resources

The majority of HCWs cited resource constraints as a barrier to QI implementation. Some noted that competing clinical duties limited time for meetings or tasks, while many reported inadequate funding for implementing change ideas and basic operational needs such as printing or graphing tools. As one noted

In Quality Improvement we need a journal and we need to print it, and we depend on partners so that we can print in their offices because we don’t have resources to print the journals. So, things like those used for plotting graphs… also the issue of allowances. For instance, it is not easy to conduct a meeting with nurses and doctors without such—Data personnel, 35 years, Site 3

Some HCWs further highlighted that having funds for incentives, refreshments and transport reimbursements in respect to QI activities (QI meeting or those that used their own transport for QI activities) would promote and sustain utilisation of QI, as it addresses both motivational and QI practical needs.

Access to knowledge and information

The majority of HCWs described having QI expertise was essential for successful project implementation. However, some mentioned that frequent staff rotations often leave only a few individuals with the necessary QI competencies (application of QI methodology). To address this, some HCWs suggested having QI mentors, while others recommended peer-to-peer QI learning through exchange visits between facilities, as depicted below:

Secondly, some providers are trained but they don’t have enough QI experience, and to attain such experience that means we always need to have a mentor available. For example, at antenatal clinic, we need to have a mentor to be mentoring our providers—Clinician, 35 years, Site 4

We need to sometimes go somewhere to see how other people are using QI… You are able to see where you are not implementing it well—Nurse, 49 years, Site 5

Implementation climate

The ability to test and implement change ideas was described as essential to QI, but many HCWs found their context restrictive due to limited resources. This was perceived as demotivating and discouraged active participation in QI, as reported below.

We need to revamp or strengthen the quality improvement because currently, it’s weak. I shouldn’t hide it; it’s weak. There should be support from the district and partners. When we discuss the same problems month after month without action on solutions, it demoralizes the team. People think, ‘We're just going to discuss the same issues again, moreover, they don’t act on solutions.’ We need actions for problems that can’t be managed at the facility level and require intervention from partners… Otherwise, if nothing changes, people stop participating in quality improvement meetings—Nurse, 46 years, Site 4

On the other hand, some HCWs emphasised that proactively identifying QI gaps within their facilities should be a priority rather than relying on external stakeholders, as expressed by the Nurse below:

We need to be proactive. We shouldn’t wait for the national supervisor to come to identify the gaps for us. We should be able to identify the gaps at least ourselves—Nurse, 40 years, Site 1

Leadership support

The majority of HCWs felt that the leadership could do more to advance implementation of QI. For example, setting protected time for QI activities or lobbying for resources beyond facility’s scope.

The challenge is that QI project meetings occur during working days when the facility is very busy. So, we need to balance seeing patients as well as attending the QI project meetings… To have full attendance of these QI projects, there should be a directive from the senior leadership to exempt the team from work maybe from 2 p.m. so that they should attend to the QI meetings. If you can go by that direction maybe it could work—Clinician, 37 years, Site 1

Characteristics of individuals

Individual stage of change

Some HCWs described resistance to change and poor work attitude were a hindrance to QI implementation despite recognising that a key characteristic of QI is continuous testing to ascertain impact. Change was often seen as additional work and therefore difficult to accept in already demanding settings, with staff attitudes and varying levels of commitment identified as the main challenges to implementation.

Change is always difficult, especially when it comes to quality improvement, as it brings more changes and additional work for the staff. The biggest challenge is the staff’s attitude—Nurse, 35 years, Site 4

Mostly the challenge is us workers. Some are really committed while others are not committed to see through changes. QI requires people to be united, but the main challenge is the attitude of staff, that’s what I feel it’s a big challenge. Sometimes we face shortages of tools, but that cannot stop us from doing our work. Mainly, I feel it’s us the staff who make the work go well or not—HSA/Counsellor, 46 years, Site 5

Knowledge and belief about the intervention

Some HCWs highlighted the importance of having a strong dedicated team, leading the implementation of QI activities. They believed that having a QI leading team member with strong motivation and persistence in nature to drive QI efforts was essential to achieving QI goals.

It is not just about having the meetings but also to see how we can sustain it. The other thing is that the leading team in QI should be committed to facilitate the meetings and they should be able to follow up on the activities to see how they are being implemented—Nurse, 53 years, Site 3

Some further noted that improving the attitudes of team members resistant to QI was necessary, suggesting strategies such as incentives, assigning tasks or small rewards.

It simply needs people who are committed and available for the work… I can say there is need to recognize by giving tasks to each person in the group so that everyone should feel important. Sometimes providing refreshments during meetings can be motivating… I cannot talk about allowances due to funding…—Nurse, 49 years, Site 5

Some HCWs questioned the value of QI, particularly when feedback and ideas were not acted on. This lack of response over time led to perceptions that the process had limited benefit, reducing engagement. For example, one HCW noted that we discuss the same problems month after month without action, leading to a sense that the meetings lacked purpose. While this quote is reflected under Implementation Climate, it also illustrates how repeated inaction shaped HCWs’ beliefs about the usefulness of QI efforts.

Implementation process

Planning

Some HCWs expressed a need for clearer guidance on QI, suggesting the development of standard operating procedures (SOPs) and mechanisms to improve communication (like setting reminders and circulating agendas before the meeting).

We should have standard operating procedures for QI which should be put in all duty stations, so that staff should refer to see what they are supposed to do. We should also have representatives from the District level during the QI meeting because there are some issues that cannot be resolved at facility level—Nurse, 40 years, Site 2

Engagement

Many HCWs observed that enthusiasm for QI activities (QI meeting or QI tasks) often faded over time, especially in the absence of incentives or support for participation during off-hours.

The challenges we face are that, most of the time, on the days we meet, some important people who are expected to attend are not available to provide feedback… As a result, we end up discussing other issues. Additionally, since there are no incentives, and meetings often take place on weekends when many people do not come to work, some are unable to attend due to transport issues—Data personnel, 41 years, Site 2

Execution

Some HCWs recommended improving the scheduling and organisation of QI meetings. Others noted that long intervals between meetings hindered monitoring and continuity.

I believe we need to improve our meeting schedule. Quarterly meetings might cause us to forget important events. Instead, we should have monthly meetings to review ongoing issues and prepare for the quarterly meetings. This way, everyone will be aware of the agenda in advance, making it easier to provide relevant information—Nurse, 30 years, Site 3

Reflecting and evaluating

Some HCWs reported poor communication stemming from a lack of timely review and feedback on QI activities, both within the QI leading team and between the team and frontline implementers. They noted that delayed or absent updates reduced interest and participation in QI efforts.

Like the project where I was taking part in quality improvement, there was a lack of implementation of meetings. We could agree that ‘let us meet on this day,’ but you would see that only two or four people were present, which was even less than the quorum. So, we were canceling the meeting for that day. Thus, no updates on the progress of QI activities, just natural death. That was the challenge—Nurse, 49 years, Site 5

Outer setting

External policy and incentive

Most of the HCWs viewed partner organisations as central to driving QI efforts and suggested lobbying or submitting proposals to sustain support. However, a few described that reliance on external partners was seen as a risk, with transitions sometimes stalling ongoing activities. One HCW described how a partner change disrupted QI progress:

Our last QI meeting was conducted a while ago. We were working on the project of linking those who tested positive to care and focusing on viral suppression, but now the activities are on hold or a break… We are not meeting, performance has not been measured… Our previous partner organization that was supporting us is gone. The new partner organization is taking over. There have been no meetings or projects. We will see how things unfold, considering different organizations have got different priorities—Nurse, 46 years, Site 4

Additionally, some HCWs reported that the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted QI activities.

Discussion

This study explored barriers and opportunities to QI practice among frontline HCWs delivering Option B+ services in high-volume urban health facilities in Lilongwe, Malawi. Reported barriers included resource constraints, poor communication, inconsistent attendance at QI meetings, resistance to change and the absence of incentives or reimbursement. Opportunities for improvement included resource mobilisation, enhanced QI meeting scheduling, increased engagement and ownership, development of SOPs, strengthened mentorship and collaboration, and leveraging support from development partners. The findings revealed the challenges and opportunities across four CFIR domains: inner setting, outer setting, characteristics of individuals and process.

Frontline HCWs reported ongoing resource constraints, which included limited funding, time and capacity to test or implement change ideas, consistent with findings from other studies.26 27 These limitations hindered innovation and often led to repeated use of the same change ideas. Such repetition reduced the perceived value of QI and risked diminishing HCWs’ motivation and confidence, ultimately threatening sustained engagement of HCWs.28 29 Strengthening QI requires consistent funding, which may be supported through evidence-based financial models such as bundled payments or pay-for-success approaches that link funding to outcomes.30 Beyond financial investment, low-cost strategies like regular mentorship, strong leadership and clear SOPs can improve coordination, accountability and resource use.

The lack of both financial and non-financial incentives emerged as a contributor to low participation in QI activities, supporting previous findings.29 31 This barrier spanned the individual and inner setting domains, underscoring the need to recognise and reward engagement in QI. While financial incentives can promote positive behaviour change and improve quality of care, they may also lead to unintended outcomes or unethical behaviour.32 33 In this study, the absence of personal reimbursements discouraged involvement. Although performance-based reimbursements have documented benefits,34,36 we did not identify studies that specifically address reimbursement for personal resource use in QI activities. This gap points to the need for fair and transparent reimbursement mechanisms that acknowledge individual contributions while safeguarding against misuse.

Effective communication is critical to QI practice, enabling shared understanding among those leading and delivering change and fostering broader adoption.37 Poor communication among HCWs was identified as a barrier to QI, in line with previous research.38,40 A structured communication mechanism is necessary to facilitate information flow between QI leaders and implementers, ensuring clarity and engagement. This should include defined channels, regular feedback loops and mechanisms for reporting project impact.41 42 As noted by West et al, when team members participate actively in decision-making and share information openly, they are more likely to support team goals, improve collaboration and contribute innovative ideas.43 Leadership support is essential to create a culture of open communication, ultimately sustaining interest and participation in QI projects.

Several opportunities were identified for strengthening QI practice, consistent with findings from other studies. These included HCW engagement and ownership,44 mentorship and collaboration,45 and partner organisation support.31 Incentives and reimbursements were seen as valuable motivators for HCW engagement to QI implementation.46 Continued partner support was also emphasised as vital in resource-limited settings. Mentorship and collaboration were considered essential for building QI capacity through ongoing skill development. This supports previous studies that have pointed out the limitations of relying solely on didactic training in resource-constrained environments, where practical follow-up and support are often lacking.47,49 Another suggested opportunity was the establishment of QI SOPs, this could foster a shared understanding and a consistent approach to QI initiatives. SOPs are well-recognised tools for improving quality to delivered tasks, this includes tasks related to QI.50 51 Building on these opportunities, strategies that may sustain engagement include involving staff in decision-making about QI priorities, providing regular feedback on progress, recognising contributions and creating peer learning opportunities. This also underscores the need for the Malawi Ministry of Health to finalise the 2019 healthcare QI guideline, which can clarify some of the identified barriers and opportunities.

Optimising QI meeting schedules to maintain momentum was also emphasised. Shorter intervals between meetings were recommended, aligning with findings from Hill et al, who noted that frequent meetings positively impact continuous QI practice.52 Beyond scheduling, training in meeting organisation could improve participation and problem-solving. O’Dea et al note that understanding how to plan and conduct meetings enhances doctors’ participation, a principle that can also apply to all HCWs.53 Integrating practical engagement strategies with mentorship, SOPs and well-structured meetings may help maintain motivation, ownership and continuity of QI initiatives in resource-limited settings.

Overall, the CFIR framework illustrates how inner setting, outer setting, individual characteristics and process domains shape barriers and opportunities in QI. These findings highlight the convergence of contextual and individual factors that either constrain or enable implementation. Aligning structural support, motivation and process facilitation is critical, consistent with evidence on the importance of leadership, engagement and context-specific strategies.26 54

Limitations

The study was limited to HCWs working in Option B+, future studies need to explore all HCWs to comprehensively understand facility QI practice. A further limitation is that the study did not formally assess the feasibility, affordability, effectiveness, acceptability, side effects or equity of the identified opportunities using structured frameworks such as Appropriateness, Practicality, Effectiveness, Affordability, Side-effects, Equity. While the findings highlight potential strategies to strengthen QI practice, future research could apply such criteria to guide the selection and implementation of the most practical and equitable interventions. Additionally, the study was conducted in high-volume urban clinics in Lilongwe, Malawi; results may not be representative of other primary care settings. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute to the sparse literature on QI practice in resource-constrained, high-volume facilities and can inform future efforts to strengthen QI strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights key barriers and facilitators to QI implementation for Option B+ in high-volume urban health facilities in Malawi. Frontline healthcare workers face structural challenges, including limited resources, inconsistent leadership engagement and weak feedback loops. At the same time, they identified actionable opportunities such as strengthening peer mentorship, improving the structure of QI meetings and developing standard operating procedures. These findings suggest that a blended QI approach incorporating these elements could enhance the delivery of evidence-based care, improve healthcare worker engagement and strengthen overall health system performance. Further research is needed to explore scalable strategies for sustaining QI practices across diverse healthcare settings.
