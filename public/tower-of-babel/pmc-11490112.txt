Correlation between CT-based phenotypes and serum biomarker in interstitial lung diseases
Authors: Beomsu Shin, You Jin Oh, Jonghun Kim, Sung Goo Park, Kyung Soo Lee, Ho Yun Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe quantitative analysis of computed tomography (CT) and Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) serum level has gained importance in the diagnosis, monitoring, and prognostication of interstitial lung disease (ILD). However, the associations between quantitative analysis of CT and serum KL-6 level remain poorly understood.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study conducted at tertiary hospital between June 2020 and March 2022, quantitative analysis of CT was performed using the deep learning-based method including reticulation, ground glass opacity (GGO), honeycombing, and consolidation. We investigated the associations between CT-based phenotypes and serum KL-6 measured within three months of the CT scan. Furthermore, we evaluated the performance of the combined CT-based phenotypes and KL-6 levels in predicting hospitalizations due to respiratory reasons of ILD patients.ResultsA total of 131 ILD patients (104 males) with a median age of 67 years were included in this study. Reticulation, GGO, honeycombing, and consolidation extents showed a positive correlation with KL-6 levels. [Reticulation, correlation coefficient (r) = 0.567, p < 0.001; GGO, r = 0.355, p < 0.001; honeycombing, r = 0.174, p = 0.046; and consolidation, r = 0.446, p < 0.001]. Additionally, the area under the ROC of the combined reticulation and KL-6 for hospitalizations due to respiratory reasons was 0.810 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsQuantitative analysis of CT features and serum KL-6 levels ascertained a positive correlation between the two. In addition, the combination of reticulation and KL-6 shows potential for predicting hospitalizations of ILD patients due to respiratory causes. The combination of reticulation, focusing on phenotypic change in lung parenchyma, and KL-6, as an indicator of lung injury extent, could be helpful for monitoring and predicting the prognosis of various types of ILD. Background

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) encompasses a group of heterogeneous lung disorders. The disease course of ILD varies widely, ranging from stable or slow decline to rapid deterioration, depending on the severity of inflammation and progressive fibrosis of the interstitium [1]. Thus, early diagnosis and accurate assessment of ILD severity are crucial for initiating treatment, monitoring disease progression, and predicting prognosis [2].

In this regard, pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and computed tomography (CT) have classically been two key methods for identification of early ILD and assessment of its severity and progression [3]. However, PFTs may be limited by insufficient respiratory effort particularly in patients with severe ILD [4]. In terms of CT assessment, different CT protocols (slice thickness, spatial resolution, or acquisition time) or CT vendors as well as inter-observer variability are obstacles for quantification, which may lead to an inability to capture subtle changes in ILD extent [5]. There is a growing need for new techniques or biomarkers that can overcome the limitations of traditional methods such as PFTs and CT assessment [6].

The CT quantitative analysis, defined as quantification of overall lung histogram features, regional CT density changes, parenchymal texture features and other assessments by advanced algorithms including unsupervised machine learning and deep learning approaches to image analysis, has the potential to standardize and advance the role of CT in ILD [7]. This analysis can be applied in clinical practice for detecting ILD, assessing disease progression, and stratifying prognosis in various types of ILD [8].

Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) is a high-molecular-weight transmembrane mucoprotein primarily expressed on bronchiolar epithelial cells and type II pneumocytes in alveoli, particularly in proliferating and regenerating type II pneumocytes [9]. KL-6 release is believed to occur as a result of lung injury, including damage and destruction of alveolar epithelial cells [10]. Repeated epithelial injury, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and myofibroblast activation are important factors in the initiation of fibrosis [11]. Elevated KL-6 is associated with both lung fibrosis and inflammation, and which has been linked to worse outcomes, prognoses, and mortality in ILD patients [12]. Serum KL-6 measurement is widely utilized for detecting ILD, assessing disease activity, and predicting prognosis in various types of ILD [13].

Although both quantitative CT analysis and KL-6 level determination have been associated with ILD outcomes, few studies have investigated their association and combinational predictive potential, especially according to heterogeneous ILD subtypes [14, 15]. This study aimed to assess the extent of specific CT-based phenotypes and to examine their correlation with serum KL-6 levels, as well as data from PFT in various ILD patients. Additionally, we planned to examine whether the CT-based phenotype measurements combined with KL-6 level can predict respiratory hospitalizations in ILD patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

We screened patients diagnosed with ILD at Samsung Changwon Hospital between June 2020 and March 2022. A total of 248 patients with baseline CT images at the time of ILD diagnosis were initially considered. Of these, 117 were excluded because of inadequate CT images (lack of volumetric images or thin section images less than 2.5 mm) for assessments (n = 6), absence of baseline lung function data (n = 32), presence of pneumonia or acute exacerbation as defined by a previous study (n = 63), and presence of lung cancer (n = 16), which could have confounded the analysis. The remaining 131 ILD patients were included in this study (Fig. 1a). This retrospective study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Changwon Hospital (approval SCMC 2022-05-010), and patient consent was not required as the data were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis.

Data collection

The collected information included age, gender, smoking history, body mass index (BMI), PFTs, KL-6 level, and CT-related parameters. All clinical and laboratory parameters were obtained within three months of the CT date as in previous study [14]. PFTs were performed according to ERS/ATS guidelines, and results were expressed as the percentage of predicted forced vital capacity (FVC %) and the percentage of diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) [16]. Patients were regularly followed up every 3 – 6 months in accordance with clinical practice guidelines [3]. Respiratory hospitalizations that occurred during the study period were further investigated, and related definitions were referred to previous studies [17–19]. Patients who were planned to be hospitalized for diagnostic procedures were excluded.

Diagnostic criteria of ILDs

The diagnosis of ILD was established through multidisciplinary discussion of medical history, clinical data, and imaging results. The specific ILD subtypes, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE), idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonia (iNSIP), cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), and unclassifiable ILD, were based on the international guidelines [3, 20–24]. CPFE was defined as the coexistence of both pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema in CT as mentioned in the research definition of previous study [21].

Connective tissue disease (CTD) was characterized by the presence of features indicating a systemic autoimmune process that fulfilled the standard diagnostic criteria for differentiated CTDs, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), Sjögren’s syndrome (SjS), systemic sclerosis (SSc), polymyositis and dermatomyositis (PM/DM), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and mixed CVD [25]. The diagnostic criteria for interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features incorporate a combination of features from clinical, serologic, and morphologic domains [26].

Measurement of KL-6

Serum KL-6 level was measured using the Nanopia KL-6 assay (Sekisui Medical, Tokyo, Japan) using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method. Blood samples were obtained by venipuncture. On evaluation, patients were categorized into two groups according to serum KL-6 level, with a cut-off of 500 U/mL as recommended by the previous studies [27, 28].

CT acquisition and ILD quantification

CT scans were performed on two different 128-channel multi-slice CT scanner (Somatom Definition AS + or Somatom Definition Flash, Siemens Healthcare, Munich, Germany). Patients were placed in a supine position, and images were obtained with a voltage of 120 kVp and a slice thickness of 1–2 mm. With the Siemens Healthcare scanner, B40f, B45f, and B50f kernels were used. When those target kernel images were not available, original CT slices were converted into the target CT style using the routable generative adversarial network (RouteGAN) [29, 30].

Volumetric analysis was performed with pre-contrast images. First, whole-lung volume was automatically calculated by the recognition of lung parenchyma, excluding pulmonary vessels or airways. After an overall analysis of the whole lung, each texture pattern analysis was completed separately. Texture patterns included emphysema, ground-glass opacities (GGO), reticulation, consolidation, and honeycombing. The absolute volume and percentage of the total lung volume affected were calculated for each texture (Fig. 1b). Representative cases are presented in detail in the images (Fig. 2) and videos in the Appendices (Supplementary Video 1). In early stages of ILD, fibrosis presents as reticular opacities on CT images. If airway dilatation and collapse of fibrotic alveoli progress, the areas show honeycombing [31]. Automatic segmentation of texture patterns was precisely re-assessed by the thoracic radiologist (H.Y.L., 20 years of experience). The entire quantitative process of kernel conversion, lung segmentation, and texture analysis was performed using commercial software based on a deep-learning algorithm (Aview, Coreline Soft, Seoul, South Korea).

On evaluation, fibrosis extent was defined as the sum of reticulation and honeycombing, and ILD extent was the sum of reticulation, honeycombing, and GGO extents as in previous study [32].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analyses between KL-6 level and various variables were performed using linear regression based on the Pearson’s correlation and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. The performance of the combined CT variables and KL-6 levels in predicting hospitalizations of ILD patients due to respiratory reasons was evaluated using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0, IBM Corp., 2018, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 131 patients with ILD, 48 patients had IPF, 30 patients had CTD-ILD, and 24 patients had CPFE. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 67 years, 79% were male, and 79% were ever-smokers. The median FVC and DLCO were 80% and 67%, respectively. The median KL-6 value was 601 U/mL. The median KL-6 levels according to ILD clinical subtypes are shown in the Appendices (Supplementary Table 1). The median follow-up period was 13 months.

Correlation between serum KL-6 and quantitative CT-based phenotypes

Serum KL-6 level was correlated with each CT variable and PFT. KL-6 showed a positive correlation with reticulation (Pearson’s r = 0.567, p < 0.001), GGO (r = 0.355, p < 0.001), honeycombing (r = 0.174, p = 0.046), and consolidation (r = 0.446, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Conversely, KL-6 was inversely correlated with lung volume (r =-0.350, p < 0.001), FVC (r =-0.457, p < 0.001) and DLCO (r =-0.409, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the CT variables, reticulation showed the moderate positive correlation with KL-6, followed by consolidation and GGO. Honeycombing correlated weakly with KL-6.

Comparison of CT characteristics according to KL-6

CT variables (reticulation, GGO, honeycombing, and consolidation) were analyzed according to ILD extent (< 5% or ≥ 5%), fibrosis extent (< 5% or ≥ 5%), and KL-6 (< 500 or ≥ 500). The results are presented in Tables 2 and 3. The reticulation extent was significantly higher in patients with KL-6 ≥ 500 than in those with KL-6 < 500 at ILD extent ≥ 5% (8.03% vs. 4.60%, p < 0.001). Similarly, the reticulation extent was also significantly higher in patients with KL-6 ≥ 500 than in those with KL-6 < 500 at fibrosis extent ≥ 5% (8.88% vs. 5.47%, p < 0.001). In addition, GGO extent was significantly higher in patients with KL-6 ≥ 500 than in those with KL-6 < 500 at fibrosis extent < 5% (1.59% vs. 0.49%, p = 0.030).

Predictive values for respiratory hospitalizations

Figure 4 illustrates the ROC curve demonstrating the predictive power of combined reticulation and KL-6 for respiratory-related hospitalizations in patients with ILD. The area under the curve value was 0.810 (95% confidence interval, 0.646 – 0.973, p = 0.001). In addition, the area under the curve value for the predictive power of KL-6 alone for respiratory-related hospitalizations in patients with ILD was 0.715 (95% confidence interval, 0.527 – 0.903, p = 0.024).

Discussion

In this observational study, the correlations were analyzed between serum KL-6 and CT-based texture features related to ILD, including reticulation, GGO, honeycombing, and consolidation. Among the CT features, reticulation showed the moderate positive correlation with KL-6. Additionally, combined reticulation and KL-6 showed satisfactory ability to predict respiratory hospitalizations in ILD patients.

ILD is a chronic progressive disease, and current evaluation methods have limitations in capturing its dynamic course [1]. To address these unmet needs, representative studies related to CT and its assessment have been conducted [33, 34]. Many studies focusing on quantitative CT analysis have demonstrated the potential use of ILD severity for prognostication and mortality prediction [35]. Such analysis is typically applied with texture-based and computer vision-based approaches, which were employed to characterize, model, and process imaging features that simulate human visual perceptual and learning processes using two dimensional or volumetric classification algorithms. Various imaging analysis tools, such as the adaptive multiple feature method (AMFM), computer-aided lung informatics for pathology evaluation and rating (CALIPER), or Aview, facilitated the assessment of texture features, allowing density and morphological assessment and determination of abnormality types, extent of disease, and global and regional information [36, 37]. Furthermore, deep learning applications in radiology, including fully automated whole lung segmentation, enable more precise and rapid determination of ILD cases with reproducible performance [38–40]. However, there are significant issues hindering its widespread adoption. Various technical parameters involved in CT image acquisition, such as reconstruction kernel, reconstruction method, and radiation dose, can impact the quantitative results [6]. Inconsistency in these factors leads to variations in quantification results, presenting a substantial obstacle to the use of quantitative CT analysis in retrospective, longitudinal, or multicenter clinical studies, as well as in clinical practice [29]. While it is viable to standardize input images to optimize imaging parameters for quantitative imaging, this is not feasible when using retrospective imaging datasets. In the present study, to address and overcome the limitations arising from different CT vendors or protocols, a CT conversion algorithm using a RouteGAN was applied, enabling unsupervised image-to-image translation and the conversion of CT images across scan parameters and manufacturers [29, 30]. This method holds promise in promoting the use of quantitative imaging in clinical practice and longitudinal multicenter studies involving CT images acquired under various conditions. The method can also aid in the assessment of incidental ILD or interstitial lung abnormalities detected on non-dedicated CT protocols.

Some previous studies reported a positive correlation between serum KL-6 and ILD severity [14, 15]. However, KL-6 alone cannot determine the overall extent of the ILD lesion [15]. Interpretations of increased KL-6 require consideration of factors such as FVC or radiologic severity for each ILD patient. In the present study, reticulation, which has been identified as the earliest sign for radiological progression, showed the moderate positive correlation with KL-6 [41]. However, the positive correlation between reticulation and KL-6 was confirmed as statistically significant in patients with ILD or fibrosis extent ≥ 5% but not in patients with a smaller ILD or fibrosis extent. Additionally, the positive correlation between GGO and KL-6 was confirmed to be significant in patients with fibrosis extent < 5%. These findings indicate that the correlations between the CT variables and KL-6 differ depending on ILD or fibrosis extent.

A subset of early ILD progresses to pulmonary fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix components within the pulmonary interstitium, leading to structural remodeling, scarring, and irreversible loss of lung function [42]. However, we lack diagnostic tests that can determine progression to pulmonary fibrosis. Physicians treating patients with early ILD are confronted with challenges with diagnosis due to non-specific and subtle respiratory symptoms. In our study, we propose that KL-6 and CT quantitative variables in ILD provide complementary information reflecting the state of ILD. The CT variable reflects phenotypic change of lung parenchyma, while KL-6 reflects extent of lung injury and potential to progress to pulmonary fibrosis. We believe that our findings have implications for improving the diagnostic approach to ILD and identifying patients at high risk of progression to fibrosis. By combining information from KL-6 and CT variables, physicians may be able to obtain a more comprehensive assessment of the disease and make more informed treatment decisions.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the diagnosis of ILD relied on multidisciplinary discussion with clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and imaging, which are convenient and non-invasive examinations. Surgical lung biopsy, an invasive procedure, is not commonly performed in clinical practice due to advancements in diagnostic technologies. Second, the enrolled ILD patients represented a heterogeneous group with different clinical diagnoses, limiting the subgroup analysis due to small sample sizes for each specific ILD. Third, this study utilizes cross-sectional data, and further evaluation is required to assess dynamic changes in CT phenotypes and KL-6 levels in relation to the longitudinal progression of ILD. Lastly, the study was conducted at a single center in Korea, potentially reducing the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or ethnic groups. Further multicenter studies are required to validate these results.

In conclusion, we found a significant positive correlation between CT-based phenotypes and KL-6, indicating their potential for assessing the severity and progression of ILD. Specifically, reticulation exhibited a moderate positive correlation with KL-6. In addition, the combination of reticulation focusing on phenotypic changes of lung parenchyma and KL-6 focusing on extent of lung injury as a more robust indicator can be helpful in predicting respiratory hospitalizations of ILD patients. Further research is needed to validate our findings and ascertain whether the integrated analysis of CT-based phenotypes and KL-6 can be utilized for predicting ILD progression.
