Vendi Novelty Scores for Out-of-Distribution Detection
Amey P. Pasarkar, Adji Bousso Dieng

Abstract
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is critical for the safe deployment of machine learning systems. Existing post-hoc detectors typically rely on model confidence scores or likelihood estimates in feature space, often under restrictive distributional assumptions. In this work, we introduce a third paradigm and formulate OOD detection from a diversity perspective. We propose the Vendi Novelty Score (VNS), an OOD detector based on the Vendi Scores (VS), a family of similarity-based diversity metrics. VNS quantifies how much a test sample increases the VS of the in-distribution feature set, providing a principled notion of novelty that does not require density modeling. VNS is linear-time, non-parametric, and naturally combines class-conditional (local) and dataset-level (global) novelty signals. Across multiple image classification benchmarks and network architectures, VNS achieves state-of-the-art OOD detection performance. Remarkably, VNS retains this performance when computed using only 1% of the training data, enabling deployment in memory- or access-constrained settings. Keywords: OOD Detection, Novelty Detection, Diversity, Machine Learning, Vendi Scoring

1
Introduction

Modern machine learning systems have made remarkable progress across a wide variety of tasks. Yet, these systems can behave unpredictably on previously unseen out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, often producing high-confidence, incorrect predictions
(
13
;
15
)
. Knowing when a model can be trusted is essential in high-stakes settings such as autonomous driving
(
49
)
and medical imaging
(
62
)
. OOD detectors provide a safeguard for these settings by identifying the inputs that are OOD and should be routed to human review or other fallback policies.

Recent methods for OOD detection span specialized models
(
16
;
20
)
, modifications to the training process
(
40
;
17
)
, and post-hoc methods that act independently of the training process
(
15
;
23
;
59
)
. As models have become increasingly large and trained on massive datasets, post-hoc methods are often the cheapest and most practical options for OOD detection. Post-hoc methods can be broadly categorized by the information from the model they exploit during inference. One class of approaches relies on the model’s prediction logits
(
30
;
29
)
, while another leverages the model’s intermediate representations and gradients to identify deviations from the in-distribution (ID) feature geometry
(
43
;
1
;
19
)
. While these approaches have achieved strong performance, they also make strong assumptions about the geometry of the model’s representations, which may not hold consistently across architectures and datasets. For example,
27
leverages the properties of neural collapse for OOD detection, which can lead to worse performance on off-the-shelf classifiers that do not exhibit neural collapse. Other similarity-based approaches like
51
do not make explicit assumptions about the representation space, but incur significant test-time overhead by performing a k-nearest neighbor search to identify OOD samples. More recent work aiming to exploit the gradients of the model
(
42
)
requires multiple forward passes, making them impractical in latency-sensitive settings. Designing computationally efficient OOD detectors without restrictive distributional assumptions is therefore critical for real-world deployment.

In this work, we introduce a new paradigm for post-hoc OOD detection based on diversity. We build on the Vendi Scores (VS), a family of similarity-based diversity metrics
(
12
;
38
)
, and propose the Vendi Novelty Score (VNS). Rather than fitting a parametric likelihood model in feature space, VNS provides a non-parametric novelty score derived from the geometry of in-distribution representations. Specifically, VNS computes class-conditional novelty contributions and aggregates them using the model’s predicted class probabilities, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

VNS offers an efficient way to evaluate sample novelty and incorporates both local and global novelty signals. We provide a series of approximations that allow VNS to scale linearly with the data dimension and number of classes. We demonstrate the effectiveness of VNS across various OOD benchmarks spanning several datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100
(
21
)
, ImageNet-1K
(
7
)
) and multiple network architectures (ResNet
(
14
)
, ViT
(
11
)
, Swin-T
(
31
)
). Our approach consistently achieves state-of-the-art detection performance, even when given access to only 1% of the training data. We also provide insights into the accuracy and efficiency of VNS theoretically and through various ablation studies.

Related Work

Post-Hoc OOD Detection.

Early work on post-hoc OOD detection focused on designing score functions based on the model’s logits. Such methods leverage characteristics of the logit distribution, such as the maximum class probabilities, energy scores, or their entropy
(
15
;
29
;
30
)
.
19
measured novelty via the gradient norm of the KL divergence of the logit distribution from a uniform prior. These approaches are attractive because they do not rely on access to the entire training data, making them easy to deploy on any model. However, this simplicity comes at the cost of ignoring in-distribution feature statistics, which can reduce OOD detection reliability.

Another line of work modifies the inference-time activations to increase separability between ID and OOD logit distributions. Activation shaping methods clip extreme activations or apply pruning or adaptive scaling to activations
(
50
;
10
;
58
)
. Input perturbation methods such as ODIN
(
24
)
, IODIN
(
18
)
, and ADASCALE
(
42
)
use gradient information to modify the input at test time, but require additional forward and backward passes.

Most closely related to our approach are feature-space density methods based on Mahalanobis distance. These methods model features as class-condition Gaussians with a background global Gaussian score
(
23
;
43
)
.
33
found that normalizing samples prior to fitting the Gaussian densities helps improve detection robustness across architectures. Related feature-space methods such as ViM
(
55
)
combine information from both logits and intermediate representations, modeling ID structure via a principal subspace of features and using the residual norm to improve OOD separation.
Other geometry-based methods include FDBD, which identifies that ID samples reside closer to decision boundaries than OOD samples
(
26
)
, and methods inspired by neural collapse
(
1
;
27
)
. These approaches are significantly faster than the k-nearest neighbor approach proposed in
51
.

Training Methods for OOD Detection.

A complementary approach for OOD detection is to modify the training process of the model so that OOD samples are easier to detect. Researchers have explored modifying the architecture itself, like in
9
, where a separate confidence-based OOD detector branch was added to the model. Alternatively, many researchers have explored modifications to the training data. For example, adding OOD samples and augmentations has been shown to improve detection accuracy
(
63
;
60
;
65
;
40
)
. Other methods have explored new training objectives, such as tuning logit activations
(
58
;
56
)
, or using contrastive learning to improve ID and OOD separation
(
48
;
57
;
52
;
32
)
. VNS is complementary to these methods, since it operates post-hoc and can be applied to pretrained models without retraining.

Vendi Scoring for Novelty.

The Vendi Score, introduced in
(
12
)
, has been used as an accurate measure of diversity across domains. Prior work has used the Vendi Score to evaluate dataset diversity in machine learning and the natural sciences
(
38
;
36
;
5
;
47
;
41
;
3
)
, as well as to encourage diverse outputs in simulations, generative models, and reinforcement learning
(
45
;
46
;
37
;
2
;
25
)
. Recent work has also explored Vendi-based novelty criteria.
For example,
35
and
28
measure the incremental change in Vendi Score induced by adding a candidate point as a selection rule in active learning. More recently,
39
proposed the Vendiscope, a method to analyze the content of large-scale datasets. The Vendiscope quantifies the rarity of samples within a dataset by optimizing over a distribution supported only on the dataset points. This formulation cannot be used to measure an unseen sample’s rarity
against
a dataset, so it is not applicable to OOD detection.

3
The Vendi Novelty Score

In this section, we briefly review the Vendi Scores (VS), and introduce our proposed algorithm to make them effective for OOD detection.

The Vendi Scores.

Consider a set of
N
data points
\bm{X}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times D}
.
Let
k(\cdot,\cdot)
denote a positive semi-definite pairwise kernel. The VS, as defined in
12
, is the entropy of the eigenvalues of the similarity matrix
\bm{K}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times N}
induced by the kernel
k
, normalized so that
\text{Tr}(\bm{K})=1
. This can be generalized to the
Rényi
entropy
(
38
)

\text{VS}_{q}(\bm{X},k)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\bm{K})^{q}\right),

(1)

where
\lambda_{i}(\bm{K})
is the
i
th eigenvalue of
\bm{K}
and
q\geq 0
is the order of the VS.

Computing the VS for arbitrary similarity matrices
\bm{K}
requires computing their eigenspectrum, which requires
O(N^{3})
time. However, when features are
\ell_{2}
normalized and we use the cosine kernel, we can more cheaply compute the VS. In this case, the similarity matrix is
\bm{K}=\bm{X}\bm{X}^{T}/N
, and its non-zero eigenvalues coincide with those of the
D\times D
Gram matrix
\bm{X}^{T}\bm{X}/N
, We compute the VS using
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/N
, which reduces the time complexity to
O(D^{2}N+D^{3})
and is efficient when
N\gg D
. Following prior work, we refer to the matrix
\rho:=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/N
as the
density matrix
.

Certain choices of order
q
have convenient forms. We highlight
q=2
and
q=\infty
. The VS of order
2
simplifies to

\displaystyle\text{VS}_{2}(\bm{X},k)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{K}^{2})}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\bm{K})^{2}}.

(2)

For
q=\infty
, the VS depends only on the largest eigenvalue:

\displaystyle\text{VS}_{\infty}(\bm{X},k)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(\bm{K})}.

(3)

Class-Conditional Novelty.
Here, we describe how we use the VS to measure the novelty of a test sample with respect to a class. We consider a trained discriminative model
f_{\theta}
(e.g., ResNet-50) with
C
classes, trained on a labeled in-distribution dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
. Let
N_{c}
denote the number of training samples belonging to class
c
. Let
h(x)\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
denote the representation of input
x
extracted from the penultimate layer of
f_{\theta}
, and let
\bm{X}_{c}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N_{c}\times D}
be the matrix of embeddings
h(x)
of all samples that belong to class
c
in
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. All representations are
\ell_{2}
-normalized prior to further processing.

We first compute class-conditional density matrices
\rho_{c}=\bm{X}_{c}^{T}\bm{X}_{c}/N_{c}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. Given a test sample
x
, we consider the class-conditional density matrix obtained after incorporating the representation
h(x)
into class
c
, which we denote by
\rho^{\prime}_{c}
. We denote by
\text{VS}_{2}(\rho_{c})
the Vendi Score of order
2
computed for class
c
under a cosine kernel. The class-conditional novelty is then defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}(x)=\log\text{VS}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}_{c})-\log\text{VS}_{2}(\rho_{c}).

(4)

\Delta_{c}(x)
measures the log-ratio increase in diversity from adding sample
x
to class
c
.

We can use Equation
2
to compute
\Delta_{c}
efficiently, using the identity
\text{VS}_{2}(\rho_{c})=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{c}^{2})}
. To do so, the updated density matrix after adding a test sample
x
can be written as
\rho^{\prime}_{c}=\frac{N_{c}\rho_{c}+h(x)h(x)^{T}}{N_{c}+1}
. Substituting this expression yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}({\rho^{\prime}_{c}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}\operatorname{Tr}((\rho_{c}N_{c}+h(x)h(x)^{T})^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{c}^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{c}^{2})+2N_{c}h(x)^{T}\rho_{c}h(x)+1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}.

Denote the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\rho_{c}
as
\lambda_{c,1},\lambda_{c,2},\ldots,\lambda_{c,D}
and
u_{c,1},u_{c,2},\ldots,u_{c,D}
. Let
\alpha_{c,j}(x)=(u_{c,j}^{T}h(x))^{2}
. We can rewrite the above equation for
\operatorname{Tr}({\rho^{\prime}_{c}}^{2})
in terms of this eigenbasis,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}({\rho^{\prime}_{c}}^{2})=\frac{N_{c}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\lambda_{c,i}^{2}+2N_{c}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\lambda_{c,i}\alpha_{c,i}(x)+1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}.

(5)

Plugging in
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{c}^{\prime 2})
into Equation
2
would give us an exact, closed-form,
O(D^{2})
time complexity operation to compute the class-conditional novelty scores. However, we find that maintaining the entire eigenbasis is unnecessary and can be sensitive to estimation noise in small classes. In fact, we achieve the strongest results if we only maintain the top eigenvalue (Section
4.3
). Let
\lambda_{c}
denote the largest eigenvalue for class
c
, with corresponding eigenvector
u_{c}
.
Then, we can compute the new trace in
O(D)
using the rank-1 approximation

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(x)

\displaystyle:=(u_{c}^{T}h(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}({\rho^{\prime}_{c}}^{2})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N_{c}^{2}\lambda_{c}^{2}+2N_{c}\lambda_{c}\alpha_{c}(x)+1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}.

The class-conditional novelty contribution is then

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}(x)=-\log\left(\frac{N_{c}^{2}\lambda_{c}^{2}+2N_{c}\lambda_{c}\alpha_{c}(x)+1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}\right)+\log(\lambda_{c}^{2}).

(6)

For each test sample
x
, we compute a vector of class-conditional novelty scores

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}(x)=(\Delta_{1}(x),\Delta_{2}(x),\dots,\Delta_{C}(x)).

(7)

Probability-weighted aggregation.

The classifier
f_{\theta}
produces a predictive distribution

\mathbf{p}(x)=(p_{1}(x),\dots,p_{C}(x)),\quad p_{c}(x)=\Pr(y=c\mid x).

Rather than relying on the novelty with respect to a single predicted class, we aggregate class-conditional novelty scores using a probability-weighted scheme. Let
\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)\subseteq\{1,\dots,C\}
denote the indices of the
K
classes with largest predicted probabilities
p_{c}(x)
.

\displaystyle S_{\text{LOCAL-OOD}}(x)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)}N_{c}p_{c}(x)\Delta_{c}(x),

(8)

where
\gamma\geq 0
and
K\leq C
are tunable hyperparameters.

Notably, Eq. (
8
) mirrors the form of GEN
(
30
)
, which aggregates over
\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)
using probability-based weights of the form
p_{c}(x)(1-p_{c}(x))
; in contrast, we use
p_{c}(x)
to weight class-conditional novelty terms
\Delta_{c}(x)
. The exponent
\gamma
controls the sharpness of the aggregation: larger values emphasize high-confidence classes, while smaller values yield a softer, more global aggregation across classes. The parameter
K
controls the number of candidate ID classes included in the aggregation (selected as the top-
K
classes by
p_{c}(x)
). While increasing
K
can incorporate additional information when predictions are ambiguous, overly large values may degrade performance by including many low-probability classes whose class-conditional novelty scores are weakly related to the sample and introduce noise into tohe aggregation. Smaller values of
K
also enable cheaper computation.

We additionally rescale
\Delta_{c}(x)
by
N_{c}
to account for class-size. Appendix Section
7.1
shows that the class-conditional novelty score scales by
O(1/N_{c})
, so we remove the effect of class-size with this multiplicative factor.

Incorporating Global Diversity.

Previous work has highlighted the importance of modeling the local density of model representations as well as the global density.
44
and
43
showed that subtracting a global novelty or likelihood score from the local novelty score can yield more robust results. Following this idea, we model a background density using a first-order approximation of the effect of a test sample
x
on the
\text{VS}_{\infty}
computed on the entire dataset.

Let
\bm{X}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N\times D}
denote the matrix whose rows are the
\ell_{2}
-normalized embeddings

h(x)
of all training samples, and let
\rho_{\text{global}}=\bm{X}^{T}\bm{X}/N
be the corresponding density
matrix. Given a test sample
x
, we denote the updated global density matrix as
\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}
and define the global novelty as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{global}}(x)=\log\text{VS}_{\infty}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})-\log\text{VS}_{\infty}(\rho_{\text{global}}).

(9)

From Equation
3
, the
\text{VS}_{\infty}
only depends on the maximum eigenvalue of
\rho_{g}
. We can approximate the effect of
x
on the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}
using the following proposition, with complete proof in Section
7.2
.

Proposition 3.1

(Accuracy of the Max Eigenvalue Update)
.

Let
\rho_{\text{global}}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D\times D}
be a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix with largest eigenvalue

\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})
and corresponding unit-norm eigenvector
u_{\max}
.
For a unit-norm vector
h(x)\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
and dataset size
N
, define the rank-one updated
density matrix

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N+1}\left(N\rho_{\text{global}}+h(x)h(x)^{T}\right).

Then the largest eigenvalue of
\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}
admits the first-order expansion

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{N\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+(u_{\max}^{T}h(x))^{2}}{N+1}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right).

Equivalently, the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}):=\frac{N\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+(u_{\max}^{T}h(x))^{2}}{N+1}

approximates
\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})
with error
O(1/N^{2})
.

Using the estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})
from Proposition
3.1
, we have the following approximation for the change in
\text{VS}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{global}}(x)\approx

\displaystyle-\log\left(\widehat{\lambda}_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})\right)+\log\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})

(10)

We note that Equation
6
used for the local diversity and Equation
10
used for the global diversity have seemingly similar forms, though Equation
6
is better suited for local diversity measurements because it better handles the variability in class’s max eigenvalues. Appendix Section
7.3
further discusses the differences in these two approaches.

We also scale the global novelty by multiplying by the total dataset size
n
:

\displaystyle S_{\text{GLOBAL-OOD}}(x)=

\displaystyle-n\log\left(\frac{n\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+\alpha(x)}{n+1}\right)+n\log\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}).

Because subtracting global density does not always yield gains, we introduce a binary coefficient variable
g\in\{0,1\}
, yielding the full VNS detector score:

\displaystyle\text{VNS}(x):=S_{\text{LOCAL-OOD}}(x)-gS_{\text{GLOBAL-OOD}}(x).

We use
\text{VNS}(x)
as a continuous OOD score, where larger values indicate more novel samples. To perform OOD detection, this score can be thresholded; throughout our experiments, we choose a threshold that achieves a fixed true positive rate of
95\%
, following standard evaluation practice. Additional details are provided in Section
4
.

Time and Space Complexity.

Due to our rank-1 approximation in Equation
6
, VNS has an inference space and time complexity of
O(CD)
, which scales linearly with the number of classes
C
and the dimension of the data
D
. Logit-based baselines such as MSP
(
15
)
, as well as NCI
(
27
)
, which leverages neural-collapse structure in the feature space, are still faster. However, VNS is more efficient than Mahalanobis-style methods, which are
O(CD^{2})

(
23
;
43
;
33
)
.

Experiments

We benchmark all algorithms on three image classification datasets: CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1K, as well as on ResNet, Swin-T, and ViT architectures. Overall, VNS exhibits strong and consistent performance across datasets and models.

Evaluation Metrics.

We mix each OOD test set with the ID test set, and ask each postprocessor to compute an ID confidence score. Using the scores computed from each method, we compute (1) False Positive Rate under a true positive rate of 95% (FPR@95) and (2) Area under the ROC curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that a randomly chosen ID sample is deemed less novel than a randomly chosen OOD sample.

4.2
Choices of Class-Conditional Novelty

VNS combines predictive uncertainty from logits with a class-conditional novelty score computed in feature space.
To isolate the effect of the novelty function, we run a controlled comparison in which we keep the probability-weighted top-
K
aggregation and optional global correction fixed, and only substitute the per-class novelty term
\Delta_{c}
with alternative distance-based scores.

In Table
3
, we replace the Vendi-based novelty computation in Equations
6
and
10
with two alternatives: cosine distance to the class mean and Mahalanobis distance to a class-conditional Gaussian.
For cosine distance, we define

\displaystyle\mu_{c}=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i:y_{i}=c}h(x_{i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}h(x_{i}),

and score a test sample by

\displaystyle s_{\text{cosine}}(x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)}p_{c}(x)\left(1-\frac{x\cdot\mu_{c}}{||\mu_{c}||_{2}}\right)-g\left(1-\frac{x\cdot\mu}{||\mu||_{2}}\right).

The global correction is the cosine similarity between the sample and the mean of the entire dataset.

For the Mahalanobis Distance approach, we follow
33
and compute a Mahalanobis distance between a normalized test sample to the class mean. The class-wise Mahalanobis distance is measured by computing the likelihood of the sample under a Gaussian centered at the class mean with a shared covariance matrix. The covariance matrix is computed as

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}^{C}\sum_{i:y_{i}=c}\left(h(x_{i})-\mu_{c}\right)(h(x_{i})-\mu_{c})^{T}.

The global correction comes from the likelihood from a gaussian distribution fit to the entire dataset. The novelty score function becomes

\displaystyle s_{\text{Maha}}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)}p_{c}(x)d_{\text{Maha}}(x,\mu_{c})-gd_{\text{Maha}}(x,\mu),

where
d_{\text{Maha}}
denotes the Mahalanobis distance. For both
s_{\text{Maha}}
and
s_{\text{cosine}}
, we follow the same hyperparameter selection strategy as VNS for fair comparison. Overall, our VS approach to measure novelty improves OOD detection over the alternative approaches (Table
3
). Another advantage of VNS over Mahalonobis is our computational efficiency. VNS has time complexity
O(CD)
, whereas Mahalanobis has time complexity
O(CD^{2})
. Another measure of class-conditional novelty could be a nearest neighbor distance, like the one used in
51
. However, this approach would add significant computational overhead because it requires iterating over the training set at inference.

4.3
Choice of rank

In Equation
6
, we choose to only maintain the leading eigendirection in our class-conditional novelty computation. We show in Table
4
that using only rank-1 preserves (and sometimes improves) accuracy. Moreover, maintaining only one eigendirection makes the time complexity linear in the dimensionality
D
. In Figure
2
, we observe that class density matrices exhibit a large spectral gap: the leading eigenvalue is on average an order of magnitude larger than the second. Since
\text{VS}_{2}
depends on the squared spectrum (Equation
2
), this gap implies that the rank-1 component captures most of the
\text{VS}_{2}
-relevant information, whereas smaller components contribute weakly and are more sensitive to noise.

4.4
Data-efficient OOD Detection

A major advantage of activation shaping and logit-based methods over other approaches is that they do not require access to a model’s training data. This is useful in contexts where the training data is not available, or is too expensive to store. While VNS cannot operate without access to the training data, we show that VNS is just as effective if given
1\%
of the data. We sample varying percentages of samples from each training ImageNet class and measure VNS’s performance after being fit on this subset. In Figure
3
, we see that even at tiny fractions, VNS provides accurate OOD detection. With
1\%
of the data, VNS achieves an average FPR@95 of
39.55\pm 0.03
across models, compared to
38.99\pm 0.02
when given all of the data.

VNS’s robustness to dataset size is likely due to two factors: (1) VNS incorporates logit information, which is unaffected by dataset size and (2) VNS only utilizes the largest eigenvalue from each class, which can be reliably estimated from small sample sizes.

5
Conclusion

This work introduces
VNS
, a post-hoc out-of-distribution detector that leverages the Vendi Scores to quantify novelty from a diversity perspective. Given a test sample, VNS is computed by first measuring its class-conditional novelty in feature space under each class using an efficient rank-1 approximation of the Vendi Score of order
2
and then aggregating these novelty signals via a probability-weighted top-K scheme. A global background correction, modulated by a binary hyperparameter, is then added to improve robustness. Across multiple image classification benchmarks and architectures, VNS achieves state-of-the-art detection performance, reducing FPR@95 by an average of
13\%
relative to the previous state-of-the-art, while remaining effective even when computed using only
1\%
of the training set.

Limitations.
While VNS demonstrates strong performance, it introduces three tunable hyperparameters, which we select using an automatically generated validation set of random noise images. However, this validation set will not be representative of the samples encountered during deployment. In addition, VNS relies on the cosine kernel to measure similarity, whereas alternative kernels may improve results.

Future Work.
An important direction for future work is to adapt VNS to kernels beyond cosine similarity. Another promising avenue is applying VNS beyond image classification to other modalities in ML and the natural sciences.

Impact Statement

This work improves OOD detection, helping machine learning (ML) sytems recognize when they are operating beyond their competence and enabling safer behaviors such as abstention, escalating to human experts, or other fallback options. By improving the reliability of ML systems while remaining efficient, our approach can support more practical deployment in high-stakes settings. However, OOD detection is not a complete safety solution: detectors can still fail under adversarial conditions. An over-reliance on automated novelty scores may create a false sense of security. We encourage deploying OOD detectors only as part of broader safety protocols, with monitoring, human oversight, and careful evaluation across diverse deployment environments.

7.1
Scaling Novelty Scores to Account for Dataset Size

When computing the local and global novelty scores in Equation
6
and Equation
10
, we scale by the number of samples. This removes the effect of dataset size because the increment
\Delta
scales according to
\frac{1}{N}
. We show this is true for both the local
\Delta_{c}
in
6
and
\Delta_{\text{global}}
in Equation
10
.

For the class-conditional novelty
\Delta_{c}
, we can use a Taylor expansion around the terms in Equation
6
to show the scaling behavior of the score.

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}(x)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\frac{N_{c}^{2}\lambda_{c}^{2}+2N_{c}\lambda_{c}\alpha_{c}(x)+1}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}\right)+\log(\lambda_{c}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\frac{N_{c}^{2}+2N_{c}\alpha(x)/\lambda_{c}+1/\lambda_{c}^{2}}{(N_{c}+1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\log(1+\frac{1}{N_{c}})-\log\left(1+\frac{2\alpha(x)}{N_{c}\lambda_{c}}+\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}\lambda^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{1}{N_{c}}-\frac{1}{2N_{c}^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{N_{c}^{3}})\right)-\left(\frac{2\alpha(x)}{\lambda_{c}N_{c}}+(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha(x)^{2}}{\lambda_{c}^{2}})\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{N_{c}^{3}})\right)

\displaystyle=(2-\frac{\alpha(x)}{\lambda_{c}})\frac{1}{N_{c}}+O(\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}}),

where we used the two Taylor expansions
\log(1+u)=u-\frac{u^{2}}{2}+O(u^{3})
and
\log(1+Au+Bu^{2})=Au+(B-\frac{A^{2}}{2})u^{2}+O(u^{3})
. With the above, we have

\displaystyle N_{c}\Delta_{c}(x)=2-\frac{2\alpha(x)}{\lambda_{c}}+O(\frac{1}{n_{c}}),

which is independent of the dataset size at the leading order.

For the global novelty, we again use a Taylor expansion around the terms in Equation
10
to show the scaling behavior of the score.

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{global}}

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\frac{N\lambda_{\max}(\rho)+\alpha(x)}{N+1}\right)+\log\lambda_{\max}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{\alpha(x)}{N\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}-\frac{\alpha(x)}{N\lambda}+O(\frac{1}{N^{2}})

Therefore, scaling by a factor of
N
gives us a score independent of the dataset size at the leading order.

\displaystyle N\Delta_{\text{global}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\alpha(x)}{\lambda}+O(\frac{1}{N})

7.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

As defined in Eq.
3
, the log-VS of order
\infty
is given by

\displaystyle\log\text{VS}_{\infty}(\rho_{\text{global}})=-\log\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}),

where
\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})
is the largest eigenvalue of
\rho_{\text{global}}=X^{T}X/N\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. Let
u_{\max}
be the corresponding eigenvector to
\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})
.
Adding a new sample
x
to
\rho_{\text{global}}
gives an updated density matrix
\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N+1}(N\rho_{\text{global}}+h(x)h(x)^{T})
. The top eigenvalue of
\rho^{\prime}
satisfies the Rayleigh principle

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})=\max_{||v||=1}v^{T}\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}v.

Because the old top eigenvector has
||u_{\max}||=1
, we can use it as a lower bound:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq u_{\max}^{T}\rho^{\prime}u_{\max}

\displaystyle=u^{T}_{\max}\left(\frac{n\rho_{\text{global}}+h(x)h(x)^{T}}{N+1}\right)u_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{Nu_{\max}^{T}\rho_{\text{global}}u_{\max}+u_{\max}^{T}h(x)h(x)^{T}u_{\max}}{N+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+\alpha(x)^{2}}{N+1},

We plug in the above expression into Eq.
9
to reach Eq.
10
.

We can use a first-order perturbation expansion to derive the accuracy of our approximation. Let
\delta=\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime}-\rho_{\text{global}}=\frac{1}{N+1}\left(h(x)h(x)^{T}-\rho_{\text{global}}\right)
. Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+u_{\max}^{T}\delta u_{\max}+O(||\delta||^{2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+\frac{(u_{\max}^{T}h(x))^{2}-\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})}{N+1}+O(\frac{1}{N^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{N\lambda_{\max}(\rho_{\text{global}})+(u_{\max}^{T}h(x))^{2}}{N+1}+O(\frac{1}{N^{2}}).

So, our approximation to the max eigenvalue is accurate to the order of
O(1/N^{2})
.

7.3
Comparison of Local and Global Novelty Scores

Here we compare Equation
6
against Equation
10
. We show that despite their reliance on only the maximum eigenvector and eigenvalue, our
\text{VS}_{2}
rank-1 approximation for measuring local diversity is better suited than our first-order
\text{VS}_{\infty}
approximation, while
\text{VS}_{\infty}
provides an accurate global correction.

In our rank-
1
approximation in Equation
6
, the data-dependent part of
\Delta_{c}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}^{(2)}(x)\;=\;-\log\!\left(1+\frac{2\alpha_{c}(x)}{N_{c}\lambda_{c}}+\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}\lambda_{c}^{2}}\right)+\text{Constant}

(11)

whereas the analogous
q=\infty
change (under our first-order approximation) has the form

\Delta_{c}^{(\infty)}(x)\;=\;-\log\!\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{c}(x)}{N_{c}\lambda_{c}}\right)+\text{Constant}

(12)

To understand why
q=2
is preferable locally, we compare derivatives with respect to the quadratic alignment
\alpha_{c}(x)=(u_{c}^{T}h(x))^{2}
.
Let
b_{c}:=(N_{c}\lambda_{c})^{-1}
. Differentiating (
11
) gives

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Delta_{c}^{(2)}\right|=\frac{2b_{c}}{1+2b_{c}\alpha+b_{c}^{2}},\qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Delta_{c}^{(2)}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\frac{2b_{c}}{1+b_{c}^{2}}.

(13)

In contrast, differentiating (
12
) yields

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Delta_{c}^{(\infty)}\right|=\frac{b_{c}}{1+b_{c}\alpha},\qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Delta_{c}^{(\infty)}\right|_{\alpha=0}=b_{c}.

(14)

The crucial distinction is that at
\alpha=0
, the
q=\infty
local sensitivity is linear in
b_{c}=1/(n_{c}\lambda_{c})
and can
therefore vary substantially across classes due to variation in
\lambda_{c}
(and, to a lesser extent,
N_{c}
),
making the local novelty overly sensitive to eigenvalue estimation error in small classes. By contrast,
the
q=2
local sensitivity is self-normalized:

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Delta_{c}^{(2)}\right|_{\alpha=0}=\frac{2b_{c}}{1+b_{c}^{2}}\;\leq\;1,

which suppresses both extremes
b_{c}\ll 1
and
b_{c}\gg 1
. Equivalently,
q=2
yields a bounded and smoother response
as
\lambda_{c}
varies, preventing a small subset of classes with atypical
\lambda_{c}
from dominating the
probability-weighted aggregation. This robustness is particularly valuable for local scoring because class-level
statistics are estimated from substantially fewer samples than the global statistics.

At the global-level,
\frac{1}{N\lambda}
is reliably estimated so we do not need the self-normalization behavior of
\text{VS}_{2}
.

Additional Experiment Details

8.1
Baselines

Here we briefly describe the
10
baselines, and how they compare in approach to VNS.

MSP.

The maximum softmax probability (MSP) detector from
15
uses the maximum class probability prediction as a measure of model confidence. MSP does not incorporate any information about the feature geometry like VNS.

ASH.

The activation shaping (ASH) method from
10
prunes the majority of a sample’s activations and scales the rest. The time complexity of ASH is
O(D\log D)
. ASH, unlike VNS, does not require access to the training data. However, this approach can effect ID accuracy unless a second forward pass in the model is made.

SCALE.

58
found that ASH does not require any pruning, and benefits from only scaling the activations. SCALE also has time complexity
O(D\log D)
, but does not affect ID accuracy. SCALE can be used on any model without accessing training data.

ADASCALE.

42
improved on SCALE by noting that using a fixed scaling factor across all samples can be overly rigid. Instead, ADASCALE uses a gradient perturbation method to estimate an ideal, per-sample scaling factor. This approach requires multiple forward passes as well as a backward pass to compute the scaling factor. There are two variants, ADASCALE-A and ADASCALE-L, that differ in how the scaling is applied. We compare against ADASCALE-A in this paper because it provides better performance on the tested benchmarks.

IODIN.

18
used an input perturbation approach to achieve better ID-OOD logit separation. In particular, they estimated an optimal perturbation
\epsilon
from a validation set that would improve the efficacy of their confidence score. This
\epsilon
perturbation would be applied to all test samples. Like ADASCALE, IODIN requires the ability to compute gradients on the model and multiple forward passes for each test sample.

GEN.

30
introduced the Generalized Entropy score, a logit-only OOD detector. It measures the entropy of the class predictions over the top-K classes, with OOD samples having higher uncertainty. The entropy is given as
s_{\text{GEN}}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{K}(x)}p_{i}(x)(1-p_{i}(x))
. We incorporate this form into our probability-weighted aggregation to reflect the model uncertainty alongside our class novelty scores.

FDBD.

The Fast Decision Boundary Detector (FDBD) introduced in
26
exploits the observation that OOD samples lie closer to decision boundaries than ID samples in the embedding space. The paper provides a hyperparameter-free, fast
O(C+D)
algorithm for estimating these distances. The authors also show that FDBD is theoretically accurate under the assumptions of neural collapse.

NCI.

The Neural Collapse Inspired OOD Detector (NCI) directly leverages the properties of neural collapse to identify OOD samples
27
. In particular, the authors leverage
4
characteristics of Neural Collapse: (1) variability within a class goes to
0
(2) class means are arranged on an Equiangular Tight frame (3) the last last layer linear classifier converges to the class means and (4) the classifier acts as a nearest center classifier. With these characteristics, NCI measures the cosine similarity between the centered feature and the class weights. NCI is also fast: it runs in
O(D)
additional time. Like FDBD, NCI is also hyperparameter-free. VNS does not make assumptions about Neural Collapse.

KNN.

The KNN OOD detector from
51
measures the distance between a test sample and its
k
th nearest neighbor in a normalized feature space. This non-parametric method requires each test sample to be compared against the entire training set. At inference time, VNS does not search over the entire training dataset.

RMDS++.

Given
\ell_{2}
-normalized embeddings
x\in\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
with
\|x\|_{2}=1
, RMDS++ measures OOD uncertainty by comparing how well
x
fits a class-conditional Gaussian model versus the global feature distribution
(
43
;
33
)
. Let
\mu_{c},\Sigma
denote the mean and covariance of class
c
, and let
\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g}
denote the mean and covariance computed over the full training set.
\Sigma
is shared across all classes. RMDS++ uses a
