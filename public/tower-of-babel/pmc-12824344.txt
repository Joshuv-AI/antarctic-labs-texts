The RNA-binding protein CPEB1 marks healthy adult β cells in mice but is dispensable for β cell identity and function
Authors: Sutichot D Nimkulrat, Matthew R Wagner, Bayley J Waters, Nicolas G Pintozzi, Maria del Carmen Mendoza Niemes, Cyrus R Sethna, Hariharan Jayaraaman, Barak Blum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA binding proteins (RBPs) are increasingly being recognized as important regulators of pancreatic β cells’ identity and function. We identified the poly-A binding RBP, CPEB1, as a potential new regulator linked to β cell failure in diabetes based on its enrichment in mature, healthy adult β cells and its sharp decrease in β cells in type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D). While CPEB1 is known to regulate an extensive range of biological processes, and its involvement in regulating genes involved in insulin signaling and apoptosis in the liver has been reported, CPEB1 function in β cells is unknown. Here, we have used two independent genetic mouse models, namely β cell-specific and whole body Cpeb1 deletions, to assess the role of CPEB1 in β cells. We show that CPEB1 is dispensable for β cell function and identity. Using bulk RNA sequencing on islets of both models, we also show that CPEB1 regulates markedly different sets of genes between islets of male and female mice, both at the transcriptional and at the polyadenylation levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32403-1.Keywords: Islets, RNA binding proteins, Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes, Beta cells, MouseSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Endocrinology, Molecular biology

Introduction

RNA binding proteins (RBPs) are gaining attention as critical regulators of pancreatic β cell differentiation, function, and survival1–4. The Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 1 (CPEB1) is a conserved, sequence-specific RBP, which regulates mRNA translation, stability, and localization within the cell by binding to a cytoplasmic polyadenylation element (CPE) at the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of selected mRNAs and modulating the length of their poly-A sequences5. CPEB1 has been known to be involved in many tissue-specific biological processes, including oocyte maturation6,7, cellular senescence8, neuronal plasticity9,10, and viral-host infection11.

In addition to the above roles, CPEB1 has been suggested to be involved in glucose metabolism in several tissues. CPEB1 regulates carbohydrate metabolism in human glioblastoma cells12, its activity is regulated downstream of insulin signaling13, and whole-body Cpeb1-null mice exhibit reduced hepatic insulin sensitivity and glucose dysregulation14. Furthermore, it has been reported that CPEB1 function in the pancreatic islets of Langerhans is linked to β cell proliferation in response to obesity15. However, direct evidence for a specific role of CPEB1 in β cells has not yet been established.

Results

Cpeb1 expression marks healthy adult β cells

To begin interrogating the role of CPEB1 in β cells, we first examined its expression in the islets of Langerhans using the Tabula Muris single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) database16. We found that Cpeb1 expression is restricted to the endocrine compartment of the pancreas and is enriched in β cells (Fig. 1A). Thus, 72% of β cells express Cpeb1, with mean expression level of 2.52 ln(1 + CPM), while 42% of α cells, 32% of PP cells, and 32% of δ cells express the gene, with mean expression levels of 1.23, 1.03, and 0.96 ln(1 + CPM), respectively (Fig. 1B). To examine the changes in Cpeb1 expression in β cells during pancreas development, as well as in diabetes, we probed our previously published RNA-seq dataset17, which follows isolated mouse β cells’ transcriptome through postnatal development as well as during the progression to diabetes in models of both T1D (NOD) and T2D (BTBR-Lepob/ob). We found that Cpeb1 mRNA expression increases during late-stage postnatal development and peaks in β cells of healthy adult mice (Fig. 1C). We further found that the expression of Cpeb1 is significantly reduced upon the progression to diabetes in both mouse models (Fig. 1D). While the down-regulation of Cpeb1 expression can be observed in both NOD and BTBR-Lepob/ob, the reduction is more prominent in NOD mice than BTBR-Lepob/ob mice. The expression of the other Cpeb1 paralogs, Cpeb2, Cpeb3, and Cpeb4, in healthy, mature β cells is negligible, and does not compensate for the reduction in Cpeb1 expression in diabetes (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, within the healthy adult group, Cpeb1 expression levels are significantly higher in female β cells compared to male β cells (Fig. 1F).

Analysis of human CPEB1 expression in scRNA-seq of islets from the Human Pancreas Analysis Program (HPAP)18 using the Elgamal et al.19 integrated dataset showed that human CPEB1 is similarly enriched in the endocrine compartment of the islet, with the highest expression in β cells; however, in contrast to the mouse, human CPEB1 is almost as highly expressed in α cells, and its reduction in diabetes is not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 1).

CPEB1 is not required for β cell differentiation or maturation

To test the role of CPEB1 in β cells, we created a β cell-specific knockout of Cpeb1 (Cpeb1 βKO) by crossing Cpeb1-flox mice20 with Ins1-Cre mice21. We confirmed β cell-specific deletion of Cpeb1 using qPCR (Fig. 2A). Immunostaining with antibodies against insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin showed similar islet architecture between Cpeb1 βKO mice and Cre-only controls (Fig. 2B). The average islet size was slightly larger in islets from Cpeb1 βKO mice than in the Cre-only controls, but this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2C). We next examined the effect of Cpeb1 deletion on β cell differentiation and maturation by immunofluorescence staining with antibodies against MAFA and UCN3, respectively. We observed no difference in the expression of either marker in islets from Cpeb1 βKO compared to Cre-only controls (Fig. 2D-E). Similarly, there was no difference in the percentage of Ki67-positive or TUNEL-positive cells between Cpeb1 βKO and controls, indicating that deletion of Cpeb1 in β cells does not affect their proliferation nor survival (Fig. 2F-I).

CPEB1 is not required for β cell function under homeostasis

To test the extent to which Cpeb1 deletion in β cells affects β cell function, we performed oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT) on both male and female Cpeb1 βKO and control mice. We observed no difference in OGTT between either male and female Cpeb1 βKO and sex-matched control animals, suggesting that the glucose clearance in male and female Cpeb1 βKO mice is unaltered (Fig. 3A-D). Similarly, in vivo glucose-stimulated insulin secretion assay (GSIS) showed no difference in insulin secretion between Cpeb1 βKO mice compared to controls following a glucose challenge (Fig. 3E-H). Finally, insulin tolerance tests (ITT) on Cpeb1 βKO and control animals showed comparable insulin sensitivity between Cpeb1 βKO and control mice in both males and females (Fig. 3I-L).

CPEB1 regulates different sets of transcripts in male and female mice

To explore the extent to which Cpeb1 deletion affects other aspects of β cell identity and function, we collected islets from Cpeb1 βKO and control mice and performed bulk RNA sequencing. We found 42 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 2-fold differential expression, p adj ≤ 0.05) between male Cpeb1 βKO islets and control islets, of which 13 were down-regulated in the Cpeb1 βKO islets compared to the controls, and 29 genes were up-regulated (Fig. 4A). Additionally, we found 29 DEGs between female Cpeb1 βKO islets and control islets, of which 17 were down-regulated in the Cpeb1 βKO islets compared to the controls, and 12 genes were up-regulated (Fig. 4B). Only 3 up-regulated and 1 down-regulated genes were shared between males and females (Fig. 4C, D and Supplementary Table 1), indicating that CPEB1 potentially regulates different sets of genes in male and female β cells.

The main regulatory activity of CPEB1 is at the post-transcriptional level, specifically by controlling the poly-A tail length of mRNAs22. To explore how β cell-specific deletion of Cpeb1 affects the post-transcriptional landscape in the islet, we applied the APAlyzer R package, a bioinformatics tool for analysis of alternative polyadenylation isoforms23, to find mRNA polyadenylation changes in our bulk RNA-seq data. We found 155 alternatively polyadenylated mRNAs between male Cpeb1 βKO and controls, of which 90 transcripts had poly-A tails at the 3’ UTR that were lengthened (“UP”), and 65 transcripts with shortened poly-A tails (“DN”) (Fig. 4E). Among females, we found 118 alternatively polyadenylated mRNAs between Cpeb1 βKO and controls, of which 63 transcripts had poly-A tails at the 3’ UTR that were lengthened (“UP”), and 55 transcripts with shortened poly-A tails (“DN”) (Fig. 4F). Gene ontology (GO) analysis showed that 43 terms are associated with elongated poly-A tails in males (Fig. 4G), while 16 GO terms are enriched in female islets (Fig. 4H). Only 1 transcript each was shared between males and females in either elongated or shortened poly-A tails (Fig. 4I, J, and Supplementary Table 2). This poly-A tail analysis confirmed our transcriptomic findings that CPEB1 regulates different sets of genes in male and female islets.

Whole-body deletion of CPEB1 confirms the sex-specific regulation of islet mRNAs and its dispensability for β cell identity and function

Because of the surprising lack of effect of Cpeb1 β cell-specific deletion on β cell identity and function, and because Cpeb1 is also expressed to some extent in non-β endocrine cells, we wanted to investigate its role in the islet with an independent genetic model. To this end, we examined islets from whole-body Cpeb1-null mice (Cpeb1 KO)7. We performed bulk RNA-seq analysis on isolated islets from male and female Cpeb1 KO mice and wildtype controls. After exocrine-related genes were excluded, we found 292 DEGs in male islets (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 3), and 23 DEGs in the female islets (data not shown). Out of the 292 DEGs between the male control and the Cpeb1 KO islets, we found that 175 genes are down-regulated, and 117 genes are up-regulated using a q-value cutoff of 0.05. When we compared the 292 filtered DEGs of the male Cpeb1 KO mice and their control to the 23 DEGs of the female counterparts (both up-regulated and down-regulated), we identified only 9 DEGs shared between the two groups (Fig. 5B). This confirmed that CPEB1 activity is sexually dimorphic in mouse islets.

While GO term enrichment analysis for the female DEGs was inconclusive due to the small number of transcripts, the GO term analysis of the male DEGs revealed 205 enrichments in biological processes; the most significant GO terms associated with down-regulated genes were related to immune signaling processes such as leukocyte differentiation and proliferation, immune response regulation, and T-cell activation. Regarding the GO biological processes of up-regulated genes, all 30 GO terms are related to homeostatic and metabolic processes (Fig. 5C). CPEB1 has been reported previously to regulate inflammatory immune response24. Therefore, we examined whether the down-regulation of immune-related terms is caused by the reduction of leukocytes in the islets of Cpeb1 KO mice by staining for CD45, a marker for all nucleated hematopoietic cells. Indeed, the number of CD45 + cells in Cpeb1 KO islets is less than the number of CD45 + cells in control islets, though this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5D-F).

We next analyzed alternative polyadenylation from Cpeb1 KO islets RNA-seq using APAlyzer. Analysis of male islets revealed 296 DEGs, which comprised 45 transcripts whose poly-A tails at 3’ UTR were lengthened (“UP”), and 251 transcripts with shortened poly-A tails (“DN”) (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 4). While very few GO terms from the male islets are associated with elongated poly-A tails, many shortened poly-A DEGs are linked to adaptive immune processes, cellular organization, and Rho GTPase signaling (Fig. 6E). Surprisingly, similar poly-A tail analysis on the female cohorts showed a complete opposite result: 506 transcripts had their 3’ UTR extended, and 18 transcripts had their 3’ UTR shortened (Fig. 6B). The top 15 GO terms enriched in female islets suggest mRNA stability in biological processes involved in cellular functions, protein transport, and responses to stimuli (Fig. 6F). In agreement with our previous analyses, there was virtually no overlap between CPEB1-regulated transcripts in male islets and CPEB1-regulated transcripts in female islets (Fig. 6C, D). This further confirms that CPEB1 is a sexually dimorphic regulator in mouse islets.

Discussion

In the last decade and a half, a plethora of RBPs have been identified to affect β cell development, function, and response to stresses25–36. We have found that the gene encoding one such RBP, CPEB1, is strongly expressed in pancreatic β cells, and its expression increases during postnatal development, peaking in β cells from adult, normoglycemic mice, and decreasing in β cells in islets from both T1D and T2D, raising the possibility that CPEB1 has a function in the mature β cell state. A potential role for CPEB1 in β cells was further suggested by the observations that CPEB1 is phosphorylated downstream of insulin signaling13; that CPEB1, together with its binding partner, GLD4, regulate carbohydrate metabolism in human glioblastoma cells12, and that Cpeb1 null mice show hepatic insulin resistance and glucose dysregulation14. Moreover, mir-455-mediated repression of Cpeb1 expression in β cells has been shown to affect obesity-mediated adaptive β cell proliferation15, and a single-cell multiome analysis recently implicated a CPEB1-regulated transcriptional program in human α-to-β cell transdifferentiation37.

To examine the functional requirement of CPEB1 in the islets, we used two independent genetic models, namely β cell-specific (Cpeb1 βKO) and whole body (Cpeb1 KO) deletion. Surprisingly, comprehensive analyses on both models revealed that deleting Cpeb1 in healthy, adult islets does not affect β cell identity, maturity, and function. There are several potential explanations to the lack of observed functional phenotype in islets of either Cpeb1 βKO or Cpeb1 KO mice. First, CPEB1 may be functionally redundant with other RBPs. However, the expression of the three mammalian Cpeb1 paralogs (Cpeb2, Cpeb3, and Cpeb4) in the β cells is much lower than that of Cpeb1 (see Fig. 1D) arguing against this explanation. Furthermore, the four CPEB homologues appear to have mostly non-overlapping functions38,39. However, it is possible that other factors involved in CPEB1 function, such as its binding partners, GLD2, GLD4, PARN, TACC3, and others, mask the Cpeb1-null phenotype. Additionally, it could be that the Cpeb1-null phenotype appears in a cellular function we did not examine, or that the effect is seen only under certain conditions, such as diabetogenic stress. Indeed, work by Hu et al.15 points to CPEB1 as an inhibitor of β cell proliferation, whose down-regulation in obesity improves β cell compensatory proliferation. Our work confirms the observation by Hu et al. that Cpeb1 is down-regulated in obesity and metabolic stresses and further shows that it is also down-regulated in T1D. Interestingly, Hu et al. did not observe a significant change in insulin secretion and blood glucose levels after indirect down-regulation of Cpeb1 in vivo (via its inhibitor, miR-455). These results are consistent with our findings that deletion of Cpeb1 does not affect β cell identity and function under homeostasis.

To further examine the regulatory role of CPEB1 in the islets, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on extracted islets from Cpeb1 βKO and Cpeb1 KO, and analyzed the total transcriptome and mRNA poly-A tail length differences between control and Cpeb1-null islets in each of the models. We found almost no overlap between male and female islets in either mRNA abundance or mRNA poly-A tail changes. The cause of this sex differences in CPEB1-regulatd genes in currently unclear, but it may be related to the sex-differences in basal Cpeb1 expression levels between male and female β cells (see Fig. 1B). The affected biological processes in the females appear to differ based on the knockout models (Cpeb1 KO or Cpeb1 βKO) and types of analysis (transcriptomic or post-transcriptomic), likely due to the CPEB1 regulatory effects in non-β islet cells such as α, δ, or PP cells, and/or the insulin resistance seen in whole-body Cpeb1-null mice14 compared to Cpeb1 βKO mice (see Fig. 3I-K).

In conclusion, our data show that CPEB1 is a marker of healthy adult β cells in mice, that CPEB1 regulates distinct sets of transcripts in males and females, but that CPEB1 is dispensable for β cell identity and function under normal, non-metabolically stressed conditions.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experimental protocols were approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison IACUC guidelines under protocol number M005221, and all methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals

Cpeb1 KO mice7 and Cpeb1flx mice20 were gifts from Dr. Joel Richter, University of Massachusetts Medical School, and Dr. Raul Méndez, Institute for Research in Biomedicine, Barcelona, respectively. Cpeb1 KO mice were maintained on C57BL/6J background, whereas Cpeb1 βKO mice were maintained on a mixed background, and all Cpeb1 βKO mice possessed one copy of Ins1-Cre21. All mice were at least 8 weeks of age before carrying out the experiments. Litter- or age-matched colony mates were used for all experiments; mice with birthdates approximately ±1 month apart were also used in some cases.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from fresh whole islets was isolated using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA library was prepared with random primers using SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Life Technologies). Relative expression of Cpeb1 was determined using Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Agilent) on Bio-Rad CFX Connect Optics Module. Primers for mouse Cpeb1 exon 4 were 5’-CATCCTGGACTCCCGTTCTAG-3’ forward and 5’-GGACCATTTCCCGTCATAGAAAG-3’ reverse. Level of gene expression was normalized to the expression of the Eif2a gene.

Tissue collection and immunostaining

Mice were euthanized with CO2 according to standard protocol, and their pancreata were immediately collected for fixation. Pancreata were fixed in 4% PFA in 1X PBS for 2 h at 4 °C. Samples were washed in 1X PBS for 1 h, equilibrated in 30% sucrose in 1X PBS, and embedded in FSC 22 Blue. Tissue was cryosectioned with four 10 μm slices per slide. Slides were stained using a standard immunofluorescence protocol with 10% normal donkey serum. The following primary antibodies were guinea pig anti-insulin 1:6 (Agilent, IR002), rabbit anti-glucagon 1:200 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2760 S), mouse anti-glucagon (Sigma, G2654), rabbit anti-UCN3 1:500 (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, H-019-29), rabbit anti-MAFA 1:200 (Cell Signaling Technology, 79737 S), rabbit anti-somatostatin 1:500 (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, G-060-03), rabbit anti-Ki67 1:400 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9129 S), and rat anti-CD45 1:10 (BD Biosciences, 550539). The following secondary antibodies were used: donkey anti-guinea pig AF647 1:500 (Jackson, 706-605-148), donkey anti-rat AF647 1:500 (Jackson, 712-605-153), donkey anti-mouse AF647 1:500 (Jackson, 715-605-150), donkey anti-guinea pig AF594 1:500 (Jackson, 706-585-148), and donkey anti-rabbit AF488 1:500 (Jackson, 711-546-152). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000). For mouse primary antibody, Mouse on Mouse Immunodetection Kit was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Vector, BMK-2202), with slight modifications. TUNEL staining was performed using 0.1% citrate buffer for antigen retrieval and In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, 11684795910). Positive control slide was treated with deoxyribonuclease to induce DNA damage for 5 min before secondary antibody incubation step (Sigma, D4263).

Oral glucose tolerance tests

Mice were fasted starting in the morning for 6 h by removing chow and transferring them to fresh cages. A 2 g glucose/kg body weight solution was prepared on the day of the experiment and administered via oral gavage. Blood glucose measurements were taken at baseline (0 min), and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after oral gavage.

In vivo glucose stimulated insulin secretion during glucose tolerance test

Mice were fasted starting in the morning for 6 h by removing chow and transferring them to fresh cages. A 2 g glucose/kg body weight solution was prepared on the day of the experiment and administered via oral gavage. Blood samples were collected in heparin-coated tubes from the tail vein at times baseline (0 min), and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after oral gavage. The samples were immediately kept on ice and then spun at 2000x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (plasma) was collected after the spinning. Plasma insulin levels were measured by an ultrasensitive mouse insulin ELISA kit (CrystalChem).

Intraperitoneal insulin tolerance tests

Mice were fasted starting in the morning for 6 h by removing chow and transferring them to fresh cages. The insulin dosages were adjusted for sex differences in insulin sensitivity. Insulin was administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection at a dosage of 0.8 U/kg bodyweight for males and 0.4 U/kg bodyweight for females from Cpeb1 βKO line. Blood glucose measurements were taken at baseline (0 min), and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-IP injection.

Library Preparation and RNA sequencing

Mouse pancreata were perfused with 0.8 mg/mL Collagenase P (Roche), and islets were purified via Histopaque (Sigma) centrifugation before hand-picking. RNA was isolated from Cpeb1 βKO and Cpeb1 KO islets using QIAGEN RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA samples were submitted to the University of Wisconsin-Madison Biotechnology Center for library preparation and sequencing. Each RNA sample was assayed on NanoDrop One Spectrophotometer and Agilent 4200 Tapestation to assess purity and integrity. RNA samples that met Illumina’s “TruSeq Stranded mRNA Reference Guide (Document# 1000000040498 v00, October 2017) quality criteria were prepared for sequencing. Briefly, 500 ng total RNA sample was enriched for mRNA by oligo (dT) selection followed by fragmentation using divalent cations under elevated temperature. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript II RT (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California) and random primers for first-strand cDNA synthesis followed by second-strand synthesis using DNA Polymerase I and then RNAse H for mRNA removal. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, CA), followed by incubation with Klenow DNA Polymerase and Adenine to add an ‘A’ base (Adenine) to the 3’ end of the blunt phosphorylated DNA fragments. DNA fragments were ligated to Illumina unique molecular index (UMI) adapters, which have a single ‘T’ base (Thymine) overhang at their 3’end. Following ligation, cDNA was purified with SPRI beads, and PCR-amplified (13 cycles) with PhusionTM DNA Polymerase using Illumina’s genomic DNA primer set and purified with SPRI beads. Quality and quantity of the finished libraries were assayed on Agilent 4200Tapestation using D1000 Screentape and Qubit HS Quantification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), respectively. cDNA fragments were sequenced for 2 × 150 bp on Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. The sequencing depth of 94 M reads / sample was used to increase the confidence level and accuracy of the poly-A tail length analysis.

Bioinformatic analyses

Bioinformatic analysis of transcriptomic data was assisted by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Biotechnology Center Bioinformatics Core Facility and adhered to recommended ENCODE guidelines and best practices for RNA-Seq (Encode Consortium, 2016). Alignment of adapter-trimmed (Skewer v0.1.123;40 2 × 150 (paired-end; PE) bp strand-specific Illumina reads to the Mus musculus GRCm38.p5 (mm10) genome (assembly accession GCA_000001635.7) was achieved with the Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR v2.5.3a) software41, a splice-junction aware aligner, using annotation provided by Ensembl release 104. Expression estimation was performed with RSEM v1.3.0 (RNASeq by Expectation Maximization;42. To test for differential gene expression among individual group contrasts, expected read counts obtained from RSEM were used as input into edgeR (v3.16.5;43. Inter-sample normalization was achieved with the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM;44 method. Statistical significance of the negative-binomial regression test was adjusted with a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at the 5% level45. Prior to statistical analysis with edgeR, independent filtering was applied and required genes to have a count-per-million (CPM) above k in n samples, where k is determined by minimum read count (10 reads) and by the sample library sizes, where n is determined by the number of biological replicates in each group. The validity of the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR multiple testing procedure was evaluated by inspection of the uncorrected p-value distribution. Intronic alternative polyadenylation (APA) analyses were performed using the R package APAlyzer (v1.2.0) using prebuilt mm10 intronic polyadenylation (IPA) and 3’- most exon regions23. APAs in 3′-UTR regions by calculating the RNA-seq read density (RD) after splitting the transcript 3′-end regions based on annotations obtained from PolyA_DB346. The RD of two regions is used to calculate the Relative Expression (RE) score. The first region (cUTR) is defined between the stop codon and the first polyadenylation site (PAS), while the second region (aUTR) is defined between the first PAS and the last PAS. The RE score is calculated by taking the log2 of the ratio of RDaUTR over RDcUTR. Finally, the function APAdiff() is used to determine the RE difference (RED) and its statistical significance. ‘UP’ indicates that the RD of aUTR in Cpeb1-ablated groups is 5% higher than in the wildtype, while ‘DN’ indicates that the RD of aUTR in Cpeb1-ablated groups is 5% lower than in the wildtype.

Data visualization and functional annotation

t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) plots of scRNA-Seq were generated from Tabula Muris16 and Cpeb1-4 expression in β cells was obtained from our previously published dataset17. Human CPEB1 expression plots were generated from the Human Pancreas Analysis Program (HPAP) consortium database18 using the Elgamal et al. integrated dataset19. Venn diagrams were generated by BioVenn47. Gene ontology enrichment analysis of DEG was performed in TargetMine using the default settings. GO biological processes, Reactome gene sets, and WikiPathways of differential polyadenylation were analyzed on Metascape. GO plots were created using ggplot2 R package. The volcano plots of DEG were made by EnchancedVolcano R package, and the volcano plots of differential polyadenylation length were created within APAlyzer.

Statistical analysis

Statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism except for RNA-seq and poly-A tail analyses. Cpeb1 expression comparison between healthy and development, and between healthy and diabetes, were analyzed using t-test and one-way ANOVA, respectively. Curve differences in glucose tolerance, insulin tolerance, and insulin secretion over time were analyzed using repeated measures two-way ANOVA (time and genotype). Differences in area under the curve (minus baseline) were analyzed using t-test. CD45 + quantification was analyzed by QuPath, and its statistics were analyzed using t-test.
