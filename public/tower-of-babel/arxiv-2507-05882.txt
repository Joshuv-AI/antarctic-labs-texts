Excess dissipation shapes symmetry breaking in non-equilibrium currents Preprint: APS/123-QED
Matteo Sireci, Luca Peliti, Daniel Maria Busiello

Abstract
Most natural thermodynamic systems operate far from equilibrium, developing persistent currents and organizing into non-equilibrium stationary states (NESSs). However, the principles determining how such systems self-organize by breaking equilibrium symmetries to cope with external and internal constraints remain unclear. Here, we establish a general connection between symmetry breaking and dissipation in mesoscopic stochastic systems described by Langevin dynamics. By introducing a geometric framework based on the inverse diffusion matrix, we decompose the velocity field into excess (gradient) and housekeeping (residual) components. This decomposition provides a natural splitting of entropy production, where the excess part measures how the system reorganizes its internal state-space under non-equilibrium conditions, while the housekeeping part quantifies the degree of detailed-balance violation due to external constraints. We derive an exact equality connecting these two parts, and an associated inequality identifying the accessible thermodynamics. We then perform a weak-noise expansion of the stationary solution to systematically characterize the general geometry of the NESS velocity field, enabling a unified classification of non-equilibrium steady states. We apply this framework to a range of systems, from molecular machines to coupled oscillators, showing how symmetry breaking in trajectory space constrains NESS organization. We also extend our approach to systems with multiplicative fluctuations, a challenging uncharted scenario, deriving the connection between the breaking of additional equilibrium symmetries and the curved (space-dependent) metric. Finally, we show that both the NESS velocity field and stationary distribution can be derived through variational functionals based on excess dissipation. This work sheds light on the intimate connection between geometric features, dissipative properties, and symmetry breaking, uncovering a classification of NESSs that reflects how emergent organization reflects physical non-equilibrium conditions. Ultimately, our results suggest that excess entropy maximization can serve as a fundamental organizing principle beyond equilibrium thermodynamics.



I
Introduction

In the natural world, thermodynamic systems are generally away from equilibrium. This particular state might be caused by environmental conditions, such as the presence of multiple thermal baths
busiello2020coarse
, non-conservative forces
seifert2012stochastic
, and odd diffusion
hargus2021odd
, or by internal features, such as many-body interactions
Zhang2019TheEC
and chemical dissipation
rao2016nonequilibrium
. In both cases, currents develop in the system, whether of mass, heat, electricity, or molecules, just to mention some possible observables.
How out-of-equilibrium systems tend to self-organize to cope with the external and internal constraints, eventually reaching a stable non-equilibrium stationary state (NESS), is still not understood under general conditions. In other words, a natural question would be to identify an a priori quantity and criterion to identify the thermodynamic configurations that are more probable in non-equilibrium conditions.
Classical general results along this line, such as Onsager’s minimum excess dissipation principle
Onsager1
;
Onsager2
and the Glansdorff–Prigogine criterion
glansdorff1964general
, have attracted recent attention in various contexts
Bertini2004MinimumDP
;
maes2015revisiting
;
ito2022information
, originating controversial statements about the role that dissipation should play in shaping the steady state of biological systems
baiesi2018life
. In particular, two thermodynamic quantities, named non-adiabatic and excess entropy production, have been the focus of many works as natural candidates to build general variational principles valid arbitrarily far from equilibrium
jiu1984stability
;
hatano2001steady
;
clausius
;
van2010three
;
excess_maes
.
Nevertheless, agreement on a unified and solid physical interpretation of such functionals is still lacking, mainly due to the fact that one needs to know the stationary distribution to derive their explicit form. Yet, a possible solution to this conundrum could arise by studying universal physical properties of non-equilibrium systems. In this respect, the last decades have witnessed the discovery of fluctuation theorems
gallavotti1995dynamical
;
jarzynski1997nonequilibrium
;
kurchan1998fluctuation
;
crooks1999entropy
;
lebowitz1999gallavotti
, universal relations holding arbitrarily far from equilibrium. The core message of these results is that non-equilibrium systems break time-reversal symmetry while preserving a weaker version of it that quantifies dissipation. It is natural to think that the breaking of time symmetry would induce symmetry breaking in other aspects of the system, hinting at the existence of additional universal features. Historically, symmetry-breaking mechanisms are also considered at the heart of the emergence of self-organization in non-equilibrium systems, following the inspiration of Prigogine’s idea of “dissipative structures”
prigogine1967symmetry
;
Anderson1987BrokenSE
;
solvay
. A simple symmetry-breaking phenomenon emerging as a consequence of non-equilibrium conditions is the preferential direction of heat currents between two different thermal baths
Jona_Lasinio_2010
;
lecomte
. Nevertheless, from a broader and more fundamental perspective, how dissipation induces symmetry breaking in the trajectory space of a general stochastic system is a fascinating topic still largely unexplored. In this work, we make a decisive step towards the understanding of several of these open questions by building a bridge between symmetry breaking, the physical meaning of excess dissipation, and the onset of self-organization of non-equilibrium systems reaching a NESS.

I.1
Outline of the work

We focus on continuous-space mesoscopic systems described by Stochastic Thermodynamics
peliti2021stochastic
, a framework encompassing well-known, experimentally realizable examples from molecular machines
seifert2012stochastic
to chemical reaction networks
rao2016nonequilibrium
and colloidal particles
ciliberto2017experiments
.

In Sections
II
–
III
, we present a general framework based on Langevin dynamics with additive noise. In particular, in Section
II
, by adopting the (inverse) diffusion matrix as a natural metric, we formulate thermodynamics in terms of the current velocity and a generalized free energy (
availability
), revealing their geometric and thermodynamic roles. This setting naturally decomposes the velocity into excess (gradient) and housekeeping (residual) parts. While the housekeeping component measures the breaking of detailed balance due to external conditions, the excess one reflects the system’s self-organization, connected to the relative entropy with respect to equilibrium. This leads to a decomposition of entropy production, implying that, at stationarity, its value equals the housekeeping dissipation
minus
the excess one. From these results, we derive an upper bound for the steady-state dissipation and interpret the excess contribution as energy absorbed from the environment to reshape the NESS. In Section
III
, we use general minimization principles to show how thermodynamics shapes symmetry breaking. We generalize the Glansdorff–Prigogine criterion by studying the
non-adiabatic
excess entropy production, determining the dynamics to reach the steady state.
Complementarily, we define an Onsager-like excess variational functional to determine the velocity geometry, once the NESS is given. We demonstrate that both the excess and non-adiabatic functionals split into a term proportional to total entropy production and another related to directional dissipation which drives symmetry breaking. A path-integral analysis further reveals that the housekeeping contribution quantifies the stress–energy tensor asymmetry and is related to cycling trajectories, while excess dissipation selects occupancy of higher-energy states.
We also obtain a variational principle satisfied by
the NESS, namely as the maximal principle of the excess entropy constrained by the value of the Jarzynski-like statistics of the
housekeeping entropy production.
Overall, these three complementary principles reveal that any NESS can be interpreted as the minimally dissipative state consistent with fluxes imposed by external non-equilibrium conditions, highlighting the minimization of excess or non-adiabatic entropy production as potentially more fundamental than the second law of thermodynamics for non-equilibrium systems
van2010three
;
hatano2001steady
;
sekimoto1998langevin
;
ito2022information
;
dechant2022geometric
;
ito_non_linear
;
maes_minimum
.

In Sections
IV
–
VII
, we apply this framework to classify NESS thermodynamics through their geometric structures. Specifically, in Section
IV
, we name
locally closed
a system characterized by closed fluxes in the trajectory space. When also excess fluxes are closed, we name the system
locally balanced
. Finally, system in
local equilibrium
satisfy a generalized Maxwell–Boltzmann stationary distributions and the availability minimum coincides with the equilibrium one. In Section
V
, a small-noise expansion of the adiabatic availability shows that, at lowest order, the adiabatic velocity remains divergence-free, constraining the housekeeping and excess geometry. By increasing the noise strength, the excess terms can develop new thermodynamic fluxes with non-zero divergence through coupling with the heat bath. In Section
VI
, we extend the framework to multiplicative noise, where variable-dependent diffusion induces non-zero curvature in state space, adding a ”Thermophoretic-like” term to the excess velocity and stabilizing high-energy states. In Section
VI.4
, we study the underdamped case in a temperature gradient, emphasizing the role of total energy changes. Finally, Section
VII
analyzes many-body coupled molecular oscillators undergoing synchronization phase transition, revealing that housekeeping dissipation links to control parameters while the excess component arises from the order parameter, shaping a complex velocity geometry reflecting free-energy transduction.

II
Dissipation and geometry of non-equilibrium currents

II.1
Thermodynamics of availability

and current velocity

In this section, we set the mathematical stage by formulating stochastic thermodynamics in a geometric perspective using the inverse diffusion matrix as a metric
Graham1977CovariantFO
. Then, we introduce the main actors: the availability (or non-equilibrium free energy) and the current velocity.

We consider a system in
d
dimensions, whose state is described by the variable
\bm{x}=(x^{i})
, which evolves according to the Langevin equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{F}(\bm{x})+\bm{\xi}(t),

(1)

where
\bm{F}
is an external drift, which may be conservative (i.e., stemming from a potential energy) or non-conservative, and
\bm{\xi}(t)=\left(\xi^{i}(t)\right)
is a zero-mean Gaussian white-noise process which satisfies

\left\langle{\xi^{i}(t)\xi^{j}(t^{\prime})}\right\rangle_{\xi}=2D^{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,d,

(2)

with a given, symmetric and positive-definite diffusion matrix
\mathsf{D}=\left(D^{ij}\right)
. We write the indices as exponents for further convenience and denote by
\left\langle{\ldots}\right\rangle_{\xi}
the average taken with respect to the noise. In the present case, the diffusion matrix is independent of
\bm{x}
, hence the metric is flat.
We define the
diffusive
scalar product
\bm{a}*\bm{b}
between the two vectors
\bm{a}=(a^{i})
and
\bm{b}=(b^{i})
by

\bm{a}*\bm{b}=a^{i}D_{ij}b^{j},

(3)

where
D=(D_{ij})
is the matrix inverse of
\mathsf{D}=\left(D^{ij}\right)
. Throughout the whole manuscript, we use the Einstein convention for summing over repeated indices, one upper and one lower. Thus,
\mathsf{D}^{-1}=D
acts as the metrics in the state space, as already noted in
Graham1977CovariantFO
;
polettini2013generally
. This geometric structure can be seen as a generalization of the
\beta=1/k_{\rm B}T
factor, with the inverse diffusivities entering in the diffusive scalar product. Then, the derivative of any scalar field
\phi(x)
is straightforwardly defined as
\partial_{i}\phi
. Likewise, the
covariant
derivative of a vector field
\bm{w}=(w^{i})
is given by
\partial_{j}w^{i}
.
On the other hand, the
contravariant
derivative of the scalar field
\phi
is given by

\left(\nabla\phi\right)^{i}=D^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi,

(4)

which is often called the gradient of
\phi
. Finally, we consider the divergence of a vector field as the covariant derivative summed with respect to repeated indices, i.e.,
\nabla*\bm{w}=\partial_{i}w^{i}
, which coincides with the scalar product of the gradient and the field. With this formalism, the Langevin equation (
1
) corresponds to the following Fokker-Planck equation for the probability
P
of finding the system at time
t
in the state
\bm{x}
:

\partial_{t}P=-\nabla*\left(\bm{F}\,P-\nabla P\right)=-\nabla*\bm{J},

(5)

where the probability current
\bm{J}=\left(J^{i}\right)
is given by

J^{i}=F^{i}\,P-D^{ij}\partial_{j}P=F^{i}-\left(\nabla P\right)^{i}.

(6)

Since we do not consider boundary-driven systems for the time being, we take boundary conditions for which
P\to 0
for
\left|\bm{x}\right|\to\infty
or for
\bm{x}
approaching the boundary of the allowed state space.

Given a time-independent drift
\bm{F}(\bm{x})
, the system eventually reaches a stationary distribution,
P_{\rm st}
, which can be characterized in terms of an non-equilibrium free energy
\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})
(hereafter called
availability

Callen:450289
):

P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{x})\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})}

(7)

and which satisfies
\partial_{t}P=0
. Then, we define the current velocity
\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)
via the relation

\bm{v}=\frac{\bm{J}}{P},

(8)

that becomes equal to
\bm{v}_{\rm st}(\bm{x})
at stationarity.
In terms of this steady current velocity, the stationarity condition takes the form

\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{st}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{st}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}=\nabla_{v_{\mathrm{st}}}\mathcal{F}.

(9)

The lhs represents the divergence of the stationary velocity, while the rhs represents the derivative of the availability with respect to the local velocity, i.e., the rate of change of
\mathcal{F}
along the current.

Following a standard approach in thermodynamics, we evaluate the time derivative of the average of a generic thermodynamic quantity
A(x,t)
by

\displaystyle d_{t}\langle A\rangle=\langle\bm{v}*\nabla A\rangle+\langle\dot{A}\rangle,

(10)

where
\left\langle{\ldots}\right\rangle
denotes the average over the probability distribution:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\ldots}\right\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}x\;\ldots\,P(x).

(11)

The change induced by the explicit time dependence of
A
is due to driving mechanisms, while the one along the velocity defines the flux of
A
and emerges by averaging the change along a single trajectory over the noise
seifert_entropy
;
busiello2019hyperaccurate
:

\displaystyle A_{\rm flux}=\langle\bm{v}*\nabla A(\bm{x})\rangle=\langle\bm{\dot{x}}*\nabla A(\bm{x})\rangle_{\xi}.

(12)

Note that a positive sign indicate a flux from the system to the environment and viceversa for a negative sign.
As a particular case, if we consider the system Gibbs-Shannon entropy
S_{\rm sys}=-\int\mathrm{d}x\;P\log P
, its time derivative
decomposes into the total and environmental entropy productions that, in our notation, take the following form:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm tot}-\dot{S}_{\rm env},

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}x\;P\,v^{i}D_{ij}v^{j}=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}}\right\rangle\geq 0,

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm env}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}x\;P(x)\,v^{i}D_{ij}F^{j}=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{F}}\right\rangle.

(15)

II.2
The geometry of the velocity field

Eq. (
13
) suggests that the velocity plays a more fundamental, yet underappreciated, role than the current
\bm{J}
. In particular, Eq. (
9
) implies that, while the stationary current is necessarily divergence-free, the stationary velocity is not.
Therefore, we expect
\bm{v}_{\rm st}
to exhibit a richer geometric structure with non-trivial interplay with dissipative thermodynamic quantities that characterize a non-equilibrium steady-state (NESS). Specifically, here we show two orthogonality conditions for the velocity field that represent the first steps of this study.
Following

van2010three
, we start by decomposing the velocity into two contributions, one reflecting the steady non-equilibrium condition (i.e.,
adiabatic
,
\bm{v}_{\rm a}
) and the other the time-dependent drivings or relaxation (i.e.,
non-adiabatic
,
\bm{v}_{\rm na}
):

\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{\rm a}+\bm{v}_{\rm na}\;.

(16)

Given the absence of time-dependent driving, these two contributions are given by

\displaystyle v_{\rm a}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{i}_{\mathrm{st}}=F-D^{ij}\partial_{j}\ln P_{\mathrm{st}}=F+D^{ij}\partial_{j}\mathcal{F},

(17)

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{na}}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D^{ij}\partial_{j}\ln\frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{st}}}=D^{ij}\partial_{j}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{na}}(x,t)\;,

(18)

where we have defined the non-adiabatic availability,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{na}}
.

Then, directly from the stationarity condition, Eq. (
9
), we obtain that the adiabatic and non-adiabatic velocities are on average orthogonal to each other at any time:

\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}}\right\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}x\;P\,v^{i}_{\rm a}D_{ij}v^{j}_{\mathrm{na}}=0,\qquad\forall t.

(19)

Eq. (
19
) implies the standard decomposition of the entropy production into adiabatic and non-adiabatic contributions as well:

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}}\right\rangle=\dot{S}_{\rm a}+\dot{S}_{\mathrm{na}},

(20)

where

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}_{\rm a}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\bm{v}_{\rm a}}\right\rangle\geq 0\;,

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{na}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}}\right\rangle\geq 0\;.

(22)

At the stationary state, the non-adiabatic entropy production vanishes, and thus the adiabatic one yields the total entropy production:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{na}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;,\qquad\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\rm tot}=\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\rm a}\;.

(23)

To derive the second orthogonality condition, let
\phi(\bm{x})
be an arbitrary time-independent scalar field.
From Eq. (
10
), we obtain that the derivative of its average is given by its flux:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\langle{\phi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\nabla\phi}\right\rangle=\phi_{\rm flux}.

(24)

Since its average is constant at stationarity, we obtain

\phi_{\rm flux}^{\mathrm{st}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\nabla\phi}\right\rangle^{\mathrm{st}}=0\;.

(25)

This holds for any
\phi
and in particular for the special choice
\phi=\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, Eq. (
25
) implies that, at the NESS, the adiabatic velocity is orthogonal to the contour lines of the availability:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}\right\rangle^{\mathrm{st}}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm flux}^{\mathrm{st}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\nabla\mathcal{F}}\right\rangle^{\mathrm{st}}=0.

(26)

Finally, from Eqs. (
17
) and (
26
), the drift
\bm{F}
can be naturally decomposed into two (on average) orthogonal parts, one dissipative,
\bm{v}_{\rm a}
, and one conservative,
\nabla\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\bm{F}=\bm{v}_{\rm a}-\nabla\mathcal{F}\;.

(27)

II.3
House-keeping and excess decomposition

of the velocity

The necessary condition for reaching an equilibrium state is detailed balance, or, equivalently, a vanishing adiabatic current,
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{a}}=0
. This implies, from Eq. (
27
), that the force stems solely from the gradient of a Boltzmann distribution,
\bm{F}=\nabla P_{\mathrm{eq}}
. In the presence of only one physical current (we generalize later to more complex scenarios), this condition reduces to:

\displaystyle\partial_{i}F_{j}=\partial_{j}F_{i}

(28)

where
F_{i}=(D^{-1}\bm{F})_{i}
. Eq. (
28
) specifies that the necessary condition for having an equilibrium state, and hence no adiabatic dissipation, is to derive the force
\bm{F}
from a scalar potential that can be identified as the equilibrium distribution. The orthogonality condition in Eq. (
25
) suggests to introduce a generalized detailed balance condition for NESSs by using Eq. (
27
) and splitting dissipative terms breaking detailed balance from those that can be written as gradient of a scalar potential and, as such, that can be “reabsorbed” into the gradient of an effective equilibrium distribution. By doing so, we find a new decomposition for the adiabatic velocity into an
excess
part (indicated by
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}
), containing all the gradient terms and a residual part that we name
house-keeping
(and indicate by
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
):

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}\;.

(29)

The scalar field contributing to the excess adiabatic contribution is named
\mathcal{F}_{\rm ex,a}
. This excess term still satisfies a generalized detailed balance condition as in Eq. (
28
):

\displaystyle v^{i}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{ij}\partial_{j}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},a}

(30)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}D_{jk}v^{k}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{j}D_{ik}v^{k}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}\;.

(31)

As we will see later,
\mathcal{F}_{\rm ex,a}
exactly accounts for the difference in availability induced by non-equilibrium conditions at steady state.

Since the adiabatic velocity only accounts for the stationary contributions, when complemented with the time-dependent part, this new decomposition gives:

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}_{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}}\,+\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}\equiv\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}

(32)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{na}}=\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\;.

(33)

in which the time-dependent excess availability
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{x},t)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}+\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{na}}\;.

(34)

where the nonadiabatic availability is defined by Eq. (
18
).

II.4
Housekeeping and excess dissipation encode external conditions and internal organization

To shed light on the physical meaning of the proposed geometric decomposition, let us start by considering the flux of the average excess availability:

\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle=\langle\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right\rangle+\langle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle\;,

(35)

where the first term on the r.h.s. can be defined as the excess dissipation:

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right\rangle\geq 0\;,

(36)

while the last contribution being the flux of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}
along the direction of
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
. From Eq. (
35
) and the perpendicularity condition in Eq. (
25
), at stationarity we have:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right\rangle^{\mathrm{st}}=-\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\right)_{\mathrm{flux},\mathrm{hk}}^{\mathrm{st}}\geq 0\;.

(37)

Similarly, we can define the housekeeping dissipation as:

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{hk}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}}\right\rangle\geq 0\;.

(38)

By combining Eqs. (
32
), (
38
), and (
36
), we can rewrite the total entropy production as:

\begin{split}\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}&=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex}}+2\langle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle\\
&=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{hk}}+d_{t}\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle+\langle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle\;.\end{split}

(39)

Hence, from Eq. (
36
), we arrive to our first main result:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{hk}}-\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex}}

(40)

implying the following upper limits for the dissipation:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{tot}}\leq\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{hk}}\;,\qquad\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex}}\leq\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{hk}}\;.

(41)

We are now in a position to propose a thermodynamic interpretation of this result. When driven away from equilibrium by external conditions, a system develops dissipative currents, while also potentially modifying its availability with respect to the equilibrium Boltzmann distribution. The housekeeping and excess part of the velocity quantify these two different contributions.
The system is kept out of equilibrium, causing a non-vanishing
\bm{v}_{\rm hk}
. Indeed, if this contribution disappeared, the stationary velocity would have only gradient term and the system would relax to an effective equilibrium distribution. The presence of
\bm{v}_{\rm hk}
results in an energy inflow from the environment which is balanced by the excess dissipation into the heat bath
\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{st}}
, Eq. (
37
). Hence, according to Eq. (
40
), the net dissipation into the environment is given by the house-keeping part, due to non-equilibrium conditions, minus the excess dissipation which allows the system to have a wider distribution than at equilibrium, for instance by populating higher-energy states more than in the corresponding equilibrium scenario.

We remark that the proposed decomposition differs from those in
dechant2022geometric
;
maes2014nonequilibrium
, whereas the one introduced in
hatano2001steady
coincides with the adiabatic splitting considered in this work. The decompositions presented here are motivated by geometric and thermodynamic considerations, and the term “excess” has been adopted in analogy with previous studies on the Onsager excess dissipation functional, whose relevance will become clear later on.

II.5
Non-equilibrium from non-conservative forces

A more transparent physical interpretation of the excess availability stems by the study of the simple case in which non-equilibrium conditions originate from the presence of non-conservative forces,
\bm{f}
(e.g., external fields, shear flows). In this scenario:

\displaystyle F^{i}=f^{i}-\partial^{i}U,\quad D^{ij}=\delta^{ij}T,\quad\nabla^{i}=T\partial^{i}

(42)

with
\partial^{i}=\delta^{ij}\partial_{j}
,
U
is the potential acting on the system, and
T
the temperature of the external bath. We also employ the Einstein relation to link diffusion and temperature to ensure that, when
\bm{f}=\bm{0}
, the system relaxes to the Boltzmann distribution. Here,
\beta=T^{-1}
with the Boltzmann constant set to
1
for simplicity.
From the force decomposition in Eq. (
27
), the adiabatic velocity can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{f}-\partial U+\nabla\mathcal{F}=\bm{f}+\nabla\left(\mathcal{F}-\beta U\right)\;,

(43)

where all gradient terms have been isolated and can be identified with
\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}
. The first contribution coincides with the non-conservative force - the sole source of non-equilibrium in this scenario - and corresponds to
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
. Thereby,

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}=\bm{f}\;,\qquad\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\nabla\left(\mathcal{F}-\beta U\right)=\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}\;,

(44)

with
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}
at stationarity since the non-adiabatic (time-dependent) contribution vanishes. Out of equilibrium, the particle could visit excited states with availability larger then the equilibrium counterpart. Hence, we identify the deviation of
\mathcal{F}
from its equilibrium value at the stationary state with the excess availability.
The adiabatic entropy production is thus given by

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{a}}=\langle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}*\bm{v}_{\rm a}\rangle=\langle\bm{v}*\bm{f}\rangle+\langle\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}\rangle,

(45)

where the first term corresponds to the heat dissipated by the non-conservative force, and the second one to the excess heat (and duly vanishes in the stationary state). In the steady state we have:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\bm{f}\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=\beta\Big(\underbrace{\langle\bm{f}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}}_{\dot{Q}^{\rm out}}-\underbrace{\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}}_{-\dot{Q}^{\rm in}}\Big)\geq 0\;.

(46)

The first term multiplying the inverse temperature can be interpreted as the heat due to the non-conservative force released into the environment, while the second one as the heat absorbed from the non-equilibrium conditions that modifies the system availability.

As a consequence, it is immediate to notice that we can define an excess entropy as follows:

S_{\mathrm{ex}}=-\int\mathrm{d}xP(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{P_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)}=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right\rangle.

(47)

This is the relative entropy with respect to the equilibrium state. As such, it is coordinate and coarse-graining independent, resulting to be a well defined physical observable
Polettini_2012
;
banavar2007maximumrelativeentropyprinciple
;
busiello2019entropy
. In this context, the equilibrium distribution acts as a
prior
with which the NESS has to be compared. As we will see in the next section, different choices of the prior might lead to different identification of excess and housekeeping contributions, especially in complex cases in which more than one physical current is present. This is somehow similar to what happens in electromagnetism, where a gauge-potential must be fixed (we will comment more on this point in the conclusions).

II.6
Non-equilibrium from multiple baths

Another paradigmatic yet more intricate scenario to induce non-equilibrium conditions involves systems coupled to multiple baths, such as the Brownian gyrator and a Brownian particle in contact with two thermal reservoirs. In these cases
the identification of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}
becomes more subtle since there are multiple possible equilibrium distributions, i.e., one for each bath,
P_{\mathrm{eq}}^{T_{i}}\propto\exp(-U/T_{i})
, where
U
is the potential. However, interaction terms in
U
between different components might result into an interaction between different baths. Indeed, in general
U=\sum_{i}U_{i}+U_{\mathrm{int}}
, where
U_{i}
depends only on the
i
-th component.
For simplicity, we assume that each degree of freedom
i
is coupled to a different bath at temperature
T_{i}
, i.e.,
D^{ij}=\delta^{ij}\sqrt{T_{i}T_{j}}
.
The stationary velocity for each component can be readily obtained as before:

v^{i}_{\mathrm{st}}=-\partial^{i}U+\nabla^{i}\mathcal{F}=-\partial^{i}U_{\rm int}+\nabla^{i}\left(\mathcal{F}-\frac{U_{i}}{T_{i}}\right)\;.

(48)

The second term is a gradient and, as such, can be identified with the contribution from the excess availability. Indeed, it accounts for the difference between
\mathcal{F}
and the equilibrium case, where no interaction exists between components and each one relaxes to the Boltzmann distribution associated with its respective bath. As a consequence, the first term coincides with the house-keeping velocity and, once again, only contains external contributions triggering non-equilibrium conditions. Thus:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}=-\partial U_{\mathrm{int}}\;,\qquad\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\nabla\Bigg(\underbrace{\mathcal{F}-\sum_{i}\frac{U_{i}}{T_{i}}}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\Bigg)\;.

(49)

In this case, it is manifest how the choice of the reference equilibrium distribution change the identification of the housekeeping and excess contributions. Another possibility would have been to compare the NESS with the equilibrium distribution where
T_{i}=T
for all baths, in the presence of an interaction potential. In such a case, the component of the velocity encoding the external non-equilibrium conditions would have encoded the fact that baths have different temperatures.

We also generalize our framework to include multiple thermal baths acting simultaneously on the same degrees of freedom and generating different currents. In this case, since there is not only one physical current at play, Eq. (
28
) does not coincide with the equilibrium condition, hence the reference equilibrium distribution does not naturally appear in the stationary velocity and has to be set a priori. As a concrete example, and for simplicity of notations, we consider a one-dimensional Brownian particle attached to two baths at different temperatures and subject to two corresponding potentials
parrondo_ratchet
;
van2010three
:

\displaystyle\dot{x}=-\partial_{x}U_{1}+\sqrt{2T_{1}}\xi_{1}(t)-\partial_{x}U_{2}+\sqrt{2T_{2}}\xi_{2}(t)\;.

(50)

The system reaches a generalized Boltzmann distribution with an effective temperature:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{st}}\propto e^{-\mathcal{F}},\qquad\mathcal{F}=\frac{U_{1}+U_{2}}{T_{1}+T_{2}}\;.

(51)

In this particular condition, the total current is zero, as the contributions from each bath balance each other. Moreover, the absence of interactions (or any other external non-equilibrium source) implies that
v_{\rm a}
stems purely from excess terms from a geometrical perspective:

\begin{split}v_{\rm a}&=v_{1,\mathrm{ex}}+v_{2,\mathrm{ex}}=0\;,\\
v_{\alpha,\mathrm{ex}}&=-\partial_{x}U_{\alpha}+T_{\alpha}\partial_{x}\mathcal{F}\qquad\alpha=1,2\;.\end{split}

(52)

However, when we set a reference equilibrium distribution, say
P_{1}\sim\exp(-U_{1}/T_{1})
, the presence of the second bath can be identified as the non-equilibrium driving of the system and, as such, it has to reflect into the housekeeping part of velocity:

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=v_{1,\mathrm{ex}},\quad v_{\rm hk}=v_{2,\mathrm{ex}}.

(53)

Hence, the total dissipation is given by the excess contribution associated with thermal conduction:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm a}=\dot{S}_{\rm ex}=\sum^{2}_{i=1}\left\langle{\frac{v_{i,\mathrm{ex}}^{2}}{T_{i}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{v_{\mathrm{ex}}^{2}}{T_{1}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\frac{v_{\mathrm{hk}}^{2}}{T_{2}}}\right\rangle\;.

(54)

We emphasize here that a purely geometrical interpretation of
v
would give only excess contributions. However, a physically informed interpretation of our results in the presence of multiple currents, based on Eq. (
47
), leads to one of the possible correct housekeeping/excess decompositions (depending on the equilibrium prior).

III
Towards a NESS: dissipation and symmetry breaking

III.1
Revisiting Prigogine and Onsager principles

We have presented how the geometric properties of the stationary velocity reflect non-trivial thermodynamic properties of the NESS. Now, we highlight how such a non-equilibrium state is reached. To answer to this question, we consider the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
P(\bm{x},t)
and the stationary distribution
P_{\rm st}(\bm{x})
that is known to be a Lyapunov function of the dynamics
schnakenberg1976network
. It is defined as:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\bm{x}~P(\bm{x},t)\log\left(\frac{P(\bm{x},t)}{P_{\rm st}(\bm{x})}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle-S_{\mathrm{ex}}=-\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm na}\rangle\;.

where equalities follow from Eq. (
47
). Its time-derivative has to be non-positive, vanishes at stationarity, and is equal to the non-adiabatic entropy production (see SI):

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}_{t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{na}}\geq 0\;.

(56)

By employing the adiabatic and the housekeeping decomposition in Eqs. (
20
) and (
29
):

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}_{t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{na}}=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}-\dot{S}_{\mathrm{a}}

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}-\langle\bm{v}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle-\langle\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{na}}}\geq 0\;,

where
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{na}}
is a functional that is minimized when
P=P_{\mathrm{st}}
and decreases during the system evolution towards stationarity. Its expression reveals how the geometrical properties of
\bm{v}_{\rm a}
enter the game. To have a clear physical understanding of the result, let us first note that in an equilibrium condition the criterion trivially corresponds to the minimum entropy production, or entropy maximization. Away from equilibrium, Eq. (
57
) states that, while reaching a NESS, the system has a non-negative non-adiabatic entropy production, equivalent to the Glansdorff-Prigogine criterion.
Given that in non-equilibrium conditions both total and adiabatic contributions are positive, Eq. (
57
) can be interpreted as the tendency to minimize the entropy production compatibly with maximizing the adiabatic dissipation, composed by the sum of the house-keeping and excess fluxes, i.e., aligning
\bm{v}
with
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
.
Once a NESS is reached, we would like to understand its associated geometric properties of the adiabatic velocity as resulting from a general variational principle. To do so, we construct a functional which is reminiscent of the Onsager’s excess dissipation functional, recently generalized to non-equilibrium conditions in the context of MFT
Onsager1
;
MFT
;
Bertini2004MinimumDP
. From Eq.(
57
), it is clear that the only part of the velocity contributing to the (unavoidable) stationary dissipation is
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
. Hence, we consider that the velocity would try to minimize the excess dissipation, i.e., those not stemming from
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
, with the constrain to satisfy the orthogonality condition, Eq. (
25
). To implement this principle, we construct a variational functional
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}
for a generic velocity field
\bm{v}(x)
with a given
P(\bm{x})
implementing the constraint via a Lagrange multiplier:

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}&=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\left(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}\right)*\left(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}\right)}\right\rangle-\lambda\left\langle{\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}}\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex}}-\lambda d_{t}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}}\right\rangle\;.\end{split}

(58)

where
\lambda
is the multiplier to be determined, Considering
P(\bm{x})
as fixed, by taking the variation with respect to
\bm{v}
to zero yields the optimal value
\bar{v}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{v}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;\Leftrightarrow\;\bm{\bar{v}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\lambda\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}.

(59)

The derivative with respect to
\lambda
gives the constraint:

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\left\langle{\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle=0

(60)

that when evaluated at the extremal values
\bar{\bm{v}}
and
\bar{\lambda}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}({\bm{\bar{v}}})=\left\langle{\bm{\bar{v}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;\bar{\lambda}=-\frac{\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle}{\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\;.

with the adiabatic excess entropy defined as

\dot{S}_{\rm ex,a}=\left\langle{\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\rm ex,a}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\rm ex,a}}\right\rangle\geq 0

(61)

in analogy with Eq. (
36
). By evaluating the functional in the minimum
(\bar{\bm{v}},\bar{\lambda})
, we obtain
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}(\bar{\bm{v}},\bar{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle^{2}}{\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex}}}\geq 0\;,

(62)

which, when evaluated at the correct stationary state
\-logP=\mathcal{F}
gives:

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}=1,\quad\bar{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}},\quad\;,

(63)

is equal to (half) the adiabatic excess entropy production,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\mathrm{st}}(\bar{\bm{v}},\bar{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\dot{S}^{\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}\;.

(64)

Hence, the correct stationary velocity minimizes
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}
while satisfying the orthogonality condition.

Furthermore, from the results of Sec.
II.3
,
\bar{\lambda}
can be interpreted as the ratio between the input and output excess heat. These contributions must balance each other at stationarity, so that
\bar{\lambda}=1
. Notice also that, while Eq. (
58
) is inspired by the Onsager quadratic excess dissipation functional, at variance with it, it determines just the stationary velocity and not the full dynamics. Indeed, Eq. (
58
) is not an explicit time derivative and is not bound to be a monotonic function of time. This is true just at equilibrium, where

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}_{t}D_{\rm KL}(t)=\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{na}}=2~\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}\;,

(65)

and the dynamics is equivalent to a variational minimization of the dissipation. Finally, Eq. (
58
) can be rewritten in a way that resembles Eq. (
57
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}}\right\rangle

(66)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}*(\bm{v}_{hk}+\lambda\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}})}\right\rangle\;.

This expression clarifies that minimizing
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ex}}
with respect to
\bm{v}
amounts to minimizing the velocity modulus, i.e., the total entropy production, while maximizing the alignment between
\bm{v}
and
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
, coherently with the intuition stemming from Eq. (
57
). The component
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
seems to act as an “external field” on the system, imposing a symmetry breaking along its geometry. While
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}
can be thought as real external field stemming from the external conditions,
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex}}
emerges from the system itself. Nevertheless, at the end of this section, we show that the excess availability can be directly derived by averaging over stochastic trajectories.

III.2
Symmetry breaking in the trajectory space

In our framework, we can improve the physical interpretation of the principles in Eqs. (
58
) and (
57
) to reveal their relationship with symmetry breaking phenomena in the trajectory space.
The first term in Eq. (
57
) only dictates that the system tends to minimize its total dissipation, as for equilibrium relaxation phenomena. The second contribution emerges only out of equilibrium, hence encoding an additional dissipation stemming from the properties of the adiabatic velocity, and coincides with the entropy fluxes associated with the housekeeping and excess part of the force.
Here, we show that it accounts for a tendency to circulate, breaking a parity symmetry holding at equilibrium and leading to a stationary dissipation into the environment. Considering a system governed by Eq. (
1
), the probability of observing a trajectory
\Gamma
– starting in
\bm{x}_{0}
at
t=0
and arriving in
\bm{x}_{\rm f}
- is given by

P(\Gamma)=\underbrace{e^{-\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)/2}}_{P(\Gamma|x_{0})}P(x_{0})

(67)

where
\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)
is the Onsager-Machlup action
cugliandolo2017rules
which, using the Stratonovich prescription, reads

\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{\bm{x}}-\bm{F}\right)*\left(\dot{\bm{x}}-\bm{F}\right)+\nabla*\bm{F}\right]\;,

(68)

in which all quantities have to be evaluated along
\Gamma
.
Eq. (
68
) can be rewritten as the time integral of a lagrangian,
A
, and it can be divided into time-symmetric
A_{\rm s}
, stemming from an “energy-stress tensor”, and time-antisymmetric parts,
A_{\rm a}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tau A,\quad A=D_{ij}A_{\rm s}^{ij}+D_{ij}A_{\rm a}^{ij}

(69)

\displaystyle A_{\rm s}^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{j}+\frac{1}{2}F^{i}F^{j}+\partial_{k}D^{ik}F^{j}

(70)

\displaystyle A_{\rm a}^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dot{x}^{i}F^{j}.

(71)

The detailed balance condition in Eq. (
28
) can thus be rewritten as a condition for the stress-energy tensor to be symmetric with respect to indices:

\displaystyle w_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A_{\rm s})_{ij}-(A_{\rm s})_{ji}=\partial_{i}F_{j}-\partial_{j}F_{i}

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(v_{\mathrm{hk}})_{j}-\partial_{j}(v_{\mathrm{hk}})_{i}\;,

where
w_{ij}
is the “torque” tensor, i.e., the antisymmetric part of the stress-energy tensor. This field encodes the non-conservation of the angular momentum, hence parity symmetry breaking, and coincide with the vorticity field
\bm{w}
in
2
and
3
dimensions. The dissipation along a trajectory
\Gamma
,
s(\Gamma)
, customarily quantified by the ratio between the probability of
\Gamma
and its time reversal
\tilde{\Gamma}

seifert2012stochastic
, is associated with the antisymmetric part of the action. To deepen our understanding, consider that in a closed trajectory
\Gamma_{\circ}
, i.e.,
x_{0}=x_{\rm f}
, we have:

s(\Gamma_{\circ})=\log\frac{P(\Gamma_{\circ})}{\tilde{P}(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\circ})}=\int_{\Gamma_{\circ}}d\bm{x}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}\equiv s_{\mathrm{hk}}(\Gamma_{\circ})\;.

(73)

In
2
and
3
dimensions, we can further apply Stokes’ theorem and thus rewrite the dissipation as the flux of the vorticity field
\bm{w}
across the surface
\bm{\Sigma}(\Gamma_{\circ})
defined by the closed trajectory:

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{hk}}(\Gamma_{\circ})=\int_{\bm{\Sigma}(\Gamma_{\circ})}d\bm{\Sigma}~\bm{w}\;.

(74)

These results establish a clear connection between the propensity of performing closed trajectories in a preferential direction, i.e., the breaking of the equilibrium parity symmetry, and the house-keeping velocity that breaks the space symmetries in the stress-energy tensor. Furthermore, these observations reinforce the validity of our identification of housekeeping contribution as stemming from the non-equilibrium conditions, and the excess availability
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}
as capturing pseudo-equilibrium modifications. Indeed, we notice that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}
does not contribute to the dissipation along any closed trajectory
\Gamma_{\circ}
.

Furthermore, if we look at the dissipation along an open trajectory, we note that the gradient part of
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
can give an additional contribution proportional to the initial and final state, indicating an additional directional preference:

\displaystyle\log\frac{P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{x}_{0})P(\Gamma|x_{0})}{P_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{x}_{f})\tilde{P}(\tilde{\Gamma}|x_{f})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{hk}}(\Gamma)

(75)

\displaystyle\log\frac{P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{x}_{0})P(\Gamma|x_{0})}{P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{x}_{f})\tilde{P}(\tilde{\Gamma}|x_{f})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{hk}}(\Gamma)+\Delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}

(76)

where
\Delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{x}_{f})-\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}(\bm{x}_{0})
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mathcal{F}-\beta U
. Eq. (
76
) explains the role on single trajectories of the housekeeping and excess velocities: The first one selects the probability of producing a fluctuation from the equilibrium state over its time reversal, while for the equivalent quantity in a NESS also the excess is necessary.
Indeed, it tends to select a trajectory over its time-reversed if it leads to a increase in excess availability When averaged over trajectories, initial, and final states
seifert_entropy
;
busiello2020entropy
;
seifert2012stochastic
, the two contributions on the rhs of Eq. (
76
) lead exactly the last two terms of Eq. (
57
). Hence, when approaching the NESS, the system minimizes the total dissipation compatibly with maximizing the breaking of two symmetries in the trajectory space: one related to the direction of rotation and associated with the house-keeping velocity; the other determining the phase-space occupation imposed by non-equilibrium conditions and connected to the excess availability flux. These findings reveal the profound physical meaning of the main results of this work, also providing a broader context for recent results on a topological fluctuation theorem
mahault2022topological
and gauge symmetries in thermodynamics
Polettini_2012
;
polettini_dice
;
wang_gauge
.

III.3
A variational principle for NESS and trajectory-dependent estimation of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}

Following our findings, we can build a variational principle to derive the NESS distribution that only requires some information on single-trajectory thermodynamics. This result resembles a non-equilibrium version of the maximum entropy approach for equilibrium systems
jaynes
.

We propose the Jarzynski statistics of the house-keeping entropy production over trajectories:

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{hk}}(x)=\log\langle e^{-s_{\mathrm{hk}}(\Gamma)}\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\Gamma}(x),

(77)

that is exponential of minus the housekeeping entropy production averaged over all trajectories starting at
x
and lasting till
t
, weighted by the equilibrium distribution
\langle\cdot\rangle^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\Gamma}(x)=\int\mathrm{d}\Gamma\cdot P(\Gamma)P_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)
. The excess entropy functional subjected to a constrain that imposes a fixed value
\Theta
for the average of
\theta_{\mathrm{hk}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ex}}=S_{\mathrm{ex}}-\lambda\left(\langle\theta_{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle-\Theta\right),

(78)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier. The maximization of Eq. (
78
) gives

P(x)=P_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)e^{-\theta_{hk}(x)}\;,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex}}(x)=\theta_{hk}(x)\;,

(79)

consistently with past and recent results in non-equilibrium physics
mclennan1989introduction
;
komatstu_ness
;
Colangeli_2011
. Equivalently to Eq. (
57
)—whose writing requires prior knowledge of the steady-state distribution—this variational principle tells that the stationary state is the one of maximum relative entropy compatible with a given adiabatic excess availability. Crucially, Eq. (
79
) provides an operative way to quantify the excess availability from an average of the housekeeping dissipation over trajectories.

\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}=-\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}\;.

\displaystyle-\bm{v}^{*}_{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}=-\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=0=\nabla*v_{\mathrm{hk}}.

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}=\beta\nabla U^{*}=0\;.

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{*}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}^{*}_{\mathrm{hk}}=0,

) implies that the mean value of the excess availability in any phase space region containing an extreme point must be zero.
In synthesis, in local equilibrium systems, the geometry of the velocities is fully captured by a solenoidal house-keeping and an harmonic excess contribution:

\displaystyle(v_{\mathrm{hk}})^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{j}B^{ij}\;,\quad B^{ij}=-B^{ji},

(89)

\displaystyle(v_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}})^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{ij}\partial_{j}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}\;,\quad\partial_{i}D^{ij}\partial_{i}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=0\;.

(90)

Eqs. (
89
) and (
87
) together signal that local equilibrium conditions support the existence of a current, induced by external conditions, that breaks rotational symmetry, while not allowing the system to stably occupy excited states at stationarity. Indeed, as we will show in the next section through two examples, local equilibrium systems can either occupy the same state space as in equilibrium, i.e.,
S_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=0
, that we hence define as
pseudo-equilibrium
, or explore excited states via non-equilibrium fluctuations while having the same minimum as in equilibrium, i.e.,
S_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}>0
.

Driven particle in a circular potential

Consider a

It is instructive to write the Langevin equations describing this system in cylindrical coordinates

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma(\rho-\rho^{*})+\frac{D}{\rho}+\sqrt{2D}~\xi_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle\frac{f}{\rho}+\frac{\sqrt{2D}}{\rho}\xi_{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle-\gamma z+\sqrt{2D}~\xi_{z}(t)

In this example, parity symmetry breaking is explicitly linked to the geometry of non-conservative driving, enabling an intuitive identification of adiabatic dissipation that tends to be maximized in a NESS, following

V
Exploring far from equilibrium with a small noise expansion

V.1
Weak-noise limit

Following the pioneering works of Graham and Tél
graham1984existence
;
graham1984weak
;
graham1985weak
, together with recent developments in Macroscopic Fluctuation Theory
MFT
;
Garrido_quasi_pot
;
falasco2023macroscopic
, we propose here that there exist a single noise amplitude
\sigma
that controls all the elements of the diffusion matrix, so that in the weak-noise regime, the stationary probability distribution exhibits the following limiting behavior:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{st}}\asymp e^{-\mathcal{F}^{(0)}/\sigma},

(96)

where
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
is the rescaled zeroth order availability, that constitute also the large deviation or rate function of the system. Furthermore, in this context the availability
\mathcal{F}_{\rm a}
is a quasi-potential that can be rigorously defined and estimated via path-integral methods
MFT
;
falasco2023macroscopic
.
From Eq. (
96
), the adiabatic velocity is then composed by a contribution from the zeroth order availability, i.e.,
\bm{v}_{\rm a}=\bm{F}+\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
, where, with abuse of notation, we identify
\nabla^{i}=D^{ij}\partial_{j}
, without the factor
\sigma
that rescales the diffusion matrix.
By taking advantage of this general form for the stationary distribution, we uncover the geometrical properties of the velocity
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}
in the limit of weak noise, i.e., at the leading order in
\sigma
.
Plugging Eq. (
96
) into the Fokker-Planck equation, expanding up to the zeroth order in
\sigma
, and equating terms of the same order, we get two consistency equations at stationarity (see appendix
A.1
for details):

\bm{v}_{\rm a}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}=0\;,\qquad\nabla*\bm{v}_{\rm a}=0\;.

(97)

The first relation states that the
v_{\rm a}
must be tangent to the quasi-potential contour lines, here identified with
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
, while the second one dictates that it also has to be a divergence-free field. These conditions imply that, in the minimum of the availability, the current is zero. Following the classification in Sec.
IV
, we conclude that, any non-equilibrium system (with additive noise) is locally closed in the weak noise limit, as also remarked by the last line of Table
1
.
Yet, by employing the house-keeping/excess decomposition,

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}\;,

(98)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}-\beta\nabla U\;,

(99)

we see that the weak noise regime is more general than the locally balanced and locally equilibrium scenarios, since there are no constraints on each component of the velocity.

Notice that, since the form of
P_{\mathrm{st}}
in Eq. (
96
) holds without correction in
\sigma
at equilibrium, i.e., with
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{\rm a}/\sigma=\beta U
, every system exhibiting a weak-noise-like stationary distribution independently of the value of
\sigma
is in local equilibrium, according to the classification in Table
1
. Indeed, on top of being locally closed, in these systems the extreme points of
\mathcal{F}
and
U
have to coincide for any
\sigma
.

V.2
Beyond weak noise: an additional gradient term in the velocity

When pushing the system beyond the weak-noise regime, the properties of the velocities are expected to change. This scenario is particularly relevant for mesoscopic thermodynamic systems in which
\sigma
can be identified with the temperature of the bath. Hence, starting from the rate function in Eq. (
96
), we consider an additional term in the expansion of the availability:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\rm a}\approx\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathcal{F}^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}^{(1)}\;.

(100)

From the decomposition of the velocity in Eq. (
27
), in addition to the zeroth order term, we obtain a first order contribution in the form of a gradient (see Appendix
A.1
for details):

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}+\sigma\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}

(101)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{F}+\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}+\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}^{(0)}

(102)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}=\nabla\mathcal{F}_{\rm a}-\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}/\sigma=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}^{(1)}.

(103)

Given that
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
is the quasi-potential or large-deviation function of the system,
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
can be identified as the excess availability that vanishes in the weak-noise limit. Therefore, the velocity stemming from its gradient can be identified as an additional excess contribution.
By solving perturbatively for the stationary solution of Fokker-Planck equation and equating the terms of order
\sigma^{-1}
, we discover that the zeroth order velocity is still orthogonal to the gradient of the quasi-potential:

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{\rm a}=0\;.

(104)

From the terms at following order,
\sigma^{0}
, we see that its divergence is not zero:

\displaystyle\nabla*\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}+\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\;.

(105)

Finally, the conditions emerging at order
\sigma
determine that the first order velocity has a positive divergence:

\displaystyle\nabla*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}=\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}\geq 0\;,

(106)

thus implying that this term will generate repellors.

V.3
Geometric and thermodynamics properties beyond weak noise: the effect of the reservoir

At stationarity, the time-derivative of the average of the quasi-potential part of the availability has to vanish,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=0\;,

(107)

therefore

\displaystyle\langle\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma\left\langle{\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

(108)

by using Eq. (
104
), we get a new orthogonality condition:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=0\;.

(109)

This relation implies that the first order velocity, which consists of only an excess term, is on average orthogonal to the contour lines of the quasi-potential. Similarly, by looking at the excess availability
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
, we find that its average change along the zeroth order velocity balances the first order velocity divergence. In fact,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle{\mathcal{F}^{(1)}}\right\rangle_{st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=0

(110)

implies that

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma\left\langle{\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

(111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}\;,

where we used the fact that
\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
and Eq. (
106
) for the last equality. Notice that, by using Eq. (
110
) and Eq. (
105
), the relationship in Eq. (
111
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=-\sigma\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}\leq 0\;,

(112)

that can be obtained by directly averaging the divergence of the total velocity, and remembering that
\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=0
at stationarity. These conditions, taken together, imply that the average fluxes of
\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}
need to match, with a consequent non-zero flux of the first-order availability that shifts the extreme points of the system. This flux, shown in Eq. (
111
), can be interpreted as emerging from local couplings with the reservoir that have been ignored in the weak-noise regime since any system in this limiting scenario results to be locally closed.
These geometric properties have an impact on the thermodynamics of the system and, in particular, they affect the excess-housekeeping decomposition of the entropy production. As a first observation, we notice that the divergence of the excess velocity gets modified by the coupling with the reservoir encoded in
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
:

\displaystyle\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\nabla*\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}+\sigma\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}.

(113)

while the housekeeping contribution stays unchanged because of Eq. (
103
). This substantiates the fact that, beyond the weak-noise limit, the system is no longer locally closed since Eq. (
80
) cannot be satisfied at any order in
\sigma
. This result highlights the crucial role of the noise amplitude in enabling the identification of a coupling with the reservoir through the geometric properties of the system, i.e., from locally closed to open by increasing
\sigma
.

Another effect of going beyond the weak-noise regime is that the excess entropy acquires new terms:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}=\dot{S}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}+\dot{S}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}+2\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(0)}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(1)}}\right\rangle\;,

(114)

where we introduced

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(0)}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(0)}}\right\rangle,\quad\dot{S}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}=\sigma\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(1)}*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(1)}}\right\rangle\;.

By using Eq. (
106
), it is immediate to realize that the first order excess entropy production measures the flow of its corresponding velocity:

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{(1),\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}=\sigma\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}^{(1)}_{\rm ex,\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

(115)

From Eq. (
112
), expressing the divergence of
\bm{v}^{(1)}
from Eq. (
111
), and then using the excess/housekeeping decomposition for the zeroth-order velocity, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{v}_{\mathrm{hk}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}-S^{(1),\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}

(116)

This equation specializes Eq. (
84
) for open systems at the first order in
\sigma
. It clearly shows that the deviation of availability concavity from equilibrium are dictated not only by the housekeeping velocity, reflecting external non-equilibrium constrains, but also by an additional excess entropy production term stemming from the system-reservoir thermodynamic coupling.

As a last point, from the definition of the excess entropy production, after simple manipulation, we get:

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{\mathrm{st}}=\dot{S}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}^{(0),\mathrm{st}}-\dot{S}^{(1),\mathrm{st}}_{\mathrm{ex},\rm a}-2\left\langle{v_{\mathrm{hk}}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}\geq 0\;.

(117)

Hence, depending on the magnitude of the house-keeping flux of
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
, the magnitude of the excess dissipation can change, while always satisfying the global constraints in Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
).

We start from the following general Langevin equation:

\dot{\bm{x}}=\bm{F}(x)+G(x)\bm{\xi}(t)\;,

(122)

with
\bm{\xi}
a Gaussian white noise with zero mean and correlation matrix
\sigma\mathbb{1}
(the identity matrix), where
\sigma
controls noise amplitude. Here,
G
is a state-dependent matrix, hence the total diffusion matrix is
D=\sigma G^{T}G
. For
G=\mathbb{1}
, this system reduces to the additive noise case. This new scenario extends and generalizes our approach by considering again the inverse of the diffusion matrix to be the metric of our space, which is now curved because of the space-dependence of
D
. Yet, by performing a coordinate change, one can often find alternative flat metrics in a transformed space.
Following the classic works by Graham
Graham1977CovariantFO
;
GRAHAM_itp
, and recent deep analysis by Polettini
polettini2013generally
, we use standard tools of differential geometry to recast the Fokker-Planck equation in a fully covariant form (i.e., coordinate independent), see Appendix
A.2
for details. First, we need to upgrade the standard derivatives to covariant ones:

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}u^{i}=\partial_{j}u^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}u^{k},

(123)

where
\Gamma
is the Levi-Civita connection, i.e. the tensor that encodes the effect of the curvature on the derivative, while the first contribution measures the transport in the tangent space (i.e., the usual phase space that one would analyze). Recall that the covariant derivative of a scalar field coincides with the partial derivative.
The contravariant derivative, or gradient, is obtained from the covariant derivative:
\nabla^{i}v^{k}=D^{ij}\nabla_{j}v^{k}
. As a consequence, the divergence now depends on the diffusion matrix determinant
|D|
:

\displaystyle\nabla*\bm{v}=\nabla_{i}v^{i}=\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{v^{i}}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)\;.

(124)

By defining the invariant probability Q and the invariant measure
d\Omega
, i.e.,
Q=P\sqrt{|D|}
, and
d\Omega=dx/\sqrt{|D|}
, one can rewrite the Fokker-Planck equation in a fully covariant form, interpreting the Langevin equation with the Stratonovich (
\alpha=0
) or Ito (
\alpha=1
) prescription:

\partial_{t}Q=-\nabla_{i}J^{i},\quad J^{i}=\tilde{F}^{i}Q-\sigma\nabla^{i}Q\;.

(125)

In this expression, the force is modified by a term that we call diffusive velocity,
\bm{v}_{D}
:

\tilde{F}^{i}=F^{i}+v_{\rm D}^{i}\;,\quad v_{\rm D}^{i}=D^{ij}\omega_{j}-\alpha\partial_{j}G^{i}_{k}G^{j}_{k}\;,

(126)

that is composed by a vector field
\bm{\omega}
that measures the breaking of detailed balance condition on the diffusive force
G
(that is satisfied when
G
can be written as the derivative of a vector field),

\omega_{j}=G^{l}_{k}\left(\partial_{j}G^{k}_{l}-\partial_{l}G^{k}_{j}\right)\;,

(127)

and by the Ito phoretic term,
G^{T}\partial G
.
This modification of the force field directly implies also a change in the velocity decomposition:

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{a}}=\bm{\tilde{F}}-\nabla Q=\bm{F}-\nabla Q+\bm{v}_{D}.

(128)

where the diffusive (geometry-driven) velocity emerges.

VI.2
Geometric and thermodynamics properties of multiplicative fluctuations

In the case of multiplicative fluctuations, equilibrium cannot generally be reached, making the identification of a potential energy less clear. Nevertheless, the zero current condition implies the following detailed balance relationship for the modified force in Eq. (
126
):

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\tilde{F}_{j}=\partial_{j}\tilde{F}_{i}\;.

(129)

It is then trivial to see that, once again, any gradient field does not break this condition. Therefore, it can be collected into the excess velocity and interpreted as a contribution to a pseudo-equilibrium distribution, i.e., a modification of the internal organization of system’s states. Yet, the diffusive velocity has a complex structure that makes challenging the general identification of the excess and housekeeping terms, along with their geometric properties. This is due mainly to the presence of
\bm{\omega}
that introduces cross dependencies into the fluctuations.

Let us investigate two different scenarios. In the first one, the diffusive force
G
can be derived by a generic potential
G_{ik}=\partial_{i}\psi_{k}
, so that
\bm{\omega}
vanishes and it is always possible to find a flat set of coordinates (called
harmonic
), i.e., the system can be mapped into an equivalent one with additive noise by a suitable change of variables
Graham1977CovariantFO
;
polettini2013generally
. Focusing on the Ito prescription for simplicity, in this simple setting the diffusive velocity turns out to be a gradient

\displaystyle v_{D}^{i}=\alpha\partial^{i}\left(\partial_{j}\psi_{k}\partial^{j}\psi_{l}\delta^{kl}\right)=v_{\mathrm{ex},\rm D}^{i}\;.

(130)

Hence, it can be absorbed into the excess part of the velocity and does not contribute to the breaking of detailed balance, Eq. (
129
). An intuitive understanding comes from considering a system subjected to a thermal gradient along each direction,
T_{i}(x_{i})

VANKAMPEN1988673
;
celani2012anomalous
. If the gradients cause independent fluctuations on the system, the diffusion matrix is diagonal
D=G^{T}G={\rm diag}(T_{i}(x_{i}))
and
G_{ii}=\partial_{i}\psi(x_{i})
. Therefore, the velocity is a “phoretic” term:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{D}=\alpha\nabla\log|D|\;\to\;v^{i}_{D}=\alpha\partial_{i}T_{i}\left(x_{i}\right)\;,

(131)

and directly appears in the definition of the covariant divergence:

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}v^{i}=\partial_{i}v^{i}-v^{i}(v_{\rm D})_{i}\;.

(132)

By considering the noise expansion for the adiabatic availability, we obtain a covariant version of the equations in Sec.
V.2
with this phoretic term entering in the first order velocity
\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}+\bm{v}_{\rm D}
(see Appendix
A.3
). As a result, the
first order excess dissipation is given by the
divergence of
\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\rm a}
minus the flux of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
along the direction of the thermophoretic drift, that can finally be written as the velocity divergence in tangent space:

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{S}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\rm a}*\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\rm a}}\right\rangle_{\rm st}

(133)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla*\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}-\left\langle{\bm{v}_{\rm D}*\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}=\left\langle{\partial*\bm{\tilde{v}}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{st}}\geq 0

\displaystyle;.

(134)

We can conclude that the system is open and the phoretic (geometry-driven) velocity will drive the system to stably occupy “excited” states.

Let us briefly discuss the more general case in which
\omega\neq 0
. Here,
\bm{v}_{D}
will contribute both to the housekeeping and excess velocities, but identifying each contribution becomes a difficult task. We leave this interesting but challenging case for future studies.

VI.3
Non-adiabatic functional in the presence of multiplicative fluctuations

The non-adiabatic non-equilibrium functional can be derived also in the presence of multiplicative fluctuations, i.e., for a system evolving according to Eq. (
122
). By computing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
P(x,t)
and the NESS, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{na}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{hk}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle+

(135)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sigma\left\langle{\bm{v}*\nabla\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ex},\mathrm{a}}}\right\rangle-\sigma\left\langle{\bm{v}*\bm{v}_{D}}\right\rangle\;.

In analogy to Eq. (
57
), the first contribution quantifies the total entropy production, which tends to be minimized as the system evolves toward stationarity. The second term amounts to the dissipated heat due to (deterministic) non-equilibrium conditions. In the case
\bm{\omega}=0
, the last three terms constitute the excess dissipation, and quantify, in order of appearance, the distance from equilibrium of the quasi-potential part of the availabiltiy, the effect of the bath in modifying the landscape, and finally, the effect of multiplicative fluctuations. This last contribution, as we have seen, is proportional to the derivative of
|D|
and vanishes in the limit of additive noise. In particular, assuming the validity of the Einstein relationship for every point in space,
it can be readily shown that this last term is
\propto T^{-1}\nabla T
, resembling an additional dissipation arising from thermophoretic effects and due to the necessity of transporting heat
liang2022emergent
;
celani2012anomalous
. Thus, we can conclude that this geometry-induced term in Eq. (
135
) is associated with a translational symmetry breaking stemming from a state-dependent diffusion that pushes the system to occupy excited states. Let us note that the fact that multiplicative fluctuations can be removed by a suitable change of coordinates when
\bm{\omega}=0
suggests to consider the new phoretic term as a “fictitious force” emerging from the apparent curved geometry imposed by the multiplicative noise
Graham1977CovariantFO
;
polettini_temperature
;
polettini_dice
;
Polettini_2012
.

In the more general case
\bm{\omega}\neq 0
, the contribution of
\bm{v}_{\rm D}
will modify also the housekeeping dissipation, inducing a further non-conservation of angular momentum and breaking of parity symmetry. Despite similar dissipative terms have been already obtained in the presence of a temperature gradient
celani2012anomalous
, our derivation is much more general and applies even outside the realm of purely thermodynamic systems.

Furthermore, the Onsager excess dissipation functional is also valid in the multiplicative case scenario, and it is minimized by the housekeeping/excess decomposition.
The idea that the system minimizes the excess dissipation, compatibly with the orthogonality constraint, suggest that, on top of trying to reach an equilibrium distribution, the system also tends to adapt to the curvature of phase space. This causes the breaking of translational symmetry and an additional contribution to the parity symmetry breaking in the most general case. The splitting of the velocity into housekeeping and excess contributions, each one associated with a different broken symmetry in the trajectory space, holds for any stochastic dynamics up to the first order in the noise amplitude and in the presence of multiplicative noise, an intricate and often analytically inaccessible scenario. The resulting non-equilibrium functionals generalize previous results and provide a new direction of exploration for the Onsager and Glansdorff-Prigogine principles in complex stochastic systems.

Example: Underdamped particle in a

It is non-zero and, as such, it allows the system to stably explore excited stated due to the presence of a thermal gradient. Conversely, the housekeeping velocity is divergence-free, contains the non-conservative force pushing the system out of equilibrium, and also reflects the presence of fluxes in the full phase space that vanish in the overdamped limit. \displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\rm na}

This suggests that, in many-body systems, the non-trivial currents geometry, i.e., the divergence of

VIII
Conclusions and perspectives

VIII.1
Summary

How order emerges far from thermodynamic equilibrium remains a far-reaching unsolved problem. In this manuscript, we shed light on how geometrical and symmetry-breaking properties of currents in non-equilibrium steady states (NESSs) emerge from the violation of detailed balance. We propose a framework capable of isolating the conditions responsible for breaking detailed balance (the housekeeping part) and the system’s internal response to them (the excess), both at the level of current velocities and thermodynamic dissipation. From this decomposition, we derive several important results, including an upper bound for the stationary entropy production and a geometric characterization of NESSs. In particular, we show that the housekeeping component is related to the tendency to perform cycles in trajectory space (breaking parity symmetry), while the excess velocity captures the conservative (gradient) part of the dynamics, pushing the system towards selected “excited” regions of the state space and, as such, reflecting the appearance of internal organization. The excess entropy production quantifies the distance from equilibrium of the system’s availability, playing the role of a non-equilibrium functional that should be minimized to derive the correct NESS velocity geometry. Consequently, any NESS can be understood as the least dissipative state compatible with maximal dissipation along these structures in trajectory space. By performing a weak-noise expansion of the system availability, we obtain a general and model-free characterization of velocity current geometry, along with its thermodynamic consequences, highlighting how environmental stochasticity increases the excess contribution. Furthermore, extending these results to systems with multiplicative fluctuations—–a largely uncharted area—–reveals that more complex scenarios can emerge. We uncover the structure of both excess and non-adiabatic dissipation in this case, showing how the system breaks both parity and translational symmetry in trajectory space due to the presence of an additional phoretic drift. Finally, applying our ideas to many-body systems with coupled molecular oscillations reveals the geometric properties of free-energy transduction and non-equilibrium phase transitions when multiple currents are simultaneously at play.

A.2
Covariant formulation of the Fokker-Planck equation with multiplicative noise

We first show consider the covariant formulation of the Fokker-Planck equation in the Ito scheme, and secondly in the Stratonovich case. Our exposition follows various works by Graham
Graham1977CovariantFO
;
GRAHAM_itp
.

In the Ito case the Fokker-Planck equation reads:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}\left(F^{i}P-\partial_{j}D^{ij}P\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}\left(\left(F^{i}-\partial_{j}D^{ij}\right)P-D^{ij}\partial_{j}P\right).

As in Sec.
II
we consider the inverse of the diffusion matrix as a metric
g_{ij}=D_{ij}
to introduce the covariant derivative:

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}u^{i}=\partial_{j}u^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}u^{k},

(165)

where
\Gamma
is the connection. By imposing the parallel transport of the metric:

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}D_{ij}=0\;,

(166)

the connection is determined to be:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\beta\kappa}=\frac{1}{2}D^{\alpha\mu}\left(\partial_{\beta}D_{\mu,\kappa}+\partial_{\kappa}D_{\mu\alpha}-\partial_{\mu}D_{\alpha\beta}\right).

(167)

It follows that the divergence reads:

\displaystyle\nabla_{i}v^{i}=\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{v^{i}}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right),

(168)

where
|D|
is the diffusion matrix determinant.
In vectorial notation of the divergence, we indicate explicitly the diffusive scalar product :

\displaystyle\nabla*\bm{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{i}D_{ij}v^{j}=D^{ik}\nabla_{k}D_{ij}v^{j}

(169)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}v^{j}+D^{ik}v^{j}\nabla_{k}D_{ij}=\nabla_{j}v^{j}+{D_{i}}^{k}v_{j}\nabla_{k}D^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}v^{j}+v_{j}\nabla_{i}D^{ij}=\nabla_{j}v^{j}.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator is the divergence of a gradient of scalar function:

\displaystyle\nabla*\nabla\phi=\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{D^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)

(170)

Now, by defining the invariant probability Q and the invariant measure

\displaystyle Q=P\sqrt{|D|},\quad d\Omega=\frac{d\bm{x}}{\sqrt{|D|}},

(171)

one can rewrite the Fokker-Planck equation in a fully covariant form. First, we need to set the position of the diffusion matrix in a compatible way with the Laplace-Beltrami operator. This will produce a disohomogeneity in the force field. In the ito prescription this reads:

\displaystyle\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}D^{ij}P=\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\left(\frac{D^{ij}Q}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)

(172)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{D^{ij}\partial_{j}Q}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)+\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(Q\partial_{j}\left(\frac{D^{ij}}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)\right)

By rewriting now everything using Eqs.(
168
,
170
,
171
) we arrive now to:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{\tilde{F}^{i}Q}{\sqrt{|D|}}-\frac{D^{j}i\partial_{j}Q}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)

(173)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla*J,

(174)

\displaystyle J^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{i}Q-D^{ij}\partial_{j}Q,

(175)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{i}=F^{i}-\sqrt{|D|}\partial_{j}\left(\frac{D^{ij}}{\sqrt{|D|}}\right)=F^{i}-\Gamma^{i}

This new term, that we name

can be rewritten as the sum of a first contribution stemming

On the other hand, in the case of the Stratonovich prescription, we have first to rewrite the Fokker-Planck to make manifest the presence of the diffusive matrix like in Eq.(

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}\left(FP-G^{i}_{b}\partial_{j}G^{j}_{a}\delta^{ab}P\right)

Hence, the drift term in the force counterbalance the second term in Eq.(

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{i}=F^{i}+D^{ij}\omega_{j}.
