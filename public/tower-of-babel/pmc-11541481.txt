Variable Activation Energy Models for the Shrinkage and Softening Melting Behavior of Iron Ore in Blast Furnace Conditions
Authors: Mingxin Wu, Jiayang Li, Junchen Huang, Qi Wang, Songtao Yang, Tingle Li, Changyu Sun, Sunny Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the shrinkage behavior of iron ore is crucial for achieving blast furnace (BF) operating efficiency and reducing fuel ratio. This study presents high-temperature reduction load experiments on iron ore, simulating BF conditions. Two new shrinkage behavior models (SAEM-PC and SAEM-S&M) were established by combining the effects of temperature and reduction. These models describe the dynamic relationships between the temperature, reduction degree, and the physical properties of iron ore such as density and apparent viscosity during the reduction process. The activation energies (EPC for the PC stage and ESM for the S&M stage) were determined to range from 79.95 to 42.69 kJ/mol and from 68.74 to 2.51 kJ/mol, respectively. The EPC demonstrates a gradual decrease, indicating a weakening of contraction resistance with temperature rise and reduction progress, while ESM exhibits fluctuations due to the interplay between the formation of liquid slag and the generation of solid sponge iron. The predictive accuracy of the model is substantiated by its average deviations. This study can deepen the understanding of the shrinkage mechanism of iron ore, which is of great significance for enhancing energy efficiency and reducing emissions in the steel industry.

Introduction

Blast furnace (BF) ironmaking continues to dominate the global production of pig iron, exerting a significant impact on energy usage and carbon emissions.1−8 As the iron and steel industry actively pursues optimization of BF operations to reduce the fuel ratio, a profound understanding of the intricate phenomena within the BF is essential.7,9−12 Studies on the dissection of blast furnaces have identified a cohesive zone as a critical factor in BF performance.13,14 Further research has revealed that the contraction and softening melting (S&M) behavior of iron ore is a predominant factor influencing this cohesive zone.15−17

A multitude of scholars have delved into the deformation behavior of iron ore within blast furnaces. After extensive research by scholars through soft melting experiments and interruption experiments,18−22 it has been found that the shrinkage process of iron ore in blast furnaces can be divided into the contraction stage and softening melting stage. Loo’s19 research has found that before 1300 °C, the shrinkage rate of the material layer has reached 50%, but the macroscopic structure of the iron ore has not undergone significant changes. Sternel and Lahiri23 provided an explanation for this incident that it is due to the continuous reduction process of individual iron ores within the layer that the generated metallic iron can aggregate and sinter together, leading to volume contraction of the iron ore and causing contraction of the entire iron ore layer. Liu24,25 proposed an experimental method for measuring the porosity of coke and iron layers in softening and melting (S&M) load tests using synchrotron radiation X-ray and neutron computed tomography techniques. Subhasish26 further applied this method to study the shrinkage behavior and related pressure drop of iron ore, thus creating a pressure drop model for iron material S&M experiments. Additionally, Subhasish27 investigated the shrinkage behavior of iron ore and coke in the blast furnace using this method and established a computational fluid dynamics model based on CT images. Yasuda28 studied shrinkage in the sintering process of ore, with the rate varying by temperature. Initially, it rises with the load and falls with softening viscosity. Later, it is linked to the melt rate and decreases as the metallic iron volume in the bed grows.

Previous investigations have revealed valuable insights into the contraction and softening behavior of iron ore in blast furnaces. However, most of these studies view the reduction process and physical changes of iron ore as isolated events, failing to reveal the intricate interactions between them. During the reduction process of iron ore, its physical properties, such as density and apparent viscosity, undergo significant changes.15 These changes have a notable impact on its contraction and S&M behavior in the blast furnace.20 Given the impact of the reduction process on the physical behavior of iron ore, it is necessary to integrate these two aspects into a comprehensive framework for research. In addition, most of these studies focus on the apparent physical properties of the shrinkage behavior of iron ore in blast furnaces. However, there is relatively little research on mathematical models for the shrinkage behavior, which makes it impossible to continuously characterize the shrinkage behavior of iron ore. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a mathematical model to describe the contraction and S&M behavior of iron ore under the combined influence of temperature and reduction. This integration will help reveal the shrinkage behavior mechanism of iron ore in blast furnaces more accurately and comprehensively.

In response to the above issues, the core lies in how to accurately express the dynamic changes in physical properties such as density and apparent viscosity of iron ore during the reduction reaction process and how these changes further affect the contraction and S&M behavior of iron ore. In the scholarly domain of chemical reaction kinetics, the constantly changing influencing factors29−33 that accompany the reaction process are usually analyzed in depth using the concept of variable activation energy. This method can effectively capture and describe the specific mechanisms by which changes in these influencing factors affect the reaction behavior. Therefore, it provides a possibility for us to deeply understand and characterize the dynamic evolution of the iron ore contraction and S&M behavior under the combined effects of temperature and reduction reactions.

Considering these considerations, in this study, a variable activation energy model is proposed for the shrinkage behavior of iron ore. This model combines the dual effects of the temperature and the degree of iron ore reduction, establishing a connection between the chemical and physical transformations that occur during the ironmaking process. This model provides a more detailed and comprehensive description of the shrinkage and softening melting behavior of iron ore, providing theoretical support for blast furnace operation and helping to achieve reasonable operation of blast furnaces. This will further contribute to achieving energy efficiency and emission reduction goals in the steel industry.

Testing

In this research, a novel high-temperature furnace, patented under the Qisunny method with the number 202310716991.2, is utilized under conditions that mimic those of a blast furnace, as depicted in Figure 1. The testing apparatus features a vertical tube electric furnace capable of reaching a maximum power output of 20 kW and operating at temperatures up to 1600 °C. A graphite crucible, characterized by an open bottom and a diameter of 75 mm, is employed to contain the test material. The weight reduction of the sample is continuously monitored by a precision balance (model ES30K-ID), which offers a measurement range of 30 kg and an accuracy level of 0.10 g. A displacement sensor (model GP911-1-S) is used to track the real-time shrinkage of the iron ore layer. An infrared gas analyzer (Gasboard-3100) is responsible for gauging the volume fractions of CO and CO2 emitted from the furnace. A PLC control panel is implemented for the regulation of the temperature and gas flow.

RL-TG-GA combination experimental equipment for ore-coke coupling metallurgical properties.

Prior to the experimental procedure, the iron ore and coke specimens are subjected to a drying process at a temperature of 150 °C for a duration of 48 h. The arrangement of the crucible follows a specific stratification, as illustrated in Figure 2, with coke at the bottom, sinter in the middle, and coke again at the top. The bottom coke layer comprises coke samples that have undergone a 25% solution loss at 1100 °C, with a particle size between 13 and 15 mm and a total mass of 75 g. The middle sinter layer is composed of sinter particles sized between 10 and 12.5 mm, weighing a total of 500 g. The top layer is made up of new coke, with particles ranging from 18 to 20 mm in size and a mass of 100 g.

Loading system for the experiment.

As shown in Figure 3, the detailed experimental parameter settings were based on the S&M experimental settings adopted by previous researchers to simulate the real temperature and atmosphere inside the blast furnace.25,34 A crucible filled with the test materials was positioned within the heating furnace. Subsequently, a compressive force of 1 kg/cm2 was exerted on the materials via a pressure-loading cylinder. The materials were subjected to a heating regimen that increased at a pace of 10 K/min, reaching a temperature of 873 K where they were maintained for a period of 50 min. Throughout this heating phase, the materials were shielded by a 5 L/min flow of nitrogen gas (purity of 99.9%). Following this, the gas environment transitioned to a reducing atmosphere composed of 30% CO (purity 99.9%) and 70% N2, with a flow rate of 15 L/min. The temperature increase continued at a rate of 10 K/min from 873 to 1173 K, then slowed to 2.5 K/min from 1173 to 1373 K, and further accelerated to 5 K/min above 1373 K. Upon conclusion of the experiment, the reducing gas was replaced with a 5 L/min nitrogen flow to cool the sample rapidly to 473 K.

Iron Ore Shrinkage Analysis

Shrinkage Mechanism

After a significant number of scholars conducted soft melting experiments, it was found that the shrinkage process of iron ore can be divided into the following two stages.

In the first stage (particles contraction stage, PC stage), before 1528 ± 25 K, the shrinkage ratio of iron ore layer had reached 50–55%, yet the macroscopic structure of the iron ore did not undergo significant changes.19,35,36 Sternel and Lahiri23 conducted an in-depth analysis of this phenomenon and pointed out that the initial contraction of the iron ore layer during the reduction process is not caused by deformation from external loads. On the contrary, it is due to the continuous reduction process of individual iron ores within the layer that the generated metallic iron can aggregate and sinter together. This leads to volume contraction of the iron ore and the contraction of the entire iron ore layer. Furthermore, external load also enhances the migration, aggregation, and sintering processes of metals. In order to more accurately verify this phenomenon, the experiment was interrupted and sampled at 1523 K, and the results are shown in Figure 8.

Sampling results of the experiment interrupted at 1523 K.

Combining Figures 8 and 4, it can be observed that at a temperature of 1523 K, the iron ore layer underwent substantial contraction, with a shrinkage ratio of 55%. However, it is worth noting that despite the significant contraction of the iron ore layer, the ore particles themselves still maintain a relatively intact particle shape without significant softening deformation. This phenomenon further confirms that the initial cause of iron ore layer contraction is not deformation caused by external loads but rather the aggregation and sintering of metallic iron produced during the reduction process, which leads to the contraction of iron ore volume.

In the second stage (S&M stage), iron ore begins to show signs of softening deformation after 1523–1573 K.15,20 Many scholars have extensively discussed this phenomenon,21,25,36,37 and they generally believe that after undergoing the first stage of reduction reaction, iron ore is gradually reduced to Fe3O4 and FeO, forming an iron layer on the surface of the iron ore. FeO in the interior of the iron ore forms low melting point compounds such as SFCA and CAS with CaO, SiO2, and Al2O3 after 1523 K and begins to form a liquid phase. At this point, the iron layer on the outer surface of the iron ore is still in a solid phase, so the liquid phase slag formed is likely to remain inside the metal shell of the ore particles until it reaches the melting temperature of iron (∼1853 K) before seeping out. During this stage, under external loads, the formation of these liquid phases promotes the softening and deformation of iron ore.18,22 As the temperature increases and reduction reactions progress, the proportion of liquid–solid phases undergoes continuous dynamic changes, resulting in significant alterations in the shrinkage rate of the iron ore layer. This process involves not only the transformation of physical states but also the influence of chemical reactions.

In the PC stage, the activation energy rapidly decreases from 80.99 to 43.31 kJ/mol before 1412 K (α = 0.611) and then tends to stabilize. This is because as the temperature rose and the reduction process progressed, the reduction rate of iron ore increases, and the contraction resistance decreases, leading to a rapid decrease in EPC. With the increase of reduced iron, the ratio of Fe to FeO may reach a certain critical point, resulting in a slow reduction rate of iron ore. As a result, the contraction resistance no longer decreases, and EPC tends to stabilize.

In the S&M stage, the ESM increased from 43.29 to 58.00 kJ/mol in the temperature range of 1496–1586 K (α,0.658–0.790), followed by multiple peak fluctuations between 58 and 46.15 kJ/mol in the range of 1586–1700 K (α,0.763–0.933). After reaching 1700 K (α = 0.933), the ESM rapidly decreased from 46.15 kJ/mol and approached zero. This is because the reduction rate rapidly increases after the S&M of iron ore. In a very short amount of time, a significant quantity of solid sponge iron was produced, increasing the solid volume fraction. This may result in an increase in the apparent viscosity of the iron ore layer, inducing an increase in the ESM. Between 1586 and 1700 K, as the temperature increases, the slag gradually melts, causing an increase in the amount of liquid phase. This will result in a decrease in softening viscosity. At the same time, reduction also generates a significant quantity of sponge iron, which can result in an elevation of softening viscosity. The combined effect of the two factors leads to multiple peak fluctuations in ESM during this interval. After reaching 1700 K, the metallic iron begins to melt, leading to a rapid increase in the liquid phase until it is completely liquefied. This results in a rapid decrease in softening viscosity, followed by a rapid decrease in ESM, ultimately approaching zero.

Moreover, the ability of models to predict the dynamic evolution of iron ore contraction and softening under the combined effects of temperature and reduction reactions can guide the adjustment of blast furnace operational parameters in real-time. This guidance could involve controlling the furnace temperature, gas flow, and burden composition to achieve optimal reduction conditions and minimizing fuel consumption.

Conclusions

This article delves into the thermal-induced contraction and softening melting characteristics of blast furnace iron ore, influenced by both temperature and reduction processes. Inspired by the variable activation energy concept from chemical reaction kinetics, we have formulated two novel models: the SAEM-PC and SAEM-S&M. These models encapsulate the dynamic changes in physical properties such as the density of the ore, the liquid and solid phase volume fractions, and the apparent viscosity as the temperature ascends. Through SAEM-PC/S&M analysis, it was found that the EPC and ESM of the PC stage and S&M stage were 79.95–42.69 kJ/mol and 68.74–2.51 kJ/mol, while for the APC and ASM were 3.38 and 2.99 s–1. Research has shown in the PC stage that EPC decreases as the reduction process progresses, indicating a decrease in contraction resistance. In the S&M stage, the ESM fluctuates due to the interaction between the formation of liquid slag and the production of solid sponge iron. The SAEM-PC/S&M model demonstrates high prediction accuracy, with average deviations of 1.16% and 0.75% in the PC and S&M stages compared to experimental results. This confirms the reliability of the model in describing the shrinkage behavior of iron ore.
