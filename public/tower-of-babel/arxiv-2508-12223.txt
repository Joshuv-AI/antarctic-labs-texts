Steering chiral active Brownian motion via stochastic position–orientation resetting
Amir Shee

Abstract
Guiding active motion is important for targeted delivery, sensing, and search tasks. Many active systems exhibit circular swimming, ubiquitous in chemical, physical, and biological systems, that biases motion and reduces transport efficiency. We show that stochastic position–orientation resetting can overcome these limitations in two-dimensional chiral active Brownian particles by interrupting circular motion, resulting in tunable dynamics. When resets are infrequent compared to chiral rotation, the steady-state mean-squared displacement varies non-monotonically with rotational diffusion. Steady state excess kurtosis and orientation autocorrelation yields spatiotemporal state diagram comprising three states: an activity-dominated chiral state, and two resetting-dominated states with and without chirality; in contrast, the achiral counterpart supports only the resetting-dominated state. Chirality thus enriches the dynamical landscape, enabling tunable transitions between transport modes absent in achiral systems. A simple reset protocol can thus transform chiral active dynamics and offer a practical strategy for optimizing search and transport in circle swimmers.

We use Eq. (

The combination of spatial and temporal diagnostics gives a clear, intuitive picture of how the particle explores space under competing influences.
The excess kurtosis tells us whether long excursions between resets (heavy-tailed) or smooth circular wandering (light-tailed) dominate the steady-state displacement distribution.
Meanwhile the orientation autocorrelation in Eq. (
7
) pinpoints whether the particle retains its heading long enough to complete many loops (oscillatory decay) or forgets its direction almost immediately (monotonic decay).

These two measures together map out a state diagram in which the relative rates of resetting, rotation, and diffusion cleanly delineate three distinct spatiotemporal behaviors.

•

Active:
Self-propulsion and chirality dominate. The particle follows persistent circular arcs, the displacement distribution remains narrow (light tailed), and the orientation autocorrelation exhibits damped oscillations.

•

Resetting I:
Occasional resets interrupt loops without fully suppressing them. The distribution develops heavy tails from rare long excursions, yet the autocorrelation still oscillates before settling to its steady value.

•

Resetting II:
Frequent resets (or weak chirality) effectively quench circular motion. The particle executes short, nearly straight flights between resets, producing a heavy-tailed distribution, and the autocorrelation decays monotonically.

Conclusions

In this work, we have analyzed the dynamics of a chiral active Brownian particle (CABP) subject to stochastic resetting, in which both its position and orientation are restored to predetermined values at a constant rate.
Using a renewal equation framework together with a Fokker–Planck formalism, we derived analytical expressions for the particle’s orientation dynamics and displacement moments, including the mean squared displacement and the fourth order displacement moment.
Our analysis demonstrates that the interplay between the resetting rate, intrinsic chirality, and rotational diffusion gives rise to spatiotemporal behaviors.
In the presence of chirality, we identify three states: active, resetting I, and resetting II, whereas in an achiral system only the resetting state is observed
Shee 2025
.
Thus, chirality suppresses the heavy tailed position distribution and produces a re-entrant transition from resetting to an active regime and back to resetting when either the resetting rate or chirality is varied independently.

Numerical simulations performed using the Euler-Maruyama method corroborate our theoretical predictions, demonstrating excellent agreement across both transient and steady-state regimes.
Our findings underscore that stochastic resetting acts as a versatile control parameter, enabling the tuning of transport properties and the manipulation of non-equilibrium steady states in active matter systems with rotational degrees of freedom, paving the way for single-particle experimental realization
Muñoz‐Gil et al. 2025
.

Resetting can be realized in practice using optical tweezers to reposition and reorient Janus particles, magnetic fields to steer magnetically coated swimmers, light to trigger tumbling in engineered bacteria, or flow-switching in microfluidic channels to reset chiral microswimmers
Tkachenko and et al. 2023
;
Duygu and et al. 2025
;
Rey and et al. 2023
.
These setups allow direct measurement of the predicted non-monotonic MSD–
D_{r}
relation, the oscillatory-to-monotonic shift in orientation autocorrelation, and the excess-kurtosis state boundaries via high-speed video microscopy and trajectory analysis.

The insights presented here deepen our understanding of non-equilibrium phenomena in chiral active systems and open new avenues for future investigation.
In particular, by independently controlling positional and orientational resets, our model facilitates the design of optimal stochastic-resetting strategies tailored to specific tasks.
Extending this framework to inertial systems
Patel and Chaudhuri 2023
;
Patel and Chaudhuri 2024
, many-particle systems
Nagar and Gupta 2023
;
Vatash and Roichman 2025
, investigating the effects of external fields and confinement
Caprini and Marini Bettolo Marconi 2019
;
Abdoli and Sharma 2021
;
Chaudhuri and Dhar 2021
;
Caprini et al. 2023
;
Patel and Chaudhuri 2024
;
Shee 2025
could further advance the control and optimization of active transport processes.

Appendix D
Calculation of excess kurtosis

We define kurtosis to capture the deviation of Gaussian nature which signify the how active is our system as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{r}=\frac{\langle{\bf r}^{4}\rangle_{r}}{2\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{r}}-1~,

(47)

where the calculation of
\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf r}^{4}\rangle
already shown explicitly.
In the small time limit(
t\to 0
), excess kurtosis

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{r}(t\to 0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Pe^{4}}{32}t^{2}+\frac{Pe^{4}}{192}\left[4+3Pe^{2}\right]t^{3}-\frac{Pe^{4}}{23040}\left[136-120\beta^{2}\right.

(48)

\displaystyle\left.-120\Omega_{0}^{2}+360Pe^{2}+135Pe^{4}\right]t^{4}+\mathcal{O}(t^{5})\,.

In the small time in two-dimension, the kurtosis deviates from zero at
t=0
, to negative values with
\mathcal{K}(t\to 0)=-(Pe^{4}/32)t^{2}
. The decrease of kurtosis reaches to a maximum negative excess kurtosis at
t^{*}=4/(4+3Pe^{2})
.

The steady state excess kurtosis in two dimension defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{r}^{\rm st}=\frac{\langle{\bf r}^{4}\rangle_{r}^{\rm st}}{2(\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{r}^{\rm st})^{2}}-1\,,

(49)

where
\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{r}^{\rm st}
is presented in Eq. (
9
) and
\langle{\bf r}^{4}\rangle_{r}^{\rm st}
is presented in Eq. (
12
).
Figure
7
displays a heatmap of
\mathcal{K}_{r}^{\rm st}
on the
D_{r}-\Omega_{0}
plane revealing a re-entrant transition, from a resetting‑dominated regime to an active regime and back to resetting at low rotational noise strengths.
The corresponding state diagram on the
r-\Omega_{0}
plane is shown in Fig.
4
(a) of the main text.

In the absence of translational noise (
D=0
), the state diagrams on the
r-\Omega_{0}
and
D_{r}-\Omega_{0}
planes (Fig.
8
(a) and Fig.
8
(b), respectively) demonstrate that the chirality‑induced re-entrant behavior observed under translational noise vanishes.
Furthermore, in the absence of both translational and rotational noise (
D=D_{r}=0
), the state diagram on the
r-\Omega_{0}
plane (Fig.
9
) shows that the re-entrant dependence on the resetting rate also disappears.
At low resetting rates
r

D_{r}^{-1}
and the stochastic‑resetting timescale
r^{-1}
.

We expand in the small activity limit (setting active speed
v_{0}\to 0
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{r}^{\rm st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{r[(r+D_{r})^{2}-\Omega_{0}^{2}]v_{0}^{2}}{D[(r+D_{r})^{2}+\Omega_{0}^{2}]^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(v_{0}^{4})\,.

(50)

The analysis indicates that
\Omega_{0}
is the critical parameter driving the second term negative, thereby suppressing the excess kurtosis.

Appendix E
Calculation of
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}

We use
\psi=\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
in Eq. (
5
) to calculate the Laplace form of
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
. We obtain
\langle\psi\rangle_{0}=0
,
\langle\nabla^{2}\psi\rangle_{s}=8\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}
,
\langle\nabla_{n}^{2}\psi\rangle_{s}=-\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
,
\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+2\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}
,
\langle{\bf r}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=5\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
, and
\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
. Substituting this into the moment equation leads to

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s+D_{r}}\left[8D\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}+v_{0}\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+2v_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}+\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}\right]\,,

(51)

where
\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}
already calculated in Eq. (
27
) and
\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
in Eq. (
34
). To proceed further we need to calculate
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}
and
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
. First, we use
\psi=\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}
in moment generator Eq. (
5
)

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s+4}\left[2D\langle 1\rangle_{s}+2\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+2v_{0}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}+2\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})\rangle_{s}\right]\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s+4}\left[2\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+v_{0}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}+\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}-\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}\right]\,.

(53)

Now, from the symmetry,
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}=\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}-\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}
,

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s+4}\left[\Omega_{0}\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+v_{0}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}-2\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}\right]\,.

(54)

Proceed further,

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})^{2}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s+4}{(s+4)^{2}+(2\Omega_{0})^{2}}\left[2D\langle 1\rangle_{s}+2\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+2v_{0}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}+\frac{2\Omega_{0}}{s+4}\left[\Omega_{0}\langle{\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}+v_{0}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}\right]\right]\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s+D_{r}}\left[8D\langle{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r}\rangle_{s}+2v_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r})({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf r})\rangle_{s}-\Omega_{0}\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}\right]\,.

(56)

Finally, we obtain
\langle({\hat{\mathbf{u}}}\cdot{\bf r}){\bf r}^{2}\rangle_{s}
,which is used in the derivation of the fourth order moment of displacement in Appendix
C
; see Eq. (
40
).
