The role of persistent inflammation in failed recovery after perinatal brain injury: is resolution the cure?
Authors: Hawley Helmbrecht, Maryam Ardalan, Lynne Kelly, Shenandoah Robinson, Bobbi Fleiss, Derek G Doherty, Pierre Gressens, Carina Mallard, Eleanor J Molloy, Lauren L Jantzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perinatal brain injury (PBI) is a major predictor of neurological disability. Commonly associated with prematurity, infection, stroke, hypoxia-ischemia, hemorrhage, and/or toxin exposure, PBI triggers acute and persistent systemic inflammation. There are many stages of vulnerability to PBI during development including pregnancy, birth – term and preterm, and neonatal age. The vulnerable stages can compound inflammation through injury to the placental-fetal-brain axis, adaptive and innate immune system development, neural-immune communication, and central nervous system maturation. Neonates exhibit unique inflammatory signatures and lasting neural-immune responses to various etiologies. Chronic immune dysregulation and priming to a secondary, later-in-life immune challenge defines different forms of PBI while shaping the neonatal and adult immune response with long-term changes. Immunomodulated changes impact regulatory, helper and innate T cells, neutrophils, natural killer cells and immune responsiveness. The major routes of persistent and compounding inflammation in PBI are perinatal neural-immune interactions, cytokine influx, and glial crosstalk. Most treatments are not administered long enough or in the optimal time window to combat sustained inflammation in tertiary and quaternary phases of PBI pathophysiology and are ineffective in reducing neonatal mortality and morbidity and promoting functional recovery. Indeed, persistent systemic and central inflammation is a likely explanation for failed recovery of PBI after the resolution of acute insults. We propose attenuating persistent inflammation and normalizing systemic immune reactivity as key to reducing the functional impact of PBI throughout the lifespan through various avenues including therapeutic treatment, gut microbiome modulation, and novel immunomodulation from preclinical research.Graphical abstract Inflammation follows the course of acute systemic inflammation followed by changes in the choroid plexus and blood-brain barrier and acute neuroinflammation impacting the function and morphology of glial cells. In normal inflammation, this acute inflammation is resolved resulting in healthy myelin, an optimal glymphatic system, and refined, functional neural circuits. However, in persistent inflammation, acute inflammation is not resolved instead dysregulating neuroimmune cross talk, causing sustained peripheral immune hyper reactivity, disrupted glymphatic clearance, and instigating a cytokine storm. The dysfunction to the choroid plexus, neuroinflammation, systemic immune response, systemic inflammation, and blood-brain barrier leads to tertiary brain damage and failed recovery. The failed recovery presents as impaired myelin formation, myelin degradation, perineuronal net degradation, damaged choroid plexus, impaired glymphatics, fragmented neural networks, and impaired anatomical and functional connectivity. Treatment options to resolve persistent inflammation include caffeine, melatonin, and novel immunomodulators, among others.

Background: perinatal brain injury persists after acute resolution

Perinatal brain injury (PBI) is a major predictor of neurological disability commonly caused by infection, stroke, prematurity, hypoxia-ischemia, hemorrhage, toxin exposure (i.e. opioids, alcohol, other substances) and/or trauma around the time of birth. Each of these triggering events is associated with increased pro-inflammatory reactivity in the brain. Inflammatory events are cumulative – subsequent exposures to antenatal and postnatal inflammation increase an infant’s risk of brain injury and neurodevelopmental disorders compared to a single exposure alone [1–4]. Indeed, the risk of brain injury is increased in infants who have persistent and/or recurrent inflammation, defined as elevated levels of inflammatory markers at more than one measurement over at least one week [2, 4–8]. Perinatal inflammation is associated with many neuropsychiatric and neuropsychological disorders [9]. Patients with PBI exhibit a range of issues including higher rates of cerebral palsy, cognitive deficits, behavioral problems, impaired learning, epilepsy, and overall poor physical health. The cumulation of these deficits contributes to increased incidence of chronic disease in adulthood [10–13]. In addition, alongside the primary etiology, PBI can trigger and subsequently feed forward persistent inflammation [14]. PBI increases inflammatory cytokine production in peripheral circulation, reprograming and priming the immune system. Fetal injury or stress during pregnancy, preterm birth, or birth often leads to poor structural and functional brain connectivity and disrupted neural networks with lasting neurological sequelae [15–18]. This injurious, inflammatory pathophysiology persists for months and years after a perinatal insult and sometimes even throughout the lifespan, increasing the risks for later health problems. Epigenetic changes have been proposed to mediate these inflammatory mechanisms of injury [9, 17].

Treatments for PBI are limited. Most focus on mitigating acute neurological and systemic inflammation, occurring immediately or up to a few days after injury, and are ineffective in reducing neonatal mortality and morbidity long-term or promoting functional recovery [19, 20]. Here, we propose that persistent systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation are plausible explanations for failed recovery after PBI and an important nexus for therapeutic modalities directed at the treatment of PBI. Acute inflammation is necessary to protect the neonate from larger injury, and we hypothesize that the failure to resolve acute inflammation potentiates a chronic inflammatory state and a toxic microenvironment that impairs brain recovery culminating in neurodevelopmental disorders. It is well established that both central and systemic inflammation, sterile or infectious, are notable antecedents of neurological injury in infants of all gestational ages, with changes in neural immune status beginning in utero [5, 21–24]. The inflammatory hypothesis is supported by observations of infection-associated morbidities and the significant differences in immune regulation recorded in children exposed to opioids [25, 26], with trisomy 21 [27, 28], cerebral palsy [29], severe neurological injury [30] and/or in combination with neonatal encephalopathy [31]. Infection and injury such as hypoxia-ischemia catalyze PBI by creating a toxic microenvironment that limits oxygen exchange and propagates inflammation during critical periods of neurodevelopment [32]. This state is associated with a long-term decreased capacity of blood cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to environmental exposures [33].

We hypothesize that acute inflammation disrupts the choroid plexus and blood-brain barrier while initiating neuroinflammation and activating glial responses. This acute inflammation drives a secondary wave of persistent inflammation by disrupting neural-immune crosstalk, propagating a cytokine storm, and causing sustained immune hyperreactivity of resident and infiltrating immune cells. This self-propagating cycle is the driver of failed recovery which subsequently leads to tertiary brain damage through impaired formation and degraded myelin, degraded perineuronal nets, altered neural networks, dysregulated functional and anatomical connectivity, neural cell death and impaired glymphatics. Thus, in pursuit of the discovery and development of effective interventions for persistent inflammation, including those that resolve neural-immune dysfunction and peripheral immune changes across developmental milestones, we review the literature and identify major pathophysiological pathways of placental-fetal-brain connection, adaptive and innate immune system development, neural-immune communication, and central nervous system maturation in both the term and preterm infant [34]. By appreciating the role of persistent inflammation in failed recovery from PBI, targeted interventions for brain injury in infants can be designed, with a wide therapeutic window providing an exciting opportunity to treat children long after injury.

Milestones of vulnerability to inflammation in PBI

The central nervous system is particularly vulnerable to perturbations in inflammatory responses, as protracted growth and increasing complexity throughout gestation overlaps with neural-immune development. The intertwining of important developmental functions increases the propensity for injury at all stages of pregnancy, birth, and perinatal development. Since the immune system and the central nervous system mature during gestation, inflammation and development are inseparable [23].

The relationship between the placenta, chorioamnionitis, and fetal inflammation

Complex fetal development co-occurs with the elaboration of white matter and cortical surface area, concomitant with parcellation and expansion of myelin maps [35]. Most injuries and diseases in pregnancy have placental involvement and confer risk for chronic disease [36]. Both acute, chronic, or a combination of acute and chronic inflammation may affect the placenta with major implications for a wide variety of outcomes including premature birth, poor neurological outcomes, and increased incidence of injury or death [37]. Additionally, placental abnormalities are an independent risk factor in the pathogenesis of perinatal stroke [38–42]. A major cause of placental abnormalities is chorioamnionitis. Chorioamnionitis, defined as sterile or infectious inflammation of the placenta and surrounding membranes, affects placental permeability and blood flow. Chorioamnionitis facilitates hypoxia-ischemia and transmission of cellular and molecular inflammation [43]. In the absence of other risk factors, the hyperinflammatory cytokine milieu of chorioamnionits can facilitate a hypercoagulable state or cause direct thromboembolism to the fetal brain [34, 43]. Chorioamnionitis can occur at any gestational age [40, 41, 43–48], and is the most common abnormality found in preterm deliveries, affecting 40–80% of all preterm deliveries, in > 80% placentae of infants born < 25 weeks of gestation [49] and is associated with a significant increase in systemic inflammation [4, 50, 51]. Chorioamnionitis is also found in 20–34% of deliveries at term [44, 46, 52]. Notably, it is recognized in as many as 42% of placentas from unremarkable pregnancies [43, 53]. The role of inflammatory cytokines in inducing and sustaining brain injury is well documented [17]. A wide variety of animal models from rodents to non-human primates are used to study chorioamnionitis as reviewed by Lutz et al., each providing improved understanding of chorioamnionitis and the placenta-fetal axis [54].

The most common infectious, causative agent of chorioamnionitis is Ureaplasma, a microorganism strongly associated with preterm birth [55, 56]. While the relationship between Ureaplasma, chorioamnionitis, and preterm birth is known, more research is required to address the relationship between duration of exposure to Ureaplasma infection and any infectious stimuli leading to unresolved cerebral inflammation and later brain injury [56]. Interestingly, not all pregnant people with in utero ureaplasma experience preterm birth or adverse outcomes [57]. Sheep are an excellent model of human pregnancy due to the placenta vascular development and fetal developmental timelines [58]. Ovine studies of Ureaplasma infection are used to explore which aspects cause pathological inflammation inflammation and adverse neurological outcomes [59].

The innate immune system protects human infants with chorioamnionits by elevating neutrophil and monocyte counts compared to infants without intra-amniotic infection [60]. However, preclinical literature confirms that cerebral and peripheral microenvironments remain profoundly inflammatory well into adulthood after cerebral palsy secondary to chorioamnionits, with abnormal distributions of circulating leukocytes over the lifespan. In rats, not only are serum cytokine levels high, including elevated interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, (C-X-C) motif ligand (CXCL) -1, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1/CCL2), but immune cells are hyper- reactive to additional immune challenge leading to release of additional pro-inflammatory mediators including interferon gamma (IFNγ), TNFα, IL-6 and chemokines monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1/CCL2) [61]. These data emphasize compartment specific modulation of cytokines and immune cell diversity following chorioamnionits. They also highlight how diverse immune cell populations and immune polarization activation change in response to inflammatory signaling throughout the maternal-placental-fetal axis.

In addition to defects in placental perfusion and immune cell recruitment to the placenta directly, chorioamnionits is commonly associated with a fetal inflammatory response syndrome (FIRS) that can last for weeks postnatally [4, 7, 50, 51, 62–65]. FIRS, the fetal equivalent of systemic inflammatory response syndrome, is a significant activation of the immune system due to direct contact with an infection present in the amniotic fluid, inflammatory cell transfer from uteroplacental circulation, or both. FIRS causes adverse effects on multiple organ systems and is defined by an increase in IL-6 and infiltrating neutrophils [66–69]. In humans, FIRS exacerbates the detrimental effects of chorioamnionitis on long-term neonatal outcomes [62, 70–72]. FIRS is highly associated with sepsis, intraventricular hemorrhage, cerebral palsy, and childhood neuropsychiatric disorders [73, 74]. Non-human primate models of chorioamnionitis confirm that FIRS leads to severe fetal inflammation and organ damage [75]. FIRS can in turn chronically disrupt neural-immune communication becoming a focal point and plausible mechanism for ongoing and persistent inflammation leading to structural and functional brain changes [76–78].

Preterm birth

Preterm birth readjusts the major milestones of development. In humans, preterm infants have known impaired anatomical, functional, and local cerebral connectivity [79, 80]. Preterm infants have fragmented functional networks resulting in less integration, reduced intra-hemispheric connectivity yielding less transmission capacity, and less global efficiency compared to term infants, and these changes impact brain function through the lifespan [81–83]. Preterm birth, intrauterine infection, and inflammation are some of the most important causative factors in the development of cerebral palsy [84]. First reported by Eastman et al. in 1955, cerebral palsy increases sevenfold after both preterm delivery and maternal intrapartum fever. Dysregulated immune function was also found in school-age children with cerebral palsy who had brain injury in the neonatal period [33]. Clinical studies in children with cerebral palsy confirm altered inflammatory responses that persist for at least 6–14 years [33, 85]. Nelson et al. demonstrated high concentrations of IL-1, 8, 9 and TNF-α in archived neonatal blood from children with cerebral palsy in comparison with control children [86]. Preterm born, school-age children with cerebral palsy secondary to periventricular leukomalacia had significantly higher levels of TNF-α in peripheral blood mononuclear cells after in-vitro lipopolysaccharide stimulation compared to peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated and cultured from control school-age preterm children with normal neurodevelopment [85]. As increased peripheral cytokines are a marker of cerebral inflammation, the time course for altered inflammatory responses in cerebral palsy is likely to last for decades. These clinical phenomena have been replicated in preclinical rodent and large animal models of chorioamnionitis and prenatal opioid exposure [61, 87–90].

Children with severe neurological injury - including those with cerebral palsy and associated motor, cognitive and medical complexities - have lower numbers of monocytes and T cells, as well as changes in lymphocyte maturation, compared to age-matched controls [30]. This imbalance can contribute to both immunosuppression, raising infection risk and modulating immune tolerance and response, and inappropriate inflammatory responses potentially exacerbating secondary brain injury [25, 30]. Altered cellular responses to lipopolysaccharide have also been observed, with hyperresponsiveness of monocyte toll-like receptor-4 expression, but hyporesponsive expression of cluster of differentiation (CD)66b and IL1β. The inflammasome and activity of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) is also higher at baseline in children with severe neurological injury compared with controls [91–93]. Indeed, neuronal damage releases damage associated molecular patterns, which further activate NLRP3, intensifying neuroinflammation [94]. In neonatal mice, the NLRP3 inflammasome dysregulated perineuronal nets, the extracellular matrix surrounding neurons [95]. Significantly, brains of children ≤ 4 years have heightened NLRP3 sensitivity, increasing susceptibility to secondary injuries including excitotoxicity and oxidative stress as they exhibit higher peak NLRP3 levels [93, 96].

The fetal central nervous system and immune system are developmentally regulated, and it is notable that an early component of both neuroinflammation and chorioamnionits is the recruitment and activation of circulating neutrophils, aiming to clear the triggering infection or resolve inflammation [64, 97–101]. Preclinical data in rats emphasize that the immune system is chronically hyper-reactive and primed to a secondary immune challenge later in life secondary to a late gestation inflammatory insult such as chorioamnionits or opioid exposure [61, 87, 89, 90, 100–102]. Kitase et al. specifically demonstrated that exposure to perinatal inflammation leads to sustained peripheral immune hyper-reactivity and immune response dysfunction that persists well into adulthood in rats (postnatal day 120) [61]. Immune function changes were concomitant with hypercytokinemia and altered T cell population dynamics in both the brain and peripheral circulation through postnatal day 120, equivalent to a rat middle age, and represents a time point over 4 months following exposure to chorioamnionits. Changes in regulatory T cell and helper T cell populations at P120 with hypersecretion of IFN-γ, CCL2, IL-6 and TNF-α were also observed. As has been eloquently described by other investigators, lipopolysaccharide is not only capable of inducing a cascade of inflammatory pathways but also initiates a refractory state, knowns as lipopolysaccharide tolerance [33, 103, 104]. To test this, at postnatal day 120, peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated from rats exposed to the chorioamnionitis model were cultured in media with or without lipopolysaccharide. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated from rats exposed to chorioamnionitis secreted lower levels of CCL2 and CXCL-1, and TNF-α by peripheral blood mononuclear cells. This lipopolysaccharide tolerance was shown to exist in the context of increased T-helper and T regulatory cells and previous lipopolysaccharide hyperreactivity [61]. Taken together, these data emphasize dynamic immune modulation with maturation and complex neural-immune pathology secondary to PBI. Lipopolysaccharide stimulation studies in clinical and preclinical models highlight that the impact of persistent inflammation is not only the unresolved neuroinflammation but also that immune priming due to perinatal brain injury that causes hyperactive functional responses to future inflammatory insults.

Term birth and the neonatal stage

Infants with neonatal encephalopathy have persistent inflammatory responses over the first week of life, with higher neutrophil, monocyte, and Toll-like receptor-4 expression [105, 106]. The early systemic inflammatory responses over the first week of life correlate with the degree of neurological injury [105–107]. Notably, the magnitude of early-life inflammatory markers predicts cognitive outcomes at 5–7 years, suggesting their utility as prognostic biomarkers [108]. School age children who had neonatal encephalopathy have persistent inflammatory responses with altered cytokine responses to endotoxins as compared to controls when measured at school-age following neonatal encephalopathy, with increased IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, TNFβ, and granulocyte monocyte colony stimulating factor [108]. Innate and conventional T-lymphocytes are functionally altered in infants with neonatal encephalopathy as newborns and these changes persist into childhood. Specifically higher invariant natural killer T cells and Vδ2+ γδ T cells were found in neonates with neonatal encephalopathy and in later childhood as well as in a cohort of children with cerebral palsy [109]. Children with cerebral palsy had decreased mucosal-associated invariant T cells and Vδ1 T cells. The pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, stroke and autoinflammatory neurological diseases are associated with invariant natural killer T cells and Vδ2 T cells. Adoptive transfer of ex vivo activated Vδ2 T cells and invariant natural killer T cells or in vivo activation are involved in ongoing clinical trials for cancer, whereas targeted suppression of these cells may have potential for future neonatal encephalopathy treatments [110].

The mechanisms and impact of inflammation

Chemokines, a family of small molecular weight chemotactic cytokines, are classically defined by their ability to induce directional migration and activation of leukocytes to areas of inflammation in the body [111–115]. They are also essential molecular mediators of sterile inflammation through the placenta-fetal-brain axis. In pregnancy and fetal development, the physiologic roles for chemokines are well described. In alignment with clinical literature confirming that perinatal brain injury often originates in utero [2, 8, 113, 116–121], chemokine interactions with their receptors and immune cell activation throughout the body impart inflammation to the developing brain, permanently changing the central nervous system [122, 123]. Many chemokines like CXCL1 are essential for neurodevelopment [112, 113, 120, 121, 124–126]. Importantly, dysregulated cytokine production due to infection has tremendous impact on the developing fetus [44, 67, 127–136]. CXCL1 is a chemokine defined by potent, (C-X-C) chemokine receptor – 2 (CXCR2) receptor-dependent neutrophil chemoattractant activity [137, 138]. In both rats and humans, CXCL1 is normally present in the uterus and creates a tissue specific immunological environment [139–141] by providing a chemotactic gradient for neutrophil infiltration to the maternal-fetal interface [139, 142–145].

While increases in CXCL1/CXCR2 are initially protective, elevated levels in chorioamnionitis are directly proportional to the driving force of pathological inflammation [146]. Pregnant women with intra-amniotic infection have significantly higher amniotic fluid concentrations of CXCL1 [147], and high CXCL1 levels correlate with maternal and newborn peripheral white blood cell counts [148]. Both CXCL1 and CXCR2 are preferentially expressed on oligodendrocyte progenitors and microglia in the fetal brains as early as 19–22 weeks gestation [125], and CXCR2 activation by CXCL1 on oligodendrocyte progenitors regulates their proliferation and migration [120, 124]. Additionally, CXCR2 is constitutively expressed on other neural cells including mature oligodendrocytes, neurons and astrocytes [125], and monocytes [113]. CXCL1 is the dominant CXCR2 ligand expressed in the inflamed central nervous system, and its levels are directly proportional to its function [111, 113, 126, 149–151]. In both term and preterm human babies with amniotic infection, CXCL1 is upregulated in amniotic fluid, umbilical cord, and maternal plasma [148]. Indeed, CXCL1 levels are biomarkers of microbial invasion of the amniotic cavity [152].

In rats, CXCL1 is upregulated in placentae with Group B Streptococcus-induced chorioamnionits [153], and is elevated in the placental and fetal circulation in models of gestational diabetes and preeclampsia [153, 154]. Signaling through CXCR2 is a non-redundant driving force for neutrophil recruitment from blood [155], and CXCR2 is cycled rapidly from the cell membrane, therefore CXCR2 expression serves as the rate limiting step in its inflammatory signal transduction [113, 155, 156]. The above data emphasize the sensitivity and specificity of CXCL1/CXCR2 signaling to pregnancy pathology as defined by severe inflammation. Intrauterine CXCL1/CXCR2 signaling also highlights the significance of peripartum stressors, the placenta, and persistent inflammation in the elaboration of brain injury associated PBI. Neurodevelopment is dependent upon CXCL1 and CXCR2 [112, 113, 120, 121, 124, 126]. In rats, CXCL1 increases following systemic lipopolysaccharide administration and unilateral carotid artery ligation are consistent with the rapid neural cell increases of CXCL1 and CXCR2 in stroke, adult central nervous system injury [150, 151, 157–164].

While CXCL1/CXCR2 signaling plays a critical role in mediating acute and subacute inflammatory responses and neural cell recruitment following various perinatal central nervous system insults, the chemokine profile in PBI is complex and involves the coordinated action of multiple chemokine families. Beyond the CXC chemokines, members of the CC chemokine family, such as CCL5 and CCL3, have also emerged as important mediators of the neuroinflammatory pathways during and after hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. These CC chemokines exhibit distinct temporal expression patterns and may contribute to the chronic phase of inflammation that influences long-term neural repair and functional recovery. In the developing and adult human brain, CCL5 is expressed by oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, microglia, and select neuronal populations, suggesting a broad role in neuroimmune communication and synaptic regulation [165].

CCL5 was found to increase over time in a rat model of neonatal hypoxia-ischemia (Vannucci, postnatal day 7, rat, 75 min, 8%), being unchanged at one day after injury, non-significantly increased at seven days and significantly higher at 17 days after injury [165]. Highlighting the specificity of the tertiary phase response, CCL3 was increased at all timepoints [166]. Altamentova et al. then demonstrated that tertiary phase treatment with the glucocorticoid methylprednisolone sodium-succinate, 20–21 days after injury in rats, substantially reduced tertiary phase inflammation and improved functional outcomes [166].

An important relationship between PBI and sustained inflammation also exists in the context of inflammation mediated by toll-like receptor-4. In normal function and development, toll-like receptor-4 serves as an important defense mechanism against infection and tissue damage with age dependent expression [167, 168]. In addition to signaling catalyzed by lipopolysaccharide and other damage-associated molecular patterns, toll-like receptor-4 signaling in the central nervous system can occur in response to commonly used NICU pharmacotherapies including natural, semisynthetic, and fully synthetic opioids, including methadone, morphine, buprenorphine and fentanyl. Opioids induce a central immune response by binding toll-like receptor-4 paired with an accessory protein known as myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD-2), inducing toll-like receptor-4 oligomerization and NFkB activation [169]. This specifically increases the major pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and the chemokines monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 CXCL1 [170]. Targets may include existing agents such as toll-like receptor-4 inhibitors, novel molecules, or neural repair strategies that mitigate white matter injury and cognitive deficits following perinatal brain injury [171–175]. Persistent hypercytokinemia can also lead to inhibition of cortical branching, dysfunctional synapse formation and pruning, and propagation of white matter injury [176, 177]. Persistent toll-like receptor-4 activation in systemic immune cells was also increased in infants with neonatal encephalopathy and correlates with severity. Increased neutrophil toll-like receptor-4 expression was associated with increased mortality in infants with neonatal encephalopathy [178].

The players of cellular inflammation

Neural-immune cross talk is an essential component of health and physiology but can become maladaptive in disease, especially in a brain primed by pre-existing inflammation or prior insult [17]. Plasticity altered after perinatal priming or programing may have long-term effects on the inflammatory responses of circulating leukocytes [85]. Notably, neonates are sensitive to programming that permanently affects neurological and immune development, and preterm infants are susceptible to inflammatory disorders [85]. After acute insult, neural-immune interactions occur via two primary routes. First, the influx of cytokines and chemokines transduces inflammation and perpetuates neuroinflammation. Second, interactions promote crosstalk with brain resident glial cells and infiltrating immune cells including at the interface of the choroid plexus [179, 180]. Although acute and persistent inflammation underlies almost all perinatal brain injury, each specific injury and initiating insult has a distinctive immune trajectory. In hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, primary energy failure is followed by a secondary energy failure and finally chronic inflammation, with prominent microglia and astrocyte activation time courses [181]. Many reviews cover different aspects of these trajectories focusing on regulated cell death [182], oxidative stress [183], microglia response [184], and monocyte contributions [185]. In chorioamnionitis, the fetus experiences ascending intraamniotic infection – or noninfectious inflammation – which is initially typified by neutrophil activation and invasion, preterm birth, and subsequent fetal inflammatory response syndrome [49]. Intraventricular hemorrhage and posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus of prematurity is defined by the accumulation of blood products, immune activation in response to heme and hemoglobin, cell death including ferroptosis, and dysregulation of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics [186].

The choroid plexus serves not only as a physical barrier but also as an immunological gateway, facilitating the entry and exit of immune cells and the exchange of inflammatory signals between the periphery and the central nervous system. Maintenance of molecular inflammation (pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines) and cellular inflammation (microglia, macrophages, monocytes, T cells, neutrophils) homeostasis is paramount, as is tuned communication with extracellular vesicles (exosomes) and other inflammatory signal transduction mechanisms (choroid plexus) [187–189]. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, are central to this process, responding rapidly to injury or infection by altering their phenotype and releasing cytokines that can either promote repair or exacerbate damage [190]. Astrocytes, another glial population, modulate synaptic activity and blood-brain barrier integrity, and can amplify or resolve neuroinflammation depending on the context [191]. Typically, neutrophil influx is a secondary response after brain injury, which further exacerbates endogenous inflammation mediated by microglia [155, 192–195].

Locations of access: the blood-brain barrier and the CSF-brain barrier

The central nervous system is surrounded by three major barrier systems: the blood-brain barrier, the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier formed by the choroid plexus, and the arachnoid barrier in the leptomeninges. These systems provide both outward and inward transport mechanisms, along with highly restrictive tight junctions that create a physical barrier at the interfaces between blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and brain tissue. The blood-brain barrier is a transport route for cytokines in and out of the brain, while the choroid plexus also serves a cytokine transport route through the cerebrospinal fluid-brain system. Cytokines in both the blood-brain barrier and the choroid plexus can impair function and increase inflammatory responses in the brain parenchyma. The barriers are established early in development and are functional already in the fetus [196] and the normal, uninjured permeability of blood-brain barrier is strictly limited by embryonic day 15 in mice [197, 198].

Clinical studies showed elevated cerebrospinal fluid/blood albumin ratios in term human infants with hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy [199]. Although the importance of blood-brain barrier integrity and lymphocyte infiltration in the acute phase of perinatal brain injury is known, the enduring consequences of chronic immune population shifts and hyper-or hypo-reactive peripheral blood mononuclear cells is not well understood. Indeed, phenotypic plasticity of lymphocytes may be maladaptive and important to injury progression, or at the very least, a hindrance to neural repair in many individuals with cerebral palsy or other forms of perinatal brain injury. Similar findings as reported here have been documented in children with Down syndrome [200, 201]. On the other hand, decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in sera and peripheral blood mononuclear cell supernatants have been found in response to lipopolysaccharide stimulation in critically ill patients in intensive care [33, 202], and in children with neonatal encephalopathy who received hypothermia [33, 108]. Hyporeactivity to lipopolysaccharide stimulation has also been observed in older children with cerebral palsy when IL-2, IL-6, IL-1α and IL-1β were studied [33].

The choroid plexus: a gateway for inflammation

Many cytokines and chemokines produced by circulating leukocytes can move across the blood-brain barrier and choroid plexus, impairing their function and increasing immune cell access to brain parenchyma. In this context, multiple overlapping mechanisms can contribute to brain injury, including direct initiation of programmed cell death pathways in neurons and oligodendrocytes, microglial and astrocyte activation, peripheral immune cell recruitment, mitochondrial damage, and endoplasmic reticulum stress [203, 204]. The choroid plexus has been identified as an important gateway for immune cell infiltration into cerebrospinal fluid and brain after toll-like receptor-2 mediated inflammation in neonatal mice [205]. In normal development, toll-like receptor-2 is an important defense mechanism against certain bacterial and lipoproteins with age dependent expression [168]. In preterm infants, polymorphisms of toll-like receptor 2 may alter immune response [206]. Using flow cytometry and transmission electron microscopy, it was found that the toll-like receptor-2 agonist Pam3CysSerLys4 significantly increased neutrophil and monocyte migration to the CSF and periventricular brain regions in neonatal mice, a response not seen with toll-like receptor-4 activation by lipopolysaccharide. RNA sequencing of the choroid plexus showed that toll-like receptor-2 activation leads to a distinct chemotaxis gene expression profile, including significant upregulation of CCL3 and CXCL2. Gene deletion of toll-like receptor-2 or disruption of CX3CR1-CCR2 signaling was neuroprotective in part by attenuating mitochondrial energy failure and monocyte and neutrophil trafficking [207, 208]. Further, the migration of neutrophils into the cerebrospinal fluid via the choroid plexuses is prevented by administering the antioxidant drug N-acetylcysteine, which also resulted in reduced brain injury [209]. Following infection or systemic inflammation, the choroid plexus not only acts as a gateway for immune cell infiltration but also releases specific microRNAs through extracellular vesicles that can significantly amplify the inflammatory response. These choroid plexus-derived extracellular vesicles penetrate the brain parenchyma, where they are taken up by astrocytes and microglia. This results in suppression of microRNA targets by the extracellular vesicles and upregulation of inflammatory genes, driving the neuroinflammatory process [210]. A study in humans found microRNA 20b, microR-93 and microR-532 were all increased in the systemic circulation of neonates with neonatal encephalopathy compared to controls [178]. In support, a study in mice found that treatment with mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles showed reduction in perinatal brain injury in relation with a marked reduction in microglial activation as an inflammatory cell in the brain [211].

The blood-brain barrier: protection vs. leakage

The blood-brain barrier is a complex barrier system with several mechanisms for transport including transmembrane diffusion, specialized transporters, and transcytosis [212–214]. Several studies in rodents indicate that the integrity of the blood-brain barrier is compromised following neonatal hypoxia-ischemia, leading to extravasation of large plasma molecules like IgG and albumin, as well as increased permeability for smaller molecules. In a neonatal mouse hypoxia-ischemia model, blood-brain barrier permeability to sucrose peaked at six hours post-insult but normalized by day three [196], while another study in rats reported prolonged permeability changes lasting up to seven days after hypoxia-ischemia [215]. In a mouse model of neonatal hypoxia-ischemia studying blood-brain barrier permeability and cerebral blood flow, blood-brain barrier proteins were found to be potential biomarkers for brain injury [196, 216]. Additionally, peripheral immune cell trafficking via the choroid plexus can be an important route in toll-like receptor-mediated perinatal brain injury [209] and a mouse study described how systemic inflammation affects the cerebral vasculature [217]. By developing a method for global gene expression in minute tissue samples in choroid plexus from neonatal mice, scientists identified a gene signature related to Toll-like receptor activation in persistent inflammation [218]. Emerging research into glymphatics, a waste clearance system formed by astrocytes and perivascular cells in CSF spaces, is informing new understanding about dysregulation of the blood-brain barrier after injury, CSF flow, and inflammation in the developing brain [219]. An impaired glymphatic system fails to remove waste from the brain leading to accumulation of toxic products, including cytokines and proteins, that contribute to glial activation [220]. Most research in glymphatics thus far has been presented in the context of healthy adults or neurodegeneration leaving a large gap for research into the development of this system and the impacts of perinatal brain injury.

In the neonatal rat, the levels of tight junction protein claudin-5 in cerebrospinal fluid correlated with the severity of brain injury at 24 h post hypoxia-ischemia and it was suggested that these proteins show potential as early biomarkers for cerebral vascular dysfunction and could be valuable tools for assessing the risk of neonatal brain injuries [196, 216]. Although mechanisms of blood-brain barrier disruption following hypoxia-ischemia are not fully known, loss of tight junctions and activation of matrix metalloproteinases have been suggested to be involved [221]. Several studies have shown that elevated metalloproteinase-9 levels in the brain contribute to blood-brain barrier disruption by causing the rearrangement and degradation of proteins associated with tight junctions, thereby compromising the barrier’s integrity [222–224].

Under normal physiological conditions, the permeability of brain microvasculature to endogenous albumin in newborn mice is not significantly different from that observed in adult mice [225]. Effects of inflammatory stimuli, like lipopolysaccharide, on the blood-brain barrier in neonatal animals has produced mixed results including age-dependent responses where reduced leakage was observed at postnatal day eight compared to postnatal day zero, and no leakage in older animals [226]. Also, the type of inflammatory stimuli and length of inflammatory exposure are important factors that affect the degree of blood-brain barrier permeability. A compromised blood-brain barrier was reported in neonatal rats and mice following repeated lipopolysaccharide administration [226], while no leakage following a single dose of lipopolysaccharide, a toll-like receptor-4 agonist was found [207]. In contrast, there was significant permeability to sucrose after a single injection of the toll-like receptor-2 agonist Pam3CysSerLys4 [207]. In the adult, systemic inflammation affects barrier transport systems in the blood-brain barrier [227]. While marked and distinct transcriptional changes in the vasculature of neonatal mice was found, there was little regulation of tight junction-associated genes in the cerebral vessels after systemic lipopolysaccharide administration [217].

Role of immune cells in persistent inflammation

PBI is characterized by astrocyte and microglial cell activation, impaired oligodendrocyte maturation, and the release of inflammatory cytokines which result in the activation and recruitment of neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes to the brain. Mechanistically, early systemic inflammatory responses reflect maladaptive activation of microglia and peripheral immune cells (i.e. neutrophils, macrophages, monocytes and T cells) causing neural cell damage through nuclear factor (NF)κB and inflammasome signal transduction [228]. While neutrophils are the most abundant leukocytes and are the first to be recruited after hypoxia-ischemia, less is known about the role of lymphocytes, which are central to the maintenance of inflammatory responses. Lymphocytes comprise antigen-specific T cells and B cells, natural killer cells, innate lymphoid cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which are found at different numbers and ratios at various body sites. Compared to healthy neonates, human infants with PBI frequently have higher lymphocyte counts and altered frequencies, phenotypes and functions of individual lymphocyte populations in the first hours of life [109, 229, 230].

Analysis of brain tissue from neonatal animals following experimental hypoxia-ischemia has indicated that lymphocytes are recruited to the brain and activated. The above-mentioned observations suggest that several lymphocyte subsets may promote or protect against neuroinflammation leading to PBI and may be targeted therapeutically. Induction of regulatory T cell expansion using a CD28 superagonist antibody led to the reduction of brain injury after ischemic stroke in adult mice [231]. IL-15 blockade also protected the brains of mice from ischemia-reperfusion injury by preventing natural killer cell and T cell activation and infiltration [232]. Cellular therapies involving Vδ2 T cells and natural killer T cells are currently being tested in cancer patients and may also be adapted for the treatment of PBI [233, 234]. However, future research into the mechanisms by which lymphocytes contribute to PBI is required before these immunotherapies can be translated to humans. A striking experimental study based on the Vannucci model – a rat model of hypoxic-ischemic brain damage that involves a carotid artery ligation followed by hypoxia [235] - has demonstrated a delayed (tertiary phase) brain infiltration of T and B lymphocytes that could play a deleterious role in the pathophysiology of long-term brain damage in term infants [236].

Infiltrating peripheral immune cells such as monocytes, T cells, and neutrophils are increasingly recognized as key players in central nervous system injury and repair. For example, monocyte-derived macrophages can adopt either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory phenotypes, influencing the outcome of neuroinflammatory events [237]. T cells, including regulatory T cells, have been shown to limit neuroinflammation in experimental models of brain injury [238–240]. Mucosal-associated invariant T cells and γδ T cells expressing the Vδ1 T cell receptor are found in very low numbers in the circulation of human neonates, but they expand rapidly after birth [109, 241, 242]. γδ T cells are functionally competent early in development and are found in significant numbers in the brains of neonatal mice, sheep, and human preterm infants with brain injury, such as periventricular leukomalacia and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [243]. These unconventional T cell subsets are capable of rapid cytokine production and cytotoxic responses, and recent evidence suggests they may play roles in neuroimmune surveillance and the regulation of inflammation at barrier sites, including the meninges and choroid plexus. Their activation in early life may influence susceptibility to central nervous system infections and the programming of long-term immune responses [244]. A recent study found altered distributions and functions of γδ T cells in neonates with neonatal encephalopathy and in children with cerebral palsy. These cells, along with natural killer T cells, were primed to produce inflammatory cytokines in neonates with neonatal encephalopathy, suggesting a role in the inflammatory milieu of neonatal brain injury [245].

B cells could potentially contribute to the pathogenesis in PBI via the release of cytokines and antibodies, which opsonize pathogens for destruction by phagocytes and cytotoxic cells. B cells have been described in the periventricular white matter and meninges of post-mortem brains from human infants with periventricular leukomalacia [246]. In murine models of hypoxia-ischemia -induced stroke, B cells infiltrate infarcted tissues leading to deficits in long term potentiation and cognitive function [181, 247] and these cognitive deficits can be prevented by pharmacologic ablation of B cells using an anti-CD20 antibody. B cells have also been detected in human postmortem tissue from people with stroke and dementia [247]. We reported that circulating B cell numbers are higher in neonates and school-age children with PBI compared to age-matched control subjects [109], however, future studies are required to determine if these cells traffic to the brain and contribute to PBI.

Preclinical studies of PBI in rats have confirmed increased levels of regulatory T cells and helper T cells that persist over four months after an in-utero insult consistent with lack of lymphocyte homeostasis and potential compensatory response to ongoing inflammation [61, 90]. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated from postnatal day 7 rat pups with prenatal opioid exposure acutely hyper-secrete pro-inflammatory mediators at baseline and following a secondary stimulus from lipopolysaccharide [88, 89]. Further, peripheral blood mononuclear cells from adult rats with prenatal opioid exposure secrete more IL-1β and TNFα following lipopolysaccharide challenge, consistent with a primed and hyper-reactive immune phenotype [61, 87–89]. Results implicate a mechanism of deleterious feed-forward inflammatory pathophysiology induced by prenatal opioid exposure, and confirm immune responsiveness and corroborate reports in humans [85, 89, 200]. Immune plasticity altered by in-utero insults may have long-term effects on the inflammatory responses of circulating leukocytes, which could serve as a biomarker of persistent or prior neuroinflammation and brain injury [248, 249].

T cells are also likely to contribute to the pathogenesis and/or resolution of PBI. In response to hypoxia-ischemia in newborn mice, astrocytes release the chemokines CXCL12, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL12 and CCL21 into the blood, leading to the recruitment of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to the brain [236, 246, 250–253]. These cells frequently express the activation markers CD69 and CD25 and they can activate, regulate or kill other cells via contact-dependent or cytokine-mediated interactions [236, 251]. Inhibition of T cell migration into the brains of newborn mice after hypoxia-ischemia using the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor agonist FTY720, has demonstrated both pathologically [254] and protective [255] roles for T cells in PBI, suggesting distinct roles for different subsets of T cells.

The properties and functions of different types of T cells are governed by cytokines released by myeloid cells and other cells, including astrocytes and microglial cells. Upon activation, naïve CD4+ T cells can differentiate into distinct subsets of helper T cells (Th1, Th2, Th17 and regulatory T cells) which exhibit distinct cytokine profiles and functions. CD4+ T cells from newborn human infants are mostly Th2 and regulatory T cells, with few Th1 or Th17 cells [256]. Severe PBI and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes are associated with higher serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 in humans, which promote Th17 cell differentiation, and neutrophil recruitment and activation [109, 257, 258]. High levels of the Th1 cytokines IL-2 and TNF-α with low levels of the Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 are associated with PBI in preterm human babies, creating an environment for the activation of cytotoxic cells and inflammatory macrophages [259]. Upon stimulation ex vivo, T cells from neonates with PBI more frequently produced IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-17 and granzyme B than T cells from healthy neonates, consistent with the biased Th1/Th17 profile in PBI.

A second class of T cells termed unconventional T cells or innate T cells recognize non-peptide antigens presented by MHC-like antigen presenting molecules, such as CD1, butyrophilins and MR1. Natural killer T cells, γδ T cells and mucosa-associated invariant T cells are the best characterized innate T cells [260–262]. They differ from conventional T cells in that they possess primed/effector phenotypes and are capable of rapid expansion without the need for prior antigen exposure. Innate T cells initiate, regulate and maintain innate and adaptive immune responses in the tissues. They recognize and respond to changes in the levels of metabolites, including lipids, pyrophosphates, riboflavin precursors and proteins, that are synthesized in response to cell stress, such as hypoxia, infection or tumor transformation. They respond by rapid cytotoxicity, potent cytokine release, and contact-dependent interactions with neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and B cells. These early immunostimulatory properties in the tissues place innate T cells as potential players in the pathogenesis of PBI.

Upon activation with specific antigen or cytokines, Vδ2 T cells from human neonates with neonatal encephalopathy more readily produced IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-17 than Vδ2 T cells from healthy neonates, indicating that these cells were primed or activated in vivo [109]. Large numbers of γδ T cells have been found in the brains of a hypoxia-ischemia mouse model, a fetal sheep asphyxia model and in post-mortem preterm infants with periventricular leukomalacia [243]. Using gene knockout mice, Zhang and co-workers demonstrated that γδ T cells, but not αβ T cells, contribute to sepsis-induced white matter injury [263]. A subset of natural killer T cells that recognizes α-galactosylceramide (type 1 natural killer T cells) is also significantly expanded in human neonates with neonatal encephalopathy, school-age children post- neonatal encephalopathy and children with cerebral palsy, suggesting a role for these cells in neuroinflammation. Type 1 natural killer T cells play roles in several [109] brain diseases including stroke, neurodegenerative diseases: multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and central nervous system viral infection [264]. They infiltrate the blood and brain of mice following experimental hypoxia-ischemia and contribute to brain injury [265]. In contrast, type 2 (sulfatide-reactive) natural killer T cells appear to protect against ischemia-reperfusion [266].

Natural killer cells, the cytotoxic lymphocytes of the innate immune system, account for about 5% of total lymphocytes in peripheral blood and have been shown to traffic to the brain during experimental hypoxia-ischemia in newborn mice and rats [232, 267]. Inactivation of natural killer cells by CD161 knockdown resulted in a decrease in brain and systemic organ atrophy and neurobehavioral deficits [267], suggesting a key role for natural killer cells in brain injury and multiorgan dysfunction in neonatal encephalopathy. Investigating human peripheral blood cell phenotypes, researchers found that circulating natural killer cell frequencies are lower in human neonates with PBI compared to healthy neonates and these cells displayed activated phenotypes and more readily produced IFN-γ, TNF-α and granzyme B upon stimulation ex vivo [109]. Their reduced numbers in peripheral blood may be the result of their recruitment to the brain. Type 2 innate lymphoid cells also accumulate in the brain after hypoxia-ischemia but do not appear to contribute to the development of PBI [268].

The role of microglia, macrophages, and immune cells and perinatal brain inflammation

Animal models of encephalopathy of prematurity based on systemic inflammation as the initial insult have demonstrated the central role of microglia/macrophages in the cellular cascade leading to brain maldevelopment (in particular oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination delay) and functional consequences (cognitive and social interaction impairment, reduced brain connectivity) [269]. Recent studies based on single-cell RNA sequencing support the hypothesis of different subpopulations of microglia and macrophages that might play a very different role in the pathophysiology of brain damage in these animal models [270]. In addition to microglia, the so-called border macrophages (meningeal, choroid plexus, and perivascular macrophages) and macrophages derived from infiltrating circulating monocytes most likely play an important role that remains to be determined.

In the encephalopathy of prematurity model based on systemic administration of IL-1β during the first postnatal days in rodents, microglia and macrophages with abnormal reactivity (different phenotype compared to the acute phase but still very abnormal when compared to age-matched microglia and macrophages) have been identified at a very delayed time-point (corresponding to late-adolescence/early adulthood) after the initial insult, further supporting the existence of an encephalopathy of prematurity tertiary phase at least partly linked to microglia and macrophages [271]. Very recent data obtained in the same model suggests that this late abnormal microglia/macrophage phenotype is associated with the brain infiltration of some specific T cell subtypes.

Most animal models of term brain damage rely on the classical “Vannucci model” of neonatal hypoxia-ischemia or on models of neonatal stroke [272, 273]. Multiple reviews address the benefits and drawbacks of each model of hypoxia-ischemia and how these preclinical approaches uniquely contribute to the mechanistic and clinical understanding of this injury [274–276]. Several studies have demonstrated the presence of activated microglia and macrophages at the early stages of brain damage, suggesting a potential role of these cells in the pathophysiology of the lesions, although no direct demonstration has been so far provided in a hypoxia-ischemia model while a direct proof has been provided in a stroke model [277]. Based on epidemiological studies, some animal models have combined systemic inflammation with hypoxia-ischemia or excitotoxic insults. These studies have shown that the sensitizing effects of systemic inflammation are at least in part mediated by microglia and macrophages [278, 279]. Although not directly studied, one potential hypothesis for a delayed infiltration of immune adaptive cells would be the presence in the brain parenchyma of microglia and macrophages displaying a pathological reactivity. Finally, the potential involvement of infiltrating monocytes differentiating into parenchymal macrophages has been suggested but not fully confirmed. To our knowledge there are no human data on microglia/macrophages in brain lesions associated with term neonatal encephalopathy.

After the initial classic acute phase reactive gliosis, a latent tertiary phase of gliosis coupled with evolving damage to the white matter in a sheep asphyxia model 21 days after injury [280]. The delay in the onset of cystic white matter injury strongly suggests that the therapeutic window is viable for at least three weeks after injury. Sex differences in injury responses also persist into the tertiary phase, reported 30 days after a hypoxic-ischemic injury in sheep. Sheep models of hypoxia-ischemia also reveal disrupted perineuronal nets and loss of interneurons [281]. Females had a lower lymphocyte infiltration into the brain, higher microglial expression of the ‘M2-like’ anti-inflammatory marker CD206, and no persisting increase in peripheral TNFα but had elevated IL10 where the males did not [282]. As this study assessed only 30-day outcomes, a resolution of whether the sex differences stem from the fact that males are more injured in the acute phase or whether the tertiary phase itself is also uniquely dimorphic is not possible.

Multiple lines of evidence support a robust neuroinflammatory response defined by upregulation of diverse cytokines and immune genes on microglia at a critical period of central nervous system development [283, 284]. Following neonatal infection, transcriptomic data shows microglia reprogramming [285] and global cerebral proteomic alterations [286]. Notably, opioids also activate NFkB via opioid receptors and activation of NFkB signaling in turn promotes transcription of opioid receptors and peptides, propagating a detrimental intracellular signaling cascade [287–291]. In adults, microglia have been implicated as a driver for opioid-induced neuroplasticity, and this provides a unique link between neural-immune activation and PBI. Mouse models have also shown that microglia mediate loss of interneurons and perineuronal nets [292, 293]. A mechanistic understanding of developmentally regulated, toll-like receptor-4-mediated, pathophysiology may reveal key neural drivers and substrates of cognition that are amenable to neurorepair. Toll-like receptor-4-mediated microglial activation may be a driver of chronic maladaptive neuroplasticity, including changes in extracellular matrix, synaptic and dendritic structures [287].

Human post-mortem studies have shown the accumulation of microglia in white matter crossroads in fetuses from 24 to 32 gestational weeks, suggesting a potential role of these microglia in the pathophysiology of white matter abnormalities in the encephalopathy of prematurity [294–296]. In addition, neuropathological studies of preterm neonates with encephalopathy of prematurity have revealed, during the acute/subacute injury phase, an increased density of microglia/macrophages in white matter [297], the used markers not allowing to distinguish between the brain resident microglia and circulating monocytes infiltrating the brain and differentiating into brain macrophages. Shape analysis and immunohistochemistry have revealed that many of these microglia/macrophages were reactive, suggesting a pro-inflammatory phenotype of these cells that could have deleterious effects on neighboring cells.

No post-mortem reported study has looked at microglia/macrophages as human infants age into adulthood. However, a couple of clinical studies support the existence of a systemic inflammatory tertiary phase in preterm infants that could trigger/favor some long-lasting abnormal microglia and macrophage reactivity, potentially contributing to the progression of the encephalopathy of prematurity over time: (i) former pre-term neonates with brain damage studied at 7 years of age showed increased circulating levels of TNF-alpha and increased production of the same cytokine by peripheral blood mononuclear cells stimulated by lipopolysaccharide, indicating a long lasting pro-inflammatory state of the peripheral immune system [85]; (ii) Similarly, several inflammatory proteins in preterm children at school age differed from values of adult controls [298]. In term neonates, to our knowledge, the involvement of microglia and macrophages in human brain lesions associated with term neonatal encephalopathy has not been thoroughly addressed. One small study (four controls and three neonatal encephalopathy cases) reported an increase of glial fibrillary acidic protein-positive astrocytes but not of CD45-positive myeloid cells in the white matter of term neonates who died during the acute phase of brain injury (between one and three days after birth) [299]. It is worth noting that in contrast to rodent studies, humans have significantly more neutrophils and this is an area requiring further research [300]. Other reviews focus on the role of neutrophils in human perinatal brain injury [301].

Polydendrocytes and oligodendrocytes: an understudied role in persistent inflammation

Poly-dendro-cytes (many-tree shaped cells), or NG2-positive glia, make up 5–10% of adult central nervous system cells, and were initially considered the immature form of oligodendrocytes or oligodendrocyte precursor cells. However, only a small fraction of polydendrocytes develop into oligodendrocytes, while some mature into astrocytes, and polydendrocytes retain lineage plasticity in an age- and region-specific manner [302]. To account for this diverse role of polydendrocytes, they will only be referred to as oligodendrocyte precursors here when specifically discussing oligodendrocyte differentiation [303, 304]. As a stable cell population, polydendrocytes express receptors and ion channels, form synapses with neurons and secrete factors regulating cell survival, growth, vascular development and activity, including factors that modulate brain immune functions [305, 306]. In acute injury and inflammation, polydendrocytes and the related subset of oligodendrocyte precursor cells rapidly respond to injury, alter their proliferation, activate to protect myelination, and engage in crosstalk with microglia and astrocytes to modulate neuroinflammation [307, 308]. Studies in adult mice have shown oligodendrocyte precursor cells release signals and soluble factors such as matrix metalloproteinase-9 that break down the blood-brain barrier [309]. On the other hand, embryonic mouse studies are unraveling the role of polydendrocytes in development of cerebral vasculature and the formation of the blood-brain barrier [310].

A lack of specific markers for polydendrocyte stymies research, as they co-express nerve/glial antigen 2 or chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha, oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2, and SOX10 but not O4, 2’,3’-Cyclic-nucleotide 3’-phosphodiesterase or myelin basic protein, like oligodendrocyte progenitors. At all ages, polydendrocytes have multiple long slender processes [311]. With age, nerve/glial antigen2 and platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha co-expressing cells decline in number [312], but they remain distributed throughout the gray and white matter [313]. Innervation of polydendrocytes occurs locally from interneurons in the cerebellum and hippocampus, hippocampal pyramidal neurons and stimulating thalamocortical axons elicited glutamatergic excitatory post-synaptic potentials on polydendrocytes in the somatosensory cortex [306, 314]. Polydendrocyte-neuron synapses exist early in development and into adulthood [315], indicating a core role for polydendrocytes in connectivity and a necessity to understand changes in them after PBI.

There are no polydendrocyte-specific studies in the context of PBI. However, a comparative analysis of the inflammatory response of platelet derived growth factor receptor alpha positive cells (oligodendrocytes and polydendrocytes) and O4 positive cells (pre-oligodendrocytes) has been undertaken in a murine model of systemic immune activation-driven encephalopathy of prematurity [316]. The oligodendrocyte progenitors /polydendrocyte cells, compared to the pre-oligodendrocytes, expressed lower levels of classically pro-inflammatory genes (IL1β, IFNy, CCL2 and CXCL10) during early postnatal life, indicating different roles in the inflammatory response that is likely to translate to differences in the tertiary phase based on data from other glia. Evidence for tertiary phase changes in a model of neonatal hypoxia-ischemia (Vannucci, rat, P10, 45 min, 8%) includes elevated protein expression of polysialic-acid neural-cell adhesion molecule at eight days post-injury. A loss of polysialic-acid neural-cell adhesion molecule is necessary for oligodendrocytes to initiate axonal myelination and to trigger GABAergic maturation [317, 318]. In an adult rat model of stroke, there is evidence for a role of polydendrocytes in the tertiary phase, with a transient spike in neurve-glia antigen 2 at 14 days after injury, aligned with the increased presence of cells morphologically like polydendrocytes in the peri-lesional cortex [319]. Interestingly, striking increases in the proliferation of nerve-glia antigen 2/platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha positive cells after focal cortical lesion did not correlate with an increased number of mature cells at five weeks after a stroke-like lesion. Transplanting neuronal progenitors who had differentiated to express neural/glial antigen 2 at seven days post-traumatic brain injury in mice led to striking and persisting increases in functional and structure outcomes [320]. These studies collectively suggest that polydendrocytes may be regenerative drivers that need further exploration after PBI.

The future for resolving persistent inflammation: novel targets and future research areas

Persistent inflammation is a central barrier to successful neural cell and neural network recovery following perinatal brain injury, shaping both immediate and long-term neurological outcomes [228, 321, 322]. While the initial inflammatory response to injury is necessary for host defense and tissue repair, failure to resolve this response can lead to chronic neuroinflammation, ongoing systemic inflammation and multiorgan cellular damage, and impaired brain function [323].

Biomarkers provide insight to developing and persistent inflammation

The unique signature of inflammatory cytokine and chemokine expression provides insight into both acute and persistent inflammation in a variety of neurodevelopmental diseases and injuries including neonatal encephalopathy and cerebral palsy [33, 107, 108, 324]. Cytokine profiling can provide an immunophenotype for systemic response. It is worth noting that systemic monocytes differ between rodent models and human models, prompting the need for future research into translational strengths and differences [325–327]. Future research areas for assessing acute and persistent inflammation in perinatal brain injury include biomarker studies in urine, cerebrospinal fluid, blood, amniotic fluid, placenta, sweat, and stool. Urine biomarkers are a non-invasive method for assessing health and predicting long-term health outcomes. Current urine biomarker studies include glial markers in intermittent hypoxia [328], correlating urine biomarkers with abnormal MRI findings in hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy [329], and metabolites of melatonin to assess neural injury and neuroprotection [330]. While cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers require invasive procedures, they offer unique insight to brain health [331]. Current research includes neuron-specific enolase profiling [332, 333], biomarker panels for congenital and acquired hydrocephalus [334–336], , longitudinal biomarker panels of tau, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and neurofilament light chain for neurologic monitoring [337, 338], seizure disorders, infection, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and traumatic brain injury [339]. Blood biomarkers are a common and well-studied [340–344]. For prenatal diagnosis, the amniotic fluid gives valuable insights to the health of the fetus [345–347]. The microbiome is a promising biomarker of in infant health that can be assessed in stool or nasal/airway secretions [348–351].

Another possible biomarker for assessing inflammation is peripheral blood mononuclear cells profiling during the perinatal period, perinatal brain injury and neurodevelopmental disorders including cerebral palsy. Indeed, baseline peripheral blood mononuclear cells reactivity, coupled with subsequent lipopolysaccharide stimulation, reveals unique signatures of inflammation that serum cytokine measurements do not capture. These collection and culture protocols are robust and reproducible, and future studies of peripheral blood mononuclear cells reactivity could be implemented in adults with cerebral palsy to guide rehabilitative and emerging therapeutic approaches. Additionally, peripheral blood mononuclear cells profiling may prove useful in defining risk of later life inflammatory sequelae in children with cerebral palsy, including alterations in the trajectory of myelination, and the development of chronic pain. Undoubtedly, future studies focused on peripheral blood mononuclear cell reactivity in older patients with cerebral palsy, including beyond the teenage years would be very valuable to the field, as would studies using immunomodulatory treatment to enable long-term recovery and regeneration. Beyond cytokine profiling and peripheral blood mononuclear cell functional assays, other current and emerging biomarkers for acute and persistent inflammation include novel neuroimaging methods for assessing inflammation [352, 353] and new cognitive, behavioral, and motor assessments [354, 355].

Therapeutic treatments for neonates

Neonatal biology is unique, differs between preterm and term infants, and changes rapidly. Identification of therapeutic treatments and related windows to decrease perinatal brain injury is imperative to decrease long-term deficits. Caffeine is a non-selective adenosine receptor antagonist (targets A1, A2A, A2B, A3 receptors), which at low doses competitively inhibits A1 and A2A receptors [356]. Multiple studies in neonatal rodents show positive outcomes with low dose caffeine administration in close proximity to the acute hypoxic-ischemic insult, including reduced microglial activation, enhanced anti-inflammatory microglia and suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome following hypoxia-ischemia [357–359]. In large animal models, protective effects are more modest and even increased mortality was observed at high doses (≥ 60 mg/kg) in fetal sheep and newborn lambs [360]. Clinical evidence of beneficial effects of caffeine administration on the newborn brain is very limited. In a phase I trial in infants with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy undergoing hypothermia, caffeine (20 mg/kg loading + 5–10 mg/kg/day) was safe [361].

Melatonin has anti-oxidative activity through scavenging of harmful radicals and is also anti-inflammatory by inhibition of toll-like receptor-4, NLRP3 inflammasome and NFκB-dependent pathways. Premature infants born < 34 weeks are disadvantaged by low blood levels of melatonin that remain persistently lower with slow development of pineal gland production. In a model of acute asphyxia in preterm fetal sheep, melatonin, directly administered to the fetus, reduced inflammation and brain injury in the cerebral white matter [362]. Maternal melatonin administration in pregnancies with intrauterine growth retarded fetuses in sheep, reduced oxidative stress, normalized myelination, and improved lamb behavior after birth [363]. However, melatonin treatment only demonstrated limited protective effects on the brain in a rat model of chorioamnionitis [364]. In neonatal rats, melatonin was neuroprotective and improved behavioral deficits when given up to 48 h after hypoxia-ischemia [365]. Neuroprotective effects and reduced neuroinflammation were also shown in neonatal piglets when melatonin was used as an adjunct drug to hypothermia [366]. However, it was later shown that the beneficial effects were partially due to the protective effect of the vehicle [367]. Based on the animal experimental data, a small randomized controlled pilot study in hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy infants has been conducted. In the melatonin/hypothermia group, there were fewer seizures on follow-up EEG and less white matter abnormalities on MRI. At 6 months, the melatonin/hypothermia group had improved survival without neurological or developmental abnormalities [368–370]. A melatonin study was completed in a cohort of 20 control and 20 infants with neonatal encephalopathy; compared to controls, patients with neonatal encephalopathy that were treated with melatonin had decreased neutrophil expression of CD11b and toll-like receptor 4 after when serum was exposed to lipopolysaccharide [370]. An upcoming study will test acute high dose melatonin for infants with moderate to severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy that are also receiving therapeutic hypothermia [369].

Erythropoietin is an endogenous, developmentally regulated molecule that plays a role in neurorepair, neurorestoration, and immunomodulation for neonates [371–373]. Erythropoietin has been explored as a treatment both pre-clinically and in clinical trials due to its anti-apoptotic, antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and cell protective properties [374–377]. Both erythropoietin and its receptors are present in the neonatal brain across a variety of cell types including neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [378–380]. Perinatal brain injury can affect the ligand/receptor balance or exacerbate preexisting imbalances [381, 382]. The imbalance between erythropoietin and erythropoietin receptors can lead to cell death via a mismatch between upregulated receptors without adequate production [382]. When combined with other therapies, erythropoietin and erythropoietin receptors hold promise for long-term neuroprotection. New cocktail treatments seek to pair erythropoietin with other treatments including magnesium sulfate for treatment of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [383]; azithromycin for treatment of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [384, 385]; hypothermia for treatment of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [386–390]; melatonin for treatment of chorioamnionitis [391, 392], cerebral palsy [172, 392], and posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus of prematurity [173, 393].

In addition to repurposed drugs and novel cocktails, many immunotherapies for perinatal brain injury are in development including cell therapies, extracellular vesicles, microbiome therapies, probiotics, nanoparticles, and human milk [394]. Cell therapies in development include stem cells, umbilical-cord derived cells, and amnion epithelial cells as well as SHED and MUSE cells [395–397] Preclinical studies are addressing optimal dosing strategies, alone and in combination with therapeutic hypothermia for hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [398, 399], in cerebral palsy [400, 401], hydrocephalus [402], and other perinatal brain injuries such as seizure disorders, traumatic brain injury, and infection. Extracellular vesicles are transport vehicles that are produced by cells throughout the human body and carry a wide range of cargo including proteins, lipids, metabolites, and nucleic acids [403]. In the normal developing brain, extracellular vesicles mediate cell-to-cell communication; in injury and neuroinflammation cells upregulate and change the cargo of blebbing extracellular vesicles [404]. Preclinical research has shown that treatment with healthy brain cell derived or stem cell derived exosomes ameliorates injury [404–406]. To target the gut-brain axis, preclinical research is focused on the use of pre-, pro- and synbiotics as well as fecal transplants to repair the gut microbiome [407–410]. To treat chorioamnionitis effectively, more effective antibiotics and antibiotic regimes are being developed [411]. With respect to improved therapeutic delivery, researchers are developing nanoparticles for pediatric physiology and the developing CNS [412–414]. As human milk contains many key nutrients, stem cells, and antibodies, novel research focuses on applications of human milk for brain health and the administration of intranasal breast milk for neuroprotection after severe intraventricular hemorrhage [415–417].

Even with standard treatments such as therapeutic hypothermia for hypoxia-ischemia, many neonates with perinatal brain injury continue to experience lasting deficits. Given the variable neuroprotective effects of adjunct drugs such as melatonin, caffeine in different animal models and limited evidence in clinical studies, there is an urgent need for better understanding of the mechanisms of PBI alongside new therapeutic cocktails, targets, and therapies that actively promote the resolution of inflammation and restoration of brain homeostasis. While many promising novel biomarkers and therapeutics emerge and progress through preclinical and clinical research stages and translational pipelines, attention must be paid to feasibility, safety, timing, and neurodevelopmental trajectory. As neonates and children are a particularly vulnerable patient population, with rapidly changing normative physiology and pathophysiology, significant effort is required to feasibly translate preclinical research to the clinic. To this end, more research is needed to thoroughly address feasibility, resource utilization, timing constraints, and risk of immune suppression in neonates for all emerging therapeutics. As we learn more about the complex and evolving nature of neuroinflammation and interaction with peripheral organs, we are at a promising frontier for improving outcomes after perinatal brain injury.

Gaps in knowledge and future research areas

To support diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker development and uncover molecularly targeted therapies with precision for persistent inflammation, additional research is needed to define normative values of cytokines across gestational ages, cerebral and peripheral immune cell differentiation milestones, and brain region specific immune changes. As infrastructure and research to support preterm infants continues to grow, and former preterm infants age, further work is necessary to address life-long physiological differences between preterm and term born infants with brain injury.

When perinatal brain injury fails to resolve, inflammatory cascades can feed forward, converge and become self-propagating. Much of this self-propagation is facilitated by crosstalk between immune cells, but little is known about how the complex and diverse cell populations of the brain and the peripheral immune system work to communicate and how this changes across development. Future focused research efforts on the impacts of a persistent inflammatory microenvironment on the development and maintenance of the glymphatic system is necessary. Little is known about the development of the glymphatic system or its dysregulation in preterm infants, and it is unclear how waste removal, astrocyte maturation, emerging circadian rhythm with development, and neurological recovery is connected in perinatal brain injury.

Conclusion

Persistent, compounding, and sustained inflammation is a major reason for failed resolution of PBI. Major points of vulnerability during pregnancy, birth, and at each neonatal age are susceptible to inflammatory changes that impact various cells, pathways, and chemical signals to propagate and perpetuate injury. Specific cytokines – IL6, TNFα, and IL-1β – and chemokines – CXCL1 and CXCR2 - are upregulated with persistent inflammation. Microglia are critical to the regulation of inflammation; multiple forms of PBI dysregulate microglia to a state that exacerbates inflammatory injury. Alongside altered microglial dynamics, the other glia of the perinatal brain are also dysregulated including astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and polydendrocytes. Inflammation is systemic and the T cell populations are altered. The impact and effect of persistent inflammation is complex and multifaceted. The complexity of persistent inflammation in PBI also means there are a variety of new avenues for treatment and modulation including therapeutic treatments such as melatonin, caffeine and modulation of the gut microbiome with pro and pre-biotics. Persistent and compounding inflammation prevents PBI from resolution; understanding, treating, and resolving persistent inflammation is the key to curing PBI.
