Good involutions of conjugation subquandles
Lực Ta

Abstract
. Posed by Taniguchi, the classification of quandles with good involutions is a difficult question with applications to surface-knot theory. We address this question for subquandles of conjugation quandles, including all core quandles. We also study good involutions of faithful racks. In particular, we obtain sharp bounds on the number of good involutions of racks in these families. As an application of our results, we implement group-theoretic algorithms that compute all good involutions of conjugation quandles and core quandles; we provide data for those up to order 23 23 . As another application, we construct infinite families of connected, noninvolutory symmetric quandles. We also classify symmetric and Legendrian racks, quandles, and kei up to order 8 8 using a computer search. Finally, we exhibit an equivalence of categories between racks and Legendrian racks that induces an equivalence between involutory racks, Legendrian kei, and symmetric kei.

1.1.
Overview of results

Our main results are the following. Theorem
1.3
states that good involutions
\rho
of conjugation subquandles
\Conj X

\Conj G

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

\Conj X
be a subquandle of
\Conj G=(G,s)
.
Then for all functions

\Conj X

\rho(x)=(\zeta(x)x)^{-1},\qquad\zeta s_{x}=\zeta=\zeta\rho

for all

and Corollary

Theorem 1.4

Let

R=(X,s)

s^{-1}:X\to S_{X}
by
x\mapsto s_{x}^{-1}
. Then the rack
R^{\mathrm{op}}:=(X,s^{-1})
is a rack called the
dual rack
of
R
.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a rack
R
, an
antiautomorphism
of
R
is a rack isomorphism
\rho:R\xrightarrow{\sim}R^{\mathrm{op}}
. Let
\Anti R
be the set of antiautomorphisms of
R
. We say that
R
is
self-dual
if
\Anti R
is nonempty.

Concretely,
\Anti R
is the set of permutations
\rho\in S_{X}
that satisfy

\rho s_{x}=s^{-1}_{\rho(x)}\rho

for all
x\in X
.

Observation 3.3

(
[
21
]
*Rem. 2.6)
.

The composition of two rack antiautomorphisms is a rack automorphism, while the composition of an antiautomorphism and an automorphism (in either order) is an antiautomorphism.

Example 3.4
.

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. Then the following are equivalent:

•

R
is involutory.

•

R
is equal (not only isomorphic) to the dual rack
R^{\mathrm{op}}
.

•

\id_{X}\in\Anti R
.

Under the above conditions,
R
is self-dual, and
\Anti R=\Aut R
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
G
be a group, let
\iota\in S_{G}
be the inversion map
g\mapsto g^{-1}
, and let
\Conj X
be an inversion-closed subquandle of
\Conj G
. Then the restriction
\iota|_{X}
is an involution of
X
. Moreover,
\iota|_{X}
is an antiautomorphism of
\Conj X
because

\iota s_{y}(x)=(yxy^{-1})^{-1}=yx^{-1}y^{-1}=\iota(y)^{-1}\iota(x)\iota(y)=s^{-1}_{\iota(y)}\iota(x)

for all
x,y\in X
. In particular,
\Conj X
is self-dual. Note that
\iota\in\Aut(\Conj X)
if and only if
\Conj X
is a kei.

Example 3.6

(
[
48
]
*Ex. 2.16)
.

The tetrahedral quandle
Q
from Example
2.9
is self-dual; indeed,
\Anti Q
contains every transposition in
S_{4}
.

Example 3.7
.

Not all quandles are self-dual.
For example, let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be relatively prime integers, define the quotient ring
X:=(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})[t^{\pm 1}]/(t-a)
, and define
\varphi:X\to X
by
x\mapsto(1-t)x
. Then
\Alex(X,\varphi)
is called a
linear Alexander quandle
. A result of Nelson
[
35
]
*Cor. 2.8 states that
\Alex(X,\varphi)
is self-dual if and only if
a
is a square modulo
n/{\gcd(n,1-a)}
.

3.2.
Good involutions

This paper concerns the category of
symmetric racks
, which Kamada
[
25
]
introduced in 2007.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. A
good involution
of
R
is an involution
\rho\in S_{X}
that satisfies the equalities

\rho s_{x}=s_{x}\rho,\qquad s_{\rho(x)}=s^{-1}_{x}

for all
x\in X
. We denote the set of good involutions of
R
by
\Good R
.

A
symmetric rack
(resp.
symmetric quandle
) is a pair
(R,\rho)
where
R
is a rack (resp. quandle) and
\rho\in\Good R
.

Observation 3.9
.

The set of good involutions is a rack invariant.
Explicitly, if
\varphi:R_{1}\xrightarrow{\sim}R_{2}
is a rack isomorphism, then the assignment
\rho\mapsto\varphi\rho\varphi^{-1}
is a bijection from
\Good R_{1}
to
\Good R_{2}
.

Symmetric racks form a category with the following morphisms; symmetric quandles form a full subcategory of this category.

Definition 3.10
.

A
symmetric rack homomorphism
between two symmetric racks
(R_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(R_{2},\rho_{2})
is a rack homomorphism
\varphi
from
R_{1}
to
R_{2}
that intertwines the good involutions:

\varphi\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}\varphi.

A
symmetric rack isomorphism
is a bijective symmetric rack homomorphism.

Example 3.11

(
[
24
]
*Prop. 3.1)
.

Given a quandle
R=(X,s)
, let
I\subset S_{X}
the set of all involutions of
X
. Then
\Good R=I
if and only if
R
is a trivial quandle.

Example 3.12

(
[
25
]
*Ex. 2.4)
.

Let
G
be a group. Then the inversion map
\iota\in S_{G}
defined by
g\mapsto g^{-1}
is a good involution of
\Conj G
.

Example 3.13
.

Example
3.12
immediately implies its own generalization.
Let
G
be a group, let
\iota\in S_{G}
be the inversion map
g\mapsto g^{-1}
, and let
\Conj X
be a subquandle of
\Conj G
. Then the restriction
\iota|_{X}
is a good involution of
\Conj X
if and only if
\Conj X
is inversion-closed; cf. Section

.

Example 3.14

(
[
28
]
*Ex. 2.3)
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
z\in Z(G)
be a central element of order
1
or
2
. Then multiplication by
z
is a good involution of
\Core G
.

Example 3.15
.

Let
R=\Alex(G,\varphi)
be a generalized Alexander quandle. A theorem of Taniguchi
[
49
]
*Thm. 1.3 states that
\Good R
is nonempty if and only if

is a kei.

3.2.1.
Involutory symmetric racks

We recall a well-known result of Kamada and Oshiro
[
24
]
.

Proposition 3.16

(
[
24
]
*Prop. 3.4)
.

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack. Then the following statements are equivalent:

•

R
is involutory.

•

\id_{X}\in\Good R
.

•

\Good R
contains an element of
\Aut R
.

•

\Good R
is a nonempty subset of
\Aut R
.

Remark 3.17
.

Although Kamada and Oshiro only stated their result for quandles, their proof holds more generally for racks.

In fact, we have the following.

Proposition 3.18
.

If
R
is involutory, then
\Good R
is precisely the set
I
of involutions
\rho:X\to X
contained in the centralizer
C_{\Aut R}(\Inn R)
.

Proof.

The containment
\Good R\subseteq I
follows from Proposition
3.16
. Conversely, if
\rho\in I
, then

s_{\rho(x)}=\rho s_{\rho(x)}\rho=\rho^{2}s_{x}=s_{x}=s_{x}^{-1},

so
\rho
is a good involution of
R
.
∎

Corollary 3.19
.

Suppose that
R
is involutory. If
\Aut R
is a simple group, then
\Good R
is either
\{\id_{X}\}
or the set of all involutory automorphisms of
R
.

Proof.

Since
\Inn R
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut R
, so is the centralizer
C_{\Aut R}(\Inn R)
. Hence, the claim follows from Proposition
3.18
.
∎

4.
General results for racks

Let
R=(X,s)
be a rack.
In this section, we characterize good involutions
\rho\in\Good R
as involutory central antiautomorphisms of
R
(Proposition
4.2
). As a result, self-duality is a necessary condition for
R
to have good involutions (Corollary
4.4
), and every good involution factors through some automorphism
\varphi
and a given antiautomorphism
\iota
(Corollary
4.7
).

4.1.
Good involutions are antiautomorphisms

We record a “two-implies-three” rule for good involutions. This will facilitate the proof of Theorem
1.3
later in the paper.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all permutations
\rho\in S_{X}
, any pair of the following three conditions implies the other:

(1)

For all
x\in X
,
s_{\rho(x)}^{-1}=s_{x}
.

(2)

\rho
lies in the centralizer
C_{S_{X}}(\Inn R)
.

(3)

\rho
is an antiautomorphism of
R
.

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

For all involutions
\rho\in S_{X}
, the following are equivalent:

•

\rho
is a good involution of
R
.

•

\rho
satisfies at least two out of the three conditions in Lemma
4.1
.

•

\rho
satisfies all three of the conditions in Lemma
4.1
.

Proof.

Combined with the assumption that
\rho
is an involution, conditions
1
and
2
form the definition of a good involution. Hence, the claim follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Together, Example
3.4
and Proposition
4.2
recover Proposition
3.16
.

The following addresses Problem
1.2
.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
R
is not self-dual, then
R
has no good involutions.

Proof.

By definition,
R
is self-dual if and only if
\Anti R
is nonempty.
By Proposition
4.2
,
\Good R
is a subset of
\Anti R
, so the claim follows.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The inverse of Corollary
4.4
is not true in general, even if
\Anti R
contains involutions. For example, all transpositions in the symmetric group
S_{4}
are antiautomorphisms of the tetrahedral quandle
Q
from Example
2.9
. Nevertheless, Table
1
implies that
Q
has no good involutions; cf. Example
6.6
. In fact, there are infinitely many examples of this nature; see Proposition
5.10
.

4.2.
Factoring good involutions

In light of Corollary
4.4
, assume for the rest of this section that
R
is self-dual, and fix an antiautomorphism
\iota\in\Anti R
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The assignment
\varphi\mapsto\iota\varphi
is a bijection from
\Aut R
to
\Anti R
.

Proof.

Since
R\cong R^{\mathrm{op}}
, this follows from Observation
3.3
and the definition of
\Anti R
.
∎

The following corollaries follow directly from Propositions
4.2
and
4.6
. In particular, Corollary
4.7
states that knowing the elements of
\Aut R
is sufficient for computing
\Good R
.

Corollary 4.7
.

For all involutions
\rho\in S_{X}
,
\rho
is a good involution of
R
if and only if there exists an automorphism
\varphi\in\Aut R
such that
\rho=\iota\varphi
and at least one of the conditions
1
,
2
hold.

Corollary 4.8
.

The number of good involutions of
R
is at most the order of
\Aut R
:

|{\Good R}|\leq|{\Anti R}|=|{\Aut R}|.

Equality holds if and only if
\Anti R
only contains involutions in the centralizer
C_{S_{X}}(\Inn R)
.

Remark 4.9
.

The bound in Corollary
4.8
is sharp in the sense that there exist quandles
R
of infinitely many orders
n
such that
{\Good R}={\Anti R}
. To see this, let
n\geq 2
be an even integer, and define a rack structure
s
on the set
X=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
in cycle notation by

s_{1},s_{2}:=\id_{X};\qquad s_{k},s_{k+1}:=(k-2\;\;k-1)

for all odd
3\leq k<n
. Then
R:=(X,s)
is a kei, so Example
3.4
implies that
\Anti R
is the elementary abelian
2
-group

\Anti R=\Aut R=\langle(12),(34),\dots,(n-1\;\;n)\rangle\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n/2}.

In particular,
R
satisfies the last condition in Corollary
4.8
, so
{\Good R}={\Anti R}
.

5.
Faithful symmetric racks

In this section, we study Problems
1.1
and
1.2
for faithful racks.

5.1.
General results

Proposition 5.1
.

If
R
is faithful, then
R
has at most one good involution.

Proof.

Let
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\Good R
. For all
x\in X
,

s_{\rho(x)}=s^{-1}_{x}=s_{\rho^{\prime}(x)},

so
\rho(x)=\rho^{\prime}(x)
because
R
is faithful. Therefore,
\rho=\rho^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Example
2.9
and Proposition
5.10
give examples of faithful, self-dual quandles with no good involutions.

On the other hand, Clark et al.
[
6
]
*Prop. 5.13 provided infinitely many faithful, self-dual quandles that do have a (necessarily unique) good involution. The next two results do the same.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
G
be a group, let
\Conj X
be an inversion-closed subquandle of
\Conj G
, and let
\langle X\rangle
be the subgroup of
G
generated by
X
. If
\langle X\rangle
is centerless, then
\Good(\Conj X)=\{\iota|_{X}\}
.

Proof.

By assumption, the action of
\langle X\rangle
on
X
by conjugation is faithful, so Observation
2.11
states that
\Conj X
is faithful. Hence, the claim follows from Example
3.13
and Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

An inverse to Corollary
5.3
holds; see Corollary
6.5
.

Corollary 5.5
.

If
R
is faithful and involutory, then
\Good R=\{\id_{X}\}
.

Proof.

This follows from Propositions
3.16
and
5.1
.
∎

Example 5.6
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
\varphi\in\Aut G
be an automorphism of
G
. By a theorem of Taniguchi
[
49
]
*Thm. 1.3, the generalized Alexander quandle
\Alex(G,\varphi)
has a good involution if and only if it is a kei. In this case, if the only fixed point of
\varphi
is the identity element
1\in G
, then Example
2.8
and Corollary
5.5
imply that
\Good(\Alex(G,\varphi))=\{\id_{G}\}
.

In particular, if
G
is abelian and
2
-torsionless, then the only good involution of the Takasaki kei
T(G)
is the identity map
\id_{G}
; cf. Corollary
7.17
. This generalizes a result of Kamada and Oshiro
[
24
]
*Thm. 3.2 for dihedral quandles
R_{n}
of odd order.

5.2.
Symmetric Galkin quandles

Generalizing a construction of Galkin
[
16
]
in 1988, Clark et al.
[
6
]
introduced a family of quandles
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
called
Galkin quandles
in 2013. Galkin quandles were studied extensively in
[
7
]
[
6
]
.

In particular, Clark et al.
[
6
]
*Prop. 5.13 constructed good involutions of certain Galkin quandles. We show that no other good involutions of Galkin quandles exist. Aside from addressing Problems
1.1
and
1.2
, this is also motivated by finding infinitely many quandles that have involutory antiautomorphisms and no good involutions; cf. Remark
4.5
.

Throughout this section, let
A
be an additive abelian group.

Definition 5.7

(
[
7
]
)
.

Fix an element
c\in A
. Define functions
{\mu:\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}}
and
\tau:\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\to A
by

\mu(n):=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }n=0,\\
-1&\text{if }n=1,2,\end{cases}\qquad\tau(n):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n=0,1,\\
c&\text{if }n=2.\end{cases}

Define a rack structure
s
on the direct product
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times A
by

s_{(y,b)}(x,a):=(2y-x,-a+\mu(x-y)b+\tau(x-y)).

Then the pair
\mathcal{G}(A,c):=(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times A,s)
is a quandle called a
Galkin quandle
.

Remark 5.8
.

A similar definition of Galkin quandles also exists
[
6
]
*Def. 3.4; its equivalence to the above definition was proven in
[
6
]
*Lem. 3.6.

Remark 5.9

(
[
6
]
*Lem. 5.2, Prop. 5.11, Cor. 5.12)
.

a

•

All Galkin quandles are faithful.

•

For all Galkin quandles
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
, the involution
(x,a)\mapsto(-x,a)
is an antiautomorphism of
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
. In particular, all Galkin quandles are self-dual.

•

\mathcal{G}(A,c)
is involutory if and only if
c=0
.

5.2.1.
Results

Clark et al.
[
6
]
*Prop. 5.13, cf. Cor. 5.12 constructed good involutions of certain Galkin quandles. Namely, let
c\in A
be an element such that
2c=0
, and define

\rho_{c}(x,a):=(x,a+c)

for all
(x,a)\in\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times A
. Then
\rho_{c}
is a good involution of
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
.

Proposition 5.10
.

If
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
is a Galkin quandle, then

\Good(\mathcal{G}(A,c))=\begin{cases}\{\rho_{c}\}&\text{if }2c=0,\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

If
2c=0
, then
\rho_{c}\in\Good(\mathcal{G}(A,c))
by the construction of Clark et al. Conversely, for all good involutions
\rho=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
of
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
and for all
(x,a),(y,b)\in\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times A
, the expression

s_{\rho(y,b)}(x,a)=(2\rho_{1}(y)-x,-a+\mu(x-\rho_{1}(y))\rho_{2}(b)+\tau(x-\rho_{1}(y))

equals the expression

s_{(y,b)}^{-1}(x,a)=(2y-x,-a+\mu(y-x)b+\tau(y-x)).

In particular,
\rho_{1}
must be the identity map
\id_{\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}}
. Letting
z:=x-y
, we obtain

\mu(z)\rho_{2}(b)+\tau(z)=\mu(-z)b+\tau(-z).

Taking
z
to be
1
and
2
respectively yields

-\rho_{2}(b)=-b+c,\qquad-\rho_{2}(b)+c=-b,

from which we deduce that
2c=0
and
\rho_{2}(b)=b+c
for all
b\in A
. In particular,
\rho=\rho_{c}
.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

If
A
is
2
-torsionless, then for all
c\in A
, there are no good involutions of
\mathcal{G}(A,c)
.

6.
Symmetric conjugation quandles

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
(Theorem
6.7
) and two corollaries. We also consider some special cases along the way (Proposition
6.4
, Corollary
6.5
).
Henceforth, let
G
be a group, let
\Conj X
be a subquandle of
\Conj G
, and let
H:=\langle X\rangle
be the subgroup of
G
generated by
X
.

6.1.
Preliminary results

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we use the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.1
.

For all involutions
\rho\in S_{X}
,
\rho
is a good involution of
\Conj X
if and only if
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
and
\rho(x)x\in Z(H)
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

“
\implies
” If
\rho\in\Good(\Conj X)
, then
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
by Proposition
4.2
. We have to show that
\rho(x)xy=y\rho(x)x
for all
x,y\in X
. Since
\rho
is a good involution,

xyx^{-1}=s_{x}(y)=s^{-1}_{\rho(x)}(y)=\rho(x)^{-1}y\rho(x),

and the desired equality follows.

“
\impliedby
” Suppose that
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
and
\rho(x)x\in Z(H)
for all
x\in X
. By Proposition
4.2
, it suffices to show that
s_{\rho(x)}^{-1}=s_{x}
for all
x\in X
. Since
\rho(x)x\in Z(H)
, the equality

\rho(x)^{-1}Z(H)=xZ(H)

holds in the quotient group
H/Z(H)
. It follows from Example
2.22
that

s_{\rho(x)^{-1}}=s_{x}.

By the definition of
\Conj X
, the left-hand side equals
s_{\rho(x)}^{-1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Observation 6.2
.

An antiautomorphism
\rho
of
\Conj X
is a precisely a permutation of
X
satisfying

\rho(yxy^{-1})=\rho(y)^{-1}\rho(x)\rho(y)

for all
x,y\in X
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
be a function, and suppose that the function
\rho:X\to H
defined by

\rho(x):=\ (\zeta(x)x)^{-1}

is a permutation of
X
. Then
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
if and only if
\zeta(yxy^{-1})=\zeta(x)
for all
x,y\in X
.

Proof.

“
\implies
” Suppose that
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
. Since
\zeta(X)\subseteq Z(H)
, Observation
6.2
yields

\displaystyle yxy^{-1}\zeta(yxy^{-1})

\displaystyle=\rho(yxy^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\rho(y)^{-1}\rho(x)^{-1}\rho(y)

\displaystyle=yxy^{-1}\zeta(y)\zeta(x)\zeta(y)^{-1}

\displaystyle=yxy^{-1}\zeta(x)

for all
x,y\in X
. Hence,
\zeta(yxy^{-1})=\zeta(x)
.

“
\impliedby
” The proof is similar.
∎

6.2.
Intermediate results

The following results address Problems

and

6.3.
Main results

The following is Theorem
1.3
. After proving the theorem, we discuss its interpretation in terms of orbits.

Theorem 6.7
.

For all functions
\rho:X\to X
,
\rho
is a good involution of
\Conj X
if and only if there exists a function
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
such that

(6.1)

\rho(x)=(\zeta(x)x)^{-1},\qquad\zeta(yxy^{-1})=\zeta(x)=\zeta\rho(x)

for all
x,y\in X
.

Proof.

“
\implies
” Given
\rho\in\Good(\Conj X)
, Lemma
6.1
states that we can define
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
by

\zeta(x):=(\rho(x)x)^{-1}.

This way,
\rho
satisfies the first equality in (
6.1
). Since
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
(by Proposition
4.2
), the second equality in (
6.1
) follows from Lemma
6.3
. For all
x\in X
, the centrality of
\zeta(x)^{-1}=\rho(x)x
implies that
x\rho(x)=\rho(x)x
, so

\zeta\rho(x)=(\rho^{2}(x)\rho(x))^{-1}=(x\rho(x))^{-1}=(\rho(x)x)^{-1}=\zeta(x)

because
\rho
is an involution. This verifies the final equality in (
6.1
).

“
\impliedby
” If
\rho:X\to X
and
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
satisfy (
6.1
), then for all
x\in X
, the first and third equalities in (
6.1
) yield

\rho^{2}(x)=(\zeta\rho(x)\rho(x))^{-1}=(\zeta(x)\rho(x))^{-1}=(\zeta(x)\zeta(x)^{-1}x^{-1})^{-1}=x

because
\zeta(x)\in Z(H)
. In other words,
\rho
is an involution, so by Lemma
6.1
, it suffices to show that
\rho\in\Anti(\Conj X)
and
p(x)x\in Z(H)
for all
x\in X
. But the former condition follows from Lemma
6.3
, and the latter follows from the first equality in (
6.1
).
∎

Example 6.8
.

Taking
\rho
to be the inversion map
x\mapsto x^{-1}
corresponds to letting
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
be the constant function
x\mapsto 1
. Hence, Theorem
6.7
recovers Example
3.13
.

Remark 6.9
.

The condition that
\zeta(yxy^{-1})=\zeta(x)
means that
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
is constant on orbits of
X
under the action of
H
by conjugation. These orbits are precisely the connected components of
\Conj X
; cf. Lemma
2.25
. Denote the set of all such orbits by
X/H
.

Therefore, Theorem
6.7
states that every good involution
\rho
of
\Conj X
factors through the natural projection
\pi:X\twoheadrightarrow X/H
and a unique function
\zeta^{*}:X/H\to Z(H)
. Moreover,
\zeta^{*}
completely determines
\rho
.

Remark 6.10
.

If
X=G
, then the condition that
\zeta(yxy^{-1})=\zeta(x)
states that
\zeta:G\to Z(G)
is a class function.

6.3.1.
Corollaries

We provide upper bounds on the number of good involutions of
\Conj X
. In the case that
\Conj X
is connected, we also simplify the characterization of
\Good(\Conj X)
in Theorem
6.7
.
In the following, let
X/H
denote the set of orbits of
X
under the action of
H
by conjugation.

Corollary 6.11
.

Suppose that
\Conj X
is connected.
Then for all functions
\rho:X\to X
,
\rho
is a good involution of
\Conj X
if and only if there exists a central element
z\in Z(H)
such that

\rho(x)=zx^{-1}

for all
x\in X
. In particular,

|{\Good(\Conj X)}|\leq|{Z(H)}|.

If
X
is not closed under inverses, then this bound is strict.

Proof.

By Remark
6.9
, every good involution
\rho
of
\Conj X
factors through the natural projection
\pi:X\twoheadrightarrow X/H
and a unique function
\zeta^{*}:X/H\to Z(H)
.
By hypothesis,
X/H=\{X\}
is a singleton set, so
\zeta^{*}
is a constant function. Hence, taking

z:=(\zeta^{*}(X))^{-1}

yields the main claim due to Theorem
6.7
. The final claim follows from Example
6.8
.
∎

In other words, if
\Conj X
is connected, then the set
\Good(\Conj X)
is in bijection with the set of central elements
z\in Z(H)
such that
zx^{-1}\in X
for all
x\in X
.

Even if
\Conj X
is not connected, the following bound holds.

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
k(X):=|{X/H}|
. Then

|{\Good(\Conj X)}|\leq\min(|{\Aut(\Conj X)}|,|{Z(H)}|^{k(X)}).

If
X
is not closed under inverses, then the bound
|{\Good(\Conj X)}|<|{Z(H)}|^{k(X)}
is strict.

Proof.

The bound
|{\Good(\Conj X)}|\leq|{\Aut(\Conj X)}|
comes from Corollary
4.8
.
On the other hand, Remark
6.9
shows that there are at least as many functions
\zeta^{*}:X/H\to Z(H)
as there are good involutions
\rho
; that is,
|{Z(H)}|^{k(X)}\geq|{\Good(\Conj X)}|
. The final claim follows from Example
6.8
.
∎

Remark 6.13
.

By Corollary
6.5
, the bounds in Corollaries
6.11
and
6.12
are equalities whenever
H
is centerless and
\Conj X
is inversion-closed. Furthermore, Example
9.2
provides a connected conjugation subquandle
\Conj X
of order
30
that attains these bounds even though
Z(H)
is nontrivial, and Example
9.5
provides a similar example whose order is infinite.

Straightforward.

Let

1.4

6.7
to
\Conj X

.

Observation 7.4

in

Observation 7.5

For all

7.1.3.
Calculation of the center

To apply Theorem
6.7
to
\Conj X
, we first compute
Z(H)
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
G
be a group containing an element
z
such that
zgz^{-1}=g^{-1}
for all
g\in G
. Then
G
is an elementary abelian
2
-group.

Proof.

For all
g,h\in G
, conjugating the product
g^{-1}h^{-1}
by
z
shows that
g
and
h
commute. Thus,
G
is abelian, so

g=zgz^{-1}=g^{-1}

for all
g\in G
.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
a\in Z(G)
. Then
(a,a,0)\in H
if and only if
a\in A
.

Proof.

We show that
a\in A
if
(a,a,0)\in H
; the proof of the reverse implication is similar.
Since
(a,a,0)\in H
, Observation
7.4
shows that
(a,a,0)
is a product of an even number of elements of
X
. That is, there exist a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{2n}\in G
such that

(a,a,0)=\prod^{2n}_{i=1}(g_{i},g_{i}^{-1},1)=\prod^{2n-1}_{i\text{ odd}}(g_{i}g_{i+1}^{-1},g_{i}^{-1}g_{i+1},0)=(g_{1}g_{2}^{-1}\cdots g_{2n-1}g_{2n}^{-1},g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\cdots g_{2n-1}^{-1}g_{2n},0)

in
H
.
Since
a\in Z(G)
, the equality of the first two coordinates shows that
a\in A
.
∎

Proposition 7.8
.

The center of
H
is

Z(H)=\{(a,a,0)\mid a\in A\}\cong A.

Proof.

If
a\in A
, then
(a,a,0)\in H
by Lemma
7.7
, and verifying that
(a,a,0)\in Z(H)
is straightforward.

Conversely, let
(a,b,i)\in Z(H)
be arbitrary.
If
i=1
, then Observation
7.5
shows that

(g,g^{-1},0)=(a,b,1)(g,g^{-1},0)(a,b,1)^{-1}=(ag^{-1}a^{-1},bgb^{-1},0)

for all
g\in G
.
It follows from Lemma
7.6
that
G
is an elementary abelian
2
-group, which contradicts the assumption we made at the beginning of this section.

Thus, we must have
i=0
. By Lemma
7.7
, it now suffices to show that
a\in Z(G)
and
a=b
. Indeed,

(g,g^{-1},0)=(a,b,0)(g,g^{-1},0)(a,b,0)^{-1}=(aga^{-1},bg^{-1}b^{-1},0)

for all
g\in G
, so
a\in Z(G)
. On the other hand,

(1,1,1)=(a,b,0)(1,1,1)(a,b,0)^{-1}=(ab^{-1},ba^{-1},1),

so
a=b
.
∎

7.1.4.
Preliminary versions of Theorem
1.4

We state two preliminary versions of Theorem
1.4
.
Denote the rack structure of
\Conj X
by
t
.

Proposition 7.9
.

For all functions
\rho^{\prime}:X\to X
,
\rho^{\prime}
is a good involution of
\Conj X
if and only if there exists a function
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
such that

\rho^{\prime}(g,g^{-1},1)=\zeta(g,g^{-1},1)(g,g^{-1},1),\qquad\zeta t_{(g,g^{-1},1)}=\zeta=\zeta\rho^{\prime}

for all
(g,g^{-1},1)\in X
.

Proof.

This follows from combining Theorem
6.7
with Observation
7.4
.
∎

In the following, denote the rack structure of
\Core G
by
s
.

Proposition 7.10
.

For all functions
\rho:G\to G
,
\rho
is a good involution of
\Core G
if and only if there exists a function
\alpha:G\to A
such that

\rho(g)=\alpha(g)g,\qquad\alpha s_{g}=\alpha=\alpha\rho

for all
g\in G
.

Proof.

We will show that the claim is equivalent to Proposition
7.9
.
Given any function
\alpha:G\to A
, define a function
\mathcal{F}(\alpha):X\to H
by

\mathcal{F}(\alpha)(g,g^{-1},1):=(\alpha(g),\alpha(g),0).

By Proposition
7.8
, the image of
\mathcal{F}(\alpha)
lies in
Z(H)
. Conversely, given any function
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
, define a function
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta):G\to G
by sending
g\in G
to the first coordinate of
\zeta(g,g^{-1},1)\in Z(H)
.
By Proposition
7.8
, the image of
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)
lies in
A
. Hence, it is straightforward to verify that the assignments
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are mutually inverse.

Now, recall that the map
\varphi
from (
7.1
) is a quandle isomorphism from
\Core G
to
\Conj X
. The equivalence of the claim with Proposition
7.9
therefore follows from Observation
3.9
and the following straightforward calculations: For all elements
g\in G
and functions
\rho:G\to G
and
\rho^{\prime}:X\to X
respectively satisfying the conditions of the claim and Proposition
7.9
, we have

\varphi\rho\varphi^{-1}(g,g^{-1},1)=\mathcal{F}(\alpha)(g,g^{-1},1)(g,g^{-1},1),\qquad\varphi^{-1}\rho^{\prime}\varphi(g)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)(g)g,

so that

\mathcal{F}(\alpha s_{g})=\mathcal{F}(\alpha)t_{(g,g^{-1},1)},\qquad\mathcal{F}(\alpha\rho)=\mathcal{F}(\alpha)\varphi\rho\varphi^{-1}

and, similarly,

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta t_{(g,g^{-1},1)})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)s_{g},\qquad\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta\rho^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\zeta)\varphi^{-1}\rho^{\prime}\varphi.

∎

8.
Algorithms

As applications of Theorems
6.7
and
7.11
, we describe procedures for computing
\Good R
when
R
is a conjugation subquandle
\Conj X
or a core quandle
\Core G
. We also discuss computational findings for groups up to order
23
.

Carrying over our notation from Section
6
, let
G
be a group, let
\Conj X
be a subquandle of
\Conj G
, and let
H:=\langle X\rangle\leq G
.

8.1.
How to compute
\Good(\Conj X)

Iterating the following procedure constructs all good involutions
\rho
of
\Conj X
. The procedure is based on Theorem
6.7
; cf. Remark
6.9
.

Algorithm 8.1
.

Let
X/H
be the set of orbits of
X
under the action of
H
by conjugation (that is, the canonical action of
\Inn X
), and let
\pi:X\twoheadrightarrow X/H
be the natural projection.

(1)

First, pick any function
\zeta^{*}:X/H\to Z(H)
, and define functions
\zeta:X\to Z(H)
and
\rho:X\to H
by

\zeta:=\zeta^{*}\pi,\qquad\rho(x):=(\zeta(x)x)^{-1}.

(2)

If
\rho(X)\subseteq X
and
\zeta\rho=\zeta
, then Theorem
1.3
states that
\rho
is a good involution, so return
\rho
. Else, return to the previous step and pick a different function
\zeta^{*}
.

To compute
\Good(\Conj X)
, repeat these steps until all possible functions
\zeta^{*}
have been considered.

Theorem
6.7
states that
\Good(\Conj X)
is precisely the set of involutions
\rho
that can be produced by the above method; cf. Remark
6.9
.

Remark 8.2
.

If
\Conj X
is connected, then Algorithm
8.1
simplifies via Corollary
6.11
in the obvious way: For each central element
z\in Z(H)
, test whether
zx^{-1}\in X
for all
x\in X
. The good involutions
\rho
of
\Conj X
are precisely the maps
x\mapsto zx^{-1}
for which this test succeeds.

8.1.1.
Example of Algorithm
8.1

In the following example, we use Algorithm
8.1
to produce a good involutions of
\rho
of a conjugation quandle such that
\rho
is neither the inversion map nor the identity map.

Example 8.3
.

Let
n\geq 4
be an even integer, and let
D_{n}
denote the dihedral group of order
2n
:

D_{n}=\langle r,s\mid r^{n}=1=s^{2},\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle.

Following Algorithm
8.1
, we construct a good involution
\rho
of
\Conj D_{n}
.

Since
n
is even, the center of
D_{n}
is
Z(D_{n})=\{1,r^{n/2}\}
. Define the function
\zeta^{*}
on conjugacy classes
C
of
D_{n}
by

\zeta^{*}(C):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }C\subset Z(D_{n}),\\
r^{n/2}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

so that
\zeta:=\zeta^{*}\pi
is defined on elements
g
of
D_{n}
by

\zeta(g)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }g\in Z(D_{n}),\\
r^{n/2}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
\rho:D_{n}\to D_{n}
is the function defined by

\rho(g):=(\zeta(g)g^{-1})^{-1}=\begin{cases}g&\text{if }g\in Z(D_{n}),\\
r^{n/2-k}&\text{if }g=r^{k}\text{ where }1\leq k<n\text{ and }k\neq\frac{n}{2},\\
sr^{n/2+k}&\text{if }g=sr^{k}\text{ for some }0\leq k<n.\end{cases}

Verifying that
\zeta\rho=\zeta
is straightforward.
Hence, Theorem
6.7
states that
\rho
is a good involution of
\Conj D_{n}
. Note that
\rho
does not equal the inversion map
g\mapsto g^{-1}
.

Combined with Corollary
6.5
(or Corollary
5.3
), Example
8.3
yields the following.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer. Then the conjugation quandle
\Conj D_{n}
has more than one good involution if and only if
n
is even.

8.2.1.
Examples of Algorithm
8.5

The following three examples show that the upper bound in Corollary

Given an integer

, let
\Dic_{n}
denote the dicyclic group of order

\Dic_{n}=\langle a,x\mid a^{2n}=1,\ x^{2}=a^{n},\ xax^{-1}=a^{-1}\rangle.

Proposition 8.6
.

For all odd integers
n\geq 3
, the core quandle
\Core\Dic_{n}
has exactly
2^{(n+3)/2}
good involutions. In particular,
\Dic_{n}
exceeds the lower bound and attains the upper bound in Corollary

Proof.

As in Algorithm
8.5
, let
\pi:\Dic_{n}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{O}
be the natural projection, and note that
T=\{1,x^{2}\}
. On the other hand, routine calculations show that
\mathcal{O}
contains exactly
(n+3)/2
orbits:

•

one orbit
\pi(x)=\{x,x^{3}\}
of size
2
,

•

one orbit
\pi(1)=\{a^{m}\mid 1\leq m\leq 2n\}
of size
2n
, and

•

the following
(n-1)/2
orbits of size
4
:

\pi(xa^{m})=\{xa^{m},xa^{m+1},xa^{2n-m-1},xa^{2n-m}\}\qquad\text{for all }1\leq m\leq\frac{n-1}{2}.

In particular, the lower and upper bounds in Corollary
7.14
equal
2
and
2^{(n+3)/2}
, respectively.

Evidently, each orbit in
\mathcal{O}
is closed under multiplication by elements of
T
. That is,

\pi(tg)=\pi(g)

for all
t\in T
and
g\in\Dic_{n}
. In particular, given any function
\alpha^{*}:\mathcal{O}\to T
, letting
g\in G
be arbitrary and taking
t:=\alpha^{*}\pi(g)
yields

\pi(\alpha^{*}\pi(g)g)=\pi(g)

for all
g\in G
.
Applying
\alpha^{*}
to both sides recovers the condition that
\alpha\rho=\alpha
in Algorithm
8.5
. Hence, Theorem
7.11
states that
\alpha^{*}
defines a unique good involution
\rho
of
\Core\Dic_{n}
.

Since
\alpha^{*}:\mathcal{O}\to T
was an arbitrary function, Theorem
7.11
states that the
2^{(n+3)/2}
possible functions
\alpha^{*}
collectively define
2^{(n+3)/2}
distinct good involutions
\rho
of
\Core D_{n}
, and no other good involutions exist. This completes the proof.
∎

Similar arguments show the following.

Proposition 8.7
.

For all even integers
n\geq 2
, the core quandle
\Core\Dic_{n}
has exactly
2^{n/2+3}
good involutions. In particular,
\Dic_{n}
exceeds the lower bound and attains the upper bound in Corollary
7.14
.

Proposition 8.8

(cf.
[
24
]
*Thm. 3.2)
.

If
n\geq 4
is a multiple of
4
, then the dihedral quandle
R_{n}=T(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
has exactly four good involutions. In particular,
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
exceeds the lower bound and attains the upper bound in Corollary
7.14
.

Constructions

In this section, we compute
\Good(\Conj X)
for various connected conjugation subquandles
\Conj X

Carrying over our notation from Section

\Conj X

9.1.
Constructing CNS-quandles

The following provides a robust, group-theoretic way to construct CNS-quandles.

Proposition 9.1
.

Suppose that
\Conj X
satisfies the following three conditions:

•

X
is a conjugacy class of
H
.

•

There exists an element
x\in X
such that
x^{2}\notin Z(H)
.

•

\Conj X
is inversion-closed.

Then
(\Conj X,\iota|_{X})
is a CNS-quandle.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
2.25
, Observation
2.11
, and Example
3.13
.
∎

9.2.
Examples

Example 9.2

Let

It follows that each of the seven aforementioned conjugacy classes

Let

An infinite-order example

Example 9.5
.

Let

be the special unitary group
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, considered as a matrix group. We compute the good involutions of certain connected subquandles of
\Conj G

, which have various knot-theoretic and representation-theoretic applications
[

[

It is well-known (see, for example,

10.
Connections with Legendrian racks

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.10
and several other equivalences of categories (Theorem
10.1
). Before that, we introduce kink-involutory racks and discuss medial racks, GL-racks, and Legendrian racks. We also discuss a canonical rack automorphism
\theta
that plays a fundamental role in the theory.

10.1.
Discussion of results

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 10.1
.

In each of the following pairs or triples, the categories mentioned are equivalent (in fact, isomorphic), and so are their full subcategories whose objects are medial:

•

Racks
(\mathsf{Rack})
and Legendrian racks
(\mathsf{LR})
.

•

Racks for which the canonical automorphism
\theta
is an involution
(\mathsf{KIR})
and Legendrian quandles
(\mathsf{LQ})
.

•

Involutory GL-racks
(R,\mathtt{u})
in which
\mathtt{u}
is an involution and involutory symmetric racks.

•

Involutory racks
(\mathsf{InvRack})
, Legendrian kei
(\mathsf{LK})
, and symmetric kei
(\mathsf{SK})
.

After we construct functors between
\mathsf{Rack}
and
\mathsf{LR}
, Theorem
10.1
will more or less follow from the definitions, all of which we provide in this section.

10.1.1.
Corollaries

In tandem with
[

]
*Thm. 5.6, Theorem

yields the following.

Corollary 10.2
.

The category
\mathsf{LR}
and the category of GL-quandles are isomorphic, and so are their full subcategories whose objects are medial.

By combining Theorem

The center of the category

Concretely,
\eta\in Z(\mathcal{C})

10.2.2.
Kink-involutory racks

We introduce a class of racks called
kink-involutory racks
that generalize quandles and involutory racks.

Definition 10.7
.

A rack
R=(X,s)
is called
kink-involutory
if its canonical automorphism
\theta
is an involution.
Let
\mathsf{KIR}
be the full subcategory of
\mathsf{Rack}
whose objects are kink-involutory.

Example 10.8
.

All quandles are kink-involutory.

Proposition 10.9
.

All involutory racks are kink-involutory.

Proof.

Let
R=(X,s)
be an involutory rack. For all
x\in X
,

\theta^{2}(x)=\theta s_{x}(x)=s_{s_{x}(x)}s_{x}(x)=s_{x}s_{x}(x)=x.

Thus,
\theta^{2}=\id_{X}
.
∎

Example 10.10
.

For an example of a kink-involutory rack that is neither involutory nor a quandle, consider the rack
R=(X,s)
with underlying set
X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
whose rack structure is given by the permutations

s_{1}=s_{2}=(12)(345),\quad\quad s_{3}=s_{4}=s_{5}=(12)

in cycle notation. Then
s_{1}^{2}\neq\id_{X}
, so
R
is not involutory. Also,
\theta=(12)
is a nonidentity involution, so
R
is kink-involutory and not a quandle.

10.3.1.
Centrality of GL-structures

By Definition
10.16
, all integer powers of GL-structures
\mathtt{u}
and canonical rack automorphisms
\theta
lie in the categorical center
Z(\mathsf{GLR})
. In fact,
Z(\mathsf{GLR})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is the free abelian group generated by these two collections of automorphisms; see
[
43
]
*Thm. 5.1.

10.3.2.
Legendrian racks

As their name suggests,
Legendrian racks
are a special class of GL-racks defined below. They were introduced by Ceniceros et al.
[
4
]
in 2021, and they generalize the constructions of Kulkarni and Prathamesh
[
31
]
.

Although the following definition differs from the original definition of Ceniceros et al., their equivalence was proven in
[
43
]
*Cor. 3.13.

Definition 10.17

(
[
43
]
)
.

Let
(R,\mathtt{u})
be a GL-rack. We say that
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a
Legendrian rack
if
\theta=\mathtt{u}^{-2}
, in which case we say that
\mathtt{u}
is a
Legendrian structure
on
R
.

We say that a Legendrian rack
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a
Legendrian quandle
if
R
is a quandle or, equivalently, if
\mathtt{u}
is an involution. Similarly, we say that
(R,\mathtt{u})
is a
Legendrian kei
if
R
is a kei.

Let
\mathsf{LR}
,
\mathsf{LQ}
, and
\mathsf{LK}
be the full subcategories of
\mathsf{GLR}
whose objects are Legendrian racks, Legendrian quandles, and Legendrian kei, respectively.

Example 10.18

(
[
30
]
*Ex. 3.6)
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
z\in Z(G)
be a central element of
G
.
Then multiplication by
z
defines a GL-structure on the conjugation quandle
\Conj G
. This GL-quandle is a Legendrian quandle if and only if
z^{2}=1
in
G
.

The following part of Theorem
10.1
follows immediately.

Proposition 10.19
.

In each of the following pairs, the categories mentioned have precisely the same objects and morphisms:

•

Involutory GL-racks
(R,\mathtt{u})
in which
\mathtt{u}
is an involution and involutory symmetric racks.

•

Legendrian kei
(\mathsf{LK})
and symmetric kei
(\mathsf{SK})
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Proposition
3.18
and the definitions of
\mathsf{GLR}
and
\mathsf{LK}
.
∎

10.4.
Proof of Theorem
10.1

To prove Theorem
10.1
, we construct functors
F:\mathsf{LR}\to\mathsf{Rack}
and
F^{-1}:\mathsf{Rack}\to\mathsf{LR}
satisfying the following criteria; cf.
[
43
]
*Sec. 5.

Proposition 10.20
.

The functors
F
and
F^{-1}
are mutually inverse.
Moreover, both functors send medial objects to medial objects.

Proposition 10.21
.

F(\mathsf{LQ})=\mathsf{KIR}
.

Proposition 10.22
.

F(\mathsf{LK})=\mathsf{InvRack}
.

Once we define
F
and
F^{-1}
, these three results will more or less follow from the definitions. Together with Proposition
10.19
, these results yield Theorem
10.1
.

10.4.1.
Construction of functors

Define
F:\mathsf{LR}\to\mathsf{Rack}
on objects by

(X,s,\mathtt{u})\mapsto(X,\mathtt{u}^{3}s),

where the rack structure
\mathtt{u}^{3}s:X\to S_{X}
is defined by
x\mapsto\mathtt{u}^{3}s_{x}
. Let
F
preserve the underlying set maps of morphisms in
\mathsf{LR}
.

To construct an inverse functor
F^{-1}:\mathsf{Rack}\to\mathsf{LR}
, define
F^{-1}
on objects by

R=(X,s)\mapsto(X,\theta^{-3}_{R}s,\theta_{R}),

where the rack structure
\theta^{-3}_{R}s:X\to S_{X}
is defined by
x\mapsto\theta^{-3}_{R}s_{x}
. Let
F
preserve the underlying set maps of morphisms in
\mathsf{Rack}
.

Using the inclusions
\mathtt{u}^{3}\in Z(\mathsf{GLR})
and
\theta,\theta^{-3}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
, it is straightforward to verify that
F
and
F^{-1}
are covariant functors.

10.4.2.
Proofs of Propositions
10.20
–
10.22

Proof of Proposition
10.20
.

It is clear that
FF^{-1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rack}}
. Using the fact that
\theta_{R}=\mathtt{u}^{-2}
for all Legendrian racks
(R,\mathtt{u})
, verifying that
F^{-1}F=\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{LR}}
is straightforward.
Finally, the claim that
F
and
F^{-1}
send medial objects to medial objects follows from the fact that, for all GL-racks
(R,\mathtt{u})
, the maps
\theta_{R}^{-3}
and
\mathtt{u}
are endomorphisms of
R
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
10.21
.

We verify that
F(\mathsf{LQ})=\mathsf{KIR}
. If
R=(X,s)
is a kink-involutory rack, then it is straightforward to check that the underlying rack
(X,\theta^{-1}_{R}s)
of the Legendrian rack
F^{-1}(R)
is a quandle.

Conversely, if
L=(X,s,\mathtt{u})
is a Legendrian quandle, then

\theta_{F(L)}^{2}=(\mathtt{u}^{3}\theta_{(X,s)})^{2}=\mathtt{u}^{2}=\id_{X},

so
F(L)
is kink-involutory.
∎

Proof of Proposition
10.22
.

We verify that
F(\mathsf{LK})=\mathsf{InvRack}
. Let
L=(X,s,\mathtt{u})
be a Legendrian kei. Since
\mathtt{u}
and
s_{x}
commute and are involutions for all
x\in X
, the rack
F(L)=(X,\mathtt{u}s)
is involutory.

Conversely, if
R=(X,s)
is an involutory rack, then Proposition
10.9
and Proposition
10.21
show that
F^{-1}(R)
is a Legendrian quandle with rack structure
\theta_{R}^{-1}s
. Since
\theta^{-1}\in Z(\mathsf{Rack})
, Proposition
10.9
shows that this rack structure is involutory. Hence,
F^{-1}(R)
is a Legendrian kei.
∎

Open questions

We conclude by proposing directions for future work.

Problem 11.1
.

Compute the set
\Anti R
for various families of racks
R
.

Problem 11.2
.

Classify the group
\Aut R\cup\Anti R
for various families of racks
R
.

Problem 11.3
.

Compute the set
\Good R
for more families of racks
R
, including those that do not embed into conjugation quandles (cf.
[
1
]
[
23
]
).

Problem 11.4
.

Generalize Theorem
1.3
to
n
-fold conjugation quandles (see
[
34
]
*Ex. 2 in Sec. 2).

Problem 11.5
.

Distinguish the symmetric quandles obtained from Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
up to isomorphism.

In light of our computational findings in Appendix
B
, we also pose the following two questions.

Problem 11.6
.

Let
G
be a finite nonabelian group of order
n
. Is the number of good involutions of
\Conj G
completely determined by
n
, the order of the center
Z(G)
, and the class number
k(G)
of
G
? If so, is there an exact formula to compute this number?

Problem 11.7
.

In the setting of Corollary
6.12
, suppose that
|Z(H)|,|X/H|\geq 2
(so, in particular,
\Conj X
is not connected). Is the upper bound on
|{\Good(\Conj X)}|

6.12
always strict?

.

In light of Proposition

Conjecture 11.8
.

Let
R
be a connected quandle. If
R
is noninvolutory, then

has at most one good involution.

By Observation

and Corollary

, the specialization of Conjecture

to subquandles

\Conj G
is equivalent to the following group-theoretic conjecture.

Conjecture 11.9
.

Let
G
be a group, and let

be a subset of

such that

. If there exist distinct central elements

such that
z_{1}x^{-1},z_{2}x^{-1}\in X
