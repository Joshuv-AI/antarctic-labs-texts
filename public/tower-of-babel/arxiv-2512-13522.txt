Collective Annealing by Switching Temperatures: a Boltzmann-type framework
Frédéric Blondeel, Lorenzo Pareschi, Giovanni Samaey

Abstract
The design of effective cooling strategies is a crucial component in simulated annealing algorithms based on the Metropolis method. Traditionally, this is achieved through inverse logarithmic decays of the temperature to ensure convergence to global minima. In this work, we propose Collective Annealing by Switching Temperatures (CAST), a novel collective simulated annealing dynamic in which agents interact to learn an adaptive cooling schedule. Inspired by the particle-swapping mechanism of parallel tempering, we introduce a Boltzmann-type framework in which particles exchange temperatures through stochastic binary interactions. This process leads to a gradual decrease of the average temperature in the system. Numerical results demonstrate that the proposed approach consistently outperforms classical simulated annealing with both logarithmic and geometric cooling schedules, particularly in terms of convergence speed.



Keywords:
Simulated annealing, parallel tempering, cooling strategies, Boltzmann equation, global optimization

Introduction

Global optimization is inherently difficult, primarily due to two factors.
First, there is no guarantee that a computed solution corresponds to the global minimum rather than a local one, particularly in nonconvex landscapes characterized by multiple attractors.
Second, in many practical settings, gradient information may be unavailable, unreliable, or too costly to evaluate, and the objective function itself may not admit an explicit analytic representation.
These issues make conventional gradient-based schemes, such as stochastic gradient descent and its variants, inapplicable or prone to premature convergence.
In such settings, a popular alternative involves the use of
metaheuristic algorithms
: stochastic, population-based methods designed to explore complex and multimodal energy landscapes. Classical examples include Simulated Annealing (SA)
[
31
]
, Genetic Algorithms (GA)
[
26
,
22
]
, Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)
[
19
]
, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
[
30
]
, and more recently, Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO)
[
12
,
40
]
. These algorithms do not rely on gradient information and instead employ sophisticated exploration-exploitation heuristics to guide the system towards a probable global minimum.

Simulated Annealing was introduced in the early 1980s by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, and Vecchi
[
31
]
. Originally designed to solve combinatorial optimization problems like the Traveling Salesman Problem, SA has since found applications across engineering, machine learning, and physics
[
17
,
43
]
. The method is inspired by the metallurgical annealing process, where a material is slowly cooled to reduce its Gibbs free energy and achieve a low-energy crystalline structure. Computationally, SA implements this idea via a Metropolis–Hastings Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) dynamic
[
24
,
35
]
combined with a global cooling schedule. At high temperatures, the system accepts both better and worse solutions, enabling wide exploration. As the temperature decreases, the system becomes more selective, favoring low-energy configurations. Under ideal conditions, convergence to the global minimum is guaranteed by an inverse-logarithmic cooling schedule
[
23
]
, although such schedules are often impractical in applications due to slow convergence rates.

Parallel Tempering (PT), also known as Replica Exchange Monte Carlo, was originally developed to enhance MCMC sampling
[
33
,
44
,
46
]
. PT generalizes SA by introducing multiple replicas of the system, each evolving at a distinct temperature
[
21
,
20
,
34
]
. High-temperature replicas explore the energy landscape more freely, while low-temperature replicas refine the search near local minima. By allowing periodic swaps of configurations between replicas, PT improves mixing and avoids trapping in poor minima.

In traditional parallel tempering, interactions among replicas are performed through Metropolis-type swaps of states or temperatures.
In this work, we propose Collective Annealing by Switching Temperatures (CAST), a generalization in which temperature exchange is modeled as a stochastic binary interaction between particles.
In contrast to other collective dynamics, the binary exchange model offers a computationally efficient scheme, with cost scaling linearly in the number of samples due to its localized pairwise interactions.
Our approach is conceptually related to thermostatting techniques such as the Nosé–Hoover method
[
37
,
27
]
, which enforce canonical sampling by coupling the system to an auxiliary dynamical variable.
Inspired by kinetic theory and Boltzmann-type models, our approach introduces gradual, probabilistic temperature exchanges, enabling a collective and adaptive cooling strategy.

A common drawback of many metaheuristic algorithms is the lack of a rigorous theoretical foundation, as they are typically formulated only at the particle level. To investigate the theoretical properties of our method, we employ tools from classical kinetic theory to characterize the behavior of the system in the limit of a large number of particles
[
38
,
8
,
39
,
1
]
. We refer also to the recent work
[
25
]
on kinetic formulations of simulated annealing with entropy-based cooling strategies. We obtain a kinetic formulation that enables a rigorous mean-field description. Specifically, we describe the evolution of the joint probability density
f(x,T,t)
of particles with position
x
and temperature
T
at time
t
through the equation

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}(f)=Q(f,f),

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{L}(f)
is a linear operator governing spatial exploration based on a Metropolis search algorithm, and
Q(f,f)
is a Boltzmann-type operator modeling stochastic temperature interactions. Let us mention that Boltzmann-type operator in global optimization have been proposed also in
[
6
,
3
,
10
]
.

The detailed expressions for
\mathcal{L}(f)
and
Q(f,f)
will be developed in the following sections.

In particular, Section
2
introduces the novel method, by first considering the algorithm at the particle-level. We introduce the Metropolis search operator and particle interaction dynamics. Next, we switch to the kinetic interpretation of the algorithm. A formal analysis is then carried on in Section
3
, where we derive evolution equations for the marginals of the probability density function, investigate the mean-field limit, and analyze the system’s asymptotic behavior. Finally, in Section
4
and
5
, we give some implementation details and choices in order to present a series of numerical experiments that validate the proposed method and compare its performance with classical Simulated Annealing on a set of benchmark problems. We conclude with a section outlining key findings and directions for future research.

2
Collective Annealing by Switching Temperatures

In this section, we describe the proposed algorithm. We start with the particle level description and then give the corresponding kinetic formulation of the collective behaviour.

2.1
The stochastic particle based algorithm

Let us consider the general problem of computing the global minimum

x^{*}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}(x),

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{F}(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
d\geq 1
is any continuous function. We assume the global minimum
x^{*}
to exist and be unique.

We start by presenting the proposed algorithm on the particle level. We initialize CAST as follows:

1.

A set of
N
particles
X_{1}^{0},\ldots,X_{N}^{0}
is randomly placed on the search space.

2.

Each particle has its own
nonnegative
temperature
T_{1}^{0},\ldots,T_{N}^{0}
.

Then, in each time step
n
, CAST performs two actions:

•

Exploration
. This is essentially fixed-temperature multi-particle Simulated Annealing and will be discussed in section
2.1.1
. This differs from classical SA, where a single particle is cooled by a fixed schedule.

•

Particle interaction
. The particles are allowed to exchange temperatures, allowing for a temperature decay. This dynamic will be discussed in section
2.1.2
. This differs from classical PT, where particles fully swap temperatures.

2.1.1
Exploring the search space

The exploration phase of the algorithm goes as follows:

1.

Generate a candidate move as

\tilde{X_{i}}^{n+1}=X_{i}^{n}+\eta(T_{i}^{n})\xi_{i}^{n},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(2.2)

where
\xi_{i}^{n}\sim p(\xi,T^{n}_{i})
,
\eta(T_{i}^{n})
is a function of the temperature and
p(\xi,T^{n}_{i})
is some probability density (possibly) depending on the temperature. The only assumption we make on the probability density is that it must be symmetric and centered in zero.

2.

Check whether the candidate is in a better position than before. If so, the move is accepted. If not, it can still be accepted in accordance to a temperature dependent Boltzmann–Gibbs probability. The move is rejected otherwise.

\begin{cases}\text{Accepted}&\text{if }\mathcal{F}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{n+1})<\mathcal{F}(X_{i}^{n}),\\
\text{Accepted with prob. }\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\mathcal{F}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{n+1})-\mathcal{F}(X_{i}^{n})}{T_{i}^{n}}\Bigg)&\text{if }\mathcal{F}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{F}(X_{i}^{n}),\\
\text{Rejected otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.3)

Remark 2.1

(Step noise)
.

Many options exist in the choice of
\eta(T)
and
p(\xi,T)
. A classical choice is
p(\xi,T)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\eta(T)=\sqrt{2T}
so that the temperature is related to the variance of the random variable
\eta(T)\xi
. The finite mean and variance allows for a passage to Langevin dynamics characterized by a Fokker-Planck equation
[
38
,
15
]
.

Another option, as shown in
[
45
,
48
]
, is Cauchy noise, i.e.,
p(\xi,T)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{T}{T^{2}+\xi^{2}}
with
\eta(T)=1
. Although Cauchy noise has an undefined mean and variance, meaning we cannot perform a mean-field scaling as shown later, the fat Lorentzian tails enable larger jumps which permit to outperform the classical SA algorithm. It is important to underline that our kinetic formulation remains valid also under this choice of noise. For this reason, we will mostly focus on Cauchy noise in our numerical examples against test functions in Section
5
. We mention here that the positive effects of larger jumps has been observed also in other recently developed collective particle search algorithms
[
29
,
9
]
.

Remark 2.2

(Classic Simulated Annealing)
.

What we have set forth until now is essentially multi-particle SA without cooling. Our temperature dynamic will be introduced in the sequel. But to give some context, in classic multi-particle SA all particles have the same temperature. This means that the temperature of each particle is reduced at the end of each move in the search space, whether it was accepted or not. The temperature is reduced according to some predefined rule of the form

T^{n}_{i}=\\
\ \theta^{n}T^{0}_{i},\quad\theta\in(0,1),\quad\forall i,

(2.4)

where the initial temperatures
T^{0}_{i}
are strictly positive. A classical choice is

\theta^{n}=\frac{1}{\ln(n+e)},

(2.5)

where the algorithm is proved to converge
[
23
,
38
]
. However, in more practical applications, a geometric reduction where

\theta^{n}=\alpha^{n}

(2.6)

with
\alpha\in(0,1)
has shown good potential.

Rather than having a fixed decay, we will now show a dynamic process that reduces the mean temperature by binary interactions.

2.1.2
Non-local binary interactions and temperature exchanges

Our particle interaction dynamic is a generalization of classical parallel tempering
[
20
]
. This is achieved by introducing an alignment process and adding stochasticity.

Grouping the particles pairwise, and for all
N/2
distinct pairs of particles
(i,j)
without replacement, we used the following notations:

1.

Call
x\coloneqq X^{n}_{i}
and
x_{*}\coloneqq X^{n}_{j}

2.

Call
T\coloneqq T^{n}_{i}
and
T_{*}\coloneqq T^{n}_{j}

3.

Apply the interaction described below and set
T^{n+1}_{i}\eqqcolon T^{\prime}
and
T^{n+1}_{j}\eqqcolon T^{\prime}_{*}

The non-local temperature interaction between a pair of particles
(x,T)
and
(x_{*},T_{*})
yielding
(x,T^{\prime})
and
(x_{*},T^{\prime}_{*})
is described as

\begin{split}T^{\prime}&=T-\lambda(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-\mu(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)+\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\xi\\
T_{*}^{\prime}&=T_{*}-\lambda(T_{*}-T)\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)-\mu(T_{*}-T)\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})+\sigma(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)\xi_{*},\end{split}

(2.7)

where
\lambda,\mu\in[0,1]
,
\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})
defines the intensity of the noise characterized by the random variables
\xi
,
\xi_{*}
assumed symmetric with mean zero and where

\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=\Psi\big(\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\big)\Psi(T_{*}<T),

is the interaction-indicator function with
\Psi(\cdot)
the classic indicator function.
Essentially, the interaction-indicator will drive the dynamic to promote exploration for poorly performing particles and exploitation for well-performing ones. We illustrate with an example.

Example 2.1
.

We consider two particles, each with their respective temperatures, i.e.,
(x,T)
and
(x_{*},T_{*})
. Due to symmetry, it suffices to only look at terms with
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})
. For now, we ignore the effect of noise. For interaction to occur, the interaction-indicator gives two conditions we consider to be satisfied, namely
\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*})
and
T_{*}<T
. We get

\displaystyle T^{\prime}

\displaystyle=T-\lambda(T-T_{*})\ =T-\mu T+\lambda T_{*}-(\lambda-\mu)T

\displaystyle T_{*}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{T_{*}+\mu(T-T_{*})}_{VP1}=\underbrace{T_{*}+\mu T-\lambda T_{*}+(\lambda-\mu)T_{*}}_{VP2}.

We discuss two viewpoints:

VP1
.

The warming/cooling viewpoint.
The well-performing particle cools down by a factor
\lambda
of the difference of temperatures. The poorly performing particle warms up by a factor
\mu
of the difference of temperatures.

VP2.

The exchange viewpoint.
The particles exchange parts of their temperatures to each other and gain or lose some from the “environment”. Figure
1
illustrates this viewpoint.

(x_{*},T_{*})
\mathcal{F}(x)

A nice consequence of either viewpoint is the observation that if
\lambda=\mu
, then mean temperatures are conserved. In the warming/cooling viewpoint, this is a consequence of warming and cooling by precisely the same amount. In the exchange viewpoint, this is a consequence of having no gain or loss from the “environment”.

The following lemma can be easily derived.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in[0,1]
and let
\xi
and
\xi_{*}
be bounded random variables with values in
[-a,a]
for some
a>0
. Assume that for all
(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the interaction kernel
\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})
satisfies the bound

\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\leq\frac{1}{a}\min\left\{T(1-\lambda)+\lambda T_{*},\;T(1-\mu)+\mu T_{*}\right\}.

(2.8)

Then, the post-interaction temperatures (
2.7
) remain non-negative for all admissible values of
\xi
,
\xi_{*}
,
T
, and
T_{*}
.

Moreover, as we will show in Section
3.1.2
, the condition
\mu<\lambda
also ensures that the expected mean temperature decreases over time.

2.2
A kinetic description

In this subsection, we move to the description of the algorithm for large numbers of samples. This can be achieved by leveraging tools from classical kinetic theory
[
39
]
and considering the particle density
f=f(x,T,t)
of samples in position
x
with temperature
T
at time
t>0
. We start from the evolution equation (
1.1
). First, we derive the form of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}(f)
. Second, we derive the form of the Boltzmann-type interaction operator
Q(f,f)
. Finally, we combine the two operators into a complete evolution equation.

2.2.1
Exploring the search space

We define the action of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}(f)
by ignoring any kind of interaction, i.e.,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}(f).

(2.9)

We begin by generalizing the trial position given by eq. (
2.2
) which now corresponds to

x^{\prime}=x+\eta(T)\xi,\quad\xi\sim p(\xi,T),

(2.10)

where
T\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the temperature,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the proposal probability density
p(\xi,T)
is assumed symmetric in
\xi
.

Next, the acceptance-rejection dynamic of eq. (
2.3
) can be summarized in kernel form. First, we introduce the Boltzmann-Gibbs probability

f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)=C\exp\left(\frac{-\mathcal{F}(x)}{T}\right)\text{ with }C=\left(\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\frac{-\mathcal{F}(x)}{T}\right)d\Omega\right)^{-1}

(2.11)

where we have used the handy notation

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\qquad d\Omega=dxdT.

The kernel now reads

\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})=\min\Bigg\{1,\frac{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime},T)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\Bigg\}.

(2.12)

Finally, to write down the evolution equation of the form (
2.9
), we split the operator into a gain part
\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)
and a loss part
\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)
, i.e.,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)-\mathcal{L}(f)^{-}.

(2.13)

The gain and loss parts model the in- and out-flow of particles respectively. To “count” the gain and loss particles, consider a volume
(\Omega,\Omega+d\Omega)
. The number of particles undergoing a move, thus leaving the volume, is given by

\mathcal{L}^{-}(f)d\Omega=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)d\Omega\right],

(2.14)

where we have used the notation

\mathbb{E}\left[g\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\xi)p(\xi,T)d\xi

(2.15)

to denote the expectation under the “movement” distribution
p(\xi,T)
. Inversely, the number of particles entering the domain is given by

\mathcal{L}^{+}(f)d\Omega=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x)f(x^{\prime},T,t)dx^{\prime}dT\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x)f(x^{\prime},T,t)d\Omega\right].

(2.16)

Substituting eqs. (
2.16
) and (
2.14
) into (
2.13
) yields the desired form

\frac{\partial f(x,T,t)}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x)f(x^{\prime},T,t)-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)\right]\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(f).

(2.17)

Remark 2.3

(Markov process viewpoint)
.

Since the exploration part of the algorithm is closely related to a Metropolis–Hastings-type algorithm, it is insightful to consider the Markov process viewpoint. Consider our particles to be states in such a process. Using classic notation, we wish to find the transition probability
P(x^{\prime}|x)
since it uniquely defines the process, i.e.,

P(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})=P(x^{\prime}|x)P(x),

where
P(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})
is the distribution of states that have undergone transition and
P(x)
is the distribution of states before transition. We know that

P(x^{\prime}|x)=A(x^{\prime},x)g(x^{\prime}|x)

where
A(x^{\prime},x)
is the acceptance distribution and
g(x^{\prime}|x)
is the proposal distribution. The acceptance distribution gives the probability to accept the proposed state
x^{\prime}
. The proposal distribution is the conditional probability of generating state
x^{\prime}
from state
x
. In our setting, the acceptance distribution is given by the kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})
. It gives the probability to accept the proposed state. The proposal distribution is given by
p(\xi,T)
since it generates the state
x^{\prime}
from
x
. The distribution of states
x
is given by
f(x,T,t)
. We now write

f(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T,t,\xi)=\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)p(\xi,T).

To get rid of the dependance of
\xi
, we integrate over the random variable
\xi
. Finally, we get

f(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)p(\xi,T)d\xi.

By simply multiplying both sides by
d\Omega
, we recover exactly eq. (
2.14
). By a similar computation, we can also recover eq. (
2.16
).

Lemma 2.2
.

For any symmetric probability density
p(\xi,T)
and any integrable function
g(x,x^{\prime})
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x^{\prime},x)dx\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x,x^{\prime})dx\right].

(2.18)

Proof.

[
38
, Lemma 3.1]

∎

Using this result, the evolution equation in eq. (
2.17
) is more conveniently written in weak form,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle f(x,T,t)\varphi(x,T)d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}(f)\varphi(x,T)d\Omega

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T)\big(\varphi(x^{\prime},T)-\varphi(x,T)\big)f(x,T,t)d\Omega\right]

(2.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}\big(\varphi(x^{\prime},T)-\varphi(x,T)\big)\big(\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T)f(x,T,t)-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x,T)f(x^{\prime},T,t)\big)d\Omega\right],

where we used Lemma
2.2
in the final step.

2.2.2
Non-local binary interactions and temperature exchanges

We define the action of the Boltzmann-type interaction operator
Q(f,f)
by ignoring any kind of search space dynamic, i.e.,

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=Q(f,f).

(2.20)

Since we are dealing with a binary interaction, the evolution equation will be nonlinear and characterized by a Boltzmann-like structure. Specifically, the resulting operator has the structure of a Boltzmann-Povzner interaction integral
[
41
]
. Similar to what we did in the prequel, we can also derive the evolution equation by splitting the operator in a gain and loss part,

\frac{\partial f(x,T,t)}{\partial t}=Q^{+}(f,f)-Q^{-}(f,f).

(2.21)

We consider the particles in a volume
(\Omega,\Omega+d\Omega)
leaving and entering. By assuming that particles interact with the same probability, the interaction kernel is unitary and the Boltzmann operator is reminiscent of the classical one for Maxwellian particles
[
39
]
. The number of particles undergoing an interaction of the form (
2.7
), thus leaving the volume is given by

\begin{split}Q^{-}(f,f)d\Omega&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}f(x,T,t)f(x_{*},T_{*},t)d\Omega_{*}\right]d\Omega\\
&=f(x,T,t)\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}f(x_{*},T_{*},t)d\Omega_{*}}_{=1}d\Omega\\
&=f(x,T,t)d\Omega.\end{split}

(2.22)

Similarly, the particles entering the domain as a consequence of an interaction between
(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T)
and
(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*})
that produces
(x,T)
and
(x_{*},T_{*})
is given by

\begin{split}Q^{+}(f,f)d\Omega&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}f(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,t)f(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*},t)dxd\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}Tdx_{*}d\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}f(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,t)f(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*},t)\frac{1}{J}d\Omega_{*}\right]d\Omega,\end{split}

(2.23)

where
J=\Big|\frac{\partial(T,T_{*})}{\partial(\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*})}\Big|
is the Jacobian determinant of the temperature interaction.

Substituting eqs (
2.22
) and (
2.23
) into (
2.21
) yields the desired form

\frac{\partial f(x,T,t)}{\partial t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}f(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,t)f(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*},t)\frac{1}{J}d\Omega_{*}\right]-f(x,T,t)\coloneqq Q(f,f).

(2.24)

Note that the above equation has the structure of a Boltzmann-Povzner process in collisional kinetic theory where interactions are non-local.
For much of the remaining analysis, we move over to the weak form of eq. (
2.24
). We can write the weak form as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\Omega}f(x,T,t)&\varphi(x,T)d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}Q(f,f)\varphi(x,T)d\Omega\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\big(\varphi(x,T^{\prime})+\varphi(x_{*},T_{*}^{\prime})-\varphi(x,T)-\varphi(x_{*},T_{*})\big)ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}\right]\end{split}

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\big(\varphi(x,T^{\prime})-\varphi(x,T)\big)ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}\right].

(2.26)

The passage to eq. (
2.26
) is only possible due to the symmetry of the interaction. Swapping
T
and
T_{*}
and
x
and
x_{*}
proves this statement.

2.2.3
Combining the search dynamic with the temperature exchange

The kinetic description of the combined processes is simply the sum of both dynamics. We write it in the following way

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}(f)=Q(f,f)

(2.27)

to make a parallel to classic kinetic theory where we expect a similar form. We notice that our evolution equation is actually a Boltzmann–Povzner equation due to the form of
Q(f,f)
.

Remark 2.4

(Historical detour)
.

Povzner analyzed this particular form of equation in the 60s as a regularization of the Boltzmann equation by averaging collisions in space
[
41
,
4
]
. Back then, there was no proof of existence and uniqueness of the classic Boltzmann equation so Povzner simplified things by “smearing” the process of interactions over the spatial domain. This has the consequence that interactions are no longer confined to single points but can occur anywhere, unlike in classic kinetic theory where interactions are inherently local due to the physical interpretation of molecules colliding at specific points in the domain. His results were long ignored by physicists because it held no physical relevance. We now know that his equation is useful when treating stochastic systems of particles that “communicate” or interact in a different way than by colliding. A general example would be the swarming of animals or the flocking of birds
[
39
,
13
,
2
]
. Our operator also “smears” local interactions over the temperature domain allowing non-local interactions.

3
Analysis and mean-field limit

In this section, we perform some analysis in order to better understand the combined dynamic. We start with the long-time behaviour of the algorithm, prove the decay of first and second moments and then discuss the mean-field limit.

3.1
Long-time behaviour

In this subsection, we analyse long-time convergence using an entropy-based argument. An important aspect of the algorithm is temperature decay. We will show that our interaction dynamic indeed drives the average temperature down. Under stronger assumptions, we can also prove the decay of variance in the temperatures.

3.1.1
Entropies and convergence to equilibrium

Suppose we can prove that
f
converges toward the Boltzmann–Gibbs probability in eq. (
2.11
) then due to the Laplace principle
[
18
]
, we know

\lim_{T\rightarrow 0}-T\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)e^{\frac{-\mathcal{F}(x)}{T}}dx\right)=\inf_{x\in\text{supp}(g)}\mathcal{F}(x),

(3.1)

where
g(x)
is any continuous probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By cooling the particles, we converge toward the global minimum.

In order to study the convergence to equilibrium of the search space exploring dynamic, we must prove the decay of the entropy functional,

H(f|f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}):=\int_{\Omega}f(x,T,t)\log\left(\frac{f(x,T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\right)d\Omega.

(3.2)

If this so called
Shannon–Boltzmann entropy
converges to zero, we can formally prove

f\rightarrow f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}=C\exp\left(\frac{-\mathcal{F}(x)}{T}\right).

Some examples of using this method are found in
[
7
,
14
,
36
]
. Furthermore, this convergence enables us to use the Laplace principle in eq. (
3.1
) to prove convergence to the global minimum. Setting

\varphi(x,T)=\log\bigg(\frac{f(x,T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\bigg)

in eq. (
2.19
) and skipping over the details found in
[
38
, Section 3.2]
, we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}(f)\log&\bigg(\frac{f(x,T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\bigg)d\Omega\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T)f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)h\left(\frac{f(x^{\prime},T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime},T)},\frac{f(x,T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\right)d\Omega\right]\end{split}

(3.3)

where we defined

h(x,y)=(x-y)\big(\log(x)-\log(y)\big)\geq 0.

We have the following result (see
[
38
, Theorem 3.1]
for more details).

Theorem 3.1
.

\frac{d}{dt}H(f|f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}})=-I_{\mathcal{F}}[f]\leq 0

(3.4)

where

I_{\mathcal{F}}[f]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime},T)f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)h\bigg(\frac{f(x^{\prime},T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime},T)},\frac{f(x,T,t)}{f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,T)}\bigg)d\Omega\right]

Assuming

I_{\mathcal{F}}[f]\geq\omega H(f|f_{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty})

(3.5)

or equivalently

\frac{d}{dt}H(f|f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}})\leq-\omega H(f|f_{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty})

(3.6)

with
\omega>0
we get for an initial datum
f(x,0)=f_{0}
,

H(f|f_{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty})\leq H(f_{0}|f_{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty})e^{-\omega t}.

(3.7)

By the Csiszár-Kullback inequality
[
16
,
32
]
, this implies that the entropy decay indeed implies the convergence in
L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
f
to
f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}}
for large time.

Remark 3.1

(Time dependent cooling)
.

As mentioned in a previous remark, classical simulated annealing employs a continuous time dependent cooling scheme. This means the Shannon–Boltzmann entropy has some additional term that must be bounded. We refer to
[
38
]
for the full details but the main idea is that eq. (
3.6
) now reads

\frac{d}{dt}H(f|f^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}})\leq-\omega H(f|f_{\mathcal{F}}^{\infty})-\frac{T^{\prime}(t)}{T^{2}(t)}||\mathcal{F}||_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

In order for the inequality to hold, we must ensure that
\frac{T^{\prime}(t)}{T^{2}(t)}
decays with time, i.e.,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{T^{\prime}(t)}{T^{2}(t)}=0.

If, for example, we choose
T(t)=\frac{1}{t}
then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{T^{\prime}(t)}{T^{2}(t)}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)^{2}}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{-\frac{1}{t^{2}}}{\frac{1}{t^{2}}}=-1\neq 0,

(3.8)

violating the aforementioned condition to prove entropy decay. If instead we use eq. (
2.5
) in continuous (and slightly different) form then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{T^{\prime}(t)}{T^{2}(t)}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\log(t)}\right)^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{1}{\log(t)}\right)^{2}}=\frac{-\frac{1}{t\log(t)^{2}}}{\frac{1}{\log(t)^{2}}}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{1}{t}=0.

(3.9)

Thus, we can prove that for such a cooling scheme, convergence to the global minimum is guaranteed (in infinite time), thanks to the Laplace principle.

3.1.2
Evolution of moments in temperature

The goal of the temperature interaction is for all temperatures to eventually drop to zero. In other words, we want the evolution of the first and second moments to decay in time. In fact, the simultaneous decay
of the mean and variance implies convergence in
L^{2}
(and therefore in distribution)
to a Dirac delta at zero.

Before starting let us prove the following lemma that will be useful in the sequel.

Lemma 3.1

(Symmetric split of pairwise orderings)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be integrable and let
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable.
Then

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi\!\big(\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\big)\,gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}=\frac{1}{2}\iint gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}-\frac{1}{2}\iint\Psi\!\big(\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\big)\,gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}.

(3.10)

In particular,

0\;\leq\;\iint\Psi\!\big(\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\big)\,gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\iint gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*},

(3.11)

with equality on the right if and only if
\iint\Psi(\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(x_{*}))\,gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}=0
.

Proof.

Partition
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
into the disjoint sets

A:=\{\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\},\qquad B:=\{\mathcal{F}(x)>\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\},\qquad E:=\{\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}(x_{*})\}.

The change of variables
(x,x_{*})\mapsto(x_{*},x)
maps
A\leftrightarrow B
and leaves
E
invariant, while the integrand

g(x)g(x_{*})
and the measure
dx\,dx_{*}
are symmetric under this swap. Hence

\iint_{A}gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}=\iint_{B}gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}.

Since

\iint gg_{*}\,dx\,dx_{*}=\iint_{A}gg_{*}+\iint_{B}gg_{*}+\iint_{E}gg_{*},

we obtain

2\iint_{A}gg_{*}=\iint gg_{*}-\iint_{E}gg_{*},

which is exactly (
3.10
). The bounds (
3.11
) follow because the tie contribution

\iint_{E}gg_{*}\geq 0
. If the tie set
E
satisfies

\iint_{E}g(x)g(x_{*})\,dx\,dx_{*}=0

(for example, if
\mathcal{F}(x)
is continuously distributed under
g
) then the equality sign holds.
∎

We can analyse the behaviour of temperature mean and variance by looking at the sign of eq. (
2.26
) when

\varphi(x,T)=T\text{ or }\varphi(x,T)=T^{2}.

(3.12)

The sign of the moment equations is governed by

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(x,T^{\prime})-\varphi(x,T)\right]

inside the double integral since
f(x,T,t)f(x_{*},T_{*},t)
is always positive. The following two lemmas discuss the behavior of these moment equations.

Lemma 3.2

(Evolution of the first moment)
.

The evolution of
M_{1}:=\int_{\Omega}fTd\Omega
for
\mu<\lambda
satisfies

\frac{dM_{1}}{dt}=(\mu-\lambda)\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*}){ff_{*}}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}\leq 0.

Proof.

We write

\mathbb{E}\left[T^{\prime}-T\right]=-\lambda(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-\mu(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)

since
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]=0
. Substituting inside the weak form yields

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{1}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\left[-\lambda(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-\mu(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)\right]ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}

\displaystyle=(\mu-\lambda)\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*}){ff_{*}}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}.

Thus, we get the additional condition
\mu<\lambda

such that the time derivative of the first moment is negative. Furthermore, from Lemma
3.1
, if the contribution of the set
E

is negligible with respect to the product measure with density
ff_{*}
, we also have

\frac{dM_{1}}{dt}=\frac{1}{2}(\mu-\lambda)\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(T-T_{*})\Psi(T>T_{*}){ff_{*}}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}=\frac{1}{4}(\mu-\lambda)\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}|T-T_{*}|{ff_{*}}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

This result is consistent with Remark
2.1
where we claimed that for
\lambda=\mu
the mean temperatures would be conserved. Indeed, from the proof of Lemma
3.2
we get

\frac{dM_{1}}{dt}=0,

if
\mu=\lambda
. For
\mu<\lambda
, we see that the particles lose temperature over time at a rate loosely proportional to
\lambda-\mu
.

Lemma 3.3

(Evolution of the second moment)
.

The evolution of
M_{2}:=\int_{\Omega}fT^{2}d\Omega

satisfies

\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\left[(\mu-\lambda)(T^{2}-T_{*}^{2})+(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}-\lambda-\mu)(T-T_{*})^{2}\right]\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}.

for
\lambda,\mu\in[0,1]
,
\mu\leq\lambda
and
\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=0
.

Proof.

We start from the post–interaction temperature update

T^{\prime}=T-\lambda(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-\mu(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T),

and compute

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[T^{\prime 2}-T^{2}]=&-2\lambda T(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-2\mu T(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)\\
&+\lambda^{2}(T-T_{*})^{2}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})+\mu^{2}(T-T_{*})^{2}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T),\end{split}

where we used

\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T)=0,\qquad\chi^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*}).

Integrating against
ff_{*}
on
\Omega\times\Omega
yields

\begin{split}\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}&=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\left[-2\lambda T(T-T_{*})-2\mu T_{*}(T_{*}-T)\right]\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}\\
&+\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2})(T-T_{*})^{2}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}.\end{split}

Expanding the first integral and rearranging yields

\begin{split}\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}&=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(\mu-\lambda)(T^{2}-T_{*}^{2})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}\\
&+\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}-\lambda-\mu)(T-T_{*})^{2}\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}.\end{split}

The first integral is either negative or zero if
\mu\leq\lambda
. Since
\lambda(\lambda-1)+\mu(\mu-1)\leq 0
for
\lambda,\mu\in[0,1]
, the second integral is non-positive, and it is strictly negative if the support of

\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})
has positive measure and
\lambda,\mu\in(0,1)
.

If we further assume the contribution of the set
E
in Lemma
3.1

is negligible with respect to the product measure with density
ff_{*}

we get the decay

\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}=\frac{1}{4}(\mu-\lambda)\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}|T^{2}-T_{*}^{2}|ff_{*}d\Omega d\Omega_{*}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}-\lambda-\mu\big)(M_{2}-M_{1}^{2}).

∎

Remark 3.3

(Effect of noise)
.

If the stochastic term
\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\xi
is included, one obtains an additional positive contribution

+\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\sigma^{2}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\,\,ff_{*}\,d\Omega\,d\Omega_{*}.

The monotone decay of
M_{2}
is preserved provided that the noise intensity remains sufficiently small,

\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]\,\sup_{x,x_{*},T,T_{*}}\sigma^{2}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})<\left|\frac{dM_{2}}{dt}\right|.

This ensures that the dissipative term associated with temperature alignment dominates the stochastic contribution.

3.2
Mean-field

Due to the complexity of the dynamic, it is difficult to analyze the convergence behaviour of
f(x,T,t)
itself. We will rather use the marginals

\rho(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\quad\text{and}\quad F(T,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,T,t)dx

(3.13)

to prove convergence in the space averaged case and a degradation to a Dirac delta in the temperature averaged case.

3.2.1
Temperature averaged case

For the temperature averaged case, we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\mathcal{L}(f)dT=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}Q(f,f)dT.

(3.14)

Expanding the last term yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}Q(f,f)dT&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\Omega}f(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,t)f(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*},t)\frac{1}{J}d\Omega_{*}dT-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T,t)f(x_{*},\reflectbox{${}^{\prime}$}T_{*},t)\frac{1}{J}dTdT_{*}dx_{*}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)f(x_{*},T_{*},t)\frac{1}{J}dT^{\prime}dT_{*}^{\prime}dx_{*}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)f(x_{*},T_{*},t)dTdT_{*}dx_{*}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\\
&=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}f(x_{*},T_{*},t)d\Omega_{*}}_{=1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(x,T,t)dT\\
&=0\end{split}

(3.15)

giving us

\frac{\partial\rho(x,t)}{\partial t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\mathcal{L}(f)dT=0.

(3.16)

In general, the above equation does not admit a closed form in terms of the marginal density
\rho(x,t)
. Even in the mean-field scaling where the operator
\mathcal{L}(f)
reduces to a Langevin dynamics (see
[
38
]
for more details), we will get

\frac{\partial\rho(x,t)}{\partial t}=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho(x,t)\nabla_{x}\mathcal{F}(x)\right)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}T\Delta_{xx}f(x,T,t)\,dT.

We now apply integration by parts and define the average temperature at point
x
as

\bar{T}(x,t):=\frac{1}{\rho(x,t)}\int Tf(x,T,t)\,dT=\frac{M_{1}(x,t)}{\rho(x,t)}.

(3.17)

This yields the nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}\rho(x,t)=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho(x,t)\nabla_{x}\mathcal{F}(x)+\bar{T}(x,t)\nabla_{x}\rho(x,t)\right).

(3.18)

This shows that the dynamic in the mean-field limit is driven by a Langevin-type exploration with noise proportional to the average temperature of the system which slowly cools down to zero accordingly to a rate proportional to
\lambda-\mu
.

3.2.2
Space averaged case

For the space averaged case, we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,T,t)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(f)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Q(f,f)dx.

(3.19)

Expanding the second term yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(f)dx&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x)f(x^{\prime},T,t)-\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)\right]dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x^{\prime}\rightarrow x)f(x^{\prime},T,t)\right]dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)\right]dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)\right]dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(x\rightarrow x^{\prime})f(x,T,t)\right]dx\\
&=0\end{split}

(3.20)

by Lemma
2.2
. Thus, we get

\frac{\partial F(T,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Q(f,f)dx.

(3.21)

Unfortunately, it is impossible to write the right-hand side as
Q(F,F)
because of the implicit dependence of
x
and
x_{*}
in
T^{\prime}
and
T_{*}^{\prime}
. The weak form is therefore similar to eq. (
2.26
) (unlike before, we do not use the
\Omega
notation to underline the integration over the spatial domain), i.e.,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}F(T,t)\varphi(T)dT=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\varphi(T^{\prime})-\varphi(T)\big)ff_{*}dxdx_{*}\right]dTdT_{*}\right].

(3.22)

3.2.3
Mean-field scaling of the temperature exchange

We consider the following scaling

t\rightarrow t/\varepsilon,\quad\lambda\rightarrow\varepsilon\lambda,\quad\mu\rightarrow\varepsilon\mu,\quad\sigma\rightarrow\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sigma.

Therefore we have

T^{\prime}-T=\varepsilon(\lambda(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})-\mu(T-T_{*})\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{*},x,T_{*},T))+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})\xi.

For
\varepsilon\ll 1
, we can write the Taylor expansion

\varphi(T^{\prime})=\varphi(T)+(T^{\prime}-T)\frac{d\varphi(T)}{dT}+\frac{1}{2}(T^{\prime}-T)^{2}\frac{d^{2}\varphi(T)}{dT^{2}}+O\left(\varepsilon^{3/2}\right).

(3.23)

Substituting into eq. (
3.22
) and splitting the integral yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}F(T,t)\varphi(T)dT=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[T^{\prime}-T\right]\frac{d\varphi(T)}{dT}ff_{*}dxdx_{*}dTdT_{*}}_{:=I_{1}}

(3.24)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[(T^{\prime}-T)^{2}\right]\frac{d^{2}\varphi(T)}{dT^{2}}ff_{*}dxdx_{*}dTdT_{*}}_{:=I_{2}}.

(3.25)

Expanding
I_{1}
under the scaling (using the same change of variables as in Lemma
3.2
) yields

\displaystyle I_{1}=(\mu-\lambda)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}(T-T_{*})\Psi(T_{*}<T)\frac{d\varphi(T)}{dT}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi(\mathcal{F}(x)<\mathcal{F}(x_{*}))f(x,T,t)f_{*}(x_{*},T_{*},t)dxdx_{*}dTdT_{*}

Expanding
I_{2}
under the scaling yields

\displaystyle I_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})^{2}\frac{d^{2}\varphi(T)}{dT^{2}}f(x,T,t)f(x_{*},T_{*},t)dxdx_{*}dTdT_{*}.

Finally, as
\varepsilon\to 0
we formally retrieve the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial F(T,t)}{\partial t}=(\lambda-\mu)\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\big(B[f](T,t)F(T,t)\big)+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\xi^{2}\right]}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial T^{2}}\big(D[f](T,t)F(T,t)\big),

(3.26)

with

\begin{split}B[f](T,t)&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{T>T_{*}>0}(T-T_{*})F(T_{*},t)\,dT_{*},\\
D[f](T,t)&=\frac{1}{F}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})^{2}ff_{*}dxdx_{*}\,dT_{*},\end{split}

where we used Lemma
3.1
and assume the set
E
has zero measure with respect to the product density
ff_{*}
.
Equation (
3.26
) represents a concentration of the temperature according to
B[f](T,t)
with a diffusion process characterized by
D[f](T,t)
.

In the case of random multiplicative noise with
\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=T
we can write

D[f](T,t)=\frac{1}{F}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}T^{2}ff_{*}dxdx_{*}\,dT_{*}=T^{2},

since our density is normalized to total mass one.

4
Implementation aspects

In this section, we give implementation details, generalizations and parameter choices used in the experiments to follow.

4.1
Implementation

The numerical implementation of our algorithm relies on the standard Metropolis algorithm combined with a direct simulation Monte Carlo method for the Boltzmann interactions.
[
5
,
11
]
We start with some notation:

•

We define the
j^{th}
dimension of particle
i
at time step
n
as
X^{n,i}_{j}
. The matrix of
N
particles with dimension
d
at time step
n
is boldfaced as
\mathbf{X}^{n}
.

•

We define the best particle position
\widehat{X}^{n}
(with corresponding temperature
\widehat{T}^{n}
) at time step
n
as

\widehat{X}^{n}=\argmin\mathcal{F}\left(X^{n,i}\right).

(4.1)

•

We define the average particle position
\widetilde{X}^{n}
at time step
n
as

Adm=\left\{X^{n,i}:\ |X^{n,i}|\leq 1,\ n=1\cdots N\right\},\quad\widetilde{X}^{n}=\frac{1}{|Adm|}\sum_{X\in Adm}X

(4.2)

Where
Adm
is the set of admissible particle positions to take into account for the average, i.e., only taking into account particles that remain in the initial domain. The reason for doing this is that empirical evidence suggests that particles outside of the domain (sometimes) have the tendency to go very far. This results in skewing the average, we therefore leave these particles out.

•

The error is measured using the mean squared error and is computed as

\text{MSE}(Y)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(Y_{j}-x^{*}_{j}\right)^{2},

(4.3)

where
Y
is some vector
\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x^{*}
is the global minimum. The subscript denotes the
j^{th}
dimension.

•

In order to avoid statistical bias, we will run the optimization algorithms over
N
runs and compute the expectation of some quantity of interest
Z
. We use a simple Monte Carlo expectation

\mathbb{E}[Z]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z^{n},

(4.4)

where
Z^{n}
denotes the quantity of interest for run
n
.

The algorithm now reads,

Algorithm 1

Collective Annealing by Switching Temperatures

1:

Vectors
\mathbf{X^{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

4.2
Noise term choice

In the numerical tests, we will take the noise term as

\sigma(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=T.

(4.5)

This is an intuitive choice since the added noise in the interaction is directly proportional to the current temperature. According to eq. (
2.8
), the noise must be bounded to ensure positivity in the temperature. Since we decided on the form of
\sigma
, we bound the support
a
of the uniformly distributed random variables
\xi
and
\xi_{*}
. The inequality is a little too long to be useful so we look directly at eq. (
2.7
). We substitute into it eq. (
4.5
). Due to symmetry, it is enough to look at
\chi_{\mathcal{F}}(x,x_{*},T,T_{*})=1
. We get

\begin{split}T^{\prime}&=T-\lambda(T-T_{*})+T\xi=T(1-\lambda+\xi)+\lambda T_{*}\\
T_{*}^{\prime}&=T_{*}-\mu(T_{*}-T)+T_{*}\xi_{*}=T_{*}(1-\mu+\xi_{*})+\mu T\end{split}

(4.6)

which implies that

a\leq 1-\lambda\quad\text{and}\quad a\leq 1-\mu

must be satisfied simultaneously. Since
\mu<\lambda
, we get,

a\leq 1-\lambda.

(4.7)

Looking at eq. (
2.8
), this is indeed an equivalent requirement. The minimum implies that

T\leq\frac{1}{a}(T(1-\lambda)+\lambda T_{*})\quad\text{and}\quad T\leq\frac{1}{a}(T(1-\mu)+\mu T_{*})

must be satisfied simultaneously. Since
T(1-\lambda)\leq T(1-\lambda)+\lambda T_{*}
and
T(1-\lambda)\leq T(1-\mu)+\mu T_{*}
we conclude the equivalence.

It will later be useful to scale the noise down a bit. We therefore introduce the
noise scaling factor
\bm{\kappa}
such that the support now reads

a=\kappa(1-\lambda),

(4.8)

where
\kappa\leq 1
.

Finally, the exploration noise in eq. (
2.10
) must be sampled from a distribution we called
p(\xi,T)
. As mentioned in Remark
2.1
, we choose Cauchy noise.

4.3
Further generalization

In order to balance the search aspect of the algorithm, controlled by
\mathcal{L}(f)
, and the interaction aspect, controlled by
Q(f,f)
, we introduce
the interaction strength parameter
\bm{\gamma}
. The dynamic of eq. (
2.27
) now reads

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}(f)=\gamma Q(f,f).

(4.9)

By decreasing
\gamma
, we allow more exploration and fewer particle-particle interactions. This can be important in some cases to allow particles to “settle” a bit before performing a new temperature interaction. Conversely, increasing
\gamma
allows for more interactions, therefore reducing the temperature faster. In classic kinetic theory, this parameter is closely related to the (inverse) Knudsen number.

On the particle-level itself, i.e., in the Nanbu-Babovsky algorithm above, this is achieved by changing the
5^{th}
line to

N_{c}\leftarrow\text{Iround}(\gamma N/2).

In order to compare our algorithm with classical SA, we want comparable particle temperatures. As a reminder, our algorithm simulates
N
particles where the highest temperature is given by
T_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and the lowest is given by
T_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. All other
N-2
temperatures are taken uniformly between
T_{h}
and
T_{l}
. Thus, the average particle temperature is given by

\bar{T}=\frac{T_{h}+T_{l}}{2}.

Classic multi-particle SA also simulates
N
particles but all have the same temperature, we set this to
\widebar{T}
. This gives us the possibility to tweak
T_{h}
and
T_{l}
while still keeping the value for
\widebar{T}
fixed. In order to somewhat limit the amount of free parameters, we employ the following logic:

1.

Choose a
fixed
value for the SA temperature/our average temperature, i.e.,
\widebar{T}
.

2.

Set
T_{h}=2\widebar{T}
and
T_{l}=0
.

3.

Tweak our algorithm’s particle temperatures by varying
T_{var}
and setting the new highest and lowest temperatures as

T_{h}^{\prime}=2\widebar{T}-T_{var}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{l}^{\prime}=T_{var}.

(4.10)

with the restriction that
0<T_{var}<\widebar{T}
.

5.1
Test functions

The two test functions we will consider were selected because they highlight complementary optimization challenges:
the Ackley function features a moderately multimodal landscape with a smooth basin around the global minimum, useful to assess convergence efficiency and stability, whereas the Rastrigin function exhibits a highly multimodal and oscillatory surface with many regularly spaced local minima, posing a much more demanding test for global exploration and avoidance of local traps.
Together, they form a minimal yet representative benchmark set to evaluate both the exploration and exploitation capabilities of the proposed algorithm.

6
Concluding remarks

In this work, we have introduced and analyzed a novel framework for simulated annealing based on kinetic theory and collective temperature dynamics. Inspired by particle interactions in Boltzmann-type models and the principles of parallel tempering, our approach constructs a stochastic mechanism through which particles interact by exchanging temperatures according to a suitably designed binary interaction. This formulation leads to a linear kinetic model for simulated annealing of the type introduced in
[
38
]
coupled with a nonlinear Boltzmann–Povzner equation for the temperature exchanges, offering a flexible and analytically tractable model for optimization with collective cooling.

Our method enables a dynamic adaptation of the temperature distribution that is emergent rather than externally prescribed, and we have shown that this interaction-driven mechanism leads to asymptotic concentration on global minima of the target energy landscape under suitable assumptions. Numerical experiments illustrate the potential of the method in escaping metastable states and adapting the cooling process to the complexity of the energy landscape.

This perspective complements and extends other recent kinetic approaches to simulated annealing, such as those based on entropy-controlled cooling [34], and opens several promising directions for future research. In particular, the introduction of a vector-valued temperature could allow for anisotropic exploration of the state space, where different directions or features of the problem are cooled at different rates. This would be especially useful in high-dimensional or stiff problems, where energy landscapes vary strongly across directions.

Furthermore, the generality of the kinetic formulation suggests potential applications beyond classical optimization. For instance, collective thermostatting mechanisms could be fruitfully integrated into stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) or variational inference frameworks in machine learning, where adaptive control of exploration and convergence plays a central role. Finally, extending the model to allow for spatially structured particle systems or interacting agent networks may yield insights into distributed optimization and decentralized learning algorithms.
