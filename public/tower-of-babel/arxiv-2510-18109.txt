PrivaDE: Privacy-preserving Data Evaluation for Blockchain-based Data Marketplaces Conference: ; ;
Wan Ki Wong, Sahel Torkamani, Michele Ciampi, Rik Sarkar

Abstract
. Evaluating the relevance of data is a critical task for model builders seeking to acquire datasets that enhance model performance. Ideally, such evaluation should allow the model builder to assess the utility of candidate data without exposing proprietary details of the model. At the same time, data providers must be assured that no information about their data—beyond the computed utility score—is disclosed to the model builder. In this paper, we present PrivaDE, a cryptographic protocol for privacy-preserving utility scoring and selection of data for machine learning. While prior works (e.g., ( Qian et al. 2024 ; Song et al. 2021 ) ) have proposed data evaluation protocols, our approach advances the state of the art through a practical, blockchain-centric design. Leveraging the trustless nature of blockchains, PrivaDE enforces malicious-security guarantees and ensures strong privacy protection for both models and datasets. To achieve efficiency, we integrate several techniques—including model distillation, model splitting, and cut-and-choose zero-knowledge proofs—bringing the runtime to a practical level. Furthermore, we propose a unified utility scoring function that combines empirical loss, predictive entropy, and feature-space diversity, and that can be seamlessly integrated into active-learning workflows. Evaluation shows that PrivaDE performs data evaluation effectively, achieving online runtimes within 15 minutes even for models with millions of parameters. Our work lays the foundation for fair and automated data marketplaces in decentralized machine learning ecosystems.



1.
Introduction

As machine learning (ML) systems proliferate and generate substantial value, concerns have grown over large-scale, uncompensated data collection and the need to return value to original contributors. Collaborative learning frameworks
(
Sim et al. 2020
)
, in which multiple parties pool data via a decentralized ledger, offer one remedy: trainers publish data requests, contributors opt in, and compensation for contributions is transparently recorded and fairly allocated. Despite the rise of blockchain-based data and ML marketplaces (e.g.,
(
Rao [n. d.]
)
), rigorous mechanisms for data evaluation remain largely overlooked.

The central challenge of data evaluation in collaborative learning is to select high-quality samples that maximally improve model performance while preserving the confidentiality of both data and model assets. Active learning
(
Dasgupta 2011
)
addresses selection by querying the most informative examples—e.g., by uncertainty, expected loss, or diversity—thereby reducing annotation cost. Yet in a decentralized, privacy-sensitive marketplace, neither contributors nor model owners are willing to reveal raw features, labels, or model parameters prior to payment. It is therefore essential to design cryptographically secure protocols that compute data-utility scores under strong confidentiality guarantees and ensure correctness in a trustless environment.

In this paper, we study how to design a secure protocol that enables a model owner (MO) and a data owner (DO) to jointly compute a data-utility score
\phi(M,D)
for any model
M
and dataset
D
, with practical applicability to blockchain-based data marketplaces. This score can subsequently serve as the basis for negotiation or automated payment for data acquisition in such marketplaces.

Our Contributions.

To the best of our knowledge, prior work (e.g.,
(
Song et al. 2021
;
Qian et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2022
)
) either operates in the semi-honest model or relies on a trusted third party (TTP) for most protocol steps. PrivaDE targets deployment in blockchain-based data marketplaces under realistic assumptions: we work in the malicious-adversary setting, where either the MO or the DO may deviate arbitrarily, and we require only minimal TTP involvement limited to setup and preprocessing. Both the model
M
and the dataset
D
are treated as private assets and are protected throughout the protocol.

PrivaDE considers the realistic setting where the DO holds a large dataset
D
, reflecting common scenarios in which a contributor possesses many samples. Our protocol enables the MO to obtain a dataset-level score
\phi(M,D)
while minimizing passive information leakage by requiring only a
representative subset
for scoring. The scoring procedure is designed so that the subset-derived score is representative of the full dataset.

We introduce several design techniques (Section
4
) that bring PrivaDE, under these strict assumptions, to practical runtimes. In particular, we combine model distillation, model splitting, and cut-and-choose ZKP to balance efficiency against potential privacy leakage.

We further adapt PrivaDE to a blockchain-based data marketplace. Smart contracts deter misuse and enforce fairness in MPC by penalizing uncooperative parties via escrow slashing. The protocol can also be integrated with the data transaction to streamline the end-to-end marketplace workflow.

Finally, we prove security in the simulation-based paradigm and demonstrate practicality through extensive experiments (Section
7
) on real-world datasets and standard model architectures. Results show that
PrivaDE
can be flexibly tuned to achieve reasonable runtimes (as low as 45 seconds online for MNIST with LeNet), and that the scoring protocol is robust, effectively identifying high-performance datasets.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Machine learning

In machine learning, we compute a model
h
from a training set
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where each
x_{i}
has label
y_{i}
(sometimes
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
). An algorithm
A
searches a hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
for
h
minimizing empirical loss
L_{D}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\bigl(h(x_{i}),y_{i}\bigr)
,
where
\ell
quantifies the error between prediction
y^{\prime}=h(x)
and true
y
. Common choices include squared loss
\ell(y^{\prime},y)=(y^{\prime}-y)^{2}
for regression and zero-one loss
\ell(y^{\prime},y)=\mathbf{1}[y^{\prime}\neq y]
for classification. For probabilistic outputs
\vec{y}^{\prime}
, cross-entropy
\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(\vec{y}^{\prime},\vec{y})=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\vec{y}^{i}\ln\vec{y}^{\prime i}
is typical, where
\vec{y}
is a one-hot vector. We denote models by
M
and, when parameterized by
\theta
, by
M_{\theta}
.

2.2.
Cryptographic protocols

This section reviews commitment schemes, zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs), and multi-party computation (MPC). We use
\lambda
as the security parameter and assume familiarity with computational indistinguishability and negligible functions
\nu
.

2.2.1.
Commitment schemes

Informally, a commitment scheme is a tuple
\Gamma=(\mathsf{Setup},\allowbreak\mathsf{Commit},\mathsf{Open})
of
\mathsf{PPT}
algorithms where:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\rightarrow\mathsf{pp}
takes security parameter
\lambda
and generates public parameters
pp
;

•

\mathsf{Commit}(\mathsf{pp};m)\rightarrow(\mathtt{com},r)
takes a secret message
m
, outputs a public commitment
Com
and a randomness
r
used in the commitment.

•

\mathsf{Open}(\mathsf{pp},\mathtt{com};m,r)\rightarrow b\in\{0,1\}
verifies the opening of the commitment
C
to the message
m
with the randomness
r
.

We require two main security properties:
hiding
and
binding
. The hiding requires that no
\mathsf{PPT}
adversary, upon receiving the output of
\mathtt{com}
, can figure out what message is committed in
\mathtt{com}
. Binding requires that
\mathtt{com}
can be
opened
only to a specific message. We refer to App.
B.1
for the formal definition of commitment.

2.2.2.
Merkle Tree Commitments

A Merkle tree commitment uses a collision-resistant hash function
H
to commit to a vector
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
by hashing leaves and iteratively hashing pairs up a binary tree; the commitment is the root, and an opening is the
O(\log n)
-length authentication path for an index
i
.

A merkle tree commitment is a tuple of PPT algorithms
\Gamma=(\mathsf{mtSetup},\mathsf{mtCommit},\mathsf{mtOpen},\mathsf{mtVerify})
where:

•

\mathsf{mtSetup}(1^{\lambda})\to\mathsf{pp}
: generates public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
./

•

\mathsf{mtCommit}(\mathsf{pp};m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\to(\mathtt{com},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}st})
: compute leaf and internal hashes; output root
\mathtt{com}
and state
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}st}
.

•

\mathsf{mtOpen}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}st};i)\to\pi_{i}
: output the sibling-hash path authenticating position
i
.

•

\mathsf{mtVerify}(\mathsf{pp},\mathtt{com};i,m_{i},\pi_{i})\to b
: recompute the root from
(i,m_{i},\pi_{i})
and accept iff it equals
\mathtt{com}
.

The position-binding property (no two different
m
open at the same
i
) holds under collision resistance of
H
. We refer to App.
B.2
for the formal definition.

2.2.3.
Zero-knowledge proofs

A
non-interactive zero-knowledge proof-of-knowledge system (NIZKPoK)
for an NP-language
L
with the corresponding relation
\mathcal{R}_{L}
is represented by a tuple of algorithms
\Pi=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{V})
, where:

•

\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\rightarrow\mathtt{CRS}
takes as the input a security parameter
\lambda
. It outputs a common reference string
\mathtt{CRS}
.

•

\mathcal{P}(\mathtt{CRS},x,w)\rightarrow\pi
takes as the input
\mathtt{CRS}
, the statement
x
and the witness
w
, s.t.
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}_{L}
. It outputs the proof
\pi
.

•

\mathcal{V}(\mathtt{CRS},x,\pi)\rightarrow b\in\{0,1\}
takes as the input
\mathtt{CRS}
,
x
and
\pi
. It outputs 1 to accept and 0 to reject.

Informally, a NIZK prof is secure if it enjoys the properties of
completeness
,
zero-knowledge
and
binding
. Completeness simply requires that when the over and the verifier are honest the verifier always accepts the proof (i.e., it returns 1). Zero-knowledge requires that even when the verifier is malicious, the proof
\pi
generated from a prover will leak no information about the witness
w
. Soundness requires that the verifier always rejects when receiving proofs for false statements.
We refer to App.
B.3
for the formal definition.

2.2.4.
Multi-party computation

We prove that our results are secure under the simulation-based paradigm of secure
Multi-Party Computation (MPC)
. In secure multiparty computation (MPC) multiple parties aim to compute a function
f
together while keeping their inputs private. The goal is to ensure the result is correct while revealing no extra information.
We say that a protocol is secure if whatever can be inferred by an adversary attacking the protocol can also be inferred by an adversary attacking a so-called
ideal-world
. In this, there is an ideal trusted third party that receives the inputs of the parties evaluates the function
f
, and returns the output of the computation back to the parties

In short, a protocol is secure if a real-world adversary cannot gain an advantage over what they would learn in the ideal-world setting (that trivially captures the best possible security guarantee).
When defining the security of a protocol
\Pi
, we will say that
\Pi

securely realizes
the function
f
, if
\Pi
guarantees the above.
In this paper we focus on the two-party setting, considering both security against
semi-honest
and
malicious
adversaries. In the former, the corrupted parties that attack the real-world protocol are assumed to follow the protocol description (but they can try to infer information from the honest parties’ inputs). In the malicious case, instead, parties can deviate arbitrarily from what the protocol prescribes.
In App.
B.4
we provide a formal definition of secure MPC and its variants.

2.3.
Blockchain and Smart Contracts

A
blockchain
is a decentralized, append-only ledger that stores an ordered sequence of blocks; each block includes transactions, a timestamp, and the previous block’s hash, yielding immutability and tamper-evidence
(
Nakamoto 2008
;
Bonneau et al. 2015
)
. A
smart contract
is a program replicated and executed by blockchain nodes that enforces state transitions and agreements without intermediaries, enabling programmable, trustless transactions
(
Buterin 2014
;
Wood 2014
)
.

PrivaDE targets an EVM-compatible chain, which provides a secure, verifiable substrate for data exchange and computation. We use the chain to:

•

Commit inputs.
Data contributions and model parameters are posted as cryptographic commitments, creating a binding, immutable audit trail.

•

Enforce MPC fairness.
Contracts escrow deposits and impose penalties for deviations, discouraging aborts and enforcing compliance during MPC.

•

Enable atomic payment.
Transfers to data contributors trigger only on verifiable completion (e.g., successful checks/proofs), preventing unilateral defection.

•

Support extensibility.
The same infrastructure integrates with off-chain storage (e.g., IPFS) for availability and scalability.

3.
Problem Description and Threat Model

Consider a scenario in which
Alice
owns a model
M
trained on her dataset
D_{A}
and seeks additional data to further improve it.
Bob
holds a relevant dataset
D_{B}
that could enhance
M
when combined with
D_{A}
(i.e.,
D_{A}\cup D_{B}
). Bob seeks fair compensation for his contribution, while Alice aims to pay in proportion to the improvement attributable to
D_{B}
. We assume
D_{B}
is authentic and pertinent to Alice’s task; authenticity can be attested, for example, via signatures by the original data source or a trusted authority (Sec.
6
).

Our objective is to compute a score
\phi=\mathsf{Score}(M,D_{B})
that quantifies the improvement
D_{B}
affords to
M
, approximating the net gain in model performance (e.g., increased accuracy on a held-out test set). In pursuit of this goal, we address two coupled questions:

(1)

What criterion should define the scoring function
\mathsf{Score}
?

(2)

How can we compute
\mathsf{Score}
securely while protecting both Alice’s and Bob’s private data?

The challenge is to devise a criterion that reliably reflects true improvement—ordinarily requiring retraining—while remaining simple enough for efficient secure computation within practical runtimes. Consequently, we co-design the scoring rule and the secure protocol to yield a feasible and efficient scoring system.

We assume Bob contributes a larger dataset (
|D_{B}|>1
), reflecting practical scenarios such as a company representative contributing bulk records (e.g., sales data). To facilitate deployment on blockchain-based systems, PrivaDE is maliciously secure: it executes in the presence of a PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
who may arbitrarily deviate from the protocol, with the identity of the corrupted party fixed prior to execution. The protocol protects the honest party by detecting any deviation and safely aborting. PrivaDE requires no trusted party beyond the setup and preprocessing stages.

PrivaDE assumes the model architecture is public, while the model weights
\theta
are Alice’s private input. Bob’s private input is the dataset
D_{B}
, formally represented by the sequences
(\overrightarrow{x},\overrightarrow{y})
. The
i
-th sample is
(x_{i},y_{i})
, the
i
-th entries of
\overrightarrow{x}
and
\overrightarrow{y}
.

The primary goal of our protocols is to protect
\theta
and
(\overrightarrow{x},\overrightarrow{y})
. For efficiency, we allow carefully bounded, protocol-specified leakage, supported by prior work and experiments, which does not reveal the full model
\theta
or any individual sample
(x_{i},y_{i})
. The remaining, unleaked components are proven secure via formal, simulation-based arguments; see Sec.
5
for definitions and proofs.

4.
Practical Optimizations for PrivaDE

In this section, we describe several design techniques that innovates PrivaDE and makes PrivaDE practical.

4.1.
Model Distillation

Model distillation for modern neural networks, introduced by Hinton et al.
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
, is widely used to reduce model size and inference cost. The original (teacher) model guides training of a smaller (student) model, typically by matching the teacher’s logits.

Distillation is also common in secure inference (e.g.,
(
Pereteanu et al. 2022
)
) to lower protocol runtime. Empirical studies
(
Romero et al. 2015
;
Cho and Hariharan 2019
)
show that students with over
10\times
fewer parameters can retain accuracy comparable to the teacher, depending on task and architecture.

In
PrivaDE
, we include an optional, local distillation step by the model owner prior to data evaluation. The distilled model is chosen to balance accuracy and size reduction. Beyond improving efficiency, distillation can also reduce leakage: due to limited capacity, a small student cannot faithfully encode all teacher information
(
Cho and Hariharan 2019
)
. We therefore deliberately select a student size that tolerates a minor accuracy loss in exchange for substantial runtime savings and reduced leakage. In Section
7
, we show that a reduced model can still support effective data evaluation and produce results comparable to the original model.

4.2.
Split Model

Figure 2
.
Conceptual diagram of split model in PrivaDE: Split A evaluated in a secure 2PC; split B evaluated by the data owner; split C evaluated by the model owner.

Most ML models, particularly neural networks, are compositions of layers: the output of one layer feeds the next until the final layer. This structure enables
split learning

(
Vepakomma et al. 2018
)
, which partitions a network at a cut layer. In a typical setup, the data owner trains the front segment and the model owner trains the back segment, coordinating forward and backward passes to reduce data exposure.

In secure inference, this idea yields
split inference
(e.g.,
(
Pereteanu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
): the front segment is executed within a secure protocol, while the remaining layers run in the clear. This reduces the cryptographic workload and thus latency, while limiting input leakage to the (carefully chosen) intermediate activations at the cut layer.

In
PrivaDE
, we also employ split-model inference, but propose a more sophisticated three-way split that factorizes
M
as
C\circ B\circ A
(Figure
2
):

(1)

Block
A
.
Contains the initial layers up to (and including) the first activation. We insert a
secret mixer
that permutes
A
’s outputs; this mixer can be an invertible linear layer or an invertible
1\times 1
convolution that mixes channels (see
(
Kingma and Dhariwal 2018
)
). Block
A
is evaluated via a maliciously secure 2PC: Alice supplies weights, Bob supplies inputs. The output of
A
is given only to Bob.

(2)

Block
B
.
Comprises intermediate layers. The first layer of
B
incorporates the inverse mixer to undo
A
’s permutation. The
B
–
C
cut is chosen by the C2PI boundary-search procedure
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
. Bob executes
B
locally after receiving the corresponding weights from Alice.

(3)

Block
C
.
Contains the remaining layers up to the output. Alice executes
C
after receiving the activations produced by
B
from Bob.

The
B
–
C
split follows prior work aiming to curb input leakage while reducing runtime. We adopt the C2PI boundary search
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, which probes candidate cut layers by attempting input reconstruction from the cut-layer activations; for images, a split is accepted when the structural similarity index with the original input falls below
0.3
.

The
A
–
B
split further minimizes 2PC overhead by restricting cryptographic computation to the minimal front segment (up to the first activation), leaving the remainder in
B
. Because
B
’s weights are sent in the clear to Bob,
A
prevents Bob from obtaining a self-sufficient front model that would yield reusable embeddings on arbitrary inputs.

Finally, we attach zero-knowledge proofs to the evaluations of
B
and
C
to enforce correctness and deter cheating. See Sec.
5
for protocol details.

4.2.1.
Leakage of model
B
to Bob:

Yokinski et al.
(
Yosinski et al. 2014
)
show that later layers tend to be more task-specific, whereas early layers capture generic features. Because neither the weights of
C
nor the model outputs are revealed, Bob cannot reconstruct the full task model. However,
B\circ A
may still yield useful embeddings and thus be valuable for fine-tuning on related domains; accordingly, we must bound the transferability of
B
.

To repurpose the model, Bob must:

(1)

Reconstruct
A
to map raw inputs into
B
’s input space; and

(2)

Apply transfer learning to
B\circ A
for other tasks.

For reconstructing
A
, Bob only observes his inputs and the corresponding outputs of
A
. We assume each contributor executes at most
k
queries through
A
. A secret mixer (which can be different on different runs) applies a random invertible permutation to
A
’s outputs, which thwarts simple plug-and-play (attaching public pretrained fronts to
B
) and impedes collusion by multiple contributors pooling I/O pairs for extraction.

Empirical reports
(
Orekondy et al. 2019
)
suggest successful extraction often requires on the order of
10^{4}
queries; thus, security improves when
k\ll 10^{4}
. To quantify robustness at budget
k
, we propose a
reconstruction-under-budget
experiment: randomly sample
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
from
D_{A}
and train
A^{\prime}
(a re-initialized copy of
A
) by minimizing

\min_{\theta}\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big\|A^{\prime}(x_{i};\theta)-u_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}\;+\;\lambda\,\big\|B\big(A^{\prime}(x_{i};\theta)\big)-B(u_{i})\big\|_{2}^{2},

with
\lambda>0
weighting feature consistency through
B
. After training, fix
B
and
C
, replace
A
by
A^{\prime}
, and report top-1 accuracy on the standard test set. The primary metric is

\Delta\mathrm{Acc}\;=\;\mathrm{Acc}\big(C\!\circ\!B\!\circ\!A^{\prime}\big)\;-\;\mathrm{Acc}\big(C\!\circ\!B\!\circ\!A\big).

A split is deemed robust if
\Delta\mathrm{Acc}
is substantially negative at practical
k
, indicating that
A
is not reconstructable and
B
is not easily reusable under the stated constraints (cf. the privacy criterion in
Zhang et al. 2025a
).

We also assess the transferability of
B\circ A
(hypothetically assuming
A
is learned). Following You et al.
(
You et al. 2021
)
, we compute
LogME
, the log marginal evidence that a Bayesian linear regressor explains labels from features. Concretely, we pass labeled images through
B(A(x))
to obtain features and take the resulting
LogME
value as a transferability score; lower scores indicate poorer linear transfer and thus reduced leakage risk.

Alice decides by herself if the leakage is acceptable based on
\Delta\mathrm{Acc}
and the
LogME
score by comparing it to baselines of other models in the marketplace.

4.2.2.
Leakage of activations
B(A(x))
to Alice:

We quantify input leakage by reconstruction quality, training an inverse model that maps
B(A(x))
back to
x
. For images, we adopt DINA
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
and report the average structural similarity index (SSIM) over a test set; we accept the split when the mean SSIM
<0.3
, indicating low-fidelity reconstructions.

4.3.
Representative Set Selection

Because Bob contributes a dataset
D_{B}
, scoring every point in
D_{B}
is costly and increases leakage: repeated inferences can expose information about Alice’s model and Bob’s data distribution.

To mitigate this, our protocol requires Bob to extract a much smaller
representative subset

D_{R}\subset D_{B}
, and then jointly and securely evaluate Alice’s model only on
D_{R}
. To ensure correctness even if Bob is malicious, we enforce honest sampling via a cut-and-choose subroutine (Sec.
4.3.2
). Similar representative-sampling strategies (without security) are standard in batch active learning
(
Sener and Savarese 2018
)
and related ML tasks
(
Mirzasoleiman et al. 2013
)
.

We formalize representativeness as follows:

Definition 4.1 (Representativeness of
D_{R}
to
D_{B}
).

Let
D_{R}\subset D_{B}
, and fix a distance threshold
d>0
and outlier tolerance
\delta\in[0,1]
. Then
D_{R}
is
(d,\delta)
-representative
if

\Pr_{x\sim D_{B}}\!\bigl[\exists\,x^{\prime}\in D_{R}:\|x-x^{\prime}\|<d\bigr]\geq 1-\delta
,
where the probability is with respect to the empirical distribution over
D_{B}
.

We write
\mathcal{R}(D_{B})
for any algorithm that, given
|D_{B}|=n>k
, returns
D_{R}\subset D_{B}
of size
k
. Common methods include
k
-center clustering and submodular maximization
(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
;
Mirzasoleiman et al. 2013
)
. The parameters
(k,d,\delta)
are chosen jointly by Alice and Bob per application.

4.3.1.
Dimension Reduction.

The selection step involves pairwise distances as in Def.
4.1
, which becomes expensive in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
d
grows. We therefore apply random linear projections
(
Bingham and Mannila 2001
)
:

x^{\prime}=R\,x,\qquad R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\;m\ll d,

which preserve distances up to
\varepsilon
-distortion by the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma
(
Johnson and Lindenstrauss 1984
)
. In a secure setting, Alice and Bob jointly sample
R
via
md
secure coin flips
(
Blum 1981
)
, deriving a shared pseudorandom seed. Bob projects his data locally and runs the representative-selection protocol on
\{x^{\prime}\}
, reducing computation and communication while retaining scoring fidelity.

4.3.2.
Challenge Protocol for Representative Set Selection

A naive way to securely implement the representative-set algorithm
\mathcal{R}
is via a maliciously secure two-party computation (2PC). However, because state-of-the-art clustering often relies on costly Euclidean distances, this approach incurs substantial overhead.

As an alternative, we introduce the
challenge protocol

\mathsf{CP}
. Bob commits each point
x_{i}\in D_{B}^{x}
(
D_{B}^{x}:=\{x\mid(x,y)\in D_{B}\}
) via commitments
\mathtt{com}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{com}_{n}
, and publishes the representative set
D_{R}
by revealing its indices
I_{R}:=\{i|x_{i}\in D_{R}^{x}\}
.

Alice selects a random index
i\in[n]
and challenges Bob to prove, in zero knowledge, that the committed point
x_{i}
is well represented by
D_{R}
. Bob recovers
x_{i}
, computes
d_{i}^{\prime}=\min_{x^{r}_{j}\in D_{R}^{x}}\|x^{r}_{j}-x_{i}\|
, and:

•

if
d_{i}^{\prime}<d
, returns a ZK proof
\pi_{i}
attesting that

\exists j,r_{i}:\ \|x^{r}_{j}-x_{i}\|\leq d\ \wedge\ \mathsf{Open}(\mathsf{pp},\mathtt{com}_{i},x_{i},r_{i})=1
;

•

otherwise replies
\mathsf{fail}_{i}
.

By repeating this challenge, Alice gains confidence that
D_{R}
is representative of
D_{B}
. The full protocol appears in Fig.
3
, and its security is established in the next section.

Parties:
Model owner
P_{1}
, data owner
P_{2}

Public parameters:
Indices
I_{R}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
; commitments of
D_{B}^{x}
:
\mathsf{COM}_{B}=\{\mathtt{com}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{com}_{n}\}
; commitment parameter
\mathsf{pp}
; maximum distance
d
; outlier ratio
\delta

(1)

P_{1}
samples a set of indices
I\subset[n]
uniformly at random and sends it to
P_{2}
.

(2)

For each
j\in I
,
P_{2}
recovers
x_{i}\in D_{B}^{x}
and computes
d_{i}^{\prime}=\min_{x^{r}_{j}\in D_{R}^{x}}\|x^{r}_{j}-x_{i}\|
.

(3)

For each
i\in I
:

–

If
d_{i}^{\prime}<d

4.4.1.
Cheating-detection probability

Because the scoring protocol aggregates all evaluated points, altering a single point has limited impact on the final score. We therefore consider an adversary that corrupts a fraction
\rho\in(0,1)
of the
N
inputs.

Theorem 4.2.

Consider
\mathsf{CnCZK}
on a model with
L
layers and
N
inference points, auditing
m
points and
s
layers per audited point (both sampled uniformly without replacement). If an adversary corrupts

points, the probability of detecting cheating is bounded below by

\Pr[\textsf{detect}]\;\geq\;\sum_{k=0}^{\min(N\rho,\,m)}\frac{\binom{N\rho}{k}\binom{N(1-\rho)}{\,m-k\,}}{\binom{N}{m}}\Bigl[1-\Bigl(\tfrac{L-s}{L}\Bigr)^{k}\Bigr].

The proof appears in Appendix
A.1
. As an illustration, with
N=100
,
L=10
,
\rho=0.10
,
m=25
, and
s=6
, the detection probability exceeds

. This setting audits only

of all layer evaluations, yielding about an
85\%
reduction relative to checking every layer of every point.

5.1.
Dataset Scoring Algorithm

Given a representative set
D_{R}
, the parties compute a score using three criteria—diversity, uncertainty, and loss—where diversity and uncertainty follow standard active-learning practice.

Diversity.

The subset should be diverse, meaning elements of
\mathcal{S}
are sufficiently different on average to avoid redundancy. We denote diversity by
\mathcal{D}
, instantiated, e.g., as a statistic (mean, median) of pairwise distances. Diverse-set constructions are common in coreset and batch active learning
(
Sener and Savarese 2018
)
.

Uncertainty.

Uncertainty indicates potential informativeness: high uncertainty suggests the model benefits from additional training on such samples. A typical measure is the entropy of
M
’s predictive distribution; margin-based variants are also used. We write the uncertainty functional as
\mathcal{U}
.

Loss.

Relevance can be captured by the loss incurred by
M
on Bob’s data
(
Yoo and Kweon 2019
)
. If
y^{\prime}=M_{\theta}(x)
, then
\ell(y^{\prime},y)
quantifies the discrepancy between prediction and label; assuming valid data, larger loss indicates greater potential value for training.

Let

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|D_{R}|}\;\;\sum_{\mathclap{(x,y)\in D_{R}}}\ell\!\left(M_{\theta}(x),y\right),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\!\left(M_{\theta}[D_{R}]\right),

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\!\left(D_{B}^{x},D_{R}^{x}\right).

The overall score is
\phi=f(l,u,d)
for a pre-agreed aggregator
f
. Algorithm
1
summarizes the cleartext procedure, where

and

6.
Blockchain Integration for PrivaDE

In this section, we describe how
PrivaDE
can be integrated into a blockchain-based decentralized data marketplace.
This is a proof-of-concept; detailed implementation aspects are out of scope.

Using a blockchain brings
four
major advantages. First, commitments and data requests can be posted on-chain, enhancing transparency and guaranteeing input immutability even before evaluation begins. Second, the fairness of MPC within
PrivaDE
—not typically guaranteed—can be enforced by slashing escrow from dishonest parties. Third, ZKP generation in
\mathsf{CP}
and
\mathsf{CnCZK}
can be tied to on-chain payments, deterring overuse and improving efficiency. Finally, data transactions can be integrated with evaluation so that, for example, when the score produced by
PrivaDE
exceeds a threshold, the transaction is triggered atomically.

Figure 9
.
Decentralized Data Marketplace Workflow with PrivaDE integration. Commitments and protocol results are posted on-chain for transparency, and data transaction is seamlessly integrated.

Step 1: Public Parameter Generation / Smart Contract Deployment

Before the genesis block, the creator chooses a security parameter
\lambda
and runs
\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{SetupCom}(1^{\lambda})
. The parameters
(\lambda,\mathsf{pp})
are embedded as chain parameters.
Once the chain is live, a marketplace smart contract is deployed to record commitments and interactions.

Step 2: Data Validation

Before responding to quotes, a data owner must validate their data with a data authority.
The data owner sends
(x,y)
to the authority for validation.
The authority verifies authenticity and ownership and, if successful, computes

\mathtt{com}_{x},r_{x}\leftarrow\mathsf{Commit}(\mathsf{pp},x)
and

\mathtt{com}_{y},r_{y}\leftarrow\mathsf{Commit}(\mathsf{pp},y)
,
then submits
(\mathtt{com}_{x},\mathtt{com}_{y})
to the marketplace contract from the authority’s contract address (serving as a signature).
The authority notifies the data owner of the outcome and, if successful, returns
(r_{x},r_{y})
.

Step 3: Model Validation

Model owners prepare a model suitable for evaluation (including preprocessing such as distillation and splitting) and submit it to a model authority for validation.
The authority verifies the reconstruction quality at the
B
–
C
split. If successful, it computes

\mathtt{com}_{\theta_{s}},r_{s}\leftarrow\mathsf{Commit}(\mathsf{pp},\theta_{s})
for
s\in\{A,B,C\}
and submits

(\mathtt{com}_{\theta_{A}},\mathtt{com}_{\theta_{B}},\mathtt{com}_{\theta_{C}})
on-chain from the
model authority
’s contract address.
The authority notifies the
model owner
and, if successful, returns
(r_{A},r_{B},r_{C})
.

Step 4: Request Data Quotes

A model owner requests quotes by calling the marketplace contract, which emits an event that contributors can monitor.
The request includes data requirements, model commitments
(\mathtt{com}_{A},\mathtt{com}_{B},\mathtt{com}_{C})
, and an escrow that is forfeited if the model owner deviates from the protocol.

Step 5: Submit Data Quotes

Data contributors respond by submitting their commitments
(\mathtt{com}_{x},\mathtt{com}_{y})
and an escrow to the contract.
Submissions must match the authority-published commitments from Step 2; otherwise, the quote is automatically rejected.

Step 6: Data Evaluation Protocol

For each accepted quote, the data owner and model owner run
PrivaDE
, with two blockchain-oriented refinements:

(1)

MPC fairness.
In
\Pi_{\mathsf{SubScore}}
, the final protocol message and output can be escrow-enforced: the last-sender must pre-commit (e.g., via an on-chain encrypted blob or hash-locked reference) to the final message/output by a deadline. Failure to comply triggers escrow slashing and compensates the counterparty, preventing an unfair abort after learning
\phi
.

(2)

Payment for ZKP generation.
In
\mathsf{CP}
and
\mathsf{CnCZK}
, verifiers may over-request proofs to raise the security if there is no cost attached. The contract can require proportional payment from the verifier before proof generation, aligning incentives and reducing prover load.

Step 7: Data Transaction

After scoring (potentially across multiple contributors), the model owner proceeds with transactions to selected data owners.
Transactions can use hash-locked exchanges
(
Poon and Dryja 2016
)
:

(1)

The data owner uploads an encrypted dataset to IPFS, submits
H(k)
on-chain, and privately sends the CID and a ZKP attesting that the ciphertext decrypts under
k
;

(2)

The model owner retrieves the CID, verifies the ZKP, and escrows payment to the contract;

(3)

The data owner reveals
k
on-chain; the contract checks
H(k)
, releases escrow to the data owner, and the model owner decrypts the data—completing the transaction.

8.
Related Works

Privacy-preserving Data Evaluation.

Prior work has explored privacy-preserving data valuation and selection.

Song et al.
(
Song et al. 2021
)
propose a data evaluation protocol based on uncertainty sampling, later refined by Qian et al.
(
Qian et al. 2024
)
. Their main advance is an inner-product-based functional encryption scheme that accelerates inference. However, both works adopt the semi-honest adversarial model, which is insufficient for blockchain-based deployments where no trust is assumed between parties.

Tian et al.
(
Tian et al. 2022
)
take a different approach by estimating Shapley values via a learned
Data Utility Model
and then executing custom MPC to perform inference and approximate Shapley scores. This method, however, requires sharing portions of the data owner’s samples and additional labeling to train the utility model; moreover, the end-to-end MPC cost of full-model inference remains high (e.g.,
\sim
16 hours for 2,000 CIFAR-10 images in their evaluation), which is ill-suited to marketplaces that demand quick, low-effort data assessment and purchase.

Data pricing.
Data pricing is usually concerned with setting a price for data in a standard currency. The research in this area adapts economic principles of pricing and markets to data
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, and is concerned with the data-specific challenges such as duplication and arbitrage
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Ohrimenko et al. 2019
)
. These works take the perspective of the broker and consider the seller and buyer’s market separately. The actual utility of the data to the consumer is assumed to be determined by simple attributes
(
Koutris et al. 2015
)
.

Game theoretic fair data valuation.
This topic emerged with the popularity of machine learning and demand for data
(
Jia et al. 2019
;
Ghorbani et al. 2020
;
Ghorbani and Zou 2019
)
, and can be seen as an aspect of explainable machine learning
(
Rozemberczki et al. 2022
)
. The aim of game theoretic data valuation is to determine fair value for data points (or subsets) in a multiple data contributor setting. These works make use of Shapley Value
(
Rozemberczki et al. 2022
)
with its Fairness properties to ensure fair valuation for all. Crucially, in these problems the fairness consideration is between different data contributors, as Shapley Value is a mechanism for determining fair share between multiple players.
Several associated approaches exist for evaluating data, such as
leave-one-out testing
(
Cook 1977
)
and influence function estimation
(
Koh and Liang 2017
;
Datta et al. 2015
;
Pruthi et al. 2020
)
, with similar objectives. Shapley value and similar techniques tend to be computationally expensive – repeatedly training and testing models on different subsets of data – and require a trusted party to carry out the computation.

Active learning.
We borrowed several ideas from this area, such as the use of uncertainty and diversity
(
Settles 2009
;
Dasgupta 2011
)
. Methods that use both are called hybrid methods. There are algorithms incorporating different scoring values non-linearly

(
Ash et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2017
;
Zhdanov 2019
)

and linearly
(
Cardoso et al. 2017
)
. In the standard active learning paradigm, loss is not a decision criterion, because loss computation requires the ground truth labels, which are assumed to be expensive, and the algorithms aim to minimize the use of labels. In
(
Yoo and Kweon 2019
)
, a loss predictor is trained simultaneously with the model to inform the active selection process.

Zero knowledge proofs in Machine learning
. Researches in this area have been mainly focused on generalizability and efficiency. For generalizability, there are works mapping machine learning operations into suitable zero-knowledge circuit, and translation of non-linear and floating point operations into suitable approximate encryption-friendly circuits (e.g.
(
Ghodsi et al. 2017
)
). Other researches in this area have approached the efficiency problem by making use of specific features of models and training algorithms
(
Angel et al. 2022
)
. However, current zero knowledge proof systems are considered useful mainly for model inference and they are not efficiently supporting the training process. Commonly they affect the model’s accuracy or can be too slow for training even moderate sized models.

Our setting of data scoring using zero knowledge proofs creates some unique challenges and possibilities. It allows the use of labels and loss in ways that are not possible in standard active learning, and ensures the transmission of enough information to make decision without revealing secret data.

Secure MPC Inference Methods.
Although zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) protect model weights and guarantee correctness of inference, they do not by themselves ensure privacy of the query input
x
. An alternative is to employ maliciously-secure multiparty computation (MPC) under a dishonest–majority model, which provides both confidentiality and integrity in a two-party setting. The most prominent example here is MD-ML
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
, which optimizes cryptographic primitives to minimize online inference latency. While MD-ML achieves low absolute latency on small models, its performance degrades sharply with complexity––incurring roughly a 40× slowdown from a Linear SVM to LeNet.

Many other maliciously-secure inference frameworks assume an honest–majority among three or more parties, which is incompatible with two-party scenarios. Representative systems in this category include Helix
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, Blaze
(
Patra and Suresh 2020
)
, and SWIFT
(
Koti et al. 2021
)
. Because these require a strict majority of honest participants, their security and performance guarantees do not directly translate to our setting.

9.
Conclusion

Large-scale AI models and training data remain concentrated among major corporations and institutions. Decentralized data marketplaces aim to lower this barrier by establishing trust and incentivizing data sharing, thereby enabling individual data owners and model builders to collaboratively develop advanced models.

This paper introduced
PrivaDE
, a practical and efficient protocol for privacy-preserving data evaluation in decentralized marketplaces.
PrivaDE
operates in the malicious-adversary setting with explicitly bounded leakage, combining representative-set selection, verified split inference, and secure scoring to deliver actionable utility signals. Our experiments demonstrate its feasibility and efficiency.

Future directions include optimizing cryptographic subroutines for lower latency and communication, improving scalability to ever-larger datasets and models, and building fully decentralized (blockchain-based) deployments that integrate data validation, payments, fair MPC, and model training into a cohesive end-to-end workflow.

Proof of Theorem

In the worst case, for each corrupted point
i
, the adversary modifies only a single value—either one activation
a_{l,i}
among the
L\!+\!1
activations (
l\in\{0,\dots,L\}
) or one layer weight
\theta_{j}
among the
L
weights. Because altering activations can be caught with slightly higher probability (if
l\in\{1,\dots,L-1\}
, detection occurs when either layer
l
or
l\!+\!1
is checked), we analyze the stronger adversary that alters only a weight.

Hence, the detection probability for a
single
corrupted point,
conditioned
on that point being audited, is bounded by

p_{\text{single}}\;\geq\;\frac{s}{L}.

If
k
corrupted points are audited, the probability of detecting at least one of them is

1-\Pr[\text{no corrupted point detected}]\;=\;1-(1-p_{\text{single}})^{k}.

Let
K
be the number of corrupted points drawn into the audit set
S
; then
K
follows a hypergeometric distribution with

\Pr[K=k]\;=\;\frac{\binom{N\rho}{k}\binom{N(1-\rho)}{\,m-k\,}}{\binom{N}{m}},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,\min(N\rho,m).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr[\textsf{detect}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(N\rho,\,m)}\Pr[K=k]\cdot\bigl[1-(1-p_{\text{single}})^{k}\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\min(N\rho,\,m)}\frac{\binom{N\rho}{k}\binom{N(1-\rho)}{\,m-k\,}}{\binom{N}{m}}\left[1-\left(\tfrac{L-s}{L}\right)^{k}\right],

which proves the claim.

B.4.
Multi-Party Computation

Here, we provide a formal definition of secure multiparty computation, with emphasis on the two-party scenario.

A multiparty protocol is cast by specifying a random process that maps tuples of inputs to tuples of outputs (one for each party). We refer to such a process as a functionality. The security of a protocol is defined with respect to a functionality
f
. In particular, let
n
denote the number of parties. A (non-reactive)
n
-party functionality
f
is a (possibly randomized) mapping of
n
inputs to
n
outputs. A multiparty protocol with security parameter
\lambda
for computing the functionality
f
is a protocol running in time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
and satisfying the following correctness requirement: if parties
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
with inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
respectively, all run an honest execution of the protocol, then the joint distribution of the outputs
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
of the parties is statistically close to
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.

The security of a protocol (w.r.t. a functionality
f
) is defined by comparing the real-world execution of the protocol with an ideal-world evaluation of
f
by a trusted party. More concretely, it is required that for every adversary
\mathcal{A}
, which attacks the real-world execution of the protocol, there exists an adversary
\mathcal{S}
, also referred to as the simulator, which can
achieve the same effect
in the ideal-world execution. We denote
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.

Malicious MPC.

In the real execution for the malicious assumption, the
n
-party protocol
\Pi
for computing
f
is executed in the presence of an adversary
\mathcal{A}
. The honest parties follow the instructions of
\Pi
. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
, the set
I\subset[n]
of corrupted parties, the inputs of the corrupted parties, and an auxiliary input
z
.
\mathcal{A}
sends all messages in place of corrupted parties and may follow an arbitrary polynomial-time strategy.

The above interaction of
\mathcal{A}
with a protocol
\Pi
defines a random variable
\real{}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}(z),I}(\lambda,\allowbreak\vec{x})
whose value is determined by the coin tosses of the adversary and the honest players. This random variable contains the output of the adversary (which may be an arbitrary function of its view) as well as the outputs of the uncorrupted parties.

An ideal execution for a function
f
proceeds as follows:

•

Send inputs to the trusted party:
As before, the parties send their inputs to the trusted party, and we let
x^{\prime}_{i}
denote the value sent by
P_{i}
.

•

Trusted party sends output to the adversary:
The trusted party computes
f(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and sends
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
to the adversary.

•

Adversary instructs trusted party to abort or continue:
This is formalized by having the adversary send either a continue or abort message to the trusted party. (A semi-honest adversary never aborts.) In the former case, the trusted party sends to each uncorrupted party
P_{i}
its output value
y_{i}
. In the latter case, the trusted party sends the special symbol
\bot
to each uncorrupted party.

•

Outputs:

\mathcal{S}
outputs an arbitrary function of its view, and the honest parties output the values obtained from the trusted party.

In the case of secure two-party computation, where an adversary corrupts only one of the two parties, the above behavior of an ideal-world trusted party is captured by functionality
\mathcal{F}_{\sf 2PC}
, described in Figure
11
below.

Definition B.4.

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter,
f
an
n

\Pi

n

n\in\mathbb{N}

\Pi
