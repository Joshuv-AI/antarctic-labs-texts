Effects of Dietary Waste Wet Dates (Phoenix dactylifera) on Growth, Feed Utilization, and Health Status of Nile Tilapia, Oreochromis niloticus, and Thin‐Lip Gray Mullet, Liza ramada, in a Polyculture System
Authors: Mohamed M Toutou, Amr Elkelish, Ali A Soliman, Arkadios Dimitroglou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, five levels of waste wet dates (WDs) (Phoenix dactylifera) (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%) were added to the diets of Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) and thin‐lip gray mullet (Liza ramada) in order to assess their growth, feed performance, whole‐body composition, fatty acids (FAs), intestinal enzyme activity, and biochemical blood parameters in a polyculture system. In 15 concrete tanks of 2 m × 5 m × 1 m, five dietary treatments were set up, each in triplicate, with 15 Nile tilapia (10.3 g) and 5 thin‐lip gray mullet (5.0 g) fingerlings per tank. The fish were fed a diet that contained ~30% crude protein (CP) for 60 days. The growth and feed utilization of Nile tilapia were all significantly improved by feeding them 10% WD, while thin‐lip gray mullet benefited most from feeding 15% WD. According to the blood parameter data, the lowest levels of triglycerides, amylase, and glucose were seen in Nile tilapia fed 10% dietary WD. Furthermore, the 15% WD diet enhanced the polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) profile in mullet flesh. On the other hand, as compared to the other groups, thin‐lip gray mullet fed 20% dietary WD had the greatest levels of glucose and amylase.Keywords: blood parameter, enzyme activity, fatty acid profile, polyculture, waste wet dates

1. Introduction

Feed costs pose a significant economic challenge to aquaculture in Egypt, particularly for tilapia farming, where they can account for roughly 70% of total production expenses [1]. This financial burden, coupled with the ongoing need to ensure adequate nutrition for farmed fish [2], highlights the importance of exploring alternative, nontraditional feed ingredients [3, 4]. The utilization of agricultural by‐products like waste wet dates (WDs) as carbohydrate sources presents a promising strategy to reduce feed costs [3]. WDs can serve as a cost‐effective partial substitute for energy‐rich ingredients like yellow corn in fish diets, leveraging locally available resources to enhance economic sustainability [5, 6].

Date palm seed meal has emerged as a promising option due to its nutritional value, affordability, and availability. Previous research indicates its beneficial effects on various aspects of aquatic animal health, including growth, immune function, oxidative status, and stress resistance [7–11].

Despite this, the use of waste WDs as a carbohydrate source in fish diets remains relatively underexplored. Egypt, a major date producer, generates a substantial amount of WD by‐products annually (~170,000–250,000 tons), representing 21% of global production [12]. This abundant availability makes it an attractive, low‐cost alternative for aquafeed formulations. Date palm, a resource utilized for millennia [13], and its products are known for their nutritional and medicinal properties, including antioxidant, immunostimulant, and anti‐inflammatory effects [14, 15]. Date fruits and products offer excellent nutritional value, containing digestible sugars (fructose, glucose, sucrose), proteins, dietary fibers, lipids, vitamins, mineral salts, bioactive compounds, and amino acids [16]. Furthermore, given the importance of fish as a source of omega‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), it’s crucial to assess factors that influence fish quality, such as shelf life [17, 18]. Considering the widespread use of polyculture systems in Egypt due to their ease of management and productivity [19, 20], this study aimed to evaluate how supplementing diets with waste WDs affects the growth performance, whole‐body composition, and biochemical blood parameters of Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) and thin‐lip gray mullet (Liza ramada) in a polyculture setting.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Fish and Experimental Design

The Fish Nutrition Laboratory, Baltim Research Station, National Institute of Oceanography and Fisheries (NIOF), Egypt, was the site of this experiment in the spring of 2023. We bought fingerlings of thin‐lip gray mullet and Nile tilapia from a private fish farm in the Kafr El‐Sheikh Governorate, Egypt. 15 Nile tilapia and five thin‐lip gray mullet fish, each weighing an average of 10.3 ± 0.2 and 5.0 ± 0.1 g, were randomly stocked in 15 concrete tanks (2 m × 5 m × 1 m), with five dietary treatments assigned randomly, each in triplicate, following 2 weeks of acclimation in a concrete tank (5 m × 10 m × 1 m). Fresh subterranean water and aeration were added to the experimental tanks after they were cleaned. Every week, measurements were made of the water quality parameters, such as temperature, pH (Jenway Ltd., Model 350 pH meter), and dissolved oxygen (Jenway Ltd., Model 970 dissolved oxygen meter). The ambient water temperature, dissolved oxygen content, and pH were 24.0 ± 2.0°C, 6.0 ± 2.0 mg/L, and 7.9 ± 0.2 during the experiment. For 60 days, the fish were given the experimental diets (5%–3%) twice a day at 09.00 and 14.00 h, 6 days a week. In this investigation, a flow‐through system with a 20% daily water exchange was employed.

It is important to acknowledge that while polyculture systems reflect realistic farming conditions, they present limitations for nutrition‐focused studies as they prevent accurate measurement of individual species’ feed intake and growth response.

2.2. Experimental Diets and Feeding Regime

As indicated in Table 1, experimental meals that were isonitrogenous (~30% crude protein) and isocaloric (~445 kcal/100 g gross energy [GE]) were produced. To create a paste for each diet, the ingredients were combined with extra water. The pastes were formed into pellets that were the right size for the fish used in the experiment. After being dried for 24 h at 60°C in a thermostatically controlled oven, the feed was kept in plastic bags at −20°C until it was needed. 0% served as the control diet, while five experimental diets 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% were created. The fish nutrition laboratory (NIOF) created the formulated feed by substituting 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. Table 1 displays the formulated feed’s proximate chemical analyses on a dry matter (DM) basis. Feed intake for each individual fish was computed based on the total amount of feed consumed by the fish in each tank during the experiment.

2.3. Experimental Procedures

2.3.1. Proximate Analyses

At the conclusion of the feeding study, five fish were netted from each tank. A total of 15 fish per treatment were then combined and homogenized for proximate composition. According to the Standard Association of Official Analytical Chemists [23] technique, the contents of moisture, total protein (TP), fat, and ash were all measured. For proximate studies, diet samples were used in triplicate.

2.4. Evaluation of Growth Performance and Feed Utilization Efficiency

The following metrics were used to assess growth performance and feed utilization: System feed conversion ratio (System FCR), System protein efficiency ratio (System PER), specific growth rate (SGR, %/day), weight gain (WG, g), and percent WG (%WG).

WG = FW − IW(g/fish). [%WG] = 100 × [(final fish weight(g) − initial fish weight(g))/initial fish weight]. SGR = 100 × [(ln final fish weight) − (ln initial fish weight)]/Days. FCR = Dry feed consumed(g)/WG(g). PER = WG(g)/protein consumed(g). Fish survival(%) = 100 × (final fish count/initial fish count).

Due to the polyculture system, individual species’ feed intake could not be accurately measured. Therefore, FCR and PER were calculated based on total system biomass and total feed input, representing system‐level indicators of feed utilization.

2.5. Blood Parameters

Ultimately, fish were deprived for a full day before the blood samples were drawn. Buffering tricaine methane sulfonate (30 mg/L) was used to anesthetize the fish in each tank. For the complete blood count (CBC), three fish were removed from each tank. Using a syringe, the blood samples were drawn from the heart and stored in tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). A Fully Automatic Blood Cell Counter (Model PCE‐210 N, Erma, Inc.) was used to determine the CBC of each blood sample. Hematocrit value (Hct, %), white blood cell count (WBC, 103/µL), hemoglobin concentration (Hb, g/dL), red blood cell count (RBC, 103/ µL), erythrocyte cell volume (MCV), mean cellular hemoglobin content (MCH), and mean cellular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) were all assessed.

2.6. Determination of Serum Biomarkers

Serum biochemical analysis was performed using an additional five fish of every species. The drawn blood was centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm after being stored at 4°C for the whole night. The supernatant was then extracted as a serum sample for further examination. In accordance with Henry et al. [24], the biuret test was used to measure TP (g/dL) and albumin (g/dL). Globulin content was determined by subtracting the albumin value from the TP value. To assess the kidney function, creatinine (mg/dL) and urea (mg/dL) were measured using kits (Pasteur, Lab, France), following the methodologies of Trinder [25] and Thomas [26], respectively. An enzymatic colorimetric approach was used to quantitatively quantify glucose (mg/dL). The lipid profile was evaluated as follows: total cholesterol (mg/dL) was calculated using Atamanalp et al.’s [27] methodology. Cholesterol was evaluated using kits (Pasteur, Lab, France). According to Chawla et al. [28], the colorimetric method was used to assess serum triglycerides (mg/dL) using specific kits (Pasteur, Lab, France). Reitman and Frankel’s [29] methodology was used to determine the activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Bergmeyer’s [30] approach was used to quantitatively assess alkaline phosphatase activity (ALP, U/L).

2.7. Gut Analysis

A cold (0°C) glass platform was used to delicately remove each fish’s digestive system. Separate sets of fish (six fish for each treatment) were used to study the activity of digestive enzymes. Weighing was done on the digestive systems. Following homogenization at a ratio of 1:10 (tissue: sample) using cold distilled water, the tissue was centrifuged for 30 min at 10,000 rpm and 4°C. The supernatant was then collected and kept at −20°C until analysis.

2.8. Digestive Enzyme Assay

By evaluating the rise in the buffered starch solution’s reducing power with 3,5‐dinitro salicylic acid, amylase activity was determined [31]. The Synergy HT Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) was used to measure the absorbance at 540 nm. The amount of maltose released per milligram of protein per hour at 37°C was used to express specific amylase activity. We used commercial kits from Bio‐diagnostic Co. (Alexandria, Egypt) to test lipase.

2.9. Determination of Glycogen, Antioxidant Status, and Fish Immunity

The fish samples were weighed, decapitated, and dissected on ice. The glycogen content in fish liver was measured using the anthrone reagent [32] after homogenizing liver tissue in hot KOH (30% w/v) to dissolve glycogen. Another liver tissues were homogenized with potassium phosphate buffer, 0.1 M, pH 7.4, and then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was subsequently used. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) was measured using the method of Marklund and Marklund [33], using a commercially available kit (Bio‐Chain, Inc.). Concentration of thiobarbituric acid reacting substances (TBARS), represented as malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration (nmol/g tissue), was measured using a commercially available kit (Bio‐Chain, Inc.) in accordance with Ohkawa et al.’s [34] description. Micrococcus lysodeikticus (M. luteus, SIGMA/M0508, USA) was used as the substrate in the turbid metric experiment to determine the lysozyme [35].

2.10. Statistical Analysis

To check the data normality distribution and homogeneity of variances among the different fish groups. The differences between the tested treatments were then evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Using the statistical program SPSS version 22 [36], Duncan’s multiple range test was employed as a post hoc test to assess the experimental treatments’ divergence at the p ≤ 0.05 level.

3. Results

The inclusion of waste WD exhibited a significant (p ≤ 0.05) effect on final body weight (FBW), WG, SGR, and FCR in both Nile tilapia and thin‐lip gray mullet (Table 2). Specifically, dietary supplementation with 10% and 15% waste WDs resulted in a significant (p ≤ 0.05) increase in FBW, WG, and SGR compared to the control group for both species. Conversely, a 20% WD inclusion led to a significant (p ≤ 0.05) decrease in these growth performance parameters.

The biochemical composition of Nile tilapia, as presented in Table 4, revealed significant differences in moisture content across the experimental groups. However, increasing levels of WD to 10% and 15% resulted in a significant reduction (p ≤ 0.05) in total crude protein (CP) content in Nile tilapia compared to the control group. Regarding the antioxidation status, fish given diets supplemented with WD showed a gradual and substantial rise (p ≤ 0.05) in SOD and MDA activity, 0% had the lowest MDA value and SOD activity as compared to the WD groups. 4. Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of varying levels of WD on the growth performance, feed utilization, body composition, and health status of Nile tilapia and thin‐lip gray mullet. The study revealed that dietary WD levels of 5%, 10%, and 15% significantly improved growth performance (FBW, WG, and SGR) in both Nile tilapia and thin‐lip gray mullet (p ≤ 0.05). The optimal growth was observed at a 15% WD inclusion level. This aligns with Toutou and Farrag [4], who highlighted the importance of carbohydrate sources in the diet for enhancing growth and survival in thin‐lip gray mullet. Interestingly, a 20% WD inclusion did not show the same growth‐promoting effects and even led to deterioration in growth performance, particularly for thin‐lip gray mullet. This negative effect at higher inclusion levels could be attributed to reduced palatability, nutrient imbalance, or potentially exceeding the carbohydrate digestive capacity of the fish.

The System FCR was significantly lower in fish fed WD diets (5%–15%) compared to the control group and the 20% WD group, indicating better feed utilization at the system level. This improvement in FCR with WD supplementation is consistent with findings on other carbohydrate sources in fish diets [37–40], suggesting enhanced nutrient digestibility [4].

From an economic perspective, the use of waste WDs as a partial substitute for conventional energy sources like yellow corn presents a promising strategy to reduce feed costs [3, 12]. Our diet formulations successfully replaced yellow corn with WD at various levels while maintaining nutritional quality. Given the substantial annual production of WD by‐products in Egypt (~170,000–250,000 tons) [12], this agricultural waste product represents a cost‐effective and readily available alternative ingredient for aquafeeds. Hassan and Abdel‐Rahman [6] demonstrated that agricultural by‐products could reduce feed costs by up to 30% without compromising growth performance in tilapia.

Fish fed WD diets exhibited lower moisture and body lipid content, while crude protein content increased. The reduced lipid content in WD‐fed fish might be attributed to the readily available energy from carbohydrates, potentially sparing the need for lipid storage [41]. Conversely, high dietary carbohydrate levels have been reported to sometimes hinder growth [42] and stimulate glycogen production [43, 44].

The study observed high lipase and amylase activity in fish fed up to 15% WD, suggesting improved carbohydrate and fat digestion. This enhanced digestive enzyme activity likely contributed to the better growth performance and feed efficiency observed in these groups [4]. However, very high WD inclusion (e.g., > 15%) might negatively influence digestion by affecting enzyme activity [45]. Notably, lipase activity in Nile tilapia was not significantly affected by varying WD levels, while it did differ significantly in thin‐lip gray mullet. This could be linked to the different feeding habits of the two species, as amylolytic enzyme activity is typically higher in herbivorous and omnivorous fish compared to carnivorous ones [46]. Factors like fish species, carbohydrate complexity, source, processing, and water temperature can influence carbohydrate digestibility in fish [47].

WD inclusion positively influenced hematological parameters (RBCs and WBCs), indicating no signs of anemia and potentially improved immunity. Blood TP, albumin, and globulin levels were also elevated in WD‐fed tilapia, suggesting enhanced humoral immunity and overall well‐being [48, 49]. Similar increases in blood TP in thin‐lip gray mullet fed WD suggest immunomodulatory effects of the carbohydrate source [9]. Serum lysozyme activity and phagocytic activity were significantly higher in WD‐fed groups, further supporting the immunostimulatory role of WD [50, 51]. These findings align with studies on other carbohydrate sources that also reported improved immune responses in fish [52–54].

Fish fed 20% WD exhibited significantly higher urea and creatinine levels, although these values remained within the normal range for both species [55, 56], indicating no negative impact on kidney function. The 20% WD group also had the lowest glucose levels, possibly suggesting reduced stress at this higher WD level [57].

Nile tilapia fed 20% WD showed significantly lower cholesterol and triglyceride levels. In contrast, thin‐lip gray mullet had the highest cholesterol, triglyceride, and lipase levels at the lowest WD inclusion (0%). These variations could be influenced by the dietary fat content and the fish species’ metabolic responses to different carbohydrate levels [58].

The study’s findings suggest that WD was a well‐utilized carbohydrate source for growth in both species, better than other carbohydrate sources in terms of growth and body composition. Fish, particularly carnivorous species, have a limited ability to utilize high levels of carbohydrates [59], often leading to postprandial hyperglycemia [60, 61]. However, the efficient utilization of WD up to 15% in this study indicates good digestibility and metabolic regulation at these levels. The main product of carbohydrate digestion in fish is glucose, absorbed through specific transporters in the intestine [62]. The ability of fish to handle different carbohydrate complexities varies among species [47, 59,68].

The study also touched upon lipid utilization, noting that the FA profile of fish reflects the dietary FA profile [63]. While supplementing WD can lower (ΣSFA) levels in fish, the overall FA content often remains adequate due to selective retention and endogenous production [64]. Fish can adjust their metabolism of C18 PUFAs to meet their long‐chain PUFA requirements [64–66]. The study suggests that thin‐lip gray mullet can efficiently utilize C18 PUFAs from WD‐containing diets to meet their essential FA needs, with the 15% WD inclusion level showing a notable increase in total PUFA.

Interestingly, a 20% WD inclusion did not show the same growth‐promoting effects and even led to deterioration in growth performance, particularly for thin‐lip gray mullet. This negative effect at higher inclusion levels could be attributed to reduced palatability, nutrient imbalance, or potentially exceeding the carbohydrate digestive capacity of the fish [45, 46]. Liu et al. [67] reported that high carbohydrate levels (> 20%) can lead to metabolic stress and reduced growth in carnivorous fish species due to their limited ability to regulate glucose metabolism.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms waste WDs as a beneficial feed ingredient. The optimal inclusion is species‐specific: 10% for Nile tilapia and 15% for thin‐lip gray mullet. These levels significantly improved growth, feed efficiency, and immune status in a polyculture system. In contrast, a 20% WD inclusion impaired growth and metabolism. Therefore, using WD at 10%–15% is recommended to effectively replace conventional energy sources, reduce feed costs, and promote aquaculture sustainability by valorizing agricultural waste, without compromising fish health or performance.
