Postoperative CT and Functional Analysis of New Larynx in Different Supracricoid Partial Laryngectomy Procedures
Authors: Chang Huang, Wei Liu, Kunyu Liu, Xiaohui Du, Weilin Dai, Rui Han, Pei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare postoperative CT images of different supracricoid partial laryngectomy procedures and to analyze the formation of a new laryngeal.Study DesignObservation and retrospective study.MethodsImaging data of 16 patients with supracricoid partial laryngectomy surgery were collected before and 6 months after different supracricoid partial laryngectomy surgeries. Parameters of larynx CT images were read and measured by three senior otolaryngologists at different layers. Function analysis of the new larynx was mainly focused on voice and swallowing. Two‐way analysis of variance was employed to analyze the course data. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between imaging parameters and mPAS score, as well as between imaging parameters and GARBS scales.ResultsIn cricohyoidopexy and cricohyoidoepiglottopexy, the body of the hyoid, the residual arytenoid cartilage, and part of the cricoid dorsal plate could be visualized at the same layer. In Tucker procedure, the first display layer of the free edge of the epiglottis was significantly lower than that before operation. The new glottis is shown at the arytenoid layer, most of which is irregular. When compared with the Tucker group, the cricohyoidopexy and cricohyoidoepiglottopexy groups showed a statistical difference in the postoperative distance between the hyoid bone and cricoid cartilage respectively (p < 0.001). The sagittal distance of the new laryngeal cavity increased in the cricohyoidoepiglottopexy and Tucker groups after surgery when compared with the cricohyoidopexy group (p < 0.01). The voice recovery function of cricohyoidopexy patients was better than that of the other two groups, while the three groups had different degrees of swallowing function recovery.ConclusionAll supracricoid partial laryngectomy procedures showed varying degrees of shortening of the new laryngeal body. The shape of the new laryngeal cavity at the narrowest point was different, but none of them affected respiration and pronunciation. Patients could acquire voice and swallowing recovery in all types of SCPL. It is necessary to evaluate postoperative CT images combined with function analysis to make a better understanding of new larynx structure and function in patients.

Introduction

Laryngeal cancer is the second most common malignancy of the upper respiratory tract [1], which is characterized by a high mortality rate [2]. The treatment of laryngeal cancer depends on tumor location, stage, and patient comorbidities, including surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy as well as biological treatment [3, 4]. Basic function of the larynx should be preserved whenever it is possible. One of the strategies to make good preservation of the larynx is partial laryngectomy. While patients who had undergone total laryngectomy have been reported to give complaints of dysphagia, speech difficulties, and some psychological problems [5, 6], partial laryngectomy could improve the patients' quality of life due to the preservation of both structure and function of the new larynx [7, 8].

Supracricoid partial laryngectomy (SCPL), first proposed by Rieder in 1959 [9] and modified by Piquet in 1974 [10], has become an alternative procedure to achieve the function preservation over the decades. While preserving at least one arytenoid, this technique consists of resections of the whole thyroid cartilage with vocal cords from the cricoid to the base of the epiglottis. SCPL can be further divided into two techniques depending on the extent of the tumor. Reconstruction is made by suturing the cricoid to the hyoid bone by cricohyoidoepiglottopexy (CHEP) if the epiglottis is not invaded; otherwise, the epiglottis will be resected by cricohyoidopexy (CHP) [7, 11] (Figure 1).

Schematic diagram of different SCPL procedures. (a) normal, (b) CHP, (c) CHEP, (d) Tucker. A, arytenoid cartilage; C, cricoid cartilage; E, epiglottis; H, hyoid bone; T, thyroid cartilage.

Computerized tomography (CT) scanning of the neck is a common technique to evaluate the infiltration of the tumor and staging of patients with laryngeal cancer. It has been reported that the combination of conventional CT and dual‐energy CT improved the sensitivity in the diagnosis of thyroid cartilage invasion to 89% [12, 13]. Moreover, CT can also be used to follow up postoperative patients to assess their prognosis. To date, there is little research about postoperative CT and function analysis in different SCPL patients; therefore, we aim to describe the CT images and find out the functions of the new larynx of different SCPL procedures.

Materials and Methods

Demographic Data

A retrospective study was carried out in our hospital. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan No. 1 Hospital. Before all experiments, informed consent was obtained from all the patients and their families. Sixteen patients underwent SCPL for treatment of primary laryngeal squamous cell carcinomas during 2016–2019. They were all males with a median age of 59.0 years (range: 47–73 years). Standard postoperative radiotherapy was performed for patients with a single positive lymph node or positive surgical margin in early, advanced, or supraglottic laryngeal carcinomas. All SCPL patients were followed up regularly after surgery. The laryngeal and neck CT plain scan and enhanced images were read and measured clinically at 6 months after surgery, and the changes of new laryngeal imaging were analyzed combined with the results of laryngoscopy at the same time. Inclusion and exclusion criteria of all patients were listed in Supporting Information.

Measurements of Parameters of Larynx CT Images

All patients were scanned with the Siemens Somatom Sensation 64‐slice spiral CT scanner. Patients were placed in the supine position with head tilted back and were asked to perform the scanning under calm breathing. Plain scan and enhanced scan were both performed. The scan plane was parallel to the hard palate, and the scan range was from the top of the nasopharynx to the thoracic entrance.

CT Image Observation and Anatomical Structure Data Measurement of New Larynx

Imaging data of 16 patients with SCPL surgery were collected before and 6 months after surgery. CT images of the larynx were read by three senior otolaryngologists, and records were observed at hyoid (epiglottis) level, neoaryngeal arytenoid and cricoid level, such as the root of the tongue, epiglottis, epiglottic valley, anterior epiglottic space, neoglottic space, aryepiglottic fold, arytenoid cartilage, piriform fossa, and other imaging changes were discussed to form a consistent image diagnosis description. Accordingly, at the level of appropriate laryngeal CT images, the following laryngeal anatomical structure data were analyzed: The preoperative and postoperative distances between the median lower edge of the hyoid bone and the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage were calculated. The postoperative distances between the lower edge of the small horn of the hyoid bone and the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage were calculated (Figure 2a). The shortest distance between the reserved lateral vocal cord and the contralateral medial anterior wall of the larynx was measured using the measuring tool provided by the imaging software for each new laryngeal arytenoid cartilage layer. The thickness of the mucosa on the retained arytenoid was also measured. The glottic area of the new larynx was measured as well (Figure 2b). The thickness of the left and right mucosa at the cricoid layer of the new larynx was also measured (Figure 2c).

Evaluation of Voice Quality

The speech frequency maps of patients with different SCPL surgical procedures after the operation were collected by XION DIVAS 2.7 software. GRBAS scales were also recorded by three senior otolaryngologists, 0 representing normal, 1 representing mild abnormal, 2 representing moderate abnormal, 3 representing severe abnormal. Average scores were adopted finally.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed by Prism 9.0 software. The measured data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Quantitative data were analyzed using the unpaired Student's t test for comparing two groups and two‐way analysis of variance for multiple groups if the data follows a normal distribution. Significance was considered when the p value was less than 0.05. Subsequently, spearman's rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between the thickness of the mucosa on the retained arytenoid and mPAS score, as well as between sagittal distance of the new laryngeal cavity and G grade/B grade.

Results

CT Findings of New Larynx After SCPL

Normal VS CHP Postoperative CT images of the larynx showed that unilateral arytenoid cartilage was preserved in four patients with CHP and hyoid structure occurred at different layers. The epiglottic and the anterior epiglottic space were replaced by low density soft tissue shadows with uniform density (Figure 3 (4b)). The body of the hyoid, most part of the dorsal plate of the cricoid cartilage, and unilateral or bilateral arytenoid cartilage could be visualized at the arytenoid cartilage layer whereas the cave‐like structure of the bilateral piriform fossa disappeared. Smooth and elevated soft tissues were observed in arytenoid cartilage and adjacent upper layers protruding into the laryngeal cavity, with uniform density and no obvious enhancement. Furthermore, the new laryngeal cavity at this layer exhibited gourd‐shaped, irregular oval, or inverted triangle (Figure 3 (4c)). At the cricoid cartilage layer, a thin low‐density soft tissue shadow was observed in front of the laryngeal cavity (Figure 3 (4d)).

Postoperative laryngoscopic images and CT imaging findings. A, arytenoid cartilage; C, cricoid cartilage; E, epiglottis; H, hyoid bone; T, thyroid cartilage.

Normal versus CHEP: The epiglottis showed high‐density arc‐shaped shadow, but in some cases the epiglottis lost median symmetry (especially those with unilateral cricoarytenoid unit preserved) (Figure 3 (3b)). Meanwhile, we found that in some cases, epiglottis displacement caused the disappearance of black shadows on both sides of the anterior epiglottis (epiglottis valley) and linear cords in the middle of the anterior epiglottis (tongue epiglottis median fold). As shown in Figure 3 (3c), an arc‐shaped hypodense anterior epiglottic space was visible behind the hyoid bone. Moreover, the arytenoid cartilage layer of the new larynx retained the cricoarytenoid unit side (the healthy side or the mild side) and still showed a hypodense para‐glottic space. Part of the residual thyroid cartilage plate, arytenoid cartilage, and part of the cricoid dorsal plate could be displayed at cricoid cartilage layer. New laryngeal cavity showed mostly an irregular oval cavity (“Ω” form or irregular triangle) (Figure 3 (3d)).

Normal VS Tucker There was no significant change in the relative position of the hyoid bone of the new larynx after Tucker operation in five cases (Figure 3 (2b)). Asymmetric bilateral black shadow (epiglottis valley), stripe shadow in the median position (median fold of tongue epiglottis) and low density soft tissue shadow in the anterior epiglottis space that was reduced compared with that before operation could still be shown at the epiglottis layer (Figure 3 (2b)). In Figure 3 (2b), the residual thyroid cartilage plate, arytenoid cartilage and a small part of the dorsal plate of cricoid cartilage could be shown at the level of the arytenoid cartilage of the new larynx. New laryngeal cavity exhibited inverted “L” shape, trapezoid shape, and “O” shape.

Discussion

SCPL involves resection of part of the laryngeal structures from the cricoid cartilage to the interhyoid bone, including thyroid cartilage and paraglottic space, vocal cord, laryngeal ventricle, ventricular band, and anterior commissure. At least one cricoarytenoid unit should be preserved to ensure postoperative pronunciation and swallowing function. Postoperative CT images can show the structural changes of the larynx with different reconstruction methods, including typical fixed laryngeal structural changes and changes in the morphology and contrast of the laryngeal soft tissue [15]. Therefore, the analysis of postoperative CT images of different SCPL procedures can reveal the regular imaging manifestations of the new laryngeal frame and cavity structure.

We found that there were different degrees of downward displacement of supraglottic structures after operation. In CHP and CHEP, the hyoid body, residual arytenoid cartilage, and part of cricoid dorsal plate could be displayed on the same plane. The first visualization level of free margin of epiglottis in Tucker procedure was significantly lower than that before operation. In the postoperative CT images, we also observed that in the CHP and CHEP operations, part of the hyoid structure could be shown at multiple axial levels, manifested as the hyoid tilted forward and shifted forward and down, most of which was still in neutral position with bilateral symmetry. However, there was one case of asymmetric change of the hyoid bone after CHP, which might be due to the large number of lesions resected and the preservation of the thyroid cartilage plate, resulting in the deflection of the heavy hyoid bone during the suture of hyoid bone and cricoid cartilage. Whether different degrees of postoperative hyoid deflection affect swallowing and phonation is not completely clear and still needs further research. Postoperative CT images showed that the epiglottis valley and the anterior epiglottis space disappeared after unilateral arytenoid cartilage resection by CHEP, and the position of the epiglottis changed, often with deflection and displacement. In the Tucker procedure, the epiglottis was also moved downward, but the displacement was relatively mild, and the structure of the epiglottis valley was still recognizable and asymmetrical.

In our study, all postoperative CT images of SCPL showed at least one preserved thyroid cartilage and inferior horn. The recurrent laryngeal nerve traverses between the cricoid cartilage and inferior horn of thyroid cartilage, and must be protected while preserving the bone structure to make sure that the voice and function of the periarytenoid sphincter (lateral cricoarytenoid muscle and posterior cricoarytenoid muscle) can be preserved. At the layer of cricoid cartilage, almost all SCPL procedures showed that the soft tissue density shadow was slightly thickened at the medial side of the anterior arch of cricoid cartilage, which might be the key suture site for laryngeal fixation. When suturing the cricoid cartilage arch with epiglottis and tongue base, the suture site should be as close as possible to the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage arch. If the suture site is too low, the tongue base or epiglottis root will move back behind the cricoid cartilage arch, which may cause laryngeal stenosis [16].

By measuring the postoperative distance between the hyoid bone and the cricoid cartilage in CT images, we found that there was a significant difference between Tucker and CHP or CHEP respectively (p < 0.05), and there was no significant difference between CHP and CHEP (p > 0.05). These results suggested that the distance between the hyoid bone and the cricoid cartilage might be related to the surgical procedure, the extent of surgical resection, and the method of surgical suture. In CHP, the epiglottis and thyroid cartilage were removed, the hyoid bone and cricoid cartilage were closed and sutured, then the hyoid bone was pulled down so the distance was the shortest after operation. In CHEP, the epiglottis, cricoid cartilage, and hyoid bone were sutured; although the hyoid bone was also displaced downward, the distance was slightly smaller than that in CHP. However, the resection range of the Tucker procedure was the smallest, so the postoperative hyoid bone migration distance was the least. N. Bily‐Toueg et al. [17]. measured the average distance between the hyoid bone and the cricoid cartilage in 18 patients after SCPL, and their study suggested that the distance was 11 mm. They believed that when the distance between the hyoid bone and the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage was greater than 30 mm, the patient would have postoperative dysphagia. Our study found that the average distance between the hyoid bone and the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage was 21.45 mm. However, the postoperative distance of individual patients was greater than 30 mm, and they did not show swallowing discomfort, possibly because the patients themselves were tall and thin, and the laryngeal body structure was longer; thus, the postoperative closure suture was still longer than the average.

There was a significant difference in the distance between the inferior horn of the hyoid and cricoid cartilage on the reserved side (mild lesion side) and the affected side (severe lesion side) after surgery in our study, especially in the Tucker group when compared with the CHP and CHEP groups, which suggested that more lesions were removed and the severe lesion side of the larynx was pulled down and displaced when the hyoid bone or epiglottis was sutured.

The new glottic structure formed after operation is the structural basis of pronunciation. We found different degrees of morphological changes in the arytenoid cartilage of the new larynx after SCPL through CT images, such as gourd shape, “Ω” shape, figure‐of‐eight shape, “L” shape, trapezoidal shape, and “O” shape. In all patients who had one arytenoid cartilage removed, a slightly lower density uniform smooth soft tissue on the retained arytenoid cartilage side protruded into the laryngeal cavity above the new arytenoid cartilage layer. This structure might be a reconstructed mucosal flap or a compensation for postoperative arytenoid cartilage hyperplasia. Although the imaging morphologies vary, preliminary summaries of normal postoperative imaging manifestations can be derived from known different normal imaging patterns, thereby distinguishing them from those indicative of tumor recurrence. Meanwhile, all these postlaryngectomy patients retained voice and swallowing functions. Our collected data and imaging findings may be progressively applied in clinical practice in the future. For instance, a follow‐up CT scan shortly after surgery could provide an initial assessment of the patient's long‐term voice and swallowing function.

Although the shape of the new glottis varies after SCPL, our previous studies have confirmed that this does not affect the breathing and articulation function of the new larynx. We found that there was a significant difference in the sagittal distance of the new glottic cavity between CHP and CHEP/Tucker. CHP had the shortest sagittal distance of the new glottic cavity. According to the GRBAS score of the postoperative hoarseness evaluation system, we found that compared with the CHEP/Tucker procedure, it is possible that the smaller sample size in other surgical groups limited our observations, as we only identified a correlation between the thickness of the mucosa on the retained arytenoid and the mPAS score in the CHEP group. Similarly, a correlation with the GRBAS scales was observed exclusively in the CHEP group. CHP procedure with unilateral arytenoid cartilage preservation had the best postoperative articulation. Similarly, Miyamaru also suggested that CHP had the best postoperative articulation [18]. This may be due to the short sagittal distance of CHP so that it is easier to form an effective vibratory sound source in the mucosa during phonation of the new larynx after CHP when compared with CHEP and Tucker procedures. Moreover, in CHP, especially for those with unilateral arytenoid cartilage preservation, can form a compensatory movement across the midline, thus moving forward and contacting the tongue base tissue moving backward, creating an effective pressure difference and regular mucosal vibration above and below the neoglottis. However, for patients undergoing the CHEP and Tucker surgeries that preserve the epiglottis, due to the support of the epiglottic cartilage after the operation, the epiglottic mucosa has poor vocal deformation ability and incomplete closure of the new larynx. As a result, a greater pressure difference between the upper and lower glottis is required, which can only produce ineffective mucosal vibrations and lead to poor postoperative pronunciation. Sometimes, the breath intensity, hoarseness, and fatigue of patients after SCPL surgery may reduce the quality of verbal communication and affect their social and psychological confidence. Therefore, some scales such as EORTC QLQ C30 and H&N 35 should be included in subsequent studies to comprehensively assess the postoperative quality of life of patients.

The recovery of swallowing function in patients with SCPL is affected by many factors, such as epiglottis preservation, arytenoid cartilage preservation, superior laryngeal nerve, and recurrent laryngeal nerve protection. The decannulation time of nasogastric tube and tracheal tube in patients after SCPL in our department was similar to the reported time previously, and there was no difficulty in decannulation after operation. We also observed that mPAS was higher in the CHP group than that in the CHEP and Tucker groups, mainly because patients who had undergone CHP lost the epiglottis, therefore resulting in swallowing difficulty when compared with the other two groups. Nevertheless, there was no significant difference in mPAS among the three groups. Michele et al. [19] also found that patients with CHP caused a delay in the recovery of swallowing, but according to their univariate analysis, types of surgery was lower than the degree of tumor stage. The closer the throat is to the base of the tongue, the less chance the patient has of aspiration after surgery. We found the distance between the hyoid and cricoid cartilage in CHP was higher than that in CHEP (Figure 4), which was in accordance with results of swallowing function in Figure 9, although sample data needed to be increased to make a significant difference.

Limits of the Study

This study shows several limits. First, the sample size of the study is small; future studies should include large sample data from multiple centers. Second, a longer follow‐up time should be required to confirm the present results. Third, we observed one death during the follow‐up period; the quality of life in these patients should be evaluated in the future.

Conclusion

Through the postoperative imaging analysis of SCPL, different surgical methods of SCPL can be distinguished, and the imaging structural changes of the new larynx of patients after surgery can be understood, which is helpful to find out whether there is tumor recurrence after surgery and give further guidance about the surgical operation. At the same time, patients can be instructed to perform effective voice and swallowing function reconstruction in the future via imaging and functional analysis of different SCPL.
