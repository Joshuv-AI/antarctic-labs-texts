Copolymers of Poly(Butylene Trans‐1,4‐Cyclohexanedicarboxylate)/Pripol as New Biomaterial Platform for Small Diameter Vascular Graft
Authors: Edoardo Bondi, Gabriele Obino, Giulia Guidotti, Alberto Sensini, Martijn van Griensven, Antonio Junior Lepedda, Nadia Lotti, Lorenzo Moroni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAutologous grafts remain the clinical gold standard for vascular reconstruction; however, their use is limited by donor site morbidity, poor availability, and long‐term failure. Synthetic alternatives, while effective in large‐caliber vessels, fail in small‐diameter applications (<6 mm) due to thrombosis, intimal hyperplasia, and biomechanical mismatch. In this context, tissue‐engineered vascular grafts (TEVGs) emerge as a solution, requiring biomaterials that closely replicate the structural, mechanical, and hemocompatible properties of native vessels. Aliphatic polyesters such as polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid, and poly(ε‐caprolactone) are extensively studied but show poor endothelialization and mechanical deficiency. In contrast, poly(butylene trans‐1,4‐cyclohexanedicarboxylate) (PBCE) attracts interest for its biocompatibility, thermal stability, and processability. Its copolymerization with Pripol 1009, a commercial fatty diacid, enables modulation of mechanical properties and degradation rate, two of the key parameters for vascular engineering. In this work, electrospun scaffolds based on these copolymers are fabricated in flat and tubular formats and characterized in terms of morphology, mechanical behavior, hemocompatibility, and endothelialization potential. Certain formulations display mechanical properties comparable to native vessels, support endothelialization and smooth muscle cell adhesion, and do not trigger coagulation pathways in in vitro assays. These results identify PBCE/Pripol copolymers as promising candidates for next‐generation TEVGs, bridging the gap between synthetic reliability and biological performance in small‐diameter vascular applications.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for over 17 million deaths annually, a figure projected to rise above 23 million by 2030, and contribute significantly to long‐term disability and healthcare costs, particularly within the European Union [1, 2, 3, 4]. Despite medical advancements, CVDs remain strongly linked to modifiable lifestyle factors, such as poor diet, physical inactivity, and tobacco or alcohol use, which can lead to severe vascular complications including atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and cerebrovascular disorders [5]. Among these, peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects approximately 113 million people over the age of 40, with a prevalence that rises steeply in the elderly population‐up to 14.91% in those aged 80–84 years [6]. PAD primarily involves the progressive narrowing of arteries in the lower limbs and is the leading cause of chronic limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI), a condition with poor clinical outcomes. Approximately one‐third of patients undergo amputation, and one in four dies as a result [7]. Although revascularization techniques such as angioplasty or atherectomy are commonly used, surgical bypass remains the treatment of choice in cases with long, calcified, or fully occluded lesions [8]. Autologous grafts are the gold standard due to their superior mechanical and immunological properties [5], but their use is constrained by limited availability and donor site morbidity. In many cases, especially in patients with advanced disease, suitable autologous or homologous grafts are not available [9, 10]. Artificial vascular grafts are routinely employed for large‐diameter bypasses; however, their use in small‐diameter (d < 6 mm) applications remains problematic due to high failure rates associated with thrombosis, infection, intimal hyperplasia, calcification, and aneurysm formation [11, 12, 13]. Regenerative medicine offers promising strategies to overcome these limitations, particularly through the development of bioactive grafts capable of integrating with and remodelling the host vasculature. Biomaterials intended for implantation must closely mimic the native environment to ensure mechanical, functional, and biological compatibility [14], with additional requirements of hemocompatibility, non‐immunogenicity, and bioactivity in vascular applications [15]. Polyesters are widely used due to their tunable physico‐mechanical properties, biocompatibility, ease of processing, and capacity for surface modification or biomolecule immobilization. Aliphatic polyesters like polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), poly(ε‐caprolactone) (PCL) and their copolymers, have been widely investigated and used for biomedical purposes [16], including small‐diameter vascular grafts. However, despite their promise, these materials have shown limited long‐term patency and various shortcomings when used in vascular graft applications. In particular, aliphatic polyester‐based vascular grafts often trigger intimal hyperplasia and calcification due to mechanical mismatch and poor endothelialization [17, 18, 19]. Studies show that tailoring polymer composition and structure can reduce these complications and improve long‐term graft performance [12]. Among aliphatic polyesters, Poly(butylene trans‐1,4‐cyclohexanedicarboxylate) (PBCE) has garnered growing attention in recent years due to its demonstrated biocompatibility, favorable thermal stability, and ease of processing [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Furthermore, the copolymerization of PBCE with Pripol 1009 (a commercial fatty diacid used also for innovative applications [25, 26, 27, 28]) has already proven to be an effective strategy to tailor its physico‐chemical and mechanical properties, overcoming the intrinsic rigidity and crystallinity of the homopolymer. The resulting copolymers, in form of flat films, exhibited improved flexibility, thermal stability, and hemocompatibility, making them potential candidates for applications in small‐diameter vascular tissue engineering [29]. However, alternative processing approaches, which have never been tested before, may result in completely different mechanical properties, surface wettability and biological response, all features of primary importance when tissue engineering applications are considered.

Indeed, to translate these material advantages into functional small‐diameter grafts, polymers must not only exhibit desirable mechanical and biological properties but also be amenable to advanced fabrication techniques. Electrospinning stands out in this context as a powerful and versatile method for producing fibrous scaffolds that closely mimic the architecture of the native extracellular matrix. These scaffolds feature a high surface‐to‐volume ratio and interconnected porosity, providing numerous sites for cellular attachment. This environment supports cell adhesion, infiltration, and remodelling, key requirements for the successful development of tissue‐engineered vascular grafts designed for small‐diameter applications [30, 31, 32, 33]. Its versatility also allows for precise control over structural and mechanical properties, making it ideal for small‐diameter graft production [34, 35]. Hence, the present study focuses on further developing P(BCExBPripoly) copolymers amenable for electrospinning, leading to the fabrication of both planar and tubular scaffolds. These constructs were subsequently subjected to comprehensive morphological, mechanical and biological evaluations to assess their potential as small‐diameter vascular grafts.

Results and Discussion

Scaffolds Preparation and Characterization

PBCE, as well as P(BCE85BPripol15) and P(BCE75BPripol25) were processed into electrospun flat scaffolds and tubular scaffolds, in order to obtain porous surfaces and devices suitable for the formation of a mature endothelium. Conversely, the copolymer P(BCE65BPripol35), which is the less crystalline material among the family, needed to be mixed with the homopolymer PBCE in a 85/15 weight amount to enable electrospinning.

All the polymers were successfully electrospun, as can be seen from SEM images (Figure S1 for the flat scaffolds; Figure 1 for the tubular scaffolds). All the mats were defect‐free, with smooth fibers and no agglomerates. In addition, representative photographs and stereomicroscope images of the tubular scaffolds with an internal diameter of 4 mm are reported in Figure S2, further confirming the good morphological quality and structural integrity of the constructs. In the case of the planar scaffolds, they were characterized by a substantial random orientation of the fibers (Figure S1B). Indeed, according to the directionality analysis, an angle of 0° means that the nanofibers are perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the sample, while an angle of 90° means that the nanofibers are aligned with the longitudinal axis of the sample: since the distribution of these angles is quite homogeneous for all the sample investigated, it could be concluded that there was no preferred orientation of the fibers inside the mats. Fiber diameters varied from 0.413 ± 0.070 µm for PBCE homopolymer to 1.506 ± 0.327 µm for P(BCE75BPripol25) copolymer, increasing with the amount of BPripol co‐unit. As to the fibers obtained from the blend P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15, they had an average diameter of 1.253 ± 0.608 µm. Of note, these values are close to the dimension of collagen fibrils (up to 0.5 µm) found in small‐diameter vessels [36] and of vascular smooth‐muscle cells diameter [37]. At the same time, surface porosity, as well as the apparent size of surface pores, decreased by increasing the amount of Pripol co‐unit, i.e. as the average fiber diameter increased (Table S1).

In the case of tubular scaffolds, analyses were conducted both internally (luminal surface, the one in contact with blood flow) and externally. As illustrated in Figure 2, which displays images of both internal and external surfaces of the scaffolds along with their average diameters, the fiber size remained consistent from the inner to the outer surface. Additionally, the average fiber diameters are comparable to those measured for planar scaffolds. Fibers were predominantly randomly oriented. However, in the case of the PBCE homopolymer (Figure 2A), a slight preferential alignment of the fibers of the internal layer was observed, following the hypothetical direction of blood flow, though this effect was much less evident on the external surface. For P(BCE85BPripol15), a slight preferential alignment of the internal fibers could be noticed, while externally the fibers tended to align perpendicularly to the flow, although overall fiber orientation remained largely random (Figure 2B). Similarly, in both P(BCE75BPripol25) and the blend (Figures 2C and D, respectively), a slight preferential alignment in the direction of blood flow was observed both internally and externally. It is important to underline that an axial orientation of the fibers may favor an early orientation of the endothelial cells in the direction of blood flow [33], which is the same phenomenon that naturally occurs in blood vessels [38]. The porosity and size of the pores on the surface remain consistent with what has been observed in planar scaffolds (Table S1). Cross‐sectional analysis performed by stereomicroscopy demonstrated a remarkably uniform wall thickness along the tubular scaffolds (Figure S2), as previously reported above. The consistency of wall thickness values, reported in Table S1, highlights the reproducibility and reliability of the fabrication process. Notably, the measured wall thicknesses fall within the range reported for human blood vessels [39], supporting the potential suitability of these scaffolds for vascular applications.

Thermal Characterization

While our previous work [29] focused on the synthesis and characterization of some of these polyesters in the form of films, their processing into electrospun fibrous scaffolds represents a critical step toward vascular applications. Electrospinning involves polymer dissolution, exposure to high electric fields, and rapid solvent evaporation, which may affect thermal transitions and mechanical performance. Therefore, it cannot be assumed that the properties observed in films are preserved upon scaffold fabrication. In order to evaluate the thermal stability of the materials after processing, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were carried out under inert atmosphere. The obtained thermograms are shown in Figure 3A while the relative thermal data (Tid and Tmax) are listed in Table 1. As previously reported [29] PBCE homopolymer was characterized by a good thermal stability (Tid = 386°C and Tmax = 410°C) which further improved by copolymerization with Pripol. In fact, both copolymers had a Tid higher than the one of PBCE, that progressively increased with the amount of Pripol moieties from 388°C for P(BCE85BPripol15) to 399°C for P(BCE75BPripol25). In the case of the blend, Tid was higher than the one of the homopolymer and comparable to the one of P(BCE85BPripol15). Similarly, the Tmax trend is a function of the amount of Pripol inserted and is also maintained in the blend. Lastly, in all cases the weight loss reached 100% (Figure 3A). Thus, processing into fibrous scaffolds did not alter the thermal stability of all the materials investigated.

The scaffolds were also analysed by Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), as reported in Figure 3B, where the I scan curves are shown, while the relevant data are shown in Table 1. In Figure S3 the II DSC scan traces are also reported and in Table S2 the corresponding thermal data are listed. All the materials show the typical behaviour of semicrystalline materials, as previously reported [29]. In particular, in the case of PBCE and of P(BCE85BPripol15) it was not possible to determine the Tg, due to the presence of a high amount of crystalline phase. In the case of P(BCE75BPripol25), Tg was observable at ‐36°C. In the blend, the Tg jump located at −41°C was attributable to P(BCE65BPripol35), while the one related to PBCE was not observable.

At the same time, copolymerisation led to a decrease in the melting temperature (from 170°C for PBCE to 116°C for P(BCE75BPripol25)) and a parallel decrease in the melting enthalpy (from 41 Jg−1 for PBCE to 16 Jg−1 for P(BCE75BPripol25)), indicating that the introduction of Pripol 1009 is able to reduce the crystallinity of the final materials, similar to what previously observed for compression moulded films [29]. Similarly, the presence of two endothermic peaks associated to the melting of both P(BCE65BPripol35) (101°C) and PBCE (167°C) could be observed in the blend.

A further endothermic peak could be observed in all materials, located at 57°C in the case of PBCE and around 50°C in the case of the other samples, which may be attributed to the presence of an ordered 2D phase, resulting either from the peculiar conformation of the aliphatic cyclohexane ring [40, 41, 42], or to annealing phenomena occurring during the electrospinning process. The rapid cooling from the melt, performed after the I scan, partially inhibited crystallization and allowed the Tg to be observed. Indeed, a Tg could be observed in PBCE at 14°C and in P(BCE85BPripol15) at −30°C, albeit with a very small Δcp (0.062 and 0.142 Jg−1°C−1, respectively). In the other materials, the values of Tg already observed in the I scan were confirmed. At the same time, the melting peaks remained visible, indicating that quenching was not effective.

Surface Wettability Evaluation

The surface wettability of all the materials has been evaluated by static water contact angle (WCA) measurements (Figure 4). All the materials under investigation turned out to be hydrophobic, with WCA values higher than 90°. This result can be attributed to the peculiar surface morphology: the highly porous scaffold surfaces, unlike the continuous films, enhance the materials’ hydrophobic character, as previously reported for other polyesters [43], according to the Wezel's equation [44]. Moreover, PBCE showed the highest WCA value (119 ± 6°) and, as a general trend, WCA values decreased with the amount of the co‐unit, reaching a value of 104 ± 4° for P(BCE75BPripol25). As already mentioned [29], this trend can be due to two counteracting effects. On one side, the decrease in crystallinity is responsible for an increased hydrophilicity, while the presence of long PE‐like segments inside Pripol can induce an increase in the contact angle values. According to the obtained results, it seems that for the scaffolds the effect of the lower crystallinity is prevailing. As to the blend, the value of WCA (118 ± 6°) is in line with the one of the homopolymer, although this sample has a much lower crystallinity. This peculiar behavior can be ascribed to the presence of a consistent amount of Pripol 1009 inside P(BCE65BPripol35) copolymer, together with the presence of 15 wt% of neat PBCE inside the blend. Additionally, the increase in the average diameter of the fibers from PBCE to P(BCE75BPripol25) (Figure S1) can be responsible for a decrease in the WCA values, as already observed in previous studies [45].

Mechanical Characterization

In order to evaluate the mechanical properties of the tubular scaffolds produced, mechanical tests were carried out both on the tubular scaffolds and on the ones cut and stretched in the direction of blood flow. Short‐term immersion in phosphate buffer saline solution (PBS) was intentionally selected to evaluate the mechanical response of the electrospun scaffolds under hydrated conditions. The obtained circumferential and longitudinal stress‐strain curves and load‐strain curves are shown in Figure 5.

Representative circumferential (A), longitudinal (B) stress‐strain curves, and circumferential (C), longitudinal (D) load‐strain curves of tubular scaffolds of PBCE, P(BCExBPripoly) copolymers and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend, each with an enlargement of the initial part of the curve.

Regarding the circumferential tests, the elastic moduli (Table S3; Figure 6A) show a decrease as a function of the composition, from 4 ± 2 MPa of PBCE to 0.7 ± 0.2 MPa for P(BCE75BPripol25), while the blend exhibited an elastic modulus comparable to that of the PBCE. It must be underlined that these values are comparable to those measured for the saphenous vein [39] and for the internal mammary artery [46]. A similar trend was observed with regard to the longitudinal elastic moduli. By comparing the values of elastic modulus keeping the composition fixed, it can be noticed that in all the cases the longitudinal modules are higher than the circumferential ones. This difference may be attributed either to the different configuration of the analysis system or the slight difference in the alignment of the fibers, as observed by SEM analysis (Figures 1 and 2).

Comparison of the mechanical data of elastic modulus (A), stress at failure (B), strain at failure (C), force at failure (D), work at failure (E), stress at yield (F), strain at yield (G), force at yield (H) and work at yield (I) for both circumferential and longitudinal tests carried out on tubular scaffolds of PBCE, P(BCExBPripoly) copolymers and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend. The statistical significance of differences is indicated (* p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, *** p ≤ 0.001, **** p ≤ 0.0001).

If the values of circumferential stress at failure (σF) are considered (Figure 6B; Table S3), both PBCE and the copolymer richest in BCE co‐unit presented a σF of 2.3 ± 0.4 MPa, which decreased to 0.9 ± 0.2 MPa for P(BCE75BPripol25). The blend is characterized by the highest σF (4.8 ± 0.9 MPa), probably due to a synergistic effect provided by the rigid and brittle homopolymer and the more elastomeric copolymer (P(BCE65BPripol35)). The trend is different in the longitudinal case (Table S4), where PBCE presented the lowest value (4 ± 2 MPa) while the copolymers and the blend showed higher and comparable values, ranging between 5.9 ± 0.1 and 7 ± 1 MPa. It is possible that the fibers’ dimension plays a key role in determining this parameter, as the homopolymer shows the smallest fibers, which progressively increase for the other compositions. Notably, the blend has a circumferential σF value comparable with that of the saphenous vein [46] and both circumferential and longitudinal values comparable with those of the internal mammary artery [46].

The circumferential failure strain (εF, Figure 6C) is in all cases above 100%, increasing with the amount of co‐unit, from 145 ± 36% for PBCE to 336 ± 18% for P(BCE75BPripol25). The value of εF measured for the blend was 194 ± 13%, which is lower than the one expected for P(BCE65BPripol35) copolymer. However, it is justified by the presence of 15 wt% of less flexible PBCE and by the fact that the two polymers are not chemically bonded but only physically mixed. In the case of the longitudinal failure strain, a similar trend was observed, except for the blend, which was the sample showing the highest elongation. In this latter case, it seems that the flexibilizing effect imparted by P(BCE65BPripol35) prevails, regardless of the presence of PBCE inside the blend. It can also be hypothesized that the higher diameter of the fibers obtained for this sample (Figure 1), may allow for a higher failure strain. In addition, the εF values obtained for all the tested materials were higher than those for the internal mammary artery [46]. Compared with the saphenous vein, all materials show comparable values circumferentially, while the values are higher longitudinally [46].

As far as the force to failure (Figure 6D) is concerned, in the circumferential tests, a comparable force was observed in all the cases (around 5.8 ± 0.7 N), apart from the blend, which showed the highest value (10 ± 3 N). Longitudinally, there was an increase with the amount of BPripol co‐unit, whereas the blend showed a value comparable with that of P(BCE85BPripol15). A very similar trend was observed for work at failure (Figure 6E), except for the blend longitudinally tested, which showed the highest value among the family.

With regard to stress (Figure 6F) and force (Figure 6H) at yield, comparable values were obtained circumferentially, except for P(BCE75BPripol25). Instead, the same elongation at yield (Figure 6G) was observed for all materials under investigation. With regard to work at yield (Figure 6I), P(BCE75BPripol25) showed the lowest value among all the materials.

In the case of the longitudinal tests, the yield stress was of about 1.8 ± 0.2 MPa for the homopolymer and P(BCE85BPripol15), decreasing for P(BCE75BPripol25) and the blend. The force at yield did not completely follow the same trend, as PBCE, P(BCE85BPripol15) and P(BCE75BPripol25) copolymers presented a value of approximately 6 N, with the blend a lower one (4.8 ± 0.3 N). The yield strength is in the range 1.3 ‐ 2.1 MPa, highest for P(BCE85BPripol15) copolymers. In terms of work at yield, it can be seen that the two copolymers have the highest value, compared to PBCE and the blend.

Lastly, burst Pressure (BP, Figure S4) values were also evaluated on the tubular scaffolds. PBCE and P(BCE85BPripol15) showed similar values around 1000 mmHg (Table S3), which became lower for P(BCE75BPripol25) to 638 ± 69 mmHg. Conversely, the blend showed the highest BP, close to 2000 mmHg. This behavior could be attributed once again to the peculiar composition of this material.

Anyway, all the values obtained are particularly interesting, as they are in line with those of the saphenous vein (1390‐3900 mmHg) [46] and internal mammary artery (1599 – 3900 mmHg) [46].

HUVECs Culture on Scaffolds: Metabolic Activity and Proliferation

HUVECs cultured on four distinct polymeric scaffolds exhibited a steady increase in metabolic activity from day 1 to day 21, with variations observed depending on the scaffold material (Figure 7A). In the early stages (days 1 and 7), endothelial cell colonization was similar across all samples, with a low percentage of cells initially attaching to the fibers (Figure 7B). Cell metabolic activity increased over this period, indicating cellular adaptation and early proliferation. By day 14, the number of cells on scaffolds fabricated from PBCE, P(BCE75BPripol25), and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 began to proliferate, whereas no significant change in cell density was observed on scaffolds produced from P(BCE85BPripol15). By day 21, all scaffold types exhibited peak metabolic activity and cellular coverage, although differences in total cell numbers were not statistically significant. Notably, immunofluorescence staining revealed that scaffolds composed of P(BCE75BPripol25) and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 supported the formation of a near‐confluent endothelial monolayer. Cells on these scaffolds displayed a more elongated morphology and increased expression of VE‐cadherin, indicative of enhanced maturation and endothelial integrity (Figure 8). As all assays were conducted on fully random‐oriented planar scaffolds, the present data do not permit inferences about orientation‐dependent HUVEC responses. Immunostaining across time‐points further confirmed that initial cell adhesion was limited on all electrospun polymers at day 1. However, by day 21, cells had progressively colonized the scaffold surfaces. Among all tested materials, the P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 scaffold induced the most efficient and rapid cellular coverage, with substantial scaffold colonization already evident by day 14. This suggests that electrospun scaffolds fabricated with P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 offer a more favorable environment for endothelialization compared to the other formulations.

Smooth Muscle Cell Adhesion, Density, and Phenotype on Scaffolds

Coronary artery smooth muscle cells (CASMCs) cultured on P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend scaffolds exhibited effective adhesion and sustained expression of α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA), as demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining for nuclei (DAPI) and α‐SMA after 1, 7, and 14 days of culture (Figure 9). At day 1, CASMCs adhered efficiently to all scaffold compositions, displaying a well‐spread morphology with elongated cytoskeletal features and organized α‐SMA‐positive stress fibers, indicative of favorable initial cell–material interactions and good cytocompatibility. Despite comparable qualitative adhesion and morphology, quantitative analysis of nuclear density revealed composition‐dependent differences in cell colonization (Figure S5). Specifically, PBCE and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend scaffolds supported significantly higher cell densities compared to the P(BCE75BPripol25) copolymer at early time points, suggesting a more permissive surface for initial smooth muscle cell attachment. After 7 days of culture, CASMCs formed an organized α‐SMA‐positive cytoskeletal network across all materials, indicating continued cell survival and cytoskeletal maturation. However, differences in cell density persisted, with the blend maintaining the highest number of nuclei per unit area, while P(BCE75BPripol25) consistently exhibited reduced cellular coverage. At day 14, the blend showed improved maintenance of cell density compared to PBCE alone, which displayed a marked reduction in nuclei/mm2 (Figure S6). Importantly, α‐SMA expression remained detectable and well organized in all groups throughout the culture period, suggesting preservation of the smooth muscle cell phenotype irrespective of polymer composition. The sustained expression and organization of α‐SMA are particularly relevant in the context of vascular tissue engineering, as α‐SMA is a hallmark of the contractile smooth muscle cell phenotype required for vessel tone regulation and structural integrity [47]. Maintenance of this phenotype in vitro has been associated with improved functional performance of engineered vascular grafts and a reduced tendency toward pathological phenotypic switching to a synthetic state characterized by excessive proliferation and altered extracellular matrix deposition [48]. Since experiments employed fully random oriented planar scaffolds, potential orientation dependent‐ effects on smooth muscle cell adhesion, density, and phenotype remain undetermined.

Overall, while all PBCE‐based scaffolds supported CASMC adhesion and phenotypic stability, the observed differences in cell density highlight the role of blend composition in modulating smooth muscle cell colonization and long‐term cellular retention, with P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend emerging as the most supportive formulation over extended culture periods.

Coagulation Assays

To assess potential alterations in the coagulation profile induced by scaffold surfaces, we performed activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT, Figure 10A) and prothrombin time (PT; Figure 10B) assays, which evaluate the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of the coagulation cascade, respectively. No significant differences were observed between plasma incubated with the scaffolds and the control plasma (incubated in wells without scaffolds) across all materials tested, as evidenced by both aPTT and PT measurements (Figure 10). These findings suggest that exposure to the scaffold surfaces does not trigger activation of the coagulation cascade. The lack of significant alterations in aPTT and PT times implies that both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways remain unaffected, thereby reducing the likelihood of scaffold‐induced thrombotic events. This outcome satisfies one of the key biocompatibility criteria for vascular graft applications.

Hemolysis Induced by the Materials

Hemolysis is a key parameter for the preliminary evaluation of hemocompatibility, as excessive red blood cell (RBC) damage may indicate potential cytotoxic or membrane‐disruptive effects of blood‐contacting biomaterials. The hemolytic activity of PBCE, P(BCE85BPripol15), P(BCE75BPripol25), and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend scaffolds were assessed and compared with negative (NaCl) and positive (0.1% Triton X‐100) controls. As shown in Figure 11 all tested materials exhibited very low hemolysis values, ranging from approximately 0.25% to 0.40%, which were comparable to the negative control and markedly lower than the positive control, which induced complete erythrocyte lysis. No statistically significant differences were observed among the different polymer compositions, indicating that variations in Pripol content did not adversely affect red blood cell integrity. According to ASTM F756 [49] and ISO 10993–4 [50] standards, materials inducing hemoglobin release below 2% are classified as non‐hemolytic. All the materials analyzed therefore meet the requirements for hemocompatible blood‐contacting materials.

Platelet Adhesion and Clot Formation

Platelet adhesion assays performed under static conditions revealed a high degree of platelet attachment, aggregation, and clot formation on all PBCE‐based scaffolds (Figure 12). A comparable platelet response was observed on poly(ε‐caprolactone) (PCL) control samples, confirming the similar thrombogenic behavior of these materials in their unmodified state [33]. In contrast, expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) exhibited minimal platelet adhesion, consistent with its well‐established thromboresistant properties and validating the sensitivity of the assay [51].

The pronounced platelet adhesion observed on PBCE‐based scaffolds is consistent with their classification as aliphatic biodegradable polyesters, a polymer family that includes PCL and is known to promote nonspecific adsorption of plasma proteins such as fibrinogen [52]. Protein adsorption onto hydrophobic polymer surfaces can induce conformational rearrangements that expose platelet‐binding domains, thereby triggering platelet adhesion and activation [53]. In line with this mechanism, no qualitative differences in platelet adhesion were observed among the different PBCE‐based compositions, indicating that surface chemistry rather than bulk copolymer composition governs the platelet‐material interaction under static conditions. To further contextualize these findings, Dacron (poly(ethylene terephthalate), PET), a polymer widely used in clinically approved vascular grafts, was also evaluated using the same assay. Under static incubation, Dacron exhibited detectable platelet adhesion and clot formation, demonstrating that even clinically established materials can induce platelet activation in the absence of physiological flow. This behavior is attributed to prolonged blood–material contact and stable protein adsorption, which are known to overestimate thrombogenicity in static hemocompatibility assays.

This observation also provides insight into the caliber‐dependent performance of synthetic vascular grafts. Materials such as Dacron and ePTFE perform reliably in large‐caliber vessels, where high flow rates and shear stress limit protein residence time and platelet activation [54, 55]. However, their performance deteriorates markedly in small‐diameter applications, where reduced flow promotes protein accumulation, fibrin deposition, and sustained platelet activation, ultimately leading to graft occlusion [53]. This limitation remains a central challenge in the development of small‐caliber vascular grafts.

The PBCE‐based scaffolds presented here may be conceptually distinct from permanent synthetic grafts such as those made of Dacron and ePTFE [56]. Rather than functioning as inert long‐term implants, these materials could instead act as bioresorbable scaffolds designed to support rapid vascular cell colonization and progressive tissue remodeling. Over time, the polymeric matrix may be gradually replaced by newly deposited extracellular matrix, potentially leading to the formation of a living vascular conduit. Within this regenerative paradigm, early platelet interaction might therefore represent a transient and manageable event rather than a determinant of long‐term graft performance. However, additional hydrolytic degradation studies will be required to substantiate this hypothesis and to elucidate the long‐term resorption kinetics of these materials.

Finally, it should be noted that all platelet adhesion experiments were conducted under static conditions, which represent a stringent and conservative assessment of thrombogenicity. The absence of shear stress and prolonged blood‐material contact inherent to static testing can exaggerate platelet adhesion compared to physiological conditions. Therefore, while static assays provide a reproducible and valuable first screening, dynamic flow‐based evaluations, together with polymer coating strategies, such as heparin or sulfated polysaccharides [47], or pre‐endothelialization, will be essential to more accurately predict the hemocompatibility of PBCE‐based scaffolds under clinically relevant conditions.

Conclusion

A series of electrospun scaffolds obtained from random PBCE‐based aliphatic copolyesters and blends, containing Pripol 1009 as co‐unit, have been successfully fabricated and their possible use as small‐diameter vascular grafts has been evaluated. To this aim, the chemical design, as well the processing technique were optimized to modulate the physico‐chemical properties of the starting homopolymer, making it also suitable for applications in contact with blood. Indeed, all the materials were electrospun into planar and tubular scaffolds without detectable morphological defects, exhibiting smooth fibers with diameters comparable to those of native collagen fibrils and vascular smooth muscle cells diameter. Fiber alignment was predominantly random, though slight preferential orientations were observed in some tubular scaffolds, particularly along the luminal surface, in a direction consistent with blood flow. Such features suggest that these scaffolds may provide a favorable structural environment for guiding early endothelial cell organization, thereby supporting their potential for vascular applications.

Thermal characterization confirmed that electrospinning processing did not compromise the thermal stability of the materials, which was even enhanced thanks to copolymerization with Pripol, preserving, at the same time, the intrinsic structural robustness of the systems. Moreover, all scaffolds exhibited a remarkable hydrophobic behavior, which could be tuned as a function of the content of co‐unit and the dimension of the fibers. Mechanical characterization revealed that the tubular scaffolds display elastic moduli. Failure stress/strain and burst pressure values were comparable to those of the saphenous vein and internal mammary artery, the blend showing synergistic enhancements in both strength and compliance, thus underscoring their potential clinical applications. HUVECs progressively colonized all the scaffolds within 21 days, with P(BCE75BPripol25) and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend supporting the fastest and most extensive endothelialization, promoting a near‐confluent monolayer with elongated morphology and enhanced VE‐cadherin expression, indicative of mature and functional endothelium. In addition to endothelialization, coronary artery smooth muscle cells (CASMCs) showed effective adhesion and sustained expression of α‐smooth muscle actin on all PBCE‐based scaffolds, indicating preservation of the contractile phenotype over time. Notably, the P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blend supported higher cell densities and improved long‐term cellular retention compared to the other formulations, highlighting the critical role of blend composition in modulating smooth muscle cell colonization and stability.

Coagulation assays demonstrated that all scaffold surfaces did not alter aPTT or PT times, indicating that neither intrinsic nor extrinsic pathways were activated and confirming that the materials do not interfere with the coagulation cascade, a key requirement for vascular graft biocompatibility. Moreover, all the scaffolds exhibited negligible hemolytic activity, with values far below the limits established by ASTM and ISO standards, confirming their hemocompatibility and potential for vascular graft applications.

Consistent with aliphatic hydrophobic polyesters, PBCE‐based scaffolds showed platelet adhesion comparable to PCL under static conditions due to non‐specific protein adsorption. As with other biodegradable vascular polymers, this underscores the necessity of rapid endothelialization and/or anticoagulant surface functionalization to achieve clinically relevant hemocompatibility.

Overall, the combination of structural integrity, appropriate fiber dimensions, mechanical properties comparable to native small‐diameter vessels, efficient endothelialization and thromboresistant behaviour highlights the suitability of these scaffolds for vascular tissue engineering, in particular for the development of small‐diameter vascular grafts.

Material and Methods

Materials

High molecular weight (> 40,000 g mol−1) Poly(butylene trans‐1,4‐ciclohexanedicarboxylate) (PBCE) and Poly(butylene trans‐1,4‐ciclohexanedicarboxylate/Pripol) (P(BCExBPripoly)) copolymers, where x and y represent the molar percentage between the two co‐units, in particular P(BCE85BPripol15), P(BCE75BPripol25) and P(BCE65BPripol35) were synthesized as previously reported [29].

Scaffolds Preparation

The polymers were dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3‐hexafluoro‐2‐propanol (HFIP, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) in the concentration of 15% w/v and then stirred overnight. The P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE blend was obtained by mixing P(BCE65BPripol35) and PBCE in the ratio of 85/15 w/w and then dissolved in HFIP, maintaining the total polymer concentration of 15% w/v. The solutions were electrospun with a voltage of 20 kV for the homopolymer and copolymers and of 24 kV for the blend, with a working distance of 15 cm. The flow rate was set at 3 mL h−1 in the case of PBCE and copolymers and 1.8 mL h−1 in the latter case. For the preparation of the scaffolds for biological assays a stainless steel 20 cm diameter mandrel was used as collector, with a speed rotation of 50 rpm. For the preparation of tubular grafts, a stainless steel 4 mm diameter mandrel was used as collector with a speed rotation of 150 rpm. In both cases, a stainless steel hypodermic needle with an internal diameter of 0.8 mm was used (Unimed, Switzerland). During the electrospinning of the tubular scaffolds the needle was set to move along the length of the collector with a velocity of 3 cm s−1 to ensure homogeneous deposition. All processes were carried out under controlled conditions, maintaining a temperature of 25°C and a relative humidity of 25%.

Morphological Characterization of Scaffolds

For the characterization of both morphology and size of the fibers, a thin layer of gold coating (Quorum Technologies SC7620, UK) was applied over the samples. Images were captured by using a JSM‐IT200 microscope (Jeol ltd., The Netherlands) at 500× and 2000× magnifications, with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 10 mm. The subsequent image processing and analysis were performed to determine fiber diameter (n = 5 samples × 40 measurements each) by using ImageJ (GNU General Public License). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the scaffolds were also analyzed to assess fibers’ orientation. In particular, the analysis was performed at 2000x magnification (n = 5 samples) and analyzed using the Directionality plugin of ImageJ [57]. For tubular scaffolds, both the inner and the outer surfaces of the scaffolds were analyzed separately at 2000x magnification (n = 5 samples). Surface porosity and surface pore size were quantified using the “Analyse Particles” tool of ImageJ as reported in literature [58] starting from SEM images. Analyses were performed on flat scaffolds and on tubular scaffolds, with inner and outer surfaces considered only for the latter, using at least 5 images per sample. To measure the wall thickness of all the tubular samples a SMZ25 automated stereomicroscope equipped with 1x and 2x objectives (Nikon, Amstelveen, The Netherlands), and a PHOTONIC LED‐Set Ring light was used.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability was evaluated through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), by means of a TGA 4000 Thermogravimetric Analyzer (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA). The measurements were carried out under nitrogen atmosphere (40 mL min−1) by heating weighed samples of about 10 mg from 40 to 800°C, at a rate of 10°C min−1. The temperature of maximum degradation rate, Tmax, was calculated from the TGA curve derivatives as the minimum of the peak, while the temperature of initial degradation, Tid, represents the temperature at which degradation starts.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

To define the characteristic thermal transitions of the materials, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was carried out using a DSC6 instrument (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA), equipped with an intracooler able to reach ‐70°C. Samples of about 8 mg were heated from ‐70°C to Tm + 20°C at 20°C min−1 under nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL min−1) then rapidly cooled to ‐70°C at 100°C/min; afterward, a second heating step was applied from −70°C to Tm + 20°C at 20°C min−1. Glass transition temperature (Tg) was taken at half‐height of the glass‐to‐rubber transition step, while the corresponding heat associated (ΔCp) was calculated from the step height; melting temperature (Tm) was taken as the maximum of the endothermic melting peak, while the associated enthalpy (ΔHm) was calculated from the area under the peak.

Water Contact Angle (WCA)

Static water contact angle (WCA) measurements were performed on flat scaffold surfaces using a DSA30S instrument (Krüss, Hamburg, Germany), equipped with Drop Shape Analysis software. Each sample was previously washed in a 70% v/v ethanol solution and then dried off overnight at room temperature. The profile images of deionized water drops (4 µL each, 100 µL min−1) were acquired after 1 s from the deposition, and the WCA values were measured by the software. At least 10 drops were analyzed for each scaffold, and WCA values were provided as the mean value ± standard deviation.

Mechanical Characterization—Circumferential and Longitudinal Tensile Test and Burst Pressure

The mechanical testing of the tubular samples was performed with the electromechanical testing machine Electroforce 3200 (TA Instruments, New Castle, USA) equipped with a ±45 N dynamic cell (TA Instruments, New Castle, USA). All the specimens were immersed in PBS for 2 min before the test. In particular, the wall thickness of the tubular scaffolds was measured by acquiring an image for each sample, which was then analyzed using ImageJ software, performing ten measurements. The circumferential tensile tests were conducted at a strain rate of 25% s−1 as indicated in ISO 7198:2016 [59]. A custom‐made system was employed, with a 1.45 mm diameter pin (Figure S7A). The stress (σ) was calculated as follows: σ=FA where F is the force recorded and A the area, this last defined as: A=2wt where w is the width and t the thickness of the tested tubular scaffold.

The burst pressure (BP) was also evaluated, using the following definition, as previously reported in the literature [39, 60]: BP=FL0Dt where L0 is the gauge length and Dt is the internal diameter at each time point.

The tubular scaffolds were also cut longitudinally and then tested along the direction corresponding to a hypothetical blood flow. To guarantee an effective alignment with the axis of the load cell of the testing machine, the scaffolds were mounted on rectangular paper frames (32.5×30 mm) the sides of which were cut off before starting the test (Figure S7B). The tests were conducted at a strain rate of 25% s−1.

As to the tensile and ring‐tensile testing, stress at failure (σF) and elongation at failure (εF) were directly determined from the end points of the relative stress‐strain curves, while the tensile elastic modulus (E) was calculated from the slope of the initial linear part. As far as stress at yield (σY) and elongation at yield (εY) are concerned, they were determined by evaluating the meeting point between the stress‐strain curve and a straight line, having modulus equal to the calculated elastic modulus, horizontally shifted by 1% with respect to the linear section of the stress‐strain curve. Work at yield (WY) and work at failure (WF) were also evaluated and calculated as the integral, using the trapezoidal method, of the stress‐strain curves to yield and failure, respectively. The burst pressure (BP) was determined as the peak pressure value recorded before sample failure. For all the tests, 6 specimens were analyzed, and the results have been provided as the mean value ± standard deviation.

Biocompatibility Assays

Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) were purchased from PromoCell (catalog number Cat.No. C‐12203). The cells were cultured according to the manufacturer's guidelines in Endothelial cell Growth Medium (EGM‐2, Sigma‐Aldrich catalog n. C‐22011). Scaffolds were placed in 24‐well plates and underwent chemical sterilization by immersion in 70% ethanol three times for 15 min each. After sterilization, scaffolds were completely air‐dried and rinsed with PBS. Subsequently, scaffolds were secured at the bottom of the wells using rubber O‐rings. HUVECs were seeded onto the scaffolds at a density of 150,000 cells per scaffold. The cells were cultured for 21 days under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2), with medium changed every 48 h. At various time points (day 1, day 7, day 14, and day 21 post‐seeding), cell metabolic activity, proliferation, and morphology changes were evaluated to assess scaffold biocompatibility and cellular response.

Metabolic Activity: PrestoBlue Assay

To assess metabolic activity, PrestoBlue reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. At every time point, scaffolds were incubated with 450 µL of PrestoBlue solution for 45 min at 37°C, 5% CO2. Then, 200 µL of the solution was transferred into a black bottom 96‐well plate and fluorescence intensity was measured using the CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 590 nm, according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Proliferation: CyQuant DNA Assay

For DNA quantification, scaffolds were frozen at −80°C to facilitate cell lysis, until analysis. CyQuant DNA Assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following thawing, scaffolds were incubated with cell‐lysis buffer followed by the CyQuant dye binding solution, and fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 nm. The DNA content was quantified based on a standard curve generated using known concentrations of DNA standards provided in the kit. DNA content was quantified in nanograms per scaffold and reported as cells number per scaffold assuming that approximately 6.6 pg of DNA is present in each cell [61].

HUVECs Immunofluorescence Microscopy Analysis

Scaffolds were washed with PBS to remove residual medium, followed by cell fixation with 3.7% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Then, scaffolds were further washed with PBS and cells permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X‐100. To block nonspecific binding sites, scaffolds were incubated with a PBS solution containing 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.05% (v/v) Tween‐20 (blocking solution), for 30 min.

Scaffolds were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies Alexa Fluor 647 Mouse Anti‐Human CD144‐Vascular Endothelial Cadherin (VE‐Cad) (1:1000 dilution; BD Pharmingen) in blocking solution. After three washes of 5 min each with PBS containing 0.05% Tween‐20 on an orbital shaker, scaffolds were incubated for 15 min with DAPI (1:1000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific) to stain cell nuclei. Finally, scaffolds were washed with PBS and mounted onto glass slides for visualization using an Eclipse Ti2 Inverted Microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Immunostaining images were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji) software.

CASMCs Culture and Immunofluorescence Microscopy Analysis

Human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (CASMCs) were purchased from Lonza Bioscience (CC‐2583) and cultured in SmGM‐2 Smooth Muscle Cell Growth Medium‐2 BulletKit (Lonza Bioscience, CC‐3182) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Electrospun scaffolds were punched into circular samples (15 mm diameter, surface area ≈ 1.77 cm2), sterilized by three washes in 70% (v/v) ethanol, rinsed thoroughly with PBS, and placed individually in 24‐well plates. CASMCs were seeded onto the scaffolds at a density of 8.5 × 104 cells cm− 2 (corresponding to 1.5 × 105 cells per scaffold) by carefully adding 1 mL of cell suspension to each sample. Cell‐seeded scaffolds were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for up to 14 days, with culture medium replaced every 48 h. For immunofluorescence analysis, scaffolds were gently washed with PBS to remove residual medium and fixed with 3.7% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, samples were rinsed with PBS and permeabilized using 0.1% (v/v) Triton X‐100. Non‐specific binding sites were blocked by incubation for 30 min with PBS containing 5% (w/v) donkey serum and 0.05% (v/v) Tween‐20. Scaffolds were incubated overnight at 4°C with a mouse monoclonal anti‐α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) antibody (clone 1A4, ab7817; Abcam) diluted in blocking solution. After washing, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a donkey anti‐mouse secondary antibody (1:2000 dilution) and with DAPI (1:1000 dilution) to visualize cell nuclei. Finally, scaffolds were washed with PBS, mounted on glass slides, and imaged using an Eclipse Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Immunofluorescence images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji) software. Cell nuclei were quantified from fluorescence microscopy images using Fiji/ImageJ software. Briefly, fluorescence images were converted to grayscale and thresholded to generate binary images, isolating nuclear signals from the background. Nuclei were then automatically identified and counted using the Analyze Particles function in Fiji, applying consistent size and circularity filters across all images to exclude noise and artifacts. Quantification was performed on at least three independent samples per condition, with a minimum of three randomly selected fields of view analyzed for each sample. The number of nuclei was normalized to the imaged area and expressed as cells per unit area where indicated.

Coagulation Assays

To evaluate potential alterations in the coagulation profile due to activation of the coagulation cascade upon contact between blood proteins and scaffold material surfaces, coagulation assays were performed using platelet‐poor plasma (PPP). Blood samples were collected from healthy donors with no history of bleeding disorders, following informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Blood was drawn into tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate as an anticoagulant and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min to obtain PPP. Scaffolds were placed in sterile 24‐well plates and incubated with 220 µL of PPP at 37°C on an orbital shaker for 30 min. Blank control samples consisted of PPP incubated under identical conditions in 24‐well plates in the absence of any scaffold material. After incubation, the PPP was collected and immediately subjected to activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and prothrombin time (PT) assays. For aPTT, 100 µL of PPP from each well was mixed with 100 µL of Dade Actin FSL Activated PTT Reagent (Siemens) and incubated at 37°C for 3 min, followed by the addition of 100 µL of 25 mM calcium chloride, after which clot formation time was measured. For PT, 100 µL of PPP was combined with 200 µL of Dade Innovin reagent (Siemens) and the clotting time was recorded.

Hemolysis Assay

Fresh human whole blood was collected from healthy volunteers into tubes containing 3.2% (w/v) sodium citrate and used within 4–6 h of collection. Blood samples were centrifuged at 200 × g for 15 min to separate red blood cells (RBCs) from the platelet rich plasma (PRP). The RBC fraction was collected, and 1 mL of concentrated RBCs was diluted with 9 mL of sterile 0.9% NaCl solution prior to use in the hemolysis assay. Scaffolds fabricated from PBCE, P(BCE85BPripol15), P(BCE75BPripol25), and P(BCE65BPripol35)/PBCE 85/15 blends were cut to a surface area of 177 mm2. Samples were pre‐wetted in saline before testing. Each material was incubated with 700 µL of diluted RBC suspension in 1.5 mL tubes at 37°C for 1 h under static conditions.

RBCs incubated in 0.9% NaCl served as the negative control, while RBCs treated with 0.1% Triton X‐100 were used as the positive control (100% hemolysis). After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 min to remove intact cells. Hemolysis was estimated by measuring the haemoglobin released from the RBCs, by recording the absorbance of the supernatants at 540 nm using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany).

The percentage of hemolysis was calculated according to the following equation: Hemolysis(%)=As−AnAp−An×100 where As is the absorbance of the sample, An is the absorbance of the negative control, and Ap is the absorbance of the positive control. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Platelet Adhesion Assay

Platelet adhesion was assessed using platelet‐rich plasma (PRP), obtained by centrifugation of whole blood anticoagulated with 3.2% sodium citrate at 250 × g for 15 min. The PRP supernatant was collected. Each scaffold, placed in a 24‐well plate, was incubated with 300 µL of PRP at 37°C for 1 h under static conditions. After incubation, scaffolds were gently rinsed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to remove non‐adherent platelets.

For SEM analysis, samples were fixed overnight in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer and post‐fixed in 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide prepared in 0.1 m sodium cacodylate buffer for 1 h. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 90%, and 100%) and sputter‐coated with a thin gold layer using a Quorum Technologies SC7620 system (UK). High‐resolution images of platelet adhesion, activation state, and scaffold surface morphology were acquired using a JSM‐IT200 scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., The Netherlands).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0. Data from metabolic activity assays and DNA quantification were analyzed for statistical significance as well as the data obtained from the mechanical tests. Differences among multiple groups at various time points were assessed using a 2‐way ANOVA analysis followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p‐value < 0.05; **p‐value < 0.01; ***p‐value < 0.001; ****p‐value < 0.0001). Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments.
