The approach of orthopaedic and dental physicians to antibiotic prophylaxis for patients who have undergone total knee or total hip arthroplasty: A Questionnaire-Based Study
Authors: Kübra Karaçam, Mustafa Erdem, Rahime Zeynep Erdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe necessity of antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures in arthroplasty patients remains unsupported by strong evidence. The aim of this study was to determine the attitudes of dentists and othopedists towards antibiotic prophylaxis.MethodsA 15-item questionnaire evaluating the demographic data of dentists and orthopaedic surgeons and their tendencies to administer antibiotic prophylaxis in various cases was delivered online. The data obtained were analyzed statistically using descriptive statistical methods and the Pearson Chi-square test.ResultsA total of 460 respondents completed the questionnaire; 104 dental practitioners, 146 dental specialists, and 210 orthopaedic surgeons. The distribution of the responses to four items in the questionnaire was similar between the orthopaedic surgeons and dentists (p > 0.05). A statistically significant difference was determined between orthopaedic surgeons and dentists in respect of the responses to 6 items (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe study results demonstrated that orthopaedic surgeons were more inclined to administer antibiotic prophylaxis than dentists. All participants were made aware of antibiotic prophylaxis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09777-3.

Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is one of the most successful orthopaedic interventions and it is predicted that by 2030, 4 million hip and knee arthroplasty operations per year will be performed [1]. Although the outcomes are generally excellent, one of the most serious complications of TJA is late-term prosthetic joint infection (PJI). This occurs at the low rate of 1–2%, but when it occurs once it leads to both destructive outcomes for the patient and very high treatment costs [2, 3]. Infections developing within the first 3 months are accepted as “early prosthetic joint infection” and infections developing between 3 and 24 months postoperatively as “delayed prosthesis infection” [4]. Both early and delayed prosthetic joint infection are generally thought to form as a result of contamination during implantation. Infections developing after 24 months are known as “late prosthetic joint infection” [4]. Although late infections are due to the spread of bacteria from a distant focus, generally from the oropharyngeal, gastrointestinal, or genitourinary systems through hematogenous pathways, there are also reports in the literature that they have been seen after dental surgical procedures [4, 5]. Although a direct causal relationship between dental procedures and TJA has not been definitively demonstrated, there are studies linking oral procedures to bacteremia and suggesting that they may pose a potential risk for TJA [1, 5, 6].

The use of antibiotic prophylaxis (AP) before a procedure in patients who have undergone TJA and are to undergo dental treatment remains a matter of debate. The American Association of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) and the American Dental Association (ADA) have made efforts to develop guidelines and recommendations related to this practice. Before 2013 the AAOS and ADA were in agreement that AP should be administered before the procedure to every patient planned to undergo dental treatment within 2 years of arthroplasty. Subsequently, differences of opinion emerged between the organizations. In the most recent guidelines published by the ADA it is clearly stated that “current evidence has shown no relationship between dental procedures and PJI, or that AP before dental procedures provides protective benefit” [7].

Additionally, a panel consisting of ADA and AAOS members developed ‘Appropriate Use Criteria’ (AUC) as a tool to define narrow clinical scenarios that might potentially benefit from prophylaxis [7, 8]. Although the recommendations in this panel were not very definitive, specific narrowed guidelines were developed. Due to the estimated low efficacy of AP in the prevention of PJI due to bacteremia related to a dental procedure, the current AAOS guideline criteria for appropriate use state that AP is only necessary for the highest risk patients undergoing invasive procedures [8, 9].

Antibiotics have been used to fight infections for many years. However, as a result of widespread and incorrect use some micro-organisms have become resistant to all types of antibiotics, and thus the problem of antimicrobial resistance has emerged [10]. Some clinical guidelines have been developed for antibiotic use because of antibiotic resistance, but it is not clear which factors affect antibiotic use in dentistry [11, 12]. Despite all these recommendations, it has been shown in many studies that AP is administered routinely to these patients before dental procedures [13, 14].

This study aimed to compare the responses of dentists and orthopaedic surgeons in Türkiye regarding antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures in patients with total joint arthroplasty with the recommendations of the AAOS guideline [7, 8, 15].

Methods

Approval for this survey study was granted by Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision no: 2024/293). The study population consisted of dental practitioners and orthopaedic surgeons. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study inclusion criteria were defined as being a dentist or orthopaedic surgeon who routinely performs invasive procedures in patients with total joint arthroplasty, and voluntary participation. Orthodontists, paediatric dentists, and oral and maxillofacial radiologists were excluded from the study, as their clinical practice does not typically involve invasive procedures requiring antibiotic prophylaxis decisions in this patient population. The items in the questionnaire were prepared according to the AUC determined in the AAOS multidisciplinary panel [15]. The questionnaire items were first translated into Turkish then back-translated into the original language by two language specialists (English-Turkish). The full English version of the questionnaire is available in the Supplementary Material. The final form of the questionnaire was prepared using Google Forms and was delivered to the participants via an online link. Taking homogenous distribution into consideration, the questionnaire was sent to dentists and orthopaedic surgeons working in all the regions of Türkiye. After sending the link to the participants once, reminders were sent 3 times at 1-week intervals, and the link was kept open for 1 month.

The questionnaire consisted of a total of 15 items in 2 sections (Tables 1, 2 and 3). In the first section, the demographic data of the participants, profession, area of specialization, and duration of professional experience were questioned. The dentists were asked on average how many times a year they performed invasive procedures, on average how many times they requested an orthopaedic consultation for these procedures, and the orthopaedic surgeons were asked how many total hip or knee arthroplasty operations they performed a year on average, and whether or not they had encountered a need for antibiotic prophylaxis for dental treatments. The second section of the questionnaire consisted of 10 case questions to determine the tendencies of the participants to use antibiotic prophylaxis for patients with a history of total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA). The items in the questionnaire were prepared according to the AUC determined in the AAOS multidisciplinary panel [15], and the corresponding AUC recommendation for each item served as the reference standard. The participants were instructed to mark the response closest to how they felt about each question.

Data analysis

Data obtained in the study were analyzed statistically using IBM SPSS version 25.0 software. Factor analysis was performed using principal components analysis with direct oblimin rotation. The Kaiser-Mayer-Olkin value was 0.789 and p < 0.001 as a result of Bartlett’s test. The Cronbach alpha coefficient for questionnaire reliability was α = 0.781. The data were evaluated using descriptive statistical methods (number and percentage). Relationships between categorical variables were examined with Chi-square independence tests using 2 × 2 and 2 × 3 cross-tables. A value of p < 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Results

The questionnaire was sent via WhatsApp online groups to 900 dentists and orthopaedic surgeons throughout Türkiye. A total of 460 completed the questionnaire in full and thus the participation rate was 51.1%. Of the 460 participants, 250 were dental professionals (104 general practitioners and 146 specialists) and 210 were orthopaedic surgeons.The duration of professional experience was < 5 years in 32.8% of the participants, 5–10 years in 32.0%, and > 10 years in 35.2%.

Of the total study participants, 52.8% reported that they had never sought or received a consultation regarding antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental treatments for patients who had undergone TKA or THA. The data according to professional branches are shown in Table 1.

The relationship between participants’ experience of seeking or receiving consultation for antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures and their professional experience and annual invasive procedure volume was examined (Table 2). The rate of consultation experience was similar across different years of professional experience. However, as the number of invasive procedures performed per year increased, the rate of participants who had sought or received consultation for antibiotic prophylaxis also increased.

The distribution of the responses to questions 2, 4, 6, and 10 was similar between the dentists and orthopaedic surgeons (p > 0.05). A statistically significant difference was determined between the dentists and orthopaedic surgeons in respect of the responses to the remaining 6 questions (Table 3).

The response given to question 1 was that AP was “rarely appropriate” by 77.2% of the dentists and “appropriate” by 22.9% of the orthopaedic surgeons (p < 0.001).

For question 3, the response of ‘rarely appropriate’ was similar between dentists and orthopaedic surgeons (13.2% and 13.8%, respectively). However, dentists responded ‘may be appropriate’ at a higher rate compared to orthopaedic surgeons (31.6% vs. 20.5%), while orthopaedic surgeons considered prophylaxis ‘appropriate’ at a higher rate compared to dentists (65.7% vs. 55.2%, p = 0.024).

For question 5, dentists and orthopaedic surgeons showed markedly different response patterns (p < 0.001). While the majority of dentists considered prophylaxis ‘rarely appropriate’ (51.6%), the majority of orthopaedic surgeons considered it ‘appropriate’ (59.5%). For question 7, the majority of both dentists and orthopaedic surgeons considered prophylaxis ‘appropriate’ (68.8% and 74.3%, respectively). However, dentists were significantly more likely to respond ‘rarely appropriate’ compared to orthopaedic surgeons (13.2% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.015).

For question 8, dentists more frequently responded ‘rarely appropriate’ or ‘may be appropriate’ (35.6% and 37.2%, respectively), while orthopaedic surgeons more frequently considered prophylaxis ‘appropriate’ (54.3% vs. 27.2%, p < 0.001). Similarly, for question 9, dentists more frequently responded ‘rarely appropriate’ compared to orthopaedic surgeons (40.0% vs. 12.9%), while orthopaedic surgeons more frequently considered prophylaxis ‘appropriate’ (43.8% vs. 18.8%, p < 0.001).

In summary, dentists were significantly less likely to consider antibiotic prophylaxis appropriate compared to orthopaedic surgeons. While orthopaedic surgeons predominantly considered antibiotic prophylaxis ‘appropriate’ in most clinical scenarios, dentists more frequently responded ‘rarely appropriate’. This difference was statistically significant for questions 3, 5, 7, 8, and 9.

Discussion

The administration of antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures to patients who have undergone arthroplasty is not based on any evidence. Although there are various recommendations for the use of antibiotic prophylaxis for all patients, the controversy continues. The aim of this study was to evaluate the approaches of dentists and orthopaedic surgeons regarding antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures in patients who have undergone THA or TKA, based on general health condition and the time since arthroplasty.

The results showed that 52.8% of participants reported never having sought or received a consultation regarding antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures in patients who had undergone TKA or THA. This finding suggests that a substantial proportion of clinicians in both groups had limited practical experience with this clinical scenario, highlighting the relevance of guideline-based education in this area.

An AAOS multidisciplinary panel evaluated 64 scenarios to determine “appropriate use” of antimicrobial prophylaxis for patients undergoing dental treatment. The AUC took 5 variables into consideration; the invasiveness of the dental procedure, immunosuppression status of the patient, glycemic control, the history of periprosthetic infection requiring surgery, and the time since the arthroplasty operation. Of the 64 scenarios, prophylaxis was determined to be “rarely appropriate” in 61%, “may be appropriate” in 27%, and “appropriate” in only 12% [15]. These criteria are helpful in determining when it is appropriate to recommend prophylaxis before a dental procedure and have been stated to be recommendations to facilitate decision-making.

Descriptively, the majority of orthopaedic surgeons responded ‘appropriate’ to 9 out of 10 questions and the majority of dentists to 6 out of 10, whereas according to the AUC, only 2 of the 10 scenarios were classified as ‘appropriate’ [15]. This suggests that both groups tended to recommend antibiotic prophylaxis more broadly than the AUC recommendations. In the evaluation of demographic data, when it was considered that 78.6% of the orthopaedic surgeons had been working for longer than 5 years, it was seen that orthopaedic surgeons were more inclined to administer antibiotic prophylaxis than the dentists. Previous studies have also shown that compared to dentists, orthopaedic surgeons tend to recommend AP more often and for longer periods [13, 16, 17]. In a questionnaire-based study of orthopaedic surgeons by McNally et al. [14], it was seen that the majority of the respondents believed that AP was necessary before dental treatment with no time limit, which was consistent with the findings of the current study. Teixera et al. [18] reported that even if they were aware of current guidelines, orthopaedic surgeons felt that they had to prescribe premedication. Therefore, it was stated that it would be possible to reduce the misuse of AP with studies which would evaluate the knowledge of orthopaedic surgeons about the AUC formed by the AAOS. The data of the current study support the opinions of Teixeria et al. [18].

In the cases of the current study questionnaire, while other parameters varied, there were non-invasive dental procedure (dental scaling not extending to the gingiva, dental filling and erosion treatment) in 4 cases. The responses of the dentists to these questions were “rarely appropriate” or “may be appropriate” whereas the majority of the orthopaedic surgeons gave the response of “appropriate”.

For the administration of AP in a non-invasive dental procedure for a patient who was not immunosuppressed or diagnosed as diabetic with a history of periprosthetic infection within the last year, 51.6% of the dentists responded as “rarely appropriate”, whereas 59.5% of the orthopaedic surgeons gav the response of “appropriate”. According to the AUC, the administration of prophylaxis in this situation is recommended as “rarely appropriate”. In the case of administration of AP in a non-invasive dental procedure for a patient who was actively diabetic with an unknown blood glucose level and a history of severe immunosuppression, 41.2% of the dentists responded of “ may be appropriate”, and 59.5% of the orthopaedic surgeons stated “appropriate”. In such a case, “rarely appropriate” is the recommendation in the AUC.

These results demonstrate that orthopaedic surgeons may have a lack of knowledge about which dental procedures are invasive or not. The decision for antibiotic prophylaxis for a patient is generally made by the dentist and orthopaedic surgeons together. When a decision is being made for the need for AP, if the orthopedic surgeon recommends AP, it is recommended by the ADA that it is prescribed by the orthopaedic surgeons whatever the recommendation of the dentist [7]. Therefore it seems to be important that dentists explain in detail whether the procedure to be performed is invasive or not when they consult with orthopaedic surgeons.

There are studies in the literature that have stated that there is insufficient evidence that AP is necessary in patients who have undergone TJA. In a study of 6101 patients with a history of TKA or THA, there was shown to be a very low risk of hematogenous spread of PJI from a distant region [19]. Berbari et al. conducted a microbiological study and stated that the risk of PJI from dental procedures was low [5]. Another large-scale study in the UK of 9427 patients with PJI concluded that there was no correlation between dental procedures and PJI [20]. Some published studies have stated that AP is not necessary as there is insufficient evidence. In a meta-analysis by Slullitel et al. [21], there was reported to be no direct evidence for the use of AP before dental procedures. Moreover, there was shown to be a need for high-quality evidence to further evaluate the actual efficacy of medical application. In a published review, it was suggested that AP may be appropriate only in high-risk patients based on the individual risk profile of the patient and the level of concern about the risk of infection of the doctor [22]. It was also stated that there is an insufficient number of cases to be able to determine the optimal treatment and outcomes of PJI due to dental procedures [22].

In the studies published to date, there is generally insufficient evidence showing the effects of AP before a dental procedure on the prevention of PJI. Nevertheless, the current study results showed that there is still concern about the effects of not using antibiotics for patients with a prosthetic joint. The lack of definitive evidence associating PJI with dental treatment may prevent clinicians from adopting the guidelines. According to the results of this study, the risk of PJI of a patient was seen to be more important to clinicians than the risk of antibiotic resistance. It is known that the acceptance or application of clinical practice guidelines in a field of medicine can take years [23, 24]. Therefore, it is extremely important that clinicians are encouraged by increasing in-service training.

There were some limitations to this study. A greater range of case problems in the questionnaire and a larger number of participants would give better results about the tendencies of clinicians on this subject. Additionally, gender differences among respondents were not analyzed, which may represent a limitation given that gender inclination could be an additional factor influencing antibiotic prophylaxis decisions. Therefore, to obtain more definitive evidence related to this subject, there is a need for further larger-scale studies that also incorporate gender-based analysis.

Conclusıon

This study concluded that orthopaedic surgeons are more inclined than dentists to administer antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures in patients with total knee and total hip arthroplasty. It is of great importance that both orthopaedic surgeons and dentists are informed about the current guidelines on this subject.
