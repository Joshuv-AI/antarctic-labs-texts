Integrative, Scalable Modeling of Hydrological Systems with MBSE and HFGT
Megan Harris *, Ehsanoddin Ghorbanichemazkati, Mohammad Mahdi Naderi, John C. Little, Amro M. Farid

Abstract
Worsening global challenges in the Anthropocene demand complex, adaptive solutions grounded in a systems-level understanding of coupled social and environmental dynamics. However, existing modeling approaches often fall short due to disciplinary silos, limited scalability, and the absence of shared ontological frameworks. Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE), when integrated with Hetero-functional Graph Theory (HFGT), offers a powerful methodology for modeling systems of systems while preserving subsystem heterogeneity and enabling cross-disciplinary integration. This paper presents the first application of the MBSE-HFGT methodology to environmental systems, using a series of worked examples involving flow through lake and land segments. These examples demonstrate how the approach enables consistent, scalable, and integrative modeling of complex environmental processes.

I-A

Original Contribution

This paper presents the first application of the MBSE-HFGT methodology to environmental systems. Specifically, it develops a framework for modeling interconnected environmental systems across space, function, and discipline, while preserving the internal complexity of each subsystem. By applying this methodology to hydrologic and land systems, the paper demonstrates how MBSE-HFGT can support convergent thinking about environmental challenges, and provides a foundation for integrated decision-making in the Anthropocene.

In doing so, this paper bridges a gap between environmental modeling and systems engineering, showing how a shared ontological and mathematical framework can enable new ways of understanding and intervening in complex socio-environmental systems.

II
Methodological Background

Historically, individual disciplines have developed computational tools tailored to their own system-specific behaviors. In hydrology, a distinct modeling ontology has emerged
[
24
]
, leading to a wide range of techniques focused on components of the hydrologic cycle. Early watershed models, including those based on unit hydrograph theory or the rational method for peak runoff estimation, were constrained by sparse data and limited computational capacity
[
25
]
. With the growth of computational power, these models evolved into more comprehensive tools
[
25
]
, extending beyond runoff to simulate multiple processes and support integrated watershed planning and ecosystem management. Contemporary models now incorporate physical, chemical, and biological dynamics, aided by advances in data availability and analysis
[
26
]
. Techniques such as stochastic modeling, distributed parameterization, and artificial intelligence (AI) have improved representation across spatial and temporal scales
[
27
]
, enabling applications to increasingly large and complex regions
[
28
]
.

Despite these advancements, hydrological models often remain specialized, simulating isolated processes

[
29
]
. While these models are powerful within their domains, they often lack the interoperability needed to capture the interactions among hydrological, ecological, and socio-economic systems
[
27
,
28
]
.

To address these limitations, researchers have adopted broader system-level modeling strategies. System dynamics (SD) models have been used to represent feedback loops and aggregate behavior in coupled human-natural systems, particularly in contexts with high uncertainty or limited data
[
30
,
31
]
. While SD models can include socio-economic drivers in watershed analysis, they rely on a narrow set of abstractions—stocks, flows, and feedback loops—and often lack the modularity needed to scale or incorporate heterogeneous subsystem structures
[
10
,
32
]
.

Co-simulation approaches offer another strategy, allowing independently developed domain-specific models to operate in parallel and exchange information during runtime
[
16
]
. While valuable for exploring system interdependencies, co-simulation methods typically assume that the output of one model serves as the sequential input to another. This structure limits their ability to represent complex, bidirectional, or multilateral feedbacks. Furthermore, co-simulation often struggles with the spatial and temporal scale mismatches that commonly emerge in climate-affected systems-of-systems.

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) addresses this limitation by offering a structured, model-centric methodology for the design and analysis of complex systems. Rather than treating hydrological, ecological, and socio-economic systems as isolated entities, MBSE provides a unified framework that emphasizes system integration, capturing both
form
(the physical or logical components) and
function
(the processes that transform inputs into outputs) within a common modeling language
[
33
,
34
]
. In this context, MBSE acts as a bridge between historically fragmented modeling approaches. For example, while mass balance models estimate changes in water storage and hydrographs predict runoff, MBSE facilitates their integration with land-use models, economic drivers, and ecosystem feedbacks. This holistic perspective enables not only detailed simulation of individual processes but also insight into how they interact within a larger coupled system
[
35
]
.

Systems Modeling Language (SysML) provides standardized diagrams to represent system architecture within MBSE. Block Definition Diagrams (BDD) define the system’s form by describing its structural elements, their properties, and relationships
[
36
]
. In hydrology, these might represent physical components like lakes, rivers, and aquifers. Activity Diagrams (ACT) define the system’s function by representing the flow of processes, showing how activities transform inputs into outputs, for instance, how precipitation becomes runoff or groundwater recharge
[
36
,
35
]
. Together, these diagrams establish the system boundary by clearly delineating internal components and their interactions with external entities.

As systems grow in scale and complexity, the challenge of linking disparate models becomes significant. Integrating models across hydrological, ecological, and socio-economic domains requires reconciling differences in assumptions, ontologies, and data structures
[
37
,
38
]
. Model-based Systems Engineering (MBSE) and Hetero-functional Graph Theory (HFGT) address this challenge by providing a unified computational framework. HFGT enables the reconciliation of ontologies across disciplines, allowing processes from distinct systems to be represented and simulated cohesively
[
36
,
35
]
. This approach enhances our ability to model the interactions and feedback loops that define complex Anthropocene systems
[
38
,
35
]
.

Hetero-functional Graph Theory Meta-Architecture & Definitions

Hetero-functional Graph Theory (HFGT) extends traditional graph theory by incorporating system functions, making it particularly suited for modeling complex, heterogeneous systems. The framework organizes systems through three primary components:
resources
,

, and

resources
(subjects) facilitate

(verbs) involving
operands
(objects)
[
33
,
34
]

Definition 1
(System Operand
[
33

An asset or object
l_{i}\in L
that is operated on or consumed during the execution of a process.

Definition 2
(System Process
[
34
,
33
]
):

An activity
p\in P
that transforms or transports a predefined set of input operands into a predefined set of outputs.

Definition 3
(System Resource
[
33
]
):

An asset or object
r_{v}\in R
that facilitates the execution of a process.

In this framework, resources are entities that enable processes to occur, such as physical infrastructure or abstract systems (Def.
3
). Processes represent activities that transform or transport operands (Def.
2
), while operands are the dynamic entities being acted upon, such as water in a hydrological model or data in a computational system (Def.
1

Resource
r_{v}
does process
p_{w}
to operand

The organization of resources, processes, and operands within the HFGT meta-architecture is structured using a
Reference Architecture (RA)
. This RA serves as a digital twin of the Anthropocene system, categorizing its form and functions under a unified ontology. Systems Modeling Language (SysML) diagrams, such as Block Definition Diagrams (BDD) and Activity Diagrams (ACT), visually represent this organization. The BDD defines the system’s form by resources and their classifications, while the ACT defines the system’s function by allocating processes to the resources that facilitate them. These
diagrams ensure consistency and interoperability across disciplines.

The components of the system are further classified for functional clarity. Resources, denoted as

M

VII
Discussion

The three illustrative examples demonstrate the scalability and integrative capacity of the Convergent Anthems framework for modeling watershed systems. The single-lake flushing example served as a pedagogical foundation, confirming that core hydrological behaviors—such as storage, flow resistance, and nutrient mixing—could be captured using a unified mathematical structure. Extending this to a three-lake configuration validated the framework’s extensibility, as system-specific tensors were automatically generated through the HFGT toolbox to support more complex dynamics without altering the underlying ontology. In the final example, the integration of three agricultural land segments introduced exogenous nitrogen loads and more intricate coupling between land and water. This increased the dimensionality of the system matrices (up to 31×31), showcasing the importance of automation in maintaining tractability as spatial and functional scales expand.

Together, these results highlight the broader value of Convergent Anthems in supporting simulation of complex, heterogeneous environmental systems. By preserving internal subsystem structure while enabling seamless integration across domains, the framework offers a powerful tool for modeling interactions between hydrological, ecological, and anthropogenic processes. The HFGT formalism ensures consistency in representing continuity and constitutive laws, while the MBSE-based Reference Architecture supports modular expansion. As demonstrated, the framework enables rigorous and scalable analysis across a range of watershed configurations, making it well suited for addressing the multi-scale, cross-domain challenges of the Anthropocene.

VIII
Conclusion & Future Work

This study presents the first application of the Convergent Anthems framework—an integration of Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) and Hetero-functional Graph Theory (HFGT)—to environmental systems. By developing a watershed-specific Reference Architecture and applying it to a progression of increasingly complex lake–land systems, the framework is shown to preserve subsystem heterogeneity while enabling integrated, scalable simulation. These results directly address the methodological gap identified in the introduction: the need for a unified, ontology-driven modeling approach capable of capturing interconnections across environmental systems without collapsing their internal dynamics.

In future work, the methodology will be applied to real-world case studies in the Chesapeake Bay Watershed. Much of the data for these applications will be drawn from the Chesapeake Bay Program’s Chesapeake Assessment Scenario Tool (CAST), a modeling system that provides spatially and temporally resolved estimates of nutrient and sediment loads across the watershed
[
28
]
. These case studies will incorporate observational data, region-specific interventions, and regulatory constraints to evaluate the framework’s utility for policy design, scenario planning, and adaptive management. Ongoing development of the HFGT toolbox will aim to accommodate larger and more interdisciplinary networks, enhance computational efficiency, and refine the SysML-to-simulation workflow. Additional work will include the integration of economic and land use subsystems.

More broadly, this research contributes to an emerging system-of-systems (SoS) convergence paradigm currently being implemented in the Chesapeake Bay region to address interdependent societal challenges such as eutrophication, agricultural sustainability, and economic growth. This paradigm emphasizes the coherent integration of land use, watershed, estuary, economic, and governance systems through a common SysML ontology. The MBSE-HFGT framework introduced in this study serves as a computational foundation for the convergence effort, enabling the translation from graphical models to formalized simulations. Future extensions are expected to support a broader SoS decision-support system and educational pedagogy aimed at training a new generation of integrators capable of addressing complex, multi-scale challenges of the Anthropocene.
