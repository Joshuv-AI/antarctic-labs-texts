Evaluation of fetal frontal lobe development and Evans index in pregnancies complicated by gestational diabetes mellitus: prospective comparative observational study
Authors: Kubilay Çanga, Recep Taha Ağaoğlu, Özgür Volkan Akbulut, Dinçer Sümer, Seyit Ahmet Erol, Şevki Çelen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess whether third-trimester fetal frontal lobe biometry and ventricular–cranial proportionality differ between pregnancies with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and normoglycemic controls, and whether these markers vary by treatment (diet vs. insulin) or composite adverse perinatal outcome (CAPO).MethodsIn this single-center prospective observational study, 73 singleton GDM pregnancies and 73 low-risk controls underwent ultrasound at comparable gestational ages (30–38 weeks). On the transventricular plane we measured frontal anteroposterior diameter (FAPD) and occipitofrontal diameter (OFD), and derived two indices (100×FAPD/OFD; 100×FAPD/head circumference [HC]). Maximal width of the frontal horns (MWFH) and maximal inner cranial diameter (MID) were recorded and the fetal Evans index (EI, MWFH/MID) calculated. Obstetric and neonatal outcomes were compared, with subgroup analyses within the GDM cohort. Multiple linear regression adjusted the main neurosonographic outcomes for estimated fetal weight, gestational age at ultrasound, maternal age, BMI, and parity.ResultsGestational age at scan was similar (33.8 ± 1.9 vs. 33.6 ± 2.0 weeks), while estimated fetal weight was higher in GDM (2504 ± 514 vs. 2320 ± 531 g; p = 0.035). FAPD was modestly higher in GDM (median 39.4 vs. 38.9 mm; p = 0.040) with comparable OFD, and both frontal indices were higher (FAPD/OFD 37.61 vs. 36.59, p < 0.001; FAPD/HC 12.74 vs. 12.55, p < 0.001). MWFH and MID were slightly larger (p = 0.025 and 0.006), yet EI was unchanged (0.263 ± 0.011 vs. 0.262 ± 0.014; p = 0.574). After adjustment, GDM remained linked to a lower OFD (− 2.516 mm; 95% CI − 3.730 to − 1.303; p < 0.001), a higher FAPD/OFD ratio (adjusted mean difference 1.088; 95% CI 0.633–1.542; p < 0.001) and FAPD/HC ratio (0.150; 95% CI 0.033–0.268; p = 0.012), but not to FAPD, MWFH, MID, or EI. Major neonatal morbidity, NICU admission, and CAPO rates were comparable. Within the GDM group, neurosonographic measures did not differ by diet vs. insulin, and CAPO tracked with lower birth weight, not EI or frontal indices.ConclusionGDM showed small but significant shifts in proportional fetal frontal indices that held after adjustment for fetal size and maternal covariates, whereas absolute FAPD, ventricular measurements, and EI showed no independent association. Because OFD was lower after adjustment while FAPD was not, the higher ratios partly reflect their denominator and cannot be read as greater absolute frontal growth. Frontal proportions appear altered while ventricular–cranial proportionality is preserved, though the clinical and neurodevelopmental meaning is still unclear. Longitudinal work using objective glycemic metrics and standardized postnatal neurodevelopmental follow-up is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09827-2.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is among the most frequent medical complications of pregnancy [1]. Screening and diagnostic strategies vary internationally, most notably the choice between one-step and two-step oral glucose tolerance testing and the thresholds applied [2, 3]. Despite this heterogeneity, GDM is consistently defined as glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy [4]. GDM is associated with increased risks of large-for-gestational-age birth, shoulder dystocia, operative delivery, neonatal hypoglycemia and longer-term cardiometabolic morbidity in both mother and offspring [5]. Current guidance recommends medical nutrition therapy and exercise as first-line treatment for GDM, with pharmacotherapy added when glycemic targets are not met [6].

Beyond these established perinatal effects, there is increasing interest in how maternal hyperglycemia may influence fetal brain development before birth [7]. Given that placental glucose transfer causes fetal glucose levels to closely reflect maternal concentrations, maternal dysglycemia may alter the intrauterine metabolic milieu and fetal endocrine signaling [8]. Hyperglycemia-related oxidative stress and inflammation are also plausible pathways that could compromise placental function and, in turn, fetal neurodevelopment [9]. These mechanisms are particularly relevant in late gestation, when cortical expansion, neuronal connectivity and white-matter maturation accelerate [10].

The link between GDM and offspring neurodevelopment is complex and may reflect shared familial, socioeconomic and postnatal factors; accordingly, observational associations do not necessarily indicate causal intrauterine programming [11, 12]. Recent large-scale syntheses also continue to report associations between maternal diabetes during pregnancy and a range of neurodevelopmental, cognitive and behavioral outcomes in children, while underscoring the need for mechanistic and longitudinal studies to clarify causality, timing and severity of exposure [13]. This context motivates the search for prenatal imaging biomarkers capable of characterizing fetal brain development before postnatal influences accumulate.

Prenatal imaging offers an opportunity to study fetal brain development in vivo. Fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can characterize cortical folding, tissue composition and regional brain volumes, but its availability is limited and it is not part of routine pregnancy care [14]. Ultrasonography, by contrast, is widely accessible and routinely used to assess fetal growth and anatomy. In a recent systematic review, ultrasound-derived neurosonographic markers in GDM-affected pregnancies showed modest but consistent differences in measures, including the cavum septi pellucidi and lateral ventricular widths, versus uncomplicated pregnancies [15].

The frontal lobe is of particular interest because it underpins higher-order cognition, executive function and socio-emotional behavior and undergoes rapid growth during the third trimester and early postnatal period [16]. In the axial transventricular plane, frontal anteroposterior diameter (FAPD) and occipitofrontal diameter (OFD) can be measured, and size-adjusted ratios such as 100×FAPD/OFD and 100×FAPD/head circumference (HC) may reflect frontal lobe growth relative to overall cranial size [17]. Ventricular–cranial proportionality can be assessed using the Evans index (EI), defined as the ratio of maximal frontal horn width to maximal inner cranial diameter [18]. Although an EI threshold around 0.30 has historically been used as a screening cut-off for ventricular enlargement in pediatric and adult neuroimaging [19], fetal application should rely on gestational age–specific reference ranges and be interpreted alongside established measures of ventricular atrial width within the broader neurosonographic context [20].

In this context, we conducted prospective comparative observational study to assess two complementary aspects of fetal cerebral morphology in pregnancies complicated by GDM. First, we evaluated frontal lobe development using the FAPD and size-adjusted frontal indices. Second, we assessed ventricular size relative to cranial diameter using the fetal EI. We also compared these measures by GDM treatment modality (diet vs. insulin) and examined their association with a composite adverse perinatal outcome within the GDM cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective comparative observational study was conducted between June 2025 and December 2025. Pregnancies complicated by GDM were enrolled consecutively as they met the eligibility criteria. The control group was selected from a larger pool of contemporaneous low-risk singleton pregnancies examined during the same period using frequency matching according to gestational age at ultrasound examination, in order to minimize gestational age–dependent variation in fetal cranial proportions. These gestational weeks (30–38 weeks) were intentionally chosen because of the relatively late maturation of the fetal central nervous system and the more apparent clinical manifestation of GDM-related fetal effects after the second trimester. The final study population comprised 73 pregnancies complicated by GDM and 73 normoglycemic low-risk pregnancies. For the prespecified subgroup analyses, the GDM cohort was further categorized according to treatment modality as diet-controlled or insulin-treated.

Within the GDM cohort, a composite adverse perinatal outcome (CAPO) was prespecified as a secondary outcome. CAPO was defined as the occurrence of at least one of the following: preterm delivery, fetal distress, neonatal acidemia (umbilical cord pH < 7.20), NICU admission, transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN), need for respiratory support (continuous positive airway pressure [CPAP] or mechanical ventilation), phototherapy requirement or neonatal hypoglycemia. We calculated the prevalence of CAPO and stratified GDM pregnancies by CAPO status for comparative analyses.

Eligibility criteria were designed to minimize confounding from conditions known to affect fetal brain growth or ventricular size. Pregnancies with known major fetal structural anomalies, chromosomal abnormalities, confirmed congenital infections or multiple gestations were excluded. Pregnancies with major hypertensive disorders, pregestational diabetes mellitus, conception via in vitro fertilization, autoimmune or rheumatologic disorders, maternal smoking, alcohol or teratogenic medication use, or fetal growth restriction were also excluded. To ensure measurement reliability, pregnancies with uncertain gestational age dating or inadequate image quality were excluded from the analysis.

Diagnosis and management of GDM

In our study, GDM diagnosis was made using a single-step 75-gram, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) in accordance with internationally accepted protocols. GDM was diagnosed when at least one plasma glucose value was at or above the following thresholds: fasting ≥ 92 mg/dL, 1-hour ≥ 180 mg/dL or 2-hour ≥ 153 mg/dL [21]. Standard management included nutritional counseling and lifestyle modification as first-line therapy. Insulin was initiated when glycemic targets were not achieved with lifestyle measures alone [22].

Ultrasound assessment and neurosonographic measurements

All pregnant women participating in the study underwent ultrasonographic imaging in the third trimester by the same operator. A Voluson S10 ultrasound system with a 3.5 MHz convex transducer was used for imaging. Standard fetal biometry included biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference, femur length, and estimated fetal weight. Amniotic fluid volume was measured using the maximum vertical pocket (MVP), and umbilical artery Doppler measurements (systolic/diastolic ratio and pulsatility index) were recorded according to standard techniques.

Estimated fetal weight was calculated using the Hadlock formula incorporating biparietal diameter (BPD), head circumference (HC), abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL) [23]:

log10(EFW) = 1.3596 − 0.00386 × AC × FL + 0.0064 × HC + 0.00061 × BPD × AC + 0.0424 × AC + 0.174 × FL, where EFW is expressed in grams and biometric measurements are expressed in centimeters.

Neurosonographic measurements were obtained in the axial transventricular plane, which is routinely used for standard BPD and HC assessment and allows simultaneous visualization of the cavum septi pellucidi (CSP) and lateral ventricles (LV) [15]. In this plane, frontal lobe biometry was recorded as the FAPD, defined as the distance from the inner table of the frontal bone to the posterior margin of the CSP along the fetal midline and the OFD, defined as the distance between the inner tables of the frontal and occipital bones; size-adjusted frontal indices were then calculated as 100×FAPD/OFD and 100×FAPD/HC [17, 24]. Ventricular and cranial inner diameters were assessed by measuring the maximal width of the frontal horns (MWFH), defined as the maximal distance between the lateral walls of the frontal horns and the maximal inner cranial diameter (MID) in the same plane; ventricular–cranial proportionality was expressed as the fetal Evans index (MWFH/MID) [25, 26] (Figs. 1 and 2). As part of the standard fetal anatomical and neurosonographic evaluation, the lateral ventricles were assessed, and fetuses with ventriculomegaly (atrial width ≥ 10 mm) or gross ventricular asymmetry were excluded; lateral ventricular atrial width was not recorded as a separate study measurement variable. Accordingly, the fetal Evans index was interpreted only as a proportional ventricular–cranial measure within a population with normal ventricular width and not as a diagnostic criterion for ventriculomegaly.

Intraobserver variability

All ultrasound examinations were performed transabdominally. A maternal fetal medicine specialist (K.Ç.) performed all scans. We used a Voluson S10 Expert system (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) with a 3.5-MHz convex transducer. Using a single operator reduced interobserver variability. Blinding of the operator to group allocation (GDM versus control) was not feasible because all examinations were performed prospectively by the same maternal–fetal medicine specialist. Therefore, expectation-related measurement bias cannot be completely excluded. Intraobserver reproducibility was evaluated in 20 randomly selected participants. The same operator performed a second ultrasound examination 5 days after the initial examination, repeating FAPD, OFD, MWFH, and MID measurements using the same acquisition and measurement protocol. Therefore, the reproducibility assessment reflects both repeated image acquisition and measurement variability rather than repeated measurements performed on identical stored images. Agreement between the first and repeated measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients based on a two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement definition, and single-measure estimates. ICC values were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Absolute agreement was additionally evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, with mean differences and 95% limits of agreement calculated as mean difference ± 1.96 × standard deviation of the differences. Because the original measurements were performed during real-time ultrasound examinations, standardized stored images suitable for complete post-hoc re-measurement of all neurosonographic parameters were not systematically available. Therefore, a blinded second-operator re-analysis could not be performed retrospectively.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the FAPD/OFD ratio reported by Sahin et al. in “The impact of gestational diabetes on the development of fetal frontal lobe: A case-control study from a tertiary center” [24]. Using the reported group means and standard deviations, we estimated the effect size for FAPD/OFD as a standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) of 0.77. An a priori power analysis was then conducted with G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). We used a two-tailed independent-samples t test (difference between two independent means), with α = 0.05 and power (1 − β) = 0.95. With equal group allocation (allocation ratio = 1), the minimum required sample size was 45 participants per group (total n = 90).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed by visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the independent-samples Student’s t test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The FAPD/OFD ratio, on which the sample size was based, was the primary neurosonographic outcome; the remaining neurosonographic parameters and all perinatal and subgroup analyses were secondary or exploratory. To address potential confounding, multiple linear regression analyses were performed for the main neurosonographic outcomes, with GDM status as the main exposure and adjustment for estimated fetal weight, gestational age at ultrasound examination, maternal age, BMI, and parity. Results are reported as adjusted mean differences (GDM versus control) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The assumptions of the multivariable linear regression models were assessed before model interpretation. Linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated by visual inspection of standardized residual-versus-predicted value scatterplots, residual normality by histograms and normal probability (P–P) plots, multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and influential observations using Cook’s distance. A summary of the regression diagnostics is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

In addition, glycemic exposure was further characterized using 75-g oral glucose tolerance test values and insulin requirement data. Total daily insulin dose was calculated as the sum of daily insulin aspart and insulin detemir doses and was expressed as IU/day. Because insulin dose was applicable only to insulin-treated GDM pregnancies, insulin requirement was evaluated descriptively within the GDM cohort as a marker of treatment intensity. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This prospective study was conducted at the Perinatology Clinic of Ankara Etlik City Hospital between June 2025 and December 2025. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara Etlik City Hospital Scientific Research Evaluation and Ethics Committee before the study started (Date: 10 June 2025; Approval No: AEŞH-BADEK2-2025-151). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Maternal characteristics and standard ultrasound findings

Of the 194 singleton pregnancies assessed for eligibility, 48 were excluded under the predefined criteria. The remaining 146 pregnancies were included in the final analysis, comprising 73 pregnancies with gestational diabetes mellitus and 73 low-risk controls. The flowchart of the study selection process is presented in Fig. 3. Maternal age was similar between groups (30.9 ± 5.5 vs. 29.9 ± 5.1). Body mass index (BMI) was not statistically different (28.40 ± 3.64 vs. 27.30 ± 3.68). Gravidity and parity distributions were also comparable. Gestational age at ultrasound examination was similar (33.8 ± 1.9 vs. 33.6 ± 2.0). Standard fetal head measurements were comparable (BPD: 85.5 ± 4.9 vs. 84.5 ± 5.5 mm; HC: 311 ± 15 vs. 308 ± 17 mm). Estimated fetal weight was higher in the GDM group (2504 ± 514 vs. 2320 ± 531 g; p = 0.035). Amniotic fluid was similar between groups (MVP: 60 [50–70] vs. 63 [53–70] mm; p = 0.343). Umbilical artery Doppler indices were also comparable (UA S/D: 2.35 [2.12–2.70] vs. 2.34 [2.20–2.70]; p = 0.849; UA PI: 0.90 ± 0.17 vs. 0.88 ± 0.15; p = 0.491) (Table 1).

Frontal lobe measurements and indices

Absolute frontal lobe length measured by FAPD was slightly higher in the GDM group than in controls (39.4 [38.1–41.2] mm vs. 38.9 [37.0–40.3] mm; p = 0.040). OFD did not differ significantly between groups. In contrast, size-adjusted indices showed consistent between-group differences. The median FAPD/OFD ratio was higher in the GDM group than in controls (37.61 [36.88–38.41] vs. 36.59 [36.05–36.93]; p < 0.001). Likewise, the median FAPD/HC ratio was higher in the GDM group (12.74 [12.53–12.92]) than in controls (12.55 [12.36–12.72]; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Ventricular measurements and Evans index

MWFH was modestly larger in the GDM group (22.4 ± 1.8 mm) compared with controls (21.6 ± 2.1 mm; p = 0.025). MID was also larger in the GDM group (85.4 [80.0–87.6] mm) than controls (83.3 [77.8–86.3] mm; p = 0.006). Despite these absolute differences, the fetal EI was similar between groups (0.263 ± 0.011 vs. 0.262 ± 0.014; p = 0.574) (Table 1).

Effect size analyses were performed for the main neurosonographic parameters. The between-group difference in FAPD was statistically significant but small in magnitude (Mann–Whitney U = 2141, p = 0.040; rank-biserial correlation = 0.196). The FAPD/OFD ratio showed a statistically significant difference with a large effect size (Mann–Whitney U = 1235, p < 0.001; rank-biserial correlation = 0.536), whereas the FAPD/HC ratio demonstrated a statistically significant difference with a moderate effect size (Mann–Whitney U = 1793, p < 0.001; rank-biserial correlation = 0.327). MID also differed significantly between groups, with a small effect size (Mann–Whitney U = 1964, p = 0.006; rank-biserial correlation = 0.263). In contrast, OFD did not differ significantly between groups and showed only a small effect size (t = 1.563, df = 144, p = 0.120; Cohen’s d = 0.259). Similarly, the Evans index was not significantly different between groups, with a negligible effect size (t = − 0.552, df = 144, p = 0.574; Cohen’s d = − 0.091).

Intraobserver reproducibility was excellent for all cranial measurements. The single-measure ICCs were 0.972 for FAPD, 0.971 for OFD, 0.953 for MWFH, and 0.943 for MID. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated minimal mean differences between repeated measurements (0.000 mm for FAPD, 0.005 mm for OFD, 0.000 mm for MWFH, and 0.005 mm for MID). The corresponding 95% limits of agreement were − 1.049 to 1.049 mm, − 2.381 to 2.391 mm, − 1.105 to 1.105 mm, and − 3.926 to 3.936 mm, respectively. These findings indicate no relevant systematic difference between repeated measurements (Supplementary Table 2).

Linear regression analysis

In the adjusted models (Table 2), GDM status was not independently associated with FAPD (adjusted mean difference: 0.229 mm; 95% CI: −0.217 to 0.676; p = 0.312), MWFH (adjusted mean difference: 0.413 mm; 95% CI: −0.167 to 0.992; p = 0.161), MID (adjusted mean difference: 1.369 mm; 95% CI: −0.139 to 2.877; p = 0.075), or Evans index (adjusted mean difference: 0.000; 95% CI: −0.004 to 0.005; p = 0.809).

In contrast, GDM remained independently associated with lower OFD (adjusted mean difference: −2.516 mm; 95% CI: −3.730 to − 1.303; p < 0.001), higher FAPD/OFD ratio (adjusted mean difference: 1.088; 95% CI: 0.633 to 1.542; p < 0.001), and higher FAPD/HC ratio (adjusted mean difference: 0.150; 95% CI: 0.033 to 0.268; p = 0.012). These findings indicate that the proportional frontal indices remained significantly different after adjustment for fetal size and maternal clinical covariates, whereas the absolute FAPD, MWFH, MID, and Evans index did not show independent associations with GDM. Because FAPD was not independently associated with GDM whereas OFD was significantly lower, the higher FAPD/OFD ratio in the adjusted model is at least partly driven by its denominator and does not by itself indicate greater absolute frontal lobe growth.

In post-hoc cranial size–adjusted sensitivity analyses, GDM remained independently associated with higher FAPD after adjustment for gestational age at ultrasound, HC, maternal age, BMI, and parity (adjusted mean difference: 0.529 mm; 95% CI: 0.169 to 0.888; p = 0.004). In contrast, GDM was not associated with MWFH after adjustment for gestational age at ultrasound, MID, maternal age, BMI, and parity (adjusted mean difference: 0.020 mm; 95% CI: −0.318 to 0.359; p = 0.905). These findings suggest that the frontal finding persisted after cranial size adjustment, whereas the apparent difference in absolute frontal horn width was largely explained by inner cranial diameter (Supplementary Table 3).

Obstetric and neonatal outcomes

Gestational age at delivery was lower in the GDM group compared with controls (38.2 [37.2–39.0] vs. 39.0 [38.0–40.0]; p < 0.001). Birth weight was similar (3358 ± 484 vs. 3274 ± 470 g; p = 0.295). Preterm birth (17.8% vs. 12.3%; p = 0.355) and fetal distress (6.8% vs. 8.2%; p = 0.754) were not significantly different. Cord blood gas pH was similar (7.39 [7.37–7.40] vs. 7.38 [7.35–7.40]; p = 0.114). Although Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min showed statistically significant between-group differences, median scores remained high in both groups. Other neonatal morbidity indicators, including NICU admission, TTN, respiratory support, phototherapy, neonatal hypoglycemia, and CAPO, were comparable. Phototherapy (4.1% vs. 1.4%; p = 0.620) and neonatal hypoglycemia (1.4% vs. 0%; p > 0.99) were rare and not significantly different. CAPO occurred in 28.8% of the GDM group and 23.3% of controls (p = 0.451) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this prospective comparative observational study, pregnancies complicated by GDM showed higher size-adjusted frontal lobe indices than normoglycemic controls. Both the FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC ratios were increased. Absolute FAPD was also modestly higher in the GDM group, although this difference did not persist in the primary adjusted model. MWFH and MID were slightly larger in pregnancies with GDM. In contrast, the fetal EI did not differ between groups. Within the GDM cohort, neurosonographic measures were comparable between diet-controlled and insulin-treated pregnancies.

Frontal lobe biometry has been proposed as a practical approach to assess regional brain growth on third-trimester ultrasound, complementing global measures such as head circumference. In pregnancies complicated by placental insufficiency, altered brain growth and volume have been reported in fetuses with fetal growth restriction (FGR) and in those who are small for gestational age. Together, these findings suggest that adverse intrauterine conditions may influence fetal brain development in a region-specific manner [17, 27, 28, 29].

The frontal lobe indices used in this study have also been evaluated in other obstetric contexts, although the number of available studies is limited. In particular, decreased FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC ratios have been reported in FGR and interpreted as evidence of relatively diminished frontal lobe development, implying that the frontal region may be relatively more vulnerable under placental insufficiency conditions [17]. In our GDM cohort the frontal indices moved in the opposite direction. We measured neither cerebral hemodynamics nor glycemic burden, so the present data cannot separate a metabolic contribution from a hypoxic–hemodynamic one; the contrast is raised as a hypothesis for future work rather than as an explanation supported by our findings.

In a study including 634 fetuses diagnosed with congenital heart disease (CHD) and 677 normal fetuses, fetal ultrasound screening was performed after 19 weeks’ gestation and BPD, HC, FAPD and the FAPD/OFD ratio were compared across CHD subtypes and controls. The FAPD/OFD ratio was significantly lower than in controls and this reduction became more pronounced as the expected cerebral arterial oxygen saturation decreased [28]. In addition, another study of 137 fetuses with CHD in the second trimester demonstrated lower FAPD/OFD ratio, MCA-PI and cerebroplacental ratio (CPR), particularly in left-sided obstructive lesions. The correlations between frontal measurements and cerebral/placental Doppler parameters suggest that regional brain development may be associated not only with the structural cardiac diagnosis but also with circulatory dynamics [29].

Our findings support a growing body of literature linking maternal hyperglycemia with measurable differences in fetal brain structures. Although GDM-specific evidence remains limited, a recent case-control study reported higher FAPD/OFD ratios in GDM pregnancies than in controls. The same study found no significant differences in ultrasound measures between insulin-treated and non-insulin-treated groups, which aligns with our findings [24]. These findings raise the possibility that the frontal differences observed in GDM are region-specific rather than a uniform reflection of overall fetal growth; however, they represent isolated prenatal imaging biomarkers of unknown functional relevance and should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Metabolic processes accompanying GDM (such as maternal glycemic fluctuations, fetal hyperinsulinemia, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses) have been proposed as potential mechanisms that may exert selective effects on the rapidly developing frontal cortex [7, 8]. In this context, the metabolic intrauterine environment associated with GDM may be hypothesized to influence frontal lobe development, although this cannot be established by the present cross-sectional design.

The EI is widely used in adult and pediatric neuroimaging to interpret ventricular enlargement [18, 26, 30], but adult or pediatric thresholds such as the commonly cited value of approximately 0.30 should not be applied as pathological cut-offs in fetal life, which requires a gestational age–specific reference framework. Using the fetal EI nomogram of Sakcak et al. [25], we classified each fetus against the gestational age–specific 95th percentile: mean EI was approximately 0.26 in both groups, and only one fetus (in the control group) exceeded that percentile. Therefore, although MWFH was slightly larger in pregnancies with GDM, EI values were overwhelmingly compatible with the expected fetal reference distribution. These findings support the absence of disproportionate ventricular–cranial enlargement at the group level. Accordingly, in the fetal period, EI should be interpreted as a supportive proportional measure within a gestational age–specific context rather than as a standalone diagnostic threshold.

In a 2023 observational study, maternal hyperglycemia was significantly associated with several fetal brain biometric measures, including larger posterior LV width, CSP width and cisterna magna. In the same study, transverse cerebellar diameter was reported to be lower [31]. Correspondingly, in a retrospective study involving 231 pregnant women, both preexisting diabetes mellitus and GDM group fetuses had significantly larger CSP and LV widths compared to their own control groups [32]. These findings suggest that hyperglycemia may affect not only overall growth but also regional morphometric differentiation in the fetal brain.

Quantitative neurosonography has also been applied to maternal nutritional exposures. In a prospective study of 422 pregnancies scanned at 28 to 30 weeks, women with vitamin D deficiency had shallower insular, parieto-occipital and calcarine fissures, a thinner corpus callosum, and lower operculization and cortical maturation grades than women with sufficient levels; the association with delayed cortical development persisted after adjustment [33]. That work concerns a different maternal exposure and cannot be read as evidence about GDM. It does, however, support the premise underlying our own study, namely that maternal metabolic and nutritional conditions can be associated with measurable differences in quantitative markers of fetal brain development.

Similarly, in our study, which is among the first to evaluate GDM and the EI concurrently, we observed a slight increase in the absolute MWFH in the GDM group, which is directionally consistent with previously reported increases in ventricular width. However, the mean EI was similar between the GDM and control groups. This finding suggests that, in the population studied, ventricular size increased proportionally with overall cranial growth rather than demonstrating disproportionate ventricular enlargement.

Because FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC share FAPD as a common numerator, these indices should not be interpreted as independent confirmations of the same biological finding. The denominators deserve equal attention. In the adjusted models OFD was lower in the GDM group while FAPD was not independently higher, so part of the FAPD/OFD difference reflects a shorter occipitofrontal axis rather than a longer frontal segment. The ratio is therefore better described as a shift in cranial proportion than as evidence of increased absolute frontal lobe growth. To address this issue, post-hoc cranial size–adjusted sensitivity analyses were performed. FAPD remained higher in the GDM group after adjustment for gestational age at ultrasound, HC, maternal age, BMI, and parity; however, because FAPD did not remain significant after adjustment for estimated fetal weight in the primary model, the persistence of this difference was sensitive to the choice of cranial-size covariate and should be interpreted with caution. Conversely, MWFH was no longer associated with GDM after adjustment for MID and gestational age, suggesting that the initial difference in frontal horn width was largely explained by inner cranial diameter rather than disproportionate ventricular enlargement.

Although the GDM group delivered at a significantly earlier gestational age, birth weight, preterm birth rates and major neonatal morbidity indicators were broadly comparable between groups. Likewise, NICU admission, respiratory support requirements, cord blood pH values and CAPO rates did not differ significantly; Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were statistically lower in the GDM group, although median scores remained high in both groups. Overall, short-term perinatal morbidity was largely comparable between groups. These outcomes were infrequent, however, and the study was not powered to test them as an explanation for the neurosonographic differences.

Within the GDM cohort, neurosonographic parameters did not differ significantly between diet-controlled and insulin-treated pregnancies. This aligns with reports from some settings indicating similar obstetric and neonatal outcomes across treatment groups [34]. The absence of between-group differences in the EI, MWFH, MID, and frontal proportional indices suggests that the observed neurosonographic pattern may not be primarily related to treatment intensification. In the CAPO analysis, and because preterm delivery is itself a defining component of the composite, gestational age at birth was not treated as an independent feature of CAPO; among the non-definitional outcomes, CAPO pregnancies had a lower birth weight, yet frontal lobe ratios and EI did not differ between CAPO and non-CAPO pregnancies. Only 21 pregnancies met the CAPO definition, so this comparison was underpowered and cannot exclude a link between perinatal morbidity and the neurosonographic measures.

In the adjusted analyses, GDM remained associated with higher FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC ratios after controlling for fetal size and maternal clinical covariates. However, because the original measurements were not blinded and stored images were not available for post-hoc blinded re-measurement, residual measurement bias cannot be excluded. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of GDM-related fetal brain remodeling.

Importantly, the frontal proportional differences observed in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of impaired neurodevelopment. They represent isolated prenatal imaging biomarkers, and their functional relevance remains unknown. Only longitudinal studies correlating fetal neurosonographic measurements with standardized postnatal cognitive, behavioral, and neurodevelopmental assessments can determine whether these findings reflect adaptive proportional scaling or indicate increased neurodevelopmental vulnerability.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the design was observational. We assessed fetuses at a single third-trimester time point; therefore, individual growth trajectories could not be evaluated. Second, detailed glycemic parameters, including HbA1c levels, glucose monitoring records, and continuous glucose monitoring indices, were not available. Important maternal metabolic variables such as gestational weight gain and lipid profile were also not assessed, limiting the evaluation of potential dose–response relationships between glycemic burden and fetal neurosonographic measurements. Third, fetal sex data were not available; therefore, fetal sex could not be included as a covariate in the adjusted analyses. Fourth, neurosonographic parameters were not reported as gestational age–adjusted z-scores because standardized and externally validated reference charts are not consistently available for several of these measures in late gestation. The diet-controlled versus insulin-treated and CAPO subgroup comparisons were based on small subgroups and were therefore underpowered; their null results should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. A further limitation is that all ultrasound examinations were performed by a single, non-blinded operator. Consequently, expectation-related measurement bias cannot be completely excluded. Although repeated measurements demonstrated excellent agreement, the reproducibility assessment was based on a second ultrasound examination rather than repeated measurements on identical stored images. Therefore, the reported reproducibility reflects both measurement repeatability and image reacquisition variability, rather than pure intraobserver measurement repeatability. Because standardized stored images were not systematically available, blinded post-hoc re-measurement by a second operator could not be performed. In addition, the 95% limits of agreement for the individual cranial measurements were wider than the observed between-group differences, indicating that small absolute differences in single linear measurements should be interpreted with caution. However, the primary analyses were based on the FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC indices, which integrate paired cranial measurements rather than relying on isolated linear dimensions. A differential bias of approximately 1.088% points for FAPD/OFD and 0.150% points for FAPD/HC would be required to fully explain the adjusted associations. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in future studies incorporating blinded image assessment and independent measurement verification.

Conclusion

In this prospective comparative observational study, pregnancies complicated by GDM showed higher fetal frontal proportional indices, including FAPD/OFD and FAPD/HC, whereas the Evans index remained unchanged. These findings suggest possible alterations in proportional frontal measurements with preserved ventricular–cranial proportionality, rather than overt ventricular enlargement. Because absolute FAPD was not independently associated with GDM in the primary adjusted model while OFD was lower, the higher ratios partly reflect their denominator and should not be read as evidence of greater absolute frontal lobe growth. However, because these measurements represent isolated prenatal imaging biomarkers, their clinical and neurodevelopmental significance cannot be determined from antenatal ultrasound data alone. Longitudinal follow-up is imperative. Only by correlating these prenatal neurosonographic measures with standardized postnatal cognitive, behavioral and neurodevelopmental assessments can it be determined whether the observed differences represent adaptive proportional scaling or a marker of neurodevelopmental vulnerability. Future prospective studies should therefore include blinded standardized image analysis, detailed glycemic profiling, and structured long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up.
