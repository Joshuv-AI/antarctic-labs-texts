Overcoming health misinformation in marginalized groups: a systematic review
Authors: Charles Senteio, Sheldon D Fields, Ranjit Kaur Pritam Singh, Regina Mariam Namata Kamoga, Emma Andrews, Dana Gandsman, Candida Halton, Veronika Rysinova, Sally Snow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Marginalized populations experience persistent health disparities that may be exacerbated by exposure to health misinformation. This systematic review aimed to identify interventions designed to address health misinformation in marginalized populations and characterize their effectiveness for changing health beliefs, intentions or behaviors.MethodsMedline, Embase, PsycINFO and Web of Science Core Collection were searched from inception to January 2025, with additional hand-searching of journals and citations. All studies were assessed for eligibility by two reviewers. Quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method studies were eligible if they aimed to address health misinformation in a marginalized population and reported an outcome relating to health beliefs, intentions or behaviors. Studies that focused only on program design or engagement, and those lacking outcome data, were excluded. The Effective Public Health Practice Project Quality Assessment Tool and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Quality Appraisal Checklist were used to assess study quality and risk of bias. Findings were described through narrative synthesis.ResultsThirty-two studies met eligibility criteria; seven were assessed as moderate quality, 25 as weak. Studies were classified based on intervention components: message-focused (n = 17); capacity- or trust-building (n = 6); or both (n = 9). Studies of message-focused interventions provided moderate-to-low quality evidence for use of pre/debunking to change misinformation beliefs and to increase intentions to comply with recommended health behaviors. Sixteen studies included community engagement in design, and 14 were delivered by community members; early community participation in ideation, design or delivery was reported to improve engagement and trust in health information, contributing to intervention efficacy. However, most studies had limitations in randomization, blinding, and outcome measurement.Conclusion Evidence on interventions to overcome health misinformation in marginalized populations remains limited and generally weak. Debunking and prebunking approaches show promise in changing health beliefs and intentions in some marginalized groups. Representatives from marginalized populations should be routinely involved in design, delivery and evaluation of such interventions.Registration The protocol and search strategy were registered and published in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024550534).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02657-2.Keywords: Systematic review, Misinformation, Marginalized population, Low-income population, Community-based participatory research

Background

Health misinformation is commonly defined as information which is false, inaccurate or misleading, according to the best available evidence at the time, and shared without an intent to mislead [1, 2]. It has been conceptualized as a contagion, with stages of susceptibility, spread, and immunization [3]. Although not a new phenomenon, the reach and speed with which misinformation can proliferate has grown exponentially with the rise of social media, amplified by algorithms that prioritize sensational content [4, 5]. As a result, misinformation can overwhelm accurate information in terms of visibility and influence, making it a pressing public health concern [3–8].

Exposure to misinformation has been correlated with false beliefs [9], and such beliefs are associated with reduced health protective behaviors, such as screening and vaccination [8–11], as well as instances of poisoning [12], and increased psychological burden [8]. Misinformation exposure can exacerbate polarization, distrust, and exclusion within societies [4, 13–18], and false beliefs are associated with reported anger and mistrust in government [19]. Misleading claims circulated in traditional and social media have fueled stigma and discrimination in contexts ranging from mpox [20], HIV [21] and female genital mutilation (FGM) [17]. COVID-19 origin rumors led to increased marginalization of some minority communities [22]. However, the link from misinformation exposure, to adoption of false beliefs, through to enacted harmful behavior is complex and evidence is limited [3, 23, 24].

‘Marginalized’ has previously been used as an umbrella term to describe people and communities who are excluded from mainstream social, educational, economic or cultural life [25–27]. In a health context, Baah et al. (2019, pg 9) describe marginalization as a process through which persons are ‘pushed to a position in society where their perceived and objective distance to basic resources … leads to an increased risk for poor health outcomes or persistent health disparity’ [26]. Examples of marginalized populations include, but are not limited to, historically excluded racial or ethnic groups [25, 26, 28], migrants or refugees [5, 27], those experiencing homelessness [29], those with differences in physical or mental health [25–27], those with low socioeconomic status [25, 26], urban slum dwellers [25, 26, 30, 31], those with non-conforming gender or sexual identity [32], and, in some cultures, women [5]. Such groups may be disproportionately susceptible to misinformation owing to greater exposure [33–36], internalized stigma [37], lower health or digital literacy [38], limited access to accurate health information in an understandable language or format [6, 39–41], increased community insularity [42], and lack of trust in official health information sources [28, 35, 36, 43–48]. False narratives rooted in historical marginalization and racism (e.g., ‘vaccines cause infertility’ or ‘HIV was created as genocide’) can further undermine trust and deepen health inequities [8, 34, 42].

Approaches to counter misinformation include debunking (fact-based correction after exposure to misinformation) [49, 50], and inoculation or ‘prebunking’ (building resistance before exposure) [50]. Strong, detailed corrections by trusted sources have been shown to shift false beliefs [8, 51, 52], while weaker or poorly framed corrections may be less effective [53] and may even inadvertently strengthen false beliefs [49, 51]. Narrative message-framing strategies, which embed corrective information within cultural and personal worldview, also show promise [54–56]. Yet most evidence for the effectiveness of these techniques comes from WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) populations [57–59]. In marginalized groups, where mistrust of authorities is common [33, 60], there is uncertainty about the most effective approaches to address misinformation [8, 33, 36].

To date, no systematic review has examined interventions to address health misinformation specifically in marginalized populations [5, 6, 28, 34]. This review therefore aimed to identify such interventions, and to characterize their reported effectiveness in changing relevant health beliefs, intentions, or behaviors.

Methods

This review was conducted with reference to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [61] and recorded using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist [62] (Supplementary File 1). The review protocol prespecified the following research question: for marginalized populations, globally, which interventions targeting health misinformation result in a change in belief in misinformation, sharing of misinformation, or intended/actual adherence to recommended health behavior(s)? The protocol was registered in PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024550534), and updated for clarification and internal consistency during final analysis (Supplementary File 2).

Search strategy

Four relevant bibliographic databases were searched to identify and retrieve studies for screening: Medline, Embase, APA PsycINFO, and Web of Science Core Collection. Initial database searches were conducted between May 24, 2024, and June 4, 2024, with an updated search of Medline, PsycINFO and Embase through to January 6, 2025. The Web of Science Core Collection search was updated through to July 23, 2025, owing to limitations with applying exact date filters. No language, geographic or publication date restrictions (other than those outlined above) were applied.

The search strategy used the Boolean operator ‘AND’ to combine terms relating to misinformation, with terms relating to marginalized populations in general, or specific marginalized groups. Full lists of subject heading and keyword search terms are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary File 3) and the Medline search is published on searchRxiv [63]. Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant review articles were screened, and five journals were hand-searched between January 1, 2014, and December 11, 2024: Journal of Health Communication, Health Communication, American Journal of Public Health, International Journal of Public Health, and International Journal for Equity in Health. Retrieved articles were reviewed to identify any unique records that were not picked up by other search strategies.

Eligibility criteria

We included qualitative and quantitative data from primary research studies of any design, published in peer-reviewed journals, with no limits on date of publication or geography. In view of the range of definitions of health misinformation used in the literature [1, 2, 64], we considered studies to be within scope if the study authors specified that the intervention was designed to address health misinformation. Marginalized groups were defined as those at risk of persistent health disparities because of systemic economic, social or environmental exclusion relative to the dominant group(s) in their own country (informed by Baah et al. 2019 [26]). In practice, factors used to determine eligibility of a population for inclusion in this review were: marginalization based on race/ethnicity, language barriers or education, gender or sexual identity, socioeconomic position, age, occupation, residency or health status. Acknowledging that there are important health disparities between countries, we chose to look at marginalized groups at a country level, because health and social care policy is primarily designed, funded and delivered at this level.

Screening and data collection process

Records retrieved from database searches were imported into the review platform Covidence to facilitate organization and removal of duplicates. A team of researchers (listed in acknowledgements) independently screened records in duplicate based on title and abstract to identify potentially relevant studies. Conflicts were resolved through discussion (VR, SS). Subsequently, full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility by two reviewers (SS, VR) against inclusion criteria (Table 1). Reasons for exclusion were noted. Disagreements between individual reviewers’ judgments were resolved through discussion and consensus achieved with a third reviewer (CH). Reviewers were not blinded to the journal titles, study authors, or institutions.

Eligible articles were manually reviewed to search for duplicates based on study identification number, author names, location and setting, intervention details, participant information, and date and duration of the study. Multiple reports of the same trial were collated, with the primary report prioritized for data extraction. If information was unclear or missing, details were inferred from context where possible; otherwise, variables were marked as “not reported.”

Relevant information was extracted systematically from included studies using a standardized extraction form, including capturing details of interventions based on the TIDieR checklist [65], as well as additional fields relevant to the topic of misinformation and marginalized populations (Supplementary File 4). Data extraction was conducted in duplicate by two independent reviewers (SS and VR) and consensus reached through discussion with a third reviewer (CH).

Study quality and risk of bias assessment

The Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies was used to assess quality and risk of bias, at study level, for studies with quantitative outcomes [66]. The EPHPP tool was chosen because it covers a wide range of study designs including randomized controlled studies, controlled clinical trials, cohort analytic (2-group pre- and post-) studies, case-control studies, cohort (one group pre-and post-) studies and interrupted time series. Each study was appraised, using the EPHPP scoring frameworks and supplementary guidance, by two independent reviewers (SS, VR). Global quality ratings reflect consensus scores based on EPHPP domain criteria: 1 (strong), 2 (moderate), and 3 (weak). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (CH). Additionally, for qualitative studies, or qualitative components of mixed-methods studies, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Quality appraisal checklist was used to assess quality [67]. Qualitative study appraisals were conducted by one reviewer (VR).

Data synthesis and presentation

A narrative synthesis was employed, considering the diverse range of included study designs, populations, and subjective and objective outcomes measured. Intervention classification was developed iteratively during data extraction and synthesis, based on delivery characteristics captured through the adapted TIDieR framework. Studies were grouped for synthesis based primarily on the component(s) of the misinformation intervention evaluated: 1) message-focused components; or 2) community-focused components (i.e., capacity building or relationship/trust building), or a combination of the two. To align with established frameworks for addressing misinformation [50], message focus was further classified based on when it addressed misinformation exposure (i.e., preemptively, to increase resistance to misinformation messages that are not yet established; or reactively, to address established misinformation). Additional factors considered for narrative synthesis included features of how messaging was framed: whether fact-based (debunking), narrative messaging or personal testimony were included. Furthermore, the extent and guiding framework(s) for patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) in intervention design and delivery were considered. Reported outcomes were categorized as: 1) knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes (KBA), which included shifts in capacity to identify and reject myths, trust in information or organizations, or changes in attitude toward health protective measures; 2) behavioral intentions, defined as stated likelihood or willingness to take a health-related action in the future, such as intention to get vaccinated, engage in a health-protective practice, share accurate information with others, or join a health program; and 3) actual or reported health behaviors, which included behavior changes or health-related actions taken after the intervention.

Study characteristics, outcomes and risk of bias were summarized in structured tables (Supplementary File 6). Effect measures (e.g. odds ratios, mean differences, percentage change) were reported as presented in the original study reports (Supplementary File 8); no statistical conversions were undertaken. A PRISMA flow diagram illustrated the study selection process, and intervention types were visually summarized in a classification diagram.

Results

Figure 1 outlines the main search process and results. Thirty-two reports were determined to meet eligibility criteria, after screening 12,850 records retrieved from bibliographic databases, 242 records identified in citation searching, and 103 records identified from handsearching journals. A further 22 studies were identified that met the inclusion criteria in terms of intervention design, but had not reported relevant outcomes at the time of this review. A list of these studies is included in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary File 5).

Nineteen studies were based in the US, and a further 14 countries are represented with one study in each (one study was conducted in both the US and UK). Nine studies were based in middle-income countries (as defined by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development [OECD] [102]) including China, Ghana, Guatemala, India, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and South Africa. One study was conducted in a country on the OECD Least Developed list (Bangladesh) [81]. COVID-19-related misinformation was the main focus of 22 studies, with other topics including misinformation relating to vaccination programs for influenza, human papilloma virus (HPV) and measles, mumps and rubella (MMR); prevention of valley fever; use of nicotine replacement and e-cigarettes; menstrual health; FGM; and breastfeeding.

Marginalized populations represented in more than one study included: Latinx or Spanish-speaking people in the US [75, 79, 85, 88, 89, 97, 101], indigenous populations [76, 87], other historically marginalized racial or ethnic groups [52, 86, 92, 97, 100], women in low- or middle-income countries [81, 83, 96], vaccine-hesitant populations [68, 95], people living in isolated rural communities [69, 81, 91, 93], young people [71, 77, 100] and older adults [70, 73]. Those with low-status employment [84], incarcerated people [90], refugee populations [82], extra-medical opioid users [74], and urban slum dwellers [72] were represented in one study each.

Seventeen studies included only message-focused components; six studies included only community-focused components; and nine studies combined these two approaches (Fig. 2). Message-focused intervention components were delivered preemptively in four studies and reactively in 22 studies (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Reactive messages were predominantly based on fact sharing or debunking, with three interventions incorporating narrative framing and one further intervention designed to discredit the source of the message [52]. Community-focused intervention components were reported in 15 studies (Table 2), which were further sub-divided into: a) capacity-building approaches that trained or equipped individuals or CBOs to recognize misinformation and share their knowledge with their wider community (5 studies); and b) outreach activities, aiming to strengthen trust in health information, institutions, or health providers (13 studies).

Sixteen of 32 studies reported some degree of community involvement in intervention design, implementation or evaluation, ranging from a survey-based needs assessment [73, 100], to consultations on content or delivery [86, 103], through to more comprehensive co-design and engagement models [89, 92, 93, 97], including community-based participatory research (CBPR) [75, 82, 85, 87, 104] frameworks. Fourteen interventions were delivered by members of the community, such as peers, faith leaders, or community-based organizations [69, 75, 81, 86, 92, 95–97]. Nine interventions were delivered in full or in part by community health workers (CHWs) or health promoters [75, 79, 85, 87–89, 91–93], defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as health workers based in communities, who have received some training but are not considered healthcare professionals (HCPs) [105]. Other interventions were delivered by academics [68, 70, 74, 76, 94], HCPs [52, 72, 75, 77, 83, 89, 91, 97, 101], politicians [52], public health organizations [77, 78], or other moderators or trainers [81, 84]. Two interventions were presented as simulated interactions between animated characters representing HCPs and patients [71, 82].

A range of relevant outcomes were reported in the included studies (Table 2), and these were classified as knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes (KBA), behavioral intentions, or actual/reported behaviors. Twenty-two studies reported KBA outcomes, including belief in misinformation, trust in information sources, or ability to recognize features associated with misinformation. Behavioral intentions, such as the intention to vaccinate self or a minor, were reported as an outcome in 11 studies. Health behaviors, such as screening or vaccination uptake, were reported as outcomes in 12 studies, of which 7 were based on self-reported data and 5 were based on health records.

Quality and risk of bias in studies

Studies included in this review were assessed to determine quality, identify confounders and potential sources of bias. Twenty-five studies were assessed as weak based on the EPHPP global rating, with only seven studies assigned a moderate quality rating based on study design and reporting (Table 2 and Supplementary File 6). However, most of these 25 studies scored strong or moderate quality for the ‘selection bias’ sub-domain, indicating that participants were likely to be adequately representative of the target population. Study design ratings were mixed, owing to the lack of randomization in most studies, potentially leading to allocation bias. Lack of blinding for participants and/or outcome assessors, lack of consideration of potential confounders, and unclear validity or reliability of data collection methods, were judged to be potential sources of bias for most of the included studies.

Three mixed-methods studies with qualitative components [88, 90, 91] were assessed using the NICE quality appraisal tool [67]. Based on what could be ascertained from the reports, few checklist criteria were fulfilled, meaning that the conclusions of the papers are likely or very likely to alter (Supplementary File 7). Issues were noted around limited sampling, lack of reflexivity, and superficial analytic depth. In González-Salinas et al. [88], the description of moderator roles and expectations lacked clarity, for example whether any training was undertaken to reduce bias, and what methods were used to ensure cultural sensitivity in their interactions. This omission made it difficult to assess how potential moderator influence on group dynamics was managed. Furthermore, reliability checks, such as double coding or confirmation by multiple researchers, were not mentioned in any study, potentially weakening the rigor of the qualitative analysis.

Evidence of effectiveness

Interventions involving inoculation (preemptive messaging)

Four studies evaluated inoculation-based approaches to increase resistance to COVID-19 misinformation in marginalized populations. In a study of unvaccinated individuals in the US, Piltch-Loeb et al. [68] found significant differences in ability to recognize rhetorical strategies used in misinformation, as well as increased intentions to vaccinate, between participants viewing video-based inoculation messages (narrative, fact-based or hybrid framing), compared with a control group. No significant differences were found between the different message framings [68]. Ramirez et al. [69] found that inoculation-based peer-modelling videos (narrative messaging), co-developed with representatives from rural communities in Southern Texas, led to significantly higher engagement with the COVID-19 vaccination program, compared to viewing generic public health messages only. There was some evidence of a stronger effect of Spanish-language messages (49.7 engagements per 1,000 people) than English-language messages (30.5 engagements per 1,000 people), suggesting possible language/cultural differences in receptiveness [69]. Two further studies employed inoculation as part of games designed for older and younger audiences, respectively: Xiong et al. [70] evaluated narrative messaging, delivered in a game format, compared with traditional media; Cook et al. [71] evaluated an inoculation approach based on logic and facts. Both game-based inoculation approaches resulted in improved accuracy in identifying misinformation or discerning facts from fallacies [70, 71], but this improved discernment appeared to be limited to the specific myths and facts encountered within the game itself [70]. The logic and fact game-based inoculation approach was additionally associated with improvements in some (but not all) beliefs about vaccination and increased intention to receive vaccination [71]. Notably, among 52 participants who were vaccine hesitant at baseline, 28 (53%) said that they were likely or very likely to receive vaccination after playing the game [71]. Limited community input was a feature of the design phase of two interventions: a co-design workshop [71] and input into localizing content [69]. Overall, these studies provide moderate to weak quality evidence that inoculation interventions (regardless of framing) can improve behavioral intentions and KBA outcomes, including ability to discern fact from myth. None of the included studies evaluated inoculation messaging for changing health behaviors.

Interventions involving reactive refutation messaging

Thirteen studies evaluated reactive refutation messaging strategies targeting marginalized populations. Of these, five directly compared different messaging strategies (four were RCTs), three evaluated refutation messaging as part of broader health information campaigns, and five were single-arm studies with a pre- and post- intervention comparison.

In one of four RCTs, Armand et al. [72] reported a modest but significant increase in intention to follow recommended COVID-19 prevention practices among participants who received a physician-delivered debunking voice message on their mobile phone, compared with uninformative control messages. Importantly, as the target population were slum residents in urban cities marked by religious tensions, the effect of the debunking intervention was enhanced by a religiously-concordant introduction to the message [72]. In a second RCT, Yousuf et al. [73] found that elderly participants in the Netherlands exposed to a video containing debunking messages were significantly more likely to reject vaccine myths, expressed more favorable views about government transparency, and had higher trust in the national vaccination program, compared to those who viewed an informational video that reinforced social norms. In an RCT conducted among US adults reporting extra-medical opioid use, Parker et al. [74] found that brief corrective messaging about nicotine replacement therapy and e-cigarettes reduced false beliefs relative to a no-message control group, but these differences were not significant in models adjusting for participant education. In a fourth randomized study conducted among low-income, Spanish-Speaking immigrant parents and caregivers in Los Angeles, Castellon-Lopez et al. [75] reported that viewing a culturally-tailored mobile phone intervention, incorporating fact-based debunking and peer testimonials, was associated with significantly higher intentions to vaccinate minors age 2–11 years, and significantly higher reported vaccination uptake among adolescents, compared with a wait list control. A non-randomized cohort study (Wood et al. [52]) that over-sampled minority communities in the US and UK, enabled comparison of both message framing (debunking vs messages that discredit the misinformation source) and message delivery (HCP vs political authority). Debunking, delivered by HCPs, significantly outperformed other experimental conditions, resulting in a significant reduction in negative beliefs about COVID-19 vaccines in the UK cohort, but these were not significant in the US cohort [52].

The impact of refutation messaging as part of broader health information campaigns for marginalized populations, was evaluated in three cohort analytic studies. Saucier et al. [78] reported improved knowledge, decreased belief in misinformation, and increased engagement with health protective behaviors both immediately after the intervention, and at one-week follow-up, among vulnerable residents in California, US. Deane et al. [77] reported a trend toward increased HPV vaccination uptake in adolescents attending socioeconomically deprived schools after a public health campaign, although vaccination rates were still considerably lower than they had been prior to misinformation exposure, and lower than those in non-deprived schools. Abascal Miguel et al. [76] reported that members of indigenous Maya communities in Guatemala who viewed culturally-tailored informational videos containing debunking messages had significantly higher odds of receiving their first COVID-19 vaccine (based on self-reported data), compared with those who reported not watching it.

Further evidence for refutation messaging interventions also comes from five single-arm studies conducted in marginalized populations. Riggle van Schagen et al. [79] evaluated a moderated social media forum for Spanish-speaking adults in the US, in which participants reported significant improvement in their ability to assess online information credibility, and more favorable vaccine-related beliefs and behaviors. Castro and Czura [81] observed sustained improvements in menstrual hygiene knowledge, as well as shifts in beliefs, intentions and behaviors, following a one-hour information session (including debunking messages) delivered to rural female artisanal workers in Bangladesh by an experienced local NGO. While these changes appeared to be durable at 2-year follow-up, the lack of a control group limits causal interpretation [81]. Streuli et al. [82] described a pilot study involving Somali refugees which identified positive changes in beliefs and intentions relating to the MMR vaccine after exposure to debunking messages embedded within a culturally and linguistically tailored virtual reality education platform. Ekwueme et al. [83] reported that a group health education intervention (including debunking messages) significantly reduced beliefs in misinformation about FGM, with corresponding rises in opposition to harmful practices and reductions in stigmatizing beliefs. Finally, Malik et al. [84] evaluated an in-person training program for Pakistani hospital sanitary workers, integrating refutation messages about COVID-19 myths (both prevalent conspiracy theories and traditional home remedies). The intervention was associated with significant reductions in belief in myths, but the durability of belief change and translation into protective behaviors were not assessed [84].

Collectively, these thirteen studies provide mixed evidence that refutation strategies (debunking, with or without narrative messaging) can significantly improve KBA outcomes, as well as health behavior intentions and actual (self-reported) health behaviors in marginalized communities. Contextual and recipient factors (e.g. religious concordance with the messenger, participant education level) were found to influence intervention effectiveness. Where directly compared, HCP-delivered debunking outperformed source-discrediting and political-authority delivery [52]; no studies were identified that directly compared the effectiveness of debunking vs narrative refutation strategies.

Across these refutation message-based studies, three incorporated substantial community engagement, starting at the early stages of intervention design: Castellon-Lopez et al. [75] integrated input from Hispanic community representatives through a series of community advisory boards and focus groups; Streuli et al. [82] describes close engagement with the Somali refugee community during needs assessment, modality determination, script development, prototyping, and product testing for an intervention to debunk MMR vaccine misinformation; and Abascal Miguel et al. [76] engaged with representatives of indigenous rural communities through project design, formative work, intervention design, evaluation and dissemination, ensuring close cultural and linguistic alignment with the audience, and resulting in statistically significant changes in reported vaccination.

Interventions combining reactive refutation messaging with capacity-building or trust-building activities

Seven studies evaluated interventions that combined fact-based refutation (debunking) with other community-focused activities, including outreach and/or capacity-building delivered by CHWs or health promoters [85, 87–89, 91], peer ambassadors [86], or by nurses [90]. DeGarmo et al. [85] described a cluster randomized trial evaluating a CHW-led outreach intervention (including refutation messages) that was shown to increase SARS-CoV-2 testing among Latinx communities in Oregon, compared to wait-list controls [85]. Sears et al. [87] described co-development of educational materials with 13 community health representatives from American Indian/Alaskan Native communities, which was associated with significant improvements in self-reported knowledge about the COVID-19 vaccine, and participants’ use of the tailored resources to talk to a family member or friend about getting vaccinated, but no difference in self-reported vaccination uptake [87]. Foley et al. [91] described a virtual case-based learning program for CHWs about HPV vaccination, including peer experience sharing and incorporating debunking messages, which improved knowledge accuracy, decreased belief in misinformation about the vaccine, and improved self-reported capability to discuss the vaccine with others. In qualitative findings from the same study, focus group participants described the reframing of the vaccine as cancer prevention as an ‘eye opener,’ and reported greater confidence in discussing it with parents and community members [91]. In another study of CHW-facilitated bilingual outreach events (including debunking and personal narratives) in a predominantly Hispanic US border community, Mansour et al. [89] found that most participants reported that attending the events helped them to identify and dispel healthcare myths, and felt comfortable or very comfortable teaching the topic to others. A mixed-methods evaluation of a private, health promoter-led Facebook group for Spanish-speaking Latinx parents (González-Salinas et al., 2024) revealed high levels of trust in the information shared about COVID-19; in qualitative findings, participants described the group as a welcoming and trustworthy space, praising the Spanish-speaking moderators for being empathetic and responsive, which they felt increased their confidence in vaccination decisions and encouraged supportive peer interactions [88]. Kandasamy et al. [86] described an intervention mobilizing youth from South Asian communities in Canada to act as peer ambassadors, resulting in increased knowledge about COVID-19 vaccination, and greater confidence in having evidence-based conversations within their community [86]. Similarly, as described by Kader et al. [90], participating in nurse-delivered dialogue-based education improved self-reported intention to vaccinate and tempered medical mistrust among an incarcerated population in the US. Qualitative findings from this study showed that many incarcerated participants expressed mistrust and confusion about COVID-19 measures before the sessions, but afterwards described the dialogue-based format as ‘helpful’ and ‘informative,’ highlighting that the opportunity to ask questions directly to a nurse increased their confidence in judging reliable information and making vaccination decisions [90].

Overall, these studies show that direct outreach and/or capacity building by local health information ambassadors (CHWs, health promoters, peers or nurses) may be an effective mechanism for delivering debunking messages along with personal narratives, helping to consolidate accurate knowledge and to build recipients’ confidence in sharing this knowledge with others. However, evidence to support the effectiveness of these interventions on behavioral intentions or actual health behaviors was limited by small sample sizes and weak study designs. Success factors across these interventions included cultural and linguistic concordance between the person delivering the message and the recipients, and three of the seven studied interventions were designed through CBPR or other community-engaged research methods [85, 87, 89].

Interventions combining misinformation monitoring, refutation approaches, and trust-building

Two studies evaluated interventions that aimed to build trust between CHWs and marginalized communities, as part of a monitor-and-respond approach to COVID-19 misinformation. In the US, Desens et al. [92] reported results of a cohort analytic study targeting Black/Haitian communities in Miami-Dade, Florida, and migrant agricultural workers in California’s Central Valley, which delivered tailored communication through trusted channels (e.g., social media, church bulletins, PSAs, and CHW-led sessions) after consultation with community leaders. Vaccination uptake increased in intervention areas, but significant effects were only observed in stratified zip code analyses; changes in county-wide and child vaccination rates were not statistically significant. Rodriguez et al. [93] described a study in rural Chiapas, Mexico, which adapted the WHO risk communication and community engagement (RCCE) framework, and found that tailored door-to-door outreach by CHWs increased acceptance of follow-up visits and willingness to seek care if symptomatic [93]. Overall, monitor-and-respond CHW models appear transferable across settings, but evidence is limited (pre/post designs; weak quality) and effects at broader administrative levels were inconsistent.

Interventions focused on capacity-building or trust-building

Six studies evaluated interventions solely designed to strengthen trust or build capacity within communities (rather than debunking specific myths). Three studies involved HCP- or academic-led outreach in the US. In San Bernardino County, a partnership with faith leaders and racially concordant faculty delivered more than 1,700 vaccine doses, nearly 90% of which went to Black and Hispanic residents, with high second-dose return rates (Abdul-Mutakabbir et al. 2022) [97]. A small pilot in urban high schools in Atlanta delivered by medical students improved COVID-19 knowledge and shifted attitudes toward vaccine safety (Kuehl et al. 2023) [100]. In Boston, a resident-led “Ask-a-Doc” program embedded in community vaccine events reached 15 zip codes with high density of Black residents or residents living in poverty, and was associated with 1,521 vaccine doses delivered, mostly among Black and Hispanic adolescents (Edwards et al. 2022) [101]. Collectively, studies suggest that outreach by culturally concordant HCPs may enhance KBAs and vaccine uptake, but outcomes were largely descriptive, statistical testing was often absent, and sample sizes were small.

Three studies assessed peer ambassador approaches. In the Philippines, three outreach visits by trained peer counselors were associated with significant changes in infant feeding practices post-intervention (Salud et al. 2009) [96]. In KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, a youth ambassador program improved vaccination intention and partially vaccinated rates compared with controls, though effects on belief and knowledge were non-significant and the estimated increase in full vaccination included the null; trust in government and healthcare workers improved, while endorsement of misinformation declined (Marinda et al. 2024) [95]. In a US-based study, training bilingual and multicultural ‘information navigators’ significantly increased confidence in detecting misinformation and communicating about COVID-19, though sustained engagement was limited, with only 20% completing six-week follow-up (Abdi et al. 2024) [94]. Overall, peer-led interventions showed potential to influence both behaviors and attitudes, particularly when peers shared linguistic, cultural, or age-group concordance, but evidence was limited by weak study designs and short follow-up.

Discussion

We conducted a systematic review to identify interventions designed to address misinformation in marginalized populations, and to characterize their effectiveness for changing relevant beliefs or behaviors. Thirty-two studies were identified that met the eligibility criteria; these were grouped based on their orientation to misinformation exposure/message framing, and on their inclusion of capacity-building or trust-building activities. Additionally, we considered the timing and extent of patient and public engagement and involvement in intervention design and delivery. A range of marginalized groups were represented, including historically marginalized racial and ethnic groups, people living in low-resource urban or rural settings, and those who may be marginalized based on their age, occupation, or behaviors (e.g. extra-medical opioid use). Recognizing that studies in this review were only of moderate or weak quality, as determined by EPHPP and NICE quality assessment, characteristics relating to effective intervention design are discussed.

Orientation to misinformation and message framing

Most interventions identified for this review involved responding to misinformation messages or myths that were already circulating within communities. Interventions employed a range of messaging strategies, including fact-based debunking, narrative countering, and discrediting sources.

Debunking is an extensively studied refutation approach to overcoming misinformation in the general population and has been found to be effective for reducing belief in misinformation in a range of settings [49, 106, 107]. Four RCTs in the present review provide direct comparisons between refutation messages and uninformative/information-only controls, demonstrating the potential for debunking strategies to positively influence health-related KBAs, intentions and behaviors among marginalized communities [72–75]. Furthermore, a moderate quality cohort analytic study [52] provides evidence for the effectiveness of debunking compared with an alternative messaging strategy (discrediting the source of the misinformation) for changing beliefs associated with misinformation. Non-randomized and single-arm studies provide further evidence to support effectiveness of debunking on behaviors including vaccination uptake [75, 79, 104] and hygienic menstrual practices [81]. The effectiveness of refutation techniques has previously been shown to be closely related to audience characteristics and prior beliefs, as well as trust in the person delivering the intervention, and the timing of the message [51]. Indeed, several studies in this review found that the effectiveness of debunking was modified by contextual or recipient factors, such as religious concordance of the messenger [72], or the role of the information provider [52]. The misinformation topic may also be a factor influencing the effectiveness of debunking: while several studies in this review found that debunking could improve KBAs toward protective health behaviors or vaccinations, debunking had limited effect on changing false beliefs about nicotine replacement and eCigarettes [74]. This may reflect that addictive behaviors are resistant to change, with relapse common as dependence and coping motives reinforce underlying beliefs; information-based interventions alone are often insufficient unless combined with tailored, multi-component strategies [108–110]. Overall, results from the present review provide moderate to weak evidence that debunking can be part of an effective strategy to address misinformation in marginalized groups, but that messenger credibility and cultural tailoring are likely to be important success factors.

Unlike debunking or educational approaches alone, narratives can help participants incorporate information coherently into their own personal and cultural worldview [54–56, 111–113]. In the present review, there was insufficient direct evidence to support a difference between narrative and fact-based message framing [68], and only limited evidence to support the effectiveness of combined debunking and narrative or storytelling strategies [75, 82]. In a previous systematic review, Lazic and Zezelj (2021) also found mixed results for the effectiveness of narrative interventions for addressing misinformation; however, they identified that narrative approaches were generally effective (in 14 out of 17 studies) when they were embedded within the socio-cultural environment of the target population [56]. For marginalized groups in particular, narratives offer the opportunity for greater representation and cultural tailoring [112]. Results from the present review suggest that incorporating narratives may be an effective way to address misinformation in marginalized groups, but the quality and quantity of published evidence is limited.

Prior research has shown that inoculation (preemptive) messages from trusted sources can reduce belief in new conspiracy theories, increase vaccine intentions and activate protective responses, such as critical thinking, when participants are exposed to misinformation [114–116]. In the present review, four studies used techniques based on inoculation theory [117] to increase resilience to future misinformation exposure in marginalized groups. Three out of four studies demonstrated a positive effect on intention to receive vaccination [68, 69, 71]. Additionally, these studies provide evidence that inoculation messaging can improve participants’ ability to identify misinformation [68, 70, 71]. Information poverty (i.e., lack of access to accurate, well-translated materials) among marginalized groups means that the ability to identify features of misinformation is a significant unmet need [40]. Preparing individuals to encounter and identify misinformation may be an important component of interventions designed for marginalized groups, who often have low levels of digital, health or media literacy [8, 30]. None of the inoculation studies included in this review reported an impact on actual or reported health behaviors, possibly owing to limited opportunity for long-term follow-up to determine exposure and impact. Recent work on behavioral inoculation positions relapse-mitigation procedures as a way of building resilience against future setbacks, suggesting that pairing inoculation with community or structural supports may help translate early cognitive resistance into sustained health behaviors [118].

Two interventions in the present review included a misinformation monitoring-and-response component, and provide limited evidence that monitoring can support successful adaptive interventions to improve vaccine uptake in situations where misinformation is emerging and evolving [92, 93]. Developing methods to track and prioritize misinformation has previously been shown to be important for developing tailored strategies to mitigate its effects [40, 92, 119]. Community insularity can be a feature of marginalized groups, and may mean that circulating misinformation does not align with prevalent or established misinformation in the general population [6, 120], increasing the need to monitor prevalent misinformation within the target communities. While social media-based monitoring provides access to large amounts of information in short amounts of time, this approach is limited by a lack of geographic specificity [92] and inability to include the perspectives of those who do not have access to or do not use social media [119]. In particular, social media analyses are unable to capture misinformation circulating through direct messages (e.g., WhatsApp, text message), or person-to-person within communities [119]. One of the studies included in this review described how researchers overcame the limitations of social media monitoring by using geo filters and Boolean searches, as well as local coordination of misinformation reporting through community hubs such as churches [92]. Similarly, other researchers have partnered with CHWs or CBOs to understand and respond to circulating misinformation [35]. Considering the benefits, as well as methodological challenges associated with monitoring circulating misinformation in marginalized groups, further research in this area is warranted.

Community involvement and cultural alignment as key factors in intervention design

Half of the studies (16 of 32) included in this review reported some degree of community involvement in design, albeit with a wide range in scope, depth and duration of engagement and, in some cases, a lack of detail in reporting. Ten studies that activated community partnerships early in the design process spanned intervention types (i.e., message-based or community-oriented) and message framing (e.g., narrative or fact-based) [75, 82, 85–87, 89, 92, 93, 97, 104]. Together, these studies demonstrate that interventions designed with early and close collaboration with communities can have a statistically significant positive impact on KBAs relating to health misinformation. Six of ten studies with early community engagement were associated with a change in health behaviors [75, 85–87, 92, 97], but levels of effectiveness and confidence in the findings were limited by weak study design and small sample sizes. Five studies in this review stated that they were based on CBPR – an approach rooted in principles of collaborative and equitable partnership in all phases of research, with all partners working together to identify mutual issues and to take action to address them [121]. In one of these studies [75], authors described community partnership as a key feature of intervention success, facilitating identification of prevalent misinformation, addressing mistrust among the community, and partnering with culturally and linguistically congruent HCPs who were trusted sources of information. Previous CBPR advocates have emphasized the length of time required to establish and build a trusting and equitable research relationship, often hampered by language or cultural barriers, or by historic mistreatment and injustices [122]. Given the short timeframes in which health misinformation can emerge, establishing a new community-based research partnership might be challenging. However, studies in this review demonstrated the feasibility of mobilizing rapid, effective responses to COVID-19 misinformation by building on established long-term relationships between academics or public health agencies and CBOs [75, 87, 97, 104]. Nevertheless, some study authors acknowledged that even with close consultation with CBOs and community leaders, they still struggled to include the opinions of the most resource-poor and least educated portion of their communities [93]. Previous studies have found that, for example, being female, being poor, having little formal education, living with a disability, and/or belonging to certain ethnic groups means community members have less of a voice during health priority-setting [123]. Others have highlighted the ongoing challenge that communities themselves are not homogenous and the CBOs or leaders who are most ready to engage with external agencies may not always represent the range of community interests and perspectives [122]. The present review demonstrates that CBPR and similar community engagement approaches are both feasible and effective for designing misinformation interventions that change health beliefs, intentions and behaviors across diverse marginalized groups.

Cultural alignment between the messenger delivering the intervention and the recipients was a feature of several of the interventions identified in this review. Fourteen of the 32 studies were delivered either fully or partly by members of the target community (e.g. community leaders, trained peer mentors or ambassadors, CBOs or CHWs). Interventions involving training community ambassadors were shown to be effective in increasing participants’ knowledge and confidence in sharing accurate information within their own community [86, 94], as well as improving vaccination uptake and trust in vaccination and testing programs [85, 95]. A systematic review by Ahmed et al. (2020) found that CHW programs successfully reach many (although not all) marginalized groups in low- and middle-income countries [124], meaning that they may be a useful mechanism for reaching and building trust with populations who are mistrustful or feel excluded from traditional public health information channels. These findings are aligned with a systematic review of CHW involvement in the early pandemic response, which found that CHWs’ ability to disseminate COVID-19 information and services was enabled by community trust and understanding of community needs [125].

Cultural concordance with the target population was also a defining feature of two of the HCP-delivered interventions in this review [72, 97–99]. One moderate-quality study found that the impact of a health information campaign was primarily driven by religious concordance between message sender and receiver, leading to increased engagement with HCP-provided debunking messages and compliance with recommended health practices [72]. Informative webinars led by cultural concordant faculty [98], delivered as part of a wider community engagement strategy, were associated with improved COVID-19 vaccine uptake within racially and ethnically minoritized, vaccine-hesitant communities [97]. In the wider literature, Hashim et al. (2023) commented that involving HCPs from the same cultural background as intervention participants diminished feelings of cultural conspiracy among vulnerable diaspora communities [120]. However, the impact of patient-provider cultural concordance has been found to vary between communities and types of healthcare interaction. For example, Ma et al. (2024) found that patient-provider cultural concordance among Hispanic communities in the US was associated with a higher probability of seeking preventive care (p < 0.001), seeking care for a new problem (p < 0.001), and seeking care for an ongoing problem (p < 0.001), but that there were differences between Hispanic subgroups (e.g. Mexican, Cuban origin) and also between interactions about preventative care or ongoing health problems [126]. Nevertheless, results of this review suggest that, for marginalized groups, cultural concordance between the recipient and the person or organization delivering the intervention can increase trust and improve engagement, potentially contributing to intervention effectiveness in changing belief in misinformation or intention to engage in protective health-related behaviors.

Limitations

There were limitations of this systematic review. Ambiguity in how misinformation is defined likely contributed to heterogeneity across included studies. Few study authors specified their definition of misinformation, so eligibility relied on whether the intervention explicitly aimed to address health misinformation. Interventions aiming to reduce stigma, build trust or improve health literacy may have had indirect effects on misinformation-related beliefs or behaviors, but were excluded. The concept of marginalization is also context-dependent, influenced by history, geography and politics, and eligibility decisions sometimes required subjective judgements. This was mitigated through discussion with a diverse authorship group, which included representation from academia and patient advocacy in low- and middle-income countries. Although the search strategy included a wide range of search terms for potentially marginalized groups, general population studies in low- and middle-income countries were excluded. While such populations may be considered marginalized in a global context, they did not meet this review’s inclusion criteria.

Most included studies were assessed to be of weak quality by EPHPP or NICE appraisal, limiting confidence in their findings. Marginalized groups can be challenging populations for academic study, and there is a persisting gap in high-quality research with these populations outside of community organizations and clinical and institutional settings [29]. Some marginalized groups, such as those who experience homelessness or lacking legal migration status, are understudied because they exist outside traditional research environments [29], and indeed these populations are not reflected in the studies included in this review. Evidence from other areas of health research suggest that people from marginalized groups may be more likely to withdraw from a study because of a lack of trust between participants and research staff, limited comprehension of research procedures, or time/financial constraints for participants with limited resources [127–129], although a strength of studies included here is their lower rates of withdrawal and dropout (as measured by EPHPP sub-domain). Inadequate reporting often prevented categorization of message framing or separation of effects from multi-component interventions, constraining the narrative synthesis. A further limitation is that meta-analysis was not feasible due to the heterogeneity of study designs, interventions, and outcomes, restricting our ability to generate pooled effect estimates. Outcomes were inconsistently reported, and very few studies assessed long-term behavior change, limiting conclusions about real-world impact.

To prioritize peer-reviewed evidence, grey literature was excluded, which may bias findings toward higher-resource settings. Publication bias may also have inflated positive results. Although no language limits were applied at search stage, 60 studies where details of methodology and results were not available in English were excluded, potentially under-representing research from some regions [130]. Database coverage may have introduced further geographic bias, amplifying the predominance of both US-based and English-language research included in this review. Reviewers were not blinded to study authors or journals, and formal assessments of reporting bias or certainty of evidence (e.g., GRADE) were not conducted.

Conclusion

The peer-reviewed evidence base evaluating interventions to overcome misinformation in marginalized groups is limited and generally of low quality. There is moderate-to-low quality evidence that debunking approaches may be effective for reducing belief in misinformation and promoting protective health behaviors in specific marginalized populations, and further moderate-to-low quality evidence that inoculation (prebunking) approaches may be effective for increasing recognition of misinformation, reducing willingness to share misinformation, and increasing intention to receive vaccination. The present review also demonstrates that community partnerships can be a powerful way to identify needs, build trust, and design linguistically and culturally resonant interventions that promote positive health behaviors among marginalized populations exposed to health misinformation. These findings support the recommendation that representatives from marginalized populations should be routinely involved in design, delivery and evaluation of interventions to overcome misinformation. There is a need for further primary research, of robust quality, to understand the impact of marginalization on susceptibility to misinformation and response to interventions.
