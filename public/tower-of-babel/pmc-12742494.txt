Hip arthroscopy for magnetic resonance imaging–verified subspine impingement syndrome: 3-year minimum outcomes study
Authors: Berkcan Akpinar, Edward S. Mojica, Ajay Kanakamedala, Mohammad Samim, Thomas Youm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:To determine the clinical outcomes of hip arthroscopy for anterior inferior iliac spine subspine impingement (SSI).Level of Evidence:Case Series; level IV.Methods:Patients who underwent unilateral, primary hip arthroscopy for SSI from February 2015 to December 2017 with minimum 3-year follow-up data were identified (SSI). Analysis of variance, Kaplan-Meier analysis, and regression modeling were used to analyze outcomes.Results:Of 23 eligible, 19 (83% inclusion) patients (mean [standard error of the mean]: age: 45.9 [3.1]; body mass index, 26.3 [0.9]; 79% female, 53% right side) had a minimum of 3-year follow-up (48.3 [2.2] months). The estimated mean survival time of index procedure across the whole cohort was 64.9 ± 3.1 months. One patient underwent total hip arthroplasty. Patients improved from baseline modified Harris Hip Score (62.7 [5.1]) to follow-up (75.1 [4.3]; P = .014). Twelve patients (63%) met minimal clinically importance difference criteria while 7 (37%) met patient acceptable symptomatic state criteria. Linear regression modeling demonstrated lower postoperative lateral center edge angle (beta = −2.1, 95% confidence interval: −0.5 to −2.9, P = .035) and presence of labral repairs (beta = 46.1, 95% confidence interval: 24.8–67.4, P = .003) were associated with higher follow-up modified Harris Hip Score.Conclusion:In conclusion, patients undergoing anterior inferior iliac spine decompression during hip arthroscopy for SSI in the setting of femoroacetabular impingement syndrome have acceptable, improved clinical outcomes at minimum 3-year follow-up. Labral repair and decreasing postoperative lateral center edge angle were predictive of improved patient-reported outcomes. Hip arthroscopy remains a viable successful treatment options in the treatment of concomitant SSI during femoroacetabular impingement syndrome surgery.

Introduction

Extraarticular hip impingement is increasingly being recognized as a primary source of hip pain and an etiology for persistent hip pain after hip arthroscopy.1,2 One form of extraarticular hip impingement can occur when the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) impinges on the distal femoral neck, termed subspine impingement (SSI).3,4 Initially, SSI was described as bony impingement because of an AIIS that extends distally on the anterior acetabular rim, which then causes pathologic abutment with the distal femoral neck.5–7 Recent studies have shown that SSI can also result from repetitive anterior hip soft-tissue trauma, athletic overuse, and chronic rectus injuries, all of which may contribute to dynamic soft-tissue interposition during hip range of motion.2,8–12

Similar to femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAI), SSI typically occurs during flexion and internal rotation.6 This can mask the diagnosis of SSI given that patients with SSI frequently present with symptoms and examination findings similar to FAI. In patients with hip impingement syndromes, the prevalence of SSI ranges from 23 to 32%.13,14 Often times, overlapping pathologies such as cam or pincer deformities coexist and are frequently present in patients with SSI.14 Frequently, SSI is diagnosed when patients undergo arthroscopic treatment of FAI without significant improvement.15–17

Although various techniques have been described for treating SSI since its initial description,4,6 SSI remains poorly understood with regards to clinical outcomes. To date, there have only been case series, cohort studies assessing isolated SSI, and heterogeneous case-control studies all with short-term follow-up rates evaluating hip arthroscopy for SSI.7,9,11,17,18 The largest study remains a retrospective review of 163 patients with less than a year mean follow-up.5 Thus, although authors have anecdotally pointed out that patients with FAI and concomitant SSI might not improve as much from treatment,15 it is unclear whether SSI, if recognized and treated, affects clinical outcomes.

The purpose of this case series was to determine clinical outcomes of SSI after hip arthroscopy. We hypothesized that patients undergoing hip arthroscopy for SSI and FAI would have improved mid-term clinical outcomes including patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and low conversion rates to total hip arthroplasty.

Methods

Patient selection

Before the commencement of this study, Institutional Review Board approval was obtained (i15-00058). Patients were retrospectively identified from a prospective clinical registry of patients with FAI who underwent hip arthroscopy between February 2015 and December 2017. All surgeries were performed by the senior author (TY). All patients provided informed consent to participation in research. FAI was diagnosed based on characteristic symptomatology and diagnostic examination maneuvers (Patrick test, anterior impingement test). Anteroposterior and Dunn view radiographs (45° and 90°) alongside magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or arthrography were used to identify cam, pincer, and pathologic AIIS lesions correlating with aforementioned symptoms and examination maneuvers. Previously described characteristic advanced imaging using MRI verified soft-tissue and bony pathognomonic findings correlating to SSI and intraoperative findings were further used to diagnose all SSI cases (Figure 1).13

The following indications were used for surgery: MRI evidence of SSI, lateral center edge angle (LCEA) ≥ 40°, alpha angle ≥ 60°, proximal focal acetabular retroversion, and chondrolabral lesion presence on imaging. All patients underwent a minimum of 6-month conservative management in the form of activity modifications, physical therapy, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications for symptom alleviation. Diagnostic intraarticular injections were used to confirm FAI in patients with atypical hip pain while therapeutic cortisone injections were offered to patients with standard FAI as part of the preoperative treatment regimen. Exclusion criteria consisted of bilateral hip arthroscopy, Tonnis grade >1, joint space narrowing ≤2 mm, hip dysplasia (LCEA ≤ 20°), age <18 years, systemic rheumatological disease, history of slipped capital femoral epiphysis, and any prior hip surgeries.

Surgical technique and postoperative rehabilitation protocol

Hip arthroscopy was performed using standard mid-anterior and anterolateral portals, and increased joint access was obtained through a horizontal interportal capsulotomy. Chondroplasty was performed in patients with significant chondral lesions (Outerbridge I-III), whereas chondrolabral junctional labral tears were all repaired using suture anchor fixation. Cam and pincer lesions were resected using dynamic fluoroscopy, and capsular repair was performed after irrigation and debris removal. Subspine decompression was performed as previously described.7 Briefly, the capsule was dissected to fully expose the AIIS. Once fully exposed, the AIIS prominence was resected, taking care to minimally disrupt the fibers of the direct head of the rectus femoris tendon. Patients were placed in hip abduction braces postoperatively to limit hyperextension and external rotation. Weight-bearing was restricted to flatfoot weight-bearing with crutches for the first month, followed by gradual increase in activity level as tolerated. Postoperative deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis with aspirin and heterotopic ossification prophylaxis with celecoxib was used.

Patient-reported outcomes

Modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS) was used to assess PROs for the current study. Based on previous studies, a minimum clinically important difference (MCID) threshold of 8 points and a patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) threshold of 74 points were used to further characterize mHHS outcomes.19,20 All patients received mHHS questionnaires preoperatively and at most recent follow-up after surgery. Any revision procedure in the follow-up period was also considered failing to meet MCID or PASS thresholds, in addition to the aforementioned criteria.

Data analysis

Demographic, intraoperative categorical (sex, laterality, labral repair, cam resection, pincer resection, Tonnis grade 1 vs. 0) and continuous (age, body mass index [BMI], preoperative LCEA, postoperative LCEA, preoperative alpha angle, postoperative alpha angle) variables were analyzed. Categorical variables were described using total number and percentages, and continuous variables were reported using mean value, standard error of the mean (SEM), and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Changes between preoperative and postoperative radiographic measurements and PRO values were assessed using repeated measures analysis of variance testing. MCID and PASS threshold achievement rates at follow-up were evaluated. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to determine mean estimated survival of index procedure for the cohort. A stepwise, multivariate linear regression predicting follow-up mHHS outcomes was performed using all aforementioned demographic and intraoperative variables. Regression fit was deemed a good fit if model P < .05, at which point coefficients were reported. Significance was set at P < .05, and all analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25; IBM Corp.).

Results

Clinical outcomes

All data are reported as a mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Of 23 eligible patients with SSI who met all inclusion criteria, 19 patients (83% inclusion; mean [SEM]: age, 45.9 [3.1]; BMI, 26.3 [0.9]; 79% female, 53% right side) had minimum 3-year follow-up (48.3 [2.2] months) undergoing primary hip arthroscopy from February 5, 2015, to December 14, 2017 (Figure 2 and Table 1). The majority of patients had Tonnis grade 0 (n = 15, 79%) vs. grade 1. There was a 79% distal cam prevalence, 79% superior capsular edema prevalence, 11% rectus femoris edema prevalence, 89% superior labral tearing prevalence, 84% superior chondral lesion prevalence, and 95% anterior labral tearing prevalence in the cohort on MRI. The estimated mean survival time of index procedure across the whole cohort was 64.9 ± 3.1 months (Figure 3). No patients underwent revision arthroscopy; 1 patient in the SSI group underwent a revision total hip arthroplasty during the follow-up period. Fifteen patients had cam resections (79%) including distal cam while 8 patients had pincer resections (42%) in the cohort. Seventeen patients had labral repairs (90%). Alpha angle (P = .001) improved from preoperative to postoperative measurements while LCEA (P = .095) was trending (Table 1).

Modified Harris Hip Score

There was a significant increase in mHHS scores from baseline (62.7 [5.1]) to follow up (75.1 [4.3]; P = .014) in the cohort. Twelve patients (63%) met MCID criteria while 7 (37%) met PASS criteria at last follow-up. The linear regression model predicting postoperative mHHS scores demonstrated an excellent fit (R2 = 96%, P = .005). Lower postoperative LCEA (beta = −2.1, 95% CI: −0.5 to −2.9, P = .035) and presence of labral repairs (beta = 46.1, 95% CI: 24.8–67.4, P = .003) were associated with higher follow-up mHHS (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that patients with SSI undergoing hip arthroscopy have improved clinical outcomes at minimum 3-year follow-up. This finding is important because it suggests that, when SSI is recognized and treated, patients with combined SSI and FAI experience satisfactory PROs with minimal to no revision rates. Only one patient received a conversion to a total hip arthroplasty in follow-up while no revision arthroscopies were performed. Multivariate analysis demonstrated the positive association of decreasing postoperative LCEA and presence of labral repairs with increasing PROs at follow-up. Finally, the majority of patients met MCID at follow-up while 37% met the PASS threshold as well.

Hetsroni et al.7 were some of the first surgeons to report the efficacy of SSI decompression. They found improved hip flexion alongside preoperative to postoperative mHHS from 64 to 98 points at 15-month follow-up comparison in a 10-patient cohort. In their study, SSI was defined patients with tenderness over the AIIS corresponding to AIIS overgrowth at or caudal to the anterior acetabular rim associated with pathological contact on the femoral neck as assessed by 3-dimensional computed tomography (CT) dynamic imaging.7 Cadaveric studies have since confirmed the importance of AIIS bony morphology in SSI and have found that the range of iliopsoas excursion prior to impingement during hip flexion-extension increases with incremental SSI decompression.21 Although Hetsroni et al.7 reported one of the first case series evaluating arthroscopic management of SSI, the study unfortunately had limited sample size and follow-up.

In one of the largest SSI cohorts (n = 163) studied to date, Hapa et al.5 reported a change from mHHS of 63 improving to 85 at a mean follow-up of 11.1 months, which is comparable with improvements seen in our cohort at an average of 48.3 months. In the same study, Hapa et al.5 further illustrate the anatomic landmarks for safe, effective AIIS decompression while preserving the rectus origin. Nwachukwu et al.18 examined a cohort of 33 patients with isolated SSI with a mean follow-up of 19 months and found an increase in mHHS from 57 to 79, similar to the results of this study again with shorter follow up. Although MCID rate was met by 78% of their SSI cohort, our study demonstrates a 41% achievement rate; however, this study population was older (46 vs. 26 years), had concomitant FAI, and had a longer follow-up time (48 vs. 19 months). Given the limited prevalence of SSI in patients with hip impingement syndromes ranging from 23.7 to 32%,13,14 many additional studies have reported similar outcomes in small-sized cohort analyses with follow-up ranging from 11- to 35-month average.5,7,9,11,18,22,23

Flores et al.22 performed a case-matched comparative study based on unknown matching thresholds of age, sex, and BMI assessing a 39-patient acetabular retroversion cohort vs. 39 focal pincer-related FAI controls at 1-year follow-up. Although their retroversion vs control groups both did well in terms of mHHS outcomes, 22 of 39 patients underwent added SSI decompression in the retroversion group resulting in superior PROs as compared with patients without SSI decompression. Interestingly, intraoperative prominence of the AIIS correlating with fluoroscopic impingement evidence was used to indicate patients with acetabular retroversion for SSI decompression during hip arthroscopy. There was no discussion of abnormal preoperative AIIS morphology suggesting that retroversion of the acetabulum caused a subset of patients to have SSI rather than traditional morphological differences as described by Hetsroni et al.6,7 possibly detracting from study generalizability to other SSI cohorts.

Additional discussions describing SSI to date have further emphasized the heterogeneity of etiologies resulting in pathological extraarticular impingement, which may account for the differences observed in demographic and radiographic characteristics of our study cohort as compared with previous studies.8,9,11,12,14,21 Studies have described chronic rectus femoris traction and avulsion-type injuries resulting in AIIS morphological changes such as hypertrophy with subsequent SSI.3,5,24 Furthermore, studies suggest in addition to bony abnormalities, the soft-tissue structures of the anterior hip including the capsule, labrum, and musculature also may contribute to SSI as a result of soft-tissue interposition during hip range of motion.8–10,13

In a 77-hip cohort of dancers undergoing hip arthroscopy for hip impingement syndromes, Larson et al.9 reported an 83% SSI prevalence rate. At mean 36-month follow-up, their cohort demonstrated similar PRO improvements and radiographic characteristics in comparison with our current study while notably younger (21 vs. 46 years). However, heterogeneity of the dancer population with respect to SSI prevalence amongst other forms of intraarticular and extraarticular hip impingement and diagnosis of SSI using a traditional AIIS morphology classification limit the applicability of the findings to patients with SSI. Nawabi et al.11 also demonstrate in a 34-hip (26 subject) cohort of soccer players undergoing hip arthroscopy for SSI and concomitant FAI vs. 115 nonkicking sport controls, both groups demonstrated improved mHHS outcomes (89 points) at a mean 35-month follow-up. As with Larson et al. Nawabi et al. also reported a mean age younger (19) than the current cohort while radiographic parameters were similar. Despite controls having a significantly lower 52 vs. 84% rate of abnormal AIIS morphology, a similar SSI decompression rate was observed between groups suggesting the potential role for anterior hip soft-tissue interposition contributing to SSI in this population. As may be the case in dancers, soccer players, or other athletes with FAI, Aguilera-Bohorquez et al. demonstrated using 3-D dynamic CT that although certain patients with FAI had normal AIIS morphology, they still had SSI as characterized by pathological contact between the distal femoral neck and the AIIS.8,14

As a result, this study is unique in that all patients with SSI were diagnosed with MRI-based pathognomonic findings as demonstrated in the literature.13 MRI-aided diagnosis of SSI builds on radiograph-based and CT-based hip joint evaluation. Given the variability of the traditional radiograph-based and CT-based AIIS morphological classification with respect to SSI diagnosis and outcomes,9,11,14 the use of MRI findings to direct SSI management as we have done improves management of these cases.

The prevalence of concomitant intraarticular and extraarticular hip impingement in patients with FAI has been shown to contribute to poor outcomes.17 Aforementioned studies illustrate the importance of evaluating all possible anterior hip structure-related causes of SSI in addition to AIIS morphology intraoperatively.14,23 This increased the frequency of multifactorial hip impingement in SSI may provide partial explanation for the lower PASS achievement rate in the current study despite adequate decompression as demonstrated by postoperative radiographic changes. In addition, the PASS mHHS threshold of 74 may not be as applicable to the current cohort given the original study deriving the cutoff was for FAI at 1-year follow-up.19

Although previous studies suggest positive outcomes after SSI decompression, follow-up less than 3 years is difficult to generalize and lends limited value from a patient counseling standpoint. Although there are case-matched studies investigating SSI in part, heterogeneity of the experimental group,22 and presence of SSI in both the experimental and control groups detract from conclusions.11

This study follow-up is the longest to date evaluating arthroscopic treatment of SSI in FAI patients at a mean of 48 months. This study reports on MCID and PASS rates achieved as well, which only one other study reported on during literature review in isolated SSI patients without FAI.18 As has been described in the literature, our SSI cohort also has increased perioperative LCEA and postoperative alpha angles which reinforces the concept of bony AIIS morphology contributing to SSI while distal cam resection remains a critical part of treatment.13,14 Given the difficulty in SSI diagnosis and subsequent indication for decompression, undertreatment of SSI during primary hip arthroscopy for FAI is evident and remains a reason for revision surgery.17 To prevent this, our MRI-based diagnosis of SSI allows a more accurate identification of cases given the variability of SSI diagnosis relying on other forms of imaging such as radiographs and CT.14 Finally, our multivariate analysis reinforces the importance of repairing the labrum after SSI resection alongside an adequate pincer and subspine resection to improve postoperative outcomes.

The limitations of this study include the retrospective nature of the study introducing recall bias throughout data collection and analysis. A limited study sample size likely resulted in underpowering causing lack of statistical significance while comparing baseline with follow-up PROs. The retrospective nature of this study also prevented acquisition and reporting of additional PROs alongside mHHS, which may have been clinically relevant. The age of the current cohort is older than previous study populations assessing SSI; however, given the various etiologies of SSI ranging from anterior hip soft-tissue impingement to AIIS bony overgrowth, different age groups may be susceptible to different forms of SSI. Our multivariate analysis is susceptible to intervariable interactions, which could result in unforeseen direct effects as well.25 We acknowledge the absence of a control group; however, the ethical dilemma of randomizing patients with SSI to resection vs. observation poses an obstacle to such a well-controlled study.

In conclusion, patients undergoing AIIS decompression during hip arthroscopy for SSI in the setting of FAI have acceptable, improved clinical outcomes at a minimum 3-year follow-up. Labral repair and decreasing postoperative LCEA were predictive of improved PROs. Hip arthroscopy remains a viable successful treatment option in the treatment of concomitant SSI during FAI surgery.
