Hydrophobic Natural Deep Eutectic Solvents for Sustainable CO2 Capture: Molecular Insights From Multiscale Modeling
Authors: Nuria Aguilar, Sara Rozas, Pedro A Marcos, Alfredo Bol, Santiago Aparicio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research delves into the physicochemical properties and CO2 capture ability of a hydrophobic natural deep eutectic solvent (HNADES) consisting of piperitone (PIP) and linoleic acid in a 1:1 molar ratio. Through a multiscale computational approach involving COSMO‐RS calculations, quantum chemistry methods, and molecular dynamics simulations, we demonstrate that the considered system, via the development of strong hydrogen bonding interactions, both homo‐ and hetero‐associations, and in parallel with nonpolar domains through van der Waals interactions, creates a suitable situation for CO2 capture. The nonpolar domains provide space available for accommodating CO2 molecules without interfering with the fluid's hydrogen bonding Gnetwork. Molecular dynamics simulations show that CO2 molecules are adsorbed following a two‐step mechanism: first, adsorption at the gas–liquid interface, and second, subsequent migration into the bulk liquid. Upon exposure to a simulated flue gas, the HNADES is significantly selective toward CO2, which permeates the fluid, with N2 and O2 molecules remaining predominantly, in the gas phase. The desorption of the adsorbed CO2 is possible after a heating process, thus enabling sorption–desorption cycles for practical use. This sorbent has advantages of being eco‐friendly, having zero vapor pressure, and low water solubility, which makes it an optimal candidate for being involved in sustainable carbon capture technologies.Keywords: deep eutectic solvents, hydrogen bonding, molecular modeling, sustainable carbon capture

Introduction

The atmospheric CO2—in mole fraction—measured in March 2025 is 429.64 ppm [1]. As stated in the Synthesis Report (SYR) of the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6): “Human activities, principally through emissions of greenhouse gases, have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching 1.1 °C above 1850–1900 in 2011–2020” [2]. In 2024, electricity demand reached its highest level ever recorded, with 54% of the growth being supplied by nonrenewable sources including coal, oil, and natural gas [3]. To achieve the Net Zero Emission (NZE) scenario, oil and gas demand must decline by approximately 2% annually on average until 2050, while clean energy technologies continue to evolve. A key technology option in this transition scenario is Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage (CCUS), with the oil and gas industry contributing to 90% of the current CCUS capacity in operation [4].

One of the primary carbon capture strategies focuses on postcombustion [carbon] capture (PCC) from fossil fuel combustion, where CO2 is separated from the streamed flue gas, with a concentration of the target gas being 3% to 15% [5]. In practice, CO2 is chemically absorbed using an aqueous solution of monoethanolamine (MEA), an organic solvent characterized by its high reactivity and selectivity toward CO2, and above all, because it is an inexpensive product. However, due to the limitations of MEA—toxicity, high vapor pressure, corrosive byproducts formation, and high energy consumption— alternative greener solvents have been proposed, beginning with ionic liquids (ILs) and progressing toward deep eutectic solvents (DESs), where type III DESs, formed by two ionic species; type V DESs formed by nonionic, i.e., molecular and species; and NADES, formed by natural components, are being tested [6, 7].

DESs, first described by Abbott et al. [8, 9], are formed upon the physical mixture of two or more compounds, primarily a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD), resulting in a liquid solvent at room temperature due to the decrease on the melting point temperature of the mixture when compared to the melting points of the constituents alone [10, 11, 12, 13]. The term deep eutectic actually refers to a specific point in the solid–liquid equilibria (SLE) of the mixture, yet the term DESs is broadly used to refer to more than just the thermodynamic eutectic point. For that matter, different names have been proposed, with low melting mixtures (LoMMs) being the term most widely accepted by the scientific community [14, 15]. DESs have been applied in various fields [16, 17, 18, 19] including pharmacology [20, 21], biotechnology [22, 23], liquid–liquid extraction [24], reaction media [25], material preparation [26], water purification [27], and CCUS [28], among others.

DESs are considered green solvents due to their low vapor pressure, biodegradability, nontoxicity, biocompatibility, ease of synthesis, and cost‐effectiveness [29, 30, 31, 32]. Nevertheless, due to the scarcity of studies evaluating these claimed properties, new research is needed to justify why DESs are substances of great interest in green chemistry [33]. The exorbitant number of possible DESs that can be designed, since not only the nature of the HBA and HBD changes but also the molar ratio or the conditions under which they are operated, challenges the scientific community to continually develop new research to clearly understand their specific properties [34, 35, 36, 37]. To overcome that, computational studies and, moreover, studies relying on screening software as COSMO‐RS and machine learning methods such as neural networks will help to address this complex task [38, 39].

As concluded by Alnajjar and Onaizi in their recent work about progress and outlooks for CO2 capture and conversion using deep eutectic solvents [5], “DESs’ unique properties make them vital in sustainable CO2 capture and conversion, potentially transforming industrial practices and significantly improving global carbon management strategies.” To this end, computational methods play a vital role not only in understanding the behavior and physicochemical properties of pure solvents but also in enabling the rational design and selection of DES components, aiming to optimize selectivity toward CO2, reduce costs, ensure low to nontoxic profiles, and minimize energy consumption during gas desorption. Furthermore, modeling the CO2 capture allows us to predict the thermodynamics and kinetics of the process, supporting the scalability of these systems for industrial applications.

Several hydrophobic DES and NADES combinations, typically pairing a monoterpenoid or phenolic hydrogen bond acceptor with a fatty‐acid or alcohol hydrogen bond donor, have already been explored for CO2 capture, mostly from a thermophysical and gas‐solubility perspective. Building on this family, our own recent experimental work showed that hydrophobic DESs combining linoleic acid (LIN) as HBD with camphor, citral, or piperitone (PIP) as HBA are able to absorb both CO2 and N2 at high pressure, with absorption efficiencies of ca. 62%–92% attained within 100 s at 10–30 bar [40]. A subsequent, broader screening extended this monoterpenoid:fatty‐acid HNADES family to fourteen combinations, including several piperitone‐based systems, using DFT calculations to confirm that non‐covalent interactions govern the high CO2 affinity of this solvent class [41]. Neither of these previous studies, however, resolved at the molecular level the thermodynamics and dynamics of the PIP:LIN hydrogen‐bonding network, its interplay with the apolar domains formed by the LIN aliphatic chains, or the atomistic mechanism of CO2 (and N2/O2) adsorption and desorption at the liquid–gas interface. The present work addresses this gap by providing a multiscale (DFT + COSMO‐RS + MD) mechanistic study specifically focused on the PIP:LIN (1:1) HNADES, thus complementing the previous, mainly experimental, characterization of this solvent family with the molecular‐level rationale required to guide its rational optimization.

In this computational study, a hydrophobic natural deep eutectic solvent (HNADES) formed by piperitone (PIP) and linoleic acid (18:2ω6; cis, cis‐9,12‐octadecadienoic acid; LIN), in a 1:1 molar ratio (Figure 1a), is extensively analyzed to understand the nature of the pure HNADES, its ability to absorb CO2 (Figure 1b), and its selectivity against a model flue gas.

PIP is a major volatile organic compound (VOC) found in essential oils (EOs) derived from plants such as Mentha longifolia (L.) [42], while LIN is the most highly consumed polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) found in the human diet [43] with the advantage that it can be recycled from waste cooking oils [44]. Regarding DES formation, PIP is a pure HBA via the O1 site (Figure 1a), and LIN is an HBD, via the OH site, which can lead to the formation of hetero‐associations (PIP–LIN) and LIN–LIN homo‐associations by creating hydrogen bonds.

To simulate and analyze the intermolecular interactions and dynamic properties of the HNADES and HNADES–gas mixtures, quantum chemistry (density functional theory, DFT), thermodynamic (COnductor‐like Screening MOdel for Real Solvents, COSMO‐RS [45]), and classical molecular mechanics (molecular dynamics, MD) methods are employed throughout this work.

Methodology

DFT Calculations

The geometry optimization of monomers (PIP, LIN, and CO2), dimers (PIP‐LIN), tetramers (2PIP‐2LIN), and systems containing CO2 molecules was carried out with TmoleX software [46] at PBE [47]/def2‐TZVP [48] with D3 [49] Grimme correction level of theory. The counterpoise (CP) [50] correction method for basis set superposition error (BSSE) was also applied.

The PBE‐D3/def2‐TZVP level of theory was adopted because the generalized‐gradient PBE functional, combined with Grimme's D3 dispersion correction, provides a reliable and computationally affordable description of both hydrogen bonding and dispersion‐dominated van der Waals interactions, which govern, respectively, the PIP–LIN association and the LIN–LIN apolar aggregation central to this study; the triple‐zeta def2‐TZVP basis set, in turn, offers an accurate electron density description for the QTAIM and NCI analyses without the prohibitive computational cost that larger clusters (up to the HNADES + 10 CO2 systems considered here) would entail at a higher basis‐set level. It should be acknowledged, however, that the DFT calculations reported in this work are necessarily restricted to finite, gas‐phase molecular clusters (dimers, tetramers, and HNADES– (CO2 n aggregates) and therefore capture only short‐range, local hydrogen‐bonding and dispersion features; long‐range cooperative and bulk polarization effects present in the condensed liquid phase are not accounted for at this level of theory. For this reason, the DFT results are used here strictly as a molecular‐level reference for the nature and strength of the elementary PIP–LIN and PIP/LIN–CO2 interactions, while the thermodynamic behavior and the structural/dynamic properties of the bulk liquid phase are independently addressed through COSMO‐RS (Section 2.2) and classical MD (Section 2.3), both of which explicitly incorporate many‐body and long‐range effects.

Vibrational frequencies of the considered systems were predicted employing the same conditions as those for the geometry optimization calculations. The initial systems were built with the Avogadro program [51], and the final structures were analyzed in terms of QTAIM (quantum theory of atoms in molecules) theory [52] and NCI (noncovalent interactions) [53] with MultiWfn [54] and VMD [55] software.

The systems studied using DFT represent (i) the minimal cluster for the HNADES considered in this work, PIP‐LIN, for which a total of five different initial structures were considered. The most stable structure was then selected to build (ii) two tetramers of HNADES and (iii) systems composed of HNADES and CO2 molecules. The tetramers comprise two molecules of each type (PIP and LIN) H‐bonded and placed facing the hydrocarbon chains of the LIN molecules. For the anti‐tetramer, the PIP molecules were placed on opposite sides, while for the para‐tetramer, the two PIP molecules were placed on the same side. The systems containing CO2 molecules were then built. A total of nine different positions of one CO2 molecule interacting with the HNADES dimer were explored, and the most stable structure was then selected to build a system containing two CO2 molecules. When the geometry optimization of this system was reached, the final configuration was then selected as the initial configuration for the system containing three CO2 molecules, and the relaxed geometry of this calculation was the initial configuration for a system containing four molecules. This recursive construction scheme was maintained until placing a total of ten CO2 molecules.

Classical MD Simulations

Classical MD simulations were conducted using MDynaMix [61] v.5.2 software where the Merck Molecular Force Fields (MMFF) [62], derived from SwissParam [63] database and corrected with DFT‐calculated CHELPG [64] charges, were employed. The utilized force fields for PIP, LIN, CO2, N2, O2, and H2O are reported in Table S2. The systems considered for MD simulations are (i) pure HNADES, (ii) HNADES–CO2 mixtures, and (iii) HNADES/CO2 and HNADES/flue gas interfaces. The model flue gas considered throughout this work is restricted to the CO2/N2/O2/H2O quaternary mixture typically assumed in postcombustion capture studies. Minor flue‐gas components such as SO x and NO x , which may be present in real flue gas streams and could influence solvent selectivity, are not included in this model system; the selectivity conclusions drawn in Section 3.2.3 therefore apply strictly to this simplified gas composition. Initial simulation boxes were built with the Packmol [65] program and are detailed in Table S1. The pure HNADES systems, containing 300 PIP and 300 LIN molecules, were simulated at six different temperature and pressure conditions: 298 K and 30 bar, 323 K and 1 bar, 323 K and 10 bar, 323 K and 20 bar, 323 K and 30 bar, and 323 K and 40 bar. The HNADES–CO2 mixtures were simulated at 298 K and 30 bar, 323 K and 1 bar, 323 K and 20 bar, 323 K and 30 bar, and 323 K and 40 bar. The molar fraction of CO2 for the mixtures was calculated from the solubility prediction described in Section 2.2 at 323 K and 30 bar, being x(CO2) = 0.13 the value obtained under the selected conditions. The simulated HNADES–CO2 mixture boxes contained 300 pairs of HNADES and 89 CO2 molecules. Two different simulation boxes for 323 K and 20 bar conditions were built considering the specific x(CO2) at 323 K and 20 bar, i.e., x(CO2) = 0.09 and thus n(CO2) = 58, and x(CO2) = 0.13 for 323 K and 30 bar.

The MD simulations for pure HNADES and HNADES–CO2 mixtures were carried out in a three‐step procedure: (i) 10 ns pre‐equilibration run in the canonical ensemble (NVT) at the corresponding temperature for relaxation, (ii) 15 ns at the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT) at the corresponding pressure and temperature for density equilibration, and (iii) 40 ns at the NVT ensemble for production run.

The MMFF/SwissParam force field combined with DFT‐derived CHELPG atomic charges, as employed here, follows the same parameterization protocol previously validated by our group for structurally related monoterpenoid‐ and fatty‐acid‐based HNADES systems, for which predicted liquid densities showed an average absolute deviation of only 0.46% from experimental values [66], supporting the transferability of this force‐field combination to the PIP:LIN system studied in this work. Equilibration of every simulated system was verified by monitoring the time evolution of density, potential energy, temperature, and pressure throughout the sequential NVT–NPT–NVT protocol, confirming that statistically stationary averages were reached well before the beginning of each production stage; the structural (RDFs, CDFs, SDFs, hydrogen bond counts) and dynamic (VDFs, hydrogen bond lifetimes) properties reported in Section 3.2 were evaluated over the full production trajectories, with block‐averaging used to assess their statistical convergence.

For simulating both CO2 gas and model flue gas interfaces, NVT production runs at 298 K and 30 bar and 323 K and 30 bar were employed, duplicating the number of gas molecules in the gas region to ensure the desired pressure. To study the desorption of CO2, 1 ns at 323, 348, 373, and 398 K were simulated using the HNADES/CO2 interface system equilibrated at 323 K and 30 bar as the initial configuration, i.e., t = 0 ns. To ensure the desorption of N2 molecules, a simulation lasting 1 ns at the NPT ensemble was conducted, using the equilibrated systems at 298 and 323 K (30 bar) as initial configuration boxes.

The Nose–Hoover [67] method was selected to control pressure and temperature, as well as the Tuckerman–Berne [68] double time‐step algorithm for the numerical integration of equations of motion, using 3 fs and 0.1 fs for long‐ and short‐time steps, respectively. The Ewald [69] method was applied for handling Coulombic interaction, and the intermolecular interactions were described with the Lennard–Jones potential, both described with a cut‐off radius of 10 Å. Lorentz–Berthelot mixing rules were considered for cross terms. Visualization, analysis, and postprocessing of trajectories were carried out using VMD [55] and TRAVIS [70] programs.

Results and Discussion

COSMO‐RS and DFT

In the first stage, the question to be answered is about the real nature of the PIP:LIN system studied, that is, can it be considered a DES or not? The SLE diagram for PIP:LIN is reported in Figure 2 both for the ideal behavior and as inferred from COSMO‐RS calculations. The mixture composition for the eutectic point, x (LIN) = 0.36 (roughly corresponding to a PIP:LIN 2:1 HNADES), is exactly the same as for the ideal behavior and COSMO‐RS predictions. The D e parameter [59], which quantifies the shifting of eutectic temperature in comparison with ideal conditions (−31.29 K), is −12.45%, which is within the range of a well‐known DES such as urea–choline chloride (D e between the −13% and −17%, depending on the source of pure compound properties) [59] or a type V DES such as menthol–thymol (D e around −6%) [59]. Therefore, the considered PIP:LIN system may be considered as a true DES, because of the noticeable melting temperature reduction, even considering that both PIP and LIN exhibit a liquid phase at room temperature (Figure 2). Additionally, the PIP:LIN system considered in this work corresponds to the 1:1 mol ratio, having a dissimilar eutectic point than the (2:1) ratio. The selection of the 1:1 ratio (melting point 240 K predicted from COSMO‐RS) stands on the consideration that the eutectic point PIP:LIN (2:1) ratio has a low amount of LIN acid, i.e., having a moderate hydrophobic nature, and thus, the LIN ratio was increased to 1:1 to have a hydrophobic fluid for practical applications.

To analyze the nature of the developed PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding, DFT calculations were carried out. In the first stage, the minimal cluster PIP:LIN 1:1 dimer was considered (Figure 3a), obtaining a very large ΔEHNADES value (−42.3 kJ mol−1), thus confirming the formation of the HNADES through hydrogen bonding. The QTAIM analysis of the developed PIP–LIN hydrogen bond (interaction characterized by a short donor–acceptor distance, 1.59 Å) shows the formation of a BCP with large ρ e and ∇2 ρ e, which is also confirmed by the largely negative CVB. Following NCI analysis, the formation of a strong interaction zone between the donor and acceptor regions can be detected, as well as a van der Waals interaction area (large green regions in Figure 3a), corresponding to the interaction between the acid CO group and the CH atoms of the PIP, notably contributing to the large ΔEHNADES value obtained. Additionally, the calculated IR spectrum shows large redshifting for all the bonds involved in hydrogen bonding, being especially remarkable the large shifting of the LIN(OH) group, as shown in Figure 3a. Additional PIP–LIN dimers were considered analyzing different interaction positions (Figure S1), all of them confirming the strong hydrogen‐bonding formation.

Although the considered dimer is a reasonable approach to hydrogen bonding, thus determining the main properties of the considered HNADES, the presence of large apolar chains in LIN should lead to the formation of apolar domains in the liquid phase via van der Waals aggregation of these chains. To assess this effect, PIP–LIN tetramers were built and analyzed considering two different intermolecular configurations: anti (where PIP‐LIN dimers are arranged in opposite directions) and para (with PIP‐LIN dimers arranged in parallel, but keeping the same direction) (Figure 3b,c). The reported results show more efficient interaction for the para arrangement, which is confirmed by the large van der Waals interaction energy (−37.7 and −98.2 kJ mol−1, for anti and para, respectively). Interestingly, the formation of LIN–LIN van der Waals interactions through apolar chains does not weaken the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonds, which are equivalent to those in the PIP–LIN dimer; thus, the large interaction among chains, as confirmed by the NCI interacting regions, would develop a pivotal role in the PIP–LIN liquid phase via the development of apolar domains.

Furthermore, since the PIP–LIN HNADES will be considered in this study as a candidate for CO2 capture, DFT calculations were also conducted to analyze the interaction of the CO2 molecules with PIP–LIN clusters, as shown in Figure 4. Initially, a PIP–LIN dimer was considered (interacting via hydrogen bonding as in Figure 3a), testing different relative positions and orientations for the CO2 molecule, obtaining the optimal interaction site shown in Figure 4a. NCI analysis confirms a high affinity among the CO2 molecule and PIP–LIN dimer as inferred from the prominent presence of van der Waals regions in the middle. The CO2 molecule interacts simultaneously with the PIP and LIN molecules, and this interaction does not alter the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding, maintaining the same characteristics as in the absence of CO2 molecules (Figures 3a and 4a). Furthermore, after studying the other proposed interaction sites for the placement of the CO2 molecules around the dimer (Figure S1), it can be stated that it is possible to find other efficient CO2 absorption sites.

For the sake of analyzing the effect of increasing the number of interacting CO2 molecules, up to 10 were tested, placing them around the PIP–LIN dimer. Results obtained indicate that increasing the number of CO2 molecules causes minor changes in the structuring of the PIP–LIN dimer, as inferred from the low RMSD values reported in Figure 4b and the structures reported in Figure 4c. Likewise, the distribution of CO2 molecules around the dimer evolves from accumulation around the PIP–LIN hydrogen‐bonded site (as in Figure 4a) to placements along the apolar LIN chain. The strength of CO2–HNADES interaction, ΔECO2, is reported in Figure 4d. Although ΔECO2 per molecule of gas decreases (in absolute value) with increasing number of CO2 molecules in comparison with the case of a single molecule strongly interacting with the PIP–LIN hydrogen‐bonded site, the values are large enough to confirm that PIP–LIN is able to accommodate (absorb) a large amount of CO2 molecules without altering its structure. Furthermore, the development of apolar domains (Figure 3b) results in the formation of a large available space to accommodate CO2 molecules (Figure 4c), thus leading to the expected adequate CO2 capture capacity.

The analysis of PIP–LIN properties and their relationship with its CO2‐capturing ability is analyzed through COSMO‐RS calculations (Figure 5). The COSMO σ‐profiles and σ‐surfaces are reported in Figure 5a, from which it is confirmed the suitability of PIP as a hydrogen bond acceptor, LIN as both donor and acceptor, and how the formation of the PIP–LIN dimer (HNADES) results in a cluster with hydrogen‐bond‐accepting capacities. Likewise, the overlapping of CO2 apolar regions with those of PIP–LIN is on the root of the interaction mechanism, as reported in Figure 4.

(a) σ‐profile, σ‐surface and σ‐potential for PIP, LIN, CO2, HNADES, and HNADES‐CO2. (b) CO2 predicted solubility at 298.15–323.15 K for temperature and 1–50 bar for pressure. (c) Environmental properties of PIP, LIN, and HNADES predicted at 298.15 K, and safety properties for PIP and LIN.

The CO2 solubility in PIP:LIN (1:1) HNADES is predicted via COSMO‐RS, considering absorption isotherms in the 298.15–323.15 K range and pressures up to 50 bar. The results show the largest solubility at 298.15 K and 50 bar is xCO2=0.31, and although it decreases to xCO2=0.21 upon reaching 323.15 K for the same pressure, it shows an adequate CO2 solubility. The comparison of CO2 solubility with other DES shows slightly lower capturing capacity, e.g., at 323 K and 40 bar, xCO2= 0.17 is obtained for PIP–LIN 1:1, whereas values of xCO2=0.31, 0.29, 0.36, and 0.26 are obtained for menthol:thymol (1:2), thymol:2,6‐xylenol (1:1), choline chloride:urea (1:2), and choline:chloride ethylene glycol (1:2), respectively [71, 72, 73].

The potential environmental impact of the molecules considered can be summarized through the parameters featured in Figure 5c, showing that both PIP and LIN have very low vapor pressure at 298.15 K, being negligible upon HNADES formation. Furthermore, although PIP has a certain but low solubility in water, in contrast with LIN, HNADES is almost water insoluble, as confirmed by the corresponding partition coefficients. Regarding the safety of PIP and LIN as components of HNADES for health, LIN is an oil which is commonly found in several food oils; therefore, no relevant safety considerations need to be applied. For PIP, the analysis of the ECHA database shows that for REACH registrations, no hazards have been classified. Nevertheless, some relevant (ADMET) properties were calculated for PIP and LIN (Figure 5c), showing that both compounds are indeed safe, with some issues for PIP, but the formation of PIP–LIN hydrogen‐bonded complexes (Figure 3) should lead to molecular entities closer to the properties of pure LIN, i.e., safe and environmentally friendly.

MD Simulations

Bulk PIP:LIN Liquid Phases

The DFT results obtained in the previous section provide information on the mechanism and strength of relevant intermolecular forces in the considered PIP–LIN pairs (short range ordering), but the behavior of the bulk liquid systems requires to consider also long‐range interactions in addition to these intermolecular forces, which were analyzed via MD simulations. The molecular arrangement is first analyzed through the snapshots reported in Figure 6a, in which highly interconnected domains formed by LIN molecules, as a result of the previously described apolar interactions (Figure 3b,c), among which the PIP domains are distributed. Therefore, the PIP–LIN system shows a large heterogeneity at nanoscopic level, which is reinforced upon compression. This effect is quantified via the domain analysis as a function or pressure and temperature, in which the reported results show a single domain for LIN in any considered condition, and a slightly larger number of PIP domains, although also highly interconnected. Nevertheless, as a result of the size difference between molecules, the LIN domains are larger than those of PIP (roughly half in terms of volume) and, in all cases, are nonspherical, as inferred from the low isoperimetric index (Figure 6).

The strength of interaction is quantified and represented in Figure 7 via intermolecular interaction energy, E int, for homo (PIP–PIP; LIN–LIN) and hetero (HNADES: PIP–LIN). The reported results confirm the presence of all the type of interactions, but those for PIP–PIP are roughly three times weaker and less extensive than those for LIN–LIN and PIP–LIN. Likewise, although the HNADES formation is produced via the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding, LIN molecules are also largely self‐associated (in agreement with DFT results shown in Figure 3), bearing a strength and extension equivalent to those for PIP–LIN. These strong and extensive LIN–LIN interactions justify the formation of a single and large LIN domain, as reported in Figure 6. Moreover, increasing the compression up to 40 bar does not lead to noticeable changes in E int for any of the developed interacting pairs.

The obtained E int clearly influences the dynamic properties of PIP–LIN, which are quantified through the center‐of‐mass velocity distribution functions (VDF) (Figure 8). The equivalent strengths for PIP–LIN and LIN–LIN interactions (Figure 7) lead to analogous molecular mobility for both PIP and LIN molecules: the most probable velocity value for PIP at 298 K and 30 bar is 1107 pm ps−1, in parallel with the value of 1108 pm ps−1 for LIN, whereas at 323 K, the average most probable value is 1155 pm ps−1 for PIP and 1153 pm ps−1 for LIN. Therefore, PIP and LIN molecules move as paired entities and in a coupled manner. The effect of pressure on VDFs is almost negligible, with the maxima evolving in the 1151–1158 pm ps−1 for the considered 1–40 bar range at 323 K, whereas slightly decreasing values upon cooling confirm the strong intermolecular interactions weakly affected by compression or heating.

PIP:LIN + CO2 Liquid Phases

In this section, the nature of PIP–LIN HNADES in the presence of (absorbed) CO2 molecules is analyzed with the aim of inferring the HNADES structural changes for the fitting of CO2 molecules and the interaction mechanism of CO2 in the considered fluid. Results featured in Figure 12 show how CO2 molecules are largely organized within the PIP–LIN structure, and the whole fluid does not undergo major changes in its structuring when fitting absorbed molecules, which is also confirmed by the domain analysis (Figures 6 and 12). CO2 molecules are not self‐associated inside the fluid as confirmed by the large number of developed domains; these molecules are evenly distributed within the hollow sites developed in the fluid.

Regarding the interaction energy among molecules, E int values show negligible changes in the fluid when the absorbed CO2 molecules are present (Figures 7 and 13). Additionally, CO2 molecules interact in the same extension with PIP and LIN molecules, as E int for CO2–PIP is in the same range as that for CO2–LIN (Figure 13), which agrees with the interaction mechanism (CO2 capturing) inferred from DFT calculations (Figure 3). Likewise, CO2 self‐association can be discarded because of the negligible E int for CO2–CO2 interactions.

The dynamic of CO2‐containing systems is analyzed through the corresponding VDFs (Figure 14). The first relevant result stands on the pairing of molecular movements as in the previously reported VDFs for PIP; nonetheless, LIN and CO2 show the same characteristics and values, which agrees with the molecular distribution reported in Figure 12. The comparison of VDFs for PIP–LIN (Figure 8) and the systems containing absorbed CO2 (Figure 14) evidenced that the presence of CO2 molecules leads to a shifting of VDF maxima toward lower values, e.g., at 298 K—30 bar shifting from 1107 to 947 pm ps−1, with analogous shifting for all the remaining considered conditions. This decrease in the molecular mobility is a result of the noticeable strength of CO2–PIP/LIN interactions, as inferred both from DFT (Figure 4) and MD simulations (Figure 14).

DFT results (Figure 4) showed that CO2–PIP/LIN interactions are not due to hydrogen bonding, and so it is confirmed via MD in bulk liquid phases. Moreover, DFT calculations indicated that the (van der Waals) interactions among CO2 and PIP/LIN do not alter the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding. This behavior is supported throughout MD simulations, in which the comparison of PIP–LIN hydrogen‐bonding extension for neat fluid (Figure 9a) and in the presence of absorbed CO2 (Figure 15a) exhibits negligible changes. The main change in the properties of PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding due to the presence of CO2 molecules stands on their dynamics, resulting in a notable decrease of their lifetime (Figures 9b and 15b) with negligible reforming time values. The molecular arrangements in the hydrogen‐bonding sites are also unaffected due to the presence of CO2 molecules (Figures 9c and 15c). Therefore, the absorption of CO2 molecules does not alter neither the structural properties of PIP–LIN fluid nor the nature of the developed hydrogen bonds (Figures 10 and 16). This effect is inferred from the characteristics of both PIP interactions (Figures 9 and 15) as well as for LIN (Figures 11 and 17).

The geometry of the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding in the presence of absorbed CO2 molecules is characterized in Figure 16, in which the distribution of the hydrogen bonding angle is reported as a function of the O1(PIP)···H2(LIN) distance for the six HNADES–CO2 mixtures. For all the considered conditions (Figure 16a–f), the distributions concentrate in a single, narrow region placed close to 180°, thus showing an almost linear arrangement of the hydrogen‐bonded PIP–LIN pairs, with the angular spreading remaining limited even at the highest CO2 loadings and pressures. The corresponding radial distributions, reported as insets, develop a single sharp peak centered around 175 pm, whose position and intensity are maintained upon changing temperature, pressure, and CO2 content. The comparison with the equivalent results for the neat fluids (Figure 10) shows that neither the angular distribution nor the O1 ··· H2 distance is perturbed by the absorbed CO2 molecules, thus confirming that CO2 occupies the voids available in the PIP–LIN structuring without competing for the hydrogen bonding sites, in agreement with the van der Waals nature of the CO2–PIP/LIN interactions inferred from DFT calculations (Figure 4).

The behavior of LIN molecules upon CO2 absorption is analyzed in Figure 17. The number of hydrogen bonds per LIN molecule (Figure 17a) remains in the 0.446–0.471 range for all the considered temperatures, pressures, and CO2 contents, in the same range as the values obtained for the neat HNADES (Figure 11a), thus showing that LIN self‐association through its carboxylic group is not disrupted by the presence of the absorbed molecules. Likewise, the SDFs reported in Figure 17b, setting LIN as the reference molecule, show that PIP molecules remain localized around the LIN acid site through their O1 atom, whereas the LIN–LIN interactions, monitored through the H2 and O3 sites, preserve the spatial arrangements described for the neat fluid (Figure 11b), with no additional density developing at new sites. Hence, the LIN hydrogen‐bonded network, which is responsible for the apolar domains in which CO2 molecules are hosted, is fully maintained upon absorption.

Likewise, the distribution and orientation of the chains in LIN molecules also remain unaltered in the presence of the absorbed CO2 molecules (Figures S2 and S3). Upon absorption, CO2 molecules remain linear as inferred from the results shown in Figure 18a (maxima at 179° for all the considered pressures and temperatures), with PIP and LIN molecules evenly distributed around the CO2 molecules (Figure 18b), which agrees with the mechanism of interaction inferred from DFT calculations (Figure 4) and confirms that CO2 molecules occupy the available voids in the PIP–LIN structuring without altering the fluid.

PIP:LIN + {CO2/Flue Gas} Interfaces

In the same vein, to gain deeper insights into the mechanism of CO2 capture by PIP–LIN, MD simulations were performed considering two cases: (i) pure CO2 gas phase and (ii) archetypical flue gas phase. In both cases of two phases, liquid–gas, MD simulations were carried out. In the case of pure CO2 gas phase (Figure 19a), MD simulations show a large affinity of the DES for the CO2 molecules, and the capture process is carried out in two stages: (i) CO2 molecules are (rapidly) adsorbed at the gas–liquid boundary, generating a CO2‐rich interface, and then (ii) CO2 molecules evolve migrating into the fluid, maintaining this two‐stage mechanism within the pressure and temperature ranges considered (Figure 19a,b). This behavior results in CO2 densification at the interface with the remaining molecules homogenously distributed within the fluid (Figure 19c,d).

Snapshots of initial and equilibrated HNADES–CO2 interfaces at (a) 298 K and 30 bar and (b) 323 K and 30 bar. PIP molecules are depicted in red, LIN in blue, and CO2 in green. Number density profiles, ρ, in the direction perpendicular to the interface, z, for (c) 298 K and 30 bar and (d) 323 K and 30 bar.

In the case of flue gas phases, results show that both CO2 and water molecules are captured, following also the same two‐stage mechanism described above (Figure 20a,b). The presence of water molecules does not hinder the capture of CO2 since they are absorbed as in the case of the gaseous phase of pure CO2 (Figures 19 and 20). Water molecules penetrate the inner regions of the fluid, whereas CO2 develops a strong adsorbed layer at the interface. For N2 and O2, although these molecules also develop an adsorbed layer at the interface region, they do not penetrate into the interior of the bulk liquid phase, remaining mostly in the gas phase (Figure 20c,d), thus establishing a simple mechanism that allows CO2/N2 separation from complex flue gas phases. It must be stressed that this selectivity conclusion applies strictly to the simplified CO2/N2/O2/H2O model flue gas considered in this work; real flue gas streams also contain trace SO x and NO x species, not included in the present simulations, which could modify the reported selectivity under real operating conditions. It should be remarked that during the two‐stage capturing process, the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding does not suffer any remarkable change (Figures 10, 16, and S4), consistently maintaining their structural integrity, thus confirming the suitability of these systems to be ideal candidates for CO2 capture.

PIP molecules are depicted in red, LIN in blue, CO2 in green, N2 in magenta, O2 in orange, and H2O in cyan. Number density profiles, ρ, in the direction perpendicular to the interface, z, for (c) 298 K and 30 bar and (d) 323 K and 30 bar.

Once the absorption mechanism is proved, the desorption of CO2 molecules was also studied for the two‐phase gas–liquid systems, since it is essential for solvent recovery in industrial CO2 capture operations. The desorption mechanism, studied in the 323–398 K range, is also characterized by a two‐stage process, in which CO2 molecules first migrate to the interface, increasing the concentration of adsorbed molecules at the phase boundary, and then drifting to the gas phase (desorbed) (Figure 21a). The desorbing ratio of the CO2 molecules is analyzed in Figure 21b, showing how they are efficiently desorbed upon heating, following a linear behavior with increasing temperature, and thus confirming that the solvent can be efficiently recovered via simple heating, allowing sorption–desorption cycles to be carried out, maintaining the fluid's integrity, an essential task for industrial capture operations.

Technoeconomic Analysis and Scalability

The computational results obtained in this study provide valuable insights into the potential industrial viability of the PIP:LIN HNADES system for CO2 capture applications. From a technoeconomic perspective, several key advantages emerge from the molecular‐level understanding developed through our multiscale modeling approach.

Economic Considerations

The constituent components of PIP:LIN HNADES present favorable economic profiles. Piperitone, as a major volatile organic compound in essential oils from plants such as Mentha longifolia, can be obtained from agricultural waste streams or dedicated cultivation. More significantly, linoleic acid offers substantial economic advantages as it can be recycled from waste cooking oils, transforming a waste management challenge into a value‐added feedstock. This circular economy approach could significantly reduce raw material costs compared to those of synthetic solvents.

The predicted zero vapor pressure and low water solubility of the HNADES translate into reduced operational costs through minimized solvent losses during operation. Unlike conventional MEA systems that suffer from significant vapor losses and require continuous make‐up, the nonvolatile nature of the PIP:LIN system would eliminate these replacement costs and reduce environmental emissions.

The reversible CO2 capture mechanism demonstrated through our desorption simulations indicates that thermal regeneration can be achieved through simple heating, with a linear temperature dependence. This suggests that energy penalties for solvent regeneration may be manageable, though the specific energy requirements would need experimental validation to compare against the high energy consumption associated with MEA regeneration.

Scalability Assessment

The MD simulations reveal several characteristics that support scalability potential. The structural stability of the HNADES across the pressure and temperature ranges studied (1–40 bar, 298–323 K) suggests robust performance under industrially relevant conditions. The maintenance of hydrogen‐bonding networks and the ability to accommodate CO2 molecules without structural degradation indicate that the system should retain its properties when scaled beyond the molecular simulation box sizes employed in this work. The two‐step CO2 capture mechanism identified through interface simulations provides insights into mass‐transfer processes that would be critical for industrial absorber design. The initial rapid adsorption at the gas–liquid interface, followed by migration into the bulk liquid, suggests that surface area maximization through appropriate contactor design could enhance capture rates. The selectivity demonstrated against N2 and O2 in simulated flue gas conditions indicates that the system could potentially operate without extensive pretreatment of feed gases.

However, several scalability challenges must be acknowledged based on our computational findings. The CO2 solubility predictions show lower capturing capacity compared to some established DES systems, which could translate into larger equipment sizes or higher circulation rates in industrial applications. The low viscosity (9.88 mPa s at 293.15 K) and mass transfer properties of the PIP:LIN system assured suitable energy and mass transfer operations.

Industrial Integration Potential

The hydrophobic nature of PIP:LIN HNADES presents both opportunities and challenges for industrial integration. The low water solubility would minimize complications from moisture in flue gas streams, potentially reducing the need for extensive gas‐drying systems. However, the integration of hydrophobic solvents into the existing plant infrastructure designed for aqueous MEA systems would require careful consideration of material compatibility and process modifications. The environmentally benign nature of both PIP and LIN components, combined with their natural origins, positions this system favorably for regulatory approval and public acceptance. The elimination of toxic degradation products, a significant concern with amine‐based systems, could reduce waste treatment costs and environmental compliance burdens.

A further consideration for industrial translation concerns the internal homogeneity of the assembled HNADES itself. As shown by the intermolecular interaction energies reported in Figure 7, the PIP–PIP homo‐association is markedly weaker than either the PIP–LIN hetero‐association or the LIN–LIN homo‐association, the latter two being of comparable strength. Because LIN–LIN interactions alone can rival the PIP–LIN hydrogen bond, a fraction of PIP molecules could remain comparatively less tightly bound within the eutectic network; taken together with the ADMET flags identified for the pure PIP molecule (Figure 5c), this suggests that the toxicological profile of the assembled 1:1 PIP:LIN HNADES, rather than that inferred solely from its pure components, should be experimentally confirmed before industrial deployment. Moderately lowering the PIP:LIN molar ratio (i.e., increasing the relative LIN content) may also be worth exploring in future work, since this would be expected to further embed PIP within the LIN‐rich hydrogen‐bonded and apolar network and could mitigate any residual toxicity concerns associated with unbound PIP, while retaining the hydrophobic character required for CO2 capture.

The computational framework developed in this study provides a foundation for further optimization through rational design. The detailed understanding of intermolecular interactions and CO2 accommodation mechanisms enables targeted modifications to improve capacity or selectivity without extensive experimental screening. This computational approach could accelerate the development timeline compared to purely empirical optimization strategies.

Technology Readiness and Development Pathway

Based on the computational characterization presented, the PIP:LIN HNADES system demonstrates sufficient promise to warrant experimental validation. The molecular‐level insights provide clear guidance for initial experimental work, including optimal operating conditions, expected performance ranges, and key characterization requirements. The transition from computational predictions to practical implementation would require systematic experimental validation of thermodynamic properties, mass‐transfer characteristics, and long‐term stability under cycling conditions. The computational framework established in this work provides benchmarks for experimental validation and could guide the interpretation of the experimental results.

While the current study focuses on postcombustion capture applications, the fundamental properties identified suggest potential applicability to other CO2 separation scenarios, including direct air capture or industrial process gas treatment, pending appropriate technoeconomic analysis for each specific application. The combination of sustainable feedstocks, favorable molecular properties, and computational design optimization positions the PIP:LIN HNADES system as a promising candidate for next‐generation CO2 capture technologies, meriting continued development toward industrial demonstration.

Conclusions

This research provides an extensive characterization of the PIP:LIN system as a HNADES and its possible application for the CO2 capture technology. The coupled theoretical framework through COSMO‐RS, DFT calculations, and MD simulations has confirmed that PIP:LIN (1:1) is, actually, a true DES characterized by a melting point depression of −12.45% relative to ideal behavior, making it a solid member of the suite of known DES systems.

The strong hydrogen‐bonding interaction between LIN and PIP molecules (−42.3 kJ mol−1) was verified using DFT calculation and QTAIM analysis also. Furthermore, LIN molecules have the ability to form large apolar domains among themselves by van der Waals interaction that form void spaces to accommodate CO2 molecules without damaging the required structure of the fluid. This double interaction method—strong hydrogen bonding between PIP–LIN and the formation of apolar domains—provides optimal conditions for CO2 absorption.

MD simulations revealed that the PIP:LIN HNADES is structurally stable for a wide range of pressures (1–40 bar) and temperatures (298.15–323.15 K), resulting in labile hydrogen bonds with the ability to reform rapidly after breaking. When CO2 is adsorbed, the molecules of the gas are uniformly dispersed in the available spaces in the fluid without disturbing the PIP–LIN hydrogen bonding system, though it reduces overall molecular mobility, indicating strong CO2–PIP/LIN interactions.

The CO2 capture is a two‐stage mechanism: initial adsorption at the gas–liquid interface, followed by migration into the bulk liquid phase. Most importantly, in demanding conditions of flue gas, the HNADES is selective, since CO2 and water are easily transported through the fluid and N2 and O2 are found largely in the gas phase, showing clear potential for industrially applicable gas separation processes. Desorption simulations confirm that the absorbed CO2 may be efficiently desorbed when heating, with a temperature linear dependence, which facilitates the performance of sufficient sorption–desorption cycles needed for applications in industry.

While the solubility of CO2 in PIP:LIN is lower than some other DES systems, HNADES offers several advantages that should be taken into consideration: it contains ecologically friendly constituents with zero vapor pressure, low miscibility with water, and positive safety profiles. The multifunctionality of this hydrophobic liquid for industrial CO2 capture, stability, and reversibility of absorption–desorption characteristics makes PIP:LIN HNADES a good candidate for sustainable carbon capture technology.
