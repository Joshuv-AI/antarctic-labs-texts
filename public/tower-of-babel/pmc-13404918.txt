Effective treatment of scabetic nodules with thalidomide: a 5-year retrospective case series
Authors: Rong-Jing Dong, You-Wang Lu, Li-Juan Cao, Qi Li, Yi-Qun Kuang, Yu-Ye Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScabetic nodules are a cutaneous hypersensitivity reaction to scabies mites and their byproducts, often accompanied by persistent pruritus. These nodules remain a therapeutic challenge due to the lack of effective treatments.ObjectiveWe sought to assess the effectiveness and tolerance of thalidomide for genital scabetic nodules.MethodsWe performed a retrospective evaluation of thalidomide treatment (50–200 mg/day for 2–3 consecutive weeks) in 38 male patients with genital nodular scabies who had previously been infested with scabies mites and were left with persistent pruritic nodules on the genitals.ResultsA total of 38 male patients (age range: 18–40 years; mean age: 28.2 ± 5.79 years) with post scabetic nodules were included. Some of them had been refractory to other forms of therapy. Of the 38 patients, 28 patients (73.7%) showed nodule resolution after 2 weeks of treatment. A significant reduction in the number of nodules was observed at the end of the three-week treatment period (P < 0.05), and there was no sign of relapsing. Meanwhile, after two weeks of thalidomide treatment, the patient’s pruritus symptoms significantly improved, manifested as a significant decrease in VAS (P < 0.05), and after three weeks of treatment, VAS further significantly decreased (P < 0.05). Infrequent adverse events, including rash, mild dizziness, and/or drowsiness, occurred in nine patients and resolved after drug withdrawal.ConclusionThalidomide may be a promising option for treating refractory scabies nodules.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: Thalidomide, Scabetic nodules, Scabies mites, Pruritus

Introduction

Scabies is a highly contagious skin disease caused by Sarcoptes scabiei which is endemic or occurs sporadically in families and communities particularly in areas with poor sanitation. It is estimated that over 200 million people are affected by scabies globally at any given time, with an annual prevalence of approximately 455 million cases [1]. Although global scabies incidence showed a declining trend from 1990 to 2017, children in impoverished and overcrowded environments remain the most affected population [2, 3]. In China, the four most burdensome skin diseases in 2019 were eczema, scabies, viral skin diseases, and acne. The prevalence and incidence of scabies increased in the 5–24 age group, with two distinct peaks observed in the 20–24 and over-95 age groups [4]. The typical lesions of scabies are characterized by scabies burrow in areas of thin skin, such as finger webs, dorsum pedis, olecranon, buttocks, axillae, woman’s nipples, genitalia (particularly in males) and so forth [5]. The accompanying symptom of excruciating pruritus is more severe at night and is considered an allergic reaction to scabies mites and their appendages [6].

A more severe form of crusted scabies, also known as Norwegian scabies, tends to develop in patients with immunocompromised states, infants or elderly individuals [7]. Moreover, many male scabies patients develop pruritic nodules on the scrotum, penis, groin, and other areas after treatment. This atypical presentation, known as “post-scabetic nodules,” “scabetic nodules,” or “nodular scabies,” is characterized by firm nodules accompanied by persistent pruritus [8]. These nodules are prone to recurrence and significantly impair patients’ daily functioning and sleep quality.

Histopathological examination of scabetic nodules typically reveals mixed inflammatory cells infiltration, predominantly T lymphocytes, surrounding the nodular lesions [9]. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear, and effective therapeutic strategie (oral or topical) for nodular scabies are lacking.

Current management options include topical or intralesional corticosteroids (for example, triamcinolone, triamcinolone, prednisolone, and physical interventions aimed at direct destruction of the scabetic nodules (for example, cryotherapy, laser therapy) [10]. Unfortunately, these approaches often yield unsatisfactory outcome and accompanied by side effects such as skin ulceration and atrophy.

Thalidomide, known as a potent anti-inflammatory agent was once banned due to teratogenicity, its therapeutic effect still acts like Pandora’s box to date [11]. Thalidomide has a wide range of therapeutic effects, ranging from neoplastic diseases (multiple myeloma and acute leukemia) to various dermatologic conditions (refractory lupus erythematosus, leprosy nodules, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, refractory pruritus) and other inflammatory diseases [12–16]. However, to date, there is no report on the use of thalidomide in the treatment of pruritic scabetic nodules. In our current work, thalidomide was found to be highly effective in treating refractory nodular scabies.

Patients and methods

The diagnosis of scabies was based on the following criteria: (1) a history of scabies exposure; (2) Typical distribution of lesions with characteristic pruritus; and (3) confirmation of mites, eggs, fecal matter (scybala), or burrows by microscopy or dermoscopy [17]. The diagnosis of scabetic nodules was based on the patient’s history of scabies infection and the presence of persistent papules and nodules on the genital area following anti-scabies treatment. Given the well-established teratogenic risk of thalidomide, the study was deliberately designed to enroll only male patients to absolutely preclude any potential fetal exposure.

Male patients over 18 years old with a history of scabies who had completed standard antiscabetic treatment and presented with one or more subcutaneous nodules (0.3–1 cm in diameter) on external genitalia were enrolled from 2018 to 2023. Some patients had previously received interventions such as topical steroid ointments, laser therapy, cryotherapy, or intralesional steroid injections, but these treatments proved ineffective.

In all patients, no clinical or dermoscopic signs of active scabies were present at the time of thalidomide initiation. All patients received oral thalidomide at a dosage of 50–200 mg/day for 2–3 consecutive weeks. Follow-up visits were conducted at weeks 2 and 3 of treatment, and at 1 month after drug withdrawal to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and monitor adverse events. Pruritus was assessed using a visual analogue scale (VAS) as follows: 0 = no pruritus; > 0 to < 4 = mild; > 4 to < 7 = moderate; > 7 to < 9 = severe; and > 9 points = very sever [18]. Comparison of lesion counts and VAS scores before and after treatment were performed using the rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Peripheral neuropathy could not be adequately assessed due to the short treatment duration and limited follow-up.

Paired non-parametric tests were used to compare baseline and post-treatment values.

All patients have provided written informed consent voluntarily. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. 2018 L-45).

Results

A total of 38 male patients with post-scabetic nodules were enrolled between 2018 and 2023 (age range: 18–40 years; mean age: 28.2 ± 5.79 years) (Table 1). Previous treatments included intralesional corticosteroid injections in 9 patients, topical halometasone or sulphur ointment in 12 patients, and cryotherapy alone or combined with laser therapy in 5 patients. The remaining 12 patients had not received any prior treatment for their nodules.

Clinical and lesions features of 38 patients with scabetic nodules

Most of the patients experienced marked relief of pruritus and reduction of nodules during thalidomide therapy. Specially, 28 patients (73.7%) achieved nodule resolution after 2 weeks of treatment. Before treatment, the median VAS score was 9 (Q1 = 9, Q3 = 9), which decreased to a median of 5 (IQR 1.75-6) at 2 weeks and further to 2 (IQR 2–2) at 3 weeks, indicating substantial pain relief. The median number of nodules decreased from 2 (IQR 2–5) before treatment to 0 (IQR 0-0.75) at 2 weeks and remained at 0 (IQR 0–0) at 3 weeks. A similar trend was observed for the number of lesions, with the median declining from 3.5 (IQR 3–5) before treatment to 0 (IQR 0-0.75) at 2 weeks and remaining at 0 at 3 weeks (Figs. 1 and 2). Overall, this treatment regimen significantly improved both pain and skin lesions in patients over the short term, no relapse was observed during the short follow-up period.

Adverse events were generally mild. One patient developed a maculopapular rash involving the trunk two weeks after initiating thalidomide therapy, accompanied by mild pruritus. The rash resolved completely after discontinuation of the drug; however, the patient stopped treatment, and the nodules persisted. Mild dizziness and/or drowsiness occurred in 8 patients but did not affect daily activities. No additional adverse effects were observed during thalidomide administration (Table 1).

Discussion

The pathogenesis of nodular scabies remains poorly understood, and no consistently effective treatment has been established to date. Current reported treatment options include topical corticosteroid ointments, calcineurin inhibitors (e.g., tacrolimus/pimecrolimus ointment), intralesional botulinum toxin A injections, and cryotherapy [19–22]. For refractory scabetic nodules, oral tofacitinib has been reported to achieve favorable outcomes [23]. However, as a newer agent, tofacitinib is associated with potential infection risks. Furthermore, its high cost also limits its application in the management of scabetic nodules.

Thalidomide, a derivative of glutamate, is not a conventional chemotherapeutic agent, yet it has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in hematologic malignancies such as multiple myeloma, relapsed mantle cell lymphoma, and transfusion-dependent myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), for which it has been approved by US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [24–26]. Beyond oncology, thalidomide and its analogues have been successfully applied in more than 25 dermatologic disorders, including cutaneous lupus erythematosus, erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL), cutaneous sarcoidosis, nodular prurigo and so on [14, 16, 27, 28]. Its therapeutic effects are attributed primarily to its anti-cytokine activity, particularly the inhibition of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-12 and granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor. In ENL, thalidomide administered at 100–400 mg/day is regarded as a first-line therapy, exerting its effects by suppressing TNF-α production and reducing dermal infiltration of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNLs) and T cells [15, 29]. Similarly, thalidomide has shown a miraculous effect on refractory cutaneous lupus erythematosus by inhibiting TNF-α production in skin tissues, with lesion improvement rates as high as 98% [30]. Besides, thalidomide also shows unique advantages in the treatment of inflammatory diseases (Crohn’s disease, inflammatory bowel disease) and autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, Behcet’s disease) due to its potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [31].

Thalidomide has therapeutic implications for nodular prurigo and chronic refractory pruritus [32, 33]. In cases where conventional therapies fail, thalidomide not only significantly alleviates pruritus but also improves associated skin lesions [16]. It is speculated that thalidomide not only inhibits the production of TNF-α but also acts as an antagonist of histamine and prostaglandins E2 and F2 [34]. In addition, thalidomide is postulated to exert a central depressant effect, which contributes to its antipruritic activity in the treatment of psoriasis, eczema, nodular prurigo, and senile pruritus [35].

Taken together, thalidomide’s combined anti-pruritic and anti-inflammatory properties render it a promising therapeutic option for nodular scabies. In our study, thalidomide demonstrated rapid and significant efficacy, with 36 patients achieving clinical resolution. The favorable safety profile observed further supports its potential application. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting both inflammation and pruritus may represent an effective approach for managing refractory scabetic nodules.

Conclusion

This study is the first to report the clinical application of thalidomide in the management of post-scabetic nodules. Our case-series analysis demonstrated encouraging efficacy and acceptable tolerability, likely mediated through suppression of the suppression of aberrant immune-inflammatory responses and modulation of cytokines like TNF-α. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and observational design. As it was not possible to retrospectively ascertain the specific dosages, concentrations, or exact treatment durations of the conventional therapies administered to these patients prior to enrollment. Future large-scale, multicenter, randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings, clarify long-term safety, and determine the comparative effectiveness of thalidomide against standard therapies (e.g., corticosteroid injections), thereby defining its role in clinical practice. In addition, a key limitation of our study is its restriction to male patients, which, while ethically mandated due to the use of thalidomide, limits the generalizability of our findings to female individuals.
