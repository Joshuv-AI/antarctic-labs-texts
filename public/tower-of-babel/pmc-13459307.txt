Embodied meaning in therapeutic discourse during cognitive-behavioral therapy for generalized anxiety disorder: an image schema analysis
Authors: Fereshteh Mohamadpour, Mehrdad Askarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImage schemas are simple mental structures that are formed from the body’s interaction with the physical environment. One of the leading theories in this area is the one proposed by Mark Johnson. The aim of this study was to identify, classify, and quantify image schemas employed by psychotherapists during cognitive-behavioral therapy sessions with patients diagnosed with Generalized Anxiety Disorder and to explore the conceptual organization of therapeutic discourse through Johnson’s image schema framework.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative cognitive discourse analysis approach, supplemented by descriptive statistical methods. Data collection was done through audio recording of psychotherapy sessions of 16 patients with generalized anxiety disorder based on cognitive-behavioral therapy approach. The sampling method was purposive. Then, the conversations of the treatment process have been investigated based on the frequency and types of image schemas used in the discourse.ResultsA total of 2,057 cognitive schemas were identified throughout the therapeutic conversations. Among these, 1017 schemas with an average of 49.44% related to types of force schemas, 557 schemas with an average of 27.08% related to types of path schemas, and 483 schemas with an average of 23.48% related to types of containment schemas. A chi-square goodness of fit test and pairwise Z-tests for proportions were conducted to assess whether these differences were statistically significant. The results showed that force schemas were used significantly more than other types.ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that integrating Johnson’s theory of image schemas with cognitive-behavioral therapy may offer a complementary conceptual framework for understanding how anxious patients might reframe their negative thoughts and underlying mental models.

Background

Cognitive linguistics is a branch of cognitive science, and explores how language organises, processes, and transmits information, focusing on the relationship between language, mind, and human experience (Boyle et al., 2022; Lakoff, 2019). Mark Johnson’s image schemas theory highlights the connection between the body, mind, and abstract concepts, emphasising that cognitive structures stem from physical interactions, with language reflecting these cognitive processes (Johnson, 2013). Mark Johnson's theory posits that human cognition is rooted in physical experiences and environmental interactions, challenging the mind-body dualism (Johnson, 2013). His concepts, including embodiment, image schemas, and conceptual metaphors, help explain the relationship between the body, mind, and language, and are applied in fields like linguistics, psychology, and cognitive science (Johnson, 2013). Image schemas, as foundational mental structures formed from physical experiences, influence abstract thinking and align with cognitive-behavioural therapy in modifying anxiety-related thought patterns (Johnson, 2013).

From a cognitive-behavioural perspective, image schemas can be understood as underlying cognitive structures that influence the interpretation of experiences, emotional responses, and behavioural tendencies. While CBT primarily focuses on identifying and modifying dysfunctional thoughts, beliefs, and cognitive distortions, image schema theory provides a deeper account of how these cognitive patterns may originate from embodied experiences and recurring interactions with the environment. Thus, integrating image schema theory with CBT offers a complementary framework in which maladaptive experiential patterns can be identified through language and addressed through cognitive restructuring. In the context of anxiety disorders, particularly generalised anxiety disorder, examining image schemas may reveal how abstract concepts such as threat, control, uncertainty, and vulnerability are cognitively organised and expressed during therapeutic interactions.

The image schema theory, in its modern form, originates from the work of English psychologist Frederic Bartlett, who described schemas as mental structures formed from past experiences, representing stereotyped scenarios from repeated events (Bartlett, 1995; Qiu & Huang, 2012). This concept, tied to Piaget's research (Leahy, 2017), explains how physical experiences shape cognitive processes and abstract thinking (Evans & Green, 2018). In psychotherapy, maladaptive schemas contribute to emotional and cognitive patterns, often causing mental disorders (Leahy, 2017). Therapeutic approach CBT believes that by changing the tendency of a person's thoughts and emotions and reorganising behaviour, it is possible to cope with pain and suffering more easily and even prevent it from occurring (Wright et al., 2017). Image schemas are crucial in understanding generalised anxiety disorder (GAD), a condition characterised by chronic worry and physical symptoms that disrupt daily life (Barlow, 2004). The aim of this study was to identify, classify, and quantify image schemas employed by psychotherapists during cognitive-behavioural therapy sessions with patients diagnosed with Generalised Anxiety Disorder and to explore the conceptual organisation of therapeutic discourse through Johnson’s image schema framework. The significance of this approach lies in its potential to modify harmful cognitive structures, offering effective treatments for GAD and enhancing psychological resilience (Clark & Beck, 2011; Leonard & Abramovitch, 2019).

Research on treating patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) using cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) has explored topics such as treatment effectiveness (Burhan et al., 2023; Öst et al., 2023; Pettman et al., 2023; Saadlou et al., 2022; Zarotti et al., 2023), telemedicine (Ardi et al., 2023; Karpov et al., 2023; Ritola et al., 2022; Stephenson et al., 2023; Trenoska Basile et al., 2022), and comparative therapies (Ghaderi et al., 2022; Solem et al., 2021; Timulak et al., 2022). However, there has been limited focus on the linguistic aspects of therapy, particularly the role of image schemas in discourse during treatment. Studies on metaphors (Deng et al., 2022; Qiu, 2023; Seeman, 2023; Tay, 2018) and image schemas in mental disorders like anxiety and depression (Yu & Tay, 2020) have been conducted, but the linguistic analysis of image schemas in GAD treatment remains underexplored. Verbal communication is essential for effective diagnosis and treatment, especially in an era where anxiety has become more prevalent. Johnson’s image schema theory, when integrated with the cognitive framework of CBT, provides insights into the cognitive and experiential mechanisms underlying anxiety. This research aims to fill the gap by analysing therapeutic discourse interactions, providing valuable insights for improving therapeutic communication and treatment outcomes in anxiety disorders.

Methods

This study utilised a mixed-method (qualitative–quantitative) approach to investigate therapeutic discourse in sessions with patients diagnosed with Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD). Initially, conversation analysis based on Johnson’s image schemas framework was conducted to identify dominant schemas and their conceptual patterns. Subsequently, descriptive statistics and inferential analyses, including the Chi-Square Goodness of Fit and pairwise Z-tests for proportions, were employed to evaluate the significance and relative importance of each schema category. This integrated methodology provided a clear and rigorous understanding of the conceptual organisation of therapeutic discourse and elucidated the functional role of image schemas in the therapeutic process.

Data for this study were collected through audio recordings of psychotherapy sessions involving 16 patients diagnosed with Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) receiving cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT). All participants had received a clinical diagnosis of Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) from their treating licensed clinical psychologists prior to participation in the study. Diagnoses were established during routine clinical assessment according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria. Following diagnosis, the same clinical psychologists conducted cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), and dialogues of treatment sessions constituted the dataset analysed in this study. The analytical focus of the study was the discourse produced by psychotherapists during CBT sessions. Image schemas were identified and coded from therapist-generated utterances, while client contributions were considered as contextual elements within the therapeutic interaction rather than primary units of schema analysis. All psychotherapy sessions included in this study were conducted by licensed clinical psychologists who had received formal training and certification in cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). Therapist–client dialogues were obtained exclusively from routine CBT sessions delivered by these qualified therapists, ensuring that the analysed discourse reflected established CBT principles. Therapeutic dialogues analysed in this study were collected longitudinally across the complete course of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), from the initial through the final treatment session. As the primary objective of this study was to investigate the linguistic and conceptual characteristics of therapeutic discourse, standardised measures of symptom severity and treatment outcomes were not included in the study design. The recordings were conducted by the psychotherapists themselves, without the presence of researchers during sessions, which helped reduce potential observer bias and preserved the natural therapeutic interaction. To ensure ethical integrity and data confidentiality, several strict measures were implemented. First, all patients provided informed consent prior to participation, including information about recording procedures, data usage, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw at any stage. Second, data anonymization was ensured by removing or altering all identifying information, including names and personal details, both during recording and transcription. Third, all audio files were stored in a secure, encrypted system, with access restricted exclusively to authorised research team members. Finally, participating psychotherapists adhered to professional ethical obligations, formally committing to confidentiality and data protection standards, ensuring that no information would be disclosed outside the research context.

The samples were selected purposefully. In purposive sampling, the sample units are selected on purpose and to represent a specific key criterion. The sampling criteria in this study include five key criteria that were considered in order to select participants who could provide valid data. These criteria are: key, well-known, theoretical knowledge, diversity, and motivation to participate. To achieve the key criterion, an effort was made to select psychotherapists who had sufficient knowledge, experience, and expertise in the field of the research topic. Selecting key individuals is important because these participants usually have deeper insight into the phenomenon being studied and can provide more accurate information. Additionally, their work experience and professional skills can help enrich the data analysis. In order to ensure that the data collected was obtained from reliable sources knowledgeable about the subject under study, an attempt was made to use psychotherapists who are well-known and reputable in the research field. These individuals are usually recognised in the scientific, professional, or clinical community and have high credibility in their field due to their research, educational, or therapeutic activities. Selecting such participants increases the validity and generalisability of the research results. To achieve the theoretical knowledge criterion, participants who had a deep and comprehensive understanding of the various dimensions of the phenomenon under study were used. Theoretical knowledge allows them to provide a more detailed analysis of research concepts and help to better understand the underlying mechanisms of the subject. Such knowledge can be the result of academic education, scientific research, or practical experience in a related field. To increase the comprehensiveness and diversity of findings, this study attempted to select patients from different cultures, social, and economic backgrounds. This criterion helps researchers gain diverse perspectives on patients' lived experiences and make the results more applicable to different groups. Considering cultural and social diversity can also help identify common patterns and meaningful differences in the phenomenon under study. Another important criterion in selecting samples was the participants' level of interest and motivation to participate in the research. The active and enthusiastic participation of participants plays an important role in the quality of the data collected. Because people who are more motivated usually provide more complete and accurate information. To ensure that the motivation criterion was met, an effort was made to select individuals with interest and commitment to the research process so that they could collaborate more effectively. These five criteria have helped researchers select a purposeful, valid, and diverse sample that can provide rich and meaningful data to investigate the phenomenon under study. Using these criteria ensures that the research findings have high scientific validity and can be generalised to similar situations.

In the qualitative research, obtaining a deep understanding of the studied phenomenon was the criterion for determining the sample size. To achieve this understanding, the sample size was determined based on theoretical saturation. That is, when no new information was observed about the phenomenon under study. In this study, researchers reached theoretical saturation by recording conversations from therapy sessions of 16 patients (On average, each patient had 8 to 10 treatment sessions).

The selection method and inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: patients with generalised anxiety disorder treated by psychotherapists with a CBT therapeutic approach, willing to participate voluntarily, fluent in Persian, and having the necessary skills and ability to express opinions and feelings and appropriately convey information. Exclusion criteria also included unwillingness to voluntarily participate in the study. Also, individuals receiving psychotropic medication during the study period were excluded from participation. This criterion was adopted to minimise potential pharmacological confounding effects on therapeutic discourse and to facilitate a more focused analysis of therapist–client interactions within the cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) process. To enhance the clinical homogeneity of the study sample, individuals with severe psychiatric disorders or psychiatric conditions constituting the primary clinical diagnosis other than Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) were excluded from participation. Accordingly, only participants whose primary clinical diagnosis was GAD were included in the final sample. The age of entry into the study was 30 to 60 years. Participants were restricted to the 30–60-year age range to obtain a relatively homogeneous sample and to minimise potential variability associated with developmental and age-related differences in cognitive functioning, language use, and interpersonal communication, thereby allowing a more consistent analysis of therapeutic discourse.

After recording the data, the conversations during the treatment process were converted into written text, and the frequency and types of image schemas used during the treatment of patients were investigated. To analyse the data, Johnson’s theory of image schemas (Johnson, 2013) was used, specifically, types of path schemas, containment schemas, and force schemas were investigated. Criterion of maximum diversity and confirmability and credibility in qualitative research makes it possible to trust the results.

In this study, multiple methodological procedures were implemented to ensure the reliability, validity, and reproducibility of the analytical process. Data collection was conducted through audio recordings of psychotherapy sessions involving 16 patients diagnosed with generalised anxiety disorder. To minimise potential observer bias, researchers were not physically present during the sessions, and all recordings were made by the psychotherapists themselves, ensuring a more naturalistic therapeutic setting.

Sample size determination followed the principle of theoretical saturation, whereby data collection continued until no new conceptual information emerged. This approach enhanced the comprehensiveness and depth of the dataset, thereby strengthening the robustness of the findings. The analytical framework was grounded in Johnson’s cognitive schema theory (Johnson, 2013), a well-established model in cognitive linguistics, which provided a validated theoretical foundation for identifying and interpreting image schemas.

Data analysis was conducted through a systematic and stepwise procedure. First, all therapeutic conversations were transcribed verbatim. Then, following Johnson’s analytical framework, the coding process proceeded through a series of structured stages. First, the discourse data were segmented into meaningful analytical units based on semantic coherence rather than sentence boundaries. Each unit was then examined in relation to the defining characteristics of the image schema categories proposed by Johnson. Coding decisions were guided by conceptual meaning, contextual function, and the role of each expression within the surrounding discourse rather than by isolated lexical items. When a segment appeared compatible with more than one schema category, the broader discourse context and the conceptual function of the expression were re-examined to determine the dominant schema. Ambiguous cases were revisited through iterative comparison with previously coded instances and the operational definitions of the schema categories until the most conceptually consistent interpretation was identified. This iterative procedure ensured that coding decisions remained theoretically grounded and internally consistent throughout the analysis. Diagram 1 summarises the coding protocol.

The identification of image schemas was conducted by both authors based on Johnson's theoretical framework. The authors independently examined the transcripts and subsequently held joint discussions to compare their interpretations. Any discrepancies in schema identification were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. This consensus-based coding approach was adopted to enhance the consistency and trustworthiness of the qualitative analysis.

To enhance the credibility of the findings, peer review was conducted by experts in linguistics, psychology, and social medicine. This interdisciplinary evaluation contributed to reducing potential interpretative errors and improving analytical consistency. Furthermore, methodological triangulation was employed, integrating qualitative analysis of schema identification with quantitative methods, including frequency and percentage calculations, as well as inferential statistical tests such as the Chi-square test and Z-test for proportions. Additionally, multiple data sources from different therapy sessions were utilised to strengthen analytical depth and reduce bias.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, incorporating both descriptive and inferential procedures. The use of standardised software contributed to minimising human error and enhancing analytical precision. All procedures were systematically documented to allow replication and external verification. Ethical considerations were strictly observed; informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality of data was maintained throughout the study, thereby ensuring compliance with ethical research standards and increasing participant trust.

By carefully following the above steps, the reliability of the analyses in this research is at a high level. Using these approaches ensures that the findings of this research are reliable and generalisable to similar contexts.

Results

Qualitative data analysis

Johnson believes that the conceptual complexity of the mental structure is the result of the embodied nature of humans and the type of concepts that humans can create (Johnson, 2013). The most important topic discussed in embodied and its relation with conceptual structure are image schemas. Johnson (2013) introduces several image schemas. These schemas are path schema, containment schema and force schema. In the following, these schemas and their types are introduced with examples from the dataset.

Path schema

One of the types of schemas is path schema. Human movement and observing the movement of other moving phenomena provide human with an experience to create an abstract schema in her/his mind from this physical movement and consider such a feature about what is not able to move (Johnson, 2013). The path schema has the types listed in Table 1 along with explanations and examples.

Table 1.Types of path schemas.Types of SchemasDescription of SchemasLocomotion schemaAny phenomenon and activity in order to change its state, requires the existence of a force, which is called a locomotion schema (Johnson, 2013).Source schemaA body to begin its motion, it must move from a specific source. The same source is considered as the starting point of phenomena in the human mind, which is called the source schema (Johnson, 2013).Path schemaBy being in a specific path during movement or by seeing the passage path of other objects and phenomena, the path schema is formed in the human mind (Johnson, 2013).Target schemaEvery movement stops somewhere and this stopping point forms the mental image of the target schema in the human mind (Johnson, 2013).

Types of path schemas.

Path schemas are mental tools that help humans understand and interpret movement and changes in the environment. These schemas include locomotion, source, path, and target, and each refers to different aspects of movement and change. These concepts are used in cognitive psychology and neuroscience to better understand the mental processes associated with movement and change. The following are the types of path schemas:

Locomotion Schema

This schema states that any change in the state of an object or phenomenon requires the application of force. This force can be physical (such as pressure or pull) or mental (such as effort or motivation). These schemas help people understand the cause of change.

Examples:

In Persian: [Yek meghdari ʃoma tu ʔin ghaziye dær vaghe ʃedæt e tævajohi ke neʃun midid ziyad hast væ ʔæmælæn ye moʃkel ʔijad mikone, chon ʃoma dige ʔun ʔenerẑi e sabegh ro nædarid.]

English Translation: The intensity of attention that you show about this issue is a little too much. This creates a problem for you because you don't have the same energy as you used to.

In Persian: [ʔægær motevajeh ʃodeʔid ke biʃ ʔæz hæd be kari mæʃǧul hæstid væ ʔæz χod miporsid chera digær ʔan tævanayi e sabegh ra nædarid, ʃayæd zæman e ʔan reside baʃæd ke be ʔin vagheʔiyæt tævæjoh konid: tæmærkoz e ʃædid bær yek mozuʔ e χas mitune baʔes beʃe ke janbehay e mohem e digæri ʔæz zendegiyetan ra nadide begirid.]

English Translation: If you find yourself over-committing and wondering why you’re not as productive as you used to be, it might be time to consider this fact: Focusing too hard on one thing can cause you to neglect other important aspects of your life.

In example 1, ‘decreased energy and attention due to excessive focus on a specific topic’ and in example 2, ‘loss of former ability due to excessive preoccupation with one task and neglect of other aspects of life’ are considered as locomotion schemas that cause a change in the person's energy state.

Illustrative analysis: In Example 1, the expression the intensity of attention presents attention as an excessive cognitive force directed toward a single issue. This force is explicitly linked to a change in the client's condition through the statement This creates a problem for you. As a consequence, the client moves from a previous state of having sufficient energy to a new state of reduced energy (you don't have the same energy as you used to). This progression from one experiential state to another constitutes a conceptual movement along a path. Since the transition is driven by the application of cognitive force, the utterance is classified as an instance of the Locomotion schema, a subtype of the Path schema, in which force brings about movement from one state to another. In Example 2, the expression focusing too hard on one thing conceptualises intense cognitive focus as a force directed toward a single goal. The adjective too hard indicates that the cognitive effort exceeds a balanced level. This excessive force is explicitly linked to its consequence through the causal expression can cause you to neglect other important aspects of your life. As a result, the individual undergoes a transition from a balanced state of attending to multiple aspects of life to a state in which important areas are neglected, leading to reduced productivity. According to the definition of the Locomotion schema, this change of state is brought about by the application of cognitive force. Therefore, the utterance is classified as an instance of the Locomotion schema, as the excessive mental effort drives a conceptual movement from one psychological state to another.

Source Schema

This schema refers to the starting point of movement or change. Every movement or change begins from a specific origin, and this origin is considered in the human mind as the starting point of phenomena.

Examples:

In Persian: [ʔæz bæchegi ba hæm ʔaʃena budid væ ʔæz hæmun ʔævæl beheʃ fekr mikærdid.]

English Translation: You've known each other since childhood and you've been thinking about her from the very beginning.

In Persian: [ʔin tæsævor ʔæz kudaki dær ʃoma ʃekl gerefte ʔæst væ ghodrætæʃ hæm besyar ziyad ʔæst. ʃoma be ʔin ʔelæt ke mæbada ʔæz ræha ʃodegi ʔasib bebinid, ʔazorde χater ʃævid, vared e rabete ba kasi nemiʃævid væ tærjih midæhid tænha væ mojæræd zendegi konid.]

English Translation: This idea has been formed in you since childhood and is very powerful. You do not enter into an intimate relationship with anyone because you fear being hurt and offended by abandonment and prefer to live alone and single.

In example 1, ‘acquaintance from childhood and the beginning of thoughts about a specific person from that time’ and in example 2, ‘the formation of ideas about the fear of abandonment (which originates from the individual's childhood experiences) and its impact on current decision-making such as avoiding intimate relationships’ are considered as the source schema.

Path Schema

This schema refers to the direction of movement or change. This type of schema is formed in the human mind by being in a certain direction or observing the movement of other objects. These schemas help humans understand and predict the direction of movement or changes.

Examples:

In Persian: [Væ ʃoma dust darid ke ʔin mæsir e dust daʃtænetun ʔedame peida kone.]

English Translation: And you want this path of your love to continue.

In Persian: [Gahi ʔoghat yek chizhayi bayæd tæǧyir konæd. Doroste ke gam bærdaʃtæn dær yek mæsir e kamelæn jædid, doʃvar ʔæst ʔæma doʃvartær ʔæz ʔan mandæn dær hæman mæsiri ʔæst ke maneʔ e roʃd væ piʃræft e ʃoma miʃævæd.]

English Translation: Sometimes things have to change. It's hard to take a completely new path, but it's even harder to stay on the same path that's holding you back from growing and progressing.

In example 1, ‘the desire to continue a desirable path in life’ and in example 2, ‘the necessity to move on the path of growth and progress’ are considered as path schemas.

Target Schema

This schema refers to the end point of a movement or change. Every movement or change stops somewhere, and this stopping point is considered the destination. These types of schemas help people understand the goals and outcomes of movement or change.

Examples:

In Persian: [ʔin hæd ʔæ negærani dær hæme ma vojud dare, chon ma bæray e residæn be hædæfhamun bayæd bærnamerizi konim væ ʔin dæghighæn mærz e bein e negærani e mofid væ ǧeir e mofid hæst.]

English Translation: We all have this level of worry. Because we have to plan to achieve our goals and this is exactly the border between useful and useless worry.

In Persian: [Ghæhr e hæmsæretan bæray e ʃoma besiyar narahætkonænde ʔæst. Hæmin mæsʔæle baʔes ʃode ke hæmsær e ʃoma gahi ʔæz ʔin ræftar bæray e residæn be χastehay e χod ʔestefade konæd.]

English Translation: It is very upsetting for you to see your spouse being angry. This is why your spouse sometimes uses this behaviour to achieve her own desires.

In example 1, ‘Planning to achieve goals and the boundary between useful and unhelpful worry’ and in example 2, ‘The spouse's use of violence as a tool to achieve her desires’ are target schemas and well demonstrate how forces and their interactions in the mind can affect human behaviour, emotions, and decision-making.

Containment schema

Types of containment schemas.

containment schemas are mental tools that help humans understand and interpret the containment, interior and exterior space, and content of objects in the environment. These schemas include container, full-empty, content, and inside-out, each of which refers to different aspects of container and space. These concepts are used in cognitive psychology and neuroscience to better understand mental processes related to containment and space. The following are the types of containment schemas:

Container Schema

This schema states that humans form an idea of ​​container and space in their minds by being in different environments and perceiving different container. This schema helps human view different environments as containers that can contain objects or people.

Examples:

In Example 1, ‘Worrying about exam results and their impact on one’s mindset’ and in Example 2, ‘Strict criteria for choosing a spouse and their impact on one’s loneliness’ are container schemas. In these examples, one’s mind is considered as a container that is filled with thoughts and worries.

This schema refers to the concept that a containment can be full or empty. This schema helps human understand that objects or environments can contain or be empty of content.

This schema refers to the content inside a containment. This schema is formed in the human mind by seeing objects that have containment and that something can fit inside them. This schema helps humans understand the content inside objects or environments.

This schema refers to the experience of placing oneself and objects inside or outside of places that have containment. This schema helps human understand that objects or people can be inside or outside of an environment or containment.

Types of force schemas.

Force schemas are mental tools that help humans understand and interpret forces, obstacles, and changes in the path of movement in the environment. These schemas include compulsion, blockage, counterforce, diversion, diversion, enablement, and attraction, and each refers to different aspects of forces and the interactions between them. These concepts are used in cognitive psychology and neuroscience to better understand the mental processes associated with forces and interactions. The following are the types of force schemas:

Compulsion Schema

This schema states that a person may be forced to move or change due to a force exerted on them. This force can be physical or psychological.

Examples:

In Persian: [Ye jurayi nemitævanid ʔæz læhaz e dæruni kontoroleʃ konid væ be ʔun halæt e tæbiʔi χodetun bærgærdid. ʔin mæsʔæle χeili ʔazardæhænde ʃode bæray e ʃoma]

English Translation: Somehow you can't control it internally and return to your normal self. This has become very annoying for you.

In Persian: [Dær vagheʔ meil be ʃadi væ færar ʔæz narahæti, mekanizm e tæbiʔi mæǧz e ʔensanhast. Be hæmin χater, væghti tæsmim be ʔænjam e kari darid ke moʔtæghed hæstid narahæti væ rænj be donbal daræd, mæǧz særdærgom miʃævæd væ nemitævanæd be ʃoma komæk konæd væ tænha chizi ke ʔæz ʔin tælaʃ bæray e ʃoma baghi mimanæd, ʔehsas e naʔomidi ʔæst.]

English Translation: In fact, the desire to be happy and avoid pain is a natural mechanism of the human brain. Therefore, when you decide to do something that you believe will cause pain and suffering, the brain becomes confused and cannot help you, and the only thing left for you from this effort is a feeling of frustration.

In example 1, ‘inability to control oneself and return to normal’ and in example 2, ‘the brain’s natural desire for happiness and escape from discomfort and its impact on decision-making’ are examples of the compulsion schema.

Blockage Schema

This schema refers to forces that act as barriers or obstacles that prevent you from continuing on your path. These blockages can be physical or psychological.

Examples:

In Persian: [ʃoma χeili zud ʔezdevaj kærdid væ moχalefæt e hæmsæretan ba ʔedame tæhsil e ʃoma baʔes ʃode ke daneʃgah ro kenar begzarid.]

English Translation: You got married very early, and your husband's opposition to your continuing your education caused you to drop out of college.

In Persian: [ʃoma fekr mikonid ke mærdha ʔæslæn ʔehsasat nædarænd væ hær cheghædr sæʔy mikonid ʔehsas e mohæbætetan ra be ʔanha neʃan bedæhid, bifayede ʔæst. ʃoma dochar e mæhrumiyæt e hæyæjani hæstid væ daʔemæn be ʔin fekr mikonid ke be ʃæχsi ke dustæʃ darid nemiresid. ʃoma dochar e noʔi ʔehsas e puchi væ mæʔnabaχtegi hæstid væ hæmiʃe dær zendegi be donbal e gomʃodeʔi migærdid ke be ʔan nemiresid.]

English Translation: You think that men have no feelings at all and that no matter how hard you try to show them your feelings of affection, it is useless. You are emotionally deprived and constantly think that you will not reach the person you love. You have a feeling of emptiness and meaninglessness and you are always looking for something missing in life that you cannot reach.

In example 1, ‘dropping out of school because the spouse opposes the person’s continued education’ and in example 2, ‘feeling of emotional deprivation and emptiness due to not being able to reach the person he loves’ are examples of the blockage schema.

Counterforce Schema

This schema is formed when two forces of equal intensity collide. This collision can lead to equilibrium or a change in the direction of motion.

Examples:

In Persian: [χob piʃ miyad kæsani ro bebinid ke ʔæz læhaz e ʃæχsiyæti hæmtæraz e ʃoma hæstæn væ be hæmin dælil ba ʔanha mitævanid rabete ye behtæri bærghærar konid.]

English Translation: Well, it happens that you meet people who are on the same level as you in terms of personality, and because of that, you can establish a better relationship with them.

In Persian: [ʔin ʔehsas e malekiyæt væ ʔenhesartælæbi væ vabæstegi ye ʔefrati nesbæt be ʔæfrad e mohem e zendegi baʔes ʃode ke ʃoma hengam e jodayi ʔæz ʔætrafiyan væ dustanetan dochar e ʔezterab o ʔaʃoftegi ye ruhi ye ʃædid ʃævid.]

English Translation: This sense of ownership, exclusivity, and extreme dependence on the important people in your life has caused you to experience severe anxiety and mental turmoil when separated from those around you and friends.

In example 1, ‘Establishing better relationships with people who are personality-aligned with the patient’ and in example 2, ‘Anxiety and mental turmoil resulting from feelings of ownership and excessive dependence on important people in life’ are examples of the counterforce schema.

Diversion Schema

This schema is formed when a force is moving along a path and collides with one or more other forces, causing the original force to change direction.

Examples:

In Persian: [ʔazmun e vorudi ye daneʃgah bæraye ʃoma yek sæd e bozorg bude væ ʃoma tælaʃ kærdid ba tæmam e moʃkelati ke piʃ e ru daʃtid, ʔin maneʔ ro poʃt e sær begzarid. English Translation: The university entrance exam was a big obstacle for you, and you tried to overcome it despite all the difficulties you faced. Your efforts have brought your life to a new level, and now you are in a better situation than before.

In Persian: [gahi ʔoghat yek chizhayi bayæd tæǧyir konæd. Doroste ke gam bærdaʃtæn dær yek mæsir kamelæn jædid, doʃvar ʔæst ʔæma doʃvartær ʔæz ʔan, mandæn dær hæman mæsiri ʔæst ke maneʔ e roʃd væ piʃræft e ʃoma miʃævæd.]

English Translation: Sometimes things have to change. It's hard to take a completely new path, but it's even harder to stay on the same path that's holding you back from growing and progressing.

In example 1, ‘Changing the direction of life after passing the university entrance exam’ and in example 2, ‘The necessity of changing direction for growth and progress, even if this change is difficult’ are examples of the diversion schema.

This schema is formed when there is an open path in the way of applying force, either without any obstacles in the way or where the obstacle can be removed.

Examples:

In Persian: [ʃoma bæray e ʔinke be χastætun beresid, bayæd sæʔy konid mævaneʔ ro poʃt e sær begzarid væ jelow berævid.]

English Translation: You are constantly worried that you will lose the people closest to you in some way. Therefore, you are constantly anxious. You express your fear of abandonment and being broken with anger, rage, and panic.

In example 1, ‘trying to overcome obstacles and achieve desires’ and in example 2, ‘anxiety caused by fear of losing loved ones and its manifestation in anger and rage’ are examples of removal of restraint schemas.

Enablement Schema

This schema refers to the ability or inability of individuals to do certain things. In this schema, potential forces move in a certain direction.

Examples:

In Persian: [Sæʔy konid mesl e ghæbl bahaʃ bærχord nækonid, kæmi mænteghitær bærχord konid, sæʔy konid hæman ʃævahed ro biyarid tuye zehnetun væ ræftar e molayemtær ro jaygozin e ʔæmælkærd e ghæblitun konid.]

English Translation: This means that the adult’s mentality must be strong enough to know where, how much, and in what way to express her anger.

In Example 1, ‘Replacing a milder behaviour with a previous behaviour using mental evidence’ and in Example 2, ‘The importance of controlling anger and expressing it in an appropriate manner’ are examples of a enablement schema. This schema is formed when an object tends to attract other objects towards itself. This attraction can be physical or psychological.

Discussion

The findings indicate that image schemas constitute fundamental embodied cognitive structures through which individuals organise and interpret both physical and abstract experience. Grounded in recurring sensorimotor interactions with the environment, these conceptual patterns extend beyond perceptual experience to support higher-level reasoning and meaning construction. Among the image schemas identified in the present study, path, containment, and force schemas emerged as prominent conceptual resources, each contributing to cognition through distinct experiential foundations.

Path schemas are rooted in recurrent experiences of movement through space. Repeated interactions involving an origin, a trajectory, and a destination provide a conceptual framework for representing progression, intentional action, and goal-directed behaviour. Because these patterns are deeply embedded in everyday bodily experience, they are readily recruited to structure abstract domains such as planning, problem solving, and personal development. The goal component of the path schema, for example, enables individuals to conceptualise desired outcomes as attainable destinations, thereby facilitating organised planning and adaptive responses when confronting stressful or anxiety-provoking situations.

In contrast, containment schemas arise from bodily experiences associated with bounded spaces and the distinction between interior and exterior regions. These recurring experiences support the conceptualisation of psychological phenomena in terms of boundaries, inclusion, exclusion, and internal organisation. Such representations allow individuals to differentiate internal emotional experiences from external environmental influences, providing a coherent framework for understanding subjective psychological states. This conceptual distinction may also have practical value in clinical settings, particularly in interventions targeting disorders such as generalised anxiety disorder, where recognising the relationship between internal experiences and external circumstances is therapeutically important.

Force schemas reflect another dimension of embodied experience by capturing interactions involving resistance, support, attraction, compulsion, blockage, diversion, counterforce, enablement, and the removal of restraint. Through repeated encounters with physical forces, individuals develop abstract conceptualisations of agency, control, constraint, and resilience. These schemas therefore provide a cognitive basis for understanding how people perceive and respond to psychological and social obstacles. Among these patterns, enablement is especially significant because it represents the perception of available capacity or opportunity for action, a conceptualisation that may reinforce self-efficacy and facilitate more effective coping with life's challenges.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the identified image schemas do not merely represent recurring linguistic expressions but rather reflect deeply embodied conceptual structures that organise human cognition. Their experiential grounding enables abstract psychological concepts to be understood through familiar patterns of bodily interaction, highlighting their relevance for both cognitive processing and therapeutic interpretation.

These findings may also be considered in relation to the cultural and discursive context in which the therapeutic interactions were produced. Although image schemas are widely regarded within cognitive linguistics as universal embodied conceptual structures grounded in recurrent sensorimotor experience, their linguistic realisation and communicative functions are shaped by the resources of particular languages as well as by the conventions of specific discourse communities. Accordingly, the patterns identified in the present Persian CBT corpus are likely to reflect the interaction of universal embodied conceptual organisation, characteristics of Persian linguistic expression, and the communicative objectives of psychotherapeutic discourse. Rather than attributing these conceptual patterns to any single source, the findings suggest that universal cognitive structures are instantiated through language- and discourse-specific practices. At the same time, because the present study is based exclusively on therapeutic interactions conducted in Persian, the relative contribution of these dimensions cannot be determined. Cross-linguistic and cross-cultural comparisons of therapeutic discourse would therefore provide valuable evidence for assessing the extent to which similar image schema patterns reflect universal embodied cognition or discourse practices shaped by particular linguistic and cultural contexts.

The identification of path, containment, and force schemas not only reveals recurring conceptual patterns in CBT discourse but also provides insight into the conceptual characteristics that make these embodied structures particularly well suited to therapeutic communication. Their prominence appears to reflect the communicative and therapeutic objectives of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, in which abstract psychological experiences are routinely conceptualised through cognitively accessible and experientially grounded representations.

CBT is fundamentally a change-oriented therapeutic approach. Its central aim is to help clients recognise maladaptive patterns of thinking, develop alternative interpretations, and implement adaptive behavioural strategies. Such therapeutic processes naturally rely on conceptual structures that represent movement toward desired outcomes, the negotiation of psychological obstacles, and the organisation of internal experiences. Consequently, path schemas provide an intuitive framework for conceptualising therapeutic progress as goal-directed movement, force schemas capture the dynamics of resistance, agency, effort, and psychological change, whereas containment schemas enable emotions, thoughts, and beliefs to be represented as bounded mental entities that can be examined, regulated, and transformed.

From this perspective, the predominance of these image schemas reflects more than their frequency of occurrence. Rather, it demonstrates the close correspondence between the embodied logic of these conceptual structures and the communicative demands of CBT. Because therapy requires clients to understand highly abstract psychological processes through familiar experiential patterns, these schemas become effective resources for constructing therapeutic meaning, facilitating cognitive restructuring, and supporting collaborative dialogue between therapist and client.

Based on the statistical analysis, force schemas were the most frequent image schema, accounting for 49.44% of the identified instances. Path and containment schemas followed, representing 27.08% and 23.48%, respectively. The predominance of force schemas suggests that themes related to power, control, and barriers are particularly salient in the analysed CBT discourse of patients with generalised anxiety disorder. Similarly, the relative distribution of path and containment schemas indicates the importance of movement, goals, and boundaries in the conceptual organisation of therapeutic interactions. These findings may have potential implications for understanding how meaning is constructed and communicated in CBT discourse.

The results of statistical data analysis showed that all three schemas, despite differences in their usage, exist in therapeutic discourses. However, force schemas have the highest frequency and use during therapy, which may be due to the context of the speech and the nature of the discourse. This shows that patients with generalised anxiety disorder pay more attention to issues related to power, control, and obstacles. Considering that the reason people with generalised anxiety disorder refer to psychologists is their inability to solve personal problems, psychologists have tried to use a variety of force schemas to solve problems and treat patient anxiety disorder. In fact, psychologists have tried to change the course and lifestyle of patients by using various schemas. They have tried to change maladaptive behaviours and incorrect emotional patterns that stem from the original maladaptive schemas by institutionalising alternative schemas in the minds of clients and encouraging clients to look at their problems from a different perspective. These results, which confirm the theory under study, are somewhat in line with the researches of Leahy (Evans & Green, 2018) and Yu and Tay (Yu & Tay, 2020). Although force schemas were the most frequently identified image schema category in the analysed dialogues, this finding should be interpreted as reflecting their greater prevalence within the examined CBT discourse rather than as evidence of greater therapeutic importance. The present findings describe patterns of schema use in therapeutic communication and do not, by themselves, establish the relative effectiveness or clinical significance of individual image schema categories. While the observed distribution of image schemas may reflect conceptual patterns characteristic of cognitive behavioural therapy discourse, alternative explanations should also be considered. The identified schema patterns may have been influenced by the clinical characteristics of individuals with Generalised Anxiety Disorder and the dynamic nature of therapist–client interactions across different stages of psychotherapy. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as patterns observed within the present dataset rather than as characteristics that are unique to cognitive behavioural therapy. Although the present study interprets therapeutic discourse through the lens of image schema theory, it is important to acknowledge that linguistic expressions are inherently multifunctional. Therapeutic utterances may simultaneously perform cognitive, interpersonal, rhetorical, persuasive, supportive, and interactional functions, depending on the communicative context and participants’ interactional goals. Accordingly, the image schemas identified in this study should be understood as one analytical dimension of discourse organisation rather than the sole explanatory framework for linguistic choices. From this perspective, cognitive interpretations complement, rather than replace, discourse-analytic accounts of language use, recognising that linguistic meaning emerges through the dynamic interaction of cognitive, communicative, and social processes.

In the analysis of conversations from cognitive-behavioural therapy sessions with patients with generalised anxiety disorder, consistent verbal patterns were identified in therapists’ speech, reflecting their cognitive interventions and underlying conceptual structures in response to activated patient schemas. These patterns are understood not as a direct reflection of the patient's language, but rather as the therapist’s response to the activation of specific schemas in the patient.

The qualitative content analysis, supported by statistical findings, revealed that therapists frequently utilise structured verbal frameworks when assessing patients’ psychological states, identifying activated schemas, and facilitating discussions associated with cognitive restructuring.

Based on these finding, a systematic verbal model can be extracted from speech therapists, which consists of four main stages: a) description of the clinical situation, b) identification and naming of the activated schema, c) cognitive-behavioural analysis of the therapist, and d) recommendation of CBT-based therapeutic interventions. Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, it can be concluded that the image schemas identified in the analysis—particularly path, containment, and force—play an important role in structuring the conceptualisation of therapeutic experiences in CBT discourse. The recurrent occurrence of these schemas across the analysed therapeutic interactions supports the view that embodied conceptual structures contribute to how patients understand and communicate abstract psychological experiences. Among these schemas, force schemas were the most frequently observed in the dataset, suggesting their central role in representing internal struggles, resistance, and coping processes in the treatment of generalised anxiety disorder. Likewise, the recurring use of path and containment schemas reflects how therapeutic discourse conceptualises progress toward recovery, goal attainment, emotional boundaries, and the management of psychological experiences. Collectively, these empirical patterns support the broader conclusion that image schemas function as fundamental embodied mechanisms of language and thought, systematically structuring more complex psychological concepts. These findings may also inform future intervention development by providing a clearer understanding of how embodied conceptualisations facilitate therapeutic communication and support patients in coping with challenges and fostering psychological resilience.

Image schemas suggest that human thinking is shaped by fundamental physical and experiential patterns. These schemas are the primary mental structures that organise human experience of the world, such as balance, path, container, full-empty, force, etc. Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) also focuses on changing maladaptive thought patterns. The identification of recurrent path, containment, and force schemas in the analysed CBT discourse suggests that Johnson's theory of image schemas provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding how cognitive change is represented in psychotherapy. Building on these empirical findings, the identified image schema patterns suggest several potential clinical implications for cognitive-behavioural therapy in the treatment of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).

Identifying maladaptive image schemas in patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)

The identified image schema patterns may provide therapists with a useful conceptual framework for recognising how patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) conceptualise and communicate their experiences. In particular, containment schemas may reflect perceptions of psychological restriction, path schemas may represent how patients conceptualise movement toward or away from therapeutic goals, and force schemas may capture experiences of internal conflict, resistance, and coping. Recognising these recurrent conceptual patterns may contribute to a more systematic understanding of patients' embodied conceptualisations during therapeutic interactions.

Using image schema transformation in cognitive restructuring

The identified image schema patterns also suggest that cognitive restructuring may benefit from addressing not only maladaptive thoughts but also the embodied conceptual structures through which those thoughts are organised. From this perspective, therapists may encourage patients to reconstruct restrictive or dysfunctional image schemas into more adaptive conceptualisations that support alternative interpretations of their experiences. Consequently, image schema transformation may serve as a complementary conceptual tool within the cognitive restructuring process in CBT for patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).

Strengthening mindfulness practices through image schemas

The identified image schema patterns further indicate that image schemas may complement mindfulness practices within CBT for generalised anxiety disorder (GAD). Because mindfulness emphasises awareness of bodily sensations and present-moment experiences, embodied conceptualisations may help patients organise and interpret these experiences more effectively. Integrating image schemas into mindfulness-oriented practices may therefore facilitate therapeutic communication and support patients' awareness of their cognitive and emotional processes. These implications should be regarded as theoretical applications derived from the findings of the present study rather than as empirically validated therapeutic techniques.

Potential implications for exposure therapy

The identified image schema patterns likewise suggest potential applications for exposure therapy in the treatment of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD). By encouraging patients to reinterpret feared situations through alternative embodied conceptualisations, therapists may support greater cognitive flexibility during gradual exposure. Such an approach may help patients perceive therapeutic progress as a dynamic process rather than a fixed psychological state. Although these implications are grounded in the findings of the present study, their effectiveness as therapeutic strategies require further empirical investigation. Overall, integrating Johnson's theory of image schemas with cognitive-behavioural therapy may provide a complementary conceptual framework for understanding and facilitating cognitive change in patients with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).

The results of this study also suggest that constructing psychotherapeutic discourse within cognitive-behavioural approaches, particularly when incorporating image schemas, is a complex process. This complexity may arise from the use of technical and specialised language, which can reshape or condense the meanings of words and influence the semantic structure of therapeutic discourse. These findings highlight the potential value of training psychotherapists in systematic linguistic principles and communication strategies. Given that verbal communication is a central component of psychotherapy, the findings may contribute to a better understanding of how linguistically informed communication can support psychotherapeutic practice. However, the extent to which such an approach improves treatment outcomes for anxiety disorders requires further empirical investigation.

Limitations and suggestions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, standardised measures of anxiety symptom severity and treatment outcomes were not incorporated into the study design. The primary objective of this research was to examine the linguistic and conceptual characteristics of therapeutic discourse throughout the course of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), rather than to quantify changes in clinical symptoms. Future studies may integrate discourse analysis with validated clinical outcome measures to investigate the relationship between therapists’ use of image schemas and changes in symptom severity over the course of treatment.

Second, the observed distribution of image schemas may have been influenced, at least in part, by therapist-specific communication styles. Although the dataset comprised therapist–client dialogues from eight licensed clinical psychologists, each treating two clients, thereby reducing the influence of any single therapist’s linguistic style, individual differences in therapeutic communication may still have contributed to the observed discourse patterns. Future studies involving a larger number of therapists and explicitly examining therapist-level variation would help distinguish therapist-specific language use from discourse characteristics that are more broadly representative of cognitive behavioural therapy.

Third, the relatively limited sample size may restrict the generalisability of the findings. In addition, the study was conducted within a specific clinical and demographic context, and the resulting discourse patterns may not fully represent those observed in other populations or treatment settings. Accordingly, caution should be exercised when extending the findings beyond the characteristics of the present sample. Replication with larger, more diverse, and multicenter samples would enhance the robustness, external validity, and generalisability of the findings across different clinical settings, populations, and cultural contexts.

Fourth, the frequency with which specific image schemas occurred should not be interpreted as direct evidence of their relative therapeutic importance, as the present study was designed to examine discourse patterns rather than treatment effectiveness. Consequently, no causal inferences can be drawn regarding the contribution of particular image schemas to therapeutic change. Experimental and longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether the observed linguistic patterns play a causal role in facilitating cognitive restructuring, symptom improvement, or other therapeutic outcomes.

Fifth, an additional limitation concerns the potential for interpretive subjectivity in the identification and classification of image schemas. Although the analysis was guided by established theoretical definitions and a systematic coding framework, the interpretation of linguistic expressions inevitably involves a degree of researcher judgement. Certain utterances may plausibly be assigned to more than one image schema category, and alternative interpretations may emerge depending on the analytical perspective adopted. To minimise this concern, coding decisions were based on predefined theoretical criteria and were applied consistently throughout the dataset. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual interpretive bias cannot be entirely eliminated. Future research could strengthen the reliability and credibility of discourse-based image schema analysis by employing multiple independent coders, reporting inter-rater agreement statistics, and incorporating additional validation procedures.

Finally, the findings are specific to adults aged 30–60 years with a primary diagnosis of Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) who received cognitive behavioural therapy and should therefore be generalised to other clinical populations, age groups, therapeutic orientations, or cultural contexts with appropriate caution. Building on the present findings, future research should examine the use and functions of image schemas in the treatment of other psychiatric disorders to determine whether similar conceptual patterns emerge across different forms of psychopathology. Comparative studies across therapeutic orientations may further clarify whether the observed discourse characteristics are specific to CBT or reflect more general mechanisms of psychotherapeutic communication. Furthermore, given the prominent role of force schemas identified in the present study, future research should investigate their functional contribution to therapeutic processes and evaluate whether their systematic and theoretically informed use by CBT therapists can facilitate cognitive restructuring and improve treatment outcomes.
