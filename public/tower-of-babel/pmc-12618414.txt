The potential of Eco-friendly synthesis of multifunctional ZnO-CuO nanocomposites using Mentha longifolia extract for their biomedical applications
Authors: Shahira H EL-Moslamy, Esmail M El-Fakharany, Omkulthom Al kamaly, Mohamed H El-Sayed, Yousra A El-Maradny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we report the biogenic synthesis of ZnO-CuO nanocomposites (NCPs) utilizing Mentha longifolia leaf extract as both a reducing and capping candidate. The synthesis process was optimized utilizing the Plackett-Burman statistical design, achieving a maximum yield of 22.18 mg/mL under controlled conditions. The resulting ZnO-CuO NCPs exhibited a crystalline structure with an average particle size of 26.61 nm, as analyzed by XRD, TEM, and SEM approaches. FTIR spectroscopy demonstrated the presence of bioactive phytoconstituents, such as phenolic derivatives and alkaloids, which stabilized the nanocomposites. The ZnO-CuO NCPs demonstrated potent antimicrobial activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 180.47 µg/mL. In anticancer evaluations, the ZnO-CuO NCPs exhibited selective cytotoxicity against A549 (lung), HepG2 (liver), and MDA (breast) cancer cell lines, with selectivity indices (SI) of 4.88, 25.19, and 46.32, respectively. Apoptosis induction was confirmed through nuclear staining and morphological analysis. Additionally, the ZnO-CuO NCPs showed promising antiviral activity against herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) (IC50 = 9.29 µg/mL, SI = 63.24) and Adenovirus-7 (IC50 = 25.88 µg/mL, SI = 22.66), suggesting potential mechanisms involving viral replication inhibition. Molecular docking studies further supported the anticancer potential of the ZnO-CuO NCPs, revealing strong interactions with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Bcl-2-associated protein x (Bax), key regulators of angiogenesis and apoptosis. These findings highlight the multifunctional therapeutic potential of plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs, offering a sustainable and effective strategy for addressing antimicrobial resistance, cancer, and viral infections, with promising implications for future biomedical applications.

Introduction

A significant threat to public health is thought to arise from multidrug-resistant (MDR) microbes, the increasing rate of resistance to anticancer drugs, and their associated toxicity [1]. Consequently, there is a constant demand for innovative antimicrobial and antitumor medications. In the area of bio-nanomedicine, the therapeutic properties of nanoparticles in addressing health issues are particularly promising [2]. To combat antimicrobial resistance, a wide range of antimicrobial agents is being developed. Moreover, organic chemists are not only focused on creating novel antimicrobial drugs but are also synthesizing a variety of powerful nanomaterials. Notably, research conducted over the past decade has demonstrated the efficacy of antimicrobials containing metallic elements as potent antimicrobial agents [3]. For more than a decade, traditional methods for producing nanomaterials primarily involved physical and chemical procedures [4]. However, restrictions imposed by the World Health Organization have limited the use of these techniques for creating nanomaterials intended for bio-applications, including antibacterial and anticancer medications [5]. As a consequence, the green fabrication of nanomaterials has emerged as a direct, cost-effective, ecologically friendly, and energy-efficient alternative to conventional methods. This innovative approach has been enabled by advancements in nano-biotechnology, offering a safe and sustainable way to produce a diverse range of nanomaterials [6]. Typically, green synthesis relies on utilizing various biological sources, including extracts from plants, fungi, bacteria, and algae [7]. Among these sources, plants show a significant role in green synthesis approach uses various plant parts, including leaves, flowers, roots, stems, and seeds. The Mentha genus, regularly referred to as “wild mint,” belongs to the Lamiaceae (Labiatae) family, which comprises approximately 25–30 species [8]. This plant family is notable for its abundance of flavonoids, phenolic acids, and essential oils, all of which have been investigated for their potential medicinal benefits [9]. In Egypt and other Arab nations, mint is a widely consumed herb, often prepared as a hot beverage. Additionally, Mentha is used as a spice in certain dishes and is extensively utilized in the food, flavor, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries [10]. Historically, preparations made from Mentha species have been utilized to address various types of ailments, including heartburn, indigestion, coughs, flu, nausea, gallbladder and bile duct issues, herpes, and skin disorders such as pigmentation and acne [11]. Recent investigations have emphasized the use of Mentha species and other plants as stabilizing and reducing candidates in the green synthesis of various nanoscale materials [12–14]. Phytochemicals in plants, including tannins, pigments, alkaloids, phenols, ketones, nitrogenous compounds, carboxylic acids, terpenoids, and flavonoids, accumulate during the green synthesis process, where they interact with metals to stabilize, cap, and reduce them into nanomaterials [15].

Despite its advantages, one challenge in green nanomaterial production remains accomplishing the desired shape and morphology. To address this, substantial academic efforts have been made to optimize synthesis features, such as temperature, precursor amount, and plant extract concentration, to produce nanomaterials with favorable structural, morphological, and optical properties as efficient nanomedicines [16]. However, most studies rely on single-factor optimization, which is time-consuming and labor-intensive [17, 18]. In contrast, statistical experimental designs offer a more efficient and comprehensive approach. The Plackett-Burman Design (PBD), a type of fractional factorial design, is particularly advantageous as it allows for the computation of main effects with a minimal number of experiments and enables thorough examination of interactions between various factors. This highly efficient screening design not only overcomes the limitations of single-factor optimization but also reduces time and process variability, making it possible to identify the most critical variables from a vast pool [18, 19]. Optimizing the synthesis of nanomaterials has been shown to yield smaller, more uniformly shaped particles with enhanced stability and higher yields compared to non-optimized counterparts [17, 18]. Interestingly, the sole and beneficial characteristics of CuO and ZnO nanoparticles have garnered significant attention for their diverse biomedical uses. These include bioimaging, drug delivery, and antimicrobial, tumor-fighting, anti-diabetic, and anti-inflammatory capabilities [20, 21]. For instance, promising ZnO–CuO NCPs were previously synthesized through a green, eco-friendly method involving Tulsi leaf extract for the removal of textile dyes [22].

This study aimed to develop an eco-friendly synthesis method for ZnO-CuO NCPs using Mentha plant extracts. The phytochemical composition of the prepared aqueous Mentha extract was analyzed utilizing qualitative techniques. The synthesis parameters were optimized using the Plackett-Burman statistical design to maximize nanocomposite yield, ensuring efficiency and reproducibility. The resulting nanocomposites were well-characterized utilizing advanced techniques like energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential thermal analysis (DTA), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Biological evaluations included antimicrobial activity, cytotoxicity and cell viability analyses on normal and different cancer cell lines, as well as antiviral activity assessments. Additionally, an in-silico docking investigation was carried out to investigate the interaction of the nanocomposites with specific anticancer targets.

Material and Methods

Materials

To ensure the validity and accuracy of experimental results, we used high-purity analytical-grade chemicals and reagents. The following chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, UK: zinc salts (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O, Zn(NO3)2, and ZnSO4·H2O), copper salts (Cu(CH3COO)2·H2O, Cu(NO3)2, and CuSO4·5H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and ethanol (99.9% purity, HPLC grade). Additionally, we sourced the medicinal plants Mentha arvensis, Mentha longifolia, and Ocimum basilicum from the Fathalla market in Alexandria, Egypt. To assess the antimicrobial activity of the bio-synthesized nanocomposite, we used multidrug-resistant human microbes, involving Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Escherichia coli (ATCC 10536), and Candida albicans (ATCC 10231). These pathogens were generously supported by the Department of Bioprocess Development (BID) at the Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology Research Institute (GEBRI), City of Scientific Research and Technological Applications, Alexandria, Egypt. The study also utilized five cell lines obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and purchased from VACCERA, Egypt. These included the normal human lung fibroblast (Wi-38) cell line, green monkey kidney (Vero) cell line, and three cancer cell lines: human breast cancer (MDA), human hepatoma (HepG2), and human lung (A549) cancer cell lines. Wi-38, Vero, A549, and MDA cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) high glucose (Lonza, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). The HepG2 cell line was cultured and maintained in RPMI-1640 (Roswell Park Memorial Institute) medium (Lonza, USA). All tested-cell lines were imaged and observed utilizing phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus, Germany).

Preparation of aquatic leaf extract

To remove dust particles, we first cleaned the examined leaves (20 g) with tap water, dried them, and washed them with dis H2O and ethanol. We cut the leaves into small fragments and crushed them into a paste using a mortar and pestle. Next, we mixed the paste with 250 mL of dis H2O to prepare an aqueous extract. We filtered the extract twice with a nylon cloth to remove solid plant residues, followed by three filtrations using Whatman filter paper to eliminate any retaining residues. Each filtrate was then used individually for the plant-mediated bio-synthesis of ZnO-CuO NCPs.

Screening for qualitative phytochemicals

We employed standard methods to screen for bioactive chemical constituents in the medicinal plant extracts [8, 23–25]. Each test indicated the presence or absence of primary phytoconstituents in the extracts. Initially, we mixed 2 mL of each extract with 1.5 mL of 1% HCl, warmed the mixture, and added a few drops of Wagner’s reagent. The production of an orange precipitate exhibited the existence of alkaloids. Adding a 5% FeCl3·6H2O solution to 2 mL of each diluted extract produced a bright green color, suggesting flavonoids, while a deep blue-black color and a dark green or brownish-green solution indicated polyphenols and tannins, respectively. For steroids, we mixed 2 mL of each extract with 2 mL of acetic acid and 5 mL of chloroform, followed by a few drops of concentrated H2SO4; the reddish-brown interface confirmed their existence. To test for terpenoids, we mixed 1 mL of each extract with 2 mL of chloroform and gently added 3 mL of concentrated H2SO4. The reddish-brown color at the interface indicated terpenoids. Finally, for saponins, we shook the extracts for an hour, and the formation of foam confirmed their presence.

Plant-mediated of ZnO-CuO NCPs

We used Mentha arvensis, Mentha longifolia, and Ocimum basilicum as reducing and capping agents for the phytosynthesis of ZnO-CuO NCPss. We examined several metal salts as precursors, including Cu(NO3)2, Zn(NO3)2, Cu(CH3COO)2·H2O, Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O, CuSO4·5H2O, and ZnSO4·H2O. We prepared 25 mL of each plant extract aqueous solution and adjusted its pH to 7 by adding 2 M NaOH. Separately, we prepared a 0.5 M precursor solution for each metal acetate and added it dropwise to the plant extract solution while stirring continuously at 50 rpm and 60 °C for one hour. We observed the color change from green to pale white-brownish precipitate, indicating the formation of ZnO-CuO NCPs. We repeated the process using different precursors (metal nitrate and metal sulfate). Afterward, we collected the ZnO-CuO NCPs precipitate by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 5 min and rinsing it with sterile tap water. We calcined each ZnO-CuO NCPs powder at 400 °C for four hours in a muffle furnace and weighed the final yield. We characterized the synthesized NCPs using UV-visible spectroscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX). We investigated the optical properties of the plant extract solutions and the resulting ZnO-CuO NCPs using a Shimadzu UV probe 1800 UV-visible spectrophotometer, scanning the samples in the 200–650 nm range. We analyzed the elemental composition and mapping of the synthesized ZnO-CuO NCPs using an EDX spectrum (INCAx-act Oxford Instrument, High Wycombe, UK). We tested the dried ZnO-CuO NCPs for antimicrobial sensitivity against Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Escherichia coli (ATCC 10536), and Candida albicans (ATCC 10231). All these pathogens were cultivated for 18 h at 37 °C and 150 rpm in sterile Muller-Hinton broth. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of each culture solution was swabbed onto Muller-Hinton agar plates. We then poured 50 µL of each ZnO-CuO NCPs (100 µg/mL) into 6 mm diameter wells drilled into the plates. All plates were incubated at 4 °C for 5 h for diffusion, followed by 48 h at 37 °C. We measured the diameter of inhibitory zones using a millimeter-scale ruler [17].

Statistical experimental design for maximizing the yield of plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPss

To optimize the synthesis process, we applied the Plackett-Burman statistical design [26]. We examined variables including metal salt concentrations (precursors), Zn/Cu precursor ratio, concentration of plant extract, reaction temperature and pH, and agitation conditions. Using Minitab18 software, we built a model with seven independent factors, each tested at high (+1) and low (−1) levels, as shown in Table 1.

In our experiments, the mean of triplicates was utilized to assess the maximum dry weight of ZnO-CuO NCPs (mg/mL). Applicability was confirmed through a comparison test between the baseline rates and the Plackett-Burman optimal model.

Characterization of the optimal ZnO-CuO NCPs produced by phyto-synthesis

We validated the biogenic fabrication process by characterizing the optimized ZnO-CuO NCPs yield with multiple analytical techniques to detect ZnO and CuO signals. We used FTIR and XRD to investigate the structure of the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs. We performed FTIR analysis approach of the plant extract and the phyto-synthesized ZnO-CuO NCPs over a wave-number range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 through using a Perkin Elmer spectrophotometer [20]. This analysis identified functional groups responsible for stabilizing, reducing, and capping NPs, focusing on the groups coating the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs. We assessed the purity and phase of the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs using XRD (PANalytical, X’Pert PRO, Netherlands) with a scan rate of 2° min−1, 40 kV, and a monochromatic filter in a 2θ range of 0–100° [27]. We examined the surface morphology of the ZnO-CuO NCPs using SEM (LEO device) and TEM (JEOL, JEM 2100 h with EELS) at 200 kV [28]. We evaluated the stability of the ZnO-CuO NCPs using zeta potential capacities with a DLS device (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). We also performed thermal stability studies using TGA, DTA, and DSC over a temperature range of 0–500 °C with a DSC-TGA device (SDTQ 600, USA).

Evaluating antimicrobial efficiency

We tested the antimicrobial potential of the ZnO-CuO NCPs towards multidrug-resistant human pathogens using agar-well diffusion and biofilm inhibition assays [17]. Initially, we employed the agar-well diffusion assay to measure inhibitory zones. For each examined pathogen, 100 µL of actively growing cultures (1.5 × 107 CFU/mL) were evenly spread over the Mueller-Hinton agar plates using cotton swabs. In order to absorb any surface moisture, the infected plates were left at room temperature for 15 min before the ZnO-CuO NCPs samples were applied. A sterile 6-mm cork-borer was used to create wells, and 150 µg/mL of the ZnO-CuO NCPs was aseptically filled to each well. Furthermore, the same method was used to load the plant extract (the control). Following a 24-h incubation time at 37 °C, the inhibitory zones were determined using a millimeter ruler. A biofilm development experiment was used to test the antimicrobial property of various ZnO-CuO NCPs dilutions in agreement with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute’s guidelines. The ZnO-CuO NCPs were serially diluted at doses of 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 µg/mL. Every dilution of ZnO-CuO NCPs was added to the actively growing cultures (2 × 10⁵ CFU/mL) of every pathogen. The pathogens’ maximum growth rate was ascertained by employing the positive control, which comprised microbial cultures devoid of ZnO-CuO NCPs. After treatment, these cultures were incubated at 37 °C and 200 rpm. Readings of optical density (OD) at wavelength of 600 nm were used to monitor each microbe’s growth during the period of incubation. To establish the percentage decrease in biofilm formation and achieve suitable amounts of ZnO-CuO NCPs for further investigation, the resultant turbidity was measured [29–31]. Additionally, its exact minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined by testing four different dilutions of ZnO-CuO NCPs (160, 170, 180, and 190 µg/mL) against the tested human pathogens using a broth microdilution approach. Growth was visually measured by analyzing the culture turbidity, which shows the presence of both live and dead microbial cells. The culture was used to calculate the bacteriostatic effect, or the lowest antimicrobial concentration at which turbidity is not detected. To verify that only live microbial cells kept proliferating as colony-forming units (CFUs), 100 µl of each tested culture was placed on an LB agar plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. According to this approach, the MIC was determined using both live and non-viable microbial cells. In this study, the MIC of ZnO-CuO NCPs that prevented at least 95% of microbial growth was determined. In the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) experiments, the grown human pathogens (2 × 105 CFU/mL) were treated with concentrated dilutions of ZnO-CuO NCPs (350, 400, 450, and 500 µg/mL) using broth microdilution approach. The ZnO-CuO NCPs-treated tubes that showed no signs of turbidity were chosen. One hundred µl of these cultures were swapped onto nutrient agar and cultivated overnight at 37 °C. The development of microbial colonies was then investigated. The concentration of ZnO-CuO NCPs that does not exhibit the formation of microbial colonies is recognized. The lowest ZnO-CuO NCPs concentration that eradicated microbial cells (99.99%) was subsequently determined to be MBC. For the time-kill kinetics assay, we inoculated microbial cultures (5 × 108 CFU/mL) into fresh nutrient broth and treated them with ZnO-CuO NCPs at the average MIC value. For the control group, we prepared untreated cultures without the nanocomposite. We incubated all cultures at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 200 rpm for 48 h. We collected samples aseptically at various intervals and plated them on Mueller-Hinton agar to count colony-forming units (CFU/mL). We calculated biofilm reduction percentages at each time point using logarithmic transformations (Log10 CFU/mL).

Cell viability and cytotoxicity

To assess the cytotoxic properties of ZnO-CuO NCPs on both healthy and malignant cell lines, the MTT assay was utilized. The study included Wi-38 cells (normal cell line) alongside three cancerous cell lines: A549, MDA, and HepG-2. Cells were seeded in sterile 96-well plates at a concentration of 1 × 104 cells per well and maintained in either RPMI-1640 or DMEM medium, both enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), for 24 h in a CO2 incubator. Once the cells adhered, ZnO-CuO NCPs were introduced at varying concentrations: 3.125–400 μg/mL for normal cells and 3.125–100 μg/mL for cancer cells. The plates were then incubated for 48 h in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After incubation, debris and non-adherent cells were eliminated by washing the cells three times with fresh RPMI-1640 medium. Next, 200 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Then, MTT solution was subsequently substituted with 200 μL of DMSO, and OD of viable cells was quantified at 570 nm and 600 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. The IC50 values, representing the concentration of ZnO-CuO NCPs required to inhibit 50% of cell viability, were derived using GraphPad Prism software 8.0. The selectivity index (SI) was computed as the ratio of CC50 values for normal Wi-38 cells to IC50 values for tumor cells. Moreover, morphological alterations in tumor cells were observed using inverted phase-contrast microscopy. The experiments were accompanied in triplicate, with results compared to healthy untreated (control) cells [32, 33].

Analysis of nuclear staining

The induced apoptotic impact of the prepared ZnO-CuO NCPs on the MDA cell line was evaluated using dual and single fluorescent nuclear staining techniques. Ethidium bromide/acridine orange (EB/AO) and Propidium iodide (PI) dyes were employed for this purpose. MDA cells were treated with the prepared ZnO-CuO NCPs at different concentrations (3.12–25 μg/mL) for 48 h. Fluorescence imaging was carried out using a ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager (Bio-Rad) to capture the results [34].

Virus stock solution preparation

Stock solutions were prepared for two cytopathic viruses: Adenovirus type 7 (Adeno-7) and Human herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). These stocks were used to determine the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) and stored at −80 °C until required for further experiments [34].

In vitro antiviral activity

The antiviral potential of ZnO-CuO NCPs was investigated by examining their anti-replicative (treatment), virucidal (neutralization), and viral entry-blocking effects. For the treatment method, Vero cells were cultivated for 24 h and then infected with 100 TCID50/100 μL of each virus for 2 h. Post-infection, the infected cells were washed three times with fresh RPMI media and exposed to ZnO-CuO NCPs at doses ranging from 1 to 100 μg/mL in triplicate. After a 72-h incubation period, the MTT method was performed as previously described to determine the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each composite. IC50 values were calculated utilizing GraphPad Prism software 8. In the neutralization mechanism, various concentrations of ZnO-CuO NCPs were mixed with 100 TCID50/100 μL of each virus and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. This mixture was then added to a pre-cultured monolayer of Vero cells and incubated for a further 2 h. Following this, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 72 h. The MTT assay was subsequently performed as outlined in the cytotoxicity protocol. To assess the blocking mechanism, pre-cultured Vero cells were exposed to different concentrations of ZnO-CuO NCPs. Following washing 3 times, the cells were infected with 100 TCID50/100 μL of each virus and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Post-infection, the infected cells were washed with fresh RPMI media and incubated for 3 days. The MTT assay was then conducted following the cytotoxicity procedure.

In silico computational analysis

The molecular docking procedure was validated using structures gained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). ZnO-CuO NCPs were modeled using VESTA (Visualization for Electronic Structural Analysis) software, accessible at https://jp-minerals.org/vesta/en/download.html. The composite model comprised 35 atoms, 86 bonds, and 22 polyhedra, with a computational processing time of 30 milliseconds. The unit cell volume was defined as 28.934441 Å3, and the resulting model was saved in CIF format. This file was subsequently converted to PDB format using the Open Babel graphical user interface (GUI) to ensure compatibility with docking software. For the docking studies, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and the pro-apoptotic protein BAX were chosen as receptor targets. Their three-dimensional structures were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), with VEGF identified by PDB ID: 1VPF and BAX by PDB ID: 1F16. The receptor proteins were prepared by eliminating any bound ligands and water molecules, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms using PyMOL software [35]. Molecular docking simulations were carried out to investigate the binding interactions between ZnO-CuO NCPs and the selected receptor proteins. The docking studies were executed using PyRx software [36]. The resulting ligand-receptor interactions were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL, with further validation performed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer software, and the interactions between ligands and receptors were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL, with additional confirmation through BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer software [37].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted via one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s post hoc test applied to assess significance at a threshold of p < 0.05. Minitab 19 software (version 19.1) was utilized for comparative analyses. Data visualization and additional analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA).

Results and Discussion

In the present study, we explored the use of various medicinal plants (Mentha arvensis, Mentha longifolia, and Ocimum basilicum) as capping and reducing agents, along with different metal salts (metal nitrate, metal acetate, and metal sulfate) as precursors, to synthesize plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs with strong antimicrobial properties. The synthesized ZnO-CuO NCPs were then characterized and tested for their in vitro anticancer and antiviral activities. Finally, the anticancer findings were validated through molecular docking with specific anticancer targets.

Screening experiments for the crude plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs

The first steps in the optimization process are often identifying the key factors influencing the plant-mediated of the ZnO-CuO NCPs and determining the best possible conditions. The One-Variables-At-a-Time (OVAT) technique was used to optimize a green nanomaterial production method, but it neglected the accuracy of the interactions between the variables under investigation [38]. Thus, more advantageous and cost-effective techniques for investigating the interaction between factors are provided by statistically based strategies [26]. However, this OVAT strategy is particularly useful when there are several essential factors that need to be improved before utilizing any statistical experimental designs. So, our investigation examined a range of medicinal plants as reducing and capping candidates, as well as different metal salts as precursors, in order to generate the high dry weight of plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs that have a powerful antimicrobial property. The herbs used were Mentha arvensis, Mentha longifolia, and Ocimum basilicum. Precursors such as metal nitrate, metal acetate, and metal sulfate were all tested. In order to yield the maximum dry weight of ZnO-CuO NCPs as a powerful antimicrobial agent, the most effective plant extract with a suitable precursor was selected (Fig. 1). The greatest dry weight of ZnO-CuO NCPs was produced by Mentha longifolia using metal acetates as precursors (Fig. 1A). To identify the most effective precursors (metal salts) and reducing agent (plant extract) for synthesizing the maximum dry weight ZnO-CuO NCPs, the statistical analysis of the average values of the calculated maximum yields was calculated using the ANOVA and Tukey Post Hoc Test. The well-controlled confidence intervals more than 95% scale are created using the Tukey simultaneous tests. The mean values for metal acetates have been considered to be the greatest computed dry weight ZnO-CuO NCPs yields. The most efficient plant extract with an appropriate precursor was chosen by measuring the maximum inhibition zones generated by each tested ZnO-CuO NCPs. The chosen ZnO-CuO NCPs, which has the highest antimicrobial effectiveness, was generated by Mentha longifolia in combination with the metal acetates Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O and Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O, as illustrated in Fig. 1B. Compared to all evaluated human pathogens, this plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs provided the biggest inhibitory zones against gram-positive bacteria.

In our work, the solutions of the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs were visible to the naked eye as a pale, white-brownish precipitate using Mentha arvensis (Fig. 2A), Mentha longifolia (Fig. 2B), and Ocimum basilicum extracts (Fig. 2C) with Cu(CH3COO)2.H2O and Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O as precursors. It was previously believed that certain bioactive ingredients, such as flavonoids, phenols, amino acids, and carbohydrates that were extracted from plant extract would act as capping and reducing candidates throughout the biogenic-fabricating procedure [28, 39, 40]. Therefore, while generating nanomaterials through the process of biosynthesis, the color change in the reacting solution signifies the bio-reduction of the precursor salt solution. Moreover, Fig. 2 displays the UV-vis spectra of ZnO-CuO NCPs that have been produced using each extract. It is known that the characteristics of surface plasmon resonance are related to the transition of electrons found on the generated nanomaterials’ surfaces [41, 42]. In our work, when compared to the extract’s spectrum, the UV-vis absorption peaks of every produced ZnO-CuO NCPs fell between 280 and 380 nm. When ZnO-CuO NCPs and Mentha arvensis extract UV-vis spectra are compared (Fig. 2A), the significant absorption peak at 380 nm indicates that ZnO-CuO NCPs has formed successfully. The main absorption peak of ZnO-CuO NCPs was observed at 320–340 nm when Ocimum basilicum extract was used in the phyto-synthesis process (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, utilizing Mentha longifolia extract for biogenic-fabricating ZnO–CuO NCP revealed two notable absorption peaks (280–300 and 360–380 nm), as illustrated in Fig. 2B. The results presented here are consistent with findings from previous studies [28, 37]. The shift in the UV-vis absorption peaks is attributed to the generation of smaller-sized nanocomposites [22]. In the nanocomposite’s spectra, it is anticipated that the strength of the peak will also become sharper as the weight of the generated nanomaterials increases [22, 43]. The surfaces of the biogenic-fabricated nanocomposites, synthesized using extracts of Mentha arvensis, Mentha longifolia, and Ocimum basilicum, exhibit highly homogeneous signals, as confirmed by EDX mapping (Fig. 2D–F). With the exception of O, Cu, and Zn, which have varying percentage values, all published EDX maps for biogenic-fabricated nanocomposites are identical. The resulting compositional atomic ratio in EDX analysis is dependent upon the reducing agent and precursor equilibrium ratios used to generate the green synthesized nanostructures. This may be influenced by the metal atoms’ distinctive crystallographic orientation or by differences in the nanostructures’ surface energy [44]. Due to the identical valence of Zn and Cu in the salts utilized in our work, the ZnO and CuO nanoparticles were reduced to the nanoscale. Additionally, the parallel generation of these ZnO and CuO nanoparticles and their subsequent clustering enabled the development of the ZnO-CuO NCPs. One possible explanation for this interaction is an intra-oxygen-metal-oxygen bond substitution, like in –O-Zn-O-Cu-O- [15, 22, 45]. Plackett-Burman design for optimizing the manufacturing process of plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs

Green synthesis approach utilizes naturally occurring bioactive metabolites to generate eco-friendly nanostructures. This is known as nano-biotechnology, a novel branch of current nanotechnology. In the process of green synthesis, these organic metabolites have the dual function of capping and reducing. These green synthetic nanostructures generated efficiently, rapidly, and non-toxically, with a precise form and size that are suitable for applications in medicine [4, 46]. Recently, plant-based green nanocomposites have become more and more popular because of their simplicity of employment, inexpensiveness, and ecologically friendly nature [28, 47–50]. However, there are several difficulties standing in the way of green fabrication’s successful industrial implementation in the market, including a desire to manufacture strong, stable, and efficient nanostructures [51]. The final nanocomposites formed throughout the biogenic manufacturing process could be unique in form, size, yield, and stability depending on a number of critical fabrication factors. Hence, a number of factors need to be taken into consideration, such as the dose of metal salts (precursors), the ratio of precursors, the concentration of reducing and capping agents (plant extract), the pH and temperature of the reaction, and the agitation setup. Prior studies have indicated that improving the yield, stability, and smaller, more evenly shaped particles of nanocomposites can be achieved through optimizing their production process as compared to non-optimized nanocomposites [16–18, 26]. In the current case, using one of the numerous statistical experimental designs might be a more effective way to handle this difficulty than identifying each variable independently to find the important components involved in the fabrication of nanocomposites. Statistical experimental designs represent a practical and efficient method for optimizing the industrial green manufacturing process [16–18, 26]. One of the fractional factorial designs that can monitor a lot of parameters with few experiments is the Plackett–Burman design (PBD). So, PBD is a screening tool that is part of the design of the experiment procedure; in particular, it is an inexpensive strategy that makes it possible to screen a great quantity of variables in order to identify the main influence on a specific response (s) [18, 26]. Therefore, PBD was employed in this work to optimize the fabrication parameters for the biogenic formation of ZnO-CuO NCPs with the necessary features and functionality. To appropriately generate biogenic ZnO-CuO NCPs, the bioactive metabolites that function as capping and reducing agents must first be extracted. Because some of the components that have been extracted (reducing agents) were the cause of the initial reduction of metal salts to nanostructures. More extracted bioactive metabolites (the capping agent) had to be added in order to decrease the amount of hydrated ZnO-CuO NCPs that precipitation caused to agglomerate uncontrollably. Therefore, we included the capping agent concentration in the matrix of the ZnO-CuO NCPs biogenic process for the first time that we know of in the statistical experimental design. The PBD, an efficient technique for picking important elements from a large number of variables that could have an impact on the process, was used to screen the process parameters. We have therefore tabulated several significant parameters along with their respective ranges that could influence the ZnO-CuO NCPs biogenic process in our work (Table 1). Each adjustable variable was inspected at two different levels: the low level (−1) and the high level (+1), based on preliminary results. As indicated in Table 2, twelve runs were used for analyzing the tested seven variables. Then the yield of ZnO-CuO NCPs was weighted (mg/ml). In order to determine the ZnO-CuO NCPs’s maximum yield, Minitab 19 software (MINITAB version 19.1) was used to examine the PBD’s matrix, including the recorded yields of ZnO-CuO NCPs. As indicated in the PBD results, the yield of ZnO-CuO NCPs varied greatly, with weight ranges from 2.72 to 19.59 mg/ml (Table 2). The biogenic build of ZnO-CuO NCPs is influenced by a multitude of variables, each of which has an individual and combined effect. Each trial set produced variations in the ZnO-CuO NCPs’s nucleation and growth processes, which in turn resulted variations in their size, shape, and dispersion. This green approach produces a more economical and ecologically friendly substitute for physical and chemical processes. Additionally, it will enable controlled and repeatable synthesis to rise production [49]. The usual method for identifying functional groups that impacted the ecologically sustainable production of any nanomaterials was Fourier transform infrared analysis (FTIR) [52]. In the example of active phytoconstituents, mainly O–H, C=O, and C–N residues of alkaloids and phenolic derivatives, were coated onto the green-generated trimetallic CuO/Ag/ZnO nanocomposite using Ziziphus spina-christi extract. Studies recently published indicate that plant-derived substances play a significant role in reducing and capping nanometal ions [53]. Herbal medicine and a combination of nanomaterials have already produced novel, more therapeutically effective nanocomposites. Recently, research on the fabrication of ZnO/CuO/Ag nanocomposite that is mediated by Mentha longifolia extract based on gauze linen fiber was published. This nano-formulation may stimulate various stages of wound healing by regulating cytokines and growth factors during the healing process [20]. Additionally, the plant-mediated ZnO/Ag/propolis extract materials dressing, in comparison to other nanoparticles, has a considerable impact on wound care performance [54]. Additionally, ZnO and ZnO/CuO nanocomposites were previously generated from Mentha longifolia leaf extract, a natural and efficient stabilizer. Moreover, this plant-mediated ZnO/CuO nanocomposite (biomolecules) demonstrated effective antibacterial activity [42]. Also, in this work, the functional groups in the biomolecules that are in charge of the precursors salt bio-reduction as well as protecting and stabilizing the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs were identified by FTIR spectrum recordings. The presence of the different peaks identifies potential bioactive substances that have the ability to stabilize the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs and reduce Zn+ and Cu+ ions. Figure 4A, B display the FTIR spectra of the extract of Mentha longifolia and the ZnO-CuO NCPs specimens, respectively. Inter-hydrogen bonding, which is evident in both the plant extract and nanocomposite spectra and represents –OH groups and water molecules, is linked to the signals at v 3350.20–3444.02 cm−1 (Fig. 4A). The O-H functional group, which is connected to alcohols and phenols, is located in the spectral band of v 3444.02 cm−1. The large peaks at ν 3444 and 1638 cm−1 are caused by the stretching frequency of the extract’s phenolic groups, which acts as a capping and reducing ligand (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the C-H stretching of the vinyl and aliphatic groups is linked to a spectral band of ν 3006.4 to 2923.31 cm−1 (Fig. 4A). The presence of organic residues may be indicated by the additional distinct peak, which is particularly noticeable at ν 2059.08 and 1153.47 cm−1, which are the vibrational frequencies of a C=O bond (Fig. 4B). Additionally, there is a peak at v 1401 cm−1, which may be connected to carboxylic acid bending and O–H bonding in polyphenols. The significant peaks at 1500 and ν 1153 cm−1 were caused by aromatic N–H and C–O stretching vibrations from phenolic groups, respectively (Fig. 4B). The peak found at ν 1102 cm−1 is linked to the stretching of C–O bonds in primary alcohols. The ZnO-CuO NCPs spectrum shows peaks in the range of 900–400 cm−1, which differ from the spectral features of the Mentha longifolia extract (Fig. 4B). The nanocomposite containing two metal oxides, such as ZnO or CuO, is the result of interactions between the extract of Mentha longifolia and several precursors. The stretching vibrations of Cu-O and Zn–O are linked to the unique peaks at v 725–940 cm−1 and roughly v 447–619 cm−1, respectively, in the green synthesized spectrum of ZnO-CuO NCPs in our study (Fig. 4B). The ZnO-CuO NCPs’s FTIR spectrum shows that it is covered with active phytoconstituents, primarily the O–H, C=O, and C–N residues of phenolic derivatives and alkaloids. The O-H, C=O, and C-N residues may stabilize the resultant ZnO-CuO NCPs by coating the surfaces of metals and reducing aggregation. Several investigations have shown that the green-generated nanocomposite is stabilized and reduced by a range of biomolecules included in the extract of Mentha longifolia [12, 20, 55]. Additionally, the XRD analysis was utilized to evaluate the crystalline state of nanomaterials that were produced using green procedures [56]. The XRD patterns in Fig. 4C, shows the crystalline structures of pure ZnO-CuO NCPs that generated using extract of Mentha longifolia. Identifiable diffraction peaks were found for Zn-O at 2θ values of 32°, 34.7°, 36.5°, 47.9°, 57.1°, 63.1°, 66.7°, and 68.3°. According to their respective crystallographic planes, these peaks can be attributed to (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (31-1), and (113). According to the COD Reference Pattern: 96-101-1259, these reflections match the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO [57]. For the Cu-O, the corresponding crystallographic planes were seen at 2θ values of 32.8°, 35.8°, 38.9°, 49°, and 53.7°. The indexing given by the COD Reference Pattern: 96-101-1149 indicates that these reflections represent the monoclinic phase of Cu-O [25]. The formation of clear, narrow observed peaks clearly confirms the crystalline character of our nanocomponent and confirms the production of ZnO-CuO NCPs. All of the ZnO-CuO NCPs peaks that were seen at 2θ values of 24°, 39.7°, 30.5°, and 34.9° can be attributed to the crystallographic planes (100), (111), (20-2), and (020). This result emphasizes the homogeneous nature of the final nanocomponent of ZnO-CuO [28, 45].

The efficacy of the plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs against multidrug-resistant human pathogens

The impact of antimicrobial nanocomposites on pathogens is still being researched in depth. There are numerous published ideas that can be used to assess, explain, and predict the antimicrobial effects of nanomaterials. The membrane and walls of a microbial cell play a crucial role in shielding microbes from medicinal products. Published studies indicate that the main factor contributing to the heightened antimicrobial efficacy of nanocomposites is the synergistic interaction among nanomaterials and the organic molecules in cell walls [61]. Nanocomposites certainly attach to the microbe’s cell wall and then break it, causing damage to its permeability because of their incredibly significant surface area. These NPs may chelate to the sulfur and phosphorus in DNA, reducing its functioning [18]. Furthermore, several essential respiratory enzymes include thiol groups that the released ions attach to, rendering them inactive and eliminating pathogenic microorganisms. These ions accumulate inside the microbial cells, wherever they might produce oxidative stress and free radicals, which could have a more lethal effect [62]. Additionally, the cell division and respiratory chain are the targets of any nanocomposite’s antimicrobial activity, which ultimately mains to cell death and amplifies its antimicrobial effects [63]. Furthermore, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) has been found to increase with the addition of nanomaterial suspensions. This generation of ROS is the main mechanism behind nanomaterials’ antimicrobial abilities [64]. The antimicrobial potential of the improved plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs was evaluated in these investigations utilizing multiple techniques. During a well-agar diffusion experiment, inhibitory halo zones were primarily detected to examine the antimicrobial effects of the tested ZnO-CuO NCPs in comparison to Mentha longifolia extract (control). As shown by the antimicrobial photos, the improved plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs exhibited powerful antimicrobial properties towards all of the pathogens analyzed when associated to the control (Fig. 6A, B). Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus (43.96 mm), had the strongest antimicrobial capability, followed by gram-negative bacteria, such as Escherichia coli (29.21 mm), and a yeast strain called Candida albicans (18.95 mm). The findings exhibited in Fig. 6C establish a significant decrease in cell viability for tested microorganisms following treatment with NCPs at 180.47 µg/mL, compared to untreated cultures. The untreated groups (cultures devoid of nanocomposite as a control), maintained consistently high colony-forming unit (CFU) counts throughout the incubation periods (0–48 h), indicating sustained microbial growth. In contrast, the treated groups exhibited a notable decline in viability, with the most significant reduction observed within the first 6 to 18 h, suggesting a rapid antimicrobial effect.

Anticancer evaluation assays

In this investigation, we evaluated the cytotoxic properties of the ZnO-CuO NCPs on the Wi-38 normal cell line and various human tumor cell lines, including A549, MDA, and HepG2, using the MTT assay. The CC50 value, which represents the concentration at which 50% cell viability is observed, was calculated to be 586.0 µg/mL for the normal Wi-38 cells, which is significantly higher than that observed in the cancer cell lines. This indicates a selective cytotoxic effect of the nanocomposite towards cancer cells. Specifically, the IC50 values were found to be 120.0 µg/mL for A549, 12.65 µg/mL for MDA, and 23.26 µg/mL for HepG2 cells (Fig. 7A). The selectivity index (SI) was also calculated to compare the nanocomposite’s effects on cancerous versus normal cells. As shown in Fig. 7B, the MDA cell line exhibited the highest SI value of 46.32, suggesting that the nanocomposite is most selective towards this breast cancer cell line, while the HepG2 cell line had an SI value of 25.19. In contrast, the A549 lung cancer cell line demonstrated a lower SI of 4.88. Furthermore, morphological analysis of the treated cells (Fig. 7C) revealed significant changes, including cell shrinkage and nuclear condensation, in the tumor cells as the concentration of the nanocomposite increased. We acknowledge that an ideal experimental design would involve comparing each cancer cell line to its non-cancerous counterpart from the same tissue of origin. In this study, the use of the Wi-38 normal lung fibroblast line provides a direct, tissue-matched control for assessing selectivity against the A549 lung carcinoma cells. However, for the breast (MDA) and liver (HepG2) cancer lines, this cross-lineage comparison means we must interpret the observed selectivity with caution, as tissue-specific factors may influence cellular susceptibility.

The AO stain marked viable cell nuclei with green fluorescence, while apoptotic cells, having compromised membranes, absorbed EB and exhibited bright greenish-yellow to red fluorescence, depending on the stage of apoptosis. Additionally, PI further confirming rise in late apoptotic and necrotic cells with higher concentrations of the ZnO-CuO NCPs. The intensity of red fluorescence increases significantly at higher concentrations. The arrows in Fig. 8 presenting a closer look at the cellular morphology under higher magnification, highlighting key features of apoptosis such as membrane blebbing, and the distinction between early and late apoptotic cells. The increase in early and late apoptotic cells further supports the nanocomposite’s potent anticancer activity, likely mediated through apoptosis, across different cancer cell types. The results of this investigation support with previous reports on the anticancer activity of ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) [66]. Researchers noted a considerable decline in the morphology and adhesion abilities of tumor cells that were treated with higher doses of ZnO NPs when compared to the untreated control groups. Inhibition of cancer cell progress was detected in breast, liver, and lung tumor cell lines at doses of 50 and 100 μg/mL of green biosynthesized ZnO NPs. The reductions in cancer cell viability were mainly due to early and late apoptosis. Specifically, at a concentration of 100 μg/mL, MCF-7, HepG2, and A549 cells showed about a 50% reduction in viability. These findings highlight the significance of biosynthesized ZnO NPs as a therapeutic strategy for various cancer cell lines with minimal side effects [67–69].

A higher IC50 value than that observed in our study was reported for a (CuO-Cu2O)Cu/ZnO nanocomposite in cervical HeLa cell lines, at 273.0 μg/mL, indicating reduced cell proliferation and the induction of apoptosis [70]. Another study reported that at 20 μg/mL of a green-synthesized ZnO–CuO NCPs, cell viability decreased to 56.3% [71]. Similarly, Rehana et al. found that CuO NPs synthesized using plant extracts exhibited greater cytotoxic effects on cancer (MCF-7, HeLa, Hep-2, and A549) cells compared to chemically synthesized CuO NPs [72]. During cancer cell proliferation, radicals or electrons are continuously transferred through a chain-growth mechanism. Nanoparticles generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which disrupt electron transport pathways, ultimately inducing apoptosis and resulting in cell death [73]. Given the individual effectiveness of both ZnO and CuO NPs in cancer therapy, their combination in a nanocomposite is expected to exhibit significantly enhanced anticancer properties.

Antiviral evaluation assay

In this investigation, the antiviral properties of the ZnO-CuO NCPs were thoroughly assessed towards HSV-1 and adenovirus infections in Vero cells using an in vitro method. The compound was found to be safe, with a CC50 of 586.0 µg/mL, indicating minimal cytotoxicity at effective antiviral doses. Three different methods were tested to elucidate the compound’s antiviral activity: treatment, blocking, and neutralization. Among these, the treatment method was the most effective against HSV-1, with a SI of 63.24, indicating a strong antiviral effect with minimal harm to normal cells. On the other hand, the blocking method had a much lower SI, suggesting that compound’s antiviral activity is likely not mediated by preventing viral entry through host receptor binding. Instead, the neutralization method showed moderate effectiveness, with an SI of 10.85 against HSV-1, indicate that the compound might exhibit its antiviral effects through direct binding to viral proteins, inhibition of viral replication, or enzyme activity rather than through receptor blocking. For adenovirus, the treatment method was also quite effective, with an SI of 22.66. However, the blocking and neutralization methods had lower SIs, further suggesting that the compound’s main way of antiviral activity might involve processes other than receptor interaction. The results depicted in Fig. 9A–C demonstrate dose-dependent effects on viral inhibition and cell viability, alongside the comparative selectivity indices for each virus evaluated. These findings align with the work of Zakharova et al. [74], which revealed that metal oxide coatings possess strain-specific antiviral properties. Their study reported complete suppression of HSV-2, underscoring the selective antiviral efficacy of such coatings. Additionally, zinc oxide nanoparticles, acyclovir, nano-chitosan, and clove oil have exhibited significant antiviral activity against HSV-1, Adenovirus, and CoxB2, with inhibitory percentage of 95.9%, 76.4%, and 86.9%, respectively [75]. Earlier research has also emphasized the antiviral potential of ZnO against HSV-1, though the precise mechanism of action remains unresolved [76]. Hypotheses suggest that ZnO may hinder viral replication by interacting with viral surface glycoproteins, thereby preventing viral attachment and entry into host cells, or by penetrating the cell membrane and disrupting cellular components critical for viral replication [75].

Docking and computational study

The computational study was conducted to evaluate the anticancer effect of the ZnO-CuO NCPs by analyzing its binding interactions with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), as shown in Fig. 10. The results demonstrated strong binding affinities of −10.9 kcal/mol with VEGF and −10 kcal/mol with Bax compared to a reference, Table 6. A key strategy in combating cancer involves leveraging anti-angiogenic and apoptotic mechanisms. Notably, advances in research have enabled the targeting of VEGF as a therapeutic approach against cancer, given its overexpression in cancer cells. VEGF showing an important role in tumor angiogenesis, promoting the formation of new blood vessels to supply nutrients to tumors [77]. Bax, on the other hand, is a vital regulator of pro-apoptotic pathways, and its activation induces cancer cell death through apoptosis [78]. Docking with a negative control, bovine serum albumin (BSA) (PDB ID: 4F5S), provided no stable binding conformation (data not shown), consistent with the ZnO-CuO/VEGF/BAX specificity.

Conclusion

This study successfully optimized the plant-mediated synthesis of ZnO-CuO NCPss using Mentha longifolia extract, demonstrating its role as both a reducing and capping agent. The Plackett-Burman statistical design facilitated the optimization process, yielding highly stable nanocomposites with a crystalline structure and an average particle size of 26.61 nm. The presence of bioactive phytoconstituents, including phenolic derivatives and alkaloids, contributed to the stability and functionality of the synthesized NCPs. Functionally, the ZnO-CuO NCPs exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, effectively inhibiting multidrug-resistant human pathogens with MIC of 180.47 µg/mL. Additionally, the nanocomposite demonstrated potent anticancer effects, particularly against MDA breast cancer cells, with findings supported by nuclear staining and computational docking studies targeting VEGFR and Bax proteins. While our computational analysis calculates selective interactions between ZnO-CuO NCPs and VEGF/BAX, further experimental validation (e.g., competitive binding assays) or biophysical methodologies (e.g., surface plasmon resonance) will be required to conclusively establish target specificity and optimize the binding energetics predicted in silico. Furthermore, the ZnO-CuO NCPs displayed significant antiviral properties against HSV-1, reinforcing its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent. These results highlight the promising biomedical applications of plant-mediated ZnO-CuO NCPs, opening new possibilities for future research into their clinical translation and commercial development as sustainable nano-bio-based therapeutics for antimicrobial, anticancer, and antiviral treatments.
