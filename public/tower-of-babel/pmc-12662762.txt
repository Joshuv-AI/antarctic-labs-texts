Cardiothoracic Surgery for a Giant Pericardial Cyst: When Nature's Precision Leads to Surgical Complexity
Authors: Romana Riyaz, Sajjad Ahmed Khan, Khadija Fatima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA giant pericardial cyst, though typically benign, can present with significant symptoms due to its compressive effects on cardiac structures. Accurate imaging and prompt surgical intervention are crucial for diagnosis and treatment, highlighting the importance of recognizing this rare condition in patients with unexplained cardiopulmonary symptoms.Keywords: cardiac compression, cardiopulmonary symptoms, imaging diagnosis, pericardial cyst, surgical intervention

Introduction

Pericardial cysts are rare, mostly benign mediastinal lesions or masses with an incidence of about 1 in every 100,000 persons [1]. The etiology can be either congenital or a consequence of inflammation, trauma or complication of surgery. Even though data analyzing the location of these cysts are limited, studies have shown that pericardial cysts are predominantly found in the right cardiophrenic angle (> 70%), less commonly in the left cardiophrenic angle (~22%), and scarcely ever in the posterior or anterior superior portion of the mediastinum (~8%) [2]. Incidence has been studied to be predominant in middle‐aged individuals, with no preponderance for either gender. The majority of pericardial cysts (about > 50%) are clinically dormant and thus patients remain asymptomatic [3], eventually leading to the unanticipated/incidental discovery of the cyst on routine chest X‐rays as an opaque and clearly delineated bulge of the corresponding cardiac border. However, symptomatic cysts can have varied presentations depending on their location, dimensions, extent of impedance, and disruption of normal structural anatomy and physiologic functions due to their bulk. Symptoms include dyspnea (as in this case), chest pain, or persistent cough, whereas palpitations, hemoptysis, pneumothorax, and fever are considered atypical presentations [3]. While the dimensions of pericardial cysts studied to date vary from to 28 cm [4] with most of them smaller than 3 cm in diameter, in this case, the diameter was measured to be 10 cm. Here, the middle mediastinal location has contributed to the diagnostic challenge in differentiating the cyst from other cardiac and mediastinal pathologies.

Diagnostic imaging is performed by trans‐thoracic echocardiography (TTEC), cardiac MRI, or contrast‐enhanced CT (CECT) with TTEC recommended for preliminary investigations and CECT being the preferred modality for follow‐up. The nature and gravity of symptoms determine management, observed growth of the cyst, and components of the cyst cavity, with cyst resection being the most favored approach (with thoracotomy, sternotomy, or video‐assisted thoracic surgery) [5] for low‐risk patients as opposed to aspiration and therapeutic sclerosing in high‐risk patients.

This case report aims to present an intriguing pericardial cystic pathology of huge dimensions, in a rare location, and its complicated surgical excision, all of which make it distinctive. We will discuss how adherence of the cyst to vital vascular structures is a major challenge for surgical resection and requires experienced surgical prowess.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 55‐year‐old male presented to the outpatient department with complaints of progressively worsening shortness of breath on exertion and frequent episodes of palpitations that had persisted for 1 week. He described the dyspnea as moderate in intensity, limiting his daily physical activity, and the palpitations as sudden onset and irregular in rhythm, occasionally associated with a sense of chest discomfort, but no chest pain. He denied orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, syncope, or recent infections. His medical history was significant for hypertension, well controlled with telmisartan 40 mg daily. He was compliant with medication and follow‐ups. There was no family history of cardiac conditions, sudden cardiac death, or similar complaints. The patient reported no history of congenital cardiac abnormalities, recent travel, trauma, or known exposure to tuberculosis or malignancy.

On physical examination, the patient appeared mildly pale, but was alert and hemodynamically stable. His blood pressure was within normal range, heart rate was slightly elevated, and oxygen saturation was normal on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed a regular rhythm with no murmurs, rubs, or gallops. There were no signs of fluid overload such as peripheral edema or elevated jugular venous pressure. Respiratory examination showed good air entry bilaterally without crepitations or wheezing. Abdominal and neurological examinations were unremarkable. Notably, no tenderness was elicited in the mediastinal or chest wall regions. Laboratory workup showed mild anemia and slightly elevated cholesterol levels, while other parameters including cardiac enzymes, thyroid function tests, and renal function were within normal limits.

Given the symptoms and subtle examination findings, a transthoracic echocardiogram was performed to evaluate for structural or functional cardiac abnormalities. The echocardiogram revealed normal biventricular function with a preserved ejection fraction and no evidence of valvular disease. However, an unexpected finding was noted—a well‐demarcated, anechoic, homogeneous, fluid‐filled structure adjacent to the heart, suggesting a pericardial origin. There was no pericardial effusion, tamponade physiology, or signs of infection. To further investigate the nature of this mass, a chest CT scan with contrast was ordered.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

The echocardiographic findings, coupled with the patient's symptoms of exertional dyspnea and palpitations, prompted a broad differential diagnosis that included pericardial cyst, pericardial effusion, cardiac tumor (such as atrial myxoma), and mediastinal masses including bronchogenic cysts, thymic cysts, or even a rare presentation of lymphoma or germ cell tumor. Each of these possibilities warranted exclusion due to potential cardiovascular compromise.

A contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax was performed to obtain a more precise anatomical assessment. The CT scan demonstrated a large, well‐defined cystic lesion measuring approximately 10 cm in diameter, located in the middle mediastinum (Figure 1). The lesion had irregular but smooth borders and appeared fluid‐filled, with no solid enhancing components. It exerted significant mass effect, compressing both the right and left ventricles (more prominently the right), and displaced the diaphragm inferiorly (Figure 2). There were no calcifications, septations, or signs of invasion into adjacent structures, but the size and location raised concern for hemodynamic compromise and an unusual cause of apparent cardiomegaly.

Computerized tomography scan sagittal slice showing a giant cystic mass, arising from the pericardium (arrow).

Given the symptomatic nature of the lesion and the compressive effects on cardiac chambers, surgical intervention was deemed necessary both for diagnostic confirmation and for definitive treatment. The patient was taken to the operating theater for excision of the lesion. The initial dissection was performed off‐pump under general anesthesia through a median sternotomy approach. Intraoperatively, the cyst was found to be densely adherent to critical cardiac structures including the right coronary artery (RCA), the right atrioventricular groove, and the coronary sinus (Figure 3). These attachments posed a high risk of injury during dissection, particularly with the use of electrocautery. Due to this complexity and the proximity of vital cardiac structures, the surgical team initiated total cardiopulmonary bypass with cardioplegic arrest to ensure a safe and controlled environment for complete excision. Under direct vision and careful dissection, the cyst was successfully excised in toto without rupture or injury to adjacent vessels (Figure 4).

Results (Outcomes and Follow‐Up)

The patient was transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit postoperatively and had an uneventful recovery. He was extubated within hours, remained hemodynamically stable, and showed no signs of postoperative complications such as arrhythmias, bleeding, or infection. Early postoperative echocardiography confirmed the absence of residual compressive masses and normal cardiac function. He was mobilized gradually and discharged home in stable condition after a few days of monitoring.

Histopathological analysis of the excised specimen confirmed the diagnosis of a benign pericardial cyst. Microscopic examination revealed that the cyst wall was lined with a single layer of flat mesothelial cells. The underlying stroma consisted of fibrous connective tissue interspersed with collagen and elastic fibers. No signs of malignancy, chronic inflammation, or infection were noted. These findings were consistent with a congenital pericardial cyst, a rare but typically benign entity.

The patient was followed up regularly in the outpatient setting. Over the next several months, he remained completely asymptomatic with no recurrence of palpitations or dyspnea. Repeat imaging at follow‐up showed no evidence of recurrence or residual cystic structures. The case underscores the importance of thorough investigation in patients with unexplained cardiopulmonary symptoms and highlights the critical role of multimodality imaging and careful surgical planning in the management of rare mediastinal pathologies. The early recognition and timely intervention contributed to an excellent outcome in this otherwise rare and potentially high‐risk presentation.

Discussion

This case presents a novel and rare instance of a cardiac anomaly. The diagnosis of a pericardial cyst, an abnormality adjacent to the cardiac border is extraordinarily rare and provides a unique opportunity to discuss the diagnostic challenges and complexities associated with such anomalies. The patient's presentation with dyspnea on exertion and severe palpitations initially pointed toward common cardiac disorders like congestive heart failure and arrhythmias. However, advanced imaging ultimately revealed the rare condition of a pericardial cyst underscoring the critical role of detailed imaging in diagnosing complex cardiac conditions.

The rarity of pericardial cysts makes this case particularly noteworthy. The majority of documented cases are discovered incidentally during imaging or surgical procedures performed for other conditions. For instance, Khanna and et al. reported a case of a 51‐year‐old male with an asymptomatic giant pericardial cyst discovered incidentally on the CT abdomen for workup of abdominal pain, but the imaging characteristics and detailed preoperative evaluation were not as comprehensive as those presented in this case [6]. Similarly, Raja et al. described a case series of three patients aged 51, 66, and 77 who were evaluated for pericardial cysts using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) with diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI). Each case demonstrated high apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values, indicating free diffusion of protons within the cysts, which is characteristic of simple cysts. These findings were consistent across all cases, suggesting that DWI can be a valuable tool in diagnosing pericardial cysts, especially when conventional imaging methods yield inconclusive results; however, their report lacked the detailed imaging sequence with surgical confirmation and differential diagnostic approach as demonstrated here [7].

The differential diagnosis of pericardial cysts includes prominent fat pad, left ventricular aneurysm, prominent left atrial appendage, aortic aneurysm, hydatid cysts, thrombus, myxoma, solid cardiac tumors, and rarely, intracardiac sarcomas [3].

In this case, the CECT scan provided critical information by revealing a thin‐walled cystic lesion adjacent to the heart. The CECT images were instrumental in differentiating this lesion. The detailed imaging allowed visualization of the cyst's communication and clarified the anatomical relationship between the heart and pericardial space, which was essential for accurate diagnosis and surgical planning.

The surgical findings corroborated the imaging results. The pericardial cyst was distended and filled with clear mucus, the identification of the right coronary artery running over the cyst and coronary sinus below the cyst provided further evidence of the complex nature of this condition.

The detailed imaging and surgical exploration in this case highlight the importance of high‐resolution imaging techniques in diagnosing rare cardiac anomalies. Accurate CECT scan and echocardiogram evaluations allowed for the differentiation of a pericardial cyst from other common cardiac diseases. This precise diagnostic approach is essential for planning effective surgical interventions and avoiding unnecessary procedures or misdiagnoses.

This case contributes valuable insights into the understanding and management of cardiac anomalies. It demonstrates how advanced imaging can improve diagnostic accuracy and guide effective treatment strategies in managing complex pericardial conditions. The integration of CECT and echocardiograph in diagnosing this rare anomaly underscores the necessity of detailed imaging in the assessment of cardiac disorders, enhancing our ability to manage and treat such uncommon conditions effectively.

Conclusion

This case of a pericardial cyst underscores the pivotal role of advanced imaging in the diagnosis and management of rare cardiac anomalies. Contrast‐enhanced CT (CECT) and echocardiography were crucial in precisely identifying the cyst and differentiating it from other cardiac conditions such as aneurysms. Accurate imaging enabled optimal surgical planning and informed clinical decision making. The case highlights that comprehensive and high‐resolution imaging is essential for distinguishing pericardial cysts from other mediastinal pathologies, thereby improving diagnostic confidence, guiding targeted treatment, and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in complex cardiac presentations.
