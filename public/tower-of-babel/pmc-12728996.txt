Integration of angiogenesis and programmed cell death mechanisms unveils potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets in spinal cord injury
Authors: Feng Lu, Zifeng Mai, Longfei Zhang, Junkai He, Lifeng Wang, Shihong Li, Maolin Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) causes permanent neurological deficits and is associated with complex pathological processes such as angiogenesis and programmed cell death (PCD). However, the crosstalk between angiogenesis and PCD in SCI remains unclear.MethodsWe integrated angiogenesis-related and PCD-related datasets to identify angiogenesis- and PCD-associated differentially expressed genes (ARPRDEGs). Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA), protein–protein interaction (PPI), and machine learning were used to screen hub genes, followed by time-course qRT-PCR and Western blot validation in a transection mouse model. We also performed immune infiltration analysis, drug–gene interaction prediction, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using GWAS and eQTL data from SCI patients, and single-cell transcriptomics with CellChat communication analysis.ResultsA total of 134 ARPRDEGs were identified and enriched in cell adhesion, leukocyte migration, and wound healing. Three hub genes (Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf) were identified and validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot. Time-course analysis revealed Cd44 was consistently upregulated, Icam1 showed early elevation followed by decline, while Tnf exhibited stage-dependent downregulation, highlighting the impact of injury stage. MR analysis provided genetic evidence supporting potential causal associations: genetically predicted Cd44 and Icam1 expression were suggested as risk factors, while Tnf showed a potential protective effect. A diagnostic model based on these hub genes demonstrated good predictive performance across species. Single-cell transcriptomics further revealed that VISFATIN signaling mediated endothelial–myeloid communication in the high-expression hub-gene group, reinforcing their biological plausibility. In addition, acetaminophen was predicted as a potential candidate through computational drug screening, with exploratory in vivo findings suggesting Cd44-related effects; its therapeutic role requires further pharmacological validation.ConclusionThis study systematically revealed the role of angiogenesis-PCD interplay in SCI and identified Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf as promising candidate diagnostic and therapeutic targets, whose clinical translation requires further validation. By integrating transcriptomic, protein-level, single-cell, clinical, and genetic evidence, our findings provide novel insights into stage-specific mechanisms and potential therapeutic directions, while highlighting the need for further pharmacological validation for SCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07405-2.

Introduction

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) is a severe damage to the central nervous system caused by trauma or disease, often resulting in irreversible loss of motor, sensory, and autonomic functions [1]. The pathological process of SCI can be divided into primary and secondary injuries. Primary injury is caused by direct mechanical damage, while secondary injury involves complex pathological processes, including inflammatory response, oxidative stress, vascular damage, and Programmed Cell Death (PCD) [2]. These secondary reactions significantly hinder the repair and functional regeneration of spinal cord tissue, making it crucial to investigate the key mechanisms involved in secondary injury for effective SCI treatment.

In the context of secondary injury in SCI, angiogenesis and PCD are two core pathological processes. Angiogenesis is critical for injury repair as it is directly related to the restoration of local blood supply and improvement of the tissue microenvironment. Following SCI, vascular damage leads to local ischemia and hypoxia, further exacerbating tissue injury. Promoting angiogenesis can provide the necessary oxygen and nutrients for nerve tissue regeneration [3]. Compared to other repair processes, angiogenesis not only enhances the local microenvironment but also provides crucial support for the growth of newly formed nerve fibers.

PCD represents another important mechanism in the pathological process of SCI, encompassing various forms such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis. Apoptosis is the earliest studied form of cell death in SCI, closely associated with the loss of neurons and glial cells [4]. Ferroptosis exacerbates oxidative stress through iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, thereby expanding the scope of damage [5]; pyroptosis, on the other hand, releases a large number of inflammatory factors that further aggravate the inflammatory response, amplifying the cascade effects of secondary injury [6]. These forms of PCD collectively contribute to the occurrence and development of secondary injuries in SCI, significantly impacting the final outcome of spinal cord repair. However, the dynamic interaction between angiogenesis and different forms of PCD in SCI remains insufficiently defined.

Previous studies have suggested that endogenous angiogenesis transiently increases vascular density within the first two weeks after SCI, but the newly formed vessels often show functional immaturity and blood-spinal cord barrier leakage [7]. Moreover, differences in experimental models (contusion vs. transection) and tissue sources (lesion vs. peripheral blood) significantly influence gene expression patterns, complicating translational interpretation [8].

To address these gaps, we integrated transcriptomic datasets with experimental validation to identify hub genes linking angiogenesis and PCD in SCI. We further evaluated their diagnostic and causal relevance across species using machine learning and Mendelian randomization(MR), mapped their cellular distribution with single-cell transcriptomics, and predicted potential therapeutic compounds via drug–gene interaction and molecular docking. This comprehensive approach aims to clarify the angiogenesis–PCD interplay in SCI and provide novel molecular targets for therapeutic development.

Geo Microarray Chip Information

GEO，Gene Expression Omnibus SCI，Spinal Cord Injury。

Angiogenesis-related genes were obtained from the GeneCards website [10] (http://www.genecards.org/). We used the keyword “Angiogenesis” for our search and retained only those genes classified as “Protein Coding” with a relevance score greater than 2, resulting in a total of 702 genes. Additionally, we collected 189 and 40 angiogenesis-related genes from the OMIM [11] (http://omim.org/) and CTD [11] (http://ctdbase.org/) databases, respectively. After merging and removing duplicates, we obtained a total of 785 unique genes. Subsequently, we converted these 785 genes to their mouse homologs using the R package homologene, yielding 737 genes, specific information is shown in Table S1. The key regulatory genes for 13 types of PCD were sourced from the literature [12], totaling 884 PCD-related genes, with the final gene list shown in Table S2.

We utilized the R package sva [13] to perform batch effect removal on GSE5296 and GSE47681, resulting in an combined GEO datasets. This combined GEO datasets includes 79 SCI samples and 51 control samples. Finally, we used the R package limma [14] to normalize the combined GEO dataset, standardizing probe annotations, and performing normalization. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted on expression matrices before and after batch effect removal to validate the effectiveness of batch effect correction, further details are presented in Figure S1.

Angiogenesis related differentially expressed genes (ARDEGs)

Based on the sample grouping of the combined GEO datasets, samples were categorized into SCI and control groups. Differential expression analysis was conducted on the genes from SCI and control samples to identify DEGs. A threshold of |logFC| > 0.5 and p-value < 0.05 was set for identifying DEGs. The p-value adjustment method used was Benjamini-Hochberg (BH). A Venn diagram was created, intersecting all genes that met the threshold with angiogenesis-related genes (ARGs) from the combined GEO datasets to obtain ARDEGs.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) analysis of PCD

We performed GSVA [15] on the expression matrix of the merged dataset. By analyzing the expression changes of gene sets across different samples, we calculated the enrichment scores of each gene set for each sample, reflecting the activity levels of various cell death pathways. Default settings, including Gaussian kernel density estimation and no normalization, were used for the GSVA analysis.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

We applied the WGCNA [16] algorithm for cross-analysis to identify potential key genes. Initially, WGCNA was used to determine suitable thresholds for constructing weighted adjacency matrices and Topological Overlap Matrices (TOM) for each analytical method. WGCNA conducted feature analysis of gene modules based on specific criteria of each method, identifying modules highly correlated with the target phenotype. Within each module, Module Membership (MM) represents the correlation between a gene and the module’s characteristic genes, while Gene Significance (GS) reflects the correlation of a gene with the target phenotype. Ultimately, intersection analysis of the WGCNA results was performed to filter for the most promising key genes from co-expression modules for subsequent functional validation and in-depth analysis.

Functional analysis

The functional analysis of ARDEGs was performed through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses, covering Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). Subsequently, a Venn diagram was created by intersecting ARDEGs and genes from the WGCNA modules. Utilizing the STRING online database [17] (https://string-db.org/) with a minimum required interaction score greater than 0.400 (medium confidence), a protein-protein interaction network(PPI network) for the key genes was constructed. The Cytoscape software (version 3.9.0) was used to identify and visualize potential core genes in the network. Five algorithms from the CytoHubba [18] plugin of Cytoscape [19]: Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Degree, Stress, Edge Percolated Component (EPC), and Closeness were employed. In the PPI network, scores for genes were calculated and arranged in order to select the top 50 genes. Finally, a Venn diagram was created from the intersections of the genes obtained from the five different algorithms for further analysis.

Bayesian network enrichment analysis

To infer the pathway interaction network, the “CBNplot” package [20] was used. The sequence data from 79 SCI mice in the combined datasets served as the background for calculating interaction directions. The “bngeneplot” function was used to create visualizations, with an intensity filter threshold set at 0.90.

Machine learning model construction

To build a diagnostic model, we employed 12 machine learning algorithms, we systematically paired the 12 base learners into 113 unique stacking ensembles. For every ensemble, the training set (combined GEO datasets) was evaluated with a stratified 10-fold cross-validation (CV): in each fold, nine-tenths of the samples were used for model fitting and the remaining one-tenth for validation; the mean area under the ROC curve (AUC) across the ten folds served as the internal performance metric. To mitigate over-fitting, four additional strategies were adopted: (1) dimensionality reduction-only 37 ARPRDEGs were retained as input features; (2) model complexity control-linear models (Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net) employed tuned L1/L2 penalties, while tree-based algorithms (RF, XGBoost, GBM) were restricted by maximum depth, minimum child weight and number of terminal leaves; (3) nested grid-search of hyper-parameters inside the CV loop; and (4) external verification on two test set (GSE151371, GSE45550). The ensemble achieving the highest average AUC in both the internal CV and the two external test sets was defined as the optimal diagnostic model [21].

Predictive model construction

A nomogram model for assessing the occurrence of SCI was constructed using hub genes via the “rms” R package. Each predictor variable was assigned a corresponding point, with the “total points” defined as the sum of the points from the three predictor variables. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were utilized to evaluate the predictive performance of the model.

mRNA-drugs prediction

hub genes were uploaded to the CTD database to match them with existing drugs. The resulting compound information was filtered based on “Organism Count = 3.” Finally, the top 5 mRNA-drugs relationships were visualized using Cytoscape.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock 1.5.6 [22]. The three-dimensional structure of human complexes was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and processed using AutoDock, which involved removing water molecules, adding hydrogens, calculating charges, merging non-polar hydrogens, and further optimizing the protein’s three-dimensional structure. For small molecule ligands, SDF format files were obtained from the PubChem database [22] (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and converted to PDB format using Open Babel. AutoDock Vina version 1.5.6 was then utilized for molecular docking to explore the interactions between the complexes and various small molecule ligands. The structures of the proteins and small molecules were processed in AutoDock to add hydrogens, remove water, and define torsional angles before determining docking box coordinates. By comparing docking scores, the optimal conformation for molecular simulation was identified, and 3D analysis figures were obtained using PyMOL 2.1.0 for visualization.

Immune infiltration

Mouse gene symbols were converted to human orthologues (R package homologene). From the combined dataset used in this study, which contained expression data for 18,229 mouse genes, 15,021 (82.4%) were successfully mapped to their human counterparts(Table S3). CIBERSORT [23] algorithm was employed and filtering to output immune cell enrichment scores greater than zero. This resulted in a detailed matrix of immune cell infiltration for the combined GEO datasets, which was then presented as a bar graph for visualization. Subsequently, the R package ggplot2 was used to create grouped comparison plots to illustrate the expression differences of immune cells in SCI samples versus control samples within the combined GEO dataset, retaining results with p-values < 0.05. Immune cells showing significant differences between the two groups were selected for further analysis. The correlation between hub genes and immune cells was calculated using the Spearman algorithm, and R package ggplot2 was again utilized to visualize the correlation analysis results of hub genes and immune cells.

Single‑cell validation

Public snRNA‑seq data (GSE189070) were processed with Seurat(5.3.0). During the quality control stage, we have set strict screening criteria: nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA < 7500 & nCount_RNA > 500 & percent.mt < 20. Subsequently, the NormalizeData function was used to standardize the expression levels, and 2,000 highly variable genes were screened out through FindVariableFeatures Then, data scaling and principal component analysis were carried out. The Harmony package was applied to integrate the dataset. The image of single-cell quality control is shown in Figure S2. The differentially expressed genes of each cluster were identified through the FindAllMarkers function, and cell type identification was conducted in combination with the expression profiles of classic cell marker genes(Table S4), Finally, we adopted the UMAP dimensionality reduction method to visually display the cell cluster distribution, cell type composition, and key gene expression.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using the R package CellChat (v2.1.2). To this end, we first constructed a CellChat object based on the RNA expression matrix and cell-type information using the createCellChat function. Subsequently, downstream analyses were conducted with the built-in ligand-receptor interaction databases, including “Secreted Signaling”, “ECM-Receptor” and “Cell-Cell Contact”. We then applied the computeCommunProb function to calculate communication probabilities.

Exposure data sources

Blood eQTL data: This study utilized blood expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTL) data provided by the eQTLGen Consortium [24]. From 4,479 known druggable genes, 2,525 genes with available expression regulatory information were screened. The database integrates data from 37 independent studies, encompassing a total of 31,684 participants of European ancestry.

Outcome data sources

We obtained spinal cord injury-related GWAS summary statistics [25] from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas). This dataset included 27,144 cases and 373,343 controls, with the following population composition:3,618 African American or Afro-Caribbean cases, 51,887 African American or Afro-Caribbean controls, 2,325 Hispanic or Latin American cases, 26,989 Hispanic or Latin American controls, 21,201 European ancestry cases, 294,467 European ancestry controls.The specific types of spinal cord injury corresponding to the GCST number information are shown in Table S5 of the attachment.

Selection of instrumental variables

Blood eQTL data: Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) showing genome-wide significant associations with the exposure (p < 5 × 10−6) were selected [26]. A strict linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold (r2 < 0.01) was applied within a 10,000 kb clumping window. The most statistically significant SNP within each LD block was retained as the representative variant.

The strength of each SNP as an instrumental variable was evaluated by calculating the F-statistic (F = (β/SE)2). Only strong instrumental variables with F > 10 were retained, while weak instruments (F ≤ 10) were excluded to minimize potential bias.

Alignment between exposure and outcome GWAS datasets was performed through effect allele harmonization. SNPs with strand ambiguity were excluded. All retained SNPs were verified to have consistent effect alleles across both datasets.

MR

The core MR analyses comprised the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method, which served as the primary approach under the assumption that all instrumental variables are valid; the weighted-median method, which yields robust estimates when at least 50% of the instruments are valid; MR-Egger regression, which tests for directional pleiotropy via its intercept term; and the simple-mode and weighted-mode methods, which were applied as supplementary confirmation techniques.

Quality-control checks included heterogeneity assessment-Cochran’s Q test (significance threshold p < 0.05) and inspection of funnel-plot asymmetry-and evaluations of pleiotropy using the MR-Egger intercept test (a p-value > 0.05 indicating no significant pleiotropy) alongside the MR-PRESSO global test, which also detects outliers.

Sensitivity analyses consisted of leave-one-out testing, whereby each single SNP was removed in turn to assess the stability of the results, and outlier correction, with recalculated estimates adjusted according to MR-PRESSO outputs.

Animal models

Healthy female Kunming mice, 7–8 weeks old and weighing 30–35 grams, were housed under standard conditions and weres provided by Slike Jingda Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Gannan Medical University (Approval No. 2024–228). The SCI group and control group each consisted of 6 mice.

SCI model construction

Mice were anaesthetized and underwent laminectomy at T8–T10, with spinal cord transection at T9 for the SCI group. Sham surgeries omitted transection. 1, 3, and 7 days post-SCI, tissue surrounding the injury was collected for further analysis. BMS locomotor scores and Mechanical pain threshold are in Figure S3.

Behavioral assessment

Mechanical pain thresholds were evaluated using a Von Frey 2390 electronic anesthesiometer, following previously described protocols [27]. The pressure required to elicit paw withdrawal was recorded as the mechanical threshold. Thermal sensitivity was assessed according to the procedure reported by Huang et al. [28], in which the latency from immersion of the tail in 55 °C water to tail flick was measured and defined as the thermal pain threshold.

In vivo validation of acetaminophen in the SCI model

Mice were randomly allocated into two groups (n = 6 per group): SCI + saline (control) and SCI + acetaminophen (100 mg/kg, intravenous injection). Acetaminophen administration commenced on day 1 post-surgery and was continued once daily for 7 consecutive days. The dosage was selected based on pharmacokinetic studies indicating that intravenous delivery increases the area under the curve (AUC) and prolongs half-life [29]. Behavioral assessments were conducted on days 0, 3, and 7 after injury. On day 7, spinal cord segments surrounding the lesion site were harvested for Western blot analysis of Cd44 and Tnf protein expression.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the FastQuant RT Kit (TIANGEN, Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR was carried out with SYBR Premix ExTaq (TaKaRa) following the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH served as the internal control, and the primer sequences are provided in Table S6. Relative gene expression levels were calculated via the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted from spinal cord tissue surrounding the lesion and from sham controls. Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies against Cd44 (Proteintech Group, 1:1000), Icam1 (Proteintech Group, 1:1000), Tnf (Proteintech Group, 1:1000), Cd31 (Proteintech Group, 1:1000), N-Gsdmd (Proteintech Group, 1:2000) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were developed by ECL and quantified by ImageJ, normalized to β-actin.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0). Unless otherwise specified, the statistical significance of differences between two groups for normally distributed variables was assessed using an independent Student’s t-test. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was applied. Comparisons involving three or more groups were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to calculate correlation coefficients between gene expression levels. Behavioral data across time were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction (time × treatment); where sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Unless otherwise noted, all statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results of MR analysis

To explore the potential causal relevance of hub genes in SCI from a human genetics perspective, we performed MR analysis using summary-level GWAS data.

For Cd44 (Fig. 10A), significant associations with SCI were detected in three datasets: Nonallopathic lesions NEC (GCST90479112, 447,317 European) (β=–0.096, p = 0.035, OR = 0.909, 95% CI = 0.831–0.993), Spinal cord injury (GCST90476964, 55,505 African American or Afro-Caribbean) (β = 0.229, p = 0.036, OR = 1.258, 95% CI = 1.015–1.559), and Spinal cord injury without evidence of spinal bone injury (GCST90480630, 121,404 African American or Afro-Caribbean + 449689 European + 59692 Hispanic or Latino) (β = 0.193, p = 0.041, OR = 1.213, 95% CI = 1.008–1.461).

For Icam1 (Fig. 10B), two datasets indicated significant risk associations: Takes medication for spinal cord injury (GCST90476968, 315,668 European) (β = 0.138, p = 0.016, OR = 1.148, 95% CI = 1.026–1.285) and Takes medication for spinal cord injury (GCST90476967, 55,505 African American or Afro-Caribbean) (β = 0.251, p = 0.035, OR = 1.286, 95% CI = 1.018–1.623).

For Tnf (Fig. 10C), protective associations with SCI were observed: Spinal cord injury (GCST90476965, 315,668 European) (β=–0.070, p = 0.004, OR = 0.933, 95% CI = 0.890–0.978) and Spinal cord injury (GCST90481198, 55,505 African American or Afro-Caribbean + 29314 Hispanic or Latino + 315668 European) (β=–0.057, p = 0.005, OR = 0.945, 95% CI = 0.907–0.983).

In vivo validation of acetaminophen treatment

In vivo pharmacological validation was conducted to preliminarily evaluate the potential effect of acetaminophen in SCI. Compared with the SCI control group, acetaminophen treatment significantly increased the mechanical withdrawal threshold on day 7 after SCI (p < 0.05) (Fig. 13A). For thermal nociception, acetaminophen markedly prolonged the tail-flick latency on day 3 (p < 0.001) and, although partially decreased by day 7, remained significantly higher than the SCI control group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 13B). Western blot analyses revealed that acetaminophen treatment significantly reduced Cd44 protein expression relative to the SCI group (p < 0.05), whereas Tnf protein expression showed no significant difference between groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 13C-E). Discussion

This study first identified 134 ARDEGs in SCI. These genes were significantly enriched in biological processes such as cell adhesion, leukocyte migration, and wound healing, suggesting their importance in SCI tissue repair and immune regulation. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the identified ARDEGs were involved in classical signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, NF-kB, MAPK, JAK-STAT, and TNF, which play critical roles in inflammation regulation, cell survival, and angiogenesis during secondary injury in SCI. For example, the PI3K-Akt pathway [9] promotes angiogenesis and cell survival, while the NF-kB signaling pathway [10] is closely associated with inflammation.

Using WGCNA, we identified two modules-brown and turquoise-that were closely related to PCD. After intersecting these modules with ARDEGs, we shortlisted 118 ARPRDEGs. Bayesian network enrichment analysis suggested that these genes are central in the SCI regulatory network and may influence the dynamic balance between angiogenesis and PCD through ECM regulation. ECM remodeling after SCI, through matrix-degrading enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and the formation of glial scars, regulates tissue repair [11], but excessive or abnormal ECM remodeling may hinder nerve regeneration and exacerbate secondary injury [12]. Additionally, ECM provides structural support and signal regulation in angiogenesis, and its degradation products activate inflammation and apoptosis pathways, further influencing the tissue microenvironment [13].

Further PPI network analysis and machine learning identified three core genes-Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf. Time-course qRT-PCR and Western blot experiments validated their expression changes in the SCI group, indicating that their regulatory roles vary across different stages of injury. Cd44, a key molecule in the interaction between the extracellular matrix and cells, may promote cell adhesion, inhibit fibrosis formation after spinal cord injury, and facilitate SCI repair [14]. Icam1, an adhesion molecule, plays an essential role in regulating immune cell migration and inflammation [15], and its reduced expression may be a protective mechanism to suppress excessive inflammation. Tnf, a classic inflammatory factor [30], is involved in the negative regulation of inflammation, potentially reducing the suppressive effects of inflammation on angiogenesis and neurorepair in secondary SCI injury. Our correlation analysis suggests that Cd44 and Icam1 are positively linked to multiple PCD pathways, potentially integrating cell-death-related signals into angiogenic regulation [31, 32], while autophagy appears to have an opposite effect [33]. Tnf showed positive correlations with inflammation-related PCD types such as pyroptosis, consistent with its role in inflammatory angiogenesis [34, 35]. These results further support the involvement of specific PCD pathways in modulating angiogenesis after SCI.

Based on these three hub genes, we developed a nomogram model, which demonstrated excellent predictive performance in both the training set and external validation sets derived from human and rat sources. This indicates that these hub genes have good diagnostic value across different species and populations. The calibration curve indicated a good fit between the predicted probabilities and the actual incidences in our datasets, and decision curve analysis suggested potential net benefits across a range of risk thresholds. These results position this model as a robust research tool with translational potential, but its applicability in clinical settings for precise diagnosis, risk assessment, or personalized treatment guidance necessitates further prospective validation in larger, well-defined clinical cohorts.

Moreover, mRNA–drug interaction analysis identified acetaminophen as a potential therapeutic agent for SCI. Molecular docking revealed strong binding affinities with Cd44 and Tnf through stable hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds, but negligible interaction with Icam1. This binding selectivity may stem from structural and physicochemical differences among the targets: the Icam1 interface is narrow and predominantly hydrophilic, making it less compatible with small, weakly polar molecules such as acetaminophen, whereas Cd44 and Tnf possess more spacious and accommodating cavities that favor multiple stabilizing contacts. Our in vivo results corroborate this pattern, as acetaminophen treatment selectively reduced Cd44 expression without significantly affecting Tnf, suggesting that its functional effects in SCI are mediated predominantly via Cd44-associated pathways. Critically, when we further examined its effects on the core angiogenesis and PCD pathways as suggested by reviewers, we found that acetaminophen significantly increased Cd31 expression while suppressing N-Gsdmd, demonstrating its coordinated regulation of both processes. We acknowledge that these observed effects are more likely mediated by acetaminophen’s established anti-inflammatory properties than through direct mRNA interactions. As a widely used analgesic and antipyretic agent with anti-inflammatory activity, exerting its effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase and reducing prostaglandin synthesis [36]. We therefore propose that its beneficial role in SCI may be attributed to a systemic improvement of the inflammatory milieu, which secondarily modulates the angiogenesis-PCD axis—promoting vascular repair while inhibiting pyroptosis, with Cd44 downregulation being one component of this coordinated response. Notably, previous studies have shown that certain NSAIDs, such as aspirin, can promote angiogenesis by upregulating vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) and modulating oxidative stress signaling [37], while also facilitating tissue repair through the inhibition of apoptosis [38]. Consistent with these observations, our in vivo experiments demonstrated that paracetamol alleviated neuropathic pain in an acute SCI model, highlighting its potential for therapeutic repositioning. The selected intravenous dose of 100 mg/kg was guided by pharmacokinetic evidence indicating enhanced CNS exposure in SCI and supported by the compound’s intrinsic ability to cross the blood–brain barrier. Future studies will further explore dose–response relationships, delineate downstream Cd44 signaling mechanisms in vitro, and quantify CNS distribution under SCI conditions using fluorescent or isotopically labeled paracetamol.

Moreover, although knockdown or overexpression studies were not feasible within the current revision window, we expanded functional validation from multiple dimensions: (1) time-course qRT-PCR and Western blot confirmed consistent transcriptional and protein dynamics; (2) MR analysis supported causal roles of the hub genes in human cohorts; and (3) single-cell transcriptomics with CellChat identified VISFATIN signaling as a key communication axis linking endothelial cells and myeloid populations in the high-expression hub-gene group. Together, these complementary approaches reinforce the biological plausibility of Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf in integrating angiogenesis and PCD during SCI.

Immune infiltration analysis revealed changes in the immune microenvironment in the SCI lesion area, with a significant increase in M2 macrophages and a decrease in M0 macrophages in the SCI group. M2 macrophages are typically associated with anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair [39]. Tnf was found to be significantly positively correlated with mast cell activation, suggesting that it may play an essential role in regulating inflammation during SCI repair [40]. These results suggest that modulating the function of specific immune cells may provide new therapeutic strategies for SCI, especially by targeting core genes to precisely intervene in the immune microenvironment.

Despite the comprehensive multi-dataset analysis and experimental validation performed in this study, several limitations should be noted. First, our transcriptomic analysis relied heavily on publicly available datasets, which may be affected by batch effects, sample heterogeneity, and variations in experimental design, including differences in SCI models (e.g., contusion in public datasets vs. transection in our experiments) and tissue sources (lesion tissue vs. peripheral blood). Such heterogeneity can influence gene expression patterns and partially explain discrepancies between public-data trends and our experimental results. Second, although we complemented public-data findings with time-course qRT-PCR, Western blot, Mendelian randomization, and single-cell communication analyses, functional validation still remains limited; future studies will include in vivo perturbation of Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf (e.g., siRNA knockdown or overexpression) to clarify their causal roles in SCI repair. Third, while we incorporated Mendelian randomization to provide human genetic support for hub-gene associations, it is important to note that these analyses utilize cross-sectional genetic data, these results are exploratory and constrained by the availability and granularity of existing GWAS and eQTL datasets; additional large-scale, well-phenotyped clinical cohorts are needed to strengthen these causal inferences. Fourth, the predicted drug-gene interactions, such as the computational identification of acetaminophen binding to Cd44 and Tnf, remain speculative. Although we provided preliminary supportive evidence, comprehensive pharmacological and toxicological validation, including dose–response relationships, BBB penetration assays, and mechanistic cell-based experiments, is indispensable before considering any translational relevance. Finally, although orthologue maPPIng followed by CIBERSORT is widely applied, species-specific expression differences may bias immune-cell estimations; the concordance between bulk deconvolution and single-cell evidence partly mitigates, but does not eliminate, this limitation. Future work employing mouse-optimized immune reference signatures or spatial transcriptomics will further refine the immune landscape in SCI.

In conclusion, systematically dissected the interplay between angiogenesis and programmed cell death in SCI and identified three hub genes-Cd44, Icam1, and Tnf-as key regulators. Through transcriptomic mining, time-course qRT-PCR, Western blot, single-cell maPPIng, and Mendelian randomization, we provided multi-layered evidence supporting their diagnostic and mechanistic roles. The nomogram model based on these hub genes demonstrated robust predictive performance across species. Furthermore, acetaminophen was computationally and experimentally suggested as a candidate targeting Cd44-associated pathways, although further pharmacological and functional studies are required to confirm its therapeutic potential. Together, these findings not only highlight new molecular targets for SCI diagnosis and treatment but also emphasize the importance of integrating multi-omics and experimental approaches to refine mechanistic understanding and guide translational strategies.
