Taste Propensity in Obese and Nonobese Older Adults
Authors: Maedeh Akbari, Ladan Alizadeh Lemani, Nasim Abedimanesh, Jalal Hejazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo examine associations between taste propensity and body mass index (BMI) in elderly individuals (≥ 60 years), compare taste propensity between obese and nonobese groups, and explore gender‐related differences to inform dietary interventions.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted with 231 elderly participants (aged ≥ 60 years) in Zanjan city, categorized into obese (n = 80) and nonobese (n = 151) groups. Taste propensity was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) evaluating six taste groups: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami, and fat. Pearson's correlation and independent samples t‐tests were used to examine relationships between BMI and taste propensity.ResultsInverse correlations were observed between BMI and sweet (r = −0.172, p = 0.009), bitter (r = −0.139, p = 0.035), and umami (r = −0.168, p = 0.010) taste propensities; whereas there were positive correlations between BMI and salty (r = 0.204, p = 0.002) and fat (r = 0.167, p = 0.011) taste scores. Moreover, obese participants showed a lower propensity for sweet and umami tastes (p = 0.049; p < 0.001), but a higher propensity for salty and fat tastes (p = 0.029; p = 0.024) compared to nonobese individuals. A gender difference was observed in umami propensity among obese participants, with women showing a stronger propensity (p = 0.033).ConclusionsObesity in the elderly is associated with altered taste perception, particularly an increased propensity for salty and fatty foods and a decreased propensity for sweet and umami tastes. These findings may inform tailored dietary interventions in older adults.Keywords: elderly, fat, obesity, salt, sweet, taste properties

Introduction

By 2030, one in six people worldwide will be aged 60 years or over, with this population growing from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion [1]. In Asia, the number of individuals aged 60 and above is expected to rise by 65% between 2017 and 2050. Estimates indicate that by 2050, Iran's elderly population will surpass 25 million [2]. This demographic shift, underscores the growing need to promote health and reduce disease risk among older adults [3]. Meanwhile, obesity has reached epidemic levels globally, affecting people across all age groups, including the elderly. It is linked to increased morbidity, poorer quality of life, higher healthcare costs, and various comorbidities such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and impaired mobility [4]. Understanding the nuances in their food choices is essential, as dietary habits can profoundly influence health outcomes.

Food propensities and preferences are shaped by a combination of biological, psychological, and cultural factors. People primarily learn to enjoy and consume specific foods while avoiding or disliking others, with cultural traditions serving as a key influence. Taste preferences may arise from the nutritional benefits or toxicity of food or the enjoyment of its taste. Additionally, the source or type of food, along with ethical or religious beliefs—such as concerns about animal welfare—can also affect food preferences. The development of food preferences and aversions is shaped by factors such as exposure, associations with positive or negative experiences, and social influences [5]. Strongly shapes dietary habits, which ultimately affect body weight and health outcomes.

Research indicates that obese individuals often display different taste preferences compared to those with normal weight. Obese individuals tend to favor energy‐dense foods rich in fats and sugars and generally have a greater preference for and intake of such items [6]. This may be linked to lower taste sensitivity and individual differences in the neurophysiology of food reward [7].

Taste propensity and taste preference, while interconnected, are distinct concepts. Taste propensity refers to the natural inclination or tendency to consume specific foods on the basis of their taste characteristics, often measured by the frequency of consumption [8]. In contrast, taste preference reflects the subjective liking or choice of particular foods. However, studies specifically focusing on the elderly population remain limited. Age‐related changes in taste and smell, dietary restrictions, and varying levels of health literacy can all significantly affect food preferences and propensities in older adults.

Food propensities play a critical role in supporting health during aging. Physiological, psychosocial, and oral health deterioration and economic factors associated with aging significantly influence the nutritional and food preferences of older adults, often impeding the adoption of a healthy diet [9]. Aging is associated with changes in food choices that often parallel shifts in taste propensity [3, 10]. The decline in taste bud density, particularly on the laryngeal surfaces, may contribute to a reduced ability to taste food, thereby altering food preferences [11]. Additionally, reduced taste and smell sensitivity, along with factors such as medication use, zinc deficiency, and systemic diseases, can influence dietary patterns and food propensities, ultimately affecting the inclination to consume specific foods on the basis of their taste characteristics [12].

Sensory perception of basic tastes such as sweet, salty, bitter, sour, and umami typically declines with age [13, 14, 15] due to physiological and behavioral factors [16]. This reduction in sensitivity can lead older adults to prefer more intensely flavored or energy‐dense foods [17]. These sensory changes, along with medication use and health conditions, may affect food propensities and, in turn, influence dietary patterns in the elderly [9].

Despite the critical role of food choices and eating behaviors in maintaining health and preventing disease among elderly individuals, research on taste propensity in this age group is still limited. Most research has focused on the physiological, psychological, and social changes that influence food choices, with fewer studies specifically addressing taste propensities in older adults. Our study aims to explore the relationship between taste propensity and body mass index (BMI) in elderly individuals, examining the differences in taste propensities between obese and nonobese older adults. These findings could provide valuable insights into the factors influencing eating behaviors in this population. On the basis of these considerations, our research is grounded in the hypothesis that obese elderly individuals may have a greater propensity for energy‐dense foods compared to their nonobese counterparts and may exhibit stronger preferences for certain tastes. Understanding the food propensities of elderly individuals is crucial for addressing the dietary factors contributing to obesity and promoting healthier eating habits in this population.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

In this study, a total of 231 older adults aged 60 years and older from Zanjan city were included. Obesity was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [18]. Participants were recruited voluntarily through notices posted on public bulletin boards across the city. The participants were deemed eligible if they possessed sufficient cognitive ability to complete the interviews. Participants suffering from diseases that affect the ability to taste flavors, such as head and neck cancers, recent viral infections of the upper respiratory tract and influenza, Sjogren's syndrome (SS), chronic renal failure, end‐stage liver disease, certain neurological disorders, and diabetes mellitus, as well as individuals who consumed alcohol, were not included in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Although the exclusion criteria were assessed, no participants were excluded after recruitment.

General Characteristics and Taste Propensity Questionnaire

A questionnaire was administered to collect general characteristics such as age, sex, height (cm), weight (kg), and health‐related behaviors, including smoking, employment status, and medication use. Smoking status was categorized as “never smoked” or “currently smokes.” Employment status was classified as “employed” or “retired.” The participants' taste propensities were assessed via a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) that included 147 items. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire in the Iranian population were assessed in the study of Mirmiran et al. [19]. The FFQ categorized into six taste groups: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami, and fat. The food items within each taste group were listed separately on the basis of expert opinions, with certain items, such as spices, placed in a separate category. To calculate the taste propensity score for each taste in both the obese and nonobese groups, the propensity score method used by Jilani et al. [8] was employed. All responses from the FFQ were converted into weekly consumption frequencies, and the score for each taste was calculated by dividing the total weekly consumption of all items within a taste group by the total weekly consumption of all taste groups and then multiplying by 100. This process generates a continuous variable representing the propensity scores for each taste group, ranging from 0% to 100%. These scores reflect the proportion of foods from each taste group in the overall diet. For example, a sweet taste score of 25 indicates that a quarter of all foods consumed in a week were high in sugar.

Statistical Analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data was assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated for all continuous variables. Group differences in taste propensity scores between obese and nonobese elderly individuals were examined using independent samples t‐tests for normally distributed data. The correlation between body mass index (BMI) and taste score was analyzed via Pearson's correlation coefficient for normally distributed data to evaluate the strength and direction of the relationship between these variables. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all the statistical tests. All tests were two‐tailed, and the results are reported with corresponding confidence intervals where applicable.

Associations Between Taste Propensity and BMI

A weak but significant inverse correlation was found between BMI and the Sweet, Bitter, and Umami scores (r = −0.172, p = 0.009; r = −0.139, p = 0.035; r = −0.168, p = 0.011, respectively), indicating that individuals with higher BMIs tend to have slightly lower Sweet, Bitter, and Umami scores. Similarly, a weak but significant positive correlation was observed between BMI and the salty (r = 0.204, p = 0.002) and fat (r = 0.167, p = 0.011) scores, indicating a slight tendency toward a greater propensity for salty and fatty tastes among individuals with higher BMI. The correlation between BMI and the Sour score was not statistically significant (r = 0.002, p = 0.977).

We compared taste propensity scores for six basic tastes (sweet, salty, bitter, sour, fat, and umami) between obese and nonobese individuals. Discussion

The findings of this study enhance the understanding of how obesity influences food propensities in elderly individuals. Our findings revealed significant associations between BMI and taste scores, including negative correlations for sweet, bitter, and umami tastes, and positive correlations for salty and fat tastes, suggesting that obese individuals tend to exhibit distinct patterns in taste propensities compared to their nonobese counterparts. However, no significant differences were found in the perception of bitter and sour tastes between the two groups. These findings provide important insights into the variations in taste propensities and perceptions that may be linked to obesity.

Although previous studies have suggested that obesity may increase the preference for sweet foods due to altered food reward sensitivity [20], our results revealed a significant negative correlation between BMI and sweet taste propensity in older adults. This finding challenges the assumption that obesity is associated with heightened preference for sweet tastes. One possible explanation lies in age‐related declines in sweet taste sensitivity, which may lead obese individuals to require higher concentrations of sucrose to detect sweetness [21]. However, in light of public health messages emphasizing reduced sugar intake—particularly among those with obesity—these individuals may intentionally limit their consumption of sweet foods, resulting in less frequent sensory exposure and consequently a lower reported taste propensity. Moreover, while Iatridi et al. [22] suggested that older adults who prefer sweet tastes tend to have higher BMI and fat‐free mass due to energy demands and lifestyle choices, this contrast may reflect the combined effects of age‐related declines in sweet taste sensitivity and greater health awareness among obese individuals. As Sergi et al. [23] noted, reduced taste sensitivity in older adults does not necessarily lead to a preference for stronger flavors, highlighting that food choices may be influenced by health considerations rather than sensory perception alone. Taken together, the lower sweet taste propensity observed among obese older adults may stem from a complex interplay of physiological sensory decline, behavioral restraint, and health‐oriented dietary decisions.

Fat taste propensity is a key predictor of obesity, as people who have a higher preference for fatty foods consume more dietary fat and, therefore, consume more calories, which may lead to weight gain [24]. Our results are consistent with the results of Deglaire et al., who reported a significant positive correlation between fat preference and higher BMI in both males and females. Their study also showed a more noticeable difference in fat taste preference among females with higher BMI, which may be influenced by hormonal or sociocultural factors [25]; however, our study did not reveal any significant sex‐based difference between the obese and nonobese groups. Studies have shown that fat taste sensitivity declines with aging [14, 26], and individuals with lower sensitivity to fat taste (hyposensitive) consume greater amounts of fat to achieve sensory satisfaction, which can promote weight gain [27]. These findings indicate the importance of individual variability in sensitivity to fat taste when planning a dietary intervention for obesity prevention. Adequate strategies for reducing fat intake, especially in those with reduced fat taste sensitivity, may help body weight management. Future research should investigate sex differences in fat taste propensity and sensitivity and also explore their interaction with dietary patterns to clarify the impact of fat taste on long‐term weight regulation.

As people age, they tend to prefer saltier foods, which can result in increasing sodium intake [23]. A decline in sensitivity to salty taste may influence these food preferences [28]. Lee [29] reported that the Korean elderly individuals who live in the countryside have a higher preference for salty taste, which is associated with consuming more sodium. This preference may lead to increased consumption of processed foods and impact an individual's BMI. While our study found a positive association between BMI and salty taste propensity, the literature presents mixed results, highlighting the complex interplay of age, dietary patterns, and blood pressure in shaping salt preference [30, 31]. Ferraris et al. [31] found no correlation between salty taste genotype, sodium intake, and overall health predictors, such as BMI. These discrepancies highlight the complexity of taste propensity, which appears to be influenced by multiple factors, including age and blood pressure. Understanding these differences is crucial to designing effective dietary interventions for improving health outcomes in older populations.

In contrast to our study, which focuses on taste propensity, most previous research has examined sour taste perception in older adults. Neumann et al. [32] found that elderly individuals have reduced sensitivity to sour taste. Barragán et al. [14] reported that sour taste perception declines with aging, and this decline was more pronounced in females. Sergi et al. [23] also reported an increase in detection thresholds for sour and bitter tastes with aging, which showed an overall decline in taste sensitivity. Moreover, Jeon et al. [3] found that sour taste threshold in older adults identified a negative association between dietary iron, thiamin, vitamin C, fiber, and energy intake, highlighting the complex relationship between nutrition and taste sensitivity. However, our study found no significant association between BMI and sour taste propensity in older adults.

Our findings revealed a weak negative correlation between BMI and bitter taste propensity. Although the literature on bitter taste sensitivity with aging is inconclusive, this aspect may influence vegetable consumption and warrants further exploration. This finding is consistent with Grinberg et al. [28], which showed underweight individuals needed a greater concentration of solute to detect bitter taste than normal‐weight or overweight individuals. Previous research has largely focused on sensitivity for the bitter taste rather than bitter taste propensity, leaving a gap in understanding how propensity for this taste affects dietary preferences and behaviors. Age‐related changes in bitter taste sensitivity have contradictory results; Cowart et al. [33] reported a decline in bitter taste sensitivity with aging, whereas Schiffman et al. [34] reported an increase in bitter taste sensitivity with age without a correlation to suprathreshold bitter intensity. Aging is also associated with a decline in bitter taste perception and an increase in its detection threshold [14, 23]. Additionally, elderly females may be more sensitive to bitter taste than males [35]. Since higher sensitivity to bitter taste may lead to lower vegetable consumption, understanding these factors is necessary for improving the dietary health of the elderly [36]. Our findings underscore the importance of considering both bitter taste sensitivity and propensity when developing nutritional interventions aimed at promoting healthier eating patterns in older adults.

A weak negative correlation between BMI and umami taste propensity was found, indicating reduced preference with increasing BMI. This indicates that higher BMI is associated with a lower propensity for umami. The recent studies have similarly reported the same decline in sensitivity and perception of umami taste with increased BMI [37, 38]. Decreased tendency to consume umami‐rich foods, which are crucial sources of dietary protein intake, might be associated with obesity‐related patterns. In support of this relationship, Mizuta et al. [37] characterized lower sensitivity to umami taste as a predictor for obesity, while higher sensitivity for umami taste may promote healthier nutritional habits and reduce obesity risk. Additionally, Gervis et al. [38] highlighted that umami taste, along with other tastes, plays an important role in shaping food choices, which may contribute to healthy eating habits. Some studies have also suggested that aging, in addition to elevated BMI, reduces umami taste sensitivity [14, 28]. For instance, Grinberg et al. [28] noted that although elderly people can taste umami at low concentrations, their reduced sensitivity may influence their food choices, especially when it comes to foods high in protein. Moreover, certain studies have linked umami foods to obesity and metabolic syndrome [37, 39, 40]; however, further research is needed to better understand these relationships.

No significant gender differences were found in sweet, salty, bitter, sour, fat, and umami taste propensities among nonobese older adults. However, in obese elderly participants, a significant gender difference was observed for umami taste, as obese females showed a higher propensity for umami taste than obese males. This finding shows that gender could affect umami taste perception in obese elderly individuals but not in nonobese individuals. Our findings are consistent with a study by Grinberg et al., who reported no relationship between gender, nutritional status, and the sensitivity to basic tastes in older adults [28]. Furthermore, Barragán et al. [14] reported no gender differences in umami perception, but noted that females perceived other basic tastes—especially bitter, sweet, salty, and sour—more intensely than males. These variations in research outcomes underscore the complexity of obesity, which is influenced by a variety of genetic and environmental factors. This complexity underscores the importance of comprehensive studies that consider the health status of older adults to reach definitive conclusions.

Taste propensity in older adults is influenced by cultural, economic, and religious factors, as well as their ability to obtain foods independently. In addition, the social environment affects food choice by limiting shopping options to locally available sources [41]. Cultural differences can influence sensory preferences, such as perceived sweetness or sourness in foods, while religious beliefs also affect dietary propensities and restrictions [42]. Given the important role of sociocultural and religious factors in shaping food choices and taste propensities among older adults, it is recommended that future research specifically focus on examining these complex influences to develop more culturally sensitive and effective nutritional interventions.

This study consists of several valuable insights in the field of food patterns in older adults and emphasizes the importance of adequate nutritional interventions tailored to the specific nutritional requirements of older adults. This study used a structured questionnaire and analyzed taste propensities across BMI categories, including a focus on sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami, and fat, the latter of which represents a novel addition to the research. However, there are some limitations, such as a small sample size, which may restrict the applicability of the results to larger populations, reliance on self‐reported data for FFQ, which may lead to response bias, and the lack of a validated questionnaire that directly evaluates taste propensities in the elderly population. Furthermore, this research did not examine the psychological and cultural elements that shape taste propensities, highlighting a significant opportunity for future research. Additionally, due to the cross‐sectional nature of the study, no causal relationships can be inferred. Future research should consider longitudinal designs that follow individuals over time to better understand the directionality and temporal dynamics of associations between taste propensities and obesity‐related outcomes. In sum, this comprehensive assessment contributes valuable knowledge while highlighting gaps for future research, laying a solid foundation for advancing our understanding of taste and nutrition in the elderly.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the relationship between taste propensities and BMI in older adults, highlighting notable associations between propensities for sweet, salty, fat, and umami tastes. The findings show that obese individuals have a lower propensity for sweet and umami tastes and a higher propensity for salty and fatty tastes. These findings also suggest that elderly individuals with higher BMI may favor foods that are higher in salt and fat, which may contribute to greater caloric intake and potential weight gain. However, the reduced propensity for sweet and umami tastes among obese individuals may reflect dietary shifts or changes in taste sensitivity, possibly influenced by age‐related sensory changes or health‐related behaviors. In nonobese individuals, no significant gender differences were found in taste propensity. However, in obese individuals, a significant gender difference was observed in umami taste, with females showing a greater propensity than males. The overall absence of significant gender differences in taste propensity in the elderly population indicates that gender might have a limited effect on taste propensities. This study emphasizes the necessity of designing adequate nutritional interventions to reduce fat and salt intake. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to evaluate the long‐term impact of taste propensity on health outcomes and to understand its changes over time, particularly in relation to obesity and metabolic disorders in elderly individuals.
