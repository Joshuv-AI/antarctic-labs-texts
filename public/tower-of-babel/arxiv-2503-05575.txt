Sharp L ​ log ⁡ L L\log L condition for supercritical Galton–Watson processes with countable types
Mathilde André, Jean-Jil Duchamps

Abstract
We investigate Kesten–Stigum-like results for multi-type Galton–Watson processes with a countable number of types in a general setting, allowing us in particular to consider processes with an infinite total population at each generation. Specifically, a sharp L ​ log ⁡ L L\log L condition is found under the only assumption that the mean reproduction matrix is positive recurrent in the sense of Vere-Jones, (1967) . The type distribution is shown to always converge in probability in the recurrent case, and under conditions covering many cases it is shown to converge almost surely. We finally apply these results to the study of a directed network built from countably many configuration models as a model of epidemics.



1
Introduction and main results

The large-time behavior of Galton–Watson processes, both in the
single-type and multi-type settings, has been thoroughly explored in
various
works
(
Harris,, 1963
;
Athreya and Ney,, 1972
,
e.g.
)
.
However, more general settings remain less investigated. The
Galton–Watson process with countably many types arises naturally when
studying discretized structured population, such as aging processes or
spatially structured populations (
e.g.

Bansaye and Camanes, 2018
;
Ligonnière, 2025
;
Braunsteins et al., 2019
).
Notably, although some Kesten–Stigum limit theorems have been
established for such processes, these results often assume additional
conditions such as compactness of the mean reproduction operator or
some kind of uniform control of the types. These assumptions
are natural to deal with general type spaces, but they may seem more
artificial for countable type space. This was apparent when studying
an age-structured branching process arising from a stochastic model
of epidemics, which we present in the last part of the article (see
Section
5
). We thus explored weak sufficient
conditions to obtain a crucial result for this application: the
general convergence of the type distribution of countable-type
branching processes in probability stated in
Theorem
6
(ii). Along the way, we found
some improvements over the long-existing Kesten–Stigum theory in
the supercritical countable-type case

(
Moy,, 1967
;
Asmussen and Hering,, 1976
)
, with assumptions
that are minimal regarding the asymptotic behavior of the iterated
mean reproduction matrix (
i.e.
we only assume a Perron–Frobenius
behavior). We also related our findings with the phenomenon of local
extinction, which concerns a number of recent works

(
Braunsteins et al.,, 2019
;
Ligonnière,, 2025
,
e.g.
)
.
To the best of our knowledge, no sharp Kesten–Stigum-like result
had been provided yet for the objects we consider, namely the general
supercritical countable-type recurrent Galton–Watson processes. Our
aim is to provide one such result, using as our main ingredient the
spinal construction adapted from the seminal work of

Lyons et al., (1995)
. This gives the theoretical results
an intrinsic interest.

This article is organized as follows. In the remainder of
Section
1
, we introduce the model and notation and
state our main results (Theorem
2
and
Theorem
6
). Section
2
is
devoted to a simple way of building examples of countable-type
branching processes, allowing us to provide examples and
counter-examples to some of our statements. In
Section
3

and
4
we present the proofs of the
main results. Finally we exploit our results in
Section
5
in the study of the local weak limit
of a countable layering of configuration models as a general model
of epidemics.

Notation

In the following, we consider a countable set
\mathcal{X}
. We will write
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}
for the column vectors indexed by
\mathcal{X}
with entries in respectively
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{N}
(in this article we denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of
nonnegative

For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}

vu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
is the matrix with entries
(v_{x}u_{y})_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}
.
Note that we will only deal with vectors and matrices with nonnegative entries, so that matrix multiplication is always well-defined and associative.
If
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}
we write
v|_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
for the vector
(v_{x}\mathds{1}_{x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}})_{x}
, so that in particular
\langle u,v|_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}u_{x}v_{x}
.
We will also use the notation
e_{y}=(\mathds{1}_{x=y})_{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}
.

Throughout this work we will use the notion of biased random variables, always in the same usual sense which we recall below.
Let
A
be any random variable taking values in a measurable space

(E,\mathcal{A})
and
B
a nonnegative random variable with positive
and finite expectation. Then, the sentence “
A

biased by

”, refers to a random variable
A^{\prime}
with values in

such that the distribution of
A^{\prime}
is characterized by

\mathbb{E}\big[F(A^{\prime})\big]\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}[BF(A)]}{\mathbb{E}[B]},

(1)

for all nonnegative measurable functionals
F:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Note that often, we will take use
E=\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
,
equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra.

The model

We consider a multi-type Galton–Watson process, with a countable set
of types
\mathcal{X}
. This framework naturally models the
dynamics of a structured population of particles.

The model is defined from a fixed family of distributions

(\mathcal{L}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}
.
For each
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, for a random variable

L^{(x)}\sim\mathcal{L}_{x}
, we write
L^{(x)}_{y}
for its

y
-marginal, and assume that the first moment

M_{xy}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[L^{(x)}_{y}]
is finite. We define the
nonnegative matrix
M=(M_{xy})_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}
and further
assume that
M^{n}
has only finite coordinates for all
n\geqslant 1
. Let
us fix some
Z(0)\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}
with a finite number
of nonzero entries. The Galton–Watson process with offspring
distributions
(\mathcal{L}_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
and started from

Z(0)
is defined as a sequence of
\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}
-valued
random variables
(Z(n))_{n\geqslant 0}
satisfying the induction:

Z(n+1)\ =\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{x}(n)}L^{(x,i,n)},\qquad n\geqslant 0,

where
(L^{(x,i,n)})_{x\in\mathcal{X},i\geqslant 1,n\geqslant 0}
is a
collection of independent random variables, with

L^{(x,i,n)}\sim\mathcal{L}_{x}
.

The random variable
Z_{y}(n)
models the number of type-
y

individuals in the
n
th generation of a population in which
individuals of any given type
x\in\mathcal{X}
reproduce
independently of the others with a progeny distributed as the random
variable
L^{(x)}
. We point out the fact that the progeny of an
individual is almost surely finite coordinate-wise, however their
total number of children need not be. Therefore, the total number of
individuals in the population at generation
n
, given by

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}Z_{x}(n)
, is allowed to be infinite. Note
that the assumption that
M^{n}<\infty
(any equality or inequality
we write concerning matrices or vectors means that it holds
coordinate-wise) for all
n\geqslant 0
implies that for any

x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, starting from
Z(0)=e_{x}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z_{y}(n)]=M^{n}_{xy}<\infty
.

We summarize here some known definitions and results on the extension of Perron–Frobenius theory to countable nonnegative matrices, for which we refer to
Kitchens, (1998, Chapter 7, Section 1)
; see also
Seneta, (1981, Chapter 6)
or
Vere-Jones, (1967)
.
The matrix
M
is said to be
irreducible
if the adjacency graph of
M
(
i.e.
the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{X}
and where
x\to y
is a directed edge iff
M_{xy}>0
) is strongly connected.
It is said to be
aperiodic
if for all
x\in\mathcal{X},\ \gcd\{n:M^{(n)}_{xx}>0\}=1
.
If
M
is irreducible the period is common to all indices and “for all” can be replaced by “for some”.
Let
M
be irreducible and aperiodic.
Its Perron value, defined as

\varrho\ \coloneqq\ \lim_{n\to\infty}(M^{n}_{xx})^{1/n}\ \in\ (0,\infty],

(2)

is independent of
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varrho^{-1}
is the radius of convergence of the power series
f^{(x,y)}:s\mapsto\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M^{n}_{xy}s^{n}
, for all pairs
(x,y)
.
Then,
M
is said to be
recurrent
if
f^{(x,x)}(\varrho^{-1})=\infty
for some
x
(hence, for all
x
), otherwise
transient
.

In the finite type setting and under assumptions of irreducibility and aperiodicity, the Perron–Frobenius theorem gives the mean behavior of the process, whose exponential growth is driven by the greatest eigenvalue of the mean matrix.
When it comes to infinite non-negative matrices, the following proposition
(
Kitchens,, 1998
, Theorem 7.1.3)
gives an analogous result.

Proposition 1
(Extension of Perron–Frobenius theory to infinite matrices).

Assume
M
is irreducible, aperiodic and recurrent, with a finite Perron value
\varrho
.
Then,
\varrho
is the largest eigenvalue of
M
and is simple. Moreover, there exists associated left and right eigenvectors
\tilde{h}
and
h
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, which are positive and unique up to multiplicative constants.

If
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle<\infty
, then
M
is said to be
positive recurrent
and normalizing the eigenvectors such that
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle=1
, we have that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varrho^{-n}M^{n}_{xy}\ =\ \big(h\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big)_{xy}\ =\ h_{x}\tilde{h}_{y}.

Otherwise, if
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle=\infty
,
M
is said to be
null recurrent
and we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varrho^{-n}M^{n}=0
coordinate-wise.

From now on, we work under the following assumption.

Assumption I
.

The mean offspring matrix
M
is irreducible, aperiodic and recurrent, with a Perron value
1<\varrho<\infty
. In the case where
M
is positive recurrent, we will always assume that the normalization
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle=1
holds.

Related works

Kesten–Stigum-like results typically consist in finding conditions for the non-degeneracy of the a.s. limit
W
of the so-called additive martingale of the process (defined as
W_{n}\coloneqq\varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h\rangle
), relating it to the extinction event
\mathrm{Ext}\coloneqq\{\exists n\geqslant 0:Z(n)=0\}
of the process, and studying the asymptotic type-distribution (
i.e.
the behavior of
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)
).
For multi-type branching processes with a
finite
set of types, under Assumption
I
and with
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle=\langle\tilde{h},1\rangle=1
, it is well-known from the seminal work of
Kesten and Stigum, (1966)
that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}L^{(x)}_{y}\log_{+}\big(L^{(x)}_{y}\big)\bigg]<\infty\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[W]\ =\ \langle Z(0),h\rangle,\\
\mathbb{P}(W>0)\ >\ 0,\\
\{W=0\}\ =\ \mathrm{Ext},\ \text{up to a null-measure set},\end{cases}

the three items on the right-hand side being equivalent.
Moreover, they showed that the normalized distribution of the types in the population converges
almost surely
to
W\tilde{h}
as
n\to\infty
.
A short proof of this result is the main result of
Kurtz et al., (1997)
, building on the spinal change of measure developed by the same authors for single type branching processes
(
Lyons et al.,, 1995
)
.

When it comes to broader sets of types, several studies investigated the limit behavior of the large time type-population vector in the supercritical Galton–Watson process.
Limit theorems for the countable-type setting already appear in the seminal works of
Harris, (1963, Chapter III)
and
Moy, (1967)
.
Both derive extinction criterion and almost sure convergence of
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)
under strong additional assumptions.

Moy, (1967)
works with a second moment assumption, and
Harris, (1963, Chapter III)
assumes the coefficients of
M
are uniformly bounded.
Some more recent
works
(
de Raphélis,, 2017
;
Vatutin et al.,, 2021
)

focus on the critical case. They derived Yaglom-type results, that is,
convergence in distribution of the type vector conditional upon
non-extinction.

Several works have focused on
L\log L
criteria, in the countable
setting or in more general multi-type settings, however, these works
generally require some kind of compactness assumption or uniform bound
on moments of the offspring distribution.
Asmussen and Hering, (1976)

give a necessary and sufficient
L\log L
condition and almost sure
convergence of the types assuming a uniformly bounded mean number of
offspring over the type space (which is not assumed to be
countable), and a uniform convergence assumption of the
renormalized first moment semigroup. Very recently,
Bansaye et al., (2025)

manage to relax these uniformity assumptions under a slightly stronger

L\log L
condition.
Kesten, (1989)
works under
polynomial bounds for the fitness (that is, the contribution of the
progeny of an individual to the demography of the population in the
long run) of an individual outside a set
A_{t}
that does not grow too
quickly.
Biggins et al., (1999)
tackle
L^{p}
convergence of
the type distribution in a random environment, under ergodicity
conditions and uniform integrability conditions.

Athreya, (2000)
and
Biggins and Kyprianou, (2004)
give
criteria for the uniform integrability of the additive martingale in
general type spaces, involving uniform
L\log L
moment assumptions.
More recently
Ligonnière, (2025)
, in the setting of varying
environment and countable types, also obtains sufficient conditions
for convergence of the type distribution, under some uniform control
of the second moments of the offspring vectors. In particular, in all
of these works the total population size is almost surely finite at
all times, but we needed to get rid of this assumption in our upcoming
work, and our present results show this is indeed perfectly viable.
Note that infinite populations in finite time appear quite naturally
in the recent theory of (growth-)fragmentation processes: see in
particular
Bertoin and Watson, (2020, Theorem 1.1)
, where the authors
study growth-fragmentation branching processes and derive ergodicity
results similar to our Theorem
6
, in a quite
different setting of continuous time and type space.

Our results could find application in other contexts, such as seed bank models or delayed branching processes where countable sets of types typically appear.
Indeed, branching processes with potentially unbounded dormant populations are used to model long-term genetic storage
(
Blath et al.,, 2013
)
.

In a more specific setting, discretized Crump–Mode–Jagers processes studied in
Jagers and Sagitov, (2008)
can also be interpreted as branching processes with a countable set of types.
For these specific processes independence between the progeny produced by a same individual at distinct time allows obtaining Kesten–Stigum results and limit theorems under minimal assumptions, as is well documented in the work of
Nerman, (1981)
.

Main results

We seek to generalize Kesten–Stigum uniform integrability and ergodicity results to the countable type setting under minimal assumptions (namely Assumption
I
).
We start from a population satisfying
\langle Z(0),h\rangle<\infty
.
Because
h
is a right eigenvector for
M
, for every
n\geqslant 0
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Z(n),h\rangle\big]\ =\ \varrho^{n}\langle Z(0),h\rangle\ <\ \infty,

and in particular
\langle Z(n),h\rangle<\infty
holds almost surely.

This way, the population vector at generation
n
can be seen as a random element of the Banach space
\mathbb{B}_{h}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}:\lVert v\rVert_{h}\coloneqq\sum_{x}\lvert v_{x}\rvert h_{x}<\infty\}
. In the case where
M
is positive recurrent,
\tilde{h}
is a unit vector in
\mathbb{B}_{h}
. In the same spirit as the additive martingale in finite multi-type branching theory, it is not hard to see that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the process

W_{n}\ \coloneqq\ \varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h\rangle

defines a (scalar) nonnegative martingale. We denote by
W
its almost sure limit as
n\to\infty
.

We will see that in the general setting of recurrence for
M
, a sufficient condition for
\mathbb{E}[W]=\langle Z(0),h\rangle
– or equivalently, for the uniform integrability of
(W_{n})_{n}
– is

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\langle L^{(x)},h\big\rangle\log_{+}\big\langle L^{(x)},h\big\rangle\Big]\ <\ \infty.

(A)

This relates to previous works, in particular to
Asmussen and Hering, (1976)
where the analog of this condition is necessary and sufficient to have
\mathbb{E}[W]=\langle Z(0),h\rangle
in their setting.
Let us also mention
Athreya, (2000)
, whose type space is only assumed to be a measurable space (not a topological space).
Translating to our notation, his sufficient (but not necessary) condition for the uniform integrability of the additive martingale reads:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{h_{x}}\mathbb{E}(\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\mathds{1}_{\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle>\mathrm{e}^{t}})\,dt\ <\ \infty.

In our positive recurrent setting, note that this is stronger than (
A
).
Indeed, using the fact that
(\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
sums up to
1
, we compute:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{h_{x}}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\mathds{1}_{\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle>\mathrm{e}^{t}}\big]\,dt\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}}{h_{x}}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\mathds{1}_{\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle>\mathrm{e}^{t}}\big]\,dt

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\log_{+}\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\Big].

Note that (
A
) is
not necessary
in our general setting, even when
M
is further assumed to be positive recurrent – in this case we can express a necessary and sufficient condition that is less easily checked.

In order to state the latter condition, we need some more definitions.
Let us define the
spinal chain
, that is a Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
on
\mathcal{X}
with transition probabilities

p_{xy}\ =\ \frac{M_{xy}h_{y}}{\varrho h_{x}}.

(3)

Note that
X
is a recurrent Markov chain when
M
is recurrent. Denoting by
\pi
the vector with coordinates

\pi_{x}\ \coloneqq\ \tilde{h}_{x}h_{x},\quad x\in\mathcal{X},

(4)

we have that
\pi
is a stationary distribution for
X
, so that positive recurrence of
M
is equivalent to positive recurrence of
X
.

Let us define the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X_{n},n\geqslant 0)
and on the same probability space, introduce the sequence of nonnegative random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
such that:

•

The
(Y_{n})
are independent conditional on
\mathcal{G}
.

•

The distribution of
Y_{n}
conditional on
\mathcal{G}
and on

(X_{n-1},X_{n})=(x,y)
is the distribution of

\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle
biased by
L^{(x)}_{y}
in the sense
of (
1
), taking
E=\mathbb{R}
. That is,

\mathbb{E}\Big[F(Y_{n})\mid\mathcal{G}\Big]\mathds{1}_{X_{n-1}=x,X_{n}=y}\ =\ \frac{1}{M_{xy}}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}F\big(\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle\big)\Big]\mathds{1}_{X_{n-1}=x,X_{n}=y},

(5)

for any nonnegative measurable function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

We will write
\mathbb{E}_{x}
to denote expectation conditional on
\{X_{0}=x\}
.
The random sequence
(X_{n},Y_{n})
allows us to express the sharp
L\log L
condition in the positive recurrent case, namely:

Theorem 2
(Uniform integrability of the additive martingale).

Fix any state
x
, and denote by

T_{1}\ \coloneqq\ \inf\big\{k\geqslant 1,\ X_{k}=x\big\}

the first return time to
x
for the spinal Markov chain
X
. Assume that the initial population satisfies
\langle Z(0),h\rangle<\infty
and consider the condition:

\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\log_{+}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{T_{1}}\varrho^{-k}Y_{k}\Big)\bigg]\ <\ \infty.

(B)

If
M
is positive recurrent, then (
B
) is equivalent to any of the following (equivalent) points:

(i)

\mathbb{E}[W]=\langle Z(0),h\rangle
;

(ii)

\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
;

(iii)

\{W=0\}=\mathrm{LocExt}
up to a null-measure set,
where we denote by

\mathrm{LocExt}\ =\ \big\{\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{x}(n)=0\big\}

the event of local extinction.

If
M
is recurrent and Condition (
A
) or Condition (
B
) is satisfied, then the points
(i)
,
(ii)
and
(iii)
also hold.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
2
states that in the positive recurrent setting, Condition (
A
) is sufficient to obtain uniform integrability of the sequence
(W_{n})_{n}
, however unlike (
B
), Condition (
A
) is not necessary (see Section
2
for an example).
If
M
is null recurrent, then both conditions are sufficient.

Remark 4
(Extinction of the branching process).

As highlighted before, the Kesten–Stigum theorem on branching
processes with a finite set of types includes the fact that
\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
is equivalent to
\mathrm{Ext}=\{W=0\}
up to a null-measure set, where
\mathrm{Ext}
is the event of extinction. This is usually checked through the immediate inclusion
\{W=0\}\subset\mathrm{Ext}
and the equality
\mathbb{P}(W=0)\in\{\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Ext}),1\}
, which is derived using a fixed point argument on the generating function of the offspring distribution
(
Harris,, 1963
)
.

However, when the set of types is infinite the question is much more involved. As a matter of fact, the number of fixed points of the offspring generating function is not 2 but is at least countably infinite, as shown in
Tan and Zhang, (2024)
.
In fact, every type could become extinct eventually while the whole population does not, and may even grow exponentially. This phenomenon of local extinction has been studied in many recent works
(
Braunsteins et al.,, 2019
;
Ligonnière,, 2025
;
Kyprianou and Palau,, 2018
)
. Examples of such processes can be found in
Braunsteins et al., (2019)
, and we give in Section
2
an instance where even under positive recurrence of the mean matrix we have
\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
yet
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{Ext}^{c}\cap\{W=0\}\big)>0
.
Nevertheless, in our countable-type setting there is a dichotomy between the events
\{W>0\}
and local extinction of the process.

Remark 5
.

Using the fact that
\log_{+}(\sum_{i}a_{i})\leqslant\sum_{i}\log(1+a_{i})
for any sequence of nonnegative numbers
(a_{i})_{i}
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\log_{+}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{T_{1}}\varrho^{-k}Y_{k}\Big)\bigg]\ \leqslant\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\log_{+}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{T_{1}}Y_{k}\Big)\bigg]\ \leqslant\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{T_{1}}\log(1+Y_{k})\bigg],

and recalling from (
4
) the stationary distribution
\pi
of
X
, the last term above can be rewritten

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)\mathbb{E}_{y}\big[\log(1+Y_{1})\big]\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{y,z\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)\frac{M_{yz}h_{z}}{\varrho h_{y}}\cdot\frac{1}{M_{yz}}\mathbb{E}\big[L^{(y)}_{z}\log\big(1+\langle L^{(y)},h\rangle\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{y}\mathbb{E}\Big[\langle L^{(y)},h\rangle\log\big(1+\langle L^{(y)},h\rangle\big)\Big],

which, in the positive recurrent case, is finite only if (
A
) holds, showing that (
A
) is stronger than (
B
).
In the null recurrent case it is not clear with this argument, but we will prove directly that (
A
) is sufficient for the uniform integrability of
(W_{n})
.

We now turn to the large population asymptotic of the type distribution
Z(n)/\varrho^{n}
. We recall that we work on the Banach space

\mathbb{B}_{h}=\Big\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}:\lVert v\rVert_{h}\coloneqq\sum_{x}\lvert v_{x}\rvert h_{x}<\infty\Big\},

and denote by
L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P},\mathbb{B}_{h})
the subspace of random variables
V:\Omega\to\mathbb{B}_{h}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\lVert V\rVert_{h}]<\infty
. For reasons of brevity, we will use the notation of
Pisier, (2016, Chapter 1)
and write
L^{1}(\mathbb{B}_{h})
instead of
L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P},\mathbb{B}_{h})
.

Theorem 6
(Convergence of the type distribution).

(i)

In the null recurrent case, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\varrho^{-n}\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{x}(n)\big]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

(ii)

In the positive recurrent case, if
\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\lVert\varrho^{-n}Z(n)-W\tilde{h}\big\rVert_{h}\Big]\ =\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\lvert\varrho^{-n}Z_{x}(n)-W\tilde{h}_{x}\big\rvert\Big]h_{x}\ \underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ 0,

in other words,
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)\to W\tilde{h}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{B}_{h})
, and so in probability for the topology of the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{h}
.

If
\mathbb{P}(W>0)=0
then
\lim_{n}\lVert\varrho^{-n}Z(n)\rVert_{h}=\lim_{n}W_{n}=0
almost surely, however
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)\to 0
does not hold in
L^{1}(\mathbb{B}_{h})
.

(iii)

In the positive recurrent case and if
\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0
, then the convergence
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)\to W\tilde{h}
also holds almost surely in
\mathbb{B}_{h}
– i.e. the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\lVert\varrho^{-n}Z(n)-W\tilde{h}\big\rVert_{h}\ =\ 0

(6)

holds almost surely – if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

1.

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathrm{Var}(L^{(x)}_{y})\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}<\infty
;

2.

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}\log_{+}\big(L^{(x)}_{y}h_{y}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}\big)\Big]h_{y}\ <\ \infty
.

Furthermore, (iii)-
2
holds as soon as Condition (
A
) holds and the distribution
\pi=(\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x})_{x}
has finite entropy, i.e.
-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}\log\big(\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}\big)<\infty
.

Remark 7
.

Condition (iii)-
1
is reminiscent of the
condition that can be read
in
Moy, (1967, Theorem 1)
. Translated to our
notation, Moy’s condition reads

\sum_{x}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}[\langle L^{(x)},h\rangle^{2}]
, and she
shows almost sure and
L^{2}
convergence of the type distribution in
the positive recurrent case. Note that
Condition (iii)-
2
is easy to check
in the case where (
A
) holds. However, we did
not manage to show almost sure convergence of
\varrho^{-n}Z(n)
in all
generality and the finite entropy condition appears, somewhat
surprisingly, from our method of proof. Let us stress that it
may well be that (
6
) holds under the sole
assumption
\mathbb{P}(W>0)
in the positive recurrent case, but
we did not manage to prove this.

Remark 8
.

The notion of irreducibility,
R
-transience and
R
-(positive) recurrence of countable matrices also extend to kernels on countably generated measurable spaces – we refer to
Nummelin, (1984)
for a general account.
Perron–Frobenius theory also extends to this very general context, see
Tweedie, (1974)
, and we would expect statement analogous to our
main results to hold more generally, without relying on topological
arguments – for instance
Athreya, (2000)
obtains in
his topology-free setting an analogue of Theorem
2
.
However, the techniques we use here are not easily adapted to general type spaces under minimal assumptions, since our proofs crucially rely on approximating the type space
\mathcal{X}
by a finite set (for instance in Lemma
16
below, which is key to proving Theorem
6
-(ii)).

2
Examples and counter-examples

A convenient way to build some examples of countable-type Galton–Watson processes with specific properties consists in first fixing a spinal Markov chain, then building the rest of the branching process around this.
Specifically, let us consider an irreducible, aperiodic, recurrent Markov chain
(X_{n})
with values on some countable space
\mathcal{X}
, with transition matrix denoted by
(p_{xy})
and a stationary distribution
(\pi_{x})
, assumed to be a probability distribution in the positive recurrent case.
Fix any
\varrho>1
and any entry-wise positive
h=(h_{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
, and define the matrix
M
and the vector
\tilde{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
via

M_{xy}\ =\ \varrho\frac{p_{xy}h_{x}}{h_{y}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{h}_{x}\ =\ \frac{\pi_{x}}{h_{x}}.

(7)

Then by construction
M
is a matrix with nonnegative entries and satisfies
Mh=\varrho h
and
\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M=\varrho\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
.
Also, it is not hard to see that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
n\geqslant 1
, we have

\varrho^{-n}M^{n}_{xy}\ =\ \frac{h_{x}}{h_{y}}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{n}=y)\ \underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ \begin{cases}h_{x}\tilde{h}_{y}&\text{if }X\text{ is positive recurrent,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

in other words,
\varrho,h
and
\tilde{h}
are exactly as in Assumption
I
for the matrix
M
, and
X
is the associated spinal chain.
Now it remains to choose, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, any random vector
L^{(x)}=(L^{(x)}_{y})_{y\in\mathcal{X}}
to fix our type-
x
offspring distribution, and as long as
\mathbb{E}[L^{(x)}_{y}]=M_{xy}
, we have built a countable-type Galton–Watson process with a given spinal chain.
Note that there is large freedom in the last step which consists in
fixing the offspring distribution, as everything that comes before
only depends on the mean reproduction matrix
M
. For instance,
we can choose the
(L^{(x)}_{y})_{y}
to be Poisson random variables
(independent or not) and tune the correlations as we see fit.
Using this construction, we now illustrate some noteworthy facts about our results.

Condition (
A
) may fail while (
B
) holds, even in the positive recurrent case

Looking at Remark
5
, it seems clear that (
B
) is weaker than (
A
). Indeed, the two conditions are not equivalent, and we provide hereafter a simple example Condition (
A
) fail yet Condition (
B
) holds and so the additive martingale is uniformly integrable.

Consider a positive probability vector
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and define
X
a Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{0\}\cup\{(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:1\leqslant m\leqslant n\}
with the following transition probabilities:

\displaystyle p_{0,(n,1)}\ =\ q_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad p_{(n,n),0}

\displaystyle\ =\ 1\qquad\forall n\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad p_{(n,m),(n,m+1)}

\displaystyle\ =\ 1\qquad\forall n>m.

We assume
\sum_{n}nq_{n}<\infty
, so that (considering the return time at
0
)
X
is positive recurrent. Besides, we choose
(q_{n})
such that
\sum_{n}n^{2}q_{n}\ =\ \infty.

It is easily seen that the distribution
\pi
such that

\pi_{0}\ =\ 1\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{(n,m)}\ =\ q_{n}\quad\forall\ 1\leqslant m\leqslant n,

defines a stationary distribution for the Markov chain.
We define
h_{0}=1
, and for any
1\leqslant m\leqslant n
, we now define
h_{(n,m)}
to be equal to
\varrho^{m}
, so that by (
7
), the left eigenvector is given by
\tilde{h}_{(n,m)}=\varrho^{-m}q_{n}
.
Choosing any
\varrho>1
, we see that (
7
) implies that for
1\leqslant m<n
,

M_{(n,m),(n,m+1)}\ =\ 1,\qquad M_{0,(n,1)}\ =\ q_{n},\qquad M_{(n,n),0}\ =\ \varrho^{n+1}.

The above construction allows us to take
L^{(n,m)}_{(n,m+1)}
to be Poisson distributed with mean
1
(let us write
V
for such a random variable).
This way, the sum in (
A
) is greater than

\sum_{1\leqslant m<n}\varrho^{-m}q_{n}\varrho^{m+1}\mathbb{E}\Big[V\log_{+}\big(\varrho^{m+1}V\big)\Big]\ =\ \varrho\sum_{1\leqslant m<n}q_{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{+}\big(\varrho^{m+1}(1+V)\big)\Big].

In turn, the latter is lower-bounded by

\varrho\sum_{1\leqslant m<n}q_{n}(m+1)\log\varrho\ \geqslant\ \varrho\log\varrho\Big(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{n}n^{2}-1\Big)

which is infinite by assumption. We still need to choose some other
distributions for
L^{(0)}
and
L^{(n,n)}
to define completely the
branching process; we choose them in a very simple way (for instance
vectors of independent Poisson random variables with parameter given
by the mean matrix). Let us now show that (
B
) holds
in this example. We take
0
as the starting point. For
n\geqslant 1
,
with probability
q_{n}
we have
X_{1}=(n,1)
and conditional on this
event, the variable
Y_{1}
defined in (
5
) has
expectation:

\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{1}\mid X_{1}=(n,1)\big]\ =\ \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\big[L^{(0)}_{(n,1)}\big]}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(0)}_{(n,1)}\sum_{n^{\prime}\geqslant 1}L^{(0)}_{(n^{\prime},1)}h_{(n,1)}\Big]\ =\ 2\varrho\ \leqslant\ 2\varrho^{2},

where we used the fact that
(L^{(0)}_{(n,1)})_{n\geqslant 1}
are
independent Poisson random variables with respective means

(q_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
. A similar calculation shows that for
2\leqslant k<n
,

\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{k}\mid X_{1}=(n,1)\big]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\big[\varrho^{k+1}V^{2}\big]\ =\ 2\varrho^{k+1},

where
V
denotes a mean-one Poisson random variable. Finally, we can
similarly compute

\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{n}\mid X_{1}=(n,1)\big]\ =\ 1+\varrho^{n+1}\ \leqslant\ 2\varrho^{n+1},

so putting everything together in (
B
) and using the
assumption that
\sum_{n}nq_{n}<\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\log_{+}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varrho^{-k}Y_{k}\Big)\;\Big|\;X_{1}=(n,1)\Big]\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varrho^{-k}\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{k}\;\big|\;X_{1}=(n,1)\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varrho^{-k}\cdot(2\varrho^{k+1})\ <\ \infty.

Therefore, we see that there exist examples in
which (
B
) holds while (
A
) doesn’t.

Note that there does not seem to be a reason for (
B
) to be necessary in the null-recurrent case to obtain the uniform integrability of
(W_{n})
, but we did not find a counter-example for this situation.

Extinction is not characterized by
W=0
, even in the positive recurrent case

As pointed out in Remark

, we do not have
\big(\mathbb{P}(W>0)>0\big)\Rightarrow\big(\{W>0\}=\mathrm{Ext}^{c}\big)

This paragraph gives a counter-example in the positive recurrent supercritical framework.
This example already appears in

Take
\varrho=4/3
and let
X
be the Markov chain with values in
\mathbb{N}
whose transitions probabilities are given by
p_{n,n+1}=p_{n,0}=1/2
, for every

.

is a positive recurrent Markov chain,
(\pi_{n})=(2^{-(n+1)})
being its stationary probability distribution.
Define the vector

as

and
M_{n,n+1}=1

\displaystyle L^{(n)}\ =\ e_{n+1}+\mathrm{Poi}\big(2\cdot(2/3)^{n+1}\big)e_{0}\qquad\forall n\geqslant 1,

It is easy to check that (

and we will see that in this example the first inclusion is not an equality.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\{W=0\}\cap\mathrm{Ext}^{c}\big)\

\displaystyle=\ \exp\Big(-1-2\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\big(2/3\big)^{n+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\ \mathrm{e}^{-5}\ >\ 0.

Proof of Theorem

Theorem
2
puts together several classical ideas, the first of which is the so-called spinal change of measure.
In order to state this change of measure, up to enlarging our probability space, let us assume that our branching process keeps track of the whole genealogy of individuals.
Specifically, it will be convenient to use a Neveu–Ulam–Harris notation and identify individuals with elements of

\mathcal{N}\ \coloneqq\ \bigcup_{n\geqslant 1}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{N})^{n},

the set of finite sequences of elements of
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{N}
.
Individuals of type
x
in generation 0 are labeled
(x,1),(x,2),\dots,(x,Z_{x}(0))
; the type-
y
children of the particle labeled
(x,i)
are then labeled
((x,i),(y,1)),((x,i),(y,2)),\ldots
, and so on.

With these labeling rules, we may view our branching process as a random forest of genealogical typed trees (each tree rooted at an individual in generation 0).
Let us write
\mathcal{T}_{n}
for the genealogy of our branching process consisting of all individuals up to generation
n
.
This genealogy
\mathcal{T}_{n}
can formally be seen as a random subset of
\mathcal{N}
,

a random variable with values in
\mathscr{T}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{N}}
endowed with the product
\sigma

indicating the presence of individual
v
in the genealogy, which we will write

To build

rigorously, first let

be independent random variables, where the progeny

x

to denote the type of
v

Then, we define

as the set of individuals

for the ancestor of

bearing type
x
.
With this construction, we can define

Lemma 15
.

For
n\geqslant 1
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let
U_{n,x}
and
V_{x}
be nonnegative random variables.
If for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(U_{n,x}\geqslant V_{x}-\varepsilon)\ =\ 1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[U_{n,x}]\ =\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]\ <\ \infty,

then we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{x}\lvert U_{n,x}-V_{x}\rvert\big]=0
.
Note that the first of the two conditions above is implied by the condition that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}U_{n,x}\geqslant V_{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

First let us fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
and show that
\mathbb{E}[U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x}]\to\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]
as
n\to\infty
.
We compute, for any
\varepsilon>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \mathbb{E}\big[U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x}\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{U_{n,x}\geqslant V_{x}-\varepsilon\}}(V_{x}-\varepsilon)\Big]

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{E}[V_{x}]-\varepsilon-\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{U_{n,x}<V_{x}-\varepsilon\}}(V_{x}-\varepsilon)\Big].

Since
V_{x}-\varepsilon
is integrable and
\mathbb{P}(U_{n,x}<V_{x}-\varepsilon)\to 0
, the last term above tends to
0
, and so
\liminf_{n}\mathbb{E}[U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x}]\geqslant\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]-\varepsilon
.
Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we get
\mathbb{E}[U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x}]\to\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]
.
Now, by dominated convergence we have
\sum_{x}\mathbb{E}[U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x}]\to C\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[V_{x}]
, and we now just use the fact that
\lvert U_{n,x}-V_{x}\rvert=U_{n,x}+V_{x}-2(U_{n,x}\wedge V_{x})
to get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lvert U_{n,x}-V_{x}\rvert\Big]\ =\ C+C-2C\ =\ 0,

concluding the proof.

\bar{M}_{xy}(n)\ \coloneqq\ \mathbb{E}\big[L^{(x)}_{y}\wedge(\varrho_{2}^{n}h_{y}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y})\big].

(14)

Then almost surely, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
and
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

Z_{y}(n+1)\ \geqslant\ \big(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n)\big)_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}.

Proof.

From the definition of the capped matrix,
\forall x,y
,
\bar{M}_{xy}(n)\leqslant M_{xy}
and converges to
M_{xy}
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
y\in\mathcal{X}
and
n\geqslant 0
be fixed.
By the branching property on
Z
, we can write

\displaystyle Z_{y}(n+1)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{x}(n)}L^{(x,i)}_{y}

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{x}(n)}\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y},\quad\text{with }\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y}\ \coloneqq\ L^{(x,i)}_{y}\wedge(\varrho_{2}^{n}h_{y}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y}),

where the
(L^{(x,i)})_{i\geqslant 1,x\in\mathcal{X}}
are independent, with each
L^{(x,i)}
distributed as
L^{(x)}
. The
(\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y})_{i\geqslant 1}
have finite variance, such that

\mathrm{Var}\big(\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y}\big)\ \leqslant\ \mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y}\big)^{2}\big]\ \leqslant\ \mathbb{E}\big[L^{(x,i)}_{y}\big]\varrho_{2}^{n}h_{y}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y}\ =\ M_{xy}\varrho_{2}^{n}h_{y}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y}.

Now we can apply the Bienaymé-Chebyshev inequality to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(Z_{y}(n+1)\leqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n))_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}\big)\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{x}(n)}\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y}\leqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n))_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{\varrho_{1}^{2n}\tilde{h}_{y}^{2}}\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{Z_{x}(n)}\bar{L}^{(x,i)}_{y}\;\big|\;\mathcal{F}_{n}\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{x}(n)\big]\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar{L}^{(x)}_{y})}{\varrho_{1}^{2n}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y}}

(15)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{x}(n)\big]M_{xy}h_{y}\Big(\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{1}^{2}}\Big)^{n}.

By the union bound and harmonicity of
h
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\exists y\in\mathcal{X},\ Z_{y}(n+1)\leqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n))_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}\big)\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Big(\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{1}^{2}}\Big)^{n}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{x}(n)\big]M_{xy}h_{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Big(\frac{\varrho_{2}\varrho}{\varrho_{1}^{2}}\Big)^{n}\varrho\cdot\langle Z(0),h\rangle.

Since we picked
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
to have
\varrho_{2}\varrho/\varrho_{1}^{2}<1
, the previous bound is summable over
n
and the result holds by Borel-Cantelli’s lemma.

Now let us prove point (ii) in Theorem
6
. Take
\mathcal{X}_{0}
as in Lemma
16
and fix
y\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
for good.
Using Proposition
1
, one can choose some
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that the following holds:

\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{0},\ M^{k}_{xy}\ \geqslant\ (1-\varepsilon)\varrho^{k}h_{x}\tilde{h}_{y}.

(16)

Using that
\tilde{h}
is the left eigenvector associated with
\varrho
, Lemma
17
implies that almost surely, for all
n
large enough, we have coordinate-wise that

\displaystyle Z(n+k)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-(\varrho^{k-1}+\varrho^{k-2}\varrho_{1}+\cdots+\varrho\varrho_{1}^{k-2}+\varrho_{1}^{k-1})\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}

\displaystyle=\ Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}(\varrho^{k}-\varrho_{1}^{k})\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}},

\displaystyle\geqslant\ Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\varrho^{k}\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}},

where
\bar{M}(n,k)\coloneqq\bar{M}(n)\bar{M}(n+1)\cdots\bar{M}(n+k-1)
.
Because
\bar{M}(n)
increase coordinate-wise to
M
as

, so does
\bar{M}(n,k)
to
M^{k}
.
We now fix
\eta>0
, and we may pick
n_{0}
large enough to ensure that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll r}(\mathrm{a})&\mathbb{P}\big(\varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}^{c}}\rangle\leqslant\varepsilon\big)\ \geqslant\ 1-\eta&(\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:restriction_finite}});\\[14.22636pt]
(\mathrm{b})&\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{0},\ \bar{M}_{xy}(n,k)\ \geqslant\ (1-2\varepsilon)\varrho^{k}h_{x}\tilde{h}_{y}&(\text{$\mathcal{X}_{0}$ finite and using~\eqref{eq:power_close_to_PF}});\\[14.22636pt]
(\mathrm{c})&\begin{multlined}\mathbb{P}\big(\forall n\geqslant n_{0},\ Z(n+k)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}>Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\varrho^{k}\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big)\\[-5.69054pt]
\geqslant\ 1-\eta\end{multlined}&(\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:cutoff}});\\[14.22636pt]
(\mathrm{d})&\displaystyle(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\Big(\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\varrho}\Big)^{n}\ <\ \varepsilon&(\text{since $\varrho_{1}<\varrho$});\\[14.22636pt]
(\mathrm{e})&\mathbb{P}\big(\forall n\geqslant n_{0},\ \lvert\varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h\rangle-W\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon\big)\ \geqslant\ 1-\eta&(\text{a.s. convergence of $W_{n}$}\big).\end{array}\right.

Let
n\geqslant n_{0}
. By
(\mathrm{d})
, we have

\big\{Z(n+k)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\ \geqslant\ Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\varrho^{k}\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big\}\\
\subset\ \Big\{\frac{Z(n+k)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{(n+k)}}\ \geqslant\ \varrho^{-(n+k)}Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k)-\varepsilon\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\Big\}.

Thus using
(\mathrm{c})
, the probability of the latter event is at least
1-\eta
.
Furthermore by
(\mathrm{b})
, we have
(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n,k))_{y}\geqslant\varrho^{k}(1-2\varepsilon)\big(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}}Z_{x}(n)h_{x}\big)\tilde{h}_{y}=\varrho^{k}(1-2\varepsilon)\langle Z(n),h|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\rangle\tilde{h}_{y}
.
Whence we derive that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{Z_{y}(n+k)}{\varrho^{n+k}}\ \geqslant\ \big((1-2\varepsilon)\varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\rangle-\varepsilon\big)\tilde{h}_{y}\Big)\ \geqslant\ 1-\eta.

Now, by
(\mathrm{a})
and
(\mathrm{e})
, we have
\mathbb{P}\big(\lvert\varrho^{-n}\langle Z(n),h|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\rangle-W\rvert\leqslant 2\varepsilon\big)\geqslant 1-2\eta
.
It follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(\varrho^{-(n+k)}Z_{y}(n+k)\ \geqslant\ ((1-2\varepsilon)(W-2\varepsilon)-\varepsilon)\tilde{h}_{y}\big)\ \geqslant\ 1-3\eta,

and since
\varepsilon
and
\eta
are arbitrary, standard arguments yield:

\forall\varepsilon>0,\qquad\mathbb{P}\big(\varrho^{-n}Z_{y}(n)\geqslant W\tilde{h}_{y}-\varepsilon\big)\ \underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\ 1.

Combined with the fact that
\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{y}\varrho^{-n}Z_{y}(n)h_{y}\big]=\mathbb{E}[W_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[W]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{y}W\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y}]
, applying Lemma
15
to
U_{n,x}=\varrho^{-n}Z_{x}(n)h_{x}
and
V_{x}=W\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}
we get that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Big\lvert\frac{Z_{x}(n)}{\varrho^{n}}-W\tilde{h}_{x}\Big\rvert h_{x}\bigg]\ =\ 0,

which concludes the proof of (ii).

4.2
Proof of (iii)

(iii)-
1
: Offspring distributions with finite variance

Assume
\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathrm{Var}(L_{y}^{(x)})<\infty
and satisfy

\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathrm{Var}(L_{y}^{(x)})\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}\ <\ \infty.

Then, we can improve Lemma
17
using the mean matrix directly in place of the capped matrix
\bar{M}
:
following the same steps as in the proof of
Lemma
17
but without replacing
L^{(x)}_{y}
by
\bar{L}^{(x)}_{y}
, we obtain the analogue of (
15
), that is

\mathbb{P}\big(Z_{y}(n+1)\leqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M)_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}\big)\;\leqslant\;\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{x}(n)\big]\frac{\mathrm{Var}(L^{(x)}_{y})}{\varrho_{1}^{2n}\tilde{h}^{2}_{y}},

so summing over
y\in\mathcal{X}
and using a union bound, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\exists y\in\mathcal{X},\ Z_{y}(n+1)\leqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M)_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}\big)\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}[Z_{x}(n)]\frac{\mathrm{Var}(L_{y}^{(x)})}{\varrho_{1}^{2n}\tilde{h}_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \Big(\frac{\varrho}{\varrho_{1}^{2}}\Big)^{n}\langle Z(0),h\rangle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathrm{Var}(L_{y}^{(x)})\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}.

By assumption, the last sum is a finite constant, so summing over
n
and using
\varrho_{1}>\sqrt{\varrho}
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma applies and we obtain that almost surely, for all
n
large enough and all

y\in\mathcal{X}
,

Z_{y}(n+1)\ \geqslant\ (Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M)_{y}-\varrho^{n}_{1}\tilde{h}_{y},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Multiplying on the right by
M
iteratively, an easy induction shows that almost surely, for all
n
large enough, all
k\geqslant 1
and all

y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle Z_{y}(n+k)\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ (Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M^{k})_{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varrho^{i-1}\varrho_{1}^{n+k-i}\tilde{h}_{y}

\displaystyle\geqslant\ (Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M^{k})_{y}-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\varrho^{k}\varrho^{n}_{1}\tilde{h}_{y}.

This implies that for
n
large enough, we have for any
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\frac{Z_{y}(n+k)}{\varrho^{n+k}}\ \geqslant\ \Big(\frac{Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{n}}\frac{M^{k}}{\varrho^{k}}\Big)_{y}-(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\Big(\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\varrho}\Big)^{n}\tilde{h}_{y},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Because
\varrho^{-k}M^{k}\to h\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
and
\varrho_{1}<\varrho
, it is not hard to see that for any
v\in\mathbb{B}_{h}
, we have
\liminf_{k}\varrho^{-k}(v^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M^{k})_{y}\geqslant(v^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}h\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}})_{y}=\langle v,h\rangle\tilde{h}_{y}
.
So by letting
k\to\infty
, we obtain that for
n
large enough,

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{Z_{y}(m)}{\varrho^{m}}\ \geqslant\ \frac{\langle Z(n),h\rangle}{\varrho^{n}}\tilde{h}_{y}\ =\ W_{n}\tilde{h}_{y},

and since the left-hand side does not depend on
n
, we derive simply letting
n\to\infty
that
\liminf_{m}\varrho^{-m}Z_{y}(m)\geqslant W\tilde{h}_{y}
. The conclusion holds again by applying Scheffé’s lemma for nonnegative functions, using the fact that
\sum_{y}W\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y}=W=\lim_{n}\sum_{y}\varrho^{-n}Z_{y}(n)h_{y}
.

(iii)-
2
: Entropy condition

The key will again be to show that for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\varrho^{-m}Z_{y}(m)\ \geqslant\ W\tilde{h}_{y},

(17)

and conclude as above.
Let us first justify that we can assume without loss of generality that the initial population
Z(0)
is finite.
In the general case, note that for any
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
, we can decompose
Z(n)=Z^{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(n)+Z^{\mathcal{X}_{0}^{c}}(n)
, where
(Z^{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(n))_{n\geqslant 0}
and
(Z^{\mathcal{X}_{0}^{c}}(n))_{n\geqslant 0}
are versions of the branching process started respectively from
Z(0)|_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}
and
Z(0)|_{\mathcal{X}^{c}_{0}}
.
Note that we can similarly write
W=W^{\mathcal{X}_{0}}+W^{\mathcal{X}_{0}^{c}}
with obvious definitions, and it is not hard to see that
W^{\mathcal{X}_{0}^{c}}\to 0
as
\mathcal{X}_{0}\uparrow\mathcal{X}
.
Therefore, if (
17
) is shown to hold for any finite initial population, then in the general case we have that for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\liminf_{m}\varrho^{-m}Z_{y}(m)\geqslant\sup_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}W^{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\tilde{h}_{y}=W\tilde{h}_{y}
, where the supremum is taken on finite
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
, and the proof will be complete.
From now on, we therefore assume that
Z(0)
is finite.

Recall the capped matrix
\bar{M}(n)
from (
14
), defined for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
From Lemma
17
, we have for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
that
Z(n+1)_{y}\geqslant(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\bar{M}(n))_{y}-\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}
almost surely for all
n
large enough, which we can rewrite as (note that each of the following terms is nonnegative)

\big(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M\big)_{y}\ \leqslant\ Z_{y}(n+1)+\big(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big(M-\bar{M}(n)\big)\big)_{y}+\varrho_{1}^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}.

Iterating the inequality above by multiplying by
M
on the right, we obtain that for
n
large enough, for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{(Z(n)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M^{k})_{y}}{\varrho^{n+k}}\ \leqslant\ \frac{Z_{y}(n+k)}{\varrho^{n+k}}\\
+\ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bigg(\frac{Z(n+i)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{n+i}}(M-\bar{M}(n+i))\frac{M^{k-i-1}}{\varrho^{k-i}}\bigg)_{y}+(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}\Big(\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\varrho}\Big)^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}.

(18)

We will first take the limit
k\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
in this inequality to obtain
\liminf_{m}\varrho^{-m}Z_{y}(m)\geqslant W\tilde{h}_{y}
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
.
As
\varrho_{1}<\varrho
, the term
(\varrho-\varrho_{1})^{-1}(\varrho_{1}/\varrho)^{n}\tilde{h}_{y}
disappears in the second limit
n\to\infty
, so the delicate term is the sum
R_{n,k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{Z(n+i)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{n+i}}\big(M-\bar{M}(n+i)\big)\frac{M^{k-i-1}}{\varrho^{k-i}}
.
First, note that

\lVert R_{n,k}\rVert_{h}\ =\ \langle R_{n,k},h\rangle\ =\ \frac{1}{\varrho}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{Z(n+i)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{n+i}}\big(M-\bar{M}(n+i)\big)h,

which is non-decreasing in

, and tends to

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lVert R_{n,k}\rVert_{h}\ \eqqcolon\ \mathcal{R}_{n}\ =\ \frac{1}{\varrho}\sum_{i\geqslant n}\frac{Z(i)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{i}}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)h.

Now
(\mathcal{R}_{n})
is nonincreasing, so to show that it converges almost surely to
0
it suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}_{n}]\to 0
.
Since we have assumed that
Z(0)
has a finite number of positive coordinates, there exists
\alpha>0
such that
Z(0)\leqslant\alpha\tilde{h}
holds coordinate-wise, which implies that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{y}(i)]=(Z(0)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}M^{i})_{y}\leqslant\alpha\varrho^{i}\tilde{h}_{y}
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
.
Using this, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{R}_{n}\big]\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{\varrho}\sum_{i\geqslant n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{Z(i)^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}{\varrho^{i}}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)h\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{\alpha}{\varrho}\sum_{i\geqslant n}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)h.

(19)

Let us show that this is the remainder of a convergent series,
i.e.
that the following converges:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)h\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\Big(\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)_{xy}\Big)h_{y}.

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\left(L^{(x)}_{y}-\varrho_{2}^{i}h_{y}\tilde{h}_{y}^{2}\right)_{+}\Big]h_{y}.

Now notice that for any

Therefore, we obtain

\sum_{i\geqslant 1}\tilde{h}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\big(M-\bar{M}(i)\big)h\ \leqslant\ \frac{1}{\log\varrho_{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}\log_{+}\big(L^{(x)}_{y}h_{y}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}\big)\Big]h_{y}.

It results that Condition (iii)-
2
in Theorem
6
implies the convergence of the series

, hence the fact that

almost surely.

This means
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\lVert R_{n,k}\rVert_{h}=0
almost surely, and so we can take the limits in (
18
) to establish
\liminf_{n}\varrho^{-n}Z(n)\geqslant W\tilde{h}
, and conclude as in the proof of (iii)-
1
.

It remains to retrieve (iii)-
2
from the conditions stated in the last part of Theorem
6
, namely: we assume Condition (
A
) and
-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}\log\big(\tilde{h}_{x}h_{x}\big)<\infty
.
Recalling from
\langle\tilde{h},h\rangle=1
that
-\log(\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y})\geqslant 0
for all

, we compute:

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}

\displaystyle\log_{+}\big(L^{(x)}_{y}h_{y}^{-1}\tilde{h}_{y}^{-2}\big)\Big]h_{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}\log_{+}\big(L^{(x)}_{y}h_{y}\big)\Big]h_{y}-2\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[L^{(x)}_{y}\log\big(\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y}\big)\Big]h_{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{x}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\langle L^{(x)},h\big\rangle\log_{+}\big\langle L^{(x)},h\big\rangle\Big]-2\varrho\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y}\log\big(\tilde{h}_{y}h_{y}\big),

5
Application to general models of epidemics

This final section is dedicated to the study of large-population
limits of some general discrete-time epidemics processes.

Motivation and related work

Individuals are often not equally infectious throughout the course of
an infection. Viral load, symptom severity, and behavioral changes
vary over time and lead to different transmission potentials at
different stages of the disease. Likewise, susceptibility to infection
may depend on the stage of infection of the transmitting individual,
reflecting differences in pathogen shedding, contact intensity, or
immune response. Incorporating such stage-dependent heterogeneity
allows the model to capture biological features. Thus, it is standard
in epidemic modeling to assume that the infectivity of an individual
depends on how long that individual has been infected, such that the
model is structured in age. We aim to describe the onset of an
epidemic in a non-Markov, varying-susceptibility epidemic model.

First consider a population of size
N\in\mathbb{N}
where
individuals are either
susceptible
or
infected
. The
epidemics process evolves in time: any individual
i
infected at time

t
carries a random point process

\mathcal{P}_{i}=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\delta_{t_{k}}
that describes its
infectious contacts after time
t
: for each
k
, at time
t+t_{k}
, an
individual is uniformly chosen in the population and becomes infected
if it was susceptible up to this time. Under the assumption that the

(\mathcal{P}_{i})
are i.i.d. and technical assumptions on their
distribution,
Barbour and Reinert, (2013)
showed a functional law of large numbers
for the size of the epidemics as a function of time, as
N\to\infty
,
and the limit is described by a general Kermack–McKendrick
integro-differential equation
(
Kermack and McKendrick,, 1927
)
. A similar result appears
in
Duchamps et al., (2023)
, where we relaxed technical
assumptions and considered the case of a varying contact rate function
modeling the effect of public intervention on the spread of the
epidemic. We refer to
Forien et al., (2022)

for a review of some Markovian and non-Markovian stochastic models of
epidemics. In these works, individuals remain infectious during
arbitrarily distributed periods of time, but there is no varying
susceptibility among the population. We propose a framework that
introduces some heterogeneity in the population – consider that
individuals have different behaviors regarding contacts with infected
individuals at different stages of the infection (for instance
healthcare workers may be more regularly in contact with advanced
stages of infections, and also may spread the infection differently
than non-healthcare workers). In the following framework, our aim is
to couple the beginning of the epidemics with a multi-type branching
process, which will allow us to exploit our main results.

An important part of these works consists in understanding the
beginning of the epidemics, which is generally approximated by a
Crump–Mode–Jagers process
(
Jagers and Sagitov,, 2008
)
. However, this
is not sufficient to describe the whole trajectory of the epidemics:
the study of a backwards-in-time process of potential
“infectors” is also important to study. Interestingly, the
backwards-in-time process in
Duchamps et al., (2023)
is more
regular than its forwards-in-time counterpart: the uniform choice of
infection implies there is additional independence when going back
through potential infectors.

In this application section, the heterogeneity we introduce in the
population will prevent this additional regularity in the
backwards-in-time process: both branching processes will be
countable-type branching processes in the large-population
approximation. We now describe the epidemics model through its
so-called infection graph
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, which in broad terms is the
weighted directed graph or directed network (weights are on the edges,
and we will in fact call them
lengths
) with vertex set

\{1,\dots,N\}
that is built by drawing an edge
i\to j
with length

x
if individual
i
would infect
j
at infection-age
x
. The
infection started from individual
1
is then described by the
subgraph composed of all vertices that are reachable from
1
.

The infection graph
\mathcal{G}_{N}
corresponding to this model is
effectively a “layering” of a countable number of directed
configuration models on
\{1,\dots,N\}
, where the edges have
integer-valued lengths. Shortest paths in configuration models with
edge lengths have been extensively studied
(
Bhamidi et al.,, 2010
;
Bhamidi et al.,, 2014
;
van der Hoorn and Olvera-Cravioto,, 2018
, see

e.g.
)
, but never in such a
layered setting where countable-type branching processes
emerge. Indeed, in order to study the distribution of geodesics (as

N\to\infty
) in this random graph, we need to understand the start of
the epidemics. Note that here we only focus on the branching
approximation, the study of geodesics in this model should be the
subject of future work.

Layered configuration model

We thus model the infection graph of the epidemic as
follows: we endow every individual
i\in[N]
with a pair of sequences

((D_{i}^{+}(x))_{x\geqslant 1},(D_{i}^{-}(x))_{x\geqslant 1})
;
D^{+}_{i}(x)

(resp.
D^{-}_{i}(x)
) is the type-
x
out-degree (resp. in-degree) of
vertex
i
, and independently for each
x\geqslant 1
a directed
configuration model is drawn using type-
x
in- and out-degrees. Each
edge formed by joining two type-
x
half-edges is assigned length
x
.
In this construction,
D^{+}_{i}(x)
represents the infectious contacts
that individual
i
makes at infection-age
x
, and
D^{-}_{i}(x)

represents the susceptibility of
i
to infectious contacts by
infected individuals with infection-age
x
. Similar models in
continuous-time (but without varying susceptibility) in

Duchamps et al., (2023)
are shown to encompass compartmental
models of epidemics.

Let us now make this construction more precise by introducing some
notation and some assumptions on the degree sequences. The directed
configuration model (DCM) is a model of random graph introduced
by
Bollobás, (1980)
, whose construction is best
described algorithmically by assigning directed half-edges (in the
following, we will call them
instubs
and
outstubs
for
short) to vertices and matching them uniformly at random (see
Figure
2
for an illustration). Let
us directly define our so-called layered version:

•

Let
(D^{+},D^{-})=((D^{+}(x))_{x\geqslant 1},(D^{-}(x))_{x\geqslant 1})=
be
a random variable taking values in the pairs of sequences of
non-negative integers, and let
(D^{+}_{i},\ D^{-}_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
be an
i.i.d. sequence of copies of
(D^{+},D^{-})
.

•

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\geqslant 1
, we attach to vertex

i
a number
D^{+}_{i}(x)
of outstubs of type
x
, and a number

D^{-}_{i}(x)
of instubs of type
x
.

•

For a fixed
N\geqslant 1
, we add a graveyard vertex denoted by

\dagger
, and such that for every type
x\in\mathbb{N}
the

x
-degree of
\dagger
correspond to the extra unmatched stubs.
That is,

D_{\dagger}^{+}(x)\ =\ \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}D^{+}_{i}(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}D^{-}_{i}(x)\Big)_{+},

and likewise for
D_{\dagger}^{-}(x)
. Note that we do not write the
dependence on
N
here to keep the notation short.

•

Independently for each
x\geqslant 1
, choose a uniform bipartite
matching of all
x
-instubs with all
x
-outstubs among those of
vertices indexed by
\{1,\dots,N,\dagger\}
. The directed
network
\mathcal{G}_{N}
is built by drawing a directed edge with
length
x
for each matched pair of
x
-stubs (the directed edge
goes from the vertex attached to the outstub and towards the vertex
attached to the matched instub).

Note that equivalently (and we will use this in the following), one
can realize this last step algorithmically as follows: pick an
arbitrary type
x
and an arbitrary unmatched
x
-instub or

x
-outstub; match it uniformly at random among the remaining
possibilities; repeat these steps until all stubs are matched (which
may take an infinite time as there may be countably many stubs to be
matched).

The DCM on
N=7
vertices. The right panel shows a possible outcome for the matching.

Note that
\mathcal{G}_{N}
may have loops and multi-edges, and also
vertices may have infinite total degrees. However, we are interested
in the lengths of directed paths in this graph: from any focal vertex

i\in[N]
and for any fixed radius
r\geqslant 1
, there are only finitely
many paths (succession of directed edges) of total length less than

r
in
\mathcal{G}_{N}
.

Recall that the directed paths in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{N}
model the
potential infections that may occur in a non-Markovian, discrete-time
epidemic with heterogeneous susceptibilities in the population. The
length of an edge
i\to j
is the time needed after the infection of

i
for
j
to become infected as a result of a contact with
i
. We
are typically interested in how the infection spreads from a focal
initially infected vertex, or conversely how it may reach this focal
vertex from potential sources of infection. By exchangeability, we use the vertex
indexed by
1
as the focal vertex. Writing
\vec{d}(i,j)
for the
minimal length of a directed path between two vertices
i
and
j
, we
define, for
t\geqslant 1
,

\mathcal{V}_{N}(t)\ \coloneqq\ \big\{i\in[N]:\ \vec{d}(1,i)\leqslant t\ \text{ or }\ \vec{d}(i,1)\leqslant t\big\}

the set of vertices that are at a directed distance of
1
that is
less than
t
in
\mathcal{G}_{N}
. We are interested in the rooted
decorated directed network
\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
whose vertex set is

\mathcal{V}_{N}(t)
, with a root indexed by
1
, whose edge set is that
induced by
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, and where vertices are additionally
decorated with their total number of in- and outstubs of all types.
More precisely, we consider rooted decorated directed networks up to
isomorphism,
i.e.
we forget about the labeling of vertices in

\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
. We refer to Figure
3
for an
illustration of the part of
\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
accessible from the
root.

Figure 3:

\mathcal{G}
from a
source vertex. To find vertices at distance
n+1
from the

x
that were added to the process at
time

n+1
, and those going backward from

We will be able to use Theorems
2

and
6
to describe the distribution of
potential infectious contacts at time
t_{N}=\Theta(\log N)
from the
focal vertex, as
N\to\infty
and under some additional assumptions.
Among those, we impose the following moment conditions:

\mathbb{E}\big[(D^{\pm}_{x})^{2}\big]\ <\ \infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}[D_{x}^{-}]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[D_{x}^{+}]\ =\ m_{x},\qquad x\geqslant 1.

(20)

In the following, we will let
\sigma_{x,\pm}^{2}
denote the variance of

D_{x}^{\pm}
.

The branching process approximation

We will exploit our results by coupling the neighborhood

\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
of the focal vertex in the infection graph

\mathcal{G}_{N}
with a similar neighborhood in a tree-like directed
network that is built from the
t
first steps of a specific
multi-type branching process. Let us first describe the tree using a
recursive random construction:

•

We first start with
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, composed of a single vertex
(the root) labeled by
1
and endowed with
D_{1}^{\pm}(x)
in- and
outstubs of type
x
, for all
x\geqslant 1
, where

(D_{1}^{\pm}(x))_{x\geqslant 1}
is a copy of
(D^{\pm}(x))_{x\geqslant 1}
.

•

For a given network
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, we build

\mathcal{T}_{k+1}
by “exploring” each type-
x
in- and outstub,
for each
x\geqslant 1
, in the following way. Replace each type-
x

outstub by a directed edge with length
x
going to a new vertex
u

equipped with a collection of stubs randomly drawn independently
of everything else with the distribution of
(D^{\pm}(x))_{x\geqslant 1}

biased by
D_{x}^{-}
,
i.e.
if
L^{+}_{x}
denotes such a random
variable, then it is characterized by: for every measurable bounded
function
f:(\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}})^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\big[f(L_{x}^{+})]\ =\ \frac{1}{m_{x}}\mathbb{E}\big[D^{-}_{x}f((D^{\pm})_{x\geqslant 1})\big].

(21)

One of the drawn type-
x
instubs is used as the head of the directed
edge going to
u
. The exploration of instubs, and in particular the
definition of
L^{-}_{x}
, is done in the obvious analogous way.

•

The rooted directed random network
\mathcal{T}
is the (unique)
network consisting of all added vertices and edges in such a
potentially infinite exploration procedure.

For
t\geqslant 1
, we define
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
similarly as

\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
, by keeping only the directed paths, going from or
going to
1
, of length less than or equal to
t
.

In order to state our result, we need to use an alternative
description of an age-structured branching process linked to

\mathcal{T}
. Let us introduce the countable state space

\mathcal{X}=\{(x,a):x\in\mathbb{N},\ a\leqslant x\}
and focus first
on outstubs. Let us say that an edge in
\mathcal{T}
is
born

at time

if it starts from a vertex

such that

\vec{d}(1,u)=s
. Then

Z^{+}(t)=(Z^{+}_{x,a}(t))_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}}
can be defined as
follows:

Z^{+}_{x,a}(t)\ =\ \text{number of edges in $\mathcal{T}$ with length $x$ born at time
}t-a+1.

Then by definition
(Z^{+}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is a countable-type
Galton–Watson process where any particle of type
(x,a)
with
a<x

always reproduce by giving birth to a single particle of type

(x,a+1)
, and particles of type
(x,x)
reproduce by giving birth to

L_{x,y}^{+}
particles of type
(y,1)
for each

, where

is the biased random variable given
by (
21
). We can see

as a branching
process of stubs (or potential infectious contacts) describing the
spread of the epidemic. We define
Z^{-}
in the obvious analogous way.
When discussing the type
(x,a)
of a stub, we say that

is the age
of the stub.

To apply our results to the
Z^{\pm}
processes, we must make some

M^{+},M^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}^{2}}

\displaystyle M^{\pm}_{(x,a),(y,a^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\ \mathds{1}_{x=y,a^{\prime}=a+1},\qquad a<x

\displaystyle M^{+}_{(x,x),(y,1)}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{m_{x}}\mathbb{E}[D^{-}_{x}D^{+}_{y}]

\displaystyle M^{-}_{(x,x),(y,1)}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{m_{x}}\mathbb{E}[D^{+}_{x}D^{-}_{y}].

Let us assume that

M^{+}\text{ satisfies Assumption~\ref{ass:recurrence} and is positive recurrent},

(23)

and denote by

and

Then, defining

and

using the remaining symmetry between

\tilde{h}^{-}

We will assume a technical condition involving the quantities related
to our problem. Recall a nonnegative sequence

grows subexponentially if it satisfies (

With these assumptions, one can define the additive martingales

, let us
define

X^{+}(t)=(X^{+}_{x,a}(t))_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{X}}

.

Under the

E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t})
the number of length-
x

edges in
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
,
S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t})
the sum of
type-
x
degrees, i.e. the number of type-
x
stubs and
half-edges in
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
, and where the random variables

({K}_{N}^{x})_{x\geqslant 1}
are independent of
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
and with
distribution given by

K^{x}_{N}\ =\ \max\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}D^{+}_{i}(x),\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}D^{-}_{i}(x)\Big).

(29)

Proof.

Let us start with two important technical remarks before trying to
couple exactly
\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
. First
note that in these objects, by definition there are no edges with
length greater than
t
. Therefore when doing computations for fixed

t
, we may fix an arbitrary cutoff value
T>t
and couple the
modifications of the rooted decorated directed networks obtained by
removing all stubs with type
x\geqslant T
. It will turn out that the total
variation bound we get does not depend on
T
, so letting

T\to\infty
, we will get the same total variation bound between the
unmodified networks
\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
.
Removing large stubs boils down to considering random variables

(D^{\pm}(x))_{1\leqslant x\leqslant T}
taking values in a discrete space,
which simplifies total variation computations.

Secondly, the exact computations that follow are much easier if we add
some combinatorial structure on the networks, namely an
ordering
of stubs and edges
in the following way. For each vertex
v
, and
type
x
, we order the finite set of type-
x
outstubs and outgoing
edges attached to
v
with a uniformly chosen total order; the same is
done for the set of type-
x
instubs and incoming edges. Let us denote
by
\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t}
and

\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t}
the
ordered
rooted decorated
directed networks we obtain; for simplicity, let us call these
combinatorial objects
configurations
. We will additionally
denote by
\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}
and

\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}
the configurations with type-
x

stubs removed when
x\geqslant T
.

For a fixed configuration
\mathcal{C}
with
k
vertices, in the
following let us fix an arbitrary labeling of vertices by integers
from
1
to
k
, such that
1
always denotes the root. We write

d_{i}^{\pm}=(d_{i}^{\pm}(x))_{x\geqslant 1}
for the in- or outdegrees of vertex

i
(counting type-
x
edges and stubs). Let us write

\mathscr{C}_{t,T}
for the set of configurations
\mathcal{C}
that
are without loops (
i.e.
tree-like) and
(a) are compatible with our notion of restriction at distance
t
in the
sense that
\mathcal{C}|_{t}=\mathcal{C}
, and (b) have no edges and
stubs with type or length
x\geqslant T
.
In other words,
\mathscr{C}_{t,T}
is such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}\in\mathscr{C}_{t,T}\big)\ =\ 1.

These technical definitions finally allow for easy computations.
Indeed, for instance when a type-
x
outstub attached to a vertex
u

is explored in the definition of
\mathcal{T}
, the stubs attached to
the new vertex
v
are distributed as
L^{+}_{x}
and the rank of the
instub attached to
v
is uniform among the
d_{v}^{-}(x)
possibilities.
As a result, the probability to see a fixed degree distribution

(d_{v}^{\pm}(y))_{1\leqslant y\leqslant T}
and fixed ordering of the stubs of
v
is

\frac{d_{v}^{-}(x)}{m_{x}}\mathbb{P}\big((D^{\pm}(y))_{1\leqslant y<T}=(d_{v}^{\pm}(y))_{1\leqslant y\leqslant T}\big)\times\frac{1}{d_{v}^{-}(x)}\ =\ \frac{1}{m_{x}}p_{T}(d_{v}^{\pm}),

where we write for conciseness

p_{T}(\,\cdot\,)\ \coloneqq\ \mathbb{P}\big((D^{\pm}(x))_{1\leqslant x<T}=(\,\cdot\,)_{1\leqslant x<T}\big).

Exchanging the role of
+
and
-
reveals the same computation for
the exploration of instubs. As a result, using the branching structure
that defines
\mathcal{T}
, we simply get, for any

\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{t,T}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\ =\ p_{T}(d^{\pm}_{1})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e:u\rightarrow v\\
\text{or }u\leftarrow v\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m_{x_{e}}}p_{T}(d^{\pm}_{v}),

(30)

where the notation
e:u\to v
means that we are considering edges
directed away from the root, going from
u
to
v
, and

e:u\leftarrow v
means that we consider edges directed towards the
root, going from
v
to
u
. In both cases
x_{e}
denotes the length of
the edge.

The computation of

\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C})
is
slightly more complex, and relies mainly on the fact that the
probability that
k
given matches are present in a uniform bipartite
matching with
K
total matches is simply

\frac{1}{(K)_{k}}\ =\ \frac{1}{K(K-1)\cdots(K-k+1)}\ \geqslant\ \frac{1}{K^{k}}.

Now recall that
\mathcal{G}_{N}
is built from matching in- and
outstubs. Therefore, let us write
K^{x}_{N}
for the total number of
matches of type-
x
stubs needed to build
\mathcal{G}_{N}
,

i.e.
we define
K^{x}_{N}
by (
29
).
For a fixed configuration
\mathcal{C}
with
k\leqslant N
vertices
labeled arbitrarily from
1
to
k
, let us denote by

A_{\mathcal{C}}
the event

A_{\mathcal{C}}\ \coloneqq\ \big\{\forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant k,\ D^{\pm}_{i}=d^{\pm}_{i}\big\},

where, as above,
(d^{\pm}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}
denote the degree
sequences of the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
. We claim that

\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\ =\ (N-1)_{k-1}\,\cdot\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{T}(d^{\pm}_{i})\,\cdot\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{x=1}^{t}\frac{1}{(K_{N}^{x})_{E_{x}}}\ \Big|\ A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big],

(31)

where
E_{x}=E_{x}(\mathcal{C})
denotes the number of length-
x
edges in
\mathcal{C}
.
To obtain this, we argue that:

•

The first factor counts the number of ways to label every vertex
except from the root in configuration
\mathcal{C}
with integers
from
2
to
N
.

•

The second factor is the probability to assign exactly the right
degree sequences to these
k
fixed vertices (recall that in the
construction of
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, vertices are equipped with degree
sequences in an i.i.d. way).

•

The third factor is the probability that to obtain the partial
matchings given by the edges of
\mathcal{C}
in each of the
configuration models defining
\mathcal{G}_{N}
.

The key is that, when
\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{t,T}
we may
use (
30
), (
31
) and the fact that
k-1
is the number
of edges to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{T}(d^{\pm}_{i})\,\cdot\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{e\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{N-k+1}{K_{N}^{x_{e}}}\ \Big|\ A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big],

\displaystyle=\ \mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\,\cdot\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{e\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{(N-k+1)m_{x_{e}}}{K_{N}^{x_{e}}}\ \Big|\ A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big],

(32)

where the product over
e\in\mathcal{C}
is a slight abuse of
notation for the product over the edges of
\mathcal{C}
, that we
wrote before
e:i\to j
or
e:j\to i
.

From now on we will use sums over
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathscr{C}_{t,T}

so to avoid confusion, we denote by
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
the number of
vertices of a configuration. We exploit the following form of the
distance in total variation:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T},\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}\big)\ \leqslant\ \sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{t,T},\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leqslant N}\big(\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C})-\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C})\big)_{+}+\mathbb{P}\big(\big|\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}\big|>N\big).

As the last term does not depend on
T
nor on the ordering of

\mathcal{T}
, we recover the first term on the right-hand side
of (
28
). We now deal with the sum.
Using (
32
) and the inequality

(1-\prod_{i}a_{i})_{+}\leqslant 1\wedge\big(\sum_{i}(1-a_{i})_{+})
for any
family of nonnegative terms
a_{i}
(this is readily checked by
induction), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{t,T},\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leqslant N}\big(\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C})-\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{N}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C})\big)_{+}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\ \sum_{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\leqslant N}\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(1-\prod_{e\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{Nm_{x_{e}}(1-(|\mathcal{C}|-1)/N)}{K_{N}^{x_{e}}-\kappa^{x_{e}}_{e}}\Big)_{+}\,\Big|\,A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\ \sum_{|\mathcal{C}|\leqslant N}\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\mathbb{E}\Big[1\wedge\bigg(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{C}}\Big(1-\frac{Nm_{x_{e}}}{K_{N}^{x_{e}}}\Big)_{+}+\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{N}\bigg)\,\Big|\,A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\ \sum_{|\mathcal{C}|\leqslant N}\mathbb{P}\big(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}=\mathcal{C}\big)\,\mathbb{E}\Big[1\wedge\bigg(\sum_{x\leqslant t}E_{x}(\mathcal{C})\Big(\Big(1-\frac{Nm_{x}}{K_{N}^{x}}\Big)_{+}+\frac{1}{N}\Big)\bigg)\,\Big|\,A_{\mathcal{C}}\Big],

(33)

where we recall that
E_{x}(\mathcal{C})
denotes the number of edges of
length
x
in
\mathcal{C}
. In order to remove the conditioning
in (
33
), we bound
K_{N}^{x}
using a copy of itself that
does not involve vertices in
\mathcal{C}
: on the event

A_{\mathcal{C}}
, for all
x\leqslant t
,

K_{N}^{x}\ \leqslant\ S_{x}(\mathcal{C})+\tilde{K}_{N}^{x},\qquad\text{with
}\tilde{K}_{N}^{x}\ \coloneqq\ \bigg(\sum_{i=\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert+1}^{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert+N}D^{+}_{i}(x)\bigg)\vee\bigg(\sum_{i=\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert+1}^{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert+N}D^{-}_{i}(x)\bigg),

where
S_{x}(\mathcal{C})
is defined by

S_{x}(\mathcal{C})\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}}(d_{u}^{+}(x)+d_{u}^{-}(x)).

Since for
a,b>0
, we have

(1-1/(a+b))_{+}\leqslant(a-1+b)_{+}\leqslant(a-1)_{+}+b
, it
follows that

\Big(1-\frac{Nm_{x}}{K_{N}^{x}}\Big)_{+}\ \leqslant\ \Big(\frac{\tilde{K}_{N}^{x}}{Nm_{x}}-1\Big)_{+}+\frac{S_{x}(\mathcal{C})}{Nm_{x}}.

Plugging this, we can bound (
33
) by

\mathbb{E}\bigg[1\wedge\bigg(\sum_{x\leqslant t}E_{x}(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T})\bigg(\Big(\frac{\tilde{K}_{N}^{x}}{Nm_{x}}-1\Big)_{+}+\frac{S_{x}(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T})}{N}\Big(\frac{1}{m_{x}}+1\Big)\bigg)\bigg],

(34)

where
\mathcal{T}
is independent of the variables

(\tilde{K}_{N}^{x})_{x\geqslant 1}
. Note that

E_{x}(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T})
and

S_{x}(\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T})
do not depend on
T
nor on the
ordering of edges and stubs, so
\overline{\mathcal{T}}|_{t,T}
may be
simply replaced by
\mathcal{T}|_{t}
. We have shown a total variation
bound on the
ordered
versions of
\mathcal{G}_{N}|_{t}
and

\mathcal{T}|_{t}
, and removing the orders may only decrease this
distance, so this concludes the proof of the lemma.

Lemma 21
.

For all
x,N\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{E}[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})]\ \leqslant\ \Big(\sup_{y\leqslant t_{N}}\big(h^{-}_{y,y}\wedge h^{+}_{y,y}\big)^{-1}\Big)\frac{\varrho^{t_{N}+2}}{\varrho-1}\Big(\tilde{h}^{+}_{x,1}+\tilde{h}^{-}_{x,1}+m_{x}\sum_{x\geqslant 1}(\tilde{h}^{+}_{x,x}+\tilde{h}^{-}_{x,x})\\
+\frac{\sigma_{x,+}}{\varrho}\sum_{y\leqslant t_{N}}h_{y,y}^{-}\sigma_{y,+}+\frac{\sigma_{x,-}}{\varrho}\sum_{y\leqslant t_{N}}h_{y,y}^{+}\sigma_{y,-}\Big),

(35)

and

\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\Big]\ \leqslant\ \Big(\sup_{y\leqslant t_{N}}\big(h^{-}_{y,y}\wedge h^{+}_{y,y}\big)^{-1}\Big)\frac{\varrho^{t_{N}+2}}{\varrho-1}(h^{-}_{x,x}+h^{+}_{x,x}).

(36)

Hence under Assumption (
26
), the subexponential bound follows:

\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\varrho^{-t_{N}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\big[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})\big]\Big)\ \leqslant\ \mathrm{e}^{o(t_{N})}.

(37)

Proof.

Let us come back to the branching process to count the stubs in

\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}}
. Let us focus on the
x
-instubs. Those that are
connected to a vertex
v
that may reach the root (
i.e.

such that
\vec{d}(v,1)\leqslant t_{N}
) are simply counted by

\sum_{t\leqslant t_{N}}Z^{-}_{x,1}(t).

To count those that are connected to a vertex
v
reachable from the
root, we first count those vertices and then use the branching
property: given that the edge pointing to
v
is of type
y
, the mean
number of
x
-instubs is by definition given by

1/m_{y}\mathbb{E}[D_{y}^{-}D_{x}^{-}]
. A similar reasoning holds for

x
-outstubs, so after some minor factoring, we get

\mathbb{E}\big[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})\big]\ =\ \sum_{t\leqslant t_{N}}\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{+}_{x,1}(t)+Z^{-}_{x,1}(t)\big]\\
+\sum_{t\leqslant t_{N}}\sum_{y\leqslant t_{N}}\frac{1}{m_{y}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{+}_{y,y}(t)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[D_{y}^{-}D_{x}^{-}]+\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{-}_{y,y}(t)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[D_{y}^{+}D_{x}^{+}]\Big).

(38)

To bound the
Z^{\pm}
terms, we may use the properties of the
matrices
M^{\pm}
. Indeed, recall that

\mathbb{E}[Z^{\pm}_{x,a}(0)]=m_{x}\mathds{1}_{a=1}
for all

(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}
. By the age structure of the branching
process
Z^{+}
, we have for all
t\leqslant t_{N}
and
(y,b)\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{+}_{y,b}(t)\big]\

\displaystyle=\ \Big((m_{x}\mathds{1}_{\{a=1\}})_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}}\times(M^{+})^{t}\Big)_{y,b}

\displaystyle=\ \Big((m_{x}\mathds{1}_{\{a=1,x\leqslant t_{N}\}})_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}}\times(M^{+})^{t}\Big)_{y,b},

where
\times
denotes matrix multiplication.
Note that

m_{x}\mathds{1}_{\{a=1,x\leqslant t_{N}\}}\ \leqslant\ \alpha_{N}\tilde{h}^{+}_{x,a}\wedge\tilde{h}^{-}_{x,a},\qquad(x,a)\in\mathcal{X},

with

\alpha_{N}^{-1}\ =\ \inf_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\frac{\tilde{h}^{+}_{x,1}\wedge\tilde{h}^{-}_{x,1}}{m_{x}}\ =\ \varrho^{-1}\inf_{x\leqslant t_{N}}h^{-}_{x,x}\wedge h^{+}_{x,x},

where we used the relations (
25
). Therefore, we get

\mathbb{E}[Z^{+}_{y,b}(t)]\leqslant\alpha_{N}\varrho^{t}\tilde{h}^{+}_{y,b}

(and a similar bound for
Z^{-}
), so plugging this
into (
38
), we get

\mathbb{E}[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})]\ \leqslant\ \alpha_{N}\frac{\varrho^{t_{N}+1}}{\varrho-1}\Big(\tilde{h}^{+}_{x,1}+\tilde{h}^{-}_{x,1}+\sum_{y\leqslant t_{N}}\frac{1}{m_{y}}\Big(\tilde{h}^{+}_{y,y}\mathbb{E}[D_{y}^{-}D_{x}^{-}]+\tilde{h}^{-}_{y,y}\mathbb{E}[D_{y}^{+}D_{x}^{+}]\Big)\Big).

We now use the straightforward bound

\mathbb{E}[D^{\pm}_{x}D^{\pm}_{y}]\ \leqslant\ m_{x}m_{y}+\sigma_{x,\pm}\sigma_{y,\pm},

where we recall that
\sigma^{2}_{x,\pm}
denotes the variance of

D^{\pm}_{x}
, to get (
35
) after some simplification.
The inequality (
36
) is obtained by similar
computations which we leave to the reader. Note that
\sum_{x\geqslant 1}\tilde{h}^{\pm}_{x,x}
is finite by (
27
), and
that by (
25
), we can rewrite
\tilde{h}^{\pm}_{x,1}=\varrho^{-1}m_{x}h^{\mp}_{x,x}
.
Finally (
37
) is obtained by noting that our
bounds divided by
\varrho^{t_{N}}
are obtained from sequences growing
subexponentially – by the
assumption (
26
) – only through
operations preserving the property of subexponential growth (sums,
products, partial sums and supremums).

We can now combine Lemmas
20

and
21
to show Proposition
19
.

Proof of Proposition
19
.

We now take
t
to equal
t_{N}
defined as in
Theorem
18
, and show
that (
28
) tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
. First let
us tackle the second term on the right-hand side. Because
of the bound by
1
inside the expectation, it suffices to show that
the random variable

\sum_{x\leqslant t}\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t})}{m_{x}}\bigg(\Big(\frac{{K}_{N}^{x}}{N}-m_{x}\Big)_{+}+\frac{S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t})}{N}(m_{x}+1)\bigg)

(39)

goes to
0
in probability.

For the first part, recalling that
\sigma_{x,\pm}^{2}
is the
variance of
D^{\pm}_{x}
, we can crudely bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{\tilde{K}_{N}^{x}}{N}-m_{x}\Big)_{+}\Big]\

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{D^{+}_{i}(x)}{N}-m_{x}\Big|+\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{D^{-}_{i}(x)}{N}-m_{x}\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(\sigma_{x,+}+\sigma_{x,-}).

Putting the last display together with (
36
) yields

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{K}_{N}^{x}}{N}-m_{x}\Big)_{+}\bigg]\ =\ O\bigg(\frac{\varrho^{t_{N}}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}(\sigma_{x,+}+\sigma_{x,-})\frac{h^{+}_{x,x}+h^{-}_{x,x}}{\inf_{y\leqslant t_{N}}h^{-}_{y,y}\wedge h^{+}_{y,y}}\bigg),

which tends to
0
by (
26
)
because
\varrho^{t_{N}}=O(N^{\eta})
, where
\eta<1/2
. For the second part
of (
34
), we first take square roots before taking the expectation and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}

\displaystyle\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\frac{S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{N}(m_{x}+1)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[1+\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\bigg(\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})(m_{x}+1)\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg(1+\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})]^{\frac{1}{2}}(m_{x}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg(1+\bigg(\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})}{m_{x}}\Big]\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{x\leqslant t_{N}}\mathbb{E}[S_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})(m_{x}+1)]\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg).

Using (
37
) and the fact that

\varrho^{t_{N}}=O(N^{\eta})
with
\eta<1/2
, this tends to
0
as

N\to\infty
. Therefore, we have shown that the random
variable (
39
) converges to
0
in probability, which implies that the second term on the right-hand side
of (
28
) vanishes. Bounding

\lvert\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}}\rvert-1
by the sum of the

E_{x}(\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}})
, similar computations easily yield

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{T}|_{t_{N}}|>N)\to 0
, so the proof is complete.
