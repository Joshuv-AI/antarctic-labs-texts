Molecular Characterisation of Treacher Collins Syndrome in a South African Cohort: Novel Disease‐Causing Variants in TCOF1 and POLR1D
Authors: Patracia Nevondwe, Maria Mudau, Heather Seymour, Robyn Kerr, Zané Lombard, Amanda Krause, Nadia Carstens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTreacher Collins syndrome (TCS) is a rare craniofacial disorder characterised by variable expressivity. It is caused by pathogenic variants in the TCOF1, POLR1D, POLR1C, or POLR1B genes. Common clinical features include hypoplasia of the zygomatic complex and mandible, downward‐slanting palpebral fissures, lower eyelid anomalies, microtia, and hearing loss. Owing to its phenotypic overlap with other craniofacial syndromes, molecular testing is essential for establishing an accurate diagnosis and guiding effective clinical management.MethodsTen South African patients with a suspected clinical diagnosis of TCS underwent targeted next‐generation sequencing (NGS) using a custom gene panel including TCOF1, POLR1C, and POLR1D genes. Variants were classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines, with validation by Sanger sequencing where necessary.ResultsDisease‐causing variants were identified in six of the ten patients (60%). These included five heterozygous variants in TCOF1 and one homozygous variant in POLR1D. Notably, five of the six variants were identified for the first time in this study. Additionally, a recurrent TCOF1 deletion was identified for the first time in an African family.ConclusionThis study expands the mutational spectrum of TCS in general and provides African data in particular. Findings support the use of panel‐based NGS for diagnosis in resource‐limited settings and highlight the need for population‐specific variant data to improve diagnostic accuracy, guide clinical care, and support genetic counselling for affected individuals and their families.Keywords: African population, POLR1D, targeted next‐generation sequencing, TCOF1, Treacher Collins syndrome

Introduction

Treacher Collins syndrome (TCS; TCS1 [OMIM 154500], TCS2 [OMIM 613717], TCS3 [OMIM 248390], TCS4 [OMIM 618939]) is a rare congenital mandibulofacial dysostosis with an incidence ranging from 1 in 25,000 to 1 in 50,000 live births (Fazen et al. 1967; Jones et al. 1975; Posnick and Ruiz 2000). Clinical features are typically evident at birth or during early infancy and include down‐slanting palpebral fissures, midface and mandibular hypoplasia, eyelid colobomas, cleft palate, microtia, and conductive hearing loss (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021). Some individuals also present with extracraniofacial anomalies, including congenital heart defects, renal abnormalities, and limb or thumb malformations (Beaumont et al. 2021). These features contribute to substantial medical complexity, often requiring coordinated multidisciplinary care, and place significant psychosocial burdens on affected individuals and their families (Smith et al. 2021). Phenotypic severity is highly variable, ranging from mild, potentially underdiagnosed cases with subtle dysmorphism to severe forms associated with feeding and airway compromise and, in rare instances, neonatal mortality (Thompson et al. 2009). There is no curative therapy, and management is supportive, focusing on improving function and quality of life through staged craniofacial reconstruction, hearing rehabilitation, and long‐term multidisciplinary care.

Four molecular subtypes of TCS are recognised, defined by pathogenic variants in TCOF1 (TCS1), POLR1D (TCS2), POLR1C (TCS3), and POLR1B (TCS4). These genes encode components of the ribosomal RNA transcription machinery, primarily associated with RNA polymerase I and III complexes, and are critical for ribosome biogenesis and normal craniofacial development. Disruption of these pathways impairs ribosomal RNA production during embryogenesis, leading to reduced proliferation and survival of cranial neural crest cells, which are essential for craniofacial morphogenesis. Most cases (63%–93%) result from heterozygous TCOF1 variants (Splendore et al. 2000; Vincent et al. 2016). POLR1C and POLR1D variants together account for fewer than 10% of cases, while POLR1B variants are rare (~1.8%) (Sanchez et al. 2020; Vincent et al. 2016). Inheritance patterns differ across genes: TCOF1 and POLR1B variants are autosomal dominant, POLR1C variants are autosomal recessive, and POLR1D variants may follow either mode of inheritance.

Clinical diagnosis is often complicated by the syndrome's variable expressivity and overlap with other craniofacial disorders, including mandibulofacial dysostosis with microcephaly, branchio‐oto‐renal syndrome (OMIM 113650), Miller syndrome (OMIM 263750), and Nager syndrome (OMIM 154400). This overlap is particularly problematic in mildly affected individuals, making molecular testing essential for accurate diagnosis, appropriate management, and informed genetic counselling. Consequently, molecular confirmation plays a central role in guiding clinical care and enabling accurate recurrence‐risk assessment. However, access to such testing remains restricted in many low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including South Africa, where public healthcare systems often lack the infrastructure and funding needed to support comprehensive clinical and genetic services (Kamp et al. 2021; Kromberg et al. 2013). Within this context, we report on 10 South African patients suspected of TCS, of whom six received a molecular diagnosis following targeted NGS of the TCOF1, POLR1C, and POLR1D genes.

Patients and Methods

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of the Witwatersrand (M170761). Ten patients with a suspected clinical diagnosis of TCS were recruited from the Genetics Clinic of the Division of Human Genetics, National Health Laboratory Service (NHLS) and the University of the Witwatersrand, South Africa. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified salting‐out method (Miller et al. 1988). Eight patients were sequenced using a custom Agilent SureSelect assay on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Mudau et al. 2024), and two patients were sequenced using a custom Ion Torrent AmpliSeq assay on the Thermo Fisher Ion Torrent S5 platform (Carstens et al. 2026). Both sequencing techniques employed in this study targeted the coding regions and intron‐exon boundaries of the known TCS causative genes: TCOF1, POLR1C, POLR1D.

Sequencing reads from both platforms were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 human reference genome. Data from the eight patients sequenced on the Illumina platform were processed using Agilent SureCall (SureCall 4.0), while data from the two patients sequenced on the Ion Torrent platform were analysed using the Ion Torrent Suite (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Variant coordinates were annotated using the NM_015972.4 (POLR1D) and NM_001371623.1 (TCOF1) mRNA reference sequences, and interpretation followed ACMG/AMP guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Sanger sequencing validation was performed selectively, in accordance with previously published internal criteria (Louw et al. 2026). The primers used for Sanger sequencing validation are provided in Table S1. Variants were selected for confirmation if they demonstrated lower‐confidence NGS metrics, including reduced read depth, low base quality scores, strand bias, or localisation within homopolymer or low‐complexity regions. Variants meeting high‐confidence NGS quality thresholds were not routinely validated by Sanger sequencing.

Results

Clinical and Molecular Results

This study recruited 10 unrelated patients with a clinical suspicion of TCS between 2017 and 2021 (Table 1). Targeted sequencing of the TCOF1, POLR1C, and POLR1D genes identified causal variants in six patients (60%) (Table 2). Among the six molecularly confirmed cases, TCOF1 variants predominated, consistent with the known genetic spectrum of TCS, while a homozygous POLR1D variant was identified in one case. Notably, five of the six variants are novel, while one TCOF1 deletion represents a recurrent pathogenic variant previously reported in the literature (Horiuchi et al. 2005; Lamzouri et al. 2024). These findings expand the variant spectrum of TCS in this population. Below, we describe the clinical features and genetic findings of the six patients with molecularly confirmed diagnoses.

FRASC13

A three‐year‐old female presented with malar and mandibular hypoplasia, microtia, and lower eyelid coloboma. No family history of TCS was reported, suggesting that the variant may have arisen de novo, although parental testing was not performed. Targeted sequencing identified a heterozygous TCOF1 variant, c.3183G > C (p. Gln1061His), affecting the final nucleotide of an exon. In silico analysis predicted disruption of the canonical donor splice site and activation of a cryptic donor Site 7 bp downstream (SpliceAI donor gain = 0.89; donor loss = 0.47), suggesting aberrant splicing and a potential loss‐of‐function effect. The variant was absent from population databases and had a high deleteriousness score (CADD = 35). Based on ACMG/AMP criteria (PVS1_Supporting, PM6, PM2, PP3), the variant was classified as likely pathogenic.

FRASC26

A 13‐year‐old female presented with ptosis, lower eyelid coloboma, malar hypoplasia, mandibular hypoplasia, low‐set ears, microtia, external auditory canal atresia, and sensorineural hearing loss. Her mother and two siblings also exhibited features suggestive of TCS, with a broader maternal family history of affected individuals. A recurrent pathogenic five‐base‐pair deletion in TCOF1, c.4372_4376delAAGAA (p. Lys1458GlufsTer12), was detected. This variant has been reported in multiple patients with TCS (Horiuchi et al. 2005; Pan et al. 2021; Splendore et al. 2002).

FRASC57

A one‐year‐old male presented with malar hypoplasia, mandibular hypoplasia, lower‐lid coloboma, external ear canal atresia, lateral cheek hair projection, microtia, and gastro‐oesophageal reflux. No family history of TCS was reported. A single‐base‐pair deletion in exon 23 of TCOF1, c.3711delC (p. Ala1238fs) was detected. This variant lies within a known mutational hotspot for TCS (Dauwerse et al. 2011).

FRASC28

A seven‐year‐old male presented with malar and mandibular hypoplasia, low‐set ears, ear tags, and hearing loss. Molecular analysis identified a homozygous deletion in POLR1D, c.261delA (p. Gly88AlafsTer13). Parental segregation analysis was not performed due to limited follow‐up feasibility. However, no clinical features suggestive of TCS were reported in either parent. Based on ACMG/AMP criteria, the variant was classified as pathogenic.

IDP117

The patient presented with mild mandibulofacial dysostosis, including mandibular hypoplasia, malar hypoplasia, lower eyelid coloboma, prominent eyes, and low‐set malformed ears. No cleft palate was observed. Maternal testing was negative, while paternal testing was not performed. Molecular analysis revealed a likely pathogenic variant in TCOF1, c.158G > A (p. Trp53Ter).

IDP115

A 12‐year‐old female attending a school for the deaf presented with malar hypoplasia with down‐slanting palpebral fissures, bilateral zygomatic clefts, mandibular hypoplasia, bilateral Grade 3 microtia with aural atresia, and bilateral lower eyelid colobomas. No cleft palate or limb abnormalities were observed. A frameshift deletion in TCOF1, c.3754delC (p. Gln1252ArgfsTer56), was identified and classified as likely pathogenic.

Overall, molecularly confirmed cases were characterised by typical TCS craniofacial features and predominantly truncating TCOF1 variants, with a high proportion of previously unreported variants. All causative variants identified in this study are summarised in Table 2.

Discussion

TCS is a rare craniofacial disorder with significant phenotypic overlap with other mandibulofacial dysostoses, often making a specific clinical diagnosis difficult without molecular confirmation (Trainor et al. 2009). In this study, the use of an expanded targeted gene panel enabled the molecular characterisation of six South African patients with clinically suspected TCS. We identified four novel TCOF1 variants, a well‐recognised, recurrent TCOF1 deletion not previously reported in an African family, and a rare homozygous frameshift variant in POLR1D.

A molecular diagnosis is particularly valuable in TCS because of the marked variable expressivity associated with pathogenic variants in genes such as TCOF1 and POLR1D. Identifying the causal variant allows accurate recurrence risk counselling, including distinguishing families in which parents are unaffected and therefore have a low recurrence risk from those in which a parent is affected and faces a 50% recurrence risk, albeit with no reliable prediction of phenotypic severity.

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in TCOF1 and in POLR1D are typically associated with autosomal dominant inheritance and variable expressivity, whereas biallelic POLR1D variants result in an autosomal recessive form of the disorder. Heterozygous POLR1D variants cause autosomal dominant TCS via haploinsufficiency, whereas biallelic loss‐of‐function variants result in autosomal recessive disease. The homozygous frameshift identified here is therefore consistent with a dosage‐dependent recessive mechanism rather than locus‐specific mutational effects. Accurate distinction between these inheritance patterns is critical for providing recurrence risks, offering appropriate carrier testing to family members, and supporting informed reproductive decision‐making. This finding also serves to illustrate the interpretive challenges posed by genes such as POLR1D, in which pathogenic variants can act via both autosomal dominant and recessive mechanisms. In these cases, the interpretation of heterozygous variants requires careful consideration of zygosity, variant type, segregation data, population frequency, and phenotypic concordance, as assumptions based solely on gene–disease association may lead to misclassification of variants and inaccurate counselling.

Early molecular confirmation has direct implications for patient care. Individuals with TCS commonly experience conductive hearing loss, feeding challenges, and airway compromise, all of which benefit from early recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary intervention (Kadakia et al. 2014). Importantly, in our resource‐constrained public healthcare setting, establishing a molecular diagnosis reduced the need for unnecessary or prolonged diagnostic investigations, thereby streamlining patient care and reducing the diagnostic burden on both patients and the healthcare system. Therefore, in settings where access to comprehensive genomic testing is limited, a targeted gene panel represents a practical and cost‐effective approach for resolving suspected TCS cases and reducing the diagnostic odyssey experienced by many families.

An additional contribution of this study is the expansion of TCS variant data in an underrepresented population. African genomes are underrepresented in global reference datasets, often leading to uncertain or misclassified variants (Lumaka et al. 2022; Yildiz et al. 2025). By characterising novel variants in a South African cohort and contributing them to public databases, we strengthen the interpretability of TCS variants and support more equitable genomic care.

A subset of patients in the cohort did not have an identifiable molecular cause despite presenting with features clinically suggestive of TCS. Most of these individuals exhibited classical TCS features, including malar and mandibular hypoplasia, microtia, and external auditory canal atresia, with some also presenting with lower eyelid coloboma. However, in certain cases, additional or atypical features were observed, including renal anomalies and limb abnormalities, raising the possibility of phenotypic overlap with other craniofacial syndromes. The absence of a molecular diagnosis may reflect limitations of the targeted gene panel, including inability to detect copy number variants (CNVs), deep intronic or regulatory variants, or involvement of genes not included in the assay.

A limitation of this study is the absence of systematic CNV analysis, as deletions and duplications are recognised causes of TCS (Bowman et al. 2012). Undetected CNVs may therefore account for a proportion of unresolved cases, highlighting the importance of incorporating robust CNV detection in future studies to capture the full molecular spectrum in this population (Louw et al. 2023). In addition, short‐read targeted sequencing does not capture deep intronic or regulatory variation. Recent evidence has demonstrated that deep intronic retrotransposon insertions within TCOF1 can disrupt splicing and cause disease, which may be missed by standard short‐read approaches but resolved using long‐read sequencing technologies (Ferraro et al. 2026). Phenotypic mimics also remain an important consideration in clinically overlapping craniofacial syndromes not included in targeted panels. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for complementary approaches, including CNV detection and broader genomic strategies such as whole exome, whole genome, or long‐read sequencing, to improve diagnostic yield in TCS. Finally, no systematic family‐based segregation was performed, as molecular testing was limited to affected probands. This reflects practical and resource‐related constraints in our setting, including limited availability of parental samples and logistical and socio‐economic factors that often preclude routine trio‐based sequencing. The absence of segregation data may limit the refinement of variant classification, particularly for variants of uncertain significance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the high yield and utility of targeted NGS for diagnosing TCS in resource‐constrained settings, provides clinically actionable information for affected families, and contributes valuable African‐population data to the global understanding of this rare condition. Continued generation and integration of African genomic data will be essential for advancing precision medicine, improving diagnostic accuracy, refining genetic counselling, and enabling more tailored patient care for rare diseases.
