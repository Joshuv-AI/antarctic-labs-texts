Approaching the scaling limit of transport through lattices with dephasing
Subhajit Sarkar, Gabriela Wójtowicz, Bartłomiej Gardas, Marek M. Rams, Michael Zwolak

Abstract
We examine the stationary–state equations for lattices with generalized Markovian dephasing and relaxation. When the Hamiltonian is quadratic, the single–particle correlation matrix has a closed system of equations even in the presence of these two processes. The resulting equations have a vectorized form related to, but distinct from, Lyapunov’s equation. We present an efficient solution that helps to achieve the scaling limit, e.g., of the current decay with lattice length. As an example, we study the super–diffusive–to–diffusive transition in a lattice with long–range hopping and dephasing. The approach enables calculations with up to 10 4 10^{4} sites, representing an increase of 10 10 to 40 40 times over prior studies. This enables a more precise extraction of the diffusion exponent, enhances agreement with theoretical results, and supports the presence of a phase transition. There is a wide range of problems that have Markovian relaxation, noise, and driving. They include quantum networks for machine–learning–based classification and extended reservoir approaches (ERAs) for transport. The results here will be useful for these classes of problems.

II
Equation of motion

We start from the equation of motion for the many–body density matrix,
\rho
, in the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad
form
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007

\dot{\rho}=-\imath[H,\rho]+\mathcal{D}_{\rm R}[\rho]{+\mathcal{D}_{\rm D}[\rho]}.

(1)

Here,
H
is the Hamiltonian, and
\mathcal{D}_{\rm R}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\rm D}
are relaxation and dephasing superoperators, respectively.
We consider quadratic—non–interacting—Hamiltonians,

H=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\bm{\bar{H}}_{mm^{\prime}}{c}_{m}^{\dagger}{{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}},

(2)

that are particle–conserving, where
\bm{\bar{H}}_{mm^{\prime}}
are the hopping coefficients and
{c}_{m}^{\dagger}
(
{{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}
) are fermionic creation (annihilation) operators for mode
m
.
This class of Hamiltonians does not have electron–electron or electron–phonon interactions.
The Markovian relaxation is also quadratic,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rm R}[\rho]=

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\gamma^{+}_{mm^{\prime}}\left({c}_{m}^{\dagger}\rho{{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}{c}_{m}^{\dagger},\rho\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\gamma^{-}_{mm^{\prime}}\left({{c}_{m}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}\rho{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}{{c}_{m}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}},\rho\right\}\right),

(3)

where
\gamma^{+(-)}_{mm^{\prime}}
are generalized injection (depletion) rates and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
gives the anticommutator.
This form appears in the auxiliary master equation approach
Arrigoni et al. 2013
;
Dorda et al. 2014
.
For onsite relaxation of others
Gruss et al. 2016
;
Brenes et al. 2020
, we denote
\gamma^{+(-)}_{mm}\equiv\gamma^{+(-)}_{m}
.
The Hermitian, positive semidefinite matrices,

\bm{\gamma}^{+(-)}=\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\gamma^{+(-)}_{mm^{\prime}}|m\rangle\langle m^{\prime}|,

(4)

completely describe the injection and depletion of fermions, and
\bm{\gamma}=\bm{\gamma}^{+}+\bm{\gamma}^{-}
is a matrix that gives the total dissipation strength.
This form of relaxation, Eq. (
II
), has a simple interpretation as the Markovian limit of linear contact with non–interacting fermionic reservoirs.
This limit, though, requires multiple completely full and empty infinite–bandwidth reservoirs, which is the source of breaking the FDT
Elenewski et al. 2017
.

Unlike relaxation, the dephasing is quartic,

\mathcal{D}_{\rm D}[\rho]=\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\sigma_{mm^{\prime}}\left(n_{m}\rho n_{m^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{n_{m^{\prime}}n_{m},\rho\right\}\right),

(5)

where
n_{m}={c}_{m}^{\dagger}{{c}_{m}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}
is particle–number operator for mode
m
.
The superoperator in Eq. (
5
) is a generalized form of dephasing that contains cross–terms between modes
m
and
m^{\prime}
.
The coefficient matrix,

\bm{\sigma}=\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\sigma_{mm^{\prime}}|m\rangle\langle m^{\prime}|,

(6)

is a real, symmetric, positive semidefinite matrix.
The interpretation of this is straightforward: When an external environment interacts with a total, or subtotal, of particles—for instance, a capacitive coupling to the electron number on a quantum dot—one obtains such cross terms.
For local dephasing, i.e.,
\sigma_{mm^{\prime}}=\sigma_{m}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
where
\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta function, the operator decoheres each mode
m
independently.

We focus on observables quadratic in fermionic operators, such as populations and particle current.
To do so, we examine the correlation matrix,

\mathbfcal{C}_{mm^{\prime}}=\mathrm{tr}[{c}_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}{{c}_{m}^{{\phantom{\dagger}}}}\rho].

(7)

Dephasing, though, is not a Gaussian process, i.e., Wick’s theorem is not obeyed.
Yet, Eq. (
1
) gives a closed system of equations for
\mathbfcal{C}
under local dephasing
Landi et al. 2022
.
This holds also for generalized dephasing, Eq. (
5
), yielding

\dot{\mathbfcal{C}}=-\imath\left[\bm{\bar{H}},\mathbfcal{C}\right]+\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm R}[\mathbfcal{C}]+\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm D}[\mathbfcal{C}],

(8)

with the superoperator

\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm R}[\mathbfcal{C}]=\bm{\gamma}^{+}-\left\{\bm{\gamma},\mathbfcal{C}\right\}/2

(9)

for relaxation
Elenewski et al. 2017
and

\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm D}[\mathbfcal{C}]=\bm{\sigma}\odot\mathbfcal{C}-\left\{{\rm diag}(\bm{\sigma}),\mathbfcal{C}\right\}/2

(10)

for dephasing.
The
\odot
signifies the Hadamard product.

In the absence of dephasing, Eq. (
8
) is a Lyapunov equation, which gives a numerically efficient approach—scaling as
N^{3}
with
N
total modes in the network—to find the dynamics and stationary states, as well as to examine stability.
As noted in Ref.
Landi et al. 2022
, common numerical routines
Bartels and Stewart 1972
;
Golub et al. 1979
for the Lyapunov equation can reach order
10^{4}
sites at
N^{3}
cost scaling.
When dephasing is present, the resulting equation of motion is no longer in Lyapunov form.
This comes with a penalty.
A general solution of Eq. (
8
) requires vectorization and diagonalization of the associated Lindblad superoperator, at a cost of
N^{6}
.
For special forms of dephasing, one expects better scaling.
We analyze the cost for generalized dephasing, as well as both local dephasing and dephasing only in a junction region.
Local dephasing comes with a cost of
N^{4}
.
Despite the additional power of
N
compared to the Lyapunov equation—
N^{4}
compared to
N^{3}
—we show that one, surprisingly, can still reach
N=10^{4}
sites in reasonable compute time (several hours) on a single machine.

III
Steady–state solution

Defining the non–Hermitian Hamiltonian,

\mathbfcal{H}=-\imath\bm{\bar{H}}-\bm{\gamma}/2-{\rm diag}(\bm{\sigma})/2,

(11)

and the particle source,

\mathbfcal{Z}=\bm{\gamma}^{+},

(12)

the stationary state has the solution
Turkeshi and Schiró 2021
;
Sarkar et al. 2024

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau e^{-\imath\mathbfcal{H}\tau}\bm{\Sigma}^{<}e^{\imath\mathbfcal{H}\tau},

(13)

where
\bm{\Sigma}^{<}=\mathbfcal{Z}+\bm{\sigma}\odot\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}\equiv\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\gamma}+\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\sigma}
.
The appearance of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
on the left– and right–hand sides makes the solution self–consistent and is a consequence of many–body correlations.
While
\bm{\Sigma}^{<}
is just playing the role of a functional of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
, it is, as the symbol implies, the lesser self–energy from non–equilibrium Green’s functions.

For the non–Hermitian Hamiltonian, Eq. (
11
), we denote
\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}
as the matrix with left eigenvectors as rows,
\mathbfcal{V}
with right eigenvectors as columns, and normalization
\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}\mathbfcal{V}=I
and eigenvalues
\bm{\lambda}
.
Equation (
13
) becomes

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}=-\imath\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathbfcal{V}\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{\lambda}}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{<}\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{\lambda}^{\star}}\mathbfcal{V}^{\dagger},

(14)

where
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{<}=\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}^{<}\mathbfcal{W}
.
This integration yields

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}=\mathbfcal{V}\left[\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}\odot\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{<}\right]\mathbfcal{V}^{\dagger},

(15)

with
\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}
having elements

\left[\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}\right]_{pq}=1/\left(\lambda_{q}^{\star}-\lambda_{p}\right).

(16)

Equation (
15
) is linear in the two terms, so we can write

\begin{split}\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}&=\mathbfcal{V}\left[\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}\odot\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\gamma}+\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\sigma}\right)\mathbfcal{W}\right]\mathbfcal{V}^{\dagger}\\
&\equiv\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}_{\gamma}+\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}_{\sigma},\end{split}

(17)

where the first term is independent of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
and the second,

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}_{\sigma}=\imath\mathbfcal{V}\left[\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}\odot\left(\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}\left[\bm{\sigma}\odot\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}\right]\mathbfcal{W}\right)\right]\mathbfcal{V}^{\dagger},

(18)

depends on
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
.
The total solution thus requires solving,

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}=\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}_{\gamma}+\mathbf{D}\left[\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}\right],

(19)

with the elements of the superoperator
\mathbf{D}
,

\mathbf{D}_{oo^{\prime},mm^{\prime}}=\imath\sigma_{mm^{\prime}}\left[\mathbfcal{V}\left[\bm{\Delta}\bm{\lambda}^{-1}\odot\mathbfcal{W}^{\dagger}|m\rangle\langle m^{\prime}|\mathbfcal{W}\right]\mathbfcal{V}^{\dagger}\right]_{oo^{\prime}}.

(20)

Both Eq. (
19
) and Eq. (
20
) can be costly operations.

IV
Computational Cost

The general solution of Eq. (
19
) has a computational cost of
N^{6}
to solve the vectorized matrix equation,

\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}=({\bm{1}}-\mathbf{D})^{-1}\left[\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}_{\gamma}\right],

(21)

where
{\bm{1}}
is the identity matrix.
There is a cost scaling of
N^{5}
to find all the elements of
\mathbf{D}
.
The lower power to compute all the elements exploits that for each of the
N^{2}
pairs

, the inner bracket in Eq. (

) requires
N^{2}
operations.
Sequentially, the outer bracket can be computed for all elements

at once via matrix multiplication, for a cost of

.
The total cost is thus
N^{2}\left(N^{2}+N^{3}\right)
.
If one were to compute the elements for each quadruple index

one by one, then the cost would be

Turkeshi and Schiró have a similar solution for a nearest–neighbor hopping model with onsite dephasing and boundary injection and depletion

, and analogous results appear in Refs.

;

.
The model they study exhibits diffusive transport with resistivity that scales with the length of the lattice.
Although they note that not all terms in the linear equations are necessary to find the density and current, their example problem does not require one to reach very large sizes in order to identify criticality or a crossover, as no such transition occurs in their model.
Moreover, as we will see via a full cost analysis, computing all elements of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
comes at no additional cost in terms of scaling with network size.

Equations (
1
)–(
5
) provide a more general form of relaxation and dephasing, including cross terms for both, as well as injection and depletion on an arbitrary number of sites (e.g., encompassing extended reservoir approaches).
The solution, Eq. (
19

20
), should be solved sequentially:
The terms of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
that correspond to non–zero elements of
\bm{\sigma}
should be solved first, and then the remaining elements of
\mathbfcal{C}^{\infty}
can be found from the Lyapunov solution, Eq. (
15
).
Below, we will provide a complete cost analysis of this approach.
This solution enables simulations for system sizes that are 10 to 40 times larger than prior works
Turkeshi and Schiró 2021
;
Sarkar et al. 2024
;
Taylor and Scardicchio 2021
, and on par with sizes achievable when Lyapunov solvers are applicable, despite the increase to

scaling.

, let

be the number of non–zero elements,

.
For each non–zero element, one has to perform the matrix multiplications in Eq. (

) of

is the number of sites in

The system

We apply an infinite bias via Markovian injection from the left
\mathcal{L}
with rate
\gamma^{+}
, and depletion to the right
\mathcal{R}
with rate
\gamma^{-}
at the boundaries.

This model has been previously studied, see, e.g., Ref.
Sarkar et al. 2024
, and is part of a broader class of physical systems with long–range interactions and hopping.
This class includes natural and artificial light–harvesting complexes
Zerah Harush and Dubi 2021
;
Mattioni et al. 2021
;
Scholes 2003
, and models implementable with controllable couplings in experimental platforms
Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
;
Joshi et al. 2022
;
Porras and Cirac 2004
.
Non–local couplings can qualitatively modify equilibrium phases, ground state structure, and dynamical response
Defenu et al. 2023
.
For instance, they can replace the ordinary diffusion under short–range models with anomalous transport, including superdiffusion and Lévy–flight behavior, as established theoretically and observed experimentally
Dhawan et al. 2024
;
Joshi et al. 2022
;
Schuckert et al. 2020a
.
They also reshape measurement–induced phase transitions (MIPT), producing new universality classes or suppressing the transition entirely
Block et al. 2022
;
Müller et al. 2022
;
Minato et al. 2022
.
Rydberg atom and trapped ion systems provide testbeds with tunable long–range couplings, with the spin–spin interactions yielding free–fermion long–range hopping as an appropriate limit
Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
.
Despite this progress, finite–size effects due to accessible system sizes hamper characterization: in free–fermion long–range hopping, MIPT critical points remain obscure even at
N\approx 400

Minato et al. 2022
and studies of anomalous transport are similarly constrained, impeding the definitive determination of the super–diffusive to diffusive critical points
Sarkar et al. 2024
;
Dhawan et al. 2024
.
These considerations motivate developing scalable methods and establishing benchmarks, such as the model here, for validating new algorithms.

The model here exhibits anomalous transport, which is a stark departure from the well–known ballistic transport, where the stationary–state (SS) resistance
R_{SS}=J_{SS}^{-1}
remains unchanged with increasing system size (the number of lattice sites
N_{\mathcal{S}}
), as well as diffusive transport, where the stationary–state resistance scales as
R_{SS}\propto N_{\mathcal{S}}

Dhawan et al. 2024
;
Sarkar et al. 2024
.
The anomalous transport and transition is reflected in the stationary–state density profile for different values of the long–range exponent
\alpha
, see Fig.
2
(b).
As
\alpha
decreases from the short–range regime (
\alpha\gtrsim 1.5
) to long–range (
\alpha<1.5
), the stationary–state equation crosses from ordinary diffusion to fractional diffusion:

\displaystyle\alpha\gtrsim 1.5\Rightarrow D\,\frac{d^{2}n(x)}{dx^{2}}=0

\displaystyle\alpha<1.5\Rightarrow D_{\alpha}\,(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}n(x)=0,

where
D
is the ordinary diffusion constant,
D_{\alpha}
the anomalous diffusion constant,
(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
the non–local fractional Laplacian, and
x
the position.
The ordinary regime yields a linear density drop,
n(x)\approx\left(1-x/L\right)
, which is approximate due to boundary effects for finite lattices.
This regime is equivalent to dephasing and nearest‐-neighbor hopping, which follows a diffusion equation in the continuum limit
Dziarmaga et al. 2012
, with a diffusion coefficient,
D=2v^{2}/\sigma
with
v
the nearest–neighbor hopping and
\sigma
the local dephasing strength.
In the anomalous regime, the lattice is “well mixed” by Lévy‐flight–type jumps
Schuckert et al. 2020b
;
Dhar et al. 2013
, flattening the bulk and concentrating the drop into sharp boundary layers near the reservoirs—hence the non–linear density profile for
\alpha<1.5
.

The defining feature of the anomalous regime is the existence of super–diffusive transport characterized by
R_{SS}\propto N_{\mathcal{S}}^{\nu}
with
\nu<1
, and a super–diffusive–to–diffusive crossover (arguably, a phase transition) at
\alpha_{c}=1.5
in the thermodynamic limit (
N_{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\infty
), where the diffusive regime appears for
\alpha>\alpha_{c}

Dhawan et al. 2024
;
Sarkar et al. 2024
.

Therefore, while the resistance diverges in both regimes, the resistivity remains finite on the diffusive side and vanishes in the thermodynamic limit on the super–diffusive side.
The analytical estimate
\alpha_{c}=1.5
for this change in scaling
in the thermodynamic limit follows the current operator norm, i.e., the maximum possible coherent transport
Sarkar et al. 2024
.

Numerical results on
N_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1024
indicate that
\nu
increases with
\alpha
, saturating well above
\alpha\gtrsim 1.6
.
Specifically, for
1<\alpha\leq 1.6
, the exponent scales as
\nu\approx 1.6\,\alpha-2
from analyzing
N_{\mathcal{S}}\leq 1024

Sarkar et al. 2024
.
However, analytical calculations, both using the current–operator norm and mapping the corresponding Lindblad equation to fractional diffusion equation in the large dephasing limit
\sigma\gg v_{\mathcal{S}}
, predict
\nu=2\,\alpha-2
in the thermodynamic limit
N_{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\infty

Dhawan et al. 2024
;
Sarkar et al. 2024
.
This deviation of the slope of
\alpha
dependence of
\nu
originates from the limited accessibility of the system size, as we shall show here.
Notably, in a different setup that replaces the onsite dephasing with number–conserving Büttiker voltage probes, the scaling analysis obtained from a larger system size
2^{13}\lesssim N_{\mathcal{S}}\lesssim 2^{14}
achieved a similar estimate of
\alpha_{c}

Dhawan et al. 2024
.

VI
Conclusions

We provided a generalized equation of motion, Eq. (
1
), for dephasing and relaxation of fermionic networks with cross–terms for each.
This setup covers a wide variety of physical scenarios, from extended reservoir approaches to boundary models.
For quadratic Hamiltonians, the single–particle correlation matrix has a closed system of equations, including in the presence of dephasing and relaxation.
We demonstrated how to solve the resulting equation efficiently.
For an example lattice with local dephasing, long–range hopping, and boundary driving, we simulate up to
10^{4}
sites.
This enhances the accuracy of locating the critical point in anomalous quantum transport, generating better agreement with theory and further confidence that there is a phase transition.
This approach will help improve benchmarking calculations—i.e., quadratic limits or approximations (mean–field)—for many–body simulations, such as tensor networks.

It will also help understand the usability of ERA, such as identifying anomalous behavior, where a scaling transition in accuracy (
1/\sqrt{N}
to
1/N
) has been observed around
10^{3}
modes
Wójtowicz et al. 2021
.

In addition, the approach is directly applicable to physical and computational networks of interest, including anomalous transport in higher dimensions and quantum networks for machine learning
Lorber et al. 2024
.
