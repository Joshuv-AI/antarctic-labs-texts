Molecularly sculpted NIR-II nano-igniter converts local photothermal heat into systemic antitumor vaccination
Authors: Qihang Ding, Tingting Liu, Chunbai Xiang, Qian Wang, Meiqin Li, Yao Sun, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotothermal therapy (PTT) holds transformative potential for precision cancer treatment, yet clinical translation remains constrained by the scarcity of molecularly defined, biocompatible, and efficiently NIR-absorbing photothermal agents (PTAs). Here we report a rational donor–acceptor–donor (D-A-D) framework that delivers ultrasmall organic PTAs with record photothermal conversion efficiencies (49.8%) and intrinsic immunogenic cell death (ICD) activity. The design exploits 6,7-diphenyl-[1,2,5]thiadiazolo[3,4-g]quinoxaline as a π-extended, multi-nitrogenated acceptor core flanked by trifluoromethyl groups to deepen the LUMO, while methoxylated triphenylamine donors intensify intramolecular charge-transfer and suppress radiative decay. Nanoprecipitation furnishes monodisperse nanoparticles that exhibit intense NIR-II absorption, exceptional photostability across five hyperthermic cycles, and lysosome-directed uptake. In vitro, single-dose FTPA NPs plus 808-nm laser irradiation trigger mitochondrial depolarization, G0/G1 arrest, and apoptosis in > 70% of 4T1 cells while releasing abundant ATP and surface calreticulin—canonical ICD signals. A prophylactic vaccination model corroborates these molecular cues: mice primed with FTPA-NP-treated tumor cells reject contralateral challenge, achieving > 90% long-term survival, expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (≈ 70% activation), and suppression of Tregs (≈ 3%). No systemic toxicity or off-target pathology is observed. Introduction

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has emerged as a minimally invasive strategy for cancer treatment, in which photothermal agents (PTAs) absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and efficiently convert photon energy into heat, thereby inducing apoptosis or necrosis within tumors [1–3]. Compared with conventional modalities, PTT offers superior spatiotemporal precision, reduced systemic toxicity, and compatibility with chemotherapy [4, 5], radiotherapy [6–8], and immunotherapy [9–11], highlighting its translational potential [12–15]. Distinct from photodynamic therapy (PDT) [16, 17], PTT relies on thermal ablation rather than reactive oxygen species generation, conferring oxygen independence and the capacity for deep-tissue tumor eradication [18–24]. A broad range of PTAs, including gold nanostructures, carbon-based nanomaterials, semiconducting polymers, and two-dimensional nanomaterials—have been developed with high photothermal conversion efficiencies [8, 25–28]. Nevertheless, challenges such as poor biodegradability, ill-defined structures, limited stability, and suboptimal conversion efficiency hinder their clinical deployment [15, 29–32]. Consequently, the development of novel, well-defined organic PTAs with enhanced efficiency, tumor selectivity, and biocompatibility represents a critical direction for advancing PTT toward clinical translation.

In the design of acceptor units, benzothiadiazole (BT) has been widely employed as a classical electron-withdrawing group owing to its simple structure, high planarity, and strong electron-accepting ability [15, 33]. In contrast, thiadiazolo[3,4-g]quinoxaline (TQ) extends the π-conjugation framework and incorporates additional heteroatoms, particularly nitrogen, which enhances electron delocalization and thereby strengthens its electron-accepting capacity. This modification substantially lowers the LUMO level and induces a pronounced red-shift in absorption spectra. The trifluoromethyl (-CF3) substituent, through its strong inductive electron-withdrawing effect, further decreases the electron density and LUMO energy level of the acceptor while exerting minimal perturbation on the conjugated backbone, thus maintaining molecular planarity and enabling precise electronic modulation [34, 35]. As such, -CF3 groups are especially suited for constructing donor-acceptor-donor (D-A-D) type molecular systems with well-defined charge-transfer pathways.

Guided by this design principle, we employed 6,7-diphenyl-[1,2,5]thiadiazolo[3,4-g]quinoxaline as the central acceptor scaffold, decorated with two trifluoromethyl substituents, and coupled it with triphenylamine (TPA) donors. Methoxy substitution further reinforced the donor strength of TPA, thereby intensifying intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) and facilitating the S1→T1 intersystem crossing (ISC) process. Following this strategy, we designed and synthesized three small-molecule PTAs: PTQ-TPA, PTQ-HTPA, and PTQ-FTPA. Among them, PTQ-FTPA exhibited the most favorable photothermal properties, with a strong NIR absorption band beyond 800 nm. For biomedical applications, PTQ-FTPA was further encapsulated into nanoparticles (FTPA NPs) via nanoprecipitation. Under NIR laser irradiation, FTPANPs demonstrated excellent photothermal stability and high conversion efficiency (49.8%), underscoring their promising potential for PTT. Notably, FTPA NPs not only achieve efficient photothermal tumor ablation under NIR irradiation but also induce immunogenic cell death, promoting T cell–mediated anti-tumor immunity and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, highlighting their potential as a synergistic photothermal–immunotherapeutic platform (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

The rational design of high-performance PTAs relies on the synergistic optimization of molecular structure and functional properties. Donor-acceptor architectures with extended π-conjugation enhance NIR absorption and photothermal conversion efficiency [36, 37]. In this context, the 6,7-diphenyl-[1,2,5]thiadiazolo[3,4-g]quinoxaline core integrates multiple strong electron-withdrawing heterocycles (quinoxaline and thiadiazole), endowing the molecule with pronounced acceptor strength. The abundance of nitrogen atoms further reinforces electron-withdrawing effects, effectively lowering the LUMO energy level. Incorporation of -CF3 substituents intensifies electron deficiency while preserving the rigidity and planarity of the conjugated backbone, thereby facilitating charge delocalization and transport.

TPA, a prototypical three-armed non-planar donor, contributes strong electron-donating capability while its bulky configuration mitigates excessive intermolecular π–π stacking, thus minimizing fluorescence and thermal quenching [38–40]. Additional methoxy substitution enables fine-tuning of the energy levels, promoting excited-state charge separation and enhancing ISC efficiency. These structural features collectively improve luminescence behavior and expand the functional potential of the resulting molecules for photothermal and photonic applications.

Building on these design principles, we synthesized three D-A-D type PTAs, PTQ-TPA, PTQ-HTPA, and PTQ-FTPA, by employing 6,7-diphenyl-[1,2,5]thiadiazolo[3,4-g]quinoxaline functionalized with trifluoromethyl groups as the electron-accepting core, coupled with TPA and methoxy-substituted TPA donors (Fig. 1A). In particular, PTQ-FTPA integrates trifluoromethyl substitution, thiophene incorporation, and methoxy-modified TPA donors, which synergistically reinforce the ICT effect. This structural optimization markedly enhances absorption in the NIR region and significantly improves photothermal conversion efficiency, establishing PTQ-FTPA as a highly promising PTA candidate. All compounds were fully characterized by NMR and mass spectrometry (Figs. S1–S9).

We first investigated the UV–vis absorption spectra of the three small molecules (Fig. 1B). PTQ-TPA exhibited a distinct absorption band around 600 nm, whereas both PTQ-HTPA and PTQ-FTPA displayed extended absorption beyond 800 nm, with maximum molar extinction coefficients (ε_max) exceeding 3 × 104 M− 1 cm− 1. Notably, their absorption profiles tail into the second near-infrared window (NIR-II). Compared with visible or short-wavelength NIR absorption, NIR-II absorbers typically provided reduced autofluorescence interference, deeper tissue penetration, and improved signal-to-noise ratios, underscoring the strong potential of these molecules for NIR-II bioimaging.

To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the changes in optical performance, the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of PTQ-TPA, PTQ-PTPA, and PTQ-FTPA were calculated at the B3LYP/6-311G(d) level, as illustrated in the Fig. 1C. The computational results demonstrated that the introduction of the thiophene bridge and the more electron-donating methoxytrianiline moiety significantly reduced the energy gap and ICT in PTQ-PTPA (1.38 eV), compared to PTQ-TPA (1.93 eV). Furthermore, the incorporation of a highly electronegative trifluoromethyl group into the receptor unit enhanced the ICT effect in PTQ-FTPA (1.24 eV). Additionally, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis of the excited-state geometry revealed that PTQ-FTPA (RMSD = 1.008) exhibited a higher structural deviation than PTQ-TPA (RMSD = 0.6252) and PTQ-PTPA (RMSD = 0.6829). These results suggested that PTQ-FTPA underwent more pronounced structural relaxation during the excited-state process, leading to a clearer energy dissipation pathway from S1 to S0 and indicating a stronger non-radiative decay process.

To enable biomedical applications, PTQ-TPA, PTQ-HTPA, and PTQ-FTPA were encapsulated into water-dispersible nanoparticles (TPA NPs, HTPA NPs, and FTPA NPs, respectively) via nanoprecipitation, achieving loading efficiencies of 79.4%, 88.7%, and 81.6%, respectively (Fig. S10). The UV-vis absorption spectra of the nanoparticles were subsequently measured and normalized for comparison (Fig. 1D). TPA NPs and HTPA NPs retained absorption profiles nearly identical to their molecular counterparts, whereas FTPA NPs exhibited a slight red-shift while preserving their overall optical characteristics. Importantly, both HTPA NPs and FTPA NPs demonstrated pronounced absorption in the NIR region, further highlighting their promise for NIR imaging and related biomedical applications.

We further characterized the hydrodynamic diameters of the three NPs using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 2A). The results revealed average diameters of approximately 112.8 nm for TPA NPs, 165.3 nm for HTPA NPs, and 182.7 nm for FTPA NPs. All three NPs exhibited negative surface charges (Fig. S11), with ζ-potentials of -36.87 ± 1.53 mV, -41.82 ± 5.72 mV, and − 44.55 ± 2.34 mV, respectively. During seven days of storage, their particle sizes remained nearly unchanged (Fig. 2F, S12), likely due to electrostatic repulsion, whereby the negative surface charge enhances colloidal stability and potentially prolongs in vivo circulation.

Subsequently, we evaluated the photothermal conversion performance of the three NPs (Fig. 2B). Upon irradiation with an 808 nm laser, the temperatures of HTPA NP and FTPA NP solutions rapidly increased within 2 min, which can be attributed to the introduction of thiophene units and methoxy modifications on the triphenylamine scaffold, promoting ICT and thereby enhancing photothermal conversion efficiency. After 5 min, the maximum temperatures of HTPA NPs and FTPA NPs reached 50 °C and 57.5 °C, corresponding to temperature increments of 25 °C and 37.5 °C, respectively. In contrast, TPA NPs exhibited a maximum temperature of only 32 °C, with a negligible temperature rise of 3.1 °C. Notably, FTPA NPs demonstrated a high photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of ~ 49.8% (Fig. 2G-I), significantly surpassing HTPA NPs (38.2%), whereas TPA NPs exhibited minimal photothermal capability (~ 0.51%). Furthermore, the temperature elevation of HTPA NPs and FTPA NPs showed a positive correlation with both sample concentration and laser power within a certain range (Figs. 2C, D), allowing for tunable photothermal effects. Importantly, after five consecutive heating–cooling cycles, the maximum temperatures of HTPA NPs and FTPA NPs remained unchanged (Figs. 2E, S13), indicating excellent photothermal stability. However, the heat generation efficiency of indocyanine green (ICG) decreased by almost 30% (Fig. S14).

Taken together, compared with the other two NPs, FTPA NPs feature extended absorption and emission wavelengths, stable particle characteristics, and superior photothermal performance, making them the optimal candidate for subsequent studies. These attributes suggest that FTPA NPs hold great promise as PTAs for cancer therapy.

To investigate the intracellular uptake behavior and subcellular localization of FTPA NPs, lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker and observed using NIR-II fluorescence microscope. The results revealed that as the incubation time increased, the red fluorescence signal of FTPA NPs gradually intensified, indicating a progressive uptake within the cells. Further analysis of the overlay images (Fig. 3A, S15) showed a significant overlap between the red (FTPA NPs) and green (LysoTracker) signals, resulting in prominent yellow areas. Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC = 0.75) analysis further confirmed the co-localization of FTPA NPs with lysosomes. This suggests that FTPA NPs predominantly accumulate in lysosomes following cellular uptake, providing crucial insight for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Next, the in vitro biocompatibility and photothermal therapeutic efficacy of FTPA NPs were evaluated using the MTT assay. The cell viability after incubation with different concentrations of FTPA NPs revealed (Fig. 3B) that even at high concentrations (100 µg/mL), the non-illuminated group exhibited negligible toxicity, indicating the excellent biocompatibility of FTPA NPs. Besides, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of ICG on 4T1 cells (Fig. S16) The results indicated that the phototoxicity of the ICG + NAC+Laser group was lower compared to the FTPA NPs+Laser group, and the dark toxicity of ICG was higher than that of FTPA NPs. Live/dead cell staining results (Figs. 3C, S17) further validated this conclusion: in the FTPA NPs+laser treatment group, almost all cells underwent apoptosis (as indicated by significant red fluorescence), while the control and non-illuminated FTPA NPs groups exhibited good cell survival. To explore the apoptotic mechanisms, mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining. The results (Figs. 3D, S18) showed that in the FTPA NPs+laser group, the fluorescence of JC-1 in cells shifted from red to green, suggesting a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and indicating mitochondrial dysfunction.

Subsequently, the effect of different treatments on cell cycle distribution was evaluated using flow cytometry (Fig. 3E, S19). In the control group, cells were predominantly in the G0/G1 phase (50%), S phase (20%), and G2/M phase (30%). Treatment with FTPA NPs alone caused accumulation of cells in the S phase (32%) and G0/G1 (55%), accompanied by a decrease in the G2/M phase (13%), indicating that FTPA NPs induced a S and G0/G1phase arrest. The FTPA NPs+laser treatment group, on the other hand, increased the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase to 61%, suggesting that photothermal treatment induced a G0/G1 phase arrest. These results indicated that FTPA NPs under laser irradiation could differentially regulate the cell cycle, potentially inhibiting cell proliferation by blocking specific cell cycle stages.

Additionally, Annexin V-FITC/PI-PE staining was performed to further investigate the cell death mechanism. As shown in Fig. 3F, approximately 67.77% of cells in the FTPA NPs+laser group underwent apoptosis, which was a 61.75% increase compared to the 6.02% apoptotic rate in the laser-only control group.

ICD is a distinct form of cell death that not only eliminates cells but also activates the immune system to recognize and clear dying cells. ICD is characterized by three hallmark signals: extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) release, which functions as a “find-me” signal to recruit antigen-presenting cells; ectopic exposure of calreticulin (CRT) on the cell surface, acting as an “eat-me” signal to facilitate dendritic cell uptake of dying cells; and passive release of high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), which can interact with Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) to enhance inflammatory signaling during antigen presentation.

To determine whether FTPA NPs could induce ICD, CRT exposure in early apoptotic cells was first analyzed by immunofluorescence. As shown in Figs. 3G, S20, S21, negligible CRT (green) fluorescence was detected in either the laser-only or FTPA NPs-only groups, indicating the absence of ICD. In contrast, the FTPA NPs plus laser group exhibited pronounced CRT expression and the CRT presented on the cell surface of 4T1 cells, suggesting ICD induction. We then assessed HMGB1 and ATP release. HMGB1 immunofluorescence demonstrated that, in the FTPA NPs plus laser group, DAPI (blue) remained nuclear, whereas HMGB1 (green) signal translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, further confirming ICD activation. Quantitative analysis of ATP levels (Fig. 3H) revealed a significant increase in extracellular ATP in the FTPA NPs plus laser group, approximately fivefold higher than in other treatment groups, demonstrating that this treatment robustly promotes ATP release and effectively induces ICD.

To further confirm our in vitro findings, a mouse vaccination experiment using 4T1 cells was conducted, as this method is a highly effective and commonly used approach to assess the ICD-inducing capability of a drug. The tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups: (i) control group, (ii) control + laser group, (iii) FTPA NPs group, (iv) FTPA NPs+laser group. First, FTPA NPs were co-incubated with 4T1 cells to form the vaccine, then the vaccine was subcutaneously injected into the left flanks of Balb/c female mice. 7 days later, 4T1 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank to establish a tumor model (Fig. 4A). The tumor volume and body weight of the mice were monitored and after 7 days of tumor growth, the representative photographs of mice with various treatments were recorded every seven days during the experiment. On day 29, the mice were euthanized, and tissue samples were collected for biological analysis. The results showed (Fig. 4B) that the FTPA NPs combined with laser treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, whereas the use of FTPA NPs alone or light exposure alone had no notable effect on tumor progression. Quantitative analysis of tumor volume revealed consistent trends (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, no significant changes in the body weight of the mice were observed during the treatment period (Fig. 4D), indicating that FTPA NPs exhibited good biocompatibility and biosafety. Survival analysis of mice in different treatment groups revealed that (Fig. S22), after 40 days, all groups exhibited 0% survival except for the FTPA NPs+Laser group, in which the survival rate remained above 90%, demonstrating the remarkable therapeutic efficacy of FTPA NPs combined with laser treatment.

Following treatment, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed no evident inflammation or tissue damage (Fig. 4E), indicating favorable biocompatibility and safety of FTPA NPs as a photothermal agent. In contrast, the FTPA NPs plus laser group exhibited extensive tumor cell necrosis and structural disruption, demonstrating the platform’s potent therapeutic effect under photothermal conditions.

To systematically evaluate the regulatory effects of different treatment modalities on the tumor immune microenvironment, we first analyzed the changes in the proportions of key T cell subsets in tumor tissues (Figs. 5A-C). Treatment with laser irradiation alone or FTPA NPs alone had a limited effect on immune cell infiltration, whereas the FTPA NPs + Laser group showed a pronounced immune activation response. Specifically, the proportion of CD8⁺ T cells significantly increased to approximately 15%, nearly double that of the control group. Further analysis revealed a marked elevation in activated CD8⁺ T cells, reaching about 70%, which was substantially higher than that in the other groups, indicating that their cytotoxic functions were fully activated. Moreover, the FTPA NPs+Laser group markedly enhanced CD4⁺ T cell recruitment, with their proportion increasing to about 10%, nearly five times higher than that of the control group. Notably, the proportion of Tregs decreased dramatically to approximately 3% in the FTPA NPs+Laser group, compared with 11–12% in the control and single-treatment groups. This indicates its potential advantage in promoting T cell expansion and immune responses (Figs. S23).

Consistent with these cellular findings, the overall immune activation pattern was further verified by immunofluorescence and flow cytometric analyses of tumor sections (Figs. 5D–G), which confirmed the enhanced infiltration of effector T cells and suppression of immunosuppressive Treg populations, highlighting the strong immunomodulatory potential of the combined FTPA NPs + Laser treatment.

Conclusion

By integrating strongly electron-withdrawing, π-extended acceptors with judiciously tuned donor substituents, we have produced a purely organic photothermal nano-agent that unites exceptional NIR-II absorbance, high photothermal conversion efficiency and intrinsic immunogenic cell-death activity. FTPA NPs self-assemble into stable, sub-200 nm carriers that accumulate in lysosomes, resist photobleaching across multiple heating cycles and elicit potent mitochondrial dysfunction, cell-cycle arrest and apoptotic death upon brief 808-nm irradiation. Crucially, the molecularly defined scaffold simultaneously releases ATP, exposes calreticulin and liberates HMGB1—canonical damage-associated molecular patterns that trigger potent anti-tumor immune activation associated with photothermal treatment. The resulting immune responses, characterized by expansion of activated CD8⁺ T cells, suppression of regulatory T cells and > 90% long-term survival, are indicative of immune involvement in long-term tumor suppression and are achieved without detectable organ toxicity or body-weight loss.

These findings establish a versatile, metal-free materials platform in which photophysical and immunological functions are co-optimized at the synthetic level, offering a clinically translatable route toward precision immuno-photothermal cancer therapy and providing a blueprint for next-generation theranostic agents thatintegrate efficient photothermal tumor ablation with immune-mediated long-term tumor control.
