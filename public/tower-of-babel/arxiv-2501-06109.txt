Scattering with depletion in strong, focussed fields
Tim Adamo, Anton Ilderton

Abstract
Theoretical approaches to QED scattering in strong fields typically treat the field as a fixed background with simple spacetime dependence, such as a plane wave. Two major challenges are therefore the inclusion of effects due to backreaction (depletion of the field) and spatial geometry (e.g. focussing). We show here that a solution to one problem can solve the other: even if particle wavefunctions in a chosen focussed background are not known, we show that they can be constructed once depletion is accounted for. We demonstrate this by giving the exact wavefunctions in a depleting flying focus beam. We then calculate and analyse the amplitude of non-linear Compton scattering, comparing with the plane wave case. Our methods thus open a new avenue of investigation in which two previously challenging effects are simultaneously brought under analytic control.



Ultra-intense lasers now access the relativistic, quantum regime of light-matter interactions
Gonoskov et al. 2022
;
Fedotov et al. 2023
, in which the effective coupling of matter to the laser easily exceeds unity.
This strong field regime demands non-perturbative methods, but typically restricts theory investigations to simple laser models, in particular background (i.e., fixed) plane waves.
Two challenging problems are, accordingly, to account analytically for focussing effects
Di Piazza 2014
;
Heinzl and Ilderton 2017
, and for backreaction on the laser
Seipt et al. 2017
.
Focussing (i.e., spatial field inhomogeneity) opens the door to richer scattering kinematics
Fedotov 2009
;
Gies et al. 2013
;
Gonoskov et al. 2013
;
Di Piazza 2014
;
Heinzl and Ilderton 2017
;
Di Piazza 2021a
, but turns the essential problem of constructing
the wavefunctions which describe particles in the field from a first order ODE in the plane wave case to a typically intractable PDE.
Depletion is conceptually more challenging, as the background field formalism assumes no depletion from the outset, by definition.
Accounting for both focussing and beam depletion analytically is a significant challenge.

However, in this letter we show that a solution to one of these problems can actually solve them
both
: including depletion from the outset can drastically simplify the inclusion of focussing effects. To show this, we construct exact wavefunctions for particles in a depleting ‘flying focus’ beam
Brittingham 1983
;
Sezginer 1985
;
Hillion 1992
;
Esarey et al. 1995
, despite the fact that the wavefunctions in the undepleted background (the analogue of Volkov solutions in plane waves
Wolkow 1935
)
are not known
.

Flying focus beams are long-known exact,
focussed
solutions of Maxwell’s equations in vacuum
Brittingham 1983
;
Sezginer 1985
;
Hillion 1992
;
Esarey et al. 1995
. They have recently regained attention
Sainte-Marie et al. 2017
;
Di Piazza 2021b
and been applied to discussions of radiation reaction
Formanek et al. 2022
and vacuum birefringence
Formanek et al. 2024
. To emphasise that our methods provide analytic insights into the physics of focussing and depletion, we calculate from our wavefunctions the amplitude for nonlinear Compton scattering (photon emission) from a charged particle in a depleting flying focus beam, and analyse the allowed harmonic ranges of the emitted photons, comparing with the plane wave case. This also demonstrates that the depleting flying focus beam is a background
beyond
plane waves where exact analytic amplitude computations are possible. More generally, modelling depleting fields via self-dual (see below) electromagnetic backgrounds provides a concrete example of the potentially broad application of chiral backgrounds to model
real
physical effects.

From depletion to focussing.

Classical electromagnetic fields are naturally described in QED by coherent states, as follows. Consider the scattering amplitude

S_{fi}=\langle\,\text{out};\beta\,|S|\,\alpha;\text{in}\,\rangle\;,

(1)

where
S
is the QED S-matrix, ‘in’ and ‘out’ label initial and final collections of particles, while
\alpha
and
\beta
label
coherent
states of photons.

S_{fi}
is equal (up to normalisation) to the amplitude
\langle\,\text{out}\,|S[A]|\,\text{in}\,\rangle
where
S[A]
is the S-matrix in the presence of a background
A_{\mu}
; this
A_{\mu}
is a solution of Maxwell’s equations in vacuum defined in terms of the coherent states by
Kibble 1965
;
Frantz 1965

A_{\mu}(x)=\int_{\ell}\varepsilon^{s}_{\mu}(\ell)\alpha_{s}(\ell)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-i\ell\cdot x}+{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{s}_{\mu}(\ell){\bar{\beta}}_{s}(\ell)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{+i\ell\cdot x}\,,

(2)

in which the
\varepsilon^{s}_{\mu}(\ell)
are polarisation vectors in a chosen gauge and the Fourier coefficients
\alpha_{s}(\ell)
and
\beta_{s}(\ell)
give mode occupancies for, respectively, incoming and outgoing photons of helicity
s=\pm 1
and momentum
\ell_{\mu}
. The assumption that an initial field is
not
depleted during some interaction thus amounts to taking the same incoming and outgoing coherent states in (
1
); the corresponding background (
2
) is then real, since
\beta=\alpha
.

It follows that some aspects of backreaction (beam depletion, energy absorption) imply changes to the initial coherent state
Endlich and Penco 2017
;
Ilderton and Seipt 2018
;
Ekman and Ilderton 2020
;
Aoude and Ochirov 2023
, so
\beta\not=\alpha
. The corresponding background (
2
) then becomes complex
Zwanziger 1973
;
Ilderton and Seipt 2018
. A simple illustrative case is complete depletion, in which
\beta=0
, i.e. the initial coherent state is completely absorbed during scattering. The corresponding complex field (
2
) is naturally given by retaining only positive energy (i.e. incoming) modes in the Fourier expansion of the real field

Now, any electromagnetic field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
can be decomposed into self-dual and anti-self-dual parts; these are just the
\pm i
-eigenspaces of the duality operation
F_{\mu\nu}\to\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}
(or
\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{B}\to-\mathbf{E}
). A complex field is therefore one for which these two parts are not related by complex conjugation. From this perspective, the simplest example of a complex electromagnetic field is one which is purely
self-dual
: a field in which only the self-dual field strength is non-zero. Any such field automatically solves the vacuum Maxwell equations (as they become equivalent to the Bianchi identity), but self-dual field configurations are in fact a classical integrable system, and can be described by free analytic data
Ward 1977
;
Belavin and Zakharov 1978
;
Mason and Woodhouse 1991
.
This hidden integrability makes self-dual fields remarkably tractable backgrounds, encoding interesting information about limits of vacuum amplitudes
Adamo et al. 2021
and enabling the determination of exact wavefunctions and even higher-loop and high-multiplicity scattering amplitudes in several examples in both gauge theory and gravity
Dunne and Schubert 2002a
;
Dunne and Schubert 2002b
;
Dunne and Schubert 2002c
;
Adamo et al. 2020
;
Adamo et al. 2023a
;
Adamo et al. 2023b
;
Bogna and Mason 2023
;
Adamo et al. 2024a
;
Adamo et al. 2024b
;
Garner and Paquette 2024
;
Dixon and Morales 2024a
;
Dixon and Morales 2024b
;
Bittleston et al. 2024
. This motivates us to consider
self-dual fields
as models of depleting focussed beams.

From plane waves to flying-focus.

Our first goal is to construct a flying focus beam with self-dual, positive frequency part. This will, following the above, represent a focussed beam completely depleted during some interaction (to be chosen later). We work throughout in lightfront coordinates
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}=(x^{0}\pm x^{3})/\sqrt{2}
,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=(x^{1},x^{2})
.

It is easily checked that any function of the form

\frac{f(\sigma)}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\;,\qquad\sigma:=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}-\frac{ik}{2}\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\;,

(3)

with
k>0
constant, is a solution of the wave equation in vacuum. Taking
f(\sigma)\propto\exp(-i\omega\sigma)
, for frequency
\omega>0
gives a scalar flying focus wave with Gaussian focussing, in which the focus moves in the opposite direction to the phase velocity. There are many ways to extend (
3
) to a solution of Maxwell’s equations
Sezginer 1985
;
Ramsey et al. 2023
with desired properties
Formanek et al. 2023
.
We add to this the following simple method.
Following
Tod 1982
;
Berman et al. 2019
, define the ‘spin raising’ operator (vector indices in order
+,-,\perp
)

{\hat{O}}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\partial_{1}+i\partial_{2},0,\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle-},i\partial_{\scriptscriptstyle-})\;.

(4)

Acting with (
4
) on (
3
) produces, as can be checked directly, a self-dual solution of the vacuum Maxwell equations. (Acting twice produces a self-dual
metric
perturbation solving the vacuum Einstein equations, allowing us to extend our results to scattering in gravity
Adamo and Ilderton
.)
The class of electromagnetic fields obtained in this way is

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}=\frac{a(\sigma)}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big(-ik\frac{\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}},0,\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\Big)\;,

(5)

in which
a(\sigma)
is arbitrary and
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=(1,i)/\sqrt{2}
.
For the particular choice

a(\sigma)=\frac{E_{0}}{\omega}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-i\omega\sigma}\,,

(6)

(
5
) is, as desired, the positive frequency self-dual part of a circularly polarised flying focus beam of frequency
\omega
, focal width
w_{0}=\sqrt{\omega k}
and peak electric field strength
E_{0}/2
.
Thus,
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
with
a(\sigma)
given by (
6
) represents a depleting flying focus beam. For now, we will leave
a(\sigma)
arbitrary, in which case the more general solution (
5
) is sometimes called a ‘focus wave’
Hillion 1992
.

It is worth briefly exploring (
5
) from another angle. Noting that
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=0
, we return to the Fourier representation of an arbitrary vacuum solution (
2
), and work from here on in lightfront gauge
\varepsilon^{s}_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(\ell)=0
, for which
\varepsilon^{s}_{\mu}=\big((\ell_{1}+is\ell_{2})/\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-},0,1,is\big)/\sqrt{2}
. A real (fixed) plane wave is given by the choice
\beta_{s}(\ell)=\alpha_{s}(\ell)\sim\delta^{2}(\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp})\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}{a}_{s}(\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-})
for some temporal wave profile
a_{s}
. The focussed fields we consider are given simply by: (i) restricting to only positive helicity
s=1
, and (ii) broadening the transverse delta functions of the plane wave Fourier coefficients into a Gaussian of finite width
Sezginer 1985
.

This procedure is not unique, though: if the width is chosen to be momentum-independent, one obtains a pulsed flying focus solution, but the
\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
integral in (
2
) cannot typically be evaluated to obtain explicit expressions for the position-space fields, see
Di Piazza et al. 2024
. If instead the width goes like
k\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
then the
\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
-integrals in (
2
) are Gaussian and the
\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
-integral is a Fourier transform, yielding
A_{\mu}=\mathcal{A}_{\mu}+\text{c.c.,}
with
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
precisely as in (
5
). For the flying focus beam (
6
)
a(\ell)
is a momentum space delta function, so both broadening methods are equivalent. Details will appear in
Adamo and Ilderton
, the important point is just that a flying focus beam is a deformation of a plane wave by the parameter
k
, so comparison with the plane wave case will be immediate upon taking the
k\to 0
limit.
This will considerably aid the physical interpretation of our results.

Particle wavefunctions in flying-focus beams.

The calculation of amplitudes on a chosen background typically begins with the construction of wavefunctions describing initial and final-state particles. We make two simplifications which allow us to focus on the key message and present compact results. First, we assume that the scattered particles are ultrarelativistic, so that their mass can be neglected, but we make no assumption on the relative strength of the flying focus background, so our calculations apply equally to strong and weak fields. Second, we work in scalar, rather than spinor, QED.

As such, the desired wavefunctions are solutions of the Klein-Gordon equation
D^{2}\phi=0
, where the covariant derivative is
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-ie\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
for chosen background
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
. A famous example is the Volkov solution for (real) plane wave backgrounds
Wolkow 1935
. We present here the solution in the depleting flying focus, i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
given by (
5
): an incoming (scalar) electron, momentum
p_{\mu}
, is described by

\phi(x)=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-iS}}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\;,

(7)

in which
S
is, writing
{\mathsf{p}}\equiv\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
,

S=\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}+p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}\sigma+\frac{g{\mathsf{p}}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\int\limits^{\sigma}\!{\mathrm{d}}s\,a(s)\;.

(8)

This wavefunction is remarkably simple, considering that it describes a particle interacting with, and absorbing energy from, a focussed field. The Volkov solution for a (depleting) plane wave is recovered as
k\to 0
. The wavefunction for an outgoing electron, momentum
q_{\mu}
, is obtained as usual by solving the conjugate equation
{\bar{D}}^{2}\bar{\phi}=0
where
{\bar{D}}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+ie\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
(note the background is not conjugated). The solution is given simply by sending
p_{\mu}\to-q_{\mu}
in (
8
).

These wavefunctions have familiar properties: for instance, it is easily checked that
S
is a Hamilton-Jacobi action obeying
(\partial S+e\mathcal{A})^{2}=0
, and that the current

j_{\mu}(x)={\bar{\phi}}D_{\mu}\phi-\phi{\bar{D}}_{\mu}{\bar{\phi}}\;,

(9)

is conserved as expected,
\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}(x)=0
.

Nonlinear Compton Scattering.

Having analytic wavefunctions in a depleting field opens the door to a host of new calculations. We show that these are tractable for flying focus beams by calculating the amplitude for
nonlinear Compton scattering; that is, photon emission from a charge interacting with an intense laser. This fundamental strong-field QED process
Gonoskov et al. 2022
;
Fedotov et al. 2023
is a target of past
Bula et al. 1996
and future experiments
Abramowicz et al. 2021
. We consider an electron scattering from momentum
p_{\mu}
to
q_{\mu}
on a flying focus beam, depleting it, and emitting a photon, momentum
\ell_{\mu}
and helicity
s
. The Feynman rules for scalar QED yield the corresponding scattering amplitude as

S_{fi}=e\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,\mathrm{e}^{i\ell\cdot x}{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{\mu}_{s}(\ell)j_{\mu}(x)\;,

(10)

in which
j_{\mu}(x)
is the conserved current (
9
).

Our goal is to perform the integrals in (
10
). The apparent additional difficulty that all quantities become complex points, happily, to its own resolution: we can exploit the possibility of deforming the integrals in the complex plane. We begin by displacing the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
contour by the non-trivial part of
\sigma
, and integrating along the line

x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\to x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}+\frac{ik}{2}\frac{x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}{1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\,,\quad x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(11)

This is valid provided the new contour does not cross any singularities of
a(\sigma)
– for the flying focus beam (
6
), we only need to avoid the pole at
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=i/k
, and we will see shortly that this is easy to control.
Due to the photon wavefunction in (
10
), this choice of contour
renders the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
–integrals
Gaussian
. We therefore proceed by rotating the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
contours in the complex plane, effectively changing variable to
y^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}/(1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+})
.
The transverse integrals then produce

(2\pi)^{2}\,\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{(q+\ell-p)_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}}{2k\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}}{2k\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}\pi}=:{\hat{\delta}}^{2}_{\text{reg.}}(q+\ell-p)\;.

(12)

As the notation suggests, this is a regulated delta function which, in the plane wave limit
k\to 0
, becomes
(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}(q+\ell-p)
, recovering transverse momentum conservation. Thus we see that the same Gaussian spread which turns a plane wave into a flying focus also generates a Gaussian spread in scattered particle momenta.

Next, the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
integral can also be performed exactly, by changing variables from
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
to
z=x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}/(1+ikx^{\scriptscriptstyle+})
, and integrating along real
z
. The singularity at
i/k
in the complex
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
-plane then becomes the point at infinity in the complex
z
-plane, and the integral over
z
gives (
2\pi
times) a delta function setting

q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\to q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}^{\star}:=p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-\frac{(p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}-q_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp})^{2}}{2\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\;,

(13)

for the outgoing electron momentum.

At this stage, only the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
integral remains and should be expected (as in a plane wave) to only be tractable for certain field profiles
a(\sigma)
.
Nevertheless, independent of the form of
a(\sigma)
, the amplitude for emission of a
negative
helicity photon is a boundary term, which vanishes upon regularisation
Boca and Florescu 2009
.
This is physically reasonable, since the electron has absorbed only
positive
helicity photons from the background.
Thus, only the amplitude for emission of a positive helicity photon is non-trivial; this is given by, writing
\hat{\delta}(x):=2\pi\delta(x)
,

\begin{split}S_{fi}=2ie\,\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}&\hat{\delta}(q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}^{\star}){\hat{\delta}}^{2}_{\text{reg.}}(q+\ell-p)\\
&\times\bigg[\frac{\mathsf{p}\,q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-\mathsf{q}\,p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{(\mathsf{p}-\mathsf{q})^{2}}\bigg]\int\!{\mathrm{d}}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\,\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}\;,\end{split}

(14)

where all dependence on the wave profile is contained in

\Phi(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})=(\ell+q-p)_{\scriptscriptstyle-}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}+e\Big[\frac{\mathsf{q}}{q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}-\frac{\mathsf{p}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big]\int^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\!\!{\mathrm{d}}s\,a(s)\;.

(15)

For the flying focus beam (
6
), we can perform the remaining integral in (
14
). To do so one simply expands the field-dependent part of the exponential as a power series, upon which the integral immediately returns

\int\!{\mathrm{d}}x\,\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi(x)}=\\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\delta}((\ell+q-p)_{\scriptscriptstyle-}-n\omega)}{n!}\bigg(\frac{eE_{0}}{\omega^{2}}\Big[\frac{\mathsf{q}}{q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}-\frac{\mathsf{p}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big]\bigg)^{n}\;.

(16)

Thus, exactly as for a monochromatic plane wave (depleting
Berestetskii et al. 1982
or not), the nonlinear Compton amplitude splits into a sum of ‘harmonic’ contributions, in which the lightfront momentum transfer is a multiple of the driving field frequency
\omega
. (Considering a finite wave-train instead of a genuinely monochromatic field broadens this delta comb into a multi-slit interference pattern, see
Heinzl et al. 2010
.)

Here, in contrast to
real
, non-depleting plane wave backgrounds, there is no ‘mass-shift’
Kibble 1965
in our wavefunctions, amplitude, or harmonics, for either the depleting flying focus or depleting plane wave. This is physically reasonable – the mass shift imprint in the photon spectrum is inherited from periodicity in the background field
Kibble 1965
;
Harvey et al. 2012
, but this periodicity must be lost as the background is depleted.

Harmonic spectra.

The simplest physical observables to extract from the amplitude (
14
)–(
16
) are the allowed emitted photon frequencies. These are determined by the support of the delta functions in (
14
) and (
16
), as is familiar from the plane wave case
Berestetskii et al. 1982
. We solve

\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}+q_{\scriptscriptstyle-}-p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=n\omega\;,

(17)

for integer
n
, with
q
evaluated on the support of the delta functions, taking
p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=0
but
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\not=0
, corresponding to a head-on collision for simplicity. In the plane wave limit, momentum conservation fixes
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=-\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
and
q_{\scriptscriptstyle+}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
(with or without depletion). Converting back to Cartesian coordinates for clarity, (
17
) then determines the emitted photon frequency
\ell_{0}
as a function of the scattering angle
\theta
:

\ell_{0}\to\ell_{\text{pw}}=\frac{n\sqrt{2}\omega}{1-\cos\theta+\frac{n\omega}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}(1+\cos\theta)}\;,

(18)

as is well known. In a flying focus beam, on the other hand, the lack of transverse momentum conservation, (
12
), means that the emitted photon frequency in each harmonic is
not
uniquely determined by scattering angle. We therefore consider, in the spirit of recent experiments
Cole et al. 2018
;
Poder et al. 2018
;
Los et al. 2024
, observations of both particles and emitted photons, scattered in particular directions. Again for a head-on collision, we select electron momenta of the form
q_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=-\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}+r_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
with
r_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=0
; that is, electrons scattered into kinematic regimes impossible in the plane wave case. The exponential suppression factor (
12
) (which is what replaces the transverse delta functions) effectively imposes the restriction
r_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}\lesssim 2k\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
. Taking this as an upper bound and solving (
17
), we find that the emitted frequencies lie between
\ell_{\text{pw}}
and

\bigg(1-\frac{k}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\bigg)\ell_{\text{pw}}\;.

(19)

This is illustrated in Fig.
1
, upper panel.
Other kinematics and observation angles allow richer structures. For example, we could also look for photons scattered in the same transverse directions as in the plane wave case, but with other energies, such that

q_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\in\left[-\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\bigg(1-\sqrt{\frac{k}{\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}\bigg),-\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{k}{\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}\bigg)\right]\;,

(20)

in which the range is again effectively imposed by (
12
). In this case (
17
) reduces to a quadratic equation, the solutions to which are plotted in Fig.
1
, lower panel.

Conclusions.

Accounting for beam depletion in laser-matter interactions can open the door to the inclusion of focussing effects, via self-dual backgrounds. We demonstrated this explicitly for flying focus beams,
providing exact particle wavefunctions in the depleting beam, despite the fact that the analogue of Volkov solutions for the undepleted background are not known. Furthermore, we used these wavefunctions to obtain the nonlinear Compton amplitude in the depleting flying focus beam, and extracted physical information on the emitted photon spectrum.

Since depleting fields are described by complex backgrounds in our approach, computational details, such as the choice of integration contours in the complex plane, depend crucially on properties of the field – but this is not dissimilar to the case of real backgrounds, where different methods are used for e.g. monochromatic
Berestetskii et al. 1982
constant crossed
Ritus 1985
and pulsed plane waves
Boca and Florescu 2009
;
Seipt et al. 2016
. We have in any case seen that despite having a complex background, our wavefunctions and amplitude are easy to analyse and have sensible properties.

We note that Particle-In-Cell simulations can also address the challenges of back-reaction and focussing, but benchmarking against theory is still required, and several implementation questions remain open
Gonoskov et al. 2015
;
Gonoskov et al. 2022
.

Turning to future work, we observe that the complete-depletion amplitude for scattering without emission vanishes in our approach, as is evident from its Feynman diagram expansion: momentum conservation forbids an electron from only absorbing photons, it must also emit. Again, this is physically sensible and
independent
of loop order, suggesting that the inclusion of depletion effects may have implications for the Ritus-Narozhnyi conjecture that higher-loop corrections in background fields exhibit a power-law growth with increasing field intensity
Ritus 1970
;
Narozhnyi 1980
;
Fedotov 2017
. We will explore this elsewhere. It would also be good to relax the idealisations we made in order to demonstrate the essential ideas here, e.g. neglecting spin, and to explore other focussed beam models.
