Reinforced Generation of Combinatorial Structures: Applications to Complexity Theory
Ansh Nagda, Prabhakar Raghavan, Abhradeep Thakurta

Abstract
We explore whether techniques from AI can help discover new combinatorial structures that improve on known limits on efficient algorithms. Specifically, we use AlphaEvolve (an LLM coding agent) to study two settings: a) Average-case hardness for MAX-CUT and MAX-Independent Set: We improve a recent result of Kunisky and Yu to obtain near-optimal upper and (conditional) lower bounds on certification algorithms for MAX-CUT and MAX-Independent Set on random 3- and 4-regular graphs. Our improved lower bounds are obtained by constructing nearly extremal Ramanujan graphs on as many as 163 163 nodes, using AlphaEvolve. Additionally, via analytical arguments we strengthen the upper bounds to settle the computational hardness of these questions up to an error in the third decimal place. b) Worst-case Hardness of Approximation for MAX-k-CUT: We obtain new inapproximability results, proving that it is NP-hard to approximate MAX- ​ 4 ​ -CUT \textup{MAX-}{4}\textup{-CUT} and MAX- ​ 3 ​ -CUT \textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT} within factors of 0.987 0.987 and 0.9649 0.9649 respectively, using AlphaEvolve to discover new gadget reductions. Our MAX- ​ 4 ​ -CUT \textup{MAX-}{4}\textup{-CUT} result improves upon the SOTA of 0.9883 0.9883 , and our MAX- ​ 3 ​ -CUT \textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT} result improves on the current best gadget-based inapproximability result of 0.9853 0.9853 , but falls short of improving the SOTA of 16 / 17 16/17 that relies on a custom PCP, rather than a gadget reduction from “standard” Håstad-style PCPs. A key technical challenge we faced: verifying a candidate construction produced by AlphaEvolve is costly (often requiring exponential time). In both settings above, our results were enabled by using AlphaEvolve itself to evolve the verification procedure to be faster (sometimes by 10,000 × 10,000\times ). We conclude with a discussion of norms by which to assess the assistance from AI in developing proofs.

Speeding up verification in AlphaEvolve:
The constructions we search for in both problems are undirected graphs.

However in studying the NP-Hardness of approximation for

We began with a code snippet containing a brute-force implementation of the verifier, using AlphaEvolve to iteratively improve its runtime. The main challenge we faced was ensuring that the evolved snippets were correct, in the sense of being functionally equivalent to the original verifier. We found that the following two ideas sufficed to achieve large speedups while maintaining correctness.

•

We used prior code snippets
\mathbf{C}_{\texttt{old}}
, whose correctness has already been verified, to evaluate synthetically generated gadgets. We compare the outputs of a new candidate
\mathbf{C}_{\texttt{new}}
to this dataset to ensure that it is also correct.

•

We used a separate LLM as a judge to ensure that all candidates
\mathbf{C}_{\texttt{new}}
are correct implementations of the verifier.

We prompted AlphaEvolve to improve the execution time of the verifier, while ensuring that it passes the two correctness tests above. To our surprise, AlphaEvolve improved the running time by

\Omega(k^{m})

We emphasize that our theorems do not depend on the correctness of these improved verifiers; even though our AlphaEvolve computations internally use them, the gadgets found by AlphaEvolve were verified using a brute-force algorithm that explicitly checks all
\Omega(k^{m})

Average Case Hardness for Random Graphs

A central problem in average case complexity is that of
certification
, where our goal is to certify a property of inputs from some distribution
\mu=\mu_{n}
over a sample space
\Omega=\Omega_{n}
. In particular, let
P:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
be a
property
such that
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{x\sim\mu}[P(x)=1]=1-o_{n}(1)
. We are given as input a sample
x\sim\mu
, and the goal is to efficiently certify that
x
satisfies
P
with high probability.

Definition 3.1
.

An efficient certificate of
P
is any property
P^{\prime}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
that is computable in polynomial time such that the following conditions hold.

1.

P^{\prime}(G)=1
implies
P(G)=1
.

2.

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{G\sim G(n,d)}[P^{\prime}(G)=1]=1-o_{n}(1)
.

We focus specifically on the setting of
refutation
, where
P
is a co-NP property. This setting has seen considerable activity, leading to innovation in various algorithmic techniques such as spectral and sum-of-squares algorithms, along with the development of a variety of methods to establish hardness (for instance,
[
4
,
35
,
3
,
26
,
25
]
).

In particular, we will consider the problems of certifying properties of
sparse random graphs
, where
\mu=G(n,d)
is the uniform distribution over random
d
-regular graphs for some fixed constant
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The main question we consider is the following: what is the strongest upper bound one can efficiently certify on the
MAX-CUT
(or
MAX-IND-SET

d
-regular graph?

Definition 3.2

(Max-Cut fraction, Max-Independent Set fraction)
.

For an undirected graph
G

\textup{MC}(G)
is defined as the fraction of edges in a maximum cut of
G
.
Similarly,
\textup{IS}(G)
is defined as the fraction of vertices in a maximum independent set of
G

Definition 3.3
.

\sigma^{\textup{MC}}_{d}
is the infimum over all
\sigma
such that the property
\mathbf{1}\{\textup{MC}(G)\leq\sigma\}
has an efficient certificate.
Similarly,
\sigma^{\textup{IS}}_{d}
is the infimum over all
\sigma
such that
\mathbf{1}\{\textup{IS}(G)\leq\sigma\}
has an efficient certificate.

There is evidence that this is a nontrivial problem exhibiting a
certification gap

\textup{MC}(G)
concentrates around a constant value strictly smaller than
\sigma^{\textup{MC}}_{d}
for
G\sim G(n,d)

[
3
,
26
]

\sigma^{\textup{MC}}_{d}
and
\sigma^{\textup{IS}}_{d}

[

Definition 3.4

(Ramanujan graphs)

Let

Comments on finding near-optimal lower bounds with AlphaEvolve.

We conclude this section with some remarks on our methodology in
Theorem

3.1
, and our results. The constructions given in
[
26
]
for
d\in\{3,4\}
are graphs on up to
10
vertices that were generated by computer-assisted experimentation. We were able to replicate their results by randomly sampling a large number of regular graphs, albeit with the post-hoc knowledge of the number of self-loops in their construction.

Improving their lower bounds necessitates constructions on many more vertices, as the granularity of
\textup{MC}(G)
or
\textup{IS}(G)
is limited by the size of
G
. At this scale, this approach of randomly sampling constructions does not work for two reasons: (1) the space of
d
-regular
n
-vertex graphs blows up combinatorially, meaning random sampling will not find interesting “extremal” graphs, and (2) the complexity of computing
\textup{MC}(G)
or
\textup{IS}(G)
is exponential in
n
, so it is hard to even compute the bound for a construction.

As stated before, we used AlphaEvolve as a tool to search for finite Ramanujan graphs witnessing the bounds in
Theorem

3.1
. We circumvent the above limitation by searching over the space
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{n}\times 2^{[n]}
, where
\Omega_{n}
is the set of
n
-vertex
d
-regular graphs. That is, we search directly for a pair
(G,S)
, where
G
is a graph and
S
is a cut (or independent set) in
G
, scoring the pair as
\texttt{score}(G,S)=-\infty
if
G
is not Ramanujan, and
\texttt{score}(G,S)={|\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})|}/{|E(G)|}
otherwise. Here,
\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})
is the set of edges crossing the cut
(S,\bar{S})
in
G
. This gives an efficiently computable score function that lower bounds
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{MC}}
(or
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{IS}}
). Consequently, AlphaEvolve isn’t limited to the space of small graphs. Indeed, it outputs constructions on as many as
163
vertices (for
\gamma_{4}^{\textup{IS}}
).

For
d=3
for
MAX-CUT
, AlphaEvolve recovered the existing bound
\gamma_{3}^{\textup{MC}}\geq 17/18
of
[
26
]
, but wasn’t able to improve on it. We note that for the sake of conciseness we restricted ourselves to the
d\in\{3,4\}
cases. We expect our techniques to achieve similar improvements over known bounds on
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{MC}}
and
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{IS}}
(
[
3
,
23
]
) for all reasonably small values of

except when

is an odd perfect square, when
[
3
]
already gives the optimal lower bound.

Gadget based NP-Hardness for Approximating MAX-3-CUT

The field of
approximation algorithms

[
39
]
is concerned with computing rigorous worst-case approximations of functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that are hard to compute exactly. In particular, in
hardness of approximation

[
1
]
the main goal is to understand when such approximations are computationally hard, i.e., to prove that even approximately computing
f(x)
is hard. We work within perhaps the simplest and most well-studied framework of Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs).

Definition 4.1

(CSP)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, and let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
be a collection of predicates over
\Sigma
, that is,
P_{i}:\Sigma^{\ell_{i}}\to\{0,1\}
for some
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. We define
\textup{CSP}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{r})
to be the collection of instances
I:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of the form

where for each

c_{j}
is a clause, i.e., it takes the form
c_{j}(\mathbf{x})=P_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{a_{1}},\mathbf{x}_{a_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{a_{\ell_{i}}})
for some
i\in[r]
and distinct
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell_{i}}\in[n]
.

In the Max-CSP setting, the input is a description of an instance
I
of a particular CSP, and the goal is to compute the
maximum
number of constraints that can be satisfied. In other words, we are trying to approximately compute the function
f(I)=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma^{n}}I(\mathbf{x})
. It will be useful to cast the approximation problem as a decision problem, as we do below by introducing the notion of
(c,s)
-approximation.

Definition 4.2

(
(c,s)
-approximation,
\alpha
-approximation)
.

Given a constraint satisfaction problem
\textup{CSP}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{r})
and constants
0\leq s\leq c\leq 1
, the
(c,s)
approximation problem for
\textup{CSP}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{r})
is the problem of, given an
n

I=\sum_{j\in[m]}c_{j}
, deciding whether (1)
\max_{x}I(x)\geq c\cdot m
or (2)
\max_{x}I(x)\leq s\cdot m
. Similarly for
0<\alpha\leq 1
, the
\alpha

such that
I(x^{\prime})\geq\alpha\cdot\max_{x}I(x)
.

In particular, a
s/c
-approximation algorithm for the Max-CSP problem gives a
(c,s)
-approximation algorithm. The main driving question can be stated as “given a CSP, what are all pairs
0\leq s\leq c\leq 1
such that there is an efficient
(c,s)
approximation for the CSP?”

By now, we have a strong qualitative understanding of this question for all CSPs under the Unique Games Conjecture
[

[
19

7

Gadget reductions
provide a methodical framework to find reductions between problems. Specifically in the context of inapproximability, let
\textup{CSP}_{\text{source}}
be a problem with known inapproximability, and suppose we would like to prove inapproximability for another problem
\textup{CSP}_{\text{target}}

\textup{CSP}_{\text{source}}
to
\textup{CSP}_{\text{target}}

\textup{CSP}_{\text{source}}

\textup{CSP}_{\text{target}}

We use AlphaEvolve to find such gadget reductions from the well-understood CSP

Developing a Search Framework for Soundness and Completeness Guarantees

4.2
Faster Verification via AlphaEvolve to Explore Larger Gadgets

The main challenge with finding large gadgets is that the cost of scoring a gadget scales exponentially in the number of variables; computing the completeness and soundness parameters described in
B.1
essentially amount to solving an instance of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
, which requires exponential time.
Even for
k=3
, AlphaEvolve slows down significantly when searching for gadgets of size as few as
11
with a brute force
\textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT}
solver.

This problem does not have an off-the-shelf solution in the form of existing fast solvers; it is unlikely that existing solvers
[
11
,
29
]
can be repurposed to solve
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
. To solve this issue, we used AlphaEvolve itself to speed up a naive brute force implementation of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
, scoring a candidate implementation by runtime and correctness. In order to calculate the runtime, we created a synthetic dataset of a wide variety of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
instances, drawn from
20
random models with varying amounts of planted structure. We then tasked AlphaEvolve to maximize the number of variables
m
for which the solver requires at most one second on average to solve instances of size
m
from our dataset.

The biggest challenge was ensuring that AlphaEvolve does not cheat and find an
incorrect
solver that is much faster. As mentioned before, we achieved correctness by (1) checking that the solver is correct on our synthetic dataset, and (2) using a separate judge LLM to certify that a candidate solver is correct. Both of these techniques were individually too lenient to avoid incorrect solvers, but we found by human inspection that they were enough in combination.

We note that once
m
is large enough, it is not possible to label our dataset with the “ground truth” scores using a brute-force implementation (which is guaranteed to be correct). Instead, we inductively rely on the correctness of previous solvers produced by AlphaEvolve (which have already passed the above correctness checks) that are fast enough to provide labels for large
m
.

We remark that we did this separately for the
k=3
and
k=4
cases, obtaining solvers that are optimized to each particular problem. We found that systems-level improvements were most important to the
k=3
case, with the final solver offloading the main
O(3^{m})
time computation to a highly optimized tensor contraction operation in numpy, and only performing slow python-based computation for
O(3^{m/3})
time. This solver resulted in a
10,000\times
speedup for instances of size
m=14
.

As for the
k=4
case, the final solver used a more sequential branch-and-bound strategy along with some systems-level improvements. At
m=19
, this provided a
10,000\times
speedup against even a numba-accelerated brute force solver.

4.3
Comparison to other computational techniques

We now survey some other computational techniques to find gadgets and discuss why they appear to be infeasible at the scale of our specific problems, even for the simpler setting of
\textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT}
.

The most straightforward method is the TSSW framework
[
36
]
, which casts the task of finding the optimal gadget as a linear program. The main difficulty in doing this is the presence of existential quantifiers in computing the completeness of the gadget. In order to eliminate these quantifiers,
[
36
]
canonicalize the auxiliary variables in the gadget. As a result, the size of the LP encoding the optimal gadget is doubly exponential in the number of satisfying assignments of the source predicate; this is
3^{3^{6}}
for a reduction from
3\textup{LIN}({3})
to
\textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT}
, which is computationally infeasible. Sometimes (as is the case for our
I_{0}
gadget for
\textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT}
) it is possible to argue that not all auxiliary variables are required, leading to a more tractable LP, but it is not clear that this is possible outside of very special cases.

If one wants to fix the number of variables in the gadget to a particular constant smaller than
3^{3^{6}}
(like
14
for our gadgets), it is possible to write a mixed integer program (MIP) instead of an LP by encoding the existential constraints using integer variables. For example, even solving the MIP with all but one existential constraint eliminated took
10
hours
10
10

10

This experiment was done with Krishnamurthy (Dj) Dvijotham, Google DeepMind.
. (We used the SCIP solver, with a direct encoding of the problem.) Consequently the MIP approach also seems infeasible with state-of-the-art solvers.

5
Discussion on AI-assisted Mathematics and Complexity Theory

We begin this section with a discussion of the norms by which to understand how AI can assist research in the mathematical sciences.
We then discuss the role of AlphaEvolve in achieving the results in this paper, highlighting how our work differs from prior work in computer-assisted theorem proving.

In studying the role of AI in assisting mathematical discovery, we must consider at least four scenarios:

1.

A human invokes a language model to summarize the state of prior art, to chart a research plan towards new theorems, or to directly generate fragments of (or entire) proofs.

2.

A human uses AI-derived tools such as AlphaEvolve to generate better proof elements (gadgets, graphs).

3.

A human uses custom code independent of AI to discover better proof elements.

4.

A human discovers the same or better proof elements by hand.

The idea that LLMs can usefully generate summaries of prior literature in a field has been mooted about for some time
[
38
,
40
,
22
,
15
]
, and indeed our experience confirmed this on a number of prompts. The results (modulo occasional hallucination) are a good starting point for deeper exploration and understanding. Interestingly, across many examples we could rapidly generate an overview of the art in an unfamiliar field; we believe this capability will increasingly be used by scientists working across fields so that — for instance — an algebraist can come up to speed with Ramsey Theory. We believe that in time, this will lead to more fluent cross-pollination across disciplines.
We have not yet been able to prompt an LLM into providing a usable research plan to obtain new results, and this is the domain of deeper efforts in this space e.g., Google’s Co-Scientist
[
17
]
program.

As mentioned in
Section

1
, there have been prior efforts to generate proofs for open mathematical statements via prompting an LLM directly
[
32
,
6
,
31
,
8
]
. However, this approach has seen limited success to date. In some cases an LLM was indeed able to generate the complete and correct proof of a previously unproven statement
[
6
]
(although it is possible that a persistent human could perhaps have derived this), but in many cases
[
32
,
31
,
8
]
the LLM could only generate a proof sketch, which ultimately had to be filled in by humans.
In general, these proofs/sketches currently require a human to verify correctness. Indeed, our
natural attempts
to prompt a standard LLM into directly generating the kinds of combinatorial structures in this paper met with failure
11
11

11

For example, GPT5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro could not generate the
16/17
-NP-hardness gadget for MAX-CUT, even though the gadget is explicitly mentioned in
[
36
, Figure 4.1]
— the prompt being
Can you generate the gadget that gives the construction for
16/17
approximation of MAX-CUT under NP hardness?
. While this may become feasible as LLM reasoning capabilities improve
[
27
]
, a formal comparison is beyond the scope of this paper. It is also worth re-emphasizing that the proof constructions generated by AI in this paper
always come with a certificate of correctness
which can be verified computationally. So, as long as the verification code is sound, there is no further need for human scrutiny.

Next we discuss whether a human could have discovered the combinatorial structures in our proofs by hand. It is conceivable that a human expert or a highly customized computational solver could eventually find these solutions. However, their discovery is likely beyond the reach of simple ”pencil-and-paper” methods, and standard SMT/MIP solvers failed to produce them.

In both the problems we study, computer assisted methods
[
36
,
19
,
26
]
have been used previously.
Kunisky and Yu
[
26
]
used computational methods to generate
d
-regular Ramanujan graphs (for
d\in\{3,4\}
) with large cut values (or independent sets). While they do not specify their computational approach, we could replicate their results by random sampling of
d
-regular graphs, and testing for the properties by brute force.
For reasons mentioned in
Section

3
,
[
26
]
could demonstrate their lower bounds for
n\leq 8
, whereas the graphs we find go up to
n=163
.

For the NP-hardness gadget reduction in
Section

4
, both the soundness and the completeness constraints can be translated into a linear program via skolemization
[
36
]
. However, the size of such a linear program is doubly exponential in the number of nodes in the constraint graph. As a result, even for
\textup{MAX-}{3}\textup{-CUT}
, running a linear program (LP) with the
canonical number of variables

[
36
]

\approx 3^{3^{6}}
becomes infeasible with the standard LP solvers we know of. One can directly attack the non-convex program using MIP/SMT solvers, but with the number of constraints being exponential in the number of variables
n\geq 14
, they too did not seem to scale. (See
Section

4.3
for a detailed discussion.)

The best test of robustness for AI-assisted mathematical discovery is durability: whether or not over time, new results such as ours are superseded by humans (possibly with computer assistance). We note that some results in AI-assisted mathematics have in fact been matched or improved quickly without the use of AI
[
13
,
5
]
.

Concluding remarks:
While our experience here is limited and far from definitive, we believe some early themes are emerging.

First, AI models with agentic feedback can generate intricate mathematical constructions, sometimes exhibiting signs of reasoning. We observed this when our model produced reasoning logic in
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
to find the gadget for reducing
x+y+z=0\mod 3
to
x-y\neq 0\mod 3
. (See
Figure

6
.)

Second, language models can generate research plans and summarize the state of the art
[
17
]
. While we have not succeeded in deriving novel results from this, the capability allows non-specialists to quickly learn new domains, which we anticipate will foster greater scientific cross-pollination.

Third, we expect a growing number of proofs of the form “AI got there first” without clear evidence of the form “this couldn’t have been done without AI”. In all of these cases (and arguably, across applications of AI to science), we expect verification to be an ongoing bottleneck. We leave it as an open question whether directly prompting an LLM can replicate our findings.

Looking ahead, it is conceivable that advances in LLM reasoning
[
27
,
17
,
37
]
could be coupled with AlphaEvolve, especially in generating the initial code-snippet and more effective problem specific prompting of the LLMs used by AlphaEvolve. It is possible that our methods can be extended to other problems in extremal combinatorics. We leave exploration of those problems as future directions.

Proof of

The goal of this section is to prove improved upper bounds on
\sigma_{d}^{\textup{MC}}
and
\sigma_{d}^{\textup{IS}}
. The same upper bounds apply to
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{MC}}
and
\gamma_{d}^{\textup{IS}}
, as will be clear in the proof. We will give a detailed argument for the Max-Cut case, and sketch the (minor) changes required for Max-Independent-Set at the end.

As with previous refutation algorithms
[
23
,
18
]
, our efficient certificate will be a bound on the
spectral expansion
of a random graph
G\sim G(n,d)
, which is defined as
\lambda^{*}(G)=\max_{i>1}|\lambda_{i}|
where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of
G
. Friedman’s theorem proves that
\lambda^{*}
concentrates around
2\sqrt{d-1}
.

Theorem A.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

For all
d\in\mathbb{N}

\varepsilon>0
, with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
over
G\sim G(n,d)
, we have
\lambda^{*}(G)\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}+\varepsilon
.

Another fact we will require about random regular graphs is that they are locally treelike, in the sense that they have no short cycles. We will require a very weak version of this fact.

Definition A.1

(
d
-ary tree)
.

The
d
-ary tree of depth
L

T_{d,L}
is a rooted tree, where the root has
d
children, and all non-root vertices at distance
<L
from the root have
d-1
children.

Fact A.1
.

For all
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
over
G\sim G(n,d)
, we have that
G
’s girth greater than
2L+1
. In other words, with high probability the induced
L
-step neighborhood of every vertex in
G
is isomorphic to
T_{d,L}

Our improvement on Hoffman’s classical bounds consists of concluding stronger bounds on
\textup{MC}(G)
and
\textup{IS}(G)
given that
\lambda^{*}(G)\leq\lambda
for
\lambda=2\sqrt{d-1}+\varepsilon
for locally treelike graphs
12
12

12

In fact, the same bounds can be proved for non-locally treelike graphs by applying the argument to a large random lift, which will be locally treelike with high probability.
. Let us denote by
\Omega_{d,\lambda,L}
the set of
d
-regular graphs
G
with girth
>2L+1
such that
\lambda^{*}(G)\leq\lambda
. We will give separate arguments in the
MC
and the
IS
cases; we suggest following the
MC
argument because it is slightly simpler.

We will require the notion of a
labeled

d
-ary tree, which we define below.

Definition A.2

(Labeled
d
-ary tree)
.

A
\{\pm 1\}
-labeling of
T_{d,L}
is a mapping
y
from the vertices of
T_{d,L}
to
\{\pm 1\}
.

Let
\Lambda_{d,L}
be the collection of distributions over
\{\pm 1\}
-labelings of
T_{d,L}
, where the vertices are labeled by elements of
\{\pm 1\}
. For most of the remainder of this section, we will show how we obtain our upper bounds on
\textup{MC}(G)
given
G\in\Omega_{d,\lambda,L}
. Our upper bound will be parametrized by a nonnegative integer
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Interestingly, we will exactly recover Hoffman’s bound
[
23
]
when
L=1
. The idea is essentially to write a
finite
LP relaxation of the problem of finding the supremum of the cut fraction
{|\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})|}/{|E(G)|}
over all
G\in\Omega_{d,\lambda,L}
, by projecting all the information about
G
and
S
onto “local” neighborhoods at distance
L
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1/2]
be a value that approximates
|S|/n
, in the sense that
|\alpha-|S|/n|\leq\delta
. Let
G\in\Omega_{d,\lambda,L}
be an
n
-vertex graph, and let
S\subseteq V(G)
be a cut in
G
. Define the cut vector
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
by
x_{i}=(-1)^{\mathbf{1}\{i\in S\}}
. We will associate the pair
(G,S)
with a distribution
\mu_{G,S}\in\Lambda_{d,L}
, which can be sampled from as follows: pick a random vertex
i\sim[n]
, and output the
\{\pm 1\}
-labeling of
T_{d,L}
consisting of
x
restricted to the
L
-neighborhood of
i
(which is guaranteed to be isomorphic to
T_{d,L}
because
G
has girth
>2L+1
). We will deduce some linear constraints on
\mu_{G,S}
, in terms of its pdf.

Lemma A.1

(Local Consistency Constraints)
.

The marginal distributions of
\mu_{d,L}
on the labelings of any two isomorphic subtrees of
T_{d,L}
are identical.

Proof.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be any two isomorphic subtrees of
T_{d,L}
. The marginal distribution of
T_{i}
-labelings of
\mu_{d,L}
is identical to the distribution of labelings on
T_{i}
obtained by sampling a random induced subgraph in
G
that is isomorphic to
T_{i}
, and applying the labeling given by
x
restricted to that subgraph. Since this process is agnostic to the value of
i
, the Lemma follows.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Average Value of root)
.

We have
2\alpha-1-2\delta\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S}}[y_{0}]\leq 2\alpha-1+2\delta
.

Proof.

We can directly compute
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S}}[y_{0}]=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim[n]}[x_{i}]=2|S|/n-1
. The sandwiching inequalities follow from the fact that
|\alpha-|S|/n|\leq\delta
.
∎

Lemma A.3

(Spectral Constraints)
.

The bound
\lambda^{*}(G)\leq\lambda
implies the bounds

(2\alpha-1-2\delta)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},\ell}[y_{0}\cdot y_{\ell}]\leq\left(2\alpha-1+2\delta\right)+(\lambda/d)^{L}

if
L
is even, and

(2\alpha-1-2\delta)^{2}-(\lambda/d)^{L}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},\ell}[y_{0}\cdot y_{\ell}]\leq\left(2\alpha-1+2\delta\right)^{2}+(\lambda/d)^{L}

if
L
is odd. Here
0
denotes the root, and
\ell
denotes the (random) endpoint of an
L
-step random walk in
T_{d,L}
starting from the root.

Proof.

We begin by computing

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},\ell}[y_{0}\cdot y_{\ell}]=\frac{x^{T}(A/d)^{L}x}{n}=\frac{x^{T}A^{L}x}{d^{L}\cdot n}.

Let us write
A=d\frac{\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}}{n}+\widetilde{A}
, where
d=\lambda_{1}
is the trivial eigenvalue of
A
and
\widetilde{A}
is the portion of
A
orthogonal to the trivial eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
. That is,
\widetilde{A}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Note that
\|\widetilde{A}\|_{op}\leq\lambda
. Let us proceed with our computation.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},\ell}[y_{0}\cdot y_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{L}\cdot x^{T}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}x}{d^{L}\cdot n^{2}}+\frac{x^{T}\widetilde{A}^{L}x}{d^{L}\cdot n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2|S|-n)^{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{x^{T}\widetilde{A}^{L}x}{d^{L}\cdot n}.

The Lemma follows from (1) the fact that
|\alpha-|S|/n|\leq\delta
, and (2) the bounds
|x^{T}\widetilde{A}^{L}x|\leq\|\widetilde{A}\|_{op}\cdot\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda n
, along with the bound
x^{T}\widetilde{A}^{L}x\geq 0
if
L
is even.
∎

Lemma A.4

(Objective Value)
.

The cut fraction
{|\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})|}/{|E(G)|}
equals
(1-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},i\sim[d]}[y_{0}\cdot y_{i}])/{2}
, where
0
denotes the root, and
i
denotes the
i^{th}
neighbor of the root of the
d
-regular tree of depth
L
.

Proof.

This is again a direct computation. We can write

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu_{G,S},i\sim[d]}[y_{0}\cdot y_{i}]=\frac{x^{T}Ax}{nd}=1-2\frac{|\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})|}{|E(G)|},

and rearranging gives the identity.
∎

A.1
,
A.2
and
A.3
are all linear constraints in the pdf of the distribution
\mu=\mu_{G,S}
, and
A.4
is a linear objective function. We may solve this LP in the variables
\mu
for small values of
L
and
d
to obtain valid upper bounds on objective function, the cut value
|\delta_{G}(S,\bar{S})|/|E(G)|
. If we solve this LP for a collection of discrete values
\alpha\in\{0,\delta,2\delta,\ldots,1\}
, we obtain an upper bound on the cut value of
any
cut of a graph
G\in\Omega_{d,\lambda,L}
.

Computational Efficiency.

We remark that the above LP very quickly becomes intractable – in particular, it has
2^{46}
variables even for
d=3
,
L=4
. We apply two optimizations to make it more tractable in practice.

1.

Use the same variable for isomorphic labelings of
T_{d,L}
. In particular, if there is a graph isomorphism of
T_{d,L}
that maps a labeling
y
to another labeling
y^{\prime}
, then
A.1
implies that they should receive the same probability in
\mu
.

2.

Only consider “locally optimal” labelings of
T_{d,L}
. To do this, we apply a preprocessing step, where we ensure that the cut
S
is locally optimal in the sense that for any vertex
i\in S
, the labeling of
T_{d,L}
generated by restricting
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
to the
L
-step neighborhood of
i
corresponds to a maximum cut of
T_{d,L}
, subject to the “boundary conditions” of labels of the leaves of
T_{d,L}
. Therefore, we can also discard any variables corresponding to such suboptimal labelings of
T_{d,L}
.

Recall that
\lambda=2\sqrt{d-1}+\varepsilon
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon
. We will now pick
\varepsilon=10^{-5}
. Using the above optimizations, we are able to solve the
1/\delta
LPs for
d=3,L=4
(with
4396
variables), and
d=4,L=2
(with
88
variables) for
\delta=0.0005
, resulting in the universal upper bounds
\textup{MC}(G)\leq 0.953
for
G\in\Omega_{3,\lambda,4}
and
\textup{MC}(G)\leq 0.916
for
G\in\Omega_{4,\lambda,2}
.

An interesting point is that the
d=4,L=3
case has
11880
variables and is technically within reach of current solvers, but resulted in the
same
bound on
\sigma^{\textup{MC}}_{4}
as the
d=4,L=2
case.

Upper bounds on
\textup{IS}(G)
.

We conclude with some comments on the modifications needed to obtain upper bounds on
\sigma_{d}^{\textup{IS}}
. The main modification is that we need to restrict ourselves to distributions in
\Lambda_{d,L}
that are supported only on
\{\pm 1\}
-labelings that correspond to actual Independent Sets of
T_{d,L}
. That is, we only consider labelings

T_{d,L}
,

and

are not both equal to

In this section, we give the details of the NP-hardness of approximation proofs for MAX-k-CUT for
k\in\{3,4\}
. Let us begin by formally defining a gadget, specifically in the context of the reduction from
3\textup{LIN}({k})
to
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
. We will need
k
separate gadgets. In particular, for each defining predicate
P_{i}^{=}
of
3\textup{LIN}({k})
, we will need an
i
-gadget, which we define below.

Definition B.1

(Gadgets)
.

Let
k\geq 2
and define
\Sigma=\mathbb{Z}_{k}
.

•

i
-gadget
: For
i\in\Sigma
, an
i
-gadget from
3\textup{LIN}({k})
to
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
is an instance
I_{i}
of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
over
3+k+n_{\textup{aux}}
variables, where
n_{\textup{aux}}\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

Primary, global, and auxiliary variables:
We partition the set of variables
[3+k+n_{\textup{aux}}]
into three
primary variables

\{1,2,3\}
,
k

global variables

\{4,5,\ldots,k+3\}
, and
n_{\textup{aux}}

auxiliary variables

\{k+4,\ldots,3+k+n_{\textup{aux}}\}
. We will always write an assignment to the variables as a tuple
(x,y,z)\in\Sigma^{3+k+n_{\textup{aux}}}
where
x\in\Sigma^{3}
,
y\in\Sigma^{k}
, and
z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}}
.

The variables
y
will be restricted in a way to break the symmetries of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
. Concretely, let
Y
be the set of strings
y
in
\Sigma^{k}
that are lexicographically not larger than any string
y^{\sigma}=(\sigma(y_{1}),\sigma(y_{2}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{k}))
that is obtained by permuting the alphabet
\Sigma
by some permutation
\sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma
. In particular, note that the vector
(0,1,2,\ldots,k-1)
is in
Y
. We will only ever assign
y
to values in
Y
.

Definition B.2

(Soundness and completeness)
.

We will set
C_{i}=(P_{i}^{=})^{-1}(1)
and
S_{i}=(P_{i}^{=})^{-1}(1)
to be the set of satisfying and unsatisfying assignments for the source predicate
P_{i}^{=}
respectively.

We will associate four real-valued parameters to the gadget
I_{i}=\sum_{j\in[m]}c_{j}
as follows.

•

(Completeness analysis)
c(I_{i})
is defined as
\min_{x\in C_{i}}\max_{z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}}}I_{i}(x,y,z)
for
y=(0,1,\ldots,k-1)
. For
x\in C_{i}
, we say that
\arg\max_{z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}}}I_{i}(x,y,z)
is the
witness
for
x
.

•

(Soundness analysis)
c^{\prime}(I_{i})
is defined as

c^{\prime}(I_{i})=\max_{x\in C_{i},z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}},y\in Y}I_{i}(x,y,z).

Also,
s(I_{i})
is defined as

s(I_{i})=\max_{x\in S_{i},z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}},y\in Y}I_{i}(x,y,z).

•

(Number of clauses)
t(I_{i})
is defined to be
m
, that is, the number of clauses present in
I_{i}
.

We will show that any collection of
i
-gadgets for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{k}
provides a reduction whose performance depends on the various parameters defined above. After this, in
Section

B.1
, we give an explicit description of our gadgets and state the parameters achieved by them.

The starting point of our reduction will be the following Theorem due to Håstad.

Theorem B.1

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Given an instance
I=\sum_{j\in[m]}c_{j}
of
3\textup{LIN}({k})
with
m/k
clauses from each of the predicates
P_{0}^{=},P_{1}^{=},\ldots,P_{k-1}^{=}
, it is NP-hard to distinguish between the following cases for any
\varepsilon>0
:

1.

(Completeness case)
\max_{x}I(x)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot m
, or

2.

(Soundness case)
\max_{x}I(x)\leq(1/k+\varepsilon)\cdot m
.

Theorem

B.2
details how our gadgets imply NP-hardness of approximation for
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
, with
k\in\{3,4\}
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
, and set
\Sigma=\mathbb{Z}_{k}
. Let
I_{i}
be an
i
-gadget from
3\textup{LIN}({k})
to
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
for each
i\in\Sigma
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, given an instance
J
of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
with
M
constraints, it is NP-hard to distinguish between the following two cases for any
\varepsilon>0
:

1.

(Completeness case)
\max_{x}J(x)\geq(a-\varepsilon)\cdot M
, or

2.

(Soundness case)
\max_{x}J(x)\leq(b+\varepsilon)\cdot M
,

where

a=\frac{\sum_{i\in\Sigma}c(I_{i})}{\sum_{i\in\Sigma}t(I_{i})},\quad b=\frac{c^{\prime}(I_{r_{0}})+\sum_{i\in\Sigma\setminus\{0\}}s(I_{r_{i}})}{\sum_{i\in\Sigma}t(I_{i})}.

Here
r_{0},r_{1},\ldots r_{k-1}
is a permutation of
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
such that
c^{\prime}(I_{r_{i}})-s(I_{r_{i}})
is maximized by
r_{0}
.

Proof of
Theorem

B.2
.

We will perform a reduction starting from the NP-hard problem in
Theorem

B.1
to
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
. Let
I=\sum_{j\in[m]}c_{j}
be an
n
-variable instance of
3\textup{LIN}({k})
, and suppose that it has
m/k
clauses using
P_{i}^{=}
for all
i\in\Sigma
. We will show how to reduce
I
to an instance
J
of
\textup{MAX-}{k}\textup{-CUT}
.

Let
n_{\textup{aux}}
be the number of auxiliary variables in all gadgets
I_{i}
; we can assume they all have the same number of auxiliary variables by introducing new dummy variables to some of them.
J
will be defined as an instance on
n+k+n_{\textup{aux}}\cdot m
variables, which we will denote as
x\in\Sigma^{n}
,
y\in\Sigma^{k}
, and
z\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}\cdot m}
. For
j\in[m]
we will denote the
j^{th}
block of size
n_{\textup{aux}}
in
z
as
z^{j}
. We will refer to
x,y,z
as the primary, global, and auxiliary variables respectively.

Let
c_{j}=P^{=}_{i}(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},x_{j_{3}})
be a clause corresponding to the predicate
P^{=}_{i}
for some
i
. We will add a copy of
I_{i}
on the variables
(x^{\prime},y,z^{j})
, where
x^{\prime}=(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},x_{j_{3}})
.
Note that the number of clauses in
J
is
M=(m/k)\cdot\sum_{i\in\Sigma}t(I_{i})
.

Now we will invoke
Theorem

B.1
, which says that it is NP-hard to distinguish between the cases that
I
is almost completely satisfiable, and
I
is essentially
1/k
-satisfiable.

1.

(Completeness case). Assuming there is an
x\in\Sigma^{n}
such that
I(x)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot m
, we show how to construct an assignment
(x,y,z)\in\Sigma^{n+k+n_{\textup{aux}}\cdot m}
such that
J(x,y,z)\geq a\cdot M
. We will set
y=(0,1,2,\ldots,k-1)
, and for each clause
c_{j}=P_{i}^{=}(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},x_{j_{3}})
,
z^{j}
will be set as the witness
z_{j}=\arg\max_{z^{j}\in\Sigma^{n_{\textup{aux}}}}I_{i}(x^{\prime},y,z^{j})
, where
x^{\prime}=(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}},x_{j_{3}})
.

It is immediate that
(x,y,z)
satisfies at least

\sum_{i\in\Sigma}c(I_{i})\cdot m/k-O(\varepsilon\cdot m)=a\cdot M-O(\varepsilon)\cdot m\geq(a-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot M

clauses of
J
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=O(\varepsilon)
can be chosen to be arbitrarily close to
0
.

2.

(Soundness case). We must show that if
\max_{x}I(x)\leq(1/3+\varepsilon)\cdot m
, then
\max_{x,y,z}J(x,y,z)\leq(b+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot M
. Let
(x,y,z)
be an assignment to the variables of
J
.

The first step we perform is to assume without loss of generality that
y\in Y
. To do this, note that by definition of
Y
, there is some permutation
\sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma
such that
y^{\sigma}=(\sigma(y_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(y_{k}))\in Y
. We will apply
\sigma
to all variables in the full assignment
(x,y,z)
, resulting in no change in the value of
J(x,y,z)
while guaranteeing that
y\in Y
.

For
i\in\Sigma
, let
\alpha_{i}
denote the fraction of
P^{=}_{i}
constraints of
I
that are satisfied by
x
. By assumption, we have
\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\alpha_{i}\leq 1+3\varepsilon
. Let us define
\hat{\alpha_{i}}=\alpha_{i}/(\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\Sigma}\alpha_{i^{\prime}})
, so
\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\hat{\alpha_{i}}=1
. In other words,
\{\hat{\alpha_{i}}:i\in\Sigma\}
defines a probability distribution.

On the other hand, since
y\in Y
we can now bound
J(x,y,z)
as follows.

\displaystyle J(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\alpha_{i}\cdot c^{\prime}(I_{i})+(1-\alpha_{i})\cdot s(I_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\hat{\alpha_{i}}\cdot c^{\prime}(I_{i})+(1-\hat{\alpha_{i}})\cdot s(I_{i})\right)+O(\varepsilon)\cdot m

(
\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\alpha_{i}\leq 1+3\varepsilon
)

\displaystyle=m\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in\Sigma}\hat{\alpha_{i}}\cdot(c^{\prime}(I_{i})-s(I_{i}))+s(I_{i})\right)+O(\varepsilon)\cdot m.

Note that subject to
\hat{\alpha_{i}}
being a probability distribution, this is maximized when
\hat{\alpha_{i}}
is a point mass on a maximizer of
c^{\prime}(I_{i})-s(I_{i})
. Therefore, this is bounded by

m\cdot\left(c^{\prime}(I_{r_{0}})+\sum_{i\in\Sigma\setminus\{0\}}s(I_{r_{i}})\right)+O(\varepsilon)\cdot m=(b+O(\varepsilon))\cdot M=(b+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot M,

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}
can be chosen to be arbitrarily close to
0
. This completes the proof of the soundness case.
