Oral hygiene predicts lower life satisfaction and subjective health: experience of post-Soviet countries
Authors: Tomas Vaičiūnas, Vladas Golambiauskas, Shynar Abdrakhmanova, Marina Melkumova, Eva Movsesyan, Lela Sturua, Dasha Pavlova, Assel Adayeva, Kastytis Šmigelskas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Oral diseases are among the most common non-communicable diseases around the globe and become a public health challenge that considerably impact general health and well-being in all ages across the lifespan. Oral health is an integral part of general health, where poor oral health and other lifestyle-related chronic diseases have a common risk factor background. The underlying causes of oral health inequalities are often complex and related to country-specific historical, economic, cultural, social, or political factors. For more than a decade, data from the Health Behavior in School-aged Children (HBSC) survey showed that tooth brushing rates are very low in post-Soviet countries. The general aim of our study was to focus on a more detailed profile of schoolchildren from post-Soviet countries, who have poor habits of teeth brushing, in terms of their family, social support, physical activity, nutrition, and other health-related behaviors. Data for this study was extrapolated from the Health Behavior in School-aged Children study, a World Health Organization collaborative cross-national study, conducted every 4 years since 1983/1984. For this study, the post-Soviet countries from Eastern Europe and Central Asia were selected: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Estonia, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Lithuania, Latvia, Moldova, Tajikistan, Ukraine, and Uzbekistan. Data from all countries (some exceptions for Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan) were collected within 2017–2018 HBSC study, following international research protocol. The sample consisted of 44,760 schoolchildren, aged 11, 13, and 15 years from 12 countries. Irregular tooth brushing is more common among boys, elder adolescents, and schoolchildren from less affluent families. Low own health measures, low life satisfaction, low physical activity, and low FAS relate to poorer adolescent dental hygiene in post-Soviet countries. Adolescents characterized by irregular tooth brushing were more likely to assess their health as fair or poor rather than excellent or good (OR = 1.22), and also had greater odds of reporting low life satisfaction (OR = 1.36). Individual mental health complaints—feeling low, being irritable, being nervous, and having sleep difficulties—were assessed as a possible outcome of irregular tooth brushing. However, irregular tooth brushing was not associated with poorer mental health outcomes (p > 0.05). Conclusions: Oral hygiene in post-Soviet countries is still a major public health challenge and have to be addressed properly. What is Known:• Oral health strongly affects the overall health of children and adolescents in the future.• Oral diseases often share common determinants and risk factors with other chronic diseases.• The underlying causes of oral health inequalities are often complex and related to country-specific historical, economic, cultural, social, or political factors.What is New:• Irregular tooth brushing was more common among adolescents with low life satisfaction and poor or fair health as well as in less affluent families.• Irregular tooth brushing was not found to be associated with poorer mental health among post-Soviet countries adolescents.

Introduction

Oral diseases are a public health challenge that considerably impacts general health and well-being in all ages across the lifespan. These problems are among the most common non-communicable diseases (NCDs), affecting around 3.5 billion people worldwide [1, 2]. Dental caries (tooth decay), gum disease, and periodontal disease are the most common of this kind among school-aged children and adults [3, 4]. Despite being preventable, evidence shows that oral diseases are still among the most prevalent globally, with a significant impact on an individual’s quality of life [2, 5, 6]. The overall level of oral health strongly affects the overall health of children and adolescents in their future [4, 7]. Dental caries in children cause pain, and tooth loss, and may also become a significant financial burden for the families [8]. Different studies indicate that the treatments applied for oral and dental diseases are considered the 4th expenditure even among developed countries [4, 6].

Oral health is an integral part of general health, where poor oral health and other lifestyle-related chronic diseases have common risk factors background [9]. There is strong evidence showing that oral health is associated with systemic diseases such as type-2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, endocrine or nutritional metabolic disorders, and cognitive impairment [9, 10]. Moreover, it has been found that poor oral care habits are associated with poorer psychosocial well-being and school performance [9] and even overall mortality [11]. The underlying causes of oral health inequalities are often complex and related to country-specific historical, economic, cultural, social, lifestyle-related, or even political factors [12, 13]. WHO highlights the need to focus not only on reducing disease but also on tackling its roots: regardless of socioeconomic factors and potential health inequalities, lifestyle-related habits and oral hygiene are among the most effective primary preventive measures, which have to be formed in early childhood [6, 14]. Knowledge of oral health is an essential requirement for oral disease prevention and healthy behavior [15].

Tooth brushing hygiene is different across countries, as a result of different cultural, social, health literacy, or socioeconomic factors [16]. For more than a decade, data from the Health Behavior in School-aged Children (HBSC) survey showed that tooth brushing rates are very low in post-Soviet countries: in these countries, on average, less than half of children brushed their teeth more than once a day [16, 17]. In comparison, in Western European countries, such as Switzerland, Denmark, or Sweden, more than 80% of children brushed their teeth more than once a day [16].

Our general aim in this study was to focus on a more detailed profile of the schoolchildren of post-Soviet countries who have poor teeth brushing habits in terms of their family and social support, physical activity, nutrition, and other lifestyle-related behaviors.

Methods

Sample and procedure

The HBSC (Health Behavior in School-aged Children) study is a World Health Organization collaborative cross-national study conducted every 4 years since 1983/1984. Over the years, the study expanded to more than 50 countries in Europe and North America. Every participant team followed a standardized research protocol [16] to study the health, behavior, and well-being of 11, 13, and 15-year-olds. The protocol ensured the data was representative of the intended population and consistent across the country spectrum regarding data collection, survey instruments, and data processing procedures.

For this study, the post-Soviet countries from Eastern Europe and Central Asia were selected: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Estonia, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Lithuania, Latvia, Moldova, Tajikistan, Ukraine, and Uzbekistan. Data from all countries (some exceptions for Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan) were collected within 2017–2018 HBSC study, following international research protocol. Comprehensive details on the sampling methodology and data collection procedures used in the HBSC survey are reported elsewhere [18].

Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan have participated only in the pilot HBSC study (provided according to the requirement of HBSC protocol). In the pilot regions, a minimum of 95 percent of the eligible target population was available for sampling. In Kyrgyzstan, urban and rural schools of the four northern and southern regions of the country were included in the pilot study. The sample frame consisted of 767 primary and secondary general education schools. Schools were chosen with a probability proportional to the number of children in the 5th, 7th, and 9th grades. A total of 2166 students in grades 5, 7, and 9 took part in the study. In Tajikistan, 25 schools (9 urban, 16 rural) were randomly selected for the pilot survey from the list of public schools. The total number of schoolchildren who participated in the pilot survey was 1802 (50.7% female, 36% urban schoolchildren). In Uzbekistan, 20 secondary schools (10 urban and 10 rural schools) were randomly selected. The total number of children surveyed was 1500 (51.2% female) with 500 schoolchildren per age group.

Instruments and measures

Teeth brushing

Teeth brushing was assessed by asking adolescents how often they brushed their teeth (1 = “more than once a day,” 2 = “once a day,” 3 = “once a week,” 4 = “less than weekly,” 5 = “never”). Adolescents were further identified as either brushing their teeth more than once a day (regular tooth brushing) or not (irregular tooth brushing).

Family affluence

Family affluence (FAS) was assessed using the Family Affluence Scale version III [19], which includes six items of family assets. Sum scores, varying from 0 to 13, were calculated, and the participants’ family affluence was identified as either low (the lowest 20%), medium (the middle 60%), or high (the highest 20%) affluence according to relative family affluence in their residential country.

Social support

Social support was measured in four aspects—family, friend, teacher, and peer support. Family and friend support subscales were based on the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [20]. Family support was measured using the 4-item family support subscale of the MSPSS. Adolescents had to assess on a scale from 1 (“very strongly disagree”) to 7 (“very strongly agree”) whether they get emotional help from and can talk about problems with their family and whether their family tries to help and helps make decisions. Adolescents were further identified as those with “high” (a score of 4 or higher) or “low” (a score of 3 or lower) family support.

Friend support was measured using the 4-item friend support subscale of the MSPSS. Adolescents had to assess whether they have friends to share joy with, can count on friends, talk about problems with friends, and whether their friends try to help on a scale from 1 (“very strongly disagree”) to 7 (“very strongly agree”). Adolescents were further identified as having “high” (a score of 4 or higher) or “low” (a score of 3 or lower) friend support.

Teacher support and classmate support were measured using the 3-item Teacher Support and 3-item Classmate Support Scale [21]. For teacher support, adolescents were asked whether they trust their teachers and whether their teachers accept them and care about them. For classmate support, adolescents were asked whether their classmates enjoy being together, are kind and helpful, and accept them. On each item, participants answered between 1 (“strongly agree”) and 5 (“strongly disagree”). Schoolchildren were identified as having either “high” (a score of 1–3) or “low” (a score of 4–5) teacher and peer support.

Subjective health and life satisfaction

Health factors included subjective health and life satisfaction. For health, adolescents had to answer how good they thought their health was (1 = “excellent,” 2 = “good,” 3 = “fair,” 4 = “poor”). Adolescents’ health score was then identified as “excellent or good” or “fair or poor.” Life satisfaction was measured using the Cantril ladder [22, 23]. Then, adolescents’ life satisfaction was identified as ‘high’ (a score of 6 or higher) or ‘low’ (a score of 5 or lower).

Physical activity

Physical activity was measured in two ways: moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and vigorous physical activity (VPA). MVPA was assessed by asking adolescents how many days during the last week they were engaged in at least 60 min of physical activity per day (ranging from 0 to 7 days). Following the official WHO guidelines for physical activity [24], adolescents were identified as either being physically active daily or not daily. VPA was measured by asking adolescents how often they exercise vigorously (1 = “every day,” 2 = “4–6 times per week,” 3 = “2–3 times per week,” 4 = “once a week,” 5 = “once a month,” 6 = “less than once a month,” 7 = “never”). In accordance with the official WHO guidelines for physical activity in schoolchildren [24], adolescents were identified as either being vigorously physically active 2–3 times per week and more often or being vigorously physically active less often.

Nutrition

Nutrition variables included breakfast frequency and eating habits. Breakfast frequency was measured by asking adolescents how many days they had breakfast during the weekdays and weekends (0–5 and 0–2, respectively). Participants were further identified as either having daily breakfast (all 7 days) or not. For eating habits, the frequency of eating fruits, vegetables, sweets, and drinking sugared soft drinks was assessed by asking adolescents how often they consume these products (1 = “never,” 2 = “less than once a week,” 3 = “once a week,” 4 = “2–4 days a week,” 5 = “5–6 days a week,” 6 = “once daily,” 7 = “more than once daily”). In each category, adolescents were identified as regular consumers of a specific food product group if consumed daily.

Risk behavior

Tobacco smoking was measured by asking participants how often they smoked tobacco during the last 30 days and in a lifetime. Response options were on a 4-point scale from “every day,” “at least once a week, but not every day,” “less than once a week” to “I do not smoke.”

Alcohol consumption was assessed by asking participants how often they drank anything alcoholic during the last 30 days and in a lifetime. Participants were asked to report the frequency of each type of alcohol they consumed with the following response options: “every day,” “every week,” “every month,” “rarely,” and “never.”

Drunkenness was assessed by asking participants how many times in their lifetime they drank so much alcohol that they were drunk. Response options were on a scale from “never,” “yes, once,” “yes, 2–3 times,” “yes, 4–10 times” to “yes, more than 10 times.” In this study, drunkenness among pupils in four countries was defined by comparing responses indicating drunkenness 2 or more times.

Mental health

Mental health variables included feeling low, irritable, nervous, and having sleep difficulties. Each of the variables was measured by asking adolescents how often they felt a certain way (1 = “about every day,” 2 = “more than once per week,” 3 = “about every week,” 4 = “about every month,” 5 = “rarely or never”). For each mental health variable, adolescents were identified as those either experiencing a specific feeling more often than once a week (a score of 2 or lower) or less often.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. The countries’ estimates of prevalence were calculated in total numbers (n) and percentages (%). Additionally, tooth brushing regularity (%) was calculated for each adolescent group for each variable. The likelihood of certain behavioral outcomes was evaluated through binary logistic regression analysis. Here, the odds ratios (OR) were calculated as effect size for each variable by the teeth brushing group.

First, the multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted for every potential predictor of irregular tooth brushing with adjustment for age, gender, and FAS. Then, the strongest predictors in each category of indicators were included in multivariate model to see which of them are independent from each other and have the largest effect size in statistical predicting of irregular tooth brushing. Finally, tooth brushing was analyzed as a potential risk factor for overall and mental health with adjustment for age, gender, and FAS.

Multivariate prediction of irregular tooth brushing

After bivariate regression calculations, a model was constructed from the most expressed factors in each category (Table 7). In this model, gender and FAS were identified as the strongest variables associated with irregular tooth brushing—boys and adolescents from the lowest FAS group had the highest odds for irregular tooth brushing (OR = 1.95 for both). Further, adolescents with sufficient vigorous physical activity, daily eating of breakfast, fruits, and vegetables as well as better sweets eating habits had better odds for adequate tooth hygiene (OR = 1.41, OR = 1.20, OR = 1.30, OR = 1.20, and OR = 1.21, respectively). Finally, friend support and alcohol consumption over the last 30 days were the least influential variables in the model (OR = 1.09 and OR = 1.07, respectively). Discussion

Dental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic conditions among children in many countries [25]. The reported outcomes of poor oral health in children are well-documented and range from serious health problems, such as dental abscess, to negative effects on nutrition, growth, development, and children’s school performance [26].

This is one of the first study in its kind that explored several risk factors for poor oral habits in post-Soviet countries using data of HBSC survey. Although WHO, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and other agencies recommend that tooth brushing should be performed for children two times a day, the overall prevalence of regular tooth brushing habits among study participants was 52%. A current study showed that irregular tooth brushing among children of post-Soviet countries is more common among boys and elder adolescents. Different reasons may explain this phenomenon: developmental changes occurring during adolescence, poor parenting practice, tantrums, pain during teething, or tiredness of the child. Effective parenting as positive involvement, encouragement, and problem-solving is associated with better oral hygiene and lower levels of dental caries in children [27].

The highest odds for insufficient tooth hygiene were in the lowest FAS category (OR = 2.13) and among boys (OR = 1.82). The burden of oral diseases has a disproportionate impact on the poorest and least educated members of society. Scientific evidence suggests that there is a strong association between family affluence and the prevalence and severity of oral diseases, a “social gradient in health” [6]. Socioeconomic inequalities in tooth brushing among adolescents persist in many countries and individuals of lower family affluence practice less frequent tooth brushing and have poorer oral health [28, 29].

Social support was among the social factors associated with improved oral health in adolescents [30]. Some studies found that the frequency of tooth brushing and self-rated oral health were significantly associated with increased levels of perceived social support [31] or emotional support from neighbors and teachers [32]. Other research has not proven the positive effect [33] or shown contradictory results [32]. However, in our study, family, peer, and teacher support were not significant factors for tooth hygiene, but friend support was (OR = 1.12).

Adolescent tooth brushing has been found to be multifactorial depending on lifestyle and social behavior [6, 13]. Certain foods with added sugar and soft drinks are linked to the risk of caries [13]. In a study by Štefanová et al. [28], it was found that insufficient tooth brushing together with the daily consumption of sweets or sweetened soft drinks dominated among respondents from lower socioeconomic groups. In our study, it was found that oral hygiene practices were associated with some of the nutrition habits; particularly, better dental hygiene was more common in schoolchildren who daily eat fruits and vegetables, have regular breakfast, and less often eat sweets.

Schmidt et al. [34] showed that the association between physical activity and favorable oral health was not altered when including socioeconomic status (SES) in the model, revealing a general, SES-independent beneficial effect. According to another study results, lower physical activity was associated with irregular tooth brushing proving the fact that tooth brushing habits most probably reflect the broader context of the lifestyle of adolescents rather than only dental behavior [35].

Although some research indicates a link between irregular tooth brushing and risky behavior (smoking in particular) [36–38], no such associations were identified in this study. However, a weak association between irregular tooth brushing and alcohol consumption was discovered, although previous findings suggested alcohol consumption to be associated with dental hygiene and preventive dental care rather than poor dental hygiene [37]. A study conducted among Brazilian adolescents also found weak associations between irregular tooth brushing and risk behaviors, such as smoking, drinking, and illegal drug use [39]. These findings suggest the association (or lack of it) between irregular tooth brushing and risk behavior is poorly understood and varies between countries.

In this study, irregular tooth brushing was more common among adolescents with low life satisfaction and poor or fair health. It meets previous research findings which also suggest that low life satisfaction is associated with poor dental hygiene [40, 41]. The role of emotional state [42] and some psychosocial factors such as self-efficacy, intention, social influences, coping planning, and action planning have been associated with better oral hygiene in adolescents [7]; the same association was found with a sense of coherence [43]. Similar results were found in the other studies highlighting the fact that positive health behavior is influenced by psychosocial factors. Life satisfaction was related to regular tooth brushing in the French HBSC study [44]. However, no links were discovered between poor dental hygiene and the chosen psychosomatic variables (feeling low, irritability, nervousness, and sleep difficulties). While research in other countries, such as Korea, India, and Luxembourg, found various mental health indicators to be linked with poor dental hygiene [45, 46]. This suggests that other factors play a bigger role in impacting irregular tooth brushing (or vice-versa) for adolescents living in post-Soviet countries.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the tooth brushing habits of schoolchildren from post-Soviet countries were not desirable. The WHO has identified key strategies for improving oral health including mix of preventive population-wide and patient-centered care, with a clear focus on health promotion and empowerment for effective self-care, with a focus on low-income and marginalized populations [4].

Promotion of oral health in children is recognized as one of the components of health-promoting schools; a supportive environment and comprehensive educational programs in communities are crucial for optimal health and well-being.

Implications

The findings of the study provide an important groundwork for population-based preventive measures and targeted preventive programs. It also provides baseline information for further investigations. To improve oral health in post-Soviet countries, a number of activities should be implemented:Skills-based oral health promotion. Reaching a majority of children through schools may be possible without requiring complex additional health structures. Skills-based oral health education includes daily group tooth brushing with fluoride toothpaste at school. Group activities are fun for children and enforce healthy social norms, which are effective drivers of oral hygiene behavior [47].Promoting access and use of appropriate fluorides. Prevention of tooth decay by using fluoride is the most realistic way to reduce the burden of tooth decay in populations.Reducing sugar consumption. Public institutions, especially schools, should not only raise awareness of but also provide healthy food and beverage options.Supportive policies for oral health. The regional and national action plans addressing NCDs may provide opportunities for the integration of simple, sustainable, and scalable oral health interventions.Increased advocacy to emphasize the burden and consequences of oral diseases to prioritize interventions for prevention and control.Intersectoral collaboration. School-based health promotion is an intersectoral challenge, with stakeholders mainly from the education; health; and water, sanitation, and hygiene sectors; but also from other sectors that jointly address key determinants of health.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several strengths, including a large representative cross-national sample and validated variables that enabled country-level comparisons. In addition, the current study adds to the literature by investigating specific oral hygiene components from post-Soviet countries.

Several study limitations should be acknowledged. First—the cross-sectional data do not allow for causal inferences. Longitudinal research on oral hygiene and risk factors is required to determine the direction of causality. Second, all measures were based on self-report instruments that may deviate from adolescents’ actual time oral hygiene patterns and other health and behavior risk factors patterns.
