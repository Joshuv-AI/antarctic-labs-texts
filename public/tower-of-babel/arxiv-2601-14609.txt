Communication-Efficient Federated Risk Difference Estimation for Time-to-Event Clinical Outcomes
Ziwen Wang, Siqi Li, Marcus Eng Hock Ong, Nan Liu

Abstract
Privacy-preserving model co-training in medical research is often hindered by server-dependent architectures incompatible with protected hospital data systems and by the predominant focus on relative effect measures (hazard ratios) which lack clinical interpretability for absolute survival risk assessment. We propose FedRD , a communication-efficient framework for federated risk difference estimation in distributed survival data. Unlike typical federated learning frameworks (e.g., FedAvg) that require persistent server connections and extensive iterative communication, FedRD is server-independent with minimal communication: one round of summary statistics exchange for the stratified model and three rounds for the unstratified model. Crucially, FedRD provides valid confidence intervals and hypothesis testing—capabilities absent in FedAvg-based frameworks. We provide theoretical guarantees by establishing the asymptotic properties of FedRD and prove that FedRD (unstratified) is asymptotically equivalent to pooled individual-level analysis. Simulation studies and real-world clinical applications across different countries demonstrate that FedRD outperforms local and federated baselines in both estimation accuracy and prediction performance, providing an architecturally feasible solution for absolute risk assessment in privacy-restricted, multi-site clinical studies.

Contributions

We develop FedRD for estimation of risk differences from distributed survival data. Our approach addresses the specific architectural and methodological limitations of current federated systems in healthcare. Our specific contributions are: (1) Novel federated estimators: we propose two FedRD estimators (unstratified and stratified) based on additive risks models tailored for right-censored survival data across multiple sites; (2) Minimal communication: stratified method requires only one round of summary statistics exchanged; unstratified method requires three rounds without iterative optimization; (3) Theoretical guarantees: we prove that both estimators achieve asymptotics equivalence to pooled individual-level analysis and (4) Empirical validation: experiments on simulated and real-world clinical data from Singapore and the United States demonstrate that FedRD matches pooled analysis and outperforms baselines in both prediction performance and estimation accuracy.

2.1
Preliminaries

We consider survival data where the goal is to estimate how covariates affect time-to-event outcomes.
Let non-negative random variable
T
denote the event time of interest and let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a vector of covariates (features).
The
additive risk model
specifies the hazard at time
t
as:

\lambda(t\mid\bm{X})=\lambda_{0}(t)+\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{X},

(1)

where
\lambda_{0}(t)
is an unspecified baseline hazard function,
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents the
risk differences
(RD), i.e., the absolute change in hazard per unit change in each covariate.
This contrasts with the Cox model’s multiplicative structure
\lambda(t\mid\bm{X})=\lambda_{0}(t)\exp(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X})
, which yields hazard
ratios
.

Due to right-censoring, we observe
(Y_{i},\delta_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
for individuals
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
Y_{i}=\min(T_{i},C_{i})
is the observed time,
C_{i}
is the censoring time, and
\delta_{i}=\mathbf{1}(T_{i}\leq C_{i})
indicates whether the event was observed. Under standard independence assumptions that
C
is independent of
T
and
\bm{X}
, and that
T
and
C
are conditionally independent given
\bm{X}
,
\bm{\beta}
can be estimated via the martingale-based estimating equation
(
10
)
to estimate model in Eq. (
1
).
Specifically, the score function is given by

U(\bm{\beta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\big\}\{dN_{i}(t)-\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{x}_{i}Y_{i}(t)dt\},

(2)

where
N_{i}(t)=\mathbf{1}(Y_{i}\leq t,\delta_{i}=1)
is the counting process,
Y_{i}(t)
is a
0-1
predictable process, taking value 1 if subject
i
is at risk at time
t
, i.e.,
i\in R(t)
.
The term
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}Y_{i}(t)~/~\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(t)

represents the average covariates vector over the risk set at time
t
,
and can equivalently be written as
\sum_{i\in R(t)}\bm{x}_{i}~/~|R(t)|
,
where
|R(t)|
denotes the number of individuals at risk at time
t
.
Finally,
\tau
is a pre-specified sufficiently large constant which satisfies
P(Y\geq\tau)>0
, in practice,
\tau
is often taken as the latest observed examination time in the study (study end time).

The resulting estimator of RD by solving Eq. (
2
) is commonly referred to as the gold standard estimate.
Specifically, solving
U(\bm{\beta})=0
gives us a closed-form estimator
A^{*-1}D^{*}
, where
A^{*}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}Y_{i}(t)\big\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\big\}^{\otimes 2}dt

, and

D^{*}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\big\}dN_{i}(t)
.
The information matrix can be estimated by
A^{*-1}\Sigma^{*}A^{*-1}
,
where
\Sigma^{*}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(t)\big\}^{\otimes 2}dN_{i}(t)

Notice that

equals 0 except at the failure time of the

further becomes
\tilde{D}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(y_{i})\}
.
Similarly, we rewrite
\Sigma^{*}

\tilde{\Sigma}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(y_{i})\}^{\otimes 2}
.

As for the
A^{*}
part, in practice, we use a Riemann sum to approximate the integration.
Specifically, let the observation times be ordered as
y_{(1)}\leq y_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq y_{(n)}

\Delta y_{(i)}=y_{(i)}-y_{(i-1)}

y_{(0)}=0
.
Then

\tilde{A}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in R(y_{(i)})}\big\{\bm{x}_{l}-\bar{\bm{x}}(y_{(i)})\big\}^{\otimes 2}\bigtriangleup y_{(i)}
.
Then the RD parameters can be estimated by

\hat{\beta}_{G}=\tilde{A}^{-1}\tilde{D}.

(3)

\hat{I}_{G}=\tilde{A}^{-1}\tilde{\Sigma}\tilde{A}^{-1}.

2.3
Federated risk difference estimation via stratified additive risk model

To account for inter-site heterogeneity, we further consider a stratified additive risk model and propose FedRD-S.
In this framework, we assume that the baseline hazards differ across different sites while the RD
\bm{\beta}
is the same across all sites, i.e., there are common effects of the covariates on the time-to-event across sites.
Consequently, the stratified addictive risk model for site
k
is defined as:

\lambda_{ik}(t|\bm{x}_{ik})=\lambda_{0k}(t)+\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{x}_{ik},

(7)

where
\lambda_{0k}(t)
is the baseline hazard function for the
k
-th site.

Following the same arguments used to derive (
2

k
-th site can be expressed as

S_{k}(\bm{\beta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\big\{\bm{x}_{ik}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{k}(t)\big\}\{dN_{ik}(t)-\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{x}_{ik}Y_{ik}(t)dt\}

(8)

where
\bar{\bm{x}}_{k}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}Y_{ik}(t)\bm{x}_{ik}~/~\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}Y_{ik}(t)
and equal to
\sum_{i\in R_{k}(t)}\bm{x}_{ik}~/~|R_{k}(t)|
with
R_{k}(t)=\{i;y_{ik}\geq t\}
.

Let the observation times of site
k
be ordered as
y_{(1)k}\leq y_{(2)k}\leq\cdots\leq y_{(n_{k})k}

\Delta y_{(i)k}=y_{(i)k}-y_{(i-1)k}
, where
y_{(0)k}=0
.

S_{k}(\bm{\beta})=0

\bm{\beta}

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=A_{k}^{-1}D_{k}

k=1,...,K

D_{k}=n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\delta_{ik}\{\bm{x}_{ik}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{k}(y_{ik})\}.

For the covariance matrix of the local estimator can be estimated by
A_{k}^{-1}\Sigma_{k}A_{k}^{-1}

\Sigma_{k}=n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\delta_{ik}\big\{\bm{x}_{i}-\bar{\bm{x}}(y_{i})\big\}^{\otimes 2}

We establish asymptotic properties of the FedRD estimators under the following regularity conditions, which are standard in additive risk models theory.

C1.

(Independence) Subjects are independent across
(i,k)
; within each site
k
,
\{(N_{ik}(t),Y_{ik}(t),\bm{X}_{ik})\}
are identically distributed.

C2.

(Bounded covariates)
\bm{X}
is time-independent with
\|\bm{X}\|\leq C
almost surely for some
C<\infty
.

C3.

(Positive at-risk probability)
Y(t)
is left-continuous with right limits;
P\{Y(t)=1|\bm{X}\}>0
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
, where
P(Y\geq\tau)>0
.

C4.

(Integrable baseline)
\lambda_{0}(t)
and
\lambda_{0k}(t)
are nonnegative and locally integrable on
[0,\tau]
.

C5.

(Positive definite information)
A=\int_{0}^{\tau}E[Y(t)\{\bm{X}-\mu(t)\}^{\otimes 2}]dt
is positive definite.

C6.

(Site-level information) For each site
k
,
A_{k}=\int_{0}^{\tau}E[Y_{ik}(t)\{\bm{X}_{ik}-\mu_{k}(t)\}^{\otimes 2}]dt
is positive definite.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymptotic Normality of FedRD-U)
.

Under the unstratified additive hazards model and above conditions, let
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{U}
be the solution to the estimating equation (
5
), with above conditions C1–C6, we have:

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{U}-\bm{\beta}_{0})\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\!\left(0,\,A^{-1}\Sigma A^{-1}\right),

where

A=\int_{0}^{\tau}E\!\left[Y(t)\{\bm{X}-\mu(t)\}^{\otimes 2}\right]dt,

and

\Sigma=E\!\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\{\bm{X}-\mu(t)\}^{\otimes 2}dN(t)\right]

with
\mu(t)=E(\bm{X}\mid Y(t)=1)
.

The covariance matrix of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{U}
can be estimated by the sandwich formula

\hat{cov}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{U})=\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}\hat{A}^{-1},

where a consistent estimator for
A
under the unstratified additive risks model is
\hat{A}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{l\in R_{k}(y_{ik})}\big\{\bm{x}_{lk}-\bar{\bm{x}}_{k}(y_{(i)k})\big\}^{\otimes 2}\Delta y_{(i)k}
and
\Sigma
can be consistently estimated by
\hat{\Sigma}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\delta_{ik}\big\{\bm{x}_{ik}-\bar{\bm{x}}(y_{ik})\big\}^{\otimes 2}.

Consistency of
\hat{A}
and
\hat{\Sigma}
follows from the law of large numbers, together with uniform convergence of
\bar{\bm{x}}(t)
to
\mu(t)
. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

In summary, the proposed distributed estimator for FedRD-U is asymptotically equivalent to the pooled estimator because both are solutions to the same estimating equation, differing only in the manner of computation rather than in statistical formulation.

Theorem 3.2

(Asymptotic Normality of FedRD-S)
.

Under the stratified additive hazards model, let
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}
be the solution to the estimating equation (

6
Discussion

We developed FedRD, a communication-efficient framework for federated risk difference estimation in multi-site survival data. Unlike engineering-based FL methods that require persistent server connections and iterative optimization, FedRD is fully server-independent: the coordinator role requires only simple aggregation and can be assumed by any participating site. This architectural simplicity makes FedRD particularly suitable for healthcare settings where IT infrastructure varies widely across institutions, protected computing environments prohibit external connections, and resources for maintaining dedicated FL servers are limited.

From the simulation study, we observe that local estimator are prone to bias when site-specific sample sizes are small. Since meta-analysis combines individual local estimators through weighted averaging, it inherits this limitation that becomes pronounced in multi-site studies with unbalanced sample sizes.
In the homogeneous setting where all sites share a common baseline risk, the unstratified estimator (FedRD-U), is asymptotically equivalent to the pooled individual-level estimator.
This equivalence follows from the special form of its estimating function and its closed-form solution. However, in terms of computational cost, the unstratified federated estimator requires three rounds of communication instead of one.

In contrast, FedRD-S accommodates heterogeneous settings where baseline hazards differ across sites, requiring only a single communication round. While it incurs some efficiency loss compared to FedRD-U under homogeneous conditions, FedRD-S substantially outperforms local and meta-analytic methods. Application to EHR data from Singapore and the United States—two healthcare systems with markedly different populations—confirms its robustness under strong cross-site heterogeneity.

Several directions for future research merit further investigation. First, extending FedRD to more general forms of censored data, such as case II
(
18
)
, case K
(
19
)
, or mixed interval-censored
(
24
)
failure time data, which frequently arise in longitudinal studies with intermittent follow-up. Second, incorporating variable selection for high-dimensional covariates would enable application to modern clinical datasets with large feature sets.

E\!\left\{dN_{ik}(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{-}}\right\}=Y_{ik}(t)\left\{\lambda_{0k}(t)+\beta_{0}^{\top}X_{ik}\right\}\,dt.

Therefore, a consistent estimator of
\Sigma_{S}
is obtained by directly replacing the intensity term with the observed counting process increments.

\hat{\Sigma}_{S}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}n_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\delta_{ik}\left\{X_{ik}-\bar{X}_{k}(y_{i})\right\}^{\otimes 2}.
