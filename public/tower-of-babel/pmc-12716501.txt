Using structural equation modelling to explore the relationship between insomnia, anxiety and depression to the severity of BPS/IC: a cross-sectional study in Chongqing, China
Authors: Chao Wu, Liang Wang, Xiaolan Zhou, Yiping Zhou, Shiyun Han, Xingyou Dong, Hongbo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesDespite the high prevalence of insomnia, anxiety and depression in patients with bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis (BPS/IC), their clinical impact is often overlooked. To address this gap, our study systematically investigated whether these three comorbidities impact BPS/IC severity.Design, setting and participantsThis cross-sectional study included 237 patients with BPS/IC recruited via convenience sampling from Shapingba Hospital affiliated to Chongqing University (Shapingba District People’s Hospital of Chongqing) and Chongqing Liangjiang Minsheng Hospital in Chongqing, China, between 24 August 2023 and 21 November 2023.Outcome measuresApart from demographic characteristics, BPS severity was assessed using the O’Leary-Sant interstitial cystitis questionnaire, insomnia was assessed using the Athens Insomnia Scale and anxiety and depression were assessed using Zung’s Self-rating Anxiety Scale and Zung’s Self-rating Depression Scale, respectively. The structural equation model (SEM) was used to analyse the role of insomnia, anxiety and depression on the severity of BPS/IC.ResultsThe proportion of severe disease, insomnia, anxiety and depression in participants was 49.37%, 70.89%, 43.88% and 47.26%, respectively. The analysis of SEM indicated that insomnia (β=0.294, p<0.001) and anxiety (β=0.364, p<0.001) were associated with BPS severity, but depression showed no significant association (β=0.042, p=0.674). According to the mediating analysis, the association between insomnia and the disease was attributable to both direct effects of insomnia (55.5%) and indirect effects involving anxiety conditions (44.5%).ConclusionsOur findings indicate that insomnia is associated with BPS/IC severity both directly and indirectly through anxiety in patients with BPS/IC. These results further support the use of psychological and sleep interventions for BPS/IC.Keywords: Insomnia, Anxiety disorders, Epidemics

Introduction

Bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis (BPS/IC) is a chronic syndrome of urinary system with unknown aetiology. The primary symptom of BPS/IC included persistent pelvic pain with lower urinary tract symptoms, such as urinary frequency, urgency and nocturia.1 2 So far, there was still no effective treatment for BPS/IC, and patients were suffering from a train of physical and mental health problems due to recurrent symptoms and persistent debilitating painful conditions.3 Patients who suffer from the sickness placed a severe burden on physical, mental, economic and quality of life.4

Evidence suggests that patients with BPS/IC are prone to co-morbid psychosocial symptoms, with prevalence of depression ranging from 16 to 70% and anxiety up to 52%.5,7 Anxiety and depression, which were common in the general population, often plague patients with different chronic diseases as concomitant states. Patients who are stuck in negative emotions will emanate nervousness, apprehension and panic to sickness, and these negative states always lead to more severe disease conditions, seriously impairing their quality of life. Unaddressed psychiatric factors of chronic pain can reshape patient perceptions and behaviour, finally leading to increased chronicity of symptoms and are associated with poorer prognosis. A study suggested patients with anxiety showed increased urinary symptom severity and lower self-esteem.8 Other findings of quantitative analysis reported that depression is consistently associated with worsened symptom presentation in early stages of illness.5

In addition, sleep disorder is also a frequent condition in BPS/IC patients, which directly leads to physical debilitation and a worsening burden on the quality of life. A nationwide cross-section study reported that patients with BPS/IC were at risk of developing insomnia with the incidence rate up to 47%6 on account of typical symptoms, such as nocturia and persistent painful lower urinary tract. Abundant evidence suggests that insomnia could be a potential independent factor and early precursor for psychiatric disorders. The transdiagnostic cognitive theory of sleep asserts that over protracted timescales, insomnia can influence the mental status as sleep deprivation may reduce one’s ability to manage emotions and acute stress triggers, contributing to the onset and maintenance of psychological disorders over time.9 Chronic sleep issues could also contribute to emotional difficulties via executive dysfunction and problem focusing, multitasking and accomplishing tasks.10 Likewise, if patients are stuck in a negative mind, it could lead to worsened sickness and poorer prognosis. The review proposed that patients with chronic medical illness and comorbid depression or anxiety had significantly higher numbers of symptoms compared with chronic illness only. The reason could be that mental disorder might cause a negative impact on patients’ healthcare behaviours.11

However, few studies have systematically examined how insomnia, anxiety and depression collectively impact BPS/IC progression. Notably, whether anxiety and depression mediate the relationship between insomnia and BPS/IC severity remains underexplored. Based on theory and prior evidence, we proposed that severe sleep disturbance is associated with BPS/IC symptom severity, and we established a structural equation modelling (SEM) to explore both direct and indirect relationships among insomnia, anxiety, depression and the illness. This model was validated using structural equation modelling and incorporated three hypotheses: H1: insomnia, anxiety and depression will directly affect the BPS/IC severity; H2: insomnia will exert an indirect effect on the illness severity through anxiety or depression and H3: insomnia will also build an indirect effect on depression through anxiety. The use of SEM provides a more nuanced understanding of these relationships, extending previous descriptive findings and supporting the integration of psychosocial management (specifically sleep and anxiety interventions) into BPS/IC clinical care.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey from 24 August to 21 November in urology departments of two care clinical centres in Chongqing, China. First, a preliminary questionnaire was developed following a literature review. Subsequently, this questionnaire was refined by clinical discussion. The final questionnaire was uploaded to the “Sojump” (www.sojump.com), which is one of the biggest professional online platforms for questionnaire design, questionnaire distribution, data collection and data analysis in China. The participants were enrolled by clinicians who shared a quick response (QR) code linked to our survey through online chat groups. We implemented restrictions on devices, WeChat IDs and IP addresses, thereby ensuring that only one response could be submitted from each unique combination. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shapingba Hospital affiliated to Chongqing University (Shapingba District People’s Hospital of Chongqing) (reference number: KY202327). All subjects had an understanding of the research situation and signed an informed consent voluntarily.

Inclusion criteria: (i) conform to the diagnostic criteria for BPS/IC in American National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK); (ii) informed consent to the investigation; (iii) without cognitive impairments, consciousness disturbance and other functional barriers, and able to complete the questionnaires.Exclusion criteria: (i) failure to follow clinical diagnosis; (ii) absence of informed consent; (iii) a history of mental illness diagnosis and treatment, such as anxiety and depression; (iv) a history of using psychotropic medications before the onset.

Measures and instruments

Demographic indications

Participants completed a series of demographic questions, including age, gender, height, weight, register, education, occupation, income and marriage.

BPS/IC severity

In this study, we assessed BPS/IC severity using the O’Leary-Sant interstitial cystitis symptom and problem questionnaire (OSS).12 The O’Leary-Sant questionnaire is an eight-term self-report instrument. It comprises the Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Index (ICSI) and Interstitial Cystitis Problem Index (ICPI), indicating the severity of symptom and sensation of BPS/IC patients.13 Participants rate each item on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 5 (always). BPS/IC severity was defined by combining with the score of patient’s ICSI and ICPI index, where severe patients were defined as those with a total score of ≥50% on both dimensions of the questionnaire, and mild patients were defined as those with the score of at least one index < 50% of the total score.14

Insomnia

In this study, we assessed insomnia using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS).15 The AIS scale is an eight-item self-report instrument covering the symptom of insomnia, and participants are rated based on the frequency of experiencing that symptom over the preceding 1 month. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (no problem) to 3 (serious impact).16 The scores for all eight items are added, with the total score indicative of thresholds for insomnia. Higher scores on the AIS indicate a greater likelihood of insomnia. We utilised a Chinese version of AIS, which demonstrated high internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach’s α=0.862).

Anxiety

In this study, we assess anxiety using the Zung’s Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS).17 The SAS scale is a self-report instrument with 20 items. Participants rate each item on a Likert scale from 1 (never) to 4 (almost always). The negative subscale items are reversed scored. The scores for all 20 items are added and multiplied by 1.25 for the standard score. The standard score indicative thresholds for anxiety is 50 points, where higher scores on the SAS indicate a greater likelihood of anxiety. Among them, 50–59 points are considered as mild anxiety, 60–69 are considered as moderate anxiety and over 70 points are represented by severe anxiety. We utilised a Chinese version of the SAS scale, which demonstrated great internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach’s α=0.803).

Depression

In this study, we assessed depression using Zung’s Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS).17 The SDS scale is a self-report instrument with 20 items. Participants rate each item on a Likert scale from 1 (never) to 4 (almost always). The negative subscale items are reversed scored. The scores for all 20 items are added and multiplied by 1.25 for standard score. The standard score indicative thresholds for depression are 53 points, where higher scores on the SDS indicate a higher likelihood of depression. The standard scores are considered as mild from 53 to 62 points, 63 to 72 are considered as moderate and excess of 72 points is considered as severe depression. We used a Chinese version of SDS scale, which demonstrated great internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach’s α=0.855).

Procedure

Clinicians included subjects according to the criteria. Once consent was obtained, each participant was requested to finish a 30-min questionnaire online in their own time. When the questionnaire was not qualified, clinicians filled missing information by telephone. Subjects who failed to complete the questionnaire were considered rejected. Participants went into the draw to win a 50 Chinese Yuan (approximately 7 United States Dollars) for their participation.

Sample size

Justification for the sample size was based on the rule of thumb in structural equation modelling (SEM) that asserts that the sample should include at least 10 cases per measured indicator.18 In the current study, the structural equation model measured nine variable indicators; thus, the sample size of 90 pupils was appropriate to test the model. It is further inflated to 108 to account for a predicted 20% attrition rate. Thus, the sample size of 237 pupils was appropriate to test the model.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25 and IBM SPSS Amos version 26 software. The descriptive analysis was performed using frequencies and percentages as well as means (x-) and SD to analyse both categorical and continuous variables. The normality assumption was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test for continuous variables. χ2 tests or independent sample t-tests were used to compare the distribution between mild and severe patients. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to examine the association between insomnia, the psychological factors (anxiety, depression) and sickness severity.

Structural equation modelling (SEM) was used to analyse whether and how psychological factors mediated the relationship between insomnia and sickness. Goodness of fit was determined for the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) as above 0.90, the Adjust Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI) as above 0.90, the Normed Fit Index (NFI) as above 0.90, the Incremental Fit Index (IFI) as above 0.90, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) as above 0.90 and the Root-Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) as below 0.05 for close fit, but values around 0.08 indicated reasonable fit. Finally, χ2 difference tests were used to compare model fit between nested models. If the hypothetical model was poorly fitted, we would gradually modify the conceptual model by modification index and significant level of path. The bootstrap method was used to estimate the CI and significance of the mediating effect and then reported the proportion of mediating effect. In our study, p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Structural equation modelling analysis

We established a conceptual psychosocial model about insomnia, negative psychological conditions and the severity of BPS/IC to explore direct or indirect relationship between insomnia, anxiety, depression and the sickness, and then validated them by developing a structural equation model for analysis. The conceptual model included the following three hypotheses:

H1: insomnia, anxiety and depression will directly affect the BPS/IC severity.

H2: Insomnia will exert an indirect effect on the illness severity through anxiety or depression.

H3: Insomnia will also build an indirect effect on depression through anxiety.

Based on the above assumptions, we constructed the following theoretical model. The model and standardised beta coefficients are presented in figure 2. The result of SEM analysis suggested that anxiety and depression mediated the association between insomnia and development of BPS. The pathways demonstrated that higher levels of insomnia symptoms are directly affecting the extent of anxiety (β=0.582, p<0.001), depression (β=0.199, p<0.001) and BPS/IC severity (β=0.294, p<0.001) to varying degrees. The level of anxiety exerts direct effects on depression (β=0.643, p<0.001) and the illness (β=0.364, p<0.001). However, the relationship between the level of depression and BPS/IC was not significant (β=0.042, p=0.654) (see figure 2 & table 3). Fit index suggested this mediation model showed good fit (χ2=0.312 (df=2), GFI=0.999, AGFI=0.996, NFI=0.999, RMSEA=0.001 and SRMR=0.004).

Discussion

The study tested the relationships between insomnia, anxiety, depression and severity of sickness using structural equation model in a sample of patients with BPS/IC from two hospitals in southwest China. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the association between sleep disturbance and the severity of sickness through adverse psychological conditions in the BPS/IC population. Our findings showed that in patients with BPS/IC, insomnia symptoms are associated with disease severity; mediation analysis indicated this link involves direct insomnia impacts and indirect effects through anxiety. Specifically, we concluded that the indirect effect accounted for 44.5% of the total effect of insomnia-BPS/IC severity relationship.

Insomnia is thought to be associated with sudden life events and cognitive-emotional arousal processes, manifesting as the activation of the body’s stress system.14 15 This connection is particularly relevant in BPS/IC patients, who demonstrate high susceptibility to sleep disturbances. A nationwide cross-sectional study reported that BPS/IC patients face an elevated risk of insomnia, with an incidence reaching 47%.5 In our cohort, this proportion was even higher at 70.89%, while the higher proportion of insomnia could be due to the longer disease duration and greater severity profile of our study population. As the disease progresses, patients with BPS/IC feel more pain, and problems like peeing more at night wake them up repeatedly. These issues also make it hard to fall asleep, which either causes insomnia or makes existing insomnia worse.

Beyond insomnia, patients with BPS/IC also exhibit high rates of emotional distress. In our study, 43.88% of patients with BPS/IC reported anxiety and 47.26% reported depression, consistent with prior findings,5 7 confirming high emotional distress comorbidity in this population. Existing research identifies insomnia as a key predictor of anxiety/depression, with shared neural mechanisms. For instance, Oliveira et al found overlapping genetic profiles and converging neural pathways that link sleep quality to depressive symptoms,19 while Palagini et al documented insomnia-related neurochemical alterations in brain regions implicated in anxiety regulation, particularly the amygdala and prefrontal cortex.20 These studies help explain why BPS/IC patients with insomnia are also more likely to experience emotional distress.

So far, BPS/IC’s aetiology remains unclear, with proposed factors including immunity, inflammation, neural pathways, urothelial integrity and anaesthetic bladder capacity.21 22 Notably, growing evidence suggests that insomnia, emotional symptoms and BPS/IC severity may be interlinked through common neural pathways. Holschneider et al, for instance, showed in rodent models that stress-induced downregulation of glial GLT-1 exacerbates both BPS/IC-related pain and depression,23 while the MAPP Network identified central nervous system (CNS) biomarkers (eg, sensorimotor network changes) that directly link BPS/IC to pain and mood disturbances.24 Collectively, these findings underscore insomnia as a modifiable target in BPS/IC management. Sleep-focused strategies hold promise for alleviating emotional comorbidity and potentially altering the trajectory of BPS/IC severity progression. Translating this to clinical practice, clinicians should (1) integrate tools like the insomnia severity index into BPS/IC severity assessments to identify insomnia early and intervene accordingly; (2) adopt complementary sleep interventions to improve sleep quality and alleviate anxiety, depression and pain amplification; (3) implement a multidisciplinary approach to address bladder symptoms, sleep health and psychological issues to improve overall quality of life of patients with BPS/IC .

Furthermore, the result of SEM analysis showed anxiety rather than depression, which in the negative psychological conditions that easily occurred in patients with BPS/IC, directly impacts BPS/IC severity, conflicting with previous studies. Yang et al found depressed patients had a higher BPS/IC incidence than the general population via 2-year diagnostic records25; a cohort study by Sutherland et al showed that women with BPS/IC showed higher depression linked to higher compensatory coping scores, while anxiety was not.26 This contradiction may stem from three factors: first, sample representativeness differs: Yang et al used Taiwan-wide large data,25 while our 237-participant convenience sample from two Chongqing centres limits representativeness and may lead to divergent findings due to regional and population-specific variations. Second, varied assessment tools: we used the self-rated Zung SDS, while Sutherland et al adopted the PROMIS Anxiety and Depression scales.26 Differences in assessment dimensions and scoring criteria may affect depression measurement accuracy and interpretation of results. Third, cross-cultural differences: we studied Chinese participants, prior research focused on Western populations[21]. Variations in symptom reporting and clinical traits may drive differences in psychological factors linked to BPS/IC severity. Additionally, differences in treatment-seeking behaviour may play a role: unlike anxious individuals who tend to seek care more readily, patients with comorbid anxiety and depression often avoid treatment due to prognostic pessimism and negative coping strategies,27,29 thereby further obscuring the potential impact of depression on BPS/IC severity. While our data did not support depression as a statistically significant factor in this model, this does not preclude its potential clinical relevance and need for further research to clarify its role.

This study had the following limitations. First, its cross-sectional design and the use of a convenience sample from only two centres in Chongqing (n=237), which limits identifying correlational rather than causal relationships between indicators and restricts the sample’s representativeness of all patients with BPS/IC. Therefore, large-sample, multi-centre prospective studies are needed to further verify our conclusions. Second, all measurements (OSS, AIS, SAS and SDS) relied on self-reports, lacking clinical validation, which may introduce reporting bias from recall errors, affecting result accuracy. Future research should integrate objective assessment methods alongside self-reports. Third, BPS/IC severity was assessed using the O’Leary-Sant Symptom and Problem Index (OSS). Although the lack of universally accepted diagnostic and severity criteria limits comparability with other studies, the OSS is derived from a 10-year cohort of 400+patients and has been validated for diagnosing BPS/IC.30 Its utility in severity stratification is further supported by its application in tracking disease progression, as demonstrated by Vinata B et al.14

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to use a structural equation model (SEM) to explore the relationship of insomnia, anxiety and depression to the severity of BPS/IC. Our findings suggest that insomnia is linked to BPS/IC severity directly and also indirectly via anxiety in patients with BPS/IC. Consequently, clinicians should manage patients’ insomnia and anxiety in addition to BPS/IC symptoms to reduce disease severity and improve treatment outcomes.
