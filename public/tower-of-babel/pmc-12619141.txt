Difference in discharge disposition, adherence to clinical care standards and functional outcomes of older hip fracture patients receiving an orthogeriatric model of care versus routine care
Authors: Seraina Netzer, Christian Ruch, Silviya Ivanova, Andreas Limacher, Lisa Meyer, Anna K Eggimann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip fractures are a common injury among older adults, leading to higher morbidity and mortality. Effective treatment pathways are needed. We aimed to compare an orthogeriatric model of care (OMC) and routine care in older hip fracture patients regarding discharge disposition, adherence to clinical care standards and functional outcomes.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study in patients aged ≥ 70 years with acute hip fracture consecutively admitted to a tertiary Swiss hospital between January 1, 2023 and December 31, 2023 (n = 100). Patients meeting eligibility criteria (≥ 3 geriatric syndromes, no severe dementia) received OMC (n = 46) including an intensive multiprofessional rehabilitation program (MRP) comprising 7 physical, 2 nutritional, and 1 occupational therapy sessions led by a geriatrician during the acute hospital stay. All other patients received routine care (n = 56) on the same acute ward led by an orthopedic surgeon. Discharge destination, adherence to nine pre-specified clinical care standards and functional outcomes (Barthel index [BI] and De Morton Mobility index [DEMMI]), were assessed.ResultsMean age was 83.6 years, 59% were female, and 66% frail (Clinical Frailty Scale ≥ 5). Overall, 30 patients (65.2%) in the OMC were discharged to inpatient geriatric rehabilitation compared to 15 patients (27.8%) in routine care. After post-acute rehabilitation, 23 patients (50%) in the OMC versus 16 patients (29.6%) in routine care were discharged home. Length of stay was similar in the OMC versus routine care (10.8 vs. 9.4 days). Mean adherence to nine clinical care standards was 87.9% in the OMC versus 58.7% in routine care. In OMC patients, adherence was lowest for pain management using a femoral nerve block (58.7%), and full-weight bearing (76.1%), while full adherence was observed for mobilization the first day after surgery. In the OMC, functional outcomes improved within 7 days (median increase BI 10 points, DEMMI 8 points).ConclusionsThe OMC including MRP resulted in higher numbers discharged to post-acute rehabilitation and to home afterwards, as well as improvement of adherence to clinical care standards and functional outcomes, suggesting that OMC is a promising care model. Refinement of OMC is needed to optimize adherence to full weight-bearing and femoral nerve block.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06335-5.Keywords: Orthogeriatrics, Rehabilitation, Trauma, Non-home discharge, Barthel-index

Background

Hip fractures, a common and serious injury among older adults, often resulting from falls due to declines in bone density and balance, are associated with high mortality and morbidity rates [1, 2]. Studies have shown that up to 30% of older adults who suffer a hip fracture die within a year of the injury [3]. Additionally, many survivors experience significant declines in physical function and quality of life [2]. The consequences of hip fractures extend beyond the individual, impacting families, caregivers, and healthcare systems [4]. This underscores the critical need for effective treatment pathways to manage and rehabilitate older hip fracture patients.

Given the severe implications of hip fractures, there is a pressing need for comprehensive care models that address both the medical and functional aspects of recovery. Traditional care models often fall short in providing the multidisciplinary approach required to optimize outcomes for these patients [5]. This gap in care has led to the development of orthogeriatric models, which integrate geriatric and orthopedic care to improve the management of older adults with hip fractures [6–8]. The meta-analysis of van Heghe et al. demonstrates that orthogeriatric models of care reduce in-hospital mortality, delirium, and length of stay, although their effect on functional outcomes remains inconsistent [6]. Similarly, the meta-analysis by Sarkies et al. found that dedicated models of care may enhance early mobilization and physical function in hip fracture patients [9]. However, there is currently no gold standard for the optimal model of care for older hip fracture patients and it remains unclear which model of care is most appropriate.

Implementation of orthogeriatric models of care is still evolving. While numerous hospitals have adopted integrated care pathways for older adults with fractures [10], the approach remains heterogeneous across institutions, and national guidelines or consensus on best practices are largely lacking, with exception of a recent effort to define optimal orthogeriatric hip care [7]. In this context, our study aims to evaluate an established Swiss orthogeriatric model of care (OMC) that incorporates a multiprofessional rehabilitation program (MRP) within the acute care setting. This model includes a comprehensive geriatric assessment, targeted management of common geriatric syndromes, and coordinated discharge planning.

We aim to evaluate the difference between an OMC and routine care regarding in terms of: (1) discharge disposition (2) adherence to clinical care standards and (3) functional outcomes. Our research aims to provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of integrated care pathways and inform future strategies for managing hip fractures in the aging population.

Methods

Study design

We performed a retrospective cohort study at the center for Orthogeriatrics at the Inselspital, University Hospital of Bern, Switzerland. This center is certified by the Registry for Geriatric Trauma (AltersTraumaRegister DGU®; ATR-DGU), a large multicenter, standardized anonymous registry, which involves meeting various criteria for certification as an orthogeriatric unit, such as standard operating procedures for pain management, mobilization and surgical treatment [11]. Consecutive patients admitted to the hospital due to a coxal femoral fracture between the a priori defined time frame from January 1st 2023 and December 31st 2023 were assessed for eligibility. Patients with acetabular fractures were not included in the study to reduce heterogeneity of the participants. The eligibility criteria required patients to be at least 70 years old and to have completed fracture treatment at the Inselspital. Patients who were transferred to another hospital for surgery because of hospital capacity limitations were not considered for inclusion.

Patient data were collected from medical records using a questionnaire based on the Australian and New Zealand Hip Fracture Registry (ANZHFR) template (“Patient Level Form with EQ5D,” 2024.). In the pre-defined time frame, 105 consecutive patient records were assessed for inclusion: 1 was excluded due to age < 70 years, 2 were excluded due to having a distal femoral fracture and 2 were excluded due to either surgery or post-operative care taking place at another hospital, which resulted in 100 patients included in this analysis (for details see Supplementary Figure. S1 ).

Patients meeting indication criteria for OMC (defined by the national CHOP Code (Swiss Operation Procedures) 93.89.9 [12]) set by the Swiss government for reimbursement were included in the OMC. The attending geriatrician was responsible for screening patients for OMC upon admission, applying the CHOP code and assessing whether the criteria for OMC were met. According to the CHOP code, main inclusion criteria for OMC were (1) expected acute hospital stay of 7 days and (2) presence of at least three of the following geriatric syndromes with relevant clinical impact [11]: cognition, mobility/falls/dizziness, incontinence, nutrition/sarcopenia/frailty, depression/anxiety, vision or hearing impairment, polypharmacy of ≥ 5 drugs, chronic pain, psychosocial problems. The only exclusion criterion for OMC according to the CHOP code, was “severe dementia” translating to the presence of a major neurocognitive disorder AND dependence in basic activities of daily living (BADL). The OMC was led by a geriatrician in co-management with orthopedic/trauma surgeons. It included performance of a comprehensive geriatric medical and social assessment upon admission, followed by an intensive multiprofessional rehabilitation program (MRP), as detailed below. The Barthel Index (BI), delirium screening, hearing and vision assessment were performed by a trained nurse. The geriatrician interpreted the geriatric assessment and implemented individual managed care and therapy based on the findings from the assessments.

Overall, the MRP included 10 individual therapy sessions of 30 minutes within seven days in the acute ward. Each patient received a minimum of seven individual physical therapy sessions (30 minutes each), which included muscle strengthening and balance exercises and fall prevention training when appropriate. Moreover, walking aids were adapted and instructed. During the first physical therapy session, the De Morton Mobility Index (DEMMI) was assessed, and individualized mobility goals for discharge were defined by the physical therapist, based on each patient’s needs and living situation (for example, the ability to walk independently along a 20-m corridor using crutches). Based on this evaluation, a targeted rehabilitation program was created. The assessments were repeated during the last session. Additionally, patients received two nutritional therapy sessions (each 30 minutes) performed by a nutritionist, who assessed the Nutritional Risk Score (NRS) and evaluated the nutritional intake after surgery upon the first session. Based on this assessment the nutritionist recommended individual measures to the patient directly and to the geriatrician (e.g. protein supplement), who was in charge of prescribing medication. At the second visit, implemented measures and nutritional intake were reevaluated and management was adapted when indicated. Moreover, patients had one occupational therapy session, which included an assessment of the need for assistive devices to support daily living at home (e.g. raised toilet seat or shower grab bars). All therapy sessions were individual sessions, there were no group therapies. A case manager was involved in discharge planning from the outset. Pre-scheduled weekly interdisciplinary team meetings—including the lead geriatrician, orthopedic surgeon, nurse, physiotherapist, nutritionist, occupational therapist, and case manager—were held to review clinical goals and coordinate discharge planning. Clinical care was overseen by the consultant geriatrician, with daily clinical visits by the orthopedic resident and at least three clinical visits by the consultant geriatrician during the acute hospital stay. The interdisciplinary team led by the consultant geriatrician was responsible for pain management and management of complications such as delirium obstipation, urinary tract infection and pneumonia. The orthopedic surgeon was responsible for managing surgery-specific complications such as poor wound healing and surgical revisions.

Patients who did not meet inclusion criteria for OMC received routine care on the same orthopedic ward, under the leadership of an orthopedic/trauma surgeon. Routine care included at least one individual physical therapy session the day after surgery. Additional therapies (nutritional, physical, and occupational therapy, as well as social services) were provided as deemed necessary by the treating orthopedic surgeon. Consultation by a geriatrician could be requested by the treating orthopedic/trauma surgeon. Clinical care in the routine care group was overseen by the orthopedic surgeon, with daily visits by an orthopedic resident.

This study was approved by the local Ethics Committee Bern Switzerland (code: Req-2024-00385, date: March 14th, 2024).

Clinical outcomes

Discharge disposition was defined aligning to the definition by Stuck et al. as follows [13]: Discharge to home included patients discharged directly to their homes (with or without assistance) as well as those discharged home following a 2-week inpatient geriatric rehabilitation. Non-home discharge included transfers to nursing homes, other hospitals, or death. Moreover, time from admission to surgery, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality were registered.

Adherence to a priori defined clinical care standards was based on the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care published in 2023 [14]. Accordingly, the following nine clinical care standards were assessed regarding performance: (1) Delirium ultra-brief screening test[15] including day of the week, and months backwards, (2) Malnutrition: Nutritional Risk Screening[16] (≥ 3 points indicating risk of malnutrition), (3) Vision: Near vision, Snellen chart [17], (4) Hearing: whisper test [18], (5) Pain management: performance of femoral nerve block [19], (6) Timing of surgery: time between admission and surgery < 36 h [20] (Patients with conservative treatment without surgery were excluded), (7) First mobilization: Documented first mobilization after surgery by a physical therapist within 24 h [21], (8) Unrestricted weight bearing: Full-weight bearing as defined by the orthopedic/trauma surgeon responsible for the surgery [22], (9) Fracture prevention strategy: Referral to a fracture liaison service (including dual x-ray absorptiometry, or implemented treatment of osteoporosis) [23].

In the OMC group functional status was assessed using the Barthel index (BI) [24] and the de Morton Mobility Index (DEMMI) [25] upon admission and upon discharge on the acute ward.

We added descriptive analysis of the following complications during hospitalization based on the discharge letter: Impaired wound healing, surgical revision, delirium, pressure ulcer, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, blood loss needing transfusion, obstipation, urinary tract retention.

Statistical analysis

Due to the small sample size and inherent selection bias due to OMC eligibility criteria, we decided on a purely descriptive analysis for the treatment groups. We indicated numerical variables with mean value and standard deviation, categorical variables with number and percentage. Variables with ordinal scales were documented with median and interquartile range. Discharge disposition and adherence to clinical care standards were stratified by model of care. A difference in adherence to clinical care standards of 10% between the groups was defined as clinically significant. The Wilcoxon signed rank test [26] was used to test the within patient difference of the median DEMMI and Barthel Index, respectively, upon admission and discharge. A p value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. The data was analyzed using Stata Version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

Of the 100 included patients, 59 (59%) were female, the mean age was 83.6 years, and 78 (78%) lived at home prior to admission (see Table 1). 46 (46%) patients met the criteria of the OMC. Patients in the OMC group were slightly older (mean age 84.9 vs. 82.5 years), and more likely to live at home preadmission (89.1% vs. 68.5% in routine care). The median scores on the clinical frailty scale (CFS) were comparable between the OMC and routine care (CFS = 5). In contrast, the proportion of frail patients (CFS ≥ 5) was higher in routine care compared to the OMC (56.5% vs. 74.1%).

Overall, 30 (65.2%) patients in the OMC group received post-acute inpatient geriatric rehabilitation, compared to 15 (27.8%) in routine care. Four (7.4%) patients died during the hospital stay in routine care. Mean length of stay was slightly longer in the OMC group (10.8 vs. 9.4 days).

Discussion

In our study, older patients receiving a multiprofessional rehabilitation program within an orthogeriatric model of care after a hip fracture were more frequently discharged home than those receiving routine care, where geriatric consultation is provided at the discretion of the lead orthopedic surgeon. Additionally, patients in the OMC group showed improved functional outcomes upon discharge compared to admission. These descriptive findings could potentially be explained by the higher adherence to clinical care standards for hip fracture treatment in the OMC compared to routine care.

This study presents a national orthogeriatric model of care that includes a multidisciplinary rehabilitation program, reimbursed and formally defined by Swiss government regulations in alignment with the OMC framework established in Germany. By providing a detailed account of the Swiss OMC model, the study aims to support its comparability and potential adaptation to other cultural and healthcare settings. A recent scoping review of hospital-based geriatric-centered interventions in trauma surgery services highlights significant variability in care approaches and underscores the need for standardized national protocols and long-term outcome measures [29]. The comprehensive description and contextual implementation applied in our study align with the findings of a recent meta-analysis, which emphasized the need for clearly defined orthogeriatric care models to facilitate cross-model comparisons and contribute to the development of a standardized approach to orthogeriatric care [27].

Overall, our study shows that patients treated within the OMC were more frequently discharged to their homes compared to those receiving routine care. These results are consistent with findings from a German study conducted between 2014 and 2018, which demonstrated that an orthogeriatric care model reduced the likelihood of nursing home admission in older patients with humeral or vertebral fractures [28]. This is further highlighted by a recent retrospective cohort study from Germany with comparable orthogeriatric care models showing a beneficial association of OMC availability and reduced hazard of readmission to acute care [29].

We also observed a clinically meaningful improvement in functional outcomes among patients receiving OMC, with a median increase of 10 points in the Barthel Index from baseline to discharge. This change meets the threshold for the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of 9.8 points derived from patients with a femoral neck fracture, indicating its clinical relevance [30]. Accordingly, an Australian Evidence Check found that orthogeriatric care models led by a geriatrician were associated with improved functional outcomes, reduced mortality and complications, enhanced medication management, and greater cost-effectiveness compared to usual care models in which geriatric input is provided only on demand [31]. A recent scoping review on predictive factors of gait recovery after hip fracture identified orthogeriatric multidisciplinary co-management as a positive predictive factor, underlining the need for effective OMC [32].

The overall benefit of a geriatrician-led multiprofessional rehabilitation program was also shown in recent registry based German study demonstrating lower in-house mortality and improved early walking ability compared to a routine care model in 9580 patients [11]. Specifically, this study found that the 7-day mortality rate was significantly lower in the multiprofessional rehabilitation group (2.89% vs. 5.11%), and a higher percentage of patients were able to walk independently or with help seven days post-surgery (86.44% vs. 77.78%). However, this study also noted a higher incidence of complications such as reoperations and pressure ulcers in the multiprofessional rehabilitation group, which may be attributed to the more intensive management and higher number of patient contacts during the hospital stay. Similarly, in our sample, cognitive impairment was more frequently detected in the OMC group possibly due to the standardized geriatric screening.

Our study further demonstrated that adherence to clinical care standards was higher in the standardized OMC group compared to routine care. The care standards we selected a priori from the Australian consensus align with recent European advances, which have defined processes of care for minimal or optimal orthogeriatric hip fracture management through a consensus-based Delphi approach [7]. Additionally, data from a recent Australian retrospective population-based study clearly show that adherence to these defined clinical care standards is associated with improved survival rates [33]. The authors found that high adherence to clinical care indicators significantly reduced 30-day, 120-day, and 365-day mortality rates among hip fracture patients. Specifically, high adherence was associated with a 60% lower risk of 30-day mortality and a 50% lower risk of 120-day mortality compared to low adherence. However, the study also found that overall adherence to these standards was not optimal, with only two-thirds of patients receiving high adherence care. Our data suggest that the OMC might improve adherence to these standards and thus improve patient outcomes.

Our study has several limitations, including its retrospective, single-center design and a small sample size of 100 participants with its inherent biases and limited generalizability. Despite these limitations, our approach allows for a pragmatic, researcher-based quality control of the Swiss OMC hospitalization pathway. Additionally, functional outcomes were only measured in the OMC group, comparing admission and discharge within the same cohort. We were thus unable to compare the functional outcomes at discharge in the OMC group to those in routine care, limiting the comparability of the treatment pathways. In a future study, the outcomes should be assessed in both groups. It is also important to note that patients in the OMC and routine care groups differed due to the inherent selection bias of the OMC eligibility criteria, further reducing comparability. The observed differences might well have been influenced by baseline characteristics such as cognitive status and preadmission living situation. Although for the latter, it is important to note that patients with preadmission living status at home were more frequently discharged home in the OMC group than in usual care (56.1% vs. 43.2%). A further aspect to consider is that cognitive impairment was assessed based on information extracted from the discharge letter, and it is possible that OMC led to a higher rate of detection of cognitive impairment per se. However, blinding was not possible due to the nature of the MRP intervention, and randomization becomes ethically difficult given the increasing evidence supporting the OMC [6]. The descriptive design of our study aligns with other research in the field, such as Solheim Salvesen et al.’s work, which also provides a primarily descriptive account of different clinical pathways in a Norwegian hospital [34].

The results have an impact on both clinical practice and research. Our study adds to global efforts in advancing orthogeriatric care models for older adults by providing detailed, specific data on the implementation and outcomes of a national Swiss OMC. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how structured, multidisciplinary approaches can enhance discharge disposition and functional recovery in older patients with hip fractures. Future research should adopt a prospective design, evaluate functional outcomes, health-related quality of life, in all patients, and incorporate the perspectives of both patients and caregivers. Additional areas of interest include long-term post-discharge outcomes and the cost-effectiveness of the OMC model. Recent research indicates that orthogeriatric co-management not only improves clinical outcomes but may also reduce direct healthcare costs compared to standard care models [35].

Our findings of increased adherence to clinical care standards highlight the importance of implementing routine, standardized geriatric screening of key functions upon admission for all older orthopedic patients. This is further supported by the higher proportion of geriatric diagnoses observed at discharge in the OMC group, indicating that such diagnoses may have been overlooked in the routine care group due to insufficient assessment. Therefore, a key clinical implication of this study is that all older patients admitted to orthopedic wards should undergo a standardized geriatric assessment as part of routine care irrespective of the model of care. This recommendation aligns with recent European guidelines, which define a brief but comprehensive geriatric assessment as a minimum standard of care for orthogeriatric hip fracture patients [7].

Conclusion

We found that a higher number of patients were discharged home after a hip fracture when treated in an OMC combined with a multiprofessional rehabilitation program compared to those receiving routine care. This is possibly due to higher adherence to hip fracture clinical care standards in the OMC. Functional outcomes showed improvement in the OMC group even within the short-term follow-up period during the acute hospital stay. Our findings provide a valuable basis for further research and support the overall advances of implementing standardized orthogeriatric care pathways for older adults with hip fractures.
