Transcatheter arterial embolization for traumatic adrenal hemorrhage with contrast extravasation: case series and literature review
Authors: Tomoya Mori, Hiroshi Kawada, Shoma Nagata, Tetsuya Fukuta, Norihide Kanda, Takahito Miyake, Yoshifumi Noda, Nobuyuki Kawai, Tomohiro Ando, Tetsuro Kaga, Taketo Suto, Masashi Asano, Takuya Seko, Hirohiko Imai, Abdelazim Elsayed Elhelaly, Hiroki Kato, Hideshi Okada, Masayuki Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the feasibility, short-term clinical outcomes, and technical considerations of transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) for traumatic adrenal hemorrhage with contrast extravasation on CT.Materials and methodsThis single-center retrospective study included consecutive patients with traumatic adrenal hemorrhage showing contrast extravasation on CT who underwent TAE. Technical success was defined as complete embolization of the bleeding artery with no contrast extravasation on post-embolization angiography. Clinical success was defined as effective control of adrenal hemorrhage during the index hospitalization, as evidenced by the absence of repeat embolization or surgery and no clinical or imaging evidence of persistent or recurrent bleeding.ResultsEight patients (4 men; median age, 78 years) underwent TAE. Adrenal hemorrhage was unilateral in all patients, involving the right side in 6 (75%) and the left side in 2 (25%). Contrast extravasation was observed in all patients during angiography. Gelatin sponge particles were used in all procedures. Technical and clinical success were both 100% (8/8). Single-artery embolization was performed in 6 patients (75%). Embolization of the middle adrenal artery was performed in 6 patients, the superior adrenal artery in 2, and the inferior adrenal artery in 3. One intraprocedural injury to the right inferior phrenic artery occurred. No procedure-related mortality or overt clinical evidence of adrenal insufficiency was observed during hospitalization. One patient died on hospital Day 8 of complications unrelated to adrenal hemorrhage.ConclusionTAE appears to be a technically feasible hemostatic option in selected patients with traumatic adrenal hemorrhage and CT-detected contrast extravasation, with favorable short-term in-hospital hemostatic outcomes in this small case series. However, the necessity of embolization and its comparative benefit over conservative management remain unclear.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Adrenal injury is an uncommon consequence of trauma, with a reported incidence of 0.5% to 4% in previous series [1–3]. In contrast, traumatic adrenal hemorrhage has been identified in up to 26% of autopsy cases of patients who died from severe blunt trauma to the chest or abdomen [4]. This discrepancy suggests that adrenal hemorrhage is frequently underdiagnosed in clinical practice, likely due to its nonspecific presentation and the inherent diagnostic challenges. Traumatic adrenal hemorrhage has been associated with substantial overall injury burden, with mortality rates ranging from 10 to 33% in previous series [1, 2]. Active bleeding may result in hemodynamic instability and fatal outcomes if not promptly managed.

Transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) has emerged as a minimally invasive and effective treatment for adrenal hemorrhage [5]. While its safety and efficacy have been demonstrated in case reports and small series, studies specifically addressing traumatic adrenal hemorrhage are limited. Furthermore, the complex and highly variable anatomy of the adrenal arteries makes embolization technically challenging and may increase the risk of procedural complications.

In this study, we report our institutional experience with TAE for traumatic adrenal hemorrhage and review the relevant literature, with particular emphasis on anatomical and technical considerations.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted over a 194-month period, from December 2006 to February 2023. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived through an opt-out process. Eight consecutive patients with traumatic adrenal injury demonstrating active contrast extravasation on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) who underwent TAE were included. One patient had been previously described in a case report from our institution [6], but the prior report did not focus on adrenal embolization; therefore, this patient was included as part of the present consecutive adrenal TAE cohort. In all cases, the indication for TAE was determined individually in the context of multisystem trauma, with consideration of hemodynamic trends, associated injuries, and the need for endovascular hemostatic management as part of the overall trauma treatment strategy. In patients requiring TAE for multiple traumatic injuries, the sequence of TAE was determined on a case-by-case basis based on the clinical priority of hemorrhage control, angiographic findings, and procedural accessibility.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were the technical and clinical success of TAE for traumatic adrenal hemorrhage. Technical success was defined as complete embolization of the bleeding artery with no residual contrast extravasation on post-embolization angiography. Clinical success was defined as in-hospital control of adrenal hemorrhage without additional hemostatic intervention, including repeat embolization or surgery, and without progressive anemia, hemodynamic instability, or clinical/imaging evidence of persistent or recurrent bleeding. Post-procedural follow-up was based on serial clinical and laboratory assessment, with imaging performed when clinically indicated; available follow-up images were reviewed by non-operator physicians. Secondary endpoints focused on adrenal arterial anatomy and the angiographic detectability of feeders relevant to embolization strategy. Laterality of the affected adrenal gland was also recorded.

Data collection

Collected data included patient demographics (age and gender), clinical parameters (hemoglobin levels, vital signs, laterality of adrenal injury, and hematoma size), trauma-related clinical variables, intraprocedural or post-procedural adverse events, including overt clinical evidence of adrenal insufficiency during hospitalization, procedural details (embolized vessels, embolic materials, and procedure duration), and treatment outcomes (technical and clinical success). Trauma-related clinical variables collected from the medical records included mechanism of injury, associated major injuries, Injury Severity Score, antithrombotic therapy, coagulation profile including platelet count, activated partial thromboplastin time, and PT-INR, electrolyte abnormalities including hyponatremia and hyperkalemia, transfusion requirement, vasopressor use, treatments performed for concomitant injuries, and overall clinical outcome. Adrenal insufficiency was assessed clinically on the basis of suggestive symptoms, routine laboratory findings, and the need for steroid replacement therapy during hospitalization, with consideration of trauma-related confounders. Anatomical characteristics of the adrenal arteries were analyzed using angiographic findings and thin-slice CT images to provide a detailed assessment of vascular anatomy relevant to embolization.

Procedure

Selective angiography of the adrenal artery considered most likely, based on contrast-enhanced CT findings, to be the bleeding source was performed first. When active extravasation was identified, embolization was performed immediately. Regardless of the initial angiographic findings, additional evaluation of possible adrenal feeders was performed, including selective angiography of the inferior phrenic and renal arteries and aortography, as needed. Vascular access was obtained via retrograde puncture of the common femoral artery using a 4-French or 5-French introducer sheath. A loop-shaped and/or Shepherd’s hook catheter (e.g., GHC-A; Hanako®, Osaka, Japan) was used as a diagnostic catheter. A microcatheter (e.g., Progreat λ17; Terumo®, Tokyo, Japan) was selectively advanced into the target artery for embolization (Figs. 1, 2, 3). Gelatin sponge particles were used as the primary embolic material in all cases. Additionally, n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA; Histoacryl; B. CT protocol

All patients underwent whole-body unenhanced and dual-phase contrast-enhanced CT examinations using a multidetector CT scanner as part of the institutional trauma imaging workflow in place during the study period. Automatic tube current modulation, combining z-axis and angular modulation based on patient size and body habitus, was used. Because the study spanned a long period, scanner hardware and detailed acquisition parameters were not identical in all cases. Representative acquisition parameters during the study period were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kVp; tube current, variable; noise index, 10.0 Hounsfield Unit (HU; 5-mm reconstruction thickness) using adaptive statistical Iterative Reconstruction–Veo (ASiR-V; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) of 40%; detector configuration, 1.25-mm section thickness; beam collimation, 40 mm; rotation time, 0.4 s; pitch, 0.984:1; scan field of view, large body; display field of view, 36 cm; scan length, 620 mm; and total exposure time, 6.81 s. Breath-hold scans were acquired craniocaudally from the supraclavicular fossa to the pubic symphysis. Contrast-enhanced images were obtained after intravenous administration of iodinated contrast agent (370 mgI/mL; Iopamiron 370, Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany) at 100 mL and 4.0 mL/s using a power injector. Arterial- and venous-phase imaging was initiated at 5 and 120 s, respectively, after a bolus-tracking program (Smart-pre; GE Healthcare) detected a 100 HU threshold in the descending aorta at the level of the bronchial carina.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Eight patients with traumatic adrenal hemorrhage treated with TAE were included (4 men and 4 women; age range, 47–89 years; median age, 78 years). All patients demonstrated active contrast extravasation on pre-procedural contrast-enhanced CT, with a median maximum hematoma diameter of 43 mm (range, 37–78 mm). Right-sided adrenal hemorrhage was observed in six patients (75%), while the remaining two patients (25%) had left-sided involvement; no bilateral cases were identified. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, vital signs, and outcomes are summarized in Table 1. All patients had multiple injuries, three required blood transfusions and two were receiving antithrombotic therapy. The median hospitalization duration was 13.0 days (range, 8–44 days). During hospitalization, follow-up CT was obtained in 7 of 8 patients, with 1 to 5 examinations per patient. These studies were independently reviewed by non-operator physicians and generally showed hematoma reduction or resorptive change without evidence of worsening active bleeding. Post-discharge follow-up was frequently continued at outside hospitals and was therefore not uniformly available for review. Mean pre- and post-procedural hemodynamic parameters were as follows: heart rate, 86 vs. 83 beats per minute; systolic/diastolic blood pressure, 138/78 vs. 123/61 mmHg; and hemoglobin, 12.3 vs. 10.3 g/dL. Post-procedural decreases in hemoglobin level should be interpreted cautiously in the context of the overall clinical course, as they may partly reflect hemodilution related to fluid administration rather than ongoing or recurrent bleeding. Anatomical patterns of the adrenal arteries

The anatomical patterns of detectable ADAs are summarized in Table 4 and Fig. 4. In our cohort, the inferior phrenic artery originated from the renal artery in three patients (38%), the aorta in two (25%), and the celiac trunk in three (38%). The superior ADA consistently arose from the inferior phrenic artery in all cases. The middle ADA arose from the aorta in two cases (25%), from the inferior phrenic artery in four cases (50%), and from the left gonadal artery in one case; it was not detected in one case (Fig. 3). The inferior ADA most commonly arose from the renal artery in four patients (50%), whereas it was not clearly visualized in the remaining four cases on angiography or contrast-enhanced CT. Discussion

Traumatic adrenal hemorrhage is a clinically important injury that may lead to life-threatening hemorrhage. While TAE has emerged as a valuable minimally invasive treatment option for selected patients with active contrast extravasation, its clinical utility and technical considerations remain insufficiently defined because of the rarity of this injury and the variability of the adrenal arterial anatomy. Therefore, we present a relatively large single-center case series of traumatic adrenal hemorrhage treated with TAE, with particular emphasis on in-hospital outcomes, angiographic anatomy, and embolization strategy, alongside a focused review of the literature. Traumatic adrenal hemorrhage is an uncommon injury, typically unilateral and predominantly affecting the right adrenal gland. Its low incidence likely reflects the protected retroperitoneal location and small size of the adrenal glands [3, 7]. Proposed mechanisms include direct compression, inferior vena cava-related venous hypertension, venous thrombosis or obstruction, and shear injury to small intraglandular vessels during deceleration [2, 4]. These anatomical and pathophysiological factors may also explain the right-sided predominance, as the right adrenal gland is particularly susceptible to compression between the liver and spine and drains directly into the inferior vena cava via a short vein, facilitating abrupt increases in venous pressure. Adrenal injury is associated with increased mortality, higher morbidity, and a greater burden of concomitant multi-organ injuries [2]. Given the rarity of this injury and the heterogeneity of mechanisms and clinical severity, optimal management remains controversial.

Currently, no consensus exists regarding the necessity or timing of intervention. Some authors advocate early treatment, whereas others report favorable outcomes with conservative management. Liao et al. [8] demonstrated that extravasation alone does not mandate intervention, while the need for treatment increases substantially when extravasation is accompanied by a mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 70 mmHg. However, several reports describe patients with MAP ≥ 70 mmHg who were initially managed conservatively but later required TAE [9–11], suggesting that MAP ≥ 70 mmHg may be insufficient as a standalone threshold. Persistent contrast extravasation may justify consideration of TAE even in initially hemodynamically stable patients. Accordingly, indications for TAE should be individualized based on hemodynamics and trends, concomitant injuries, transfusion requirements, and imaging findings. A thorough understanding of adrenal arterial anatomy, including potential access routes, is essential for patient selection and procedural planning.

Each adrenal gland is supplied by three principal arteries: the superior, middle, and inferior ADAs. These vessels show marked variability in origin, most commonly arising from the inferior phrenic artery (IPA), aorta, and renal artery, respectively [12–14]. Each ADA divides into numerous fine branches forming a dense capsular plexus, frequently resulting in multifocal bleeding from small feeders during hemorrhage [7, 12, 15, 16]. While the superior and inferior ADAs typically originate from the IPA and renal artery, respectively, the middle ADA demonstrates the greatest variability, potentially arising from the aorta or IPA or may be absent altogether. Rare variants—including branches from the celiac axis, intercostal, polar renal, gonadal, or even superior mesenteric arteries—necessitate a systematic angiographic search when initial selective studies are negative [12–14, 17].

Given their frequent involvement with the IPA, detailed knowledge of its branching patterns is critical. Aslaner et al. reported that a common IPA trunk is present in approximately 30% of patients, typically arising from the aorta [18]; otherwise, IPAs usually originate from the celiac axis. This distribution shifts in the presence of celiac axis variants, such as hepatosplenic or hepatomesenteric trunks, where IPAs more frequently arise from the aorta, left gastric artery, or renal artery. Consistent with this variability, we observed the right IPA originating from the right renal artery in three of eight patients. In these cases, the superior and middle ADAs arose from the IPA, and the inferior ADA originated directly from the right renal artery. This pattern suggests that early selective renal angiography should be considered to identify an IPA originating from the renal artery and its adrenal branches, particularly when conventional IPA or aortic origins are not visualized. Building on prior case reports and smaller series, our study provides a more structured description of adrenal feeder anatomy, the angiographic search strategy, and the technical implications of variable adrenal arterial supply for embolization planning in traumatic adrenal hemorrhage. The available English-language evidence on TAE for traumatic adrenal injury remains limited, consisting mainly of case reports and small series. For contextual comparison, we reviewed 10 relevant original articles and extracted data only for TAE-treated patients from studies including mixed treatment modalities [5, 9–11, 15, 19–23]. Across these published cases, the culprit arteries were variable, with frequent involvement of adrenal branches from the inferior phrenic and renal arterial territories. Some cases involved multiple bleeding sources or required multi-artery embolization, supporting the need for a systematic angiographic search strategy (Tables 5 and 6). A systematic angiographic approach—beginning with the IPA and renal arterial territories and extending to adjacent feeders if needed—may be critical for timely identification of bleeding sources and minimizing incomplete hemostasis or recurrent hemorrhage. Although embolization of multiple ADAs is sometimes required, adrenal insufficiency appears to be uncommon in unilateral injuries. However, adrenal insufficiency has been reported in bilateral adrenal trauma [24, 25], underscoring the need for vigilant endocrine follow-up in such patients. In our cohort, no patient received post-procedural steroid replacement therapy. Patient 8 presented with hyponatremia and hyperkalemia on admission, but these abnormalities were clinically attributed to trauma-related acute kidney injury secondary to rhabdomyolysis rather than definitive adrenal insufficiency. In the setting of severe polytrauma, electrolyte abnormalities may be multifactorial and should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the 2017 SCCM/ESICM guidelines [26] for critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency suggest against routine corticosteroid use in major trauma and reserve corticosteroid therapy for selected critically ill patients, particularly those with refractory shock rather than hemodynamically stable trauma patients. Accordingly, even if trauma-related adrenal insufficiency is theoretically possible after adrenal hemorrhage or embolization, post-procedural hormone replacement should be approached cautiously in unilateral injuries without persistent biochemical, hemodynamic, or clinical findings strongly suggestive of adrenal failure. This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design, small sample size, and absence of a comparator group limit generalizability. Second, treatment effectiveness was assessed primarily on the basis of short-term in-hospital hemorrhage control. Because all patients had multisystem trauma, changes in hemoglobin level, transfusion requirement, overall clinical course, and post-embolization electrolyte trends may have been influenced by associated injuries, fluid resuscitation, and other trauma-related factors rather than adrenal hemorrhage alone. Third, endocrine assessment was based mainly on clinical course and routine laboratory findings, and formal hormonal testing such as serum cortisol measurement or ACTH stimulation testing was not uniformly performed; therefore, subclinical adrenal insufficiency cannot be completely excluded. In addition, follow-up imaging was not protocolized, and post-discharge follow-up care was often provided at outside hospitals, limiting uniform evaluation of delayed rebleeding and late endocrine sequelae. In conclusion, TAE appears to be a technically feasible hemostatic option in selected patients with traumatic adrenal hemorrhage and CT-detected contrast extravasation; however, the necessity of embolization and its comparative benefit over conservative management remain unclear. Careful evaluation of the complex and variable adrenal arterial anatomy may facilitate identification of the culprit vessel and procedural planning. However, the small sample size, retrospective design, lack of a comparator group, and limited follow-up preclude definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy or long-term safety.
