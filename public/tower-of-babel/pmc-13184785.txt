Pharmacist Outreach and SGLT2 Inhibitor Uptake in Patients With Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Deborah L. Pestka, Daniel Murphy, Adam N. Kaplan, Brent C. Taylor, Pearl Huynh, Jessica A. Rechtzigel, Shari Kjos, Lisa Marie Ellich, Melissa Atwood, Beth A. Polsfuss, Amber R. Thomas, Joseph Y. Lee, Areef Ishani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This quality improvement study investigates whether pharmacist-led outreach is associated with improved uptake of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor medication among patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and type 2 diabetes.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common condition affecting nearly 15% of adults in the US,1 and it contributes substantially to morbidity, mortality, and health care costs.2,3,4,5,6 Previously, medications to decrease CKD progression were limited mainly to renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibition. However, studies have demonstrated significant benefits of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors in patients with CKD.7,8,9,10

SGLT2 inhibitors work by lowering the kidney tubular threshold for glucose excretion via reducing the reabsorption of filtered glucose and consequently increasing urinary glucose and sodium excretion.11,12 While these medications were initially developed for the treatment of diabetes, several large randomized clinical trials have demonstrated significant benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with CKD.7,8,9,10 These trials consistently showed that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce progression of CKD, reduce progression to kidney failure, lead to fewer cardiovascular events, and improve survival. The evidence supporting the benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors marks a significant paradigm shift in the management of CKD. As a result, Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and the American Diabetes Association both recommend SGLT2 inhibitors for patients with type 2 diabetes, CKD, and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 or greater.13,14

Given the substantial benefits offered by SGLT2 inhibitors, it is important to initiate treatment with these medications in eligible patients. Nevertheless, prescribing patterns remain low.15,16,17,18 Use of clinical pharmacists, key members of the care team with expertise in medication optimization, is a strategy that could be explored to increase initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors. Pharmacists, with their ability to initiate, adjust, and/or discontinue medications in many clinical areas, offer a promising approach to identify eligible patients and initiate underused but high-impact medications such as SGLT2 inhibitors. The objective of this study was to assess whether pharmacists’ proactive outreach to patients with CKD and type 2 diabetes would be associated with increased initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor use.

Methods

Design

This quality improvement study involved a multicenter learning health system initiative across 8 Veterans Affairs (VA) health systems (ISRCTN12374636). Using a pseudorandomization approach, eligible veterans were assigned to the intervention group if their Social Security number ended in an odd digit, while those with a number ending in an even digit remained in the usual care group. In most clinical experimental trials, randomization is a means of unbiased condition assignment that typically ensures a balance in baseline patient characteristics between conditions. While designating condition based on the value of the final Social Security number digit is not perfectly random, it can be an unbiased condition assignment strategy, and in a large study sample such as this one, balance in patient characteristics between conditions can be attained.19 Furthermore, it is an unbiased assignment approach that has been successfully used in other studies and is a pragmatic approach conducive for learning health systems.20,21 Group assignment began in February 2022 and continued through April 2024. The study protocol has been previously published.22 The Minneapolis VA Health Care System Human Research Protection Program determined that as a quality improvement initiative this study did not meet the definition of human participants research and therefore did not need institutional review board oversight. Since the goal was to ultimately reach all patients with this initiative, the need for patient informed consent was also waived. This study followed the Standard for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 reporting guideline.23

Setting

The VA is divided into different regional systems of care across the US called Veterans Integrated Service Networks (VISNs). This study focused on the Midwest VISN, which includes 8 VA health systems across Minnesota, Iowa, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Nebraska.

Participants

As part of its Academic Detailing Diabetes Campaign and to monitor SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, the VA developed a clinical diabetes dashboard to identify patients with CKD who would benefit from an SGLT2 inhibitor. The dashboard includes several demographic and clinical variables as well as inclusion and exclusion criteria, which served as the criteria used for this study. Veterans were included if they had CKD (defined as eGFR≥25 and <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 upon 2 measurements, as extracted from the VA electronic health record [EHR]) and type 2 diabetes (defined as either an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision [ICD-9] or International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] code of type 2 diabetes or a most recent glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] level of ≥7% of total hemoglobin [to convert to proportion of total hemoglobin, multiply by 0.01]). Veterans were excluded if they had type 1 diabetes, were already prescribed an SGLT2 inhibitor at the time of data acquisition, had an SGLT2 inhibitor allergy, or had a diagnosis of pancreatic cancer or pancreatitis (the latter 2 were preexisting exclusion criteria from the VA diabetes dashboard).

Participants’ race and ethnicity, ascertained from the EHR, were included in the study because of their association with kidney disease. Race categories were American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Pacific Islander, White, and other (included unknown race and patients who declined to answer). Ethnicity categories were Hispanic and missing data or not known to be Hispanic.

Patient lists were extracted from the VA dashboard at fixed time points throughout the study and were considered the dates of treatment assignment. The intervention began at the Minneapolis, Minnesota, VA and was implemented at other systems in a rolling fashion (ie, implementation was staggered but not by any fixed time).

Intervention

The outreach team consisted of 2 clinical pharmacists (P.H., J.A.R.), 1 medical assistant (L.M.E.), and 1 licensed practical nurse (S.K.). After confirming that patients were not using an SGLT2 inhibitor, the study team mailed each participant in the intervention group a letter describing the benefits of SGTL2 inhibitors and notifying patients that they would be receiving a call from the study team. Patients were then called and scheduled for a 30-minute appointment with a pharmacist via telephone. If the pharmacist determined that the patient was an ideal candidate for the medication and the patient agreed, empagliflozin was prescribed by the pharmacist. Thirty days after the medication was initiated (determined by the pharmacist prescribing the medication at the initial visit), a study team member followed up with the patient to assess any medication-related adverse effects and review medication-related laboratory results. If the patient had any adverse effects or abnormal laboratory findings, the pharmacist would address these, including discontinuing the medication, if necessary. However, there was a nephrologist (J.Y.L.) available to consult with the pharmacists, and patients were encouraged to continue taking the medication if any adverse effects could be addressed with simple advice or were reassessed with additional laboratory tests. The study pharmacists were encouraged to have the patient keep taking the SGLT2 inhibitor while managing any metabolic abnormality, if able. If the patient’s condition was stable on the medication at 30 days, a 1-year prescription was provided. SGLT2 inhibitor prescribing was then transitioned back to the patient’s primary care practitioner after 12 months.

The usual care group did not receive any targeted outreach but could be prescribed the medication during the natural course of care. More details of the intervention are published elsewhere.22

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was initiation of an SGLT2 inhibitor among patients deemed candidates for SGLT2 inhibitors as determined by the VA dashboard. Patients who filled an SGLT2 inhibitor prescription at any point during the study time frame, as determined by EHR data, were considered to have initiated the medication.

Our secondary outcome was SGLT2 inhibitor prescription fill 1 year after treatment assignment. Our exploratory outcome was a hierarchical composite of time to occurrence of outcomes presented in the order of their importance: patient mortality, kidney failure (estimated eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2), heart failure, myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke. We also assessed these as outcomes independently in a supporting sensitivity analysis.

All process data were collected through a tracking app developed specifically for this project. Demographic and clinical outcome data were collected and stored in the VA EHR.

Statistical Analysis

We summarized patient demographics and clinical measures using descriptive statistics (ie, means with SDs, frequencies with percentages). We fit a nested Cox proportional hazards regression model regressing the time to first prescription during follow-up onto the intervention arm label with parent facility as the cluster and graphically displayed the results using Kaplan-Meier curves. Prescription rates at less than 3 months, at 6 months, and at 12 months after treatment assignment are graphically represented. We regressed the secondary outcome of an SGLT2 inhibitor prescription filled at 12 months after assignment onto the intervention arm label using Poisson regression with log link. Subgroup analyses were carried out based on baseline age, body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared), Care Assessment Needs (CAN) score (a VA risk score for predicted mortality at 1 year),24 HbA1c, urine albumin-creatinine ratio, and eGFR. Analysis-of-variance tables were constructed to assess the significance of the interaction between levels of each subgroup’s characteristics and the intervention’s association with prescription fill rates.

We constructed a hierarchical composite exploratory outcome of the following exploratory outcomes: all-cause mortality, eGFR less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m2, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke (in the order of importance). We carried out a win ratio analysis of time to these events fit to this hierarchy.25,26,27 The win ratio compared each intervention patient (odd Social Security number) with every control patient (even Social Security number) on the event durations: starting at the top of the hierarchy, if the intervention patient had a longer event duration (eg, longer time to all-cause mortality) than the control patient, then this paired comparison was considered a win; if not, then it was deemed a loss. If both patients had equivalent durations, then it was considered a tie at that hierarchical level, and the comparison was progressed to the time to the next event (eg, eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2). This process continued until the final outcome in the hierarchy; if there was no win or loss at the final outcome, then this paired comparison was considered a tie. Ties between the pharmacist-outreach and control arms occurred because many patients shared both a follow-up start date and a VA medical facility, with the latter determining the fixed censoring date. In our win ratio analysis, these equal follow-up times were treated as neutral observations and were not manually altered. When a tie occurred in the time-to-mortality outcome, the pair was subsequently compared based on the next prioritized outcome in the clinical hierarchy.

The number of wins divided by losses represents the estimate for the win ratio; subsequently, 1000 bootstrap samples were taken to construct the 95% CI of the win ratio. We carried out a complier-inverse probability-weighted analysis for the win ratio and report its estimate, 95% CI, and P value using the methods described by Cui et al.28 We carried out subgroup win ratio analyses based on the subgroup variables listed for the primary outcome. As a sensitivity analysis, we used a standard Cox proportional hazards regression survival analysis to measure the hazard of each event individually and compositely due to intervention label status.

All analyses were intention-to-treat analyses (ie, patients were analyzed based on their original intervention or control arm label). The primary and secondary analyses used a 2-sided P value less than .05 to determine statistical significance; subgroup and sensitivity analyses were considered exploratory. Estimates (incidence rate ratios [IRRs], win ratios, and risk ratios) are presented with 95% CIs and P values. For the analyses, we used R, version 4.5.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing),29 with the following packages: tidyverse, WinRatio, survival, and table1.

Results

The initial study population included 8684 veterans. Due to delayed mortality data ascertainment in the VA EHR system, 26 patients (0.3%) died prior to their treatment assignment date, yielding an analysis dataset of 8658 patients—4400 (50.8%) in the intervention and 4258 (49.2%) in the control arm (Figure 1). This patient sample mostly comprised older adults (mean [SD] age, 78.7 [8.1] years); 290 (3.3%) were females and 8368 (96.7%) were males. A total of 76 patients (0.9%) were American Indian or Alaska Native; 11 (0.1%), Asian; 327 (3.8%), Black; 44 (0.5%), Pacific Islander; 7435 (85.9%), White; and 765 (8.8%), other race. Forty-two (0.5%) were Hispanic and 8616 (99.5%) had missing ethnicity data or were not known to be Hispanic. Patients had mean (SD) baseline eGFR of 46.1 (9.8) mL/min/1.73 m2, HbA1c of 7.02% (1.16%) of total hemoglobin, BMI of 31.8 (6.0), and systolic blood pressure of 132.8 (18.5) mm Hg (Table). In addition, 319 patients (3.7%) were prescribed a glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and 4506 (52.0%) an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker within 1 year of the index date. Between the 2 study conditions, patients had similar baseline characteristics.

After initial EHR review by the outreach team, 974 patients in the intervention group (22.1%) were deemed ineligible. Of those, 373 (38.3%) were deemed not a candidate due to health status, 229 (23.5%) had already started taking an SGLT2 inhibitor prior to contact, and 189 (19.4%) reported prior severe intolerability to an SGLT2 inhibitor. Of the 3426 patients contacted to schedule a pharmacy visit, 1629 (47.5%) indicated they were not interested (the primary reason was that they wanted to first consult with their practitioner). The other 1797 (52.5%) attended a pharmacist visit, and of those, 980 (54.5%) were prescribed an SGLT2 inhibitor. The biggest reason patients did not initiate the medication was because they wanted to consult with their practitioner first.

The cumulative proportion of patients with an SGLT2 inhibitor prescription filled within 1 year after group assignment was 1476 (33.5%) in the intervention group compared with 659 (15.5%) in the control group (Figure 2). The survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the SGLT2 inhibitor arm were significantly more likely to fill a prescription for empagliflozin (hazard ratio, 1.91; 95% CI, 1.69-2.16; P < .001) compared with the usual care arm (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Within the proactive outreach arm, the overall mean (SD) number of days between group assignment and the pharmacist outreach call was 132 (80) days. Among the patients that initiated an SGLT2 inhibitor, it was 134 (81) days. Overall, the median time between group assignment and pharmacist call in the proactive outreach arm was 122 days (IQR, 86-169 days).

Cumulative Initiation of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) Inhibitors Over 12 Months by Assignment Group

At 12 months after treatment assignment, the intervention group had a significantly higher prescription fill rate than the control group (IRR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.80-2.27); raw proportions were 864 patients (19.6%) vs 415 (9.7%) (P <.001) (Figure 3). The number needed to treat was 10; that is, for every 10 patients approached using SGLT2 inhibitor outreach, 1 additional patient had a prescription filled at 12 months. The subgroup analyses (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1 and eTable in Supplement 2) suggested that there were no differences in the intervention and control outcomes in any subgroup but 1: the IRR among the highest-quartile subgroup of HbA1c level (7.5%-16.6%) was 1.44 (95% CI, 1.16-1.79), compared with the 2 lowest quartiles (HbA1c of 6.3%-6.8%: IRR, 2.38 [95% CI, 1.84-3.11]; HbA1c of 4.2%-6.3%: IRR, 2.57 [95% CI, 2.01-3.31]).

Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) Inhibitor Prescription Fill Rates at Each Follow-Up Time Point

We observed no difference between the 2 conditions in terms of the hierarchical composite outcome in the win ratio analysis (Figure 4). The win ratio was 1.02 (95% CI, 0.97-1.07; P = .54). This win ratio did not statistically differ within any of the subgrouping characteristics (for each subgrouping variable, every subgroup level’s bootstrap 95% CIs overlapped). In our sensitivity analyses, not only did SGLT2 inhibitor outreach and control conditions not differ in their hazard of the nonhierarchical composite secondary outcomes but they also did not differ in any of these outcomes separately. The complier-propensity weighted win ratio estimate was 1.01 (95% CI, 0.96-1.07; P = .58). The inference was similar to that of the estimate and variability in the reported exploratory outcome analysis.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate whether a proactive pharmacist outreach intervention was associated with greater uptake of SGLT2 inhibitors using a learning health system approach. This multicenter initiative across 8 VA health systems found that pharmacist outreach was associated with higher initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors among patients with CKD and type 2 diabetes. Compared with usual care, patients that received the pharmacist outreach intervention were nearly twice as likely to receive a prescription for an SGLT2 inhibitor and twice as likely to be filling their prescription after 12 months. Despite the increase in uptake, there were no statistically significant differences in mortality, kidney failure, or cardiovascular events between the intervention and control groups during the relatively short study period.

A key strength of this study was that it relied on pharmacists to identify eligible patients through a clinical dashboard, initiate the medication, and conduct appropriate follow-up. While there was a nephrologist available for ad hoc consultations, this model did not require any additional resources or time from already busy primary care teams. Previous studies integrating pharmacists in other clinical areas, such as diabetes, blood pressure, and heart failure management, have similarly shown improved patient outcomes.30,31,32,33,34,35 Prior research identifying barriers to prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors found that primary care practitioners were less knowledgeable about the benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors outside of diabetes management, were cautious with new therapies, and had difficulty overcoming established prescription habits.36 In addition, it may be difficult for primary care practitioners to address all guideline-recommended assessments and treatments given the limited time of a primary care visit.37 As a result, pharmacists can play a pivotal role, as this study showed, in closing evidence-based gaps, specifically in the initiation of guideline-recommended therapies like SGLT2 inhibitors.

Although this study found significantly increased medication uptake with the intervention, there was no improvement in clinical outcomes over 12 months. This differs from results observed in a meta-analysis of clinical trials with larger cohorts,38 which may be attributed to several factors. Most importantly, in a clinical trial, nearly all of the intervention group receives the treatment and almost none of the control group does. Because only about one-quarter of assigned patients received the intervention in this study and patients in the usual care arm continued to have access to a new SGLT2 inhibitor prescription via their usual practitioners, this dilutes the ability to assess clinical impact. Also, for this study, clinical outcomes were a composite exploratory outcome, and the study was not powered using this outcome. Additionally, this was a quality improvement study with broad inclusion criteria conducted without the confines of a randomized clinical trial, which likely minimized an observable difference in outcomes, particularly over a relatively short follow-up period. Other studies demonstrating the clinical benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors often had follow-up periods of 2 to 3 years or longer.39,40,41 As a learning health system initiative, all eligible patients in this study were included, unlike traditional trials, which assess inclusion prior to randomization. However, the benefits to this approach are faster implementation, application to broad cohorts of eligible populations, and reduced costs. Clinical challenges, such as medication adherence, also likely limited the expected benefits seen in controlled trials. While several randomized clinical trials have shown the substantial benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors,7,8,9,10 more research is needed that uses pragmatic methods to increase the uptake of these therapies in the general population.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study was its novel design and learning health system approach. By assigning patients to groups using the final digit of the Social Security number, we achieved balance in baseline characteristics without the logistic complexity of formal randomization. This allowed for quick, systemwide implementation while still maintaining validity. It also exemplifies the benefits of using a learning health system approach to test evidence-based practices using routinely collected data and embedded clinical resources. Eligible veterans with an even final digit of their Social Security number were eventually approached to start the medication. However, assigning patients based on their Social Security number created even groups and allowed us to compare the intervention with usual care to evaluate outcomes. The study also required minimal resources beyond staffing and represents a feasible model for wide adoption, especially in time-constrained primary care settings.

There were a number of limitations of this study that should be noted. First, although patients were identified as treatment candidates based on a preestablished quality dashboard, upon EHR review, many were deemed ineligible (eg, not a good candidate due to health status or prior SGLT2 inhibitor intolerability). This likely diluted the association of the pharmacist intervention with SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, since a similar EHR review was not completed for control patients. However, this does mimic natural confounders that occur when doing studies within the context of a learning health system. Second, the intervention team was based out of Minneapolis. Therefore, receiving a telephone call from a different area code or receiving a letter from someone outside of the patient’s care team, despite still being in the VA, may have led to skepticism and reluctance among some patients to start a medication initiated by someone from outside their personal care team. Third, this study took place in the VA, which may limit generalizability to other health systems with different patient populations and different barriers to patients obtaining SGLT2 inhibitors (ie, insurance barriers). In addition, within the VA, pharmacists have prescribing authority for certain medications, which does not exist in all states or health systems.

Fourth, there are limitations to using Social Security numbers to determine groupings, and it is important to carefully consider the most pragmatic ways to assign intervention groups in an unbiased or minimally biased way. For example, if this Social Security number–based strategy were always used in this population, then the same people would always end up clustered together in the same intervention groups. When possible, fully randomized treatment assignment should be considered. If that is not possible, then other design-sided approaches for reducing the bias in treatment assignment should be considered.42 Fifth, this study only examined initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors. Patients may have been initiated on other medications (eg, RAS inhibitors, GLP-1 RAs, or nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) that could impact secondary outcomes, but given pseudorandomization and our intent-to-treat analysis, initiation of other CKD medications was likely similar between groups. This study also did not explore adherence. Therefore, the number needed to treat represents a filled prescription at 12 months rather than continuous therapy throughout the follow-up period.

Conclusions

This quality improvement study found that pharmacist-led outreach vs usual care was significantly associated with higher SGLT2 inhibitor initiation among patients with CKD and type 2 diabetes across multiple sites and US states, using an outreach team of minimal size. While there were no between-group differences in clinical outcomes during the follow-up period, this intervention may represent a strategy to improve uptake of guideline-recommended therapies, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, without adding burden to primary care teams for drug initiation and initial monitoring. Future work should explore strategies to optimize patient engagement and medication adherence and evaluate longer-term clinical and economic outcomes.
