Systematic identification and characterization of nine interferon regulatory factor (IRF) genes in spotted sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus) reveals teleost-specific expansions and pathogen-responsive specialization
Authors: Fei'e Liu, Yushuai Xie, Zhaosheng Sun, Zhang Luo, Lina Lei, Sidi Zheng, Zhao Jia, Hai Yang, Qingling Zhang, Qian Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First comprehensive identification and analysis of nine IRF genes in spotted sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus).•These genes contained characteristic IRF-family functional motifs and were broadly expressed across a range of tissues.•Duplicated genes IRF4a and IRF4b showed divergent expression, indicating subfunctionalization.•IRF6, IRF7, and IRF10 were strongly induced by antiviral stimulation, while IRF8 and IRF11 showed strong responsiveness to bacterial challenge.•IRF9 responded to both viral and bacterial challenges, suggesting a broad immunoregulatory role.Keywords: Lateolabrax maculatus, Interferon regulatory factor, Gene cloning, Gene expression, Immune challenge

Introduction

The interferon (IFN) system is a fundamental component of vertebrate innate immunity and a first line of defense against viral invasion [1]. Establishment of an effective antiviral state relies on precise transcriptional activation of IFN genes [2], a process primarily regulated by interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) [3]. IRFs coordinate the expression of IFNs and numerous interferon‑stimulated genes (ISGs) [4,5]. Beyond antiviral functions, IRFs participate in antibacterial defense, immune cell differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Mammals possess nine IRF members (IRF1-IRF9) [10], all sharing a conserved N‑terminal DNA‑binding domain (DBD) of approximately 120 amino acids [11]. The DBD contains a hallmark series of five to six tryptophan residues that recognize DNA motifs similar to the interferon‑stimulated response element (ISRE) [12,13]. The C‑terminal IRF‑association domain (IAD) facilitates homodimerization, heterodimerization, and interactions with transcription co‑factors [14,15], enabling assembly of functionally specific transcriptional complexes [16]. While the IAD is well conserved, flanking C‑terminal sequences are variable, contributing to functional diversity [17]. Phylogenetically, mammalian IRFs are classified into four subfamilies: IRF1, IRF3, IRF4, and IRF5 [18,19]. Based on regulatory activity, IRF1, IRF3, IRF5, IRF7, and IRF9 are primarily activators [5,18], whereas IRF2, IRF4, IRF5, and IRF8 can act as context‑dependent activators or repressors [3,5].

To date, IRFs have been extensively identified and functionally characterized in a variety of teleost species, including mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) [20], Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) [21], Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) [22], olive flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) [23], Sichuan taimen (Hucho bleekeri) [2], zig-zag eel (Mastacembelus armatus) [24], blunt snout bream (Megalobrama amblycephala) [25,26], large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) [27], and golden pompano (Trachinotus ovatus) [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. These studies collectively underscore the pivotal role of IRFs in antiviral defense against pathogens such as ranaviruses [20,32], nodaviruses [33,34], and rhabdoviruses [35,36], as well as in antibacterial immunity against Vibrio [37,38], Aeromonas [[24], [25], [26]], and Edwardsiella species [39,40]. Expression analyses reveal that IRF genes are rapidly induced upon immune challenge; for example, most IRFs are upregulated by Poly (I:C) stimulation in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) and large yellow croaker [27,41], while IRF7, IRF9, and IRF11 respond specifically to Vibrio infection. Compared to mammals, the teleost IRF family has undergone lineage-specific expansion, largely attributed to whole‑genome duplication events [41,42]. This expansion has led to a more complex repertoire, featuring teleost‑specific members (e.g., IRF11) [18] and duplicated paralogs (e.g., IRF4a/4b) [43,44], which may facilitate enhanced immune adaptability to aquatic pathogens. Such duplicated genes often acquire subfunctionalized or neofunctionalized roles, allowing for fine‑tuned, stimulus‑ or tissue‑specific regulatory responses [[45], [46], [47]]. Despite these advances, a systematic characterization of the complete IRF family is still lacking for many economically important teleost species. This gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of their immune regulatory networks and functional diversification.

The spotted sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus) is a high‑value mariculture species in East Asia [48]. According to the China Fishery Statistical Yearbook (2025), the aquaculture production in China reached approximately 257,076 tons in 2024 [49]. However, the intensification of aquaculture practices has increased the incidence of infectious diseases, resulting in substantial economic losses [50,51]. Major threats include viral pathogens such as iridoviruses and nodaviruses [52,53], and bacterial infections like edwardsiellosis caused by Edwardsiella tarda [54]. Although a few IRFs (e.g., IRF1 and IRF3) have been reported in L. maculatus [55], a comprehensive analysis of the IRF family, key regulators of pathogen‑induced immunity, is unavailable.

In this study, we isolated and characterized nine IRF family members from spotted sea bass, designated LmIRF4a, LmIRF4b, LmIRF5, LmIRF6, LmIRF7, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, LmIRF10, and LmIRF11. We performed sequence analyses and investigated their tissue distribution and temporal expression patterns following challenge with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), Poly (I:C), and E. tarda. This work provides the first comprehensive overview of the IRF gene family in spotted sea bass, establishing a molecular foundation for understanding its immune regulation and supporting future disease management strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All procedures complied with local animal welfare regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Ocean University (Approval No. SHOU-DW-2019-003).

Experimental animals, in vivo challenge, and sample collection

Healthy spotted sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus) with a mean total length of 21.0 ± 4.0 cm and a body weight of 200.0 ± 40.0 g were obtained from Xiaoxiang Aquatic Farm (Xiaoshan, Hangzhou, China). Prior to experimentation, the fish were acclimatized for two weeks under controlled conditions: water temperature was maintained at approximately 26 °C, dissolved oxygen > 6.0 mg/L, and pH ≈ 7.0. During this acclimation period, no feed was supplied. Two-thirds of the rearing water was replaced daily, and a 14 h light : 10 h dark photoperiod was implemented.

Fish were randomly divided into five groups (A-E). Groups A-D (35 fish each) received intraperitoneal injections of 200 μL of LPS (5 μg/μL), Poly (I:C) (4 μg/μL), live E. tarda (5×10⁵ CFU/mL), or sterile PBS (control), respectively. Group E (10 fish) served as unchallenged controls for baseline expression. At 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post‑injection (hpi), five fish per group (A-D) were anesthetized with 0.05% MS‑222 (Sigma‑Aldrich, USA). Head kidney (HK), spleen, gill, and intestine were collected, snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C. For baseline profiling, six unchallenged fish (Group E) were sampled, and gill, skin, muscle, liver, spleen, intestine, HK, and brain were collected. Spleen tissues from three unchallenged fish were also preserved for gene cloning. All samples were processed individually to maintain biological independence.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For expression analysis, first‑strand cDNA was synthesized from individual RNA samples using Hifair® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (YEASEN, China). For cloning, cDNA was synthesized from pooled spleen RNA; 5′‑ and 3′‑RACE‑ready cDNAs were prepared with the SMARTer RACE 5′/3′ Kit (Takara, Japan). All cDNAs were diluted 20‑fold and stored at -40 °C.

Cloning of full-length LmIRF cDNAs

Partial coding sequences were identified by local BLASTn searches against the spotted sea bass genome (assembly GCA_004023545.1) using IRF sequences from related perciforms as queries. Corresponding fragments were amplified from pooled spleen cDNA by PCR with gene specific primers (Tables 1) and 2× Ex Taq PCR Mix (Takara, Japan). Full‑length sequences were obtained by 5′‑ and 3′‑RACE using the SMARTer™ RACE 5′/3′ Kit (Takara, Japan). Sequences were assembled using SeqMan (DNASTAR). ORFs were predicted with NCBI ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ORF finder/), and final sequences were verified by PCR with 2× PrimerSTAR Max Premix (Takara, Japan) and Sanger sequencing (GENEWIZ, China).

Bioinformatics analysis of LmIRFs

The deduced amino acid sequences of the LmIRF genes were obtained using the Translate tool on the ExPASy server (http://web.expasy.org/translate/). Protein molecular weight and theoretical isoelectric point (pI) were predicted using ProtParam (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The presence of a signal peptide was predicted for each IRF protein using SignalP-5.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/SignalP/). Protein domain architectures were predicted using the Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (SMART; http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). Multiple sequence alignment of LmIRF amino acid sequences with their homologs from other species was conducted using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/), and the resulting percent identity matrix was generated with MegAlign software (DNASTAR). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA 11.0 software, with bootstrap confidence tested by 1000 replicates. Subcellular localization and nuclear localization signals (NLS) were predicted using the PSORT II online tool (https://psort.hgc.jp/). Synteny analysis was conducted using the Genomicus v108.01 platform (https://www.genomicus.bio.ens.psl.eu/genomicus-108.01/cgi-bin/search.pl), comparing genomic regions around LmIRF loci with those of representative vertebrates.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Gene-specific primers (Table 2) were designed based on the ORF sequences to quantify the baseline expression of LmIRFs and their expression changes in response to challenge with LPS, Poly (I:C), or live E. tarda. qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Germany). The qPCR product of each target gene was cloned into the pMD™19-T vector (Takara, Japan) and verified by sequencing. Recombinant plasmids were serially diluted 10-fold from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁸ to generate a standard curve. The resulting standard curves exhibited a primer amplification efficiency of 90–110% and a correlation coefficient (R²) ≥ 0.99. Reactions (10 μL) contained 5 μL SYBR Green Mix, 3 μL diluted cDNA, 0.2 μL each primer (10 μM), and 1.6 μL nuclease‑free water. Cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 20 s; followed by melting curve analysis. All reactions were run in triplicate. Expression levels were calculated via the standard curve method and normalized to elongation factor 1‑alpha (LmEF1α).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was tested with SPSS 25.0 (IBM, USA). Differences between treatment and control groups at each time point were analyzed by two‑tailed unpaired Student’s t‑test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with significance set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

Sequence characteristics of LmIRFs

In this study, nine LmIRF genes, including LmIRF4a, LmIRF4b, LmIRF5, LmIRF6, LmIRF7, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, LmIRF10, and LmIRF11, were successfully cloned from the spotted sea bass (Supplementary Figure 1). The complete cDNA sequences ranged from 1803 to 2465 bp, containing ORFs of 795‐1485 bp that encode putative proteins comprising 264 to 494 amino acid residues. Each 3′‑UTR contained a canonical polyadenylation signal (AATAAA, ATTAAA, or ATAAA). An instability motif (ATTTA) was present in the 3′‑UTRs of LmIRF4b, LmIRF6, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, and LmIRF11. Predicted molecular weights ranged from 30.87 kDa (LmIRF11) to 55.69 kDa (LmIRF5), with pI values of 5.12-9.30. All proteins except LmIRF11 contained both the DBD (with 4-6 tryptophan repeats) and IAD. LmIRF11 only contains the DBD and lacks a canonical IAD. Subcellular localization predictions indicated nuclear localization. Sequence features are summarized in Table 3.

Synteny analysis of LmIRFs

Comparative synteny revealed conserved genomic neighborhoods for LmIRF4, LmIRF5, LmIRF6, LmIRF7, LmIRF8, and LmIRF9 (Fig. 1A-F). LmIRF4a and LmIRF4b resided on separate scaffolds within distinct syntenic blocks. The LmIRF10 locus showed partially conserved microsynteny (Fig. 1G), while the LmIRF11 neighborhood exhibited lower syntenic conservation (Fig. 1H). IRF10 and IRF11 were absent from mammalian genomes.

Multiple sequence alignment and identity analysis

Multiple sequence alignment revealed that IRF sequences are remarkably conserved across vertebrates. With the exception of IRF11, all IRF proteins exhibited a conserved domain architecture consistent with their vertebrate counterparts, comprising a conserved DBD characterized by typical tryptophan repeats, along with a C-terminal IAD. In contrast, IRF11 possesses only a DBD (Supplementary Fig. 2A-G). LmIRF4a shared 69.2-94.8% amino acid identity with teleost IRF4a orthologs, highest (94.8%) with large yellow croaker. LmIRF4b showed 60.4-89.0% identity, highest (89.0%) with butterflyfish (Chaetodon trifascialis). LmIRF5, LmIRF6, and LmIRF8 displayed ≥90% identity to orthologs in large yellow croaker and mandarin fish. LmIRF7 shared 53.6% identity with common carp (Cyprinus carpio) IRF7, while its identity to other teleost IRF7 sequences exceeded 80.0%. The highest sequence identities for LmIRF9, LmIRF10, and LmIRF11 were observed with their mandarin fish counterparts, at 85.5%, 86.9%, and 89.4%, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method to elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the LmIRFs. All LmIRF members robustly clustered within established vertebrate IRF subfamilies (Fig. 2). Specifically, LmIRF5 and LmIRF6 grouped together, while LmIRF4, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, and LmIRF10 formed a distinct cluster; LmIRF7 constituted a separate branch. Each LmIRF exhibited closest affinity with its teleost orthologs: LmIRF4a with large yellow croaker IRF4a; LmIRF5, LmIRF6, LmIRF9, LmIRF10, and LmIRF11 with their counterparts in mandarin fish; and LmIRF7 with golden pompano IRF7.

Tissue distribution of LmIRFs in healthy fish

Basal expression of the nine LmIRF genes was detected in all eight examined tissues (gill, skin, muscle, liver, spleen, intestine, HK, brain), with distinct tissue‑specific patterns (Fig. 3). LmIRF4a mRNA was highest in spleen, with substantial levels in intestine, brain, and gill. LmIRF4b, LmIRF5, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, and LmIRF10 showed relatively higher expression in spleen, brain, and gill. LmIRF6, LmIRF7, and LmIRF11 were most abundant in gill.

Discussion

The IRF family plays pivotal roles in vertebrate innate immunity, particularly in regulating IFN responses against pathogens [56,57]. In this study, we systematically identified and characterized nine IRF genes (LmIRFs) in the spotted sea bass. Our analyses confirm their orthology within the teleost IRF family and reveal distinct expression profiles under pathogen‑associated stimuli, highlighting functional complexity and specialization.

Synteny analysis showed conserved genomic neighborhoods for several LmIRF genes. For example, LmIRF7 resides in a syntenic block flanked by PHRF1 and PNPLA2, a configuration conserved across vertebrates [58,59], indicating strong evolutionary constraint. The duplicated genes LmIRF4a and LmIRF4b are located on separate scaffolds within distinct syntenic contexts, supporting their origin from teleost‑specific whole‑genome duplication [60]. This pattern is consistent with reports in other teleosts such as yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) [40], mandarin fish [20], and Atlantic cod [22], and likely provided the basis for subfunctionalization. The presence of IRF10 and IRF11 [61], which are absent in mammals [62], underscores lineage‑specific expansion in teleosts, potentially enhancing immune adaptability in aquatic environments. Lower syntenic conservation around LmIRF11 suggests greater evolutionary plasticity for this teleost‑specific gene. Phylogenetic reconstruction robustly grouped the nine LmIRFs into established vertebrate subfamilies, confirming orthology assignments. The topology followed the four‑subfamily scheme: IRF1 (IRF1, IRF2, IRF11), IRF3 (IRF3, IRF7), IRF4 (IRF4, IRF8, IRF9, IRF10), and IRF5 (IRF5, IRF6) [18,20]. Each LmIRF clustered closely with orthologs from species such as mandarin fish and large yellow croaker, further validating their annotation and reflecting shared evolutionary history.

Structurally, all LmIRF proteins except LmIRF11 contain both the conserved DBD and IAD, which are essential for transcription factor function [12,63]. The DBD contains characteristic tryptophan repeats that mediate DNA binding to ISRE motifs [64,65], while the IAD enables dimerization and cofactor interactions, thereby contributing to functional diversification [14,66]. LmIRF11 retains only the DBD and lacks a canonical IAD, a feature also observed in other teleosts [32,[67], [68], [69], [70]]. As a teleost‑specific member of the IRF1 subfamily [18,69], LmIRF11 lacks the IAD, and the mechanism by which it regulates transcription remains unknown. Studies in other teleosts have demonstrated that IRF11 can synergize with STAT1 and STAT2 to promote type I interferon production despite the absence of the IAD [68], and structural analyses have shown that IRF11 binds DNA through its DBD without requiring IAD‑mediated dimerization [61]. These findings suggest that LmIRF11 may act as a dominant‑negative regulator by competing with other IRFs for ISRE binding sites or participate in non‑canonical transcriptional complexes. Co‑immunoprecipitation, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, and reporter gene analyses are required to elucidate the precise mechanism of LmIRF11 in spotted sea bass.

All nine LmIRF genes were constitutively expressed across tissues, consistent with broad immune‑regulatory roles [71]. Tissue‑specific expression implied functional specialization. As a primary mucosal interface constantly exposed to pathogens [72], the gill maintains heightened immune surveillance. High basal expression of LmIRF6, LmIRF7, and LmIRF11 in gill aligns with their postulated role in frontline mucosal defense [27,28,67,73,74]. IRF11 is a teleost‑specific gene arising from lineage‑specific duplications within the IRF1 subfamily [18,41]; its elevated basal expression in gill suggests specialized functions in mucosal immunity. In contrast, the spleen as a major secondary lymphoid organ [75] showed predominant expression of LmIRF4a, LmIRF4b, LmIRF5, LmIRF8, LmIRF9, and LmIRF10, reflecting conserved functions in systemic immunity and lymphocyte regulation [[76], [77], [78]].

LPS, a component of the Gram‑negative bacterial outer membrane [79], primarily activates TLR4/MyD88‑dependent signaling [80], whereas live E. tarda presents multiple PAMPs and virulence factors [81], potentially engaging a broader array of sensors (e.g., NLRs, cytosolic DNA sensors) and integrating inflammatory with type I IFN responses [82]. LPS and live E. tarda induced distinct LmIRF expression profiles, reflecting differential PRR pathway activation. Notably, LmIRF8 and LmIRF11 were specifically and strongly upregulated by E. tarda but not by LPS, indicating specialized antibacterial roles. Similar induction of IRF8 and IRF11 by bacterial pathogens has been observed in other teleosts. For instance, IRF8 was significantly induced by Aeromonas hydrophila in Nile tilapia and blunt snout bream [25,83], and IRF11 responded vigorously to Vibrio alginolyticus infection in large yellow croaker [27]. Their activation may require signaling from intracellular sensors or specific bacterial effectors absent in pure LPS stimulation. Furthermore, LPS induced only limited LmIRF expression, suggesting a relatively weak transcriptional response to this stimulus in fish, consistent with earlier reports [84,85].

The synthetic double‑stranded RNA analog Poly (I:C), a well-established viral mimic, potently activates antiviral defense mainly through TLR3 and cytosolic RIG‑I‑like receptors (RLRs) such as MDA5 [86]. LmIRF6, LmIRF7, LmIRF9 and LmIRF10 were particularly responsive to Poly (I:C) across multiple tissues, implicating them as central players in antiviral defense in spotted sea bass. Strong induction of LmIRF7 aligns with its conserved role as a master regulator of type I IFN production [87,88]. Similar induction patterns have been reported in Sichuan taimen, where Poly (I:C) injection rapidly upregulated IRF7 [2], and in mandarin fish, where IRF7 was strongly induced by Poly (I:C) and ISKNV infection [20]. Co‑induction of LmIRF6 and LmIRF10 suggests collaborative action in establishing an antiviral state. IRF9 is typically associated with IFN signaling via the ISGF3 complex [89]. LmIRF9 responded to both Poly (I:C) and bacterial challenge, indicating broader immunoregulatory functions, as seen in common carp and half‑smooth tongue sole (Cynoglossus semilaevis) [47,90]. Tissue‑specific modulation, such as widespread downregulation in gills at late time points, may reflect feedback control to prevent excessive inflammation. The pronounced response of LmIRF7 and LmIRF10 mirrors findings in lumpfish and mandarin fish [20,46], underscoring a conserved yet potentially species‑specific hierarchy among IRFs in antiviral immunity. Together, these data highlight that the Poly (I:C)‑responsive IRF module (LmIRF6/7/10) in L. maculatus is evolutionarily conserved for antiviral defense, yet its precise regulatory networks and target gene repertoires may exhibit teleost‑ or species‑specific features shaped by aquatic pathogen pressures. Moreover, divergent expression patterns of LmIRF4a and LmIRF4b in response to LPS, Poly (I:C), and live E. tarda provide further evidence for functional divergence after gene duplication. A particularly notable finding was the significant downregulation of LmIRF5 expression in the gill and intestine following stimulation with both the bacterial pathogen E. tarda and the viral mimic Poly (I:C). This tissue-specific repression stands in stark contrast to the canonical role of IRF5 as a transcriptional activator typically associated with the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I IFNs in response to microbial challenge. While orthologs in other teleost species, such as common carp [19], rock bream (Oplegnathus fasciatus) [91], golden pompano [31], Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) [92] and half-smooth tongue sole [47], are commonly upregulated following infections with bacterial pathogens and virus or Poly (I:C), the consistent downregulation observed in the mucosal tissues of spotted sea bass suggests a distinct, and potentially species- and/or context-dependent, immunoregulatory function for LmIRF5.

Although this study provides a systematic baseline covering gene structures, phylogeny, synteny, tissue distribution, and stimulus‑responsive expression profiles, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the functional conclusions are drawn solely from transcriptional profiling after PAMP or bacterial challenge. Direct experimental manipulation, such as gene overexpression or knockdown, is required to establish causal links between individual LmIRFs and specific immune outcomes. Second, our mechanistic interpretations, particularly regarding the teleost‑specific IRF11 which lacks a canonical IAD, remain speculative without biochemical validation, including co‑immunoprecipitation, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, or reporter gene analyses. Third, the expression data are limited to mRNA levels. Protein abundance, post‑translational modifications, and subcellular localization under immune stimulation have not been examined.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents the first comprehensive characterization of the IRF family in spotted sea bass. The results demonstrate conserved structural features, evolutionary relationships, and tissue distributions, while revealing stimulus‑specific expression patterns that delineate specialized roles in antibacterial versus antiviral pathways. These findings expand the functional diversification and adaptation of the IRF family in teleost immunity, providing a molecular foundation for understanding host defense mechanisms in this important aquaculture species.
