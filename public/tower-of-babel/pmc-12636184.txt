Integrating host–pathogen interaction genomics to boost wheat defense against septoria tritici blotch
Authors: Alejandro Domínguez-Rondón, Rocio Tirado, Ignacio Solís, Andrea Sánchez-Vallet, Julio Isidro y Sánchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeSeptoria tritici blotch, caused by the ascomycete fungus Zymoseptoria tritici, is one of the major threats to global food security, significantly reducing wheat yields. Current control strategies rely heavily on fungicide applications, which can accelerate fungal adaptation and resistance, highlighting the urgent need for sustainable and durable disease management approaches.MethodsWe performed genome-wide association studies in wheat and fungal populations to identify novel marker–trait associations linked to virulence within the pathosystem. Additionally, we integrated marker information from both the host and pathogen to evaluate whether a dual-source approach could improve genomic prediction of wheat resistance and reveal specific host–pathogen interactions.ResultsWe identified five novel marker–trait associations potentially involved in pathogen recognition across six wheat chromosomes and two overlapping known QTL regions. On the pathogen side, we identified 29 candidate genes potentially associated with fungal virulence, including an effector-like protein. The integrated host–pathogen genomic selection models improved predictive accuracy by capturing both wheat genotype and pathogen variation, although host genetics explained most of the variation.ConclusionConventional single-genome models lacked power, underscoring the value of dual-genome approaches in complex pathosystems. These findings support modeling genotype-by-genotype interactions for breeding resistant cultivars while concurrently monitoring pathogen evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12174-9.

Background

Plant diseases are a major threat to global food security, significantly reducing crop yields and increasing reliance on chemical control methods. Among wheat diseases, Septoria tritici blotch (STB), caused by the ascomycete fungus Zymoseptoria tritici, is one of the most damaging, with estimated average yield losses of 2.5%, and up to 50% under severe epidemics [1, 2]. The most common strategy for STB control is the application of synthetic fungicides. However, their intensive use disrupts agroecosystems, increases production costs, and contributes to the rapid emergence of fungicide-resistant pathogen strains [3, 4]. This dynamic adaptation is fueled by the high evolutionary potential of Z. tritici, driven largely by its frequent sexual reproduction and large effective population size [5]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for sustainable disease management strategies, including the development of resistant wheat varieties through breeding.

Several wheat Stb resistance genes have been identified to date. These genes mediate recognition of pathogen avirulence genes following the gene-for-gene model and confer isolate-specific resistance [6]. In addition to these qualitative resistance genes, over 150 Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) and 190 marker-trait associations (MTA) have been reported in QTL mapping and association mapping studies [7–17]. These loci provide broad-spectrum resistance, which is independent of the specific pathogen genotype [6].

For a resistance gene to be effective, the corresponding avirulence gene should be present in the pathogen population. Thus, a thorough understanding of both plant resistance and pathogen virulence is required. Despite the economic importance of Z. tritici virulence, only three avirulence genes (Avr3D1, AvrStb6, and AvrStb9) have been cloned so far [18–21]. Identifying fungal virulence factors and their interactions with wheat resistance genes is crucial for developing durable resistance strategies and reducing reliance on fungicides.

Among the most sustainable strategies for combating disease is the use of genomics tools to accelerate the development of disease-resistant cultivars [6]. Genomic Selection (GS) [22] is a promising approach to enhance complex traits such as disease resistance in breeding programs by enabling the accurate estimation of genomic estimated breeding values (GEBV) across large populations [23]. Traditional GS models rely solely on the host genome and have already shown success in improving resistance to Z. tritici in wheat [24–26]. Furthermore, approaches integrating significant GWAS hits into GS models have proven effective in increasing prediction accuracy for quantitative resistance traits [27]. However, the rapid evolutionary potential of fungal pathogens poses a major challenge for breeding durable resistance. In this context, novel methodologies are required to anticipate pathogen adaptation and enhance the durability of host resistance. One such innovation is the development of host-pathogen genomic integration model that integrates both host and pathogen genomic information. This strategy has recently been explored for Fusarium Head Blight resistance, demonstrating improved prediction of host performance in the presence of diverse pathogen genotypes [28]. However, this approach has not yet been applied to the Z. tritici-wheat pathosystem.

Here, we aim to advance durable resistance to Z. tritici in wheat by integrating genomic information from both the host and the pathogen. Specifically, our objectives are to i) identify novel QTL associated with wheat resistance and fungal virulence, ii) enhance genomic prediction accuracy for host resistance traits, and iii) evaluate the potential for predicting the pathogenicity of future Z. tritici strains. While previous studies have focused primarily on either the host or the pathogen, we hypothesize that the integration of fungal genomic data provides deeper insights into the genetic basis of quantitative resistance and improves selection accuracy. To test this, we performed GWAS on both host and pathogen populations and implemented a Host-Pathogen Genomic Integration Model on a wheat population exposed to genetically diverse fungal isolates.

Genotypic data

Wheat genotyping was conducted by the North Central Small Grains Genotyping Laboratory at USDA-ARS-ETSARC in Fargo, ND, using the Illumina 90K single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array chip [34]. Variants were filtered based on minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.05) and missingness per variant missingness (< 20%), resulting in a final dataset of approximately 21 K SNPs. Z. tritici DNA was extrated from liophilized blastospores and following the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB method) [29] and DNA libraries of 119 Z. tritici isolates were sequenced with Illumina NovaSeq6000 using 150 bp paired-end reads (Novogene Europe, UK).

Variant calling followed Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) guidelines [35]. Paired-end reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [36] and mapped to the reference genome of IPO323 [37] using the mem algorithm from BWA v0.7.14 with default options [38]. Duplicate reads were tagged using GATK’s MarkDuplicatesSpark, and variants were called per sample using HaplotypeCaller (ploidy= 1), retaining only biallelic markers. SNPs with MAF < 0.05 or >20% missing data were excluded. Given the high variability of fungal accessory chromosomes, only core chromosome (CC 1–13) markers were considered. The final dataset of 665 K markers was annotated using SnpEff [39]. The filtered marker data was used to generate additive Genomic Relationship Matrices (GRMs) following the VanRaden method. We calculated the GRMs for each fungal mix from the corresponding marker matrices, using the mean marker values of the strains in each mix. Then, we computed Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} between all pairs of markers within 20-kb windows per region using PLINK [40], and modelled the decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} over distance using a non-linear model as described by [41].

Genome-Wide association studies

Wheat analyses were conducted separately for each treatment using a two-step approach. First, we estimated wheat genotype Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUEs) using the following model:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y = l + m + r + \epsilon \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where y represents the vector of phenotypes, l, m, and r denote wheat genotypes, mixes, and replication fixed effects, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} is the residual error. The BLUES per wheat line was used as the response variable in the second step.

We applied two multi-locus models available in the Genome Association and Prediction Integrated Tool (GAPIT) [42] to identify marker-trait associations (MTAs): Bayesian-information and linkage disequilibrium iteratively nested keyway (BLINK) [43] and Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) [44]. To control the population structure and minimize false positives, we incorporated principal components (PCs) and a kinship matrix (K). We assessed model performance under the null hypothesis using Quantile-Quantile (Q-Q) plots of observed versus expected -log10(p) values. The Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold was set at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-log10(0.05/n)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 20969$$\end{document}, resulting in a threshold of 5.6. Manhattan plots were generated with CMplot [45], showed the distribution of significant markers across chromosomes. Additionally, we identified genes within a 50-kb region around each significant marker and annotated their secreted protein functions and associated Gene Ontology (GO) terms using the Uniprot database [46]. If no gene was found inside the window, we retrieved the information of the nearest gene to the MTA.

We conducted GWAS for the pathogen independently for each wheat cultivar using the best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) of the Z. tritici isolates as phenotypic inputs. In this case, markers with a logarithm of the odds (LOD) score greater than 7.8 were considered significant. Additionally, we calculated pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) between all marker pairs within a 20 kb window using PLINK [40] and used the average LD decay distance to build a search around each MTA. Candidate genes around each MTA were retrieved from the latest genome annotation available [47]. Finally, we visualized LD for selected loci using the LDheatmap package [48].

Functional annotation and gene expression analysis

We predicted N-terminal signal peptides using the hidden markov model (HMM) scoring method in SignalP 6.0 [49] and identified transmembrane domains with DeepTMHMM 1.0 [50]. Effector probabilities were assessed with EffectorP 3.0 [51], while conserved protein domains were detected using InterProScan [52], applying HMM searches against the PFAM 37.0 database [53]. Protein descriptions were assigned based on BLASTp searches against protein databases from all organisms.

RNA-seq data from in planta samples were obtained from a previous study [47], covering time points at 4, 11, 13, and 20 days post-inoculation (dpi). We quantified gene expression quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) counts and computed pairwise log2-fold expression changes for in planta RNAseq samples relative to glucose culture medium. Genes with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$log2|FC|>2$$\end{document} were considered differentially expressed during the infection.

Integrated host-pathogen predictions

We implemented five-fold cross-validation (CV) by randomly partitioning wheat lines into training (TRS) and validation sets (VS). First, we estimated BLUEs for each plant:strain combination. Then, we solved the following mixed model equation using the BGLR (Bayesian Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Predictions) package [54]:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y = \mu + Z_wu_w + Z_mu_m + \epsilon \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where y is the concatenated vector of BLUEs from the first step. The design matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_w$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_m$$\end{document} relate to the genetic effects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_w, \sim N(0,K_w\sigma _{w}^2)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_m, \sim N(0,K_m\sigma _{m}^2)$$\end{document}. Both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_w$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_m$$\end{document} are the variance-covariance structures used to solve the model and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} represents the vector of residual errors.

We tested four covariance structures to solve Eq. 2, using either the genomic relationship (G) or the Identity matrix (I) for wheat lines (w) and fungi mixes (m). In addition, we designed models accounting for wheat-fungi interactions by adding the terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{wm}u_{wm}$$\end{document} to Eq. 2, representing each host and pathogen pair and its associated random effect so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{wm} \sim N(0,G_{wm} \sigma _{wm}^2)$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{wm}$$\end{document} was obtained as the Hadamard product of relatedness matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_m\odot K_w$$\end{document}. Furthermore, we included a weighted scenario in which significant GWAS markers were entered as fixed effects. To prevent overfitting, GWAS was performed only in the TRS at each CV iteration, with the following selection criteria: if no SNP surpassed the Bonferroni threshold, the one with the lowest p-value was selected; if one to three SNPs exceeded the threshold, all were included; if more than three did, only the top three were retained. Selected markers were then removed from the marker matrix before recalculating the GRM.

Briefly, we tested four matrix combinations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_w/I_m, I_w/G_m, G_w/I_m, G_w/G_m$$\end{document}) and four modeling approaches (called Standard, Standard+interaction, Weighted, and Weighted+interaction) across 30 CV rounds. Then, we assessed model performance by computing prediction accuracy, defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between BLUEs from Eq. 1 and BLUPs from Eq. 2. Finally, we estimated genetic parameters and analyzed variance distribution across 16 models. We quantified the variance explained by each effect as the ratio of estimated genetic variation to total variation, determining the proportional contribution of the host, mixes, host-mix interactions, and errors in each model.

Zymoseptoria tritici genomic prediction

We ran 500 iterations of 5-fold CV within the TRS population and analyzed each host separately to ensure consistent model evaluation across iterations. We modeled all traits using a single-stage approach, applying the mixed model equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y = i + t + yr + brep + r + \epsilon \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where i is the random effect of the isolates, and i, t, yr, brep and r are trial, year, biological replicates, and province fixed effects, and e is the error. Prediction accuracy was calculated as the correlation between BLUPs and the BLUEs corresponding to the tested cultivar. Then, we computed the mean and standard deviation for each province across iterations.

Model variance estimations

The parameters from CV showed that the error term accounted for most of the model’s variance in nearly every scenario (Table 1). However, this proportion was largely reduced when the interaction term was included. Z. tritici genomic predictions

Within-province prediction accuracies varied substantially depending on the cultivar (Fig. 6). For instance, predictions for Svevo consistently yielded the highest accuracies, while those for Don Ricardo were the lowest. Although significant differences were observed among provinces, these differences were highly cultivar-dependent; thus, no single province was consistently better predicted across all cultivars. Overall, prediction reliability remained low throughout the analysis, with some negative accuracies resulting from large standard deviations. Huelva consistently exhibited the greatest variability across all traits and cultivars, whereas Córdoba showed the least. These discrepancies likely stem from the composition of the training set. Córdoba, which contained the highest number of isolates (37 out of 119), had a greater likelihood of being represented in the TRS set, potentially leading to improved predictions compared to Huelva, which had fewer strains and a lower probability of inclusion. However, despite its larger representation, Córdoba still ranked among the lowest-performing provinces in several scenarios.

Discussion

Sustainable disease management in crops depends on deploying resistant varieties capable of withstanding both current and emerging pathogen populations. In this study, we explored the genomic mechanism underlying fungal adaptation and host resistance, providing a comprehensive view of the host-pathogen interaction landscape. By integrating genomic data from both the pathogen and the host, we developed predictive models that anticipate disease outcomes. We propose that this dual-genome prediction framework is a powerful approach to optimize the deployment of genetic resources and enhance long-term disease control.

Wheat resistance towards septoria tritici blotch

We identified seven MTAs associated with seedling-stage resistance that spanned across 6 wheat chromosomes (Table S7). Two markers overlapped with previously reported QTL or MTAs from earlier studies. The marker on chromosome 2B colocalized with QStb.ihar2B.4, identified by Piaskowska et al. [55], and linked to necrosis and pycnidia coverage. Similarly, marker Tdurum_contig13746_167 overlapped with QStb.teagasc6B.3, mapped to chromosome 6B between 199 Mb and 529 Mb [56]. Another marker, D_contig01739_117, was located next (14 Mb) to QSTB12, a QTL reported by Mekonnen et al. [13] associated with Z. tritici virulence. We additionally identified two MTAs, Kukri_c43701_385 and BobWhite_c36827_261, near the genomic region mapped to Stb10. Specifically, markers associated with pycnidia per leaf and lesion surface were located 22 Mb and 27 Mb downstream of the designated Stb10 QTL, which spans from 36 Mb to 230 Mb on chromosome 1D. The remaining two MTAs, located on chromosomes 1 A and 4B, have not been previously reported and may represent novel QTL linked to seedling-stage resistance to STB in wheat, since they were not closely located to previously reported MTAs.

Mechanistically, the identified QTL could contribute to resistance in various ways. Several genes within these QTL regions encode proteins or domains linked to resistance. For instance, kinase domains were identified in the proteins encoded by genes near markers Kukri_c43701_385 on chromosome 1D and Excalibur_c3683_328 on chromosome 4A. Receptor-like kinases (RLKs) are typically resistance proteins that either recognize avirulence factors (such as Stb6, Stb16q, and Stb15) or microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) [57–59]. Thus, we suggest that the kinase-containing genes identified in this work might also act as wheat resistance proteins. Additionally, a gene on chromosome 6B encodes a Glycosyltransferase, a protein family involved in biotic stress responses in wheat and other plant-pathogen interaction species, against pathogens such as Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Botrytis cinerea in rapeseed [60, 61]. Other identified candidate genes encode transcription factors, including zinc finger and B3 domains. Zinc finger proteins regulate responses to biotic and abiotic stress [62], while the B3 transcription factor superfamily has been linked to plant stress responses and an increased abundance of small secreted proteins in wheat [63, 64]. Overall, we identified several candidate genes contributing in various ways to resistance in durum wheat against Z. tritici.

Z. tritici adapts to its host genotype

On the pathogen side, we identified 29 candidate genes associated with virulence or reproduction potential. Six of them were differentially expressed during the infection, and only one gene exhibited effector-like characteristics. The effector-like protein encodes a 250-amino acid polypeptide containing seven cysteine residues. Although its overall expression is lower than that of known effectors AvrStb6 and AvrStb9, its differential expression relative to control medium is comparable to, or even higher than, theirs (Table S9).

We also identified proteins involved in stress response, toxic compound degradation, and nutrient uptake. For instance, ZtIPO323_006440 encodes a monofunctional catalase that degrades \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_2 O_2$$\end{document}, a compound plants release to induce an immune response. However, previous studies suggest that this catalase has little effect on modulating host-derived \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_2 O_2$$\end{document} [65], and mutants lacking homologous enzymes exhibit reduced spore germination [66]. This aligns with findings in Neurospora crassa, where the homologous Cat2 promotes asexual spore formation [67]. Since the gene’s peak expression coincides with pycnidia formation, we hypothesize that Cat2's primary function is to promote pycnidia production.

We identified a fungal-specific histone acetyltransferase, Rtt109-like, belonging to the lysine acetyltransferase (KAT) family. KATs acetylate histone lysine residues, influencing chromatin remodeling, modulating DNA accessibility, and regulating gene expression [68]. Effector genes, often located in heterochromatic regions, require chromatin modifications for activation during host colonization [69]. Consequently, histone acetylation plays a crucial role in the spatiotemporal regulation of effector gene expression. Recently, Suarez-Fernandez et al. [30] investigated the role of several KATs in Z. tritici growth, development, and virulence. Their study demonstrated that histone acetylation at effector loci increases during infection, and that KAT loss-of-function mutants exhibit reduced effector expression due to decreased histone acetylation. However, their analysis did not include the Rtt109 homologue ZtIPO323_088990. Our findings indicate that ZtIPO323_006440, which was not included in that study, could also positively regulate pycnidia formation, reinforcing the role of histone acetylation in Z. tritici virulence.

Additionally, we identified a significant marker in the intergenic region of ZtIPO323_059020, a Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) transporter with 12 predicted transmembrane domains. MFS proteins contribute to nutrient uptake, effector secretion, and tolerance towards plant-derived toxins [70]. Previous GWAS studies in Z. tritici have also identified MFS-encoding genes associated with significant genetic markers associated with virulence [71, 72], suggesting MFS´s role in pathogenicity. We additionally identified a polyketide synthase (PKS), ZtIPO323_124400, a key enzyme involved in the synthesis of secondary metabolites [73], suggesting its role in plant colonization.

Host-pathogen integrated genomic prediction models

Results from CV showed that error variance accounted for at least 80% in 10 out of 16 models 1, highlighting the complexity of modeling disease severity and suggesting that factors beyond genomic data, such as environmental variation, sample size, and measurement errors, introduce substantial noise. However, models incorporating host-pathogen interactions improved prediction accuracy, capturing a substantial proportion of genetic variance, at times exceeding the combined host and pathogen effects. Among these models, wheat genetic effect (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$8.6 - 19.21$$\end{document}%) had a greater impact than that of the fungal mixes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.18 -- 1.82$$\end{document}%), suggesting that wheat genotype plays a more significant role than pathogen variation. Our findings emphasize the need for further exploration of non-additive effects in disease severity, such as strain-strain interaction within mixes. Some studies have suggested that strain competition can reduce disease severity [74], but its impact on predictive models remains unknown. Since co-infections are common in the field [75], modeling them accurately could significantly improve STB predictions in breeding programs.

On the other hand, Weighted models did not outperform conventional GS models. We hypothesize that strong host-isolate interactions lead to line–mix-specific MTAs rather than broad-spectrum markers, as evidenced by the GWAS results from the initial experiment. Since the TRS in each CV round combines observations from all mixes, the model struggles to identify markers consistently associated with resistance. As a result, few markers surpass the significance threshold, and the marker with the lowest p-value, regardless of true significance, is included as a fixed effect. This likely leads to poor model fit, with a large proportion of the variance being absorbed by the residual term (Table 1). These results contrast with previous findings, though methodological differences may account for the discrepancy. For instance, [25] performed GWAS on the entire population, which may have led to overfitting in the Weighted rr-BLUP model. Similarly, [24, 26] used a single strain or inoculum per wheat panel, reducing host–pathogen specificity to only the host component of the interaction.

Z. tritici virulence forecasting

Virulence predictions for Z. tritici remained unreliable, with high variability and frequent negative accuracy estimates (Fig. 6). Genomic predictions rely heavily on LD between markers and the causal variants. Despite high marker density, Z. tritici’s rapid sexual recombination leads to low, highly variable LD across provinces, potentially reducing prediction accuracy. By contrast, the wheat genotype appears to play a dominant role in disease progression, aligning with the integrated host-pathogen GS models in which wheat variance consistently exceeded pathogen variance. Our findings suggest that fungal virulence cannot be reliably predicted solely from pathogen genotypic data, though larger sample sizes will be needed to test this more rigorously .

Conclusion

Our study underscores both the value and complexity of integrating host and pathogen genomics in dissecting STB pathogenesis. On the wheat side, we identified seven MTAs across six chromosomes, revealing both novel and known resistance loci linked to kinase domains, glycosyltransferases, and transcription factors. In Z. tritici, 29 virulence-related genes were identified, including an effector-like protein, a catalase potentially important for pycnidia formation, and a histone acetyltransferase that may modulate effector gene expression. Although host–pathogen GS models improved disease predictions, results varied markedly by isolate–cultivar pairing, highlighting strong host specificity and the limitations of single-genome approaches. Taken together, these observations provide a foundation for targeted breeding of more durable resistance, as well as for refined pathogen monitoring to stay ahead of evolving virulence profiles.
