Metabolic factors are associated with a higher risk of conventional adenoma in males during surveillance colonoscopy: findings from a South Australian cohort
Authors: Meseret Derbew Molla, Erin L. Symonds, Jean M. Winter, Charles Cock, Molla M. Wassie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic factors are associated with an increased risk of colorectal neoplasia, but whether metabolic factors impact individuals already considered at elevated risk of CRC due to a family or personal history of neoplasia is unknown. This study aimed to determine whether metabolic factors are associated with an increased risk of colorectal neoplasia in individuals undergoing surveillance colonoscopy in an Australian cohort. A retrospective cohort study design with a sample size of 2806 was implemented using colonoscopy and metabolic data from a hospital surveillance program. The association between self-reported metabolic factors (obesity, hypertension, type two diabetes (T2DM), dyslipidemia, and a cluster of these factors called metabolic syndrome (MetS)) and diagnosis of colorectal neoplasia, its advanced form, or histopathological subtypes at surveillance colonoscopy was analysed using logistic regression models. Metabolic factors that increased the odds of colorectal neoplasia diagnosis included obesity (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 1.61; 95% CI 1.06–2.44), T2DM (AOR = 1.40; 95% CI 1.09–1.80), and MetS (AOR = 1.47; 95%CI 1.10–1.99), with similar findings observed for the association with advanced colorectal neoplasia (obesity: AOR = 1.24; 95% CI 1.01–1.51; T2DM: AOR = 1.29; 95%CI 1.06–1.56; MetS: AOR = 1.45; 95% CI 1.18–1.80). These associations were driven by conventional adenomas (87% of all neoplasia) in males. No associations were observed between metabolic factors and colorectal neoplasia in females, nor for the odds of serrated polyps (p > 0.05). Obesity, T2DM and MetS were significant risk factors for the risk of conventional adenoma in a surveillance population, specifically in males. These metabolic disorders should be considered when determining the frequency of colonoscopy surveillance for males.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31239-z.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer and the second most common cancer-related cause of death worldwide1. CRC develops from either conventional adenomas or serrated precancerous polyps, collectively named colorectal neoplasia2,3. Screening and surveillance are the most effective strategies for reducing the incidence of CRC and associated mortality through early detection and resection of precancerous polyps4. Surveillance colonoscopy guidelines recommend regular colonoscopy to remove the precancerous polyps for those who are at increased risk of CRC due to a family history of CRC and/or a personal history of neoplasia (precancerous polyps and/or CRC)5–8.

Recent studies have increasingly shown that various metabolic factors may also affect the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia in patients undergoing surveillance colonoscopy9–11. These factors include obesity, high blood glucose or insulin resistance (such as in type 2 diabetes mellitus), high blood pressure (hypertension), abnormal lipid levels (dyslipidemia), and metabolic syndrome (MetS), a cluster of these metabolic abnormalities12. Each component of MetS may affect the risk of colorectal neoplasia through different biological mechanisms such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and gut microbiome changes, with varying degrees of impact13–15. Assessing the association between the individual component of MetS and risk of colorectal neoplasia separately provides a clearer picture of which metabolic abnormalities are most strongly associated with risk and can help to guide more targeted prevention strategies stratified by metabolic health conditions for people undergoing surveillance colonoscopy.

However, there are conflicting evidence on the association between each metabolic factor and colorectal neoplasia as well as its histopathological features in people undergoing surveillance colonoscopy. Some studies have identified MetS as a significant risk factor for colorectal neoplasia9,16. On the contrary, a recent systematic review of five studies found no association between MetS and colorectal neoplasia recurrence17, although this review did not include data from individuals undergoing surveillance due to a family history of CRC. While some studies have not found risk of association between obesity and colorectal neoplasia18, others have reported an increase in risk for surveillance populations19–22. On the other hand, one study found that obesity is associated with colorectal polyp recurrence but not with advanced adenoma recurrence23. Positive associations between T2DM24, hypertension25, dyslipidemia26, and risk of colorectal neoplasia have been reported. However, another study did not find any risk of association between diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia with precancerous polyp recurrence27. Furthermore, metabolic factors may not influence the development of sessile serrated polyps. For example, two retrospective cohort studies conducted in Spain26 and South Korea18 reported that none of the investigated metabolic factors were associated with sessile serrated polyps in a surveillance colonoscopy population. Conversely, a retrospective cohort study from China reported that only obesity but not T2DM or hypertension were associated with an increased incidence of sessile serrated polyps19, while a retrospective cohort study conducted in the United States of America found that high BMI was not associated with an increased risk of sessile serrated polyps, whereas T2DM was protective against this risk28.

Despite this evidence, current guidelines often do not consider metabolic factors when determining how frequently surveillance colonoscopies should be performed. For instance, the updated 2020 European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) Guideline5, the United States Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF) on Colorectal Cancer (2020) and the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) Guidelines (2021) do not consider metabolic factors as primary surveillance criteria, and surveillance intervals are based primarily on polyp findings at colonoscopy, family history of CRC, and hereditary syndromes6,7. In contrast, the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) guideline recommend shorter surveillance intervals for men diagnosed with MetS who also have small tubular adenomas8. However, the individual components of MetS that contribute to the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia are not utilised to determine the frequency of surveillance colonoscopy.

There are clear inconsistencies between the association of metabolic factors with colorectal neoplasia amongst colonoscopy surveillance cohorts. This may be due to reasons such as: study setting difference (surveillance colonoscopy guidelines vary across countries), too short or delayed frequency of surveillance colonoscopy in some studies29, small sample size16, participant differences in risk at baseline27, and utilisation of self-reported metabolic factor measurements in some studies30. Furthermore, most studies to date have been conducted in Asian populations and among those with a prior history of precancerous polyps and have not included individuals with a significant family history of CRC, which limits the generalisability of the evidence to countries where metabolic factors are currently more prevalent31. The majority of the previous studies have also only assessed the association between metabolic factors and presence or absence of neoplasia in people undergoing surveillance colonoscopy, without consideration of the significance of the histopatholigical subtype colonoscopy findings. In addition, there have been limited studies related to whether the association between metabolic factors and neoplasia is sex-dependent, despite Australian surveillance guidelines shortening the frequency of surveillance colonoscopy intervals only for males living with MetS8. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between various metabolic factors and the risk of various histopathological subtypes of colorectal neoplasia in an Australian surveillance cohort, as well as to assess the findings stratified by sex and histopathological polyp subtypes.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study of individuals undergoing surveillance colonoscopy with the Southern Co-Operative Program for the Prevention of Colorectal Cancer (SCOOP) program due to a family history of CRC and/or a personal history of colorectal neoplasia. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist32. The SCOOP program was established in 1999 as a nurse-led program to ensure that individuals at increased risk of CRC have regular surveillance colonoscopies following the guidelines provided by the NHMRC33, with the data associated with the program collected into a clinical database. Colonoscopies were undertaken at Flinders Medical Centre (FMC, Bedford Park, South Australia) and Noarlunga Hospital (NH, Noarlunga, South Australia).

Eligibility criteria

The target population included adults aged > 18 years who were scheduled for surveillance colonoscopy (from February 2021 to May 2024), and who had at least one prior colonoscopy, with complete data on their metabolic factors. Participants were excluded with the following criteria: (1) a poor quality colonoscopy due to incomplete intubation or poor bowel preparation (based on either a poor qualitative score, or a Boston Bowel Preparation Score of < 2 in any segment), (2) personal history of inflammatory bowel disease, (3) hereditary syndromes (e.g. Lynch syndrome), (4) history of organ transplantation, (5) indication for repeat colonoscopy within six months of the prior colonoscopy examination, (6) type 1 diabetes or gestational diabetes and (7) missing or incomplete subsequent surveillance colonoscopy data. Data collection

Data were collected from the SCOOP clinical database, which included demographic details, metabolic factors, and findings at colonoscopy. The data collected includes smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and medical comorbidities including body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and aspirin use. The metabolic factors were collected through a standardised clinical interview at least three months prior to scheduling the next surveillance colonoscopy. While metabolic data were collected close to the time of surveillance colonoscopy, the measurements reflect chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, that would have been present before the neoplasia developed. There were patients living with these metabolic factors for more than ten years, however, the exact time when these diseases were diagnosed were not recorded for all participants. Although colorectal polyp development can occur within colonoscopy intervals, metabolic conditions are generally stable over time; therefore, metabolic factors measured within this period were considered a reasonable proxy for chronic metabolic exposure while maintaining temporality between exposure and outcome.

Tobacco smoking was grouped into three categories: (1) non-smokers, with no self-reported history of tobacco smoking; (2) former smokers, cessation at least one month before the interview; and (3) current smokers, regardless of the frequency and dose. Alcohol consumption was grouped into four categories based on the NHMRC guidelines: (1) non-drinkers, with no self-reported history of alcohol intake; (2) occasional drinkers: ≤1 standard drink monthly, (3) low-risk drinkers: drinkers who are not categorised as occasional or high-risk drinkers and (4) high-risk drinkers: >10 standard drinks per week or > 4 standard drinks on any one day34.

BMI was grouped into four categories: (1) underweight, BMI < 18.5 kg/m2; (2) normal, BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2; (3) overweight, BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2; and (4) obese, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m235. Hypertension, T2DM, and dyslipidemia status were self-reported by individuals. MetS was defined as meeting the WHO definition criteria as follows: T2DM, including prediabetes, plus any two of the following metabolic factors: (1) BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, (2) hypertension, and (3) dyslipidemia36.

The indication for the surveillance colonoscopy (either family history of CRC and/or personal history of colorectal neoplasia), as well as the location, size, number, and histopathological characterization of polyps, and the time interval between prior and surveillance colonoscopy were collected from the SCOOP clinical database. The immediate colonoscopy examination performed before the standardized clinical interview was considered as the prior colonoscopy. The family history of CRC was categorised into significant and non-significant based on the NHMRC guidelines8. Significant CRC family history is defined as CRC diagnosis either of the following: at least one first-degree relative before age 55, ≥ 2 first-degree relatives at any age, ≥ 3 s-degree relatives on the same side of the family, with at least one diagnosed before age 55, or any combination thereof. Non-significant family history is defined as one first degree relative affected at age 55 or older or ≤ 2 s-degree relatives with CRC at any age8.

Colonoscopy procedure and definition of outcomes

Each participant had information collected from one surveillance colonoscopy and one prior colonoscopy where the surveillance was enrolled from 24/02/2021 to 25/05/2024. Each participant completed bowel preparation before undergoing a colonoscopy examination by an experienced and colonoscopy-credentialled gastroenterologist or surgeon. A complete colonoscopy was considered as full cecal intubation. Colonoscopy quality was assessed throughout the study using Australian colonoscopy quality metrics37.

Histologically, polyps were classified as either adenomatous (conventional adenoma) or serrated polyps (sessile serrated lesions or traditional serrated adenoma). Colonoscopy findings were grouped into non-neoplastic (no polyp, inflammatory polyps or hyperplastic polyps), conventional adenoma (advanced/non-advanced adenoma), serrated polyps (advanced/non-advanced serrated polyps), and synchronous polyps (diagnosis of both conventional adenoma and serrated polyps)8. Advanced adenoma included adenomas with villous features, high-grade dysplasia, and/or size ≥ 10 mm. Advanced serrated lesions included sessile serrated lesions with size ≥ 10 mm and/or dysplasia, or any size of traditional serrated adenoma (TSA). Colorectal neoplasia included any non-advanced or advanced adenoma or serrated lesion and any stage of CRC8. Advanced colorectal neoplasia was defined as the presence of advanced adenoma, advanced serrated lesion, and/or CRC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R 4.2.3 and R Studio statistical software38. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and range, as appropriate. The incidence rate of colorectal neoplasia was calculated using the Poisson regression model per person-time. The denominator for the incidence rate was the total person-time at risk contributed by participants. Person-time was calculated from the date of the prior colonoscopy to the date of the outcome measurement at surveillance colonoscopy. Individuals who did not develop the outcome contributed person-time until censoring. Therefore, the model estimates incidence rate ratios while accounting for varying follow-up durations among participants. The association between metabolic factors and risk of colorectal neoplasia at the surveillance colonoscopy was analysed using multivariable logistic regression, with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test to check for model fitness. A Propensity Score Matching (PSM) using the “MatchIt” package in R was used to strengthen the robustness of the findings. A two-step approach (Covariate-Specific Matching) or a Stratified PSM Model (a separate PSM analysis for each exposure) was used for matching. Covariate Balance for each PSM model was assessed to check whether the matching balanced the covariates correctly. After matching, an adjusted multivariable logistic regression was conducted to estimate the effect of each metabolic factor on the risk of colorectal neoplasia. The interactions between metabolic factors and covariates such as sex, age, smoking and alcohol consumption were tested during the regression analyses. The statistical significance of effect modification was determined by likelihood ratio test. We further used a visual tool (a predicted probability plot) that helps to observe whether the relationship between metabolic factors and colorectal neoplasia differs by sex as the NHMRC guideline recommends shortening the frequency of surveillance colonoscopy interval for only males with MetS. Then, a stratification analyses for the association between metabolic factors and colorectal neoplasia by sex was conducted.

The association between metabolic factors and advanced colorectal neoplasia was analysed using multivariable ordinal logistic regression, where the outcome was non-neoplastic, non-advanced colorectal neoplasia, and advanced colorectal neoplasia. The proportional odds assumption was checked using Brant test of the “Brant package”. The effect of each metabolic factor on the histological subtypes of polyps found at surveillance colonoscopy was analysed using multinomial logistic regression, where the outcome was non-neoplastic, adenomatous polyps, serrated polyps, and synchronous polyps. The “nnet” package in R was used to run the multinomial logistic regression using the “multinom()” function. Multinomial logistic regression was assessed using the multicollinearity with Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) with the Hausman-McFadden test assumptions. The “car” package was also used to check for multicollinearity, and the “mlogit” package was used to test the IIA assumption. The multinomial model assumption was considered as valid when VIF was < 10 (no perfect multicollinearity), and the Hausman-McFadden test was non-significant (p > 0.05). The linearity of the continuous predictors in the multinomial logistic regression model was assessed using the generalised additive model (GAM), where the model was valid if the smoothed terms were insignificant. A statistical significance was defined for all analyses using p-value < 0.05.

For all multivariable analyses, adjustment for confounders and covariates were considered. The risk of association between MetS and colorectal neoplasia was assessed after adjusting for age (as a continuous variable), smoking status (never, former or current), alcohol consumption (non, occasional, low risk or high risk), use of aspirin/nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (no or yes), and characteristics of pre-interview colonoscopy reports (non-neoplastic, low risk or high risk) and the presence and degree of family history of CRC (no, non-significant or significant). Other metabolic factors were also adjusted such as BMI (normal weight, overweight or obesity), T2DM (no or yes), hypertension (no or yes) and dyslipidaemia (no or yes) to analyse the association between each metabolic factor and colorectal neoplasia. Time between prior and current surveillance colonoscopy was not adjusted due to multicollinearity with surveillance intervals.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

As described in Fig. 1, of the 4224 participants who completed 4893 standardised clinical record interviews, 2806 unique individuals fulfilled the eligibility criteria for this study. More than half (1514/2806 (54%)) of the participants were male, with a median of 65 (range: 24–85) years (Table 1). Only 750/2806 (26.7%) of the participants did not consume alcohol, while 334/2806 (11.9%) were high-risk drinkers. In terms of smoking history, 467/2806 (16.6%) and 1084/2806 (38.6%) were current and former smokers, respectively. Aspirin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use was recorded for 324/2806 (11.5%) participants. The median time since the prior colonoscopy was 43 months (ranging from 6 to 198 months), and 542/2806 (19.3%) of the individuals had a significant family history of CRC. The most common prior colonoscopy outcome was colorectal neoplasia (1921/2806 (68.5%); Table 1), of which, most had a finding of conventional adenoma (1532/1921 (79.8%)). Table 2 presents the frequency of each metabolic factor amongst the study cohort, with 387/2806 (13.8%) classified as having MetS, 78.2% either overweight or obese (2194/2806), 19.3% as having T2DM, and over one-third of the cohort with hypertension and/or dyslipidemia.

Surveillance colonoscopy outcomes

Over half of the participants (1526/2806 (54.4%)) developed colorectal neoplasia at the surveillance colonoscopy, with only two (0.13%) participants diagnosed with adenocarcinoma and 285/1526 (18.7%) were advanced colorectal neoplasia. Histologically, conventional adenomas were the most prevalent neoplasia (1167/1526 (76.5%)), followed by serrated lesions (192/1526 (12.6%)) and finally synchronous adenoma (167/1526 (10.9%)) (Fig. 2). Of participants who developed colorectal neoplasia, 248/1526 (16.3%) met the definition of MetS (Table 3). A high proportion of those with MetS were diagnosed with conventional adenoma either without (17.4%) or with (16.2%) serrated lesions, while the prevalence of MetS was lower (9.4%) in participants with only serrated lesions. The distribution of metabolic factors for tubular, tubulovillous, villous, sessile serrated lesions, and TSA is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Association between metabolic factors and colorectal neoplasia

Multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 4) showed that the odds of developing colorectal neoplasia were 57% higher in those with MetS (AOR = 1.57; 95% CI 1.24–1.99), 31% higher with obesity (AOR = 1.31; 95% CI 1.06–1.61) and 36% higher with T2DM (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI 1.10–1.68) compared to surveillance participants without MetS, normal weight and without T2DM, respectively. Conversely, being overweight or having dyslipidemia or hypertension was not associated with the risk of colorectal neoplasia (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study assessed the association between metabolic factors and the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia in an Australian surveillance colonoscopy cohort. Obesity, T2DM, and MetS were associated with the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia (including advanced neoplasia), with this finding driven by the conventional adenoma subtype of neoplasia. No association of metabolic factors with the risk of developing serrated polyps was observed. Obesity was also statistically associated with the diagnosis of synchronous polyps, likely due to the presence of conventional adenoma. Association between each metabolic factor and colorectal neoplasia was dependent on male sex, in line with the revised Australian NHMRC surveillance colonoscopy guideline, which states that male participants diagnosed with MetS should have more frequent surveillance after a colonoscopy finding of small tubular adenomas8. Being overweight, hypertensive or dyslipidemic were not significantly associated with a diagnosis of colorectal neoplasia at surveillance colonoscopy. These findings indicate that factors other than prior pathology findings or a family history of CRC play a role in the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia. This highlights the need to consider metabolic factors for surveillance colonoscopy strategies, as most international colorectal surveillance guidelines do not currently include metabolic factors.

The positive association between MetS and colorectal neoplasia aligns with a similar study conducted in the Korean surveillance colonoscopy population that reported MetS increases the risk of colorectal neoplasia39. A study of males only found a positive association between MetS and overall adenoma recurrence and advanced adenoma16. Some studies did not find any risk of association between MetS and adenoma recurrence in both sexes combined40,41. In contrast, another study found that MetS was associated with increased odds of nonadvanced colorectal neoplasia only in females and advanced colorectal neoplasia in both females or males42. The discrepancy of the studies may be due to several reasons, such as the target population of the studies (risk of association between MetS and colorectal neoplasia is substantial in males)16, baseline colonoscopy findings (if the baseline colonoscopy finding is high risk, the risk of association between MetS and colorectal neoplasia may not be significant, rather the advanced features of neoplasia at baseline mainly determines the recurrence)9 and level of adjusted confounders. Since MetS is driven by modifiable factors, lifestyle changes such as regular exercise, healthy eating, and weight loss could help lower the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia. However, the existing evidence to support this is from observational studies and causality may not be established in the surveillance colonoscopy populations. More long-term, randomised controlled trials are needed to confirm whether managing MetS through lifestyle modifications can effectively reduce the risk of colorectal neoplasia in a surveillance cohort.

The current study revealed that obesity, but not being overweight, increased the risk of developing any colorectal neoplasia, including advanced neoplasia. Various observational studies support this association in the surveillance colonoscopy population19–22. Excess body fat, specifically higher levels of adiposity, may increase the development of colorectal neoplasia through chronic inflammation15, insulin resistance/Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signalling pathway13, and oxidative stress15. Observational studies conducted in South Korea18, China23 and Spain26 reported that both being overweight or obese were not linked with advanced colorectal neoplasia in the surveillance colonoscopy population. Possible discrepancies with prior studies may be due to differences in weight and height measurement sources (self-reported measurements may underestimate the actual value of BMI), the duration and severity of obesity since diagnosis, and the influence of other lifestyle factors that might exacerbate and risk of excess body fat. Most reported studies collected BMI from medical records and did not account for how long a person had been obese or how severe their obesity was, which may lead to weaker or inconsistent associations18,23,26.

The current study also found that T2DM was associated with an increased risk of developing colorectal neoplasia (including advanced neoplasia), and the association remained significant only for conventional adenoma and in males. This result aligns with retrospective cohort studies conducted in Spain26, China24, and Japan22. However, the current finding is in contrast to other studies conducted in China19, Korea20 and Australia43. The discrepancy in the study findings may be due to several reasons, such as the study population’s ethnic background differences, the method of measuring T2DM (collected from medical records or self-reported), the small sample size (i.e., the previous Australian study was conducted with a small sample size (n = 412 patients < 50 years and 824 sex-matched controls ≥ 50 years)43, and the inability to clearly differentiate the type of diabetes mellitus (i.e. type 1 or type 2).

Hypertension was not associated with the risk of colorectal neoplasia in this study. The association between hypertension and colorectal neoplasia is not well defined. For instance, some studies conducted in Korea18,44, China19,24, and Spain26 did not find any association, however, other cohort studies conducted in Korea20, Taiwan28, and China25 found a positive association. The differences may be due to variations in study design, differences in how hypertension is defined or measured (for instance, in our study, we used a self-reported history of hypertension while the Chinese study25 used measurements for medical records), and the impact of confounding factors such as obesity or T2DM. Additionally, differences in chronic versus newly diagnosed hypertension, as well as controlled versus uncontrolled hypertension using medications, could contribute to differences in risk for developing neoplasia in those with hypertension.

The present study also found that dyslipidemia was not statistically associated with the risk of developing overall or advanced colorectal neoplasia, and previous studies have reported inconsistent findings22–24,26. A retrospective cohort study conducted in Spain revealed that dyslipidemia is statically associated with overall precancerous polyps26. A retrospective cohort study in Japan also reported that dyslipidemia, but not its components like low HDL-C, high LDL-C, total cholesterol, and triglyceride, increases the risk of overall precancerous neoplasia22. On the other hand, a retrospective cohort study of China found that high levels of triglyceride but not low HDL-C is a statistically significant factor for the increased risk of overall precancerous polyps23. Another retrospective cohort study also reported that self-reported hyperlipidemia is not statistically associated with an increased risk of overall precancerous polyps after colon cancer surgery24. This discrepancy may be due to different reasons, including the sample size of the participants and the source of lipid metabolism dysregulations. The study findings might reflect that the dietary source of dyslipidemia, usually characterised by high triglyceride levels as a result of consuming foods high in fats (i.e., saturated fats), is the most likely risk factor for colorectal neoplasia rather than the metabolic dysregulation of lipid metabolism23. This study did not have access to data on the different components that can contribute to dyslipidemia, but rather just the presence or absence of dyslipidemia.

Our study also found that none of the metabolic factors were found to be significant risk factors for developing sessile serrated polyps. Retrospective cohort studies conducted in Spain26 and South Korea18 also showed that none of the metabolic factors measured such as obesity, hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, and MetS were statistically associated with sessile serrated polyps. A Chinese retrospective cohort study reported that only obesity, but not hypertension and T2DM, is a statistically significant factor for the increase.

d risk of serrated polyps19. On the contrary, a retrospective cohort study conducted in the United States of America showed that T2DM is a protective metabolic factor for sessile serrated polyps28. The insignificant association between metabolic factors and serrated polyps may be due to several reasons. First, serrated polyps develop through unique pathways involving specific genetic mutations (e.g., BRAF) and epigenetic changes (e.g., CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP)), which differ from the mechanisms driving conventional adenoma development. Thus, serrated pathways differ from conventional adenoma pathway and may not be strongly influenced by metabolic conditions such as insulin resistance or chronic inflammation45,46. The term serrated polyps includes a broad spectrum of polyps, from low-risk hyperplastic polyps to higher-risk sessile serrated lesions, which can dilute any potential associations47. To make it clear, we did not include hyperplastic polyps for our analysis. Lifestyle factors such as smoking may have a stronger association with developing sessile serrated lesions compared to metabolic factors48. Lastly, differences in study designs, limited sample sizes, or inadequate differentiation between polyp types may contribute to the lack of a clear association.

This study had a relatively large sample size (n = 2,806) among individuals undergoing regular surveillance colonoscopy and assessed the risk of association between individual metabolic factors and colorectal neoplasia, histopathological subtypes of polyps, and severity of neoplasia. Previous similar studies have only assessed colonoscopy outcomes as a simple binomial of absence or presence of neoplasia. However, the following limitations should be acknowledged for this study. The main limitation of the current study was generalizability to all above-average risk population due to the nature of the SCOOP program, since this program is limited to two hospitals and a relatively small catchment area within South Australia. Changes in metabolic factors over time and the duration of presence of metabolic factors were also not measured for this analysis, where a single measurement at one point in time may not reflect long-term exposure risk. The effect of controlled (using medications) or uncontrolled metabolic health conditions on colorectal neoplasia risk was also not evaluated. Although surveillance colonoscopy outcomes were well-characterised by the clinical team, the use of self-reported metabolic health history in this study might also lead to inaccuracies. This study used routinely collected clinical data; however, some variables such as diet and physical activity were not available and not adjusted in the analysis.

Conclusion

The current study identified that MetS, obesity, and T2DM can all increase the risk of developing colorectal neoplasia in males who are at an elevated risk of CRC. However, being overweight (not obese), hypertension and dyslipidemia were not associated with surveillance colonoscopy outcomes, and the development of serrated polyps was not linked to any metabolic factors. Therefore, when determining surveillance colonoscopy intervals, it might be beneficial to consider the status of metabolic conditions, particularly MetS, obesity, and T2DM. This study provides evidence to enact a more personalised surveillance colonoscopy schedule, tailored to the individuals’ metabolic health conditions. However, differences in the frequency of surveillance colonoscopy intervals and follow-up period may affect the detection rates of neoplasia, which limits the generalisability beyond similar surveillance settings. To further strengthen the evidence, a prospective cohort study is warranted.
