Semigroup automorphisms of total positivity
Projesh Nath Choudhury, Shaun Fallat, Chi-Kwong Li

Abstract
. Totally positive (TP) and totally nonnegative (TN) matrices connect to analysis, mechanics, and to dual canonical bases in reductive groups, by well-known works of Schoenberg, Gantmacher–Krein, Lusztig, and others. TP matrices form a multiplicatively closed semigroup, contained in the larger monoid of invertible totally nonnegative (ITN) matrices. Whitney and Berenstein–Fomin–Zelevinsky found bidiagonal factorizations of all n × n n\times n ITN and TP matrices into multiplicative generators; a natural question now is to classify the multiplicative automorphisms of these semigroups. In this article, we classify all automorphisms of these semigroups of ITN and TP matrices. In particular, we show that the automorphisms are the same, and they respect the multiplicative generators.

These includes analysis, approximation theory, cluster algebras, combinatorics, differential equations, Gabor analysis, integrable systems, matrix theory, probability and statistics, and
representation theory

The notion of total positivity was first studied by Fekete–Pólya
[
11
]
in 1912 in terms of the one sided Pólya frequency sequences and their variation diminishing property – which may be regarded as originating in the famous 1883 memoir of Laguerre
[
20
]
. In the same paper, Fekete showed that TP matrices are characterized by the positivity of the contiguous minors which was later extended in 1955 to totally positivity of order
k
by Schoenberg
[
26
]
. Subsequently, in 1937 Gantmacher–Krein
[
14
]
characterized TP (or TN) matrices in terms of the positivity (or non-negativity) of the spectra of all submatrices. In 1941, Gantmacher–Krein
[
15
]
provided a fundamental characterization of total positivity in terms of variation diminution and very recently Choudhury
[
7
]
refined these results by establishing a single-vector test.

Given an integer
n\geq 1
, the Cauchy–Binet formula implies that the set of all invertible TN (ITN) matrices and the subset of all TP matrices are closed under multiplication. Denoting these by
ITN(n)\supsetneq TP(n)
respectively, we see that
TP(n)
is a semigroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and that
ITN(n)
is a submonoid of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The following classical result of Cryer
[
8
,
9
]
shows that the study of
ITN(n)
can be reduced to the investigation of its subsemigroup of upper triangular ITN matrices with one on the diagonal and subgroup of diagonal ITN matrices:

Lemma 1.1
.

A matrix
A\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is totally nonnegative if and only if
A
has Gaussian decomposition
A=LDU
, where
L,U
are lower and upper triangular ITN matrices with ones on the main diagonal and
D
is a diagonal ITN matrix.

In 1952, Whitney
[
29
]
(a student of Schoenberg) identified the infinitesimal generators of
ITN(n)
and
TP(n)
as the Chevalley generators of the corresponding Lie algebra. Whitney showed that each ITN and TP matrix can be factorized using elementary bidiagonal matrices. An
n\times n
matrix of the form
I+wE_{ij}
with
w\geq 0
,
I
the identity matrix, and
E_{ij}
an elementary matrix, is called an elementary bidiagonal matrix if
|i-j|=1
. Elementary bidiagonal matrices are invertible totally nonnegative. We owe to Whitney the following factorization of invertible TN matrices.

Theorem 1.2

(Whitney
[
29
]
)
.

Every
n\times n
ITN matrix
A
is of the form

(1.1)

A:=A({\bf w,w^{{}^{\prime}},d})=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\prod_{k=n-1}^{j}\big(I+w_{j,k}E_{k+1,k}\big)~D\prod_{j=n-1}^{1}\prod_{k=n-1}^{j}\big(I+w^{\prime}_{j,k+1}E_{k,k+1}\big),

where
{\bf w}=(w_{j,k})_{1\leq j\leq k\leq n-1}\in[0,\infty)^{n\choose 2},~{\bf w^{\prime}}=(w^{\prime}_{j,k+1})_{1\leq j\leq k\leq n-1}\in[0,\infty)^{n\choose 2}
;
{\bf d}\in\mathbb{(}0,\infty)^{n}
and
D=\diag({\bf d})
, and
D
can be made to appear at either end of the factorization or between any of the elementary bidiagonal factors, and the factors are multiplied in the given order of
j
and
k.
Equivalently, the factorization can be arranged such that the product of upper bidiagonal factors appears on the left, while the product of lower bidiagonal factors appears on the right.

Building on this factorization, Lusztig
[
21
]
in 1994 extended the notion of total positivity to other semisimple Lie groups
G
, by defining the set
G_{\geq 0}
of TN elements in
G
as the semigroup generated by the (exponentiated) Chevalley generators. A few years later, using the factorization (
1.1
), Berenstein–Fomin–Zelevinsky
[
4
]
showed that
(0,\infty)^{n^{2}}
is diffeomorphic to the space of
n\times n
TP matrices via the map

({\bf w,w^{{}^{\prime}},d})\xrightarrow{\psi}A({\bf w,w^{{}^{\prime}},d}).

The primary goal of this article is to investigate semigroup automorphisms of total positivity. The automorphisms of various groups and semigroups of matrices were studied in the literature
[
1
,
6
]
. In the present work, we show that the automorphisms of both semigroups
ITN(n)
and
TP(n)
are equal, and involve a function purely of the determinant and diagonal/antidiagonal conjugation. Here is our first main result:

Theorem A
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
G(n)=\text{TP}(n)
or
\text{ITN}(n)
. Then the following are equivalent for an arbitrary map
T:G(n)\to G(n)
.

(i)

T
is a semigroup isomorphism.

(ii)

T
has the form

A\mapsto\mu(\det A)(\det A)^{-\frac{1}{n}}RAR^{-1},

where
\mu:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a bijective multiplicative map and

R=\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&r_{n}\\
\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&r_{1}\\
\vdots&\iddots&\vdots\\
r_{n}&\cdots&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

for some positive real numbers
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Here are two immediate consequences:

(i)

Every semigroup automorphism of
ITN(n)
sends each elementary bidiagonal generator to another such generator.

(ii)

In particular, every automorphism not only preserves the multiplicative structure of the semigroup of TP/ITN matrices, but it takes these “multiplicative generators” onto themselves, and hence, every automorphism takes each bidiagonal factorization to another such.

Our second main result shows that the automorphisms of both semigroups
ITN(n)
and
TP(n)
– and in fact, of every multiplicative semigroup in between them satisfying some additional assumptions – are equal.

Theorem B
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
G(n)
be a semigroup with
TP(n)\subseteq G(n)\subseteq ITN(n)
such that
G(n)
is closed under taking positive scalar multiples, and under conjugating by (anti-)diagonal matrices with positive (anti-)diagonal entries. Let
T:G(n)\to G(n)
be an arbitrary map. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is a semigroup isomorphism with
T(TP(n))=TP(n)
.

(ii)

For all
A\in G(n)
,

T(A)=\mu(\det A)(\det A)^{-\frac{1}{n}}RAR^{-1},

where
\mu
and
R
are defined as in Theorem
A
(ii).

Example 1.4
.

Note that for
n\geq 2
, the semigroup
G(n):=TP(n)\sqcup\{\diag(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}):d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}>0\}
strictly contains
TP(n)
and lies strictly within
ITN(n)
. By Corollary
3.3
, any semigroup automorphism of
G(n)
preserves
TP(n)
. Thus Theorem
B
completely classifies the automorphisms of
G(n)
.

Remark 1.5
.

The multiplicative map
\mu:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
can be constructed as follows.
Consider
\mathbb{R}
as an infinite-dimensional vector space over the rational
numbers. Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear isomorphism. Then
\mu(x)=\exp(\phi(\log(x))

is a bijective multiplicative map on
(0,\infty)
, and there are no others. It is perhaps worth remarking here that the classical works of Banach
[
2
]
, Sierpińsky
[
28
]
, and others show that
\mu
is continuous if and only if it is measurable, if and only if
\phi
is measurable; but there exist non-measurable
\mathbb{Q}
-linear maps
\phi
.

We conclude our discussion of the main results with the following remark: The entrywise preservers of the set of TP matrices and of TN matrices were recently classified by Belton–Guillot–Khare–Putinar
[
3
]
. However, this uncovered only the set-theoretic (entrywise) preservers. In contrast, the present work characterizes the matrix functions preserving the (square) TP or ITN matrices, which form semigroups under multiplication. Thus the maps we classify preserve not only the TP/ITN matrices, but their respective multiplicative structure as well.

Organization of the paper

The remaining sections of the paper are devoted to proving our main results. In Section 2, we show that a semigroup automorphism of total positivity can be extended to invertible total nonnegativity. In Section 3, we establish properties of some special subgroups and sub-semigroups of matrices in
ITN(n)
in terms of their centralizer. In Section 4, we prove Theorem
A
for the
2\times 2
case and in Section 5 for the general case. We conclude the article with a quick proof of Theorem
B

2.
Reduction of the Main Problem

The entirety of this paper (save a few lines to show Theorem B) is devoted to proving Theorem A. As the proof is rather technical and involved, we split it into three sections. We begin in this section by extending an automorphism of the semigroup of
n\times n
TP matrices to a monoid automorphism of
n\times n
invertible TN matrices. This requires three preliminary results. The first one is a classical density result by Whitney.

Theorem 2.1

(Whitney,
[
29
]
)
.

Given an integer
n\geq 1
, the set of
n\times n

TP

matrices is dense in the set of
n\times n

TN
matrices.

We now recall a result of Karlin concerning invertible totally nonnegative matrices and positivity of their principal minors.

Theorem 2.2

(Karlin,
[
18
]
)
.

If
A\in ITN(n)
, all principal minors of
A
are positive.

The next preliminary result is the well known Cauchy–Binet determinantal identity. To state this identity, we introduce two pieces of notation.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

(i)

Define
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

(ii)

Given subsets
\alpha\subseteq[m]
and
\beta\subseteq[n]
, define
A[\alpha|\beta]
to be the submatrix of
A
with rows indexed by
\alpha
and the columns indexed by
\beta
.

Theorem 2.4

(Cauchy–Binet formula)
.

Given matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we have

\det(AB)=\sum_{\gamma\subseteq[n],~|\gamma|=m}\det A\left[[m]|\gamma\right]\det B\left[\gamma|[m]\right].

We now provide a characterization of
ITN(n)
in terms of the following sets: Given an integer
n\geq 1
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{L}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:XA\in TP(n)\ \forall A\in TP(n)\},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{R}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:AX\in TP(n)\ \forall A\in TP(n)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{L}(n)\cap\mathcal{S}^{R}(n).

It is easy to verify that all of these are submonoids of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The monoids
\mathcal{S}^{L}(n),\mathcal{S}^{R}(n),\mathcal{S}(n)
are all equal, and equal to
ITN(n)
, the monoid of all invertible
n\times n
TN matrices.

Proof.

We first claim that
ITN(n)=\mathcal{S}^{R}(n)
. To show
ITN(n)\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{R}(n)
, suppose
X
is
ITN(n)
. Then for all
A\in TP(n)
, we may deduce that
AX
is TP, by a simple application of the Cauchy–Binet identity and Theorem
2.2
. Indeed, for any two nonempty subsets
\alpha,\beta\subseteq[n]
with the same cardinality, we have

\det(AX)[\alpha|\beta]=\sum_{\gamma\subseteq[n],~|\gamma|=|\alpha|}\det A[\alpha|\gamma]\det X[\gamma|\beta]\geq\det A[\alpha|\beta]\det X[\beta|\beta]>0.

For the reverse inclusion, suppose
X\in\mathcal{S}^{R}(n)
, so that
X
is invertible. By the Whitney density theorem
2.1
, there exists a sequence
A_{k}\in TP(n)
converging to
I_{n}
. Now for any
\alpha,\beta\subset[n]
with
|\alpha|=|\beta|>0
, we have

\det(A_{k}X)[\alpha|\beta]\to\det X[\alpha|\beta].

Since the left-hand sequence of determinants is positive, we get
\det X[\alpha|\beta]\geq 0
for all
\alpha,\beta
with
|\alpha|=|\beta|>0
. Hence
X\in ITN(n)
, and so
ITN(n)=\mathcal{S}^{R}(n)
.

Similarly, one shows that
ITN(n)
equals
\mathcal{S}^{L}(n)
, and hence equals their intersection
\mathcal{S}(n)
.
∎

For the rest of our discussion, we will use
\mathcal{S}(n)
to denote the monoid
ITN(n)
. We may drop the dependence on the parameter
n
if it is clear from context. Using Proposition
2.5
, we now show that any semigroup automorphism of
TP(n)
can be extended to a monoid automorphism of
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
T
be a semigroup automorphism of
TP(n)
. Then
T
can be extended to a monoid automorphism
\hat{T}
of
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

Proof.

Let
A\in TP(n)
and define
\hat{T}:\mathcal{S}(n)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(n)
by

\hat{T}(X):=T(A)^{-1}T(AX)=T(XA)T(A)^{-1}\quad\hbox{for all }X\in\mathcal{S}(n).

Then
\hat{T}(I_{n})=I_{n}
and
\hat{T}\equiv T
on
TP(n)
, since
T
is an automorphism on
TP(n)
.
We now show that
\hat{T}
is well defined and independent of the choice of
A
. Let
X\in\mathcal{S}(n)
. For any
B\in TP(n)
, by Proposition
2.5
,
AX,BX,XA,XB,AXB\in TP(n)
. Thus
T(A)T(XB)=T(AXB)=T(AX)T(B)
.
It follows that

(2.2)

T(A)^{-1}T(AX)=T(XB)T(B)^{-1}~~\hbox{for any}~~B\in TP(n).

If
A=B
then
T(A)^{-1}T(AX)=T(XA)T(A)^{-1}=\hat{T}(X)
. By (
2.2
), the definition of
\hat{T}
is independent of the choice of
A\in TP(n)
. Now to show
\hat{T}(X)\in\mathcal{S}(n)
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}(n)
, it suffices to show that
\hat{T}(X)C\in TP(n)
for all
C\in TP(n)
. Let
C\in TP(n)
. Since
T
is bijective, there exists
B\in TP(n)
such that
C=T(B)
. By the definition of
\hat{T}
,
\hat{T}(X)C=T(XB)\in TP(n)
, since
T
is an automorphism of
TP(n)
. Thus
\hat{T}
is a well-defined function from
\mathcal{S}(n)
to
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

We next show that
\hat{T}
is multiplicative on
\mathcal{S}(n)
. For any
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(n)
and
A\in TP(n)
,

\displaystyle\hat{T}(X_{1}X_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(A)^{-1}T(AX_{1}X_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(A)^{-1}T(AX_{1}X_{2}A)T(A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(A)^{-1}T(AX_{1})T(X_{2}A)T(A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{T}(X_{1})\hat{T}(X_{2}).

Thus
\hat{T}
is multiplicative. To show
\hat{T}
is bijective, let
A\in TP(n)
and denote
T(A)=B
. Define the map
U:\mathcal{S}(n)\to\mathcal{S}(n)
by

U(X):=(T^{-1}(B))^{-1}T^{-1}(BX)~~\hbox{for all}~~X\in\mathcal{S}(n).

Then for any
X\in\mathcal{S}(n)
,

\displaystyle U\circ\hat{T}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U(B^{-1}T(AX))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T^{-1}(B))^{-1}T^{-1}(BB^{-1}T(AX))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{-1}(AX)=X

Similarly, one can show
\hat{T}\circ U(X)=X
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}(n)
. Thus
U
is the inverse of
\hat{T}
.
∎

By Proposition
2.6
, to prove
(i)\implies(ii)
of Theorem
A
, it suffices to consider
G(n)=\mathcal{S}(n)
and
T
as a map from
\mathcal{S}(n)
to
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

3.
Special ITN Matrices and their centralizer

To study an automorphism
T
of
\mathcal{S}(n)
, it is useful to study some special submonoids of
\mathcal{S}(n)
that will be preserved by
T
. To begin with, we need some basic definitions and notations. These are isolated here and used below without further reference.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
be integers.

(i)

We denote the
n\times n
antidiagonal matrix with all antidiagonal entries
1
by
P_{n}:

P_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\iddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}.

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{D}(n)
denote the set of all
n\times n
diagonal matrices with positive diagonal entries.

(iii)

We denote by
Z^{+}(n):=Z(GL_{n}(\mathbb{R}))\cap((0,\infty))^{n}
the subgroup of scalar matrices with
positive
diagonal entries.

(iv)

Define
\mathcal{L}(n):=\{aI+bE_{i,{i-1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:a,b>0\hbox{ and }2\leq i\leq n\}
and
\mathcal{U}(n):=\{aI+bE_{i,{i+1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:a,b>0\hbox{ and }1\leq i\leq n-1\}
.

(v)

For positive integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
, define
\mathcal{S}(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{S}(n_{k}):=\{A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{k}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}(n_{i})\text{ for all }i\in[k-1]\}
.

(vi)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}(n):=SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{S}(n)
. Similarly, we define the subsets
\mathcal{D}_{0}(n),\mathcal{L}_{0}(n)
and
\mathcal{U}_{0}(n)
.

We first identify the maximal subgroup contained in
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. The set
\mathcal{D}(n)
is the unique maximal subgroup in
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{D}(n)
is a group in
\mathcal{S}(n)
. Now, suppose
H
is a group under multiplication in
\mathcal{S}(n)
. Let
A\in H
be a non-diagonal matrix. Since
A
is ITN, by Theorem
2.2
, all the diagonal entries of
A
are positive. Thus
A^{-1}\in H
has a negative entry, a contradiction. Thus
H\subseteq\mathcal{D}(n)
.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above lemma is the following:

Corollary 3.3
.

If
G(n)=TP(n)\sqcup\mathcal{D}(n)
, and
\tilde{T}:G(n)\to G(n)
is a multiplicative semigroup automorphism, then
\tilde{T}
maps
TP(n)
onto itself.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{T}(I)=I
,
\tilde{T}(\mathcal{D}(n))
is a subgroup in
G(n)
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\tilde{T}(\mathcal{D}(n))\subseteq\mathcal{D}(n)
. Since
\tilde{T}
is bijective, similarly one can show that
\tilde{T}^{-1}(\mathcal{D}(n))\subseteq\mathcal{D}(n)
, and so
\tilde{T}(\mathcal{D}(n))=\mathcal{D}(n)
. This implies
\tilde{T}(TP(n))=\tilde{T}\left(G(n)\setminus\mathcal{D}(n)\right)=G(n)\setminus\tilde{T}(\mathcal{D}(n))=TP(n)
.
∎

To determine some special submonoids of
\mathcal{S}(n)
on which
T
will induce an automorphism, we now study matrices
D\in\mathcal{D}(n)
and their centralizers.

Definition 3.4
.

For
A\in\mathcal{S}(n)
, the centralizer of
A
with respect to
\mathcal{S}(n)
is denoted and defined as

C(A):=\{X\in\mathcal{S}(n):AX=XA\}.

Note that if
T
is a semigroup automorphism of
\mathcal{S}(n)
, then
T
will map
C(A)
onto
C(T(A))
. This leads us to the following properties:

Remark 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
T
be an automorphism on
\mathcal{S}(n)
. We have the following consequences:

(i)

By a straightforward calculation, one can verify that
A\in\mathcal{S}(n)
satisfies
C(A)=\mathcal{S}(n)
if and only if
A=aI_{n}
for some
a>0
. Thus
T
induces an automorphism on the subgroup
Z^{+}(n)=\{aI:a>0\}
of
\mathcal{S}(n)
.

(ii)

T(aI_{n})=\gamma(a)I_{n}
for some bijective multiplicative map
\gamma:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
.

(iii)

Since
T(I)=I
, by Lemma
3.2
,
T(\mathcal{D}(n))=\mathcal{D}(n)
. Thus
T
induces an automorphism on
\mathcal{D}(n)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
n,n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
be positive integers. Suppose

D=d_{1}I_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus d_{k}I_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{D}(n)
with
d_{j}\neq d_{j+1}
for

j\in[k-1]
. Then

C(D)=\mathcal{S}(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{S}(n_{k})
.

Proof.

Clearly
\mathcal{S}(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{S}(n_{k})\subseteq C(D)
. For the converse, let
X\in C(D)
. Then
DX=XD
and
X
is ITN. Note that
DX=XD
if and only if

X=(X_{ij})
with
X_{jj}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}\times n_{j}}
for
j\in[k]
and

X_{ij}=0
whenever
d_{i}\neq d_{j}
. Furthermore, since
X
is ITN,
X_{ij}=0
if
d_{i}=d_{j}
but
|i-j|>1
. Hence
C(D)=\mathcal{S}(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{S}(n_{k})
.
∎

We now present an outline of the proof strategy for our first main result.

Proof-sketch for Theorem
A
.

By the Cauchy–Binet formula,

and

is immediate. To show

, Proposition

, allows us to restrict our attention to

4.
Proof of Theorem
A
for
n=2

In this section, we prove
(i)\implies(ii)
of Theorem
A
for
2\times 2
matrices. We begin with a few lemmas that play a crucial role in establishing the main result. As a first step, we characterize the elements of the semigroups
\mathcal{U}(2)
and
\mathcal{L}(2)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(2)
be a non-diagonal matrix. Then
DAD^{-1}\in C(A)
for all
D\in\mathcal{D}(2)
if and only if
A=aI_{2}+bE_{12}
or

A=aI_{2}+bE_{21}
for some
a,b>0
.

Proof.

For the sufficiency, note that

C(A)=\{xI_{2}+yE_{12}:x>0,~y\geq 0\}
if
A=aI_{2}+bE_{12}
and
C(A)=\{xI_{2}+yE_{21}:x>0,~y\geq 0\}
if
A=aI_{2}+bE_{21}
. Thus
DAD^{-1}\in C(A)
for all
D\in\mathcal{D}(2)
. To show the necessity, suppose
A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}(2)
is a non-diagonal matrix such that
DAD^{-1}\in C(A)
for all
D\in\mathcal{D}(2)
. We now claim that
a_{11}=a_{22}
and either
a_{12}=0
or
a_{21}=0
. Let
D=\diag(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathcal{D}(2)
with
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
. Since
ADAD^{-1}=DAD^{-1}A
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}a^{2}_{11}+\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}a_{12}a_{21}&a_{12}a_{22}+\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}a_{11}a_{12}\\
a_{11}a_{21}+\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}a_{21}a_{22}&a^{2}_{22}+\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}a_{12}a_{21}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{2}_{11}+\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}a_{12}a_{21}&a_{11}a_{12}+\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}a_{12}a_{22}\\
a_{21}a_{22}+\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}a_{11}a_{21}&a^{2}_{22}+\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}a_{12}a_{21}\end{pmatrix}.

Comparing the
(1,1)
entries of the above matrices, we have either
a_{12}=0
or
a_{21}=0
. If
a_{12}\neq 0
, comparing the
(1,2)
entries of the above matrices, we have
a_{11}=a_{22}
. Otherwise comparing the
(2,1)
entries of the above matrices, we have
a_{11}=a_{22}
. Thus
A=aI_{2}+bE_{12}
or

A=aI_{2}+bE_{21}
for some
a,b>0
.
∎

Using the above result, we now show that if
T
is an automorphism on
\mathcal{S}(2)
, then either

, or the map

\mathcal{U}(2)
and

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T:\mathcal{S}(2)\to\mathcal{S}(2)
be a semigroup isomorphism. Then
T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)
and
T(\mathcal{L}(2))=\mathcal{L}(2)
or
T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{L}(2)
and
T(\mathcal{L}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)

Proof.

Let

. We first show that

or
\mathcal{L}(2)
using Lemma

. Let
D\in\mathcal{D}(2)
. Since
T(\mathcal{D}(2))=\mathcal{D}(2)
,

for some
\hat{D}\in\mathcal{D}(2)
and
T(A)
is a non-diagonal matrix. Thus
DT(A)D^{-1}=T(\hat{D}A{\hat{D}}^{-1})\in T(C(A))
, by Lemma
4.1
. Since

,

or
\mathcal{L}(2)
. Similarly, we can show that if

or
\mathcal{L}(2)
. Since
T
is an automorphism on
S(2)
,
T^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{U}(2)
or
\mathcal{L}(2)
, if

or
\mathcal{L}(2)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{U}(2)
and without loss of generality assume that

. We next claim that
T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)
and
T(\mathcal{L}(2))=\mathcal{L}(2)
. If
T(\mathcal{U}(2))\nsubseteq\mathcal{U}(2)
, then there exists

such that

. This implies

but

. Similarly, if

and
T(B)\in\mathcal{U}(2)
then
T(AB)\in\mathcal{U}(2)
but

is not triangular, a contradiction. Thus

. Again, by a similar argument one can conclude that

and

T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)
and

. This completes the proof.
∎

Let
T:\mathcal{S}(2)\to\mathcal{S}(2)
be a semigroup isomorphism. By the above lemma, we may replace

with the map
X\mapsto P_{2}T(X)P_{2}
if needed, and henceforth

(4.1)

T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)\quad\text{and}\quad T(\mathcal{L}(2))=\mathcal{L}(2).

We next show that

induces an automorphism on the semigroups
\mathcal{U}_{0}(2)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(2)

Lemma 4.3

Let

Proof.

By (

T(\mathcal{U}(2))=\mathcal{U}(2)
and
T(\mathcal{L}(2))=\mathcal{L}(2)
.
To show

, let
x>0
. Then
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(2)
and
T(A)=\begin{pmatrix}a&a_{12}\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}
for some
a,a_{12}>0
, since
\mathcal{U}_{0}(2)\subset\mathcal{U}(2)
. We claim that

. Note that

(4.2)

\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\

0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2x\\

0&1/2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\

Since

Thus

and

T
bijective, by an analogous argument we can show that

Similarly, one can show that

∎

of Theorem

We next show that

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

. From (

and

for all

.

Next we show that

for all

Proof of Theorem

for

In this section, we conclude the proof of Theorem

and also prove Theorem
B
. Throughout this section we assume

, unless stated otherwise. To apply the induction hypothesis, we first show that if

Notation 5.1
.

Given integers

, define the diagonal matrix

to have

entry

Remark 5.2

More generally, in the rest of this section, one can work with any diagonal matrices

, the centralizer of

for all

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

such that

for some

if and only if there is no

.

For the converse, let

. Since

. Since

for some

.

T:\mathcal{S}(n)\to\mathcal{S}(n)
be a semigroup isomorphism. Then either

T(C_{j})=C_{j}\text{ for all }1\leq j<n,\text{ or }T(C_{j})=C_{n-j}\text{ for all }1\leq j<n.

Proof.

We first claim that for each
j\in[n-1]
there exists
k\in[n-1]
such that
T(C_{j})=C_{k}
. Fix
1\leq j\leq n-1
. By Lemma
5.3
,
T(C(D_{j}))=C(D_{k})
for some
k\in[n-1]
. Since
C_{j}\subsetneq C(D_{j})
,
T(C_{j})\subseteq C(D_{k})
. Let
X=aI_{j-1}\oplus Z\oplus bI_{n-j-1}\in C_{j}
and claim that
T(X)\in C_{k}
. If not, then there exists non-diagonal
Y\in C(D_{k})
such that
C(T(X))
is a proper subset of
C(Y)
. Since
T
is invertible and
T(\mathcal{D}(n))=\mathcal{D}(n)
,
T^{-1}(Y)\in C(D_{j})
is non-diagonal and
C(X)\subsetneq C(T^{-1}(Y))
, a contradiction by Lemma
5.5
. Thus
T(C_{j})\subseteq C_{k}
. Since
T
is bijective, it follows using similar reasoning that
T^{-1}(C_{k})\subseteq C_{j}
. Thus
T(C_{j})=C_{k}
, where
k\in[n-1]
.

Given the above, the result is trivial for
n=3
. Assume henceforth that
n\geq 4
. Since
T
is bijective, we have
(T(C_{1}),\dots,T(C_{n-1}))

is a permutation of
(C_{1},\dots,C_{n-1})
. We now claim that
T(C_{1})=C_{1}
or
T(C_{1})=C_{n-1}
. By Lemma
5.4
, the matrices in
T(C_{1})
commute with all matrices in the
n-3
sets
T(C_{3}),\dots,T(C_{n-1})
, and the matrices in
T(C_{n-1})
commute with all matrices in the
n-3
sets
T(C_{1}),\dots,T(C_{n-3})
. Notice that
C_{1}
and
C_{n-1}
are the only two sets whose all elements commute with all matrices from exactly
n-3
of the sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{n-1}
. Thus
T(C_{1})=C_{1}
or
C_{n-1}
and
T(C_{n-1})=C_{n-1}
or
C_{1}
.

Assume that
T(C_{1})=C_{1}
. Then
T(C_{n-1})=C_{n-1}
. To complete the proof, it remains to show that
T(C_{j})=C_{j}
for
2\leq j\leq n-2
. By Lemma
5.4
,
C_{2}
is the unique set among
C_{1},\dots,C_{n-1}
whose elements do not commute with all elements of
C_{1}
, and similarly
C_{n-2}
is the unique set whose elements do not commute with all elements of
C_{n-1}
. This implies that not all matrices in
C_{1}
and
T(C_{2})
commute, and not all matrices in
C_{n-1}
and
T(C_{n-2})
commute. Thus
T(C_{2})=C_{2}
and
T(C_{n-2})=C_{n-2}
. Proceeding similarly, we can show that
T(C_{j})=C_{j}
and
T(C_{n-j})=C_{n-j}
for
3\leq j\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Thus
T(C_{j})=C_{j}
for
j\in[n-1]
.

If
T(C_{1})=C_{n-1}
, then
T(C_{n-1})=C_{1}
and by an argument similar to the above we can conclude that
T(C_{j})=C_{n-j}
for all
j\in[n-1]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Fix
i\in[n-1]
. By Lemma
5.3
, we have
T(C(D_{i}))=C(D_{j})
for some
j\in[n-1]
. Since
C_{i}\subset C(D_{i})
, by Lemma
5.6
,
T(C(D_{i}))=C(D_{i})
or
C(D_{n-i})
. Using this observation and Lemma
5.6
, one can conclude the following.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
T:\mathcal{S}(n)\to\mathcal{S}(n)
be a semigroup isomorphism. Then either

T(C(D_{j}))=C(D_{j})\text{ for all }1\leq j<n,\text{ or }T(C(D_{j}))=C(D_{n-j})\text{ for all }1\leq j<n.

Remark 5.8
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
T:\mathcal{S}(n)\to\mathcal{S}(n)
be a semigroup isomorphism. By replacing

with the map

if necessary, we may henceforth assume that

(5.2)

T(C_{j})=C_{j}\quad\text{for all }1\leq j<n.

We now focus on subgroups of
\mathcal{D}(n)

induces an automorphism on the following subgroups in

•

\{aI_{k-1}\oplus D\oplus cI_{n-k-1}:a,c>0\text{ and }D\in\mathcal{D}(2)\}

•

\{aI_{k-1}\oplus bI_{2}\oplus cI_{n-k-1}:a,b,c>0\}

Lemma 5.9
.

Let

be an integer and let

5.2

and

for all

Fix

. Let

, where

and

. Since

. For a non-diagonal matrix

. Since

is an automorphism, a similar argument shows that

. Thus

for

.

To show

for all

for all

for
k\in[n-1]
.

Theorem 5.10

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer and let

induces an automorphism on the following monoids:

(i)

\{\alpha I_{k-1}\oplus X\oplus\beta I_{n-k-1}:X\in\mathcal{S}(2),\alpha,\beta>0\}

.

(ii)

\mathcal{S}(n-1)\oplus S(1)=\{X\oplus\alpha:X\in\mathcal{S}(n-1),\alpha>0\}

Proof.

Fix
k\in[n-1]
and claim that

5.2
),

,

, where

can be factorize as

, where

We next claim that

induces an automorphism on

, where
X\in\mathcal{S}(n-1),a>0
. Let
\hat{X}=\frac{1}{a}X

3.5
. Since

(5.3)

\hat{X}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-2}\prod_{k=n-2}^{j}\big(I_{n-1}+w_{j,k}E_{k+1,k}\big)~D\prod_{j=n-2}^{1}\prod_{k=n-2}^{j}\big(I_{n-1}+w^{\prime}_{j,k+1}E_{k,k+1}\big),

1\leq j\leq k\leq n-2
, and

3.5

T(\mathcal{D}{(n)})=\mathcal{D}(n)
and by the first part,

maps

. Thus, using (

) and the multiplicativity of

, we have

T
induces a semigroup automorphism on

\{aI_{n-2}\oplus X:a>0,X\in\mathcal{S}(2)\}
by Theorem
5.10
.
Define
T_{2}:\mathcal{S}(2)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(2)
by

T_{2}(X):=\frac{1}{g(X)}Z,\quad\text{where }T(I_{n-2}\oplus X):=g(X)I_{n-2}\oplus Z,

where
g:\mathcal{S}(2)\to(0,\infty)
is a multiplicative map. We now show that
T_{2}
is a semigroup automorphsim on
\mathcal{S}(2)
. Using a similar argument as above, it follows that
T_{2}
is multiplicative. It remains to show that
T_{2}
is bijective.

•

Injective:
Let
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(2)
such that
T_{2}(X_{1})=T_{2}(X_{2})
. If
T(I_{n-2}\oplus X_{1})=g(X_{1})I_{n-2}\oplus Z_{1}
and
T(I_{n-2}\oplus X_{2})=g(X_{2})I_{n-2}\oplus Z_{2}
, then
\frac{1}{g(X_{1})}Z_{1}=\frac{1}{g(X_{2})}Z_{2}
. Thus

\displaystyle T(I_{n-2}\oplus X_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(X_{2})I_{n-2}\oplus\left(\frac{g(X_{2})}{g(X_{1})}\right)Z_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g(X_{2})}{g(X_{1})}T(I_{n-2}\oplus X_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\left(\gamma^{-1}\left(\frac{g(X_{2})}{g(X_{1})}\right)(I_{n-2}\oplus X_{1})\right),

by Remark
3.5
(ii). This implies
\gamma^{-1}\left(\frac{g(X_{2})}{g(X_{1})}\right)=1
and
X_{1}=X_{2}
.

•

Surjective:
Let
Y\in\mathcal{S}(2)
. Since
T
is bijective on

\{aI_{n-2}\oplus X:a>0,X\in\mathcal{S}(2)\}
, there exists
X_{1}\in\mathcal{S}(2)
and a positive real number
\alpha
such that
T(\alpha I_{n-2}\oplus X_{1})=I_{n-2}\oplus Y
. Since
T(\frac{1}{\alpha}I_{n})=\beta I_{n}
for some
\beta>0
, define
X:=\frac{1}{\alpha}X_{1}
and
Z:=\beta Y
. Then

\displaystyle T(I_{n-2}\oplus X)=T\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}(\alpha I_{n-2}\oplus X_{1})\right)=\beta I_{n-2}\oplus Z.

Thus there exists
X\in\mathcal{S}(2)
such that
T_{2}(X)=\frac{1}{\beta}Z=Y
.

This implies that
T_{2}
is an automorphism on
\mathcal{S}(2)
. By the induction hypothesis,
T_{2}
has the form

X\mapsto\gamma_{2}(\det X)(\det X)^{-\frac{1}{2}}SXS^{-1}
, where
\gamma_{2}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a bijective multiplicative map and
S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&0\\
0&s_{2}\end{pmatrix}
or
\begin{pmatrix}0&s_{1}\\
s_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}
with
s_{1},s_{2}>0
. Thus for all
X\in\mathcal{S}(2)
,

(5.11)

T(I_{n-2}\oplus X)=g(X)\left(I_{n-2}\oplus\gamma_{2}(\det X)(\det X)^{-\frac{1}{2}}SXS^{-1}\right).

We next show that
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&s_{1}\\
s_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}
is infeasible. Let

A=I_{n-3}\oplus\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad B=I_{n-3}\oplus\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&\alpha\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

with
\alpha\neq 0
. Then
AB=BA
and by (
5.10
),
T(A)=A
. If
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&s_{1}\\
s_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}
then using (
5.11
) we have
T(B)=g\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right)\left(I_{n-3}\oplus\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}\right)
, and so
T(A)T(B)\neq T(B)T(A)
, a contradiction. Thus
S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&0\\
0&s_{2}\end{pmatrix}
for some
s_{1},s_{2}>0
. Let
S^{\prime}:=\diag(1,\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}})
. Then
SXS^{-1}=S^{\prime}X{S^{\prime}}^{-1}
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. Define
\tilde{S}:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-2}&0\\
0&S^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}(n)
. By replacing
T
with the map
A\mapsto\tilde{S}^{-1}T(A)\tilde{S}
, we may assume that

(5.12)

T(I_{n-2}\oplus X)=g(X)\left(I_{n-2}\oplus\gamma_{2}(\det X)(\det X)^{-\frac{1}{2}}X\right)\quad\text{for all }X\in\mathcal{S}(2).

We now show that
T(I_{n-2}\oplus X)=I_{n-2}\oplus X
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(2)
. By a similar argument as above, one can show that
g(X)=1
for
X=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
for all
\alpha>0
. Again by Whitney’s bidiagonal factorization,
g(X)=1
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(2)
. Since
\gamma_{2}(1)=1
, by (
5.12
), we have

(5.13)

T(I_{n-2}\oplus X)=I_{n-2}\oplus X\quad\text{for all}\quad X\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(2).

Thus far, we have shown that
T
fixes every upper and lower elementary bidiagonal matrix. We now prove that
T(D)=D
for all
D\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(n)
. Let
D=\diag(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(n)
. Then
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1
and
D=D_{1}D_{2}
with
D_{1}=\diag\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-2},\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n-2}a_{i}},1\right)
and
D_{2}=I_{n-2}\oplus\diag(\frac{1}{a_{n}},a_{n})
. By (
5.10
) and (
5.13
), we have
T(D)=T(D_{1})T(D_{2})=D_{1}D_{2}=D
. Thus

(5.14)

T(D)=D\quad\text{for all }\quad D\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(n).

Now note that all the bidiagonal factors of an
n\times n
invertible TN matrix, with determinant
1
, are either of the form
I_{k-1}\oplus X\oplus I_{n-k-1}
with
\det(X)=1
, or a diagonal matrix with determinant
1
. Thus
T(A)=A
for all
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(A)
.

To summarize the above analysis, we have used the maps
X\mapsto P_{n}T(A)P_{n}
(see Remark
5.8
),
X\mapsto\tilde{R}^{-1}T(X)\tilde{R}
and
X\mapsto\tilde{S}^{-1}T(X)\tilde{S}
to conclude that
T(A)=A
for all
A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(A)
, where

P_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\iddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{R}=\begin{pmatrix}R&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{S}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
0&s\end{pmatrix}\text{ with }R\in\mathcal{D}(n-1)\text{ and }s>0.

To conclude the proof, write a general
A\in\mathcal{S}(n)
as
A=((\det A)^{\frac{1}{n}}I_{n})A_{0}
with
\det A_{0}=1
. Thus

\displaystyle T(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T((\det A)^{\frac{1}{n}}A_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma((\det A)^{\frac{1}{n}})T(A_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma((\det A)^{\frac{1}{n}})A_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\det A)(\det A)^{-\frac{1}{n}}A,

where
\mu(x):=\sqrt[n]{\gamma(x)}
is a bijective multiplicative map on
(0,\infty)
. Hence

T(A)=\mu(\det A)(\det A)^{-\frac{1}{n}}A\text{ for all }A\in\mathcal{S}(n).

This completes the proof.
∎

Using Theorem
A
, we now prove our second main result.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

That
(ii)\implies(i)
is trivial by hypothesis. For
(i)\implies(ii)
, using Proposition
2.6
,
T
can be extended to a monoid automorphism
\hat{T}
of
ITN(n)
such that
\hat{T}\equiv T
on
G(n)
. By Theorem
A
, we have the desired structure of
\hat{T}
and hence of
T
.
∎

We conclude with the following remark.

Remark 5.11
.

Let
G
and
H
be two groups. If
\phi
is an automorphism of
G
and
\psi
is an automorphism of
H
, then the product function
\phi\times\psi:G\times H\to G\times H
defined by

(\phi\times\psi)(g,h):=(\phi(g),\psi(h))\quad\text{for all }g\in G\text{ and }h\in H,

is an automorphism of
G\times H
. But it is not true in general that every automorphism of
G\times H
has the above form.

Now we see that every automorphism of the direct product of the group
Z^{+}(n)=\{aI_{n}:a>0\}
and submonoid
\mathcal{S}_{0}(n)
has the above factorization. Note that
\mathcal{S}(n)=Z^{+}(n)\times\mathcal{S}_{0}(n)
. By Theorem
A
, if
T
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{S}(n)
then
T_{1}=T|_{Z^{+}(n)}
and
T_{2}=T|_{\mathcal{S}_{0}(n)}
are automorphisms on
Z^{+}(n)
and
\mathcal{S}_{0}(n)
, respectively. Thus
T=T_{1}\times T_{2}
.
