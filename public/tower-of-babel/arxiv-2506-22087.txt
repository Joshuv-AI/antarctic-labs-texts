An Introduction to Zero-Order Optimization Techniques for Robotics
Armand Jordana, Jianghan Zhang, Joseph Amigo, Ludovic Righetti

Abstract
Zero-order optimization techniques are becoming increasingly popular in robotics due to their ability to handle non-differentiable functions and escape local minima. These advantages make them particularly useful for trajectory optimization and policy optimization. In this work, we propose a mathematical tutorial on random search. It offers a simple and unifying perspective for understanding a wide range of algorithms commonly used in robotics. Leveraging this viewpoint, we classify many trajectory optimization methods under a common framework and derive novel competitive RL algorithms.



I
Introduction

In recent years, zero-order (or derivative-free) optimization techniques
[
1
]
have gained a lot of popularity in the robotics community. While zero-order optimization is a well-established field, its widespread deployment in robotics has only been made possible by the recent advances in parallel computing and GPU hardware. These improvements have enabled the use of zero-order Trajectory Optimization (TO) techniques in a Model Predictive Control (MPC) fashion on complex robots
[
2
,
3
]
. In parallel, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool and demonstrated state-of-the-art capabilities in tasks such as locomotion or manipulation
[
4
,
5
]
.

While these approaches may appear unrelated at first, they share a common property: they do not require access to the simulator’s gradients.
This characteristic allows them to optimize non-smooth objective functions, which typically arise in problems involving contact such as locomotion or manipulation. Another benefit is that it avoids the tedious development of efficient gradient implementations. Furthermore, while deterministic gradient-based algorithms, such as gradient descent or Newton method, are prone to getting stuck in local minima, zero-order techniques are mostly stochastic, which can help escape local minima.

Zero-order optimization has a long history
[
6
]
. In its most generic form, zero-order optimization performs random search by generating samples at random and updating an estimate of the optimal solution based on the performance of each sample.
In the 1970s, Evolutionary Strategies (ES) gained popularity
[
7
]
and later culminated with the CMA-ES algorithm
[
8
]
.
In TO, predictive sampling
[
9
]
is a well-known and simple random search technique. Another popular algorithm is MPPI
[
10
]
, which was initially derived from information theory.
In RL, Reinforce
[
11
]
was one of the first major algorithms proposed for continuous action space problems.
These algorithms have since inspired many variations.

While most of these algorithms have strong theoretical foundations, this is not necessarily well known in the robotics community. One reason for this is that these algorithms often fall at the intersection of many fields such as optimization, statistics, machine learning and control.
By looking at results from these different fields, we aim to answer two questions: 1) What are the convergence guarantees of the algorithms used in robotics? 2) What are the theoretical tools explaining the ability of these algorithms to escape local minima?

Recently,
[
12
]
introduced Gaussian smoothing and understood random search from an optimization perspective. Inspired by this work, we propose a unified perspective to understand the zero-order optimization techniques popular in the robotics community. More specifically, we are interested in algorithms that can find solutions to the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)

(1)

This formulation encompasses a wide range of problems encountered in robotics, such as TO and RL.
Both of these techniques define a high-level goal through a cost function that has to be minimized (or a reward function that has to be maximized). However, they differ in the optimization space. TO optimizes directly over trajectories, whereas RL optimizes over policies.
Furthermore, they differ in the way they are deployed.
These algorithms can be used either online (i.e. executed at runtime) or offline. On one hand, TO can either be solved online in an MPC loop or solved offline to generate data to train a policy. On the other hand, RL is mostly solved offline.
While these differences may seem fundamental, we show that the algorithms used to solve these problems share many conceptual similarities.

Related work

[
13
]
first showed the similarities between MPPI and CMA-ES.
More recently,
[
14
]
understood MPPI as an approximate gradient method and
[
3
]
connected MPPI to diffusion models. Our work builds on these insights and further investigates explanations for the success of MPPI. In particular, we shed light on the connection between MPPI and recent results showcasing the benefits of the log-sum-exp transform
[

. Lastly, we benchmark several state-of-the-art approaches on various robotic examples.

[

]
first made the connection between RL and random search. These works used this insight to derive TO algorithms based on random search. In contrast, we study how to leverage this understanding to derive novel RL algorithms.

Contribution

In this work, we propose a mathematical introduction to zero-order optimization algorithms used in robotics. This unified perspective provides a simple way to understand techniques such as MPPI, Covariance Matrix Adaptation (CMA) or RL policy gradient methods.
In addition, we show how this unified view allows us to naturally derive novel competitive methods.
Lastly, we discuss the theoretical concepts explaining why stochastic algorithms are well-suited to avoid getting stuck in local minima.

II
Random search

II-A

Simple random search

In this first section, we present how to use a population of samples (or particles) to search for a global solution. We divide the population search algorithms in two broad classes: global search and local search algorithms.

II-A
1
Global Search

Global search can be considered as the simplest global optimization technique. The idea is to iteratively sample a random point. If the sample has a value lower than the previous one, it is kept as the current best guess. Otherwise, it is disregarded. Algorithm
1
summarizes the procedure.

2

\tilde{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

3

if

f(\tilde{x})<f(x)

then

4

x\leftarrow\tilde{x}

5

Output:

x

Algorithm 1

Simple Global Search

Interestingly, the sampling distribution of Algorithm
1
ignores all the previous estimates. For this reason, it can be referred to as a global algorithm.
Under some mild conditions on the objective function, Algorithm
1
converges to a global minimum
[
18
]
.
Unfortunately, in practice, this method performs poorly as it is subject to the curse of dimensionality. That is to say that the number of function evaluations required to find a global solution grows exponentially with the dimension of the problem.

II-A
2
Greedy Local Search

A common attempt to break the curse of dimensionality is to exploit problem-specific knowledge. For instance, exploiting previous information about the shape of the function can help design an empirically more efficient algorithm. This is called Greedy Local Search. The idea is to iteratively search around the previous estimate, with additive Gaussian noise, for instance.
Then, as in the previous algorithm, the sample is kept only if it improves the function value. Algorithm
2
summarizes the procedure.

Input:

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

x\leftarrow x_{0}

1

stopping criterion is not met

do

2

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

//
with a centered Gaussian distribution

if

f(x+d)<f(x)

then

3

x\leftarrow x+d

4

Output:

x

Algorithm 2

Greedy Local Search

By design, Algorithm
2
depends on previous estimates. For that reason, it can be referred to as a local algorithm. However, this notion should not be confused with the concepts of local and global solutions. In fact, although the convergence guarantees of Algorithm
2
are more restrictive than those of Algorithm
1
, it is still possible to prove convergence of Algorithm
2
to a global solution under some regularity conditions
[
18
]
.
Consequently, both local and global search can have global convergence guarantees. However, while both are subject to the curse of dimensionality, local search techniques can be more effective empirically. In fact, as we will see in the next section, it is well-known that local search techniques relying on gradient information can exhibit strong convergence rates and guarantees in convex settings.

Approximate Gradient Descent

Here,
\alpha
is the step size (or learning rate). There exist many ways to estimate the gradient with multiple function evaluations
[
19
]
. Among the most widely used ones is the forward finite differences technique:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{f(x+\mu e_{j})-f(x)}{\mu}e_{j}

(2)

where
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the
j^{th}
canonical vector. Alternatively, one may use central finite differences. While this estimate can be very effective, it requires

II-B
1
Random Coordinate Descent

One way to avoid performing that many function evaluations is Random Coordinate Descent
[
20
]
. As its name suggests, this method alternates descent steps on random coordinates.

\displaystyle g=\frac{f(x+\mu e_{j})-f(x)}{\mu}e_{j}

(3)

where
j
can be chosen uniformly at random in
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Note that
g
depends on
x
.

Remark 1
.

In the convex case and without finite difference approximation, Random Coordinate Descent requires at most
n
times more steps compared to classical gradient descent
[
20
]
. In addition, on a certain class of functions, it can require as many steps as gradient descent while requiring
n
times less function evaluations.

Random Coordinate Descent samples discrete coordinates, but it is also possible to sample along arbitrary directions.

II-B
2
Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA)

In the 1990s, Spall introduced Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA)
[
18
]
. The main idea is to replace finite differences with the following stochastic estimate:

\displaystyle g=\frac{f(x+\mu\Delta)-f(x-\mu\Delta)}{2\mu}\Delta

(4)

where each component of
\Delta
is equal to
\pm 1
according to independent Bernoulli distributions. Note that Spall derived SPSA using central differentiation instead of the forward finite difference.
Under reasonable assumptions, SPSA can reach similar performances as finite differences while requiring
n
times less samples
[
18
]
.

II-B
3
Gaussian Smoothing

In
[
12
]
, Nesterov proposes another approach that samples the gradient of a smoothed surrogate function

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(x+\mu u)]

(5)

where
u\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
. This is Gaussian smoothing, commonly referred to as randomized smoothing. As
\mu
goes to zero,
f_{\mu}
tends to
f
. As a result, for small values of
\mu
, the gradients of the surrogate function
f_{\mu}
are close to those of the original function
f
. The advantage of this surrogate is that its gradient can be evaluated via an average of function evaluations. To understand this, let’s first define
\kappa=\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det(\Sigma)}
, the normalization factor of the multivariate Gaussian distribution. With a change of variables, we can write:

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{u}f(x+\mu u)e^{-\frac{1}{2}u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}u}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa\mu^{n}}\int_{y}f(y)e^{-\frac{1}{2\mu^{2}}(y-x)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(y-x)}\,dy

(6)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa\mu^{2}}\int_{y}f(x+\mu u)e^{-\frac{1}{2}u^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}u}\Sigma^{-1}\mu u\,du

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\mu}f(x+\mu u)\Sigma^{-1}u\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x)}{\mu}\Sigma^{-1}u\right]

(7)

The last equality comes from the fact that
\mathbb{E}\left[f(x)\Sigma^{-1}u\right]=0
.
Hence, the gradient of
f_{\mu}
can be estimated using only function evaluations of the original function,
f
(at the cost of approximating an expectation with samples).
Note that the term
\Sigma^{-1}u
is the gradient of the logarithm of the probability density function with respect to the mean; this is often called the log-likelihood trick. This is sometimes written:

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)}\left[f(z)\nabla_{x}\log(p_{x}(z))\right]

(8)

where
p_{x}
is the density of the Gaussian distribution with mean,
x
, and with covariance,
\Sigma
. This alternative view will be useful to understand CMA. In the general case, the expectation is intractable, however, one can rely on sampling to estimate it. For instance, one can use the following estimate in Algorithm
3
:

\displaystyle g=\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x)}{\mu}\Sigma^{-1}u

(9)

While
\frac{1}{\mu}f(x+\mu u)\Sigma^{-1}u
is a valid gradient estimate, it can be arbitrarily large. In contrast, the estimate (
9
) is invariant to constant translations of the function. Intuitively, this will help reduce the variance of the estimate.
Note that it is also possible to use an estimate with central differentiation:

\displaystyle g=\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x-\mu u)}{2\mu}\Sigma^{-1}u

(10)

In the convex setting, using the forward estimate (Eq. (
9
)) or the central estimate (Eq. (
10
)) as a gradient estimate requires at most
n
times more iterations than the standard gradient method
[
12
]
.

Remark 2
.

Alternatively, one may sample from the surface of a unit sphere
[
21
]
. This technique guarantees that the norm of the direction vectors is bounded.

To summarize, although the assumptions in
[
18
,
20
,
12
]
vary, the underlying ideas are of the same nature; sampling along random directions may match the performance of gradient descent while requiring
n
times less function evaluation.

II-C

Simulated Annealing

So far, we have seen that performing random approximate gradient descent can be sample efficient in the convex setting. However, one may ask whether those algorithms can converge to global solutions in nonconvex settings, as it is the case for the Greedy Local Search introduced in Algorithm
2
.
It is well known that, in its vanilla form, gradient descent is prone to local minima. However, additive Gaussian noise can lead to asymptotic global convergence guarantees. To understand why, we need to study the following Langevin dynamics:

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\log p(X_{t})+dW_{t}

(11)

Here
p
is a probability distribution and
W_{t}
is a Brownian motion.
\nabla_{x}\log p
is the score function of the distribution. Note that the gradient is taken with respect to the input of
p
(not with respect to parameters of
p
as in the log-likelihood trick of Gaussian smoothing). This Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) has been extensively studied and it can be shown that
X_{t}
converges to the distribution defined by
p

[
22
]
. Consequently, this SDE can be used to sample from a distribution with probability density
p
. One way to leverage this result in the context of global optimization is to choose the following probability distribution:

\displaystyle p\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f\right)

(12)

where
\lambda>0
is called the temperature. As
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, the distribution concentrates on the set of global minima of
f

[
23
]
.
Furthermore, the score function of this distribution is proportional to the gradient of
f
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\log p(x)=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla_{x}f(x)

(13)

Therefore, this SDE can be discretized in order to derive a recursive algorithm.
Following this idea,
[
24
]
shows that with an appropriate schedule of step sizes
\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
, the following discretized Stochastic Langevin Gradient Dynamics guarantees asymptotic convergence to global solutions.

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}g_{k}+\gamma_{k}w_{k}

(14)

where
w_{k}
is Gaussian noise and where
g_{k}
is of the form
(\nabla f(x_{k})+\xi_{k})
with
\xi_{k}
representing stochastic noise. Hence,
g_{k}
can be interpreted as a noisy approximation of the gradient.
Intuitively, the Gaussian noise
w_{k}
helps to escape local minima.
The step sizes
\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
are chosen such that the corresponding SDE has a temperature
\lambda
that tends slowly to zero. This is called Simulated Annealing. Intuitively, slowly decreasing the temperature will guide the iterates towards a global solution.

These results can be used to prove the global convergence of SPSA. More precisely, the iterative scheme in Equation (
14
) with
g
as the SPSA estimate defined in Equation (
4
) converges to a global minimum
[
25
]
.
In fact, in its default version (i.e. without additive noise), SPSA converges to a global minimum
[
25
]
. This is because the noise of the estimate (i.e.
\xi_{k}
) is enough to escape local minima and to achieve global convergence. We are not aware of such results in the context of randomized smoothing. However, we can conjecture that similar results may be valid.

To summarize, we have seen that stochasticity can help gradient descent algorithms to reach global solutions. Although these results are highly relevant theoretically, they have limited practical impact as they are only asymptotic.

III
Trajectory Optimization

In this section, we show how random search allows us to understand derivative-free optimization algorithms commonly used in robotics. In practice, Trajectory Optimization aims to solve

\displaystyle\min_{\{u_{0},u_{1},\dots u_{T-1}\}}

\displaystyle\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}c_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})+c_{T}(x_{T})

(15)

such that

\displaystyle\,x_{0}=x,\,\mbox{and}\;\;x_{t+1}=f_{\text{dyn}}(x_{t},u_{t})

Here
x
denotes the state,
u
the control variable,
(c_{t})_{t}
the running cost functions,
c_{T}
the terminal cost function,
T
the time horizon and
f_{\text{dyn}}
the dynamics. Note that we abuse the notation by using
x
to denote the state instead of the optimization variable.
Importantly, the constraints are implicit; the optimization is only done with respect to the control variables. Therefore, this problem is unconstrained and called the single shooting approach. In the end, it can be written in the form of Problem (
1
).

III-B

The log-sum-exp transform: MPPI

Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)
[
10
]
is a derivative-free TO method initially derived from an information theoretic point of view. MPPI iteratively samples around the current guess and updates it using a weighted exponential average. More specifically, given a current guess
x
, MPPI samples
K
variables
x_{k}
following a Gaussian distribution,
\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)
. Then, it takes an update according to the following rule:

\displaystyle x\leftarrow\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}x_{k}

(16)

where

\displaystyle w_{k}=\frac{\exp(-\frac{1}{\lambda}(f(x_{k})-\rho))}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(-\frac{1}{\lambda}(f(x_{j})-\rho))}

(17)

where
\rho=\min_{j}f(x_{j})
(to prevent numerical stability issues). These weights,
w_{k}
, are called Exponential Average weights. Algorithm
5
describes the complete procedure.

Input:

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\Sigma,K

x\leftarrow x_{0}

1

while

stopping criterion is not met

do

2

Sample
x_{1:K}\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)

//
sample

Compute weights
w_{1},\dots w_{K}

3

x\leftarrow\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}x_{k}

//
update

Output:

x

Algorithm 5

Model-Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)

Let’s see how this update rule can be interpreted as an instance of Gaussian smoothing. Instead of the surrogate function defined in Equation
5
, let’s consider the (continuous) log-sum-exp function.

\displaystyle f_{\mu,\lambda}(x)=-\lambda\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon)\right)\right]\right)~

(18)

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
and where
\lambda>0
is called the temperature. As
\mu
goes to zero, we recover
f
. Therefore, the gradients of this surrogate can be used to approximate the gradients of the original function,
f
. Furthermore, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty,f_{\mu,\lambda}\rightarrow f_{\mu}

[
15
]
. Consequently, this surrogate can be seen as a generalization of Gaussian smoothing.
The gradient of the surrogate reads:

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\nabla f_{\mu,\lambda}(x)=\frac{-\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon)\right)\Sigma^{-1}\epsilon\right]}{\mu\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon)\right)\right]}~

(19)

In practice, both expectations can be approximated with
K
samples
\epsilon_{1:K}
following the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
. This leads to the following estimate:

\displaystyle g=\frac{-\lambda}{\mu}\frac{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon_{k})\right)\Sigma^{-1}\epsilon_{k}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon_{j})\right)}

(20)

Similarly to the randomized smoothing case, this update is invariant to additive translation of the function
f
. Let’s consider
\mu=1
. We will show that MPPI follows a natural gradient step
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle x\leftarrow x-\alpha F^{-1}g

(21)

where
\alpha
is the step size and where
F
is the Fisher information matrix which is equal to the inverse of the covariance matrix for Gaussian distributions. The idea of the natural gradient is to build an update step that is invariant to changes of variables. More details on the natural gradient are provided in the appendix
B
.
If
\alpha=\frac{1}{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle x-\alpha F^{-1}g

\displaystyle=x+\frac{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon_{k})\right)\epsilon_{k}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon_{j})\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{K}w_{k}(x+\epsilon_{k})

(22)

As
(x+\epsilon_{k})\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)
, we recover the MPPI update.
Furthermore, if
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I
, then
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{L}
where
L
is the Lipschitz function of the surrogate function
[
15
]
. Therefore, the step size chosen by MPPI can be interpreted as a conservative estimate of the standard optimal step size from convex optimization
[
28
]
.

Figure
1
illustrates how the log-sum-exp smoothing differs from Nesterov’s randomized smoothing. This simple example function has two types of local minima: one minima is global but narrow and the other one is local but wide. Interestingly, Gaussian smoothing gives similar importance to both minima; the two minima now have a similar depth and width. In contrast, the log-sum-exp smoothing gives more importance to the global minima; the global minima is much wider and almost absorbs the local one.

To the best of our knowledge,
[
15
]
proposes the first explanation of the success of MPPI from an optimization perspective.
[
15
]
shows that the log-sum-exp smoothing can be interpreted as an interpolation between the default randomized smoothing and the Moreau envelope. The idea is that the Moreau envelope is a better approximation than RS but is harder to compute. MPPI elegantly trades-off approximation quality and computational complexity. At the cost of more samples, relying on this surrogate can improve convergence rates
[
15
]
.
Intuitively, this estimator requires many samples because of the presence of the expectation in the denominator in Equation (
19
).
With the progress of parallel computing, this requirement of multiple samples might not be a limitation at all. However, using many samples will render the update step essentially deterministic, which will make the algorithm prone to getting trapped in local minima.

The log-sum-exp smoothing reminds of the simulated annealing idea. Similarly to simulated annealing, as the temperature

\displaystyle p\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f_{\mu,\lambda}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}f(x+\mu\epsilon)\right)\right]

III-C

Covariance Matrix Adaptation (CMA)

So far, we have kept the covariance matrix
\Sigma
fixed across various iterations. However, in practice, it is not necessarily clear how to choose this parameter according to the problem. In this section, we show how to derive a Covariance Matrix Adaptation (CMA) scheme.
Interestingly, the optimization of the smooth surrogate function can be interpreted as an optimization in the space of Gaussian probability distribution. Indeed, optimizing Equation (
5
) can be interpreted as a search over the mean of a Gaussian distribution. More specifically, we can write

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(x+\mu u)]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)}\left[f(z)\right]

(23)

Therefore, Gaussian smoothing can be seen as the search of a Gaussian distribution that minimizes the expectation of
f
under that distribution. This distribution can be interpreted as the belief of where the global minimum of the function
f
might be. A natural generalization is to not only optimize the mean but also the covariance. More specifically, one can apply gradient descent to the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta=(x,\Sigma)}J(\theta)

(24)

where

\displaystyle J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)}\left[f(z)\right]

(25)

J(\theta)
is minimal when the distribution
\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)
concentrates around the minima of
f

[
30
]
. Note that this idea do not necessarily need to rely on Gaussian distributions. Similarly to the randomized smoothing case, we can use the log-likelihood trick:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(x,\Sigma)}\left[f(z)(z-x)\right]

Following the natural gradient is especially relevant in this setting, as it is crucial that the update does not depend on the parametrization choice of the Gaussian distribution.
We refer the reader to the appendix

When dealing with TO, it is often preferred to design algorithms that can achieve linear complexity with respect to the time horizon

IV
Policy optimization

One of the main limitations of TO is that it is not always straightforward to use it to derive a controller. Indeed, performing MPC implies solving TO problems online, which can be extremely challenging due to limited compute time. Even though recent works were able to achieve impressive zero-order MPC demonstrations, these performances have not yet matched those obtained by RL. The main strength of RL is that it relies solely on policy evaluation at runtime. All the computation is moved offline where the goal is to search for a policy that will maximize performance across all possible initial conditions. This can be written:

\displaystyle F(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[J(\theta,s)\right]

(29)

where the expectation is taken over initial states
s
and where
J(\theta,s)
is the cost of the trajectory with initial state
s
and with the controller
\pi_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle J(\theta,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k})

(30)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t. }\,s_{0}=s,\,

\displaystyle s_{k+1}=f_{\text{dyn}}(s_{k},a_{k})\,\mbox{ and }\,a_{k}=\pi_{\theta}(s_{k}).

\theta
denote the parameters of the policy (e.g. the weights of a neural network).
r
is the reward,
a
is the control action.
Note that we follow the RL formalism by trading the minimization of a cost function for the maximization of a reward. Furthermore, in contrast to TO, the optimization for each trajectory is done with an infinite horizon and a discount factor
\gamma
.
We do not consider stochastic dynamics for simplicity as most robotic models are deterministic (e.g. locomotion or manipulation). However, the ideas presented in this section should extend to the stochastic dynamics case.

Remark 8
.

The formulation in Equation (
29
) can easily be extended to encompass domain randomization by taking the expectation across multiple parameters of the environment.

Because the policy is deterministic, RL algorithms aiming to solve this formulation are called Deterministic Policy Gradient (DPG) algorithms
[
34
]
.
While stochastic policies can be relevant in the context of games or partially observable settings, most robotic applications such as locomotion or manipulation can be solved with deterministic policies. In fact, in practice, even works that rely on RL algorithms derived with stochastic policies generally deploy a deterministic policy on the robot.

There exist two main sampling approaches to maximize (
29
). The first one is to directly rely on the zero-order techniques which implies sampling in the parameter space of a neural network, similarly to
[
35
]
. The second approach, used in RL, is to leverage the structure of the problem to sample in the action space.
In this work, we are interested in the latter one. In the deterministic setting, the gradient can be estimated with the DPG theorem
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}J(\theta,s_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\partial_{\theta}\pi(s_{k})\partial_{a}Q(s_{k},a_{k})

(31)

where the
Q
-function is defined as

\displaystyle Q(s,a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r(s_{k},a_{k})

(32)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t. }\,s_{0}=s,\;

\displaystyle a_{0}=a,\;s_{k+1}=f_{\text{dyn}}(s_{k},a_{k}),\;a_{k}=\pi_{\theta}(s_{k})

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\nabla_{\theta}F(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\partial_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(s_{k})\partial_{a}Q(s_{k},a_{k})\right]

(33)

Note that we follow the formalism of
[
36
]
and express the policy gradient as an expectation over entire trajectories rather than making the
\gamma
-discounted state distribution explicit.
Unfortunately, this formula cannot be directly implemented in an algorithm because of the presence of the
Q
-function. Indeed, the
Q
-function cannot be computed analytically. One solution is to estimate it with function approximation at each gradient descent step. Algorithms relying on this mechanism are called actor-critics. The actor refers to the policy while the critic refers to the
Q
-function. In practice, the
Q
-function is learned in a self supervised way via the Bellman equation. There exist many techniques to estimate the critic efficiently between gradient updates of the actor; we refer the reader to
[
11
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the subject.

Remark 9
.

A key distinction between TO and policy optimization lies in the objective function: the policy optimization objective is averaged across various initial conditions. As a result, the objective cannot be computed analytically and must instead be estimated through sampling. Consequently, gradient estimates are inherently noisy and introduce stochasticity into the optimization process
[
37
]
.
In light of our discussion on Langevin dynamics, this inherent stochasticity of RL algorithms could explain their empirical ability to avoid poor local minima.

IV-C

Connection to the Reinforce algorithm

One may ask how the update rule introduced in Equation (
34
) compares to the classical Reinforce algorithm. First, let’s recall how this algorithm is derived. In its most general form, Reinforce relies on the gradient of the following formulation.

\displaystyle J(\theta,s_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r_{k}(S_{k},A_{k})\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t. }\;\;S_{0}

\displaystyle=s_{0}\;,A_{k}\sim p_{\theta}(\;.\;|S_{k}),\;S_{k+1}=f_{\text{dyn}}(S_{k},A_{k})

The policy is no longer a deterministic function but a parametrized distribution over the space of possible actions. Note that the expectation is now taken over both the initial conditions and random actions. We now use capitalized letter for actions and states to emphasize their stochastic nature.
The stochastic policy gradient theorem states that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}J(\theta,s_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}Q(S_{k},A_{k})\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(A_{k}|S_{k}))\right]

Note that the definition of the
Q
-function is no longer the same as in the deterministic case. It is now defined as the expectation of stochastic rollouts starting at state
s
with action
a
. In practice, in most RL algorithms, the actions are sampled from a Gaussian probability distribution with mean
\pi_{\theta}(s)
and covariance
\Sigma
where
\pi_{\theta}
is a neural network. Hence, we have:

\displaystyle a\sim\mathcal{N}(\pi_{\theta}(s),\Sigma)

(36)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(a|s))=\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(s)}{\partial\theta}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(a-\pi_{\theta}(s))

(37)

Consequently, we have:

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}J(\theta,s_{0})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\left(Q(S_{k},A_{k})-V(S_{k})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(S_{k})}{\partial\theta}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(A_{k}-\pi_{\theta}(S_{k}))\right]

(38)

The baseline
V(S_{k})
can be subtracted because the random variable
A_{k}-\pi_{\theta}(S_{k})
has zero-mean conditioned on the state. Similarly to the randomized smoothing case this induces an invariance to translation by a constant of the reward function.
Interestingly, we recover a formula very similar to Equation (
34
). The only difference is that the rollouts are now stochastic. This changes two things. The first one is that the
Q
-function and the partial derivatives are now evaluated at
S_{k}
(instead of
s_{k}
in Equation (
34
)). The second is that the definition of the
Q
-function has changed. In Equation (
34
), the
Q
-function denoted deterministic rollouts. In contrast, it is now an expectation of the outcome of stochastic rollouts.

The additional stochasticity in Equation (
IV-C
) (as compared to Equation (
34
)) can be understood as exploration noise.
One formal justification could be to understand the stochastic policy in Equation (
35
) as a smoothing operating in action space. Similarly to Gaussian smoothing, the smoothing allows to derive the gradient of the cost with only
Q
-function evaluation. Consequently, the policy gradient theorem is analogous to the log-likelihood trick of Gaussian smoothing.

In the end, Reinforce can be seen as a way to solve the deterministic formulation introduced in Equation (
29
) without the simulator’s gradients by sampling in the action space. Interestingly, the smoothing of the Reinforce formulation introduces additional stochasticity as compared to Equation (
34
). It would be interesting to understand whether this extra stochasticity that can be interpreted as exploration noise is useful in practice. Intuitively, smoothing helps to simplify the problem but adding too much noise might render the surrogate too different from the initial problem.
Future work could explore connections to other popular stochastic policy gradient algorithms such as TRPO or PPO.

V

Theoretically, sampling-based algorithms following Langevin dynamics can sample from any probability distribution
[

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have demonstrated how random search provides a unifying perspective on zero-order algorithms commonly used in robotics. We also discussed theoretical concepts that help explain why sampling-based zero-order techniques can escape local minima.
Leveraging this understanding, we proposed novel and competitive RL algorithms. These algorithms are only examples of the potential outcomes enabled by this unified viewpoint.
In the future, we hope this tutorial will help the robotics community tackle open challenges such as constrained zero-order optimization and the search for global solutions.

Appendix B
About the natural gradient

The idea of the natural gradient
[
27
]
is to find the steepest descent according to an arbitrary metric
d
.
More precisely, the natural gradient looks for a small step
h
satisfying:

\displaystyle\min_{h}

\displaystyle f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{T}h

(41)

s.t.

\displaystyle d(x+h,x)=\epsilon^{2}

where
\epsilon
is a small scalar. Typically,
d
is such that (at least locally):

\displaystyle d(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{2}-x_{1})^{T}A(x_{2}-x_{1})

(42)

Let’s write the Lagrangian of the problem (
41
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(h,\lambda)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{T}h-\lambda(\frac{1}{2}h^{T}Ah-\epsilon^{2})

(43)

We have:

\displaystyle\nabla_{h}\mathcal{L}(h,\lambda)=0

\displaystyle\iff\nabla f(x)-\lambda Ah=0

\displaystyle\iff h=\frac{1}{\lambda}A^{-1}\nabla f(x)

(44)

The sign of
\lambda
can be recovered with the second order optimality condition
[
43
]
.

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{hh}\mathcal{L}(h,\lambda)\succeq 0\implies-\lambda A\succeq 0\implies\lambda\leq 0

(45)

Hence, the natural gradient step is of the form:

\displaystyle h\propto-A^{-1}\nabla f(x)

(46)

In the context where
\theta
characterizes a probability distribution, a typical choice of distance is:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})=\int_{x}p_{\theta+h}(x)\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta+h}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)}\right)dx

(47)

Locally, we can show that this metric is quadratic. Let’s look at the partial derivatives of Equation (
47
) with respect to
h
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})}{\partial h}=\int_{x}\partial_{h}p_{\theta+h}(x)\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta+h}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)}\right)dx

As
\int_{x}p_{\theta+h}(x)dx=1
implies
\partial_{h}\int_{x}p_{\theta+h}(x)dx=0
for all
h
. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})}{\partial h}(h=0)=0

(48)

Then, we can compute the Hessian with respect to
h
.

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\frac{\partial^{2}\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})}{\partial^{2}h}=\int_{x}\partial^{2}_{h}p_{\theta+h}(x)\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta+h}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)}\right)dx

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\int_{x}\partial_{h}p_{\theta+h}(x)^{T}\partial_{h}p_{\theta+h}(x)\frac{1}{p_{\theta+h}(x)}dx

(49)

And, we find that:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})}{\partial^{2}h}(h=0)

(50)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\partial_{h}p_{\theta}(x)^{T}\partial_{h}p_{\theta}(x)\frac{1}{p_{\theta}(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\partial_{\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(x))^{T}\partial_{\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(x))p_{\theta}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{\theta}}\left[\partial_{\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(X))^{T}\partial_{\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(X))\right]=F(\theta)

where
F(\theta)
is the Fisher matrix. In the end,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p_{\theta+h}||p_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}h^{T}F(\theta)h+o(||h||^{2})

(51)

Let’s consider the specific case where
\theta=m
and:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x)=\frac{1}{\kappa}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-m)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-m)\right)

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\log(p_{\theta}(x))=(x-\theta)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}

(52)

Therefore, the Fisher matrix reads:

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma^{-1}(x-\theta)(x-\theta)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[(x-\theta)(x-\theta)^{T}\ \right]\Sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-1}

Hence, in that case, the Fisher matrix is equal to the inverse of the covariance matrix.

Appendix C
About the connection between the log-sum-exp and risk-sensitive control

Interestingly, the surrogate defined in Equation (
18
) reminds of the risk-seeking control formulation
[
29
]
. At first, this can seem counter-intuitive as in practice, we prefer designing risk-averse controllers rather than risk-seeking ones. However, from an optimization perspective, the risk-seeking formulation has the advantage of giving more importance to lower values. Figure
4
illustrates the landscape for each formulation. The risk-neutral formulation corresponds to the default randomized smoothing. One way to interpret these landscapes is to see the smoothing noise as a disturbance from the environment. On one hand, the risk-seeking formulation expects the noise to act in its favor by shifting the state towards the global solution. Therefore, it will lower the function value in the surroundings of the narrow global minima. On the other hand, the risk-averse formulation expects the noise to be adversarial. Therefore, it will increase the function value (as compared to the neutral formulation) around the narrow global minima.
