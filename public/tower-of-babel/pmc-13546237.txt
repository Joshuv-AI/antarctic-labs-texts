Long-Term Graft Survival Following Penetrating Keratoplasty in Eyes with Preoperative Corneal Neovascularization
Authors: Matteo Menean, Andrea Servillo, Pasquale Viggiano, Cecilia Mularoni, Giulio Ferrari, Paolo Rama, Giorgio Paganoni, Francesco Bandello, Karl Anders Knutsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe aimed to evaluate long-term graft survival following penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) in eyes with preoperative corneal neovascularization (CorNV), and to assess the impact of the extent of CorNV on graft outcomes.MethodsThis was a retrospective longitudinal study. Patients with preoperative CorNV who underwent PKP between 2003 and 2024 were included. Demographics, underlying pathology, and CorNV stage (clock-hour extent) and depth were recorded at baseline before PKP. Visual acuity and endothelial cell density (ECD) were assessed during follow-up. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify risk factors for graft failure. Graft failure was set as primary outcome.ResultsA total of 80 eyes were included (mean age 55 ± 16 years; 42 male, 53%; 38 female, 47%). Moderate to severe CorNV (> 3 clock-hours) showed no significant association with increased graft failure risk (HR 1.51; p = 0.31). Survival was mainly influenced by etiology: patients with Acanthamoeba keratitis had significantly better outcomes than those with burns (HR 11.02; p = 0.032). One-year visual acuity improved notably. CorNV depth was not linked to graft survival.ConclusionsIn this cohort, preoperative CorNV extent was not significantly associated with graft failure after PKP; outcomes depend largely on the underlying etiology. Thorough assessment of CorNV features and etiology is advised to refine risk stratification. Standardized imaging and multicenter studies are needed to clarify additional prognostic factors and further improve management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40123-026-01487-x.

Introduction

Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) consists in a full-thickness corneal transplantation and still represents a cornerstone in the management of several advanced corneal diseases of different etiologies [1, 2]. Although lamellar techniques have revolutionized corneal surgery by offering less invasive approaches, PKP remains the standard of care for a broad spectrum of corneal pathologies involving all layers, such as full-thickness scars, advanced keratoconus, and rapidly progressive or aggressive infectious keratitis [3–5]. However, failure of the corneal graft after PKP represents one of the most significant long-term concerns and complications [6, 7].

In order to improve PKP prognostication, longitudinal studies have extensively investigated factors associated with graft failure, identifying preoperative corneal neovascularization (CorNV) as a significant risk factor for immune-mediated graft rejection [8, 9]. Corneal immune privilege, crucial for successful transplantation, is maintained through multiple mechanisms including the blood–aqueous barrier, corneal avascularity, and absence of mature antigen-presenting cells in the central cornea [10]. It has been demonstrated that CorNV compromises the immune-privileged status of the cornea by promoting the infiltration and trafficking of antigen-presenting cells and effector immune cells. As a result, CorNV transforms the graft bed into a high-risk microenvironment, predisposing the allograft to immune-mediated rejection [11–14]. More specifically, both the extent stage and depth of neovascularization correlate with decreased long-term graft survival [9, 15, 16]. However, although CorNV is generally considered a risk factor for graft failure, its prognostic impact is not uniform and may significantly depend on the underlying pathogenic mechanisms driving neovascularization. Different etiologies may induce distinct inflammatory and immunological microenvironments, which in turn can variably influence graft survival. Despite ongoing advancements, graft survival in vascularized host beds still represents a challenge, as CorNV contributes to suboptimal conditions for successful transplantation [17].

Both pharmacological and surgical strategies have recently been employed to mitigate the adverse impact of CorNV. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents may help reducing neovascular burden prior to PKP, while intraoperative approaches (such as the use of small or mushroom-shaped grafts) are employed to improve graft survival in high-risk corneal transplants [18–23]. Nevertheless, the long-term efficacy of these interventions remains controversial, and the prognostic significance of CorNV in contemporary patient cohorts continues to be a subject of debate. Moreover, it remains unclear whether characteristics such as the depth and extent stage of CorNV contribute to the pathological process, or whether additional cofactors are involved.

As a primary objective, this study aims to assess the long-term graft survival after PKP and to explore the prognostic role of CorNV, including its extent and depth. As per secondary aim, we will investigate long-term functional visual outcomes and the possible role of demographic, clinical, and eye-specific cofactors in conditioning graft failure.

Methods

This study followed a longitudinal retrospective observational design and was conducted at the Cornea and Ocular Surface Unit, Department of Ophthalmology, IRCCS San Raffaele Scientific Institute in Milan, Italy, from January 2003 and December 2024. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. According to our institutional policies and national regulations, formal ethics committee approval was not required for this type of retrospective non-interventional study. Patients undergoing PKP, regardless of the underlying etiology, were included in the study and categorized according to the presence of CorNV during the preoperative period. Patient demographics, including age, sex, and medical history before PKP (baseline) were collected. For each eye, the underlying pathology leading to PKP was documented. Baseline ophthalmological assessment included uncorrected and best-corrected visual acuity (UCVA and BCVA, respectively), anterior segment biomicroscopy, and dilated fundus examination. The presence of CorNV, along with its stage (defined by the number of quadrants involved) and depth (superficial, deep, or mixed), was documented [24, 25].

The extent of CorNV was assessed using a clock-hours-based approach to better characterize the circumferential distribution of corneal vessels across the 360° corneal surface. Importantly, we deliberately moved beyond a simple comparison between absence and presence of neovascularization, as all included eyes exhibited preoperative CorNV and the intrinsic aim of the study was to investigate whether its extent influenced graft survival. Severe and moderate CorNV were defined by neovascular involvement exceeding 6 and 3 clock hours, respectively, of the 360° corneal circumference. Conversely, mild CorNV was defined as neovascular involvement limited to ≤ 3 clock hours (≤ 1 quadrant). To ensure statistical robustness and maintain adequate events-per-variable ratios in the multivariate Cox model, the extent of CorNV was subsequently dichotomized into mild (≤ 3 clock hours) and moderate-to-severe (> 3 clock hours) categories, balancing clinical detail with methodological reliability.

Follow-up assessments were conducted at personalized intervals based on patient requirements after PKP, with a maximum interval of 6 months between visits. Postoperative management following PKP was standardized across all patients and did not differ according to underlying etiology or prior limbal stem cell transplantation; it consisted of topical dexamethasone administered four times daily with a gradual taper (initiated up to six times daily in selected cases during the early postoperative period), preservative-free lubricating drops in all patients, and a topical antibiotic administered during the first two postoperative weeks. Each follow-up examination included UCVA and BCVA measurement, anterior segment biomicroscopy, and endothelial cell count. In cases of graft failure, the last available endothelial cell count prior to failure was analyzed as a potential risk factor. In eyes without graft failure, the endothelial cell count at the last follow-up was recorded. Signs of graft rejections, if any, were noted, and topical steroid therapy was appropriately increased.

Primary outcome was determined by graft failure. Graft failure was defined as persistent corneal cloudiness or opacity, resulting in visual impairment, lasting for at least three consecutive months, in the absence of active rejection or any reversible causes [26–28]. We deliberately selected graft failure as the primary outcome because it is a dichotomous variable. In contrast, the number of rejection episodes represents a separate, continuous variable that was not included in the analysis, as it would have introduced unnecessary complexity to the statistical model, particularly given the relatively small sample size (n = 80).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were summarized as their mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as their median, interquartile range (IQR), and minimum–maximum range otherwise. Normal distribution of continuous variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were summarized as their absolute and relative prevalence.

Binary logistic regression models were used to examine the association of independent variables with binary variables. Each predictor was evaluated individually to estimate its effect on the odds of belonging to each outcome category relative to a reference category. Odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and p values were calculated to assess the strength and significance of these associations. Analyses were performed using the multinomial function from the nnet package in R.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was employed to estimate the survival probability of corneal grafts, and 95% CI have been provided. One-year follow-up comparison of UCVA and BCVA was explored through paired t test. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was utilized to identify demographic, clinical, and ocular factors associated with graft failure risk. To limit random variability and the potential for overfitting, variables showing a p value < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model [29]. Given the overall sample size and the number of graft failure events, a simplified modeling strategy was adopted to maintain statistical validity. For this reason, the extent of CorNV was dichotomized (mild vs. moderate-to-severe) rather than analyzed as four separate categories. This approach aimed to retain clinically meaningful stratification while ensuring reliable and interpretable estimates, minimizing the likelihood of overfitting and imprecise effect estimates. Hazard ratios (HR) and corresponding 95% CI were calculated to quantify the effects of these variables. All statistical analyses have been performed setting a threshold for statistical significance at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Baseline Evaluation

A total of 80 eyes from 80 patients (42 male, 53%; 38 female, 47%; mean age 55 ± 16 years, minimum 14 years, maximum 91 years; all patients were white) were included in the study. All eyes exhibited preoperative CorNV and underwent PKP between January 2003 and December 2024. Patient age ranged from 14 to 91 years, with 70% within the working-age population (18–64 years). Most common underlying pathologies were chemical or thermal burns (n = 33, 41%) and Acanthamoeba keratitis (n = 24, 30%). Among the 33 patients with burns, 29 presented with limbal stem cell deficiency, and all 29 (100%) had previously undergone limbal stem cell transplantation. All the underlying pathologies are included in Table 1. Preoperative mild CorNV was observed in 21 eyes (26%), while moderate and severe in 34 (43%) and 25 (31%) eyes, respectively. Superficial neovascularization was observed in 35 (43%) patients, deep neovascularization in 25 (31%), and a mixed phenotype was found in 20 (25%). Follow-up

Median follow-up was 51 months (IQR 25–76 months). Overall, graft failure occurred in 36 eyes during the follow-up period, with 8, 15, and 13 failures observed in the mild, moderate, and severe CorNV groups, respectively. Both 1-year UCVA and BCVA significantly improved compared to baseline evaluation (0.90 ± 0.50 logMAR vs 1.77 ± 0.73 logMAR, p < 0.001 and 0.71 ± 0.35 logMAR vs 0.32 ± 0.27 logMAR, p < 0.001, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 1). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that the median survival time was not reached during the observation period, as less than 50% of eyes experienced graft failure after 5 years. Graft Survival Analysis

As previously discussed, only the two most frequent underlying pathologies (chemical/thermal burns and Acanthamoeba keratitis) were included in multivariate survival analyses. In this subgroup analysis (n = 57), graft failure occurred in 23 eyes. Less common etiologies were excluded because of limited sample sizes [30].

Several associated variables have been examined using both univariate and multivariate analysis as potential risk or protective factors (Table 2). The presence of moderate or severe extent of neovascularization (coverage of more than 3 clock-hours of 360° of cornea, respectively) did not exhibit any significant increased risk of graft failure (HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.69–3.34; p = 0.31) (Fig. 2). The underlying indication for PKP was a significant determinant of graft prognosis. Specifically, multivariate analyses confirmed that a history of chemical or thermal burns was associated with a higher risk of graft failure compared to eyes with a history of Acanthamoeba (HR 11.02, 95% CI 1.22–99.45; p = 0.032). Of note, eyes with burns and history of Acanthamoeba keratitis had similar distribution in terms of extension of CorNV (mild CorNV in 7 [21%] and 8 [33%] eyes and moderate-to-severe CornNV in 26 [79%] and 16 [67%] eyes, respectively). No other significantly associated clinical factors were found. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of long-term graft survival and visual outcomes following PKP in a cohort of patients presenting with different extent of preoperative CorNV, focusing on the prognostic impact of severity of CorNV [9, 31, 32]. Importantly, we aimed to further characterize the risk by evaluating the depth of neovascular involvement, which yielded clinically significant insights into the pathophysiology of graft failure in vascularized host beds and provided better insight into PKP prognostication.

Visual outcome after PKP in our series was generally favorable, with both UCVA and BCVA showing significant improvement at 1 year postoperatively. The mean BCVA significantly improved from 0.71 ± 0.35 logMAR at baseline to 0.32 ± 0.27 logMAR at 12 months (p < 0.001), reflecting the surgical efficacy of PKP even in challenging vascularized beds [33, 34]. Despite the higher risk of immunological complications, remarkable functional gain remains achievable in well-selected patients. Moreover, previous studies have further investigated the impact of CorNV on BCVA, showing that severe CorNV is associated with lower visual acuity. It can be speculated that patients with severe CorNV, who present with poorer visual function, are more likely to undergo PKP due to both lower preoperative visual acuity and higher potential for postoperative improvement. However, these patients also represent a group at higher risk for graft failure [35].

The presented results from our cohort of patients are consistent with prior large-scale studies on high-risk PKP. Specifically, the Corneal Transplant Epidemiological Study reported a global 5-year survival rate of 83%, with lower survival rates in eyes with moderate-to-severe risk factors for rejection, including inflammation and vascularization [36]. Bachman et al. confirmed significantly reduced graft survival in eyes with pre-existing CorNV [9]. However, they reported poorer survival rates when multiple quadrants were involved [9, 32]. Similarly, Williams et al. reported higher graft failure rates in eyes with 360° CorNV compared to those with involvement of three or fewer quadrants [8]. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether the extent of CorNV truly is not a significant risk factor in this cohort, as suggested by our analysis, or whether our cohort, although numerically meaningful, was not sufficiently large to achieve statistical significance.

The underlying immunological mechanisms promoting this phenomenon have been largely explored and well depicted. CorNV promotes the breakdown of the cornea’s immune privilege by allowing the migration of antigen-presenting cells and effector T cells into the graft bed, thereby enhancing the likelihood of alloimmune activation and ultimately leading to graft rejection [10, 14, 37]. Our results suggest that CorNV may act as an epiphenomenon reflecting the type of underlying pathological process, rather than functioning as an isolated determinant of graft outcome. In this context, its prognostic impact appears to be largely driven by the underlying etiology and the specific pathogenic mechanisms involved, rather than by its clinical extent or depth alone. Accordingly, the mere presence of CorNV, rather than its stage or extent, emerges as a key factor predisposing to graft failure [9, 24, 25]. This observation likely reflects the heterogeneity of CorNV, whose biological behavior and impact on graft survival vary according to the underlying disease processes driving its development. Therefore, considering CorNV as a uniform risk factor for graft failure may be an oversimplification, as its characteristics and prognostic implications are closely tied to its etiology.

Thus, it is important not to focus solely on the presence and extent of CorNV but also to consider the underlying etiology and pathogenic mechanisms that have led to CorNV formation, as these may profoundly influence the inflammatory milieu and graft prognosis. Interestingly, eyes with a history of chemical or thermal burns demonstrated a worse graft prognosis compared to those eyes with CorNV secondary to Acanthamoeba keratitis (HR 11.02, 95% CI 1.22–99.45; p = 0.032). This suggests that different mechanisms may drive the development and progression of CorNV, and that the underlying etiology may influence the pathophysiological processes involved in graft rejection. Specifically, CorNV may act as a surrogate marker of disease severity rather than an independent biological determinant of graft failure. This limitation is particularly relevant in eyes with chemical burns and limbal stem cell deficiency, where chronic inflammation, ocular surface instability, and neovascularization coexist and are difficult to disentangle statistically. In particular, the risk associated with CorNV may differ between eyes with a resolved or self-limited insult (such as infectious keratitis) and those with chronic ocular surface disease. In the latter, including conditions such as limbal stem cell deficiency, cicatricial disorders, herpetic disease or severe dry eye, persistent inflammation and surface instability may play a major role in graft failure, potentially outweighing the contribution of neovascularization itself. For instance, HSV is one of the primary infectious causes of CorNV. The impact of HSV on the cornea is extremely well documented, and it is known that prior HSV infection can adversely affect graft survival following PKP [38, 39]. This is likely due to the virus-induced inflammatory response, which promotes neovascularization and increases the risk of immune-mediated graft rejection. This highlights that understanding the heterogeneity beyond the etiology of CorNV is essential for graft outcomes prognostication.

Additionally, the depth of CorNV did not emerge as a significant factor in our study, despite existing evidence suggesting that deep CorNV can enhance alloimmune activation and elevate the risk of graft rejection. This discrepancy may be attributed to the complexity of the statistical model and the relatively small sample size, which may have limited our ability to detect this association.

Recent advances in corneal angiography, such as optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) and its routine use in daily clinical practice, may enable a standardized quantification of CorNV in future studies. The ability to objectively assess vessel extension, flow, and density could improve future research quality, refine risk stratification, and guide personalized treatment planning [25, 40].

The relatively long follow-up period empowers the clinical impact of the presented study. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, including its retrospective observational design, the modest sample size with a relatively small number of events per group, and the absence of a control group without CorNV. Furthermore, it should be noted that the two predominant etiologies in our cohort (Acanthamoeba keratitis and corneal burns) represent relatively uncommon indications for PKP in the broader clinical population. The heterogeneity in baseline etiologies and the choice of dichotomization boundaries of CorNV in our cohort represents the main limitation of the study and warrants consideration. To reduce heterogeneity, we included only large sample sizes in the regression analyses. However, this approach consequently limited our analyses to comparisons between eyes with Acanthamoeba keratitis and those with ocular burns. Additionally, the wide confidence intervals observed for some estimates suggest limited precision and warrant cautious interpretation. The underlying pathology may also influence graft outcomes through independent inflammatory or structural mechanisms. For example, eyes with a history of chemical injury may exhibit chronic inflammation or limbal stem cell deficiency, both of which can compound the risk of graft failure. Although only eyes that underwent limbal stem cell transplantation for limbal stem cell deficiency were included, the effect of etiology may still be attributable to differences in the baseline severity of limbal stem cell deficiency. Moreover, the diverse underlying conditions are associated with neovascularization driven by different pathogenic mechanisms, implying that the resulting neovessels may differ in their biological behavior and, consequently, play distinct prognostic roles [34, 41]. Although we clinically stratified CorNV by stage and depth, quantification relied on slit-lamp examination and clinical judgment, which may be less precise than imaging-based assessments or CorNV area quantification through slit-lamp photographs [42]. Furthermore, focusing solely on graft failure as the primary outcome, without accounting for the number of rejection episodes, represents a statistical limitation that may have led to the loss of potentially relevant prognostic information.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that, in our cohort of patients with CorNV of varying severity, the extent of CorNV is not significantly associated with graft failure or immune-mediated rejection. These findings suggest that the mere presence of neovascularization, rather than its stage or extent, may be the key determinant of graft outcome. Nevertheless, the etiology and pathophysiological mechanisms underlying vessel formation should also be considered, as they may critically influence postoperative inflammation and graft survival. Future multicenter studies and standardized imaging are needed to clarify additional prognostic factors and further improve management.
