Adaptive finite element methods based on flux and stress equilibration using FEniCSx
Maximilian Brodbeck \orcidID 0000-0002-1792-7318, Fleurianne Bertrand \orcidID 0000-0001-5111-5105 and Tim Ricken \orcidID 0000-0001-8515-5009

Abstract
This contribution shows how a-posteriori error estimators based on equilibrated fluxes – H(div) functions fulfilling the underlying conservation law – can be implemented in FEniCSx. Therefore, dolfinx_eqlb is introduced, its algorithmic structure is described and classical benchmarks for adaptive solution procedures for the Poisson problem and linear elasticity are presented.

Introduction

The accurate resolution of physical quantities in numerical simulations is of significant importance across various fields of engineering and applied sciences. Finite element methods have been extensively employed, and convergence and optimal a priori error estimates have been proven.
Various software packages have been proposed, while the latest trend – followed e.g. by FEniCSx
FEniCSx_2023
– focuses on abstraction, generality and automation without lousing computational efficiency.
It is well known that on general domains, numerical solutions often lack the regularity, required to directly apply a priori estimates.
To maintain optimal convergence, adaptive procedures based on a posteriori error estimates, combined with local mesh refinement, have been developed.
FEniCSx is currently well-suited for handling residual-based error estimators, making it a valuable tool for many adaptive finite element methods.
However, another important class of a posteriori error estimation involves the equilibration of flux or stress, leading to guaranteed, fully localized, and easily computable upper bounds on the error, especially in the energy norm.
Rooted in the hypercircle identity of Prager and Synge
Prager_Equilibartion_1947
, the Poisson problem is discussed in works such as
Braess_EqlbFluxes_2008
,
Cai_SemiexplzEqlb_2012
,
Ern_FluxEqlb_2015
or
Bertrand_Hypercircle_2020
while applications to liner elasticity can be found e.g. in
Bertrand_EqlbElast_2021
.
Even tough this method is powerful, a direct implementation into FEniCSx is challenging and currently not available in literature.

This gap is addressed by introducing dolfinx_eqlb, a library for the efficient computation of equilibrated fluxes and stresses.
Error estimates for the Poisson problem and linear elasticity alongside with requirements on the equilibration process are reviewed in section
2
.
The basic algorithmic structure of the software is presented in section
3
, while this contribution is concluded by discussing two benchmarks for adaptive finite element methods in section
4
.

2
A-posteriori error estimation based on equilibrated fluxes

2.1
Equilibration with full gradient

Equilibration in presence of the full gradient starts from the Poisson problem in first order form

\nabla\cdot\bm{\varsigma}(\mathrm{u})=\mathrm{f}\;\text{in}\;\Omega\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\varsigma}(\mathrm{u}):=-\kappa\,\grad\mathrm{u}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{cases}\mathrm{u}=0&\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\\
\bm{\varsigma}(\mathrm{u})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{n}}=0&\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}\\
\end{cases}\;,

(1)

as it is discussed e.g. in
Braess_EqlbFluxes_2008
or
Ern_FluxEqlb_2015
. For any
\mathrm{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
, the weak solution
\mathrm{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)
satisfies

\left(\bm{\varsigma}(\mathrm{u}),\>\grad\mathrm{v}\right)=\left(\mathrm{f},\>\mathrm{v}\right)\quad\text{for all}\quad\mathrm{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)\;.

(2)

Defining the space
\mathrm{V}_{k}:=\left\{\mathrm{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}\left(\Omega\right):\,\mathrm{v}_{h}|_{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(\mathrm{T})\right\}
,
\mathrm{u}
can be approximated in
\mathrm{V}_{\Gamma,k}:=\mathrm{V}_{k}\cap\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)
.
For further deviations, the equilibrated flux is introduced:

Definition 1

An equilibrated flux is a function
\bm{\varsigma}^{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathrm{H}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\left(\mathrm{div},\,\Omega\right)

fulfilling

\nabla\cdot\bm{\varsigma}^{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{f}\;\text{in }\;\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\varsigma}^{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{n}}=0\;\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}\ .

(3)

For any arbitrary approximation
\mathrm{u}_{h}\in\mathrm{V}_{\Gamma,k}
, any equilibrated flux
\bm{\varsigma}^{\mathrm{R}}
and the differences
\delta\bm{\varsigma}=\bm{\varsigma}(\mathrm{u})-\bm{\varsigma}^{\mathrm{R}}
respectively
\delta\mathrm{u}=\mathrm{u}-\mathrm{u}_{h}
, the so called Prager-Synge identity

\left(\delta\bm{\varsigma},\>\grad\delta\mathrm{u}\right)=\left\langle\delta\bm{\varsigma}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{n}},\>\delta\mathrm{u}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega}-\left(\nabla\cdot\delta\bm{\varsigma},\>\delta\mathrm{u}\right)=0\;,

(4)

is satisfied. Based thereon, the following error estimate holds:

be constant,
\mathrm{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)

be arbitrary and

2.2
Equilibration with weakly symmetric gradient

This section introduces the equilibration procedure when a symmetric gradient
\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})=\mathrm{sym}\>\grad\bm{\mathrm{u}}
is present.
Based on the linearised Piola-Kirchoff stress
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})=2\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})+\tilde{\lambda}\,\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathrm{u}}\,\bm{\mathrm{I}}
, the balance of linear momentum reads

\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})=-\bm{\mathrm{f}}\;\text{in}\;\Omega\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\mathrm{u}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}\;\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{n}}=\bm{\mathrm{t}}\;\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}\;.

(7)

For any
\bm{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
, the weak solution
\bm{\mathrm{u}}\in\left(\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)\right)^{2}
fulfills

\left(\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}}),\>\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathrm{v}})\right)=\left(\bm{\mathrm{f}},\>\bm{\mathrm{v}}\right)-\left\langle\bm{\mathrm{t}},\>\bm{\mathrm{v}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{\mathrm{v}}\in\left(\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)\right)^{2}\;.

(8)

Considering the symmetry of the stress tensor in a weak sense (see
Bertrand_EqlbElast_2021
), the following definition of the equilibrated stress tensor is introduced:

Definition 2

An equilibrated stress is a function
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\in\left(\mathrm{RT}_{m}\right)^{d}

fulfilling

\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}=-\Pi_{m-1}\,\bm{\mathrm{f}}\;\text{on}\;\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{n}}=\bm{\mathrm{t}}\;\text{on}\;\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}

(9)

and the weak symmetry condition

\left(\sigma^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\big|_{12}-\sigma^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\big|_{21},\>\gamma_{h}\right)=0\quad\text{for all}\quad\gamma_{h}\in\mathrm{V}_{1}\;.

(10)

Let further error- and
\mathcal{A}
-norm be defined by

|||\bullet|||^{2}=\left|\left|\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\bullet\right)\right|\right|^{2}+\tilde{\lambda}\,\left|\left|\nabla\cdot\left(\bullet\right)\right|\right|^{2}\,,

(11)

respectively

\left|\left|\left(\bullet\right)\right|\right|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}=\left(\left(\bullet\right),\>\mathcal{A}\left(\bullet\right)\right)\;\text{with}\;\mathcal{A}\left(\bullet\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\bullet\right)-\frac{\tilde{\lambda}}{2\,(1+\tilde{\lambda})}\mathrm{tr}\>\left(\bullet\right)\,\bm{\mathrm{I}}\right]\,,

(12)

the following error estimate holds:

Theorem 2.2

Let
\bm{\mathrm{u}}\in\left(\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}\left(\Omega\right)\right)^{2}
be the solution of (
8
),
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h}\in\left(\mathrm{V}_{\Gamma,k}\right)^{d}
be arbitrary, and
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}
following Definition
10

\bm{\mathrm{u}}-\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h}
by
\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}

|||\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}|||^{2}\leq\left|\left|\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h})\right|\right|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}+C_{K}\sum_{\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left[\left|\left|\mathrm{skw}\>\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{T}}+C_{P}\left|\left|\bm{\mathrm{f}}+\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{2}\;.

(13)

Proof

Evaluating the
\mathcal{A}
-norm of the
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h})
yields

\left|\left|\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h})\right|\right|_{\mathcal{A}}\geq|||\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}|||^{2}+\left|\left|\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right|\right|^{2}-2\,\left(\delta\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}},\>\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right)\;,

(14)

where
\delta\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}
denotes the difference between true- and equilibrated stress
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\mathrm{u}})-\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}

Integration by parts under consideration of the symmetry of the true stress and the equilibration conditions (
9
) allows a reformulation of the mixed term:

\left(\delta\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}},\>\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right)=\left(\bm{\mathrm{f}}+\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h},\>\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)+\left(\mathrm{skw}\>\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h},\>\grad\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)

(15)

Based on the weak symmetry

\left(\delta\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}},\>\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right)\leq C_{K}\sum_{\mathrm{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left[\left|\left|\mathrm{skw}\>\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{T}}+C_{P}\left|\left|\bm{\mathrm{f}}+\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}_{h}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{2}+\left|\left|\bm{\varepsilon}\left(\delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}\right)\right|\right|\;.

(16)

Inserting (
16

4.1
The Poisson equation

As a first example, a rectangular domain with discontinuous coefficients
\kappa
in each of the four quadrants is considered.
Two sets of parameters
\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{4}=1
and
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{3}
with either
\kappa_{1}=5
(20 refinement levels) or
\kappa_{1}=100
(40 refinement levels) are tested.
Dirichlet BCs on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}=\partial\Omega
are prescribed based on the analytical solution
\mathrm{u}_{\mathrm{ext}}
from
Riviere_ApostPoissonCoeffDG_2003
.
Meshes are refined based on a Dörfler marking strategy with
\theta=0.5
.

Convergence orders (e.o.c) and efficiency indices after the final refinement step are reported in Fig.
2(a)
, while the final meshes for first- and second-order approximations of the case
\kappa_{1}=100
are shown in Fig.
2(b)
and
2(c)
.
The solutions are in good agreement with expectations from literature.
Meshes are refined around the singularity in the center of the domain and the convergence rates are
\approx-0.5
for
\mathrm{u}_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\approx-1
for
\mathrm{u}_{h}\in\mathrm{P}_{2}
.
For
\kappa_{1}=100
a convergence rate
>1
indicates a pre-asymptotic state of convergence.
As further refinement would lead to cells with a characteristic length close to machine precision, the refinement has nevertheless been terminated.
The efficiency of the error estimate (
6
) depends on the degree
m
of the equilibrated flux.
Choosing
m=k+1
yields efficiency indices close to one for
\kappa_{1}=5
and between 1.2 and 1.4 for
\kappa_{1}=100
.
For
m=k
they are slightly worse.

Up to this point only the accuracy of the error estimates has been compared.
Within the following the focus will be on the total solution time
t_{\mathrm{tot}}
, which is made up from
t_{\mathrm{prime}}
– the time for assembly and solution, using PETSc and MUMPS, of the primal problem – and
t_{\mathrm{eqlb}}
, the time for performing the equilibration.
Comparing in a first step the relative equilibration costs
t_{\mathrm{eqlb}}/t_{\mathrm{tot}}
for the Cooks membrane with fixed meshes in Fig.
4(a)
,
t_{\mathrm{eqlb}}
is – for a sufficient size of the primal problem – considerably smaller than
t_{\mathrm{prime}}
.
Increasing
k
increases the effort required for equilibration, while using an equilibration with
m=k+1
is significantly more expensive than the respective lowest order case
m=k
. Similar timings for the adaptive solution of the Cooks membrane (timings are accumulated until
|||\bm{\mathrm{u}}-\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{h}|||\leq 10^{-3}
) are summarised in Fig.
4(b)
.
While for the cases
k=m=2
the total solution time is dominated by the solution of the primal problem, this trend is reversed for more accurate (guarantied) estimate with
k=m-1=2
.
Even though having the fewest primal degrees of freedom, the entire solution time is the highest due to the high computational costs for the equilibration.
Using primal approximations based on
k=3
reduces the overall computation time, but leading to a significant share of the equilibration in the total computation time.
An effect, amplified by the small sizes of the primal problems.
As for
k=2
the heuristic indicator (
24
) with
m=k
performs best, while the higher order estimate with
m=k+1
is outperformed.
Clearly these results will have to be reevaluated in a parallel context – which is beyond the current scope – are also for primal problems of larger size.

5
Conclusions

Within this contribution dolfinx_eqlb, a FEniCSx based library for the efficient equilibration of fluxes and stresses, has been introduced.
Throughout characteristic examples for the Poisson problem and linear elasticity the efficiency of the resulting error estimates were shown.
An computationally efficient, but heuristic error indicator for elasticity is introduced, neglecting the asymmetry of the equilibrated stress.
Within our future work we intend to quantify computational efficiency of the presented implementation on problems of more realistic sizes.
We further intend to generalise the implementation for 3D domains and transient problems like e.g. poroelasticity.
