Weakly-unambiguous Parikh automata and their link to holonomic series
Alin Bostan, Arnaud Carayol, Florent Koechlin, Cyril Nicaud

Abstract
We investigate the connection between properties of formal languages and properties of their generating series, with a focus on the class of holonomic power series. We first prove a strong version of a conjecture by Castiglione and Massazza: weakly-unambiguous Parikh automata are equivalent to unambiguous two-way reversal bounded counter machines, and their multivariate generating series are holonomic. We then show that the converse is not true: we construct a language whose generating series is algebraic (thus holonomic), but which is inherently weakly-ambiguous as a Parikh automata language. Finally, we prove an effective decidability result for the inclusion problem for weakly-unambiguous Parikh automata, and provide an upper-bound on to its complexity.

Introduction

This article investigates the link between
holonomic
(or
D-finite
) power series and formal languages. We consider the classical setting in which this connection is established via the generating series
L(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\ell_{n}x^{n}
counting the number
\ell_{n}
of words of length
n
in a given language
\mathcal{L}
.

On the languages side, the Chomsky–Schützenberger hierarchy
[
15
]
regroups languages in classes of increasing complexity: regular, context-free, context-sensitive and recursively enumerable. For power series, a similar hierarchy exists, consisting of the rational, algebraic and holonomic series. The first two levels of each hierarchy share a strong connection,
as the generating series of a regular (resp. unambiguous context-free) language is a rational (resp. algebraic) power series.

This connection has borne fruits both in formal language theory and in combinatorics. In combinatorics, finite automata and unambiguous grammars are routinely used to establish rationality and algebraicity of particular power series. In formal languages, this connection was (implicitly) used to give polynomial-time algorithms for the inclusion and universality tests for unambiguous finite automata
[
43
]
. In
[
22
]
, Flajolet uses the connection between unambiguous context-free grammars and algebraic series to prove the inherent ambiguity of certain context-free languages, solving several conjectures with this tool. Using analytic criteria on the series (for instance, the existence of infinitely many singularities), he establishes that the series of these context-free languages is not algebraic. Hence these languages cannot be described by unambiguous context-free grammars and are therefore inherently ambiguous.

In this article we propose to extend the connection to holonomic series. Holonomic series enjoy non-trivial closure properties whose algorithmic counterparts are actively studied in computer algebra. Our aim is to show that these advances can be leveraged to obtain non-trivial results in the formal languages and verification worlds.
The work of extending the connection was already initiated by Massazza in
[
33
]
, where he introduces two families of languages, named RCM and LCL, whose generating series are holonomic. These classes are, however, not captured by well-known models of automata, and this limits their appeal. Recently, Castiglione and Massazza addressed this issue and conjectured that RCM contains the languages accepted by deterministic one-way reversal bounded machines (RBCM for short)
[
12
]
; Massazza proved the result for RBCM for two subclasses of one-way deterministic RBCM
[
34
,
35
]
.
This conjecture hints that the class RCM is related to models of automata such as RBCM, which are used in program verification.

Our first contribution is to prove a stronger version of this conjecture. We show that RCM and LCL respectively correspond to the languages accepted by weakly-unambiguous

version of Parikh automata (PA, for short)

and pushdown Parikh automata. In terms of RBCM, these classes correspond to unambiguous two-way RBCM and unambiguous one-way RBCM enriched with a stack. Parikh automata are also commonly used in program verification. In view of the literature, these results might seem expected but they still require a careful adaptation of the standard techniques in the absence of a stack and become even more involved when a stack is added.

After having established the relevance of the classes of languages under study, we provide two consequences of the holonomicity of their associated generating series.

The first consequence follows Flajolet’s approach mentioned previously and gives criteria to establish the inherent weak-ambiguity for languages accepted by PA or pushdown PA, by proving that their generating series are not holonomic. These criteria are sufficient but not necessary; this is not surprising as the inherent ambiguity is undecidable for languages accepted by PA. Yet, the resulting method captures non-trivial examples with quite short and elegant proofs. In contrast, we give an example of inherently weakly-ambiguous PA language having a holonomic series (and therefore not amenable to the analytic method) for which we prove inherent weak-ambiguity
by hand
. The proof is quite involved but shows the inherent ambiguity of this language for a much larger class of automata (i.e., PA whose semi-linear sets are replaced by arbitrary recursive sets).

The second consequence is of an algorithmic nature. We focus on the inclusion

Primer on holonomic power series in several variables

In this section, we introduce power series in several variables and the classes of rational, algebraic and holonomic power series. We recall the connection with regular and context-free languages via the notions of generating series in one or several variables.

Let
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
be the ring of polynomials in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}

\mathbb{Q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

The
generating series of a sequence

.
In this article, we only consider power series whose coefficients belong to the field
\mathbb{Q}
.
The set of such
k

\mathbb{Q}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]]
.
Power series are naturally equipped with a sum and a product which generalize those of polynomials, for which
\mathbb{Q}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]]
is a ring.
We use the bracket notation for the coefficient extraction:
[x_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots x_{k}^{n_{k}}]A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=a(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
.
The
support

is the set of

The inverse

of a series

For instance, the inverse of

\frac{1}{1-x_{1}x_{2}^{2}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}x_{1}^{n}x_{2}^{2n}

The

. Similarly the

\mathcal{L}
such that

,
|w|_{a_{2}}=n_{2}
, …, and
|w|_{a_{k}}=n_{k}

denotes the number of occurrences of

Observe that the univariate generating series of a language is exactly

.

The generating series of the language recognized by a weakly-unambiguous Parikh automaton is holonomic.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a weakly-unambiguous PA with a constraint set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
. We first prove that its weighted series

is holonomic. As holonomic series are closed under Hadamard product (see Theorem
8
), it suffices to express

as the Hadamard product of two rational series
\overline{A}
and
\overline{C}
in the variables
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}
.

The first series
\overline{A}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
is such that for all
n,i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\geq 0

[x^{n}y_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots y_{d}^{i_{d}}]\overline{A}
counts the number of runs of
\mathcal{A}
starting in

, ending in a final state and labeled with a word of length
n
and the vector
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
. Note that we do not require that

belongs to
C
. As this series simply counts the number of runs in an automaton, its rationality is proved via the standard translation of the automaton into a linear system of equations.

For the second series, we take
\overline{C}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}):=\frac{1}{1-x}C^{\prime}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
where

is the support series of

, which is rational (see
[
17
,
26
]
). A direct computation yields that for all

is equal to
1
if

belongs to

and

The univariate series

of
\mathcal{A}

5
Algorithmic consequence of holonomicity

Generating series of languages have already been used to obtain efficient
algorithms on unambiguous models of automata. For instance, they were used by
Stearns and Hunt as a basic tool to obtain bounds on the length of a word
witnessing the non-inclusion between two unambiguous word
automata
[
43
]
. More precisely, the proof in
[
43
]

relies on the recurrence equation satisfied by the coefficients of the
generating series (which is guaranteed to exist by holonomicity in one
variable). In the rational case, this recurrence relation can be derived from
the automaton and does not require advanced results on holonomic series. In
this section, our aim is to obtain a similar bound for the inclusion problem
for weakly-unambiguous Parikh automata. The inclusion problem for RCM (and hence for weakly-unambiguous PA) is shown to be
decidable in
[
12
]
but no complexity bound is provided. Note that this problem is known to be
undecidable for non-deterministic PA
[
25
]
. We follow the same approach as
for the rational case
[
43
]
and for RCM
[
12
]
. In
stark contrast with the rational case, it is necessary to closely inspect
holonomic closure properties in order to give concrete bounds.

Fix
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
two weakly-unambiguous PA. We can construct a weakly-unambiguous PA
\mathcal{C}
accepting
L(\mathcal{A})\cap L(\mathcal{B})
. We rely on the key fact that the series

counts the number of words of length
n
in
L(\mathcal{A})\setminus L(\mathcal{B})
. In particular,

if and only if

.

As

is the difference of two holonomic series, it is holonomic. As equality between holonomic series is decidable,
[
12
]
concludes that
the problem is decidable. But without further analysis, no complexity upper-bound can be derived. The coefficients of

satisfy a recurrence equation of the form

for

with

.
This equation fully determines

in terms of its

,

provided that

, then

.
Consequently, if

is equal to

for all

where

denotes the largest positive root of

(which is bounded from above by its

.
It is clear that the coefficients

.

Taking

Remark 25
.

In
[
12
]
, the author proposes a different
construction to prove the holonomicity of the generating series of languages
in RCM. This proof uses the closure of holonomic series under Hadamard product
and algebraic substitution

. It is natural to
wonder if this approach would lead to better bounds in
Proposition
22
(using the equivalence between
weakly-unambiguous PA and RCM). It turns out that the operation

is more complicated than it seems at a first glance. Indeed,
to our knowledge, no proof of the closure under algebraic substitution
explains what happens if, during the substitution process, the equations
become trivial. This issue can be overcome by doing the substitution step by
step:
x_{2}=x_{1}
, then
x_{3}=x_{1}
, etc. However, this naive approach would
produce worse bounds.

Perspectives

The bounds obtained in Section
5
are derived directly from constructions given in the proofs of the closure properties. In particular, we did not use any information on the special form of our series. The bounds are certainly perfectible using more advanced tools from computer algebra. Also it seems that the complexity of the closure under the algebraic substitution deserves more investigation, as discussed in Remark
25
.

A more ambitious perspective is to find larger classes of automata whose generating series are holonomic. This would certainly require new ideas, as for instance any holonomic power series with coefficients in
\{0,1\}
is known to be the characteristic series of some semilinear
[
3
]
.

B.4.2
Generalized
\PA

We consider a variant of Parikh automata, called
generalized Parikh automata
(generalized PA for short), in which transitions are no longer labeled by a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
but by a semilinear set in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. As a consequence, a run
\pi
of a generalized PA with is labeled by a couple
(w,S)
, where
w
is a word and
S
is a semilinear equal to the sum of all the semilinear sets labelling the transitions of
\pi
. A run
\pi
labeled by
(w,S)
is accepting if it starts in the initial state and ends in a final state and
S\cap C\neq\emptyset
where
C
is the semilinear constraint of the generalized PA.

The model of generalized PA is implicitly used in work of
[
29
,
30
]
.

Definition 29

(generalized Parikh automaton)
.

A generalized Parikh automaton
\mathcal{A}
is given by a tuple

(\Sigma,Q,q_{I},F,C,\Delta)
where
\Sigma
is the input alphabet,
Q
is the finite set of states,
q_{I}\in Q
is the initial state,
F \subseteq Q
is the set of final states,
C
is a semilinear set in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
\Delta
is the set of transitions of the form
p\xrightarrow{a,S}q
with
p
and
q\in Q
,
a\in\Sigma
and
S
a semilinear set in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

A run
p_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0},S_{0}}p_{1}\cdots p_{n-1}\xrightarrow{a_{n-1},S_{n-1}}p_{n}

is labeled by
(w,S)
with
w=a_{0}\cdots a_{n-1}
,
S=S_{0}+\cdots+S_{n-1}
. By convention, a run
\pi=q
is labeled by
(\varepsilon,\{\bm{0}\})
.

It is accepting if it starts in the initial state (i.e.,
p_{0}=q_{I}
) and ends in an accepting state (i.e.,
p_{n}\in F
) and
S\cap C\neq\emptyset
.

A generalized
\PA
is called (weakly)-unambiguous if every word has at most one accepting run.

A
\PA
can be seen as a generalized
\PA
by replacing every transition
(p,(a,\bm{v}),q)
with
(p,(a,\{\bm{v}\}),q)
.
This transformation preserves both the accepted language and weak-unambiguity. We will give in Proposition
31
a converse translation from generalized
\PA
to
\PA
which preserves weak-unambiguity.

For this, we need a technical lemma on semilinear sets.
For a subset
L
of
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we define
L^{r}
by
L^{0}=\{\bm{0}\}
and
L^{r+1}=L^{r}+L
for
r\geq 0
. In other terms,
L^{r}
contains the vectors that can be obtained as the sum of
r
vectors in
L
.

Lemma 30

(
[
29
]
)
.

For a semilinear set
S
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the relation
\bm{v} \in S^{r}
is semilinear, meaning that there exists a Presburger formula
\varphi(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
such that for all
r,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}
and
v_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi[r,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]
holds if and only if
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
belongs to
S^{r}
.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
S=\bm{c}+P^{*}
is a linear set. For all
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
r>0
, we have
\bm{v}\in S^{r}
if and only if
\bm{v}-r\bm{c}\in P^{*}
. This is due to the fact that
(P^{*})^{r}=P^{*}
when
r>0
. Hence the formula
\varphi_{S}
, given below, defines the relation
\bm{v}\in S^{r}
.

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\varphi_{S}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})&:=&x=0\rightarrow v_{1}=\cdots=v_{d}=0\\
&&\wedge\;x\neq 0\rightarrow\varphi_{P}(v_{1}-c_{1}x,\ldots,v_{d}-c_{d}x)\\
\end{array}

where
\varphi_{P}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is a Presburger formula defining
P
. Remark that the multiplication by is
c_{i}
allowed as the vector
\bm{c}
is a constant.

Let us now handle the general case where
S
is semilinear, of the form
S=\cup_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
where the
S_{i}
’s are linear sets. We define:

\displaystyle\varphi_{S}(r,\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\exists\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n},\ \exists\bm{z_{1}},\ldots,\bm{z_{n}},

\displaystyle\phantom{\wedge}\ \ \ell_{1}+\ldots+\ell_{n}=r\wedge\bm{v}=\bm{z_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{z_{n}}

\displaystyle\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{S_{i}}(\ell_{i},\bm{z_{i}})

where
\bm{z_{i}}
designates the vector of
d
variables
(z_{i,1},\ldots,z_{i,d})
.

Let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\bm{v}\in S^{r}
.
If
r=0
then
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
and
\varphi_{S}[\bm{v},r]
holds.
Otherwise,
r>0
and
\bm{v}
is the sum of
r
vectors from
S
: we can write
\bm{v}=\bm{v_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}
, where
\bm{v_{j}}\in S
for
1\leq j\leq r
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let’s denote
D_{i}
the disjoint sets of vectors among
\bm{v_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v_{r}}
that belong to
L_{i}
, and don’t belong to
L_{k}
for
k<i
. Reordering the sum
\bm{v}=\bm{v_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}
by grouping the vectors according to the sets
D_{i}
, we can rewrite it into
\bm{v}=\bm{z_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{z_{n}}
, where
\bm{z_{i}}
is the sum of every vectors in
D_{i}
(and is the null vector if
D_{i}
is empty). Let’s denote
\ell_{i}=|D_{i}|
. Then
\bm{z_{i}}\in L_{i}^{\ell_{i}}
and
\ell_{1}+\ldots+\ell_{n}=r
. So
\varphi_{S}[r,\bm{v}]
is true.

Reciprocally, let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
satisfying
\varphi_{S}
.
Then we can find
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\ell_{1}+\ldots+\ell_{n}=r
, and vectors
\bm{z_{1}},\ldots,\bm{z_{n}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\bm{v}=\bm{z_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{z_{n}}
and
\bm{z_{i}}\in L_{i}^{\ell_{i}}\subseteq S^{\ell_{i}}
.

If
r=0
, then every
\ell_{i}
equals
0
, so
\bm{z_{i}}=\bm{0}
for every
i
. Then
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
, and consequently
\bm{v}\in S^{r}
.

If
r>0
, then some
\ell_{i}
are not null. The equality
\bm{v}=\bm{z_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{z_{n}}
can be rewritten as
\bm{v}=\bm{v_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}
, where
\bm{v}_{i}\in S
. So
\bm{v}\in S^{r}
.

∎

We can now provide a translation from generalized
\PA
to
\PA
which preserves weak-unambiguity.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
a generalized
\PA
(resp. weakly-unambiguous generalized
\PA
), the language
L(\mathcal{A})
is recognized by a PA (resp. weakly-unambiguous
\PA
)
\mathcal{B}
with the same number of states and whose dimension is equal to the number of different semilinear sets appearing in the transitions of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Fix a generalized
\PA

\mathcal{A}=(Q,q_{0},F,\Sigma,\Delta,S)
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
be an enumeration of the different semilinear sets appearing in the transitions of
\mathcal{A}
.

We construct a
\PA

\mathcal{B}=(Q,q_{0},F,\Sigma,\Delta^{\prime},S^{\prime})
of dimension
r
accepting
L(\mathcal{A})
. The automaton
\mathcal{B}
shares the same structure as
\mathcal{A}
(i.e., the same set of states, initial state, final states) and only differs by the transitions and semilinear constraint. For every transitions
p\xrightarrow{a,S_{i}}q
of
\mathcal{A}
, there is a
transition
p\xrightarrow{a,\bm{e_{i}}}q
, where
\bm{e_{i}}
is the vector that has a
1
in its
i
th coordinate,
0
everywhere else. The semilinear constraint is
S^{\prime}:=\{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\ :\ \exists\bm{v}\in S\cap\sum_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}^{n_{i}}\}
. It is indeed semilinear, since it is recognized by the formula:

\varphi_{S^{\prime}}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=\exists\bm{v_{1}},\ldots,\bm{v_{r}},\ \bm{v_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}\in S\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{i=1}^{r}\bm{v_{i}}\in S_{i}^{n_{i}}

where
\bm{v_{i}}\in S_{i}^{n_{i}}
is syntactic sugar for the formula constructed in Lemma
30
.

We denote by
\varphi
the transformation taking a run
\pi
of
\mathcal{A}
and translating it into a run of
\mathcal{B}
by replacing every transition in
\Delta
by its associated transition in
\Delta^{\prime}
. The mapping
\varphi
is a bijection between the runs of
\mathcal{A}
and the runs of
\mathcal{B}
preserving the states and the input word.

To show that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
accept the same languages, we are going to prove that for every run
\pi
of
\mathcal{A}
,
\pi
is accepting for
\mathcal{A}
if and only if
\varphi(\pi)
is accepting for
\mathcal{B}
. In particular, as
\varphi
is a bijection preserving the input word, this implies both that
L(\mathcal{A})=L(\mathcal{B})
and that if
\mathcal{A}
is weakly unambiguous then so is
\mathcal{B}
.

Let
\pi
be an accepting run in
\mathcal{A}
labeled by
(w,S_{\pi})
. Let
m_{i}
denote the number of occurrences of
S_{i}
in the transitions of
\pi
, for
1\leq i\leq r
and let
\bm{m}
denote the vector
(m_{i})_{i\in[1,r]}
. By definition, the semilinear set
S_{\pi}
labelling the run
\pi
is
S_{\pi}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}^{m_{i}}
. The run
\varphi(\pi)
of
\mathcal{B}
is labeled by
(w,\bm{m})
and starts in the initial state and ends in a final state. To show that
\varphi(\pi)
is accepting for
\mathcal{B}
, we need to show that
\bm{m}\in S^{\prime}
. By definition of
S^{\prime}
, this is equivalent to
S\cap\sum_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}^{m_{i}}=S\cap S_{\pi}\neq\emptyset
which holds as
\pi
is accepting for
\mathcal{A}
.

Let
\pi^{\prime}
be an accepting run in
\mathcal{B}
labeled by
(w,\bm{m})
, with
\bm{m}=(m_{i})_{i}\in S^{\prime}
. Then
\pi=\varphi^{-1}(\pi^{\prime})
is a run in
\mathcal{A}
labeled by
(w,S_{\pi})
and starting in the initial state and ending in a final state. Furthermore
m_{i}
is the number of occurrences of the semilinear set
S_{i}
in the transition of
\pi
and consequently
S_{\pi}=\sum_{i}S_{i}^{m_{i}}
. To show that
\pi
is accepting for
w
, we need to prove that
S_{\pi}\cap S\not=\emptyset
. By definition, as
\bm{m}
belongs to
S^{\prime}
,
\sum_{i}S_{i}^{m_{i}}\cap S=S_{\pi}\cap S\neq\emptyset
.
∎

B.5
\PA
with
\varepsilon
-transitions

A
\PA
with
\varepsilon
-transitions is defined is the same fashion as a
\PA
except that we allow transitions of the form
(p,\varepsilon,\bm{v},q)
where
\varepsilon
denotes the empty-word. The notions of run and accepted languages are adapted in the usual way.

In the non-deterministic case, it is known that adding
\varepsilon
-transitions does not increase the expressivity of the model
[
30
]
. A little more care is needed to ensure that the
\varepsilon
-removal preserves weak-unambiguity.

Proposition 32
.

For every weakly-unambiguous
\PA
with
\varepsilon
-transitions, there (effectively) exists an equivalent weakly-unambiguous
\PA

\mathcal{B}
without
\varepsilon
-transitions. Furthermore
\mathcal{B}
can be constructed with
n
states and dimension
n^{2}t
if
\mathcal{A}
has
n
states and
t
transitions.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{I},F,C,\Delta)
be a weakly-unambiguous PA with
\varepsilon
-transitions. Without loss of generality, we assume that the initial state
q_{I}
has no incoming transitions. Thanks to Proposition
31
, it is enough to construct a weakly-unambiguous generalized
\PA

\mathcal{B}
without
\varepsilon
-transitions accepting
L(\mathcal{A})
.

For every pair of states
(p,q)
of
\mathcal{A}
, we consider the
set, denoted by
C_{p,q}^{\varepsilon}
, of vectors
\bm{v}
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
which can label a run
\pi
of the form
p\xRightarrow{\varepsilon,\bm{v}}q
.

Notice that
C_{p,q}^{\varepsilon}
is semilinear. Indeed, it is recognized by the automaton over
(\mathbb{N}^{d},+)
obtained from
\mathcal{A}
by setting the initial state to
p
, the final state to
q
, and keeping only the
\varepsilon
-transitions.

The automaton
\mathcal{B}
is defined as follows: it has the same set of states, initial and final states as
\mathcal{A}
does.

For every pair
(q,r)
of states of
\mathcal{A}
, for every transition
(p,(a,\bm{d}),q)\in\Delta
, where
a\not=\varepsilon
and
p\not=q_{I}
, we add to
\Delta^{\prime}
the transition
(p,(a,(\{\bm{d}\}+C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}),r)
, and the transition
(q_{I},(a,(\{\bm{d}\}+C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}+C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}),r)
. Since
q_{I}
has no incoming transition, there can only be exactly one use of a transition of the form
(q_{I},(a,(\{\bm{d}\}+C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}+C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}),r)
in a run of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, at the very beginning.

Let us show that
L(\mathcal{A})\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
. Let
w=w_{1}\ldots w_{r}\in L(\mathcal{A})
, and
q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi]{(w,\bm{d})}q_{f}
its accepting run in
\mathcal{A}
, with
\bm{d}\in S
. We can decompose
\pi=\pi^{0}_{\varepsilon}\pi^{1}_{w_{1}}\ldots\pi^{r-1}_{\varepsilon}\pi^{r}_{w_{r}}\pi^{r}_{\varepsilon}
, where each
\pi^{i}_{w_{i}}
is a transition that reads the letter
w_{i}
, and each
\pi^{i}_{\varepsilon}
is a (possibly empty) path of
\varepsilon
-transitions.

At the beginning of the run, we have a run of the form
q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi^{0}_{\varepsilon}]{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{0}})}p\xrightarrow{(w_{1},\bm{d_{1}})}q\xRightarrow[\pi^{1}_{\varepsilon}]{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{1}})}r
, with by definition
\bm{v_{0}}\in C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}
and
\bm{v_{1}}\in C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}
. By construction, we have in
\Delta^{\prime}
the transition
(q_{I},(w_{1},(\{\bm{d}\}+C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}+C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}),r)
. For written convenience we write
L_{0}=C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}
and
L_{1}=C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}
.

For any
2\leq j\leq r
,
\pi^{j}_{w_{j}}
and
\pi^{j}_{\varepsilon}
are of the form
\pi^{j}_{w_{j}}=p\xrightarrow{w_{j},\bm{d_{j}}}q
and
\pi^{j}_{\varepsilon}=q\xRightarrow{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{j}})}r
, where
p,q,r
are three states and
\bm{v_{j}}\in C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}
, so that in
\Delta^{\prime}
we have the corresponding transition
(p,(w_{j},(\{\bm{d_{j}}\}+C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}),r)
. For written convenience we write
L_{j}=C_{p,q}^{\varepsilon}
. Notice that this decomposition is unambiguous, as there is only one way to decompose
\pi
under this form.

This leads to a run in
\mathcal{B}
of the form
q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi_{\mathcal{B}}]{(w,L)}q_{f}
with
L=L_{0}+\ldots+L_{r}+\{\bm{d_{1}}\}+\ldots\{\bm{d_{r}}\}
. It is accepting if
L\cap S\not=\emptyset
. It is immediate since
\bm{v_{0}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}+\bm{d_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{d_{r}}=\bm{d}\in S
, and
\bm{v_{i}}\in L_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq r
.

Let us now show that
L(\mathcal{B})\subseteq L(\mathcal{A})
.
Let
w=w_{1}\ldots w_{r}\in L(\mathcal{B})
, and
q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi^{\prime}]{(w,L)}q_{f}
its accepting run in
\mathcal{B}
, with
S\cap L\not=\emptyset
. We can decompose
\pi^{\prime}=\pi^{\prime}_{1}\pi^{\prime}_{2}\ldots\pi^{\prime}_{r}
, where
\pi^{\prime}_{1}
is of the form
q_{I}\xrightarrow{w_{1},\{\bm{d_{1}}\}+L_{0}+L_{1}}r
, where
p,q,r
are three states,
L_{0}=C_{q_{I},p}^{\varepsilon}
and
L_{1}=C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}
. Likewise, for
j\leq 2
, each
\pi^{\prime}_{j}
is of the form
p\xrightarrow{w_{j},\{\bm{d_{j}}\}+L_{j}}r
for
p,q,r
three states, and
L_{j}=C_{q,r}^{\varepsilon}
.

As
S\cap L\not=\emptyset
, let’s take a
\bm{d}\in S\cap L
. As
L=L_{0}+\ldots+L_{r}+\{\bm{d_{1}}\}+\ldots+\{\bm{d_{r}}\}
, we can find
\bm{v_{i}}\in L_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq r
such that
\bm{d}=\bm{d_{1}}+\ldots+\bm{d_{r}}+\bm{v_{0}}+\ldots+\bm{v_{r}}
.

As
\bm{v_{0}}\in L_{0}
and
\bm{v_{1}}\in L_{1}
, it means that from
\pi^{\prime}_{1}
we can build a run in
\mathcal{A}
of the form
\pi_{1}=q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi^{0}_{\varepsilon}]{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{0}})}p\xrightarrow{(w_{1},\bm{d_{1}})}q\xRightarrow[\pi^{1}_{\varepsilon}]{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{1}})}r
.

Likewise, for
2\leq j\leq r
, as
\bm{v_{j}}\in L_{j}
, we can write from
\pi^{\prime}_{j}
a run in
\mathcal{A}
of the form
\pi_{j}=p\xrightarrow{w_{j},\bm{d_{j}}}q\xRightarrow[\pi_{j}^{\varepsilon}]{(\varepsilon,\bm{v_{j}})}r
.

This leads to run in
\mathcal{A}
,
q_{I}\xRightarrow[\pi]{w,\bm{d}}q_{f}
, which is accepting since
\bm{d}\in S
. So
w\in L(\mathcal{A})
.

Notice that we have defined a construction that transforms a run
\pi
of
\mathcal{A}
into a run
\pi^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{B}
, that is obtained by contracting the
\varepsilon
paths in
\pi
. This construction is well defined, there is only one way to contract the run into a run of
\mathcal{B}
. Reciprocally, every run from
\mathcal{B}
is the contraction of at least one run in
\mathcal{A}
.

Suppose that
w\in L(\mathcal{B})
is accepted by two different runs
\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}
. Then we can create two accepting runs
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
in
\mathcal{A}
labeled by
w
, such that
\pi^{\prime}_{1}
is the contraction of
\pi_{1}
, and
\pi^{\prime}_{2}
is the contraction of
\pi_{2}
. By weak unambiguity of
\mathcal{A}
,
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}
. As there is only one way to contract a run of
\mathcal{A}
into a run of
\mathcal{B}
,
\pi^{\prime}_{1}=\pi^{\prime}_{2}
.
∎

B.6
Proof of Proposition
13

The aim of this section is to prove that the class of languages accepted by unambiguous (two-way) RBCM and by weakly-unambiguous PA coincide. This equivalence is stated in Proposition
13
which is restated for convenience below.

See
13

The result is known to hold in the non-deterministic case
[
30
]
. The proof in the unambiguous case follows the same general outline but some care is need to ensure that the different steps preserve unambiguity/weak-unambiguity. Using the normalizations of RBCM obtained in
[
25
]
, we can show that unambiguous RBCM accept the same languages as weakly-unambiguous PA with
\varepsilon
-transitions.

Proposition 33
.

The unambiguous RBCM and the weakly-unambiguous PA with
\varepsilon
-transitions accept the same languages.

Proof.

For the direct inclusion, consider an unambiguous two-way RBCM
\mathcal{M}
with
k
-counters each making at most
r
reversals. In
[
25
]
, the author constructs an equivalent one-way RBCM
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
with
k^{\prime}\geq k
counters, each making at most one reversal. With very little adaptation, this construction can be shown to preserve unambiguity. To simplify the presentation, we present the construction of an equivalent weakly-unambiguous
\PA

\mathcal{A}
in the case of one counter. The adaptation to several counters is straightforward.

The weakly-unambiguous PA
\mathcal{A}
is of dimension 3 and has states of the form
(q,\tau)
with
q
a state of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
\tau\in\{0,1,2\}
. Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}
simulates
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
using the first coordinate of the output vector to store the number of increments, and the second coordinate stores the number of decrements. In particular, the value of the counter is obtained by subtracting the second coordinate to the first one. During the simulation,
\mathcal{A}
keeps track using the
\tau
-component of whether
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is incrementing or decrementing. More precisely,

•

\tau=0
if the machine
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
has never incremented (and the counter is equal to
0
),

•

\tau=1
if the machine has incremented its counter but has never decremented,

•

\tau=2
if the machine has decremented but the counter is not-null,

•

\tau=3
if the machine has decremented and the counter is null.

The automaton
\mathcal{A}
increments its third component by
\tau
upon accepting.

When
\tau=0
or
\tau=3
,
\mathcal{A}
can only simulate transitions of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
guarded by
c=0
and when
\tau=1
or
\tau=2
, only transitions guarded by
c\neq 0
.

The automaton
\mathcal{A}
can deterministically compute if
\tau=0
or
1
but it needs to non-deterministically guess if and when, it should enter
3
. The semilinear constraint of
\mathcal{A}
is used to check that the guess on value of
\tau
is correct. More precisely, the semilinear constraint is given by the formula
\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=x_{1}=x_{2}\Leftrightarrow x_{3}=0\vee x_{3}=3
. To ensure that the simulation is weakly-unambiguous, we ask that the automata enters
\tau=3
from
\tau=1
and
\tau=2
only when performing a decrement on the counter.

For the converse inclusion, consider a weakly-unambiguous
\PA

\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,q_{I},F,C,\Delta)
with
\varepsilon
-transitions of dimension
d
. We construct an unambiguous RBCM
\mathcal{M}
accepting
L(\mathcal{A})
. The machine
\mathcal{M}
is one-way and has
d
counters which are used to store the coordinates of the vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
computed by
\mathcal{A}
. The machine
\mathcal{M}
works in two phases: it first simulates
\mathcal{A}
on the input word only incrementing the counters and then checks that the computed vector belongs to the semilinear set
C
by simulating an unambiguous automaton over
\mathbb{N}^{d}
accepting
C
. During the first phase, for every transition

B.7.2
Equivalence of definitions for RCM

The definition of RCM in
[
12
]
actually differs from the definition given in this article (see
Section
3.4
). In
[
12
]
, the RCM class is defined with a subset of the semilinear sets: the authors only consider semilinear sets which can be expressed as boolean combinations of inequalities: they do not allow quantifiers nor equalities modulo constants. For this section, we will call such semilinear sets
modulo-free semilinear sets
.
Modulo-free semilinears form a strict subclass of the semilinears.
For instance the semilinear set
\{2n\;|\;n\geq 0\}
cannot be defined using only inequalities and is therefore not a modulo-free semilinear. However this difference does not impact the resulting RCM class.

To show this, we will use the equivalence between RCM and weakly-unambiguous PA. Remark that the translation in both directions transforms a modulo-free semilinear set into a modulo-free semilinear set. Hence to prove the equivalence of RCM defined with general semilinears and RCM defined with modulo-free semilinears, it is enough to establish the same equivalence for weakly-unambiguous PA. A similar idea can be found for affine PA in
[
10
]
.

, and for

,
w_{i}\in(\Sigma\cup N)^{*}
and

is obtained from

by applying the rule

to the leftmost non terminal symbol in

.

For any symbol

, we define

the set of every left derivations starting from

It is classical to show by induction that to every element of

of the form

, we can associate a run in

that starts in state

, with top stack symbol

, such that this stack symbol will be popped for the first time when arriving in state

, after reading exactly the word

, with vector value the sum of the vectors of

. Furthermore this correspondence is a one-to-on correspondence, in the sense that the sequence of left derivations simulates exactly the runs of the automaton.

Hence, the left derivations of the grammar run the pushdown automaton, and every derivation of the grammar is a valid run of the pushdown automaton, both having the same weight. Consequently the notion of weak-unambiguity is preserved during this transformation.
∎

In the following, the weakly-unambiguous

every non terminal symbols is productive, and such that every rule has at most three symbols in its right member, the first symbol being a letter in

Let us modify the rules of such grammars to obtain an equivalent weakly-unambiguous grammar such that the head symbol of every right member is a letter in

C.1.2
Removal of nullable symbols

A symbol

. Notice that

We want to modify the rules of a grammar in order to remove the nullable symbols, by using the usual algorithm.

If

Claim 43
.

is semilinear.

{claimproof}

Let us create a simple context-free grammar

from

.

The grammar

is obtained from

, and changing every production rule of the form

simulates the derivation of

corresponds to the vector labelling

in

is semilinear.

.

C.1.3
Removal of unit rules

Let a generalized weakly-unambiguous

of dimension

, without any nullable symbol.

A unit rule is a rule of the form

, where

and

are non terminal symbols.
For every couple of non terminal symbols

Claim 45
.

The set

is semilinear.

{claimproof}

It suffices to consider the automaton of dimension

Proposition 46
.

Let a generalized weakly-unambiguous

i<j
, the pieces
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
have same origin
q
. Consider the
a
-path
S^{\prime}
defined by (it is depicted on Figure
8
):

S^{\prime}=w_{1}\sigma_{1}^{s_{1}}w_{2}\cdots w_{i-1}\sigma_{j}^{s_{j}}\sigma_{i}^{s_{i}}w_{i}\cdots w_{j-1}w_{j}\cdots w_{f}\sigma_{1}^{s_{f}}w_{f+1}.

The two paths are not equal:

•

If
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}
then necessarily there is a non-empty
w_{k}
or a
\sigma_{k}\neq\sigma_{i}
between them, otherwise the path would contain
\sigma_{i}^{s_{i}+s_{j}}
instead, by construction.

•

If
\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{j}
then we do some
\sigma_{j}
-loops around
q
before the
\sigma_{j}
-loops.

Moreover, the two
a
-paths
S
and
S^{\prime}
have same length and compute the same vector, by commutativity of the vector addition. This is not possible as
S
is part of an accepting run in a weakly-unambiguous automaton: all the
\sigma_{i}
’s have different extremities. This implies that there are at most
|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|
different
a
-pieces, which establishes the property
f\leq|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|
.

To prove that there is a bounded number of distinct possible signatures for a given
\mathcal{A}
, we just have to observe that there are finitely many possibilities for the
w_{i}
’s, as they are simple paths, and that there also are finitely many
a
-pieces.

E.2
Decidability of the ambiguity of a PA

Proposition 71
.

Given a Parikh automaton
\mathcal{A}
, it is decidable if it is weakly-ambiguous.

Proof.

We prove that given a PA
\mathcal{A}
, we can decide if it is weakly-ambiguous.

We use the same construction for the automaton of the intersection of two weakly-unambiguous automata: we starts by building the product-automaton
\mathcal{C}
recognizing
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
, by taking
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}
and we modify it slightly by adding a new dimension. The construction is the same except for the transitions.

For every pair of transitions
(t_{1},t_{2})\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}\times\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
, labeled by the same letter
a\in\Sigma
, with
t_{1}=(q_{1},(a,\bm{v_{1}}),q^{\prime}_{1})
and
t_{2}=(q_{2},(a,\bm{v_{2}}),q^{\prime}_{2})
, then
((q_{1},q_{2}),(a,(\bm{v_{1}},\bm{v_{2}},x_{t_{1},t_{2}})),(q^{\prime}_{1},q^{\prime}_{2}))\in\delta_{\mathcal{C}}
, where
x_{t_{1},t_{2}}=0
if
t_{1}=t_{2}
, 1 otherwise. In other words, the last dimension counts by how many transitions the two simulated runs differ.

The semilinear set is the same as for the intersection, with the supplementary condition that the last value for the additional dimension must be bigger than
0
.

As
\mathcal{A}
is weakly-unambiguous if and only no accepted word has two accepted runs, it is direct to see that
\mathcal{A}
is weakly-unambiguous if and only the language recognized by
\mathcal{C}
is empty. And Emptiness is decidable for PA.
∎

E.3
Undecidability of inherent weak-ambiguity

Proposition 72
.

Inherent weak-unambiguity is undecidable for PA.

The notion of weak-unambiguity, for languages recognized by Parikh automata, verifies all the criteria given in
[
24
]
, and thus is undecidable. We unroll here the key elements of the general proof from Greibach, for completeness.

We reduce the universality problem for languages recognized by PA, which is undecidable (see
[
29
]
). Given an instance
L_{1}
of the universality problem, we want to build an instance
L
for the problem of weak-unambiguity such that

\hfill L_{1}=\Sigma_{1}^{*}\Leftrightarrow L\textnormal{\ is\ inherently\ weakly-unambiguous}.\hfill

The language
L
is built as follows. Let’s denote by
\Sigma_{1}
the alphabet of
L_{1}
,
c
a new symbol not in
\Sigma_{1}
, and finally let
L_{2}
be an inherently weak-ambiguous language in PA (for instance the language
S
of Section
4
) over an alphabet
\Sigma_{2}
(we can have
\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}\not=\emptyset
).

We define then
L=L_{1}c\Sigma_{2}^{*}\ \cup\ \Sigma_{1}^{*}cL_{2}
. Then:
L_{1}=\Sigma_{1}^{*}\Leftrightarrow L\textnormal{\ is\ weakly-unambiguous}
. Indeed:

\Rightarrow

If
L_{1}=\Sigma_{1}^{*}
then
L=\Sigma_{1}^{*}c\Sigma_{2}^{*}
is regular, so can be recognized weakly-unambiguously by a PA.

\Leftarrow

If
L
is weakly-unambiguous, let’s show that
L_{1}=\Sigma_{1}^{*}
.

By contradiction, if
L_{1}\not=\Sigma_{1}^{*}
, then there would exist a word
y\in\Sigma_{1}^{*}
such that
y\not\in L_{1}
.

As
yc\Sigma_{2}^{*}
is regular,
L\cap yc\Sigma_{2}^{*}
is weakly-unambiguous since the class of weakly-unambiguous PA languages is stable by intersection.

As
y\not\in L_{1}
and
c\not\in\Sigma_{1}
,
L\cap yc\Sigma_{2}^{*}=ycL_{2}
.

So
ycL_{2}
is weakly-unambiguous, and by stability by left quotient with a word,
L_{2}=(yc)^{-1}(ycL_{2})
is weakly-unambiguous. This leads to a contradiction since
L_{2}
in inherently weakly-ambiguous.

Remark 73
.

The proof needs at least 3 symbols. It can be proved that the problem is still undecidable for
2
-letters languages by encoding every letter with a sequence of 2 letters.

Remark 74
.

One of the main point in the proof is the stability of weakly-unambiguous automaton under left quotient with a word. Notice that although this property is true (by adding
\varepsilon
-transitions in an automaton without
\varepsilon
-transitions), it cannot be generalized by replacing
w
by a regular language, since the language
L=\{a^{m}ba^{n}bva^{n}w\ :\ v,w\in\{a,b\}^{*},|v|=m\}
is weakly-unambiguously recognizable, whereas
(a^{*}b)^{-1}L=S
is not.

Appendix F
Toolbox, and general bounds

F.1
Notations, Hadamard’s bound, Cramer’s rule

When working on multivariate polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, we
denote by
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
the
multi-index

({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{n})
and by
x^{\bm{\alpha}}
the monomial
x^{\bm{\alpha}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
. The
(total) degree
of

x^{\bm{\alpha}}
is defined as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
, but in the sequel we
will mainly work with the
maxdegree

\deg_{m}(x^{\bm{\alpha}})=\max_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
.

A nonzero polynomial
P
in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a finite
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of monomials

P=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma_{P}}\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}x^{\bm{\alpha}},

where
\Gamma_{P}
is a finite set of multi-indices, called the monomial support of
P
. The
degree

\deg(P)
of
P
is the maximum degree of its monomials:

\deg(P)=\max\left\{\deg(x^{\bm{\alpha}}):\ \bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma_{P}\text{ and }\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}\neq 0\right\}.

Similarly, the maxdegree of a polynomial is the maximum maxdegree of its monomials:

\deg_{m}(P)=\max\left\{\deg_{m}(x^{\bm{\alpha}}):\ \bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma_{P}\text{ and }\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}\neq 0\right\}.

We will use the following classical norms for polynomials of
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
:

\|P\|_{1}=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma_{P}}|\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}|,\;\text{ and }\;\|P\|_{\infty}=\max_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma_{P}}|\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}|.

Since there are
(m+1)^{n}
monomials of maxdegree at most
m
, on
n
variables, we get directly:

\|P\|_{1}\leq(\deg_{m}(P)+1)^{n}\|P\|_{\infty}.

(1)

Lemma 75

(product)
.

If
P
and
Q
are polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, then

\|PQ\|_{\infty}\leq(m+1)^{n}\|P\|_{\infty}\|Q\|_{\infty},\text{ where }m=\min(\deg_{m}(P),\deg_{m}(Q)).

Proof.

W.l.o.g., we can assume that
\deg_{m}(Q)\leq\deg_{m}(P)
and hence
m=\deg_{m}(Q)
. For
P=\sum\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}x^{\bm{\alpha}}
and
Q=\sum\mu_{\bm{\beta}}x^{\bm{\beta}}
, we have
PQ=\sum c_{\bm{\gamma}}x^{\bm{\gamma}}
with
|c_{\bm{\gamma}}|=|\sum_{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}=\bm{\gamma}}\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}\mu_{\bm{\beta}}|\leq\|P\|_{\infty}\|Q\|_{1}\leq(m+1)^{n}\|P\|_{\infty}\|Q\|_{\infty}
.
∎

Lemma 76
.

Let
P\neq 0
be a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.
Then
P(n)\neq 0
for all
n\geq\|P\|_{\infty}+1
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq\|P\|_{\infty}+1
. We write
P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}x^{k}
. Assume toward a contradiction that
P(n)=0
. In particular, we have:

n^{d}\leq\left|a_{d}n^{d}\right|=\left|\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}a_{k}n^{k}\right|\leq\|P\|_{\infty}\frac{n^{d}-1}{n-1}\leq n^{d}-1

which brings the contradiction.
∎

Let
A
be a
\mathbb{C}
-valued
p\times p
matrix. The classical
Hadamard bound
states that:

|\det(A)|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}|A_{i,j}|^{2}},

(2)

that is, the modulus of the determinant is bounded from above by the product of the length of the column vectors of
A
.

We adapt to several variables a result of Goldstein and Graham
[
23
]
, which gives bounds for the coefficients of a determinant of polynomials, generalizing the classical Hadamard bound. Note that similar bounds appear in
[
18
, Lemma 3.1]
and in a different context, in
[
9
, Eq. (21)]
(without proof).

Lemma 77

(determinant)
.

Let
A
be a
p\times p
matrix whose coefficients are polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

\|\det(A)\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}^{p}p^{p/2}\;\textrm{where $R_{1}=\max_{i,j}\|A_{i,j}\|_{1}$.}

In particular, using the upper bound given by Equation (
1
), it also satisfies:

\|\det(A)\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\infty}^{p}p^{p/2}(M+1)^{np}\;\textrm{where $R_{\infty}=\max_{i,j}\|A_{i,j}\|_{\infty}$ and $M=\max_{i,j}\deg_{m}(A_{i,j})$}.

Proof.

Let
f
be the map from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}
defined using
the polynomial
\det(A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))
as follows:

f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\det\left(A(e^{it_{1}},\ldots,e^{it_{n}})\right).

For any given
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
is defined as a complex-valued determinant, so we can apply Hadamard bound, which yields that

|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|\leq\prod_{k=1}^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}|A_{k,j}(e^{it_{1}},\ldots,e^{it_{n}})|^{2}}.

But when setting
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(e^{it_{1}},\ldots,e^{it_{n}})
, every monomial is changed into a complex number of modulus
1
. Hence
|A_{k,j}(e^{it_{1}},\ldots,e^{it_{n}})|\leq\|A_{k,j}\|_{1}
, for all
k,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. This yields:

|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|\leq\prod_{k=1}^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|A_{k,j}\|_{1}^{2}}\leq\prod_{k=1}^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}R_{1}^{2}}.

Therefore, we get an upper bound for
|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|^{2}
:

|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|^{2}\leq p^{p}R_{1}^{2p}.

(3)

We now consider the real number

I(A)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|^{2}dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}.

By Eq. (
3
), we have
I(A)\leq p^{p}R_{1}^{2p}
. We will now prove that

\|\det(A)\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq I(A)
.

Observe that
\det(A)
is a polynomial, and can therefore be written
\det(A)=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma}\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}x^{\bm{\alpha}}
, for a finite set of multi-indices
\Gamma
. Thus, when setting
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(e^{it_{1}},\ldots,e^{it_{n}})
, using the fact that
|z|^{2}=z\overline{z}
(where
\overline{z}
is the complex conjugate of
z
), we obtain:

|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|^{2}=\left(\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma}\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}\exp(i\alpha_{1}t_{1}+\ldots+i\alpha_{n}t_{n})\right)\left(\sum_{\bm{\beta}\in\Gamma}\overline{\lambda_{\bm{\beta}}}\exp(-i\beta_{1}t_{1}-\ldots-i\beta_{n}t_{n})\right).

Hence,

I(A)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\sum_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}\in\Gamma}\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}\overline{\lambda_{\bm{\beta}}}\int_{t_{1}=0}^{2\pi}\cdots\int_{t_{n}=0}^{2\pi}e^{i(\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1})t_{1}}\cdots e^{i(\alpha_{n}-\beta_{n})t_{n}}dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n}.

The integral part is null if
\bm{\alpha}\neq\bm{\beta}
and it is equal to
(2\pi)^{n}
if
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\beta}
. Hence,

I(A)=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\Gamma}|\lambda_{\bm{\alpha}}|^{2}\geq\|\det(A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))\|_{\infty}^{2}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Recall that a
minor
of an
m\times n
matrix with
m<n

m
columns of
A
, in the same order as in

F.2
Bounds for the generating series of a vector automaton

We introduce a very simple model of non-deterministic finite state automaton, called a vector automaton, whose transitions are labeled by vectors in
\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. A run of a vector automaton is labeled by the sum of the vectors appearing in its transitions. In particular, a vector automaton accepts a set of vectors in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

Formally, a
vector automaton

\mathcal{A}
over
\mathbb{N}^{d}
is a tuple
(Q_{\mathcal{A}},q_{I},F,\Delta)
where
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
is a finite set of states,
q_{I}\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}
is the initial state,
F\subseteq Q_{\mathcal{A}}
is the set of final states and
\Delta
is the set of transitions of the form
(p,\bm{v},q)
with
p
and
q
in
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. The runs of
\mathcal{A}
are defined in the usual way. We denote
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{(p,\bm{v},q)\in\Delta}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}
and
d^{out}_{\mathcal{A}}
the maximum out-degree of
\mathcal{A}
.

The (multivariate) generating series of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, is defined by:

A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a(\bm{v})x^{{v}_{1}}_{1}\cdots x^{{v}_{d}}_{d},

where
a(\bm{v})
counts the number of runs of
\mathcal{A}
starting in the initial state, ending in a final state and labeled by
\bm{v}
. As we do not allow transitions labeled by
\bm{0}
, the number of such runs is finite and the series is well-defined.

Lemma 79
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a vector automaton over
\mathbb{N}^{d}
with state set
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
. Its multivariate generating series
A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is equal to
\frac{P}{Q}
, where
P
and
Q
are two polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that:

\deg_{m}(P),\deg_{m}(Q)\leq|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|\cdot\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty},\text{ and }\|P\|_{\infty},\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq{(1+d^{out}_{\mathcal{A}})}^{|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|}{|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|}^{|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|/2}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q_{\mathcal{A}},q_{I},F,\Delta)
be a vector automaton. For every state
q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}
, we consider the series

q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{q}(\bm{v})x_{1}^{{v}_{1}}\ldots x_{d}^{{v}_{d}},

where
a_{q}(\bm{v})
counts the number of runs from the state
q
to a final state, labeled by the vector
\bm{v}
. In particular, the multivariate generating series
A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
of
\mathcal{A}
is equal to
q_{I}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
.

Classically, for any state
q
, we have the relation:

\displaystyle q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=

\displaystyle\llbracket q\in F\rrbracket+\sum_{(q,\bm{v},q^{\prime})\in\Delta}x_{1}^{{v}_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{{v}_{d}}\cdot q^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}),

where
\llbracket P\rrbracket=1
if
P
holds and
\llbracket P\rrbracket=0
otherwise.

As a consequence, the vector of generating series
\bm{q}=(q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}))_{q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}}
satisfies the equation:

\bm{q}=M\bm{q}+\bm{f},

where
\bm{f}=(\llbracket q\in F\rrbracket)_{q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}}
, and
M
is the
|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|\times|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|
matrix of polynomials defined by
M_{p,q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{(p,\bm{v},q)\in\Delta}x_{1}^{\bm{v}_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\bm{v}_{d}}
for all
p
and
q
in
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
. In particular, for all
p
and
q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}
,
\|M_{p,q}\|_{1}\leq d^{out}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
M
is the null matrix if we set
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{d}=0
.

The vector
\bm{q}
satisfies the equation
(Id-M)\bm{q}=\bm{f}
. The matrix
Id-M
is invertible on the field of rational fractions
\mathbb{Q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
. Indeed
\det(Id-M)(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is not the null polynomial as
\det(Id-M)(0,\ldots,0)=\det(I)=1
. Therefore, by Cramer’s rule (see Proposition
78
), we have

A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=q_{I}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\frac{P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})}{Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})},

with
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\det(M^{\prime})
, where
M^{\prime}
is the matrix obtained by replacing the column
q_{I}
of
Id-M
by the vector
\bm{f}
, and with
Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\det(Id-M)
.

For all
p,q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}
, we have
\|(Id-M)_{p,q}\|_{1}\leq 1+d_{\mathcal{A}}^{out}
and
\deg_{m}(Id-M)_{p,q}\leq\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}
. As the vector
\bm{f}
substituted in
Id-M
to create
M^{\prime}
only contains
0
’s and
1
’s, the same bounds also hold for
M^{\prime}
. Hence, using Lemma
77
, we can bound the
\infty
-norm of both
P
and
Q
by
(1+d_{\mathcal{A}}^{out})^{|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|}|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|^{|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|/2}
.

By definition of the determinant,
P
and
Q
are linear combinations of products of
|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|
polynomials, each of maxdegree at most
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}
. As a consequence
\deg_{m}(P)
and
\deg_{m}(Q)
are at most
|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|\cdot\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}
.
∎

F.3
Bounds for the transformation of a differential equation into a recurrence equation

In one variable, the coefficient sequence of a holonomic series
H(x)
satisfies a linear recurrence relation with polynomial coefficients. In this section, we provide a bound on the order of the recurrence relation and on the degree and
\infty
-norm of the leading polynomial in terms of the similar quantities of a differential equation satisfied by
H(x)
.

Proposition 80
.

Let
H(x)=\sum u_{n}x^{n}
be a series verifying an equation of the form:

q_{r}(x)\partial_{r}H(x)+\ldots+q_{0}(x)H(x)=0

Then
u_{n}
verifies a recurrence of the form:

\sum_{k=-s}^{S}t_{k}(n)u_{n+k}=0,\,\text{for $n\geq s$, with $t_{S}\not=0$,}

and
0\leq s\leq\max_{i}(\deg(q_{i}))
,
0\leq S\leq r
,
\deg(t_{S})\leq r
,
\|t_{S}\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{i}(\|q_{i}\|_{\infty})\cdot r^{r+1}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that for some
k\in[0,r]
,
q_{k}(0)\neq 0
. If this is not the case, we can divide the equation by the largest
x^{m}
which divides all
q_{k}
’s. This operation does not impact the
\infty
-norm of the polynomials and decreases their degree.

Let
D=\max_{i}\deg(q_{i})
. We can rewrite the equation satisfied by
H(x)
as:

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{D}a_{k,k^{\prime}}x^{k^{\prime}}\partial_{x}^{k}H(x)=0.

This equation translates into the following recurrence relations on the coefficient of
H(x)
which holds for
n\geq D

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{D}a_{k,k^{\prime}}(n-k^{\prime}+1)(n-k^{\prime}+2)\ldots(n-k^{\prime}+k)u_{n-k^{\prime}+k}=0.

By taking
j=k-k^{\prime}
, we can rewrite the relation as:

\sum_{j=-D}^{r}t_{j}(n)u_{n+j}=0\;\text{with}\;t_{j}(n)=\sum_{k=\max(0,j)}^{r}a_{k,k-j}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(n+j-\ell+1)

Some of the polynomials
t_{j}
are possibly zero. Therefore, we need to characterize the leading non-zero polynomial in the recurrence.

Consider
S:=\max\{k-k^{\prime}\mid k\in[0,r],k^{\prime}\in[0,D]\;\text{and}\;a_{k,k^{\prime}}\neq 0\}
. The assumption that
q_{k_{0}}(0)\neq 0
for some
k_{0}
translates to
a_{k_{0},0}\neq 0
for some
k_{0}\geq 0
. This implies that
S\geq k_{0}\geq 0
.

We now prove that
t_{S}(n)
is the leading polynomial in the recurrence relation. By maximality of
S
, we have
t_{j}(n)=0
for
S<j\leq r
. Moreover by definition,
t_{S}(n)=\sum_{k=S}^{r}r_{k}(n)
with
r_{k}(n)=a_{k,k-S}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(n+S-\ell+1)
for all
k\in[S,r]
. By definition of
S
, at least one on the
r_{k}
is non-zero. As
\deg(r_{k})=k
for all
k\in[S,r]
, we have that
t_{S}(n)\neq 0
and
t_{S}(n)
has degree at most
r
.

It only remains to bound the
\infty
-norm of
t_{S}
. Notice that when developing the product
\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(n+S-\ell+1)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}b_{m}n^{m}
, then for
m\in[k]
,
b_{m}=\sum_{I\subseteq[k],|I|=k-m}\prod_{\ell\in I}(S-\ell+1)
, so that
|b_{m}|\leq\binom{k}{m}r^{k-m}\leq k^{m}r^{k-m}\leq r^{r}
. Hence:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\|t_{S}\|_{\infty}&\leq&\sum_{k=S}^{r}|a_{k,k-S}|\cdot\|\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}(n+S-\ell+1)\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq&\sum_{k=S}^{r}|a_{k,k-S}|\cdot r^{r}\\
&\leq&\max_{i}(\|q_{i}\|_{\infty})\cdot r^{r+1}.\\
\end{array}

∎

F.4
Bound for the sum of two univariate holonomic series

In this section, we specialize the bounds of
[
28
, Theorem 2]
for the sum of holonomic series to our notations.

Proposition 81

(
[
28
]
)
.

Given two holonomic series
A(x)
and
B(x)
satisfying non-trivial differential equations of the form:

\begin{array}[]{lcr}p_{r}^{A}(x)\partial^{r}A(x)+\cdots+p_{0}^{A}(x)A(x)=0&\textrm{and}&p_{r}^{B}(x)\partial^{r}B(x)+\cdots+p_{0}^{B}(x)B(x)=0\\
\end{array},

let
D=\max_{i\in[r]}(\deg(p_{i}^{A}),\deg(p_{i}^{B}))
and

. Then,
C(x)=A(x)+B(x)
satisfies a non-trivial differential equation of the form:

q_{2r}(x)\partial^{2r}C(x)+\cdots+q_{0}(x)C(x)=0,

with for all
i\in[2r]
,
\deg(q_{i})\leq 2(r+1)D
and
\log(\|q_{i}\|)\leq O(r^{2}(1+\log(r)+\log(D)+\log(S_{\infty}))
.

Proof.

By
[
28
, Theorem 2]
, we immediately have that
C(x)
satisfies a non-trivial differential equation of the form:

q_{2r}\partial^{2r}C(x)+\cdots+q_{0}C(x)=0

with for all
i\in[2r]
,
\deg(q_{i})\leq 2(r+1)D
.
Furthermore for all

,

\mathrm{ht}(\|q_{i}\|_{\infty})\leq\mathrm{ht}(2r)+\mathrm{ht}((2r+1)!)+(2r+1)\mathrm{ht}(d)+(2r+2)((2r)(\mathrm{ht}(1)+\mathrm{ht}(d))+\mathrm{ht}(S_{\infty}))

with
\mathrm{ht}(x)=\log(1+|x|)
.

As for all

,
\log(1+x)\leq 1+\log(x)
, we have that:

\log(\|q_{i}\|_{\infty}))\leq 8(r+1)^{2}+(2r+2)\log(r)+(4r^{2}+6r+1)\log(D)+(2r+2)\log(S_{\infty}).

∎

F.5
Bounds for specialization

In this section, we consider a holonomic multivariate series
H(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
such that
H(x)=H(x,1,\ldots,1)
is well-defined. As stated in Proposition
26
,
H(x)
is holonomic. We derive bounds on the differential equation satisfied by
H(x)
, given an equation satisfied by
H(x,\bm{y})
.

Before proceeding, we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 82
.

Let
P_{0},P_{1}
, …,
P_{d}
be a sequence of polynomials such that for all
\ell\geq 0
,
P_{\ell}
belongs to
\mathbb{Q}[y_{\ell},\ldots,y_{d}]
and for all
\ell\geq 0
,
P_{\ell+1}(y_{\ell+1},\ldots,y_{d})
is obtained by dividing

P_{\ell}(y_{\ell},\ldots,y_{d})
by
(y_{\ell}-1)^{k_{\ell}}
for some
k_{\ell}\geq 0
and setting
y_{\ell}
to
1
.

We have
\deg(P_{d})\leq\deg_{m}(P_{0})
and
\|P_{d}\|_{\infty}\leq(\deg_{m}(P_{0})+1)^{d}\;2^{\deg(P_{0})}\|P_{0}\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

The polynomial
P_{0}
can be written as the sum of its monomials
\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{0}}a_{\bm{\alpha}}y_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\cdots y_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}
for some set
A_{0}\subset\mathbb{N}^{d+1}
.

We will prove by induction on
\ell
that for all
\ell\in[0,d]
,

P_{\ell}(y_{\ell},\ldots,y_{d})=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{\ell}}a_{\bm{\alpha}}\binom{\alpha_{0}}{k_{0}}\cdots\binom{\alpha_{\ell-1}}{k_{\ell-1}}y_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}}\cdots y_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}.

where
A_{\ell}=\{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{0}:\alpha_{\ell^{\prime}}\geq k_{\ell^{\prime}}\;\textrm{for $\ell^{\prime}\in[0,\ell-1]$}\}
.

The property trivially holds for
\ell=0
. For the induction step, it is enough to remark that:

P_{\ell+1}(y_{\ell+1},\ldots,y_{d})=\frac{1}{k_{\ell}!}(\partial_{y_{\ell}}^{k_{\ell}}P_{\ell})(1,y_{\ell+1},\ldots,y_{d}).

In particular, we have the following characterization of
P_{d}
in terms of
P_{0}
.

P_{d}(y_{d})=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{d}}a_{\bm{\alpha}}\binom{\alpha_{0}}{k_{0}}\cdots\binom{\alpha_{d-1}}{k_{d-1}}y_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}.

Clearly, the degree
\deg(P_{d})
is bounded by the maxdegree of
P_{0}
. For all
k\in[0,\deg(P_{d})]
, consider the coefficient of
c_{k}
of
y_{d}^{k}
in
P_{d}
. We have:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}|c_{k}|&=&|\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{d},\alpha_{d}=k}a_{\bm{\alpha}}\binom{\alpha_{0}}{k_{0}}\cdots\binom{\alpha_{d-1}}{k_{d-1}}|\\
&\leq&\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{d},\alpha_{d}=k}|a_{\bm{\alpha}}|\cdot 2^{\alpha_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{d-1}}\\
&\leq&\|P_{0}\|_{\infty}\cdot 2^{\deg(P_{0})}\cdot(\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A_{d},\alpha_{d}=k}1)\\
&\leq&\|P_{0}\|_{\infty}\cdot 2^{\deg(P_{0})}\cdot(\deg_{m}(P_{0})+1)^{d}.\end{array}

This implies the announced bound on
\|P_{d}\|_{\infty}
.

∎

Proposition 83
.

Let
H(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
a multivariate series satisfying a non-trivial equation of the form:

p_{r}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\partial_{x}^{r}H(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})+\ldots+p_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})H(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})=0.

If
H(x)=H(x,1,\ldots,1)
is well defined, then
H(x)
satisfies a non-trivial equation of the form:

q_{s}(x)\partial_{x}^{s}H(x)+\cdots+q_{0}(x)H(x)=0

with
s\leq r
and for
i\in[0,s]
,
\deg(q_{i})\leq\deg_{m}(p_{i})
and
\|q_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\|p_{i}\|_{\infty}(\deg_{m}(p_{i})+1)^{d}\;2^{\deg(p_{i})}
.

Proof.

For all
\ell\in[0,d]
, we inductively define a series
H_{\ell}
in the variables
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell}
by taking

H_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})=H(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
and for
\ell\in[1,d]
,
H_{\ell}
is obtained by setting the variable
y_{d-\ell+1}
to 1 in
H_{\ell-1}
(i.e.,
H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})=H_{\ell-1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell},1)
).
In particular,
H_{d}(x)
is equal to
H(x)
.

We prove by induction that for all
\ell\in[0,d]
,
H_{\ell}
satisfies a non-trivial equation of the form:

p_{r}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})\partial_{x}^{r}H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})+\ldots+p^{[\ell]}_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})=0

and for all
\ell\in[1,d]
, there exists
k_{\ell}\geq 0
such that for all
j\in[0,r]
,
p_{j}^{[\ell]}
is obtained by dividing
p_{k}^{[\ell-1]}
by
(y_{d-\ell+1}-1)^{k_{\ell}}
and then setting
y_{d-\ell+1}
to
1
.

The announced bounds on the polynomial
q_{i}
are then obtained by applying Lemma
82
to the sequence of polynomial
p_{i}^{[0]}=p_{i},p_{i}^{[1]},\ldots,p_{i}^{[d]}=q_{i}
.

The base case is immediate. Assume that the property holds for some
\ell
. That is to say that
H_{\ell}
satisfies a non-trivial equation:

p_{r}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})\partial_{x}^{r}H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})+\ldots+p^{[\ell]}_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})=0

Let
k_{\ell+1}\geq 0
be the greatest
k_{\ell+1}
such for all
j\in[0,r]
,
p_{j}^{[\ell]}
can be written as
(y_{d-\ell}-1)^{k_{\ell+1}}\cdot s_{j}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})
. Hence the series
H_{\ell}
satisfies the non-trivial equation:

s_{r}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})\partial_{x}^{r}H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})+\ldots+s^{[\ell]}_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})H_{\ell}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell})=0

The series
H_{\ell+1}
satisfies the equation:

s_{r}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1},1)\partial_{x}^{r}H_{\ell+1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1})+\ldots+s^{[\ell]}_{0}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1},1)H_{\ell+1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1})=0

This equation is non-trivial as otherwise the polynomials

s_{j}^{[\ell]}
would all be divisible by
(y_{d-\ell}-1)
which would contradict the maximality of
k_{\ell+1}
.
Hence we can take
p_{j}^{[\ell+1]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1})=s_{j}^{[\ell]}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-\ell-1},1)
for all
j\in[0,r]
.
∎

F.6
Bounds for the Hadamard product of two rational series

In this section, we are interested in giving upper bounds on
the differential equations for
the Hadamard product of two rational series
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
: we therefore aim at providing upper bounds for the maxdegrees and the coefficients of the polynomials that appear in the description of
F_{1}\odot F_{2}

as partial differential equations in its variables
x_{1}
, …
x_{n}
.

Our approach consists in taking Lipshitz’s construction that proves that the Hadamard product of two holonomic series is holonomic, and studying it in details when the two series are rational. Let
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
be vectors of formal variables. In a nutshell,
starting with
F_{1}(\bm{x})
and
F_{2}(\bm{x})
, Lipshitz introduces the series
F(\bm{x},\bm{y})
defined by

F(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{y_{1}\cdots y_{n}}F_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}\right)\cdot F_{2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}).

(4)

Though this doubles the number of variables,
it transforms the Hadamard product into a simple product and a coefficient extraction, as one can establish that

F_{1}(\bm{x})\odot F_{2}(\bm{x})=[y_{1}^{-1}\cdots y_{n}^{-1}]F(\bm{x},\bm{y}).

(5)

Indeed, let us write
a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}=[x^{i_{1}}\ldots x^{i_{n}}]F_{1}
and
b_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}=[x^{i_{1}}\ldots x^{i_{n}}]F_{2}
, so that:

F(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{y_{1}\ldots y_{n}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}b_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\big(\tfrac{x_{1}}{y_{1}}\big)^{i_{1}}\ldots\big(\tfrac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}\big)^{i_{n}}y_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots y_{n}^{j_{n}}.

Hence
\displaystyle[(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{-1}]F=[(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{0}]\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\end{subarray}}a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}b_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{i_{n}}y_{1}^{j_{1}-i_{1}}\ldots y_{n}^{j_{n}-i_{n}}
. The terms in
y_{1}^{0}
in the sum appear when
i_{1}=j_{1}
. More generally, the terms in
y_{1}^{0}\ldots y_{n}^{0}
in the sum appear every time in the sum we have
i_{1}=j_{1}
, …,
i_{n}=j_{n}
.

So
[(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{-1}]F=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}b_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{i_{n}}=F_{1}(\bm{x})\odot F_{2}(\bm{x})
.

Remark 84
.

Multiplying by a factor
\frac{1}{y_{1}\ldots y_{n}}
in the definition of
F
, to extract later
[(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{-1}]F
, may seem useless. It turns out that Lipshitz’s proof relies strongly on the fact that the extracted coefficient to retrieve the Hadamard’s product is indeed
[(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{-1}]F
. The main reason is that
(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{-1}
cannot come from the derivation of any term
y_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots y_{n}^{i_{n}}
, so that the differential equations for the Hadamard’s product can be extracted from the differential equations for
F
.

Remark 85
.

We used the most straightforward approach, as our primary goal is to provide a first upper bound for the complexity of the inclusion test for weakly-unambiguous automata. Other techniques, based on integral representation of the Hadamard product, may give better bounds, as it was done for some combinatorial walks in
[
7
]
.

In this section, as we already did in the presentation above, we use vector notations for readability:

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
\bm{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
,
[\bm{y}^{-\bm{1}}]=[y_{1}^{-1}\cdots y_{n}^{-1}]
, and so on.

F.6.1
Statement and main steps

The bounds for the Hadamard product of two rational series are given in the following theorem.

Theorem 86
.

Let
P_{1},Q_{1},P_{2}
and
Q_{2}
be four polynomials of
\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x}]
. We define the quantities

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=1+\max\left\{\deg_{m}(P_{1}),\deg_{m}(P_{2}),\deg_{m}(Q_{1}),\deg_{m}(Q_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle S_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\|P_{1}\|_{\infty},\|P_{2}\|_{\infty},\|Q_{1}\|_{\infty},\|Q_{2}\|_{\infty}\right\}

For every
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
there exist
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
r+1
polynomials
p_{0}(\bm{x})
,…,
p_{r}(\bm{x})
of
\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x}]
, not all null, such that the series
\frac{P_{1}}{Q_{1}}\odot\frac{P_{2}}{Q_{2}}(\bm{x})
satisfies the equation:

p_{r}(\bm{x})\partial_{x_{j}}^{r}f(\bm{x})+\ldots+p_{0}(\bm{x})f(\bm{x})=0,

with
r+\deg({p_{i}})<(nM)^{O(n)}
and
\log\|p_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq(nM)^{O(n^{2})}(1+\log(S_{\infty}))
.

Remark 87
.

In the statement of the previous theorem, the notation
O(n)
(resp.
O(n^{2})
) is only here to simplify the writing and does not imply that
n
tends to infinity. It simply means that there exists a constant
c
(independent of all the parameters) such that for all
n\geq 1
, the inequality holds when
O(n)
(resp.
O(n^{2})
) is replaced by
cn
(resp.
cn^{2}
).

The proof consists of several steps. As announced at the beginning of the section, we first build a rational function
F
in
2n
variables. In the following lemma we compute bounds for
F
.

Lemma 88
.

With the notations of Theorem
86
,
if
F
is defined by

F(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{y_{1}\cdots y_{n}}\frac{P_{1}}{Q_{1}}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}\right)\cdot\frac{P_{2}}{Q_{2}}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),

then there exist two polynomials
P
and
Q
of
\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x},\bm{y}]
such that
F=P/Q
,

\deg_{m}(P)<2M
,
\deg_{m}(Q)<2M
,

\|P\|_{\infty}\leq S_{\infty}^{2}
and
\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq S_{\infty}^{2}
.

Proof.

In
P_{1}(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}})
, the variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
have negative exponents, the lowest negative exponent being
\deg_{m}(P_{1})\leq M-1
. Hence
(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{M-1}P_{1}(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}})
is a polynomial in
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, of maxdegree at most
M
, and of same infinite norm as
P_{1}
. Hence the function
P
defined by

P(\bm{x},\bm{y})=(y_{1}\cdots y_{n})^{M}P_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}\right)P_{2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),

is a polynomial of
\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x},\bm{y}]
, with
\deg_{m}(P)\leq 2M-2
. Moreover, as
P_{2}
has no variable of the form
x_{i}
, when expanding the product of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, both written as a sum of monomials, there is only one way to produce each monomial of
P
: each coefficient of
P
is the product of a coefficient of
P_{1}
and a coefficient of
P_{2}
. Hence

\|P\|_{\infty}\leq\|P_{1}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|P_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq S_{\infty}^{2}
.

The same argument applies for
Q=(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})^{M}Q_{1}(\frac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\ldots,\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}})Q_{2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, except for the additionnal factor
(y_{1}\ldots y_{n})
which increases by
1
the maxdegree:
\deg_{m}(Q)\leq 2M-1

∎

Once we have bounds on the representation of
F
as a quotient of two polynomials, the main computations consist in studying the partial differential equations in
F
by following Lipshitz’s constructions. This is done in the following proposition, whose technical proof is given in the next subsection.

Proposition 89
.

Let
F
be a rational fraction given under the form
F=\frac{P}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, where
P(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
Q(\bm{x},\bm{y})
are two polynomials of
2n
variables with integer coefficients. Let
M=\max(\deg_{m}(P),\deg_{m}(Q))+1
,

S_{\infty}=\max(\|P\|_{\infty},\|Q\|_{\infty})
, and
N=((2n+1)M)^{2n}-2n
. Then
f=[y_{1}^{-1}\ldots y_{n}^{-1}]F
is an element of
\mathbb{Z}(\bm{x})
that satisfies for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
a non-trivial partial differential equation in
x_{j}
with polynomial coefficients (the
p_{i}
’s and
r
depends on
j
):

p_{r}(\bm{x})\partial_{x_{j}}^{r}f(\bm{x})+p_{r-1}(\bm{x})\partial_{x_{j}}^{r-1}f(\bm{x})+\ldots+p_{0}(\bm{x})f(\bm{x})=0,

with
r+\deg_{m}({p_{i}})<N
, and
\log\|p_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 6N^{2n+1}M^{2n}\log(NS_{\infty})
.

The proof of Theorem
86
just consists in combining Lemma
88
and Proposition
89
, keeping in mind that Lemma
88
doubles the maxdegree bound and squares the infinite norm bound.

F.6.2
Proof of Proposition
89

In this section, we prove Proposition
89
. We thus use the notations of its statement. We start with three technical lemmas.

Lemma 90
.

Let
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2n})\in\mathbb{N}^{2n}
. We denote by
\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}
the operator defined by
\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}\partial_{y_{1}}^{\alpha_{n+1}}\ldots\partial_{y_{n}}^{\alpha_{2n}}
. There exists a polynomial
P_{\bm{\alpha}}
of
\mathbb{Z}[\bm{x},\bm{y}]
such that
Q^{s+1}\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}F=P_{\bm{\alpha}}
,
\deg_{m}(P_{\bm{\alpha}})\leq(s+1)(M-1)
and

\|P_{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{s}s!M^{s(2n+1)}S_{\infty}^{s+1}
,
where
s=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}
.

Proof.

By induction on
s=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}
. If
s=0
, then
QF=P
with
\deg_{m}(P)<M
, and
\|P\|_{\infty}\leq S_{\infty}
.

If the proposition is true for
s-1
, let
\bm{\alpha}
be such that
\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}=s
. We write
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\beta}+\bm{e_{i}}
, for some
i
such that
\alpha_{i}>0
, and we have
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}=s-1
. By induction hypothesis,
Q^{s}\partial_{\bm{\beta}}F=P_{\bm{\beta}}
, where
P_{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies the hypotheses for
s-1
. Deriving this relation according to
x_{i}
yields:

(\partial_{x_{i}}Q)sQ^{s-1}\partial_{\bm{\beta}}F+Q^{s}\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}F=\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}.

By multiplying by
Q
, we obtain

Q^{s+1}\partial_{\bm{\alpha}}F=Q\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}-(\partial_{x_{i}}Q)sQ^{s}\partial_{\bm{\beta}}F=Q\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}-(\partial_{x_{i}}Q)sP_{\bm{\beta}}.

Let
P_{\bm{\alpha}}=Q\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}-(\partial_{x_{i}}Q)sP_{\bm{\beta}}
. The polynomial

P_{\bm{\alpha}}
has integer coefficients, and

\deg_{m}(P_{\bm{\alpha}})\leq M-1+\deg_{m}(P_{\bm{\beta}})\leq(s+1)(M-1)
, as
\deg_{m}(P_{\bm{\beta}})\leq s(M-1)
by induction hypothesis. Moreover, by Lemma
75
, we have

\|Q\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leq M^{2n}\|Q\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\partial_{x_{i}}P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leq M^{2n}\|Q\|_{\infty}\cdot s(M-1)\|P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}.

And similarly,

\|(\partial_{x_{i}}Q)sP_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leq M^{2n}\|\partial_{x_{i}}Q\|_{\infty}\cdot s\|P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leq M^{2n+1}\|Q\|_{\infty}\cdot s\|P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}.

Thus,
\|P_{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{\infty}\leq 2M^{2n+1}s\|Q\|_{\infty}\cdot\|P_{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}
. This concludes the proof as
P_{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies the induction hypothesis.
∎

Lemma 91
.

Let
N=((2n+1)M)^{2n}-2n
,
let
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
be two vectors of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{i}\beta_{i}+\gamma<N
. Let also
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. There exists a polynomial
R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta},\gamma,j}
of variables
x_{1}
,…,
x_{n}
,
y_{1}
, …

\deg(p_{i})\leq(dM)^{O(d)}
and
\log(\|p_{i}\|)\leq(dM)^{O(d^{2})}
.

By Proposition
81
,
H
satisfies a non-trivial differential equation of the form:

q_{r}(x)\partial^{r}H(x)+\cdots+q_{0}(x)H(x)=0

with
r\leq(dM)^{O(d)}
,
\deg(p_{i})\leq(dM)^{O(d)}
and
\log(\|p_{i}\|)\leq(dM)^{O(d^{2})}
.

By Proposition
80
, its coefficients satisfy a recurrence equation of the form:

\sum_{k=-s}^{S}t_{k}(n)u_{n+k}=0,\,\text{for $n\geq s$, with $t_{S}\not=0$}

with
s\leq(dM)^{O(d)}
,
S\leq(dM)^{O(d)}
and
\log(\|t_{S}\|_{\infty})\leq(dM)^{O(d^{2})}
.

Hence we can take
W=2^{2^{O(d^{2}\log(dM))}}
.

∎

Appendix I
Algorithmic consequences: the inclusion problem

The aim of this section is to prove our announced result on the complexity of testing the inclusion:

See
24

To prove this statement, we need some preliminary algorithms to be able to count the numbers of accepted words of a fixed size
n
in a weakly-unambiguous automaton.

I.1
Counting the runs in a vector automaton

In this section, we are given a vector automaton
\mathcal{V}=(Q,q_{I},F,\Delta)
over
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. We recall that
Q
is the set of states,
q_{I}
the initial state,
F
the final state,
\Delta
the set of transitions of the form
(q,\bm{v},q^{\prime})
with
\bm{v}\neq\bm{0}
. We denote by
\|\mathcal{V}\|_{\infty}
the greatest coordinate appearing in the vectors in
\Delta
.

For two vectors
\bm{v},\bm{v^{\prime}}
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we note
\bm{v}\preceq\bm{v^{\prime}}
if for every
i\in[d]
,
v_{i}\leq v^{\prime}_{i}
.

For every
q\in Q
, for every vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we denote by
q[\bm{v}]
the number of runs in the automaton from
q_{I}
to
q
labeled by the vector
\bm{v}
.

The goal of this section is to compute, for any given a bound
K
, the numbers
q[\bm{v}]
for all state
q\in Q
, and all vector
\bm{v}
such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq K
.

A naive approach consists in enumerating every run of length at most
(K+1)^{d}
in the automaton, and keep track of the vectors labelling them, leading to an algorithm exponential in
K
. To improve on this, first observe that the numbers
q[\bm{v}]
for
q\in Q
,
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
verify the following recurrence:

q[\bm{v}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q^{\prime},\bm{u},q)\in\Delta\\
\text{ with }\bm{u}\preceq\bm{v}\end{subarray}}q^{\prime}[\bm{v}-\bm{u}].

(8)

Using Equation (
8
) and
dynamic programming, we get the following statement.

Proposition 94
.

Given
K\in\mathbb{N}
and a vector automaton
\mathcal{V}=(Q,q_{I},F,\Delta)
, we can compute all the values
q[\bm{v}]
for
q\in Q
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq K
in
\mathcal{O}((K+1)^{2d}|\Delta|\log{|\Delta|})
time.

Proof.

Notice that since there are no transitions labeled by
\bm{0}
, a run in
\mathcal{V}
labeled by
\bm{v}
verifying
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq K
takes at most
(K+1)^{d}
transitions. So
q[\bm{v}]\leq|\Delta|^{(K+1)^{d}}
for every
q\in Q
and every
\bm{v}
such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq K
.

We simply unroll the recurrence formula in Equation (
8
), by enumerating all vectors
\bm{v}
such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq K
in lexicographic order.
Enumerating such vectors is done with
(K+1)^{d}
iterations. Inside these iterations, we make at most
|\Delta|
operations consisting in computing the vectors
\bm{v}-\bm{u}
, which is done in
\mathcal{O}(d\log K)
, and adding
q^{\prime}[\bm{v}-\bm{u}]
to
q[\bm{v}]
, which is done in
\mathcal{O}((K+1)^{d}\log|\Delta|)
.
∎

I.2
Counting the accepted words in a weakly-unambiguous automaton

In this section, we want an algorithmic solution to count the total numbers of words of length at most
n\in\mathbb{N}
accepted by a given weakly-unmabiguous automaton.

Proposition 95
.

Given a weakly-unambiguous PA
\mathcal{B}=(Q,q_{I},F,C,\Delta)
of dimension
d
, without
\varepsilon
-transitions, the total number of words of length at most
n
accepted by
\mathcal{B}

can be computed in
(n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{B}|)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
time.

Proof.

We rely on the same constructions as in the proof of Lemma
93
. First, from
\mathcal{B}
we can build the vector automaton
\mathcal{V}
of dimension
d+1
for the paths in
\mathcal{B}
from
q_{I}
to a final state: such a path
(w,\bm{v})
in
\mathcal{B}
is in one-to-one relation with a path labeled
(|w|,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})
in
\mathcal{V}
. By construction,
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{B}
share the same transition system. As there is no
\varepsilon
-transitions in
\mathcal{B}
, any run labeled by
(w,\bm{v})
has length
|w|
and is such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq|w|\,\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}
. Hence, applying Proposition
94
to
\mathcal{V}
with
K=n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}
we can compute in time
\mathcal{O}((n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+1)^{2d+2}|\Delta|\log|\Delta|)=\mathcal{O}((n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+1)^{2d+2}|\mathcal{B}|\log|\mathcal{B}|)=(n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{B}|)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
every
q[\bm{v}]
for
q\in Q
and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}
such that
\|v\|_{\infty}\leq n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}
.

For the semilinear part, we use the construction of the automaton
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, also given in the proof of Lemma
93
. For a unambiguous representation

C=\cup_{i=1}^{p}C_{i}
, where
C_{i}=\bm{c_{i}}+P_{i}^{*}
, the associated vector automaton
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
has
p+1
states,
s_{I}
the initial state and
s_{1},\ldots s_{p}
for the
C_{i}

94
, we can compute all the
s_{i}[\bm{v}]
, for all
i
and all
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}\|_{\infty}
, also in time
(n\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{B}|)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
.
As
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is unambiguous,
s_{i}[\bm{v}]
can only value
1
or
0
, whether
\bm{v}
is in
C_{i}
or not (except for
s_{I}[\bm{v}]

iff
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{0}\in C

Finally, to obtain the total number of words of length
k\leq n
accepted by the
\mathcal{B}

for every state
q\in F

\bm{v^{\prime}}=[k,\bm{v}]

I.3
Proof of Corollary
24

We can finally prove the statement.

Proof of of Corollary
24
.

As sketched in the main presentation, the proof relies on the fact that
L(\mathcal{A})\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
if and only if
L(\mathcal{A})\cap L(\mathcal{B})=L(\mathcal{A})
. By Theorem
23
, if

L(\mathcal{A})\cap L(\mathcal{B})\neq L(\mathcal{A})
, there exists a word
u\in L(\mathcal{A})
that is not in
L(\mathcal{A})\cap L(\mathcal{B})
of length at most

2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d^{2}\log(dM))}}
, where
d=d_{\mathcal{A}}+d_{\mathcal{B}}
and

M=|\mathcal{A}|\,|\mathcal{B}|\,\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}
: we get this bound by studying the maxdegree and infinite norm of the product automaton of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
built to recognize the intersection.
It means that there exists some constant
c>0
such that looking for words up to size
W=\lfloor 2^{2^{cd^{2}\log(dM)}}\rfloor
is sufficient to decide whether

L(\mathcal{A})\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
or not.

Thus, we just have to compute the number of words in

L(\mathcal{A})\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
and
L(\mathcal{A})
up to size
W
to check if they are equal. By Proposition
95
, this can be done in time
(WM)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
. Hence there exists some constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that

(WM)^{\mathcal{O}(d}\leq(WM)^{c^{\prime}d}\leq\left(2^{2^{cd^{2}\log(dM)}}\right)^{c^{\prime}d}M^{c^{\prime}d}=2^{c^{\prime}d2^{cd^{2}\log(dM)}+c^{\prime}d\log M}\leq 2^{2^{c^{\prime\prime}d^{2}\log(dM)}}.

for a sufficiently large constant
c^{\prime\prime}
. This concludes the proof.
