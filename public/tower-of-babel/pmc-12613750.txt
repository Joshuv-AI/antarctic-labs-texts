Molecular identification and associated risk factors of Hepatozoon canis among dogs visiting veterinary clinics in Tehran, Iran
Authors: Iradj Ashrafi Tamai, Hamid Staji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatozoon canis is a protozoan parasite with a complex life cycle, involving asexual development in vertebrate hosts and sexual reproduction within tick vectors. This study aimed to explore the prevalence and associated risk factors of H. canis in dogs admitted to veterinary clinics around in Tehran province, Iran. Blood samples were collected from dogs, including clinically asymptomatic and symptomatic individuals, and screened using PCR targeting the 18 S rRNA gene. Molecular analysis revealed 33 Hepatozoon-positive cases from 170 samples (19.4%; 95% CI: 13.5–25.4%), demonstrated significantly higher prevalence in rural and suburban environments, among female dogs aged 1–13 years. The most frequent clinical manifestations were also for lymphadenomegaly, fever, and muscular pain. While phylogenetic analysis demonstrated isolate clustering patterns consistent with established Hepatozoon species, alignment of partial 18 S rRNA gene sequences revealed both conserved domains and polymorphic sites, featuring nucleotide variations at 33 distinct positions. Our findings emphasize also the importance of molecular tools in diagnosing and characterizing infections caused by Hepatozoon, offering insights into the epidemiology and genetic diversity of this pathogen in canine populations around the world. These results also underscore the need for further studies on the ecology, transmission, and control strategies for H. canis in endemic regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05118-8.Keywords: Dogs, Genetic diversity, Hepatozoon canis, Iran, Protozoan parasites

Introduction

Hepatozoon species belong to the phylum Apicomplexa, order Eucoccidiorida, suborder Adeleorina, and family Hepatozoidae [1]. The Hepatozoon genus has more than 340 species [2]. These tick-borne protozoan parasites mainly infect a variety of vertebrate hosts, including mammals, reptiles, birds, and amphibians [3, 4]. Among the various species within this genus, domestic dogs and other wild canids worldwide are common hosts of that can be infected by H. canis [5, 6]. Tropical, subtropical, or mild climates are more likely to have autochthonous diseases with H. canis [7]. Wild vertebrates play a role in the transmission cycles of numerous pathogens and can also influence arthropod vector abundance [8, 9]. Unlike many other tick-borne diseases, where the route of transmission from ticks to the host is primarily hematophagous, H. canis is primarily transmitted orally by ingestion of infected ticks, specifically the brown dog tick (Rhipicephalus sanguineus), which acts as the definitive host [10]. It is also thought that self-grooming or feeding on infected prey can facilitate infection [9].

In addition to tick ingestion, it has been shown that pathogens can be passed from mother to offspring by transplacental transmission, further complicating the epidemiology of this parasite. The complex life cycle of H. canis incorporates both asexual development (merogony) within the vertebrate host and sexual reproduction (gamogony) inside the tick vector [10]. Clinically, H. canis infections exhibit a broad spectrum of manifestations, extending from asymptomatic conditions in healthy dogs to severe systemic disease, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or those with concurrent infections [11]. The most common clinical symptoms include anorexia, fever, depression, weight loss, and lymphadenopathy [12]. However, in hepatozoonosis, nonregenerative anemia, leukocytosis (with neutrophilia and monocytosis), or mild thrombocytopenia are often less severe. Hematological abnormalities such as eosinophilia, leukocytosis, and anemia are commonly observed, especially in co-infection [13, 14]. In histopathological specimens, the parasite’s micro-merozoites can form the characteristic “wheel spoke” pattern [15]. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that dogs naturally infected with H. canis can sometimes show no clinical signs. In cases of high parasitemia, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and severe systemic impacts may occur, compromising overall well-being and the quality of life in infected dogs [12].

Generally, H. canis infection rates and genetic diversity vary widely worldwide, determined by geographic, ecological, and host-associated variables. Recent research has emphasized the parasite’s presence in various canine populations, including domestic dogs, police dogs, and wild canids [16]. Among the diagnostic approaches, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a molecular method, has surpassed other techniques due to its heightened sensitivity and specificity in detecting target pathogens in peripheral blood. In addition to genetic characterization and sequencing, phylogenetic analysis has been developed to better understand the evolutionary relationships of different H. canis strains [17]. Furthermore, biochemical tests and blood parameters have been examined, and histopathological variations in infected tissues from freshly deceased dogs have been considered [18]. At present, information on H. canis in dog populations remains limited, and many aspects of its epidemiology and pathogenesis are poorly understood. The available data present discrepancies, as some sources indicate that molecular techniques (e.g., PCR) provide more dependable outcomes compared to smear preparation and microscopic examination [18, 19], whereas while opposing views argue otherwise [20]. Furthermore, the scientific literature presents conflicting evidence regarding age predisposition. Interestingly, some studies have detected an association between older dog age [21] and Hepatozoon infection, others have categorically refuted this correlation [22]. The age-related observed pattern may probably reflect greater pathogen exposure in older dogs, consistent with documented higher infection rates among stray and rural dog populations in several studies [19, 20]. In parallel with these findings, sex has been shown to be an inconsistent risk factor, alternately demonstrating significant effects in some studies [23] and no discernible association in others [19]. Although some studies have begun to explore genetic characterization, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis, data remain scarce. Genotypes, the genetic constitution of an organism, serve as the blueprint for phenotypic variation, shaping observable characteristics and functional diversity. Genetic variability is a fundamental aspect of biological diversity, driving adaptation, evolution, and species resilience [24].

Understanding genetic differences among individuals or populations provides critical insights into traits such as disease resistance, environmental adaptability, and evolutionary potential. Therefore, the aim of this study, in addition to isolating H. canis from tick-infested dogs and conducting their phylogenetic analysis, is to also investigate associated risk factors.

Materials and methods

Collection of samples

Blood samples were gathered throughout March 2024 and October 2024 from 170 dogs that were brought to veterinary clinics for standard medical checkups to perform this survey (Fig. 1). The animals were privately owned by separate farm, and informed consents were obtained from the owners to use the animals in this study. The clinics were dispersed around Tehran and its suburbs. Accordingly, the study evaluated clinic-presenting dogs that had confirmed tick infestations for the clinical signs documented in the Table 1. Each dog underwent a physical examination in which the body condition, attitude, capillary refill time, temperature, mucous membrane color, and clinical signs of H. canis were assessed. Each dog’s owner completed a questionnaire (Supplementary 1) that asked about the dog’s breed, age, gender, living situation (rural or urban), and location of residence (province). Information about the use of chemoprophylaxis methods, history of tick infestation, and frequency of treatment with external antiparasitic was also requested. The sample has been obtained directly from the animal for this study. Blood samples (about 2–3 mL) were taken by using clean needles to draw blood from a vein, and the blood was put into special tubes with EDTA. First, the thin blood smears were prepared from all samples and stained with the Giemsa method.

DNA extraction and PCR analysis

DNA from each sample was extracted by using the DNA Blood & Tissue Kit (SinaClon, Iran) with some modifications. The time allotted for incubation was overnight, following DNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s protocols. PCR master mix was prepared for a total volume of 25 µl; including 2.5 µl DEPC-treated water, 12.5 µl master mix (Cat No. A180306, AMPLIQON), 10 pmol of each primer (BIONEER, Korea) (HepF: 5’- ATACATGAGCAAAATCTCAAC A-3’ and HepR: 5’- CTTATTATTCCATGCTGCAG − 3’) for detection of 18 S rRNA (666 bp) [25], and 2 µl of DNA template. The amplification reactions were carried out in a thermocycler (BIORAD T-100) as follows: an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 10 min, then 30 cycles of 95 ℃ for 30 s, 58℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s, and a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. Five microliters of the amplification products were electrophoresed for one hour at 100 V on a 1.5% agarose gel in 1X TBE buffer. A UV-trans-illuminator (BIORAD, UK) was then used to screen the agarose gel after it had been dyed with 1 µg/ml ethidium bromide (CinnaClon, Iran). The genomic DNA of H. canis isolated from a tick was a positive control (the Microbial culture collection of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, University of Tehran) and negative controls (sterile distilled water) consisting of the PCR mixture without addition of DNA templates were included in all PCR.

Primers were chosen based on the reference publication, demonstrating specificity for both genus and species identification. As indicated in the Fig. 3, the band weight also corresponds to approximately 666 base pairs (bp).

Agarose gel electrophoresis of polymerase chain reaction products of 18 S rRNA gene (666 bp) of H. canis. Lane C-: Negative control, Lane C+: Positive control, Lanes 1–5: PCR products amplified, Lane M: 100 bp DNA ladder

Using the same pair of primers as for PCR, positive samples were purified and sequenced in both directions using the Sanger’s method on an automated sequencer, the ABI-PRISM 377 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Sequences obtained were analyzed utilizing Geneious Prime software (https://www.geneious.com) and compared with each other and with other sequences accessible within the GenBank database through Blast http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, Bethesda, MD, USA) BankIt v3.0 submission tool (http://www3.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BankIt/, accessed on 29 November 2024) was used to deposit Hepatozoon 18 S rRNA gene sequences into the GenBank.

The evolutionary history was reconstructed using the Neighbor-Joining method. The resulting optimal phylogenetic tree, with a total branch length of 0.23026072, is presented. Bootstrap analysis, performed with 1000 replicates, indicates the percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa formed consistent clusters, and these values are displayed along the branches [26]. The phylogenetic tree is depicted to scale, with branch lengths represented in the same units as the evolutionary distances used for its construction. These evolutionary distances, calculated using the p-distance method, are expressed as the number of nucleotide substitutions per site [27]. As samples were collected from different areas across the extensive city of Tehran, multiple (2 to 3) samples per region was chosen for sequencing. Accordingly, the study analyzed a total of 33 nucleotide sequences, with the coding data translated based on the Standard Genetic Code. Positions with gaps or missing data were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a final dataset comprising 169 positions. Evolutionary analyses were performed using MEGA7 software [28].

Statistical analysis

In addition to descriptive statistics, inferential analyses were performed to assess the association between potential risk factors and Hepatozoon canis infection. Categorical variables (sex, age group, use of ectoparasiticides, environment, and clinical signs) were first analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of infection. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Collection of samples and detection of Hepatozoon spp. by PCR

This study conducted on 170 dogs visiting veterinary clinics in Tehran for general examinations and vaccinations found that, based on initial check-ups and owner-provided histories, 137 dogs were identified as either currently infested with ticks or having a previous history of infestation. Table 1 presents the general and clinical data of the dogs involved in the study, including their sex, age, living environment, and clinical status. Among the subjects, 63 dogs displayed no clinical symptoms. However, the remaining dogs exhibited signs such as lethargy, fever, anorexia, and lymphadenopathy upon more detailed examination. Through blood smear analysis with Giemsa staining, seven samples were reported as positive (Figs. 2 and 3; Supplementary 2). Subsequently, 18 S rRNA analysis revealed that 33 samples were infected with Hepatozoon parasites (33/170, 19.4%). It is noteworthy that all seven smear-positive samples were included within this group of PCR-positive cases.

Out of the positive samples, 24.51% were female, and the main cases were aged between 1 and 13 years old. A higher prevalence of positive cases was observed in suburban and rural areas, where dogs had greater freedom to roam and were exposed to more favorable environmental conditions compared to urban settings. The most important clinical symptoms were also related to increased lymphadenomegaly and muscular pain, rectal temperature, respectively. In dogs with hepatozoonosis, no significant changes in hematological and biochemical values were shown.

The overall prevalence of H. canis infection was 19.4%. Univariate analysis revealed that both sex and environment were significantly associated with infection, whereas age group, ectoparasiticide use, and clinical signs showed no significant associations.

In multivariable logistic regression, female dogs were found to have a 2.4-fold higher risk of infection compared with males (OR = 2.4, 95% CI = 1.0–5.7, p = 0.048). Similarly, dogs living in rural areas had a 2.1-fold higher risk compared with those in urban areas (OR = 2.1, 95% CI = 1.0–4.4, p = 0.040). Other variables were not statistically significant in the final model. The full results are presented in (Table 1).

Discussion

Hepatozoon species are parasitic hemoprotozoan organisms that infect domestic and wild animals all over the world [29]. A few complete surveys have been conducted, which consisted of case reports or kennel studies. As part of research studies and a control program on diseases transmitted by ticks, the present study analyzed blood samples collected from 170 dogs in 2024 from different collection sites in Tehran, Iran. Additionally, 18 S rRNA analysis confirmed Hepatozoon infection in mentioned 33 PCR-positive samples (19.4%; 95% CI: 13.5–25.4%), including 7 cases that were also microscopy-positive smears. Consistent with other studies from Iran, these findings indicate a comparable prevalence of canine hepatozoonosis to that reported in other regions of the country. Generally, among the available direct detection methods, molecular techniques demonstrate superior sensitivity and specificity for identifying Hepatozoon species compared to other diagnostic approaches, such as blood smear analysis [30]. Although gamont represents the final stage of the parasite’s development within the vertebrate intermediate host, they are occasionally found in blood smears. It can be related to the fact that the parasite is visible at the early stage of infection, chronic aparasitemic infection, or low parasitemia [31]. Moreover, the Hepatozoon prevalence rate varies depending on weather, the presence of ticks in the region, and hygiene in the area. Accordingly, in this study, all cases that were found to have ticks during the clinical examination were included for monitoring and screening of parasitic diseases as part of the physical evaluation [10, 15, 30].

Our results support previous evidence that molecular methods provide higher sensitivity than blood smear examination, as only 7 cases were microscopy-positive compared with 33 PCR-positive samples. Similar findings have been reported in other Iranian and international studies, highlighting the reliability of PCR for the detection of Hepatozoon spp. For instance, one PCR-based study in Ardabil, Iran, found a 23.07% prevalence of H. canis (24/104 samples), aligning with our results [21]. Another study in Urmia, northwestern Iran, using microscopy and PCR on 246 blood samples, detected Hepatozoon spp. gamonts in 2.03% (5/246) and H. canis DNA in 9.34% (23/246) of samples, respectively [22]. Further corroborating this, Soltani et al. (2018) reported no microscopic detection but a 22.1% PCR-based prevalence (32/145 samples) in Tehran, Iran, underscoring the limitations of microscopy and the higher sensitivity of molecular methods [19]. Contrary to our findings, a study on stray dogs in northeastern Iran reported a higher diagnostic concordance between microscopy and PCR. Among 150 blood samples, microscopy detected Hepatozoon gamonts in 3.3% (5/150) of cases, while PCR identified Hepatozoon spp. DNA in 8.0% (12/150). Phylogenetic analysis confirmed these results and demonstrated strong agreement between both methods [20]. Prevalence rates reported in international studies vary widely. For instance, research by Oliveira et al. (2021) in Brazil, El-Dakhly et al. (2013) in Japan, Khalifa et al. (2023) reported that Hepatozoon gamont infection rate was of 8.1%, 23.6% and 30%, respectively, in dog peripheral blood samples. This variation in the prevalence of H. canis was probably caused by various ecological conditions, including the degree of tick manifestation [32–34]. Consistent with these trends, Aktas et al. (2015) detected Hepatozoon gamonts via direct microscopy in only 1.0% (3/285) of samples, and further suggested a correlation between infection status and host age as well as geographical origin [35].

While some studies report no significant age-related association with H. canis infection, our findings indicate a higher prevalence in young (>1 age) dogs, consistent with another certain previous reports [35, 36]. This aligns with broader patterns observed in dog populations, as stray dogs consistently demonstrate significantly higher infection rates compared to pet and shelter dogs across multiple studies. However, discrepancies exist among specific demographic analyses. For example, one study found higher (though statistically insignificant) infection rates in older and male dogs [19], while another study in Urmia Municipality reported no significant differences in infection rates across age or sex groups [22]. Despite these variations in demographic patterns, the consistently elevated infection rates among stray dogs highlight the critical role of environmental exposure and tick infestation risk. This study found that 14.5% of positive cases were female. While this contrasts with earlier investigations reporting no sex-based predilection for hepatozoonosis in dogs, we suggest these discrepancies may arise from differing environmental conditions leading to variable tick exposure [37]. To further investigate this theory, a follow-up study assessed the molecular prevalence of H. canis in ticks collected from stray dogs in Kerman, southeastern Iran. Among 50 tick-infested free-ranging dogs (24 females, 26 males, aged 5 months to 6 years), H. canis DNA was detected in 14 tick pools (28%). These findings support the use of tick-based molecular screening as a reliable alternative to blood sampling for surveillance. The study also emphasized the importance of regular dog examinations as part of integrated external parasite control strategies [23].

Our findings indicate that female sex and living in rural environments are independent predictors of H. canis infection in dogs. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting higher prevalence in rural dogs, likely due to increased exposure to tick vectors [38]. The observed sex difference may reflect behavioral or hormonal factors influencing susceptibility, although further studies are needed to clarify this association. Importantly, other potential risk factors, including age, ectoparasiticide use, and the presence of clinical signs, were not significant predictors in the multivariable model.

A molecular analysis confirmed the presence of H. canis in dogs from Shiraz, Iran, with all amplified sequences showing 100% homology among themselves and 98.47%–100% identity to reference sequences from GenBank [18]. Consistent with the study by Zoaktafi et al., this finding represents the first documented occurrence of H. canis in this region. However, beyond this consensus, a subset of isolates in our study displayed slightly lower similarity (96%–97%) to reference strains. This pattern mirrors reports from Turkey, where sequences with ≈ 95%–97% identity were provisionally designated as Hepatozoon sp. MF [38].

Accordingly, a separate study in Turkey found eight blood samples (3.61%) positive for Hepatozoon. Genetic analysis revealed six sequences had 98–99% similarity to H. canis, while two others showed only 95–97% similarity, leading to their classification as Hepatozoon sp. MF. BLAST analysis against Turkish H. canis sequences indicated these likely represent a novel genotype. This finding of nucleotide differentiation contrasts with earlier studies that reported uniform 18 S rRNA sequences and aligns with our preliminary data from Tehran [38]. The nucleotide sequence homology analysis of the 21 isolates obtained in this study, together with two Spanish isolates, revealed a very high level of similarity, ranging from 96% to 100%. Such a high degree of sequence identity indicates that the isolates are genetically closely related and likely belong to the same species or closely associated lineages. Minor variations observed in 7 isolates (96–97%) suggest the presence of limited genetic diversity, which may reflect natural geographic or host-associated variations. Interestingly, the Spanish isolates showed more than 97% similarity with the isolates analyzed in this study, further supporting the notion of a conserved genetic background across different geographical regions. These findings highlight the genetic stability of the organism and provide valuable insights into its evolutionary relationships [28]. These observations point the need for broader sampling, including ticks and wildlife, and extended sequencing of longer 18 S rRNA regions or additional genetic markers to fully characterize the diversity and taxonomy of Hepatozoon spp. in Iran. Together, these results confirm the genetic variability of H. canis in the region, highlighting a need for expanded molecular surveillance and tick control to manage canine hepatozoonosis. This pathogen also infects a wide range of carnivores, including jackals, foxes, and domestic cats [32, 39, 40]. However, researching its presence in wildlife remains challenging due to the elusive nature of wild canids. The transmission of H. canis to dogs is facilitated by the brown dog tick, Rhipicephalus sanguineus sensu lato. This tick, which is the most prevalent species in Tehran, thrives year-round in the region’s favorable climate [9, 15, 34]. While H. canis infection is often associated with mild non-regenerative anemia or thrombocytopenia in other studies, our analysis of 33 positive dogs revealed no such hematological abnormalities [8, 10, 34]. These clinical changes are typically more pronounced during co-infections with pathogens like Ehrlichia, Babesia, or Anaplasma.

Although these data remain preliminary, we anticipate that future studies will further explore this field. The development and implementation of molecular differential approaches will be essential prerequisites for establishing reliable diagnostic methods to track Hepatozoon spp. infections. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze genetic variability in H. canis, contributing to the expanding knowledge of Hepatozoon species. Specifically, we sought to characterize molecular diversity within these parasites to enhance understanding of their epidemiology and evolution.

Conclusion

Despite the use of antiparasitic drugs, dogs in Iran remain at high risk of tick-borne hepatozoonosis. Many stray, shelter, and owned dogs lack adequate protection against external parasites such as ticks, fleas, mosquitoes, and sandflies, which increases the chance of disease transmission and environmental contamination. Since no treatment fully eliminates the parasite, hepatozoonosis often persists as a chronic infection. Analyzing genetic diversity helps veterinarians gain a deeper understanding of the epidemiology, transmission dynamics, and evolutionary relationships of Hepatozoon species. This knowledge enables them to identify different strains or genotypes, trace sources of infection, and develop more targeted diagnostic and treatment strategies for affected animals.
