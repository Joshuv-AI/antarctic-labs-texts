Association between sodium–chloride difference at ICU admission and 30-day mortality: a retrospective cohort study of critically ill adults
Authors: Rafał Świstek, Jakub Konieczyński, Jakub Droś, Patryk Kasongo, Wojciech Szpunar, Tomasz Lonc, Anna Kwinta, Rafał Depukat, Elżbieta Glück, Joanna Jóźwik, Marcin Zuwała, Patrycja Machno, Paulina Górnicka, Jan Potoniec, Justyna Chomej, Emilia Torbus, Aleksandra Kowalska, Alicja Hojda, Ewa Imielska, Alicja Warecka, Kacper Melka, Aleksandra Sielska, Gabriela Piotrowska, Alicja Włodarczyk, Błażej Mioduszewski, Anna Górowska, Tomasz Drygalski, Michał Terlecki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe difference between serum sodium and chloride concentrations (SCD) is a simple parameter reflecting the interplay between major extracellular electrolytes. We investigated whether SCD assessed upon intensive care unit (ICU) admission holds predictive value for short-term outcomes in critically ill adults.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 1,726 consecutive patients admitted to a mixed (medical-surgical) ICU (median age 67 years; 70.7% male). The relationship between admission SCD and 30-day mortality was first assessed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to explore non-linearity. To define potential thresholds, cut-off values were evaluated using ROC analysis with the Youden index. Cox proportional hazards models were then applied. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations.ResultsRCS analysis revealed a non-linear association between SCD and mortality, with risk increasing markedly at lower values. Both spline modeling and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis consistently indicated 30 mmol/L as the threshold of prognostic significance. Patients with SCD < 30 mmol/L (n = 343; 19.9%) had significantly higher 30-day mortality compared with those with SCD ≥ 30 mmol/L (55.7% vs. 44.3%, p < 0.001). In Cox regression, SCD < 30 mmol/L was associated with increased mortality (unadjusted HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.66; p < 0.001). This relationship remained robust after adjustment for age, SOFA score, lactate, and major comorbidities (adjusted HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.24–1.77; p < 0.001) and was confirmed by bootstrap validation.ConclusionsLow SCD at ICU admission was independently associated with higher 30-day mortality. This routinely available parameter, reflecting the sodium–chloride interplay, may support early risk stratification in this group of patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03438-8.

Introduction

Disturbances in serum electrolyte levels and imbalances in acid–base status are frequently observed among patients in critical condition [1]. Chloride, being the predominant extracellular anion, contributes to the majority of all strong anionic charges and all negative charges in plasma (Fig. 1). Although it is involved in several physiological processes—such as acid–base regulation, osmotic balance, and neuromuscular function—its clinical significance is often underrecognized, especially compared to other electrolytes, resulting in the unintentional underestimation of its clinical importance [2]. In routine clinical practice, serum chloride is seldom prioritized, typically remaining a background parameter rather than a central focus in diagnostic or therapeutic strategies [3, 4].

Conversely, sodium is the primary extracellular cation and plays a crucial role in numerous physiological pathways, particularly in maintaining fluid balance [5]. Its concentration in serum has long been investigated as a marker of clinical severity, and both hypo- and hypernatremia are known to correlate with unfavourable outcomes in critically ill populations [6–9].

Although electrolyte disturbances involving both sodium and chloride are common, the data remain inconclusive. Various studies report differing conclusions, and comprehensive studies investigating these associations are still lacking [10]. Some findings support a correlation between sodium and chloride levels, implying potential interdependence [11]. Others suggest that alterations in chloride alone may independently influence prognosis. However, most studies fail to explain the underlying mechanisms responsible for these observations, leaving the clinical relevance of these interactions uncertain [12, 13].

One pragmatic approach that may help clarify these relationships is the evaluation of the sodium–chloride difference (SCD). This parameter has been proposed as a clinically accessible surrogate reflecting the balance between major extracellular cations and anions [14, 15].

Our previous work demonstrated the prognostic value of SCD in patients with acute myocardial infarction [16]. In the present study, we sought to build upon those findings by evaluating SCD in a broader and more heterogeneous population of critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). The prognostic implications of SCD remain underexplored in this setting. Given this gap in knowledge, we aimed to investigate the prognostic value of SCD assessed in critically ill patients upon admission to the ICU.

Methods

Ethical declaration

The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Jagiellonian University Medical College (Kraków, Poland; approval no. 118.0043.1.160.2024) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for obtaining informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations: not applicable. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective analysis of clinical records from adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) admitted consecutively to the ICU of the University Hospital in Kraków, Poland, between January 2020 and December 2022. We assessed serum sodium and chloride concentrations based on the first blood sample taken immediately upon ICU admission. All measurements were performed using the ABL90 FLEX point-of-care testing (POCT) device (Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). For each patient, the sodium–chloride difference (SCD) was calculated from the admission value and used for all analyses. Additional clinical data—covering patient demographics, comorbidities, course of hospitalization, laboratory findings, treatment modalities, and in-hospital outcomes—were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical documentation system. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was determined for each patient based on data collected within the first 24 h following ICU admission. Mortality within 30 days after ICU admission was determined based on information retrieved from the National Electronic Population Registration System in Poland. In a post-hoc analysis, acute kidney injury (AKI) was also evaluated as an additional outcome. AKI was defined using serum creatinine changes based on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, adapted to our dataset: an increase in serum creatinine ≥ 26.5 µmol/L (≥ 0.3 mg/dL) or ≥ 50% from baseline within the first 48 h after ICU admission, or the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) during the ICU stay. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting observational studies [17]. A completed STROBE checklist, indicating where each item is addressed in the manuscript, is provided as Supplementary Table S1. A visual overview of the patient inclusion process is presented in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study data. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on the distribution. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess normality, and Levene’s test was used to evaluate the homogeneity of variances. Comparisons between groups were conducted using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test for normally distributed variables, depending on the equality of variances. For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed. Ordinal data were analyzed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test; when over 20% of expected cell counts were below 5, a Monte Carlo simulation was applied as an alternative to Fisher’s exact test. To explore the relationship between SCD and 30-day mortality, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression modeling, placing knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the SCD distribution. For further descriptive analyses, patients were stratified into two groups according to admission SCD: <30 mmol/L and ≥ 30 mmol/L. The threshold of 30 mmol/L was first identified from RCS regression as the approximate inflection point of risk and was subsequently confirmed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using the Youden index. Baseline characteristics of the whole cohort are presented in Table 1 and compared between SCD strata in Table 2. To examine the association between SCD on admission and 30-day mortality, we constructed Cox proportional hazards regression models. SCD was evaluated both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable using a threshold of 30 mmol/L. The cutoff was determined by the Youden index from ROC analysis and was consistent with the inflection point identified in RCS regression. Two models were specified: model 1: unadjusted and model 2: adjusted for age, SOFA score, lactate on admission, and major comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic hepatic failure, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, active malignancy, and history of stroke). Candidate covariates such as sepsis, mean arterial pressure (MAP), norepinephrine use/dose, and pH were considered but not included in the final model. Sepsis partially overlaps with the SOFA definition of organ dysfunction and is strongly linked with lactate; including it alongside SOFA and lactate led to redundancy and unstable estimates. MAP and norepinephrine dose are already embedded within the cardiovascular subscore of the SOFA score, and pH was highly correlated with lactate, which remained the more robust prognostic marker. We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All variables retained in the final model had VIFs below conventional thresholds (< 3). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical methods. Internal validation of the final model was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. In each resample, a Cox model including SCD group (< 30 vs. ≥30 mmol/L), age, SOFA score, lactate on admission, and comorbidities was fitted, and the distribution of hazard ratio estimates was used to calculate median HR and percentile-based 95% confidence intervals. A post-hoc power analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of our study to detect the observed difference in 30-day mortality between patients with SCD < 30 mmol/L and those with SCD ≥ 30 mmol/L. Based on the total sample size (n = 1,726), the number of events (n = 803), and the observed hazard ratio of 1.43, the calculated statistical power exceeded 80% at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. All statistical procedures were carried out using R software (Version R 4.4.1). Sample size adequacy was confirmed via power analysis conducted in G*Power. Additionally, in a post-hoc analysis, we examined the association between SCD and acute kidney injury (AKI). The incidence of AKI was compared between SCD strata (< 30 vs. ≥30 mmol/L) using chi-square tests. Logistic regression was applied to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. The multivariable model included age, SOFA score, and lactate on admission as covariates. The potential non-linear relationship between SCD and AKI was further assessed using RCS regression.

Results

Clinical characteristics of study subjects

The cohort analyzed in this study encompassed 1726 adult individuals admitted to the ICU over the specified study period. The median age of the population was 67 years (interquartile range: 59–76), with a predominance of male patients (70.7%). Of all admissions, 59.5% were classified as medical, while 40.5% followed surgical procedures. The mean SOFA score was 10.2 ± 3.8. Thirty-day all-cause mortality was observed in 803 cases, corresponding to a rate of 46.5%. Admission laboratory data (point-of-care) showed a median serum sodium of 139.5 mmol/L (IQR 136.5–142.5) and chloride of 106.5 mmol/L (IQR 103.5–109.5). The SCD was 32.5 mmol/L (IQR 30.5–35.0). Baseline characteristics of the entire cohort are summarized in Table 1.

Clinical characteristics and the relationship between SCD and 30-day mortality

RCS were fitted separately for sodium, chloride, and the SCD. Both sodium and chloride individually demonstrated significant non-linear associations with bidirectional mortality risk observed at both low and high values, whereas the SCD showed a monotonic relationship with a clearer prognostic gradient, with a visually apparent cut-off indicating that values below 30 mmol/L were associated with increased 30-day mortality (Fig. 3A–C). This cut-off was further confirmed by ROC analysis, where the Youden index identified 30 mmol/L as the optimal threshold (sensitivity 28.1%, specificity 79.3%; AUC = 0.52). Patients were subsequently stratified into two groups according to SCD level (< 30 mmol/L and ≥ 30 mmol/L). Baseline characteristics of these strata are presented in Table 2. Compared with patients with SCD ≥ 30 mmol/L, those with SCD < 30 mmol/L were older, more frequently female, and had a lower BMI. Importantly, sepsis as the admission diagnosis was more common in the low-SCD group. Other comorbidities and admission categories were similarly distributed. Notably, admission SCD was not associated with differences in key severity markers, including SOFA score, lactate concentration, mean arterial pressure, or norepinephrine dose. Despite this, patients with SCD < 30 mmol/L experienced significantly higher 30-day mortality than those with SCD ≥ 30 mmol/L (Table 3). The prognostic significance of SCD was further evaluated in Cox regression models (Table 4). In the unadjusted analysis, SCD < 30 mmol/L was significantly associated with higher 30-day mortality. This relationship remained robust in the multivariable model adjusted for age, SOFA score, lactate level, and major comorbidities. Bootstrap validation with 1000 iterations confirmed the stability of these results, yielding nearly identical hazard ratio estimates (Table 4).

Association between AKI and SCD

In the post-hoc analysis, RCS regression demonstrated a non-linear association between SCD and the risk of AKI (Supplementary Figure S1). In line with this pattern, AKI occurred more frequently among patients with SCD < 30 mmol/L compared with those with SCD ≥ 30 mmol/L (62.9% vs. 60.2%; p = 0.010; Supplementary Table S2). In logistic regression, SCD < 30 mmol/L was associated with increased odds of AKI (crude OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.09–1.80; p = 0.010), and this relationship remained significant after adjustment for age, SOFA score, and lactate on admission (adjusted OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.05–1.84; p = 0.022; Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the prognostic value of the SCD in a population of critically ill patients admitted to the ICU. Our findings demonstrated a non-linear relationship between SCD values and 30-day mortality, with an elevated risk of death observed in patients whose SCD was below 30.0 mmol/L, even after adjusting for potential confounders. These results support the potential utility of SCD as an early, accessible prognostic indicator in ICU settings and highlight the clinical importance of sodium–chloride dynamics in critically ill individuals.

It is worth emphasizing that we additionally analyzed sodium and chloride separately, each of which showed significant but complex non-linear associations with outcome, limiting their interpretability. In contrast, the SCD integrated the interplay between both ions, resulting in a clearer and more monotonic gradient of risk. This highlights the potential advantage of SCD as a clinically useful marker that provides more consistent prognostic information than sodium or chloride evaluated in isolation. In this context, the ROC-derived performance of SCD appeared modest, which likely reflects the non-linear nature of its relationship with outcome. Nevertheless, the Youden index identified 30 mmol/L as the optimal cut-off, in close agreement with the inflection point observed in RCS analysis. This concordance strengthens the validity of the chosen threshold and supports its clinical interpretability.

Interestingly, patients with low SCD did not differ from those with higher SCD in terms of lactate levels or global severity indices (SOFA score). This finding suggests that SCD provides prognostic information independent of conventional markers of critical illness severity and metabolic stress and underscores the potential complementary value of this marker in risk stratification.

Although ICU patients represent a highly diverse population in terms of diagnoses and disease severity, they share a common need for rapid risk assessment upon admission to guide early management decisions. While established prognostic scoring systems such as APACHE II or SOFA are valuable, they often require the integration of numerous variables, making them less practical for immediate use in emergency contexts. Consequently, there is a pressing need for simple and quickly obtainable biomarkers to support triage and therapeutic planning in this high-risk group [18, 19].

To date, prognostic research in critical care has predominantly focused on dysnatremia, with less attention paid to chloride abnormalities or the functional interplay between these electrolytes. Despite chloride being the primary anion in extracellular fluid, its clinical implications remain underappreciated. However, many of these investigations fail to provide a mechanistic explanation for such associations, and few have examined the combined significance of sodium and chloride concentrations [10–13].

Chloride represents the most abundant anion in the extracellular compartment and has been implicated as a prognostic indicator in various clinical contexts, including sepsis, postoperative states following non-cardiac surgery, and congestive heart failure [20–23].

Moreover, in selected cohorts of critically ill patients, aberrant serum chloride levels have been independently associated with prolonged hospitalization, deterioration of renal function, and increased short-term mortality [10, 12, 21–26]. Nevertheless, the majority of these investigations have not sufficiently elucidated the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms that might account for these associations. This gap in understanding may, in part, reflect the historically marginal role attributed to chloride in clinical assessment. Its contribution to acid–base disturbances and its interaction with other major electrolytes remain underappreciated in daily critical care practice.

Our study focused on SCD as a simple parameter that reflects the balance between sodium and chloride. Unlike more complex physicochemical models, SCD can be rapidly obtained in routine practice and may provide clinically meaningful information at the bedside. Our findings suggest that SCD may function not only as a reflection of acid–base status but also as a meaningful prognostic signal in patients admitted to the ICU.

While several studies have highlighted the predictive relevance of acid–base parameters, most have relied on traditional measures such as the anion gap or base excess. In contrast, investigations into SCD as a surrogate marker of SID remain relatively limited and have yielded mixed conclusions [10]. Some reports indicate that reduced SCD is associated with adverse outcomes such as acute kidney injury or prolonged hospitalization, especially in patients with sepsis or COVID-19, though evidence linking SCD to mortality remains inconsistent [27, 28]. For example, while Cortés-Román et al. identified an SCD < 31 mmol/L as a predictor of 30-day mortality in septic shock, other studies failed to replicate this association in broader ICU cohorts [29].

Furthermore, recent analyses suggest that isolated evaluation of individual electrolyte disturbances may have limited prognostic value compared to integrated assessments like SCD [30]. Notably, hyperchloremia at ICU admission continues to emerge as a robust predictor of mortality, highlighting the need to reconsider how electrolyte data is interpreted in critical care [31, 32]. Drawing unambiguous conclusions about the prognostic utility of SCD or chloride levels alone in mortality prediction remains challenging based on current literature. Our results, at least partially, contribute to addressing this knowledge gap.

Although some clinicians recognize that dysnatremia and dyschloremia do not occur in isolation, clinical practice often assumes that changes in sodium concentration will be accompanied by corresponding changes in serum chloride levels (or vice versa). In our analyses, both sodium and chloride individually demonstrated significant non-linear associations with 30-day mortality. However, evaluating these electrolytes in isolation is limited by their collinearity and by the bidirectional risk observed at both low and high values. In contrast, the SCD provides an integrated and clinically simple measure that yielded a clearer and more consistent association with outcome in our cohort. It is important to emphasize that our study was not designed to test complex physicochemical models of acid–base balance but rather to evaluate SCD as a clinically useful prognostic marker that may outperform sodium or chloride alone in risk assessment.

In our exploratory post-hoc analysis, low SCD was associated with a higher risk of AKI. This observation aligns with prior evidence suggesting that chloride-rich saline solutions may contribute to renal dysfunction, whereas balanced crystalloids are associated with lower rates of AKI and adverse kidney outcomes. For instance, the SMART randomized trial demonstrated that balanced crystalloids reduced the incidence of major adverse kidney events compared with saline [33], and similar findings were reported for urinary biomarkers of kidney injury [34]. Although the BaSICS [35] and SPLIT [36] trials did not demonstrate significant differences in mortality or AKI incidence, the overall trend across trials and meta-analyses supports the hypothesis that prevention of AKI mediates part of the survival benefit observed with balanced fluids [37]. In line with this interpretation, our results suggest that some of the association between low SCD and higher mortality may indeed be mediated through the development of AKI.

Therefore, instead of focusing solely on correcting individual electrolyte imbalances (primarily dysnatremia), the assessment of critically ill patients may benefit from also considering dyschloremia and their combined reflection in SCD. Recognition of a low SCD at admission could serve as an additional signal for clinicians to carefully evaluate acid–base status and overall patient risk. A low SCD may not only indicate electrolyte imbalance but could also reflect impaired tissue perfusion. Its recognition should prompt consideration of broader supportive measures, including circulatory support, fluid supplementation, diuretic therapy, oxygen delivery, and hemoglobin optimization.

It should also be noted that a reduced SCD does not invariably reflect impaired tissue perfusion, as it may also occur in iatrogenic contexts such as isotonic saline administration. Furthermore, whether active restoration of physiologic SCD improves outcomes remains uncertain, since large randomized ICU trials have not demonstrated consistent mortality benefits of balanced crystalloids compared with saline. Finally, SCD should not be regarded as identical to the strong ion difference (SID), but rather as a pragmatic and readily available surrogate.

Finally, our study underscores the potential of SCD to enhance awareness among ICU clinicians regarding fluid therapy strategies in critically ill patients. Although our observational data cannot determine treatment effects, previous large randomized trials have demonstrated that balanced crystalloids may reduce the risk of adverse outcomes compared with saline [33, 38–40]. Although this remains speculative, these results, together with our findings, raise the hypothesis that patients with low SCD may represent a subgroup in whom fluid composition deserves particular attention.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. It was conducted retrospectively at a single tertiary care center, which introduces potential risks of selection and misclassification biases. These inherent limitations of retrospective design may affect the generalizability of our findings. We deliberately focused on the first admission measurement of SCD, as subsequent values may be influenced by early ICU therapies (e.g., fluid resuscitation, diuretics). While dynamic changes in SCD could provide additional prognostic information, these were beyond the scope of our study. Measurements in our study were performed using an ABL90 Flex point-of-care device. Since results obtained from point-of-care devices may differ from those obtained using other point-of-care analyzers or central laboratory systems, especially for calculated parameters such as the SCD, the generalizability of our findings to other settings may be limited. An additional potential limitation of our study is the lack of external validation, which restricts the generalizability of our findings to other ICU populations or clinical settings. However, this limitation is partially mitigated by the internal validation we performed using bootstrap resampling, which demonstrated consistent effect estimates and supports the robustness of our primary results. Furthermore, unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the relationship between SCD and 30-day mortality. Information on the specific causes of death, including circumstances such as withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining therapies, was not available in our dataset. As we relied on national registry data to ascertain 30-day mortality, our analysis was limited to all-cause mortality without further classification. Another limitation is the lack of longitudinal assessment of SCD. Changes in this parameter over time might offer valuable insights into patient trajectory and prognosis, but such dynamics were not captured in this analysis. Additionally, we did not assess the impact of medications, such as diuretics, which are known to alter sodium and chloride concentrations and might influence SCD-related outcomes. Furthermore, information regarding the type and volume of intravenous fluids administered before ICU admission was unavailable, and we were unable to determine whether low SCD reflected an acute imbalance or a chronic condition. Finally, while other markers of acid–base status (such as base excess, anion gap, or albumin-corrected measures) could provide complementary insight, we were unable to analyze these parameters due to incomplete data availability. Therefore, we did not explore the potential associations between SCD and these indicators of acid–base balance—areas that merit future investigation. In addition, the post-hoc AKI analysis has its own limitations. AKI was defined using serum creatinine changes based on KDIGO criteria, but this definition was adapted to our dataset and restricted to the first 48 h after ICU admission, together with the need for CRRT during the ICU stay. We did not have urine output data or longer-term creatinine trends, and therefore, taken together, these limitations underscore the need for prospective, multicenter studies to validate our findings and to further explore the mechanistic pathways linking SCD, AKI, and mortality in critically ill patients.

Conclusions

In critically ill patients admitted to the ICU, a reduced SCD at admission is independently associated with higher 30-day mortality. This simple, routinely available parameter may support early risk stratification in the intensive care setting, and our findings may support the prognostic significance of sodium-chloride interplay in critically ill patients.
