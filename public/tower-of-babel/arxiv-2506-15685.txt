Ignition Phase : Standard Training for Fast Adversarial Robustness
Wang Yu-Hang†, Liu ying, Fang liang, Wang Xuelin, Junkang Guo, Shiwei Li, Lei Gao, Jian Liu†, Wenfei Yin†

Abstract
Adversarial Training (AT) is a cornerstone defense, but many variants overlook foundational feature representations by primarily focusing on stronger attack generation. We introduce Adversarial Evolution Training (AET), a simple yet powerful framework that strategically prepends an Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) phase to conventional AT. We hypothesize this initial ERM phase cultivates a favorable feature manifold, enabling more efficient and effective robustness acquisition. Empirically, AET achieves comparable or superior robustness more rapidly, improves clean accuracy, and cuts training costs by 8-25%. Its effectiveness is shown across multiple datasets, architectures, and when augmenting established AT methods. Our findings underscore the impact of feature pre-conditioning via standard training for developing more efficient, principled robust defenses. Code is available in the supplementary material.

Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable success in numerous domains, such as image recognition and natural language processing
[
23
,
17
,
2
]
. However, since the existence of adversarial examples was first revealed by
Szegedy et al. 2013
, the vulnerability of DNNs to carefully crafted, small adversarial perturbations has become a critical challenge. To enhance the adversarial robustness of models, the research community has explored various strategies, which primarily fall into three categories: data augmentation-based methods
[
11
,
24
]
, modifications to model architectures
[
28
,
30
,
39
,
41
]
, and Adversarial Training (AT)
[
12
,
27
,
45
,
10
,
36
,
16
]
.

Adversarial Training (AT), by incorporating adversarial examples into the training process, is widely regarded as one of the most effective methods for enhancing model robustness against adversarial attacks
[
27
]
.
Nevertheless, standard AT approaches are often hampered by significant limitations, including substantial computational costs primarily tied to the iterative generation of adversarial examples
[
29
]
, and a notable degradation in accuracy on benign, natural examples
[
34
]
.
While recent research has achieved considerable progress in refining AT algorithms to concurrently improve robustness and natural accuracy
[
16
,
38
,
46
]
, a deeper understanding of the fundamental nature of adversarial perturbations remains critical to fundamentally overcoming model vulnerability.
We contend that the prevailing emphasis on algorithmic advancements, with comparatively less focus on this foundational understanding, limits our ability to address the core challenge of adversarial fragility.

In this regard,
Ilyas et al. 2019
offered a key insight: adversarial examples are not merely "bugs" arising from model training, but rather exploit "non-robust features" inherent in the data. These features, while highly predictive for the model, are often imperceptible or meaningless to human observers. Consequently, model failures on adversarial examples are attributed to an over-reliance on these non-robust features.

This perspective not only offers a theoretical explanation for the effectiveness of AT (i.e., compelling models to learn more robust features) but also highlights a central research question: How can we design effective learning mechanisms that guide models to identify and prioritize predictive features that are genuinely robust to perturbations?

Inspired by the challenges posed by AT on long-tailed datasets,
YuHang et al. 2025
on long-tail proposed a two-stage training strategy. Recognizing that the sparsity of long-tailed data in feature space can destabilize AT when applied directly, their strategy first employs standard training to stabilize the model, followed by a subsequent phase of adversarial training. Experimental results demonstrated that this approach achieves faster convergence within a similar total number of training epochs, while concurrently enhancing both adversarial robustness and natural accuracy.

However, this work primarily targets the long-tailed setting and does not delve into the underlying mechanisms explaining its effectiveness. Notably, this staged approach—progressing from standard (easier) to adversarial (harder) training—echoes the principles of Curriculum Learning (CL)
[
4
]
. Yet, it differs from prior Curriculum Adversarial Training (CAT) methods
[
5
]
, which typically focus on gradually increasing the perturbation magnitude by smoothly intensifying the adversarial perturbation strength throughout the training process.

Drawing inspiration from the feature-centric perspective on adversarial examples
[
14
]
, this paper systematically investigates the influence of an initial phase of standard training on the subsequent development and overall effectiveness of adversarial robustness.
We conceptualize this preliminary standard training period as an “Ignition Phase,” designed to efficiently prepare the model for the challenges inherent in adversarial learning.
Our core hypothesis is:

Standard training facilitates faster and more stable learning of adversarial robustness.

To operationalize this, our proposed strategy begins with the Ignition Phase, allowing the model to quickly converge to a stable state and establish foundational features. This is followed by a dedicated AT phase to instill robustness. We hypothesize that this staged, curriculum-like approach, "ignited" by the initial standard training, leads to significantly faster convergence towards robust models compared to conventional end-to-end AT. We posit that this acceleration stems from the Ignition Phase mitigating the optimization instabilities often encountered when immediately applying strong adversarial constraints in end-to-end training.

Through extensive empirical evaluations, we demonstrate that our proposed paradigm of standard training followed by AT surpasses conventional end-to-end AT in achieving adversarial robustness. Our findings highlight two primary advantages under a fixed total training epoch budget: (1) Our method achieves superior natural accuracy while attaining comparable adversarial robustness to end-to-end AT, often reaching high robustness levels significantly faster. (2) The strategy markedly reduces computational cost, as the initial standard training phase bypasses the need for generating expensive adversarial examples.

2
Related Work

2.1
Adversarial Training

Adversarial Training (AT) is a foundational technique for enhancing deep neural network robustness. Its core idea is to iteratively generate adversarial examples during parameter updates and include them in training, directly bolstering model resilience.
Goodfellow et al. 2014
pioneered this with the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM). Subsequently, Madry et al. introduced Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) based AT (PGD-AT)
[
27
]
, which uses stronger, iterative attacks and has become a de facto standard.

Despite PGD-AT’s robustness improvements, its significant computational overhead and common degradation of natural accuracy on benign examples have spurred further research. Active efforts aim to mitigate these drawbacks by developing more efficient AT algorithms, exploring advanced optimization, leveraging distributed training, and incorporating various regularization methods
[
1
,
8
,
37
]
.

2.2
Curriculum Adversarial Training

Curriculum Adversarial Training (CAT)
[
5
]
aims to mitigate the training instability often observed in standard Adversarial Training (AT) and achieve a better trade-off between natural accuracy and robustness by employing strategies that generate adversarial examples with gradually increasing difficulty. Recent theoretical work by
Shi and Liu 2024
provides justification for CAT’s effectiveness, leveraging an online learning perspective to derive novel adversarial generalization error bounds; their analysis shows that curriculum strategies can provably bound the expected adversarial error. This progressive generation approach is also thought to enable a gentler suppression of model reliance on non-robust features, facilitating smoother feature purification
[
32
]
, and potentially guiding optimization towards flatter regions of the adversarial loss landscape that aid convergence to more robust minima
[
31
]
.

Although Curriculum Adversarial Training (CAT) is often presented as broadly superior to standard Adversarial Training (AT), with some claims of enhanced performance potentially linked to specific evaluation contexts
[
31
]
, our preliminary investigations under standardized evaluation protocols reveal a more nuanced outcome. We observe that while CAT frequently improve natural accuracy when benchmarked against PGD-AT, a consistent and significant advantage in final adversarial robustness is not always substantiated in our findings (detailed in Appendix
C
). This underscores the ongoing need for methodologies that can more reliably and efficiently advance model robustness beyond current capabilities.

2.3
Understanding Adversarial Examples from a Feature Perspective

From a feature learning perspective, model vulnerability to adversarial examples is primarily attributed to an over-reliance on
non-robust features
present in the data, rather than to encoding errors.

Ilyas et al. 2019
systematically argued that adversarial examples are not simply “bugs” but consequences of models exploiting these non-robust yet highly predictive features, which often lack semantic meaning or are imperceptible to human observers.
This understanding is further supported by work such as
Tsipras et al. 2018
, who, from an information-theoretic viewpoint, explored the fundamental trade-offs between robustness and standard accuracy, offering a theoretical basis for phenomena like the common drop in natural accuracy during adversarial training.
Motivated by this feature-centric view, subsequent research has investigated various techniques—including feature regularization, contrastive learning, and imposing constraints on learned representations
[
42
,
15
,
19
]
—aimed at guiding models towards learning more human-aligned, genuinely robust features, often shifting the focus from merely achieving high adversarial accuracy on benchmarks towards learning inherently more robust feature extractors.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Learning Setup

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the input space (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and
\mathcal{Y}
the output space. The joint instance space is
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We assume data samples
(x,y)\in\mathcal{Z}
are drawn independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from an unknown underlying distribution
P
.

We consider a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, which consists of functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
. In many modern machine learning settings, particularly deep learning, models
h\in\mathcal{H}
often possess a composite structure:

h(x)=g(f(x)),

(1)

where
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
is a feature extractor mapping inputs to an intermediate feature space
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, and
g:\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{Y}
is typically a simpler classifier or predictor (e.g., a linear layer) operating on these features.

To evaluate the performance of a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we employ a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. The value
\ell(h(x),y)
quantifies the penalty for predicting
h(x)
when the true label is
y
. We impose the following standard assumptions on the loss function
\ell
:

•

\rho
-Lipschitz Continuity:
\ell
is
\rho
-Lipschitz with respect to its first argument (the prediction):

|\ell(y^{\prime}_{1},y)-\ell(y^{\prime}_{2},y)|\leq\rho\|y^{\prime}_{1}-y^{\prime}_{2}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
is an appropriate norm on
\mathcal{Y}
.

•

Boundedness:
\ell
is bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
M>0
such that for all
(x,y)
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
,
0\leq\ell(h(x),y)\leq M
.

3.2
Standard and Adversarial Generalization Error

The goal of learning is typically to find a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
that performs well on unseen data drawn from the distribution
P
.

Standard Generalization Error.

The standard measure of performance is the expected loss, also known as the population risk or natural generalization error:

R_{\mathrm{nat}}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}[\ell(h(x),y)].

(2)

Adversarial Generalization Error.

To assess model robustness against adversarial perturbations, we consider attacks under a specific threat model. This model defines the permissible scope of perturbations an adversary can apply. Given an input
x\in\mathcal{X}
, a
p
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
(e.g., the
\ell_{\infty}
or
\ell_{2}
norm), and a maximum perturbation radius
\delta>0
, the set of allowed adversarial examples
x^{\prime}
around
x
is defined by the
p
-norm ball:

\mathcal{B}_{p}(x,\delta)=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:\|x^{\prime}-x\|_{p}\leq\delta\}.

(3)

The adversarial generalization error (or adversarial risk) for a hypothesis
h
is then defined as the expected loss under the worst-case perturbation within this ball for each sample
(x,y)
drawn from the data distribution
P
:

R_{\mathrm{adv}}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{p}(x,\delta)}\ell(h(x^{\prime}),y)\right].

(4)

This metric evaluates the model’s performance against an adversary capable of finding the most detrimental perturbation
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{p}(x,\delta)
for any given input
x
.

3.3
Wasserstein-1 Distance

The Wasserstein-1 distance (
W_{1}
),quantifies the difference between two probability distributions
P
and
Q
on a metric space
(\mathcal{Z},d)
. It represents the minimum cost required to transport mass from
P
to match
Q
, where
d(z,z^{\prime})
is the cost of moving a unit of mass from
z
to
z^{\prime}
.

Formally, the
W_{1}
distance is defined as:

W_{1}(P,Q)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(P,Q)}\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}d(z,z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(z,z^{\prime}),

(5)

where
\Gamma(P,Q)
denotes the set of all joint probability distributions (couplings) on
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
with marginals
P
and
Q
, respectively.

Metric for Analysis.

In analyses relating different settings (e.g., standard vs. adversarial, or cross-domain), the instance space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is often equipped with the following metric:

d\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)\coloneqq\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{p}+c|y-y^{\prime}|.

(6)

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
p
-norm used to define the perturbation ball
B_{\delta}(x)
, and
c\geq 0
is a constant balancing the input and output contributions (often
c=1
or related to the L-ipschitz constant
\rho
of the loss
\ell
).

3.4
Rademacher Complexity

Rademacher complexity is a key concept in statistical learning theory for measuring the richness of a function class
\mathcal{F}
. It provides a way to bound the deviation between empirical quantities and their population counterparts, thus enabling the derivation of generalization bounds.

Given a fixed dataset
Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}
with
z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
, and a class
\mathcal{F}
of real-valued functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, the empirical Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
Z
is defined as:

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{Z}(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}f(z_{i})\right],

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
is a vector of
n
independent Rademacher random variables, each
\sigma_{i}
taking values in
\{-1,+1\}
with equal probability. Intuitively,
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{Z}(\mathcal{F})
measures the ability of the function class
\mathcal{F}
to fit random noise on the dataset
Z
.

4
How Standard Training Improves Adversarial Training

4.1
Adversarial Evolution Training

Adversarial Evolution Training (AET) is a structured training framework designed to improve the effectiveness and stability of conventional adversarial training procedures. The central premise of AET involves a strategic modification to the standard adversarial training pipeline: an initial phase dedicated to standard training on natural (non-adversarial) examples precedes the main adversarial training phase. This preliminary stage, termed the "Ignition Phase," is intended to guide the model to a more advantageous state in the parameter space. It potentially fosters the development of more robust feature representations or a smoother loss landscape, thereby providing a better initialization before the model undergoes the subsequent, typically more complex and computationally intensive, adversarial training process. In the subsequent sections , we will delve into a detailed analysis of why AET can achieve superior performance and rapidly attain adversarial robustness.

4.2
Optimization Objective of AET

Conventional adversarial training (AT) endeavors to minimize the loss on adversarially perturbed examples. This is typically formulated as a min-max optimization problem, where the inner maximization identifies the most disruptive perturbation within a specified threat model, and the outer minimization updates the model parameters to defend against such attacks. Formally, for a model
h_{\theta}
parameterized by
\theta
, a loss function
\ell
, a data distribution
P
, and a perturbation set
B_{\delta}(x)
around an input
x
(e.g., an
\ell_{p}
-norm ball of radius
\delta
), the standard AT objective is:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[\max_{x^{\prime}\in B_{\delta}(x)}\ell(h_{\theta}(x^{\prime}),y)\right]

(8)

While this approach directly optimizes for worst-case robustness, it can sometimes face challenges in achieving high generalization performance on clean data or may exhibit instability during training.

The AET framework operationalizes its strategy through a two-stage optimization process.
In the first stage (Ignition Phase), the model is trained for
T_{0}
epochs using standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) on natural examples. This yields an intermediate model, whose parameters we conceptually denote as
\theta_{T_{0}}
.
In the second stage, this pre-trained model (with parameters
\theta_{T_{0}}
) serves as the initialization for
T_{1}
epochs of adversarial training, optimizing the objective defined in Eq. (
8
).
Let
\text{Train}(\mathcal{L},\theta_{\mathrm{init}},K)
denote the model parameters obtained by optimizing the objective function
\mathcal{L}
for
K
epochs, starting from initial parameters
\theta_{\mathrm{init}}
. The overall AET procedure to obtain the final model parameters
\theta^{*}
can be expressed in a nested form as:

\theta^{*}=\text{Train}\Bigg(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}\left[\max_{x^{\prime}\in B_{\delta}(x)}\ell(h_{\theta}(x^{\prime}),y)\right]}_{\text{Adversarial Training Objective}},\;\;\underbrace{\text{Train}\Big(\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}[\ell(h_{\theta}(x),y)]}^{\text{Standard ERM Objective}},\theta_{0},T_{0}\Big)}_{\text{Ignition Phase}},\;\;T_{1}\Bigg)

(9)

Here,
\theta_{0}
represents the initial parameters before any training. The inner
Train
call executes the Ignition Phase: it optimizes the Standard ERM Objective for
T_{0}
epochs starting from
\theta_{0}
. The parameters resulting from this phase (conceptually
\theta_{T_{0}}
, as indicated by the underbrace) are then used as the initial parameters for the outer
Train
call. This outer call performs adversarial training for
T_{1}
epochs using the Adversarial Training Objective, yielding the final AET-trained parameters
\theta^{*}
. This sequential optimization, captured in the nested structure, is the core of the AET approach.

4.3
Generalization Analysis under Adversarial Distribution Shifts

We aim to analyze the
true adversarial risk
of the final hypothesis
h_{T}
generated after
T
rounds of adversarial training. Each round
t
is associated with a data distribution
D_{t}
. We assume that the final adversarial distribution,
D_{\text{adv}}
, is well-approximated by the distribution of the final training round, i.e.,
D_{\text{adv}}\approx D_{T}
. The adversarial risk is formally defined as:

R_{\text{adv}}(h_{T}):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D_{\text{adv}}}[\ell(h_{T},x,y)]

(10)

where
\ell
denotes the loss function.

The adversarial risk
R_{\text{adv}}(h_{T})\approx R_{T}(h_{T})
under the approximation
D_{\text{adv}}\approx D_{T}
.
The risk
R_{T}(h_{T})
can be expressed via a telescoping sum identity:

R_{T}(h_{T})=R_{1}(h_{1})+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\big(R_{t}(h_{t})-R_{t-1}(h_{t-1})\big)

(11)

where
R_{t}(h_{t})
is the true risk of hypothesis
h_{t}
on distribution
D_{t}
. Direct analysis of this sum is intricate. For tractability,
R_{T}(h_{T})
can be upper-bounded by the average of per-round risks:

R_{T}(h_{T})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}(h_{t})

(12)

The true risk per round
R_{t}(h_{t})
is bounded by its empirical counterpart
\widehat{R}_{t}(h_{t})
and a generalization error term
\epsilon_{t}
:
R_{t}(h_{t})\leq\widehat{R}_{t}(h_{t})+\epsilon_{t}
. Standard learning theory bounds
[
3
]
state that for a sample size
n
and confidence parameter
\delta>0
:

\epsilon_{t}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

(13)

Averaging over
T
rounds yields:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}(h_{t})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{R}_{t}(h_{t})+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

(14)

To account for evolving data distributions
D_{t}
, we consider the change in risk for a hypothesis
h_{t}
when the distribution shifts from
D_{t}
to
D_{t+1}
. Assuming the loss
\ell
is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to its input
x
, this change
|R_{t}(h_{t})-R_{t+1}(h_{t})|
(where
R_{t+1}(h_{t})
is the risk of
h_{t}
under
D_{t+1}
) is bounded by the 1-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})
:

|R_{t}(h_{t})-R_{t+1}(h_{t})|\leq L\cdot W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})

(15)

Averaging these drift terms over
T-1
transitions (scaled by
1/T
for consistency) gives:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}|R_{t}(h_{t})-R_{t+1}(h_{t})|\leq\frac{L}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})

(16)

By integrating these components, an upper bound on the adversarial risk of
h_{T}
can be established. With high probability
1-\delta
, the following holds
[
31
]
:

R_{\text{adv}}(h_{T})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{R}_{t}(h_{t})+\frac{L}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})+\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

(17)

The generalization bound presented in Equation (
17
) highlights the cumulative distribution shift, quantified by
\frac{L}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})
, as a pivotal factor influencing the final adversarial risk. We now analyze how the Adversarial Evolution Training (AET) framework mitigates this risk.Consider a model
h=g\circ f
, where
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
is a feature extractor and
g:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a classifier. Assume that
f
is
L_{f}
-Lipschitz continuous, implying that for any distributions
P,Q
over
\mathcal{X}
, the following holds:
W_{1}(f_{\#}P,f_{\#}Q)\leq L_{f}W_{1}(P,Q)
, where
f_{\#}P
denotes the pushforward distribution of
P
under
f
.

The “Ignition Phase” within AET, comprising
T_{0}
rounds of standard, non-adversarial training, is designed to guide the feature extractor
f
towards learning a representation where classes are more readily separable and the intrinsic data structure is well-captured. We posit that this guides the feature extractor into a “more favorable state.” Such an improved representation might manifest as the learned feature map
f
possessing a smaller effective Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
, or more directly, it may significantly reduce the 1-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}(f_{\#}P_{nat},f_{\#}P_{adv})
between the feature representations of representative natural (
P_{nat}
) and adversarial (
P_{adv}
) distributions. This reduction in feature-space distance is considered relative to the distance
W_{1}(P_{nat},P_{adv})
in the original input space, as well as to the feature-space distances typically achieved by feature maps without such specialized pre-training.

Consequently, during the subsequent
T_{1}
rounds of adversarial training, the effective distributional shifts perceived by the classifier
g
are anticipated to be less severe. These shifts originate from distributions
D_{t}
in the input space, which are transformed into the feature space via the feature map
f
, specifically manifesting as discrepancies between
f_{\#}D_{t}
and
f_{\#}D_{t+1}
. This suggests that the impact of the cumulative distribution drift term,
\frac{L}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}W_{1}(D_{t},D_{t+1})
, in the generalization bound (Eq. (
17
)) is effectively attenuated. If we conceptualize the primary distribution drift relevant to the classifier
g
as being characterized by an average distance in the feature space, then the reduction of this feature-space distance due to the “Ignition Phase” would contribute to a tighter effective generalization error bound.

In summary, the standard training in AET’s “Ignition Phase” provides a beneficial initialization for the feature extractor, fostering the development of robust feature representations—an observation corroborated by our experiments in
5
. This allows subsequent adversarial training to proceed within a feature space where adversarial perturbations induce smaller effective distributional discrepancies, thereby potentially lowering the overall generalization error and contributing to superior performance and more rapid attainment of robustness.

4.4
Explaining the Rapid Attainment of Robustness: The “Magic Phase” in AET

A notable phenomenon in Adversarial Evolution Training (AET) is the remarkably accelerated acquisition of adversarial robustness. As illustrated in Figure
1
, models can achieve significant robustness within a few epochs, or even a single epoch, upon transitioning from standard to adversarial training. We term this period of rapid improvement the “Magic Phase” and explore its underlying mechanisms here.

During the initial standard training phase (
T_{0}
epochs), termed the “Ignition Phase,” the model learns features—such as edges and textures—that are highly discriminative for the training distribution. While these features are effective on clean data, they may lack core invariance and thus be susceptible to adversarial perturbations, resulting in initially low robustness (e.g., as potentially depicted in the 0-20 epoch range for a baseline in Figure
1
). Nevertheless, this Ignition Phase imparts a crucial preliminary structure to the feature space, where samples begin to form nascent class clusters and initial decision boundaries are established.

Crucially, when adversarial training commences post-Ignition Phase, it operates not on a randomly initialized network, but on a model already possessing substantial feature extraction capabilities. Gradient signals from adversarial examples can therefore more effectively guide the model in refining its existing, structured feature representations to withstand these attacks. This targeted refinement contrasts sharply with adversarial training initiated from scratch, where the model faces the more formidable and slower challenge of concurrently learning fundamental features from raw inputs and imbuing them with robustness within a vast parameter space.

We posit that the emergence of the “Magic Phase” is principally attributable to the advantageous “warm start” provided by the standard pre-training. The model is not compelled to navigate the optimization landscape from an arbitrary state. Instead, it leverages and adapts features already learned, which possess task-relevance and discriminative power. Because these initial features have already captured some aspects of class separability, subsequent adversarial training can more rapidly steer these features towards regions of the feature space that confer greater adversarial robustness, facilitating the observed accelerated learning dynamics.

5
Experiments

To validate the efficacy of our proposed Adversarial Evolution Training (AET) methodology, we conducted comprehensive experiments across diverse datasets, various adversarial training paradigms, and different model architectures. The empirical results consistently demonstrate that, compared to conventional adversarial training (AT) approaches, AET not only achieves superior clean accuracy and comparable or slightly enhanced adversarial robustness but also offers notable improvements in training efficiency by reducing computational overhead.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Models and Optimization.

We employed several standard architectures: ResNet-18, ResNet-50, WRN-50-2, and ViT-S.
All models were trained using the Adam optimizer
[

Main Results

The results indicate that incorporating AET leads to significantly better clean accuracy while achieving comparable or even slightly superior adversarial robustness. Furthermore, this advantage extends to enhanced adversarial training methods; AET versions of TRADES, ADT, and MART also demonstrate these improvements. These findings substantiate our hypothesis that an initial phase of standard training can facilitate a more effective and efficient acquisition of adversarial robustness. 6
Conclusion

In this work, we have systematically investigated, both theoretically and empirically, the impact of an initial phase of standard training on the subsequent development and ultimate quality of adversarial robustness. Adopting a feature-centric perspective—which we contend is a crucial avenue for fundamentally understanding and addressing adversarial perturbations—we have demonstrated that this Ignition Phase” enables the model to learn more foundational feature representations. These pre-learned features subsequently prove highly beneficial for the ensuing adversarial training stage, facilitating a distinct period of accelerated robustness acquisition, which we term theMagic Phase.” Indeed, the manifestation of this accelerated learning period and its dependence on the initial feature quality formed a key aspect of our empirical investigation. Our extensive experimental evaluations further substantiate this hypothesis.

These findings resonate with the insights of Ilyas et al.
[
14
]
, reinforcing the notion that comprehending adversarial vulnerability through the lens of feature learning offers a critical pathway towards its mitigation. Consequently, we advocate for future research to delve deeper into optimizing training methodologies specifically designed to foster the learning of genuinely robust feature representations, thereby moving beyond the mere pursuit of incremental performance gains on specific benchmarks. Such a focus on the intrinsic quality of learned features promises more principled and generalizable solutions to the challenge of adversarial vulnerability.

100\times 123.85\text{s}=12385\text{s}

The AET (Adversarial Evolution Training) strategy modifies this by substituting an initial
N_{\text{CE}}
epochs of AT with standard CE epochs, within the same
100

(N_{\text{CE}}\times 19.25\text{s})+((100-N_{\text{CE}})\times 123.85\text{s})

Appendix C
Limitations of CAT

Curriculum Adversarial Training (CAT)
[
5
]
attempts to alleviate the training instability and enhance the trade-off between natural accuracy and robustness often associated with standard Adversarial Training (AT). This is achieved by employing a curriculum strategy where the model is initially trained against weaker adversarial attacks. The attack strength is progressively increased only after the model achieves a high level of accuracy against the current attack intensity, continuing until a predefined maximum strength is reached. Conceptually, CAT defines an attack function
A(k)
corresponding to difficulty level
k
. Training commences with minimal attack strength (
A(0)
) and escalates. Transition to a higher attack strength
A(k+1)
occurs when the model’s validation accuracy at stage
k
ceases to improve over a specified ‘patience period’ (a set number of epochs). Upon such a transition, the model’s parameters are typically reverted to the state that yielded the best performance during that patience period. This design ensures the model masters easier tasks before progressing to more challenging ones, effectively creating a learning trajectory from
A(0)
to
A(k_{\text{max}})
.

Despite its design rationale, CAT faces notable challenges. Firstly, regarding performance, our observations indicate that while CAT variants often exhibit an advantage in natural accuracy over conventional AT, this can come at the cost of diminished adversarial robustness, as suggested by the results in Table
4
. Secondly, curriculum transition mechanisms frequently rely on validation-set performance to trigger an increase in attack difficulty or to revert to a previous best state if no improvement is seen within a ‘patience window’ (e.g.,
k^{\prime}
epochs). This necessitates performing adversarial evaluations at each epoch during the training process, significantly increasing the computational burden. Although the total number of nominal training epochs might be fixed, this validation and potential rollback process can lead to a substantial number of effective additional training computations, approximately
n\times k^{\prime}
epochs (where
n
is the total number of curriculum stages), creating disadvantages in terms of both training time and resource consumption.

Appendix D
Performance of ViT

Regarding the performance of Adversarial Evolution Training (AET) on Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures, observations from Figure
2
indicate that AET consistently achieves superior final outcomes compared to standard adversarial training (AT).
A notable distinction, however, arises in the duration of the "Magic Phase"—the initial period of accelerated robustness acquisition. For ViT models, this phase appears visibly prolonged, spanning approximately 10 epochs, in contrast to the more rapid onset typically observed in Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) based models, as illustrated. We hypothesize that this extended adaptation period in ViTs may be attributed to their inherent architectural complexity, potentially requiring more training iterations to effectively integrate the foundational features learned during AET’s "Ignition Phase" with subsequent adversarial objectives.
Nevertheless, this longer transition does not negate the overall advantages of AET, which ultimately leads to improved performance over standard AT for ViTs.

It is also pertinent to acknowledge that, across our experiments, the absolute performance levels (encompassing both clean and robust accuracy) achieved by ViT models were generally lower than those attained by their CNN counterparts on the same tasks. This performance discrepancy might be attributed to several factors, including the scale of the datasets being potentially suboptimal for data-intensive ViT architectures, or the need for more extensive and ViT-specific hyperparameter tuning within the adversarial training regime.

Appendix E
Limitations

The empirical validation presented in this work was primarily constrained by available computational resources.
Consequently, while our experiments demonstrate the benefits of AET (Adversarial Evolution Training) across several model architectures and benchmark datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and MNIST, as detailed in our experimental setup) when applied to standard Adversarial Training (AT) and three other prominent adversarial training methods (TRADES, MART, ADT), we were unable to extend our validation to very large-scale datasets, such as ImageNet.
Furthermore, the scope of our comparison was limited to these selected adversarial training techniques, and a more exhaustive evaluation against a wider spectrum of emerging or diverse methods was not performed.
Although we hypothesize that the advantageous trends conferred by AET—such as improved clean accuracy and comparable or enhanced robustness with greater training efficiency—would generalize to these broader settings and a more extensive range of adversarial training paradigms, empirically verifying its consistent effectiveness and scalability under such conditions remains a key avenue for future investigation.
