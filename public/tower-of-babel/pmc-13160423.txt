GraphLooper: predicting chromatin loops based on hierarchical multi-view graph pooling method
Authors: Siguo Wang, Zhipeng Li, Hailin Feng, Zhen Cui, Zhen-Hao Guo, Qi Liao, Zuquan Hu, Wenjian Liu, Qinhu Zhang, De-Shuang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChromatin loops serve as fundamental functional units of three-dimensional genome organization, playing pivotal roles in regulating gene expression and maintaining genomic spatial organization. Accurate identification of these fine-scale structures is crucial for advancing our understanding of cellular biological processes and the mechanisms underlying disease. However, due to the inherent complexity and dynamic of chromatin interactions, existing methods often fail to adequately characterize and capture multi-dimensional features. To address these limitations, we introduce GraphLooper, a novel framework using hierarchical multi-view graph pooling to enhance training and inference on large-scale data. GraphLooper transforms Hi-C data into a graph-structured representation, integrating multi-dimensional epigenomic features to construct a robust chromatin interaction model. Employing a hierarchical multi-view graph pooling mechanism, it effectively aggregates multi-scale features, enhancing representation learning. Evaluations across diverse cell lines demonstrate that GraphLooper outperforms state-of-the-art methods in prediction accuracy and generalization, particularly in capturing long-range chromatin interactions critical for precise spatial gene regulation.Keywords: chromatin loops, multi-view graph pooling, epigenomic data, graph neural networks

Introduction

The three-dimensional (3D) genome organization, formed by hierarchical chromatin folding across millions of base pairs, is critical for regulating gene expression, cell fate, and evolutionary dynamics [1–4]. With the development of high-through chromosome conformation capture (HiC) technologies, the multi-scale chromatin structures have revealed, including chromosomal territories [5], A/B compartments [5], topologically associating domains (TADs) [6, 7], and finer-scale structural elements such as chromatin loops [8, 9]. While Hi-C technology has provided unprecedented insights into large-scale chromatin organization [5, 10–15], our understanding of fine-grained 3D chromatin structures at nucleosome resolution remains limited. Chromatin loops, critical regulatory units, are typically mediated by interactions between promoters and distal enhancers to enable precise gene regulation through spatial proximity. Therefore, identifying and analyzing chromatin loops is crucial for uncovering the mechanisms of gene regulation and cellular function.

The rapid advancement of high-throughput sequencing technologies has generated vast datasets, enabling in-depth studies of fine-scale genomic structures and transcriptional regulation mechanisms. Some classical statistical methods such as HiCCUPS [16] and FitHiC [17] identify chromatin loops by detecting interactions within global or local contexts, but suffer from limited computational efficiency. In contrast, some computional methods like Peakachu [18], RefHiC [19], and DLoopCaller [20] have reframed chromatin loops detection as a supervised learning problem, utilizing random forests and CNNs to enable genome-wide identification. scGSLoop [21]utilizes a two-layer GraphSAGE [22] encoder framework, which transforms the task of chromatin loop identification into an edge prediction problem within a graph representation. In addition, scCross [23], scHiClassifier [24], and MomicPred [25]. This approach facilitates the precise detection of chromatin interactions at single-cell resolution.

The integration of epigenetic modifications with three-dimensional genome architecture significantly advances our understanding of gene regulation, yet it remains limited by three key challenges: (i) the limitations of sequencing technologies: Current most of the sequencing technologies have some specific constraints. For example, targeted 3C-based technologies [26], including 4C [27] and 5C [28], facilitate high-resolution interaction detection at specific genomic loci, but probe dependency limits its ability to perform systematic analysis across the entire genome. Subsequently developed ChIA-PET technology enables genome-wide mapping of chromatin interactions, however, it is inherently limited to protein-mediated interactions due to its dependence on antibody specificity. Notably, Hi-C technology [29] has achieved a breakthrough in unbiased genome-wide interaction detection, but the requirement for extremely high sequencing depth to meet high-resolution data needs significantly increase experimental costs. Meanwhile, existing epigenomic detection technologies (such as ChIP-seq [30], RNA-seq [31], and DNase-seq [32]) primarily provide chromatin feature information distributed along the linear genome, failing to effectively reflect the spatial regulatory network at the megabase scale. (ii) Simple multi-omics fusion data strategy: Current integrated analysis methods often employ simplistic data fusion strategies, such as mapping histone modification signals onto Hi-C-defined topologically associated domains [33] or directly associating CTCF binding sites with chromatin loop anchors [34, 35]. This ‘linear modification + spatial framework’ two-dimensional integration model fails to fully consider the biological context and interaction relationships. (iii) Lack of effective model frameworks: Despite recent advances in deep learning-based 3D epigenome reconstruction, current methods remain limited in capturing the genome’s true spatial structural features. For instance, DeepTACT [36] leverages sequence features and chromatin accessibility information to predict chromatin interactions at the level of individual regulatory elements. Similarly, EpiTensor [37] employs high-order tensor decomposition to infer 3D spatial associations from epigenetic features, while CAESAR [38] utilizes graph convolutional layers combined with 1-D convolutional layers to predict fine-scale chromatin structures at nucleosome resolution. In summary, researchers need to further develop more precise data integration and methods to address the technical challenges in studying the synergistic regulatory mechanisms between epigenetic regulation and 3D genome structure.

To address these challenges, this work introduces a deep learning framework based on multi-view hierarchical graph pooling, aimed at achieving accurate prediction and functional annotation of chromatin loop structures across the entire genome. The framework comprises three core modules: (i) Multi-omics data integration and graph construction: This module systematically integrates diverse multi-omics datasets, including 3D genomic maps, epigenetic modification data, and chromatin accessibility profiles, transforming low-dimensional features into graph-structured representations to comprehensively characteriz the 3D genome architecture. (ii) Multi-view feature extraction and representation learning: This module applies hierarchical graph pooling to extract high-order features from multi-omics data and uses multi-view learning to capture multi-scale genomic interactions, thereby building a global feature representation to improve chromatin loop prediction accuracy. (iii) Chromatin loop prediction and specificity analysis: This module uses learned high-dimensional features for genome-wide chromatin loop prediction and conducts systematic functional analysis of their regulatory roles across diverse cell lines and types. This framework not only significantly improves the accuracy of chromatin loop identification but also provides a robust methodological foundation for elucidating the complex relationships between 3D genome organization and gene regulatory mechanisms. Materials and data

The overall design of GraphLooper

Our chromatin loop identification framework comprises three critical steps. First, 3D genome maps (Hi-C data) and epigenomic profiles are transformed into graph-structured representations along with their corresponding feature embeddings. Second, a multi-view graph pooling model is utilized to comprehensively integrate topological features and epigenomic signals for precise chromatin loop detection. Finally, the identified loops are rigorously filtered based on the same strategy of Peakachu2 [39] to minimize false positives and ensure robust results.

Data preparation and preprocessing

In this work, we conducted a comprehensive series of experiments across multiple cell lines, including GM12878, K562, and H1-ESC to evaluate the performance and robustness of our model. To ensure the stability and reproducibility of our findings, we specifically collected two biological replicates of the K562 cell line. The raw data types employed in our work include 3D genome maps (Hi-C data) and 6 categories of epigenomic data, encompassing chromatin accessibility data (DNase), histone modifications (H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K27me3), and chromatin architectural proteins (CTCF), which maintain consistency of CASEAR [38]. All cell lines were annotated using CTCF ChIA-PET enrichment data, with SMC1 HiChIP data additionally incorporated for GM12878 to enhance experimental diversity.

The raw Hi-C contact maps were initially converted into contact matrices at 10 kb resolution and subsequently normalized using the hic2cool and cooler Python packages to ensure data accuracy and comparability. To obtain the corresponding epigenomic feature values, we calculated the average values of one-dimensional epigenomic data at 10 kb bins, ensuring resolution consistency with the Hi-C data. Training samples generation and problem statements

In this work, the input data comprised Hi-C contact maps and multiple epigenomic datasets, while enrichment experimental data were utilized for annotation to generate training samples. To generate and characterize training samples, we performed 3 key steps: (i) annotating and extracting subHi-C maps: The interaction regions validated by enrichment experiments are expanded along both sides in the original Hi-C matrix to generate annotated samples (N × N sub-matrices). Subsequently, same-sized sub-Hi-C maps were randomly selected from non-interacting genomic regions, with constraints on contact density and genomic distance from annotated samples to ensure diversity and biological relevance. (ii) constructing graph-structured training data: The annotated and randomly selected genomic sub-matrices are converted into graph-structured data. Specifically, each N × N submatrix is treated as an adjacency matrix, where nodes represent genomic bins and edge weights denote the interaction strength between them. Therefore, the training samples are represented as subgraphs with N nodes, effectively capturing the characteristics of Hi-C maps for the model training process. (iii) Feature embedding: To comprehensively capture both the spatial localization and epigenomic information of nodes in the 3D genome map, this study combines position encoding with epigenomic features to construct a multi-dimensional feature representation for each node. Methods

GraphLooper model architecture

The model framework mainly includes three hierarchical multi-view graph pooling modules, a readout module and a classification module, which ultimately realize the genome-wide prediction of chromatin loops.

(i) The hierarchical multi-view graph pooling module.

Inspired by [41], we proposed a lightweight multi-view graph pooling module that integrates variant graph neural networks, multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), and pooling layers to jointly capture structural information and multi-omics epigenomic features. This module facilitates efficient information propagation and feature extraction from graph-structured representations of Hi-C contact maps and their associated epigenomic features. By capturing nonlinear relationships among multi-omics data, a more accurate representation of key characteristics associated with chromatin loops across the genome is achieved.

To effectively extract the information of the graph topology, we employ a variant of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) to evaluate node importance [42]. (ii) The readout module.

This module is designed to integrate the features output by the hierarchical multi-view module to form a comprehensive representation of chromatin loop features. The readout module effectively combines topological structure features, epigenomic features, and whole-genome position features, thereby more comprehensively capturing the global characteristics of genomic chromatin loop data. (iii) classification layer module.

In this module, fully connected layers are used to further process and learn from the features extracted by the readout module, aiming to generate final predictions for genomic chromatin loops. The application of fully connected layers allows for nonlinear transformations and combinations among features, enabling a more accurate capture of the complex characteristics in the genomic chromatin loop. Finally, a sigmoid function is applied at the output layer to predict the chromatin loops.

(iv) Model training.

To comprehensively evaluate the generalization performance of the model, the Leave-One-Out method was employed for model training and validation. Specifically, data from 22 chromosomes (excluding chromosome Y) were used, with one chromosome selected as the test set in turn. The remaining chromosome data were divided into a training set and a validation set in an 8:2 ratio. The validation set was exclusively used for early stopping and model selection, enabling robust chromosome-independent performance evaluation. Since the chromatin loop prediction task is treated as a binary graph classification problem, the binary cross-entropy loss function (BCELoss) was employed for model optimization. The model was trained with Adam (lr = 0.001, weight decay = 0.001), batch size = 128, for a maximum of 30 epochs, and random seed fixed at 666 for reproducibility. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 15 epochs on validation performance; the best checkpoint was retained for testing. The competing methods

To comprehensively evaluate the classification performance of the proposed GraphLooper, four competing methods were designed for experimental validation: GCN, scGSLoop [21], GraphSAGE+, and CAESAR [38]. To ensure a fair comparison, all competing methods were trained using the same strategy as GraphLooper, including seed initialization and optimization of the same hyperparameters.

GCN: It employs a three-layer GCN architecture and serves as a baseline for experimental comparison.

scGSLoop: It employs a three-layer GraphSAGE framework as a comparative baseline, ensuring its model depth aligns with Graphlooper to enable a fair performance evaluation.

GraphSAGE+: It adopts a pooling architecture similar to GraphLooper, integrating GCN with a self-attention graph pooling mechanism (SAGPool).

CAESAR: It integrates CNN with GCN. Specifically, a 1D-CNN architecture is employed to capture epigenomic features, while a two-layer GCN is utilized to extract higher-order interactions from 3D genomic features.

Results

Ablation comparison experiment

In this work, the effective construction of training samples is crucial to ensuring the reliability of experimental results. The size of 3D genomic subgraphs and their positional encoding are two key parameters because their spatial scale directly affects the model’s ability to represent the spatial structure of chromatin. To systematically evaluate the impact of subgraph size and positional encoding on classification performance, a series of ablation experiments were conducted on the K562 cell line (Replicate 1) annotated with CTCF enrichment experimental data, and evaluated model performance using F1-score and PR-AUC metrics.

Three subgraph size configurations were designed: 11 × 11, 22 × 22, and 33 × 33. As shown in Fig. 2a–b, the experimental results demonstrate a clear size-dependent performance trend: (1) the highest performance is observed with the medium-sized 22 × 22 configuration; (2) model performance improves as the subgraph size increases from 11 × 11 to 22 × 22; and (3) a performance decline occurs when the size is further increased to 33 × 33. This non-monotonic pattern indicates that subgraph size may have an optimal range. Subgraphs that are too small may provide limited spatial information, while overly large subgraphs may introduce redundancy or noise, affecting the model’s ability to represent chromatin spatial structure. In this work, we use 22*22 as the default parameter.

The impact of subgraph size and positional encoding on model performance in the K562 CTCF ChIA-PET dataset. (a) Impact of subgraph size on F1-score performance; (b) impact of subgraph size on PRAUC performance; (c) impact of positional encoding on F1-score performance; (d) impact of positional encoding on PRAUC performance.

Based on the above findings, we further explored the impact of position encoding on model performance. As shown in Fig. 2c–d, position encoding significantly improves the classification performance of chromatin loops. This result shows that position encoding can more effectively represent the spatial structural information in 3D genomic data, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to understand the spatial organization of chromatin. Based on these experimental results, we adopt a submatrix graph size of 22 × 22 and use position encoding as the default parameters of Graph Looper. Furthermore, to assess the impact of random initialization, we conducted additional experiments using three independent random seeds (3057, 888, and 2020). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the average performance fluctuation across seeds was within 1% for both F1-score and PRAUC, indicating that GraphLooper is highly robust to seed variations. The performance distribution shown in Fig. 2 reflects the inherent biological heterogeneity of different chromosomes rather than model instability.

Classification performance comparison between GraphLooper and SOTA methods

To comprehensively evaluate the classification performance of GraphLooper in chromatin loop identification tasks, a series of comparative experiments were conducted across three distinct cell lines: K562 (annotated with CTCF ChIA-PET enrichment data), GM12878 (annotated with CTCF ChIA-PET and SMC1 HiCH Penrichment data), and H1ESC (annotated with CTCF ChIA-PET enrichment data). GraphLooper was systematically compared with several state-of-the-art chromatin loop prediction methods, including GCN, GraphSAGE+, scGSLoop, and CAESAR.

As shown in Fig. 3, the performance of GraphLooper in the task of chromatin loop identification was experimentally evaluated. The results show that compared with existing methods, GraphLooper exhibits comprehensive performance advantages: (i) compared with GCN, its F1 score and PR-AUC are significantly improved by 5%–10% and 4%–8%, respectively. This difference highlights the key role of pooling operations in chromatin loop identification; (ii) compared with scGSLoop, GraphLooper achieves 4%–7% and 3%–4% improvements in F1 score and PR-AUC, respectively, confirming the effectiveness of the hierarchical graph pooling strategy. It is worth noting that even when GraphSAGE+ and CAESAR yield comparable results, GraphLooper maintains superior performance in evaluation metrics.

Further analysis indicates that this advantage arises from GraphLooper’s model architecture: the hierarchical multi-view graph pooling mechanism not only provides feature extraction capabilities comparable to those of CNNs, but more importantly, enables the effective integration of topological structural features and epigenetic information, thereby facilitating accurate modeling of chromatin spatial organization.

GraphLooper captures long-range chromatin loops

To comprehensively evaluate the genome-wide chromatin loops identified by GraphLooper, a systematic comparison was conducted against four representative loop-calling methods: two classical enrichment-based approaches (HiCCUPS [8] and FitHiC [17]) and two computational prediction methods (Peakachu and our proposed DloopCaller). This comparison covers different methodological approaches, allowing for a thorough assessment of GraphLooper’s performance. The genome-wide chromatin loop identification process consists of two key stages: scoring and filtering. Initially, all non-zero pixels (representing potential chromatin loops) across chromosomes are scored using our optimally trained model. Based on previous findings that Hi-C interaction intensity is positively correlated with chromatin loop probability [8], we retained only candidate loops with contact frequencies exceeding the genome-wide average. Notably, we employed a greedy clustering algorithm [39] to select the most representative pixels as the final predicted loops, thereby achieving an effective trade-off between prediction accuracy and computational efficiency. All comparative methods are based on the GM12878 cell line, with GraphLooper specifically annotated using CTCF ChIA-PET enrichment data.

As shown in Fig. 4a, under default parameters, the number of chromatin loops identified by GraphLooper is comparable to that of Fit-HiC, while Peakachu and HiCCUPS produce similar results. The differences in the number of loops detected by all five methods are within 20%. However, upon conducting an overlap analysis of the chromatin loops identified by each method (where loops with identical anchor points are considered duplicates), the results in Fig. 4(b) show that GraphLooper identifies a larger number of unique chromatin loops. This difference may be attributed to the varying detection preferences of each method for interactions occurring at different spatial scales. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 4(c), existing methods primarily focus on detecting short-range interactions, while GraphLooper demonstrates a stronger ability to capture long-range genomic interactions. It is noteworthy that, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, most of these long-range interactions identified by GraphLooper are mediated by regulatory elements such as promoters, suggesting their potential regulatory significance. As shown in Fig. 4e, GraphLooper not only recapitulates chromatin loops commonly detected by existing methods but also uniquely identifies a substantial number of additional high-confidence loops that are missed by competing methods. Additionally, in the comparison of APA (Anchored Polymerase Array) distribution patterns (Fig. 4d), although the competing methods show similar distribution patterns, GraphLooper exhibits more diverse distribution characteristics. This may indicate a more comprehensive ability to identify complex chromatin spatial interaction networks. In conclusion, these comprehensive results not only demonstrate the superior performance of GraphLooper in chromatin loop prediction but also highlight its distinctive ability to detect long-range genomic interactions. This capability is particularly significant for advancing the study of distal regulatory mechanisms and deepening our understanding of the 3D genome structure.

GraphLooper reveals cell type-specific transcription factor enrichment

The cell type specificity of long-range genomic interactions, particularly enhancer-promoter connections, is likely underpinned by the cooperative effects of specific transcription factors (TFs). To further investigate the regulatory basis of chromatin loops identified genome-wide, the specialized bioinformatics tool Spatzie [43], was employed. This allowed us to systematically evaluate the synergistic effects of TF combinations in mediating these crucial 3D genomic interactions. We configured Spatzie to identify enhancers and promoters using a co-occurrence method based on genome annotation. To ensure high-confidence TF binding predictions, we applied a stringent transcription factor presence threshold of 0.95.

Our experimental results reveal a striking cell type specificity in the synergistic interactions of transcription factors (TFs) associated with chromatin loops across different cell lines. We observed distinct patterns of TF enrichment: In the GM12878 cell line, a significant enrichment was found between members of the KLF and SP families of transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, within the K562 (replicate1) cell line, notable interactions were identified between ZN770 and ZSC22/SP2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 5, the H1-ESC cell line exhibited a more complex pattern of TF cooperativity. Beyond the significant enrichment of KLF and SP families, strong synergistic interactions were also observed between SALL4/HTF4 and members of the SP/KLF families. In addition, the types of chromatin loops identified across the three cell lines also exhibit variability (Supplementary Fig. 5). These findings not only shed light on the diverse molecular mechanisms that regulate chromatin loops in different cell types, but also offer novel theoretical insights into the formation of dynamic 3D genomic structures and their pivotal regulatory roles in cell fate determination.

GraphLooper exhibits generalization capability

In order to evaluate the generalization performance of GraphLooper in identifying chromatin loops from Hi-C data across varying sequencing depths. A series of systematic analyses were conducted for this purpose. First, low-coverage training samples were generated by downsampling whole-genome data to 0.7× coverage using a binomial probability-based approach. The results indicated significant similarities between both datasets in APA analysis and transcription factor enrichment enrichment (Supplementary Figs. 6–7). Furthermore, the chromatin loops identified in these downsampled data exhibited high concordance with those detected at the original sequencing depth (Fig. 6a–b). These findings suggest that GraphLooper effectively identifies chromatin loops in low-coverage data while maintaining high specificity and without introducing additional false-positive results. To further validate GraphLooper’s generalization capabilities, it was applied to different biological replicates of the K562 cell line. The results demonstrated high reproducibility in chromatin loop predictions across these replicate samples (Fig. 6c–d). Generally speaking, these results demonstrate that GraphLooper exhibits robust adaptability to variations in sequencing depth and sample differences, maintaining stable prediction performance under varying conditions, which underscores its high stability and sensitivity.

Conclusions and future work

The 3D architecture of the genome is fundamental to understanding genome function, gene regulation, and disease pathogenesis. However, precisely identifying chromatin loops, key functional units within this architecture, remains a significant challenge. Existing computational methods fall short in two critical areas: insufficient integration of multi-omics data and inadequate incorporation of intrinsic biological features underlying chromatin loop interactions. These limitations severely hinder the in-depth exploration of dynamic chromatin interactions and their regulatory mechanisms.

To overcome these challenges, we proposed a novel framework GraphLooper that significantly enhances chromatin loop prediction accuracy. It employs a multi-level graph pooling mechanism to deeply mine multi-scale genomic features, dynamically linking local chromatin unit interactions with global topological structures. Notably, we designed a multi-view graph data structure that innovatively integrates multi-omics features, such as histone modifications and chromatin accessibility, into the 3D genomic map. Through a cross-view feature interaction module, GraphLooper effectively captures the biological associations among these multi-modal data. The experimental results demonstrate that GraphLooper not only improves the performance of genome-wide chromatin loop identification but also exhibits strong generalization capability. More importantly, GraphLooper shows superior ability in capturing long-range chromatin loops and reveals enrichment patterns of cell type–specific enhancer–promoter interactions. Beyond serving as a powerful tool for chromatin loop detection, GraphLooper effectively integrates 3D genome architecture and epigenetic information, providing new insights into genome organization and functional regulation.

While GraphLooper has provided valuable insights into chromatin loop research, it still has limitations. Building on our current foundational work, future efforts will primarily focus on the following key areas: (i) Enhancing model interpretability and causal inference: from Fig. 6(e), we observe that different epigenomic datasets exert varying influences on individual chromatin loops. Therefore, improving model interpretability and causal inference capabilities will offer deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying chromatin loop formation and their functional consequences. (ii) Integrating high-resolution single-cell Hi-C data: We aim to effectively integrate high-resolution single-cell Hi-C data. This will enable us to analyze cell-to-cell variability in chromatin interactions, ultimately revealing cell-type-specific loops and their roles in cellular identity and function. (iii) Optimizing multi-omics data integration strategies: Both GraphLooper and CAESAR integrate Hi-C data with multiple epigenomic datasets. However, the most effective fusion strategies and whether the inclusion of more data consistently enhances performance remain open questions. These aspects warrant further investigation and deliberation. Building on the aforementioned research, future work will focus on further elucidating the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of chromatin loops within the 3D genome structure.
