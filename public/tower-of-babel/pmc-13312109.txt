Feasibility of rearing broilers in open-sided houses for extended periods: impacts of feed withdrawal approaches on growth, carcass traits, and meat quality metrics
Authors: Hazera Khatun, Tanvir Ahmed, Jannatul Ferdows, Mohamed E. Abd El-Hack, Ayman E. Taha, Asmaa F. Khafaga, Bambang Ariyadi, Ankon Lahiry, Anguara Khatun, Shahina Rahman, Masuda Akter, Shubash Chandra Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study evaluated the feasibility of rearing fast-growing broiler chickens in an open-sided house for 42 days in Bangladesh using different feed withdrawal strategies, with particular emphasis on growth performance, meat quality, survivability, and economic returns. A total of 480 Indian River meat-type day-old chicks (DOCs) were reared up to 42 days and randomly allocated into four treatment groups. The first group was a control that provided ad libitum feeding throughout the production cycle (AdLF). The remaining groups received ad libitum feeding for the first 7 days, followed by daily feed withdrawal for 8 h. Accordingly, the second group was subjected to feed withdrawal from 8 to 28 days (3-WksFW), the third group from 8 to 35 days (4-WksFW), and the fourth group from 8 to 42 days (5-WksFW). Each treatment consisted of six replicates with 20 birds per replicate. Key performance indicators, including body weight (BW), body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), survivability, carcass yield, meat quality, and benefit–cost ratio (BCR), were evaluated. As expected, the overall BWG was significantly higher in the AdLF group (3170.52 g/bird; P = 0.000), followed by the 3-WksFW (3040.43 g/bird) and 4-WksFW (2878.86 g/bird) groups, with the lowest value observed in the 5-WksFW group (2510.85 g/bird). Similar trends were observed for final BW and FI. Despite the higher BW, BWG, and FI in the AdLF group, feed withdrawal strategies improved survivability, meat quality, and, notably, economic efficiency. The highest BCR was recorded in the 4-WksFW group (1.14), followed by the 3-WksFW (1.07), 5-WksFW (1.07), and AdLF (1.05) groups. FCR was not significantly affected by treatment; however, abdominal fat deposition was reduced in birds subjected to feed withdrawal. Overall, feed withdrawal improved water holding capacity with a significant decreased of cooking loss, although the meat color was unaffected. In conclusion, the 4-WksFW strategy is recommended for rearing broilers up to 42 days in open-sided housing under local conditions, as it optimizes meat quality, survivability, and profitability in small-scale production systems.

Introduction

In modern poultry production systems, fast-growing broiler chickens are typically reared under controlled environmental conditions for 40 to 50 days to achieve a market weight of approximately 2.5–3.5 kg per bird, ensuring desirable carcass traits and meat quality (Baéza et al., 2012). Continuous genetic improvement, optimized nutrition, and advanced management practices have significantly accelerated growth rates and improved feed conversion ratio (FCR) in commercial broilers (Korver, 2023). However, these advancements have also raised concerns regarding potential compromises in meat quality, bird welfare, and overall production sustainability. A critical challenge in broiler production is balancing rapid growth with acceptable meat quality and bird welfare. In practice, production decisions are often driven by economic considerations, with emphasis placed on maximizing growth performance and feed efficiency, sometimes at the expense of meat quality and survivability. This challenge is particularly evident in developing countries such as Bangladesh, where small- and medium-scale farmers predominantly rear broilers in open-sided housing systems under variable environmental conditions (Banaszak et al., 2021; Rahman et. al., 2025).

In Bangladesh, broilers are commonly marketed at an early age (28–32 days), often at a live weight of 1.6–2.0 kg per bird. This early marketing strategy is primarily adopted by the small-scale farmers to minimize production risks associated with increased FCR, high mortality, environmental stress, disease susceptibility, and fluctuating market prices of the live broilers. However, early slaughter may result in underdeveloped muscle structure, inferior meat quality, and uncertain economic returns. Moreover, limited information is available regarding the feasibility of extending the rearing period of fast-growing broilers to achieve standard market weights under such production systems (Shamma et al., 2014).

Additionally, as birds age, FCR typically deteriorates (Shehata and Elsokary, 2024), further discouraging farmers from prolonging the production cycle. Consequently, producers often prioritize short production cycles and rapid turnover over achieving optimal body weight and meat quality. Feed restriction (limiting the amount or access to feed) or feed withdrawal (complete removal of feed for a certain period) strategies have been proposed as promising and practical management tools to mitigate these challenges. Both these feed management strategies have been shown to reduce metabolic stress, improve survivability, and enhance economic efficiency in broiler production systems (Teyssier et al., 2022). Previous studies have demonstrated that controlled feed withdrawal can improve feed efficiency, reduce mortality, and enhance profitability, although it may reduce final body weight (Ebeid et al., 2022a; Lahiry et. al., 2025; Pizolotto et. al., 2024).

Despite these findings, limited information is available on the application of feed withdrawal strategies for extending the rearing period of fast-growing broilers to 42 days under open-sided housing conditions. Therefore, it is hypothesized that implementing feed withdrawal strategies may enable the rearing of fast-growing broilers to a standard market age of 42 days while maintaining acceptable growth performance, meat quality, survivability, and profitability. The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of different durations of feed withdrawal on growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, and economic returns of broiler chickens reared for 42 days in an open-sided housing system.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The study was conducted at the Bangladesh Agricultural University research and demonstration poultry farm, where fast-growing broiler birds were subjected to different feed withdrawal strategies. All procedures involving fasting, general management, slaughtering, and sample collection were approved by the Animal Welfare and Experimentation Ethics Committee (AWEEC) of Bangladesh Agricultural University (Approval No.: AWEEC/BAU/2025(2)/29(a)).

Experimental design, chick collection and distribution

In total, 480 Indian River Meat broiler day-old chicks (DOCs), purchased from a local hatchery, were randomly assigned to four treatment groups, of which the first group was the control subjected to ad libitum feed supply for the whole production cycle (AdLF). The other three groups were provided ad libitum feed for the first 7 d, and then exposed to 8 h feed withdrawal (fasting from 9:00 am to 5:00 pm). Thus, the second group was exposed to a feed withdrawal schedule from 8 to 28 d, followed by a two-week ad libitum feeding (3-WksFW), and the third group had a feed withdrawal schedule from 8 to 35 d, followed by a one-week ad libitum feeding (4-WksFW). Finally, the fourth group was subjected to the longest duration of feed withdrawal from 8 to 42 d i.e. up to the end of the experiment (5-WksFW). The fasting period from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM was chosen because it falls during the warmest part of the day. Soon after arrival, the individual body weights of the DOCs were recorded, and they were then randomly distributed to different floor pens based on treatments and replications.

Experimental house preparation, litter and light management

A gable-style open-sided house at the university poultry farm was partitioned into 24 pens, each encompassing 24 square feet, providing approximately 1.2 square feet per bird. The stocking density was provided to the birds, which small-scale farmers typically follow in winter. The DOCs were brooded by following the standard management guide of the Indian River Meat broiler strain, providing a temperature of 33°C in the first week, and then it was gradually decreased by 3°C every week. The ambient temperature outside of the experimental house ranges from 16 to 25°C. The experimental house underwent comprehensive dry cleaning, was washed with water, and subsequently disinfected using a 1% Virkon S solution (DuPontTM Virkon® S, Antec International, USA) to destroy disease-causing agents. Additionally, the feeders, drinks, buckets, and other requisite equipment were thoroughly cleaned, washed, and completely disinfected with the same disinfectant before being placed in each pen. The house was subsequently allowed to rest for seven days before the placement of the DOCs. Fresh, dry rice husks were used as bedding material, with a depth of approximately 2 inches. Old newspapers were placed over the litter materials as chick paper, and the chicks were brooded in designated pens for 7 days. The lighting schedule of 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness was followed during the first 3 days, and then, a standard lighting schedule of 20 h of light during days 4-10, and 18 h of light from day 11 onward was maintained for the remaining rearing period. At each stage, the entire lighting management was maintained by adjusting both natural and artificial lighting.

Feed and water management

The feeding regimen was divided into two phases: starter feed in crumble form for the initial three weeks (1-3 weeks), followed by broiler grower feed in pellet form for the subsequent three weeks (4-6 weeks). Commercial feed was purchased from Agro Industries Ltd., a reputed and leading feed milling company in Bangladesh. Table 1 presents the chemical composition of the starter and grower feeds. Before introducing the DOCs into the pens, they were provided with fresh and clean drinking water supplemented with a 5% glucose solution @ 1g/2L (Albi Glucose-C vet, Albion Laboratories Limited, Chattogram, Bangladesh) and vitamin C @ 1g/5L (Rena-C Premix, Renata Limited, Dhaka, Bangladesh) to mitigate stress associated with transportation and handling. All experimental birds were provided with ad libitum access to feed for the first 7 days, after which the feed was supplied according to the feed withdrawal protocol set for AdLF, 3-WksFW, 4-WksFW, and 5-WksFW groups. In the AdLF group, ad libitum feeding was provided throughout the entire trial period (from day 1 to 42). At the same time, birds in 3-WksFW, 4-WksFW, and 5-WksFW groups underwent an 8-h fasting regimen with varying durations of feed withdrawal. The 3-WksFW and 4-WksFW treatment groups had feed withdrawal from days 8 to 28 and days 8 to 35, respectively, followed by ad libitum feeding. The 5-WksFW group experienced feed withdrawal from days 8 to 42, until the end of the trial. During fasting periods, feeders were removed from the pens at 9 am and replaced at 5 pm to ensure 8 hours of feed withdrawal. Conversely, in the ad libitum feeding regimen, feed was supplied at least twice daily—once in the morning (6:00 am) and again in the afternoon (6:00 pm), ensuring that the feeders were never left empty. Vaccination of the birds, biosecurity measures and sanitation

The IB+ND vaccine [RaniVexTM Plus - containing the LaSota strain of Newcastle Disease (ND) Virus and the Massachusetts B48 strain of Infectious Bronchitis (IB) Virus in live, freeze-dried form] was administered to all experimental birds on day 5, with a booster dose given on day 24. Additionally, birds received the Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD) vaccine (GumboMedTM Vet, containing a live attenuated intermediate strain of the Gumboro virus) on day 12, followed by a booster on day 19. All vaccinations were given via eye drops. A strict biosecurity protocol was enforced in and around the poultry shed, including thorough hand and foot sanitation, visitor restrictions, and the installation of a footbath at the entry to the experimental units. Essential fencing was constructed around the shed. All necessary measures were taken to prevent disease contamination and protect the birds from insects, rodents, and wildlife.

Carcass yield and meat quality determination

At 35 days of age, and at the end of the trial (42 days), all experimental birds were weighed individually with an electronic balance. Subsequently, three birds from each treatment group (both on days 35 and 42) with body weights very close to the average of the total weight in that particular group were randomly selected. Since the flock was quite uniform, and the body weight of all the birds was very close to the pen average value, only three birds were considered for the carcass and meat quality analysis. The selected birds were subsequently weighed, euthanized via cervical dislocation, plucked, and then processed for carcass metrics and meat quality analysis. To ensure pre-slaughter fasting, feed was removed from the pens 12 h before the birds were sacrificed, while water was continuously provided to facilitate proper bleeding. The birds were euthanized via cervical dislocation in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act and processed following the standard protocol. The carcass weight of each sampled slaughtered bird was measured and documented following the removal of all inedible parts, including blood, head, neck, shank, feathers, skin, and visceral organs. The dressing percentage was then calculated by determining the ratio of carcass weight to liveweight. The weights of cut-up parts (breast, wing, thigh, and drumstick) and other anatomical parts (head, liver, gizzard, heart, neck, shank, and abdominal fat) were measured, and the percentage of each part in relation to the live weight was calculated accordingly. The dorsal side of the breast fillets, weighing about 25 g with a thickness of approximately 2.5 cm, were collected and quickly stored at 4°C for 24 h to evaluate the meat quality attributes.

After 24 h of chilling, the electrode probe of a pH meter (Lovibond, Sensodirect, pH110) was inserted up to 5 mm into the breast meat from the skinless surface to measure the sample pH. The color (L*-lightness, a*-redness, and b*-yellowness) of the breast fillets was measured individually using a Konica Minolta Chroma Meter CR-410 (Konica Minolta, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). For each measurement, three readings were obtained, and the final value for each sample was calculated by averaging these readings. To calculate the water-holding capacity (WHC), 1g of the meat sample was placed in an Eppendorf tube sealed with cotton to absorb moisture. The tube was then centrifuged at 10,000 RCF for 10 minutes at 4°C. Lastly, the weight loss due to centrifugation was calculated. To calculate cooking loss (CL), weighed meat samples were placed in separate double-layer polythene bags and cooked in a water bath at 80°C for 30 minutes. The weight of the cooked meat samples was recorded after ten minutes of drying. Samples of breast meat weighing about 25 g (wet weight) were collected from the same place, suspended on a string in an airtight container, and refrigerated at 4°C for 24 h. The weight loss from freezing was measured to determine the meat sample's drip loss (DL). Finally, WHC, CL, and DL percentages were calculated using the formulas described by Khatun et al. (2025).

Data collection, record keeping, and cost-benefit analysis

The experiment was performed in an open-sided gable-type house, and temperature and relative humidity were recorded three times daily at 6:00 am, 12:00 pm, and 10:00 pm. The initial body weight of the DOCs was recorded before allocation to the pens, and their body weight was measured weekly. Birds were weighed in the morning before being fed. The weekly average weight gain of birds in each replication was determined by subtracting the initial body weight from the final body weight. The feed intake of the birds in each replicate group was determined weekly by subtracting the leftover feed from the amount supplied that week. Subsequently, the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated by dividing the weekly recorded feed intake by the weight gain of experimental birds. European production efficiency factor (EPEF) and European Broiler Index (EBI) were calculated considering body weight, average daily weight gain, FCR and survivability following the formula: EPEF = {Body weight x Survivability (%)/FCR x days of slaughter} x 100; and EBI = Average daily gain {(g/chick)/d} x Survivability (%)/FCR x 10) (Brankovic Lazic et al., 2021; Marcu et al., 2013; Van, 2003). The death of the birds, if any, was recorded accordingly to determine the survivability. At the end, birds are dressed into several cut-up parts, such as thigh, breast, wing, head, neck, shank, or giblet, and all of the cut-up parts are weighed and recorded. A cost-benefit analysis was performed based on total production costs and revenue generation. The total production cost was calculated by considering expenses for DOCs, feed, electricity, litter materials, utilities, vaccines, medicine, labor, and birds’ mortality. The costs incurred for DOCs, electricity, litter, vaccines, medicines, and labour are fixed and equal for all the treatment groups; however, the amount of feed consumed by the birds differs according to the implementation of various feed withdrawal strategies. Further, birds’ mortality was also varied according to the treatment application. Both factors directly influenced the costs of production in different treatment groups. In contrast, revenue was derived from the sale of birds, which was also varied due to the variation of final body weights of the birds among the treatment groups. The profit was determined by deducting total cost (TC) from total revenue (TR) generated from the selling of live birds.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27). Growth performance, production efficiency, and benefit–cost ratio indices were analyzed using the pen as the experimental unit, whereas carcass and meat quality traits were analyzed using three birds per treatment as the experimental unit. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance by Levene’s test. A one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with treatment as the fixed factor, applying the model: Y = μ + α + ε, where Y is the dependent variable; μ is the overall mean; α is the fixed variable; and ε is the random error. When ANOVA indicated significant differences (P<0.05), means were separated using Tukey's honest significant difference test as a post-hoc test. The results are presented as mean values with pooled SEM, and different superscripts denote statistical significance.

Feed intake

Weekly feed intake (FI) is presented in Table 2. No significant differences were observed among treatments during the 1st week. However, FI differed significantly (P = 0.000) from the 2nd to the 6th weeks after feed withdrawal. Cumulative FI was highest in the AdLF group (5578.83 g/bird), followed by the 3-WksFW (5373.43 g/bird), 4-WksFW (4850.10 g/bird), and 5-WksFW (4458.63 g/bird) groups, indicating a progressive reduction in feed consumption with increasing restriction duration. In terms of percentage, 3-, 4-, and 5-WksFW groups consumed 3.68, 13.06, and 20.08 % lower amounts of feed compared to the birds fed ad libitum.

An increase in FI was observed in the 3-WksFW group during the 5th week, coinciding with the reintroduction of ad libitum feeding. In contrast, birds in the 4-WksFW group did not exhibit a comparable increase in FI following refeeding.

Feed conversion ratio, survivability, EPEF, and EBI

The weekly feed conversion ratio (FCR) is presented in Table 2. Similar to BW and BWG, no significant differences were observed during the 1st, 3rd, and 4th weeks, whereas significant differences (P = 0.000) appeared during the 2nd, 5th, and 6th weeks.

Despite these weekly variations, the overall FCR values across treatments were comparable and not statistically different. The final FCR values were 1.76, 1.77, 1.68, and 1.78 for the AdLF, 3-WksFW, 4-WksFW, and 5-WksFW groups, respectively. A total of seven birds died during the experiment: four in the AdLF group and three in the 3-WksFW group, while no mortality was recorded in the 4-WksFW and 5-WksFW groups. All mortalities occurred during the later stages of production (5th and 6th weeks). The overall survivability rates were 76.67%, 83.33%, 100%, and 100% for the AdLF, 3-, 4-, and 5-WksFW groups, respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

Body weight and body weight gain

Birds given continuous access to feed (AdLF) achieved the highest final body weight, which is expected under unrestricted feeding conditions. Interestingly, broilers in the 3-WksFW group reached comparable body weight, suggesting an effective recovery following early feed withdrawal. This pattern indicates that a relatively short restriction phase followed by a longer refeeding period allows birds to regain lost growth, most likely through compensatory mechanisms. In contrast, extending the withdrawal period reduced the opportunity for recovery. Birds in the 4-WksFW group showed intermediate body weight, while those subjected to the longest restriction (5-WksFW) exhibited the lowest values. These findings highlight the importance of the duration and timing of feed access in determining final growth outcomes. Compensatory growth, often described as accelerated growth following a restriction phase (Ebeid et al., 2022a; Lahiry et al., 2025; Radulovic et al., 2021), was evident in birds exposed to 3- and 4-WksFW, particularly during the refeeding phase. However, this recovery was only partial, as none of the restricted groups fully matched the AdLF group. Similar observations have been reported where short-term restriction allows recovery, whereas prolonged fasting limits growth potential (Shafiei et al., 2018; Shamma et al., 2014). On the other hand, some studies have reported minimal or no negative effects of feed withdrawal on final body weight (Azis and Afriani, 2017; David and Subalini, 2015; Saleh et al., 2019), suggesting that responses may vary depending on management conditions and environmental factors.

Body weight gain followed a similar trend. Birds under AdLF showed a steady and uninterrupted growth pattern, reflecting optimal nutritional conditions (Radulovic et al., 2021). In contrast, restricted birds displayed periods of reduced growth followed by accelerated gain once feed was reintroduced. Despite this recovery, final gains remained lower, indicating that compensatory growth has biological limits and is strongly influenced by total feed intake and utilization efficiency.

Feed intake and feed conversion ratio

Feed intake declined progressively as the duration of feed withdrawal increased, confirming the direct relationship between feeding time and consumption. Birds under AdLF consumed the greatest amount of feed, while those in the 5-WksFW group consumed the least. However, temporary increases in feed intake during the refeeding phase in the 3- and 4-WksFW groups suggest an adaptive response aimed at compensating for earlier restriction.

These findings are consistent with previous reports showing reduced intake with increasing restriction periods (Azis and Afriani, 2017; Shafiei et al., 2018; Shamma et al., 2014). Despite these differences in intake, FCR remained statistically unchanged among treatments. This indicates that birds subjected to feed withdrawal were able to utilize feed as efficiently as those fed ad libitum. Similar outcomes have been reported in earlier studies (Azis and Afriani, 2017; David and Subalini, 2015; Fondevila et al., 2020), although improvements in FCR under restriction have also been documented (Lahiry et al., 2025; Saleh et al., 2019; Shamma et al., 2014).

The ability of restricted birds to maintain FCR may be linked to physiological adaptations, including improved digestive efficiency and possible changes in gut morphology (Ebeid et al., 2022a; Fondevilla et al., 2020). Together, these adjustments help birds cope with intermittent feeding without compromising feed utilization. Notably, economic efficiency indices (EPEF and EBI) were similar or improved in restricted groups, suggesting that reduced feed intake combined with acceptable performance can enhance overall production efficiency (Lahiry et al., 2025; Rahman et al., 2025).

Carcass characteristics and meat quality

Most carcass traits were not influenced by feed withdrawal, except for carcass weight and abdominal fat. Birds under AdLF had higher carcass weight and greater fat deposition, while increasing restriction duration led to a gradual reduction in both parameters. The 3-WksFW group-maintained values close to the control, likely due to sufficient refeeding time.

Excess abdominal fat is generally undesirable in broiler production, and its reduction under feed restriction aligns with previous findings (Abdel-Hafeez et al., 2017; Ebeid et al., 2022b; Englmaierová et al., 2021; Koçer et al., 2018). This reduction may result from lower energy intake and increased mobilization of fat reserves during restriction (Englmaierová et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2010).

Meat quality traits showed noticeable improvement in restricted birds, particularly in water-holding capacity (WHC) and cooking loss (CL). Lower body weight in these groups may have reduced metabolic stress, contributing to better muscle characteristics. Previous studies have also reported improved meat quality following feed restriction (Ebeid et al., 2022b).

One possible explanation involves oxidative stress. Birds with higher body weight may experience increased metabolic activity, leading to greater production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Aryal et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2022). Excess ROS can impair muscle structure and reduce WHC (Cartoni Mancinelli et al., 2023), whereas moderate levels are necessary for normal cellular function (Nawaz and Zhang, 2021). This may explain the relatively poorer meat quality observed in the AdLF group. Importantly, meat color parameters remained unaffected, indicating that feed withdrawal did not adversely affect the visual quality of the meat.

Bird survivability

Survivability is a key concern when extending the rearing period under open-sided housing conditions. Mortality in this study was observed mainly in later growth stages and was higher in birds with continuous feeding or shorter restriction periods. In contrast, no mortality was recorded in birds subjected to longer withdrawal durations (4- and 5-WksFW). Lower feed intake and reduced body weight in these groups may have lessened metabolic stress, improving the birds’ ability to cope with environmental challenges. Similar improvements in survivability under feed restriction have been reported previously (Ebeid et al., 2022a; Shamma et al., 2014).

These findings are particularly relevant under tropical conditions, where heat stress remains a major constraint. Although the present study was conducted in winter, the results suggest that feed withdrawal could be an effective strategy to mitigate mortality risks when birds are reared for extended periods.

Cost–benefit analysis

Feed cost remains the dominant expense in broiler production (Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025; Lahiry et al., 2025; Widiarta et al., 2024). As expected, birds under AdLF incurred the highest feed cost due to greater intake, whereas feed costs declined with increasing restriction duration. Despite lower feed costs, the 5-WksFW group did not achieve the highest economic return because of lower body weight and income. Conversely, although AdLF birds generated higher gross returns, profitability was negatively affected by mortality losses.

The best economic outcome was achieved in the 4-WksFW group, where moderate growth, lower feed consumption, and minimal mortality were effectively balanced. These findings highlight that profitability is not solely determined by growth rate but rather by the combined effects of feed cost, feed efficiency, and survivability. Similar conclusions have been reported in recent studies (Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2023; Vanlalhmangaihsanga et al., 2025).

Conclusion and future work

The results indicate that moderate feed withdrawal (8 h daily from day 8 to 35) can support rearing broilers up to 42 days in open-sided housing while maintaining acceptable growth, improving meat quality, enhancing survivability, and optimizing profitability. Further research during the summer season under extreme heat stress would be interesting for validation of the current findings, with optimization of growth, rearing duration, meat quality, survivability, gut health, bird’s welfare, and profitability of small-scale farming in an open-house. Several other physiological and welfare indicators, including stress hormones and metabolic responses, can also be considered to better understand the long-term implications of feed withdrawal strategies in broiler production systems.
