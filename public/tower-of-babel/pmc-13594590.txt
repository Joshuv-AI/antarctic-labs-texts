Invisible impact: 3D scanning sprays do not inhibit forensic STR analysis
Authors: Vivien Fejes, Dominika Szűcs, Viktor S Poór
Published: 2026

Introduction

The 3D reconstruction of crime scenes or evidence is an emerging technique alongside traditional photography-based documentation. The purposes of rendered 3D models can range from documentation [1] and education [2] to demonstrations in court [3,4] and to support calculations, such as bloodstain pattern analysis [5,6]. Also, the scanned model can serve as a source for 3D printing [7]. The most common methods are photogrammetry- and Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR)-based [8].

Photogrammetry captures multiple images of the same object from different viewpoints, and computer algorithms then calculate a 3D model from the relative distances of corresponding points in the photographs [9,10]. LiDAR measures distances from the scanner on multiple points for reconstruction [11]. Both techniques struggle with transparent or reflective surfaces (e.g., glass, mirrors, polished metal) because they rely on reflected light. A widely adopted solution to this problem is the use of 3D scanning sprays, which create a matte layer on the surface of the object being examined [12]. For ease of use, most of these sprays are volatile and sublime after application, leaving no visible residue, so cleaning of scanned surfaces is unnecessary.

Although 3D scanning sprays are widely used for 3D crime scene reconstruction, their effects on genetic material have not yet been investigated. Sampling for genetic analysis as early as possible during crime scene investigation is always preferable, but in some cases (e.g., newly identified traces or unsuccessful DNA recovery), additional sampling may be necessary.

Therefore, our study aimed to examine the effects of four different 3D modeling sprays on blood stain samples and on low quantities of DNA deposited through touch DNA.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

To simulate biological evidence at a crime scene, blood and fingerprints were deposited on separate microscope slides. Samples were obtained from two healthy volunteers following informed consent.

Clean microscope slides (Menzel-Gläser Microscope Slides, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) were taken directly from unopened, factory-sealed packaging. While the slides were not labeled of certified as DNA-free, three blank slides were tested, and none showed DNA contamination.

For blood samples, 1 μl of anticoagulated (with Na-citrate) venous blood was pipetted on one set of microscope slides and allowed to air-dry at room temperature. To model touch DNA, volunteers were instructed not to wash their hands or shake hands with others for at least 1 h prior to sample collection. For each experiment replicate, each finger was pressed onto a separate microscope slide. The slides were randomized for the following experiment and sprayed with a thin layer of 3D scanning spray. After application, the slides were left at room temperature for 24 h to allow the spray coating to sublime.

Four 3D scanning sprays from two manufacturers (AESUB: Scanningspray Vertriebs GmbH, Recklinghausen, Germany; 3D-basics: AprintaPro GmbH, Vienna, Austria) were purchased (Fig. 1). The main practical difference between these sprays is the volatility of the coating layer (Table 1).

DNA extraction

A DNA and DNase-free (ISO 18385 certified) forensic swab (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) was moistened with lysis buffer and rubbed over areas either stained with blood or containing fingerprint residues. The swabs were immediately subjected to DNA extraction using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The protocol “Purification of Total DNA from Animal Tissue” was applied to both blood and fingerprint samples.

DNA was eluted in a two-step procedure; 100 μl of elution buffer was added to the membrane, and after a 1-min incubation, the pure DNA was eluted. The same volume of elution buffer was added again to increase the total DNA yield.

The touch DNA samples were concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter Unit (Merck Millipore, Burlington, USA) with a molecular weight cutoff of 30 kDa.

STR profiling

STR amplification was carried out using the Investigator ESSplex SE QS Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). For blood samples, 1 ng of DNA was added to the PCR mixture, whereas for fingerprint samples, the maximum amount of template DNA was added.

Following amplification, amplicons were separated and detected using an ABI PRISM 310 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The analytical threshold was set to 50 RFU.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out with the open-source software JASP (ver. 0.96 JASP Services B.V., Hilversum, The Netherlands). Groups were compared with the Kruskal-Wallis test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The spray treatment did not hinder the sampling procedure or the DNA extraction. The sublimating coating disappeared completely, and the treated microscope slides were indistinguishable from the untreated controls (Fig. 2). In contrast, the permanent spray remained on the surface, covering the traces that were also collected during swabbing, resembling fine powder along the biological material. However, this white substance did not interfere with the DNA extraction steps or clog the column.

Sublimation of the tested scanning sprays over 24 h. A black background was used to illustrate the white paint layer, but it reduces the visibility of the bloodstains.

All alleles were successfully detected in the blood droplet samples regardless of the spray treatment; there was no significant difference in the number of alleles detected between the groups (p = 0.416).

The low-copy-number (LCN) touch DNA samples exhibited much higher variability, with the number of detected alleles ranging from 0 to 34 (Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed between the tested groups (p = 0.163).

Fig. 4 shows representative electropherograms of two sets of experiments. All alleles of the blood stain samples (Fig. 4a–e) were detected. The peaks between and within the samples do not differ significantly, and the alleles are in balance, except for the vWA locus of the AESUB "orange"- treated sample (Fig. 4 d). Signal intensities of the longer DNA fragments did not decrease. The Q and S peaks are the internal control and quality marker of the kit; similar peak heights indicate that no inhibition occurred during amplification.

Examples of electropherograms. The blood (a-e) and touch (f-j) samples shown here were processed in two sets (one for blood and one for touch) and belong to the same volunteer. The graphs are on the same scale.

In the touch samples (Fig. 4f–j), the almost identical peak heights of Q and S show no sign of DNA polymerase inhibition either. Amplification of three (Fig. 4f–h) out of five samples was unsuccessful; some allele peaks do appear on the electropherograms, but they are below the detection threshold (for example, X of amelogenin in Fig. 4 f and h). All alleles were identified in two of the samples (Fig. 4i and j). The peak heights are similar to those of the blood stain samples, but please note that the DNA samples from the touch samples underwent concentration (the 100 μl volume of eluted DNA was reduced to ∼20 μl, resulting in an approximate fivefold increase in DNA concentration). Furthermore, considerably more template was added to the PCR mixture of the touch samples than of the blood stain samples (15 μl vs. ∼1 μl). The decreasing trend in peak heights of the longer DNA fragments indicates degradation, even though only 24 h passed between deposition and DNA extraction. Allele imbalance was observed in both samples, most notably at the amelogenin locus. In the case of low-copy-number (LCN) samples, non-specific amplification can occur. In the AESUB "orange"-treated sample (Fig. 4 i) one extra peak appeared, but since it is an off-ladder peak, it does not complicate the evaluation of the profile.

Discussion

While DNA sampling should generally be prioritized to prevent contamination or degradation, additional analysis and sample collection may be needed after 3D scanning. Such situations may arise when DNA amplification fails and repeat sampling is necessary, or when previously unnoticed traces are discovered during subsequent examination.

A similar situation is fingerprint analysis, where the development of latent fingermarks and DNA sampling can compete with one another. On one hand, dactyloscopy focuses on the visualization, recording, and assessment of fingerprints, as well as their subsequent comparison with reference databases [13]. On the other hand, the analysis of genetic material deposited alongside fingerprints is part of forensic genetics. Fingerprint-associated samples contain only trace amounts of DNA and are considered low-copy-number (LCN) samples. Their analysis can be further complicated by the reagents used in routine dactyloscopic evidence collection (e.g., fluorescent or magnetic powders and chemicals such as ninhydrin or cyanoacrylate), whose effects on STR profiling have been investigated [13,14].

Our study indicates that the tested 3D scanning sprays do not interfere with DNA extraction, unlike fingerprint visualization. All 1 μl bloodstain samples yielded complete STR profiles regardless of spray treatment. In the simulated touch DNA samples, substantially greater variability was observed, with the proportion of detected alleles ranging from 0% to 100%. Although the recovery rate in some of the sprayed groups was marginally higher than in the untreated control group, the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.163).

This variability is expected for LCN samples. Allelic drop-out and occasional allele drop-in can complicate the correct evaluation of DNA profiles. These factors are commonly attributed to stochastic effects during PCR, including preferential amplification, in which one allele of a heterozygous locus is amplified more efficiently than the other [15].

Fingerprints contain DNA in very low quantities; therefore, even under controlled laboratory conditions, when deposited biological material is not exposed to any DNA-damaging effects, successful STR typing cannot be guaranteed. The results observed in our control samples are consistent with findings reported in the literature [[15], [16], [17], [18]].

DNA contamination remains a major concern in forensic genetics. No evidence of contamination was detected in our experiments. However, the airflow generated by spray propellants could mobilize dust, skin particles, or hair fragments, contributing to secondary transfer of biological material.

Conclusion

In our experiments, the 3D scanning sprays tested did not inhibit DNA recovery or STR profiling from either bloodstains or simulated touch DNA samples. Manufacturers are required to publish the solvent components of these products in their Safety Data Sheets (SDSs), but the detailed compositions are proprietary information. PCR-based STR profiling is a robust method; other molecular techniques might be more sensitive to the effects of spray application. Therefore, laboratories intending to combine 3D scanning with subsequent biological analyses are advised to validate the compatibility of the specific scanning spray and analytical method used in their workflow.
