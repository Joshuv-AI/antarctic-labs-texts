A Systematic Evaluation of Multi-modal Approaches to Complex Player Profile Classification
Jason Starace, Terence Soule, Ph.D

Abstract
Modern adaptive games require nuanced player understanding for personalization, yet current modeling approaches use simplified 5-10 category taxonomies that inadequately capture player diversity. Existing behavioral clustering cannot distinguish players with different motivations who exhibit similar actions. We present a systematic evaluation of multi-modal classification at realistic scales, examining how behavioral telemetry combined with semantic context performs when supporting 36 distinct player profiles. Using 19,413 gameplay sessions from an AI-controlled text-based Role Playing Game (RPG), we compared behavioral-only baselines against multi-modal approaches integrating action sequences with semantic descriptions. Traditional behavioral clustering achieved only 10% accuracy for 36-category classification, limited by semantic conflation where morally opposite actions produce identical statistical features. Our multi-modal LSTM architecture processing complete action-text pairs improved accuracy to 21%, revealing both the potential and limitations of non-conversational data. Analysis by behavioral complexity revealed the core limitation: non-neutral profiles achieved 42% accuracy (15× above random baseline), matching established benchmarks, while neutral profiles’ performance dropped to 25% (still 9× above random). This performance gap exposes why behavioral-only approaches fail—identical actions like ’help the merchant’ cannot reveal whether players are genuinely neutral or employing strategic patience (players who appear neutral while waiting for advantageous moments to act). Without access to player reasoning, even multi-modal approaches struggle with this distinction, though the above-baseline performance confirms that a meaningful signal exists. With game-based prediction beyond 20 categories being virtually unexplored these findings establish empirical benchmarks for complex player modeling: behavioral data plateaus at ∼ \sim 10% for 36 categories, while multi-modal integration enables 25% accuracy. For game designers, this provides concrete evidence that meaningful personality-based adaptation requires conversational interaction—our results demonstrate that predefined action choices alone cannot reliably capture player intent. Our systematic evaluation at a 36-category scale offers essential guidance for developing adaptive games that truly understand their players.

Related Work

As noted previously, player classification declines rapidly as the number of profiles increases with almost no research being completed for more than 20 categories. This complexity ceiling stems from fundamental limitations in traditional approaches. Most studies rely on aggregated behavioral metrics—average actions per minute, total resources collected, completion rates—that discard temporal context distinguishing player personalities. Two players may collect identical resources but differ fundamentally in when and why they make these choices. For example, early-game resource hoarding suggests different motivations than late-game collection, yet aggregation treats these behaviors identically.

3
Sequential Model Architecture and Analysis

To establish performance benchmarks, we first evaluated traditional aggregated approaches on our dataset of 19,413 gameplay sessions. XGBoost processing behavioral features (action frequencies, normalized ratios, completion metrics) achieved only 10.4% accuracy for 36-category classification versus 2.8% random baseline, confirming fundamental limitations when behavioral patterns decouple from player intent. Sequential modeling preserves temporal dependencies lost in aggregation, enabling distinction between similar actions with different moral contexts. Thus, we developed LSTM architectures processing complete gameplay sequences with multi-modal features, to capture these temporal dependencies that aggregation discards.

3.1
Dataset and Architecture

All 36 profile classes (9 alignments × 4 motivations), discussed by Starace et al.
[
15
]
, combine the 9 Dungeons & Dragons alignments with 4 core motivations. Each profile class has at least 500 games, with sequence counts varying (4,437-7,911 per profile) as games contain different numbers of sequential decision points. Sequence-aware balancing under-samples to
\sim
4,100 sequences per class, reducing imbalance from 1.78:1 to 1.15:1.

Our initial LSTM model used 530 features per game decision, but 96.6% of these were BERT text embeddings—numerical representations of the game’s descriptions. Despite having all this information, the model achieved only 2.75% accuracy, no better than random guessing. The problem: the model focused on memorizing how rooms and actions were described rather than learning how players actually behaved.

We tried compressing all 530 features into 256 dimensions, hoping the model would focus on what mattered, but this failed. So we completely redesigned our approach. Instead of letting text dominate, we created a balanced 176-feature representation where text takes up only 72.7% of the space. We expanded behavioral tracking from 10 to 48 features, capturing: how players transition between action types (15 features), what choices were available versus what was selected (12 features), temporal patterns like early-game versus late-game behavior (15 features), and movement patterns through the dungeon (6 features). This re-balancing improved accuracy to 22.77%—the model finally learned to recognize behavioral patterns rather than memorize text.

3.3
Investigating the Accuracy Ceiling

To understand why performance plateaued, we conducted systematic complexity reduction experiments using bi-directional LSTM classifiers with attention for binary classification:
Binary Classification: Lawful (Good, Neutral, Evil) vs Non-Lawful achieved 62-63% accuracy—barely above random for a binary task. This suggests alignment ambiguity persists even at the simplest level.
Three-way Classification: Lawful/Neutral/Chaotic classification reached only 44.7% (vs. 33.3% random baseline), confirming that the problem isn’t just category count but fundamental semantic ambiguity.
Neutral Correction: Attempting to correct the model’s tendency to over predict neutral alignments improved overall accuracy to 53.67% for alignment-only tasks, but profile accuracy remained limited.

3.4
Neutral Versus Non-Neutral

Non-Neutral Profiles (16 categories: excluding True Neutral, Lawful Neutral, Chaotic Neutral) have clear behavioral signatures—good characters help others, evil characters cause harm. For these profiles the model achieved 42.13% accuracy, matching benchmarks for 16-class personality prediction from social media text.
Neutral Profiles (20 categories: all neutral alignments × motivations): These profiles lack distinctive moral indicators. The same action—"help the merchant"—could indicate genuine neutrality, strategic patience (waiting for an advantageous moment to act), or role-playing inconsistency. 3.5
Implications for Complex Player Modeling

With many categories and only behavioral data, the models plateau at relatively low accuracies. Integration of temporal data leads to significantly higher accuracy, and the removal of neutral behaviors produces even higher accuracy. In these experiments specifically, behavioral data alone plateaus at
\sim
10% for 36 categories, temporal integration with action semantics reaches 22% accuracy, and non-neutral alignments reach 42% accuracy. The consistent under-performance across all complexity levels—from binary to 36-class—confirms that the limitation is semantic, not architectural.

The significantly higher classification rates observed in experiments on personality prediction using interactive social media data (e.g. extended conversations) strongly suggests that games requiring nuanced personality modeling or identification will need to incorporate more sustained interactions between the player and the classifying AI, such as interactive dialogue. Just as social media personality prediction leverages conversational text, adaptive games can infer player intent through natural dialogue and exploratory questions, capturing the subtle cues that distinguish genuine neutrality from strategic patience. The consistency of these limitations across multiple architectures and complexity levels confirms that current approaches face a fundamental ceiling rather than merely requiring better optimization.

4
Discussion

Our findings reveal a fundamental limitation in gameplay-based player modeling: behavioral sequences alone cannot capture the intent behind player actions. The 17-point accuracy gap between non-neutral (42%) and neutral (25%) profiles demonstrates that while distinctive moral behaviors can be classified at rates comparable to personality prediction benchmarks, ambiguous or balanced playstyles require a better understanding of player reasoning.

These empirical thresholds have direct implications for serious game design. For simple adaptation (5-10 profiles), behavioral telemetry suffices. Our baselines achieving 51.2% motivation accuracy suggest that broad player categories (Safety, Speed, Wanderlust, Wealth) remain detectable through aggregated metrics. For complex adaptation (20+ profiles), conversational interaction becomes essential. Games requiring nuanced understanding—distinguishing strategic patience from genuine neutrality—must incorporate dialogue systems and/or design mechanics that discourage neutral behavior patterns. This could manifest as reflective prompts (’Why did you help the merchant?’), character dialogue with NPCs asking about player motivations, journal systems where players document their reasoning, or voice interaction enabling natural language explanations during gameplay.

The semantic gap manifests most clearly in neutral alignments, where identical actions stem from different motivations. Consider the action “help the merchant”: for Lawful Good players, this reflects genuine altruism; for True Neutral players maintaining balance; for Chaotic Evil players, it might represent a strategic deception before betrayal. This finding parallels personality prediction research in other domains. Social media studies achieving 60-80% accuracy on Myers-Briggs types
[
3
,
12
]
benefit from rich conversational data where users explain their thoughts and feelings. Game telemetry, constrained to predefined actions, lacks this explanatory dimension. Even our multi-modal approach—incorporating action semantics and context—cannot bridge this gap without access to player reasoning.

Our results suggest current player modeling pipelines require fundamental restructuring. Rather than mining increasingly complex behavioral patterns, adaptive games should integrate conversational AI from the design phase. The recent availability of large language models makes this feasible—players can now engage in natural dialogue about their choices, providing the semantic context our models desperately need. This shift from behavioral to conversational player modeling aligns with broader trends in AI. Just as recommender systems evolved from click-through rates to understanding user intent through search queries, game AI must evolve from action tracking to understanding player reasoning through dialogue.

5
Limitations

Several limitations bound the interpretation of our findings:

Synthetic Data Generation:
Our gameplay sessions were generated by LLMs simulating player behavior, which may not capture human inconsistencies, learning curves, and emotional responses.

Single Game Genre:
Experiments used a text-based dungeon crawler with discrete choices, limiting generalizability to other game genres with continuous actions or social mechanics.

Text-Based Environment:
Our semantic features derived from textual descriptions, limiting applicability to visual games requiring 3-D interaction mapping.

Profile Granularity:
Our static 36-profile taxonomy simplifies human personality and cannot capture contextual motivational shifts.

6
Conclusion

This work reveals a fundamental semantic gap in gameplay-based player modeling through the first systematic evaluation at 36-category complexity. Our core discovery—that non-neutral profiles achieve 42.1% accuracy while neutral profiles plateau at 24.8%—exposes why behavioral approaches fail: identical actions cannot reveal player intent. Multi-modal LSTM integration improves accuracy from 10% to 21% (7.5× baseline), but performance ceilings persist across all complexity levels.

These findings establish concrete design guidelines for adaptive games. For broad player categories (
\leq
10 profiles), behavioral telemetry achieves practical accuracy thresholds around 50%. For complex personality modeling (20+ profiles), our 21% ceiling indicates that conversational interaction becomes essential—games requiring nuanced adaptation must integrate dialogue systems to capture player reasoning. Educational games distinguishing learning styles, therapeutic applications assessing emotional states, and social platforms modeling user personalities all exceed behavioral modeling capabilities.

Our empirical benchmarks provide the game AI community with essential complexity thresholds: behavioral aggregation plateaus at
\sim
10%, multi-modal sequential modeling reaches
\sim
22%, and even optimal conditions cap at
\sim
42% for complex taxonomies. These results demonstrate that meaningful personality-based adaptation requires fundamentally different approaches than current behavioral clustering methods.

These limitations open promising research directions. Conversational integration represents the most direct path forward—implementing real-time dialogue within gameplay and developing hybrid models that combine behavioral sequences with periodic dialogue to capture player reasoning with minimal disruption. The recent availability of large language models makes natural player-AI dialogue feasible, potentially bridging the semantic gap our models encountered.

Deception detection emerges as a critical parallel direction, enabling identification of players deliberately misleading classification systems—essential for competitive games where players may manipulate matchmaking or difficulty adjustment. This framework could extend beyond games to any adaptive system vulnerable to strategic user behavior.

Additional opportunities include validating findings with human players to confirm whether the neutral/non-neutral performance gap persists, investigating cross-domain transfer from social media personality models, and developing interpretable player modeling that explains classifications to both developers and players. While our synthetic data revealed fundamental limitations, human studies may uncover behavioral signals invisible to current approaches, offering new pathways toward truly adaptive gaming experiences.
