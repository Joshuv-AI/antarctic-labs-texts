Integrative bioinformatics and experimental analysis identify S100A10 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in pancreatic adenocarcinoma
Authors: Pengzhao Song, Tianqi Chen, Qingqun Guo, Yahui Sun, Pengfei Gu, Yong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is an extremely invasive malignancy with limited effective treatment options. S100 calcium-binding protein A10 (S100A10), a protein-coding gene, plays a critical role in tumor progression. However, its correlation with immune cell infiltration and PAAD prognosis has not been previously reported. This study aimed to systematically explore the expression pattern, biological function and clinical value of S100A10 in PAAD.MethodsThe expression of S100A10 in PAAD was investigated in a number of databases, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER 2.0), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). To evaluate the association between S100A10 expression and patient survival, both the Kaplan-Meier plotter and TCGA datasets were utilized. Furthermore, Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, and protein-protein interaction network evaluations were carried out to clarify the molecular mechanisms related to S100A10. Immune infiltration data were sourced from TCGA, and the methylation profile of S100A10 was evaluated using the UALCAN and MethSurv platforms. The expression levels of S100A10 were further confirmed in PAAD cell lines using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and western blot analyses. To explore the functional implications of S100A10, assays including Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), wound healing, Transwell, and colony formation, were performed to assess its influence on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of PAAD cells.ResultsThe expression levels of S100A10 were notably elevated in PAAD compared with control group (P<0.05). S100A10 demonstrated high diagnostic precision, as evidenced by an area under the curve of 0.989, also with P<0.05. Higher levels of S100A10 correlated with poor prognosis in patients with PAAD. Cox regression analysis showed that S100A10 expression, tumor (T) stage, lymph node (N) stage, and histological grade were independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS). A prognostic nomogram incorporating S100A10 expression was created to predict the patient outcomes. Functional analyses revealed that S100A10 was implicated in JAK-STAT and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways. Additionally, S100A10 was associated with humoral immune responses and DNA methylation processes, and hypomethylation of S100A10 was correlated with poor prognosis in PAAD. Immune infiltration analysis indicated that S100A10 expression was negatively correlated with various immune cell types. Furthermore, in vitro investigations showed that the silencing of S100A10 notably obstructed cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionsS100A10 is an oncogenic protein that promotes PAAD progression. Its elevated expression and hypomethylation status make it a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for PAAD.Keywords: S100A10, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), biomarker, cancer immune infiltrates, methylation

Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is one of the most fatal cancers and is distinguished by an extremely poor prognosis and high mortality rates (1,2). The predominant form of PAAD is pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, which accounts for 80–90% of all pancreatic malignancies (3). As one of the most aggressive carcinomas, PAAD is often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the lack of distinctive early symptoms (4). Consequently, detection frequently occurs after metastasis or during heightened tumor progression, both of which drastically reduce the survival rate (5). Despite advancements in immunotherapy and targeted therapy, substantial improvements in patient outcomes are yet to be achieved (6). Therefore, it is critical to understand the core mechanisms driving PAAD progression in order to identify new therapeutic targets.

S100 Calcium Binding Protein A10 (S100A10), a member of the S100 protein family, consists of two subunits that combine to create an active dimer (7). At the cell membrane, S100A10 associates with annexin A2 (ANXA2) to create a heterotrimeric complex, which plays a pivotal role in cytoskeletal organization, endocytosis, and intracellular cargo transport (8). Additionally, S100A10 plays a role in the regulation of Toll-like receptor signaling, which in turn influences innate immune responses and pathways associated with inflammation (9). It also influences the production of serotonin (5-HT) by interacting with the 5-HT receptor (10). S100A10 exerts a substantial impact on diverse physiological processes, and its dysregulation has been linked to multiple disorders, including schizophrenia, irritable bowel syndrome, and depression (10,11). Recent evidence has connected S100A10 to cancer development and progression. Specifically, its association with ANXA2 enhances plasminogen activation by increasing the efficacy of both urokinase and tissue-type plasminogen activators, thereby promoting the transformation of plasminogens into plasmin. This process initiates the activation of matrix metalloproteinases, resulting in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix and supporting tumor proliferation and invasiveness (12). In various malignancies, including gastric, ovarian, and breast cancers, increased S100A10 expression is correlated with poor prognostic outcomes (13-15). However, comprehensive studies of S100A10 expression and its functional role in PAAD are limited. Further investigations are crucial to clarify its contribution to the progression of this aggressive malignancy.

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the correlation between S100A10 expression and clinical features, especially its impact on PAAD prognosis. Through enrichment and immune infiltration analysis, we explored how S100A10 might contribute to PAAD development. In vitro experiments preliminarily verified these findings, providing insights for understanding S100A10’s role in PAAD and developing better diagnostic and treatment strategies. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0444/rc).

Methods

Expression and prognostic analysis of S100A10 in PAAD

To explore S100A10, we used Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER 2.0) (https://compbio.cn/timer2/) (16) for a pan-cancer analysis of its gene expression. Data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) dataset GSE28735 were employed to analyze S100A10 mRNA levels. We also confirmed S100A10 expression in PAAD via the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) (17). Using the R package “survival” (version 3.6), we generated survival maps for S100A10, dividing the cohort into high- and low-expression groups at a 50% threshold. Visualizations were done with ggplot2. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, based on TCGA data, determined S100A10’s prognostic relevance and built a visual prognostic model. The diagnostic performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Moreover, the cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/) for Cancer Genomics was used to explore S100A10 amplification and mutation patterns. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Clinical statistical analysis regarding prognosis, model construction, and evaluation

Statistical evaluations were performed using the R software version 4.2.1. Clinicopathological variables related to overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) among patients from TCGA were investigated using univariate Cox regression analyses and the Kaplan-Meier approach. Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was utilized to appraise the prognostic implications of S100A10 expression in conjunction with diverse clinical parameters. The median value of S100A10 expression was determined as the cutoff point to classify patients into high- and low-expression groups. Differences in OS, PFI, and DSS between these groups were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and comparisons were conducted using a two-sided log-rank test.

The independent prognostic factors determined via multivariate Cox analysis were subsequently employed to develop nomograms as individualized predictions of 1-year and 3-year survival probabilities. Nomograms were generated using the “rms” package. These nomograms incorporated significant clinical variables and included calibration plots to assess predictive accuracy. Calibration curves were analyzed by comparing the probabilities predicted by the nomograms against the actual survival outcomes, with the 45° line representing perfect concordance. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with the significance threshold set at P<0.05.

S100A10 co-expression and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis in PAAD

To investigate the genes that were co-expressed with S100A10 in PAAD, we utilized the LinkedOmics database (http://www.linkedomics.org) (18), which integrates multi-omics along with clinical records originating from 32 separate cancer classifications. Co-expression analysis was conducted using Spearman’s correlation, and the outcomes were represented as heat maps. Furthermore, the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) (19) was employed to construct PPI networks associated with S100A10. This database predicts the functional associations of proteins spanning 261,033 orthologs derived from 89 fully sequenced genomes. Interaction networks were visualized using the Cytoscape software. To clarify the functional and pathway-related implications of the co-expressed genes, we utilized the clusterProfiler R package to carry out and visualize Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis [including cellular component (CC), biological process (BP), and molecular function (MF)], as well as gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA).

DNA methylation analysis

To investigate the potential function of S100A10 in PAAD progression, we used the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) (20), which offers a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic cancer data. Our principal objective was to assess the methylation status of the S100A10 promoter region, which is a crucial regulatory element affecting gene expression. To evaluate the clinical significance of S100A10 methylation, we employed the MethSurv database (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/methsurv/) (21), an online platform developed for multivariate survival analysis based on DNA methylation profiles. We evaluated the prognostic implications of S100A10 methylation in patients with PAAD by combining methylation data with clinical outcomes.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

The magnitude of infiltration of 24 diverse immune cell types in PAAD was measured by utilizing the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) approach implemented in the GSVA R package (22). The relative enrichment scores of immune cells were calculated, and correlations between S100A10 expression and immune cell infiltration were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Moreover, the differences in immune cell infiltration between cohorts with high and low S100A10 expression were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Cell culture and transfection

All laboratory experiments in this study were performed with three biological replicates to ensure reliability. Human PAAD cell lines SW-1990, MIA PaCa-2, BxPC-3, PANC-1, and AsPC-1 along with the pancreatic ductal cell line hTERT-HPNE (HPNE) were obtained from the Center for Molecular and Cellular Sciences. These cell lines were cultivated in either RPMI-1640 or DMEM/high glucose medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China), both of which were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 IU/mL of penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained under standard conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2.

For gene silencing experiments, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs)—si-negative control (NC), si-S100A10-1, and si-S100A10-2 (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China)—were transfected into cells at 70–80% confluency using Lipofectamine® 3000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. L3000015, MA, USA). The sequences of S100A10 siRNAs were as follows: sense, 5'-CCA UGA UGU UUA CAU UUCA-3' and anti-sense, 5'-UGA AAU GUA AAC AUC AUGG-3'. The S100A10‑2 siRNA sequences were as follows: sense, 5'-CCA UUG CAU GCA AUG ACUA-3' and anti-sense, 5'-UAG UCA UUG CAU GCA AUGG-3'. The siRNA negative control primer sequences were: forward, 5'-ATT CAC CTG CCA TGT AT-3' and reverse, 5'-GAA CAC TAA TGT TGA CAG-3'.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using an RNA extraction kit (LS1040; Promega, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a one-step RT-PCR kit (A6120; Promega, Shanghai, China). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis was conducted with the SYBR Green SuperMix system (TSE201, Tsingke Biotechnology, Beijing, China), and the relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The thermal cycling conditions for the qRT-PCR were set as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 5 seconds, and annealing/extension at 60 ℃ for 30 seconds. The primer sequences employed were as follows: S100A10-forward: 5'-GGCTACTTAACAAAGGAGGACC-3', S100A10-reverse:5'-GAGGCCCGCAATTAGGGAAA-3'; β-actin-forward: 5'-CACCATTTGCAATGAGCGGTT-3', β-actin-reverse: 5'-AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT-3'.

Protein isolation and western blotting

Total protein was extracted using a Protein Extraction Kit (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For immunoblotting, membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk/Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA): β‑actin (1:10,000, 20536‑1‑AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and S100A10 (1:1,000, 11250‑1‑AP, Proteintech). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 in TBST/1% BSA, Proteintech) for 2 h at 37 ℃. Following further washes, signals were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged using a chemiluminescence system (ChemiDocTM MP, Bio‑Rad, Shanghai, China).

Wound healing assay

After transfection, PAAD cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultivated until they reached confluence. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was employed to generate a linear scratch in the monolayer, creating a cell-free area. Images of wound closure were acquired at 0 and 48 h. For each condition, five random fields were photographed. This experiment was performed in three biological replicates to ensure the reliability of the results.

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers were used to evaluate the cell migration and invasion. The transfected PAAD cells were re-suspended in serum-free medium at a density of 4×105 cells/mL, and a volume of 200 µL from this suspension was added to the upper chamber. Subsequently, the lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of medium containing 10% serum. After a 24-hour incubation, the cells adhering to the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 5 min, and imaged using an optical microscope. This experiment was performed in three biological replicates to ensure the reliability of the results.

Colony formation assay

PAAD cells were cultivated in six-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells/well and maintained for 12 days, with the medium refreshed every two days. After formation of discernible colonies, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Beijing, China) for a duration of thirty minutes, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio) for the same duration. The resulting colonies were subsequently quantified using the ImageJ software. This experiment was performed in three biological replicates to ensure the reliability of the results.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

PAAD cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells/well. Subsequently, the plates were placed in a humidified incubator set at 37 ℃ with a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h to allow cell growth and adhesion. After incubation, cells were transfected with either siRNA-NC (negative control) or siRNA-S100A10. To monitor cell viability over time, measurements were performed at 0, 24,48,72, and 96 h post-transfection. For each measurement, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Mishu Biotech, Xi’an, China) was added to each well, and the optical density at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader. This experiment was performed in three biological replicates to ensure the reliability of the results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0) as well as R software (version 4.2.1). Data sourced from TCGA were processed utilizing various R packages. Differences in S100A10 expression across groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test or Wilcoxon test. A logistic regression model was used to evaluate the relationship between S100A10 expression and clinical characteristics of patients with PAAD. The prognostic relevance of S100A10 was determined through Cox regression analysis, with results depicted in forest plots and nomograms created using the ggforest and survival R packages. The accuracy and reliability of the model were assessed, and externally validated using calibration curves.

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or the median with the interquartile range, and analyzed by means of two-sample t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-squared test. Image data derived from western blotting, wound healing, Transwell assays, and colony formation assays were analyzed using ImageJ software. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All statistical tests were performed as two-tailed.

Results

Elevated expression of S100A10 in PAAD

Pan-cancer examination sourced from TIMER 2.0, relying on TCGA clinical patient data, demonstrated that S100A10 expression was remarkably increased in the majority of tumor tissues, such as PAAD, cholangiocarcinoma, and cervical squamous cell carcinoma, in comparison to normal tissues (Figure 1A). Analysis of TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases confirmed that the expression of S100A10 was elevated in PAAD tissues compared to normal pancreatic tissues (Figure 1B). To further validate these findings, we incorporated the GSE28735 dataset from the GEO database (GPL6244 platform) and observed consistently increased S100A10 levels in both paired and unpaired samples (Figure 1C). Moreover, ROC curve analysis demonstrated the high discriminatory power of S100A10 expression in identifying patients with PAAD, with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.989 (Figure 1D). These results highlight S100A10 as a robust biomarker for the diagnosis of PAAD, offering potential utility in disease management. Consistent with the transcriptomic data, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of the HPA revealed significantly upregulated S100A10 protein expression in PAAD tissues (Figure 1E). To explore the genetic basis of S100A10 dysregulation, we investigated genomic alterations and copy number variations using the cBioPortal platform. Analysis of the OncoPrint map for patients with PAAD in the TCGA dataset showed that 13% of cases harbored S100A10 gene amplifications or missense mutations (Figure 1F). These findings suggest that both transcriptional and genetic alterations may contribute to elevated S100A10 expression in PAAD.

Prognostic value of S100A10 in PAAD

The prognostic relevance of S100A10 expression in PAAD was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Patients were categorized into two distinct cohorts, those exhibiting high expression levels and those with low expression levels, based on the optimal threshold determined by the minimum P value associated with S100A10 expression levels. Notably, individuals with elevated S100A10 expression had a significantly poorer OS than those in the low-expression cohort (Figure 2A). Similarly, PFI and DSS were markedly reduced in the high-expression group (Figure 2B,2C). To further clarify the prognostic implications of S100A10 levels, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. S100A10 expression, tumor (T) stage, lymph node (N) stage, and histological grade were identified as independent risk factors for OS in patients with PAAD (Figure 3). These results indicate that the expression level of S100A10, when considered along with various clinicopathological characteristics, may function as a significant prognostic marker. Construction and validation of a prognostic nomogram

The nomogram effectively illustrates how an increase in the total score, representing the accumulation of adverse factors, corresponds to a worse prognosis for patients with PAAD (Figure 4A). Calibration curves were used to assess the accuracy and reliability of the nomogram’s forecasting ability and revealed a high level of agreement between the predicted and actual results (Figure 4B). Heatmaps were used to identify 50 genes that exhibited both positive and negative correlations with the expression of S100A10 (Figure 5A,5B). Additionally, a PPI network was established incorporating 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were constructed using the STRING database and visualized using Cytoscape (Figure 5C).

GSEA identified key pathways associated with S100A10, including transcriptional regulation by small RNAs, immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells, MET signaling, base excision repair, G1/S DNA damage checkpoints, TP53-regulated transcription of cell cycle genes, apoptosis, homology-directed repair, and DNA methylation (Figure 5D).

Relationship between S100A10 expression and methylation

To investigate the possible mechanisms contributing to the overexpression of S100A10 in PAAD, we analyzed the correlation between S100A10 expression and methylation status using various online resources. Evaluation of the UALCAN database indicated a notable decrease in DNA methylation within the promoter region of S100A10 in PAAD tissues relative to normal pancreatic tissues (P<0.001) (Figure 6A). Subgroup analysis further demonstrated elevated DNA methylation levels in normal pancreatic tissues across various factors including grade, sex, age, lymph node metastasis status, and clinical stage (Figure 6B-6F). Notably, the majority of methylation sites within the S100A10 gene were hypomethylated in PAAD. Moreover, PAAD patients with low S100A10 methylation had significantly poorer OS rates than those with high S100A10 methylation (P<0.05) (Figure 6G,6H).

We conducted a comprehensive analysis to explore the relationship between S100A10 expression and immune cell infiltration. The results of our study revealed a clear inverse relationship between the expression levels of S100A10 and the presence of diverse immune cell populations, including natural killer (NK) CD56bright cells, mast cells, cytotoxic cells, gamma delta T cell (Tgd) cells, T follicular helper (TFH) cells, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (Figure 7A). Figure 7B presents a comparative analysis of immune cell infiltration levels between patient groups characterized by low and high S100A10 expression. Additionally, Figure 7C-7I illustrate that the group exhibiting elevated S100A10 expression demonstrated significantly reduced enrichment scores for NK CD56bright cells, cytotoxic cells, mast cells, Tgd cells, pDCs, and TFH cells compared to the group with lower S100A10 expression levels.

Knockdown of S100A10 inhibits PAAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To investigate the functional role of S100A10 in PAAD, we assessed both the protein and mRNA expression levels in several PAAD cell lines. S100A10 expression was significantly higher in PANC-1, AsPC-1, MIA PaCa-2, SW-1990, and BxPC-3 cells compared to hTERT-HPNE cells, both at the mRNA (Figure 8A) and protein levels (Figure 8B,8C). Subsequently, we knocked down S100A10 using siRNA vectors in PAAD cell lines. S100A10 mRNA and protein levels were markedly reduced in both AsPC-1 (Figure 8D-8F) and PANC-1 (Figure 8G-8I) cells compared with those in the negative control.

To assess the effects of S100A10 knockdown on cell migration, we performed a wound healing assay. Knockdown of S100A10 significantly reduced cell migration compared to the negative control (Figure 9A-9C). Furthermore, the Transwell assay demonstrated a notable reduction in the invasiveness of PAAD cells following the knockdown of S100A10 (Figure 9D-9F). Colony formation assays provided additional evidence that the knockdown of S100A10 significantly reduced the proliferative potential of both AsPC-1 and PANC-1 cells (Figure 9G-9I). Finally, CCK-8 assay results revealed a marked decrease in the proliferation capacity of cells with S100A10 knockdown after a 96-hour period in both the AsPC-1 and PANC-1 cell lines (Figure 9J,9K). Collectively, these results support the conclusion that S100A10 is an oncogenic factor in PAAD.

Discussion

PAAD is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide (23). Despite being the eighth most prevalent type of cancer, it is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality (24). Although some moderate enhancements in OS have been attained, PAAD prognosis continues to be rather poor, as only 11 percent of patients survive for 5 years following diagnosis (24). This poor outlook is largely attributed to the fact that most PAAD cases are diagnosed at advanced, unresectable, or metastatic stages, stressing the urgent need for novel biomarkers to improve diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis.

As a plasminogen receptor, S100A10 plays a key role in its conversion to plasmin, thereby promoting extracellular proteolytic activity (25). This protein is closely associated with key oncogenic processes, including intratumoral angiogenesis and tumor cell migration, invasion, and metastasis (26). Tumor-associated macrophages are now regarded as vital to the tumor microenvironment. They affect cancer processes such as angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), chemoresistance, immunosuppression, and macromolecule handling (27). Swisher et al. found that the surface presence of S100A10 on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) significantly contributes to cancer progression (28). These findings emphasize the essential role of S100A10 in cancer and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

This study revealed an increased mRNA and protein expression of S100A10 in PAAD through analyses utilizing data from TCGA database, HPA, and IHC assays. Increased S100A10 expression was substantially correlated with unfavorable clinicopathological characteristics and was proven to be an independent prognostic indicator of reduced OS, shorter PFI, and diminished DSS. ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.989, underlining the diagnostic accuracy of S100A10. Functional in vitro assays demonstrated that S100A10 enhanced PAAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, confirming its potential as both a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.

The plasminogen receptor S100A10 is crucial for the transformation of plasminogen into plasmin, thereby molding extracellular proteolytic activity (29). Oncogenesis involves intratumoral angiogenesis, intratumoral blood flow, invasive capacity, migratory behavior, and metastatic spread of tumor cells (2,30). Recent studies on S100A10 have focused on its roles in cancer progression and inflammation, and as a potential biomarker, particularly in tumor microenvironment interactions and cellular signaling pathways (2,25). S100A10 involvement in modulating immune responses and promoting metastasis has attracted significant attention in cancer research (31). This upregulation may correlate with processes such as DNA methylation and infiltration of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, GSEA and KEGG/GO analyses highlighted the roles of DNA methylation and immune pathways in pancreatic cancer, revealing potential therapeutic targets for combating tumor progression. Functional assays suggested that S100A10 enhances cell proliferation, migratory activity, and invasive propensity in PAAD.

Enrichment analysis revealed significant pathways associated with S100A10, which comprised the mechanisms underlying pancreatic cancer proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. The DNA methylation pathway illustrates the consequences of irregular DNA methylation, which leads to the repression of tumor suppressor genes and the stimulation of oncogenes. This methylation alteration contributes to tumor proliferation, enhances resistance to apoptotic processes, and increases the ability of pancreatic cancer cells to evade programmed cell death, all of which are critical for their aggressive nature. Additionally, the immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cell pathways underscore the complex immune landscape of pancreatic tumors to effectively evade immune detection and enhance their metastatic potential. Furthermore, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, as indicated in the KEGG analysis, plays a vital role in modulating cytokine signaling, which can drive cancer cell proliferation and survival. The activation of this pathway may also facilitate inflammatory responses that support tumor growth. Collectively, our findings underscore S100A10’s the role in multiple signaling pathways that drive pancreatic cancer progression.

Notably, pathways associated with DNA methylation underscore the significant role of epigenetic regulatory mechanisms in tumorigenesis (32). Aberrant DNA methylation can lead to silencing of critical tumor suppressor genes, facilitating unregulated cellular proliferation and contributing to the complex genetic landscape of pancreatic cancer (33). Such epigenetic modifications not only disrupt normal cellular functions, but also create a permissive environment for additional mutations and genomic instability, which are hallmarks of aggressive cancer phenotypes. Understanding these methylation patterns offers potential therapeutic avenues for targeting epigenetic alterations to restore normal gene function and inhibit tumor growth (34). Numerous studies have demonstrated that stimulation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway through cytokines is essential for the differentiation and maturation of immune cells, as well as for sustaining the balance of the immune system (35,36). The JAK-STAT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in complex immune regulation and is activated by a diverse array of cytokines (37). These functions encompass not only the identification of cancer cells, which are primarily facilitated by interferons and involve signaling via STAT1 and STAT2, but also immune evasion, mainly propelled by IL-6-STAT3 signaling (38). Moreover, it is well established that the JAK-STAT pathway strengthens antitumor immune surveillance by promoting the activation and cytotoxic functions of natural killer cells in response to cytokines such as IFNs, IL-2, and IL-15 (39,40). Coupled with findings related to the estrogen signaling pathway, hormonal influences may intersect with immune dynamics, potentially exacerbating tumor development (41,42). In summary, S100A10 may facilitate the initiation and progression of PAAD through intricate cancer-associated signaling pathways.

Considering the critical significance of the TME in cancer progression, along with the fundamental involvement of immune cells infiltrating the tumor within this environment (43), our objective was to investigate the relationship between S100A10 and immune cell infiltration in PAAD. Recent studies have emphasized the composition of the TME and its connection to immune activity, highlighting the infiltration of immune cells such as B cells, T cells, macrophages, and NK cells in the tumors of patients (44). Although these cells are capable of contributing to antitumor immune responses and aiding immune surveillance, a disruption in immune activity may permit tumors to bypass immune-mediated destruction (44,45). In our study, we observed a positive correlation between S100A10 expression and NK CD56bright cells, whereas there was a negative correlation with the presence of cytotoxic cells, cytotoxic cells, mast cells, Tgd cells, pDCs, and TFH cells. This indicates that S100A10 might act as a pro-tumorigenic immune protein in PAAD, with its increased expression possibly promoting immune cell infiltration, which helps tumors evade the immune system. Immune checkpoint molecules expressed on immune cell surfaces play essential roles in modulating the immune response (46). Monoclonal antibodies targeting these checkpoints have become essential tools for the treatment of various advanced cancers (47). In this context, elevated S100A10 levels are linked to adverse checkpoint expression patterns, suggesting that this increase may predict poor response to immunotherapy in patients.

The uncontrolled multiplication of tumor cells driven by oncogenic mutations is a typical feature of cancer (48). In the present study, S100A10 mutations were identified in 13% of the patients with PAAD. Recent studies have demonstrated that epigenetic changes, especially gene methylation patterns, can provide significant insights into cancer-related biomarkers (49). On numerous occasions, studies centered on DNA have played a part in the creation of improved approaches for diagnosing, assessing, and treating particular types of tumors (50). Consequently, this study identified crucial CpG residues that were methylated in a manner related to the prognosis of patients with PAAD.

Although our results provide new perspectives on the association between S100A10 and PAAD, it is important to recognize the inherent limitations of this investigation. A potential sample bias may arise from data downloaded directly from public databases. To enhance the reliability of our results, the sample size should be increased in future studies. Furthermore, we were unable to evaluate the chemotherapy regimens that patients had undergone, which might have had an impact on both their prognosis and the expression of S100A10. Further mechanistic-related and in-vivo experiments are required to confirm the results of this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study revealed that the expression of S100A10 can serve as a significant biomarker of PAAD, with substantial relevance for diagnostic, prognostic, and immunotherapeutic applications. The insights gained from this research provide a crucial basis for subsequent studies focused on elucidating the pivotal pathways that underlie the aggressive characteristics and progression of PAAD tumors. Ultimately, this will aid the formulation of innovative targeted treatment strategies for this malignancy.
