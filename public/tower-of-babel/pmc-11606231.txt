Wellen’s syndrome caused by severe ostial stenosis of the left main coronary artery: a case report
Authors: Jie Tan, Xiaoxian Liao, Tao Tan, Xingyu Li, Huilong Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWellen’s syndrome may indicate severe stenosis or even occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending coronary artery. It may progress to acute myocardial infarction. Early recognition and an early invasive strategy are critical to avoiding impending myocardial injury. To our knowledge, only one case of Wellen’s electrocardiogram patterns caused by left main coronary artery (LM) lesions has been reported.Case presentationA 61-year-old man with a history of hypertension and smoking presented to the emergency department complaining of paroxysmal chest pain for the preceding 6 days. In the present case, the T-wave symmetric inversions in the V2 and V3 leads is associated with severe ostial stenosis of LM, which appears similar to Wellen’s type B pattern.ConclusionsPractitioners should familiarize themselves with such uncommon LM lesions patterns and perform emergent reperfusion therapy.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Wellen’s syndrome, Electrocardiogram, Left anterior descending coronary artery, Left main coronary artery

Introduction

Wellen’s sign is highlighted in the 2023 ESC Guidelines for managing acute coronary syndromes as an electrocardiographic abnormality in patients with non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) [1]. It is an important pattern to recognize, as it reflects critical stenosis of the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, which may lead to a large anterior myocardial infarction if left untreated. The Wellen’s pattern, characterized by biphasic or deeply inverted T waves in the anterior precordial leads, is thought to represent a phase of reperfusion following a transient occlusion of the LAD. This explains the absence of ST-segment elevation, despite the fact that these patients are at high risk for complete occlusion and significant myocardial damage.

The recognition of Wellen’s syndrome is clinically significant because it provides a window of opportunity for intervention before full occlusion occurs. Indeed, in some scenarios, Wellen’s syndrome may be equivalent to a ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) pattern due to the high likelihood of progression to complete LAD occlusion [2–4]. Timely identification of these electrocardiographic changes, even in the absence of ST-segment elevation, can prevent misdiagnosis and allow for early intervention, reducing mortality and improving outcomes. Severe stenosis of the left main coronary artery (LM) is more dangerous and, although rare, should be considered when presented with this ECG pattern. We present a case of Wellen’s syndrome type B caused by severe ostial stenosis of LM.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man with a history of hypertension and smoking presented to the emergency department complaining of paroxysmal chest pain for the preceding 6 days, which persisted for approximately 5 min each time. The patient did not have a family history of cardiac disease. On arrival, the patient was asymptomatic. On physical examination, he was vitally stable, and cardiac auscultation revealed normal first and second heart sounds with no murmurs. Other systemic examinations were normal.

His cardiac troponin I level was < 0.1 ng/mL (normal, < 0.3 ng/mL) and peaked at 0.15 ng/mL over the next 3 h; other routine laboratory results were within normal ranges. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated apical and anterior septal wall hypokinesia, with an ejection fraction of 52%. The initial ECG and the ECG performed the next day without percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) are shown in Fig. 1. The initial ECG (Fig. 1A) showed sinus bradycardia, biphasic T-wave in lead V2 with minimal ST-segment elevation, ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in lead aVL, and minimal ST-segment elevation in inferior wall leads. The second-day ECG (Fig. 1B) without PCI showed sinus bradycardia, T-wave symmetric inversions in leads V2 and V3, ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in lead aVL, T-wave inversion in lead I and minimal ST-segment elevation in inferior wall leads. The ST-segment was no dynamic change in inferior wall leads.

These ECG findings, along with the patient’s history of intermittent chest pain, were consistent with Wellen’s syndrome type B. The patient was treated for unstable angina using aspirin (300 mg), ticagrelor (180 mg), high dose atorvastatin (40 mg), and therapeutic heparin drip (4000u). Coronary angiography on the first day showed severe ostial stenosis of the LM, moderate stenosis in the distal left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) and mild proximal stenosis of the right coronary artery. Although the patient was pain-free, he initially declined coronary stent implantation. Over the following days, the patient had paroxysmal angina. We emphasized that delaying intervention could result in a significant anterior myocardial infarction and potentially fatal outcomes due to the involvement of the LM coronary artery. After a thorough discussion with the surgical team, we concluded that, for cases of ostial or mid-shaft left main disease without bifurcation involvement or multivessel coronary artery disease, coronary artery bypass grafting is generally the preferred approach. However, PCI is also considered a reasonable alternative [5]. The patient subsequently provided informed consent for PCI.

Pre-PCI, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) was performed to further assess the lesion’s characteristics, confirming severe ostial stenosis of the LM without significant calcification. PCI was attempted in the LM, which was cannulated with a 6-French guide catheter (JL 3.5 Guiding). The LAD was crossed with a 0.014-inch wire (BMW wire). The LCX was crossed with a 0.014-inch wire (Runthrough wire). The LM lesion was deployed with a 4.0 mm×15 mm rapamycin eluting stent. A 5.0 mm×12 mm post-dilatation balloon was delivered into the stent along the guide wire, and the balloon was expanded at 10-12 atm×6s from distal to proximal segment. IVUS post-PCI confirmed optimal stent expansion and apposition. There was no residual stenosis and no evidence of complications (Fig. 2).

B1 and B2, Coronary angiography after stent implantation revealed revascularization of LM. C1 and C2, Coronary angiography showed a dominant right coronary artery

He was discharged after another 3 days in a stable, pain-free condition. The day 7 (the first day after PCI) ECG (Fig. 3) showed a biphasic T-wave in lead V2, T-wave inversion in lead V3, T-wave inversion in lead aVL and minimal ST-segment elevation in inferior wall leads. The patient was in good health at the 1-month follow-up visit. His ECG during his outpatient appointment 1 month later showed resolution of the T-wave inversions (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The abnormal ST-segment and T-wave morphology, now known as Wellens’ syndrome, was first described in the early 1980s [4]. In a study of 1,260 patients hospitalized for unstable angina between July 1980 and December 1985, 204 (16%) displayed this ECG pattern. After excluding patients with recent myocardial infarction and missing data, 180 patients were further analyzed. All had at least 50% stenosis in the proximal LAD, with 18% having total occlusion [6]. Another study showed Wellen’s syndrome has an incidence of 5.7% in patients with acute coronary syndrome [7]. Wellen’s syndrome is classified into type A or type B, which account for approximately 75% and 25% of the cases, respectively. Type A is characterized by a biphasic T-wave in leads V2 and V3, whereas type B is characterized by a deep T-wave inversion in the same leads. However, in both types these ECG changes can extend to involve any pericardial chest leads. In addition, the criteria require a history of angina, the absence of precordial Q waves, and normal or slightly elevated cardiac biomarkers [8]. The pathophysiological mechanisms of the ECG pattern have not been elucidated yet. The Wellens’ ECG pattern was originally related to atherosclerotic subocclusion of the LAD, it was subsequently observed in other conditions characterized by a reversible left ventricular dysfunction (stunned myocardium) or myocardial edema, by either ischemic or nonischemic causes, including takotsubo syndrome and intracranial bleeding [9–13].

Surprisingly, despite severe ostial stenosis of the LM, typical Wellen’s syndrome type B was documented. Patients with an ACS due to LM culprit are at high risk of in-hospital mortality and at continued mortality risk post hospital discharge [14, 15]. This patient was successfully treated with PCI. His symptoms resolved following stent implantation. After the reperfusion therapy, the ECG showed resolution of the T-wave inversions in the V2 and V3 leads, indicating there was resolution of the Wellen’s pattern. The patient’s coronary angiograph revealed a dominant right coronary artery and a small left circumflex artery. Myocardial ischemia of the anterior left ventricular wall may explain the patient’s ECG pattern, which is reflected in the characteristic T-wave changes. In LM disease, transient ischemia followed by reperfusion may produce these characteristic T-wave changes in the anterior wall leads.

Hu H et al. first reported that Wellen’s ECG pattern caused by LM terminal bifurcation lesions involving the ostium of LAD and left circumflex coronary artery, with 95% and 90% stenosis, respectively [16]. However, to our knowledge, there are no reports regarding severe ostial stenosis of LM with ECG features similar to Wellen’s type B pattern.

The clinical implications of recognizing atypical presentations of Wellen’s syndrome are significant. Delayed diagnosis or misinterpretation of the ECG findings could lead to missed opportunities for timely intervention. As demonstrated by De Zwaan et al., 75% of patients with Wellen’s syndrome developed extensive anterior wall myocardial infarction only weeks after admission [4]. Once Wellen’s syndrome is identified in patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome, an urgent or even emergent invasive strategy is recommended [1, 4, 9]. If there is significant proximal LAD occlusion, PCI or coronary bypass surgery should be performed to prevent extensive anterior myocardial infarction. LM stenosis can be particularly hazardous due to the extensive myocardial territory it supplies. Although rare, this condition should be considered when the corresponding ECG pattern is observed. Prompt recognition and management of such cases are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and prevent fatal outcomes.

Conclutions

Characteristic T-wave changes in the V2 and V3 leads, resembling Wellen’s type B ECG pattern, may indicate involvement of the left main coronary artery. This high-risk pattern requires prompt recognition, and emergent reperfusion therapy should be considered to prevent myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death.
