Advancements in in vitro simulated digestion-derived peptides for type 2 diabetes mellitus management: From therapeutic challenges to dietary opportunities☆
Authors: Pravin Ojha, Tang Xia, Zhou Liangfu, Sheng Qinghai, Suqin Chen, Bimal Chitrakar, Liu Jielin, Bhaskar Mani Adhikari, Roman Karki, Lu Yaxin, Tan Jianxin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a growing global health concern, with significant economic implications. Bioactive peptides (BAPs) produced by in vitro simulated digestion (IVSD) can be safe and cost-effective alternatives to pharmacological treatments. These peptides act holistically by inhibiting key enzymes, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and restoring β-cell functions. Although the efficacy of IVSD-derived peptides has been validated through in vitro, in vivo, and in silico assays, translating these findings into therapeutic medicine faces several challenges, from limited clinical trial data to scaling up the peptide-synthesis process. Despite these obstacles, IVSD-derived peptides offer opportunities for functional food formulation and nutraceutical development for T2DM regulation. This review focuses on developing innovative strategies to incorporate these peptides into dietary interventions for managing T2DM. By addressing these knowledge gaps and challenges, the transition of IVSD-derived BAPs from laboratory discoveries to industrial and therapeutic applications offers a promising approach for managing T2DM.Keywords: Bioactive peptides, Enzyme inhibition, Functional foods, In vitro simulated digestion, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a significant and escalating global public health concern that imposes a substantial economic burden worldwide. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reported that 90–95 % of all diabetes cases are type II diabetes mellitus (T2DM). In 2024, an estimated 589 million people aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes, a figure projected to increase to 853 million by 2050. The financial burden of T2DM is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries, contributing significantly to global healthcare expenditure, which reached USD 1.015 trillion by 2024 (IDF, 2025). In addition to economic impact, as reviewed by Rangel-Galván et al. (2024), T2DM was responsible for 1.5 million direct deaths in 2019, and 7,60,000 indirect deaths due to renal and cardiovascular disorders.

The increasing prevalence of T2DM is driven by multiple factors such as obesity, poor dietary habits, sedentary lifestyle, genetic predisposition, and environmental influences (IDF, 2025). Although pharmacological treatments are effective in managing hyperglycemia, their long-term use is often associated with adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal discomfort, cardiovascular complications, and metabolic disturbances (Barrientos-Ávalos et al., 2024). These limitations have spurred a growing interest in alternative and adjunct therapies that are safe, effective, and more accessible.

Naturally derived bioactive peptides (BAPs), particularly those produced by in vitro simulated digestion (IVSD), have emerged as promising candidates for T2DM management. IVSD mimics human gastrointestinal processes, offering a reliable and reproducible method for generating therapeutic peptides (Brodkorb et al., 2019). These peptides exert a range of beneficial effects, including the inhibition of digestive enzymes (α-amylase and α-glucosidase), suppression of dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV), enhancement of insulin sensitivity, antioxidant activity, and support for β-cell function (Du, Gong, et al., 2024).

The antidiabetic properties of food-derived BAPs, including those produced by IVSD, have been investigated using a combination of in vitro assays, in vivo animal models, and in silico computational approaches (Du, Gong, et al., 2024; Peredo-Lovillo et al., 2022). However, translating these findings into effective therapeutic interventions or developing functional foods and nutraceuticals remains challenging. Key challenges include a lack of clinical trial data in humans, insufficient understanding of peptide absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profiles, as well as ethical considerations and technical difficulties associated with large-scale production, purification, and ensuring stability (Du & Li, 2022; Ye et al., 2022). Moreover, their potential allergenicity and bitterness can limit their practical applications (Du & Li, 2022; Fadimu et al., 2022). Despite these obstacles, research continues to identify potent antidiabetic peptides from various food sources, such as milk (Dang et al., 2025), legumes (Feng et al., 2025), grains (Fu et al., 2023), and marine organisms (Gong et al., 2020).

While previous reviews have discussed the general role of BAPs in health (Du & Li, 2022; Ye et al., 2022) and the bioactivity of peptides from IVSD (Du, Gong, et al., 2024), a comprehensive review focusing specifically on the advancements, therapeutic challenges, and dietary opportunities related to IVSD-derived peptides for T2DM management remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to bridge this critical knowledge gap, particularly regarding the specific challenges related to the bioavailability, stability, clinical translation, and scalability of IVSD-derived peptides. These aspects are crucial for translating the current scientific findings into industrial, therapeutic, and nutritional applications. This review also attempts to identify key areas for future research to realise the full potential of IVSD-derived peptides. This accumulated understanding can significantly contribute to the development of innovative food-based dietary interventions for the effective prevention and management of T2DM that are potentially safer, more accessible, and more sustainable than conventional pharmaceuticals.

Type II diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

T2DM is caused by a combination of lifestyle, dietary, genetic, and environmental factors. It typically begins with insulin resistance, in which the body compensates by producing insulin. During this period, β-cell function is impaired, which leads to inadequate insulin release and elevated postprandial glucose levels. As β-cell dysfunction progresses, both postprandial and fasting glucose levels increase, leading to the onset of T2DM (Lima et al., 2022). T2DM is a multifaceted disease characterised by insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction. These two pivotal aspects are explored in detail below to provide insights into their contributions to T2DM progression.

Insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction

Insulin plays a critical role in glucose homeostasis by facilitating the cellular uptake of glucose during energy production and storage. Insulin resistance is the inability of cells, such as muscle cells, liver cells, or adipose tissue, to respond properly to insulin, resulting in elevated blood insulin levels, a condition known as hyperinsulinemia (Norton et al., 2022). In adipose tissue, insulin resistance increases lipolysis, thereby elevating free fatty acid levels in the plasma. Conversely, it impairs glycogen synthesis, promotes hepatic glucose production, and stimulates lipogenesis and protein synthesis in the liver. Similarly, insulin resistance in muscle cells reduces glucose uptake. Many factors, including inflammatory markers, DPP-IV enzymes, lipid accumulation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and genetic factors, contribute to insulin resistance (Ramezan et al., 2025).

Pancreatic β-cells produce and secrete insulin in response to elevated blood glucose levels. In T2DM, β-cell dysfunction is characterised by a progressive decline in insulin secretion and β-cell mass, often due to hypertriglyceridemia, inflammatory cytokines, elevated glycoproteins, C-reactive protein, ROS, and genetic factors (Dludla et al., 2023). These impairments increase reliance on exogenous insulin.

Chronic complications of T2DM

Chronic hyperglycemia leads to complications, such as cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, and hypertension (IDF, 2025). The increased risk of atherosclerosis due to fat deposits in the blood vessels is a major issue that correlates with a higher risk of heart attack and cardiovascular mortality in diabetic individuals. Hyperglycemia impairs endothelial function and reduces blood vessel dilation (Giri et al., 2018). Another risk of chronic hyperglycemia is neural damage, which occurs when the blood vessels within the brain are damaged. Similarly, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and ischaemic stroke are risk factors (Machado et al., 2023). Another complication of T2DM is diabetic nephropathy, in which elevated blood sugar levels can harm the blood vessels within the kidneys, and the cells are also exposed to glucose toxicity (Natesan & Kim, 2021). Therefore, insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction are pivotal in disease progression. Peptides produced from food may enhance insulin sensitisation and β-cell function, thereby playing a key role in diabetes management. Before understanding the mechanism, it is necessary to understand peptide production from IVSD.

Bioactive peptides (BAPs) and their generation via in vitro simulated digestion

Bioactive peptides (BAPs) are short amino acid chains, typically composed of 2–20 residues, with diverse physiological activities and potential health benefits (Du & Li, 2022). Their bioactivity includes antimicrobial, antidiabetic, antihypertensive, antithrombotic, cholesterol-lowering, and immunomodulatory effects. These properties make them attractive for pharmaceutical and food applications (Fernando & Wu, 2025; Hu et al., 2024).

BAPs can be produced through chemical synthesis, genetic engineering, or extraction from food proteins. Although chemical and genetic methods offer controlled production, they are often complex, costly, and may generate undesirable side reactions. In contrast, food-derived peptides are increasingly favoured because of their natural origin, safety, and cost-effectiveness (Du, Gong, et al., 2024).

Food-derived peptides

Food-derived peptides are primarily obtained by enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation. Other emerging methods include ultrasonication, high-pressure processing, microwave treatment, germination, and chemical hydrolysis of food (Fernando & Wu, 2025). A wide range of sources, such as animals (milk, meat, eggs, and fish), plants (legumes, cereals, and oilseeds), marine organisms (algae and invertebrates), insects, and food processing byproducts, are used as precursor proteins for BAPs (Du & Li, 2022; Fernando & Wu, 2025). Despite the advantages of food-based extraction, challenges such as peptide stability, bioavailability, interactions with food components, toxicity, and allergenicity remain (Du, Gong, et al., 2024; Fadimu et al., 2022). To address these challenges, IVSD has gained prominence as a physiologically relevant method for the production of bioactive peptides.

In vitro simulated digestion (IVSD) for BAPs production and assessment

IVSD is a valuable tool for assessing the digestibility of food proteins, the stability of existing BAPs, and the generation of potentially bioactive peptides under physiologically relevant conditions (Brodkorb et al., 2019). This method mimics sequential oral, gastric, and intestinal digestion, yielding peptides that are more likely to remain stable in vivo than those produced by nonphysiological hydrolysis methods.

There are several advantages of using IVSD, including physiological relevance, stability assessment, reproducibility, ethical considerations, and cost and time efficiency. IVSD mimics the natural process of protein breakdown, generating peptides that are likely to be encountered in vivo. It simultaneously evaluates the stability of peptides under digestive stress (pH changes and proteolysis), and standardised protocols, such as INFOGEST 2.0, enhance comparability across studies (Brodkorb et al., 2019). This method does not require animal or human studies for initial screening and is generally less expensive and faster than in vivo studies (Du, Gong, et al., 2024).

IVSD typically begins with oral digestion. However, some researchers have chosen to begin with gastric digestion when studying peptide production and bioactivities. For example, oral digestion has been omitted when producing peptides from defatted walnut protein meals (Feng et al., 2019) and peas (Lei et al., 2025). However, some researchers have included oral digestion in their studies on the production of peptides from whey protein hydrolysates (Tenenbaum et al., 2023) and Porphyra haitanensis glycoproteins (Ou et al., 2025).

In the gastric phase, the oral bolus is mixed with simulated gastric fluid (SGF) containing pepsin, which breaks down the proteins into peptides. In the intestinal phase, the gastric chyme is mixed with simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) containing pancreatin (a mixture of trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, and carboxypeptidases) and bile salts. Intestinal digestion is a crucial phase in which vigorous hydrolysis of proteins occurs via the pancreatin enzyme, a mixture of enzymes that breaks down polypeptides into smaller peptides (Brodkorb et al., 2019). Pancreatin is commonly used in the process of intestinal digestion. For example, pancreatin has been used to produce peptides from the glycoprotein of Porphyra haitanensis (Ou et al., 2025) and proteins of kiwicha (Amaranthus caudatus) (Vilcacundo et al., 2019). However, some researchers have used trypsin alone or a mixture of trypsin and chymotrypsin. For example, trypsin and chymotrypsin were used at a 4:1 ratio to derive peptides from sea cucumber protein (Gong et al., 2020). In contrast, trypsin has only been used for peptide generation during intestinal digestion of goat milk (Dang et al., 2025), foxtail protein (Fu et al., 2023), and milk casein (Cao et al., 2024). The combination of trypsin and chymotrypsin not only better replicates the intestinal conditions but also generates smaller peptides compared to trypsin alone, increasing the bioactivity potential (Brodkorb et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2017).

Basically, pepsin, trypsin, and chymotrypsin are digestive enzymes to break down proteins to produce peptides. As reviewed by Berraquero-García et al. (2023), digestive enzymes favour the production of peptides with α-glucosidase and DPP-IV inhibitory effects. However, peptides produced using other methods have also been shown to inhibit α-glucosidase and DPP-IV activity. The cleavage site of pepsin is typically a hydrophobic amino acid in the C-terminal region, specifically leucine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine. Similarly, trypsin cleaves the protein at arginine and lysine residues at the C-terminus, whereas chymotrypsin cleaves aromatic amino acids at the C-terminus, such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan (Du, Gong, et al., 2024). However, the N-terminus, peptide chain length, and other physicochemical properties such as solubility, pI, hydrophobicity, and net charge depend on the protein type and ultimately define the inhibitory potential of the peptide. Thus, the presence of a hydrophobic amino acid in the peptide sequence cleaved by a combination of enzymes used in IVSD may increase the possibility of inhibiting both digestive and regulatory enzymes (Berraquero-García et al., 2023).

Cao et al. (2024) reported that hydrophobic amino acids, particularly phenylalanine, bind to the hydrophobic catalytic zone of α-glucosidase. Similarly, the presence of one hydrophilic amino acid in a peptide sequence allows it to bind to an enzyme via a hydrogen bond, forming an enzyme-peptide complex. Similarly, Rodhi et al. (2023) reported that arginine and phenylalanine at the C-1 position may bind to the hotspot of DPP-IV through a hydrophobic bond. As discussed earlier, phenylalanine, arginine, and hydrophilic amino acids are produced by the duo or trio effect of digestive enzymes when they cleave proteins. However, compared to α-glucosidase and DPP-IV inhibition, very few documents were available on the inhibition of α-amylase by IVSD-derived peptides.

Based on the above discussion, the oral digestion stage is not noteworthy for protein isolates/concentrates or foods with minimal carbohydrate content, when peptide production is the primary goal. INFOGEST 2.0 specifies that salivary amylase is only relevant for starch-containing food (Brodkorb et al., 2019). However, the electrolyte composition in simulated gastric digestion needs to be balanced so that oral digestion can be mimicked without the use of salivary amylase, or the electrolyte used for gastric digestion needs to be recalculated accordingly. Protein hydrolysis kinetics during IVSD

Monitoring the extent and rate of protein breakdown during IVSD provides insight into digestibility and peptide release. Common metrics include the degree of hydrolysis (DH), concentration of soluble peptides, changes in molecular weight distribution (e.g., via SDS-PAGE or size-exclusion chromatography), and alterations in particle size and zeta potential (Chen, Gao, et al., 2025; Li, Guan, et al., 2024).

Studies have consistently shown that protein hydrolysis is limited during the gastric phase, but increases significantly during the initial stages of the intestinal phase owing to the action of pancreatic proteases, which further slows down as digestion progresses (Yan et al., 2025). A similar trend was observed in the kinetics of milk fat globule membranes (Li, Guan, et al., 2024), jack bean protein sprouts (Agustia, Purnamasari, & Rokhmah, 2024), and precooked jack bean sprouts (Agustia, Supriyadi, & A., & Indrati, R., 2024). Furthermore, the particle size and zeta potential (negative value) decreased after IVSD digestion of the milk fat globule membrane (Li, Guan, et al., 2024) and Pleurotus eryngii protein (Wang et al., 2024). A higher negative zeta value indicates a more negative charge, resulting in reduced aggregation and improved stability.

The protein concentration used in the IVSD ranges from 0.4 to 4 % in the final volume of the digest (Brodkorb et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2023; Lorieau et al., 2018; Ou et al., 2025; Tenenbaum et al., 2023). Currently, there are no standard recommendations for maintaining a specific protein concentration during IVSD. However, Ménard et al. (2023) suggested that maintaining a protein concentration of 2 to 4 % can help minimise the amount of endogenous enzyme products in the final digest.

Various factors, including the composition of food, the specific type of protein, and the textural properties of the food source, influence the digestibility of proteins. For instance, differences in digestibility have been observed between mycoproteins (cell walls with β-glucan and chitin) and traditional meat products (Chen, Gao, et al, 2025), four different types of legumes with different protein profiles (Feng et al., 2025), and two cheeses with similar composition but different textures (Lorieau et al., 2018).

Processing plays a crucial role in protein digestibility during IVSD. The digestibility has been increased for germinated jack bean sprouts (Agustia, Supriyadi and A., & Indrati, R., 2024), enzyme-hydrolysed bovine and camel casein (Mudgil et al., 2021), microwaved pigeon pea flour (Sun et al., 2020), ultrasonicated ovalbumin (Yang et al., 2025), and high-pressure-treated sunflower meal protein (Qin et al., 2025). These techniques may contribute to improved hydrophilicity, reduced particle size, facilitated protein unfolding, induced structural changes, and increased exposure of hydrophobic groups (Jia et al., 2025; Mudgil et al., 2021). In contrast, the DH of cooked foxtail millet prolamin (Fu et al., 2023) and cooked beef tripe (Sun et al., 2025) were reported to be lower than those of their raw counterparts, which might be due to the formation of disulfide bonds, protein aggregation, and alteration of the side chain of amino acids (Fu et al., 2023). This indicates that peptide generation via IVSD is affected by food type and its processing.

Protein breakdown during IVSD is related to bioactivity. For example, the DPP-IV inhibition percentage was positively correlated with the increased DH of jack bean sprouts (Agustia, Purnamasari & Rokhmah, 2024) and precooked jack bean sprouts (Agustia, Supriyadi and A., & Indrati, R., 2024). Similarly, Vilcacundo et al. (2019) found that the intestinal digest of kiwicha (Amaranthus caudatus) proteins exhibited enhanced enzyme inhibition (both digestive and regulatory) compared to the gastric digest. Similarly, Arnal et al. (2024) demonstrated that the intestinal digest of soybean, chickpea, green pea, and navy bean displayed better α-glucosidase inhibitory activity compared to their gastric digests. In contrast, the α-glucosidase inhibition of the IVSD-digested hydrolysate from the black cricket protein (obtained from the commercial enzymatic hydrolysis) was improved, but the α-amylase inhibition was reduced (De Matos et al., 2024). Furthermore, Mudgil et al. (2023) reported that the gastric digest of different camel milks had better DPP-IV and α-amylase inhibition properties compared to the intestinal digest. The author further revealed that over-hydrolysis might break down the inhibitory peptide, resulting in less bioactivity.

Furthermore, the effects of advanced processing methods, such as ultrasound pretreatment or microwave heating, on digestion kinetics and peptide size distribution can be studied. This will ultimately aid in bioactivity assessment, as the higher the protein breakdown, the more likely small-sized peptides are to occur with a high probability of bioactivity. Nevertheless, a comprehensive study is necessary because higher protein breakdown is not consistently correlated with increased antidiabetic potential. Consequently, understanding the relationship between digestion kinetics during IVSD and antidiabetic properties is crucial for identifying the digestion stage/period to screen for more potent peptides for further validation.

Antidiabetic mechanism of IVSD-derived BAPs

With insights into BAPs production using the IVSD process, it is required to understand the mechanisms and different fates of BAPs in T2DM management. Conventional management strategies for T2DM primarily involve lifestyle modifications (diet and exercise) and pharmacological interventions. However, the long-term use of antidiabetic drugs has been reported to cause significant side effects and adherence challenges and contribute to substantial costs, emphasising the need for safer and more sustainable alternatives (Barrientos-Ávalos et al., 2024; Borse et al., 2021). For example, metformin, commonly used to treat T2DM, is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency, lactic acidosis, and liver and cardiovascular disorders (Borse et al., 2021). Acarbose, another common medication for T2DM, has been associated with gastrointestinal discomfort, including flatulence and diarrhoea (Barrientos-Ávalos et al., 2024). Additionally, other medications, such as sodium-glucose transporter protein-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists, may lead to gastrointestinal disorders such as nausea, vomiting, acute pancreatitis, acute renal injury, hypotension, and diabetic ketoacidosis. Similarly, dependence on incretin-based therapies has been linked to diseases, such as pancreatic cancer (Borse et al., 2021). Thus, BAPs production via IVSD has emerged as a promising alternative owing to its potential to modulate glucose homeostasis through multiple complementary mechanisms.

Numerous studies have explored BAPs production via IVSD and demonstrated several health benefits. This review focuses on the antidiabetic properties of IVSD-derived BAPs. These peptides regulate hyperglycemia through various mechanisms, including inhibition of digestive enzymes (α-amylase and α-glucosidase), suppression of regulatory enzymes such as DPP-IV, enhancement of insulin sensitisation and production, antioxidant activity, anti-inflammatory effects (Du, Gong, et al., 2024), and modulation of the gut microbiota (Zhang et al., 2023). Understanding these mechanisms will help elucidate the translation of peptides into therapeutic drugs and foods, which is key to developing BAP-based strategies for T2DM management.

Inhibition of digestive and regulatory enzymes

Controlling postprandial hyperglycemia is important for T2DM management. Inhibiting enzymes involved in carbohydrate digestion and incretin hormone degradation are key strategies employed by several existing antidiabetic drugs (e.g., acarbose and sitagliptin). IVSD-derived peptides inhibit α-amylase (Lei et al., 2025), α-glucosidase (Aquino et al., 2024), and DPP-IV (Zheng et al., 2025).

α-amylase inhibition

Pancreatic α-amylase is the primary enzyme responsible for initiating the breakdown of complex carbohydrates (starches) into smaller oligosaccharides in the small intestine. The inhibition of α-amylase slows down carbohydrate digestion, leading to a gradual increase in postprandial blood glucose levels. IVSD-derived peptides can interact with α-amylase and potentially bind to its catalytic, substrate-binding, or allosteric sites, thereby hindering its activity. Berraquero-García et al. (2023) suggested that both hydrophobic and hydrophilic amino acid residues within peptides can contribute to binding through hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and polar interactions. Furthermore, Lei et al. (2025) identified 13 peptides from a pea protein digest that inhibited α-amylase, highlighting the importance of aromatic amino acids and suggesting binding sites that are distinct from the catalytic cleft. The specific structural features that determine potent α-amylase inhibition by peptides are still being elucidated; however, this mechanism represents a viable target for IVSD-derived BAPs.

α-glucosidase inhibition

α-Glucosidase, found in the intestinal mucosa, catalyses the hydrolysis of α-1,4-glycosidic bonds of oligosaccharides and disaccharides to produce α-glucose, which is absorbed into the bloodstream and increases blood glucose levels (Fan et al., 2024). Inhibiting α-glucosidase, which plays a key role in glucose metabolism, further delays glucose absorption and reduces postprandial glycemic elevations (Fan et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2023). It is a well-established therapeutic target (e.g., acarbose). Numerous IVSD-derived peptides have demonstrated α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. Structure-activity relationship studies have suggested that inhibitory peptides often contain 3–6 amino acids. Residues like serine, threonine, tyrosine, lysine, or arginine at the N-terminus, proline often near the C-terminus, and alanine or methionine at the C-terminus appear favourable. Peptide charge (neutral or + 1) and specific sequence motifs, rather than overall hydrophobicity, seem to be critical (Ibrahim et al., 2018). For instance, peptides like AMFLPGA, TMMMLLP, FFLPQ, and FMLPQ from foxtail millet prolamin (Fu et al., 2023) and SGR, NPIY, and GPFPSI from soy protein (Xue et al., 2024) have demonstrated potent inhibition, with molecular docking (MD) suggesting binding interactions similar to acarbose.

Dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP-IV) inhibition

DPP-IV is a serine protease that is widely expressed in various tissues, including the pancreas, kidney, liver, intestine, and bloodstream. It plays a critical role in glucose regulation by rapidly inactivating incretin hormones, primarily GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide. These incretins are released postprandially and stimulate glucose-dependent insulin secretion from β-cells while suppressing glucagon release from α-cells. DPP-IV inhibition prolongs the half-life and activity of endogenous incretins, thereby leading to improved glycemic control (Gupta & Sen, 2019). DPP-IV inhibitors (e.g., sitagliptin and vildagliptin) are established T2DM therapies.

Food-derived peptides generated via IVSD have emerged as promising natural DPP-IV inhibitors with four binding sites: S1, S2, S1’, and S2’. Effective inhibitory peptides often include hydrophobic or aromatic amino acids (e.g., Pro, Ala, Trp, Tyr, and Phe) at the N-terminus or within the sequence, facilitating interaction with the enzyme's hydrophobic S1 pocket (Ayoub et al., 2024; Dang et al., 2025; Mora-Melgem et al., 2023). Peptides like AESE from Macadamia integrifolia protein (Zhao et al., 2021), FRLPNGGL and DQLSAIQGIQ from sea cucumber (Gong et al., 2020), and GPFPLL and LPYPY from goat milk (Dang et al., 2025) have demonstrated potent DPP-IV inhibition, with MD studies revealing interactions (hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions) with key residues in the DPP-IV active site (S1, S2, S1’). Kinetic studies have often revealed competitive or mixed-type inhibition (Chen et al., 2024; Dang et al., 2025).

Insulin sensitisation and production

In addition to modulating glucose absorption and incretin levels, IVSD-derived BAPs can directly affect the core defects in T2DM, including insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction.

Insulin sensitisation

Enhancing the sensitivity of peripheral tissues (muscle, liver, and adipose tissue) to insulin is crucial for improving glucose uptake and utilisation. BAPs influence insulin signalling pathways. Some peptides have been shown to promote the phosphorylation/activation of key downstream mediators such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and protein kinase B (Akt), while potentially reducing the inhibitory phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) (e.g., at Ser307), a crucial factor in glucose uptake and metabolism. This will facilitate the translocation of glucose transporter type 4 and glucose uptake. BAPs also promote the secretion of adipokines such as adiponectin, which plays a crucial role in insulin secretion and lipid metabolism, thereby aiding in the management of hyperglycemia (Gao et al., 2022; Kirichenko et al., 2022). For example, the soy-derived peptide SGR enhanced glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells (Xue et al., 2024).

BAPs can also indirectly improve insulin sensitivity via their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress are known contributors to insulin resistance (Martínez Báez et al., 2024; Ramezan et al., 2025). Pro-inflammatory markers, such as tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), may activate kinases, such as ribosomal protein S6 kinase beta-1 (S6K1), which mediates the undesirable phosphorylation of IRS-1 and promotes insulin resistance (Martínez Báez et al., 2024). Peptides, such as KILDK from bovine α-lactalbumin, can downregulate pro-inflammatory signalling pathways (e.g., NF-κB and JNK) (Gao et al., 2022). BAPs from egg whites (Zhao et al., 2017) exhibit potent antioxidant activity by downregulating pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, in Caco-2 cells. Ding, Xiong, et al. (2024) identified peptides (LLGDV and LGDEPL) from rice wine lees protein hydrolysate that showed effective antioxidant activity and improved insulin sensitivity in HepG2 cells. Thus, BAPs with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects may contribute to insulin sensitivity.

Stimulating β-cell function and survival

Preserving and enhancing pancreatic β-cell function is vital for sustained glycemic control. BAPs may potentiate insulin secretion, promote GLP-1 secretion, protect β-cells from damage, and enhance insulin biosynthesis. Similar to incretins, some peptides may directly stimulate insulin release from β-cells, potentially via mechanisms involving cAMP signalling and calcium influx (Smith et al., 2019). GLP-1 secretion by STC-1 enteroendocrine cells was significantly increased by dosing peptides derived from the simulated digestion of whey protein isolates. GLP-1 further promotes insulin secretion, resulting in increased glucose-dependent insulin biosynthesis and prevention of β-cell apoptosis (Chavda et al., 2024).

Oxidative stress and inflammation significantly contribute to β-cell apoptosis in T2DM (Dludla et al., 2023). Peptides with antioxidant properties, such as SGGY from walnuts (Feng et al., 2019) and digested donkey milk (Li et al., 2020), can protect β-cells from glucolipotoxicity and inflammatory insults, thereby preserving β-cell mass and function. Moreover, certain peptides can stimulate the release of endogenous GLP-1 from intestinal L-cells (e.g., STC-1 cells), indirectly enhancing insulin secretion and β-cell function (Tenenbaum et al., 2023).

Enhancing gut microbiota

The gut microbiome influences host metabolism through various mechanisms, including the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), modulation of gut hormone secretion (like GLP-1), regulation of inflammation, and maintenance of gut barrier integrity. BAPs can potentially modulate the gut microbiota composition and exhibit selective antimicrobial activity by inhibiting pathogens while protecting beneficial microbes (Hu et al., 2024; López-García et al., 2022). Antibacterial peptides prevent pathogenic microbial growth by permeabilising the cell membrane and maintaining the integrity of the beneficial microbes (López-García et al., 2022).

BAPs may also serve as substrates for microbial fermentation, promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) and the production of beneficial metabolites, such as SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate) (Guo et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). SCFAs can improve insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis. For example, treatment with foxtail millet protein hydrolysate (produced by commercial enzymatic hydrolysis) in T2D mice beneficially altered the gut microbiota (increasing Firmicutes and decreasing Bacteroidota) and increased SCFAs production, correlating with improved metabolic parameters (Zhu et al., 2025). Similarly, camel milk peptides have a positive influence on the gut microbiota and metabolite production, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, in T2DM mice. However, the composition of these metabolites in SCFAs varied, with acetate (2.5 μg/mg) being the dominant component, followed by propionate (0.3–0.4 μg/mg) and butyrate (0.2–0.3 μg/mg) (Zhang et al., 2023). As reviewed by Xie et al. (2025), the acetate, butyrate, and propionate contribute more than 95 % of the SCFAs produced in the gut. All the SCFAs may contribute directly or indirectly to improving insulin sensitivity and maintaining glucose homeostasis. However, among these three major SCFAs, butyrate is a significant contributor to improving insulin sensitivity because of its ability to inhibit histone deacetylase (HDACs) compared to propionate and acetate (Xie et al., 2025).

SCFAs maintain glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity through several mechanisms, such as increased AMPK activity in muscles, increased secretion of gut-derived GLP-1 and peptide YY, reducing the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (Xie et al., 2025), inhibiting HDACs, suppressing the expression of protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), phosphorylated eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (p-eIF2α), activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein homologous protein (CHOP) (Hu et al., 2018), upregulating the expression of GLUT2, SGLT1 and glycogen synthase kinase 3 in the liver to improve glycogen storage (Zhang, Zhao, Gui, et al., 2019), and upregulating the expression of key genes such as fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4), fatty acid transporter protein 4 (FATP4), fatty acid synthase, and lipoprotein lipase, center for fat metabolism (Yu et al., 2018).

The management of T2DM using IVSD-derived peptides encompasses a broad spectrum of mechanisms and offers a promising alternative to conventional pharmacological interventions. Peptides play a significant role in managing the potential complications of diabetes by reducing glucose absorption from the intestine, enhancing glucose uptake and utilisation in cells, and supporting beneficial gut microbes. This multifaceted mechanism highlights the potential use of IVSD-derived peptides as novel candidates for T2DM management. Furthermore, antidiabetic properties of these peptides were tested in vitro, in vivo, and in silico to validate their efficacy for future therapeutic applications.

Evaluating the antidiabetic properties of IVSD-derived peptides/hydrolysates

Protein hydrolysis via IVSD generates numerous peptides that must be identified, tested, and validated for the desired antidiabetic potential and efficacy. Two primary approaches are used: conventional and computer-based (Du, Gong, et al., 2024). Typically, the initial assay involves in vitro testing followed by peptide sequence identification. After identification, peptides undergo further in vitro and in vivo testing using a conventional approach, whereas in silico analysis uses bioinformatics tools to identify peptides with specific functions (Fig. 3).

General flow sequence for testing antidiabetic properties of IVSD-derived peptides from screening to clinical trial.

In vitro test

In vitro tests are primarily used for the initial screening of IVSD hydrolysates and purified peptide fractions, as well as for elucidating specific mechanisms of action. Common in vitro assays include enzyme inhibition assays, antioxidant activity assays, and cell-based assays. Typically, peptides obtained from IVSD are desalted and fractionated by size based on the molecular weight cut-off size for preliminary screening by centrifugal ultrafiltration, gel filtration chromatography, and high-pressure liquid chromatography techniques (Cao et al., 2024; De Matos et al., 2024; Pérez-Gálvez et al., 2024). This finding supports the identification of peptide mixtures with higher bioactivity during preliminary screening, thereby reducing the use of additional resources. Typically, smaller peptides demonstrate greater bioactivity than larger peptides (Gong et al., 2020; Valencia-Mejía et al., 2019). In vitro tests are commonly used to evaluate the efficacy of peptides in treating diabetes.

Enzyme inhibition assay

These are used to assess the ability of peptides to target key enzymes such as α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-IV. Assays typically measure the reduction in enzyme activity in the presence of varying peptide concentrations, allowing the determination of IC50 values. Kinetic studies can further reveal the mode of inhibition (competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive, or mixed), providing mechanistic insights (Cao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Dang et al., 2025). Cao et al. (2024) identified SFFL as a competitive inhibitor of α-glucosidase, whereas the peptides YPFP and FPA demonstrated uncompetitive inhibition. Similarly, Chen et al. (2024) reported that peptides WPR and MPR obtained from sesame protein exhibited competitive and noncompetitive inhibition of DPP-IV, respectively. Furthermore, Dang et al. (2025) demonstrated that two peptides from goat milk, GPFPLL and LPYPY, competitively inhibit DPP-IV.

Numerous studies have successfully used these assays to identify potent inhibitory peptides from IVSD in various food sources such as peas (Lei et al., 2025), adzuki beans (Kwan et al., 2025), sea cucumber (Gong et al., 2020), milk casein (Cao et al., 2024), and goat milk (Dang et al., 2025). However, their inhibitory properties depend on the amino acid composition of the peptide. For instance, the IC50 (μM) of DPP-IV was 179 and 474 for the peptides LPYPY and RPWR, respectively (Dang et al., 2025). The better inhibition property of LPYPY might be due to the presence of leucine (aliphatic and hydrophobic) at the N1 position compared to arginine (basic) in RPWR. Leucine at the N1 position binds to the primary and secondary hotspots of DPP-IV. However, the position of the amino acid at other positions also plays a crucial role in DPP-IV inhibition (Rodhi et al., 2023). Similarly, SFFL showed significantly better α-glucosidase inhibition properties than GPFPI. This may be attributed to the presence of phenylalanine and lysine (Cao et al., 2024). Furthermore, Berraquero-García et al. (2023) reported that the serine at the N1 position binds to the catalytic domain of the α-glucosidase enzyme via hydrogen bonds and electrostatic attraction, thus inhibiting the enzyme. Antioxidant activity assay

Given the role of oxidative stress in T2DM pathogenesis, assessing the antioxidant capacity of IVSD hydrolysate/peptides is crucial. Du, Hui, et al. (2024) reported a 70 % ABTS radical scavenging rate from a 0.5 mg/mL intestinal digest of Meretrix meretrix L-derived peptides by IVSD, whereas Zhang et al. (2021) reported an IC50 for ABTS radical scavenging activity of the silkworm pupae protein digest to be 11.55 mg/mL. The difference in antioxidant activity depended on the peptide composition of the digest, highlighting the need for further validation.

Xue et al. (2021) reported the IC50 value for superoxide anion radical scavenging activity to be 189.3 μmol/L by IVSD-derived peptide YPEL from bovine α-casein. Similarly, the peptide KPPPEKPKP derived from sorghum by IVSD shows an ABTS radical scavenging activity (IC50) value of 44.44 mg/mL (Jiang et al., 2022). The antioxidant activity of a peptide depends on its hydrophobicity and amphiphilicity, amino acid composition, molecular weight, and amino acid chain length (Prakash Nirmal et al., 2023). Among the 19 potential antioxidant peptides identified in the IVSD digest of Morchella septimelata proteins, peptide VEEDHPIPEE showed the highest antioxidant activity. This might be due to a high proportion of acidic amino acids (glutamic acid) with high electron transport capacity, presence of histidine with the ability to chelate metal, low pI, and high hydrophilicity of the peptide (Chen, Xu, et al., 2025).

Cell line test

In addition to enzyme inhibition and antioxidant assays, assessing the effects of peptides on cellular models is crucial for further understanding their antidiabetic potential. The critical aspect of these cell line-based assays is that the bioactivity observed may not always translate to cell-based systems (Feng et al., 2019), and poor absorption can limit the efficacy of even potent peptides (Lacroix et al., 2017). Cell-based assays are critical intermediaries between in vitro screening and in vivo studies.

Among the various parameters, glucose metabolism and uptake in cell line tests are the most significant. The soy protein peptide SGR (Xue et al., 2024), Micromesistius poutassou protein hydrolysate (Harnedy et al., 2018), brewer's spent yeast hydrolysate (Aquino et al., 2024), and sea cucumber protein hydrolysate (Gong et al., 2020) produced by IVSD have demonstrated improved glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity. Cell line assays serve as essential tools for elucidating the mechanisms underlying the antidiabetic properties of peptides derived from IVSD. The IVSD-digested adzuki bean β-vignin protein significantly improved the expression of IRS-1, Akt-1, and GLUT2 proteins compared to insulin-resistant (IR) HepG2 cells (Kwan et al., 2025). Furthermore, among the four peptides obtained from IVSD of camel milk, peptide TYYPPQ significantly increased Akt expression compared to the remaining peptides in IR HepG2 cells, but did not correspondingly increase phosphorylated Akt expression (Mohamed et al., 2025). Similarly, Yao et al. (2022) demonstrated that the IVSD hydrolysate obtained from rapeseed napin protein improved both GLUT4 expression and translocation in a HepG2/Caco-2 co-culture model.

IVSD-derived BAPs are effective in diabetes management, not only by improving insulin signalling, glucose utilisation, and glucose metabolism, but also by numerous mechanisms, including the minimisation of oxidative stress and inflammatory markers. For instance, Xuanwei ham peptides (Hu et al., 2023), Meretrix meretrix L. hydrolysate (Du, Hui, et al., 2024), and milk fat globule membranes peptides (Li, Guan, et al., 2024) from IVSD upregulated the antioxidant enzymes and prevented oxidative damage. In addition to antioxidant activity, an anti-inflammatory activity assay was conducted in the cell lines. The expression of inflammatory markers were minimised, whereas anti-inflammatory markers were upregulated by bovine α-lactalbumin peptide (Gao et al., 2022) and preserved egg white hydrolysate (Zhao et al., 2017; Zhang, Zhao, Yao, et al., 2019). Some authors have also assayed the enzyme inhibition ability of peptides using cell line tests. For example, Tenenbaum et al. (2023) assayed DPP-IV inhibition in Caco-2 liver cells and found that whey protein isolate hydrolysate derived from IVSD inhibited DPP-IV in Caco-2 liver cells.

For bioactive peptides to exert their effects, they must be absorbed by the intestinal cells and reach their target sites. The peptides (WR and IPIQY) derived from whey protein isolate through pepsin digestion, which are potent inhibitors of human DPP-IV, were tested for their absorption capacity using a Caco-2 monolayer cell model, which demonstrated a poor absorption capacity (Lacroix et al., 2017). In contrast, a fraction of peptides derived from the IVSD of soybean protein hydrolysate could pass through Caco-2 monolayer cells; however, their antioxidant activity decreased after passing through intestinal cells (Zhang et al., 2018). This emphasises the necessity of conducting absorption tests in cell lines to accurately predict the efficacy of IVSD-generated peptides for diabetes management. Overall, cell line assays are key to further developing peptides for therapeutic purposes; however, before proceeding, these peptides are additionally assayed through in vivo tests.

In vivo assay

Although in vitro testing offers valuable insights into peptide activity as an antidiabetic compound, in vivo studies provide a broader perspective on their systemic effects within a living organism. In vivo assays using animal models are valuable tools (Domínguez-Oliva et al., 2023). Unlike in vitro studies, in vivo studies reflect the complex interactions of physiological processes, including digestion, absorption, and excretion, which validate the therapeutic value of BAPs in the treatment of diabetes. Key parameters assessed during in vivo studies include glycemic control, insulin sensitivity and secretion, lipid profile, body weight and composition, hormone levels, inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, histopathology, gut microbiota analysis, and metabolomics.

Rats and mice are commonly used due to their similar physiology to humans and the ease of controlling experimental conditions. However, other animals were also used in this assay. For example, the efficacy of IVSD-derived peptides has been tested in diabetes-induced zebrafish (Chen et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024). The blood glucose and maltase activities of diabetic zebrafish larvae were significantly reduced by administration of peptide SGR (Xue et al., 2024). Similarly, the peptides WPR and MPR derived from sesame protein reduced blood glucose and glucagon levels and increased insulin levels in diabetes-induced zebrafish larvae (Chen et al., 2024).

The experiment was conducted using two controls: a negative control (healthy animals) that did not receive any treatment, and a positive control that received compounds with known effects. This setup facilitates the evaluation of the efficacy of the peptides in hyperglycemia management. Few studies, such as those by Zhang et al. (2023) and Guo et al. (2021), have assessed IVSD-derived peptides through in vivo experiments. For example, rats with streptozotocin-induced diabetes in combination with a high-fat diet were treated with camel milk protein hydrolysate, which not only lowered blood glucose levels but also improved insulin sensitivity, modulated the gut microbiota, and improved the serum lipid profile. The authors further performed histopathological examinations and discovered that hydrolysate administration minimised histopathological damage to the liver, pancreas, and adipocytes in T2DM mice (Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, Guo et al. (2021) revealed that the administration of yak bone collagen peptide generated by IVSD in mice increased the concentration of SCFAs by 50 %. Furthermore, Li et al. (2020) reported that IVSD-derived peptides from donkey milk not only improved insulin resistance in diabetic rats but also improved β-cell viability.

However, some researchers have experimented with healthy mice (Harnedy et al., 2018; Parthsarathy et al., 2019). Blue whiting hydrolysate has been shown to reduce blood glucose levels in normal mice (Harnedy et al., 2018). Similarly, boarfish protein hydrolysate increased insulin secretion and reduced blood glucose levels in healthy mice (Parthsarathy et al., 2019). Valencia-Mejía et al. (2019) demonstrated that hydrolysate fractions of less than 10 kDa from beans controlled postprandial blood glucose levels in rats.

There has been limited research on the absorption and transport potentials of peptides. However, whey protein hydrolysate derived from simulated digestion has been shown to control the weight of high-fat diet-fed mice by enhancing their metabolism and suppressing their appetite. Additionally, this hydrolysate increased brain levels of GLP-1, indicating its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier (Rai & Priyadarshini, 2023). Similarly, peptides derived from walnuts are absorbed into the bloodstream through the gut and cross the blood-brain barrier, enhancing neuroprotective functions. The authors further revealed that these peptides also significantly influenced brain function in zebrafish (Wang et al., 2025).

Some scientists have found positive effects of IVSD-derived hydrolysates and peptides during in vivo assays. However, in vivo research specifically using IVSD-derived peptides remains relatively limited compared to in vitro studies, and translating findings from animal models to humans requires caution due to physiological differences (Domínguez-Oliva et al., 2023).

In silico assay

Computational or in silico methods are powerful tools for accelerating the discovery and characterisation of BAPs, complementing experimental approaches (Du et al., 2023; Peredo-Lovillo et al., 2022). This computational screening employs bioinformatics software integrated with molecular modelling and machine learning to evaluate peptides derived from IVSD or other processes (Du et al., 2023). The general process involves the identification of peptides, screening them based on bioactivity, allergenicity, and toxicity using specialised bioinformatics software, conducting docking simulations to understand peptide interactions, and assessing peptide stability. These results were further verified through in vivo and ex vivo experiments (Peredo-Lovillo et al., 2022). Thus, in silico assays play a crucial role in the screening of desired peptides with antidiabetic properties from peptide mixtures.

Generally, in silico analysis begins after peptide identification; however, with in silico digestion software, such as PeptideCutter, prediction can be started with the enzymatic breakdown of proteins. For instance, Zhao et al. (2021) carried out in silico digestion with pepsin, trypsin, and chymotrypsin to generate peptides from Macadamia integrifolia antimicrobial protein, whereas Cao et al. (2024) performed an in silico analysis after identifying peptides from milk casein hydrolysates.

In virtual screening, after peptide sequence identification, they were compared with available databases, such as Biopep or other software. The physicochemical properties of the peptides, including molecular weight, peptide length, peptide isoelectric point (pI), net charge, hydrophobicity index, stability index, solubility, and amphipathicity, were evaluated (Du et al., 2023). The hydrophobicity index is particularly important because it influences solubility, binding affinity, and absorption. Increased hydrophobicity is associated with high bioactivity, adsorption, and functionality, but it is also linked to poor solubility and increased bitterness (Berraquero-García et al., 2023; Mirzapour-Kouhdasht et al., 2023). As reviewed by Berraquero-García et al. (2023), among the 40 peptides identified, α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and DPP-IV inhibitors contained more than 44 %, 50 %, and over 80 % hydrophobic amino acids, respectively, in 75 % of the identified peptides. These findings indicate that solubility decreases with increasing hydrophobicity, which limits its application as a functional ingredient. Additionally, peptides with neutral or positive charges are highly effective against α-amylase and α-glucosidase. In contrast, DPP-IV inhibitors have neutral charges, eliminating the need for a positive charge to exert their inhibitory activity.

The released peptides were screened for bioactivity using a quantitative structure-activity relationship approach. Furthermore, MD, molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) , and free energy calculations have been used to analyse the peptide-binding affinity, stability, and energy changes over time (Du et al., 2023; Du & Li, 2022). For example, six peptides from Macadamia integrifolia antimicrobial proteins have been identified as potential DPP-IV inhibitors based on their water solubility, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity. However, MD and MDS have confirmed that only one of these peptides is the most potent DPP-IV inhibitor (Zhao et al., 2021). Similarly, MD and MDS studies showed that two peptides, WPR and MPR, derived from the sesame protein, exhibited binding stability to the DPP-IV enzyme (Chen et al., 2024).

It was noted that hydrophobic amino acids predominated in all identified peptide sequences that inhibited the activity of diabetic enzymes. The presence of reactive hydrophobic residues (isoleucine, phenylalanine, valine, leucine, tryptophan, and methionine) and reactive hydrophilic residues (arginine, lysine, aspartic acid, glutamine, asparagine, and glutamic acid), as well as the positioning of serine in the N1 position and the overall hydrophobicity of the peptide sequence, enhances the likelihood of α-glucosidase inhibition (Berraquero-García et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2024). Furthermore, the final verification requires an in vivo or ex vivo test before proceeding to a clinical test. For example, eight peptides derived from soy protein by in silico digestion using pepsin, trypsin, and chymotrypsin were identified as effective against α-glucosidase by in silico analysis. Among them, the peptide SGR was found to have the highest efficiency against α-glucosidase. The efficacy of this peptide was further validated through cell line assays using HepG2 cells and in vivo experiments using zebrafish (Xue et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2024) verified the efficacy of sesame seed peptides through in vivo experiments using zebrafish larvae as an animal model.

ADME tests and digestive enzyme resistance tests were conducted to evaluate the potential therapeutic applications of the identified peptides (Du et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). However, to date, no documentation has been found regarding the ADME testing of IVSD-derived peptides. This suggests that further research is needed to accurately predict the ADME of IVSD-derived peptides. Understanding the ADME of these peptides will facilitate the validation of their use as drugs in preclinical trials. In silico tools are invaluable for rapidly screening, prioritising experimental validation, and providing mechanistic insights. Combining MD with MDS offers a particularly robust approach (Dang et al., 2025). However, in silico predictions require experimental verification (in vitro and/or in vivo) (Chen et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024).

Clinical trial

Translation of IVSD-derived peptides into clinical applications is still in its infancy. Although several in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated promising antidiabetic effects, clinical evidence remains scarce. Limited trials with protein hydrolysates derived from black beans and milk proteins have shown beneficial effects on postprandial glucose levels in prediabetic adults (Mojica et al., 2024; Sartorius et al., 2019). However, no clinical studies have specifically evaluated IVSD-derived peptides for the treatment of T2DM.

Establishing effective therapeutic dosages, safety profiles, delivery methods, and long-term stability is critical for regulatory approval. Although some therapeutic peptides (e.g., GLP-1 analogues) have been successfully developed, most peptide-based products remain restricted to the nutraceutical sector or experimental models (Du & Li, 2022).

Toxicology study of IVSD-derived peptides

One of the less-explored areas of research is the toxicity of peptides derived from IVSD. As reviewed by Liu et al. (2020), the enzymes and processing parameters selected during simulated digestion may not accurately replicate the human digestive system, potentially resulting in the formation of novel peptides that have not been previously identified and could pose risks of toxicity and allergenicity. It is essential to establish a specific dose and dosage to ensure the effectiveness of the identified peptides. However, it is equally important to investigate the potential toxicity and allergenicity of these peptides at their effective doses. To the best of our knowledge, no research has been conducted on the toxicological aspects of peptides derived through IVSD, apart from in silico analysis. For instance, six peptides were identified as potential allergens among 23 peptides from Meretrix meretrix Linnaeus (Li et al., 2025), and five peptides were identified among eight peptides derived from rabbit meat (Hu et al., 2025).

It is generally believed that large proteins with allergic properties are resistant to digestion, suggesting that peptides generated from IVSD may not exhibit allergenic potential. Nevertheless, Di Stasio et al. (2017) indicated that certain allergenic proteins from peanuts undergo degradation, resulting in the formation of peptides that can induce allergenicity. Conversely, Picariello et al. (2010) reported that certain allergenic fractions of whey protein displayed resistance to digestive enzymes, which may result in a potential allergen.

Challenges for utilising IVSD-derived peptides in regulating T2DM

Despite the promising potential of IVSD-derived peptides as shown through in vitro, in vivo, and in silico assays for managing hyperglycemia, several challenges limit the translation of IVSD-derived peptides into clinical and industrial applications. These challenges include a lack of data on absorption and metabolism from in vivo studies, the need for validation through clinical experiments, and difficulties in large-scale preparation and purification.

Absorption, metabolism, and stability

In vivo studies on the absorption and metabolic effects of these peptides, particularly in individuals with diabetes, are insufficient. This represents a major obstacle to their use as nutraceuticals or therapeutic drugs. Additionally, the stability of peptides during digestion, absorption, and metabolism poses challenges. Some peptides that exhibit promising activity in vitro and are stable in the gastrointestinal system encounter difficulties during absorption by cell lines. For instance, peptide WR with DPP-IV exhibits poor absorption properties (Lacroix et al., 2017). Moreover, various modification techniques are available, such as side-chain modifications, backbone modifications, and peptide cyclisation, aimed at enhancing properties including stability, absorption, binding affinity to specific sites, and transport (Wang et al., 2022). For instance, macrocyclization (stapling) of peptides not only improves the resistance of peptides against hydrolysis but also improves cell permeability. It has been reported that Exendin-4, a peptide used to control hyperglycemia, is 15 times more effective during in vivo assays after stapling (Li, Wu, et al., 2024).

In addition, encapsulation and microencapsulation have gained popularity for enhancing stability and retaining the bioactivity of peptides. However, it is crucial to choose a suitable encapsulation or microencapsulation process that does not compromise peptide stability (Chen, Yu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2022). Ding, Zhu, et al. (2024) reported that peptides were microencapsulated in gelatin and cyclodextrin to improve their resistance to further proteolysis and pH denaturation. The authors further noted that nanoparticles can be used to minimise mucosal barriers.

Thus, demonstrating antidiabetic activity alone is insufficient, and enhancing its absorption, stability, and systemic availability remains essential. Further research on the absorption and metabolism of antidiabetic peptides derived from IVSD, confirmed through in vitro studies, is necessary. This area remains largely unexplored and requires rigorous investigation to generate data for future pharmaceutical applications.

Lack of clinical evidence

The most significant barrier to therapeutic application is the near absence of robust clinical trial data for IVSD-derived peptides in patients with T2DM. Although preclinical studies provide proof of concept, human physiology is far more complex. Demonstrating efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing in humans requires well-designed, large-scale randomised controlled trials (Mojica et al., 2024). From preclinical discovery to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval (or equivalent regulatory body) for a peptide drug, the process is lengthy, expensive, and has a high attrition rate, often taking over a decade (Ding, Zhu, et al., 2024). Even for functional food or nutraceutical applications with health claims, substantial evidence, often from human intervention studies, is required from regulatory agencies.

However, the number of peptide-based drugs for T2DM is increasing, and numerous clinical investigations are currently underway. Peptides must undergo three phases of clinical trials to obtain FDA approval. A few peptides, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide tablets), have been approved by the FDA for oral dosing, which took almost 19 years after the first-phase clinical trial (Ding, Zhu, et al., 2024). However, clinical evidence supporting the health benefits of IVSD-derived peptides in regulating blood glucose levels is lacking. For instance, SGR derived from soy protein was effective against α-glucosidase and was further validated through in vitro and in vivo studies (Xue et al., 2024). However, they are not recommended for therapeutic purposes without preclinical tests.

However, the effective dosage of the peptide required to regulate blood glucose levels must be determined, considering the potential allergenicity and safety issues associated with the proposed dosage. Since peptides are typically present in very low amounts in foods, alternative methods such as solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) have been employed. Currently, the chemical synthesis of BAPs are primarily conducted using SPPS, which simplifies the production in a straightforward reactor setup without the use of biological agents, thereby simplifying the purification process (Wang et al., 2022). Most therapeutic peptides have been developed using SPPS because they are often found in trace amounts in food-derived hydrolysates. This further demands new strategies, such as innovations in food, to intervene in T2DM management by using IVSD-derived peptides.

Large-scale production and purification

As mentioned earlier, the low yield of BAPs with the target function from the IVSD has also been a major obstacle, because low production makes the purification process highly expensive. However, pretreatments, such as ultrasonication, microwave heating, or high-pressure treatments before enzymatic hydrolysis, can yield more BAPs than enzymatic hydrolysis alone (Mora & Toldrá, 2023). It is also important to consider the potential synergistic effects of other peptides that should be eliminated during the purification process (Ye et al., 2022). In addition, to obtain BAPs with an antidiabetic role, the technology and procedures must be precise and delicate to avoid compromising the stability of the BAPs, making the process even more complicated and time-consuming. Therefore, before translating lab research on a commercial scale, a cost-profit analysis should be conducted.

Key purification techniques include ultrafiltration, gel chromatography, ion-exchange chromatography, and reversed-phase HPLC, all of which facilitate the screening of bioactive peptides from the IVSD digests. Recently, electrodialysis with ultrafiltration (EDUF) has emerged as a promising purification method that offers scalability, cost-effectiveness, reduced time requirements, the use of non-toxic solvents, and greater selectivity than traditional ultrafiltration. The incorporation of non-toxic solvents into purification methods could further enhance the application of peptides in nutraceuticals and functional foods. Additionally, advancements in the scalability of EDUF have resulted in improved peptide yields and effectiveness (Geoffroy et al., 2022).

Furthermore, after purification, digested hydrolysates or peptide mixtures, if they are below a specific size, can be used as a source of BAPs and incorporated into various food formulations. This may minimise the cost of further purification and result in a holistic effect, including antidiabetic, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant properties. However, peptide hydrolysates, particularly those resulting from extensive hydrolysis, can possess bitter or undesirable flavours, limiting their incorporation into food products at effective concentrations (Fadimu et al., 2022). Masking these off-flavours using flavouring agents or encapsulation techniques adds to the complexity and cost. Consumer perception and acceptance of foods fortified with peptide hydrolysates also need to be taken into consideration.

Regulatory hurdles

Navigation of the regulatory landscape of peptide-based products is a complex process. Depending on the intended use and claims, products may be regulated as foods, dietary supplements, or drugs, each with different requirements for safety and efficacy substantiation. Obtaining approved health claims for functional foods or nutraceuticals containing IVSD-derived peptides requires significant scientific evidence, often obtained from human intervention studies.

The above discussion highlights the need for further research on BAPs from food sources as a functional food ingredient versus pharmaceutical drugs. This will provide insight into the development of peptides for use in drugs, nutraceuticals, or functional foods. Interdisciplinary collaborative research between different sectors, including food science, biotechnology, medicine, and regulatory agencies, is essential for translating research outputs into therapeutic value. This collaboration must put its efforts into establishing standardised protocols for the testing, production, and approval of IVSD-derived BAP-integrated products. Despite these challenges, several future opportunities exist in the IVSD-derived peptides sector for managing T2DM.

Future opportunities for IVSD-derived BAPs for T2DM management

Despite the existing challenges, IVSD-derived peptides present substantial opportunities for the development of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and innovative dietary interventions for T2DM management. The growing interest in food-derived bioactive peptides has opened new avenues for their commercial application as therapeutic agents for controlling hyperglycemia. Functional foods

Functional foods provide health benefits beyond basic nutrition and hold great promise as long-term dietary interventions for T2DM management. Although not intended to replace pharmacological treatments, regular consumption of functional foods enriched with bioactive peptides may support glycemic control, enhance metabolic health, and reduce diabetes-related complications.

An emerging strategy in functional food development is food-to-food fortification, which involves incorporating peptide-rich hydrolysates into existing food products to improve their antidiabetic properties. Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility of such fortification. For example, Rivero-Pino et al. (2020a) observed enhanced DPP-IV inhibitory activity when fish hydrolysates were blended with apple juice compared with fish soup. Similarly, combining sardine hydrolysate with pistachio green hull extract improved antioxidant capacity without impairing DPP-IV inhibition, although α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities were reduced (Sarteshnizi et al., 2021). Functional yoghurt fortified with collagen-derived peptides also demonstrated increased antioxidant and DPP-IV inhibitory effects (Ayati et al., 2022). Furthermore, peptides have been shown to enhance the stability of probiotic cultures in food products. In a review by Zhang et al. (2020), it was noted that the excessive production of organic acids during fermentation by probiotics creates acidic environments that can lead to the self-autolysis of viable microbes. Additionally, the study reported that incorporating pepsin-treated casein macropeptide into yoghurt significantly improved the resistance of L. rhamnosus (a beneficial probiotic) to acid stress (pH 3 for 60 min) compared to yoghurt made with regular casein macropeptide.

Processing conditions can significantly influence peptide stability and bioactivity. Heat treatment, high-pressure processing, and ultrasonication have varying effects depending on the food matrix. For instance, Rivero-Pino et al. (2020b) found that heating improved DPP-IV inhibitory activity in fish hydrolysates, whereas high-pressure and ultrasound treatments had minimal impact. Consumer acceptance remains a key consideration because fortification can alter the texture, flavour, and sensory characteristics of food. Acevedo-Martinez and Gonzalez De Mejia (2021) demonstrated improved DPP-IV inhibition in fortified maize tortillas, although sensory evaluation was necessary due to changes in texture.

In summary, incorporating IVSD-derived peptides into functional foods is a practical and sustainable approach to support glycemic control. Future research should focus on optimising processing conditions, ensuring peptide stability within complex food matrices, and assessing consumer acceptability to facilitate commercial development.

Nutraceuticals for therapeutic values

In addition to functional foods, IVSD-derived peptides have exciting potential as nutraceuticals for therapeutic applications. Although some milk-derived peptides have been developed as nutraceuticals for hypertension management, few studies have explored IVSD-derived peptides specifically for hyperglycemia control (Du & Li, 2022). This represents a key area for future research, which requires extensive in vivo validation and clinical trials.

Advancing IVSD-derived peptides into nutraceutical formulations will require in-depth characterisation of their bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, safety, and dosage optimisation. Understanding their molecular mechanisms of action will further support their clinical translation and regulatory approval. The development of peptide-based nutraceuticals offers an attractive middle ground between food-based interventions and pharmaceuticals, as regulatory barriers for nutraceuticals are generally less stringent than for drugs. However, robust scientific evidence must first establish its efficacy and safety in human populations.

Alternative source of peptide production for T2DM management

Exploring alternative sources of peptide production provides both economic and environmental benefits. Insects, food industry byproducts, and underutilised protein sources are sustainable reservoirs for antidiabetic peptides generated via IVSD. Recent research has demonstrated the production of bioactive peptides from insects, such as mealworms and black crickets, which exhibit inhibitory effects on enzymes associated with diabetes (De Matos et al., 2024; Zielińska et al., 2020). Similarly, IVSD of jack bean sprouts (Agustia, Supriyadi, & A., & Indrati, R., 2024), yak bone collagen (Guo et al., 2021), spent brewer's yeast (Aquino et al., 2024), and oilseed protein meals (Pérez-Gálvez et al., 2024) successfully generated peptides with antidiabetic potential.

Utilising whole hydrolysates containing synergistic peptide mixtures may also offer cost-effective advantages, as extensive purification is not always necessary. These partially purified peptide mixtures can be directly incorporated into functional foods to provide multi-targeted biological effects, including antidiabetic, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities. This minimises production costs, supports sustainable food systems, and promotes circular economy principles by repurposing food waste into valuable therapeutic products.

Conclusion

IVSD-derived peptides represent a promising complementary strategy for T2DM management, offering multiple therapeutic mechanisms that extend beyond conventional pharmacotherapy. This review systematically examines the various mechanisms through which these bioactive peptides exert their antidiabetic effects, including the inhibition of key enzymes involved in glucose metabolism, enhancement of insulin sensitivity, and restoration of β-cell function.

However, significant challenges remain in translating these laboratory findings into clinical and commercial applications. The absence of robust clinical trial data, incomplete understanding of absorption, metabolism, and pharmacokinetics, and technical difficulties in large-scale production and purification continue to hinder commercial translation. Addressing these challenges is crucial for developing IVSD-derived peptides as viable therapeutic agents from experimental models.

Beyond pharmaceutical applications, IVSD-derived peptides hold considerable promise for integration into functional foods and nutraceuticals, offering accessible, food-based interventions for long-term glycemic control. The development of peptide-enriched products may face fewer regulatory and ethical barriers than drug development, providing a more immediate route to public health impact.

To fully harness the antidiabetic potential of IVSD-derived peptides, interdisciplinary collaboration among food science, biotechnology, pharmacology, and regulatory bodies is essential. Advances in peptide engineering, encapsulation technologies, and alternative protein sources such as food byproducts and insect proteins offer additional pathways for innovation and sustainable production. By strategically addressing these challenges and opportunities, IVSD-derived peptides may emerge as novel, safe, and effective components in the global fight against T2DM.
