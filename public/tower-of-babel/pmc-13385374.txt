Efficient elastocaloric drive system enabled by constant-torque and work-recovery design
Authors: Andrej Žerovnik, Stefano Dall’Olio, Simon Krašna, Žiga Ahčin, Jaka Tušek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elastocaloric cooling is a promising alternative to vapor-compression systems, offering high efficiency and reduced environmental impact. Despite significant advances in elastocaloric technology in recent years, limited attention has been given to the mechanical systems required to drive elastocaloric materials. Here we show a cam-disc-based elastocaloric drive system with integrated direct work recovery, synchronized by four phase-shifted elastocaloric elements. The system introduces a constant-torque driving approach that maintains nearly constant input power during operation. Experimental results demonstrate a nearly constant camshaft torque of 14.4 Nm and a mechanical-level work-recovery efficiency of about 70% at an actuation force of 40 kN. Numerical simulations further show that integrating force amplification mechanisms can reduce the required camshaft torque and increase work-recovery efficiency to up to 88%. These results demonstrate that efficient drive systems can be realized for elastocaloric devices, paving the way toward their practical deployment.

Introduction

Refrigeration and air-conditioning are indispensable to modern society but pose a major environmental challenge. The refrigeration sector consumes over 20% of global electricity and contributes 7–10% of greenhouse gas emissions, with demand projected to triple within the next three decades1,2. Yet standard vapor-compression technologies remain relatively inefficient, with exergy efficiencies typically below 30%3. In addition, most refrigeration systems still rely on hydrofluorocarbon refrigerants with high global warming potential, which are being phased out under the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol and replaced by natural refrigerants such as hydrocarbons, ammonia, and carbon dioxide4. However, natural refrigerants suffer from intrinsic limitations, including flammability, toxicity, or reduced performance in warm climates. These constraints highlight the need to either overcome their drawbacks or develop fundamentally new, non-vapor-compression refrigeration concepts.

Elastocaloric cooling and heat pumping have emerged as promising alternatives to vapor-compression systems5,6. They rely on the elastocaloric effect, commonly associated with stress-induced martensitic phase transformations in shape memory alloys (SMAs)7,8. Beyond SMAs, significant elastocaloric effects have also been demonstrated in polymer-based elastomers, where temperature changes arise from conformational entropy variations and strain-induced crystallization9–11. A typical elastocaloric operating cycle (i.e., Otto cycle12) consists of four phases. In the first phase, an SMA (or polymer) acting as the elastocaloric material (eCM) is uniaxially loaded, inducing an austenite-to-martensite transformation (in the case of an SMA) and releasing latent heat under near-adiabatic conditions. In the second phase, this heat is dissipated while the material is held at constant high displacement, returning to its initial temperature. In the third phase, unloading triggers a reverse transformation, which is endothermic and cools the material below its initial temperature. In the final phase, the cooled eCM absorbs heat from the surroundings at constant low displacement, providing active cooling. Among potential eCMs, near-equiatomic Ni-Ti alloys have been most extensively studied due to their large adiabatic temperature changes (>25 K), relatively good fatigue resistance, and commercial availability13. However, their large hysteresis and high transformation stresses limit device efficiency, motivating the recent development of improved eCMs with reduced hysteresis and lower stresses, such as Ni-Fe-Ga, Ni-Mn-Sn-Gd14, Ni-Ti-Cu15, Ni-Ti-Fe16, Ni-Co-Mn-Ti17, and Co-V-Ga alloys18,19, which exhibit improved efficiency while still providing substantial adiabatic temperature changes. In parallel with recent advances in eCMs, substantial progress has also been made in elastocaloric device design, including systems based on SMAs and polymer-based elastomers. Elastocaloric devices can be classified into several categories based on their heat-transfer mechanism (contact20–27, convective10,11,28–35, two-phase36), operating principle (single-stage10,11,20,22,24–27,37,38, cascade21,39,40, heat-recovery41, active regeneration28,29,31–35), and loading mode (tension10,11,21,24–28,34,35,38,40, compression29–33,36,42, bending43–46, twisting47,48). The best performance to date has been achieved by compressively loaded active elastocaloric regenerators, which have demonstrated fatigue-resistant operation, maximum fluid-side temperature spans exceeding 30 K29,31–33 and maximum cooling/heating powers above 200 W30,31,33. However, these extrema are obtained under different operating conditions: the maximum temperature span occurs at near-zero cooling/heating power, whereas the maximum cooling/heating power occurs at near-zero temperature span. Active regenerators typically employ macroscopically porous structures made of eCMs (e.g., arrays of tubes, plates, or other geometries) through which a heat-transfer fluid is pumped, enabling the formation of a temperature gradient along the regenerator28,49. This allows device temperature spans to exceed the adiabatic temperature change of the eCM, enabling cooling well below ambient and making elastocaloric technology viable for practical refrigeration.

Despite major advances in eCMs and elastocaloric regenerators, comparatively little attention has been paid to drive systems responsible for mechanically loading and unloading, although they play a critical role in overall device efficiency. Various actuation approaches have been proposed50–52, including linear and hydraulic actuators, rotary electromotors with crankshafts or cam-discs, and less conventional solutions such as magnetostrictive53 and shape-memory actuators54. Drive systems must typically deliver ~5% strain, stresses up to ~1000 MPa, and operate at moderate frequencies (0.5–4 Hz). Most elastocaloric demonstrators developed to date rely on linear electric or hydraulic actuators23,28–33,44 due to their modularity and ability to meet mechanical demands, but these systems are relatively inefficient and difficult to scale beyond laboratory prototypes. In contrast, rotary electromotors offer high efficiencies exceeding 90%55–57, compactness, and high-power density, and, when combined with suitable mechanical transmissions, represent a promising path toward efficient and scalable elastocaloric drive systems. Moreover, efficient elastocaloric devices critically require recovery of the mechanical work released during unloading of elastocaloric elements as effective work recovery is essential for maximizing the system’s coefficient of performance (COP). Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the experimentally measured cooling COP (adapted from Ahčin et al.29) for different assumed work-recovery efficiencies, highlighting the direct impact of work recovery on overall device performance. Although complete work recovery is often assumed when estimating efficiency of elastocaloric devices and eCMs at the material level, its practical feasibility at the drive-system level remains largely unexplored. Work recovery can be indirect21, via temporary mechanical energy storage (i.e., a flywheel) or battery with associated conversion and storage losses, or direct30, where unloading work from one phase-shifted elastocaloric element is immediately used to load another without intermediate storage. Elastocaloric device architectures can be broadly classified into single-element systems10,20,24–29,31–35,44,58, two phase-shifted element systems11,21,30,37 and multi-element rotary systems38,46,59,60. In single-element designs, direct mechanical work recovery is fundamentally impossible and can only be achieved indirectly via energy storage (e.g., a flywheel). Conventional two-element designs with linear or sinusoidal ramps (i.e., without the constant-torque approach introduced in this work) allow only partial direct work recovery. Namely, as shown by Kabirifar et al.61, in conventional designs with two phase-shifted elastocaloric elements, unloading work released by one phase-shifted element at the start of unloading exceeds the work required to load the other element, resulting in temporary negative net work and thus excess energy that cannot be recovered, thereby limiting the fraction of recoverable work. In contrast, rotary-based actuator designs with multiple phase-shifted elastocaloric elements enable a more uniform distribution of mechanical loading and unloading, with several elements being loaded and unloaded simultaneously, each at different stress values at any given time. Although not yet demonstrated experimentally, this approach would effectively prevent negative net work, enabling continuous work exchange and, in principle, complete recovery of unloading work (assuming negligible frictional losses). However, to date, in-depth analyses of elastocaloric work recovery have been reported only for a limited number of different elastocaloric systems, and the evaluation methodologies differ substantially across studies. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2, work recovery in elastocaloric systems can be defined hierarchically, beginning at the material level, where recovery is limited by intrinsic hysteresis (and full recovery on unloading work is usually assumed), and extending to the drive-system level, which may be evaluated either at the mechanical shaft boundary (mechanical level—including transmission and friction losses) or at the electrical input boundary (electrical level—additionally incorporating motor and power-electronic conversion losses). Yao et al.21 investigated work recovery using two phase-shifted elastocaloric elements driven by a rotary electromotor in oscillatory mode with indirect recovery. By incorporating kinetic energy storage (i.e., a flywheel-like mechanism), temporary negative net work during unloading was buffered, and an experimental work-recovery efficiency of approximately 78% was achieved on mechanical actuator level (neglecting motor and power-electronic inefficiencies). The recovered unloading work was determined using a comparative method, defined as the difference between the mechanical input work required to load individual elastocaloric elements without recovery and the reduced input work when two phase-shifted ribbons were mechanically coupled. Using a similar comparative approach, Zhang et al.11 evaluated the work-recovery efficiency of a two-element, phase-shifted polymer-based elastocaloric cooling device (without kinetic energy storage) but at the electrical system boundary and reported a work-recovery efficiency of 56.3%. Recovered work was determined from differences in measured electrical input energy between single- and double-unit configurations, after subtracting motor no-load losses, thereby inherently including motor and power-electronic conversion inefficiencies. Unmüßig et al.62 introduced a resonator-based elastocaloric drive system employing four phase-shifted spring elements actuated by reluctance actuators and evaluated its work-recovery efficiency at different operating frequencies, including resonance. The analysis was performed at the electrical level, where input power was determined from voltage–current measurements and mechanical output power was obtained by subtracting estimated electrical losses (coil, diode, and magnetic circuit) from the measured input power. A peak work-recovery efficiency of 95.7% was reported at the resonance frequency of 18.8 Hz, but it decreases to below 5% at off-resonance. However, such high operating frequencies may limit the available heat-transfer time, potentially constraining regenerator performance in practical elastocaloric applications. Supplementary Table 1 summarizes reported elastocaloric work-recovery approaches and efficiencies, highlighting differences in energy-balance boundaries, evaluation methodologies, and operating conditions to enable consistent cross-study comparison. While these approaches11,21,62 demonstrate the feasibility of efficient work recovery, they are limited to actuating small amounts of eCM at low forces (≤5 kN), which typically results in cooling and heating powers of only a few watts11,21. In contrast, scaling elastocaloric technologies to cooling and heating powers of several hundred watts or kilowatts typically requires actuation forces exceeding 30 kN, as demonstrated by the most powerful elastocaloric regenerators developed to date30,31,33; however, efficient drive systems capable of operating at such force levels, where frictional losses become significant, have not yet been reported. Beyond efficient work recovery and compact system design, an effective elastocaloric drive system must also operate under near-constant torque conditions. This requirement is critical for achieving high electric motor efficiency, which is typically realized only within a narrow operating window defined by constant torque and frequency56. However, to date, constant-torque operation has not been addressed in elastocaloric drive-system design. The integration of high-force work recovery with constant-torque operation in a medium-to-large-scale elastocaloric drive system therefore constitutes a primary innovation of this work.

Here, we present the design and experimental validation of a rotary, electromotor-driven, cam-disc-based drive system for elastocaloric devices that enables continuous operation and direct mechanical work recovery using four phase-shifted loaded (elastocaloric) elements, aiming to maximize work-recovery efficiency and minimize torque fluctuations at relevant operating frequencies (around 1 Hz) and high actuation forces (around 40 kN). The key innovation is a constant-torque strategy achieved by shaping the cam-disc profile to match the nonlinear force–displacement response of the loaded elements, thereby maintaining uniform torque and constant input power. This approach enables balanced mechanical work transfer between loading and unloading phases, preventing negative net work and allowing complete direct work recovery with only two or four phase-shifted elements (assuming negligible frictional losses). In contrast to previous approaches11,21,62, work-recovery efficiency in this study is defined and quantified at the mechanical shaft boundary, including material hysteresis and mechanical transmission losses, based on direct torque measurements. This enables evaluation of the mechanical drive system independent of electric motor efficiency, which is not considered because the employed motor was not optimized for efficiency characterization. Experimental validation using four disc-spring assemblies with elastocaloric-like responses, acting as loaded elements, demonstrates nearly constant torque of about 14.4 Nm (with a normalized root-mean-square torque deviation of about 15% relative to the mean torque) and a mechanical-level work-recovery efficiency of around 70% at actuation forces of up to 40 kN. Experimentally validated numerical simulations further show that incorporating force amplifiers reduces actuation forces and thus frictional losses, lowering torque demand to ~11 Nm and increasing work-recovery efficiency to up to 88%. This work represents a significant advance in elastocaloric technology, as the integration of constant-torque operation with efficient work recovery at high forces is essential for the development of high-performance elastocaloric cooling and heating systems and their future commercialization.

Results and discussion

A constant torque approach and system design

The proposed constant-torque approach is based on maintaining constant torque and, consequently, constant mechanical input power throughout the operating cycle. The mechanical input power is defined as the product of torque and angular velocity (Eq. (1)) and is linearly related to the operating frequency. Elastocaloric devices typically operate at a fixed frequency within the range of 0.5–4 Hz29,61, making constant-torque operation particularly advantageous. Under these conditions, maintaining constant input power requires the torque to remain constant over the entire cycle. To meet this requirement, a cam-disc displacement profile that enables different loading and unloading deformation rates was derived directly from the intrinsic force–displacement response of the loaded elements. In this work, disc-spring assemblies were used as loaded elements to validate the concept, while the same approach is directly applicable to elastocaloric regenerators29. This constant-torque approach enables optimal matching between actuator kinematics and the material response, ensuring constant torque throughout operation. Specifically, although the force is high at the beginning of unloading of one element and low at the beginning of loading of the phase-shifted element, the cam-disc profile reduces deformation rate in high-force regions and increases it in low-force regions. This prevents negative net work and enables direct recovery of unloading work even with only two phase-shifted regenerators. Such alignment between delivered and required work is not achievable with conventional linear or sinusoidal loading profiles when only a moderate number (i.e., two or four) of elastocaloric elements are engaged. As discussed in the Introduction, employing many phase-shifted elastocaloric elements in a rotary system can distribute the mechanical load more uniformly, but at the cost of significantly increased system complexity. Supplementary Fig. 3 illustrates that both torque fluctuations and average camshaft torque decrease as the number of elastocaloric elements increases, indicating that more than eight loaded elements are required to prevent negative net work when using a conventional linear loading profile. This corresponds to at least two elements being loaded and two other elements being unloaded simultaneously at different stress levels, enabling complete direct work recovery and preventing negative net work.

Following the constant-torque approach outlined in the Methods section (Eqs. (1)–(6)), distinct cam-disc displacement profiles were generated for the loading and unloading phases to drive four loaded elements arranged in a phase-shifted configuration. In this study, which focuses on validating the constant-torque concept, disc-spring assemblies were used in place of elastocaloric regenerators, tuned to exhibit a relatively similar mechanical response, including hysteresis (see Fig. 1). Due to the intrinsic hysteresis of both disc-spring assemblies and elastocaloric regenerators, the force–displacement response differs between loading and unloading (Fig. 1). Accordingly, two separate displacement functions were defined as a function of cam-disk shaft rotation: one for the loading phase and one for the unloading phase (Fig. 2a). These functions were combined into a complete four-phase operational cycle (Fig. 2b), as required for an active elastocaloric regenerative cycle28. The operating cycle is thus composed of four segments of equal duration: (1) a loading segment with a displacement rise profile designed to maintain constant torque; (2) a constant-displacement dwell segment at maximum load (i.e., holding phase at high displacement); (3) an unloading return segment with a distinct displacement profile designed to maintain constant torque; and (4) a constant-displacement dwell segment at minimum load (i.e., holding phase at low displacement). The constant-displacement segments are essential for efficient and high-power elastocaloric operation, as they enable heat transfer between the eCM and the heat-transfer fluid. To experimentally validate the concept, a complete drive-system prototype was constructed, comprising four loaded elements (disc-spring assemblies or elastocaloric regenerators), actuated by two cam-discs positioned 90° out of phase (see Fig. 3). Each cam-disc drives a pair of loaded elements arranged in an antagonistic configuration. This means that at any point during the operating cycle, one element is being loaded, one unloaded, and the remaining two are held at constant displacement (i.e., holding phases), one at low displacement and one at high displacement (Fig. 2c). During the constant-displacement segments of the cycle, no mechanical deformation occurs, and only some torque due to friction at the cam-disc–cam-follower interfaces and bearings is present. Figure 2d presents the calculated torque over a complete operating cycle, as detailed in the Methods section (see Eqs. (7)–(9)), including the individual loading and unloading torques of four disc-spring assemblies and the resulting net camshaft torque (i.e., the difference between the loading and unloading torques). Because the loading and unloading torques act in opposite directions, the mechanical work released during unloading is directly transferred to facilitate loading, substantially reducing the net camshaft torque and the overall power demand. Figure 3 shows a CAD model and a photo of the developed elastocaloric drive-system prototype. The system converts rotary motion supplied by an electric motor into precisely controlled reciprocating motion through a custom cam-disc-based mechanism, as described above. The assembly comprises a camshaft with two cam-discs that interact with four cam-followers, enabling accurate linear actuation of the loaded elements. The loaded elements (disc springs or elastocaloric regenerators) are mounted between the frame and two crossbeams, which are guided along linear guides to allow adjustable element length. Displacement and force limits are set using pre-loading bolts on each loaded element. In addition, real-time monitoring of mechanical loading is achieved with an LVDT displacement sensor and a load cell mounted on each loaded element, while a torque meter is mounted on the camshaft (see Methods for details).

Numerical evaluation

Numerical evaluation of the constant-torque approach and the direct work-recovery concept was performed using a multibody dynamics simulation in the Hexagon Adams environment (see “Methods” section for details). The simulations reproduced the operating cycle while accounting for the realistic mechanical response of four disc-spring assemblies (Fig. 1), including hysteresis, frictional losses, and component interactions, and were used to evaluate the resulting camshaft torque and power characteristics under different loading and unloading profiles. Figure 4a compares the three displacement profiles considered in the analysis: a linear profile, a sinusoidal profile, and the profile generated by the constant-torque approach. All profiles include two dwell segments (low and high constant displacement), as required for elastocaloric cycle28. Figure 4b shows the simulated camshaft torque for all three displacement profiles without drive-system friction, while including the experimentally measured hysteresis of the disc-spring assemblies shown in Fig. 1. For the constant-torque profile (red line), the torque remains essentially constant at approximately 7.9 Nm throughout the cycle, with only narrow spikes occurring at the beginning and at the end of each loading and unloading segment. These spikes arise from the discontinuous transitions between the constant-displacement segments and the loading or unloading segments, which lead to abrupt acceleration changes, brief velocity fluctuations, and minor torque oscillations. Importantly, these effects are solely a consequence of idealized, discontinuous profile switching and do not compromise the overall validity of the constant-torque approach. In contrast, the sinusoidal (blue line) and linear (yellow line) profiles exhibit pronounced torque oscillations, including intervals of negative torque, with peak-to-peak amplitudes of approximately 60 Nm and 98 Nm, respectively. These large oscillations reflect higher torque demand, while the temporary negative torque associated with conventional loading profiles prevents complete direct work recovery, both of which highlight the advantages of the constant-torque driving approach. It should be noted that, for the sinusoidal profile in particular, these torque fluctuations are closely related to the presence of constant-displacement segments (i.e., holding phases). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, a purely sinusoidal displacement profile without constant-displacement segments results in substantially reduced torque fluctuations, because such a cam-disc continuously engages two elements in loading and two in unloading, thereby enabling a more uniform balance between loading and unloading work and preventing negative net work throughout the operating cycle. However, the absence of constant-displacement segments leads to a non-optimal elastocaloric cycle due to insufficient time for heat transfer and, consequently, inferior device performance.

Figure 4c illustrates the influence of drive-system friction and material hysteresis of the loaded elements (disc-spring assemblies) on the camshaft torque. As described in the Methods section, the simulations account for multiple friction sources, including radial and linear bearings as well as rolling contact between the cam-discs and cam-followers. The numerical model assumes constant friction coefficients, independent of relative surface velocities and, consequently, of operating frequency. Simulations excluding material hysteresis were conducted using a cam-disc design derived solely from the force–displacement response of the disc-spring assemblies during loading, assuming identical behavior during unloading. When both drive-system friction and material hysteresis are included, the average camshaft torque increases compared to the frictionless case, as expected. In addition, friction within the drive system introduces torque fluctuations in the range of approximately 15–20 Nm (excluding the sharp peak torques discussed above), with an average torque of 17.3 Nm (in the case of bearings friction coefficient of 0.002). These fluctuations arise from dynamically varying radial forces acting on the camshaft and cam-followers’ bearings, which produce a time-dependent frictional torque. Because this dynamic friction component was not considered in the cam-disc profile design, it contributes to additional torque variations and should be incorporated into future cam-disc optimization. In contrast, when both frictional effects and material hysteresis are excluded, the average camshaft torque approaches 0 Nm, validating the constant-torque approach and demonstrating the effectiveness of the direct work-recovery concept.

Experimental demonstration and validation

The drive-system prototype (Fig. 3) was experimentally tested under various operating conditions, including different numbers of engaged disc-spring assemblies (one, two, or four) and operating frequencies (defined as the number of camshaft rotations per second) ranging from 0.04 Hz to 2 Hz, all at a maximum actuation force of 40 kN. The system response, including displacement, force, and torque as functions of time, together with the corresponding force–displacement behavior measured over one complete operating cycle (i.e., one full camshaft rotation), is shown in Fig. 5. These results correspond to tests performed at an operating frequency of 0.04 Hz and evaluated sequentially with one, two, and all four disc-spring assemblies engaged. Additional results obtained at higher operating frequencies (0.5 Hz, 1 Hz, and 2 Hz) are provided in the Supplementary Figs. 5–7. The camshaft torque of the empty drive system, measured with all four spring assemblies disengaged, ranged from approximately 0.28 Nm at 0.04 Hz to 1.15 Nm at 2 Hz (see Supplementary Fig. 8). When only one disc-spring assembly is engaged (first segments in Fig. 5a, b, d—between 0 and 25 s), the drive system exhibits pronounced force asymmetry. During the first quarter of the cycle, the system is nearly unloaded (displacement close to zero with a 5 kN preload), whereas in the third quarter it is fully loaded (2.1 mm displacement and 40 kN force). During the holding phases at both minimum and maximum force, the resisting forces arise primarily from friction within the drive system, resulting in low motor torque and power demand. In the second and fourth quarters, the disc-spring assembly is progressively loaded and unloaded following the constant-torque profile. This asymmetric actuation leads to significant camshaft torque fluctuations: the torque approaches zero during the holding phases, increases to approximately 32 Nm during loading, and decreases to about −25 Nm during unloading. The difference between loading and unloading torque primarily reflects the hysteresis of the disc-spring assembly. Because only one spring assembly is engaged, with no phase-shifted counterpart or mechanism for temporary energy storage (e.g., a flywheel), work recovery is not possible in this configuration.

The second segment in Fig. 5a, b, d—between 30 and 55 s—shows the experimental results obtained with two phase-shifted disc-spring assemblies mounted on the same upper cam-disc (see Fig. 3). In this configuration, one spring assembly is loaded while the other is unloaded; when one reaches full load, the other is fully unloaded, and vice versa. A slight mechanical clearance (play) in the upper cam-disc introduces minor asymmetry, resulting in a displacement difference of approximately 0.2 mm between the two disc-spring assemblies and a small force drop of about 2 kN in the fully loaded phase. Despite these imperfections, the constant-torque cam-disc design effectively prevents negative torque and negative net work, even when only two phase-shifted spring assemblies are engaged. For approximately half of each operating cycle, when the spring assemblies are either fully loaded or fully unloaded and no active loading or unloading occurs, the camshaft torque is close to zero, whereas during the loading and unloading phases it reaches approximately 18 Nm, with an average camshaft torque of about 8.5 Nm.

The third segment in Fig. 5a, b, d—between 60 and 85 s—shows the results for the configuration with all four disc-spring assemblies engaged, which corresponds to the intended operating configuration for which the drive system and cam-disc were designed. In this configuration, one spring assembly is being loaded, one is held in the loaded state, one is being unloaded, and one is held in the unloaded state (at all times during the cycle). As shown in Fig. 5d, with four phase-shifted spring assemblies engaged, the torque remains nearly constant at approximately 14.4 Nm, with only some fluctuations due to friction in the drive system, as predicted by numerical simulations. Additionally, small torque peaks observed at the onset of loading/unloading of each spring assembly, occurring four times per rotation of the cam-disc due to its discontinuous profile, also correlate well with the numerical results (see Fig. 4).

Figure 6 summarizes the experimentally measured and subsequently calculated performance metrics of the drive system as a function of operating frequency, corresponding to configurations with two and four phase-shifted disc-spring assemblies. The evaluated performance metrics include (see “Methods” for details Eq. (11)–(17)): the loading and unloading work, calculated from the force–displacement response (Fig. 5d); the hysteresis work, defined as the difference between loading and unloading work and representing the portion of work that cannot be recovered; the drive-system mechanical input work, calculated from the measured camshaft torque (includes hysteresis work and drive-system frictional work); the drive-system mechanical work losses, defined as the difference between the drive-system mechanical input work and the hysteresis work (primarily associated with frictional losses); and the recoverable work, calculated as the difference between the unloading work and the drive-system mechanical work losses, representing the fraction of released work that can be recovered by the drive system. Finally, the mechanical-level work-recovery efficiency is calculated as the ratio of recovered work to unloading work. As shown in Fig. 6, the loading, unloading, and hysteresis work are, in principle, independent of operating frequency, consistent with the frequency-independent force–displacement responses (see Supplementary Figs. 5–7 for detailed results at different frequencies). In contrast, the drive-system mechanical input work increases slightly with frequency due to higher drive-system mechanical losses, leading to reduced recoverable work and lower work-recovery efficiency at higher operating frequencies. These trends are observed consistently for configurations with both two and four phase-shifted disc-spring assemblies. The highest work-recovery efficiency is achieved at low operating frequencies (~0.04 Hz), reaching approximately 71% for the two-spring configuration and 68% for the four-spring configuration. The slightly higher efficiency observed for the two-spring configuration can be attributed to lower overall friction when fewer spring assemblies are engaged. For the four-spring configuration, the mechanical-level work-recovery efficiency remains around 60% at realistic operating frequencies between 0.5 and 1 Hz. To quantify torque constancy and its impact on work-recovery efficiency, we evaluated two complementary metrics: the normalized torque ripple, which captures peak-to-peak variation relative to the mean torque (Eq. (20)), and the normalized RMS torque deviation, which reflects both the magnitude and temporal extent of torque fluctuations (Eq. (21))63,64. While torque ripple highlights instantaneous extrema, the RMS torque deviation provides a more physically meaningful measure of fluctuation-induced losses. Supplementary Fig. 9 shows both metrics for configurations employing one, two, and four disc-spring assemblies. As expected, a clear reduction in torque fluctuations is observed as additional assemblies are engaged, due to improved work balancing. The four-disc-spring configuration (which corresponds to the intended operating configuration for which the drive system was designed) maintains RMS torque deviations in a range between 10 and 15% across all tested frequencies, indicating operation close to a constant-torque regime. The impact of torque uniformity on mechanical performance further is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 10, which plots mechanical work-recovery efficiency as a function of normalized RMS torque deviation at different operating frequencies. The single-disc-spring configuration exhibits RMS torque deviations of 300–500%, but such a configuration does not permit mechanical work recovery (unless a flywheel or equivalent energy-buffering component is introduced), and its work-recovery efficiency is therefore zero. The two-disc-spring configuration shows an RMS torque deviation of roughly 80% and achieves moderate work-recovery efficiency. In contrast, the four-disc-spring configuration maintains an RMS torque deviation of approximately 10–15%, corresponding to the highest measured mechanical work-recovery efficiencies. Although the two quantities do not appear to be directly related, a systematic relationship between normalized RMS torque deviation and mechanical work-recovery efficiency is observed. Specifically, at the lowest operating frequency, the work-recovery efficiencies of the two- and four-disc-spring configurations are similar despite their differing RMS torque deviations. At higher frequencies, however, the efficiency of the two-disc-spring configuration deteriorates more rapidly with increasing frequency (also evident in Fig. 6). This accelerated decline is likely driven by stronger force asymmetry acting on the camshaft compared to the four-disc-spring configuration, which in turn increases radial bearing loading and related frictional losses. These effects become more pronounced at higher frequencies (Fig. 6), where the associated torque fluctuations for the two- and four-disc-spring configurations are also somewhat larger (Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast, the four-disc-spring configuration maintains much better force balancing over the full cycle, which limits these mechanical losses and helps preserve higher work-recovery efficiency. Nevertheless, as noted in the Introduction, the present work evaluates performance at the mechanical system level, and the efficiency of the prototype electric motor is therefore not included in the reported work-recovery efficiency. Modern permanent-magnet drive motors (particularly high-torque direct-drive and traction-class machines) can achieve peak efficiencies of 95–97% under conditions of nearly constant torque and moderate speed56. Such high efficiency is typically attainable only within narrow operating windows, since torque fluctuations increase RMS current and degrade electromagnetic efficiency65. Because the four-disc-spring configuration maintains relatively low RMS torque deviations, the developed constant-torque architecture is well aligned with the torque stability required for efficient motor operation56. Consequently, given the potentially high electric-motor efficiency associated with near-constant-torque operation, the expected electrical-level drive-system work recovery (see Eq. (18)) is not anticipated to be significantly lower than the mechanical-level work recovery reported here.

To further evaluate the impact of cam-disc profiles on drive-system performance, the system was also assessed, both experimentally and numerically, at lower actuation forces of 30 kN (see Supplementary Fig. 11). When the drive system operates at actuation forces up to 30 kN using a cam-disc profile designed for higher forces (40 kN), the resulting force–displacement behavior no longer matches the cam-disc profile. Consequently, the loading and unloading velocities deviate from those required to maintain constant torque, leading to torque fluctuations between approximately 25 Nm and −5 Nm during operation, as confirmed experimentally and numerically. In contrast, when the cam-disc is designed specifically based on the disc-spring assembly behavior at a maximum force of 30 kN, constant torque operation is restored, as demonstrated by the numerical simulations shown in Supplementary Fig. 11b.

Enhancing work-recovery efficiency through force amplifiers: a numerical study

Although the current drive system already achieves relatively low camshaft torque and high work-recovery efficiency, further improvements were explored numerically by introducing force amplifiers between the cam-followers and the loaded elements, as shown in Fig. 7a. These force amplifiers reduce the force transmitted to the cam-disc, thereby lowering frictional losses within the drive system. To assess the impact of this design modification, the multibody dynamics model was extended in the Hexagon Adams simulation environment and additional simulations were performed. The idealized amplifiers, assumed to have no internal losses and a 4:1 force–displacement ratio, were implemented to reduce the transmitted force while proportionally increasing the required displacement on the cam-disc side. The practical efficiency of the piston–hydraulic oil–piston force amplifier can reach 94–97% (based on reported seal-friction losses in hydraulic cylinders and established stiffness/compressibility models for cylinder–fluid systems66,67), allowing intrinsic losses to be neglected in this work. Importantly, the application of force amplifiers does not alter the force or displacement experienced by the loaded elements, thereby preserving their intended mechanical behavior. Prior to the simulations, the cam-disc profile was adjusted accordingly to account for the modified kinematics. Figure 7b compares the modeled camshaft torque of the baseline drive system – assuming friction coefficient of 0.002 (see Fig. 4b), the experimentally measured torque (see Fig. 5d), and the modeled torque of the drive system incorporating force amplifiers. The numerical (baseline) results show good agreement with the experimental measurements in both overall trends and absolute values, although the simulations slightly overpredict the torque, most likely due to slightly overestimated friction coefficients. The results further demonstrate a clear reduction in average of simulated camshaft torque from approximately 17.3 Nm to about 11 Nm when force amplifiers are employed, owing to reduced frictional losses in the drive system, including those from radial and linear bearings and the cam-disc – cam-follower interface. This reduction enables further downsizing of structural components such as the frame, shaft, bearings, and electric motor, decreases mechanical wear at the cam-follower interface, and most importantly increases the work-recovery efficiency to approximately 88% (calculated following Eq. Finally, in this work, we developed and validated an efficient high-force elastocaloric drive system combining a constant-torque cam–disc design with direct mechanical work recovery. Using phase-shifted disc-spring assemblies representative of elastocaloric regenerators, the system achieves nearly uniform camshaft torque (~14.4 Nm), with a normalized RMS torque deviation below 15% of the mean torque. The work-recovery efficiencies (measured at the mechanical shaft boundary) of up to 70% are achieved with only a moderate number of loaded elements. Numerical and experimental investigations confirm that the constant-torque strategy substantially reduces peak torque demands, enabling more compact, mechanically balanced, and energy-efficient drive architectures. Numerical simulations show that integration of idealized force amplifiers further reduces average camshaft torque from ~17.3 Nm to ~11 Nm, increasing work-recovery efficiency to ~88% while enabling downsizing of structural components and reducing mechanical wear.

While the present validation uses disc-spring assemblies to capture the mechanical behavior of Ni–Ti elastocaloric regenerators, practical devices will require cam–disc geometries tailored to material composition and operating temperature to maintain constant-torque operation according to the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship. However, the methodology presented here, combining experimentally measured force–displacement behavior with constant-torque cam–disc design, multibody dynamics modeling, and experimental validation, provides a general framework applicable to elastocaloric and other mechanocaloric systems. By maintaining low RMS torque deviations, the approach enables smooth operation and high efficiency of coupled electric motors. Consequently, the expected overall drive-system (electrical-level) work recovery is not anticipated to be significantly lower than the mechanical-level work recovery reported here, as optimized motors operating near constant torque maintain high efficiency and low losses. When combined with such motors, this strategy offers a scalable pathway toward efficient elastocaloric heat pumps and cooling systems competitive with conventional vapor-compression technologies in both performance and environmental impact.

Numerical validation methodology

A multibody dynamics (MBD) model of the drive system was developed using Hexagon Adams/View 2024.1.1. The mechanical components of the drive system, including the shaft, cam-discs, cam-followers, and base frame, were modeled as rigid bodies with mass and geometry consistent with the physical prototype (see Supplementary Fig. 13). Rotary and linear bearings were implemented via revolute and translational joints. The interaction between the cam-disc and the cam-follower was modeled using the built-in IMPACT contact model with a planar circle-curve geometry. The loaded elements (disc-spring assemblies) were represented by spring-damper model featuring nonlinear stiffness characteristics derived from experimentally measured force–displacement curves using spline interpolation functions for both loading and unloading phases (see Fig. 1). The cam-disc profile itself was generated separately according to the constant-torque approach as described above and imported into the simulation as a spline geometry. The cam-follower displacement was constrained to linear motion through translational joints (i.e., linear bearings). Standardized bearing models were included using the Adams/Machinery plugin. The cam-disc—cam-follower interface was modeled as an INA NUTR3072 A, while the rotary shaft bearing as an INA NKI45/35–TV–XL. Both were implemented in Adams/Machinery elements, where the revolute joint option was applied. For the shaft, only a single radial-thrust bearing was implemented into the model, to prevent redundancy in the kinematical constraints. The simulations were performed by first applying a static preload of 5 kN to each spring assembly. Sequential loading and unloading of the disc-spring assemblies were then implemented to simulate the four-phase operation.

To systematically evaluate the impact of friction on system dynamics and the camshaft torque, three simulation cases were performed:Frictionless case: All contacts (cam-disc – cam-follower interfaces and bearings) were treated as ideal, i.e., without friction. Experimental validation methodology

The drive system shown in Fig. 3 was equipped with a direct–drive servo electric motor (type SKA.DDR.245.60.70.53) featuring 28 poles, a stall torque of 70 Nm, a peak torque of 241 Nm, and a maximum speed of 150 rpm (at 400 V). An integrated measurement chain was designed to enable synchronized monitoring of forces and deformations in each loaded element (spring assembly in the present setup), as well as the torque applied to the camshaft. It is based on a National Instruments (NI) CompactDAQ cDAQ-9174 chassis, providing a modular and flexible platform for integrating multiple sensors and ensures fully synchronized data acquisition across all input channels, which is essential for accurately capturing time-dependent force–displacement behavior. Data was recorded at a sampling frequency of 85 Hz, which is sufficient to capture the dynamic loading cycles during operation. A custom NI LabVIEW application was developed to interface with the CompactDAQ system and manage real-time data logging from all sensors along with real-time visualization of measured data. Mechanical load was measured using a force transmitter equipped with an AEP load cell (model CM) with a measuring range up to 50 kN. The overall measurement uncertainty of the force transmitter (including accuracy, linearity, and repeatability) is less than ±0.29% of full scale. Displacements were measured using DCTH400 linear variable differential transformers (LVDTs), which provide a voltage output proportional to linear motion within a measuring range of ±10 mm. Each sensor measures the displacement of the armature (sliding core) relative to the fixed body of the transducer. The combined measurement uncertainty of the LVDTs is less than ±0.5% of full scale. The mechanical work delivered by the motor to rotate the camshaft was determined from torque measurements using a transducer installed between the motor and the camshaft. Camshaft torque was measured using an MRT2100NM transducer capable of measuring in both clockwise and counter-clockwise directions, with a measuring range from −25 Nm to +100 Nm and a maximum rotational speed of 4000 rpm. The combined measurement uncertainty of the torque transducer is below ±0.2% of full scale. Measurement uncertainties of the directly measured parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 3, while a complete uncertainty analysis is presented in Supplementary Note 1.
