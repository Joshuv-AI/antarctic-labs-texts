Substrate specificity controlled by the exit site of human P4-ATPases, revealed by de novo point mutations in neurological disorders
Authors: David C. Calianese, Tomoyasu Noji, Jennifer A. Sullivan, Kelly Schoch, Vandana Shashi, Vanda McNiven, Luiza Lorena Pires Ramos, Albena Jordanova, Judit Kárteszi, Hiroshi Ishikita, Shigekazu Nagata
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceATPase 11A (ATP11A) is a flippase that specifically translocates phosphatidylserine from the outer to the inner leaflet of plasma membranes. We previously reported that a point mutation at its exoplasmic site causes the aberrant translocation of phosphatidylcholine (PtdCho), leading to the upregulation of a sphingomyelin (SM) synthetase and the accumulation of SM in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. Here, we identified three patients with a neuronal disorder, each carrying de novo point mutations near the substrate exit site of ATP11A. These mutations also resulted in the abnormal translocation of PtdCho. Using Molecular Dynamics Simulation Analysis, we found that these mutants bind PtdCho tightly at the exit gate, which may induce conformational changes at the entry gate, altering substrate specificity.

Plasma membranes in eukaryotic cells comprise a lipid bilayer that primarily acts as a barrier separating the interior of a cell from the extracellular environment. The primary constituents of this bilayer are phospholipids and cholesterol (1). Phospholipids are categorized into glycerophospholipids and sphingophospholipids. Major glycerophospholipids include phosphatidylcholine (PtdCho), phosphatidylethanolamine (PtdEtn), phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), and phosphatidylinositol (PtdIns). The predominant sphingophospholipid is sphingomyelin (SM). Most phospholipids are synthesized on the cytoplasmic side of the ER and mitochondrial-associated membranes (2, 3). As an exception to this, SM is synthesized on the luminal side of the Golgi apparatus, where sphingomyelin synthase 1 (Sgms1) catalyzes the transfer of phosphocholine from PtdCho to ceramide, yielding SM and diacylglycerol (4).

Phospholipids are asymmetrically distributed between leaflets of the plasma membrane of mammalian cells (5–7). Anionic phospholipids, such as PtdSer, PtdEtn, and PtdIns, are predominantly confined to the inner leaflet, whereas neutral phospholipids, including PtdCho and SM, are primarily found in the outer leaflet (8, 9). Flippases play a crucial role in maintaining this asymmetry by selectively transferring PtdSer and PtdEtn from the outer to the inner leaflet (6, 10–12). Most flippases comprise two subunits: type IV P-type (P4) ATPases and CDC50A (12, 13). P4-ATPases represent a family of proteins that has 14 members in humans. These proteins feature ten transmembrane domains (TM) and an ATPase domain comprising three subdomains: an actuator domain (A-domain), a nucleotide-binding domain (N-domain), and a phosphorylation domain (P-domain) (12). The second subunit, CDC50A, not only facilitates flippase activity but also plays a crucial role in localizing P4-ATPases to the plasma membrane (14–18).

The structure of P4-ATPases at different catalytic stages has been examined using cryogenic electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) or X-ray crystallography (19–27). A characteristic of P4-ATPases is the movement of the A-domain, which generates a sizable cavity bordered by TM2, TM4, and TM6, serving as an entry point to accommodate large substrates, such as phospholipids. The movement of phospholipids through this cavity is governed by a conserved hydrophobic PISL/PVSM motif at the center of TM4 (28), acting as a gate. The polar head group of phospholipids is then considered to traverse a groove between TM2 and TM4 until the inner leaflet is reached.

P4-ATPases have been implicated in various diseases, particularly those affecting the nervous system (29). We recently identified a de novo heterozygous point mutation in the ATP11A gene in a patient displaying global developmental delays and neurological degeneration (30). This mutation results in an amino acid change at position 84 from glutamine to glutamic acid (Q84E) at the phospholipid entry site, which affects the substrate specificity of the enzyme, enabling it to flip PtdCho along with its authentic substrates PtdSer and PtdEtn. This aberrant transfer of PtdCho to the inner leaflet of plasma membranes leads to internalization, prompting the upregulation of SM on the cell surface to compensate for the loss of PtdCho on the outer leaflet.

In the present study, we report two de novo heterozygous mutations in the ATP11A gene in three patients exhibiting clinical phenotypes similar to the patient with the ATP11A Q84E mutation. The two new mutations (E114G and S399L) are situated at the opposite side of the enzyme from the Q84 residue near the predicted phospholipid exit site. These mutations shift substrate specificity toward flipping PtdCho and result in elevated levels of SM on the plasma membrane through the up-regulated expression of the Sgms1 gene. The introduction of equivalent mutations into ATP11A’s paralogs, ATP11C and ATP8A2, which harbor a well-conserved structure at the exit site, yielded similar characteristics to the ATP11A mutants, underscoring the structural significance of these residues. The present results reveal the coordinated role of entry and exit sites in substrate selection and show how mutations in these residues affect cellular and clinical outcomes in humans.

Results

Human ATP11A Mutations near the Predicted Phospholipid Exit Site.

Patients with neurological disorders originating from various countries (USA, Canada, and Hungary) underwent screening for causal mutations using whole exome sequencing (WES) or whole genome sequencing (WGS) (Fig. 1A). Candidate genes were filtered using data from the ClinVar database in the National Library of Medicine (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and the genome aggregation database (gnomAD: https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), as well as family-related information. All three patients harbored a de novo variant in the coding sequence of the ATP11A gene (NM_015205.3). Patient 1 in Canada, a 21-y-old male, and Patient 2 from Hungary, analyzed in Belgium, a 15-y-old female, both carry a c.1196C>T variant. Patient 3 in the USA, a 33-y-old male, bears a c.341A>G variant. Initial growth and psychomotor development appeared normal for all three individuals; however, they subsequently developed toe walking and progressive lower extremity spasticity during childhood. Patients 1 and 2 exhibit average intelligence, while Patient 3 has mild intellectual disability and experiences seizures. Patients 1 and 3 both displayed bilateral hypomyelination on MRI brain scans; in Patient 1, this anomaly was detected at the age of 9 y. In Patient 3, this was detected at 19 y following previously normal MRI scans. His recent MRI scan displayed global ventriculomegaly and atrophy. The most recent brain MRI for Patient 2 at the age of 11 y showed no abnormalities. Patients 2 and 3 present mild-to-moderate peripheral polyneuropathy, whereas the results of electromyography (EMG) and a nerve conduction study (NCS) for Patient 1 were normal. The results of the sural nerve biopsy and EMG for Patient 2 suggested an axonal type of neuropathy.

The c.1196C>T mutation identified in Patients 1 and 2 causes a missense amino acid change at position 399 (S399L), while the c.341A>G mutation in Patient 3 results in a missense replacement at position 114 (E114G) (Fig. 1B). The residue E114 is situated within the second TM helix (TM2), proximal to the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane. In contrast, the S399 residue is in the P-domain (Fig. 1 C, Left). The Q84E variant, previously identified in a patient with similar clinical phenotypes (30), is positioned on the outer membrane interface of TM1 or at the entry site for phospholipid internalization. In contrast, the new mutations (E114G and S399L) are near the predicted phospholipid exit site, situated at the opposite side of the membrane domain from the Q84 residue (Fig. 1 C, Right). Residues E114 and S399 are conserved across multiple species, underscoring their evolutionary significance and potential functional relevance (Fig. 1D).

Flipping of PtdCho and High Cell Surface SM in E114G- and S399L-ATP11A Mutants.

The substrate specificity of the Q84E-ATP11A mutant differs from that of WT-ATP11A; the former flips PtdCho, whereas the latter does not (30). To examine the substrate specificities of the E114G and S399L mutants of ATP11A, these mutant alleles, as well as WT-ATP11A, were tagged with Enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (EGFP) at the C terminus and introduced into Atp11c−/− and Atp11a−/− W3 mouse T cell-lymphoma (DKO), which stably expressed Flag-tagged CDC50A (CDC50A-Flag) (30). Confocal microscopy of the stable transformants indicated that WT-ATP11A and all mutants reached the plasma membrane and were expressed at a similar level (Fig. 2A).

The substrate specificity of each mutant was then evaluated by the incorporation of nitrobenzoxadiazole (NBD)-linked lipid analogs (Fig. 2B). WT-ATP11A incorporated NBD-labelled phosphatidylserine (PS) and NBD-labelled phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), but not NBD-labelled phosphatidylcholine (PC). On the other hand, Q84E-ATP11A incorporated NBD-PC, NBD-PS, and NBD-PE, which was consistent with previous findings (30). Similar to WT-ATP11A, mutant ATP11A with the Q84E, E114G, or S399L replacement did not flip galactosylceramide (GalCer), glucosylceramide (GlcCer), or SM. However, the E114G and S399L mutants incorporated NBD-PC at a slightly lower level than the Q84E mutant.

One of the characteristics of Q84E-expressing cells is the upregulation of SM on the outer surface of the plasma membrane due to the up-regulated expression of the Sgms1 gene (30). Since E114G and S399L-ATP11A mutant cells behaved similarly to Q84E-expressing cells in flipping PtdCho, we examined the SM exposure of these cells with Red Fluorescent Protein (RFP)-tagged nontoxic lysenin (NT-Lys), which specifically binds SM (31). Similar to the Q84E control, E114G- and S399L-bound 13.1- and 11.6-fold more NT-Lys (Fig. 3A), suggesting a substantial increase in outer leaflet SM. Accordingly, these mutant cells were as sensitive as Q84E-expressing cells against neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase)-induced cell lysis (Fig. 3B), or they were lysed within 5 min with a 10-fold lower concentration of nSMase than that needed to lyse WT cells, confirming the high level of SM on the cell surface. A real-time PCR analysis of Sgms1 mRNA and Western blotting for SGMS1 protein showed that they were highly up-regulated (6.5- to 14.6-fold for mRNA) in cells expressing the E114G or S399L mutant (Fig. 3 C and D). A high concentration of SM binds cholesterol and makes it inaccessible to perfringolysin O (PFO) (32), a cholesterol-binding toxin. Accordingly, similar to Q84E-expressing cells, E114G- or S399L-expressing cells bound 3.6- and 3.1-fold less mCherry-tagged D4 (domain 4) of PFO (Fig. 3E), indicating a substantial decrease of accessible cholesterol levels on the cell surface of these mutant cells.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations.

Fourteen members of the human P4-ATPase family are divided into six classes based on the amino acid sequence (12). The three PtdSer-flippases (ATP8A2, ATP11A, and ATP11C) present in the plasma membrane (17) belong to two different classes that comprise distinct members (Class 1a, ATP8A1 and ATP8A2; Class 6, ATP11A, ATP11B, and ATP11C). Among the Class 6 P4-ATPases, the tertiary structure of human ATP11C but not ATP11A was available (21, 23). Therefore, to understand the molecular mechanism for the PtdCho-flipping activity of the ATP11A mutants, the MD simulations were performed based on the ATP11C structure. We analyzed the number of electrostatic interactions, including hydrogen bonds and Coulomb interactions, formed between phospholipids and the protein environment at the exit site. In the WT-ATP11C structure, the total number of electrostatic interactions with residues at the exit site (i.e., E109, K277, and D395; SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2) is 0.11 times lower for PtdCho compared to PtdSer (Table 1). This finding is consistent with the observed high specificity for PtdSer over PtdCho in WT-ATP11A (Fig. 2). Upon introducing the E109G and S394L mutations (corresponding to E114G and S399L in ATP11A), the relative specific interaction of PtdCho over PtdSer at the exit site increased approximately fivefold and twofold, respectively (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S6). These altered characteristics of the electrostatic interactions at the exit site likely explain the ability of the E114G and S399L mutant ATP11As to flip PtdCho (Fig. 2).

To reach the exit site, phospholipids must first bind at the entry site and then permeate the channel connecting the two sites. We analyzed the structural landscape of the channel when phospholipids are at the entry and exit sites. In the WT ATP11C structure, the overall channel shape with PtdSer at the entry site is substantially similar to that with PtdSer at the exit site (Fig. 4). However, the presence of PtdCho at the exit site requires significant increases in the channel radii in the exit region compared to its presence at the entry site, indicating that significant structural changes are needed for PtdCho permeation (i.e., activation process). This likely contributes to the observed high specificity for PtdSer over PtdCho in WT-ATP11A. Intriguingly, the corresponding structural changes in the exit region are not observed in the E109G mutant ATP11C structure (Fig. 4). The channel radii in the exit region are already sufficiently large for PtdCho permeation even when PtdCho is still at the entry site. The absence of structural changes for PtdCho permeation likely explains the observed flipping activity for PtdCho in the E114G mutant ATP11A (Fig. 2).

The Functional Conservation of the Exit Site among Subfamilies of P4-ATPases.

Residues Q84, E114, and S399 of human ATP11A were fully conserved among members of Class 1a and Class 6 P4-ATPases (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We previously reported that the mutation corresponding to the ATP11A’s Q84E confers the PtdCho-flipping activity in ATP11C (Q79E) belonging to the same Class 6, but not to ATP8A2 (Q107E) in Class 1a (30). To examine the effects of mutations corresponding to E114G and S399L of ATP11A in other members of P4-ATPases, E109 of ATP11C and E138 of ATP8A2 were mutated to glycine, while S394 of ATP11C and S413 of ATP8A2 were replaced by leucine. These mutants (E109G and S394L of ATP11C and E138G and S413L of ATP8A2) were tagged with EGFP at the C terminus and introduced into DKO cells. Observations by confocal microscopy indicated that these mutant proteins, as well as WT-ATP11C-EGFP and WT-ATP8A2-EGFP, were present in plasma membranes (SI Appendix, Figs. S8A and S9A). The flippase assay with NBD-PS as a substrate indicated that these mutants exhibited similar flippase activity against PtdSer to the respective WT P4-ATPase (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Figs. S8B and S9B). Similar to ATP11A mutants, not only the Q79E mutant but also the mutants of ATP11C (E109G and S394L) and ATP8A2 (E138G and S413L) internalized NBD-PC. These results contrast those obtained on the Q107E mutant of ATP8A2, which exhibited negligible activity against NBD-PC. The NBD-PC-flipping activity of the S394L-ATP11C mutant was significantly lower than those of the E109G-ATP11C mutants, which may align with the structural changes required for PtdCho binding at the exit site as indicated by MD simulations (Fig. 4).

Like the ATP11A mutant, the NBD-PC-incorporating activity of cells expressing different mutants correlated with SM and accessible cholesterol levels on the cell surface. Cells exhibiting intense NBD-PC-incorporating activity (Q79E-ATP11C and E109G-ATP11C) had high SM levels on the cell surface and reduced accessible cholesterol levels (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix Fig. S8). However, cells with intermediate PtdCho-flipping activity (E138G-ATP8A2, S394L-ATP11C, and S413L-ATP8A2) had low SM levels. The reduction in accessible cholesterol levels was also marginal (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). On the other hand, surface SM and accessible cholesterol levels in cells expressing Q107E-ATP8A2 with no PtdCho-flipping activity were similar to those in cells expressing WT-ATP8A2. These results confirmed that the aberrant flipping of PtdCho to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane up-regulated SM at the plasma membrane. The mutation in Q84 to glutamic acid affected the substrate specificity of Class 6 P4-ATPases (ATP11A and ATP11C) but not Class 1a P4-ATPase (ATP8A2). In contrast, E114G- and S399L- equivalent mutations changed the substrate specificity of P4-ATPases in both classes.

The tertiary structures of human ATP8A1 and ATP11C at various activation stages were examined by X-ray crystallography or Cryo-EM (21–23). A comparison of their structures (PDB: 6K7L for ATP8A1 and 7BSU for ATP11C) in the PtdSer-occluded E2-P state indicated similar overall structures (Fig. 5C). A close inspection of the residues surrounding Q88 in ATP8A1 and Q79 in ATP11C showed differences in the configurations of the charged residues in the entry site. In contrast, the configuration of charged residues in the exit site, involving E109/S394 in ATP11C and E119/S394 in ATP8A1, was similar and well-conserved. These structural data may explain why the Q107E mutant of ATP8A2 cannot incorporate PtdCho, in contrast to the corresponding mutants of ATP11A and ATP11C.

Discussion

In contrast to the plasma membranes of mammalian cells (34, 35), PtdCho is exclusively confined to the inner leaflet of the plasma membranes in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (36). To maintain this asymmetrical distribution of PtdCho, the yeast carries distinct flippases (Dnf1 and Dnf2) that translocate PtdCho, GlcCer, and PtdEtn in addition to those (Drs2, Neo1, and Dnf3) that translocate PtdSer and PtdEtn (29). Based on analyses of the phospholipid-flipping activities of chimeric molecules between Drs2 and Dnf1, followed by a mutagenesis analysis of Dnf1, Drs2, and Neo1, Graham’s group proposed a two-gate mechanism for substrate selection (29, 37, 38). The entry gate was assigned to the region close to the exoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane, while the exit gate was near the cytoplasmic side. The present results and previous findings (30) with the mutations found in human patients support this proposal, and human P4-ATPases (ATP8A2, ATP11A, and ATP11C), at least those working at the plasma membrane as PtdSer/PtdEtn flippases, use the entry and exit gates to recognize the substrate.

The arranged mutated amino acids in the entry site (Q84E in ATP11A) accommodate PtdCho and PtdSer (30). In contrast, the mutants (E114G and S399L) at the exit site on the cytoplasmic side carry the same amino acids at the entry site as the WT-ATP11A. Since flippases translocate phospholipids from the outer leaflets to the inner leaflets, recognizing substrates by the exit gate is puzzling (39). The tertiary structures of Dnf1-Lem3 (the yeast homolog of CDC50A) in the E2-Pi state observed by Cryo-EM indicate that PtdCho is captured at both the entry and exit gates (24), which contradicts the classical Post-Albers cycle, postulating an alternating half-channel transport model for P-type ATPases (40). The results of the MD analysis showed that phospholipids bound to the exit gate in the E2-Pi state of human ATP11C. Affinity to PtdCho was significantly more potent with the E109G and S394L mutant in the exit gate than the WT, correlating with its PtdCho-flipping activity (Table 1). The replacement of E98 of bovine ATP8A1, corresponding to E114 of human ATP11A, with lysine or alanine was previously shown to enhance PtdCho-stimulated ATPase activity (41), which is consistent with the results of the cell-based assay in the present study. The entry-exit path bordered by TM2 and TM4 may be conformationally coupled, and phospholipids have been suggested to move through this path, changing the conformation of neighboring regions (41). Although mutations in the exit site may cause a conformational change in the entry site, our results support that the binding of phospholipids to the exit gate induces a conformational change at the entry site. Since the membrane environment or phospholipid composition in membranes affects the enzymatic activity or conformation of membrane proteins, including flippases (27, 42, 43), the increased affinity of a mutant flippase to PtdCho at the exit gate may affect its substrate specificity. An elaborate cell-free method has recently been developed to reconstitute the lipid bilayer with asymmetrically distributed phospholipids (44–46). It will be interesting to determine the PtdCho-flipping activities of exit gate mutants of ATP11A using this reconstituted system.

In addition to ATP11A, mutations in ATP8A2 and ATP11C corresponding to ATP11A’s Q84E, E114G, and S399L caused PtdCho-flipping, except for the Q107E mutant of ATP8A2, which markedly reduced flipping activity against PtdSer. In the ClinVar database, many variants are registered for ATP8A2 (185 variants), ATP11A (99 variants), and ATP11C (55 variants); however, their clinical significance currently remains unclear for most cases. According to the present results, the E138G mutation of ATP8A2 registered in the ClinVar database (VCV001700839.2) (Fig. 6) appears to flip not only PtdSer but also PtdCho from the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane to the inner leaflet, suggesting that it is a pathogenic mutation. In the nervous system, the ATP8A2 gene is expressed at a markedly higher level than the ATP11A gene (47). Its expressing cell types differ from those expressing ATP11A (Brainspan: Atlas of the developing human brain, Allen Institute of Brain Science, https://www.brainspan.org/static/home), suggesting that the phenotype differs between patients carrying E138G-ATP8A2 or E114G-ATP11A. A comparison of the clinical phenotypes of these patients may provide a more detailed understanding of how the flipping of PtdCho in the nervous system leads to human diseases.

The residues in yeast flippases, the mutations of which affect substrate specificity, are highlighted in green. Missense mutations identified in human patients registered in the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) are highlighted in purple. The point mutations (Q84E, E114G, and S399L) of human ATP11A characterized in this study are highlighted in yellow. The regions that form the entry and exit gates are indicated.

The residues corresponding to two clinical mutations (Q84E and S399L of ATP11A) were previously analyzed in the yeast system (25, 38). PtdCho-flipping Dnf1 carries GA at amino acid positions 230 and 231 in the entry site. The corresponding residues in the PtdSer-flipping flippase of Drs2 are QQ at positions 237 and 238, similar to human ATP8A2 (Fig. 6). Notably, the replacement of G230 and A231 of Dnf1 by glutamine (GA→QQ) converted the mutant to translocate PtdSer (38). In contrast, replacing QQ at position 230/231 of Drs2 by glycine and alanine GA eliminated PtdSer-flipping activity without affecting PtdEtn-flipping activity. The mutation of S488 at the exit gate of Neo1 to tryptophan or alanine also reduced growth due to a reduction in flippase activity (25). Collectively, these findings suggest that the substrate recognition mechanism is similar between P4-ATPases in yeast (Dnf1, Neo1, and Drs2) and humans (ATP8A2, ATP11A, and ATP11C), at least for members localized at the plasma membrane or endosomes. In this regard, it is noteworthy that the residues corresponding to Q84 and S399 of ATP11A are changed to Asn and Leu in ATP10A (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which has the ability to flip PtdCho (48).

In addition to the mutants described above, many mutations were introduced into the entry and exit gate regions of yeast Dnf1, Dnf2, Drs2, and Neo1 (24, 25, 37, 38, 42, 49) (Fig. 6). Among the missense mutations registered in the ClinVar database, some are located in the entry or exit gate (Fig. 6). For example, P110R and N372S of ATP8A2 and F356L of ATP11A are in the entry gate region, while I333L, I136S, D139N, K144M, and N416I of ATP8A2 and H119L and G393W of ATP11A are in the exit gate. The phospholipid-flipping assay system used in the present study with Atp11a-Atp11c-double deficient cells as the host and NBD-labeled phospholipids as the substrate will clarify whether these mutations are functionally null mutations or mutations that change substrate specificity.

Materials and Methods

Study Approval and Informed Consent.

The present study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee and the Institutional Review Boards of Antwerp University Hospital/the University of Antwerp, University of Toronto, and Duke University School of Medicine. Informed consent was obtained from all the families according to protocols approved by local Institutional Review Boards and human research ethics committees.

Cell Lines.

W3 cells are mouse WR19L (ATCC: TIB-52) lymphoblast transformants expressing mouse Fas (50). DKO (D31) is a W3 derivative deficient in the Atp11a and Atp11c genes (17). WR19L and DKO cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS). Human HEK293T cells (ATCC: CRL-3216) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FCS.

Cells were transformed as previously described (51). Briefly, pMXs-puro vectors harboring Flag- or EGFP-tagged cDNA were introduced together with pGag-pol-IRES-bsr, pCMV-VSV-G, and pAdvantage into HEK293T cells by lipofection using Fugene 6 (Promega). Forty-eight h after transfection, the retrovirus in the culture medium was concentrated 10-fold by centrifugation at 6,000 × g at 4 °C for 16 h and then suspended in the medium. Target cells were infected with the concentrated virus, and transformants were selected by culturing them in the medium containing 1 µg/mL puromycin. In some cases, the transformant expressing the EGFP-tagged protein was sorted via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) Aria II (BD Biosciences).

Recombinant RFP-NT-Lys and mCherry-D4.

Nontoxic Lysenin (NT-Lys), an SM-binding protein (31), and D4, a cholesterol-binding protein (32), were produced in Escherichia coli, as previously described (52). In brief, the plasmids pCold I-His6-mRFP-NT-Lys and pCold I-His6-mCherry-D4, carrying the DNA fragment coding for His6-mRFP-NT-Lys or His6-mCherry-D4 under the cold-shock promoter in the pCold DNA I vector (TaKaRa, 3361), were introduced into chaperone-competent E. coli pGro7/BL21 (TaKaRa, 9122) and cultured at 37 °C in the presence of 20 µg/mL chloramphenicol and 100 µg/mL ampicillin. Recombinant proteins were produced by culturing at 15 °C for 24 h in the presence of 0.8 mg/mL L-arabinose and 0.2 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 6,000× g for 10 min, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and suspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM dithiothreitol, and protease-inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai, 04080-11). Bacteria were disrupted at 4 °C by sonication (Branson digital Sonifer SFX 250, EMERSON) at power 4, duty 50% for 1 min with 4 on–off cycles. Unbroken cells were removed by centrifugation at 20,000× g at 4 °C for 15 min, and supernatants were incubated at 4 °C overnight with Ni-NTA superflow (Qiagen). Bound proteins were then eluted with a linear gradient of 10 to 500 mM imidazole in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 140 mM NaCl. Fractions containing RFP-NT-Lys or mCherry-D4 were pooled, concentrated using Amicon Ultra 10 K (Millipore, 801024), and dialyzed against PBS at 4 °C.

Flippase Assay.

Flippase activity was assessed according to previously described methods (14, 30, 52). In brief, 3 × 105 cells were incubated at 20 °C with 1 µM NBD-conjugated lipids in 300 µL Hanks’ Balanced Salts Solution (HBSS) containing 1 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM CaCl2 (HBSS++). Aliquots of cells (5 × 104 cells) were transferred to 50 µL of HBSS++ containing 5 mg/mL fatty acid-free BSA to remove nonincorporated NBD-linked phospholipids and 10 nM SYTOX™-Blue or SYTOX™-Red Dead Cell Stain (ThermoFisher S11348, S34859), and then analyzed by FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences). Live cells in the SYTOX™-negative population were selected following Forward Scatter/Side Scatter singlet gating. The incorporation of NBD-phospholipids at each time point was represented as Fluorescein-5-isothiocyanate fluorescence after subtracting fluorescence at time 0. The rates of lipid incorporation were calculated based on mean fluorescence units per min and were compared to WT flippase.

MD Simulations.

To perform the MD simulation analysis, we used the cryo-EM structure of the human ATP11C-CDC50A complex in the PtdSer-occluded E2-Pi state (PDB: 7BSU) (23). Hydrogen atoms were generated and energetically optimized using the CHARMM program (v. 40b) (53). The positions of all nonhydrogen atoms were fixed, and all titratable groups were maintained in their standard protonation state at pH 7. The protein was embedded in a lipid bilayer of approximately 1,000 1-Palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine molecules and soaked in ~180,000 water molecules. Fifteen chloride ions were added to the model using the CHARMM-GUI program (54).

The following steps were performed after structural optimization with positional restraints on heavy atoms of the WT ATP11C–CDC50A PtdSer assembly. 1) Heating and initial equilibration: The system was heated from 0.1 to 300 K over 5.5 ps with time intervals of 0.01 fs. It was then equilibrated at 300 K for 1 ns with time intervals of 0.5 fs. 2) Positional restraints on protein backbone: The same heating and equilibration procedure was repeated with positional restraints on the heavy atoms of the protein backbone. 3) Equilibration without positional restraints: The system was equilibrated at 300 K for 60 ns with time intervals of 1.5 fs without positional restraints on any atoms.

For the ATP11C–CDC50A PtdCho system, initial structures were modeled based on the ATP11C–CDC50A–PtdSer structure, and the equilibration procedures (steps 2 and 3) were repeated. E109G or S394L mutant ATP11C–CDC50A lipid structures were modeled based on the WT ATP11C–CDC50A–lipid structures, and the same equilibration steps were repeated. All equilibrating simulations were conducted using the NAnoscale Molecular Dynamics program program (v. 2.12) (55). The SHAKE algorithm (56) was used for hydrogen constraints with a time step of 1.5 fs where applicable. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the Langevin thermostat and piston, respectively (57).

The pattern of electrostatic interactions at the binding moiety was analyzed using data from 8,000 conformations sampled in the last 60 ns of MD simulations. The tunnel/channel was investigated using the CAVER program (58) (https://www.caver.cz), which enabled the identification of access gates (entry and exit) and the determination of the channel’s spatial configuration.Typically, the channel length varies between conformations with phospholipids at the entry and exit sites. For clarity, the channel length is defined by the conformation with a phospholipid at the exit site in equilibrium after performing MD simulations, in which the “phosphate” of phospholipids forms an H-bond with E109. The channel axis was defined using the conformation with a phospholipid at the exit site. The entry site was tentatively defined as the side-chain O position of S358 due to the absence of the phospholipid at the entry site in the atomic coordinates, as this agrees with our previous result for the phospholipid binding site. The channel length was determined as the distance between the entry and exit sites. However, it should be considered an approximate guideline rather than a quantitative value, as it can be significantly influenced by several factors, such as the orientation of residues at the bottleneck (e.g., V357).

Staining of SM and Cholesterol on the Cell Surface.

To detect SM and cholesterol on the cell surface, 2 × 105 cells were collected via centrifugation, washed twice with HBSS++, and resuspended in 100 µL of HBSS++. RFP-NT-Lys or mCherry-D4 (100 µL of 20 µg/mL in HBSS++) was added to cells with SYTOX™-Blue at a final concentration of 250 nM. Samples were incubated on ice for 15 min and analyzed by flow cytometry using FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences).

SMase Cytotoxicity Assay.

5 × 104 cells were washed with serum-free DMEM prewarmed at room temperature and resuspended in 50 µL of serum-free DMEM in a 96-well plate. An equal volume of Staphylococcus aureus neutral SMase (Sigma S8633-50UN) in DMEM was added to cells to a final concentration of 2.0 to 8.0 × 10−3 U/mL and cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. After removing cells by centrifugation, the amount of LDH in the supernatant was quantitated using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche 11644793001).

Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS)-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE) and Western Blotting.

Cells were lysed by incubating at 4 °C for 2 h in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and a cocktail of protease inhibitors (Nacalai: 03969-21). After removing debris via centrifugation at 15,000× g at 4 °C for 30 min, the supernatant was collected as the cell lysate, and its protein concentration was measured using the Bicinchoninic Acid assay (ThermoFisher: 23225).

Aliquots of lysates (10 µg of protein) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a Polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. After incubation at 4 °C for 1 h in blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 5% nonfat skim milk), the membranes were incubated in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight with mouse mAb against SGMS1 (Santa Cruz; sc-166436), followed by an incubation with Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG2a (Abcam 97245). The membrane was also probed with HRP-conjugated anti-GFP (MBL; 598-7). Proteins recognized by the Ab were visualized using Western Lightning Plus ECL (PerkinElmer; NEL104001EA).

Confocal Microscopy.

Cells (4.2 × 105) were washed twice with HBSS++, resuspended in HBSS++ containing 5 µg/mL Hoechst nuclear staining dye (Invitrogen; 33342), and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. After being collected via centrifugation, cells were suspended in 200 µL HBSS++, transferred to glass-bottomed plates (Matsunami, D141400), and observed by confocal microscopy (Olympus Fluoview FV1000).

Statistical Analysis and Reproducibility.

Statistical analyses were performed with a one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to assess the significance of differences. Error bars indicate SD. At least three biological replicates were performed in all experiments presented as represented images in Figs. 2B and 3 A–E and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 B–E and S9 B–E. Experiments presented in Fig. 3E (SDS-PAGE) were performed twice. All other experiments were performed at least three times, as described in the legend of each figure.
