Isomorphisms of Birkhoff-James orthogonality on finite-dimensional C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebra Thanks: This work is supported in part by the Slovenian Research Agency (research program P1-0285 and research projects N1-0210, N1-0296 and J1-50000) and by the Serbian Ministry of Education, Science and Technological Development through Faculty of Mathematics, University of Belgrade.

Abstract
. We classify bijective maps which strongly preserve Birkhoff-James orthogonality on a finite-dimensional complex C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. It is shown that those maps are close to being real-linear isometries whose structure is also determined.

Primary: 46L05, 47B49, Secondary: 46B20, 46B80

1.
Introduction

Two vectors in an inner product space are orthogonal if their inner product is zero. There are (infinitely) many equivalent ways to state this relation without explicitly using the inner product
[
1
, part (C) p. 3]
and many of them can be used to extend orthogonality and define it on general normed spaces. The drawback is that those definitions (which all agree on inner product spaces) typically are no longer equivalent on normed spaces where the norm is not induced by the inner product. Arguably, one of the best known and useful is the Birkhoff-James (BJ for short) orthogonality, whereby
x
is orthogonal to
y
(notation
x\perp y
) if for every scalar
\lambda
we have

\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|.

It has a nice geometrical interpretation in that the origin is the closest point to
x
on a line passing through
y
.

Presently, we are interested in preservers of this relation. The first results in this direction were obtained by Koldobsky
[
14
]
who classified linear maps which in one direction preserve BJ orthogonality on a real Banach space. This was later extended to normed real or complex spaces by Blanco-Turnšek
[
7
]
. The result is in both cases the same: such linear maps are scalar multiples of isometry. We refer to Wójcik
[
33
]
for a simple proof as well as further generalization to additive BJ preservers on real space. We were informed by A. Peralta that, in a recent preprint
[
21
]
, they were able to classify additive BJ preservers also on complex normed spaces.

One can try to remove linearity assumption at the expense that BJ orthogonality is preserved strongly and bijectively. This was done in
[
7
]
for projective smooth Banach spaces and extended recently by Ilišević and Turnšek
[
13
]
, who showed that any BJ isomorphism between two smooth (possibly noncomplete) normed spaces
X
and
Y
is a (conjugate)linear isometry, multiplied by a scalar-valued function. However, if the norm is nonsmooth the results are different; for example, not every BJ isomorphism on abelian
C^{\ast}
-algebra
{\bf c}_{0}
(i.e., all sequences converging to
0
) is of this nice form; see Blanco-Turnšek
[
7
, Example 3.5]
for more. This counterexample works also for finite sequences as long as they contain at least three terms. It is perhaps a little surprising that, at least among finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebras, these are the only pathological examples (see our main Theorem
1.1
below). We remark in passing that even without linearity, BJ orthogonality alone knows a lot about normed spaces – it can compute the dimension
[
26
, Theorems 3.6 and 3.9]
and characterize smooth spaces up to (conjugate)linear isometry
[
28
,
3
]
and knows if a given
C^{\ast}
-algebra is abelian or not
[
29
, Theorem 3.5]
or if the norm is induced by the inner product
[
2
, 18.7]
.

Throughout we will regard a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}:=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})
to be embedded into
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
for
N:=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}
(every complex finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
has such decomposition, see a book by Goodearl
[
11
]
). Following
[
15
]
we call the sum of
1
-by-
1
blocks an
abelian summand of
\mathcal{A}
; this is
\ast
-isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, the space of column vectors equipped with supremum norm, for some
k\geq 0
. The center,
Z(\mathcal{A})
equals
\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}\mathbb{C}I_{n_{k}}
and is
\ast
-isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}^{\ell},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
. Thus, any positive-definite
P\in Z(\mathcal{A})
can be identified with an
\ell
-tuple of positive numbers
(r_{1},\dots,r_{\ell})
. Given
A\in\mathcal{A}
, recall that

M_{0}(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}(\|A\|^{2}I-A^{\ast}A)=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{N};\;\;\|Ax\|=\|A\|\cdot\|x\|\}

is the set of all vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
where
A
attains its norm (i.e., such that
\|Ax\|=\|A\|\cdot\|x\|
; here and throughout, a norm without an index will always refer to an Euclidean norm
\|x\|:=\sqrt{x^{\ast}x}
) see
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
and
[
15
]
for more details. By decomposing
A=A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{\ell}
we see that
M_{0}(A)=M_{0}(PA)
for some
P\in Z(\mathcal{A})
if and only if
A
and
PA
attain their norms at the same summands.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be the unimodular group. For brevity, we call a bijective map which strongly preserves BJ orthogonality to be a
BJ isomorphism
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
be a BJ isomorphism between complex
C^{\ast}
-algebras
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. If
\mathcal{A}
is finite-dimensional and has no abelian summand, then
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are isomorphic
C^{\ast}
-algebras and there exists a real-linear surjective isometry
\Psi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
, a function
\gamma\colon\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{T}}
and a central-valued function
P\colon\mathcal{A}\to Z(\mathcal{A})
with
P(X)>0
(positive-definite) and
M_{0}(X)=M_{0}(P(X)X)
such that

\Phi(X)=\Psi(\gamma(X)P(X)\cdot X),\qquad\ X\in\mathcal{A}.

Remark 1.1
.

The proof will establish even more: By identifying
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, for every
\Psi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
from Theorem
1.1
, there exists a
C^{\ast}
direct sum decomposition
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\Psi|_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(X_{1})

\displaystyle=U_{1}\sigma(X_{1})V_{1}^{\ast},

\displaystyle\Psi|_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}(X_{2})

\displaystyle=U_{2}\sigma((X_{2}^{\ast})^{T})V_{2}^{\ast}=U_{2}\sigma(\overline{X_{2}})V_{2}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\Psi|_{\mathcal{A}_{3}}(X_{3})

\displaystyle=U_{3}\sigma(X_{3}^{\ast})V_{3}^{\ast}

for some unitary
U_{i},V_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
and permutation
\sigma
which permutes the minimal ideals of the same dimension in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., if
A=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}A_{k}\in\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}M_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})
, then
\sigma(A):=\bigoplus A_{\sigma(k)}
and
n_{k}=n_{\sigma(k)}
.

When specializing to a simple finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra the result has a more compact form, interesting in it own:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\Phi\colon M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a BJ isomorphism. Then there exists a nonzero scalar-valued function
\gamma\colon M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
X\mapsto\gamma(X)\Phi(X)
is a (conjugate)linear isometry.

Remark 1.2
.

We will also show within the proof of Theorem
1.2
that the group of (conjugate)linear isometries on a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
n\geq 2
, is generated by

(1.1)

X\mapsto X^{\ast}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad X\mapsto UXV.

where
(U,V)
are either both unitary or both conjugate-linear unitary.

Actually, we will first prove Theorem
1.2
, and then Theorem
1.1
will follow as a corollary, with the help of some of our previous results
[
16
]
.

For a general finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
it was shown in
[
16
, Corollary 2.9]
that a BJ isomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
will map nonabelian summand of
\mathcal{A}
onto nonabelian summand of
\mathcal{B}
and will map abelian summand of
\mathcal{A}
onto abelian summand of
\mathcal{B}
. The restriction to nonabelian summand is covered within Theorem
1.1
; the restriction to abelian summand is covered by Tanaka
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
and takes the form

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto(\gamma_{1}(x)x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\gamma_{k}(x)x_{\sigma(k)})

for a fixed permutation
\sigma
and suitable scalars
\gamma_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{C}
. One should mention that
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
gives much more: it classifies BJ isomorphisms on a general (possibly nonunital) complex abelian
C^{\ast}
-algebra; they are weighted composition operators where weights are not constant but depend on the argument and might be discontinuous (consider, e.g., a weight
\omega_{f}(x):=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2x}\right)
associated to a function
f(x)=x\in{\mathcal{C}}([0,1])
and notice that
\omega_{f}(x)f(x)\in{\mathcal{C}}([0,1])
). Interestingly, this was proven by showing that BJ isomorphisms on abelian
C^{\ast}
-algebra are also isomorphisms of a strong BJ orthogonality, defined by
[
4
]
, and then using
[
25
, Theorem 5.2]
.

2.
Preliminaries

For a vector
x
in a normed space
\mathcal{V}
we let

x^{\bot}=\{y\in\mathcal{V};\;x\perp y\}\quad\text{ and }\quad{}^{\bot}\!x=\{y\in\mathcal{V};\;y\perp x\},

be its
outgoing
and
incoming neighbourhood
, respectively (throughout,
\perp
denotes BJ orthogonality). In general they are different; when
\dim\mathcal{V}\geq 3
, they always coincide if and only if the norm is induced by the inner product (see
[
2
, 18.7]
), in which case they further coincide with the usual orthogonal complement. Also,
x
is
left-symmetric
if
x\perp y
implies
y\perp x
, or equivalently, if
x^{\bot}\subseteq{}^{\bot}\!x
. For
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, let

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}:=\{x\in\mathcal{S};\;\;x^{\bot}\cap{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{}^{\bot}\!x\cap{\mathcal{S}}\}.

denote the set of all left-symmetric vectors relative to
\mathcal{S}
.
In particular, if
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}
, then
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of all left-symmetric vectors.

It is known that the whole algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
contains no left-symmetric points different from zero; see, e.g.,
[
31
, Corollary 3.4]
. However, there might exist a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
within which we do find nontrivial relative left-symmetric points; an example is the subspace of all diagonal matrices, which is an abelian
C^{\ast}
-algebra and where every rank-one matrix is left-symmetric (see
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
).
Within the present section we determine the maximal possible dimension of such subspace
\mathcal{V}
, obtained as intersections of common outgoing neighborhoods of smooth elements (they will be called
outgoing spaces
) and also determine all relative left-symmetric points within them. This will be the starting point to prove Theorem
1.2
, which will then be used to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Our main tool is the following characterization of BJ orthogonality, valid for finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebras. This result was first obtained by Stampfli
[
24
]
for Hilbert-space operators with
A=I
, the identity; later it was generalized by Magajna
[
22
]
for general Hilbert-space operators. A different proof valid for complex or real matrices under the spectral norm was discovered by Bhatia-Šemrl
[
6
]
, see also
[
20
]
and
[
5
]
for more. Recall that a finite-dimensional
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
is tacitly embedded into
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
for
N=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}
; since the definition of BJ orthogonality of elements
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
requires only two-dimensional space spanned by
A,B
, we have that
A\perp B
in
\mathcal{A}
if and only if
A\perp B
regarded as elements of
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Denote also

\langle x,y\rangle:=y^{\ast}x
for
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
15
]
, Lemma 2.1)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{k}^{\ell}\mathcal{M}_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{C})
be a
C^{*}
-algebra and
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
. Then,
B\in A^{\bot}
if and only if
\langle Ax,Bx\rangle=0
for some normalized
x\in M_{0}(A)
. Moreover, if
\|A_{i}\|<\max\{\|A_{k}\|;\;\;1\leq k\leq\ell\}=\|A\|
, then

A^{\bot}=\big(A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{i-1}\oplus 0_{n_{i}}\oplus A_{i+1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{\ell}\big)^{\bot}.

We apply this on rank-one
A=xy^{\ast}
, which attains its norm only on the scalar multiples of
y
. Hence,
A\perp B
if and only if
0=\langle Ay,By\rangle=\langle x,By\rangle:=(By)^{\ast}x=y^{\ast}B^{\ast}x
. By conjugating we thus get

(2.1)

(xy^{\ast})^{\bot}=\{X;\;\;x^{\ast}Xy=0\}.

Definition 2.1
.

We call
A\in\mathcal{A}
to be
smooth
if there does not exist
B\in\mathcal{A}
such that
B^{\bot}\subsetneq A^{\bot}
.

By
[
15
, Lemma 2.5]
this definition agrees with the classical definition of smoothness, i.e., of having a unique supporting functional. Moreover, it was proved in
[
15
, lemmata 2.5 and 2.6]
that
A=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}A_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
is smooth if and only if
there exists exactly one index
j
such that
\|A_{j}\|=\|A\|
and
\dim M_{0}(A_{j})=1
. Moreover, if
A
is smooth and
x=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}x_{k}\in M_{0}(A)
, then
x_{k}=0
for all
k\neq j
and

(2.2)

A^{\bot}=\big(0\oplus((A_{j}x_{j})x_{j}^{*})\oplus 0\big)^{\bot}.

For example,
A=0\oplus E_{st}^{j}\oplus 0
are smooth elements for all matrix units
E_{st}^{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})
.

3.
Maximal subspaces of relative left-symmetry

Throughout,
\mathbb{C}^{n}
will denote the Euclidean
n
-dimensional complex normed space of column vectors (i.e.,
n
-by-
1
matrices) with
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
being its standard basis. Hence, on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the BJ orthogonality coincides with the usual one.

A well-known singular value decomposition (SVD for short) says that each complex matrix
A
can be written as

A=\sigma_{1}(A)x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}+\dots+\sigma_{n}(A)x_{n}y_{n}^{\ast}
, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
are two orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}(A)\geq 0
are the singular values of
A
.

We start with a technical lemma which we will use to prove that some subspaces of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

cannot have
nontrivial left-symmetricity.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
, let
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and let
B=xe_{n}^{\ast}+e_{n}y^{\ast}
have nonzero entries at positions
(1n),(n1)
, and
(nn)
. Then its SVD has two summands and both have nonzero
(11)
entry. Moreover,
\sigma_{1}(B)>\sigma_{2}(B)
.

Proof.

Clearly,
B
is of rank-two so its SVD,

B=\sigma_{1}u_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}u_{2}v_{2}^{\ast}

has two summands.

If both
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
have a zero as a first component, then
B
has
0
on position
(1n)
, which is contradictory. Similar conclusion holds for
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, for otherwise the
(n1)
.position of
B
would vanish. So, at least one among
u_{1},u_{2}
is not orthogonal to
e_{1}
and the same is true for
v_{1},v_{2}
.

Assume, contrary to the claim, that the
(11)
entry of
\sigma_{1}u_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}
or of
\sigma_{2}u_{2}v_{2}^{\ast}
is zero. Then, due to
e_{1}^{\ast}Be_{1}=B_{11}=0
, both of them will have
(11)
entry equal to zero. This is possible only if

e_{1}^{\ast}u_{1}=e_{1}^{\ast}v_{2}=0\quad\hbox{ and }\quad e_{1}^{\ast}v_{1},e_{1}^{\ast}u_{2}\neq 0,

or, vice-versa,

e_{1}^{\ast}u_{2}=e_{1}^{\ast}v_{1}=0\quad\hbox{ and }\quad e_{1}^{\ast}v_{2},e_{1}^{\ast}u_{1}\neq 0.

In the former case, notice that
\sigma_{1}u_{1}(v_{1}^{\ast}e_{1})=(\sigma_{1}u_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}u_{2}v_{2}^{\ast})e_{1}=Be_{1}\in\mathbb{C}e_{n}\setminus\{0\}
. It follows that
u_{1}
is parallel to
e_{n}
and by transferring the appropriate scalar from
u_{1}
to
v_{1}
we achieve that
u_{1}=e_{n}
. Thus, comparing zero entries in
e_{n}y^{\ast}+xe_{n}^{\ast}=\sigma_{1}e_{n}v_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}u_{2}v_{2}^{\ast}
and keeping in mind that
u_{2}
is orthogonal to
u_{1}=e_{n}
and
e_{1}^{*}u_{2}\neq 0
, we see that
v_{2}
is parallel to
e_{n}
and again we can assume
v_{2}=e_{n}
. But then,
v_{1}\in v_{2}^{\bot}=\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\oplus 0
and
u_{2}\in u_{1}^{\bot}=\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\oplus 0
, so
e_{n}^{\ast}Be_{n}=0
, a contradiction. Thus, the former case is impossible.
The latter case is analogously contradictory.

Lastly,
\sigma_{1}(B)=\sigma_{2}(B)
if and only if
B
is a scalar multiple of a partial isometry, or equivalently (by
[
23
, p. 3]
),
B^{\ast}B
is a multiple of a projection. We easily compute

(3.1)

B^{\ast}B=(e_{n}x^{\ast}+ye_{n}^{\ast})(xe_{n}^{\ast}+e_{n}y^{\ast})=\|x\|^{2}e_{n}e_{n}^{\ast}+yy^{\ast}+(x^{\ast}e_{n})e_{n}y^{\ast}+(e_{n}^{\ast}x)ye_{n}^{\ast}.

Now,

B=xe_{n}^{\ast}+e_{n}y^{\ast}=x^{\prime}e_{n}^{\ast}+e_{n}(y^{\prime})^{\ast}

where
x^{\prime}=x+(y^{\ast}e_{n})e_{n}
and
y^{\prime}=y-(e_{n}^{\ast}y)e_{n}\perp e_{n}
. This way we can achieve that in (
3.1
),
y
is perpendicular to
e_{n}
. Then, there exists a unitary
W
with
(W^{\ast}y,W^{\ast}e_{n})=(\|y\|e_{1},e_{2})
and hence, conjugating (
3.1
) with
W
simplifies into

W^{\ast}B^{\ast}BW=\begin{pmatrix}\|y\|^{2}&\|y\|(e_{n}^{\ast}x)\\
\|y\|(x^{\ast}e_{n})&\|x\|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\oplus 0_{n-2}.

By the assumptions,
e_{n}^{\ast}x=B_{nn}\neq 0
, so the upper
2
-by-
2
block of
W^{\ast}B^{\ast}BW
is not a scalar matrix. Consequently,
\sigma_{1}(B)^{2}\neq\sigma_{2}(B)^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
be two collections of
k\leq n-1
normalized vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Define

\mathcal{V}:=\{X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C});\;\;v_{1}^{\ast}Xu_{1}=\dots=v_{k}^{\ast}Xu_{k}=0\}.

If a nonzero
A\in\mathcal{V}
is left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A\in\{1,n\}
.

Proof.

There is nothing to do if
n=2
, so we assume
n\geq 3
. Suppose otherwise that
2\leq\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A\leq n-1
and let

A=\sigma_{1}x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}y_{2}^{\ast}+\dots+\sigma_{n}x_{n}y_{n}^{\ast}

be its SVD (so
\sigma_{2}>0
). Assume there exist a nonzero

x\in(v_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\bot})\ominus x_{2}^{\bot}.

Then,
B:=xy_{2}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}
and, by Proposition
2.1
(see also (
2.1
)), clearly
A\perp B\not\perp A
. Likewise, if there exists a nonzero
y\in(u_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap u_{k}^{\bot})\ominus y_{2}^{\bot}
, then
B:=x_{2}y^{\ast}
has this property. In neither case
A
is left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
. Similarly we argue if
\sigma_{3}>0
and there exists a nonzero
x\in(v_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\bot})\ominus x_{3}^{\bot}
(then we form
B=xy_{3}^{\ast}
) or a nonzero
y\in(u_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap u_{k}^{\bot})\ominus y_{3}^{\bot}
(then we form
B=x_{3}y^{\ast}
), etc.

It remains to consider the case when

v_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\bot}\subseteq x_{2}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap x_{r}^{\bot}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad u_{1}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap u_{k}^{\bot}\subseteq y_{2}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap y_{r}^{\bot}

with
r:=\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A
.
By taking the orthogonal complements on both sides, these are equivalent to

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x_{2},\dots,x_{r}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{y_{2},\dots,y_{r}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}.

We now perform a series of simplifications in the form of transformations
X\mapsto\kappa UXV^{\ast}
for unitary
U,V
and
\kappa>0
; these are scalar multiples of norm isometries so preserve Birkhoff-James orthogonality. As such, the newly obtained
\kappa UAV^{\ast}\in U\mathcal{V}V^{\ast}
will remain to be left-symmetric relative to a newly obtained space
U\mathcal{V}V^{\ast}
. For simplicity, and with a slight abuse of notation, we retain the original symbols.

To start with, by applying transformation
X\mapsto\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}X
we achieve that
\sigma_{1}=1
. Next, the two sets on the right of (
3.2
) are at most
k\leq n-1
dimensional, so there exist unitary
U
and
V
such that

(3.3)

\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{Vv_{1},\dots,Vv_{k}\},\,\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{Uu_{1},\dots,Uy_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\oplus 0.

Notice that
v^{\ast}Xu=(Vv)^{\ast}(VXU^{\ast})(Uu)
. With this in mind, by applying the transformation
X\mapsto VXU^{\ast}

we may replace
A\in\mathcal{V}
by
VAU^{\ast}\in V\mathcal{V}U^{\ast}
(and replace
u_{i},v_{i}
with
Uu_{i}
and
Vv_{i}
, respectively). By a slight abuse of notation, we continue using the original symbols to achieve that (
3.3
) holds with
U=V=I
.

We may further apply unitaries of the form
U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 1
to achieve that
U^{\prime}x_{i}=V^{\prime}y_{i}=e_{i-1}
for
i=2,\dots,r
. We denote the matrix
U^{\prime}A(V^{\prime})^{\ast}
again by
A
, the space
U^{\prime}\mathcal{V}(V^{\prime})^{\ast}
again by
\mathcal{V}
and
U^{\prime}u_{i},V^{\prime}v_{i},U^{\prime}x_{i},V^{\prime}y_{i}
again by
u_{i},v_{i},x_{i},y_{i}
. Recall that
x_{1}
is orthogonal to every member of
\{x_{2},\dots,x_{r}\}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r-1}\}
, so there further exists a unitary
U
which fixes

and maps

into

. By transformation

we achieve that

; likewise, by transforming with
X\mapsto XV^{\ast}
, we can achieve

. This way we achieve that

and that every
B
of the form

belongs to
\mathcal{V}
(because
u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\oplus 0
).
Therefore, if
c:=c_{x}
and
s:=s_{x}
are both nonzero, one defines

\displaystyle B_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}E_{1n}-\bar{s}E_{(n-1)n}\pm\bar{s}E_{n1}+\bar{c}E_{nn}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+|c|}\left(\frac{\bar{c}|c|e_{1}-|c|\bar{s}e_{n-1}+\bar{c}e_{n}}{\bar{c}\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\pm\bar{c}se_{1}+(|c|^{2}+|c|)e_{n}}{\bar{c}\sqrt{2(1+|c|)}}\right)^{\ast}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{}+\sqrt{1-|c|}\left(\frac{-\bar{c}|c|e_{1}+|c|\bar{s}e_{n-1}+\bar{c}e_{n}}{\bar{c}\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\pm\bar{c}se_{1}+(|c|^{2}-|c|)e_{n}}{\bar{c}\sqrt{2(1-|c|)}}\right)^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}

and checks that the right hand side is a SVD for

.
Since

achieves its norm on

and maps it into

so

. On the other hand,

and maps it into

\sigma_{2}c\neq 0

is not left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
.

It only remains to consider the case when
c_{x}s_{x}=0
. If
c_{y}s_{y}\neq 0
we apply an isometry
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
to
A\in\mathcal{V}
. Then,

A^{\ast}=\left(\sum_{i=2}^{r}\sigma_{i}E_{(i-1)(i-1)}\right)+y_{1}x_{1}^{\ast}

and we reduced
to the just considered case.

If
c_{x}s_{x}=c_{y}s_{y}=0
, then

x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}\{E_{(n-1)(n-1)},E_{(n-1)n},E_{n(n-1)},E_{nn}\}.

If now
x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}
equals one of the last three matrices, then
x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}
and since also
A\in\mathcal{V}
we get that
\hat{B}:=A-x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}=\sigma_{2}E_{11}+\dots+\sigma_{r}E_{(r-1)(r-1)}\in\mathcal{V}
and
A\perp\hat{B}
but
\hat{B}\not\perp A
.

Lastly, if
x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}E_{(n-1)(n-1)}
, then
A\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 0
, it achieves its norm on
e_{n-1}
and maps it into itself. Here we can choose
B=E_{1n}+E_{n1}+E_{nn}\in\mathcal{V}
and by Lemma

again conclude that

.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
k,u_{i},v_{j}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be as in Lemma
3.2
.
If
A\in\mathcal{V}
has
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A>k
, then it is not left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
. Specially, there are no full-rank left-symmetric points in
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
r:=\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A\geq k+1
and let

A=\sigma_{1}x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}y_{2}^{\ast}+\dots+\sigma_{r}x_{r}y_{r}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}

be its SVD, so it attains its norm on
y_{1}
. We enlarge
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\}
to an orthonormal basis
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The subspace
v_{1}^{\perp}\cap v_{2}^{\perp}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
has codimension at most
k\leq n-1
so it does contain nonzero vectors. Pick, if possible, any such, say
v
, which is not orthogonal to
x_{i}
for some
2\leq i\leq r
. Then
B_{i}=vy_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
. Further,
A
attains its norm on
y_{1}
and
\langle Ay_{1},B_{i}y_{1}\rangle=\langle x_{1},0\rangle=0
, so
A\perp B_{i}
.

However,
B_{i}
attains its norm only on scalar multiples of
y_{i}
and
\langle B_{i}y_{i},Ay_{i}\rangle=\langle v,\sigma_{i}x_{i}\rangle\neq 0
giving
B_{i}\not\perp A
. Thus,
A
is not left-symmetric.

Now, suppose that every vector in
v_{1}^{\perp}\cap v_{2}^{\perp}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\perp}
is orthogonal to all
x_{i}
(
2\leq i\leq r
). Then,

v_{1}^{\perp}\cap v_{2}^{\perp}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\perp}\subseteq x_{2}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap x_{r}^{\bot}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x_{1},x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}.

The dimension of the space on the left is at least
n-k
. So we must have
n-k\leq 1+(n-r)
or
r\leq k+1
.

In the extremal case, when
r=k+1
, we have equality

v_{1}^{\perp}\cap v_{2}^{\perp}\cap\dots\cap v_{k}^{\perp}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{x_{1},x_{r+1},\dots,x_{n}\}

and then
x_{1}
is orthogonal to all
v_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
). This implies that
x_{1}y_{1}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
, and then
B:=\sigma_{2}x_{2}y_{2}^{\ast}+\dots+\sigma_{r}x_{r}y_{r}^{\ast}=A-\sigma_{1}x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}
and again
A\perp B\not\perp A
, so
A
is not relative left-symmetric.

Indeed, the only possibility for
A\in\mathcal{V}
to be left-symmetric within
\mathcal{V}
is that its rank is at most
k\leq n-1
. In particular,
A
cannot be invertible.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
k
,
u_{i},v_{j}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be as in Lemma
3.2
. Assume, in addition, that
\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}\geq 2
and
\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}\geq 2
. Then,
\mathcal{V}
contains no rank-one matrix
A
which would be left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

There is nothing to do if
n=2
, so we assume
n\geq 3
. Assume otherwise that a normalized rank-one matrix
A=xy^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}
is left-symmetric relative to
\mathcal{V}
. Without loss of generality,
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
. We will now apply a sequence of isometries of the form
X\mapsto UXV^{\ast}
and
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
where
U,V
are unitary, on an incidence pair
A\in\mathcal{V}
; the newly obtained
A
will clearly remain to be left-symmetric relative to a newly obtained subspace
\mathcal{V}
.

To start, recall that by the assumptions,
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
span at least two-dimensional subspace, so we can reorder them to achieve that
u_{k}
and
x
are linearly independent.
If
x,u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
spans a proper
r
-dimensional subspace, we choose unitary
U
so that
Ux=e_{1}
and
Uu_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}\oplus 0_{n-r}
for each
i
. Otherwise,
x,u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
forms a basis, and then we recursively choose unitaries
U_{0},\dots U_{k}
so that
U_{0}x=e_{1}
and
U_{i}
fixes
e_{1}\dots,e_{i}
(hence also fixes the vectors
u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1}
obtained previously during the process) and maps
u_{i}
into vector
U_{i}u_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{i+1}\oplus 0_{n-i-1}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. By the abuse of notation we still denote the obtained vectors by
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
. Likewise we recursively change
y,v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
. This way we achieve that

(3.4)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=E_{11}

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{u}}\oplus 0_{n-r_{u}};\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,k;

\displaystyle r_{u}:=\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{1},u_{1},\dots,,u_{k}\}

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}^{i+1}\oplus 0_{n-i-1};\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,k

\displaystyle\hbox{if $\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{1},u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}=\mathbb{C}^{n}$}

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{v}}\oplus 0_{n-r_{v}};\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,k;

\displaystyle r_{v}:=\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{1},v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}^{i+1}\oplus 0_{n-i-1};\qquad

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,k

\displaystyle\hbox{if $\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{1},v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}=\mathbb{C}^{n}$}.

Notice also that, due to our initial reindexation,
u_{k}
and

are both linearly independent of
e_{1}
.
We now consider three possibilities:

Case 1.

v_{k}\notin\mathbb{C}e_{n}
. Then, since
n\geq 3
,
there exists a nonzero scalar
\alpha
and a vector

(3.5)

x^{\prime}\perp\{e_{1},e_{n}\}

so that

(we can always take

except if

). Due to
u_{k}\notin\mathbb{C}e_{1}
there also exists a vector

with
u_{k}^{\ast}(e_{1}+y^{\prime})=0
.
Hence,

belongs to
\mathcal{V}
, because
u_{i}^{\ast}e_{n}=v_{i}^{\ast}e_{n}=0
if

, and clearly
v_{k}^{\ast}Bu_{k}=0
. Also,

because

achieves its norm on

and

. However, by Lemma
3.1
,

so

achieves its norm on a unique vector

,

.

Case 2.

v_{k}\in\mathbb{C}e_{n}
, but
u_{k}\notin\mathbb{C}e_{n}
. In this case there exists

and a nonzero

so that

and satisfies

.

Case 3.

and

inside

. If at least one among

, respectively,

) and in addition

.

∎

We next give the first examples of a maximal-dimensional outgoing space that does contain relative left-symmetricity. Notice that a matrix
X
has zero at position
(1i)
if and only if

; thus the spaces

which appear in Lemma below are specializations of spaces from Lemma

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\mathcal{V}_{p}\subseteq M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a subspace of all matrices with zeros at positions
(1p),\dots,(1n)
. If
p\geq 3
, then
\mathcal{V}
contains no nonzero left-symmetric points. If
p=2
, then there exist nonzero left-symmetric points in
\mathcal{V}
and all are of rank-one. In addition, if
n\geq 3
, then
A\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
is left-symmetric if and only if

A\in\mathbb{C}E_{11}
.

Remark 3.1
.

If
n=2
, then, with additional arguments, one can show that the set of relative left-symmetric points in
\mathcal{V}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbb{C}&0\\
\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{C}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\subseteq M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
equals
\mathbb{C}E_{11}\cup\mathbb{C}E_{21}\cup\mathbb{C}E_{22}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Let a nonzero
A\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
, with SVD

A=\sigma_{1}x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}y_{2}^{\ast}+\dots+\sigma_{n}x_{n}y_{n}^{\ast};\qquad\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0

be left-symmetric. Clearly
A
attains its norm on
y_{1}
and maps it into
Ay_{1}=\sigma_{1}x_{1}
.

Assume first
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A\geq 2
. If
x_{2}^{\prime}
, the projection of
x_{2}
to
\mathrm{span}\{e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
is nonzero, then

satisfies

, a contradiction. If
x_{2}^{\prime}=0
, i.e., if
x_{2}\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}
, then
x_{1},x_{3},\dots,x_{n}
are orthogonal to

and hence

. Then also

which again satisfies

, a contradiction.

Thus,
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A=1
and

.
We proceed under the assumption that

. Decompose

and assume next that

is nonzero.

Then, the compression

belongs

). Therefore, there exists

with

. Notice that the image,

and notice that
A
and

. Then,

, a contradiction.

Thus,

and

. If now

∎

.

Let
n\geq 2
and let

be smooth points. If

. If

, and

, then the set of relative left-symmetric points in

equals

for

or
A=v_{n}u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{V}
, respectively. Same holds if

, but we have additional left-symmetric matrices, all of rank-one.

By (

) and (

) there exist rank-one

. Assume

. By lemmata

,

We conclude with a general lemma that describes the linear dependence of two matrices using BJ orthogonality. Define

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}=\{A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C});\;\;\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\,A=1\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
A,B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
Then the following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathbb{C}A=\mathbb{C}B
.

(ii)

({}^{\bot}\!A)\cap\mathcal{R}_{1}=({}^{\bot}\!B)\cap\mathcal{R}_{1}
.

Proof.

We only prove the nontrivial part. Notice that rank-one
R=xy^{\ast}
belongs to
{}^{\bot}X
if and only if
R\perp X
, and since
R
attains its norm only on scalar multiples of
y
, this is further equivalent to
x^{\ast}Xy=0
(c.f. (
2.1
)).

The condition (ii) is, hence, equivalent to
x^{\ast}Ay=0\Longleftrightarrow x^{\ast}By=0
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It is easily seen that this is further equivalent to the fact that
Ay
and
By
are linearly dependent vectors for every
y
, that is,
A,B
are locally dependent matrices. Now, if
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(A)\geq 2
or if
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(B)\geq 2
, then it is well known, and easy to see that (i) holds (c.f.
[
12
, Lemma 2.2]
).

If
A=uv^{\ast}
and
B=zw^{\ast}
are both of rank-one, then, there exist
x
not perpendicular to
v
nor to
w
. So,
Ax
and
Bx
are both nonzero. Being locally dependent, implies, after transferring the appropriate scalars to the second factor, that
u=z
.
If
v,w
are linearly independent, there would exist
y
with
v^{\ast}y\neq 0
but
w^{\ast}y=0
, and hence
uy^{\ast}
would be rank-one in
{}^{\bot}\!A
but not in
{}^{\bot}\!B
, a contradiction. So,
u=z
and
\mathbb{C}v=\mathbb{C}w
.

Finally,
A=0
if and only if
{}^{\bot}\!A\cap{\mathcal{R}}_{1}={\mathcal{R}}_{1}
, so (ii) implies
B=0
.
∎

4.
BJ isomorphisms on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),n\geq 3

Within this and the next section, we will classify BJ isomorphisms
\Phi\colon M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have
A=0
if and only if
A\perp A
.

Proof.

Trivial verification.
∎

It follows easily that every (possibly nonbijective) map between two normed spaces, which strongly preserves BJ orthogonality, maps
0
into
0
and maps nonzero elements to nonzero elements. We will constantly rely on this fact.

We now characterize rank-one matrices in terms of BJ orthogonality alone:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. The following are equivalent for a nonzero
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

(
i)

\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A=1
.

(
ii)

There exist
n-1
smooth elements
A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
such that
A
is left-symmetric relative to
\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}A_{i}^{\bot}
.

Proof.

(
i
)
\Longrightarrow
(
ii
). Write
A=xy^{\ast}
, enlarge
x_{1}:=x
with
x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
to orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and define
A_{i}:=x_{i}y^{\ast},2\leq i\leq n
. Note that
A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
are all smooth (see text after Definition
2.1
). By applying BJ isomorphism, induced by a linear isometry
X\mapsto\kappa UXV^{\ast}
for suitably chosen unitaries
U,V
and scalar
\kappa>0
we can assume that
A=E_{11}
and
A_{i}=E_{1i}
for all
2\leq i\leq n
. By (
2.1
),
A_{i}^{\bot}=\{X;\;\;e_{1}^{\ast}Xe_{i}=0\}
. Then,
A=E_{11}\in\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}E_{1i}^{\bot}=\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}A_{i}^{\bot}=\{X;\;\;e_{1}^{\ast}Xe_{2}=\dots=e_{1}^{\ast}Xe_{n}=0\}
and, by Lemma
3.5
(and its Remark
3.1
if
n=2
), it is left-symmetric relative to
\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}A_{i}^{\bot}
.

(
ii
)
\Longrightarrow
(
i
) By (
2.2
), for every smooth element
X
, there exists rank-one
R=xy^{\ast}
such that
R^{\bot}=X^{\bot}
. Hence, we can assume that every smooth
A_{i}
is of rank-one and write it as

A_{i}=u_{i}v_{i}^{\ast}.

From lemmata
3.2
–
3.4
we know that there is no nonzero left-symmetric point inside
\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}A_{i}^{\bot}
if both
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{2},\dots,u_{n}\}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
are at least two-dimensional. By applying BJ isomorphism induced by conjugate linear isometry
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
and absorbing a appropriate scalars to
u_{i}
’s, we can assume that
v_{i}=v
for all
2\leq i\leq n
. By applying a linear isometry
X\mapsto UXV^{\ast}
, we can achieve
v=e_{1}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{2},\dots,u_{n}\}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{p},\dots,e_{n}\}
for some
p\geq 2
(actually,
p=n-\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{2},\dots,u_{n}\}-1
). It is easy to see by (
2.1
) that then
\bigcap\limits_{i=2}^{n}A_{i}^{\bot}
coincides with the space of all matrices which vanish at positions
(1p),\dots,(1n)
. Since we assumed that
A
is left-symmetric in this intersection, by Lemma
3.5
(and its Remark
3.1
if
n=2
)
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A=1
(and
p=2
).
∎

We apply the previous two lemmata on bijective maps which strongly preserve BJ orthogonality. Recall that

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}=\{A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C});\;\;\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\,A=1\}

and recall that matrices
A,B
are adjacent if
A-B\in{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
, that is, if
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(A-B)=1
. Notice that if
A=xy^{\ast}
and
B=uv^{\ast}
are both of rank-one, then
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}(A-B)\leq 1
if and only if
x\in\mathbb{C}u
or
y\in\mathbb{C}v
(or both).

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
.
If
\Phi\colon M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a BJ isomorphism, then
\Phi({\mathcal{R}}_{1})={\mathcal{R}}_{1}
. Moreover,
\Phi|_{{\mathcal{R}}_{1}}
maps linearly dependent pairs to linearly dependent pairs and, if
n\geq 3
, strongly preserves adjacency.

Proof.

By Definition
2.1
, every BJ isomorphism
\Phi
maps the set of smooth elements bijectively onto itself. Also,
\Phi(A^{\bot})=\Phi(A)^{\bot}
, and, moreover, the implication
A\perp B\Rightarrow B\perp A
holds for every
B\in\mathcal{V}
, if and only if also
\Phi(A)\perp Y\Rightarrow Y\perp\Phi(A)
holds for every
Y\in\Phi(\mathcal{V})
(i.e.,
\Phi
maps relative left-symmetric points in
\mathcal{V}
to relative left-symmetric points in
\Phi(\mathcal{V})
). Thus, as an immediate corollary of lemmata
4.1
–
4.2
,
\Phi
strongly preservers rank-one matrices.

It remains to prove that the restriction
\Phi|_{{\mathcal{R}}_{1}}
preserves linear dependence and, if
n\geq 3
, adjacency. To see this, choose adjacent rank-one
R_{1}=x_{1}y_{1}^{\ast}
and
R_{2}=x_{2}y_{2}^{\ast}
. Then, either
\mathbb{C}x_{1}=\mathbb{C}x_{2}
or
\mathbb{C}y_{1}=\mathbb{C}y_{2}
. By
applying, if needed, the BJ isomorphism induced by isometry
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
(and absorbing the appropriate scalars to the other side of the product) we can achieve that

x_{1}=x_{2}=:x.

Now, if
R_{1}=\alpha R_{2}
for some nonzero scalar
\alpha
, then
R_{1}^{\bot}=R_{2}^{\bot}
, so also
\Phi(R_{1})^{\bot}=\Phi(R_{2})^{\bot}
. In view of (
2.1
) this implies that rank-one
\Phi(R_{1}),\Phi(R_{2})
are linearly dependent.

Otherwise,
\mathbb{C}y_{1}\neq\mathbb{C}y_{2}
. Here, we assume
n\geq 3
extend
y_{1},y_{2}
with
y_{3},\dots,y_{n}
to a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and form
n-2
additional pairwise adjacent smooth elements
R_{i}=xy_{i}^{\ast}
. By applying suitable isometry
X\mapsto UXV^{\ast}
, we can achieve that
x=e_{1}
and

\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n-1}\}=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{e_{2},e_{3},\dots,e_{n}\}.

By (
2.1
) we then have

\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}R_{i}^{\bot}=\{X;\;\;e_{1}^{\ast}Xe_{2}=\dots=e_{1}^{\ast}Xe_{n}=0\}

and, by Lemma
3.5
, this space contains a nonzero left-symmetric point, so the same must hold for
\Phi\left(\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}R_{i}^{\bot}\right)=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\Phi(R_{i})^{\bot}
. Moreover, since
R_{i}
are rank-one, then
\Phi(R_{i})=u_{i}v_{i}^{\ast}
are also rank-one by the first part of the proof. Hence, by lemmata
3.2
—
3.4
, either
\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n-1}\}=1
or
\dim\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}\}=1
. In either case,
\Phi(R_{1}),\dots,\Phi(R_{n-1})
must be pairwise adjacent. Since we assumed
n\geq 3
, then in particular,
\Phi(R_{1}),\Phi(R_{2})
are adjacent. By applying the same arguments to
\Phi^{-1}
we see that adjacency is strongly preserved.
∎

From here on, the arguments for the case
n\geq 3
are standard (see, e.g.,
[
32
,
12
]
); we provide them in full for the sake of completeness. We assume throughout the remainder of this section that
n\geq 3
and defer the case
n=2
(where we still need to verify that adjacency is preserved) to a separate section. Given a subset
Y\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, let us define:

\hbox{a row: }x\otimes Y:=\{xy^{\ast};\;\;y\in Y\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\hbox{ a column: }Y\otimes x:=\{yx^{\ast};\;\;y\in Y\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

For each nonzero
x
there exists a nonzero
y
such that
\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes y
.

Proof.

Notice that
x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes x
are maximal (in set-theoretical sense) subsets of
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of pairwise adjacent rank-one matrices, and every maximal subset of pairwise adjacent rank-one matrices is of this form. From here, the result follows easily from Corollary
4.3
.
∎

If needed, we can replace
\Phi
with a BJ isomorphism
X\mapsto\Phi(X)^{\ast}
to achieve that

(4.1)

\Phi(e_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}

for some nonzero
y
. In the sequel, we will always assume (
4.1
).

Lemma 4.5
.

For each nonzero
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
there exists a nonzero
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

We have two cases:

(i)
x\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. Then
x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}=e_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the conclusion of the lemma is a consequence of the normalization (
4.1
).

(ii)
x\notin\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. Then
(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})\cap(e_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=0
so
\Phi((x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})\cap(e_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}))=0
. By Lemma
4.4
, we know that
\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
is either a row or a column. It cannot be a column, because every row and column have a nonzero intersection.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For each nonzero
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
there exists a nonzero
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\Phi(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes x)=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes y
. In particular,
\Phi
maps rows onto rows and columns onto columns.

Proof.

A column
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes x
intersects every row in a rank-one matrix, while two rows intersect in a rank-one matrix if and only if they are equal. Hence, if
\Phi(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes x)=y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
would be a row, then, by Lemma
4.5
,
\Phi|_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
would have to map every row to
y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, contradicting its surjectivity.
∎

Let
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\{[x]:=\mathbb{C}x;\;\;x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\}
be a projective space. We call the one-dimensional space
[x]
a line for short (or also a projective point). Two lines,
[x]
and
[y]
, are perpendicular, denoted by
[x]\perp[y]
, if any of their representatives are perpendicular, i.e, if
y^{\ast}x=0
.

Notice that
[x_{1}]=[x_{2}]
if and only if
x_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}=x_{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Therefore, by lemmata
4.5
—
4.6
,
\Phi
induces a well-defined bijection
\phi\colon\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, given by

\phi([x])=[y]\quad\hbox{if}\quad\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Lemma 4.7
.

\phi
strongly preserves perpendicularity of lines.

Proof.

By (
2.1
),
[x]\perp[y]
if and only if every
R\in x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
and every
S\in y\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
are BJ orthogonal.
∎

It now follows from Faure’s paper,
[
8
, Corollary 4.5 and Lemma 4.2.]
that
\phi[x]=[Ux]
for some (conjugate)-linear isometry
U
. (We caution here that if
U
is conjugate-linear, then its adjoint is also conjugate-linear and defined by
\langle Ux,y\rangle=\langle U^{\ast}y,x\rangle
; we still have
U^{\ast}U=I
, however, now,
\langle Ux,Uy\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle
.) As such,

\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=(Ux)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n};\qquad x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

We next temporarily form a BJ isomorphism
\Psi\colon X\mapsto\Phi(X^{\ast})^{\ast}
. By Lemma
4.6
, it maps a row
x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
onto
\Psi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=\Phi(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes x)^{\ast}=(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes z)^{\ast}=z\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
for some
z=z_{x}
, i.e., into another row. We can, hence, repeat the above arguments on
\Psi
to see that there exists a (conjugate)linear isometry
V
such that
\Psi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})=(Vx)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Equivalently,
\Phi(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes y)=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes(Vy)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Phi(xy^{\ast})\in\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\cap\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes y)

\displaystyle=\Phi(x\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})\cap\Phi(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes y)

\displaystyle=(Ux)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\cap\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes(Vy)=\mathbb{C}(Ux)(Vy)^{\ast}.

It implies that

\Phi(xy^{\ast})=\gamma_{xy^{\ast}}(Ux)(Vy)^{\ast};\qquad\gamma_{xy^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

Assume
U
is conjugate-linear, while
V
is linear. Let

\hat{J}\colon x=\sum x_{i}e_{i}\mapsto\bar{x}:=\sum\bar{x}_{i}e_{i}

be the conjugation relative to the standard basis. Then,
U=(U\hat{J})\hat{J}
where
(U\hat{J})
is unitary. As such, the map
\hat{\Phi}\colon X\mapsto(U\hat{J})^{\ast}\Phi(X)V
is again a BJ isomorphism and its restriction to rank-one matrices satisfies

\hat{\Phi}(xy^{\ast})\dot{=}\bar{x}y^{\ast}

(for simplicity, we have denoted and will continue to denote the fact that
\mathbb{C}A=\mathbb{C}B
, i.e., that
A
and
B
are equal up to a scalar, by
A\dot{=}B
).
In particular,
\hat{\Phi}
fixes all
E_{ij}
(modulo scalars). Then,

\mathcal{V}:=\bigcap_{(ij)\notin\{(11),(12),(21),(22)\}}E_{ij}^{\bot}=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 0_{n-2}=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant for
\hat{\Phi}
. Conversely, if
\hat{\Phi}(B)\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\hat{\Phi}(E_{ij})\dot{=}E_{ij}\perp\hat{\Phi}(B)
, so
E_{ij}\perp B
for
i,j\geq 3
giving
B\in\mathcal{V}
. Hence,
\Phi|_{\mathcal{V}}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
is a BJ isomorphism. We will treat such maps separately in a self-inclusive section devoted to the case
n=2
. The result (see Lemma
5.6
below) is that
\Phi|_{\mathcal{V}}(xy^{\ast})\dot{=}\phi(x)\phi(y)^{\ast}

for some bijection
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
. This clearly contradicts
\hat{\Phi}(xy^{\ast})\dot{=}\bar{x}y^{\ast}
.

Hence, if
U
is conjugate-linear, then
V
is also; likewise one can show the converse. As such,
(Ux)(Vy)^{\ast}=U(xy^{\ast})V^{\ast}
.

Notice that
X\mapsto U^{\ast}XV
is a (conjugate)linear isometry on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
hence it induces a BJ isomorphism. As such, we can replace
\Phi
by a map
X\mapsto U^{\ast}\Phi(X)V
to achieve that rank-one matrices are fixed, modulo scalar multiples.

(4.2)

\Phi(xy^{\ast})=\gamma_{xy^{\ast}}xy^{\ast};\qquad xy^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{1}.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
for
n\geq 3
.

We can find (conjugate)linear isometries
U,V
such that the map
X\mapsto U^{\ast}\Phi(X)V
, which we still denote by
\Phi
, is a BJ isomorphism and satisfies (
4.2
) for rank-one
A=xy^{\ast}
. By Lemma
4.1
,
\Phi(0)=0
, so (
4.2
) holds also for
A=0
. Finally, choose any matrix
A
with
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A\geq 2
and in Lemma
3.7
insert
B:=\Phi(A)
to get that
B=\gamma_{A}A
.
∎

5.
BJ isomorphisms on
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})

We continue with the proof of Theorem
1.2
and now consider BJ isomorphisms
\Phi
on
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. This requires separate treatment since fundamental theorem of projective geometry is no longer available. We will make a series of modifications on
\Phi
, whereby we compose
\Phi
with (conjugate)linear isometries of the form
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
or
X\mapsto UXV^{\ast}
; the newly obtained maps, which we will, with a slight abuse of notation, continue to denote by
\Phi
, will remain BJ isomorphism, but will fix an increasingly large sets. Our modifications are based on a series of lemmata.
To start with, by Corollary
4.3
we know that
\Phi
maps rank-one onto rank-one (however we have not yet shown that
\Phi
preserves adjacency). By modifying it with
U\Phi(\cdot)V^{\ast}
for suitable unitaries
U,V
we can achieve that

(5.1)

\Phi(E_{11})\dot{=}E_{11}

Lemma 5.1
.

The following are equivalent for a rank-one
B\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

(i)

E_{11}\perp B
and
E_{11}^{\bot}\cap B^{\bot}
consists of matrices with rank-one at most.

(ii)

B\in\mathbb{C}E_{12}\cup\mathbb{C}E_{21}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). By (
2.1
)
E_{11}^{\bot}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{C}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Then, rank-one
B\in E_{11}^{\bot}
if and only if
B=e_{2}x^{\ast}
or
B=xe_{2}^{\ast}
for a nonzero
x\in\mathbb{C}
. By applying, if needed, a conjugate-linear isometry (hence, a BJ isomorphism)
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
, we can reduce to the later case, when
B=xe_{2}^{\ast}
. Let
x^{\prime}\neq 0
be perpendicular to
x
and consider a matrix
A=e_{2}e_{1}^{\ast}+x^{\prime}e_{2}^{\ast}\in E_{11}^{\bot}
. Since
Be_{2}=x\perp x^{\prime}=Ae_{2}
we have
A\in E_{11}^{\bot}\cap B^{\bot}
. However,
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}A=2
except when
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}e_{2}
, or equivalently, except when
x\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}e_{2}^{\ast}
, so (ii) holds.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). If
0\neq B\in\mathbb{C}E_{12}
, then, by (
2.1
),
E_{11}^{\bot}\cap B^{\bot}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{C}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
so (i) clearly holds. Likewise if
0\neq B\in\mathbb{C}E_{21}
.
∎

We apply the lemma on
B:=\Phi(E_{12})
, while keeping in mind (
5.1
) and the fact that
\Phi
strongly preserves matrices of rank-one. Consequently,
\Phi(E_{12})
is either a scalar multiple of
E_{12}
or a scalar multiple of
E_{21}
.
By further modifying, if needed,
\Phi
with the isometry
X\mapsto X^{\ast}
we can hence achieve that

\Phi(E_{11})\dot{=}E_{11}\hbox{ and }\Phi(E_{12})\dot{=}E_{12}.

Notice that, by the same arguments,
E_{21}
is also mapped to a scalar multiple of
E_{12}
or of
E_{21}
. The former case is impossible because
E_{12}^{\bot}\neq E_{21}^{\bot}
, so
E_{12}^{\bot}=\Phi(E_{12})^{\bot}\neq\Phi(E_{21})^{\bot}
. Hence, also
E_{21}
is fixed (modulo scalars). Then,
\mathbb{C}E_{22}=E_{11}^{\bot}\cap E_{12}^{\bot}\cap E_{21}^{\bot}
, so also
E_{22}
is fixed (modulo scalars).

It follows that
\Phi
also fixes the set of matrices living in the first/second column/row (these sets coincide with
E_{ij}^{\bot}\cap E_{ii}^{\bot}
). It also fixes the set of diagonal (=
E_{12}^{\bot}\cap E_{21}^{\bot}
) and the set of anti-diagonal (=
E_{11}^{\bot}\cap E_{22}^{\bot}
) matrices. Moreover,
\Phi
also preserves unitary matrices, modulo the scalars, because these coincide with right-symmetric points (see
[
30
, Theorem 2.5]
or
[
17
, Lemma 3.7]
). In particular,
\Phi(I),\Phi(J)
are (scalar multiples of) diagonal and anti-diagonal unitary matrices, respectively; here

J:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right).

By modifying
\Phi
with
X\mapsto U\Phi(X)V^{\ast}
for diagonal unitary
U,V
we can achieve that
\Phi(I)
and
\Phi(J)
are fixed, modulo the scalars — in fact, if
\Phi(I)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&0\\
0&d\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\Phi(J)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&b\\
c&0\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
then we achieve this with unitary
U=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{c}\sqrt{d}}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right),V=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{\sqrt{a}\sqrt{c}}{\sqrt{b}\sqrt{d}}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Notice that this modification still preserves the matrix units
E_{ij}
, modulo the scalars.

In particular, the columns
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes e_{1}
and
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes e_{2}
and the rows
e_{1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
e_{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}

Lemma 5.4
.

The following are equivalent for rank-one matrices
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&0\\
\beta&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\gamma\\
0&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(i)

A^{\bot}\cap B^{\bot}
consists of rank-one matrices only.

(ii)

(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{C}(\gamma,\delta)\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Write
A=(\alpha e_{1}+\beta e_{2})e_{1}^{\ast}
and
B=(\gamma e_{1}+\delta e_{2})e_{2}^{\ast}
; by (
2.1
)

A^{\bot}\cap B^{\bot}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda\bar{\beta}&-\mu\bar{\delta}\\
\lambda\bar{\alpha}&\mu\bar{\gamma}\end{pmatrix};\;\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

This consists of matrices of rank-one at most if and only if the two columns are linearly dependent (i.e., are parallel), or equivalently, if and only if their orthogonal complements,
\alpha e_{1}+\beta e_{2}
and
\gamma e_{1}+\delta e_{2}
are parallel, as claimed by (ii).
∎

We apply this to rank-one matrices

\beta&0\end{smallmatrix}\right))=\phi_{1}(\alpha e_{1}+\beta e_{2})e_{1}^{\ast}

0&\beta\end{smallmatrix}\right))=\phi_{2}(\alpha e_{1}+\beta e_{2})e_{2}^{\ast}

to deduce that
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}=:\phi
, modulo a scalar multiple.

Similar conclusion (e.g, by applying the previous arguments on BJ isomorphism

\psi_{1}\dot{=}\psi_{2}=:\psi
is such that
\Phi(e_{i}x^{\ast})=e_{i}\psi_{i}(x)^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}\,e_{i}\psi(x)^{\ast}

There exists a scalar-valued function

c,s\in\mathbb{C}
such that

z=cx_{i}\oplus sx_{j};\qquad|c|^{2}+|s|^{2}=1.

Then,

z^{\ast}Rz=(cx_{i}\oplus sx_{j})^{\ast}(cx_{i}\oplus\mu sx_{j})=|c|^{2}(x_{i}^{\ast}x_{i})+\mu|s|^{2}(x_{j}^{\ast}x_{j}),

so
z^{\ast}Rz=0
if and only if
\mu=-\frac{|c|^{2}}{|s|^{2}}<0.

Since
\mu
is unimodular,
\mu=-1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
has no abelian summand we have
2\leq\dim\mathcal{A}<\infty
.
Within our recent paper
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
we showed that in this case, if
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
is a BJ isomorphism between complex
C^{*}
-algebras, then
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are isometrically
\ast
-isomorphic and we can identify
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, by using the properties of smooth and of relative left-symmetric points, we further showed in
[
16
, Corollary 2.9]
that
\Phi
maps elements which belong to a single block of
\mathcal{A}
onto matrices which belong to a single block of
\mathcal{B}
, and the sizes of the blocks are the same. Consequently, by writing

\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{1}^{\ell}M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C}),

and identifying individual blocks with
0\oplus M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 0=M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
, there exists a permutation of block constituents
\sigma\colon\{1,\dots,\ell\}\to\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, such that

n_{i}=n_{\sigma(i)}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\Phi_{i}:=\Phi|_{M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})}\colon M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{\sigma(i)}}(\mathbb{C}).

By
[
16
, Corollary 2.9]
,
\Phi_{i}
is a bijection; by the definition of BJ orthogonality it clearly strongly preserves BJ orthogonality. Such BJ isomorphisms were classified within Theorem
1.2
and take the form (
1.1
), modulo multiplication by a scalar-valued function (note
n_{i}\geq 2
since
\mathcal{A}
has no abelian summand). Observe also that
the permutation of indices
\sigma
induces an isometry on
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
by

\sigma\colon\bigoplus X_{i}\mapsto\bigoplus X_{\sigma(i)}.

Hence, by Theorem
1.2
, there exist isometries
\Psi_{i}\colon M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
such that, upon identifying
M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})=M_{n_{\sigma(i)}}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\Phi_{i}(X)\dot{=}\Psi_{i}(X)
.
Then,

\Psi:=\sigma\circ(\Psi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Psi_{\ell})^{-1}

is an isometry of
\mathcal{A}
and
\Psi^{-1}\circ\Phi
is a BJ isomorphism which fixes every rank-one element of
\mathcal{A}
, modulo a scalar multiple. We denote it again by
\Phi
.

Choose now an arbitrary
A=\bigoplus A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
and let

B=\bigoplus B_{i}:=\Phi(A).

Applying Lemma
3.7
on each individual block reveals that
B_{i}=\gamma_{i}A_{i}
for some nonzero scalar
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i}(A_{i})
.
Therefore

\Phi(A)=\Gamma(A)A

for some function
\Gamma\colon\mathcal{A}\to Z(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus\mathbb{C}I_{n_{i}}
, the center of
\mathcal{A}
, such that
\Gamma(A)
is invertible for every
A
. It remains to show that
\Gamma(A)=\gamma(A)P(A)
, where
\gamma(A)\in\mathbb{C}
is unimodular, and
P(A)\in Z(\mathcal{A})
is positive definite. To ease the flow of arguments, we proceed in steps.

Step 1
.
A
and
B=\Phi(A)
attain their norm on the same summands. Moreover, if those summands are indexed by
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
, then
|\gamma_{i_{1}}(A)|=\dots=|\gamma_{i_{k}}(A)|
.

To see this, we assume for simplicity
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=(1,\dots,k)
, i.e.,
\|A_{1}\|=\dots=\|A_{k}\|=\|A\|
and
\|A_{i}\|<\|A\|
for
i\geq k+1
. Then,
A\not\perp X:=(A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{k})\oplus 0
, so also

\bigoplus\gamma_{i}(A)A_{i}=\Phi(A)\not\perp\Phi(X)=\gamma_{1}(X)A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\gamma_{k}(X)A_{k}\oplus 0.

It easily follows that
\Phi(A)
cannot attain its norm on some block with index greater than
k
, that is,

\|\gamma_{i}(A)A_{i}\|<\|\Phi(A)\|\quad\hbox{ for }i\geq k+1.

Conversely, if, say,
\|\gamma_{k}(A)A_{k}\|<\|\Phi(A)\|
, then

\Phi(A)\not\perp(\gamma_{1}A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\gamma_{k-1}A_{k-1}\oplus 0)=:{Y}.

By surjectivity there exists
X\in\mathcal{A}
with
\Phi(X)=Y
, and clearly

X=\alpha_{1}A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\alpha_{k-1}A_{k-1}\oplus 0

for some nonzero scalars
\alpha_{i}
. However, since
A
attains its norm also on
k
-th summand,
A\perp X
, a contradiction.

Step 2
. With
A=I
this shows that
\Phi(I)={\bm{\gamma}}(I)I
where
{\bm{\gamma}}(I)=\bigoplus\gamma_{i}(I)I_{n_{i}}
is a scalar multiple of a unitary. By replacing
\Phi
with a BJ isomorphism
{\bm{\gamma}}(I)^{\ast}\Phi(\cdot)
we can hence assume that

\Phi(I)=I.

Step 3
.
If
R=0\oplus z_{i}z_{i}^{\ast}\oplus-z_{j}z_{j}^{\ast}\oplus 0
where
\|z_{i}\|=\|z_{j}\|=1
, then
\Phi(R)=\gamma(R)R
, for some
\gamma(R)\in\mathbb{C}
.

Choose normalized
z_{i}z_{i}^{\ast}\in 0\oplus M_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 0
and
z_{j}z_{j}^{\ast}\in 0\oplus M_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus 0
with
i\neq j
and for
R=0\oplus(z_{i}z_{i}^{\ast})\oplus(-z_{j}z_{j}^{\ast})\oplus 0
. Since
R
attains its norm on
z=z_{i}\oplus z_{j}
(we omitted zero summands), and

\langle Rz,Iz\rangle=\bigl\langle z_{i}\oplus(-z_{j})\,,\,z_{i}\oplus z_{j}\bigr\rangle=0

so
R\perp I
. Applying
\Phi
we deduce by Lemma
6.1
, that
\gamma_{i}(R)=\gamma_{j}(R)=:\gamma(R)\in\mathbb{C}
, as claimed.

Step 4
.
If
\|A_{i}\|=\|A_{j}\|=\|A\|
and their numerical ranges satisfy
W(A_{i})\cap W(A_{j})\setminus\{0\}\neq\emptyset
, then
\gamma_{i}(A)=\gamma_{j}(A)
.

By definition, there exist normalized vectors
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
(corresponding to blocks containing
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
) such that
\lambda:=z_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}z_{i}=z_{j}^{\ast}A_{j}z_{j}\in W(A_{i})\cap W(A_{j})\setminus\{0\}
. Choose
R
and
z
as in Step 3. Then from

\overline{\langle Rz,Az\rangle}=\overline{\bigl\langle z_{i}\oplus(-z_{j})\,,\,A_{i}z_{i}\oplus A_{j}z_{j}\bigr\rangle}=z_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}z_{i}-z_{j}^{\ast}A_{j}z_{j}=0

we have
R\perp A
. Applying
\Phi
, which fixes
R
modulo scalars by Step 3, we get

R\dot{=}\Phi(R)\perp\Phi(A)=\bigoplus\gamma_{i}(A)A_{i}
. Now,
R
achieves its norm only on
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{z_{i},z_{j}\}
and since
z_{i},z_{j}
are orthonormal vectors (they correspond to different blocks), there exist
c,s\in\mathbb{C}
,
|c|^{2}+|s|^{2}=1
, such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle R(cz_{i}\oplus sz_{j}),\Phi(A)(cz_{i}\oplus sz_{j})\rangle=\langle cz_{i}\oplus(-sz_{j}),\gamma_{i}(A)A_{i}cz_{i}\oplus\gamma_{j}(A)A_{j}sz_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=|c|^{2}z_{i}^{\ast}(\gamma_{i}(A)A_{i})^{\ast}z_{i}-|s|^{2}z_{j}^{\ast}(\gamma_{j}(A)A_{j})^{\ast}z_{j}

\displaystyle=|c|^{2}\overline{\lambda\gamma_{i}(A)}-|s|^{2}\overline{\lambda\gamma_{j}(A)}.

After simplifying (while keeping in mind that
\lambda\gamma_{k}(A)\neq 0
) we get

\frac{\gamma_{i}(A)}{\gamma_{j}(A)}=\frac{|s|^{2}}{|c|^{2}}>0
.
Since
\|A_{i}\|=\|A_{j}\|=\|A\|
, then, by Step 1,
\gamma_{i}(A)=\gamma_{j}(A)
.

Step 5
.
If
A=0\oplus x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast}\oplus x_{j}y_{j}^{\ast}\oplus 0
, where
\|x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast}\|=\|x_{j}y_{j}^{\ast}\|
, then
\Phi(A)=\gamma(A)A
for some
\gamma(A)\in\mathbb{C}
.

Notice that
W(x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast})
is a (possibly degenerate) elliptic disc with foci at
0
and
y_{i}^{\ast}x_{i}
(the eigenvalues of
x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast}
) and minor axis equals to
\sqrt{\text{tr}((x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast})^{*}x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast})-|0|^{2}-|y_{i}^{\ast}x_{i}|^{2}}
(see
[
18
]
) and two such elliptic discs always intersect in a nonzero point, except when they are both degenerate, i.e., when
y_{i}\dot{=}x_{i}
and
y_{j}\dot{=}x_{j}
. In the former case, we are done by Step 4. In the latter case, after omitting zero summands,

A\dot{=}x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\oplus\mu x_{j}x_{j}^{*}

where
\mu
is unimodular and
\|x_{i}\|=\|x_{j}\|
. Here we choose
R=x_{i}x_{i}^{*}\oplus\mu x_{j}z_{j}^{*}
, where
z_{j}\not\in\mathbb{C}x_{j}
,
\|z_{j}\|=\|x_{j}\|=\|x_{i}\|
and

z_{j}^{\ast}x_{j}<0

(it exists since
j
-th summand of
\mathcal{A}
has size at least
2
). Notice
W(\mu x_{j}z_{j}^{*})
is a nondegenerated eliptic disc, so by the first part of Step 5,
\Phi(R)\dot{=}R
. Also,
R
attains its norm on
w=cx_{i}\oplus sz_{j}
, and we can find
c,s
such that
|c|^{2}+|s|^{2}=1
and
\frac{|c|^{2}}{|s|^{2}}=-z_{j}^{*}x_{j}>0
. One can show that
\langle Rw,Aw\rangle=0
and hence
R\perp A
. Then also

R\dot{=}\Phi(R)\perp\Phi(A)\dot{=}\gamma_{i}(A)x_{i}x_{i}^{*}+\gamma_{j}(A)\mu x_{j}x_{j}^{*}

and
|\gamma_{i}(A)|=|\gamma_{j}(A)|
by Step 1.

Also

\begin{split}0&=\langle R(c^{\prime}x_{i}\oplus s^{\prime}z_{j})\,,\,\Phi(A)(c^{\prime}x_{i}\oplus s^{\prime}z_{j})\rangle\\
&=\langle c^{\prime}x_{i}\oplus\mu s^{\prime}x_{j}\,,\,c^{\prime}\gamma_{i}(A)x_{i}\oplus\mu s^{\prime}\gamma_{j}(A)x_{j}x_{j}^{*}z_{j}\rangle\\
&=|c^{\prime}|^{2}\overline{\gamma_{i}(A)}(x_{i}^{*}x_{i})+|s^{\prime}|^{2}\overline{\gamma_{j}(A)}(z_{j}^{*}x_{j})(x_{j}^{*}x_{j}),\end{split}

so
\overline{\left(\frac{\gamma_{i}(A)}{\gamma_{j}(A)}\right)}=-\frac{|s^{\prime}|^{2}(z_{j}^{*}x_{j})}{|c^{\prime}|^{2}}>0.
This shows that
\gamma_{i}(A)=\gamma_{j}(A)=:\gamma(A)\in\mathbb{C}
.

Step 6
. Consider now the general case when summands
A_{i},A_{j}
are both nonzero.
Let

A_{i}=\sigma_{1}(A_{i})x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast}+\dots

A_{j}=\sigma_{1}(A_{j})x_{j}y_{j}^{\ast}+\dots

be their SVD.

Notice that

x_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}y_{i}=\sigma_{1}(A_{i})\neq 0\quad\hbox{ and }\quad x_{j}^{\ast}A_{j}y_{j}=\sigma_{1}(A_{j}).

So,
R:=x_{i}y_{i}^{\ast}\oplus(-x_{j}y_{j}^{\ast})
achieves its norm on the span of
y_{i},y_{j}
. These two vectors belong to different summands so they are orthonormal. In particular,
R
achieves its norm on a normalized vector

z:=cy_{1}\oplus sy_{2},

where
c,s>0
,
|c|^{2}+|s|^{2}=1
are chosen so that
\frac{|s|^{2}}{|c|^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{1}(A_{i})}{\sigma_{1}(A_{j})}
, and maps it into

Rz=cx_{i}\oplus(-sx_{j}),

while
Az=cA_{i}y_{i}\oplus sA_{j}y_{j}
(we are not writing the zero direct summands). So,

\overline{\langle Rz,Az\rangle}=|c|^{2}x_{i}^{\ast}A_{i}y_{i}-|s|^{2}x_{j}^{\ast}A_{j}y_{j}=|c|^{2}\sigma_{1}(A_{i})-|s|^{2}\sigma_{1}(A_{j})=0

and hence
R\perp A
.

Apply
\Phi
to get, by Step 5,

R\dot{=}\Phi(R)\perp\Phi(A)=\bigoplus\gamma_{k}(A)A_{k}.

Hence, there exist a normalized vector
z^{\prime}\in M_{0}(R)=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\{y_{i},y_{j}\}
such that

\langle Rz^{\prime},\Phi(A)z^{\prime}\rangle=0
. We can write it as

z^{\prime}=c^{\prime}y_{i}\oplus s^{\prime}y_{j}
for some
c^{\prime},s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
,
|c^{\prime}|^{2}+|s^{\prime}|^{2}=1
, and then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle\Phi(R)z^{\prime},\Phi(A)z^{\prime}\rangle}=\overline{\langle\gamma(R)Rz^{\prime},(\bigoplus\gamma_{k}(A)A_{k})z^{\prime}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\overline{\gamma(R)}\bigl(|c^{\prime}|^{2}\gamma_{i}(A)x_{i}^{\ast}Ay_{i}-|s^{\prime}|^{2}\gamma_{j}(A)x_{j}^{\ast}Ay_{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\overline{\gamma(R)}\bigl(|c^{\prime}|^{2}\gamma_{i}(A)\sigma_{1}(A_{i})-|s^{\prime}|^{2}\gamma_{j}(A)\sigma_{1}(A_{j})\bigr).

We see that

\tfrac{\gamma_{i}(A)}{\gamma_{j}(A)}=\tfrac{|s^{\prime}|^{2}}{|c^{\prime}|^{2}}\cdot\tfrac{\sigma_{1}(A_{j})}{\sigma_{1}(A_{i})}>0.

In addition, if
\|A_{i}\|=\|A_{j}\|=\|A\|
, then, by Step 1, also
|\gamma_{i}(A)|=|\gamma_{j}(A)|
, and so
\|A_{i}\|=\|A_{j}\|=\|A\|
implies
\gamma_{i}(A)=\gamma_{j}(A)
. Finally, choose any index
i_{0}
with
A_{i_{0}}\neq 0
, (re)define
\gamma_{i}(A):=\gamma_{i_{0}}(A)
whenever
A_{i}=0
and then let
\gamma(A):=\frac{\gamma_{1}(A)}{|\gamma_{1}(A)|}
and
P(A)=|\gamma_{1}(A)|\left(I_{n_{1}}\oplus\frac{\gamma_{2}(A)}{\gamma_{1}(A)}I_{n_{2}}\oplus\dots\oplus\frac{\gamma_{l}(A)}{\gamma_{1}(A)}I_{n_{\ell}}\right)>0
.
∎

7.
Concluding remarks

Let us show that if
C^{*}
-algebra is not simple, then not every BJ isomorphism on
\mathcal{A}
is an isometry multiplied by a scalar-valued function.

Example 7.1
.

Consider
\mathcal{A}=M_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})
with
n_{1},n_{2}\geq 2
and let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
be a map which fixes all elements except those inside the set
I\oplus(0,1)I
, and let

\Phi(I\oplus rI)=I\oplus\gamma(r)I

for some bijection
\gamma\colon(0,1)\to(0,1)
.
Such
\Phi
is bijective, and clearly strongly preserves BJ orthogonality except possibly if
A\perp B
where one among
A,B
belong to
I\oplus(0,1)I
.

Case 1.

A=I\oplus rI\in I\oplus(0,1)I
.
Then
A
achieves its norm only on its first summand, so
B=B_{1}\oplus B_{2}
satisfies
A\perp B
if and only if
I\perp B_{1}
. Then,
B_{1}\neq I
, and so
\Phi(B)=B
. It follows that
\Phi(A)=I\oplus\gamma(r)I\perp B=\Phi(B)
.

Case 2.

B=I\oplus rI\in I\oplus(0,1)I
and decompose
A=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
. Due to
A\perp B
we clearly have that
A\not\in I\oplus(0,1)I
, so it is fixed by
\Phi
.
Hence, to prove

(7.1)

A=\Phi(A)\perp\Phi(B)=I\oplus\gamma(r)I

we only need to consider the case
\|A_{1}\|=\|A_{2}\|=\|A\|\neq 0
. By Proposition
2.1
there exists a normalized vector
z=z_{1}\oplus z_{2}
, where
A
attains its norm, such that

(7.2)

z_{1}^{\ast}A_{1}z_{1}+rz_{2}^{\ast}A_{2}z_{2}=\langle Az,Bz\rangle=0.

If
z_{1}=0
, or if
z_{1}\neq 0
but
z_{2}^{\ast}A_{2}z_{2}=0
, then
A_{2}\perp B_{2}=rI
, so also
A_{2}\perp\gamma(r)I
, which implies (
7.1
); likewise if
z_{2}=0
(or if
z_{1}^{\ast}A_{1}z_{1}\neq 0
). So can assume

z_{1}^{\ast}A_{1}z_{1}\neq 0\hbox{ and }z_{2}^{\ast}A_{2}z_{2}\neq 0.

Notice that
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
then attain their norms on
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, respectively. As such
A
attains its norm also on
z_{c}:=cz_{1}\oplus sz_{2}
for every
c,s\in\mathbb{C}
with
|c|^{2}+|s|^{2}=1
. We can choose
c
and
s
such that

\tfrac{|s|^{2}}{|c|^{2}}\gamma(r)=r.

Then, by (
7.2
),
\Phi(A)z_{c}=cA_{1}z_{1}\oplus sA_{2}z_{2}
and
\Phi(B)z_{c}=cz_{1}\oplus\gamma(r)sz_{2}
are perpendicular vectors, so
\Phi(A)\perp\Phi(B)
. The same arguments can be applied on
\Phi^{-1}
to show that
\Phi
strongly preserves BJ orthogonality.

If
\mathcal{A}
has abelian summand, then not every BJ isomorphism takes the form of Theorem
1.1
.

Example 7.2
.

Consider
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
and let
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
be a map which fixes all elements except those on the line
(1,ri),r\in\mathbb{R}
, and let

\Phi((1,ri))=(1,-ri)=(1,-1)(1,ri).

This
\Phi
is bijective and strongly preserves
BJ
orthogonality. However, it is not of the form from Theorem
1.1
since it multiplies some of the arguments with a non-definite element
(1,-1)
.
