Rapidly Progressive Intracranial Arterial Stenosis in Giant Cell Arteritis: A Case Report with Longitudinal Imaging and Implications for Endovascular Management
Authors: Makoto Hosokawa, Ryota Kimura, Ichiro Nakagawa, Yoshimitsu Doi, Nagaaki Marugami, Yoshinari Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe natural history of intracranial arterial stenosis caused by giant cell arteritis (GCA) and the effects of therapeutic interventions remain unclear. We report a case of rapidly progressive intracranial arterial stenosis associated with GCA and discuss its clinical course and the implications for endovascular management.Case PresentationA 75-year-old man developed multiple intracranial arterial stenoses and occlusions over several months, resulting in cerebral infarction. Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty was performed for severe stenosis of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery, which exhibited delayed distal contrast opacification. Temporal artery biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of GCA. Although inflammatory markers improved with optimal medical treatment, the untreated intracranial arterial stenoses progressed rapidly, leading to extensive cerebral infarction. In contrast, no restenosis was observed at the site treated with angioplasty during the 6-month follow-up period.ConclusionRapidly progressive intracranial involvement in GCA may lead to a devastating clinical course, with progression of arterial stenosis occurring despite apparent control of systemic inflammation. This case highlights that intracranial arterial stenosis due to GCA can progress rapidly even under immunosuppressive therapy, underscoring the importance of serial vascular imaging and suggesting that some patients may benefit from early consideration of endovascular treatment.

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is the most common form of systemic vasculitis, primarily affecting large- and medium-sized arteries, including the aorta and its major branches.1–3) Although ischemic stroke has traditionally been considered an uncommon complication of GCA, recent studies suggest that its incidence may be higher than previously recognized.4–7) Given that GCA predominantly affects older adults, a population in which neuroendovascular treatment is frequently performed, clinicians must remain alert to the possibility of cerebrovascular involvement in GCA.8,9)

Intracranial involvement in GCA is rare, reported in only approximately 4% of patients with GCA. As revascularization procedures are often deferred until disease activity is controlled, reports of endovascular treatments remain scarce,10–13) leaving both its efficacy and optimal timing poorly defined. In addition, the long-term natural history of intracranial arterial stenosis in GCA, particularly its behavior under immunosuppressive therapy, has not been fully characterized.

Here, we report a case of rapidly progressive intracranial arterial stenosis in a patient with GCA, highlighting its clinical course and implications for management.

Case Presentation

A 75-year-old man was admitted with a 1-week history of dizziness and was diagnosed with ischemic stroke. No visual disturbances or headaches were identified throughout the clinical course. His C-reactive protein (CRP) level had been elevated for approximately 2 months before admission and increased to 12.6 mg/dL at presentation. Thereafter, CRP levels remained elevated at approximately 3–8 mg/dL.

MRI revealed an acute infarction in the left medial temporal lobe. Moreover, CTA demonstrated stenosis of the bilateral internal carotid arteries and the left vertebral artery, whereas the right vertebral artery was occluded (Fig. 1A–1C). No significant stenosis was identified in either the extracranial carotid artery or the subclavian artery. Accordingly, aspirin (100 mg/day) was initiated. Notably, approximately 1.5 years earlier, the patient had undergone MRA for cerebrovascular screening prompted by his history of diabetes mellitus; no intracranial arterial stenosis was identified at that time. However, at presentation, the right vertebral artery was dominant, and the left vertebral artery was hypoplastic (Fig. 1D–1F).

On day 27, the patient developed left hemiparesis. MRI revealed multiple new acute infarctions predominantly in the right cerebral hemisphere, and arterial spin labeling demonstrated relative hypoperfusion. Therefore, prasugrel (3.75 mg/day) was incorporated into the regimen, and further evaluation was undertaken to assess the indication for endovascular treatment.

DSA revealed severe stenosis of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery with delayed distal contrast opacification (Fig. 2A and 2B), along with additional stenosis in the paraclinoid segment of the left internal carotid artery and occlusion of the right vertebral artery (Fig. 2C and 2D). Stenosis of the superficial temporal artery, predominantly on the left side, was also noted.

Given the severe stenosis of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery with delayed distal contrast opacification and ongoing cerebral infarction despite medical therapy, revascularization was deemed necessary. On day 31 of hospitalization, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) was performed (Fig. 2E–2H); post-procedural angiography showed adequate dilation and improvement of delayed distal contrast opacification. No perioperative stroke was observed.

Contrast-enhanced MRI subsequently demonstrated marked vessel wall enhancement and thickening at the occluded segment of the right vertebral artery and along the petrous and cavernous segments of the bilateral internal carotid arteries (Fig. 3A–3C). Moreover, ultrasonography of the superficial temporal arteries revealed a bilateral halo sign, which is a characteristic finding in GCA, appearing as homogeneous hypoechoic circumferential wall thickening that reflects inflammatory arterial wall edema (Fig. 3D–3E). Although stenosis was more pronounced in the left superficial temporal artery, the right superficial temporal artery was selected for biopsy to preserve the left vessel as a potential donor vessel for future revascularization, should the untreated left internal carotid artery stenosis progress. A right superficial temporal artery biopsy was subsequently performed on day 39, and the histopathological findings were consistent with those of GCA (Fig. 3F–3H). Together with the patient’s age (≥50 years) and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (85 mm/h), these superficial temporal artery biopsy findings fulfilled the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) Classification Criteria for GCA, with a total score of 8 points (threshold ≥6 points).14)

Follow-up MRI performed on day 36 demonstrated a new infarction in the territory of the left anterior cerebral artery; however, no further progression of ischemic lesions was observed thereafter. Following temporal artery biopsy, intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1 g/day for 3 days) was initiated, followed by oral prednisolone therapy (35 mg/day; subsequently tapered to 20 mg/day), resulting in normalization of CRP levels. Tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 receptor inhibitor used for the treatment of GCA, was subsequently administered to optimize disease control and improve the likelihood of treatment response for the intracranial vascular involvement.

The patient was transferred to another hospital for specialized rheumatologic management, including tocilizumab therapy. At the time of transfer, the ESR had normalized. Tocilizumab was initiated, and prednisolone therapy was escalated to 50 mg/day at the discretion of the treating physician. However, progressive stenosis of the bilateral internal carotid arteries resulted in a large left hemispheric stroke (Fig. 4A–4D). As the patient’s level of consciousness deteriorated, continuation of tocilizumab was considered unlikely to provide additional clinical benefit, and the patient was transferred back to our institution. Additional endovascular treatment was not indicated because a large cerebral infarction, considered likely to represent irreversible neurological injury, had already developed.

On transfer, the patient presented with impaired consciousness, aphasia, and quadriplegia. Prednisolone therapy was continued. More than 6 months after the initial presentation, a halo sign persisted on ultrasonography of the superficial temporal artery. CTA demonstrated severe stenosis of the supraclinoid segment of the left internal carotid artery, with poor opacification of the left middle cerebral artery and occlusion of the anterior cerebral artery. In contrast, the treated cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery remained patent, with no significant restenosis (Fig. 4E–4H). At this time, the modified Rankin Scale score was 5. Discussion

In the present case, intracranial arterial stenosis due to GCA progressed rapidly despite intensive immunosuppressive therapy, resulting in a large ischemic stroke. Serial vascular imaging demonstrated the progression of multiple intracranial stenotic lesions from disease onset to the chronic phase, whereas the lesion treated with PTA remained patent without restenosis during the 6-month follow-up period.

Beuker et al.15) reported that a subgroup of patients with GCA and intracranial involvement exhibited a rapidly progressive clinical course characterized by recurrent ischemic stroke, with progression of arterial stenosis despite immunosuppressive therapy, resulting in poor neurological outcomes and high mortality.15) Our patient followed a similar clinical trajectory, with intracranial arterial stenosis also progressing over a short period despite medical therapy and ultimately leading to a large stroke.

Glucocorticoids remain the first-line treatment for GCA, and tocilizumab has been shown to be effective in controlling disease activity.1,16–19) Inflammatory lesions gradually diminish over time, and GCA-related vascular stenosis or occlusion may improve to varying degrees in response to treatment.15,16) In our patient, glucocorticoid therapy was initiated promptly, and tocilizumab was introduced at an early stage. In response to treatment, the levels of systemic inflammatory markers, such as CRP and ESR, which are widely used to monitor disease activity and treatment response in GCA,17,18) decreased rapidly. Nevertheless, vascular stenosis progressed, and stroke ensued. This discrepancy suggests that normalization of systemic inflammatory markers may not reliably indicate adequate control of intracranial vascular inflammation. One possible explanation is the persistence of localized vascular inflammation despite apparent control of systemic inflammation. In addition, structural vascular changes following inflammatory injury, such as intimal hyperplasia and vascular remodeling, may contribute to progressive arterial narrowing.9) These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on laboratory parameters when assessing disease activity in the intracranial vasculature, as this may lead to underestimation. In this context, this case suggests that earlier consideration of alternative treatment strategies, including endovascular treatment, may be warranted in patients who demonstrate progressive stenosis or recurrent strokes despite medical therapy.

Caton et al.13) reviewed 19 endovascular procedures in 14 patients, including 15 PTAs alone and 4 PTAs combined with stent placement, and reported immediate improvement in luminal diameter in all cases. In contrast, Beuker et al.15) reported a poor safety profile in a small cohort, in which 2 of 3 patients with extensive intracranial stenoses died from procedure-related complications following endovascular treatment. In the present case, PTA for severe stenosis of the cavernous segment of the right internal carotid artery was performed for symptomatic disease. The treated lesion remained patent without evidence of considerable restenosis during follow-up; however, progressive stenoses developed in other intracranial vessels. Collectively, these findings suggest that successful focal revascularization may contribute to maintaining vessel patency relative to the natural course. However, in our patient, endovascular treatment was limited to a single lesion in the cavernous segment, which is an anatomical site considered to be associated with a relatively lower procedural risk among intracranial vessels. Therefore, these findings cannot be generalized and are insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy, safety, and risks of endovascular therapy in this disease.

In the present case, optimal medical therapy was prioritized for the left internal carotid artery stenosis, and endovascular treatment was deferred. This approach is consistent with current guidelines and previous reports recommending that endovascular treatment be deferred during the active inflammatory phase unless urgently indicated.12,13) However, at the time, rapidly progressive intracranial GCA had only recently been described in a limited number of patients, and we did not initially fully recognize the risk of rapid progression of intracranial stenosis or resistance to medical therapy. In retrospect, repeat cerebral angiography before transfer, with additional endovascular treatment guided by those findings, may have prevented the subsequent major stroke.

In the present case, treatment was performed with PTA alone without stent placement, and no restenosis was observed at the treated site. In terms of the use of stents in GCA, GCA and Takayasu arteritis share overlapping immunopathological mechanisms,9) and evidence from Takayasu arteritis may offer useful insights into stent use in GCA. A meta-analysis of endovascular treatment for Takayasu arteritis revealed significantly higher restenosis rates with stent placement than with PTA alone.20) This difference was attributed to mechanical stent-strut–induced stimulation promoting neointimal hyperplasia, which is a mechanism that may also apply to GCA.

The present case is notable for its longitudinal intracranial vascular imaging, capturing GCA progression from onset through the chronic phase. To the best of our knowledge, few reports have described such a detailed longitudinal clinical course of rapidly progressive intracranial arterial stenosis in GCA requiring endovascular treatment. We believe this case contributes to a better understanding of the progression of intracranial arterial stenosis and may be informative for neuroendovascular specialists.

Conclusion

Rapidly progressive intracranial involvement in GCA may result in a severe clinical course, characterized by continued progression of arterial stenosis occurring despite apparent control of systemic inflammation. This case highlights that intracranial arterial stenosis can advance rapidly even under immunosuppressive therapy, underscoring the importance of serial vascular imaging in patients with suspected or confirmed intracranial involvement. The findings also suggest that selected cases may benefit from early consideration of endovascular treatment.
