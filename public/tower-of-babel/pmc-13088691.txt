Causal effects of single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels on autism spectrum disorder risk: a bidirectional Mendelian randomized study
Authors: Jun Zhu, Yun Yi, Ling Ma, Sheng Tao, Jingjing Sun, Danwei Zhang, Xue Sun, Kaimo Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObservational epidemiology studies suggested a relationship between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) risk. We aimed to explore the causal relationship between them at the genetic level.MethodsWe performed a two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using genome-wide association studies summary statistics. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis. We applied other complementary methods, including weighted median, weighted mode, and MR Egger regression. MR Egger and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) are used to detect and correct the effects of horizontal pleiotropy.ResultsThere were no causal associations between genetically predicted single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk in IVW analysis (Homocysteine: 95% CI: 0.952–1.082, P = 0.652; Folate in Serum or Plasma: 95% CI: 0.968–1.249, P = 0.143; VB12: 95% CI: 0.989–2.133, P = 0.057; VB6: 95% CI: 0.647–1.247, P = 0.522; VC: 95% CI: 0.794–1.881, P = 0.362; VD: 95% CI: 0.825–1.551, P = 0.443; and VE: 95% CI: 0.87–1.711, P = 0.249). Reversely, we did not find significant causal effects of ASD on single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels in MR analysis (all P > 0.05). Based on sensitivity analyses, heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy are unlikely to distort causal estimates.ConclusionsThe MR study suggests that no evidence of a causal association was found between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk. Further studies are needed to validate these conclusions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02254-1.

Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a serious developmental disorder that can lead to serious changes in social interaction behavior, mainly manifested by social difficulties, communication difficulties, repetitive and stereotyped behaviors, and delayed speech development [1]. According to statistics, the prevalence of ASD is increasing in many countries, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States reports that men are four to five times more likely to have ASD than women [2]. With the increasing incidence, ASD has become the leading disease of mental disability, causing a huge social and economic burden to families and society [3]. However, the cause of ASD has not been identified, and there is a lack of effective treatment [4]. The causes of ASD are unknown and multifaceted, involving complex interactions between genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors [5].

Carbon metabolism refers to the transfer and metabolic process of a carbon unit (such as methyl group, formyl group, etc.) in living organisms. Carbon metabolism plays a key role in a variety of biochemical processes, including DNA methylation and the synthesis of purines and pyrimidines [6]. Studies have indicated that folate, as an important cofactor in one-carbon metabolism, is involved in the synthesis and transformation of purine and pyrimidine nucleotides and may be associated with the pathogenesis of ASD [6]. Folate gene polymorphisms have been associated with other psychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases, particularly polymorphisms on the DHFR, MTHFR, and CBS genes, all of which are closely associated with the one-carbon metabolic pathway [7]. In addition, sex-specific brain epigenome may also play a role in the pathogenesis of ASD, which may be related to the sex-specific expression of a carbon metabolic pathway [8].

Studies have shown that low plasma B vitamin levels and high homocysteine (Hcy) are associated with neurological diseases [3, 9]. Certain B vitamins, such as vitamin B2, vitamin B6, vitamin B9, and vitamin B12, act as coenzymes or coenzyme precursors and participate in multiple stages of single-carbon metabolism [10]. B vitamins play a key role in single-carbon metabolism, affecting nucleic acid synthesis, cell function, and disease risk [10]. Since B vitamins are important cofactors in Hcy metabolism, genetic variations affecting B vitamin concentration may affect plasma Hcy concentration [9]. Previous observational studies have suggested that metabolic factors may have a significant impact on the onset and development of ASD, particularly the role of Hcy and B vitamins [3, 11]. Among these nutrients, B vitamins, including vitamin B6, folate, and vitamin B12, play a crucial role in the metabolism of Hcy [12]. Carbon metabolism is related to amino acid metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, and methylation reaction, and vitamin D is mainly related to calcium and phosphorus metabolism and bone health [13, 14]. Although both carbon metabolism and vitamin D are important metabolic pathways in the human body, they are independent in their biochemical functions and metabolic pathways. Previous clinical studies have found that vitamin D deficiency and vitamin D receptor (VDR) polymorphisms are associated with abnormalities in brain structure and function as well as behavioral disorders or ASD [15, 16]. To sum up, the relationship between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk has not been clearly explained, and the exact mechanism is uncertain. However, given that observational studies have certain methodological limitations, further investigation into the causal relationship between ASD and these nutrients is warranted.

We adopted the method of bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) study to verify previous findings in different GWAS samples and explore some new associations. MR analysis uses genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IVs) to infer causality between exposure and outcome. Because genetic variants are single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) independent of confounding factors or reverse causation [17]. The principles of MR studies are based on Mendelian laws, similar to randomized controlled trials (RCT) while avoiding high costs [18]. In epidemiological studies, confounders greatly interfere with the causal inference of exposure and results. In theory, the MR method can effectively avoid the influence of confounding factors and eliminate the interference of reverse causality.

Hence, the present study aimed to investigate the causal relationship between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk from the genetic level through a bidirectional, two-sample MR analysis. While a previous MR study investigated the impact of vitamin levels on ASD risk [11], the present study investigated the bidirectional relationships between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD. The present study could provide data for a better understanding of the pathogenesis of ASD.

Methods

Study design

Our study adhered to the STROBE-MR guidelines [19]. We performed a bidirectional two-sample MR to infer causal relationships between single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk, which includes Hcy levels, Vitamin B6 levels, Folate in Red Blood Cells, Folate in Serum or Plasma, and Vitamins (VB12, VB6, VC, VD, and VE). The valid IVs of MR analysis must satisfy three core assumptions [19]. We select genetic variants that meet the following conditions for MR analysis [20]: (1) Relevance: Select SNPs that were associated with significant genome-wide exposure in GWAS summary level data; (2) Independence: Independent SNPs with no linkage imbalance between instruments; (3) Exclusion restriction: the genetic variant influences the outcome only through the risk factor for interest. GWAS summary data source

The genetic variants associated with circulating Hcy, vitamin B6, Folate in Red Blood Cells, and Folate in Serum or Plasma were derived from two previous GWAS, including 1,208, 1,758, 4,409, and 5,998 individuals of European ancestry, respectively [21, 22]. GWAS summary-level data on vitamins were obtained from the UK Biobank (https://www.nealelab.is/uk-biobank) and are publicly available. We obtained GWAS summary-level data of VB12, VB6, VC, VD, and VE from the UK Biobank, which included a total of 64,979 individuals of European ancestry. Publicly available summary statistical data on genetic associations for ASD were derived from the latest Psychiatric Genomics Consortium. This dataset comprised 46,351 individuals of European descent, including 18,382 cases and 27,969 controls [23]. All patients with ASD were diagnosed based on the 10th version of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD 10) and received treatment at psychiatric hospitals or outpatient psychiatric clinics. This GWAS summary statistic of ASD may be downloaded from https://pgc.unc.edu/for-researchers/download-results/. These data are detailed in Table S1.

Selection of genetic instruments

Firstly, we selected independent SNPs that were strongly associated with single-carbon metabolism and supplementary vitamin levels with p-values less than 5 × 10− 6. Secondly, the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNPs was eliminated (R2 < 0.001, clumping distance = 10,000 kb) [24]. Thirdly, SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) of less than 0.01 were eliminated as well. Fourthly, if there was no corresponding SNP in the obtained GWAS, the proxy SNP with a higher LD relationship with the missing SNP was selected using the criterion that R2 was greater than 0.8 [25]. Furthermore, we calculated the F statistic for the IVs using the following formula: F = R2*(N-2)/(1-R2), R2 denotes the variance of exposure explained by each IV. As a rule of thumb, if the average F statistic is greater than 10, it can be considered that there is no weak tool bias [26]. We utilized these carefully chosen SNPs as the final genetic IVs for the subsequent MR analysis.

Statistical analysis

We carried out the MR analysis in four different ways: the random-effects inverse variance weighted (IVW) as the main method [27], and MR-Egger regression [28], Weighted Median [29], and Weighted Mode methods [30] as the supplementary methods. Detailed descriptions of these methods have been published elsewhere [31].

Sensitivity analysis

The presence of horizontal pleiotropy was examined using the MR-Egger intercept test [28] and the MR-PRESSO global test [32]. Outliers identified by the MR-PRESSO global test were excluded, and a robustness analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method to validate the results. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was chosen for the analysis. To detect heterogeneity, we used the IVW method and MR Egger regression to quantify heterogeneity through Cochran’s Q statistics. The asymmetry of the funnel plot can also be used as an indicator of horizontal pleiotropy [33]. In addition, we used the leave-one-out analysis to test the robustness and consistency of the results. All analyses were carried out using the package “TwoSampleMR” in R version 4.0.5.

Results

Causal effects of single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels on ASD

We incorporated 5, 14, 6, 9, 10, 18, 11 14, and 12 independent SNPs with significant p-values less than 5 × 10 − 6 as IV SNPs for single carbon metabolism (circulating Hcy, vitamin B6, folate in red blood cells, and folate in serum or plasma) and vitamin levels (VB12, VB6, VC, VD, and VE). The F-statistic values were all more than 10. Detailed information on IVs for exposure, including the F-values and R2 values, are listed in Table S2.

The MR estimates of different methods are presented in Table 1; Fig. 2. Overall, there were no causal associations between genetically predicted single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels and ASD (Table 2). The results of IVW showed that single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels were not statistically associated with ASD risk (Hcy levels: OR = 1.015, 95% CI: 0.952–1.082, P = 0.652; vitamin B6 levels: OR = 0.994, 95% CI: 0.932–1.060, P = 0.848; folate in red blood cells: OR = 1.095, 95% CI: 0.961–1.247, P = 0.173; folate in serum or plasma: OR = 1.100, 95% CI: 0.968–1.249, P = 0.143; VB12: OR = 1.452, 95% CI: 0.989–2.133, P = 0.057; VB6: OR = 0.898, 95% CI: 0.647–1.247, P = 0.522; VC: OR = 1.222, 95% CI0.794–1.881, P = 0.362; VD: OR = 1.131, 95% CI: 0.825–1.551, P = 0.443; and VE: OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.87–1.711, P = 0.249) (Table 2). The results of MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and weighted mode methods were consistent with those of the IVW method. The scatter plots and forest plots for effect sizes of SNPs for single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels and ASD were shown in Figure S1 and Figure S2.

Causal effects of ASD on single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels

Reverse MR analyses were then conducted to assess the causal effect of ASD on single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels. We screened 37 independent SNPs as IVs for ASD (F statistic > 10). Detailed information on IVs for ASD are listed in Table S2. Similarly, no potential causal association was found between ASD with single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels (Homocysteine levels: OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.63–1.16, P = 0.317; Vitamin B6 levels: OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.89–1.28, P = 0.469; Folate in Red Blood Cells: OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.87–1.1, P = 0.71; Folate in Serum or Plasma: OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.88–1.1, P = 0.759; VB12: OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.02, P = 0.83; VB6: OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01, P = 0.16; VC: OR = 0.99, 95% CI:0.97–1.02, P = 0.71; VD: OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.97–1.02, P = 0.57; and VE: OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.97–1.02, P = 0.73) (Table 2). The findings of the MR-Egger regression, Weighted Median, and Weighted Mode methods were consistent. The scatter plots and forest plots for effect sizes of SNPs for ASD with single carbon metabolism and vitamin levels are shown in Figure S5 and Figure S6.

Additionally, Cochran’s Q test revealed no significant heterogeneity (Table 3). The funnel plots are shown in Figure S7. The MR-Egger’s intercept and the MR-PRESSO did not reveal significant horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) (Table 3 and Table S3). The distortion test of MR-PRESSO analysis showed that the MR analyses had no outliers (Table S3). Furthermore, the leave-one-out test further confirmed the stability of our findings (Figure S8).

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the bidirectional associations between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk using bidirectional MR analysis. Our study did not observe evidence supporting that genetically predicted single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels were associated with ASD risk. The reverse MR analysis found that an increase in genetically predicted ASD risk was not associated with single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels. Based on multiple sensitivity analyses, our results are robust and reliable.

Previous studies have suggested that folate and other B vitamins play an important role in single-carbon metabolism, a pathway that regulates Hcy remethylation to methionine, which affects DNA methylation [11]. The epidemiological evidence shows inconsistencies. Although several studies have reported higher Hcy levels and lower B vitamin concentrations in children with ASD compared to controls [3, 34, 35], this may be related to the highly selective diet of children with ASD [36]. Previous observational studies have shown that appropriate supplementation of B vitamins can alleviate clinical symptoms in children with ASD [37]. However, a study of 30 children diagnosed with ASD showed no significant improvement in their clinical performance after 12 weeks of additional daily vitamin B12 supplementation [38]. The results of these controversial studies should be interpreted with caution at this time, as they mainly looked at the effects of therapeutic or small doses of B vitamin supplements. The effects of high-dose B vitamin supplements are unclear. Our study did not find a significant causal association between folate and ASD risk. However, Jin et al.’s MR Study maintains a causal relationship between serum vitamin B12 levels and an increased risk of ASD [11], which is inconsistent with our findings. The study differs from the GWAS data sources we used, and serum B vitamin levels do not reflect dietary intake. We also believe that metabolic and absorption pathways play an important role in influencing the outcome [39]. More efforts are needed in the future to build data of larger and multi-ethnic GWAS to build robust IVs to explore the relationship between different metabolites and ASD risk, and more epidemiological and RCT studies are needed to validate our results.

It is believed that vitamin D deficiency is common in children with autism, and vitamin D plays a role in regulating immunity, as well as increasing glutathione synthesis and gene stability [40]. Studies have shown that vitamin D supplementation can improve the behavior of children with autism, suggesting that vitamin D may be involved in the development of autism [40]. However, our study did not find a significant association between vitamin D and ASD risk. Additionally, Yu et al. did not find a causal association between genetically predicted 25(OH)D levels and ASD in a previous bidirectional Mendelian randomization study, which is consistent with our findings [41]. Considering the different doses of vitamin D supplementation and the influence of metabolic mechanisms, the protective effect of vitamin D supplementation on ASD needs to be interpreted with caution. In addition to this, the study ruled out a genetically predicted causal association between vitamins C and E and ASD risk. Vitamins C and E are important antioxidants that protect cells from oxidative stress [42]. Research suggests that oxidative stress may play a role in the pathogenesis of autism, but more research is needed to explore the specific mechanisms involved [42]. Further studies are needed to reasonably rule out confounding factors to further explore and confirm the link between multiple vitamin supplementation and ASD risk.

MR can reduce confounding bias and reverse causality inherent in traditional observational studies. Therefore, if MR findings, particularly those yielding null results that have been rigorously validated through sensitivity analyses, are inconsistent with observational evidence, it may suggest that the observed association is not causal but rather driven by confounding factors or reverse causation. Indeed, emerging evidence highlights complex interactions between dietary patterns, socioeconomic factors, and prenatal care in shaping vitamin status and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) risk. These elements intersect to influence both biological pathways and diagnostic trajectories. Children with ASD exhibit 5× higher rates of extreme food selectivity, leading to pervasive micronutrient deficiencies. Common deficiencies include vitamins A, D, and B12, as well as calcium and zinc, due to the avoidance of fruits, vegetables, and dairy [43, 44]. Children with ASD have a preference for processed carbohydrates and fatty foods that exacerbate deficiencies while increasing obesity risks [44]. Vitamin D deficiency correlates with immune dysfunction and neurodevelopmental abnormalities [45], while B vitamin deficits impair neurotransmitter synthesis [46]. Furthermore, socioeconomic status (SES) and disparities influence ASD outcomes through multiple pathways. In the USA, Black and Hispanic children receive ASD diagnoses 1.5 years later than White peers, partly due to limited healthcare access [47]. Lower SES households face higher rates of vitamin D deficiency and reduced intake of fortified foods [48]. Children from disadvantaged backgrounds receive fewer therapy hours and specialist referrals, compounding developmental challenges [47]. Finally, maternal nutrition during pregnancy modulates ASD risk. Early pregnancy use reduces ASD recurrence by 50% in high-risk families [49, 50]. Folic acid and vitamin D are particularly linked to neuroprotective effects [51]. Neonates with deficient vitamin D levels (< 20 ng/mL) face 54% higher ASD risk [45]. In addition, lower-income mothers are less likely to meet prenatal vitamin recommendations, highlighting inequities in preventive care [49]. Therefore, it is advisable that clinicians interpret observational studies more cautiously, taking into account potential confounding factors. Considering the vast impacts of vitamin deficiencies, even in the absence of a direct causal relationship on ASD, maintaining normal vitamin levels remains important for overall health. Still, efforts for ASD prevention and treatment may need to focus on other factors with stronger causal evidence.

The outstanding advantage of this study is that the implementation of the bidirectional MR method effectively reduces the interference of reverse causality and confounding factors. Firstly, we selected genetic variations that were randomly assigned as IVs to avoid the potential effects due to conventional confounders, as well as reverse causation common in observational studies. Secondly, we could more clearly determine the causal relationship and causal direction between two features by using a bi-directional MR. Moreover, our findings were reached after comprehensive studies using four MR methods, including IVW, weighted median, MR-Egger, and weighted mode, and several sensitivity tests demonstrated the robustness of MR analysis. Furthermore, compared with a previous MR study of the impact of vitamin levels on ASD risk [11], the present study used the latest Psychiatric Genomics Consortium ASD GWAS data, ensuring robust and up-to-date genetic information. Furthermore, even though the present MR study had null results, the robustness of the null findings is supported by extensive sensitivity analyses, indicating minimal bias from heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. Importantly, even null results have scientific value. Indeed, although the present study suggests no significant causal associations between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD, they provide valuable insights by challenging observational study-derived hypotheses and refining future research focus.

There are also some limitations in this study. Firstly, the GWAS summary level data associated with exposure and outcomes in this study came only from individuals of European descent, and although racial bias can be reduced when analyzed, the results may not be generalizable to all ethnicities. It was due to the availability of publicly available GWAS datasets. Still, it is true that large-scale surveillance studies in the USA consistently report that ASD prevalence is lower among White children compared with children from other racial and ethnic groups. For example, CDC data from 2022 showed the following prevalence per 1000 children aged 8 years: White, 27.7; Asian or Pacific Islander, 38.2; American Indian or Alaska Native, 37.5; Black, 36.6; Hispanic, 33.0; multiracial, 31.9. Similar patterns were observed in 2020 and 2021 data, with White children consistently having a lower prevalence than Black, Hispanic, and Asian children [52, 53]. Therefore, the results obtained from European populations cannot be generalized to other ethnicities without confirmation. It strongly calls for the completion of large-scale GWASs in non-European populations. Secondly, the SNPs selected in this study have the potential to be inconsistent or even contradictory in their effects on traits, making it difficult to select appropriate IVs to act as true proxies for exposure. Thirdly, this study mainly used GWAS data on vitamin concentration levels, and GWAS data on vitamin supplements can be further supplemented in subsequent analysis. Finally, the genetic evaluation of the effect of single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels on ASD in this study often ignores the influence degree of environmental factors, which may only partially explain the effect of single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels on ASD risk.

Conclusions

In summary, the present two-sample bidirectional MR Study suggests that no evidence of a causal association was found between single-carbon metabolism and vitamin levels with ASD risk. Further studies based on larger GWAS are needed to validate these conclusions.
