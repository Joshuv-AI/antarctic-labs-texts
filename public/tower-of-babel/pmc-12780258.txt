Maximization of bioactive potential of Fomes fomentarius via artificial Intelligence-Assisted optimization
Authors: Emre Cem Eraslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, in order to maximize the biological activity of the medicinal mushroom species Fomes fomentarius, extraction parameters were evaluated by Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Network-Genetic Algorithm (ANN-GA) hybrid optimization. The effects of extraction temperature, time and ethanol/water ratio on total antioxidant capacity (TAC) were modeled. As a result of the study, the optimum conditions were determined as 41.080 °C, 41.751 min and 49.149% ethanol/water ratio for RSM. For ANN-GA, the optimum conditions were determined as 39.848 °C, 39.689 min and 88.490% ethanol/water ratio. Extracts obtained under optimal conditions were compared in terms of antioxidant (FRAP, DPPH, TAS, TOS, OSI), anticholinesterase (AChE, BChE), antiproliferative (A549, MCF-7, DU-145) activities, and LC–MS/MS phenolic compound profiles. RSM extracts exhibited higher activities than ANN-GA in most parameters. Particularly high values were obtained for FRAP, DPPH, phenolic compound concentrations, and antiproliferative activity. The results demonstrated that the optimization approach was decisive in the bioactive compound profile and pharmacological potential of the extract. These results support the position of F. fomentarius as an important natural resource that can be used in pharmaceutical and functional food fields.

Introduction

Medicinal mushrooms are organisms found widely in nature and have been known for centuries to exert various beneficial effects on human health thanks to the bioactive compounds they contain. In many cultures, particularly in traditional Chinese and Japanese medicine, these mushrooms have been used as immune-boosting, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and anticancer agents1,2. Modern pharmaceutical research has reported that these effects of medicinal mushrooms are due to their numerous pharmacologically active compounds, particularly polysaccharides, triterpenoids, phenolic compounds, sterols, and protein-derived molecules3–5. However, it is known that the biological activities of mushroom species are directly dependent not only on the diversity of compounds present but also on the methods by which these compounds are obtained. Variables such as the extraction technique used, the type of solvent selected, the extraction temperature, and the processing time significantly affect the composition and biological activity of the resulting extract6. Therefore, in recent years, systematic optimization of extraction protocols has become a fundamental area of research in medicinal mushroom research.

Fomes fomentarius, commonly known as "tinder fungus," is a perennial polypore species that decays wood and possesses remarkable ecological and biotechnological properties. Its chemical composition includes various bioactive molecules, primarily phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, and sterols. The high antioxidant capacity of its ethanol and methanol extracts, in particular, makes this species a prominent natural antioxidant source7,8. Furthermore, novel sterol derivatives such as fomentarol A-D isolated from F. fomentarius have been reported to exhibit moderate cytotoxic effects on certain human cancer cell lines8. The biological activities of this species are not limited to antioxidant capacity; its ethanol, ethyl acetate, and chloroform fractions have been reported to exhibit antimicrobial activity against pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus9. Some of its compounds have been reported to have a blood glucose-lowering effect in diabetic animal models8,10. Ecologically, F. fomentarius plays a key role in the decomposition of dead wood, providing habitats for numerous microorganisms and microfauna11,12. Furthermore, its mycelium network is used in the production of biocomposite materials by combining it with agricultural waste. These materials also offer environmentally friendly alternatives to synthetic foams in thermal insulation and construction13,14. These versatile properties position F. fomentarius as a promising resource for both pharmaceutical biotechnology and sustainable materials production. Furthermore, more comprehensive clinical and applied research is needed to fully demonstrate its effects on human health and its potential for industrial application.

In this context, our study provides an additional overview of the biological components of the Fomes fomentarius mushroom and aims to maximize its potential. After determining the optimal extraction conditions, the antioxidant, anticholinesterase, antiproliferative activities, and phenolic contents of the optimally produced extracts were determined. The findings aim to reveal the widespread AI-assisted integration of Fomes fomentarius biological products and to determine the transport potential of this species for pharmaceutical applications. Although F. fomentarius is known to contain a diverse spectrum of bioactive molecules, the relationship between extraction conditions, phenolic composition, and resulting biological activities has not been systematically optimized using both statistical and artificial-intelligence based approaches. This gap limits the ability to maximize its pharmacological potential and prevents standardization of extract quality. Therefore, the present study aims to identify extraction conditions that maximize the bioactive potential of F. fomentarius using a dual-optimization strategy combining Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and an Artificial Neural Network-Genetic Algorithm (ANN-GA). We hypothesize that optimized extraction parameters will selectively enrich key phenolic compounds and consequently enhance antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative activities. The novelty of this research lies in (i) the first direct comparison of RSM and ANN–GA optimization frameworks for F. fomentarius, (ii) the integration of phenolic composition with activity-based outcomes, and (iii) the mechanistic linkage of optimized conditions to structure–activity relationships of quantified phenolics.

Materials and methods

The fungal samples were collected from Belgrad Forest (Istanbul, Türkiye; 41.1850° N, 28.9490° E), a mixed broadleaf forest ecosystem known for its diverse macrofungal flora. The taxonomic identification of the fungal material was performed based on macroscopic characteristics (pileus morphology, pore surface structure, context color and texture) and microscopic features (basidiospore size and shape) following standard diagnostic keys for Polyporales. The specimen was confirmed as Fomes fomentarius by Dr. Emre Cem Eraslan (Department of Biology, Osmaniye Korkut Ata University), a specialist in macrofungal taxonomy. A representative voucher specimen has been deposited in the Osmaniye Korkut Ata University Fungarium under the collection number ECE-366.

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical or LC–MS grade and obtained from certified suppliers. Enzymes, substrates, and inhibitors used in anticholinesterase assays, including acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, DTNB, acetylcholine iodide, butyrylcholine iodide, and galantamine, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Antioxidant analyses employed DPPH, TPTZ, FeCl₃·6H₂O, sodium acetate, acetic acid, hydrochloric acid, methanol, and dimethyl sulfoxide obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). TAS and TOS assay kits were obtained from RelAssay Diagnostics (Gaziantep, Turkey). Cell culture studies used MTT reagent, Trypsin–EDTA, and culture-grade DMSO purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). LC–MS/MS analyses utilized a broad panel of phenolic and bioactive standards supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), while LC–MS–grade methanol and formic acid were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ultrapure water was produced using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA).

Extraction procedure method

In this study, the effects of three key parameters were systematically investigated to maximize the extraction efficiency of samples of the species Fomes fomentarius. Mushroom samples collected from the Belgrade Forest in Istanbul in 2025 were transported to the laboratory, sliced, and dried in a fruit dryer (WMF KITCHENminis, Germany) at 45 °C for 48 h. Following drying, the samples were ground to powder in a mechanical grinder. For each experimental condition, 5 g of dried mushroom material was extracted using 100 mL of solvent. The experimental design defined three independent variables: extraction temperature, exposure time, and ethanol/water ratio. Each variable was tested at three different levels, creating a full factorial design encompassing a total of 27 conditions. Temperature levels were 30, 45, and 60 °C; exposure times were 30, 45, and 60 min; and solvent ratios were 0%, 50%, and 100% ethanol. All extraction processes were carried out in an ultrasonic bath (WIGGENS UA22MFDN, Germany) at 40 kHz, 100% power, and 400 W. The resulting extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and then the solvents were removed under vacuum at 40 °C. Each experimental condition was applied in triplicate (n = 3), and the resulting extracts were stored at + 4 °C for further analysis.

Data obtained from the experiments were analyzed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM). This method statistically examined the interactions between parameter combinations, and optimal processing conditions were determined. In addition to the modeling process, a hybrid optimization strategy was designed by integrating Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and Genetic Algorithm (GA) techniques to comprehensively evaluate the simultaneous effects of the parameters. This approach enabled a more comprehensive analysis of the complex relationships between variables and provided a strong scientific basis for artificial intelligence-based decision-making processes.

Modeling

Response surface methodology (RSM)

This study used the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to determine the parameters of the extraction process under optimal conditions. Within the experimental design, extraction temperature, application time, and the ethanol/water ratio were considered as independent variables. The Total Antioxidant Status (TAS) values of the obtained extracts were considered as the response variable.

Optimization studies were conducted using Design Expert 13 software, and analyses were based on a second-degree polynomial regression model. In this model, Yk is defined as the dependent variable (TAS value). The symbols Xi represent the coded independent variables representing the extraction temperature, extraction time, and solvent ratio, respectively. βk0 represents the constant term at the center point of the model. The statistical suitability and reliability of the model were evaluated in detail using basic statistical parameters such as the coefficient of determination (R2), analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the significance level (p-value). Critical points were calculated and analyzed using derivatives to determine the conditions that ensured the model’s optimal response. Furthermore, three-dimensional response surface plots were used to more clearly visualize the interactions between the independent variables, and the obtained findings were interpreted.

Artificial neural network–genetic algorithm (ANN-GA)

In this study, the Artificial Neural Network (ANN) method was used in the predictive model creation process. Extraction temperature, extraction time, and the ethanol/water ratio were determined as input variables. TAS values were used as output variables. The analysis process was structured by dividing the dataset into three groups: 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% test. The network was trained using the Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) algorithm. To optimize the network architecture, 20 different structures were tested, with the number of neurons in the hidden layer varying from 1 to 20. The learning coefficient and momentum values were set constant at 0.5. The maximum number of iterations was 500, the validation stopping limit was 50, and the error tolerance was 1 × 10−5. Each network configuration was evaluated with 1000 independent training sessions, and performance levels were compared.

The predictive power of the model was analyzed using two key statistical criteria: Mean Square Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The Genetic Algorithm (GA) approach was applied as the basic algorithm in the optimization process. To evaluate the effectiveness of the algorithm, different population sizes were tested, and the effects of these parameters on solution quality were systematically analyzed. The selection of individuals to be transferred to the next generation was performed using the probability-based roulette wheel selection method. Genetic crossover operations were implemented using the single-point crossover technique. The appropriate number of iterations was determined by examining the convergence plots, and each optimization cycle was repeated at least 50 times to increase the probability of reaching the global optimum solution.

The use of both RSM and ANN-GA was intentionally selected to provide a more comprehensive optimization framework. RSM offers a statistically structured and interpretable model that enables the evaluation of individual and interactive effects of extraction parameters. However, its predictive capability is limited when the system exhibits nonlinear or non-polynomial relationships. In contrast, the ANN-GA hybrid approach is capable of modeling highly complex, nonlinear patterns without requiring predefined functional relationships between variables. By comparing both approaches, the study aimed not only to determine optimal extraction conditions but also to evaluate the stability, predictive power, and biological relevance of each model. This dual-strategy design allows the extraction behavior of F. fomentarius to be interpreted from both a statistical perspective and a machine-learning-based perspective, providing a more reliable and generalizable understanding of the system.

Extraction for bioactivity tests

In this study, the optimum extraction conditions to maximize the biological activity of extracts obtained from mushroom samples were determined. Extracts were then produced with parameters closest to these conditions to determine their biological activity. Optimization using RSM yielded ideal conditions of 41,080 °C, 41,751 min, and a 49.149% ethanol/water ratio. The hybrid optimization approach, based on ANN-GA integration, yielded optimal values of 39,848 °C, 39,689 min, and an 88.490% ethanol/water ratio. Parameters closest to the values predicted by both methods were obtained using an ultrasonic bath (WIGGENS UA22MFDN, Germany) at 40 kHz frequency, 100% power, and 400 W capacity. Extracts obtained under these conditions were evaluated in the biological activity analyses conducted in the study.

Anticholinesterase activity tests

Anticolinesterase activities of mushroom extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions were evaluated using the colorimetric method developed by Ellman and colleagues15. Galantamine hydrobromide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used as a reference inhibitor for comparison in the analyses. For this purpose, stock solutions were prepared from mushroom extracts at concentrations ranging from 200 to 3.125 μg/mL. In the experimental setup, 130 μL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), adjusted to pH 8.0, was first added to each microplate well. Then, 10 μL of extract solution and 20 μL of acetylcholinesterase (AChE, Type VI-S, EC 3.1.1.7) or butyrylcholinesterase (BChE, EC 3.1.1.8) enzyme solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added. The mixtures were incubated at 25 °C for 10 min under dark conditions. After incubation, 20 μL of DTNB [5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid), Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA] solution and 20 μL of substrate (acetylcholine iodide or butyrylcholine iodide, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to each well to initiate the reaction. Absorbance values were measured using a microplate reader at 412 nm wavelength. According to the obtained data, enzyme inhibition rates were calculated and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were determined in μg/mL.

Antioxidant activity tests

Total antioxidant and oxidant analysis

The total antioxidant capacity of mushroom extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions was determined using the TAS Assay Kit (RelAssayDiagnostics, MegaTıp, Gaziantep, Turkey; Product Code: RL0017), and the results were expressed in mmol Trolox equivalents/L. The TOS Assay Kit (Rel AssayDiagnostics, MegaTıp, Gaziantep, Turkey; Product Code: RL0024) was used to determine total oxidant levels, and the data obtained from this analysis were evaluated as µmol hydrogen peroxide equivalents/L. The Oxidative Stress Index (OSI) value was calculated by dividing the TOS value by the TAS value and taking its percentage16–18.

DPPH free radical scavenging activity

The antioxidant capacities of mushroom extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions were evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging test. For this purpose, stock solutions of each extract were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). 1 mL of the prepared stock solutions were taken and homogeneously mixed with 4 mL of 0.004% methanol (Methanol, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) containing 160 µL of 0.267 mM DPPH solution (DPPH, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The resulting mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Following incubation, the absorbance values of the mixtures were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at a wavelength of 517 nm. The data obtained were calculated and expressed as mg Trolox equivalent/g extract (mg TE/g)19.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay

The ferric reducing capacities of mushroom extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions were determined using the FRAP (Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power) method. For this purpose, 100 µL of stock solutions were prepared from each extract and homogeneously mixed with 2 mL of FRAP reagent. The FRAP reagent was dissolved in 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6) (acetic acid and sodium acetate, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 40 mM HCl. Then, it was prepared by mixing 20 mM FeCl₃·6H₂O (Iron(III) chloride hexahydrate, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 10 mM 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solutions at a ratio of 10:1:1. The resulting mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 4 min and then the absorbance values were measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 593 nm. The results were expressed as mg Trolox equivalent/g extract (mg TE/g)19.

Antiproliferative activity test

The antiproliferative effects of optimized mushroom extracts were evaluated on three different human cancer cell lines: A549 (lung adenocarcinoma; ATCC CCL-185), MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma; ATCC HTB-22), and DU-145 (prostate carcinoma; ATCC HTB-81). All cell lines were supplied by the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Working solutions of the extracts were prepared at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. When cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were detached from the surface with 3.0 mL of Trypsin–EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. T3924) and transferred to appropriate culture plates. After the seeding process, a 24-h incubation was applied to allow the cells to attach to the surface. Then, the relevant extract solutions were added to the cultures, and incubation was continued for 24 h. When the incubation period was completed, the culture medium was removed and MTT solution [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. M5655] was added to each well at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The cells were incubated at 37 °C until purple formazan crystals formed. The crystals were dissolved with Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. D8418). DMSO was also used as a negative control group and was applied in equal volumes to all experimental groups. Absorbance values for cell viability were measured with an Epoch spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm19.

LC–MS/MS analysis

The phenolic compound profile of mushroom extracts obtained under optimized extraction conditions was analyzed using a Shimadzu LC–MS/MS-8030 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a SIL-20ACXR autosampler and a CTO-10ASvp column oven set at 40 °C. Chromatographic separation was performed on a SUPELCO Discovery C18 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 5 µm; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) operated under a binary gradient delivered by two LC-20ADXR pumps. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid + 20% water + 80% methanol, while mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid + 20% methanol + 80% water. The total flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min, the injection volume at 2 µL, and the maximum system pressure at 400 bar. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2.50 min, B = 100%; 2.50–4.00 min, B decreased to 40%; 4.00–7.00 min, B held at 40%; 7.00–7.01 min, B decreased to 20%; 7.01–8.00 min, B held at 20%; 8.00–9.00 min, B increased back to 100%; and 9.00–15.00 min, re-equilibration. Detection was carried out in MRM mode with optimized transitions for each analyte, and total run time was 15 min. In this study, LC–MS/MS profiling was performed using a targeted panel of 24 phenolic and bioactive molecules commonly reported in F. fomentarius and other medicinal mushroom species. The panel included phenolic acids (fumaric acid, gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, caffeic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, salicylic acid, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid), flavonoids (kaempferol, naringenin, quercetin, luteolin, catechin hydrate, myricetin), aromatic ketones and quinones (acetohydroxamic acid, thymoquinone, 2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, alizarin, curcumin), phenolic glycosides (phloridzin dihydrate, oleuropein), stilbenes (resveratrol), and polyphenolic structures such as silymarin. These compounds were selected due to their relevance to antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative mechanisms. Quantification was performed using external calibration curves prepared at five concentration levels (0.5–50 mg/L), yielding linear fits with R2 ≥ 0.998 for all analytes. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined using signal-to-noise criteria of 3 and 10, respectively. Each extract was injected in triplicate, and three independent technological replicates were analyzed to ensure reproducibility. Retention time stability (RSD < 1%) and peak area precision (RSD < 5%) confirmed method robustness, and representative chromatograms of mixed standards and optimized extracts were obtained to validate separation quality and analyte identification. Accurate mass confirmation was performed by comparing the theoretical m/z values of each analyte with the experimentally observed m/z values, reported with four-decimal precision (0.0000). Mass accuracy was calculated using the formula: ppm error = [(measured m/z-theoretical m/z) / theoretical m/z] × 10⁶.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the data obtained in this study were performed using SPSS 21.0 software, compatible with the Windows operating system. An independent samples t-test was used for pairwise group comparisons. One-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA) was applied to data sets containing three or more groups. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 to determine significant differences between groups in the ANOVA, and Duncan’s multiple range test was used for multiple comparisons. To more clearly support the comparative interpretation of biological activities, all FRAP, DPPH, TAS, TOS, OSI, anticholinesterase (AChE/BChE), and antiproliferative assays were statistically analyzed using independent samples t-tests for pairwise comparisons between RSM- and ANN–GA–derived extracts. Significant differences were considered at p < 0.05. The majority of bioactivity endpoints (FRAP, DPPH, AChE, BChE, and cell viability at 100 and 200 µg/mL) exhibited statistically significant superiority of the RSM extract. Parameters without statistical differences (e.g., TAS) were also explicitly reported to ensure transparency. These statistical results corroborate the claim that extraction conditions optimized via RSM enriched bioactive phenolics more effectively and consequently produced stronger biological performance.

Extraction studies show that different temperatures, times, and solvent ratios have significant effects on TAS values. In particular, it was observed that the extract obtained using 100% ethanol at 30 °C and 45 min reached the highest TAS value of 8.227 ± 0.031 mmol/L. This suggests that the use of a high percentage of organic solvent at low temperatures can significantly increase the solubility of antioxidant compounds. However, the fact that the TAS level decreased to 6.648 ± 0.021 mmol/L at the same temperature only when the extraction time was extended and the solvent consisted entirely of water suggests that prolonged extraction time has a negative effect, especially in water-based systems. A decreasing trend in TAS values was observed when the extraction temperature was increased to 60 °C, and the lowest TAS value (6.448 ± 0.024 mmol/L) was measured after 60 min despite the use of 100% ethanol. This provides evidence supporting the degradative effect of thermal stress on phenolic structures. On the other hand, the data obtained at the intermediate temperature level of 45 °C exhibited a more balanced distribution. TAS values ranged from 6.814 ± 0.032 to 8.150 ± 0.013 mmol/L. The 8.150 ± 0.013 mmol/L value obtained at this temperature with a 45-min time period and 50% ethanol use indicates that maximum yield can be achieved without the need for high temperatures if conditions are optimized. When all the findings are evaluated together, high ethanol content and moderate extraction time play a critical role in preserving and maximizing antioxidant potential. Conversely, excessive time and temperature applications stand out as suppressive factors for this potential.

In this study, two different optimization methods were applied based on experimental data. Linear, two-factor interactive (2FI), quadratic, and cubic regression models were comparatively analyzed within the RSM. As a result of the statistical evaluations, it was determined that the quadratic model, with an R2 value of 0.981, demonstrated the highest fit with the data set. This R2 value indicates that 98.1% of the total variance observed in the dependent variable is explained by the independent variables included in the model, supporting the validity of the model. This high coefficient of fit demonstrates that the model accurately represents the relationships between variables and successfully reflects the patterns among observations. Consequently, it confirms that the quadratic model can produce reliable and predictable outputs during the optimization process.

In the AI-assisted optimization process, the ANN algorithm was used to model experimental data. Based on evaluations conducted on different architectures, the structure that provided the highest prediction accuracy was integrated with a GA for further optimization processes. In this context, the ANN model with a 3–4-1 architecture and six neurons in a single hidden layer was determined to be the most suitable structure with superior performance criteria. The accuracy of the relevant model was statistically confirmed with a mean square error (MSE) of 0.009, a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 1.069%, and a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.984. Anticholinesterase activity

Mushrooms are important natural resources with various pharmacological effects due to the bioactive molecules they contain, such as terpenoids, phenolic compounds, alkaloids, and polysaccharides. Some secondary metabolites among these compounds exhibit the ability to inhibit the enzymes acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE)20. In our study, it was determined that the anticholinesterase activity of F. fomentarius extracts varied depending on the optimization approach used. The extract obtained with RSM had higher inhibitory capacity with lower IC₅₀ values for AChE (IC₅₀: 59.65 ± 0.86 µg/mL) and BChE (IC₅₀: 99.13 ± 0.91 µg/mL) activities compared to the extract obtained with ANN-GA (AChE: 70.71 ± 1.18 µg/mL; BChE: 113.90 ± 1.79 µg/mL). In our study, although the reference inhibitor galantamine (AChE: 7.86 ± 0.12 µg/mL; BChE: 15.76 ± 0.27 µg/mL) provided much more potent inhibition than expected, mushroom extracts also appear to have significant enzyme inhibitory potential. The findings obtained in our study are consistent with literature reports that ethanolic and hydromethanolic extracts from three different F. fomentarius strains exhibit AChE inhibition. Rašeta et al.21 emphasized that the extracts exhibited significant AChE inhibition and that the strain has pharmaceutical potential for biological targets related to neurodegenerative diseases. However, different results are also available in the literature. Dundar et al.22 reported that F. fomentarius did not inhibit AChE, but did inhibit BChE. This difference may be due to variability in the geographic origin, developmental stage, extraction method, solvent type, and analysis conditions of the mushroom samples used.

The results of our study indicate that the RSM method increases the extraction efficiency of bioactive molecules, particularly those that can enhance AChE and BChE inhibition, such as phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Although the ANN-GA method offers parametric diversity, it may not be as effective as RSM in recovering these specific compound groups. From an optimization perspective, the RSM method appears more advantageous when the goal is to maximize AChE/BChE inhibition, while ANN-GA may be considered when targeting different compound profiles in multi-component products. In conclusion, the F. fomentarius used in our study was determined to have high anticholinesterase activities.

In our study, two different optimization approaches (RSM and ANN-GA) were comparatively evaluated for the antioxidant capacity of optimized extracts of F. fomentarius. The results revealed that the optimization method used had significant effects on the antioxidant activities of the extract. FRAP analysis revealed that the extract obtained by the RSM method (174.60 ± 1.49 mg TE/g) showed a higher ferric reducing capacity compared to the ANN-GA extract (164.03 ± 1.99 mg TE/g). Similarly, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity was found to be higher in the RSM extract (140.16 ± 1.40 mg TE/g) than in the ANN-GA extract (128.33 ± 1.51 mg TE/g). This suggests that the combinations of temperature, time, and solvent ratio determined in the RSM method can increase extraction efficiency by preserving the stability of phenolic compounds and other redox-active molecules.

Although no significant difference was observed between the two methods in the TAS analysis (RSM: 8.305 ± 0.014 mmol/L; ANN-GA: 8.143 ± 0.013 mmol/L), these values were well above the value of 3.270 mmol/L reported for F. fomentarius in the literature (Bal et al., 2017). In terms of TOS, the ANN-GA extract (18.344 ± 0.052 µmol/L) was found to be higher than the RSM extract (17.194 ± 0.030 µmol/L). In addition, both values were significantly higher than the value of 2.601 µmol/L reported by Bal et al.25. Similarly, the OSI values in our study (RSM: 0.207 ± 0.001; ANN-GA: 0.225 ± 0.001) were above the 0.080 value reported by Bal et al.25. These differences indicate the potential of the optimization-based extraction strategies used in our study to increase the yield of both antioxidant and oxidant metabolites, especially phenolic compounds.

In studies on different wild mushroom species in the literature, the TAS value of Paralepista flaccida was reported as 4.054 mmol/L, the TOS value was reported as 10.352 µmol/L, and the OSI value was reported as 0.2555. Lactarius deliciosus has a TAS value of 7.468 mmol/L, a TOS value of 13.161 µmol/L, and an OSI value of 0.17626. Hericium erinaceus has a TAS value of 5.426 mmol/L, a TOS value of 6.621 µmol/L, and an OSI value of 0.12227. Cantharellus cibarius has a TAS value of 5.511 mmol/L, a TOS value of 7.289 µmol/L, and an OSI value of 0.13228. When compared with these studies, it is shown that the TAS values obtained in our study are higher than many wild mushroom species, but the OSI values are similar to or below those of P. flaccida. In conclusion, in our study, the RSM method appears to be more advantageous in terms of phenolic compounds and radical scavenging capacity, while the ANN-GA method can create different oxidant-antioxidant balance profiles while preserving total antioxidant capacity. This suggests that F. fomentarius has a high potential for use in functional food and pharmaceutical formulations, and the optimization strategy should be carefully selected based on the targeted biological activity profile.

Antiproliferative activity

Polysaccharides such as β-glucan found in mushrooms have been reported to enhance the immune response against tumor cells by activating the immune system. Furthermore, some phenolic and terpenoid compounds in mushrooms may exert direct antitumor effects through mechanisms such as cell cycle arrest, apoptosis induction, and metastasis prevention. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that medicinal mushroom species can exhibit antiproliferative, cytotoxic, and apoptotic effects on various cancer cell lines29,30. Extracts obtained by both optimization methods caused a significant decrease in cell viability with increasing concentrations. Extracts obtained by RSM method exhibited stronger proliferation suppression compared to ANN-GA extracts in all three cell lines, especially at higher concentrations (100 and 200 µg/mL). This suggests that RSM parameters (temperature, time, and solvent ratio) may have increased the extraction efficiency of phenolic acids, flavonoids, and other bioactive compounds that contribute to the cytotoxic/antiproliferative effect. Although the ANN-GA method demonstrated significant antiproliferative activity, it lagged behind RSM extracts at most concentrations. In the control groups, cell viability was highly preserved, while DMSO application had no significant effect on proliferation. This finding confirms that the observed effect is directly due to the mushroom extracts. The results obtained in our study are consistent with the cytotoxic activities of F. fomentarius reported in the literature on various cancer and normal cell lines. Previous studies have reported that this species exhibits antiproliferative or cytotoxic effects against gastric adenocarcinoma (MKN45, AGS), lung adenocarcinoma (A549), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (KYSE-30), bladder carcinoma (5637), murine fibroblast (L929), colon adenocarcinoma (Caco-2, SW742), and cutaneous melanoma (COLO-818) cell lines25,31–33. This broad spectrum of biological activities supports the remarkable potential of F. fomentarius among the candidate natural materials in cancer treatment. When the findings of our study are evaluated from an optimization perspective, the RSM method, which maximizes the recovery of phenolic and other biologically active compounds, stands out for its targeted high antiproliferative effect. The ANN-GA method, on the other hand, offers potential for multicomponent formulations with its ability to generate diverse bioactive profiles. Consequently, it has been determined that optimized extracts of F. fomentarius may be among the potential natural anticancer agent candidates.

The findings of our study indicate that the optimization approach resulted in significant differences in the amounts of phenolic compounds. The extract obtained with the RSM method was generally found to have higher phenolic concentrations compared to the ANN-GA extract. Especially, protocatechuic acid (RSM: 11,591.01 ± 1.32 mg/kg; ANN-GA: 10,215.77 ± 1.05 mg/kg), 2-hydroxycinnamic acid (RSM: 7411.86 ± 6.77 mg/kg; ANN-GA: 5181.71 ± 1.76 mg/kg) and caffeic acid (RSM: 2912.26 ± 0.79 mg/kg; ANN-GA: 2155.94 ± 1.22 mg/kg) amounts were found to be significantly higher in the RSM optimization. On the other hand, the ANN-GA method showed a slight superiority in fumaric acid recovery (RSM: 1180.20 ± 1.84 mg/kg; ANN-GA: 1203.17 ± 1.24 mg/kg). Comparisons with the literature reveal the significance of these differences. Nowacka et al.36 reported the presence of protocatechuic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and salicylic acid in Fomes fomentarius extracts. They also reported that ferulic acid was present in trace amounts, while vanillic and syringic acids were not detected. These findings reveal that the phenolic concentrations obtained in our study were significantly higher than those obtained by conventional methods. In addition, Dundar et al.22 reported the presence of protocatechuic acid, syringic acid, caffeic acid, vanillic acid, o-coumaric acid, p-coumaric acid, catechin, and quercetin in F. fomentarius extracts. When our study was evaluated in terms of the common compounds detected in this study, the amounts of each compound were significantly higher. This difference demonstrates that optimization-based extraction (especially RSM) is more efficient in the recovery of phenolic compounds than conventional methods. Considering the contribution of phenolic compounds to biological activity, high concentrations of compounds such as protocatechuic acid, gallic acid, caffeic acid, and catechin are among the potential explanations for the strong antioxidant and anticholinesterase activities observed in our study37–40. Our study demonstrates that RSM is appropriate for studies aimed at maximizing phenolic profiles. It also demonstrates that the ANN-GA method can be evaluated in formulations targeting specific organic acids and different bioactive profiles.

Linking extraction conditions, chemical composition, and biological activity

Comparative evaluation of extraction conditions revealed that both solvent composition and thermal parameters directly shape the phenolic profile of F. fomentarius, which in turn strongly influences its biological activities. In particular, phenolic acids such as protocatechuic acid41, gallic acid39, caffeic acid42, and 2-hydroxycinnamic acid43, which are compounds with well-documented redox potentials and hydrogen-donating properties, were significantly higher under RSM-optimized conditions. Their structural features, including hydroxyl substitution patterns and conjugated aromatic systems, are strongly correlated with ferric reducing capacity and free radical scavenging44, explaining the higher FRAP and DPPH activities of RSM extracts. Similarly, anticholinesterase activity can be attributed to the presence of phenolics that can interact with the enzyme active site. Compounds such as catechin hydrate, luteolin, quercetin, and kaempferol possess structural motifs known to inhibit AChE/BChE via reversible binding45,46, consistent with the more potent inhibition observed in RSM extracts. Antiproliferative effects also appear to be linked to elevated levels of caffeic acid, protocatechuic acid, and flavonoids, which have been reported to induce apoptosis and oxidative stress-induced cytotoxicity in cancer cells39,41,42. These findings suggest that optimized extraction parameters not only increase yield but also selectively enrich bioactive phenolics that drive the pharmacological properties of the extracts. Previous studies on F. fomentarius have predominantly focused on conventional solvent extraction or polysaccharide-enriched fractions, with limited attention to multi-parameter optimization and systematic comparison of extraction strategies. Reports by Nowacka et al.36, Dundar et al.22, and Rašeta et al.21 describe antioxidant, anticholinesterase, or cytotoxic properties, yet none integrate statistical modeling with machine-learning-based optimization or link extraction efficiency directly to structure–activity relationships. The present study addresses this gap by providing the first side-by-side evaluation of RSM versus ANN-GA optimization for F. fomentarius and demonstrating how parameter-dependent enrichment of specific phenolics mechanistically explains variations in bioactivity. This integrated approach offers a novel methodological framework and contributes a more predictive, data-driven perspective to the phytochemical investigation of medicinal mushrooms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the biological activity of F. fomentarius can be effectively enhanced through systematic, artificial intelligence-assisted optimization of extraction parameters. A comparative assessment of RSM and ANN–GA showed that RSM yielded higher total phenolic content and stronger antioxidant activity (FRAP and DPPH), whereas ANN–GA preserved total antioxidant capacity and provided improved recovery of selected organic acids. These complementary outcomes highlight the importance of applying multiple optimization approaches to complex natural matrices. Anticholinesterase assays revealed pronounced inhibitory effects on both AChE and BChE enzymes, whereas antiproliferative analyses confirmed that RSM-derived extracts produced more substantial proliferation suppression in A549, MCF-7, and DU-145 cell lines. Consistent with LC–MS/MS profiling results, optimization strategies markedly increased the yields of key phenolic acids and flavonoids relative to non-optimized extraction methods. These compositional enhancements directly contributed to the observed biological activities. Overall, the findings suggest that F. fomentarius exhibits noteworthy pharmaceutical, neuroprotective, and anticancer potential based on in vitro assays. Our findings highlight that optimization strategies should be tailored to the specific biological activity of interest, such as maximizing antioxidant capacity or enhancing enzyme inhibition efficiency. In line with our initial research hypothesis, optimized extraction conditions selectively enriched bioactive phenolics, thereby enhancing antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative properties.
