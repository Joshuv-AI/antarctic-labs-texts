Detection of bovine coronavirus in myocardial tissue of a naturally infected calf
Authors: Violetta Iris Vasinioti, Adriana Lo Giudice, Marco Cordisco, Maria Stella Lucente, Valeria Buonfrate, Canio Buonavoglia, Paolo Capozza, Nicola Decaro, Annamaria Pratelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronaviruses are RNA viruses with high genomic plasticity and with the ability to infect a wide range of animal hosts, including humans. Among them, the highly pathogenic SARS-CoV-2, a betacoronavirus, is the causative agent of severe respiratory infection, and has also been associated with extrapulmonary manifestations, including cardiac involvement. BCoV, another betacoronavirus, is a major pathogen of cattle, primarily affecting the respiratory and enteric systems. Herein, a case of death in a calf with clinical signs consistent with BCoV infection is described. Potential cardiac involvement was further investigated, motivated by evidence of SARS-CoV-2 associated cardiac pathology in humans. Pulmonary and cardiac tissues were subjected to molecular and histological analyses. BCoV RNA was detected in both heart and lungs while immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed BCoV antigen presence in both tissues. Cardiac detection was mainly observed in the conduction system and the endothelial cells. This study provides the first evidence of cardiac detection of BCoV and lays the foundation for future research on the closely related human coronavirus OC43, contributing to the One Health approach.Keywords: BCoV, Immunohistochemistry, Antigen, Conduction system, Endothelial cells

Introduction

Coronaviruses (CoVs) are members of the family Coronaviridae, within the order Nidovirales, that can infect a broad range of mammalian and avian hosts. They are enveloped viruses with a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genome of approximately 26–32 kb length, which encodes four, or in some species five, structural proteins [1]. Among these, the spike (S) protein is the most divergent and plays a critical role during the early stages of infection as it mediates viral attachment and fusion with the host cell membrane [2]. The evident genomic plasticity of CoVs, characterized by high mutation and recombination rates, facilitates the emergence of novel variants with enhanced potential for cross-species adaptation [3].

CoVs are classified in four distinct genera, namely Alpha-, Beta-, Gamma-, and Deltacoronavirus [4]. Betacoronavirus genus encompasses several human CoVs (HCoVs), including the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2; species Betacoronavirus pandemicum), which is responsible for the recent coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. SARS-CoV-2 is transmitted primarily through respiratory droplets or aerosols, as well as indirectly through contaminated environment and objects. Airborne transmission contributed to the rapid global spread of the virus, causing severe consequences for public health [5]. Beyond respiratory disease, SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with a wide range of extrapulmonary manifestations that can result in multi-organ dysfunction [6]. The virus binds to angiotensin-converting enzyme-2 (ACE2) receptors, which are highly expressed on the alveolar epithelial type II cells [7]. ACE2 receptors are also expressed, albeit at lower levels, in cardiac, renal, endothelial, and intestinal tissues, which may explain the broad spectrum of clinical manifestations observed in patients with COVID-19 [8].

Early evidence from the pandemic suggested that SARS-CoV-2 may cause several cardiac abnormalities, including myocardial damage and myocarditis [9]. Whether cardiac injury is caused directly by viral invasion of cardiac tissue, or arises secondarily through indirect mechanisms such as cytokine storm and atypical inflammatory responses, remains uncertain [10], [11]. Several studies have described cases of COVID-19-associated myocarditis, largely diagnosed based on non-invasive approaches, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) findings and abnormal laboratory markers [12]. Interestingly, SARS-CoV-2 RNA has been occasionally detected in heart tissues obtained from intraoperative biopsies or post-mortem autopsy samples using reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and in situ hybridization (ISH) techniques [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. In addition, immunohistochemical evidence of SARS-CoV-2 proteins' expression in heart tissues has also been reported [19], [20], [21]. In addition to infection-related effects, myocarditis has been described as result of COVID-19 mRNA (messenger RNA) vaccination in some cases [22], [23], [24].

Bovine coronavirus (BCoV, species Betacoronavirus gravedinis) is another member of the Betacoronavirus genus, characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates. BCoV is shed in feces and nasal secretions and can cause respiratory and enteric disease in cattle and other ruminants [25]. The virus was initially identified as the causative agent of severe diarrhea in calves and adult cattle [26], [27]. Subsequent studies demonstrated its involvement in the bovine respiratory disease complex (BRDC) and the shipping fever of feedlot cattle [28], [29], [30]. Different factors are involved in the interaction of BCoV with the host cells. To date, it is believed that BCoV uses the S protein and the hemagglutinin esterase (HE) to bind to the 5-N-acetyl-9-O-acetylneuraminic acid on epithelial cell surfaces, thereby starting infection [31], [32]. Interestingly, a recent study reported molecular and immunohistochemical detection of BCoV in the liver and kidneys of experimentally infected calves, suggesting that BCoV may exhibit a broader tissue tropism beyond the pneumonoenteric system [33]. The potential dissemination of BCoV to other organs beyond the primary sites of infection appears analogous to the systemic involvement described for SARS-CoV-2. Indeed, BCoV and SARS-CoV-2, as betacoronaviruses, share several key characteristics. In addition to similarities in pathogenic mechanisms, comparative analysis of major antigenic epitopes within the S and the nucleocapsid (N) proteins has revealed high levels of homology between the two viruses [34], [35], [36].

The present study aimed to describe a case of death in a calf presenting respiratory clinical signs consistent with BCoV infection. Given the documented cardiac SARS-CoV-2 involvement in human cases, we further investigated the potential cardiac localization of BCoV by assessing its presence in the myocardium of the affected calf.

Materials and methods

Case description

In November 2025, a morbidity case involving a 2-month-old Friesian calf was reported in a cattle feedlot located in the Apulia region of southern Italy. Prior to death, the animal exhibited marked depression and a tendency to isolate itself from the herd. Clinical examination revealed ptyalism, neck hyperextension, and a body temperature ranging between 39.0 and 39.5 °C. As the clinical condition progressed, increased respiratory distress became evident, characterized by tachypnea and abnormal respiratory sounds. With disease persistence, the animal developed inappetence followed by progressive weight loss, culminating in cachexia and ultimately death.

Post-mortem examination and sample collection

The animal underwent post-mortem examination 24 h after death. Tissue samples from lungs, heart, liver, intestine and kidney, were collected using separate, sterile forceps and scalpels for each organ and washed three times with Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). To avoid potential surface contamination, internal tissue segments were aseptically excised for histological and molecular analyses targeting respiratory pathogens, including BCoV. Lung tissue was prioritized for the initial screening due to the respiratory nature of the case. In case of positive findings, heart tissues were analyzed in a subsequent step.

Diagnostic techniques

Nucleic acids extraction and molecular detection of respiratory pathogens

An investigation of viral and bacterial pathogens associated with BRDC was conducted. The viral pathogens analyzed included BCoV, bovine herpesvirus 1 (BoHV-1), bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV), bovine parainfluenza virus (BPiV), and bovine adenovirus (BAdV). The bacterial pathogens investigated were Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, Histophilus somni and Mycoplasma bovis.

Tissue samples were processed using a Tissue Lyser (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). Homogenization was carried out in 2 mL Eppendorf safe-lock tubes containing 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS) and a 4.8 mm stainless-steel bead, operated at 30 Hz for 5 min. Homogenates were then centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 3 min. Subsequently, a total of 200 μL of each supernatant were subjected to nucleic acid extraction using a IndiSpin Pathogen Kit (Indical Bioscience GmbH, Leipzig, Germany), according to the manufacturer's instruction and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Viral and bacterial pathogens were detected by quantitative PCR (qPCR) as previously described by Pratelli et al. [37]. For RNA viruses (BCoV, BRSV, and BPiV), complementary DNA (cDNA) was first synthesized through reverse transcription using random hexamers and MuLV reverse transcriptase (GeneAmp® RNA PCR, Applied Biosystems) in a 10 μL reaction volume. Subsequently, qPCR was conducted for both cDNA and extracted DNA samples to detect the target viral and bacterial agents. Specific primers and TaqMan probes were used as previously reported [38], [39]. Reactions were performed in a 25 μL total volume using IQ™ Supermix (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial activation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10s and annealing and extension at 56 °C for 30s. Data acquisition and analysis were carried out using the i-Cycler iQ™ Real-Time Detection System and its associated software.

Histopathology

For histological examination, samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Following fixation, tissues were routinely processed to obtain formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples. Subsequently, sections 4 μm-thick were cut, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for evaluation of pathological changes.

Immunohistochemistry assay

Additional FFPE sections were prepared as described above and were mounted on poly–L–lysine–coated slides for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. Slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in a microwave oven at low temperature for 20 min in pH 9 buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by protein block with horse serum for 10 min. Sections were incubated overnight with the anti-nucleoprotein bovine coronavirus primary antibody (MBCV-10 A-9G10; ICL Inc., Portland, OR, USA 97223) at 7 °C at a dilution of 1:100. After washing in PBS, IHC sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by streptavidin, for 10 min each and visualized with DAB for 4 min. Two negative controls were used for this immunohistochemical analysis. In the first negative control, a section of positive myocardium was processed with omission of the primary antibody. In the second negative control, myocardial tissue known to be negative was incubated with the antibody, and its negativity was verified. These controls were used to confirm that the positivity observed in the myocardium of the studied case was not due to nonspecific or artifactual staining.

Immunofluorescence assay

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis was performed on deparaffinized cardiac and pulmonary tissue sections. Both sample types were tested for the presence of BCoV antigen by an IF assay using the same monoclonal antibody for BCoV nucleoprotein used for the IHC assay (MBCV-10 A-9G10; ICL Inc., Portland, OR, USA 97223) and a mouse anti-goat IgG conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (Sigma Aldrich srl, Milan, Italy). Similarly to IHC, two negative controls were also used for the IF analysis to rule out non-specific or artifactual staining, as described above.

Results

The carcass was well preserved, with minimal autolysis. The body condition score was 2.5/5. Upon skinning, a poor nutritional status was evident, with marked depletion of adipose tissue reserves. The most significant lesions were observed in the thoracic cavity, where pleural adhesions were present in the cranioventral lobes region. This area was diffusely firm and dark red. On cut surface, multifocal areas exuded dense, whitish-yellow material consistent with necropurulent exudate. The caudal lobes were pale pink with prominent lobular patterning and variably sized translucent spaces compatible with interstitial emphysema.

Molecular analyses of lung tissue revealed positive results for BCoV, H. somni and M. bovis. No other viral or bacterial pathogen associated with BRDC was detected in the lungs. Subsequent PCR testing of the heart tissue yielded positive results for BCoV and H. somni whereas M. bovis was not detected.

On H&E examination of the lung, a bronchiolocentric inflammatory process was evident. The bronchiolar lumen and epithelium were infiltrated by neutrophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells (Fig. 1B). Occasional epithelial cells exhibited hypereosinophilic cytoplasm and pyknotic and hypersegmented nuclei, consistent with necrosis. The interstitium was diffusely expanded by edema. A mild interstitial infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils was also present. Multifocally, similar inflammatory infiltrates extended into adjacent alveoli. In the heart, the overall parenchymal architecture was preserved. Blood vessels were diffusely congested. Multifocally, small numbers of mature lymphocytes and plasma cells were present in the perivascular region (Fig. 1A).

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed mild, multifocal labelling in in cells morphologically consistent with components of the cardiac conduction system (Fig. 2A). The immunoreactivity was cytoplasmic and uniformly distributed within the cells of the conduction system. Occasional granular labelling was also observed in endothelial cells. Specifically, endothelial cells exhibited multifocal cytoplasmic positivity in granules of variable size (Fig. 2C).

(B) Immunofluorescence (H&E; 20×): Cells of the cardiac conduction system show a similar multifocal cytoplasmic green fluorescence consistent with BCoV positivity.

Finally, tissue section slides were examined under an inverted immunofluorescence microscope. Cytoplasmic fluorescence was observed in cells from both lung and heart tissues, indicating positive immunoreactivity for the BCoV N protein. In cardiac sections, immunofluorescence was mainly detected in the same cells described for IHC (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

In the present study molecular analysis confirmed the presence of BCoV in the lungs and heart of a calf died with respiratory signs. BCoV antigen was detected in both tissues by immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence.

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first report describing cardiac positivity for BCoV in the heart tissue. While cardiac positivity and myocarditis have been well documented for SARS-CoV-2, involvement of the cardiac conduction system has been only rarely described [40], [41]. Herein, immunoreactivity was detected within the conduction tissue in the absence of histological evidence of myocarditis. Although the presence of the virus in the cardiac conduction system could potentially impair cardiac function, the absence of functional cardiac data, electrocardiographic findings, or markers of active viral replication, precludes establishing any association with arrhythmias or sudden death. Importantly, the subject presented with more severe respiratory pathologies and ultimately died of respiratory failure due to extensive pulmonary involvement.

The interpretation of these cardiopulmonary findings should consider the presence of concurrent pathogens. In this case, M. bovis was detected in the lung, while H. somni was present in both the lung and heart. Both are key contributors to bovine respiratory disease, and H. somni is known to disseminate systemically and affect vascular structures via endothelial damage [42], [43], [44]. Although BCoV immunolabeling was strongly associated with the conduction system and endothelial cells, suggesting a non-random distribution, the lack of pathogen-specific co-localization studies (e.g., bacterial IHC) prevents exclusion of a possible role for bacterial infection in influencing viral distribution or contributing to the observed tissue changes. Therefore, while the highly specific localization of BCoV antigen within cells of the conduction system suggests a distinct—though not yet fully understood—role of viral presence in cardiac tissue, these findings should be interpreted in the context of a mixed infection.

Notably, this study also highlights endothelial cell positivity for BCoV, which, to the authors' knowledge, has not previously been reported. Although this finding is subject to several limitations and its biological significance is not yet fully understood, endothelial labelling has also been described in SARS-CoV-2 infection [45]. These observations further emphasize the similarities between the two viruses.

It is important to highlight that the presence of BCoV in both the conduction system, as already described, and endothelial cells was not associated with myocarditis. However, as mentioned in the histologic description, the myocardium showed a mild, focally extensive perivascular lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate. Based solely on histological and immunohistochemical evaluation, this finding does not appear to be associated with BCoV; rather, it is more likely related to the severe H. somni infection affecting the subject.

Moreover, certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although the diagnostic tools employed were previously validated, their specificity was not independently reassessed for potential cross-reactivity with other bovine pathogens, including phylogenetically related or unrelated viruses. Therefore, non-specific reactions cannot be completely excluded. In addition, while qPCR and IHC confirmed the presence of BCoV RNA and antigen, the absence of quantitative comparisons between pulmonary and cardiac viral loads, together with the lack of assays targeting replicative intermediates (e.g., negative-strand RNA) or virus isolation, precludes confirmation of active viral replication within the myocardium. Consequently, these findings support the presence of BCoV in cardiac tissue but do not provide definitive evidence of cardiotropism or productive infection. These limitations highlight the need for further investigations on a larger case series, incorporating experimental infections and approaches such as viral sequencing or in situ hybridization, to better define systemic dissemination and clarify the potential significance of BCoV's cardiac involvement. Additionally, a broader tissue inclusion will be necessary to fully characterize viral distribution.

Beyond SARS-CoV-2, BCoV also shows remarkable similarity to another human betacoronavirus, HCoV-OC43. Interestingly, molecular clock analyses suggested that a 290-nucleotide deletion downstream of the spike gene in BCoV facilitated a zoonotic transmission event from cattle to humans, leading to the emergence of HCoV-OC43 in the 1890s [46]. Indeed, HCoV-OC43 shares a high degree of antigenic and genetic similarity with BCoV, with up to 96% nucleotide identity [47]. Moreover, both BCoV and HCoV-OC43 use 9-O-acetylated sialic acids as receptors for viral entry [48]. While studies on the exact distribution of these receptors in the heart are still emerging, recent data has confirmed their presence in the cardiac tissue of a murine model [49]. This finding may help explain the viral localization observed in this calf, however, further studies are needed to confirm the distribution and functional role of these receptors in both animal and human hearts. HCoV-OC43 is currently recognized as one of the endemic HCoVs, commonly associated with mild respiratory infections in humans [50]. The present study provides evidence of antigenic detection of BCoV in cardiac tissue. Considering the close evolutionary relationship, common viral entry receptors and antigenic overlap between BCoV and HCoV-OC43, these findings raise an important question regarding the tissue tropism of HCoV-OC43 in humans. However, current evidence for cardiac involvement of HCoV-OC43 in humans remains extremely limited. To date, only isolated case reports have suggested a possible association with cardiac manifestations, and no systematic clinical or histopathological studies have demonstrated a consistent cardiotropic pattern for this virus [51], [52], [53]. Therefore, extrapolation of the present findings to HCoV-OC43 should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further studies are therefore warranted to investigate the potential cardiotropic properties and associated clinical relevance of HCoV-OC43. Specifically, future efforts should prioritize clinical investigations to assess cardiac abnormalities in patients with confirmed HCoV-OC43 infection, alongside in vitro modeling using human cardiac or endothelial cell lines. Moreover, exploring the presence of HCoV-OC43 in the heart, in cases of unexplained cardiac deaths would facilitate the systematic molecular and histopathological collection of data to clarify its role in cardiac pathology.

To date, no other reports describing the cardiac detection of BCoV are available in the literature. This lack of evidence likely stems from sampling bias, as BCoV is primarily known as a pneumonoenteric pathogen and the myocardium is not routinely screened. In this study, our investigation of cardiac tissue was specifically prompted by the documented cardiotropism of SARS-CoV-2 and its known similarities to BCoV. The identification of the virus within the heart highlights a previously unexplored aspect of BCoV dissemination, potentially influenced by viral load, stage of infection or co-pathogens like H. somni. These findings, along with recent reports of BCoV in the kidney and liver [33], suggest that BCoV can be detected in more tissues than previously thought.

In conclusion, this study expands current knowledge on BCoV's tissue distribution, by demonstrating molecular and subsequently antigenic positivity in the heart, including the cardiac conduction system and endothelial cells. Although the clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain, they highlight important similarities between BCoV and SARS-CoV-2. Moreover, from a One Health perspective, this study contributes to a better understanding of betacoronaviruses and provides a foundation for future investigation on HCoV-OC43, a widespread HCoV.

Ethical statement

The authors confirm that this study complied with the ethical guidelines for the use of animal tissues in research. This study did not involve experimental procedures on live animals. The animal included in this report was a male 2-month-old Friesian calf found dead in a cattle feedlot prior to any veterinary intervention. No animals were euthanized or harmed specifically for the purpose of this study. A full post-mortem examination was conducted by licensed veterinarians from the Department of Veterinary Medicine, University of Bari Aldo Moro as part of routine diagnostic activities, and tissue samples were collected for microbiological analysis. As no procedures were performed on a living animal, ethical approval for animal experimentation was not required under EU Directive 2010/63. Written authorization for the use of the carcass for diagnostic purposes was obtained from the owner and is available from the corresponding author upon request.
