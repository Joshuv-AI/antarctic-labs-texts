Stability and Accuracy Trade-offs in Statistical Estimation
Abhinav Chakraborty Yuetian Luo, Rina Foygel Barber

Abstract
Algorithmic stability is a central concept in statistics and learning theory that measures how sensitive an algorithm’s output is to small changes in the training data. Stability plays a crucial role in understanding generalization, robustness, and replicability, and a variety of stability notions have been proposed in different learning settings. However, while stability entails desirable properties, it is typically not sufficient on its own for statistical learning—and indeed, it may be at odds with accuracy, since an algorithm that always outputs a constant function is perfectly stable but statistically meaningless. Thus, it is essential to understand the potential statistical cost of stability. In this work, we address this question by adopting a statistical decision-theoretic perspective, treating stability as a constraint in estimation. Focusing on two representative notions—worst-case stability and average-case stability—we first establish general lower bounds on the achievable estimation accuracy under each type of stability constraint. We then develop optimal stable estimators for four canonical estimation problems, including several mean estimation and regression settings. Together, these results characterize the optimal trade-offs between stability and accuracy across these tasks. Our findings formalize the intuition that average-case stability imposes a qualitatively weaker restriction than worst-case stability, and they further reveal that the gap between these two can vary substantially across different estimation problems.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, stability has emerged as a central principle in modern data science. At a high level, algorithmic stability expresses the idea that the output of an algorithm should not change massively under a slight perturbation to the input data. Intuitively, it is a desirable property for an algorithm on its own—but it also leads to many downstream implications, as well.
For instance, one of the most important and early implication of stability is that stable algorithms generalize
(
Rogers and Wagner, 1978
;
Devroye and Wagner, 1979
;
Kearns and Ron, 1997
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
;
Poggio et al., 2004
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010
)
. Stability also plays a critical role in a number of other problems, such as model selection
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2010
;
Yu, 2013
;
Shah and Samworth, 2013
;
Ren et al., 2023
)
, differential privacy
(
Dwork and Lei, 2009
;
Dwork et al., 2014
)
, predictive inference
(
Steinberger and Leeb, 2023
;
Barber et al., 2021
;
Liang and Barber, 2025
)
, adaptive and selective inference
(
Dwork et al., 2015a
;
Zrnic and Jordan, 2023
)
and inference for cross-validation estimate of model and algorithm risks
(
Austern and Zhou, 2025
;
Bayle et al., 2020
;
Kissel and Lei, 2022
;
Luo and Barber, 2024b
)
. Stability is also one of three foundational principles of the PCS framework—Predictability, Computability, and Stability—introduced in
Yu and Kumbier, 2020
for veridical data analysis. Finally, stability is closely related to many other notions in learning theory and statistics, such as learnability, replicability and sensitivity
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010
;
Impagliazzo et al., 2022
;
Kalavasis et al., 2023
;
Trillos et al., 2025
)
.

Tailored to different downstream tasks, numerous notions of stability have been proposed in the literature. We refer readers to
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010
;
Kim and Barber, 2023
and the references therein for a few important notions. There are also a few algorithms which are known to satisfy various stability notions. A few canonical examples include
k
-nearest neighbors (kNN)
(
Devroye and Wagner, 1979
)
, ridge regression
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
)
, and stochastic gradient descent
(
Hardt et al., 2016
;
Lei and Ying, 2020
)
. Despite its long history in the literature, there is still a lack of a systematic understanding of the relation between different stability notions. This leads to our first motivating question:

What is the connection between different stability notions, and can we make a quantitative comparison between them?

At the same time, when we consider using stable algorithms for our tasks, another important question we need to address is what level of stability we want. Is more stability necessarily better? In fact, while stability has a number of appearing features, it is typically not the whole story in a statistical learning context. For example, in the seminar work of
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
, stability was leveraged as an important tool to bound the generalization error of a predictor. Typically, as the predictor becomes more stable, it can also have smaller generalization error; however, this does not necessarily imply it has smaller test error or excess risk
(
Chen et al., 2018
)
, which is the ultimate goal we care about in the prediction task. A naive but extremely stable predictor is the constant predictor, which ignores the whole dataset and could have a huge test error. Similarly, in the predictive inference context, algorithmic stability enables a jackknife-based predictive interval to have distribution-free coverage guarantee
(
Barber et al., 2021
)
. This is a surprising result as, without stability, jackknife-based predictive intervals can have severe undercoverage. On the other hand, in most practical settings, we also want the predictive interval to be short so that it can be practically useful. Unfortunately, it is not guaranteed that boosting stability would yield a shorter predictive interval. In fact, the reverse implication is more likely to be true, i.e., as the algorithm becomes more stable, the predictor is forced to become more and more like a constant predictor, and as a result, the corresponding statistical efficiency will also degrade. These considerations lead to our second key question:

Is there any trade-off between stability and statistical utility, and if yes, can we characterize it?

The main goal of the current paper is to provide a sharp understanding of these trade-offs in the setting of different notions of stability.

Trade-offs and constraints in statistical decision theory.

Statistical decision theory has served as a classical approach to assessing the utility of a statistical estimator in the frequentist sense. Classical statistical decision theory aims to look for an estimator with the smallest risk among all possible estimators. However, this classical approach can suffer from a significant deficiency nowadays, as modern data analysis is complex and often has many constraints. Motivated by that, people have started to incorporate different additional desiderata in statistical estimation, other than a small risk. Encouragingly, over the past decade or so, a flurry of research has been devoted to developing statistical decision theory under various practical constraints, and many of them have even been employed in practice. This includes but is not limited to computational constraints
(
Berthet and Rigollet, 2013
;
Zhang et al., 2014
;
Ma and Wu, 2015
;
Brennan and Bresler, 2020
)
, communication constraints
(
Zhang et al., 2013
;
Garg et al., 2014
;
Braverman et al., 2016
)
, and privacy constraints
(
Bassily et al., 2014
;
Dwork et al., 2015b
;
Duchi et al., 2018
;
Barber and Duchi, 2014
;
Cai et al., 2021
)
. In this work, we introduce another desiderata, stability, into this family. Specifically, we take a statistical decision theory point of view, treating stability as a type of constraint in statistical inference tasks. Our focus lies on characterizing the effect of stability in statistical estimation and the fundamental trade-off between stability and accuracy.

1.1
Our Contributions

Building our work on the classical minimax theory, in this paper, we provide a formal framework to compare different notions of stability and characterize the effect of stability as a constraint in statistical estimation. We focus on two general notions of stability considered in the literature: worst-case stability (or uniform stability) and average-case stability. We first provide a couple of general techniques for deriving minimax lower bounds under these types of stability constraints. These bounds are useful in that they not only characterize the “statistical cost of stability”, but they also allow us to compare different stability notions and stable estimators.

Based on our lower bounds results, we also develop optimal stable estimators, meaning that they preserve the maximum amount of statistical utility for a given stability level, across a number of canonical estimation problems, including bounded mean estimation, heavy-tailed mean estimation, sparse mean estimation, and nonparametric regression function estimation. These results together help us to identify the optimal stability and accuracy trade-off curves in these estimation tasks.

From our results, we confirm the intuition that average-case stability is, in general, a qualitatively weaker constraint than the worst-case stability in statistical estimation. But we also find that the degree of the difference between these two stability notions can vary significantly across different problems. For example, in Figure

Background and Problem Formulation

In this section, we begin by reviewing the classical minimax framework, and then introduce the notions of stability studied in this paper and the minimax framework under stability constraints.

2.1
Classical Minimax Framework

Suppose we have a class of distributions
\mathcal{P}
with support
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\theta(P)\in\Theta
denotes a functional defined on
\mathcal{P}
. The space in which the parameter
\theta(P)
takes values depends on the underlying statistical model, but in this paper, we consider
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a fixed but unknown distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
and a data set
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
consisting of
n
i.i.d. data drawn from
P
, our goal is to estimate the target parameter
\theta(P)
. Formally, an estimator of
\theta(P)
is a measurable function
\widehat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\Theta
, returning an estimate
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)
, where
\xi\in[0,1]
could be viewed as an (optional) random seed to allow randomness in the estimator. In order to assess the quality of any estimator, we let
\rho:\Theta\times\Theta\to[0,\infty)
be some non-negative measure of the error on the parameter space
\Theta
, and consider the associated risk function:

R_{n}(\theta(P),\widehat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}[\rho(\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi),\theta(P))],

where implicitly, the expected value is taken with respect to the distribution of
\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim P^{\otimes n}
and
\xi\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]
.
Throughout the paper, we will mainly focus on the mean-square risk, i.e.,
\rho(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{2}^{2}
. In the classical minimax setting, for each estimator
\widehat{\theta}
, we can compute its worst-case risk
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}R_{n}(\theta(P),\widehat{\theta})
, and rank estimators based on this quantity. An estimator
\widehat{\theta}
is called rate optimal (or simply optimal) if it can achieve the minimax rate up to a constant factor,
R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\widehat{\theta})\lesssim R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}))
, where the minimax rate is defined as

R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})):=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}R_{n}(\theta(P),\widehat{\theta})=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}.

2.2
Worst-case and Average-case Stability

Generically speaking, the various notions of stability in the literature can be classified into two big categories: worst-case stability (or uniform stability) and average-case stability. Worst-case stability describes that the output of the procedure is insensitive to the change of any given data point in the data set. So the influence of every data point should be low to satisfy worst-case stability. A few common worst-case-stable estimators have been identified in the literature
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
;
Hardt et al., 2016
)
, but it can be hard to achieve in many other scenarios. For example,
Xu et al., 2011
showed that any algorithm that is constrained to obey sparsity cannot be worst-case-stable. This motivates researchers looking for relaxed stability notions that are still useful in the downstream statistical inference. Average-case stability is one such notion, which is based on the average perturbation on the output of the procedure if we replace one data point at random: this requires that, on average, the influence of each data point is low. Bagging
(
Breiman, 1996
)
is a method that can achieve average-case stability in fairly general settings
(
Elisseeff et al., 2005
;
Soloff et al., 2024
)
.

Now, let us define these notions of stability precisely. First, we consider worst-case stability.

Definition 1
.

Given any estimator
\widehat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\Theta
, we say
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies worst-case stability with parameter
\beta_{n}
(or, equivalently, we say
\widehat{\theta}
is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable) if

\begin{split}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime};\xi)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n},\end{split}

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, and the notation
\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
means
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}
differ by only one data point (i.e.,
d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\leq 1
).

Note that the expectation in Definition
1
is only taken with respect to the random seed
\xi
, while the data could be arbitrary. Worst-case stability is also known as ‘global sensitivity’ in the differential privacy (DP) literature. In particular, the celebrated Laplace mechanism adds Laplace noise proportional to the global sensitivity to construct differentially private estimators
(
Dwork, 2008
)
. In Section
8.1
, we will provide a detailed exploration of the connection and difference of DP as a constraint compared to stability.

Next, we turn to average-case stability.

Definition 2
.

Given any estimator
\widehat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\Theta
, we say
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies average-case stability with parameter
\beta_{n}
(or, equivalently, we say
\widehat{\theta}
is
\beta_{n}
-average-case-stable) if

\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n},

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
denotes a data set of size
n+1
, and the notation
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i}
denotes the data set obtained by deleting the
i
th data point from
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
.

Note again that in (
3
), the data set could be arbitrary, but the influence of each data point on the estimator is averaged out. The existing notions of average-case stability are typically defined based on dropping one data point at random
(
Soloff et al., 2024
)
. In practice, we expect the two definitions to be essentially equivalent. We refer readers to Appendix
A
for more details.

In general, the worst-case and average-case stability can be unified under the following notion of
\ell_{p}
-stability.

Definition 3
.

Given any
p\in[1,\infty]
and an estimator
\widehat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\Theta
, we say
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies
\ell_{p}
-stability with parameter
\beta_{n}
(or, equivalently, we say
\widehat{\theta}
is
\beta_{n}
-
\ell_{p}
-stable) if

\left\{\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)\|^{p}_{2}\right\}^{1/p}\leq\beta_{n}.

(4)

(For the case
p=\infty
, we should interpret this definition as taking a limit as
p\to\infty
, i.e., we require

\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n}
.)

Note that when
p=\infty
,
\ell_{p}
-stability reduces to worst-case stability (we can view
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
in (
2
) as
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j}
in (
4
)), while when
p=1
, this definition reduces to average-case stability. By definition,
\ell_{p}
-stability implies
\ell_{q}
-stability whenever
p\geq q
. In this paper, we will mainly focus on
p=\infty
and
p=1
, i.e., worst-case stability and average-case stability, but in some examples we extend our results to general
p
.

Finally, we note that in the literature, there are other types of average-case stability where the average is over the randomness of the data. We will briefly discuss those notions and make a comparison in Section
8.2
.

2.3
Stability-Constrained Minimax Risks

Given the notions of stability of interest, we are now ready to define stability-constrained minimax risk:

\begin{split}R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n}):=\inf_{\widehat{\theta}\textnormal{ is $\beta_{n}$-$\ell_{p}$-stable}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Notice that given any
p\in[1,\infty]
,
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
is a nonincreasing function with respect to
\beta_{n}
, and given any
\beta_{n}
,
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
in a nondecreasing function of
p
.
When
p=\infty
or
1
,
R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
and
R_{n,1}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
, respectively, denote the minimax risk under the worst-case and average-case stability constraint. In addition, when
\beta_{n}\to\infty
, the constraint on stability becomes vacuous, and
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
reduces to the classical minimax risk (
1
). The main goal of this paper is to characterize
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
as a function of
\beta_{n}
across different
p
’s and different problems, identify optimal estimators for achieving these rates, and compare how worst-case stability and average-case stability differ as a constraint in statistical estimation tasks.

We conclude this section by introducing some terminology to describe the phase transition patterns for the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curves, i.e.,
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
. We will use this terminology to discuss and compare our findings throughout the paper.

Definition 4

(Sharp vs. gradual phase transition)
.

Given a stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
, we say it has a sharp phase transition at
\beta_{n}^{*}
if
R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\asymp R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),0)
when
\beta_{n}\ll\frac{\beta_{n}^{*}}{\textnormal{poly}\log n}
and
R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\asymp R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\infty)
when
\beta_{n}\gg\beta_{n}^{*}\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log n
. Otherwise, we say it has a gradual phase transition.

A sharp phase transition means that a certain stability budget is critical for this estimation task: if we require stronger stability below the critical threshold, then the best estimator we can hope for is a trivial constant estimator; while, as long as we only need stability above the critical threshold, then we can find an estimator that achieves the unconstrained minimax rate. In other words, we can get
\Omega(\beta_{n}^{*})
level stability for free while still maintaining the optimal performance, but when
\beta_{n}=o(\beta_{n}^{*})
, nontrivial estimation is impossible, and there is no trade-off between stability and accuracy. When the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve has a gradual phase transition, then we instead see a smoother trade-off between these two quantities.

General Tools for Lower Bounding
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})

In this section, we present two general results on lower-bounding the stability-constrained risk. Let us begin with a lower bound under worst-case stability.

Theorem 1
.

For the statistical estimation setting considered in Section
2.1
under the worst-case stability constraint, we have

R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\geq\sup_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{(\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})]\beta_{n})_{+}}{2}\right]_{+}^{2}\vee R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})),

(5)

where in the expected value, the joint distribution of
(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})
could be any coupling between
P_{1}^{\otimes n}
and
P_{2}^{\otimes n}
.

Notice that the first term on the right-hand side of (
5
) appears due to the stability constraint. In a typical setting, the two terms
\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})]\beta_{n}
have a trade-off, as when
\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}
increases, more likely, the Hamming distance between
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
will also increase. The proof idea of Theorem
1
is inspired by the classical Le Cam’s two-point method for lower bounding the minimax risk (
1
), but it also differs in an important way. The classical Le Cam’s method first reduces the unconstrained minimax risk to a two-point hypothesis testing problem and then lower bounds the testing risk. But in our context, it is not clear how to incorporate the stability constraint into the testing problem. To overcome that obstacle, here, instead of reducing to testing problems, we directly look at two data sets
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
with i.i.d. data drawn from two distributions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, respectively. Then, for any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
that is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}[d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})],\end{split}

(6)

where the expectation is taken with respect to any given coupling between
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
. At the same time,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}&=\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P_{1})+\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})+\theta(P_{2})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}\\
&\geq\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta(P)\|_{2}.\end{split}

(7)

Since (
6
) and (
7
) hold for any
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable estimator, combining these bounds yields a lower bound for
R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
. The detailed proof of Theorem
1
will be provided in Appendix
D
.

Now, we move on to the statistical lower bound under the general
\ell_{p}
-stability constraint. When
p<\infty
, the bound in (
6
) no longer holds, but we can modify this analysis to provide a different upper bound on
\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}
using the
\ell_{p}
metric; we defer details to the Appendix. This next result provides a generic lower bound for
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
.

Theorem 2
.

Given any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\geq\sup_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{(\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-(n+1)(\log n+1)^{1/p}\beta_{n})_{+}}{2}\right]^{2}\vee R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})).

(8)

If, in addition the functional of interest
\theta(P)
is linear in
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then for any
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
(1-t)P_{1}+tP_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
for all
t\in[0,1]
,

\begin{split}&R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\geq R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}))\vee\\
&\sup_{\eta\in[0,1/2]}\left(\frac{(1-2\eta)\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2^{1/p}(n+1)\beta_{n}\left(\frac{1-2\eta+\frac{p}{2(n+1)}}{(2\eta)^{1/p}}\wedge(n+1)^{1/p}\right)}{2}\right)^{2}_{+}.\end{split}

(9)

A corollary of Theorem
2
for linear functionals under the average-case stability, i.e.,
p=1
, is given as follows.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
\theta(P)
is linear in
P
and
p=1
. Then for any
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
(1-t)P_{1}+tP_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
for all
t\in[0,1]
,

R_{n,1}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2(n+1)\beta_{n}\right)_{+}^{2}\vee R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})).

Since
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
in a nondecreasing function of
p
, Corollary
1
immediately suggests that when the parameter space is unbounded and connected, then
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
is infinite for any
p\geq 1
unless
\beta_{n}=\infty
. A similar phenomenon has also appeared in statistical estimation under the pure differential privacy constraint, where the task is only possible when the parameter space
\Theta
is bounded
(
Bun et al., 2021
)
. Thus, for the rest of the article, we will be mainly interested in the setting where the parameter space
\Theta
is bounded.

Next, we will show how Theorem
1
and
2
can be used to derive sharp lower bounds in a number of common statistical estimation tasks, including bounded mean estimation, heavy-tailed mean estimation, sparse mean estimation, and nonparametric regression. For bounded mean estimation and sparse mean estimation, we are able to provide matching upper and lower bounds for
R_{n,p}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
under the general
\ell_{p}
-stability for all
p
, while for the other two examples, we will focus on
p=\infty
and
p=1
, i.e., the worst-case stability and the average-case stability.

4
Mean Estimation with Bounded Support

In this section, we study the first of our four examples: the problem of estimating the mean of a distribution, in a bounded setting.
Let

\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)=\{\textnormal{distributions }P\textnormal{ supported on }\mathbb{B}_{2}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

be the class the distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with support on
\mathbb{B}_{2}(r)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|_{2}\leq r\}
, a
\ell_{2}
-ball with radius
r
. Without any stability constraint, it is easy to check that the minimax risk for mean estimation in this family is of rate

R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P}))\asymp\frac{r^{2}}{n},

and it can be achieved by the sample mean. Under the stability constraint, we have the following result which establishes a sharp phase transition of
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
at
\beta_{n}^{*}=\frac{r}{n}
for any
p
.

Theorem 3
.

Given any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\begin{split}R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{r^{2}}{n}&\text{ if }\beta_{n}\geq\frac{2r}{n}\\
r^{2}&\text{ if }\beta_{n}\leq\frac{r}{10n}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

One estimator that achieves this rate is the shrinkage estimator
\left(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{2r}\wedge 1\right)\widebar{X}
, where
\widebar{X}
is the sample mean.

In Theorem
3
, we can see that the phase transition of
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
happens around
\beta_{n}\asymp\frac{r}{n}
for all
p
. This suggests that stability can be obtained ‘for free’ for mean estimation in this class at level
\beta_{n}\gtrsim r/n
(in fact, in this case, we may simply use the sample mean as the estimator), while for smaller
\beta_{n}
, nontrivial estimation is impossible.

Next, we show that for this simple class, we can derive a more refined characterization of how the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve behaves when
\beta_{n}\in[\frac{r}{10n},\frac{2r}{n}]
in the special setting when
d=1
.

4.1
Refined Characterization When
d=1

Our first refined result is on characterizing the exact location where the phase transition occurs.

Theorem 4
.

Given any
p\geq 1
and any fixed constant
\delta>0
, there exists a sufficiently large
C>0
depending on
\delta
only such that as long as
n\geq C
,

\begin{split}R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{r^{2}}{n}&\text{ if }\beta_{n}\geq\frac{2^{1-1/p}\cdot r}{n}\\
r^{2}&\text{ if }\beta_{n}\leq\frac{2^{1-1/p}\cdot r}{n(1+\delta)}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

One estimator that achieves this rate is
\left(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{2^{1-1/p}\cdot r}\wedge 1\right)\widebar{X}
.

Theorem
4
shows that if we zoom in, we can see that different
p
’s in
\ell_{p}
-stability do have a different effect on the exact location where the phase transition happens. For example, the exact location where the phase transition happens shifts from
\beta_{n}^{*}=\frac{r}{n}
in
\ell_{1}
-stability to
\beta_{n}^{*}=\frac{2r}{n}
in
\ell_{\infty}
-stability.

Our second refined result is to further characterize how does
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
transit from
r^{2}
to
r^{2}/n
in the narrow phase transition regime. We will focus on the problem when
p=\infty
and
p=1
. In particular, when
p=\infty
, we will provide an exact formula for
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
over the entire range of
\beta_{n}
; and when
p=1
, we are also able to capture the optimal rate of
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
over the entire range of
\beta_{n}
. Perhaps surprisingly, we will see that if we look for optimal estimators in this narrow regime, then the naive shrinkage estimator is actually not enough.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose
d=1
, then we have

\begin{split}R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)=\left(\frac{\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{2}r^{2}\vee\frac{r^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{n}+1\right)^{2}},\end{split}

and one optimal estimator, which achieves the above equality, is given by
\widehat{\theta}=\left(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{2r}\wedge\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)\bar{X}
.

When
\beta_{n}\leq\frac{r}{10n}
and
\beta_{n}\geq\frac{2r}{n}
, we can see the results in Theorem
3
can be reduced from Theorem
5
. But Theorem
5
also tells us what happen when
\beta_{n}\in[\frac{r}{10n},\frac{2r}{n}]
. In particular, for any
\delta_{n}\geq 0
, Theorem
5
implies that

R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\frac{2r}{(1+\delta_{n})n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{2}}{n}&\text{ if }\delta_{n}\leq n^{-1/2}\\
\delta_{n}^{2}r^{2}&\text{ if }n^{-1/2}<\delta_{n}\leq 1\\
r^{2}&\text{ if }\delta_{n}>1.\end{array}\right.

(10)

At the same time, we can also characterize the optimal rate of
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
for all ranges of
\beta_{n}
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
d=1
. There exists a large universal constant
C>0
such that for any
n\geq C
, we have
1
1

1

Throughout the paper, the notation
R_{n,p}(\cdot,\beta_{n})\asymp f(n,\beta_{n})
means that there are universal constants
0<c<C
independent of
\beta_{n}
such that
cf(n,\beta_{n})\leq R_{n,p}(\cdot,\beta_{n})\leq Cf(n,\beta_{n})
.

R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\left(\frac{\left(\frac{r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{3}r^{2}\vee\frac{r^{2}}{n},

and one optimal estimator is

\widehat{\theta}=\left(1-\left(\left(\frac{r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}\wedge 1\right)\cdot\left(\frac{2r\sqrt{\left(\frac{r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}+\frac{2r}{\sqrt{n}}}{|\bar{X}|}\wedge 1\right)\right)\bar{X}.

(11)

As we have already see in Theorem
4
, the exact phase transition location of
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
is lower than the one of
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{bound}}),\beta_{n}\right)
. Similarly, given any
\delta_{n}\geq 0
, Theorem
6
shows

R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\frac{r}{(1+\delta_{n})n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{2}}{n}&\text{ if }\delta_{n}\leq n^{-1/3}\\
\delta_{n}^{3}r^{2}&\text{ if }n^{-1/3}<\delta_{n}\leq 1\\
r^{2}&\text{ if }\delta_{n}>1.\end{array}\right.

(12)

Compared with (
10
), we can see that not only does the location of phase transition change in
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
comparing with
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
, the estimation error rate also becomes strictly faster, i.e., shifting from
\delta_{n}^{2}r^{2}
to
\delta_{n}^{3}r^{2}
, near the phase transition location under the average-case stability constraint. A pictorial illustration on the difference of
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
and
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
is provided in Figure
1
(a). Finally, we would also like to comment on the novelty of the optimal estimators in Theorems
5
and
6
. Under worst-case stability, we first note that
\frac{\bar{X}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}
is the estimator that can achieve the exact unconstrained minimax risk. To modify it to be stable, the basic idea is that if
\beta_{n}
is greater than the worst-case stability level of
\frac{\bar{X}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}
, we just use
\frac{\bar{X}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}
, but if we require the estimator to be more stable than that, we can further shrink the sample mean until it achieves the desired stability level. Under average-case stability, following this strategy, a naive extension of the estimator would be
\widetilde{\theta}=\left(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{r}\wedge 1\right)\bar{X}
, which is qualitatively the same as

\widetilde{\theta}=\left(1-\left(\frac{r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}\wedge 1\right)\bar{X}

(13)

if we ignore the exact constant in the error rate. Then
\widetilde{\theta}
can be shown to be
\beta_{n}
-average-case-stable. However, in general, when
\beta_{n}=\frac{r}{(1+\delta_{n})n}
for some
\delta\in(n^{-1/3},1]
, the bias of
\widetilde{\theta}
is of order
\delta_{n}^{2}r^{2}
, which is bigger than the desired
\delta_{n}^{3}r^{2}
error rate. What we find, surprisingly, is that we can shrink the sample mean less aggressively when the magnitude of
|\bar{X}|
is large while still maintaining the same level of average-case stability. As a result, the bias of the new estimator can be reduced to
\delta_{n}^{3}r^{2}
. A pictorial illustration of the difference between
\widehat{\theta}
and
\widetilde{\theta}
under average-case stability constraint is provided in Figure
2
. The main technical work here lies in checking that even for this data-driven shrinkage, the new estimator can still achieve the desired
\frac{r}{(1+\delta_{n})n}
average-case stability.

A pictorial illustration on the shrinkage factor of our estimator
\widehat{\theta}
(

), and the naive estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
(

) relative to the sample mean, given average-case stability
\beta_{n}=\frac{r}{(1+\delta_{n})n}
for some
\delta_{n}\in[0,1]
.

5
Heavy-tailed Mean Estimation

While the bounded setting considered in the previous section offers sharp theoretical results, in many practical settings the data does not lie in a bounded domain, and it is common to encounter heavy-tailed distributions
(
Catoni, 2012
)
. In this section, we consider heavy-tailed mean estimation. Let

\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)=\{\textnormal{distributions }P\textnormal{ supported on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\textnormal{ with }(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|X\|_{2}^{k}])^{1/k}\leq r\}

be the class of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
k
th moment bounded by
r^{k}
, where
k\geq 1
is fixed. Without any stability constraint, when
k\geq 2
, the minimax risk for mean estimation in this family is of rate
\frac{r^{2}}{n}
and the optimal estimator is the sample mean
(
Devroye et al., 2016
)
. When
k\in[1,2)
, the minimax risk for mean estimation in this family is of rate
\frac{r^{2}}{n^{2(1-1/k)}}

(
Devroye et al., 2016
)
and this rate can be achieved by the truncated mean
(
Bubeck et al., 2013
)
. At the same time, a trivial error rate is
r^{2}
as
\|\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\|_{2}\leq r
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)
. Under a stability constraint, we show that the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
has a gradual phase transition, while
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
still has a sharp phase transition.

Theorem 7
.

Given any
k\geq 1
,

(a)

Under worst-case stability (i.e.,
p=\infty
),

R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\frac{r^{2k}}{(n\beta_{n})^{2(k-1)}}\wedge r^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{n^{1\wedge 2(1-1/k)}}.

One optimal estimator is given by the truncated mean,
\widehat{\theta}=\widebar{Y}
, where
Y_{i}=X_{i}{\mathds{1}}(\|X_{i}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{k})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and where the truncation level is given by
\rho_{k}=\frac{n\beta_{n}}{2}\wedge rn^{1/k}
for
k\in[1,2)
and
\rho_{k}=\frac{n\beta_{n}}{2}
for
k\geq 2
.

(b)

Under average-case stability (i.e.,
p=1
),

R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{2}}{n^{1\wedge 2(1-1/k)}}&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\geq\frac{24r}{n}\\
r^{2}&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\leq\frac{r}{10n}.\end{array}\right.

One estimator that achieves the above rate is given by
\widehat{\theta}=\left(\frac{\beta_{n}n}{24r}\wedge 1\right)\widebar{\theta}
where

\widebar{\theta}=\left(1\wedge\frac{2nr}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}\|_{2}}\right)\bar{Y}.

(14)

Again,
Y_{i}=X_{i}{\mathds{1}}(\|X_{i}\|_{2}\leq\rho_{k})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, but now the truncation level is given by
\rho_{k}=rn^{1/k}
for
k\in[1,2)
and
\rho_{k}=\infty
for
k\geq 2
.

Comparing with Theorem
3
, we can see that the worst-case stability constraint has a more severe effect on heavy-tailed mean estimation. In particular, while
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\frac{2r}{n}\right)\asymp\frac{r^{2}}{n}
, now we instead have
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\frac{2r}{n}\right)\asymp r^{2}
. In terms of estimators, in contrast to the bounded class, here since the range of
X
is unbounded, we have to truncate each data point to guarantee worst-case stability, and the optimal truncation level also depends on the stability requirement.

Comparing the Part (a) and Part (b) result in Theorem
7
, we can see that
R_{n,1}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
and
R_{n,\infty}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}_{k}(r)),\beta_{n}\right)
have a completely different phase transition behavior: the former one is sharp while the latter one is gradual (see a pictorial illustrate in Figure
1
(b)). In particular, a
\frac{24r}{n}
-level average-case stability is enough to obtain the unconstrained minimax rate, while it is impossible to estimate the heavy-tailed mean under the same level worst-case stability constraint. This confirms the intuition that average-case stability is indeed a qualitatively much weaker constraint than the worst-case stability.

Finally, we would like to comment on the difference between the estimators under worst-case and average-case stability in Theorem
7
. If we consider the sample mean, then

\begin{split}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\sup\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}+\|X_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{n}.\end{split}

Thus to guarantee worst-case stability, we have to truncate each data point, with a truncation level that corresponds to the stability requirement. In contrast, under the average-case stability constraint, the sample mean has the following guarantee:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j})\|_{2}&\leq\frac{2}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n+1}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}+\|X_{j}\|_{2}}{n}\\
&=\frac{2}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n+1}\|X_{i}\|_{2}\right).\end{split}

(15)

Therefore, as long as
\sum_{1\leq i\leq n+1}\|X_{i}\|_{2}
is bounded, then we can control the average-case stability. That motivates the self-normalized shrinkage strategy in the estimator (
14
), where we use the sample mean if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}\|_{2}
is small, and normalize only if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|Y_{i}\|_{2}
is large. Note that when
k\in[1,2)
, we still need to additionally truncate each data point, as that is needed for minimax optimality even when there is no stability constraint.

Sparse Mean Estimation

In many modern applications, the dimension
d
of the observed data may be larger than the sample size
n
, but a low-dimensional latent structure makes performing inference possible. In this section, we consider the task of sparse mean estimation under a stability constraint. Let

\mathcal{P}^{d}(r,s)=\{\textnormal{distributions }P\textnormal{ supported on }\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\textnormal{ with }\|\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\|_{0}\leq s\}

be the class of distributions with sparse mean vectors (here
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(r)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|_{\infty}\leq r\}
and
\|v\|_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathds{1}}\{v_{j}\neq 0\}
denotes the sparsity, or
\ell_{0}
‘norm’, of a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
). Without the stability constraint, it is well known that estimators based on hard-thresholding or soft-thresholding the sample mean
\widebar{X}
, i.e.,

\begin{split}\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{hard}}_{j}=\widebar{X}_{j}{\mathds{1}}(|\widebar{X}|_{j}\geq\tau)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}_{j}=\textnormal{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})(|\widebar{X}_{j}|-\tau)_{+}\quad\textnormal{ for }j=1,\ldots,d,\end{split}

(16)

with
\tau\asymp\frac{r\sqrt{\log d}}{n}
can yield the nearly optimal estimation error rate
\frac{r^{2}s\log d}{n}

(
Johnstone, 2017
)
. Here
\widebar{X}_{j}
denotes the
j
th coordinate of the sample mean. Under a stability constraint, we can show that the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}(r,s)),\beta_{n}\right)
has a sharp phase transition.

Theorem 8
.

Given any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}(r,s)),\beta_{n}\right)\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{2}s\log d}{n}&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\geq 4\sqrt{2}\frac{r\sqrt{s}}{n}\\
r^{2}s&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{16}\frac{r\sqrt{s}}{n}.\end{array}\right.

One optimal estimator for achieving the above bound is given by a soft-thresholding estimator with a data-driven thresholding value:

\widehat{\theta}_{j}=\left(1\wedge\frac{n\beta_{n}}{4\sqrt{2s}r}\right)\textnormal{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})(|\widebar{X}_{j}|-|\widebar{X}|_{(s+1)})_{+}\quad\textnormal{ for }j=1,\ldots,d,

(17)

where
|\widebar{X}|_{(1)}\geq\dots\geq|\widebar{X}|_{(d)}
denote the order statistics of
\{|\widebar{X}_{i}|\}_{i=1}^{d}
, so that
|\widebar{X}|_{(s+1)}
is the
(s+1)
-th largest (absolute) value in the sample mean.

From Theorem
8
, we can see that for sparse mean estimation, the phase transition is sharp for
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}(r,s)),\beta_{n}\right)
, with a threshold
\beta_{n}^{*}=\frac{r\sqrt{s}}{n}
that is independent of the ambient dimension
d
and of the choice of
p
.

In terms of the optimal estimator, our estimator is also novel and different from the estimators in (
16
) in an important way that we use a soft-thresholding estimator with a
data-driven
thresholding value, which is guaranteed to be
s
-sparse due to the particular thresholding we choose. Moreover, due to the Lipschitz property of the soft-thresholding operator, it turns out that such a sparsity-ensured soft-thresholding estimator can achieve the optimal stability we need. In Appendix
B
, we illustrate that in general the estimators in (
16
) are not as stable as the one we propose. Interestingly, we note the same soft-thresholding operator as the one in (
17
) is studied in Section 4.3 of
Liu and Barber, 2020
in an optimization setting for the purpose of getting an optimal convergence rate.

Finally, we comment that similar to bounded mean estimation setting, we also expect that the exact phase transition location in
R_{n,p}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{d}(r,s)),\beta_{n}\right)
would vary over
p
and these
risks can also have different rates when
\beta_{n}
is near the phase transition threshold. Narrowing down the exact phase transition location and identifying the quantitative difference between worst-case and average-case stability in the sparse mean estimation would likely require new lower bound and upper bound techniques, and we leave this question to future exploration.

Nonparametric Regression Function Estimation

While the previous three examples all study the problem of mean estimation in various settings, our last example departs from this question and considers the problem of function estimation in nonparametric regression.

Suppose we have
n
i.i.d. data points
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
generated from the following model:

Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\xi_{i},

(18)

where
f
is some unknown function,
X_{i}\sim\textnormal{Unif}[0,1]
, and
\xi_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
. The goal is to estimate the function
f
at a fixed point
x_{0}\in(0,1)
, that is, to estimate
f(x_{0})
, under a stability constraint. When there is no assumption on
f
, we cannot hope to estimate
f(x_{0})
well in general. Here, we assume
f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
, where
\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
denotes the unit Besov ball:

\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)=\{f\in L_{2}[0,1]:\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}}\leq 1\},

where the Besov norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}}
will be defined below. In this section, we adopt the standard setting that
\nu:=s-1/p\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty

(
Cai, 2003
)
.

Wavelet methods provide a natural framework for constructing both global and pointwise estimators in nonparametric problems, achieving optimal approximation rates through finite-resolution truncation (see, e.g.,
Gine and Nickl, 2016
).
Formulating our analysis over Besov balls
\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}
is convenient because they unify classical smoothness classes: Sobolev spaces correspond to
p=q=2
, while Hölder (Lipschitz) spaces correspond to
p=q=\infty
.
Thus, our results simultaneously recover these familiar cases while also encompassing more refined smoothness regimes.

We adopt the compactly supported, orthonormal,
A
-regular wavelet basis of Cohen-Daubechies-Vial
(
Cohen et al., 1993a
)
, with
A>s
.
The basis functions
\phi_{l_{0}+1,m}
and
\psi_{lk}
are indexed by resolution level
l\geq l_{0}+1
, location
k\in\{0,\dots,2^{l}-1\}
, and
m\in\{0,\dots,2^{l_{0}+1}-1\}
, defined by

\psi_{lk}(x)=2^{l/2}\psi(2^{l}x-k),\qquad\phi_{l_{0}+1,m}(x)=2^{l_{0}+1}\phi(2^{l_{0}+1}x-m).

Here
l_{0}
is the starting resolution level and
\phi(\cdot)
,
\psi(\cdot)
are father and mother wavelets, respectively. With a mild abuse of notation, we write the father wavelets as
\psi_{l_{0}k}:=\phi_{l_{0}+1,k}
and represent any
f\in L_{2}[0,1]
as

f=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}f_{lk}\psi_{lk},\qquad f_{lk}=\langle f,\psi_{lk}\rangle,

so that
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{l,k}f_{lk}^{2}
by orthonormality. With this representation of the function, we can define the Besov norm as

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}}=\begin{cases}\left(\underset{l=l_{0}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\left(2^{l(s+1/2-1/p)}\left\|(f_{lk})_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\right\|_{p}\right)^{q}\right)^{1/q}&\text{ if }1\leq q<\infty\\
\;\underset{l\geq l_{0}}{\sup}\;2^{l(s+1/2-1/p)}\left\|(f_{lk})_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\right\|_{p}&\text{ if }q=\infty,\end{cases}

where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the
\ell_{p}
norm of a vector. Membership in
\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
is thus characterized by the decay rate of the wavelet coefficients
\{f_{lk}\}
, with parameters
(s,p,q)
controlling smoothness, integrability, and fine-scale behavior, respectively.

A key property essential to our stability analysis is the
local support
of wavelets: each
\psi_{lk}
has compact support of size
O(2^{-l})
, ensuring that any single sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})
influences only a bounded number of coefficients. This localization directly limits the estimator’s sensitivity to perturbations in the data. Owing to these favorable structural properties, wavelet-based methods have also been widely employed in the design of differentially private estimators for nonparametric problems; see, for example,
Cai et al., 2024a
;
Cai et al., 2024b
.

In Appendix
H.1
, we collect a few more basic properties of Besov spaces.
Interested readers are also referred to
Cohen et al., 1993a
;
Johnstone, 2017
for a more detailed exploration of Besov space and wavelet basis.

When there is no stability constraint, it has been shown in
Cai, 2003
that the minimax pointwise
\ell_{2}
-risk for function estimation in Besov space is
n^{\frac{-2\nu}{1+2\nu}}
. The rate-optimal estimator is based on the following truncated wavelet estimator:

\begin{split}\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}(x_{0})=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L_{\text{opt}}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\widehat{f}_{lk}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\end{split}

(19)

where
\widehat{f}_{lk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})
and
L_{\text{opt}}=\lfloor\frac{\log_{2}n}{2\nu+1}\rfloor
. On the other hand, a trivial error rate is
1
as
\|f\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1
for all
f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)

(
Cai, 2003
, Lemma 1)
. With the worst-case and average-case stability constraints, let us denote the
\ell_{2}
-pointwise risk for function estimation in Besov space as
R_{n,\infty}\left(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\right)
and
R_{n,1}\left(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\right)
, respectively. We can show that the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n,\infty}\left(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\right)
has a gradual phase transition, while the trade-off curve
R_{n,1}\left(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\right)
has a sharp phase transition.

Theorem 9
.

(a)

Under worst-case stability ( i.e.,
p=\infty
),

\begin{split}(n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}\wedge 1+n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}\lesssim R_{n,\infty}(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n})\lesssim(n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}(\log n)^{\nu}\wedge 1+n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}.\end{split}

One estimator achieving the upper bound is the clipped-truncated wavelet estimator:

\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}(x_{0})=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\widehat{f}^{T}_{lk}\psi_{lk}(x_{0}),

(20)

where
L=\left\lfloor\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{\psi}n\beta_{n}}{T}\right)\vee(\ell_{0}-1)\right\rfloor\wedge L_{\text{opt}}
and
\widehat{f}^{T}_{lk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[Y_{i}]_{T}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})
. Here
[\cdot]_{T}
is the clipping operator at level
T
(see its definition in Section
1.2
) and we choose
T=c_{T}\sqrt{\log_{2}n}
, where
c_{T},c_{\psi}
depends only on
\sigma,s,p
and the wavelet basis.

(b)

Under average-case stability (i.e.,
p=1
), there exist universal constants
0\leq c_{\star}\leq C_{\star}
such that

\begin{split}R_{n,1}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\geq C_{\star}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\\
1&\textnormal{ if }\beta_{n}\leq c_{\star}\frac{1}{n\log n}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(21)

In addition, one estimator that achieves the above bound is

\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}(x_{0})=\left(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{C_{\star}\log^{2}n}\wedge 1\right)\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L_{\text{opt}}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\left(\frac{B_{L}2^{-l/2}}{S_{lk}}\wedge 1\right)\widehat{f}_{lk}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})

(22)

where
S_{lk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|
,
\widehat{f}_{lk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})
, and
B_{L}=C\log n
for some
C>0
depending only on
\sigma,s,p
.

From Theorem
9
, we see that imposing worst-case stability has a dramatic impact on nonparametric function estimation. In particular, Part (a) shows that any estimator attaining the unconstrained minimax rate must have stability at least
\beta_{n}\asymp n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}
, and that the resulting optimal estimator differs substantially from the classical wavelet estimator (
19
): the resolution level must depend on
\beta_{n}
, and the responses must be clipped, leading to a fundamentally different bias-variance trade-off. In sharp contrast, Part (b) demonstrates that average-case stability is a much weaker requirement: up to logarithmic factors,
\beta_{n}\asymp 1/n
suffices to achieve the optimal estimation rate, whereas the same stability level yields only trivial guarantees under worst-case stability. The corresponding estimator reflects this difference, requiring only selective shrinkage of large empirical wavelet coefficients to control average-case sensitivity, without the need for response clipping.

8
Discussion and Future Directions

To conclude, we examine some connections to the literature, including the relationship between stability and privacy, and a comparison to alternative formulations of the stability constraint, and discuss some open questions raised by this work.

8.1
Conversion between Stability and Differential Privacy

In this section, we show that by leveraging the connections between worst-case stability and privacy, we obtain some interesting implications on the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve and privacy-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve.

To illustrate the idea clearly, we focus on the pure differential privacy (DP) in the setting of a
1
-dimensional parameter estimation problem. Recall from
Dwork et al., 2006
that a randomized algorithm
M:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\epsilon
-differential private if for every pair of adjacency data sets
\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
that differ by one individual data and every measurable
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{D}_{n})\in S)\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}(M(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\in S)

where the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is taken with respect to the randomness of
M
only. The Laplace noise mechanism enables us to convert a worst-case-stable estimator to a differentially private estimator:

Lemma 1

(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
.

If
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable, then
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})+\xi
, where
\xi
is independent of
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
and follows
\textnormal{Laplace}(\beta_{n}/\epsilon_{n})
, satisfies
\epsilon_{n}
-differential privacy.

In fact, we can also convert a differentially private estimator to a stable one if we have a mild assumption about the differentially private estimator.

Lemma 2

(Modified from Lemma 8 of
Wang et al., 2016
)
.

Suppose
M(\mathcal{D}_{n})
satisfies
\epsilon_{n}
-differential privacy and
|\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})|\mathcal{D}_{n}]|\leq r
a.s., then
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})|\mathcal{D}_{n}]
satisfies
r(e^{\epsilon_{n}}-1)
-worst-case stability. When
\epsilon_{n}<1
, then
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is
2r\epsilon_{n}
-worst-case-stable.

Similar to the development of the stability-constrained minimax risk in Section
2
, here we will also consider the privacy-constrained minimax risk
(
Barber and Duchi, 2014
)
:

R_{{\textrm{D}P}}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\epsilon_{n}):=\inf_{M\textnormal{ satisfies }\epsilon_{n}-{\textrm{D}P}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P)]^{2}.

Then Lemma
1
and
2
together yield a few interesting conversion between
R_{{\textrm{D}P}}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\epsilon_{n})
and
R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})
.

Proposition 1
.

In the
1
-dimensional parameter estimation setting with
n
i.i.d. samples, suppose the parameter of interest lies in
[-r,r]
. Then the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n,\infty}(\cdot)
may be used to provide upper and lower bounds for privacy-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{\textnormal{DP}}(\cdot)
, as follows:

R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),r(e^{\epsilon_{n}}-1))\leq R_{\textnormal{DP}}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\epsilon_{n})\leq\inf_{\beta_{n}}\left(R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})+\frac{2\beta_{n}^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}^{2}}\right).

(23)

Equivalently, by inverting the above bounds, the privacy-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{\textnormal{DP}}(\cdot)
may be used to provide upper and lower bounds on the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve
R_{n,\infty}(\cdot)
, as follows:

\sup_{\epsilon_{n}}\left(R_{\textnormal{DP}}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\epsilon_{n})-\frac{2\beta_{n}^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}^{2}}\right)\leq R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\leq R_{\textnormal{DP}}\left(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\log\left(1+\frac{\beta_{n}}{r}\right)\right).

(24)

Proposition
1
reveals that the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve and the privacy-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve are closely related, and each may be used to derive bounds on the other. Moreover, we will illustrate in Appendix
C
using mean estimation examples to show a few implications of these inequalities. While the first inequality in (
24
) provides an alternative approach to prove a lower bound under a worst-case stability constraint, our approach in Section
3
is still useful as it is more direct and can provide sharper bounds (in addition to generalizing to
p\in[1,\infty]
, allowing for average-case in addition to worst-case stability).

8.2
A Comparison of Different Stability Notions

In the literature, there are many other notions of stability, which are developed for different purposes. One advantage of our framework is that it provides a unified way to compare different notions of stability from a statistical decision theory point of view. Other than the worst-case stability, average-case stability, and in general the
\ell_{p}
-stability we have discussed before, here we mention a few other common stability notions. For example, in many statistical applications, a type of average-case stability, where the average is over the randomness in the data, has also been considered: we might say
\widehat{\theta}
satisfies
\beta_{n}(P)
stability with respect to
P
if

\begin{split}\sup_{i,j\in[n+1]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}P;\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n}(P)\end{split}

(25)

for data
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
drawn i.i.d. from
P
(rather than for an arbitrary data set, as in the definitions used throughout this paper). One application of such a stability condition is the problem of inference on cross-validation estimate of model risk
(
Austern and Zhou, 2025
;
Bayle et al., 2020
;
Kissel and Lei, 2022
)
. However, it is known that testing whether
\beta_{n}(P)
is small or not is impossible
(
Kim and Barber, 2023
;
Luo and Barber, 2024a
)
. Similar to the question we have explored in this paper, we can also ask if
\widehat{\theta}
is required to satisfy the notion of stability in (
25
) uniformly over all distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
for some distribution class
\mathcal{P}
, how will that affect the estimation task?

In addition, for all the stability notions we have considered so far, the measure of the closeness of the outputs given a perturbation in the inputs is taken with respect to a metric on the parameter or function space. Such a notion has a long history as a tool for ensuring generalization
(
Rogers and Wagner, 1978
;
Devroye and Wagner, 1979
;
Kearns and Ron, 1997
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
)
. More recently, lots of progress has been made on another class of distributional algorithmic stability, where the output object is a distribution and the metric of closeness is based on distributional closeness. Broadly speaking, this includes differential privacy
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
(as discussed in Section
8.1
), perfect generalization
(
Cummings et al., 2016
)
, replicability
(
Impagliazzo et al., 2022
)
, KL-stability and TV-stability
(
Bassily et al., 2016
)
. We refer readers to
Bun et al., 2023
;
Kalavasis et al., 2023
;
Moran et al., 2023
for a recent summary of these notions and their connections.

8.3
Additional Directions and Open Questions

The stability property on its own demonstrates some robustness of the statistical procedure to data perturbation. In the robust statistics literature, one related notion is the influence function
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009
;
Hampel et al., 2011
)
: given any functional
T
of a distribution
F
defined on
\mathbb{R}
, the influence curve
\textnormal{IF}(\cdot;T,F):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
at
x
is defined by

\begin{split}\textnormal{IF}(x;T,F)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{T((1-t)F+t\delta_{x})-T(F)}{t},\end{split}

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the point mass at
x
. The maximum absolute value of the influence curve, i.e.,
\gamma^{*}(T,F)=\sup_{x}|\textnormal{IF}(x;T,F)|
, is called the gross error sensitivity
(
Hampel et al., 2011
)
.
If the gross error sensitivity is unbounded, then outliers may be problematic. To estimate the gross error sensitivity, we can use the following empirical version:

\begin{split}\gamma^{*}(T;\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})=\sup_{x}\left|(n+1)\left(T\left((1-\frac{1}{n+1})F_{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\delta_{x}\right)-T(F_{n})\right)\right|,\end{split}

where
F_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
denotes the empirical distribution of the data. We note that this quantity can be viewed as a “local” version of the worst-case stability, as it fixes the data set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
in computing the worst-case perturbation of replacing one data point. In robust statistics, a number of robust estimators have been developed to offer control over the sensitivity. It is interesting to consider whether some of these ideas may be useful for deriving a more principled approach to develop stable estimators.

Finally, careful readers may notice that across all four estimation problems we have explored in this paper, the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curves exhibit a sharp phase transition under the average-case stability, while for worst-case stability, the phase transition may be sharp or gradual, depending on the setting (see Table
1
). This suggests an interesting open question of determining whether this is a common phenomenon or if we can find an example where the stability-vs.-accuracy trade-off curve exhibits a gradual phase transition even under average-case stability.

\widehat{\theta}
is also well defined on a data set of size
n+1
, then another average-case stability notion based on dropping one data point at random would read as

\frac{1}{n+1}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n}.

(26)

We first show that the version in Definition

can be implied by (

).

\widehat{\theta}

n+1
. Suppose
\widehat{\theta}

n

n+1

Appendix B
Stability of Standard Soft- and Hard-Thresholding Estimators

We now compute the worst-case stability for the classical soft- and hard-thresholding estimators introduced in (
16
).
Throughout, we assume
X_{i}\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
r>0
, and denote the sample mean by

\widebar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\qquad\widebar{X}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}.

B.1
Soft-thresholding

The soft-thresholding estimator is defined coordinatewise as

\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}_{j}\;=\;\textnormal{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})\,\bigl(|\widebar{X}_{j}|-\tau\bigr)_{+},\qquad j=1,\dots,d,

for some threshold
\tau>0
.

Consider replacing one sample
X_{i}
with
X_{i}^{\prime}
.
For any coordinate
j
,

\bigl|\widebar{X}_{j}-\widebar{X}_{j}^{\prime}\bigr|=\frac{1}{n}\,\bigl|X_{ij}-X_{ij}^{\prime}\bigr|\leq\frac{2r}{n}.

The soft-thresholding operator
x\mapsto\textnormal{sign}(x)(|x|-\tau)_{+}
is
1
-Lipschitz, so this perturbation translates directly to the estimate:

\bigl|\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\bigr|\leq\frac{2r}{n}.

In the worst case, the equality can be achieved. Aggregating across all coordinates,

\bigl\|\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\bigr\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\frac{2r}{n}.

Hence, the worst-case stability of
\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{soft}}
is bounded by

\beta_{n}^{\textnormal{soft}}\;\leq\;\frac{2r\sqrt{d}}{n},

and again the equality can be achieved in the worst case.

B.2
Hard-thresholding

The hard-thresholding estimator is defined as

\widehat{\theta}^{\textnormal{hard}}_{j}\;=\;\widebar{X}_{j}\,\mathbf{1}\{|\widebar{X}_{j}|\geq\tau\},\qquad j=1,\dots,d.

Replacing one sample again perturbs
\widebar{X}_{j}
by at most
2r/n
.
When
|\widebar{X}_{j}|
lies far from the threshold
\tau
, this change only induces a perturbation of order
O(r/n)
.
However, if
|\widebar{X}_{j}|
lies within
O(r/n)
of
\tau
, a single replacement may flip the indicator
\mathbf{1}\{|\widebar{X}_{j}|\geq\tau\}
, causing a discontinuous jump as large as
O(\tau)
in that coordinate.
Consequently,

\beta_{n}^{\textnormal{hard}}\;\leq\;\left(\tau+\frac{2r}{n}\right)\sqrt{d}.

When
\tau\asymp\frac{r\sqrt{\log d}}{n}
, the hard-thresholding estimator is
C\frac{r\sqrt{d\log d}}{n}
-worst-case-stable for some constant
C>0
.

B.3
Comparing to our proposed estimator

From the above two subsections, we can see that the soft and hard-thresholding estimators in (
16
) have worst-case stability that scales at least as large as
\frac{r\sqrt{d}}{n}
and it depends on the ambient dimension
d
. While as we illustrated in the proof of Theorem
8
, specifically Lemma
10
, our estimator is at least
\frac{4\sqrt{2}r\sqrt{s}}{n}
-worst-case-stable. The stability of our estimator only scales with the intrinsic sparsity level, not with the ambient dimension.

Appendix C
Conversion Between Stability and Differential Privacy

(27)

The minimax rates for mean estimation in bounded and heavy-tailed classes under the worst-case-stability constraint have been established in Theorem

Corollary 2
.

Suppose

D.2
Proof of Theorem
2

First, we note again we just need to show the first term on the right-hand side of (
8
). In addition, without loss of generality, we are going to assume
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)
is symmetric with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{n}

\sigma
on
[n]

\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)=\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n}),\xi)
. This is because for any estimator, we can always symmetrize it so that the symmetrized estimator has the same stability guarantee and has risk no bigger than the original one. In particular, given any
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)
, define
\tilde{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n}),\xi)
, where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denotes the set of all permutations supported on
[n]

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\widetilde{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widetilde{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)\|^{p}_{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\left(\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i});\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j});\xi)\right)\right\|_{2}^{p}\\
&\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i});\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j});\xi)\right\|_{2}^{p}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i});\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j});\xi)\right\|_{2}^{p}\leq\beta_{n}\end{split}

where (a) is by Jensen’s inequality as
\|\cdot\|_{2}^{p}
is a convex function when

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}\|\tilde{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{n}|^{2}}|\mathcal{S}_{n}|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{n});\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

We divide the rest of the proof into two parts: Part I is for estimating a functional that is linear in
P
and Part II is for the general setting.

(Part I: Linear functional setting)

We will prove the lower bound based on a two-point mixture distribution construction. Take any
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
(1-t)P_{1}+tP_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
for all
t\in[0,1]

\eta\in[0,1/2]

and
P^{\prime}=\eta P_{2}+(1-\eta)P_{1}
. Then
\theta(P)=\eta\theta(P_{1})+(1-\eta)\theta(P_{2})
and
\theta(P^{\prime})=\eta\theta(P_{2})+(1-\eta)\theta(P_{1})
. For any symmetric estimator that is
\beta_{n}
-average-case-stable, let

\sigma_{n}^{2}:=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}P,\xi}\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)\|_{2}^{2}.

Let us generate
\mathcal{D}_{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim P^{\otimes n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}=(X_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{n}^{\prime})\sim P^{{}^{\prime}\otimes n}
in a coupled way as follows:

•

B_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\textnormal{Bernoulli}(2\eta)
for

;

•

for each
i=1,\ldots,n

B_{i}
= 1, then independently generate
A_{i}\sim\textnormal{Bernoulli}(1/2)

A_{i}=1

and if
A_{i}=0

X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}\sim P_{2}
; if
B_{i}=0
, then set
A_{i}=-1

X_{i}\sim P_{2}

X_{i}^{\prime}\sim P_{1}

First, we have

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}&=\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\theta(P)+\theta(P)-\theta(P^{\prime})+\theta(P^{\prime})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}\\
&\geq\|\theta(P)-\theta(P^{\prime})\|_{2}-2\sigma_{n}=(1-2\eta)\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2\sigma_{n}.\end{split}

(36)

Second, given
T_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}

T_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}A_{i}

\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}

and

,

and

(37)

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}^{p}&=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)|T_{1},T_{2}\right]\right]\right\|_{2}^{p}\\

(44)

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{n-T_{1}+T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)+\frac{1}{T_{2}+1}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{2n}{q(n+1)}-1\right)+\frac{2}{q(n+1)}.

(45)

Then, we can further have

\begin{split}&\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}^{p}\\
\overset{\eqref{ineq:exp-diff-upper},\eqref{eq:binomial-exp-1}}{\leq}&(n+1)\beta^{p}_{n}\mathbb{E}\left((n-T_{1})^{p-1}\left(\log\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-1\right)+\frac{2}{T_{1}+1}\right)\right)\\
\leq&(n+1)\beta^{p}_{n}\mathbb{E}\left((n-T_{1})^{p-1}\left(\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-2\right)+\frac{2}{T_{1}+1}\right)\right)\quad(\textnormal{as }\log(1+x)\leq x,\forall x>-1)\\
=&2(n+1)\beta^{p}_{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{(n-T_{1})^{p}}{T_{1}+1}\right).\end{split}

(46)

The next lemma provides an upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{(n-T_{1})^{p}}{T_{1}+1}\right)
and the proof of this lemma is provided in the subsequent subsections.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
T_{1}\sim\textnormal{Binomial}(n,q)
, then for any
p>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{(n-T_{1})^{p}}{T_{1}+1}\right)\leq\frac{((n+1)(1-q))^{p}\left(1+\frac{p}{2(n+1)(1-q)}\right)^{p}}{(n+1)q}\wedge n^{p}.

By replacing
q
by
2\eta
in Lemma
4
, we got from (
46

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}^{p}&\leq 2\beta^{p}_{n}\left(\frac{((n+1)(1-2\eta))^{p}\left(1+\frac{p}{2(n+1)(1-2\eta)}\right)^{p}}{2\eta}\wedge n^{p}(n+1)\right)\\
&\leq 2\beta^{p}_{n}\left(\frac{((n+1)(1-2\eta))^{p}\left(1+\frac{p}{2(n+1)(1-2\eta)}\right)^{p}}{2\eta}\wedge(n+1)^{p+1}\right)\end{split}

(47)

Combining (
47
) and (
36
), we get

\begin{split}2^{1/p}\beta_{n}(n+1)\left(\frac{(1-2\eta)\left(1+\frac{p}{2(n+1)(1-2\eta)}\right)}{(2\eta)^{1/p}}\wedge(n+1)^{1/p}\right)\geq(1-2\eta)\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2\sigma_{n}.\end{split}

Thus, we get

\sigma_{n}\geq\left(\frac{(1-2\eta)\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2^{1/p}\beta_{n}(n+1)\left(\frac{(1-2\eta)\left(1+\frac{p}{2(n+1)(1-2\eta)}\right)}{(2\eta)^{1/p}}\wedge(n+1)^{1/p}\right)}{2}\right)_{+}.

Since this holds for any
\eta\in[0,1/2]
and any estimator satisfies the stability constraint, we have finished the proof.

(Part II: General setting)
In this setting, we can set
\eta=0
in the proof of Part I, and the proof still goes through in the general setting. When
\eta=0
,
T_{1}=T_{2}=0
. From (

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|_{2}\geq\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-2\sigma_{n}.\end{split}

(48)

At the same time, following the same analysis as in (

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n};\xi)]\|^{p}_{2}\leq(n+1)^{p}\beta_{n}^{p}\left(\log n+1\right).\end{split}

A combination of these two yields

\begin{split}\sigma_{n}\geq\left(\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-(n+1)\beta_{n}(\log n+1)^{1/p}\right)_{+}/2.\end{split}

Since this holds for any

First, it is easy to check that if

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{X+1}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{1+k}{n\choose k}q^{k}(1-q)^{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{(1+n)q}{n+1\choose k+1}q^{k+1}(1-q)^{n-k}=\frac{1-(1-q)^{n+1}}{(n+1)q}.\end{split}

(49)

Since
T_{2}\sim\textnormal{Binomial}(T_{1},1/2)
, then
\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}+1}|T_{1}\geq 1\right)=\frac{1-(1/2)^{T_{1}+1}}{(T_{1}+1)/2}
. Notice that even when
T_{1}=0
, we still have
\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}+1}|T_{1}=0\right)=\frac{1-(1/2)^{T_{1}+1}}{(T_{1}+1)/2}
. Thus,
\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}+1}|T_{1}\right)=\frac{1-(1/2)^{T_{1}+1}}{(T_{1}+1)/2}\leq\frac{2}{T_{1}+1}
. Next,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{n-T_{1}+T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)|T_{1}\leq n-1\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(1+\frac{n-T_{1}-1}{T_{2}+1}\right)|T_{1}\leq n-1\right)\\

&\overset{\eqref{eq:binomial-inverse-expectation}}{\leq}\log\left(1+(n-T_{1}-1)\cdot\frac{2}{T_{1}+1}\right)=\log\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-1\right).\end{split}

In addition, when
T_{1}=n
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{n-T_{1}+T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)|T_{1}=n\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)\right)=-\infty\leq\log\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}-1\right)=\log\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-1\right).

Thus, we have shown
\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{n-T_{1}+T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)|T_{1}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-1\right)
holds almost surely. In summary,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{n-T_{1}+T_{2}}{T_{2}+1}\right)+\frac{1}{T_{2}+1}\right)&\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\log\left(\frac{2n}{T_{1}+1}-1\right)+\frac{2}{T_{1}+1}\right)\\
&\leq\log\left(2n\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{1}+1}\right)-1\right)+2\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{T_{1}+1}\right)\quad(\text{Jensen's inequality)}\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:binomial-inverse-expectation}}{=}\log\left(2n\cdot\frac{1-(1-q)^{n+1}}{q(n+1)}-1\right)+2\frac{1-(1-q)^{n+1}}{q(n+1)}\\
&\leq\log\left(\frac{2n}{q(n+1)}-1\right)+\frac{2}{q(n+1)}.\end{split}

Proof of Theorem

We first show that

when

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)|^{p}\\

(50)

Given any
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1})

[-r,r]

\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}

(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n+1})
and optimizing

\max_{X_{1}\in[-r,r]}g(X_{1})=\sum_{2\leq j\leq n+1}|X_{j}-X_{1}|^{p}

is convex in
X_{1}

and it is optimized over a compact set, then a standard result in convex optimization theory says that the maximum of a convex optimization on a compact set is achieved at the boundary point

Rockafellar, 1997

X_{1}\in\{r,-r\}
here. By a similar argument for each coordinate, we get that the maximum of

for all

m

\begin{split}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sup_{m\in[n+1]}m(n+1-m)(2r)^{p}\leq\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{4}(2r)^{p},\end{split}

and the last inequality can be achieved if

\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)|^{p}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2r}{n}\right)^{p}.

\beta_{n}\geq\frac{2^{1-1/p}r}{n}

\beta_{n}
-
\ell_{p}
-stable and it can achieve the

error bound. The lower bound in this regime is the minimax lower bound without any stability constraint.

when

for any small constant
\delta>0
given
n

\delta
only. First, we note that in terms of the upper bound, it is trivial as
|\widebar{X}|\leq r
and
|\theta|\leq r
. So we just need to focus on the lower bound. We note that the result in Corollary
1
is not sharp enough to yield such a result and we will use (

•

Let us first consider

, where

(51)

So as long as

Proof of Theorem

Let us begin with the upper bound. We begin by deriving a generic upper bound for an estimator of the form
\widehat{\theta}=\frac{\widebar{X}}{1+\delta}
for some
\delta\geq 0
.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}&=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\bar{X}}{1+\delta}-\theta\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\bar{X}^{2}}{(1+\delta)^{2}}+\theta^{2}-2\frac{\theta\widebar{X}}{1+\delta}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+\delta)^{2}}\left(\frac{{\textrm{V}ar}(X)}{n}+\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\right),\end{split}

(53)

where
\theta=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Since
|X|\leq r
,
{\textrm{V}ar}(X)+\theta^{2}=\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}\leq r^{2}
. Thus,

\begin{split}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)}\frac{1}{(1+\delta)^{2}}\left(\frac{{\textrm{V}ar}(X)}{n}+\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\right)&\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)}\frac{1}{(1+\delta)^{2}}\left(\frac{r^{2}-\theta^{2}}{n}+\theta^{2}\delta^{2}\right)\\
&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{r^{2}}{n(1+\delta)^{2}}&\delta\leq 1/\sqrt{n}\\
\frac{\delta^{2}r^{2}}{(1+\delta)^{2}}&\delta>1/\sqrt{n}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(54)

For our estimator, we can easily verify that it is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable as the sample mean is
\frac{2r}{n}

•

Case 1:

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}=\frac{r^{2}}{(\sqrt{n}+1)^{2}}=\left(\frac{\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{2}r^{2}\vee\frac{r^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{n}+1\right)^{2}},

where in the second equality, we leverage the observation that
\left(\frac{\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{2}
is a decreasing function of
\beta_{n}
and when
\beta_{n}=\frac{2r}{n(1+1/\sqrt{n})}

\left(\frac{\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{n}+1\right)^{2}}
.

•

Case 2:

•

\begin{split}R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n})\geq\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{binary}}}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}\geq\frac{r^{2}}{(\sqrt{n}+1)^{2}}.\end{split}

Case 2:
\beta_{n}<\frac{2r}{n(1+1/\sqrt{n})}
. In this regime,
\left(\frac{\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}{1+\left(\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1\right)_{+}}\right)^{2}r^{2}\vee\frac{r^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{n}+1\right)^{2}}=\left(\frac{\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1}{1+\frac{2r}{n\beta_{n}}-1}\right)^{2}r^{2}=\left(\frac{2r-n\beta_{n}}{2}\right)^{2}

\begin{split}R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\textnormal{bound}}(r)),\beta_{n})&\geq\left(\frac{2r-n\beta_{n}}{2}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

In summary, we have shown

We divide the proof into two parts. In Part I, we show the upper bound and in Part II, we show the lower bound.

Part I: Upper Bound.
We begin by showing the estimation error guarantee of the proposed estimator and then check it also satisfies the desired average-case stability.

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}^{n+1}\textnormal{ satisfies }\eqref{eq:scenario1}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n+1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i};\xi)-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j};\xi)|\\

for all

Lemma 10

(Worst-case stability of the sparsity-ensured soft-thresholding estimator)
.

Fix
\beta_{n}>0
, let
X\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
be neighboring datasets (they differ in exactly one sample).
Consider the data-driven soft-thresholding estimator in (
17
)
evaluated on
\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
given by
\widehat{\theta}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})
. Then

\bigl\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\bigr\|_{2}\ \leq\ \beta_{n}.

Hence
\widehat{\theta}
is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case-stable (Definition
1
) .

Proof.

Let

c\ :=\ \left(1\wedge\frac{n\beta_{n}}{4\sqrt{2s}\,r}\right)\in(0,1].

By definition,

\widehat{\theta}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=c\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})\big(|\widebar{X}_{j}|-\tau_{s}\big)_{+},\qquad\widehat{\theta}_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})=c\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j})\big(|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|-\tau_{s}^{\prime}\big)_{+},

where
\tau_{s}=|\bar{X}|_{(s+1)}
and
\tau^{\prime}_{s}=|\bar{X}^{\prime}|_{(s+1)}

Bound the change in sample means.

Since
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
differ in one observation and
X\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}(r)
,

\Delta\ :=\ \|\widebar{X}-\widebar{X}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \frac{2r}{n}.

Order-statistic stability.

Let
s_{j}:=|\widebar{X}_{j}|
and
s^{\prime}_{j}:=|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|
, with order statistics
s_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq s_{(d)}
and
s^{\prime}_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq s^{\prime}_{(d)}
.
Since
x\mapsto|x|
is
1
-Lipschitz,
|s_{j}-s^{\prime}_{j}|\leq\Delta
for all
j
. Hence, for each
k
,

\big|\,s_{(k)}-s^{\prime}_{(k)}\,\big|\ \leq\ \Delta,\qquad\text{in particular}\qquad|\tau_{s}-\tau_{s}^{\prime}|\ =\ \big|\,s_{(s+1)}-s^{\prime}_{(s+1)}\,\big|\ \leq\ \Delta.

Coordinatewise change of the thresholded magnitudes.

Define
a_{j}:=(|\widebar{X}_{j}|-\tau_{s})_{+}
and
a^{\prime}_{j}:=(|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|-\tau_{s}^{\prime})_{+}
. Since
x\mapsto(x)_{+}
is
1
-Lipschitz,

|a_{j}-a^{\prime}_{j}|\ \leq\ \big||\widebar{X}_{j}|-|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|\big|+|\tau_{s}-\tau_{s}^{\prime}|\ \leq\ \Delta+\Delta\ =\ 2\Delta.

Translate to the signed (and scaled) estimator; handle sign flips.

We have
\widehat{\theta}_{j}=c\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})\,a_{j}
and
\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}=c\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j})\,a^{\prime}_{j}
.

(i) No sign flip:
If
\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})=\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j})
, then

|\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}|\ =\ c\,|a_{j}-a^{\prime}_{j}|\ \leq\ c\cdot 2\Delta.

(ii) Sign flip:
If
\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}_{j})\neq\mathrm{sign}(\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j})
, then

|\widebar{X}_{j}|+|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|\leq|\widebar{X}_{j}-\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|\leq\Delta
, so
|\widebar{X}_{j}|\leq\Delta
and
|\widebar{X}^{\prime}_{j}|\leq\Delta
.
Therefore
a_{j}\leq(\Delta-\tau_{s})_{+}\leq\Delta
and
a^{\prime}_{j}\leq(\Delta-\tau_{s}^{\prime})_{+}\leq\Delta
, yielding

|\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}|\ \leq\ c\,(a_{j}+a^{\prime}_{j})\ \leq\ 2c\Delta.

Combining (i)-(ii), for all
j
,

|\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}|\ \leq\ 2c\Delta\ \leq\ 2c\cdot\frac{2r}{n}\ =\ \frac{4cr}{n}.

Sparsity of the outputs and the
\ell_{2}
bound.

By construction
\tau_{s}=s_{(s+1)}
, hence
a_{j}>0
only on at most
s
coordinates; the same holds for
a^{\prime}_{j}
.
Thus the union of supports of
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
and
\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})
has size at most
2s
, and by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}\ \leq\ \sqrt{2s}\cdot\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{j}|\ \leq\ \sqrt{2s}\cdot\frac{4cr}{n}\ =\ c\cdot\frac{4\sqrt{2s}\,r}{n}.

Finally, by the definition of
c
,

c\cdot\frac{4\sqrt{2s}\,r}{n}\ \leq\ \beta_{n}.

Hence
\|\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\beta_{n}
, proving
\beta_{n}
-worst-case stability.
∎

Corollary 4

(In-expectation risk)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta(P)\big\|_{2}\ \leq\ C\,r\,\sqrt{\frac{s\log(4d)}{n}},

(one may take

Proof.

Let’s proceed in a step-by-step fashion.

Tail bound to a usable form.

Theorem
11
says that for every
u\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\ \|\widehat{\theta}-\theta\|_{2}\;>\;8r\sqrt{\frac{s(\log(4d)+u)}{n}}\ \right)\ \leq\ e^{-u}.

Let
Z:=\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta\|_{2}
. Setting

and solving for

\mathbb{P}(Z>t)\ \leq\ \min\!\left\{\,1,\ 4d{\rm{exp}}\!\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{64r^{2}s}\Big)\right\}.

Define

Pick the splitting point where the two pieces match.

Choose

t_{0}\ :=\ 8r\sqrt{\frac{s\log(4d)}{n}},

\mathbb{P}(Z>t)\ \leq\ \begin{cases}1,&0<t\leq t_{0},\\[2.0pt]
4d\,e^{-at^{2}},&t>t_{0}.\end{cases}

Tail-integration identity.

For any nonnegative random variable
Z

\ \mathbb{E}Z=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z>t)\,dt
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}Z\ \leq\ \underbrace{\int_{0}^{t_{0}}1\,dt}_{=\,t_{0}}\ +\ \int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}4d\,e^{-at^{2}}\,dt.

Using the standard bound

for

\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}4d\,e^{-at^{2}}\,dt\ \leq\ 4d\cdot\frac{e^{-at_{0}^{2}}}{2at_{0}}\ =\ \frac{1}{2at_{0}}\qquad\text{since }\ 4d\,e^{-at_{0}^{2}}=1.

With
a=\frac{n}{64r^{2}s}

Here we collect a few standard properties of the wavelet basis that will be invoked throughout the proofs in this section. In our work, we consider the Cohen, Daubechies, and Vial construction of a compactly supported, orthonormal,
A
-regular wavelet basis of
L_{2}[0,1]

Cohen et al., 1993b

one can follow Daubechies’ construction of the father
\phi(\cdot)
and mother

A

and
[-A+1,A]

Daubechies, 1992

\displaystyle\big\{\phi_{l_{0}+1,k},\psi_{lk}:\,\quad l\geq l_{0}+1,\quad k\in\{0,...,2^{l}-1\}\big\},

with
\psi_{lk}(x)=2^{l/2}\psi(2^{l}x-k)

\phi_{l_{0}+1,k}(x)=2^{l_{0}+1}\phi(2^{l_{0}+1}x-k)

, while for other values of
k
and
m
, the functions are specially constructed, to form a basis with the required smoothness property.

Wavelet basis properties.

Let
\{\psi_{lk}\}
denote a compactly supported,

-regular wavelet basis (with

) on
[0,1]
.
By the definition

\|\psi_{lk}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\psi}\,2^{l/2},\qquad\#\{k:\ \psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0\}\leq K_{\mathrm{loc}},\qquad\mathrm{Leb}(\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{lk}))=O(2^{-l})\quad\text{for all }l\geq l_{0}.

Lemma 11

(Worst-case stability lower bound for nonparametric pointwise estimation)
.

Consider the nonparametric regression model defined in (
18
). Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{f}:\ f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)\}
and the target functional be
\theta(P_{f})=f(x_{0})
for a fixed
x_{0}\in(0,1)
. Then for any
\beta_{n}\geq 0
,

R_{n,\infty}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\;\;\geq\;\;c\,((n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}\wedge 1)\ \ \vee\ \ n^{-2\nu/(2\nu+1)},

where
c>0
depends only on
(s,p,q,\sigma)
and the chosen wavelet system, and the second term is the classical (unconstrained) minimax pointwise risk.

Proof.

We invoke Theorem
1
, which states that for any
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
and any coupling
(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})
of
P_{1}^{\otimes n},P_{2}^{\otimes n}
,

R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\ \geq\ \left[\frac{\big(\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\,\beta_{n}\big)_{+}}{2}\right]^{2}\ \vee\ R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})).

Here
\theta(P_{f})=f(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}=|f_{1}(x_{0})-f_{0}(x_{0})|
.

Two-point construction.

We borrow the following construction from proof of Theorem 4 in
Cai, 2003
.
Choose a compactly supported, function
g\geq 0
, with compact support,
g(0)>0
, and
g\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
. For scale
\alpha>0
and amplitude
\eta>0
, set

h_{\alpha,\eta}(x):=\eta\,g\!\big(\alpha(x-x_{0})\big),\qquad f_{0}\equiv 0,\quad f_{1}:=h_{\alpha,\eta}.

For
\alpha
large (so that the support of
h_{\alpha,\eta}
lies in
(0,1)
) i.e
\exists C
such that
\alpha>C
, and by Besov scaling (via wavelet characterization, Lemma 1 in
Cai, 2003
),

\|h_{\alpha,\eta}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}}\ \lesssim\ \eta\,\alpha^{\nu},\qquad\nu=s-\frac{1}{p}.

Hence choosing
\eta=c_{\star}\,\alpha^{-\nu}
with
c_{\star}>0
small (depending only on
(s,p,q)
and the basis) ensures
f_{0},f_{1}\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
. The separation at the target point is

|f_{1}(x_{0})-f_{0}(x_{0})|=\eta\,g(0)\ \asymp\ \alpha^{-\nu}.

Single-sample total variation.

Let
P_{f}
denote the law of
(X,Y)
under
f
.
Conditioning on
X=x
,
Y\sim N(f(x),\sigma^{2})
. Using

\mathrm{TV}\big(N(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}),N(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})\big)=2\Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|}{2\sigma}\Big)-1\ \leq\ \frac{|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}},

and
X\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,

\mathrm{TV}(P_{f_{1}},P_{f_{0}})=\mathbb{E}_{X}\ \mathrm{TV}\big(N(f_{1}(X),\sigma^{2}),N(0,\sigma^{2})\big)\leq\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\ \mathbb{E}|f_{1}(X)|=C_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{1}|h_{\alpha,\eta}(x)|\,dx.

Since
h_{\alpha,\eta}
has height
\asymp\eta
and support width
\asymp\alpha^{-1}
,

\int_{0}^{1}|h_{\alpha,\eta}(x)|\,dx\ \asymp\ \eta\,\alpha^{-1}\ \asymp\ \alpha^{-(\nu+1)}.

Therefore

\mathrm{TV}(P_{f_{1}},P_{f_{0}})\ \leq\ C\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)},

for a constant
C
depending only on
(s,p,q,\sigma)
and
g
.

Product coupling and Hamming cost.

By maximal i.i.d. coupling of the
n
draws, see
(
Roch, 2024
, Lemma 4.1.13)

\mathbb{E}d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\ =\ n\,\mathrm{TV}(P_{f_{1}},P_{f_{0}})\ \leq\ C\,n\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}.

Plug and optimize.

Theorem
1
specialized to
\theta(P_{f})=f(x_{0})
yields

R_{n,\infty}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\ \geq\ \left[\frac{\Big(\underbrace{|f_{1}(x_{0})-f_{0}(x_{0})|}_{\asymp\alpha^{-\nu}}\ -\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}d_{{\rm Ham}}(\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime})\,\beta_{n}}_{\ \ \leq\ Cn\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}}\Big)_{+}}{2}\right]^{2}\ \vee\ R_{n}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)\big).

The inner difference is of order
\alpha^{-\nu}-Cn\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}
. Balance the two by choosing

\alpha\asymp n\beta_{n}\vee 1
, which gives

\alpha^{-\nu}\ \asymp\ (n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}\wedge 1,\qquad n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}\ \asymp\ (n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}\wedge n\beta_{n}.

Consider the two regimes
n\beta_{n}<1
and
n\beta_{n}>1

Case 1:
n\beta_{n}<1

In this regime
\alpha^{-\nu}\asymp 1

n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}\asymp n\beta_{n}

Thus,

\alpha^{-\nu}-C\,n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}\ \asymp\ 1-n\beta_{n}\ \asymp\ 1,

so the positive part remains a fixed fraction of
(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}\wedge 1

Case 2:
n\beta_{n}>1

Here
\alpha^{-\nu}\asymp(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}

n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}\asymp(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}
.
Therefore,

\alpha^{-\nu}-C\,n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}\ \asymp\ (1-C)\,(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}.

Choosing
C<1
(or absorbing constants into
\asymp
) ensures that the expression remains a constant fraction of
(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}
.

In both regimes, the positive part of

\alpha^{-\nu}-C\,n\beta_{n}\,\alpha^{-(\nu+1)}

is always a constant fraction of
(n\beta_{n})^{-\nu}\wedge 1
.
Squaring yields

R_{n,\infty}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\ \geq\ c\,((n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}\wedge 1)\ \vee\ R_{n}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)\big),

for some constant
c>0
depending only on
(s,p,q,\sigma)
and
g
. Using

(e.g.,
Cai, 2003

∎

Lemma 12

(Worst-case stability of the clipped-truncated wavelet estimator)
.

Fix
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and consider

\widehat{f}(x_{0})=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\widehat{f}_{lk}\,\psi_{lk}(x_{0}),\qquad\widehat{f}_{lk}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[Y_{i}]_{T}\,\psi_{lk}(X_{i}),

[y]_{T}:=\operatorname{sign}(y)\min\{|y|,T\}

for a universal constant
c_{T}>0
. The wavelet basis is chosen to satisfy (
80
).
Let

and choose

Then
\widehat{f}(x_{0})
is
\beta_{n}
-worst-case stable.

Proof.

If
\Big\lfloor\log_{2}\!\Big(\frac{c_{\psi}\,n\beta_{n}}{T}\Big)\Big\rfloor\leq(\ell_{0}-1)
,
L\leq\ell_{0}-1
which implies
\hat{f}=0
and hence it is trivially
\beta_{n}
stable. In the other case
\Big\lfloor\log_{2}\!\Big(\frac{c_{\psi}\,n\beta_{n}}{T}\Big)\Big\rfloor\geq\ell_{0}
, for the rest of the proof we work under this regime.
Write the estimator in kernel form using orthonormality:

\widehat{f}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[Y_{i}]_{T}\,K_{L}(X_{i},x_{0}),\qquad K_{L}(x,x_{0}):=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\psi_{lk}(x)\psi_{lk}(x_{0}).

For neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}
differing only at index
j
,

\bigl|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}(x_{0})\bigr|=\frac{1}{n}\,\bigl|\,[Y_{j}]_{T}K_{L}(X_{j},x_{0})-[Y^{\prime}_{j}]_{T}K_{L}(X^{\prime}_{j},x_{0})\,\bigr|\leq\frac{T}{n}\big(|K_{L}(X_{j},x_{0})|+|K_{L}(X^{\prime}_{j},x_{0})|\big).

Taking the suprema over the neighboring pair and inputs gives

\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}(x_{0})\bigr|\ \leq\ \frac{2T}{n}\,\|K_{L}(\cdot,x_{0})\|_{\infty}.

(81)

Bounding
\|K_{L}(\cdot,x_{0})\|_{\infty}
.

Using (
80
), for fixed
l
and any
x\in[0,1]
,

\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\big|\psi_{lk}(x)\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\big|\leq\Big(\max_{k}|\psi_{lk}(x)|\Big)\sum_{k:\,\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0}|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\ \overset{\eqref{eq:wavelet-localization}}{\leq}\ C_{\psi}\,2^{l/2}\cdot(K_{\rm loc}\cdot C_{\psi}\,2^{l/2})=K_{\rm loc}C_{\psi}^{2}\,2^{l}.

Summing over

yields

\|K_{L}(\cdot,x_{0})\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}C_{\psi}^{2}K_{\rm loc}\,2^{l}\ \leq\ C_{\psi}^{\prime}\,2^{L},\qquad C_{\psi}^{\prime}:=2K_{\rm loc}C_{\psi}^{2},

(82)

uniformly in
x_{0}
.

Concluding stability.

Combining (
81
) and (
82

\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\prime}}(x_{0})\bigr|\ \leq\ \frac{2T}{n}\,C_{\psi}^{\prime}\,2^{L}.

By the condition on

\Big\lfloor\log_{2}\!\Big(\frac{c_{\psi}\,n\beta_{n}}{T}\Big)\Big\rfloor\geq\ell_{0}

.

We proceed in a step-by-step fashion. We deal with the bias clipping as follows: Define

\mathbb{E}(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}\big]\;+\;\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}^{c}}\big],

A_{T}^{c}
the contribution is polynomially small in
n
and negligible compared to the dominant terms above.

Conditioning on the clipping event.

Define
A_{T}:=\{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Y_{i}|\leq T\}

[Y_{i}]_{T}=Y_{i}
for all

\widehat{f}(x_{0})\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}=\Big(\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})}_{\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}_{lk}}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\Big)\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}=\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}(x_{0})\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}.

\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}-f)^{2}\big],

so it suffices to bound the (classical) risk of the
unclipped
truncated estimator with the same
L
.

Classical bias and variance on
A_{T}
.

Let
\Pi_{\leq L}f(x_{0}):=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k}f_{lk}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})

L

\big|f(x_{0})-\Pi_{\leq L}f(x_{0})\big|^{2}\;\lesssim\;2^{-2\nu L}.

(83)

For the variance, write the estimator in kernel form

\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\,K_{L}(X_{i},x_{0}),\quad K_{L}(x,x_{0}):=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\psi_{lk}(x)\psi_{lk}(x_{0}).

Cai, 2003

\mathbb{E}K_{L}(X,x_{0})^{2}=\sum_{l,k}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:wavelet-localization}}{\lesssim}\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}2^{l}\lesssim 2^{L}

contribute at each level and

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{f}^{\,\mathrm{unclip}}(x_{0}))\;\leq\;\frac{M^{2}_{f}+\sigma^{2}}{n}\,\mathbb{E}K_{L}(X,x_{0})^{2}\;\lesssim\;\frac{2^{L}}{n}.

where

is such that
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq M_{f}

Cai, 2003

Combining (
83

84

\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}}\big]\;\lesssim\;2^{-2\nu L}+\frac{2^{L}}{n}.

Since
L=L_{\mathrm{stab}}\wedge L_{\mathrm{opt}}

(i) Stability-limited:
L=L_{\mathrm{stab}}

Then
2^{L}\asymp(n\beta_{n})/T\vee 1

With

2^{-2\nu L}+\frac{2^{L}}{n}\ \lesssim\ (n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}(\log n)^{\nu}\wedge 1\;+\frac{2^{L_{\rm opt}}}{n}\ \asymp\ (n\beta_{n})^{-2\nu}(\log n)^{\nu}\wedge 1+n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}},

L\leq L_{\mathrm{opt}}
.

(ii) Classical-optimal:
L=L_{\mathrm{opt}}
.

Then

2^{-2\nu L}+\frac{2^{L}}{n}\ \asymp\ n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}.

Controlling the clipping complement
A_{T}^{c}

We now bound
\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{T}^{c}}]

\mathbb{P}(A_{T}^{c})

|\widehat{f}(x_{0})|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|[Y_{i}]_{T}|\;|K_{L}(X_{i},x_{0})|\;\leq\;T\,\|K_{L}(\cdot,x_{0})\|_{\infty}\;\lesssim\;T\,2^{L}

(85)

Next, control

\mathbb{P}(A_{T}^{c})\;=\;\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Y_{i}|>T\Big)\;\leq\;n\cdot 2{\rm{exp}}\!\Big(-\frac{(T-M_{f})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\;\leq\;n^{-\gamma}

\gamma>2

sufficiently large depending only on
(\sigma,M_{f})

Complement term via
L\leq L_{\mathrm{opt}}

Since
L\leq L_{\mathrm{opt}}=\lfloor\frac{\log_{2}n}{2\nu+1}\rfloor

Choosing
T=c_{T}\sqrt{\log n}
with
c_{T}
large (depending only on
(\sigma,M_{f})

\mathbb{P}(A_{T}^{c})\leq n^{-\gamma}

so the complement term is negligible relative to the main rate.

Conclusion.

Combining the

Lemma 14

(Average-case stability lower bound: two-regime phase transition)
.

Consider the nonparametric regression model (
18
).
Then there exist absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{*}>0
(depending only on the Besov triplet and the wavelet system) such that for average-case (
p=1
) stability:

R_{n,1}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\ \geq\begin{cases}\ \frac{c_{0}^{2}}{16}\quad&\beta_{n}\leq\dfrac{c_{*}}{n\log n},\\
c_{1}n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}\quad&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

In particular, the lower bound exhibits a threshold at order
\beta_{n}\asymp 1\big/\!\big(n\log n\big)
: below it, the minimax risk is constant; above it, the minimax risk is (at least) the classical rate.

Proof.

We invoke Theorem
2
with
p=1
, which states that for any
\beta_{n}\geq 0
,

R_{n,1}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\beta_{n})\ \geq\ \sup_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{\big(\|\theta(P_{1})-\theta(P_{2})\|_{2}-(n{+}1)(\log n{+}1)\beta_{n}\big)_{+}}{2}\right]^{2}\ \vee\ R_{n}(\theta(\mathcal{P})).

Choice of the pair.
Let
f_{0}\equiv 0
and
f_{1}\equiv c_{0}
be constants. Constants belong to
\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}
with norm proportional to
|c_{0}|
; taking
c_{0}>0
small enough (depending only on
s,p,q
) ensures
f_{0},f_{1}\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
. For these,

\theta(P_{f_{1}})-\theta(P_{f_{0}})\;=\;f_{1}(x_{0})-f_{0}(x_{0})=c_{0},\quad\text{hence}\quad\|\theta(P_{f_{1}})-\theta(P_{f_{0}})\|_{2}=c_{0}.

Plugging into the lower bound yields

R_{n,1}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\ \geq\ \left[\frac{\big(c_{0}-(n{+}1)(\log n{+}1)\beta_{n}\big)_{+}}{2}\right]^{2}\ \vee\ R_{n}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)\big).

(86)

Regime (i).

Suppose
\beta_{n}\leq\dfrac{c_{0}}{2(n+1)(\log n+1)}
. Then

c_{0}-(n{+}1)(\log n{+}1)\beta_{n}\geq c_{0}/2
, and the first term in (
86
) is at least
(c_{0}/4)^{2}
. Since
R_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1))\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the max is dominated by this constant. Renaming
c_{*}:=c_{0}/4
gives the claim.

Regime (ii).

R_{n,1}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1),\beta_{n}\big)\ \geq\ R_{n}\!\big(\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)\big)\ \asymp\ n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 15

(Average Case Stability bound)
.

The estimator defined in (
21
) with wavelet basis satisfying (
80
) has the following bound on
\ell_{1}
stability

\frac{1}{(n{+}1)^{2}}\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\big|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i}}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j}}(x_{0})\big|\;\leq\;\beta_{n},

i.e.,
\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(x_{0})
is average-case
\beta_{n}
-stable.

Proof.

Write the estimator as a finite linear combination of one-dimensional, data-dependent, clipped means:

\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(x_{0})=\alpha\sum_{l,k}\theta_{lk}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\,\psi_{lk}(x_{0}),\qquad\theta_{lk}(\mathcal{D}_{n}):=\Big(\frac{B_{L}2^{-l/2}}{S_{lk}}\wedge 1\Big)\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{(lk)}}_{=:~\bar{Z}^{(lk)}},

where
Z_{i}^{(lk)}:=Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})
and
\alpha:=\big(\frac{n\beta_{n}}{C_{1}\log^{2}n}\wedge 1\big)
.

Per-coefficient average-case stability.

For a fixed
(l,k)
,
\theta_{lk}(\mathcal{D}_{n})
is exactly of the form in Lemma
9
with “radius”

r=B_{L}2^{-l/2}
and scalar observations
\{Z_{i}^{(lk)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(note
S_{lk}=(1/n)\sum|Z_{i}^{(lk)}|
).
Thus, for any
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
,

\frac{1}{(n{+}1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\big|\theta_{lk}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i})-\theta_{lk}(\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j})\big|\;\leq\;\frac{24\,B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}}{n{+}1}.

Sum over the (level,location) indices that affect
x_{0}
.

Using the triangle inequality and
(\star)
,

\frac{1}{(n{+}1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\big|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i}}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j}}(x_{0})\big|\;\leq\;\alpha\sum_{l,k}|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\cdot\frac{24\,B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}}{n{+}1}.

By localization, for each
l
there are at most
K_{\mathrm{loc}}
such
k
, and
|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\leq C_{\psi}2^{l/2}
, so

\sum_{k:~\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0}|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\,2^{-l/2}\;\leq\;K_{\mathrm{loc}}\,C_{\psi}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{(n{+}1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\big|\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus i},\xi}(x_{0})-\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n+1}^{\setminus j},\xi}(x_{0})\big|\;\leq\;\alpha\cdot\frac{24\,B_{L}\,K_{\mathrm{loc}}\,C_{\psi}}{n{+}1}\cdot\#\{l_{0},\ldots,L\}.

Since
\#\{l_{0},\ldots,L\}=L-l_{0}+1\lesssim L
and
L\asymp\log n
,
B_{L}=C\log n
, we get

\text{LHS}\;\leq\;\alpha\cdot\frac{C_{1}\,\log n\cdot\log n}{n{+}1}\;=\;\alpha\cdot\frac{C_{1}\,\log^{2}n}{n{+}1},

for a constant
C_{1}
depending only on
(C_{0},K_{\mathrm{loc}},C_{\psi},l_{0})
.

Choice of the global shrinkage
\alpha
.

By definition
\alpha=\min\{n\beta_{n}/(C_{1}\log^{2}n),\,1\}
. This gives

\alpha\cdot\frac{C_{1}\,\log^{2}n}{n{+}1}\;\leq\;\frac{n\beta_{n}}{C_{1}\log^{2}n}\cdot\frac{C_{1}\,\log^{2}n}{n}\;\leq\;\beta_{n},

for all
n
large enough (the
(n+1)
vs.
n
difference is absorbed into constants). Hence the displayed average-case stability bound holds with parameter
\beta_{n}
.
∎

Lemma 16

(Average-case estimator attains the unconstrained minimax rate)
.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. data generated from (
18
). Fix
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and let
\{\psi_{lk}\}
be a wavelet basis satisfying (
80
).
Then for
\hat{f}(x_{0})
as defined in (
21
) we have that there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
(depending only on
x_{0},\sigma,s,p
and the wavelet system) such that

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)}\ \mathbb{E}\bigl(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\bigr)^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}\ \,n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}\quad&\beta_{n}\geq\frac{C_{1}(\log n)^{2}}{n}\\
\ 1&\beta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let’s proceed in a step-by-step fashion. We first deal with the case
\beta_{n}\geq\frac{C_{1}(\log n)^{2}}{n}
. In this case the estimator is given by

\widehat{f}_{\mathcal{D}_{n},\xi}(x_{0})=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\left(\frac{B_{L}2^{-l/2}}{S_{lk}}\wedge 1\right)\widehat{f}_{lk}\psi_{lk}(x_{0})

Define the high-probability event.

Let

\mathcal{S}\ :=\ \Big\{\,\forall\,l\in\{l_{0},\dots,L\},\ \max_{k:\ \psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0}\ S_{lk}\ \leq\ B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}\,\Big\}.

where
S_{lk}
and
B_{L}
are as defined in (
21
).
By standard Bernstein/union-bound arguments for the family
\{Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})\}
together with the envelope and support bounds of
\psi_{lk}
(see Lemma
18
), for
C
large enough
B_{L}\geq C_{L}
(where
C_{L}
is defined in Lemma
18
), hence we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})\ \geq\ 1-2n^{-c}

for some absolute
c>2
. We will condition on
\mathcal{S}
and its complement separately.

Estimator equals the classical truncated projection on
\mathcal{S}
.

By construction, on
\mathcal{S}
the “if” branch is taken, hence

\widehat{f}(x_{0})=\sum_{l=l_{0}}^{L}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{l}-1}\ \widehat{f}_{lk}\,\psi_{lk}(x_{0}),

i.e., the usual truncated linear (projection) estimator at level
L
.

Risk on
\mathcal{S}
.

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\ \mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\right]\ \leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\ \right]\lesssim\ n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}.

where the last inequality follows from the usual bias-variance tradeoff for the unconstrained estimator (see
Cai, 2003
).

Deterministic bound on the estimator on
\mathcal{S}^{c}
.

On the complement event
\mathcal{S}^{c}
the “otherwise” branch is taken for at least one pair
(l,k)
. We first show that each
capped
coefficient has controlled magnitude:

\Bigg|\frac{\widehat{f}_{lk}\,B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}}{S_{lk}}\Bigg|\ \leq\ B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}.

This follows from the basic inequality

|\widehat{f}_{lk}|=\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})\Big|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|\ =\ S_{lk},

so whenever
S_{lk}>0
,

\frac{|\widehat{f}_{lk}|}{S_{lk}}\ \leq\ 1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Bigg|\frac{\widehat{f}_{lk}\,B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}}{S_{lk}}\Bigg|\ \leq\ B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}.

At any fixed level
l
, by locality at most
K_{\rm loc}
indices
k
satisfy
\psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0
, and for them
|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\leq C_{\psi}\,2^{l/2}
. Therefore the level-
l
contribution in absolute value is bounded by

\sum_{k:\ \psi_{lk}(x_{0})\neq 0}\ B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}\,|\psi_{lk}(x_{0})|\ \overset{\eqref{eq:wavelet-localization}}{\leq}\ K_{\rm loc}\,B_{L}\,2^{-l/2}\cdot C_{\psi}\,2^{l/2}\ =\ K_{\rm loc}C_{\psi}\,B_{L}.

Summing over
l=l_{0},\dots,L
,

\big|\widehat{f}(x_{0})\big|\ \leq\ K_{\rm loc}C_{\psi}\,B_{L}\,(L-l_{0}+1)\ \lesssim\ B_{L}\,L\ \lesssim\ (\log n)^{2}.

(87)

Hence, deterministically on
\mathcal{S}^{c}
,

\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\ \leq\ 2\,\widehat{f}(x_{0})^{2}+2\,f(x_{0})^{2}\ \lesssim\ (\log n)^{4}+1\ \lesssim\ (\log n)^{4}.

Put the pieces together.

Decompose the risk:

\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\right]\ +\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\right].

By Step 3, the first term is
\lesssim n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}
. For the second term, use the deterministic bound from Step 4 and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}^{c})\leq 2n^{-c}
(Step 1):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\right]\ \leq\ (\log n)^{4}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}^{c})\ \lesssim\ (\log n)^{4}\,n^{-c}\ =\ o\!\big(n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}}\big)

since
c>2
and
\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}\leq 1
for all
\nu\geq 0
. Therefore,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)}\ \mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{f}(x_{0})-f(x_{0})\big)^{2}\ \lesssim\ n^{-\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+1}},

as claimed.

Next we deal with the case
\beta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}
. Using (
87
) we have that in this case too
\widehat{f}(x_{0})\lesssim\frac{n\beta_{n}}{\log^{2}n}(\log n)^{2}\lesssim 1
which implies that the risk in this case is bounded by
O(1)
.
∎

Auxiliary Lemmas

We record here two auxiliary concentration lemmas used throughout the average case analysis of the clipped-truncated wavelet estimator in Section
7
. Both results exploit the bounded support and regularity of the wavelet basis (
80
), as well as sub-Gaussian concentration for the regression noise.

Lemma 17

(Concentration for wavelet-weighted absolute responses)
.

Let
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. samples from the model (
18
)
Let
\{\psi_{lk}\}
be a wavelet basis satisfying (
80
).
Then for every resolution level
l
and index
k
, the empirical averages satisfy, for all
t\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|\;\leq\;C_{0}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{-l/2}\;+\;C_{1}\,\sqrt{(M_{f}+\sigma)\frac{t}{n}}\;+\;C_{2}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{l/2}\frac{t}{n}\right)\;\geq\;1-2e^{-t},

(88)

where
C_{0}=C_{\psi}S_{\psi}
,
M_{f}
is a constant (depends on Besov space parameters) and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are absolute constants independent of
(n,l,k,f,\sigma)
.

Proof.

We outline the argument, invoking the technical Lemma
19
to verify the required moment condition.

Bounding the mean.

Since
f\in\mathcal{B}^{s}_{p,q}(1)
, the standard embedding for Besov spaces implies
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq M_{f}
for some constant
M_{f}>0
.
Define
Z_{i}:=|Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|
with
Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\xi_{i}
. Then

\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\leq\mathbb{E}\!\big[|f(X_{i})|\,|\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|\big]+\mathbb{E}|\xi_{i}|\,\mathbb{E}|\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|.

By
\mathbb{E}|\xi_{i}|\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma
and the localization property (
80
),

\mathbb{E}|\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|\leq\|\psi_{lk}\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Leb}(\mathrm{supp}\,\psi_{lk})\leq C_{\psi}S_{\psi}\,2^{-l/2}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}Z_{i}\leq C_{\psi}S_{\psi}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{-l/2}=:C_{0}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{-l/2}.

Variance and moment condition.

To apply the Bernstein inequality, we need the centered variables
W_{i}:=Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i}

to satisfy the Bernstein moment condition.
Lemma
19
(proved above using the localization and boundedness of
\psi
) ensures that, for all
m\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}|W_{i}|^{m}\;\leq\;\frac{m!}{2}\,v\,b^{\,m-2},\qquad v=C_{v}\,C_{\psi}^{2}S_{\psi}(M_{f}+\sigma)^{2},\qquad b=C_{b}\,C_{\psi}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{l/2}.

In particular,
\mathrm{Var}(W_{i})\leq v
and
W_{i}
is sub-exponential with tail parameter
b
.

Bernstein inequality.

Applying the standard Bernstein inequality for independent mean-zero variables
satisfying the above
(v,b)
condition (see, e.g., Proposition 2.10 from
Wainwright, 2019
),
we obtain, for all
t\geq 1
,

\Pr\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right|\geq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{vt}{n}}+C_{2}\,b\,\frac{t}{n}\right)\leq 2e^{-t}.

Substituting the expressions for
(v,b)
and simplifying constants gives

\Pr\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}Z_{i})\right|\geq C_{1}^{\prime}(M_{f}+\sigma)\sqrt{\frac{t}{n}}+C_{2}^{\prime}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{l/2}\frac{t}{n}\right)\leq 2e^{-t}.

Combine with the mean bound.

Finally, adding the mean estimate from above yields

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}\psi_{lk}(X_{i})|\leq C_{0}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{-l/2}+C_{1}(M_{f}+\sigma)\sqrt{\frac{t}{n}}+C_{2}(M_{f}+\sigma)\,2^{l/2}\frac{t}{n}

with probability at least
1-2e^{-t}
, completing the proof of
(
88
).
∎

Lemma 18

(Uniform control over wavelet levels and locations)
.

Let the setup be as in Lemma
17
.
Fix an integer
L\geq l_{0}
satisfying
2^{L}\leq n
.
For
c\geq 1
, define

C_{L}\;:=\;C_{0}(M_{f}+\sigma)\;+\;C_{1}\sqrt{\,c\log n+\log(K_{\rm loc}L)\,}\;+\;C_{2}(M_{f}+\sigma)\big(c\log n+\log(K_{\rm loc}L)\big),

\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})|\mathcal{D}_{n}]
, then

\begin{split}R_{\textnormal{DP}}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),\epsilon_{n})&=\inf_{M\textnormal{ satisfies }\epsilon_{n}-{\textrm{D}P}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P)]^{2}\\
&\geq\inf_{M\textnormal{ satisfies }\epsilon_{n}-{\textrm{D}P}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P)\right]^{2}\\
&=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\textnormal{ satisfies }\epsilon_{n}-{\textrm{D}P}\\
|\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{D}_{n})|\mathcal{D}_{n}]|\leq r\textnormal{ a.s. }\end{subarray}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P)\right]^{2}\quad(\textnormal{as }|\theta|\leq r)\\
&\overset{\textnormal{Lemma }\ref{lm:privacy-to-stability}}{\geq}\inf_{\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\textnormal{ is }r(e^{\epsilon_{n}}-1)-\textnormal{worst-case-stable}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{n})-\theta(P)\right]^{2}\\
&=R_{n,\infty}(\theta(\mathcal{P}),r(e^{\epsilon_{n}}-1)).\end{split}

This finishes the proof of this proposition.
