Toward systems agroecology: Design and control of intercropping
Authors: Sirio Belga Fedeli, Stanislas Leibler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding how one can mitigate water scarcity, soil infertility, and the impact of changing weather conditions is essential to guaranteeing future food security. Here, we examine intercropping, a practice considered to be a promising alternative to monocrop agriculture, as it requires limited inputs and often provides comparable yields. In order for it to be largely adopted, it is imperative to assess its outcomes, robustness, and controllability. Based on a dataset regrouping the results of thousands of experiments, we establish the foundations of intercropping design and control. More experiments are needed before computational methods can guide the practice of intercropping with high confidence. Our results represent a prefatory step on the way to more robust and sustainable multiplant farming.

Among the pressing existential problems facing humanity is the shortfall in sustainable food production. Scientific efforts have focused predominantly on industrial agriculture, which, despite its successes, is potentially unstable, ecologically destructive, and ultimately unsustainable (1). Typically, this “conventional” agriculture has reduced the rich plant diversity of natural ecosystems to a single, genetically identical crop. At the same time, its use of tilling and chemical fertilizers has drastically modified the structure, of the soil, its chemical composition, and its ability to retain water (2, 3). Moreover, fertilization has altered natural soil biodiversity and affected symbiosis relationships between microbes and plants (4).

Our diet is now based on a handful of grain crops, selected from more than 7000 known edible plant species (5). From a systems biology point of view, such a simplistic farming design might arguably be the least stable one: Not only does it make farming completely dependent on external inputs of nutrients, but it also makes it very sensitive to various perturbations connected with weather, parasites, and diseases (6). It has become clear that this design needs to be fundamentally modified in order to develop a kind of agriculture that is not only productive but also ecologically friendly, sustainable and robust (1, 7–10).

Many researchers have suggested that future farming methods should be inspired by natural terrestrial ecosystems, which are characterized by the persistent coexistence of diverse plants, microbes, and animals (11–13). Unfortunately, practical efforts to create such nature-inspired farms and gardens, e.g., within the permaculture movement (14), have not produced much quantitative data, of a kind that could be used to systematically evaluate their results. This is also the case of more diversified types of ancestral agriculture, which still feed a significant fraction of humans and are a constant source of traditional knowledge and inspiration (15). Consequently, it is difficult to assess whether these forms of farming could be scaled up so as to be able to feed the growing human population and could be adapted to rapid climatic changes. An essential initial step toward a more systematic science-based alternative to industrial monocrop agriculture would thus be to gather quantitative agronomic data on a variety of diversified multiplant farming systems. Such data could be analyzed in order to suggest different agricultural designs, extending beyond conventional monocrop farming, which would be well-adapted to local environmental and climatic conditions.

Unfortunately, such data are currently missing. Therefore, we focused our efforts on two-plant intercropping (IC), for which published quantitative data could be found. In typical IC farming, two plants, the main crop and its companion crop, are grown in tandem on the same piece of land (1, 16). The IC experiments included in our meta-analysis compare the results of such mixed cultivation with the cultivation of a sole main crop on a nearby field. Although IC experiments did not allow us to directly address more general questions associated with the design of highly diversified multiplant sustainable agriculture, they permitted us to test some basic computational tools and approaches that might become useful for this more general task.

In particular, we show here how one could address four fundamental questions connected with IC, which will also need to be answered in the future for multiplant agricultural designs. These questions are as follows: (A) Based on the gathered data on planted-crops traits, local ecological variables, and chosen farming practices, is it possible to predict the productivity for a given IC cultivation? (B) Provided such highly accurate predictions are possible, can one assess which agroecological variables contribute the most to these predictions? (C) Is it possible to evaluate the robustness of such predictions to external perturbations, such as unusual weather conditions? (D) Finally, is it possible to prescribe ways to control IC farming by changing alterable practices (e.g., sowing densities, choice of companion crop, soil pH, etc.), so as to increase IC productivity?

Positive answers to all four questions constitute the main results of our work. We would like to state right away, though, that despite being very encouraging, these positive computational results are not without shortcomings, several of which will be described below. Before applying the computational approach presented here to real situations, an important expansion of systematically gathered experimental data would be needed. Larger, better datasets would not only ameliorate the accuracy of predictions and control, but would also make possible a careful verification of the practical applicability of the recent computational methods used here.

Dataset of IC Experiments and T-EF Variables

We first built a dataset that grouped together publicly available quantitative data on the results of IC experiments. A detailed description of the information-gathering process can be found (SI Appendix, sections 1 and 2). The main sources of the data were hundreds of peered-reviewed articles, books, and catalogs, the information found on the websites of nonprofit organizations, and several existing databases of plant traits. IC experiments span the 1979–2021 time period and involve 274 pairs of 69 different plants. The data on the IC experiments include: i) the ratio of yields for the main crop in IC and sole conditions YMIC/YM, ii) experimental measurements of environmental conditions, and farming practices, F, which include the densities of the main and companion plants, dM, dMIC and dCIC, and iii) data on the plant traits, TM’s (for the main plant) and TC’s (for the companion plant), found in other publications and plant databases (for details about the format and composition of the dataset, see SI Appendix, sections 1 and 2 and Fig. S9).

Table 1 lists all 25 variables chosen for our dataset: 8 traits for each crop, 6 environmental variables including 4 soil characteristics, and 3 crop densities. We eliminated additional trait variables that are strongly correlated with the selected ones (SI Appendix, section 2G). Some of the environmental variables can be modified by farmers, e.g., through irrigation, addition of lime, shading plants from the sun, or soil regeneration practices. For this reason, we divide all the variables into only two groups: plant traits, or “T variables,” and all environmental and farming conditions, or “EF variables” (together called “T-EF variables,” Table 1). It should be noted that in conventional monoculture, dM, as well as several other EF variables, such as pH, are often modified so as to approach the highest yields, and it is only these partly optimized values that one encounters in publications.

IC Productivity and Importance Values

There is no general agreement on what IC productivity is; many proposed measures have been used in literature to characterize IC performance (SI Appendix, sections 1D and 5). Here, we consider the ratio YMIC/YM, often called the partial land equivalent ratio, pLER (17), and define IC productivity based on this quantity. Since we compare IC yields with yields in monocrop conditions, pLER is a comparative quantity and does not carry any information on the increase or decrease of individual yields. In other terms, this quantity is useful only to assess the effects of growing main plants in tandem with their companions, compared to growing them alone. To fully assess the overall effects of agricultural modifications, the effect of these modifications on individual plants and yields should also be evaluated. (Discussion and Perspective).

In order to gain knowledge on the dependency of IC pLER on the T-EF variables, xi’s, we used a machine-learning algorithm based on the Random Forest Regressor (RFR, 18) by training it over a part (80%) of the experimental IC dataset. This regressor allowed us to estimate pLER with R2≃0.7 when presented with T-EF variables xi’s corresponding to the remaining (20%) IC experiments, which were held back from training (Methods and SI Appendix, section 7A). For the experiments in the training set we found that the coefficient of determination R2 is ≃0.9. Subsequently, in order to elucidate the contribution of each variable to the pLER of an IC experiment, we used an explainable and model-agnostic machine learning tool (based on SHapley Additive exPlanations, or SHAP values (19, 20); see Methods and SI Appendix, section 7B). In brief, pLER value was approximated by a sum of N nonlinear terms, ϕ’s: pLER≈ϕ0+∑i=1Nϕi(xi). The term ϕ0 is the sample mean of pLER over the dataset and it is equal to 0.67. Each of the remaining terms ϕi(xi) depends only on a single T-EF variable xi. One can find its contribution to the estimate of pLER by calculating the value of ϕi(xi): Large positive (negative) values of ϕi(xi) contribute importantly to the high (low) values of pLER. The contribution of xi is negligible whenever the absolute value of ϕi(xi) is sufficiently small. Therefore, following the nomenclature of ref. 19, for a given IC experiment, we call ϕi(xi) the “importance” of the T-EF variable xi. In SHAP procedure, the function ϕi(xi) is not computed for the full range of the variable xi. Instead, a unique value of each ϕi is computed for every experiment in the IC dataset. Subsequently, one can obtain a “trend line” showing the full dependency of ϕi on xi by calculating a nonlinear fitting trough, e.g., LOWESS (locally weighted scatter-plot smoothing) regression procedure (Methods). The sample mean of |ϕi(xi)| across the dataset, indicated by |ϕi|¯, gives an overall estimate of the importance of the variable xi.

To our knowledge, there is no general, mathematically rigorous way to estimate the CIs of SHAP values. Therefore, we use the following procedure. First, we calculate the SHAP values for the IC experiments with the outcomes pLER randomly shuffled. Then, for each variable xi, we use two times the sample SD of the new ϕi’s as a conservative estimate of the significance levels of the original ϕi’s. These significance levels are depicted as dotted horizontal lines in Figs. 1 and 2. Outside of the interval bounded by these lines, the values of ϕi(xi)’s are considered significant.

Major T-EF Variables.

The collection of |ϕi|¯s in descending order provides a rough ranking of the importance of all T-EF variables (Fig. 1A). Additionally, by plotting the importance ϕi(xi) as a function of xi we can uncover the existence of different regimes or trends. The lines of LOWESS nonparametric regression, depicted in Fig. 1B–G, are an indication of trends, and are accompanied by the measure of statistical confidence. In the following, we will consider only the first 7 variables with the largest values of |ϕi|¯. These “major variables” contribute the most to determining the values of pLER, i.e., predicting the performance of an IC experiment, as well as to assessing the robustness of such predictions (see SI Appendix, sections 7D.2 and 7D.3 and Fig. S25 and Methods for more details on the definition and properties of major variables). Our proposal regarding the control and design of IC experiments will thus be based on these 7 major variables.

Importance Values for EF Variables.

The importance values of the main plant density, ϕdM, are depicted in Fig. 1B. Each point in the figure corresponds to a single IC experiment (colored by its corresponding pLER value). The trend line, calculated by using LOWESS regression, shows that at low dM (pink region) the importance values ϕdM are significantly positive and higher pLERs are favored. Similarly, at high dM (blue region) the importance values ϕdM are significantly negative and lower pLER outcomes are favored. The significance of ϕdM is evaluated with respect to the importance values calculated with the randomly shuffled outcomes pLER (two dotted horizontal lines in Fig. 1B).

The box plot depicted in Fig. 1C shows the range of the values of dM for different families of plants represented in our dataset. It is clear that all the 2258 experiments explored only typical sowing densities dM for the used crops. Similarly, the dMIC densities were not systematically varied in the experiments but rather were chosen to be within the range of typically used values. For all experiments taken together, however, the values of dM, or dMIC, span almost four decades.

The IC density of the main crop, dMIC, ranks first in terms of the importance value |ϕi|¯ (Fig. 1A). Fig. 1D includes the graph of importance values ϕdMIC for all IC experiments. In contrast to dM (Fig. 1B), the importance values ϕdMIC are significantly positive and high pLER outcomes are favored at high dMIC (blue region), while the importance values ϕdMIC are significantly negative, and low pLER outcomes are favored at low dMIC (pink region).

The nonparametric regression lines both for dMIC and dM are monotone for the whole range of used densities. Both dMIC and dM become less important across the region comprising densities between 10 and 100 m−2 (central white area in Fig. 1 B and C). If one limits oneself only to experiments within this density region, one finds that the importance of other T-EF variables becomes comparable to that of dMIC and dM (Fig. 1D–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

Fig. 1E is the graph of the importance value for the companion plant density dCIC. Similarly to dM, the LOWESS regression line of ϕdCIC has a clear decreasing trend. In the region for intermediate densities 10≲dCIC≲100 m−2, no conclusions can be reached for low and intermediate dCIC since the importance values may be statistically indistinguishable from those obtained by shuffling pLER values within the IC dataset.

The next major EF variables are pH and Rad. Fig. 1F depicts the graph of pH importance, ϕpH. For low and neutral pH the nonparametric fitting line is basically flat and the importance values for the IC outcome pLER are very low. However, IC does seem advantageous in strongly alkaline soils. Although a positive trend of the nonparametric fitting line for ϕpH can be observed for pH>7 there is also an important scatter around it. This indicates that a positive effect on pLER might be due not only to a general principle [such as low cation availability, or high calcium carbonate soil content (21)], but it might also be enhanced by various hidden causes that may be idiosyncratic to particular experiments. Furthermore, the experiments that have extensively addressed the dependency of sole-crop yields on pH indicate higher values of pH to be preferable (22–24). However, to our knowledge, no systematic IC experiments have been performed along a large gradient of pH while keeping the remaining variables as similar as possible. If one limits the analysis to the region where ϕpH>0.05 and pH>7 one finds that pH and either dM, dMIC or dCIC are the most important EF variables. Hence, before claiming that high pH strongly promotes high values of pLER outcomes, one should explore the main crop density values in the mid-range 10 to 100 m−2, where their importance values are low (white regions in Fig. 1 B and D). We should note that pH might also have high importance values due to indirect effects. For instance, since soil pH is considered a good predictor of the composition of microbial communities (25), it is possible that plant–microbe interactions are responsible for the observed behavior of the pH importance function. Unfortunately, the composition of microbe communities is currently too rarely assessed and reported to be included as an EF variable in the IC dataset. It is also interesting to note that a large majority of the experiments with pH>7 and ϕpH>0.05 are legume-based (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

The graph of the importance of ϕRad, i.e., of the solar radiation received by the plants, Rad, is depicted in Fig. 1G. The nonparametric fitting line for ϕRad is basically flat and close to zero for a wide range of Rad values. The importance values for IC experiments with Rad≥3,500 MJ/m2 indicate that pLER could be positively influenced in the regime of high Rad values. However, except for very large values of Rad, the LOWESS regression line lying within the shuffling significance levels (black dashed lines in Fig. 1F) does not allow to draw further conclusions. Unfortunately, similarly to the situation with pH, no systematic IC experiments have been performed over a wide range of Rad values.

For a given choice of crop species and environment, both pH and Rad, are considered to have a range of values that is optimal for plant growth. Any deviation outside this range would result in stress, whose intensity would depend on the deviation extent. Several experiments showed that positive interactions between plant species may arise as stress intensity increases. This phenomenon is the foundation of the so-called stress gradient hypothesis (26). The convex shape of ϕpH and ϕRad might be a partial manifestation of this hypothesis, since large values of pH and large values of Rad both seem to promote high pLER outcomes in some measure. We will return to the stress gradient hypothesis and its validity in Discussion.

Importance Values for T Variables.

The fact that plant densities seem to be by far the most important variables for determining pLER raises the question of whether the choice of plant combination for intercropping is of any importance. Both scientific literature and practical gardening books (27, 28), however, contain numerous recommendations or suggestions for plant combinations. We will now address this apparent contradiction.

The two traits of the companion plants with significant absolute importance values are SRLC and DNAC. These are the specific root length, i.e. the total length of roots of a single plant divided by their dry biomass and the weight of the haploid 1C-DNA multiplied by the minimal ploidy, respectively. They only rank fourth and seventh, respectively, among the major 7 variables (in blue, Fig. 1A). However, it can be instructive to focus our attention on the experiments performed in the intermediate range of densities, typically considered in gardening and companion-planting books (27, 28). There are 109 experiments for which |ϕdM|, |ϕdMIC| and |ϕdCIC| have values smaller than the corresponding significance levels (SI Appendix, section 7C.3 and Fig. S14). For almost all of these experiments, the range of densities is 10<dM,dMIC<102 m−2 (intermediate white region in Fig. 1 B and C). For these 109 experiments, SRLC becomes the first ranked T-EF variable, followed only by pH (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 and sections 7C.3–7C.5). Therefore, in this range of intermediate densities, the choice of the companion species becomes highly relevant.

The choice of the main crop, on the other hand, is encoded in the density dM, which is a “hybrid” T-EF variable as it is also a characteristic of the crop being grown.

Recommendations of plant combinations are usually made upon invoking the complementarity hypothesis (also called the principle of complementarity, or “niche differentiation;” see e.g., refs. 29, 30). In particular, this principle implies that pairs of plants with opposite functional characteristics, niches, and diverging traits values should be preferred in IC. In particular, one would expect that: i) the ranks of importance for the main and companion for a trait T (i.e, for |ϕTM|¯ and |ϕTC|¯, respectively) would be large and close to each other; ii) the two importance functions ϕTC and ϕTM would show opposite trends. This does not seem to be the case for the plants in our dataset (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20): Only SRLC and DNAC, and not SRLM and DNAM, are among major important T variables. Traits LA and SeedP might show some complementarity, although it may not be statistically significant (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). More data are clearly needed to further explore these observations.

Indeed, it is important to recall that the data used here for the plant trait variables were typically not measured in the IC experiments themselves, and were obtained from other publications and databases. Therefore, in order to emphasize this difference, we are not depicting them as separate points corresponding to IC experiments, as in Fig. 1, but rather only in a statistical manner, as the box plots in Fig. 2 A and B. From Fig. 2A, which depicts the graph of the importance values ϕSRLC, we can see that companion plants with small SRL seem to be preferred in IC experiment. The graph of importance values ϕDNAC for DNAC is shown in Fig. 2B. Companion species with smaller genomic content DNA seem to be preferred to achieve higher pLER. Both these results are a priori surprising.

Obviously, SRLC and DNAC influence effective plant–plant interactions only indirectly—they can be viewed as “proxies,” or indicators, of IC-reinforcing mechanisms. For details, see SI Appendix, sections 7C.5 and 7C.6. We will describe some possible connections to plant physio-ecological phenomena in Discussion (See also SI Appendix, Table S7 for companion species suggestions).

Dimensional Reduction of the IC Space

Ultimately, two of the main aims of this work are to assess the robustness of IC experiments to perturbations, and to find how IC can be controlled. To do this, we want to understand how the outcome pLER changes when the T-EF variables are jointly (intentionally or unintentionally) changed. The N=25 dimensional space of T-EF variables is clearly cumbersome to portrait and handle while investigating IC robustness and IC control. To overcome this problem, we seek a low dimensional representation of the 25-dimensional space. To do this, we proceed in a few successive steps as described below.

Until now, we have considered the space of the importance values ϕ(x) for all 2258 IC experiments, which we can call the “intercropping space” (IC space). In this 25-dimensional space, in order to compare two IC experiments characterized by the sets of variables xa and xb, one can simply use the Manhattan distance (metric) between their “importance vectors” ϕ(xa) and ϕ(xb) (SI Appendix, section 7D). What makes such a comparison possible is the fact that each ϕ(x) is a unit-free vector, and thus does not depend on the disparate units in which different T-EF variables were measured. The problem with this high-dimensional space is that any search for the T-EF variables associated with high IC productivity becomes extremely difficult. We have, therefore, investigated the possibility of a dimensionality reduction of the IC space, i.e. the existence of a lower-dimensional description of the experiments (with dimension d<N=25) that would retain most of the information necessary to make accurate predictions. For this, we used multidimensional scaling (MDS), a nonlinear statistical method aiming to project a dataset onto a low-dimensional space, while preserving distances (see ref. 31 and Methods). Fig. 3A shows the behavior of the normalized stress for MDS as a function of dimensions of the projection. Remarkably, even d=2 seems to provide a satisfactory reduced dimension for the IC space. The MDS dimensional analysis seems to be corroborated by the results of the ranking of |ϕi|¯ values, depicted in Fig. 1A where few T-EF variables are relevant to predicting pLER.

Therefore, we used d=2 for further analysis. It is important to stress that d=2 is not chosen merely to simplify the presentation of the results, but rather because it happens indeed to be an intrinsic property of the IC space generated by the IC dataset. A MDS projection make it possible to capture nonlinear correlations between ϕi’s. MDS identifies two new “effective” variables, z1 and z2, which can be used to assess the IC productivity of any vector of T-EF variables (Fig. 3B).

Indeed, a given vector of importance values of T-EF variables corresponds to a unique point on the (z1,z2) space. We call this two-dimensional space the “reduced intercropping space” (RIC space). Note that, in contrast to the often-used linear principal component analysis representation of the reduced space, the nonlinear MDS projection is not unique, e.g., it even depends on the used random number initialization. For now, we choose one realization of the MDS (Figs. 3 and 4), and we will see that the main results, such as robustness and control of the RIC space, do not depend on this choice in a significant manner (SI Appendix, section 7D.4).

A paramount feature of the RIC space is the separation between high and low pLER values, as shown in Fig. 3B. Fig. 3B shows that the z1 variable roughly separates the low pLER experiments (z1>0) from the high pLER ones (z1<0). Also, the values of z2 partially separate high and low values for the T-EF variables (SI Appendix, Fig. S22), while the values of z1 partially separate functional types of plant combinations. As an example, we depict in Fig. 3C the distribution of different IC plant-family pairs across the RIC space.

Robustness of Intercropping to Perturbations.

Dimensionality reduction and the construction of the RIC space drastically simplify the study of IC experiments and makes it possible to analyze the robustness of IC to various perturbations, such as a change in solar radiation or precipitation. Let us suppose that, based on the knowledge of previously measured T-EF variables, one chooses the companion crop and the farming practice (through variables such as dMIC, dCIC, pH, etc.) so as to place IC in the region where pLER≈0.82 (point p in Fig. 3D, data from ref. 32). Now, let us imagine that the external EF conditions after sowing unexpectedly deviate from those previously anticipated, for instance, the seasonal solar radiation Rad is significantly higher or lower than expected (by δRad). The predictions for IC productivity will then have to be modified: Point p will move to another position p′ in the RIC space (for details, see SI Appendix, section 7D.3), which may correspond to a different value of pLER. Fig. 3D shows a cloud of such modified points (and their pLER values) corresponding to perturbations in 3 major EF variables: pH, Rad and dM. Importantly, we expect in general that the perturbations in these 3 variables will be correlated. For instance, a large amount of solar radiation can modify the soil pH. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, there have been no detailed quantitative studies of such correlations (and their dependence on other EF variables). Thus, as a simple illustration, we assume that possible correlations are described by multivariate Gaussian distributions. As we can see from Fig. 3D, the cloud of perturbed points can extend into the region of low pLER. The extension of this cloud, as well as the pLER values it encompasses, can be used as a measure of the robustness of IC performance predictions to possible external perturbations (see Methods and SI Appendix, section 8 for an example of possible measure).

Control of Intercropping.

The main goal of “IC control” would be to satisfactorily increase the pLER and its probability, e.g., to move from a set of T-EF variables that results in low pLER to another one that may result in high pLER. In practice, this can be done by changing one or several alterable T-EF variables (Table 1), such as pH or plant densities. We will call such changes agroecological modifications (AMs). In principle, IC control could involve a search in a high-dimensional space (d=25) aimed at finding the appropriate set of variable changes that is required. One could perform such a search randomly until a higher pLER is hit. However, such random method is inefficient, costly, and does not constitute any form of real control procedure. More efficient methods are clearly needed.

One such method is to use the 2-dimensional RIC space to design a transition from the low pLER IC region (red points in Fig. 4A) to the high pLER one (green points in Fig. 4A). The movement away from a given point in the RIC space can be achieved by AM’s involving any set of alterable T-EF variables, but we will consider only changes in the 7 major ones (Fig. 1A). A change of one of these variables corresponds to a movement in the (z1,z2)-space along one of the directions depicted in Fig. 4B (for details, see SI Appendix, section 7D.3). A rule of thumb (which is only approximate, and of probabilistic nature) tells us that AM’s have to be more pronounced for larger moves across the z1 axis. The choice of the set of T-EF variables for AM’s that allows such a transition, on the other hand, depends largely on the starting z2 value. For example, if one starts with the farming practice corresponding to a point with z2≃0.4 in Fig. 4A, then increasing the pH or choosing a main crop with higher dM would be a bad modification, since the point would move toward unexplored regions of the projection or toward points with low pLER (see Fig. 4B for directions corresponding to the increase of the major variables). On the other hand, when starting at point b, with a very different starting value of z2, an increase of pH could become beneficial, especially if it is accompanied by other appropriately chosen changes. Furthermore, note that one can also introduce a particular change that lowers the pLER, provided that the complete AM, which includes this change, results in a total movement toward smaller z1’s with higher pLER.

Let us illustrate this concept of a controlled transition likely leading from a low pLER to a higher pLER through AM’s consisting of appropriately chosen alterable-T-EF-variable changes. For this, we choose as starting points three IC experiments from our dataset (marked as a, b, c in Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S8). They involve different main crops: b and c are grass-based while a is legume-based. One or two alternative AM’s are proposed for each starting point (SI Appendix, Table S8) and are depicted in Fig. 4A. For clarity’s sake, in Fig. 4A, we represent only one AM starting at a [named AM(1)] as a path formed by four arrows, corresponding to four individual T-EF variable changes. Since the final results of AM’s do not depend on the order in which we calculate the effects of individual changes, they should be thought of as being applied simultaneously. Therefore, for other modifications AM(2) to AM(4), we regroup individual changes into single “composite arrows,” starting at b [AM(2)] or c [AM(3) and AM(4)]. For all four AM’s, the final position has smaller z1, showing that, indeed, the proposed AM’s are likely to lead to higher pLER.

We will now describe some details of the proposed four AM’s using the names of the crops, EF variables, and alterable modifications corresponding to real IC experiments. One should not forget, however, that we are presenting results of theoretical computations, rather than presenting truly practical proposals. Both used data and computational tools present important limitations described in Discussion, Methods, and SI Appendix. In addition, as mentioned before (IC Productivity), the proposed changes, although likely leading to high pLER, may substantially decrease individual yields themselves. Therefore, any modification, such as an alteration of the soil pH above 7, shading from solar radiation Rad, or replacement of the main crop, would need to be tested first in sole crop cultivation. For the starting farming practice a, pLER≃0.7 result can be achieved through increasing dMIC (by 33%), lowering dCIC (to 5% of its original value), and replacing the companion plant (oat) with a crop having lower SRLC and DNAC (lentil) [see AM(1) in SI Appendix, Table S8]. The grass-based IC example b has instead dMIC equal to half of dM. Halving of dCIC and increase of pH could then give a satisfactory outcome [pLER≃0.7; see AM(2) in SI Appendix, Table S8]. Last, the grass-based IC intercropping experiment c corresponds to a high soil pH. Predicted AM’s include replacing broad bean with another legume companion, e.g., grass pea, while increasing dMIC to 180 m−2. This intervention leads to the final value pLER≃0.8 [AM(3) in SI Appendix, Table S8]. Possible constraints of keeping both wheat and broad bean, rather than replacing one or both of them, would necessitate a different modification, such as further increasing dMIC and decreasing dCIC to 230 m−2 and 5 m−2, respectively. This modification, listed as AM(4) in SI Appendix, Table S8, would likely lead to a high value pLER≃0.9.

Farming practices that correspond to points lying in the RIC space near these three IC experiments (a, b, c) include herb-legume and shrub-legume combinations (Fig. 3C). It seems that for all of them, one can similarly identify multiple appropriate agroecological modifications likely leading to higher pLER. In general, for a grass-based IC, the choice of the appropriate T-EF variables to be modified strongly depends on the position of the starting point in the RIC space (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix). The upper subregion (z2>0) corresponds to grass-based and legume-based IC experiments, with low densities dMIC, dM and dCIC, and shrub-based IC experiments. In this area possible AM’s include increasing dMIC. Increasing pH, on the other hand, should probably not be considered as a possible remedy, since such a change could lead to an unexplored RIC region. The lower subregion (z2<0) corresponds to grass-based and legume-based IC experiments, with high dMIC, dM and dCIC. Changing the companion plant to one with lower SRLC and DNAC is an alternative or a complementary solution. A decrease of dCIC might be considered, provided it is economically manageable. The legume-based IC experiments are the hardest to modify as they lie further away from the high pLER region (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S23). Hence, more than a single change is necessary in order to obtain reasonably high pLER [similar to AM(1)].

Certain companion species, coupled with an appropriate choice of alterable EF variables, might increase the yield per plant of the main crop. Arguably, increasing per-plant yields may represent one of the reasons behind adopting IC practices. More importantly, using per-plant yields may teach us more about the fundamental biology of intercropping. The increase of per-plant yield can occur when pLER>dMIC/dM. We will call “successful” an IC experiment that satisfies this inequality. The outcomes of AM(2) and AM(3) are indeed categorized as successful, while the outcomes of AM(1) and AM(4) are categorized as unsuccessful. One might have liked to predict such “success”-outcomes in parallel with pLER, rather than evaluate them a posteriori. Dimensional reduction of the ϕ’s by itself does not provide clear indications on how to achieve success as defined above (SI Appendix, section 9 and Fig. S29). Unfortunately, we find that S cannot be investigated with machine-learning techniques if per-plant yields are not directly recorded in the experiments (in contrast to measuring the yield and dividing it by the density; see SI Appendix, section 1D).

Discussion and Perspective

The work presented here is by no means the first meta-analysis of IC experiments. We describe some of the main conclusions of previous meta-analyses in SI Appendix, section 5. Compared with these published studies, our meta-analysis increased the number of IC experiments in the dataset by approximately one order of magnitude and took into account a larger number (25) of T-EF variables simultaneously. This allowed us to use a supervised learning algorithm (RFR), which generated surprisingly accurate predictions (result A in SI Appendix, Fig. S28). Not all of the 25 variables were found to have the same importance values: 7 major variables made a statistically significant contribution to the IC performance predictions (result B, SI Appendix, Fig. S28). Moreover, MDS dimensional reduction showed that the majority of the information necessary to make these predictions can be included in a couple of “effective variables.” Furthermore, we were able to create a nonlinear representation of the 2-dimensional “reduced IC space” so that experiments with different values of the IC outcome, measured by pLER, are not intermixed. This allowed us to explore the robustness of IC to perturbations (result C, SI Appendix, Fig. S28), and to show how one can control the IC outcome by changing alterable T-EF variables (result D, SI Appendix, Fig. S28).

The use of available information to build up a low-dimensional representation of a complex system and to develop a computational framework for quantitative predictions and control is one of the main characteristics of successful systems biology approaches in cellular and organismic biology (33) and medicine (34). Systems biology, at its best, aspires to achieve two goals: i) to uncover a small number of relevant effective variables that capture the essence of the collective (system-level) phenomenon under study; ii) to uncover underlying general mechanisms that can be expressed through phenomenological models using these effective variables. The quantitative models should have predictive power so that they could be falsified by further experimental results. It would be interesting to think about our results from this perspective. First, let us consider the search for relevant effective variables.

The high ranking of the importance of crop densities (Fig. 1A) confirmed the intuitive view that they should be paramount for pLER, and it is not difficult to enumerate the many potential reasons for this, such as their effect on yield, light, and water usage (35–38).

We start with describing the effect of dM, the sole crop density, on pLER. Indeed, dM is more than a simple EF variable as it is a characteristic of the field (i.e., its carrying capacity) and of the crop species M. The importance of dM for single crop exhibits the decreasing trend shown in Fig. 1B (SI Appendix, section 7C.2 and Fig. S13). Therefore, high pLER might be achieved for species sown at low density, or on low-fertility fields. The effect of dMIC on pLER is easy to understand by noticing that, for dMIC≪dM, i.e., away from the optimal density dM, pLER∝YMIC∝dMIC (see ref. 35). For dMIC>dM, on the other hand, one should not expect pLER>1, as the field has a carrying capacity close to dM. Indeed, in the IC dataset for dMIC>dM, pLER¯≃0.81. Finally, increasing dCIC seems to make the companion plants to compete with the main crop.

Compared to a relatively simple explanation of the effects of three crop densities, the presence of SRLC and DNAC among the 7 major variables is more intriguing. Obviously these two traits are only “proxies” for underlying complex ecological phenomena that need to be elucidated.

In ecology, SRL is believed to reflect plant’s resource-acquisition strategies, for nutrients obtained from the soil pool. In particular, SRL represents a qualitative measure of efficiency between soil exploration and amount of biomass invested. Hence, large values of SRL are associated with more “exploratory” roots. In the context of IC, the field experiment of ref. 39 may elucidate a possible agroecological significance of SRL. The experiment consisted of 20 crop species of multiple families and genera grown in identical conditions, at the locally recommended densities. The specific root length SRL negatively correlated with carbon over nitrogen (C:N) and carbon over phosphorous (C:P) ratios of the root tissues. Indeed, roots with high SRL were able to sequester more nutrients per unit root-tissue mass. Therefore, the trend and values for the importance of SRLC, depicted in Fig. 2A, might be a manifestation of this phenomenon [for a comparison between ϕSRL and the experiment of (39), see SI Appendix, section 7C.5 and Fig. S16]. Indeed, ϕSRLC, has an increasing number of negative values for large SRLC. Thus, to achieve high pLER, one might want to avoid companion plants with highly exploratory roots as they better sequester soil nutrients, to the disadvantage of the main crop.

Note that another trait, DNAC, strengthens this agroecological interpretation. A possible agroecological interpretation of the DNAC-importance result is offered by the Rothamsted Park Grass experiments, in which many individual plots were maintained at different pH values and within multiple fertilization regimes. One of the results from this series of long-term experiments was that plots continuously receiving a large amount of fertilizers shifted their plant communities toward few species (mostly grasses) with larger genomic contents (40). A similar long-term experiment in Germany (41) studying this ecological phenomenon and the role played by the ploidy of the plants, seemed to confirm this conclusion (for a reanalysis of the data and comparison with the Rothamsted Park results, see SI Appendix, section 7C.6 and Fig. S17). It is plausible that DNAC is a proxy for the nutrient requirements and the competitive advantage of certain plants grown in fertilized environments. In view of these results, one can argue that the disadvantage of having companion plants with large genomes, as shown in Fig. 2B, could reside in their ability to thrive in fertilized fields at the expense of the main crop.

As attractive as these arguments might seem, only future quantitative experiments and further studies will be able to shed light on the mechanisms underlying the IC importance of SRLC and DNAC. With an expansion of the IC dataset through additional experiments, an “agnostic” approach should also make it possible to uncover further relevant effective variables, so as to increase the accuracy of IC performance predictions and of IC control.

While our approach made dimensional reduction and the uncovering of the effective variables possible, it has not reached the second goal of systems biology, namely, it did not lead to a quantitative phenomenological model that would generate accurate predictions and control procedures using the uncovered effective variables. For this, further experimental, computational, and theoretical work is clearly needed. At the same time, our results test the generality of two phenomenological hypotheses formulated in the past to explain IC results. The first is the stress gradient hypothesis (26). Although we found that the convex shape of ϕpH and ϕRad might be partially supporting this hypothesis, this was not the case for ϕH2O, which did not show any clear trend with increasing stress (SI Appendix, section 7C.7 and Fig. S18). The absence of H2O among the major T-EF variables might be explained as follows. The majority of IC experiments performed in agricultural experimental stations might have not been limited by water. In case of scarcity of rainfall, irrigation might have been provided, masking the veritable contribution of H2O to the outcome pLER.

The second often-evoked hypothesis is the complementarity principle (29, 30). As noted before, we could not confirm this principle either for SRLC or DNAC, the two major traits of plants in our dataset. Also, the complementarity principle is often put forward invoking the roots of main and companion plants (42). However, in our analysis, the main crop traits SRLM and DNAM were both found to be of negligible importance.

Despite encouraging results, our approach still has inherent shortcomings, mainly related to the sparsity and limitations of the experimental dataset collected: i) the lack of extensive quantitative data forced us to limit our measure of IC performance to pLER, based only on the seed yields of the main crop. We were also unable to use more biologically relevant per-plant yields, as these were not directly measured and reported in the IC experiments; ii) published IC experiments generally do not involve any systematic variation of EF variables, such as densities, pH, radiation, etc. Additionally, many T-EF variables are not measured directly in the experiments, so we had to obtain them from other sources. At present, published data include only traditional, generic, or simply “easy-to-measure” quantities. Ideally, T-EF variables should be chosen by taking into account plant physiology and agroecological aspects of IC, i.e., they should be close to functional variables that influence plants growth, their mutual interactions, and their interactions with the environment (43); iii) when searching for realistic, manageable agroecological interventions that would increase the productivity of IC experiments, it would be important to take into account the consequences of all modifications, including the decrease of densities or increase of pH. Unfortunately, there are only few published quantitative data for sole-crop experiments, in which the variables, such as densities, were systematically varied (35). It might seem paradoxical that we found better data for intercropping than for sole cropping. As noted before, IC experiments are comparative in nature, i.e., they compare IC results with those obtained from cultivating the main crop grown in the same environmental conditions and with the same farming practices (other than densities). In contrast, data for sole-crop experiments alone, spanning many different conditions and practices, would need to be much more extensive and precise so as to be statistically significant; iv) our approach is static in nature, while farming is an inherently dynamic process. Only a small part of IC experimental data includes any temporal aspects, such as the variation in relative sowing times (44). Under present circumstances, we have not been able to evaluate to what extent IC performance depends on the choice of slow- or fast- growing cultivars or species, on temporal variations in weather conditions, etc. While describing possible agroecological modifications, we assumed that they were performed simultaneously. It is easy to imagine that with the future expansion of IC data into the temporal domain, it should be possible to generalize our approach to dynamic interventions consisting of nonsimultaneous AM’s; v) the differences found while measuring LER using biomass versus seed yields suggest that the plasticity in resource allocation might be an important factor. Additionally, recent IC experiments showed that IC itself induces plants’ phenotypic plasticity and some changes in plant functional traits (45). The limitation of such data to only a few IC systems prevented their inclusion in our meta-analysis.

In addition to the limitations connected with the scarcity of the IC data, there are other potential technical shortcomings, which could be tested and reduced in the future. Among those are i) the dependence on a simple (Manhattan) metric, based on the “importance values,” while comparing different farming practices; ii) the use of particular statistical methods, such as RFR, SHAP, or MDS, each of which comes with a set of assumptions, approximations, and limitations, which should be explored in future studies (see SI Appendix, section 7E in for a more complete discussion), especially once more high-quality experimental data become available.

In view of all these limitations, we consider the computational framework presented here as an encouraging opening move, rather than a set of definitive results ready to be used in practical applications. Clearly, what we urgently need are more extensive, quantitative experiments that could confirm the validity of this and similar approaches and ultimately ameliorate the quality of predictions and control methods. Computational approaches could then be applied in practice to improve farming through better IC. In our opinion, it would be even more important, however, to extend this approach to multiple plants grown in a variety of combinations and geometries, and thus to design a multitude of biodiverse systems, such as those envisioned, e.g., by the so-called Natural Systems Agriculture (11). The idea of the utility of multiplant agriculture has a long and interesting history, going back to farming practices originally developed by indigenous and ancestral cultures (15). The present interest in multicropping is strongly inspired by traditional farming practices, some of which are still alive today. A great example of this is provided by the “three-sisters”—polyculture of squash, maize, and climbing beans—which originated in Mesoamerica thousands of years ago (46, 47). Closer to our times, Darwin wrote in “The Origin of Species”: “It has been experimentally proved, that if a plot of ground be sown with one species of grass, and a similar plot sown with several distinct genera of grasses, a greater number, and greater weight of dry herbage can be raised in the latter than in the former case” (48). More recent long-term experimental studies clearly showed that plant biomass production increases with genetic and phenotypic functional diversity on grassland plots (49). Despite all of this, multicrop agriculture remains understudied and is still relatively little understood (50). Unfortunately, at present, there are almost no quantitative data that could help to design and control such systems, to extend them beyond well-known examples, and even more importantly, to adapt them to the rapidly changing climate conditions.

Ideally, future multicrop agricultural systems would thus include a large variety of edible annual and perennial plants, coexisting with microbes, soil fauna, and animals, grown together in appropriately designed and controlled settings. It is our hope that recent progress in systems biology (33) will inspire collaborations between computational and data scientists, systems biologists, agroecologists, and, above all, farmers, ultimately resulting in the discovery of emerging (51), generally applicable principles that will facilitate the design of future sustainable farming.

Methods

SHapley Additive exPlanations, SHAP.

One may want to estimate agnostically the contribution of each T-EF variable to the predictions made by RFR. In essence, RFR is approximated here by a (simpler) linear model (see refs. 19 and 20), i.e. pLE^R(x)≈ϕ0+∑i=1Nϕi(xi). The first term, ϕ0, is the sample mean of pLER over the whole dataset while the N values ϕi(xi) are the so-called SHAP values. In the text, we refer to the ϕi(xi)’s as the “importance values” of the T-EF variables xi (not to be confused with importance values used in the RFR literature). The contributions of the T-EF variables are additive and sum up to the predicted pLER. The analysis of ϕi(xi) across the samples of the dataset makes it possible to infer how statistically relevant xi is with respect to other T-EF variables (for more details, see SI Appendix, section 7B).

Fitting with LOWESS.

It is a nonparametric and local fitting of the data points (xis) such that ϕi≈βixi+αi (52). Similar to classical linear regression, a loss function is minimized. However, LOWESS introduces a local weight (the tricube weight function) for each of these points based on the distance between a point of interest x and the data points xis. Fitting was produced by considering only the 70% of the points closest to x. The CI was obtained by bootstrapping; we produced 100 fittings. For each bootstrap, we randomly selected 15% of the samples and produced a LOWESS line. The mean and the CIs are depicted, respectively, by the black lines and the gray-shaded regions in Fig. 1B–F.

Robustness to Perturbations.

The assessment of robustness to perturbations is one of the goals of this work (for a theoretical approach in SI Appendix, section 8). Here is an illustrative example depicted in Fig. 3D. As an example, we selected an experiment p from the IC dataset with pLER=0.82 (see ref. 32 for details). We assessed the robustness to perturbations by changing the 3 T-EF variables most sensitive to year-to-year variations, i.e., the vector (pH,dM,Rad). pH was independently and uniformly sampled in the interval 80 to 120% of its original values while dM and Rad were independently and uniformly sampled between 70 to 130% of their original values. In this way, we produced 20·103 random realizations and plotted the envelope of these variations, shown in Fig. 3D as a protrusion of points surrounding p. Since in reality perturbations of these variables may be correlated, we assumed that the distribution of perturbations δxi=(xi−xip)/xip around the point p follows a Gaussian density function Px(δx) with zero mean (so they are centered around the values for point p) and nontrivial covariance matrix Σ:[2]Σ=10−21.250.06250.06250.06251.5−0.1250.0625−0.1251.5.

We assumed that this covariance matrix is a plausible example and we considered the convex hull of the perturbed points such that Px(δx)>1. With this threshold, the probability of any disturbance within the convex hull to occur is ≈75%. Inside the convex hull region, marked by a dashed line in Fig. 3D, the mean and the SD of pLER are ≈0.77 and ≈0.08, respectively. These two numbers represent a measure of robustness to the assumed perturbations of the original experiment. The local pLER was evaluated on the RIC space, for each generated point, as the sample mean over the total number of randomly chosen points within the Manhattan distance r=0.03.
