On the Weak Lefschetz Property for ideals generated by powers of general linear forms
Matthew D. Booth, Pankaj Singh, Adela Vraciu

Abstract
. We provide a description of initial ideals for almost complete intersections generated by powers of general linear forms and prove that WLP in a fixed degree d d holds when the number of variables n n is sufficiently large compared to d d . In particular, we show that if n ≥ 3 ​ d − 2 n\geq 3d-2 then WLP holds for the ideal generated by squares at the degree d d spot and for n ≥ 3 ​ d − 3 2 n\geq\frac{3d-3}{2} WLP holds for ideal generated by cubes at the degree d d spot. Finally, we prove that WLP fails for the ideal generated by squares when n < 3 ​ d − 2 n<3d-2 at the d d th spot by finding an explicit element in the kernel of the multiplication by a general linear form. This shows that our bound on n n is sharp in the case of the squares.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field of characteristic zero,
P_{n}=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, and
A=P_{n}/I
where
I
is an Artinian monomial complete intersection. Given a general linear form
L\in A_{1}
, it was first proven in
[
21
]
(see also
[
22
]
) that the homomorphism
\times L^{p}:A_{d-p}\rightarrow A_{d}
has maximal rank (i.e. is either injective or surjective) for all positive integers
d
and
p
. Any standard graded Artinian algebra satisfying this condition is said to have the
Strong Lefschetz Property
(
SLP
). An interesting and fruitful offshoot is the following weakening of the SLP: if
A
is a standard graded Artinian algebra, then we say
A
has the
Weak Lefschetz Property
(
WLP
) if the homomorphism
\times L^{p}:A_{d-p}\rightarrow A_{p}
has maximal rank for
p=1
and all positive integers
d
. Since its introduction, the Weak Lefschetz Property has been examined from a variety of angles. An excellent overview of its development can be found in
[
16
]
.

One expects the Weak Lefschetz Property to hold for “most” algebras, so it is interesting to find classes of Artinian algebras where it fails. It is shown in
[
11
]
that all homogeneous ideals in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},x_{2}]
enjoy SLP (hence also WLP) and that all Artinian complete intersections in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
enjoy WLP. Moreover, it is conjectured (see, for instance, the last few lines in
[
18
]
) that any complete intersection over a field of characteristic zero has WLP, but this question remains open outside some special cases. Indeed, as the number of variables increases, deciding whether a given Artinian algebra has WLP is generally a rather difficult question. Section 2 of
[
14
]
provides some tools to limit the number of required verifications, but even then only a handful of defining ideal formats are subsequently dealt with there. One of the results from
[
14
]
that is relevant to the work in this paper is Proposition (2.1) part (b): given a level Artinian standard graded algebra, if injectivity holds for multiplication by a general linear form at some degree
d_{0}
, then injectivity also holds for all degrees
d\leq d_{0}
. (There is also a similar statement regarding surjectivity). This means that when we encounter an Artinian algebra which fails WLP, it is interesting to ask for the smallest degree where injectivity fails.

A case which has received particular attention recently is that of an ideal generated by
n+1
general linear forms raised to a uniform power, which is to say
I=(\ell_{1}^{p},\dots,\ell_{n+1}^{p})
. We note that via a change of coordinates, the
n+1
general linear forms can be taken to be
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
. It was conjectured in
[
10
]
that
A=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/(\ell_{1}^{p},\dots,\ell_{n+1}^{p})
fails to have WLP if
n
is sufficiently large. The case
n=3
was settled by Theorem 2.4 in
[
19
]
, where it is shown that WLP holds for any Artinian quotient of
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
generated by powers of linear forms. In another direction, the case
p=2
was settled by Theorem 2.12 in
[
17
]
, which states that
A
enjoys WLP precisely when either
2\leq n\leq 5
or
n=7
holds. A partial general resolution to this conjecture was provided in Theorem 6.1 of
[
15
]
(when
n\geq 6
is even), and a complete classification followed in Theorem 1.1 of
[
1
]
. The content of the latter theorem is that
A
as defined above enjoys WLP precisely when one of
n\leq 3
,
p=1
, or
(n,p)\in\big\{(4,2),\,(5,2),\,(5,3),\,(7,2)\big\}
holds.

Given a general linear form
L
, showing failure of the WLP for an algebra comes down to finding a degree where the
\times L
map fails to be injective or surjective. The results of
[
15
]
and
[
1
]
show failure of surjectivity in some degree. While these results settle the question of whether WLP holds for such algebras, the present paper represents a sort of counterpoint in the sense that our results concern failure of injectivity when multiplying by a general linear form. In particular, we provide a lower bound on the earliest degree for which WLP can fail, and we improve this bound when the defining ideal is generated by squares or cubes. We conclude by showing that the bound we obtain in the square case is sharp.

We set some notation:
P_{n}=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, and
I_{n,a}=\big(x_{1}^{a},\dots,x_{n}^{a},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{a}\big)
. For a graded ring
R
the notation
R_{d}
indicates the
d
th graded piece of
R
. Similarly, if
I
is a homogeneous ideal in a graded ring,
I_{d}
is the
d
th graded piece of
I
.

Our general result for the lower bound is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n,a,d
be positive integers with
d\geq a\geq 2
, and define
N(a,d)=\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{2d}{a-1}\right\rceil
. If
\displaystyle n\geq N(a,d)+\frac{2d-1}{a-1}
, then the map given by multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d}
is injective.

As previously mentioned, this bound is not sharp. When
a=2
(i.e. the defining ideal is generated by squares), our bound gives
n\geq 4d-1
, but we will show in Theorem (
3.7
) that
n\geq 3d-2
is enough to guarantee injectivity from degree
d-1
to degree
d
in this case. Moreover,
n\geq 3d-2
is sharp for an ideal generated by squares per Theorem (
5.1
), whose content is the failure of injectivity from degree
d-1
to degree
d
when
n\leq 3d-3
. In a similar way,
a=3
gives the bound
n\geq 2d
, but Theorem (
4.10
) shows
n\geq\frac{3d-3}{2}
will suffice to guarantee injectivity from degree
d-1
to degree
d
when the defining ideal is generated by cubes.

The presence of the Weak Lefschetz Property is closely connected to the famous conjecture of Fröberg predicting the Hilbert series of a quotient of a polynomial ring by general forms of specified degrees. More precisely, if
R=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
with
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
general forms of respective degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
, Fröberg’s conjecture states that

(1)

\mathrm{HS}(R,t)=\left[\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{r}(1-t^{d_{k}})}{(1-t)^{n}}\right]

where
\mathrm{HS}(R,t)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}(R_{d})t^{d}
and
[\ \ \ \ ]
denotes truncation before the first non-positive term.
We will say that (
1
) holds for
R
up to degree
D
if

(2)

\big[\mathrm{HS}(R,t)\big]_{D}=\left[\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{r}(1-t^{d_{k}})}{(1-t)^{n}}\right]_{D}

where
[\ \ \ ]_{D}
means truncation at the
t^{D}
term or before the first non-positive term, whichever is sooner.

It is shown in
[
6
]
that the inequality
\geq
in (
1
) is always true for any
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
, and the existence of one choice of such
f_{1},\ldots f_{r}
where equality holds implies that equality holds for general forms.
Although the conjecture is widely believed to be true, there are only a few instances in which it is known. In particular, the Conjecture (
1
) is true when
r=n+1
(see
[
20
]
) as well as when
(d,n)=(2,12),(2,13),(3,9)
(see
[
7
]
). We refer the interested reader to the survey
[
9
]
for more information.

The following observation will allow us to derive some cases where the Fröberg conjecture holds up to a certain degree as corollaries of our results about injectivity.

Observation 1.2
.

Let
R=P_{n}/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
are forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
, and let
L
be a linear form. Assume that (
1
) holds for
R
up to degree
D
, for some
D
with
R_{D}\neq 0
.

Then the map given by multiplication by
L:R_{d-1}\to R_{d}
has maximal rank for all
d\leq D
if and only if (
1
) holds for
R/(L)
up to degree
D
, where we view
R/(L)
as the quotient of
P_{n-1}
by
r
forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
.

Proof.

Since
R/(L)
is the cokernel of this map, injectivity of the map given by multiplication by
L:R_{d-1}\to R_{d}
is equivalent to

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}((R/(L))_{d})=\mathrm{max}\{\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}(R_{d})-\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}(R_{d-1}),0\}.

Therefore, injectivity for all
d\leq D
is equivalent to

\big[\mathrm{HS}(R/(L),t)\big]_{D}=\big[(1-t)\mathrm{HS}(R,t)\big]_{D}.

∎

In particular, since WLP holds for monomial complete intersections by
[
21
]
, we have the following:

Remark 1.3
.

\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n}}{I_{n,a}}\right)_{d}\right)=\mathrm{max}\{\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n+1}}{(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n+1}^{a})}\right)_{d}\right)-\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n+1}}{(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n+1}^{a})}\right)_{d-1}\right),0\}.

This disparity between the general bound of Theorem 1.1 and the bounds obtained in the cases of
a=2
and
a=3
is due to our understanding of the initial ideal in each case. Indeed, the proofs of our results concerning injectivity share a similar structure in which we first find the initial ideal with respect to the reverse lexicographic order. The cases
a=2
and
a=3
permit explicit descriptions per Theorems (
3.2
) and (
4.4
), respectively; the corresponding description in Theorem (
2.1
) for an arbitrary exponent
a
is less precise. We shall outline this structure in more detail after recording two results which are critical for our arguments. The first is due to Wiebe (and is a quick consequence of Theorem 1.1 in
[
2
]
):

Proposition 1.4

(
[
23
]
, Proposition 2.9)
.

Let
I\subseteq P_{n}
be an
\mathfrak{m}
-primary graded ideal where
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, and fix any term order
\sigma
. If
P_{n}/\text{in}_{\sigma}(I)
has WLP (resp. SLP), then
P_{n}/I
also enjoys WLP (resp. SLP).

Because an initial ideal is a monomial ideal, this result pairs well with our second vital tool due to Eddings and Vraciu:

Lemma 1.5

(
[
4
]
, Lemma 3.1)
.

Let
I\subseteq P_{n}
be a monomial ideal, and let
\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. Let
Q=\sum_{\mu}a_{\mu}\mu\in P_{n}
be a form of degree
d-1
, where the summation is over all monomials
\mu
of degree
d-1
. Assume
\ell Q\in I
. Then for every monomial
M
of degree
2d-1
, if
M\notin I
then
a_{\mu}=0
for every
\mu
of degree
d-1
such that
\mu
divides
M
.

The outline of Sections 2, 3, and 4 can now be provided. After fixing the notation, the first step will be a description of the initial ideal with respect to the reverse lexicographic order. Then we use the Lemma (
1.5
) to prove that injectivity holds for the initial ideal, whence the conclusion follows from Proposition (
1.4
). Section 5 is of an altogether different nature, constructing a nonzero element in the kernel of multiplication by a general linear form when the defining ideal is generated by squares.

We next record two more standard results that will be needed at various points. Their statements have been paraphrased from the source material to suit the context of this paper.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
18
]
, Theorem 1)
.

The Hilbert function of a complete intersection is symmetric about half the socle degree. Moreover, the Hilbert function is increasing until half the socle degree and is decreasing afterward.

Fact 1.7

(
[
5
]
, Proposition 15.12)
.

Consider
P_{n}=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
with reverse lexicographic order, and let
I
be a homogeneous ideal of
P_{n}
. Then the initial ideal enjoys the following two properties:
\mathrm{in}\big(I+(x_{n})\big)=\mathrm{in}(I)+(x_{n})
and
\big(\mathrm{in}(I):_{P_{n}}x_{n}\big)=\mathrm{in}(I:_{P_{n}}x_{n})
.

Remark 1.8
.

Shortly after the completion of this paper, we were made aware of
[
12
]
in which Kling, Lundqvist, Mohammadi, Orth, and Sáenz-de-Cabezón independently provided a description of the initial ideal of
(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n})^{2})
by computing the Gröbner basis for it. In particular, their methodology differs significantly from our proof of Theorem (
3.2
).

2.
A lower bound on the number of variables such that injectivity holds

The main result of this section is Theorem (
2.2
), in which
we find an explicit bound depending on
a
and
d
(which is linear in
d
for a fixed exponent
a
) such that the map given by multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d}
is injective whenever
n
is larger than the bound.

The key ingredient is the following description of the initial ideal of
I_{n,a}
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n,a,d
be positive integers with
d\geq a\geq 2
. Let

\displaystyle N(a,d):=\left\lceil\frac{2d}{a-1}\right\rceil.

For a fixed degree
d
and all
n\geq N(a,d)
, the minimal generators of degree
\leq d
of the initial ideal of

I_{n,a}
in reverse lexicographic order consist of
x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a}
, and monomials that only involve the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N(a,d)}
.

In the following proof, the notation
P_{n,d}
refers to the
d^{\text{th}}
graded piece of
P_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N(a,d)}]
denote the initial ideal of
I_{N(a,d),a}
and let
K_{n}
denote the ideal of
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
generated by the image of
K
under the natural inclusion for
n\geq N(a,d)
. The containment
K_{n}+(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a})\subseteq\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
is clear from the definition of
K_{n}
.

Since
I_{n,a}
and
\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
have the same Hilbert function, the conclusion will follow once we prove that

(3)

\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{(K_{n}+(x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a}))_{d}}\right)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{(I_{n,a})_{d}}\right)

For a fixed
a
, let
E(n,d)
denote the left-hand side of (
3
) and
F(n,d)
denote the right-hand side.

The two sides of equation (
3
) are the same for
n=N(a,d)
by definition. We claim that both satisfy the same recurrence relation as functions of
n
(for fixed
a
and
d
) for
n\geq N(a,d)
.

Since the generators of
K_{n}
only involve the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N(a,d)}
, we have

\frac{P_{n}}{K_{n}+(x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a})}=\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]}{K_{n}+(x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{a})}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{n}]}{(x_{n}^{a})}.

Using a standard property of the tensor product, for each
d\geq 0
we have

\left(\frac{P_{n}}{K_{n}+(x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a})}\right)_{d}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d}\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]}{K_{n}+(x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{a})}\right)_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{n}]}{(x_{n}^{a})}\right)_{d-i}.

Since the dimension of
\displaystyle{\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{n}]}{(x_{n}^{a})}}
as an
\mathbb{F}
-vector space is
1
in each degree less than
a
and
0
otherwise, we obtain the recurrence relation

E(n,d)=E(n-1,d)+E(n-1,d-1)+\cdots+E(n-1,d-a+1).

For the right-hand side of (
3
), an appeal to Remark (
1.3
) gives the following:

F(n,d)=\mathrm{max}\{P(n+1,d)-P(n+1,d-1),0\},

where

P(n+1,d)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]_{d}}{(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n+1}^{a})_{d}}\right).

The socle degree of the complete intersection
\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]}{(x_{1}^{a},\dots,x_{n+1}^{a})}
is
(a-1)(n+1)
, so by Theorem (
1.6
) we have

(4)

P(n+1,d)\geq P(n+1,d-1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\leq\frac{(a-1)(n+1)}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\geq\frac{2d}{a-1}-1.

We have the recurrence relation

P(n+1,d)=P(n,d)+P(n,d-1)+\cdots+P(n,d-a+1).

When (
4
) holds, it follows that

F(n,d)=P(n+1,d)-P(n+1,d-1)=\sum_{d^{\prime}=d-a+1}^{d}\big(P(n,d^{\prime})-P(n,d^{\prime}-1)\big).

On the other hand, when
\displaystyle n\geq\frac{2d}{a-1}
, we have

P(n,d^{\prime})-P(n,d^{\prime}-1)=F(n-1,d^{\prime})
for all
d^{\prime}\leq d
(from (
4
) applied to
n-1
in the role of
n
), and thus

F(n,d)=F(n-1,d)+F(n-1,d-1)+\cdots+F(n-1,d-a+1).

Since
E(n,d)
and
F(n,d)
satisfy the same recurrence relation and are equal for
n=N(a,d)
, it follows that they continue to be equal for all
n\geq N(a,d)
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
n,a,d
be positive integers with
d\geq a\geq 2
. If

\displaystyle n\geq N(a,d)+\frac{2d-1}{a-1}
, then the map given by multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n,a})_{d}
is injective.

Proof.

By Proposition (
1.4
), it suffices to prove injectivity when
I_{n,a}
is replaced by
\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
. Moreover, the injectivity from degree
d-1
to degree
d
is not affected by the generators of the ideal of higher degree. Therefore, we may replace
I_{n,a}
with the ideal generated by the degree
d
piece of
\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
.

Assume
Q=\sum a_{\mu}\mu
is in the kernel of the map given by multiplication by
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
from
(P_{n}/\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a}))_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a}))_{d}
, where the first summation is over all monomials
\mu
of degree
d-1
. We need to prove that
a_{\mu}=0
for all
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
.

Let
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
be a monomial of degree
d-1
. Let
\mu^{\prime}:=\mathrm{gcd}(\mu,x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a-1}\cdots x_{n}^{a-1})
, and define

M:=\mu\cdot\frac{x_{N(a,d)+1}^{a-1}\cdots x_{n}^{a-1}}{\mu^{\prime}}.

Multiplying by any variable
x_{j}
with
j>N_{a,d}
does not affect membership in the ideal generated by the degree
d
piece of
\mathrm{in}(I_{n,a})
; therefore,
M
is not in this ideal.
We have

\mathrm{deg}(M)\geq(a-1)(n-N(a,d))\geq 2d-1,

and now the result follows from Lemma (
1.5
).
∎

As a corollary, we see that Fröberg’s conjecture holds for
n+2
forms of degree
a
up to
D:=\lfloor\frac{n(a-1)+1}{4}\rfloor
:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
R=P_{n}/(x_{1}^{a},\dots,x_{n}^{a},\ell_{1}^{a},\ell_{2}^{a})
where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are general linear forms. We have

\big[\mathrm{HS}(R,t)\big]_{D}=\left[\frac{(1-t^{a})^{n+2}}{(1-t)^{n}}\right]_{D}.

where
D:=\lfloor\frac{n(a-1)+1}{4}\rfloor

Proof.

This follows immediately from the preceding theorem and Observation (
1.2
).
∎

3.
Injectivity for ideals generated by squares

Notation 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. We fix the following:

(1)

P_{n}
denotes the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
where
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic
0
. In addition, the notation
P_{n,d}
will refer to the
d^{\text{th}}
graded piece of
P_{n}
.

(2)

The quotient ring
R_{n}:=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}]/(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n+1}^{2})
and the linear form
\ell:=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}
. Note that
R_{n}
has WLP according to
[
21
]
.

(3)

The ideal
I_{n}:=\big(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}\big)
in the ring
P_{n}
. In addition,
I_{n,d}
will refer to the
d^{\mathrm{th}}
graded piece of
I_{n}
, and
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
is the initial ideal of
I_{n}
with respect to the reverse lexicographic order.

The main result of this section is Theorem (
3.7
), which shows that if
n\geq 3d-2
, the map given by multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d}
is injective.

The key ingredient is the following description of the initial ideal of
I_{n}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The initial ideal
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
with respect to the reverse lexicographic order is generated by
x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}
, and all square-free monomials of degree
d
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2d-2}
for
2d-2\leq n
.

Before giving the proof, we set up some notation. Let
D_{k}
be the set of square-free monomials of degree
2
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
, for
2k-2\leq n
, which are not divisible by any monomials of degree
k^{\prime}
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k^{\prime}-2}
for any
k^{\prime}<k
. For instance, the first few
D_{k}
are given explicitly by

D_{2}=\{x_{1}x_{2}\},\qquad D_{3}=\{x_{1}x_{3}x_{4},\,x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\},\quad\text{and}

D_{4}=\{x_{1}x_{3}x_{5}x_{6},\,x_{2}x_{3}x_{5}x_{6},\,x_{1}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6},\,x_{2}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6},\,x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6}\}.

After proving the theorem, we will obtain Corollary (
3.3
), which expresses the initial ideal in terms of the
D_{k}
. It will say, for example, that
\mathrm{in}(I_{6})
is minimally generated by
\{x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{6}^{2}\}\cup D_{2}\cup D_{3}\cup D_{4}
.

Proof.

For all degrees
d
, let
K_{n,d}
denote the vector space spanned by all the degree
d
monomials that are multiples of a monomial in
D_{k}
for some
k\leq d
.

We claim that

(5)

\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{K_{n,d}}\right)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{I_{n,d}}\right)

Remark (
1.3
) gives

(6)

\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{I_{n,d}}\right)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{R_{n}}{(\ell)}\right)_{d}\right)=\mathrm{max}\{\mathrm{dim}((R_{n})_{d})-\mathrm{dim}((R_{n})_{d-1}),0\}

=\mathrm{max}\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
d\\
\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n+1\\
d-1\\
\end{array}\right),0\right\}=\mathrm{max}\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d\\
\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d-2\\
\end{array}\right),0\right\}.

Note that this is equal to zero for
n\leq 2d-2
. On the other hand,
K_{n,d}
consists of all monomials of degree
d
in
P_{n}
for
n\leq 2d-2
, and therefore the left-hand side of equation (
5
) is also equal to zero for
n\leq 2d-2
. Moreover, all monomials of degree
d
in
P_{n}
must belong to
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
for
n\leq 2d-2
, so we have, in fact,
(\mathrm{in}(I_{n}))_{d}=K_{n,d}
.

It remains to prove that equation (
5
) holds for all
n\geq 2d-2
, and we proceed by induction on
d
.

For
d=2
:

\mathrm{dim}(P_{n,2}/K_{n,2})
is the number of square-free monomials, excluding
x_{1}x_{2}
(since
K_{n,2}
is the span of
x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},x_{1}x_{2}
), i.e.
\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)-1
; equation (
6
) gives the same result for
\mathrm{dim}(P_{n,2}/I_{n,2})
.

Assume
d\geq 3
. Let
E(n,d):=\mathrm{dim}(P_{n,d}/K_{n,d})
and
F(n,d):=\mathrm{dim}(P_{n,d}/I_{n,d})
. We need to prove that
E(n,d)=F(n,d)
for all
n\geq 2d-2
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have
E(n,d-1)=F(n,d-1)
for all
n\geq 2d-4
. Fixing
d
, we now induct
n
.

For
n=2d-2
, we have
E(2d-2,d)=F(2d-2,d)=0
. Let
n\geq 2d-1
.
We claim that

(7)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}E(n,d)&=&E(n-1,d-1)+E(n-1,d)\ \ \ \mathrm{and}\\
F(n,d)&=&F(n-1,d-1)+F(n-1,d).\end{array}

For the first equality in claim (
7
), note that
E(n,d)
is the number of monomials of degree
d
in
n
variables that are not divisible by any monomial of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
, for any
k\leq d
. We partition this set into monomials that are divisible by
x_{n}
and monomials that are not divisible by
x_{n}
. Those divisible by
x_{n}
can be written as
x_{n}m
, with
m
a monomial of degree
d-1
in
n-1
variables, not divisible by any monomial of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
for any
k\leq d
. The number of such monomials is
E(n-1,d-1)
. The number of monomials that are not divisible by
x_{n}
is
E(n-1,d)
, since we can view these as monomials in
n-1
variables.

The second equality in claim (
7
) follows from (
6
) and the recurrence relation for binomial coefficients.

The inductive step now follows from (
7
), which concludes the proof of equation (
5
).

For
n>2d-2
, let
\overline{I_{n}}
and
\overline{\mathrm{in}(I_{n})}
denote the images of
I_{n}
and
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
in
P_{n}/(x_{2d-1},\ldots,x_{n})=P_{2d-2}
. Note that
\overline{I_{n}}=I_{2d-2}
. By Fact (
1.7
), we have
\mathrm{in}(\overline{I_{n}})=\overline{\mathrm{in}(I_{n})}
.
Recalling that we have already established
K_{2d-2,d}=(\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-2}))_{d}
, it now follows that
K_{n,d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(I_{n}))_{d}
. Coupled with (
5
), we now conclude that
K_{n,d}=(\mathrm{in}(I_{n}))_{d}
.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we have the following description of a minimal set of generators for
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
:

Corollary 3.3
.

A minimal set of generators for
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
is
\displaystyle\{x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}\}\cup\bigcup_{2k-2\leq n}D_{k}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem (
3.2
).
∎

Before proving the main result of this section, we will need two lemmas. They allow us to construct a multiple of a given monomial that avoids membership in the initial ideal, positioning us to use Lemma (
1.5
). To illustrate their utility and the main idea of their proofs, we provide a concrete example.

Example 3.4
.

Fix
d=7
and
n=2d-2=12
. Given a monomial
\mu\in P_{12}
of degree
d-2=5
which is not in
\mathrm{in}(I_{12})
, our goal is to build a monomial
\widetilde{\mu}
of degree
d-1=6
which is a multiple of
\mu
and is not in
\mathrm{in}(I_{12})
.

We choose
\mu=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{12}
first. Note that
\deg{(\mu)}=5=d-2
, and moreover
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{12})
by Theorem (
3.2
). In this case, define
\widetilde{\mu}=x_{11}\mu
. This satisfies
\deg{(\widetilde{\mu})}=\deg{(\mu)}+1
, and Theorem (
3.2
) ensures
\widetilde{\mu}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{12})
. More generally, this construction will work, provided that
\mu
is not divisible by
x_{11}x_{12}
.

Now, let us take
\mu=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{11}x_{12}
. In this case, define
\mu^{\prime}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
so that
\mu=x_{11}x_{12}\mu^{\prime}
. Observe that because it is square-free,
\mu^{\prime}
may be viewed as an element in
P_{10}
. It follows from Theorem (
3.2
) that
\big(\mathrm{in}(I_{12})\big)_{t}=\big(\mathrm{in}(I_{10})\big)_{t}
whenever
t\leq d-1=6
. Since
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{12})
and
\deg{(\mu^{\prime})}=3<6
, we deduce that
\mu^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{10})
. With this, we define
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}=x_{10}\mu^{\prime}
to reach
\deg{(\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}})}=\deg{(\mu^{\prime})}+1
and
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{10})
. Finally, define
\widetilde{\mu}=x_{11}x_{12}\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}
to obtain a monomial with the desired properties. More generally, this construction works when
x_{11}x_{12}
divides
\mu
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
n=2d-2
. If
\mu\in P_{n}
is a monomial with
\mathrm{deg}(\mu):=\delta\leq d-2
and
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
, then there exists
\widetilde{\mu}\in P_{n}
a multiple of
\mu
with
\mathrm{deg}(\widetilde{\mu})=\mathrm{deg}(\mu)+1
and
\widetilde{\mu}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
.

Proof.

We induct on
d
. The base case is
d=2
, in which case
\mathrm{deg}(\mu)=0
and the statement is trivial.

Let
d>2
. The assumption that
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
means that
\mu
is square-free and is not divisible by any monomial of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
for any
k\leq\delta
.
If there exists
j\in\{2d-3,2d-2\}
such that
\mu
is not divisible by
x_{j}
, let
\widetilde{\mu}:=\mu x_{j}
. Observe that
\widetilde{\mu}
is not divisible by any monomial of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
for any
k\leq\delta+1
. Indeed, since
\delta+1\leq d-1
and
2(\delta+1)-2\leq 2d-4
, multiplication by
x_{2d-3},x_{2d-2}
does not affect this property, and therefore
\widetilde{\mu}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
.

Otherwise, say
\mu=x_{2d-2}x_{2d-3}\mu^{\prime}
with
\mathrm{deg}(\mu^{\prime})=\delta-2
. Since
\mu
is square-free,
\mu^{\prime}
does not involve the variables
x_{2d-2},x_{2d-3}
, and therefore we can view
\mu^{\prime}
as a monomial in
P_{2d-4}
. Theorem (
3.2
) implies that the square-free monomial generators of
\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-2})
that have degree
\leq d-1
are the same as the square-free monomial generators of
\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-4})
of degree
\leq d-1
, so the assumption
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-2})
implies
\mu^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-4})
. Thus, we may apply the inductive hypothesis to
\mu^{\prime}
in the role of
\mu
, whence there is a monomial
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\in P_{2d-4}
that is a multiple of
\mu^{\prime}
of degree
\mathrm{deg}(\mu^{\prime})+1=\delta-1
with
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{2d-4})
. We can now define
\widetilde{\mu}:=x_{2d-2}x_{2d-3}\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\in P_{2d-2}
, which satisfies the required properties.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
n=2d-2
. If
\mu\in P_{n}
is a monomial with
\mathrm{deg}(\mu)\leq d-2
and
\mu\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
, then there exists
\widetilde{\mu}\in P_{n}
a multiple of
\mu
with
\mathrm{deg}(\widetilde{\mu})=d-1
and
\widetilde{\mu}\notin\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
.

Proof.

This follows by a repeated application of Lemma (
3.5
).
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
d
be a positive integer. If
3d-2\leq n
, then multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d}
is injective.

Proof.

By Proposition (
1.4
), it suffices to prove injectivity when
I_{n}
is replaced by
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
. In other words, we need to prove that
\times L:(P_{n}/\mathrm{in}(I_{n}))_{d-1}\to(P_{n}/\mathrm{in}(I_{n}))_{d}
is injective, where
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. Since this map is not affected by the higher degree generators of
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
, we may replace
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
by the ideal generated by the elements of
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
of degree
\leq d
. Denote this ideal by
K_{n,d}
. Assume there is
Q=\sum_{\mu}a_{\mu}\mu\in P_{d-1}
such that the image of
Q
in
P/K_{n,d}
is in the kernel of the multiplication by
L
map. We need to prove that
a_{\mu}=0
for every monomial
\mu
of degree
d-1
such that
\mu\notin K_{n,d}
.
This will follow from Lemma (
1.5
) provided that we can construct a monomial
M\in P_{n}
such that
M
is a multiple of
\mu
,
\mathrm{deg}(M)\geq 2d-1
, and
M\notin K_{n,d}
.

If all of
x_{2d-1},\ldots,x_{3d-2}
do not divide
\mu
, we can take

M:=\mu x_{2d-1}\cdots x_{3d-2}
.
M
is a square-free monomial of degree
2d-1
, and is not divisible by any monomials of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2k-2}
for any
k\leq d
, and therefore
M\notin K_{n,d}
.

Otherwise, say that
\mu=x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{s}}\mu^{\prime}
where
j_{1},\ldots,j_{s}\in\{2d-1,\ldots,,3d-2\}
and
\mu^{\prime}
only contains variables up to
x_{2d-2}
. Applying Lemma (
3.6
) to
\mu^{\prime}
in the role of
\mu
gives us a monomial
\widetilde{\mu}
of degree
d-1
in the variables up to
x_{2d-2}
which is a multiple of
\mu^{\prime}
and is not in
K_{n,d}
. We can now take
M:=\widetilde{\mu}x_{2d-1}\cdots x_{3d-2}
.
∎

It was pointed out to us by the referee that Theorem (

) can be derived as a corollary of the results in
[
3
]
. More precisely, Theorem 8 in
[
3
]
shows that

(8)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n}}{(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2})}\right)_{d}\right)\leq a_{n,d}

where

is a quantity defined as the number of lattice paths of a certain type. Moreover, Proposition 7 in
[

shows that for
\displaystyle d\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor+1

\end{array}\right)-2\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\

\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\

\end{array}\right),

which is the coefficient of

in

\displaystyle\frac{(1-t^{2})^{n+2}}{(1-t)^{n}}
, the dimension predicted by the Fröberg conjecture for the quotient of the polynomial ring in

\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2}))
is obtained as the quotient of

in the role of

and using Observation (
1.2
). In Section 5, we will prove that injectivity fails in degree

, and we will discuss how the failure of injectivity relates to Conjecture 2 from
[

, which asserts that

(9)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}\big((P_{n}/(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2}))_{d}\big)=a_{n,d}

holds for all

.

The following corollary shows that Fröberg’s Conjecture holds for
n+2
quadratic linear forms up to degree

.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
R=P_{n}/(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2})
where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are general linear forms. We have

\big[\mathrm{HS}(R,t)\big]_{D}=\left[\frac{(1-t^{2})^{n+2}}{(1-t)^{n}}\right]_{D}.

where
D:=\lfloor\frac{n+2}{3}\rfloor

Proof.

This follows immediately from the preceding theorem and Observation (
1.2
).

4.
Injectivity for ideals generated by cubes

Notation 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. We fix the following:

(1)

P_{n}
denotes the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
where
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic
0
. In addition, the notation
P_{n,d}
will refer to the
d^{\text{th}}
graded piece of
P_{n}
.

(2)

The ideal
J_{n}:=\big(x_{1}^{3},\dots,x_{n}^{3},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{3}\big)
in the ring
P_{n}
.

Further,
\mathrm{in}(J_{n})
is the initial ideal of
J_{n}
with respect to the reverse lexicographic order.

(3)

For
k\geq 3
,
D_{k}
is the set of monomials of degree
k
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
which are not divisible by
x_{k-1}^{2}
. (If
n<k-1
, then
D_{k}
contains all monomials of degree
k
in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
). For instance, the first few
D_{k}
are given by

D_{3}=\{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}\},\quad D_{4}=\{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2},\,x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{3},\,x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}\},\quad\text{and}

D_{5}=\{x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3},\,x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{4},\,x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{3}^{2},\,x_{1}^{2}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4},\,x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2},\,x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}x_{4},\,x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}^{2}x_{4},\,x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}x_{4},x_{1}^{2}x_{3}^{2}x_{4}\}.

(4)

The ideal
K_{n}\subseteq P_{n}
generated by
x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{n}^{3}
, and
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=3}^{n+1}D_{k}
.

(5)

We define
G(n,d):=\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n}}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{n}^{3})}\right)_{d}\right)
.

We make two preliminary observations regarding the notation above.

Observation 4.2
.

For
n\leq d-2
, we have
(K_{n})_{d}=(P_{n})_{d}
.

Proof.

Let
m\in P_{n}
be a monomial of degree
d
. If
x_{n}^{2}
does not divide
m
, then
m
lies in
D_{n+1}
by definition and we are done. If instead
x_{n}^{2}
divides
m
, then we can write
m=x_{n}^{2}m^{\prime}
where the degree of
m^{\prime}
is
d-2
. If
x_{n}
divides
m^{\prime}
, then
x_{n}^{3}
divides
m
and so
m
lies in
K_{n}
. If not, then write
m=x_{n}(x_{n}m^{\prime})
. The monomial
x_{n}m^{\prime}
is not divisible by
x_{n}^{2}
, so
x_{n}m^{\prime}
lies in
D_{n}
. This implies that
m
belongs to
(K_{n})_{d}
.
∎

Observation 4.3
.

The function
G(n,d)
satisfies the following two relations:

(a)

G(n,d)=G(n-1,d)+G(n-1,d-1)+G(n-1,d-2)
;

(b)

G(d,d)-G(d,d-1)=G(d-1,d-2)-G(d-1,d-3)
.

Proof.

For (a), note that
G(n,d)
is the number of monomials
x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{n}i_{j}=d
and
0\leq i_{j}\leq 2
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The set of such monomials can be partitioned into monomials with
i_{n}=0,i_{n}=1
, and
i_{n}=2
, which correspond to
G(n-1,d),G(n-1,d-1)
, and
G(n-1,d-2)
, respectively.

Now, (b) follows from (a) and the fact that
G(d-1,d)=G(d-1,d-2)
. The latter fact is because when
n
is
d-1
, the socle degree of
\displaystyle\frac{P_{d-1}}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-1}^{3})}
is equal to
2d-2
, and the Hilbert function is symmetric about half of the socle degree by Theorem (
1.6
).
∎

The main result of this section is Theorem (
4.10
), which shows that if
\displaystyle n\geq\frac{3d-3}{2}
, the map given by multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/J_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/J_{n})_{d}
is injective.

The key ingredient is the following description of the initial ideal of
J_{n}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Using the reverse lexicographic order, we have
\mathrm{in}(J_{n})=K_{n}
.

Proof.

For each degree
d\geq 3
, we want to prove that
(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}=(K_{n})_{d}
. The statement is clear for
d=3
.

For all
n,d
, we will prove

(i)

(10)

\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{(K_{n})_{d}}\right)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{(J_{n})_{d}}\right)

(ii)

(K_{n})_{d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}
.

We prove both parts by induction on
d
. The statement is clear for
d=3
.

We calculate the right-hand side of (
10
). Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]}{J_{n}}
is isomorphic to the cokernel of the map
\displaystyle\times L:\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{n+1}^{3})}(-1)\to\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{n+1}^{3})}
, where
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}
. Since the WLP holds for the complete intersection, Remark (
1.3
) yields

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{P_{n,d}}{(J_{n})_{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{max}\{G(n+1,d)-G(n+1,d-1),0\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}G(n+1,d)-G(n+1,d-1)&\mathrm{if}\ d\leq n+1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where the last equality follows from Theorem (
1.6
).

For
n\leq d-2
, (
11
) shows that
(J_{n})_{d}
consists of all homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
P_{n}
; the same holds for
(K_{n})_{d}
by Observation (
4.2
), and therefore we have
(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}=(J_{n})_{d}=(K_{n})_{d}
, as desired. For the rest of the proof, we will assume
n\geq d-1
.

For
n=d-1
, the left-hand side of (
10
) is equal to the number of monomials of the form
x_{d-1}^{2}m
, where
m
is a monomial in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}
with
m\notin K_{d-2}
and
\mathrm{deg}(m)=d-2
; therefore it is equal to
\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}]_{d-2}}{(K_{d-2})_{d-2}}\right)
. By the inductive hypothesis and (
11
), this is equal to

\mathrm{dim}\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}]_{d-2}}{(J_{d-2})_{d-2}}\right)=G(d-1,d-2)-G(d-1,d-3).

The right-hand side of (
10
) is equal to
G(d,d)-G(d,d-1)
by (
11
). The equality asserted in (
10
) now follows from Observation (
4.3
) part (b).

For
n\geq d
, let
E(n,d)
and
F(n,d)
denote the left-hand side and right-hand side respectively of equation (
10
). Using the recursive equation from Observation (
4.3
) part (a), we obtain

\displaystyle F(n,d)

\displaystyle=(G(n,d)-G(n,d-1))+(G(n,d-1)-G(n,d-2))+(G(n,d-2)-G(n,d-3))

\displaystyle=F(n-1,d)+F(n-1,d-1)+F(n-1,d-2),

where the last equality uses equation (
11
) and the assumption
n\geq d
.

We claim that the same recurrence is satisfied by
E(n,d)
. Indeed,
E(n,d)
is the number of monomials of degree
d
of the form
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\{0,1,2\}
that are not divisible by any monomial in
D_{k}
for any
k\leq d
. These monomials can be partitioned according to the value of
a_{n}\in\{0,1,2\}
. The number of such monomials with
a_{n}=0
is
E(n-1,d)
, the number of such monomials with
a_{n}=1
is
E(n-1,d-1)
, and the number of such monomials with
a_{n}=2
is
E(n-1,d-2)
. (Note that the assumption
n\geq d
ensures that the set
D_{k}
in
P_{n}
is the same as the set
D_{k}
in
P_{n-1}
for all
k\leq d
.)

The conclusion now follows by induction on
n
. This ends the proof of (i).

Proof of (ii): We observe that it is sufficient to prove that the inclusion
(K_{n})_{d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}
holds for
n=d-1
. Recall that we already proved equality holds for
n\leq d-2
. Assuming that the inclusion holds for
n=d-1
, consider
n\geq d
and induct on
n
. Let
\overline{\ \ \ }
denote modding out by
x_{n}
. We have
\overline{(K_{n})_{d}}=(K_{n-1})_{d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{n-1}))_{d}=\overline{(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}}
. The first equality is clear from the definition of
K_{n}
, the middle inclusion is the inductive hypothesis, and the second equality is from properties of the initial ideal with respect to the reverse lexicographic order. Since the generators of
K_{n}
in degrees
\leq d
only involve the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}
, it follows that
(K_{n})_{d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{n}))_{d}
.

Now let
n=d-1
. We wish to prove the inclusion
(K_{d-1})_{d}\subseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}))_{d}
, but in light of (i) this is equivalent to proving the opposite inclusion
(K_{d-1})_{d}\supseteq(\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}))_{d}
. Aiming to prove the latter, we will induct on
d
with the statement being obvious for
d=3
.

Note that
(K_{d-1})_{d}
consists of all monomials of degree
d
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}
, as well as all monomials of the form
x_{d-1}m
where
m
is a monomial of degree
d-1
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}
(all of these belong to
D_{d}
); additionally, a monomial of the form
x_{d-1}^{2}m
(with
m
a monomial of degree
d-2
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2}
) is in
K_{d-1}
if and only if
m\in K_{d-2}
.

Therefore, the inclusion
(\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}))_{d}\subseteq(K_{d-1})_{d}
is equivalent to the condition that the only degree
d
multiples of
x_{d-1}^{2}
that are in
\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1})
are the multiples of
x_{d-1}^{3}
and those obtained as
x_{d-1}^{2}m
with
m\in(K_{d-2})_{d-2}
. In other words, we want to prove that

((x_{d-1}^{2})\cap\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}))_{d}=(x_{d-1}^{2}K_{d-2}+(x_{d-1})^{3})_{d}

or, equivalently,

(12)

(\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}):x_{d-1}^{2})_{d-2}=(K_{d-2}+(x_{d-1}))_{d-2}.

In order to calculate the left-hand side of (
12
), note that

\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}):x_{d-1}^{2}=\mathrm{in}(J_{d-1}:x_{d-1}^{2})
by Fact (
1.7
).
We have

(13)

J_{d-1}:x_{d-1}^{2}=(x_{d-1})+(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1})^{3}):(x_{d-1}^{2})

Using the change of variables
x_{d-1}^{\prime}:=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1}
, note that
(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x^{\prime}_{d-1})^{3})
is a complete intersection ideal and therefore
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2},x_{d-1}^{\prime}]/(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,(x^{\prime}_{d-1})^{3})
has the Strong Lefschetz Property.
Thus, the map given by multiplication by
x_{d-1}^{2}=(x^{\prime}_{d-1}-x_{1}-\cdots-x_{d-2})^{2}

(14)

{\times}\,x_{d-1}^{2}:\left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2},x_{d-1}^{\prime}]}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,(x^{\prime}_{d-1})^{3})}\right)_{d-2}\ \longrightarrow\ \left(\frac{\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-2},x_{d-1}^{\prime}]}{(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-1}^{\prime 3})}\right)_{d}

has maximal rank. The socle degree of the complete intersection is
2d-2
. Since the Hilbert function of the complete intersection is symmetric about
d-1
(half of the socle degree) by Theorem (
1.6
), it follows that the domain and target of the map in (
14
) have the same dimension; therefore, the map is bijective and so, in particular, injective. This implies

((x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x^{\prime}_{d-1})^{3}):x_{d-1}^{2})_{d-2}=(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x^{\prime}_{d-1})^{3})_{d-2}.

Equation (
13
) now implies

(J_{d-1}:(x_{d-1}^{2}))_{d-2}=((x_{d-1})+(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1})^{3}))_{d-2}.

Hence, the left-hand side of (
12
) is equal to the degree
d-2
piece of the initial ideal of
(x_{d-1})+(x_{1}^{3},\ldots,x_{d-2}^{3},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1})^{3})
, which is equal to
((x_{d-1})+\mathrm{in}(J_{d-2}))_{d-2}
. By the inductive hypothesis, this is equal to
((x_{d-1})+K_{d-2})_{d-2}
as desired.
∎

Before proving the main result of this section, we need a few preliminary results to set the stage for an appeal to Lemma (
1.5
).

The following observation is an immediate consequence of the definition of
K_{n}
.

Observation 4.5
.

Let
\mu\in P_{n}
be a monomial of degree
d
. Assume
\mu\notin K_{n}
.

a. If
j\geq d+1
and

does not divide

.

b. If
\mu
is divisible by

In the next two results, we will use the following terminology: we say that a monomial

if

. The lemmas below are roughly analogous to Lemmas (
3.5
) and (

), when the defining ideal was generated by squares.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mu\in P_{d}
be a monomial in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
.

a. If
\mu\notin K_{d}
and
\mathrm{deg}(\mu)=\delta\leq d-1
then there exists a monomial
\widetilde{\mu}\in P_{d}
with the following three properties:

(i)
\widetilde{\mu}
is a multiple of
\mu
of degree
d
,
(ii)
\widetilde{\mu}\notin K_{d}
, and (iii)
\widetilde{\mu}
ends in
x_{d}
or
x_{d}^{2}
.

b. If
\mu\notin K_{d}
,
\mathrm{deg}(\mu)=d
, and
\mu
does not end in
x_{d-1}^{2}
, then there exists
\widetilde{\mu}\in P_{d}
such that
\widetilde{\mu}
is a multiple of
\mu
of degree
d+1
and
\widetilde{\mu}\notin K_{d}
.

Proof.

a. We prove this by using induction on
d
. The statement is obvious for
d=1
. Let
d\geq 2
. Note that it suffices to construct a monomial
\widetilde{\mu}
of degree
\delta+1
, and then iterate the construction.

If
\mu
is not divisible by
x_{d}^{2}
, let
\widetilde{\mu}:=\mu x_{d}
. We have
\widetilde{\mu}\notin K_{d}
by Observation (
4.5
) (a). Otherwise, say
\mu=x_{d}^{2}\mu^{\prime}
, where
\mu^{\prime}\in P_{d-1}
,
\mathrm{deg}(\mu^{\prime})=\delta-2<d-2
.
\mu\notin K_{d}
implies
\mu^{\prime}\notin K_{d-1}
. By the inductive hypothesis, there is a
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\in P_{d-1}
a multiple of
\mu^{\prime}
of degree

with
\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}\notin K_{d-1}
. Then
\widetilde{\mu}=\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}x_{d}^{2}

b. The assumption that

means that

must divide
\mu
(otherwise,

would be a monomial of degree

in the variables

).

If
\mu
is not divisible by
x_{d}^{2}
, let
\widetilde{\mu}:=\mu x_{d}
. We have
\widetilde{\mu}\notin K_{d}
by Observation (

) (b).
Otherwise, say
\mu=\mu^{\prime}x_{d}^{2}

. From part (a), there exists

\widetilde{\mu}:=\widetilde{\mu^{\prime}}x_{d}^{2}\notin K_{d}

∎

In the next result, we use the following notation:

Remark 4.9
.

The assumption that
\mu
does not end in
x_{d-2}^{2}
is crucial for Lemmas (
4.6
) and (
4.8
). Indeed, let
\mu\in P_{n}
have
\deg{(\mu)}=d-1
,
\mu
ends in
x_{d-2}^{2}
, and
\mu\notin K_{n}
. We claim that if a monomial
M
is a multiple of
\mu
with degree
2n-d+2
, then
M\in K_{n,d}
. As a multiple of
\mu
, we may write

M=\mu\cdot x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\quad\text{where}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=2n-2d+3.

If
\alpha_{j}\geq 3
for any
j
, then
M
is a multiple of a cube and therefore
M\in K_{n,d}
provided
d\geq 3
. Now suppose that
\alpha_{j}\in\{0,1,2\}
for all
j
. There are two cases:

(1)

If
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
, then consider
\widetilde{\mu}=\mu\cdot x_{j}
. As
\mu
ends in
x_{d-2}^{2}
, it follows that
\widetilde{\mu}
is not divisble by
x_{d-1}^{2}
, so
\widetilde{\mu}\in K_{n,d}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\mu}
is a divisor of
M
and so
M\in K_{n,d}
in this case.

(2)

If
\alpha_{j}=0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
, then
M
has the form
M=\mu\cdot x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
. The maximum possible degree of
M
is given by choosing
\alpha_{j}=2
for all
d\leq j\leq n
. Thus,

\deg{(M)}\leq(d-1)+2(n-d+1)=2n-d+1.

This contradicts the assumption that
\deg{(M)}=2n-d+2
.

Because of this, the case when
\mu
ends in
x_{d-2}^{2}
will need to be treated separately in the proof of our theorem below.

Theorem 4.10
.

If
n\geq\frac{3d-3}{2}
, then the multiplication by a general linear form from
(P_{n}/J_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/J_{n})_{d}
is injective.

Proof.

By Proposition (
1.4
), it suffices to prove injectivity when
J_{n}
is replaced by
\mathrm{in}(J_{n})=K_{n}
.
Also note that the injectivity of the map in question is not affected by the generators of
K_{n}
of degree
>d
, so we may replace
K_{n}
by
K_{n,d}

Assume
Q=\sum a_{\mu}\mu
is in the kernel of the map given by multiplication by
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
from
(P_{n}/K_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/K_{n})_{d}
, where the summation is over all monomials
\mu
of degree
d-1
. We need to prove that
a_{\mu}=0
for all
\mu\notin K_{n}
.

For each monomial
\mu
of degree
d-1
that does not end in
x_{d-2}^{2}
, Lemma (
4.8
) shows that we can construct a monomial
M
of degree
2d-1
which is a multiple of
\mu
and is not in
K_{n,d}
, and Lemma (
1.5
) implies
a_{\mu}=0
.

Consider
\mu
of degree
d-1
which ends in
x_{d-2}^{2}
. Then
\mu x_{d}\notin K_{n}
. The assumption
LQ=0
implies that the coefficient of
\mu x_{d}
in
LQ
must be zero, and therefore

\sum_{\nu}a_{\nu}=0
, where the summation is over all divisors
\nu
of
\mu x_{d}
of degree
d-1
. One of these divisors is
\mu
; the others are
\mu^{\prime}x_{d}
where
\mu^{\prime}
is a divisor of
\mu
of degree

. Lemma (
4.8
) implies that
a_{\mu^{\prime}x_{d}}=0
for all such

∎

The following shows that the Fröberg conjecture holds for

linear forms of degree 3 up to

Corollary 4.11
.

Let

where
\ell_{1}
and

are general linear forms. We have

\big[\mathrm{HS}(R,t)\big]_{D}=\left[\frac{(1-t^{3})^{n+2}}{(1-t)^{n}}\right]_{D}.

This follows at once from the preceding theorem and Observation (

∎

Remark 4.12
.

Although we do not prove it in general, experimental evidence from

5.
Failure of injectivity for ideals generated by squares

Recall the ideal
I_{n}:=\big(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n}^{2},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}\big)
in the ring
P_{n}
. Further,
\mathrm{in}(I_{n})
is the initial ideal of
I_{n}
with respect to the reverse lexicographic order. The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
d>2
and
n<3d-2
. For
\ell
a general linear form, the multiplication by
\ell
map from
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d}
fails to be injective.

Note that when
n=3d-3
and
d\geq 3
, we have
\dim(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d}\geq\dim(P_{n}/I_{n})_{d-1}
. Hence, the failure of injectivity for the multiplication by a general linear form is indeed equivalent to the failure of WLP at degree
d
.

Remark 5.2
.

The first case where Theorem (
5.1
) applies is
d=3,n=6
. For this case, the failure of injectivity also follows from a result in
[
8
]
, where it is shown that an ideal generated by the squares of 7 generic forms of degree 2 in 5 variables fails to have the Hilbert function predicted by Fröberg (equation (
1
)) in degree 3.

The proof of Theorem (
5.1
) is constructive; an explicit nonzero element in the kernel of multiplication by
\ell
will be provided. In preparation for this, we define two recursive functions. For
0\leq t\leq d-1
, define
\varepsilon(t)
as follows:
\varepsilon(0)=1
, and for
t\geq 1
,
\displaystyle\varepsilon(t):=-\frac{t\varepsilon(t-1)}{d-t}
. For any two subsets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
of
\{1\,\ldots,n\}
, let
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathcal{J}}:=\varepsilon(|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}|)
. Further, we define
\psi(t)
by
\psi(0)=1,\psi(1)=-2/(d-2)
, and for
2\leq t\leq d-2
,
\psi(t)
satisfies

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d-t\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)\psi(t)+t(d-t)\psi(t-1)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}t\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)\psi(t-2)=0.

For any two subsets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define the notation
\psi_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathcal{J}}:=\psi(|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}|)
.

These two functions have the following properties that will prove useful later.

Observation 5.3
.

Let
d>2
and
n<3d-2
. Then the functions
\varepsilon(t)
and
\psi(t)
satisfy the following:

(a)

if
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=d
and
|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+2
, we have

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i\}}^{\mathcal{J}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}d&\mathrm{if}\ \mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}=\emptyset,\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(b)

if
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{L}
are subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=d
and
|\mathcal{L}|=d-2
, we have

\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathcal{I}}\psi_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{u,v\}}^{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)&\mathrm{if}\ \mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{L}=\emptyset,\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(a) Note that we have
n-2d+2\leq d-1
, so
|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}|
is also bounded above by
d-1
. In the case when
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}=\emptyset
, each of the
d
terms in the summation is equal to
\varepsilon(0)=1
, while in the case when
|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}|=t>0
, there are
t
terms that are equal to
\varepsilon(t-1)
(when

), and
d-t

\varepsilon(t)
(when
i\notin\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}
). Therefore, the summation is equal to
t\varepsilon(t-1)+(d-t)\varepsilon(t)=0
.

(b) If
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{L}=\emptyset
, then each term in the summation is equal to
\psi(0)=1
. If
|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{L}|=1
, then
d-1
of the terms in the summation are equal to
\psi(0)
(when
u
or
v
is in the intersection) and the remaining
\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d-1\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)
terms are equal to
\psi(1)
, whence the sum is equal to
(d-1)\psi(0)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d-1\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)\psi(1)=0
. Lastly, if
|\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{L}|=t\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}t\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)
of the terms in the summation are equal to
\psi(t-2)
(when both
u
and
v
are in the intersection),
t(d-t)
of the terms are equal to
\psi(t-1)
(when exactly one of
u
or
v
is in the intersection), and the remaining
\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d-t\\
2\\
\end{array}\right)
terms are equal to
\psi(t)
(when neither
u
nor
v
are in the intersection); therefore, the summation is equal to 0 by the definition of
\psi(t)
.
∎

We proceed to the proof of Theorem (
5.1
).

Proof of (
5.1
).

For any subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we let
a_{\mathcal{I}}:=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}
and
x_{\mathcal{I}}:=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}x_{i}
. Define a form of degree
d-1
as follows:

Q:=\sum_{|\mathcal{I}|=d-1}a_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\sum_{|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+2}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathcal{J}}a_{\mathcal{J}}\right)x_{\mathcal{I}}

where the summations are over all subsets
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with the indicated cardinalities.

We verify that
LQ\in J_{n}
.

For each
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=d
, the coefficient of
x_{\mathcal{I}}
in
LQ
is

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{i}b_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\{i\}},

where

b_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}=a_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}\cdot\sum_{|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+1}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}^{\mathcal{J}}a_{\mathcal{J}}

is the coefficient of
x_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}
in
Q
.
Since
a_{i}a_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}=a_{\mathcal{I}}
, we obtain

(15)

a_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+1}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}^{\mathcal{J}}a_{\mathcal{J}}\right)=a_{\mathcal{I}}\sum_{|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+2}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{i\}}^{\mathcal{J}}\right)a_{\mathcal{J}}=d\cdot a_{\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{I}=\emptyset\\
|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+2\end{subarray}}a_{\mathcal{J}}

where the second equality follows from part (a) of Observation (
5.3
).

We will now define a polynomial
Q^{\prime}
of degree
d-2
such that

(16)

LQ\equiv Q^{\prime}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}\ \ \ \ \mathrm{mod}\ (x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}).

This will prove
LQ\in J_{n}
.

We define
Q^{\prime}
as follows:

Q^{\prime}=\sum_{|\mathcal{K}|=d-2}\left(\sum_{|\mathcal{L}|=d-2}\psi_{\mathcal{K}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}\right)x_{\mathcal{K}}

where the summations are over all subsets
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with the indicated cardinality.
Let
\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{K}}:=\sum_{|\mathcal{L}|=d-2}\psi_{\mathcal{K}}^{\mathcal{L}}\,\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}
denote the coefficient of
x_{\mathcal{K}}
in
Q^{\prime}
.

In order to verify equation (
16
), we calculate the coefficient of
x_{\mathcal{I}}
in
Q^{\prime}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}
for every subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
|\mathcal{I}|=d
, and we confirm that it is equal to the corresponding coefficient of
LQ
(calculated in (
15
)).
The coefficient in question is equal to

\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathcal{I}}c_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\{u,v\}}=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathcal{I}}\,\sum_{|\mathcal{L}|=d-2}\psi_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{u,v\}}^{\mathcal{L}}\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}=\sum_{|\mathcal{L}|=d-2}\left(\sum_{\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathcal{I}}\psi_{\mathcal{I}\,\backslash\,\{u,v\}}^{\mathcal{L}}\right)\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}.

By part (b) of Observation (
5.3
), the coefficient of
x_{\mathcal{I}}
in
Q^{\prime}(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}
is equal to

(17)

\binom{d}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\cap I=\emptyset\\
|\mathcal{L}|=d-2\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}.

For each term in (
17
),
\displaystyle\frac{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}{a_{\mathcal{L}}}=a_{\mathcal{I}}a_{\mathcal{J}}
where
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
is disjoint from
\mathcal{I}
and satisfies
|\mathcal{J}|=n-2d+2
, whence the right-hand side of (
17
) is the same as the right-hand side of (
15
).

It remains to be shown that
Q\notin I_{n}
for general choices of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
.

Assume
Q\in I_{n}
. Let
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
denote the coefficient of
x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d-1}}
for each
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d-1}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The assumption that
Q\in I_{n}
implies that the coefficients
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
satisfy certain linear equations.

Indeed, if
\displaystyle C=\sum_{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{d-3}}c_{j_{1}\cdots j_{d-3}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d-3}}
is such that

Q=C(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}
(mod
(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2})
), then

(18)

b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}=\sum_{u<v}c_{i_{1}\cdots\hat{i_{u}}\cdots\hat{i_{v}}\cdots i_{d-1}}

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the matrix with rows indexed by subsets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d-1}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, columns indexed by subsets
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-3}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and entry equal to 1 if the column index is a subset of the row index, 0 otherwise. Arranging the coefficients
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
in a vector
{\bf b}
with entries indexed by the subsets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d-1}\}
,
(
18
) shows that
Q\in I_{n}
if and only if the vector of coefficients
{\bf b}
is in the vector space spanned by the columns of
\mathcal{C}
. We have
\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d-3\\
\end{array}\right)<\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d-1\\
\end{array}\right)
, and therefore there are certain linear equations (with coefficients depending only on
n
and
d
) that are satisfied among the
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
.

On the other hand, we can view the coefficients of
Q
as polynomials in the variables
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
. Note that
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
has degree 2 in the variables
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{d-1}}
, and degree 1 in all other variables. Therefore,
b_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d-1}}
are linearly independent as polynomials in
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
.
∎

We now discuss how the failure of injectivity proved in Theorem (
5.1
) relates to Conjecture 2 from
[
3
]
:

Conjecture 5.4

(
[
3
]
, Conjecture 2)
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
be general linear forms in
P_{n}
.
Then

\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n}}{(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2})}\right)_{d}\right)=a_{n,d}

where
a_{n,d}
is the number of lattice paths from
(0,0)
to
(n+2-2s,n+2)
with moves of the type
(i,j)\to(i+1,j+1)
(right move) or
(i,j)\to(i-1,j+1)
(left move) such that the first and the last move are to the right, and the path does not cross the lines
x=0
and
x=n+2-2d
.

The conjecture is proved in
[
3
]
for
n\geq 3d-3
. As a corollary of Theorem (
5.1
), we are able to prove that the conjecture holds for
n=3d-4
, which is the first case of interest.

Corollary 5.5
.

Conjecture (
5.4
) holds for
n=3d-4
.

Proof.

Let
P_{n+1}
denote the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}]
and
I_{n+1}:=\big(x_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{n+1}^{2},(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n+1})^{2}\big)
. Via a change of coordinates, we can take

\ell_{1}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
.
Consider the map
\phi_{n+1,d}
given by multiplication by a general form
\ell
from
(P_{n+1}/I_{n+1})_{d-1}
to
(P_{n+1}/I_{n+1})_{d}
.
The cokernel of this map is isomorphic to
\displaystyle\left(\frac{P_{n}}{(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2})}\right)_{d}
. Therefore,

\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n}}{(x_{1}^{2},\ldots,x_{n}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2},\ell_{2}^{2})}\right)_{d}\right)=\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n+1}}{I_{n+1}}\right)_{d}\right)-\mathrm{dim}\left(\left(\frac{P_{n+1}}{I_{n+1}}\right)_{d-1}\right)+\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\phi_{n+1,d})).

On the other hand, the proof of Proposition 7 in
[
3
]
shows that

a(n,d)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d\\
\end{array}\right)-2\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d-2\\
\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n\\
d-4\\
\end{array}\right)+T(n,d)

where we define
T(n,d)
to be the number of paths that cross the line
x=n+2-2d
first and then also cross the line
x=0
later.
In view of Remark (
1.3
), Theorem 8 in
[
3
]
implies

(19)

T_{n,d}\geq\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\phi_{n+1,d})),

and so the conjecture is equivalent to equality in (
19
).

We claim that when
n=3d-4
,
T_{n,d}=1
. To prove this, we argue along the same lines as in the proof of Proposition 7 in
[
3
]
. Indeed, a path that crosses first
x=n+2-2d
and then
x=0
consists of three segments; the first segment from
(1,1)
to
(n+3-2d,k_{1})
(the first time when the line
x=n+2-2d
is crossed) the second segment from
(n+3-2d,1+k_{1})
to
(-1,k_{1}+k_{2})
(the first time when the line
x=0
is crossed) and the third segment from
(-1,k_{2})
to
(n+1-2d,k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})
, where
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=n
(the total number of steps). If the first segment has
s_{1}
steps to the left, then
n+2-2d=k_{1}-2s_{1}
. If the second segment has
s_{2}
steps to the right, then
n+4-2d=k_{2}-2s_{2}
. Finally, if the third segment has
s_{3}
steps to the left, then
n+2-2d=n-2s_{3}
.
Adding the three equations above and recalling
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=n
, we obtain
3n+8-6d=n-2(s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3})
. This implies
s_{1}=s_{2}=s_{3}=0
, and therefore such a path must consist of only steps to the right for the first segment, only steps to the left for the second segment, and only steps to the right for the third segment. In particular, only one such path is possible.

On the other hand, Theorem (
5.1
)
shows that when
n\leq 3d-4
,
\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\phi_{n+1,d}))\geq 1
. When this is paired with (
19
), we have

(20)

1=T_{n,d}\geq\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Ker}(\phi_{n+1,d}))\geq 1

and therefore equality follows.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The next case of interest for Conjecture (
5.4
) is
n=3d-5
. As in the proof of Corollary (
5.5
), one can see that for this case, a path that crosses first
x=n+2-2d
and then
x=0
has exactly one step in one of the three segments in the direction opposite to the direction of the segment. Since this step can occur at any one of the
n
steps, we have
T(n,d)=n
. In order to establish that Conjecture (
5.4
) holds for this case, one would need to be able to produce
n
linearly independent elements in the kernel of
\phi_{n+1,d}
.
A potential way to accomplish this is by choosing various specializations of the one element that is known to exist in the kernel of
\phi_{n+2,d}
.
