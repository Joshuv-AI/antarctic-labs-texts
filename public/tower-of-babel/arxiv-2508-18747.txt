Some optimal recovery problems for operators on classes of L L -space valued functions
V. Babenko, V. Kolesnyk, O. Kovalenko

Abstract
. We solve three optimal recovery problems for operators on classes of L L –space (which is a semilinear metric space with two additional axioms that connect the metric with the algebraic operations) valued functions that are defined by a majorant of their modulus of continuity. Consideration of L L –spaces valued functions allows to treat multi- and fuzzy-valued functions, as well as random processes and other non-real valued functions in a unified manner.

41A55, 41A65, 41A17, 41A44

1.
Introduction

Let a metric space
(X,h_{X})
, sets
Z
,
Y
, a class of elements
W\subset Z
, as well as mappings
\Lambda\colon Z\to X
and
I\colon W\to Y
be given. We call an arbitrary mapping
\Phi\colon Y\to X
a method of recovery of the mapping
\Lambda
on the class
W
based on the information given by the mapping
I
. The error of recovery of the mapping
\Lambda
on the class
W
by the method
\Phi
based on the information given by the mapping
I
is given by the formula

{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda,W,I,\Phi,X)={\sup\nolimits_{w\in W}h_{X}(\Lambda(w),\Phi(I(w)))}.

The quantity

(1)

{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda,W,I,X)=\inf\nolimits_{\Phi}{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda,W,I,\Phi,X)

is called the optimal error of recovery of the mapping
\Lambda
on the class
W
based on the information given by the mapping
I
.
The problem of optimal recovery of the mapping
\Lambda
on the class
W
with the information given by
I
in the metric of the space
X
is to find quantity (
1
)
and a method
\Phi^{*}
(if such a method exists) on which the infimum on the right-hand side of (
1
) is attained.

In this article we solve three problems of optimal recovery on the classes
W=H^{\omega}(T,X)
of functions
f\colon T\to X
that take values in an
L
-space
X
(i.e., a semilinear metric space with two additional axioms that connect the metric with the algebraic operations) and have a given majorant
\omega
of their modulus of continuity; precise definitions will be given in the following sections.

Problem 1

Solve the problem
of optimal recovery of the operator

(2)

\Lambda f=\int_{Q}f(s)d\mu(s)

on the class
H^{\omega}(T,X)
based on the information operator

(3)

I(f)=(P(f(x_{1})),\ldots,P(f(x_{n}))),

where

Let
X
be an
L
-space, and
T
be a measurable space. We say that a function
f\colon T\to X
is measurable, if for each
x\in X

h_{X}(f(\cdot),x)
is measurable.
By
M(T,X)
we denote the space of all measurable functions
f\colon T\to X

We say that a function
f\in M(T,X)
is bounded, if for some
C>0
and
x\in X

h_{X}(f(t),x)<C
for all
t\in T
. We denote by
B(T,X)
the set of all bounded functions
f\in M(T,X)
. In the space
B(T,X)
one can introduce the uniform metric

h_{B(T,X)}(f,g)=\sup_{t\in T}h_{X}(f(t),g(t)).

Assume
Y
is also an
L
-space,
\lambda\colon X\to Y
is a positively homogeneous Lipschitz mapping and
\varphi\colon B(T,\mathbb{R})\to Y
is a monotone mapping (in the sense that if
0\leq f(t)\leq g(t)

\varphi(f)\leq\varphi(g)
). An operator
\Lambda\colon B(T,X)\to Y
is said to be of
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type, if the following properties hold:

(4)

h_{Y}(\Lambda f,\Lambda g)\leq\varphi(h_{Y}(\lambda\circ f,\lambda\circ g)),

and for any non-negative function
u\in B(T,\mathbb{R})
, and element
x\in X

(5)

\Lambda(u(\cdot)x)=\varphi(u(\cdot))\lambda(x).

The set
\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\varphi)
of all
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type operators is a subset of more general classes of operators that satisfy analogues of properties (

Solve the optimal recovery problem for the operator

\Lambda(f)=\lambda(f(t)),

2.
L
-spaces

2.1.
Definitions

Definition 1
.

A set
X
is called a semilinear space, if operations of addition of elements and their multiplication on real numbers are defined in
X
, and the following conditions are satisfied for all
x,y,z\in X
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle x+y=y+x;

\displaystyle x+(y+z)=(x+y)+z;

\displaystyle\exists\;\theta\in X\colon x+\theta=x;

\displaystyle\alpha(x+y)=\alpha x+\alpha y;

\displaystyle\alpha(\beta x)=\left(\alpha\beta\right)x;

\displaystyle 1\cdot x=x,\;0\cdot x=\theta.

Definition 2
.

We call an element
x\in X
convex, if for all
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
,
(\alpha+\beta)x=\alpha x+\beta x.

Denote by
X^{\rm c}
the subspace of all convex elements of the space
X
.

Remark 1
.

Some authors (see e.g.
[
26
]
) include into the axioms of a semi-linear space the requirement
X=X^{\rm c}
.

Definition 3
.

A semilinear space
X
endowed with a metric
h_{X}
is called an
L
-space, if it is complete, separable, and for all
x,y,z\in X
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

h_{X}(\alpha x,\alpha y)=|\alpha|h_{X}(x,y);

(9)

h_{X}(x+z,y+z)\leq h_{X}(x,y).

Remark 2
.

It follows from the triangle inequality and (
9
) that

(10)

\forall x,y,z,w\in X\;\;\;h_{X}(x+z,y+w)\leq h_{X}(x,y)+h_{X}(z,w).

Definition 4
.

An
L
-space
X
is called isotropic, if inequality (
9
) turns into equality for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Next we list some of the examples of
L
-spaces. More examples can be found in
[
13
,
15
,
16
]
.
Arbitrary separable Banach space and arbitrary complete and separable quasilinear normed space (see
[
27
]
) are
L
-spaces. The space
\Omega(X)
of non-empty compact subsets of a separable Banach space
X
endowed with the usual Hausdorff metric, the space
\Omega_{\rm conv}(X)
of convex elements from
\Omega(X)
, and spaces of fuzzy sets (see e. g.
[
28
]
) are also examples of
L
-spaces. All
L
-spaces mentioned above are isotropic.

An example of a non-isotropic
L
-space can be built as follows. Let
X=[0,\infty)
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
x,y\in X
set
x\oplus y=\max\{x,y\}
,
\lambda\odot x=|\lambda|x
. Then
(X,\oplus,\odot)
with the metric
h_{X}(x,y)=|x-y|
,
x,y\in X
, is a non-isotropic
L
-space.

2.2.
Approximation of Measurable Functions by Simple Ones

Let
\left(T,\mu\right)=\left(T,\mathcal{F},\mu\right)
be a measurable space with a complete finite measure
\mu
and
(X,h_{X})
be a separable metric space.
From
[
29
, Theorem I.4.20]
it follows that
f\in M(T,X)
if and only if for each open set
A\subset X
one has
f^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{F}
. Moreover, for two functions
f,g\in M(T,X)
, the function
t\mapsto h_{X}(f(t),g(t))
is measurable according to
[
29
, Theorem I.4.22]
.

The following variant of Egorov’s theorem is true, see e.g.
[
29
, Theorem I.4.18]
.

Theorem 1
.

If a sequence of functions
f_{i}\in M(T,X)
converges to
f\colon T\to X
almost everywhere as
i\to\infty
, then
f\in M(T,X)
. Moreover, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists a set
T_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mu(T\setminus T_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
and
f_{i}
converges to
f
uniformly on
T_{\varepsilon}
as
i\to\infty
.

Definition 5
.

A mapping
f\colon T\to X
is called simple, if it has a finite number of values
\left\{f_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
on pairwise disjoint measurable sets
\left\{T_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following lemma holds.

Lemma 1
.

For each
f\in M(T,X)
there exists a sequence of simple functions that converges to
f
almost everywhere. If
f
is bounded, then the sequence of simple functions can be chosen to be uniformly bounded.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots\}
be a countable dense in
X
set. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
set

A_{k,1}=\{t\in T\colon h_{X}(f(t),x_{1})<1/k\}
, and for each
i\geq 2
,
A_{k,i}=\{t\in T\colon h_{X}(f(t),x_{i})<1/k\}\setminus\bigcup_{s=1}^{i-1}A_{k,s}
. Then each of the sets
A_{k,i}
is measurable, and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
the sets
A_{k,i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, constitute a partition of the set
T
. Since for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{k,i})=\mu(T)<\infty
, there exists
N(k)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu\left(B_{k}\right)<2^{-k}
, where
B_{k}=\bigcup_{i=N(k)+1}^{\infty}A_{k,i}
. Set

f_{k}(t)=\begin{cases}x_{i},&t\in A_{k,i},i\leq N(k),\\
x_{1},&t\in B_{k}.\end{cases}

Then for each
t\in T\setminus B_{k}
one has
h_{X}(f(t),f_{k}(t))\leq 1/k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Set
C_{m}=\bigcup_{s=m}^{\infty}B_{s}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
, all
s\geq k
and all
t\in T\setminus C_{k}
one has
h_{X}(f(t),f_{s}(t))\leq 1/s
; this implies that the sequence of simple functions
f_{s}
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
, converges uniformly to
f
on the set
T\setminus C_{k}
. Since
C_{m+1}\subset C_{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu(C_{m})\to 0
as
m\to\infty
, we obtain that the sequence
f_{s}
converges to
f
almost everywhere and the first statement of the lemma is proved.

If for some
x\in X
one has
C:=\sup_{t\in T}h_{X}(f(t),x)<\infty
, then for the considered sequence
f_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and arbitrary
t\in T\setminus B_{k}
one has

h_{X}(f_{k}(t),x)\leq h_{X}(f_{k}(t),f(t))+h_{X}(f(t),x)\leq 1+C.

On the other hand, if
t\in B_{k}
, then

h_{X}(f_{k}(t),x)=h_{X}(x_{1},x)
. Thus the estimate from above for the distance
h_{X}(f_{k}(t),x)
does not depend on
k
for all
t\in T
, hence the sequence of functions
f_{i}
is uniformly bounded.
∎

2.3.
Integration in
L
-spaces

Let
X
and
Y
be
L
-spaces.

Definition 6
.

An operator
\lambda\colon X\to Y
is called Lipschitz if for all
x,y\in X

h_{Y}(\lambda(x),\lambda(y))\leq h_{X}(x,y).

Definition 7
.

(See
[
30
,
27
]
)
A surjective operator
P\colon X\to X^{\rm c}
is called a convexifying operator, if it is Lipschitz and

(1)

P\circ P=P
;

(2)

\forall x,y\in X
and
\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
P(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha P(x)+\beta P(y)
.

For example, the operator
{\rm co}\colon\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
that maps each
x\in\Omega(\mathbb{R}^{m})
to its convex hull
{\rm co}\,x
is a convexifying operator.

Remark 3
.

From the definition of a convexifying operator it follows that
P(x)=x
for all
x\in X^{\rm c}
.

Indeed, if
x\in X^{\rm c}
and
y\in X
is such that
P(y)=x
, then
P(x)=P(P(y))=P(y)=x
.

For completeness, we present the definition and some of the properties of the Lebesgue integral for the functions
f\in B(T,X)
, where
T
is a metric compact, see
[
30
]
and
[
27
, §5]
.

The Lebesgue integral of a simple mapping
f\in B(T,X)
with respect to a Borel measure
\mu
is by definition

\int_{T}f(s)d\mu(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(f_{i})\mu(T_{i}).

The following properties hold for all simple functions
f,g\in B(T,X)
.

(1)

For all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

\int_{T}\left(\alpha f(t)+\beta g(t)\right)d\mu(t)=\alpha\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t)+\beta\int_{T}g(t)d\mu(t).

(2)

The function
t\mapsto h_{X}\left(f(t),g(t)\right)
is integrable and

h_{X}\left(\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t),\int_{T}g(t)d\mu(t)\right)\leq\int_{T}h_{X}\left(f(t),g(t)\right)d\mu(t).

(3)

The function
P(f(\cdot))
is integrable and

\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t)=P\left(\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t)\right)=\int_{T}P(f(t))d\mu(t).

(4)

For disjoint measurable sets
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
such that
T=T_{1}\cup T_{2}

\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t)=\int_{T_{1}}f(t)d\mu(t)+\int_{T_{2}}f(t)d\mu(t).

By Lemma
1
, any function
f\in B(T,X)
is an almost everywhere limit of a uniformly bounded sequence
\{f^{k}\}
of simple functions. Using Theorem
1
and standard arguments, one can prove that the sequence
\left\{\int_{T}f^{k}(t)d\mu(t)\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is fundamental. By definition, the integral
\int_{T}f(t)d\mu(t)
of the function
f
is set to be the limit of this sequence; the fact that the limit does not depend on the choice of the sequence
\{f^{k}\}
is easy to verify.
It is clear that properties 1-4 of the Lebesgue integral for simple functions hold for arbitrary functions from
B(T,X)
.

Note that in the case of
X
being a Banach space, the integral defined above becomes the Bochner integral, see
[
31
, Sections 3.7–3.8]
; in the case
X=\Omega({\mathbb{R}^{m}})
, the integral coincides with the Aumann integral, see
[
27
, Theorem 12]
.

2.4.
Some Properties of
L
-spaces

Definition 8
.

We say that an element
x\in X
is invertible, if there exists an element
x^{\prime}\in X
such that
x+x^{\prime}=\theta
. In this case the element
x^{\prime}
is called the inverse to
x
. Denote by
X^{\rm inv}
the set of all invertible elements of the space
X
.

It is easy to see that an inverse element is unique whenever it exists.

In the space
\Omega(X)
any element of the form
\{x\},

x\in X
, is convex and invertible.

Remark
3
immediately implies the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

If
P
is a convexifying operator in an isotropic
L
-space
X
, then
P(\theta)=\theta
. If
x\in X^{{\rm inv}}
, then
P(x)\in X^{\rm inv}
and
P(x^{\prime})=(P(x))^{\prime}
.

We also need the following lemmas, see e.g.
[
14
]
. We give their proofs for completeness.

Lemma 3
.

If
x\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{\rm c}
, then
x^{\prime}\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{\rm c}
.

Proof.

The implication
x\in X^{\rm inv}\implies x^{\prime}\in X^{\rm inv}
is obvious. We prove that
x\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{\rm c}\implies x^{\prime}\in X^{\rm c}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
.

\theta=0\cdot\theta=((\alpha+\beta)\cdot 0)\cdot\theta=(\alpha+\beta)\cdot(0\cdot\theta)=(\alpha+\beta)\theta\\
=(\alpha+\beta)(x+x^{\prime})=(\alpha+\beta)x+(\alpha+\beta)x^{\prime}=\alpha x+\beta x+(\alpha+\beta)x^{\prime}

i.e.,
(\alpha+\beta)x^{\prime}=(\alpha x+\beta x)^{\prime}
. On the other hand,

(\alpha x+\beta x)^{\prime}=\alpha x^{\prime}+\beta x^{\prime}
, since

\alpha x+\beta x+\alpha x^{\prime}+\beta x^{\prime}=\alpha(x+x^{\prime})+\beta(x+x^{\prime})=\theta.

Due to uniqueness of the inverse element, we obtain

\alpha x^{\prime}+\beta x^{\prime}=(\alpha+\beta)x^{\prime}

i.e.,
x^{\prime}\in X^{\rm c}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For any
x\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{c}
, one has

h_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=2h_{X}(x,\theta).

Proof.

We have

h_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=h_{X}(x+\theta,x^{\prime})=h_{X}(x+x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\\
\leq h_{X}(x+x,\theta)=h_{X}(x+x,x^{\prime}+x)\leq h_{X}(x,x^{\prime}).

Hence all inequalities necessarily turn into equalities and thus

h(x,x^{\prime})=h_{X}(x+x,\theta)=h_{X}(2x,\theta)=2h_{X}(x,\theta).

∎

Definition 9
.

For a set
T
, a function
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
and an element
x\in X^{\rm inv}
define the function
f_{x}\colon T\to X
,

f_{x}(t)=f_{+}(t)\cdot x+f_{-}(t)\cdot x^{\prime},

where for real
\xi
,
\xi_{\pm}:=\max\{\pm\xi,0\}
, and
f_{\pm}(t)=(f(t))_{\pm}
.

Let a modulus of continuity
\omega
(i.e., a non-decreasing continuous semi-additive function that vanishes at
0
), a metric space
(T,r)
, and an
L
-space
X
be given.

Definition 10
.

By
H^{\omega}(T,X)
we denote the space of functions
f\colon T\to X
such that for all
t,s\in T
one has
h_{X}(f(s),f(t))\leq\omega(r(t,s))
.

The following lemma can be proved similarly to
[
18
, Lemma 6]
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
X
be an isotropic
L
-space and
f\in H^{\omega}(T,\mathbb{R})
. If
x\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{\rm c}
is such that
h_{X}(x,\theta)\leq 1
, then
f_{x}\in H^{\omega}(T,X)
and

\int_{T}f_{x}(t)dt=\int_{T}f_{+}(t)dt\cdot x+\int_{T}f_{-}(t)dt\cdot x^{\prime}\\
=\left(\int_{T}f(t)dt\right)_{+}\cdot x+\left(\int_{T}f(t)dt\right)_{-}\cdot x^{\prime}.

If
f
is non-negative on
T
, then it is enough to require
x\in X^{\rm c}
instead of
x\in X^{\rm inv}\cap X^{\rm c}
.

2.5.
Operators of
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type

In this subsection
X
and
Y
are
L
-spaces.

Definition 11
.

We call an operator
\lambda\colon X\to Y
positively homogeneous, if for all
\alpha\geq 0
and
x\in X
,

\lambda(\alpha\cdot x)=\alpha\cdot\lambda(x),

in particular
\lambda(\theta)=\theta
.
Denote by
\mathcal{H}(X,Y)
the set of all positively homogeneous Lipschitz operators
\lambda\colon X\to Y
.

Definition 12
.

A functional
\varphi\colon B(T,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
is called monotone, if
\varphi(f)\leq\varphi(g)
, provided
0\leq f(t)\leq g(t)
for all
t\in T
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}(T)
the set of all monotone functionals
\varphi\colon B(T,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 13
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}(X,Y)
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(T)
be given. An operator
\Lambda\colon B(T,X)\to Y
is said to be of
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type, if conditions (
4
) and (
5
) hold. Denote by
{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,\varphi)
the set of all
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type operators.

Below are two examples of
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type operators.

(1)

The operator
\Lambda
from (
2
)
is of
(\lambda,\varphi)
-type, where
\lambda
is the convexifying operator, and
\varphi
is the Lebesgue integral for the real-valued functions.

3.

Let

be a metric compact with metric

Definition 14

For

a sequence

for all

Theorem 2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be
L
-spaces,
(T,r)
be a metric compact,
\lambda\in\mathcal{H}(X,Y)
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(T)
. Let also
\chi\in B(T,\mathbb{R})
be a non-negative function such that
\varphi(\chi)>0
,
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\varphi)
and
t\in T
. For each

h_{Y}\left(\lambda(f(t)),\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi f)\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\varphi(\omega(r(t,\cdot))\chi(\cdot)).

If
X
is isotropic and
X^{\rm c}
contains a
\lambda
-extremal sequence, then the inequality is sharp.

Proof.

\displaystyle h_{Y}\left(\lambda(f(t)),\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi f)\right)=h_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi f(t)),\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\varphi(h_{Y}[\lambda(\chi f(t)),\lambda(\chi f)])\leq\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\varphi(h_{X}[f(t),f(\cdot)]\chi(\cdot))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\varphi(\omega(r(t,\cdot))\chi(\cdot)).

Let
X
be isotropic and
\{x_{n}\}\subset X^{\rm c}
be a
\lambda
-extremal sequence. Then due to Lemma

,

\displaystyle h_{Y}\left(\lambda(f_{n}(t)),\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi f_{n})\right)=h_{Y}\left(\theta,\frac{1}{\varphi(\chi)}\Lambda(\chi\omega(r(t,\cdot))x_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=h_{Y}\left(\theta,\frac{\varphi(\omega(r(t,\cdot))\chi)}{\varphi(\chi)}\lambda(x_{n})\right)\to\frac{\varphi(\omega(r(t,\cdot))\chi)}{\varphi(\chi)},n\to\infty.

∎

4.1.
One Auxiliary Lemma

We need the following well known estimate for the value of the optimal recovery (
1

[

Lemma 6
.

If

are such that
I(f)=I(g)
, then

{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda,W,I,X)\geq\frac{1}{2}h_{X}(\Lambda(f),\Lambda(g)).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in W}h_{X}(\Lambda(w),\Phi(I(w)))\geq\max\left\{h_{X}(\Lambda(f),\Phi(I(f))),h_{X}(\Lambda(g),\Phi(I(g)))\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(h_{X}(\Lambda(f),\Phi(I(f)))+h_{X}(\Lambda(g),\Phi(I(f)))\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}h_{X}(\Lambda(f),\Lambda(g)),

as desired.
∎
