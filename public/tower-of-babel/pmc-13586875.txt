Clinical impact of baloxavir-induced PA I38T/M substitutions in diverse influenza populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuxin Zhao, Haitian Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•PA I38T/M substitutions occur in 10.5% of baloxavir-treated patients.•Resistance disproportionately affects pediatric populations (up to 23.4%).•Mutants prolong viral shedding from 24 h to 180 h in children.•Clinical recovery delay is 36.8 h in pediatrics vs 6 h in adults.•Structural modeling reveals I38T disrupts baloxavir binding via Tyr24.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains a formidable global public health challenge, causing approximately one billion infections annually, including up to 650,000 respiratory-related deaths (World Health Organization, n.d.), and imposing an annual economic burden of tens of billions of dollars driven by direct medical costs and lost productivity (Popovian and Winegarden, 2025). The pathophysiology of influenza is highly destructive; it directly kills respiratory epithelial cells and triggers an excessive immune response, leaving patients highly susceptible to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and secondary bacterial pneumonias (Kalil and Thomas, 2019). This burden extends notably to pediatric populations, with substantial mortality observed even among previously healthy children without high-risk underlying conditions (Wong et al., 2013). Recent epidemiological data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) underscores this escalating threat, as the 2024–2025 season witnessed remarkably high pediatric influenza-associated hospitalizations and deaths, frequently complicated by rapid-onset severe respiratory illness (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, n.d.)).

To mitigate this burden, the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) strongly advocates for the early initiation of antiviral therapy to shorten symptom duration and control institutional outbreaks (Uyeki et al., 2019). Currently, neuraminidase inhibitors (NAIs), such as oral oseltamivir and inhaled zanamivir, serve as the standard treatments. These agents function by targeting the viral neuraminidase glycoprotein to block the enzymatic cleavage of sialic acid, thereby preventing the release of progeny virions from infected host cells (Javanian et al., 2021). However, their clinical efficacy is continually threatened by the unpredictable emergence of resistance-conferring mutations. In comparison, baloxavir marboxil (BXM) has emerged as a highly effective, single-dose cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor that circumvents NAI-resistance pathways (Hayden et al., 2018; Ison et al., 2020). As an orally bioavailable prodrug, BXM undergoes rapid in vivo hydrolysis by arylacetamide deacetylase into its active metabolite, baloxavir acid (BXA) (Omoto et al., 2018). BXA selectively targets the polymerase acidic (PA) subunit to block the essential 'cap-snatching' process, thereby arresting viral mRNA transcription. Despite its robust antiviral efficacy, the emergence of treatment-induced PA protein substitutions—most notably I38T/M—presents a critical challenge. While the virological resistance conferred by these substitutions is recognized, their precise clinical penalty remains poorly quantified across different demographic groups. This study systematically reviews the incidence and clinical impact of PA I38T/M substitutions, integrating these findings with structural biology to elucidate the mechanistic basis of this vulnerability.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021; Supplementary PRISMA Checklist). The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform (Registration URL: https://osf.io/a7jw5). All review procedures were strictly executed in adherence to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The study mainly focused on evaluating the clinical and virologic implications of treatment-emergent PA I38T/M substitutions across diverse influenza patient populations.

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to April 2026. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms including "baloxavir marboxil," "influenza," "polymerase acidic protein," and "PA I38 substitutions" (including I38T and I38M) (Supplementary Text S1).

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) Study Design: Prospective clinical trials (including randomized controlled trials and phase 3 open-label studies). (2) Population: Outpatients with uncomplicated, laboratory-confirmed influenza aged ≥ 1 year. Studies enrolling infants younger than 1 year were excluded. Infants < 1 year possess entirely distinct immune baselines, which introduces critical heterogeneity into the assessment of viral shedding and clinical recovery. Furthermore, studies enrolling hospitalized patients with severe or complicated influenza were excluded, as the viral shedding dynamics and immune responses in severe cases fundamentally differ from those in the general outpatient population. (3) Intervention and Comparator: Administration of baloxavir marboxil (with or without a comparator group). (4) Outcomes: Reporting of treatment-emergent PA I38T/M substitutions (explicitly encompassing both pure mutant populations and mixed wild-type/mutant populations, such as I38T/I or I38M/I) and clinical efficacy in the treated index patients, specifically the TTIA. While trials evaluating post-exposure prophylaxis in household contacts were included for the qualitative and quantitative synthesis of mutation incidence, they were strictly excluded from the pooled analysis of the TTIA to maintain the clinical homogeneity of this therapeutic endpoint.

Observational studies and in vitro experimental models were excluded from quantitative synthesis but retained for mechanistic evaluations.

Data extraction and outcome measures

The primary investigator screened the retrieved articles and extracted the initial data using a standardized, pre-piloted form. To minimize extraction bias, a second independent investigator cross-checked the extracted virologic and clinical endpoints (including the incidence of PA substitutions, TTIA, and viral shedding duration) against the original full texts. Any discrepancies were resolved through rigorous discussion to reach a consensus. Notably, while the parent studies are randomized controlled trials, the pooled comparison of TTIA between wild-type and mutant variants constitutes an observational synthesis, given that PA substitutions are treatment-emergent events rather than randomized baseline allocations.

Quality and certainty assessment

The methodological quality of the included prospective clinical trials was evaluated according to their study designs. The four randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) (Sterne et al., 2019) across five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Correspondingly, the non-randomized, single-arm prospective trial was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. The overall certainty of the evidence for primary outcomes was graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework (Guyatt et al., 2008).

Statistical analysis

For dichotomous outcomes, pooled proportions and relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, the weighted mean difference (WMD) and 95% CIs were pooled. All analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.95.4; University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands). A Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method was utilized a priori to account for anticipated clinical heterogeneity (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q test (P < 0.10) and the I2 statistic (I2 > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity) (Higgins et al., 2003). Pre-specified leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to isolate the impact of immunologically naive pediatric cohorts versus adult cohorts. Given the limited number of included trials (k < 10), formal assessment of publication bias via funnel plots and Egger’s regression test was waived a priori in accordance with Cochrane Handbook recommendations (Egger et al., 1997).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of the four included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool (Fig. 2). The single-arm prospective trial (Hirotsu et al., 2020) demonstrated a moderate risk of bias primarily due to its open-label nature, yet it maintained high quality in outcome measurement (Supplementary Table S2). Overall, the four RCTs exhibited a low risk of bias across the five assessed domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result (Supplementary Table S3). Specifically, the double-blind, randomized design employed in all trials ensured high internal validity. Incidence of treatment-emergent PA I38T/M substitutions

The pooled incidence of treatment-emergent PA I38T/M substitutions was 10.5% (Fig. 3; 95% CI: 0.0% to 21.1%; I2 = 94.6%). High statistical heterogeneity was observed among the studies. To evaluate the stability of this pooled estimate, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Table 2; Fig. 4) was performed. This analysis demonstrated that the removal of any individual trial did not meaningfully alter the overall pooled incidence, thereby confirming the robustness of our primary findings. For instance, exclusion of the prophylactic trial (Ikematsu et al., 2020) increased the pooled incidence to 12.8% (95% CI: 0.0% to 26.1%; I2 = 90.0%). Similarly, excluding the Japanese pediatric trial reduced the pooled estimate to 7.3% (95% CI: 0.0% to 16.8%; I2 = 89.4%). Crucially, where variant subtypes were fully specified in the primary literature (Hirotsu et al., 2020; Ikematsu et al., 2020; Ison et al., 2020), the I38T substitution emerged as the overwhelmingly dominant variant. Among 41 precisely characterized substitution events, the I38T variant (including pure I38T and I38T/I mixtures) accounted for 36 cases (87.8%), while the I38M variant (including pure I38M and I38I/M mixtures) was detected in only 5 cases (12.2%). Given that in vitro assays demonstrate the I38T substitution confers a notably more profound reduction in baloxavir acid susceptibility compared to I38M, the epidemiological dominance of the I38T variant signifies that it acts as the primary driver of the virological rebound and delayed clinical recovery observed in these highly susceptible demographic groups.

It is important to note that this analysis specifically focused on I38T/M variants due to their epidemiological dominance in the included Phase 3 trials. While other substitutions (e.g., I38F, I38N) exist, they were exceedingly rare in these cohorts (e.g., I38F was primarily restricted to the Phase 2 cohort of CAPSTONE-1). Furthermore, our pooled incidence robustly incorporated cases with mixed viral populations (e.g., I38T/I or I38M/I), as the emergence of even a fractional mutant population is sufficient to confer reduced baloxavir susceptibility and drive the observed clinical penalties.

Furthermore, subtype analysis stratified by viral etiology revealed distinct mutational vulnerabilities between influenza A subtypes. Notably, the pediatric study by Baker et al. (2020) was excluded from this specific TTIA pooling due to the lack of extractable TTIA data stratified by PA I38T/M substitution status. Only three studies contributed to the overall TTIA analysis.

Subgroup analysis revealed a stark demographic divergence in the clinical penalty of PA substitutions. Specifically, when the single pediatric study (Hirotsu et al., 2020), which reported a substantial individual TTIA delay of 36.8 h for pediatric mutants was omitted, the pooled estimate dropped sharply to 5.67 h (Hayden et al., 2018; Ison et al., 2020) (95% CI: -142.07 to 153.42; P = 0.711; I2 = 53.7%). This remaining 5.67-hour delay accurately reflects the pooled effect size of the adult cohort (Hayden et al., 2018; Ison et al., 2020).

Although these intra-group estimates did not reach statistical significance due to the limited number of available trials and wide confidence intervals, this stark demographic divergence successfully elucidates the primary source of the overarching clinical heterogeneity. These findings underscore that the clinical penalty of the mutation is highly age-stratified, aligning with observations that PA I38T/M variants cause delayed initial symptom resolution predominantly in immunologically vulnerable populations (Uehara et al., 2019).

Publication bias and certainty of evidence

Formal assessment of publication bias via visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test was not conducted. In accordance with Cochrane Handbook recommendations, these tests are statistically underpowered and potentially misleading when fewer than ten studies are included in the meta-analysis. The overall certainty of the evidence for the primary outcomes (incidence of PA substitutions and TTIA prolongation) was evaluated using the GRADE framework (Table 5). Although all included studies were high-quality RCTs, the certainty of evidence was downgraded from 'High' to 'Moderate'. Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrates a pooled incidence of PA I38T/M substitutions of 10.5% following baloxavir monotherapy. The substantial statistical heterogeneity (I² = 94.6%) was primarily driven by pediatric populations. These treatment-emergent substitutions significantly prolong infectious viral shedding. While the overall pooled prolongation of the time to TTIA did not reach statistical significance, our sensitivity analyses confirm that this is heavily masked by adult cohorts, with pediatric populations driving a profound and clinically meaningful delay.

The virological basis for this resistance-driven clinical delay lies in the structural biology of the influenza PA endonuclease. Following oral administration, the prodrug baloxavir marboxil (BXM) undergoes in vivo hydrolysis to form its active antiviral metabolite, baloxavir acid (BXA) (Supplementary Figure S1A). BXA inhibits viral replication by directly targeting the viral RNA polymerase complex, especially the PA endonuclease, which executes the essential 'cap-snatching' process - a mechanism in which the virus cleaves capped RNA fragments from host messenger RNAs and uses these host-derived primers to initiate the synthesis of its own viral messenger RNAs (Kumar et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2023b). Structural analyses show that the BXA molecule binds to the RNA binding site of the PA endonuclease, particularly at the positions where bases P-1 and P0 bind. The residues important for stabilizing P-1 and P0 bases (Tyr24 and Ile38, respectively), are also responsible for BXA binding (Supplementary Figure S1B).

Further structural analyses indicate that BXA binds directly to the endonuclease active site by chelating two divalent metal ions and forming extensive van der Waals contacts with residues including Ala20, Tyr24, Ala37, and critically, Ile38 (Omoto et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022) (Supplementary Figure S2A). Although the overall architectures of BXA-bound wild-type and mutant PA endonucleases are essentially identical, the I38T substitution replaces hydrophobic isoleucine with a polar threonine. Specifically, the I38T mutation disrupts these interactions and reduces binding affinity by 30- to 50-fold (Li et al., 2026; Omoto et al., 2018). This effect most likely arises because I38T introduces an extra hydrogen bond between Thr38 and Tyr24 (Kohlbrand et al., 2024) (Supplementary Figure S2B), the latter being a key residue for binding both RNA substrates and inhibitors such as BXA (Kumar et al., 2021). This altered active site conformation impairs both inhibitor binding and intrinsic enzymatic activity, simultaneously imposing a severe replicative "fitness cost".

Omoto et al. confirmed that these variant viruses exhibit attenuated growth kinetics and reduced pathogenicity in animal models compared to wild-type strains, because the viral RNA polymerase complex depends on highly specific structural integrity and host components to function (Ren et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023a). As demonstrated in a rigorous ferret model, structurally demanding substitutions such as I38F severely compromise viral replication to the extent of entirely abrogating airborne transmission (Jones et al., 2020). In contrast, the epidemiologically dominant I38T variant represents an optimal evolutionary compromise: despite its attenuated fitness, it retains sufficient capacity for both contact and aerosol transmissibility.

This viral fitness cost mechanistically explains the observed demographic divergence. In immunocompetent adults, a mature immune system clears the fitness-impaired mutant virus, resulting in minimal TTIA prolongation (WMD: 5.67 h). In contrast, immunologically naive pediatric patients rely heavily on direct antiviral inhibition. Upon resistance emergence, the lack of robust adaptive immunity leads to prolonged virological rebound (shedding duration increased from 24.0 to 180.0 h) and pronounced clinical delays. This "immunological maturation hypothesis" is further corroborated by an age-based pooled analysis revealing a striking gradient: children under 6 years exhibited significantly higher rates of recurrent fever (50.0%) and viral rebound (54.5%) compared to those aged 6 to 12 years (Hirotsu et al., 2023). These observations confirm that host immunological status is a primary determinant of the mutation-associated clinical penalty.

Admittedly, the differing resistance frequencies and virological outcomes between pediatric and adult cohorts could potentially be influenced by previous or concurrent use of other antiviral treatments. Children experience recurrent seasonal influenza infections and may possess a higher likelihood of recent prior exposure to NAIs. Although direct cross-resistance between NAIs and baloxavir is mechanistically improbable due to their distinct viral targets, previous antiviral exposures might alter baseline viral dynamics, mucosal immune priming, or the underlying viral genetic background. Because the trials included in this meta-analysis strictly evaluated baloxavir monotherapy, we could not directly adjust for the precise history of prior antiviral usage as a covariate. This potential clinical confounder warrants explicit consideration in future observational studies comparing pediatric and adult virological outcomes.

Furthermore, our subtype analysis revealed a notably higher incidence of treatment-emergent PA substitutions in patients infected with influenza A(H3N2) compared to those with influenza A(H1N1)pdm09. This subtype-specific divergence can likely be elucidated through the lens of viral epistasis and genetic background. As demonstrated in evolutionary models, the phenotypic and fitness impacts of a specific mutation are highly context-dependent and vary significantly across different viral genetic backgrounds (Lyons and Lauring, 2018). It is highly plausible that the overarching genetic architecture of influenza A(H3N2) provides a more permissive epistatic environment for the I38T substitution. Given that resistance-conferring mutations generally impose a replicative fitness cost that must be mitigated by compensatory mutations, influenza A(H3N2) variants may either better buffer this intrinsic endonuclease fitness cost or more readily acquire compensatory mutations across the polymerase complex compared to influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains, which may face steeper evolutionary constraints.

Crucially, households with school-aged children serve as central hubs for influenza transmission (Fox et al., 1982). Despite the aforementioned fitness cost, definitive epidemiological evidence confirms that PA I38T variants retain sufficient replicative capacity to be directly transmitted from baloxavir-treated pediatric patients to unexposed household contacts (Takashita et al., 2019), highlighting a severe public health implication. While recent systematic reviews have reaffirmed baloxavir’s overall efficacy and tolerability in children (Ji et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025), quantitative synthesis of viral resistance mutations was previously precluded by methodological heterogeneity and incompatible data reporting across observational studies. Nevertheless, real-world evidence underscores the virological penalty of these mutations in immunologically naive populations. Several pediatric observational studies from Japan have laid the groundwork for this understanding using comprehensive virological approaches (Nezu et al., 2023; Saito et al., 2020; Wagatsuma et al., 2022). While retrospective data highlight baloxavir's rapid initial fever resolution in early childhood (Nezu et al., 2023), foundational cohorts employing serial virus isolation before and after baloxavir treatment, quantitative viral load assessment by PCR, antiviral susceptibility testing, and genetic analysis of resistance-associated substitutions reveal the underlying virological dynamics (Saito et al., 2020; Wagatsuma et al., 2022). Building upon this, a comprehensive four-season multicenter analysis by Saito et al. (2025) demonstrated a significantly slower decline in viral RNA loads among pediatric patients harboring PA variants, with higher treatment-emergent variant frequencies observed in younger cohorts (Saito et al., 2025). This prolonged viral persistence supports the hypothesis that host immunological maturity modulates the clinical consequences of these mutations. The present study addresses this critical methodological gap by aggregating mutational endpoints exclusively from pivotal phase 3 trials, definitively establishing that the clinical impact of PA I38T/M substitutions is strongly demographic-dependent.

Consequently, age-stratified resistance monitoring is imperative. Recent data from the 2025 CENTERSTONE (Monto et al., 2025) trial and the miniSTONE-1 (Palmu et al., 2025) pediatric cohort underscore this urgent need to prevent household transmission of these robust variant viruses. To accelerate viral clearance and reduce selection production, combination antiviral regimens may fundamentally benefit pediatric populations. Our proposal for this combination therapy is directly supported by the recent FLAGSTONE trial (2022), which demonstrated that pairing baloxavir with oseltamivir in hospitalized patients dramatically suppressed the emergence of PA variants to as low as 2% (Kumar et al., 2022). Although primarily evaluated in severe adult cases, this virological proof-of-concept provides a rationale to extend dual-target inhibition strategies to high-risk pediatric outpatients, pending rigorous pharmacokinetic and safety profiling in this vulnerable demographic, thereby halting the cascade of community transmission.

Limitations of this study include the small number of eligible prospective trials (k = 5), which precluded formal meta-regression and publication bias assessments. Additionally, the precise distribution of influenza virus subtypes among the specific subsets of patients evaluated for resistance was not fully detailed in the pediatric trials by Hirotsu et al. and Baker et al., nor were resistance-associated substitutions evaluated in all enrolled patients due to various exclusions. These methodological data gaps may limit the precision of our subtype-specific estimates and potentially introduce selection bias. Moreover, as delineated in our methodology, the comparative analysis of TTIA between variant groups is inherently observational. Nevertheless, the high methodological rigor, strict double-blind execution, and balanced baseline demographics of the parent randomized trials largely mitigate the susceptibility to critical confounding. While the substantial heterogeneity (I² > 80%) reflects clinical diversity, sensitivity analyses successfully identified its primary source. Finally, given the confirmed household transmission risk of I38T-variant viruses (Takashita et al., 2019), further real-world studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of combination therapies in interrupting transmission chains.

Conclusions

Treatment-emergent PA I38T/M substitutions cause significant virological rebound and delayed clinical recovery predominantly in pediatric populations. These findings underscore the critical role of host immune maturation in mitigating viral fitness costs. To preserve the efficacy of cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitors in immunologically vulnerable patients, future clinical guidelines should evaluate combination antiviral regimens. Specifically, pairing baloxavir with neuraminidase inhibitors exerts a synergistic blockade, successfully suppressing the emergence of PA variants to as low as 2%. Furthermore, as the influenza polymerase complex relies on host cell factors (e.g., HMGB2 and RPAP2) to toggle between transcription and replication (Rasmussen et al., 2026), integrating host-directed therapies offers a formidable strategy to thwart resistance in these high-risk demographics.
