Addendum on data driven regularization by projection
Martin Hanke, Otmar Scherzer

Abstract
We study the stability of regularization by projection for solving linear inverse problems if the forward operator is given indirectly but specified via some input-output training pairs. We extend the approach in [ 1 ] to data pairs, which are noisy and, possibly, linearly dependent.



Keywords:

Data driven regularization, variational regularization, regularization by projection, inverse problems

Last modified: August 24, 2026

1
Introduction

Data driven regularization by projection attempts to reconstruct a solution of a linear operator equation

Kx=y

(1)

without making explicit use of the operator
K
but only of some of its input-output relations
(x_{k})_{k}\subseteq{\cal X}
and

(y_{k})_{k}\subseteq{\cal Y}
.

In
[
1
]
we have considered two different strategies how the solution of (
1
)
can be computed in a data driven manner:

1.

Method I - least squares projection (called
regularization
by projection
in
[
1
]
);

2.

Method II - dual least squares method, originally suggested by Natterer
[
7
]
, which, however, requires additional measurements and therefore limits the practical applicability in a data driven setting.

In addition to the methods and results from
[
1
]
we consider here the following extensions:

1.

We introduce a third method (Method III), which employs a Bayesian approach, and – in a way – combines the merits of Methods I and II.

2.

While in
[
1
]
the explicit assumption has been made that the training data
(y_{k})_{k}
satisfy

Kx_{k}=y_{k}\,,

(2)

we omit this simplifying assumption in this note. But we emphasize that if the training data are exact and if
K
is injective, then Method III reduces to Method I.

3.

We also provide a preliminary error analysis for Methods I and III.

4.

In the appendix we give some simple examples concerning the rank of
covariance matrices.

2
Setting

These notes are concerned with the following setting from
[
1
]
.

Let
K:{\cal X}\to{\cal Y}
be a bounded linear operator between the Hilbert spaces
{\cal X}

and
{\cal Y}
, which has non-closed range
{\cal R}(K)
.
Assume further, that the only available information about
K

is given in terms of two sequences
(x_{k})_{k}\subset{\cal X}
– called
images
in
[
1
]
– and data

(y_{k})_{k}\subset{\cal Y}
with

\|y_{k}-Kx_{k}\|_{\cal Y}\,\leq\,\delta\,,\qquad k=1,2,3,\dots\,,

(3)

for some noise level
\delta>0
. Compared to
[
1
]
the data can be noisy, and neither the images nor the data need to be linearly independent.

Let

{\cal X}_{n}\,=\,\operatorname{span}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\,,\qquad n\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}\,,

denote the increasing subspaces of
{\cal X}
associated with the given images
x_{k}
. Further, let
P_{{\cal X}_{n}}
be the orthogonal projector of

{\cal X}
onto
{\cal X}_{n}
. In order to work with the given training pairs we first need an
approximate representation
K_{n}
of
KP_{{\cal X}_{n}}
.

To this end define the ”matrix”

X_{n}\,=\,[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\,,

i.e., the linear map from
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
to
{\cal X}
with

X_{n}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{k}x_{k}\,\in{\cal X}_{n}\,,\qquad{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\,=\,\bigl[\xi_{k}\bigr]_{k=1}^{n}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}\,,

(4)

and its adjoint

X_{n}^{*}:x\,\mapsto\,\bigl[\,\langle\,x,x_{k}\,\rangle_{\cal X}\,\bigr]_{k=1}^{n}\,.

Further, let

X_{n}\,=\,U_{n}\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*}

(5)

be the singular value decomposition of
X_{n}
,
where
\Lambda
is a diagonal
p\times p
matrix with the positive singular
values on its diagonal (
p\leq n
is the dimension of
{\cal X}_{n}
); furthermore,

\mathchar 260\relax\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times p}
has orthonormal columns, while
U_{n}
is a linear map from
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p}

to
{\cal X}
in analogy to
X_{n}
, and the columns
u_{k}
of

U_{n}\,=\,[u_{1},\dots,u_{p}]

provide an orthonormal basis of
{\cal X}_{n}
.
It follows that every
x\in{\cal X}_{n}
can be written as
U_{n}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
for some

{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p}
with

\|x\|_{\cal X}\,=\,\|{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\|_{2}\,.

To compute approximations
z_{k}\approx Ku_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,p
,
collect the given data from
{\cal Y}
and the unknown
z_{k}
in two “matrices”

Y_{n}\,=\,[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{n}\,=\,[z_{1},\dots,z_{p}]\,,

(6)

and denote by
e_{k}
the
i
th Cartesian basis vector in
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
for

k=1,\dots,n
. Then, the singular value decomposition (
5
) yields

y_{k}-Kx_{k}\,=\,(Y_{n}-KX_{n})e_{k}\,=\,(Y_{n}-KU_{n}\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*})e_{k}\,,

and hence we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|y_{k}-Kx_{k}\|_{\cal Y}^{2}\,=\,\|Y_{n}-KU_{n}\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*}\|_{HS}^{2}\,,

where
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{HS}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
of the corresponding operator. In view of (
3
) and (
6
) it is
now natural to define
Z_{n}
as the minimizer of the functional

\Phi(Z)\,=\,\|Y_{n}\,-\,Z\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*}\|_{HS}

(7)

over
Z\in{\cal Y}^{p}
. Let
\mathchar 260\relax^{\prime}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n\times(n-p)}
be such that the columns of

[\mathchar 260\relax\ \mathchar 260\relax^{\prime}]
form a full orthonormal basis of
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
. Then

\Phi^{2}(Z)\,=\,\|(Y_{n}-Z\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*})[\mathchar 260\relax\ \mathchar 260\relax^{\prime}]\|_{HS}^{2}\,=\,\|Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax-Z\Lambda\|_{HS}^{2}\,+\,\|Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax^{\prime}\|_{HS}^{2}\,.

(8)

From this we conclude that the unique minimizer of
\Phi
is given by

Z\,=\,Z_{n}\,:=\,Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax\Lambda^{-1}\,,

(9)

and therefore our best approximation of
K
on
{\cal X}_{n}
is given by

K_{n}\,:=\,Z_{n}U_{n}^{*}\,=\,Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax\Lambda^{-1}U_{n}^{*}

(10)

as approximation of
KP_{{\cal X}_{n}}
, once the first
n
training pairs
(x_{k},y_{k})
,

k=1,\dots,n
, have been seen.

In view of (
5
) we can rewrite (
10
) as

K_{n}\,=\,Y_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\,=\,Y_{n}X_{n}^{*}(X_{n}X_{n}^{*})^{\dagger}

(11)

in terms of the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
X_{n}^{\dagger}
of
X_{n}
,
and we mention that this is a well-known estimator of
K
in statistics;
cf., e.g., Crambes and Mas
[
2
]
and
Hörmann and Kidziński
[
4
]
, and the references therein.
We further note that for exact data, i.e., when
\delta=0
in (
3
),
then we have

K_{n}\,=\,KX_{n}\mathchar 260\relax\Lambda^{-1}U_{n}^{*}\,=\,KU_{n}\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*}\mathchar 260\relax\Lambda^{-1}U_{n}^{*}\,=\,KP_{{\cal X}_{n}}\,.

(12)

In the general case, however, it follows from (
10
), the triangle inequality, and (
8
) that

\displaystyle\|(K_{n}-K)U_{n}\Lambda\|_{HS}

\displaystyle\,=\,\|(Z_{n}-KU_{n})\Lambda\|_{HS}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\|Z_{n}\Lambda-Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax\|_{HS}\,+\,\|Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax-KU_{n}\Lambda\|_{HS}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\Phi(Z_{n})\,+\,\|Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax-KU_{n}\Lambda\|_{HS}\;.

Again, applying (
8
) to the second term in the last line we get

\|Y_{n}\mathchar 260\relax-KU_{n}\Lambda\|_{HS}\leq\Phi(KU_{n})
, and since
Z_{n}
is a minimizer
of
\Phi
it follows from (
5
) and (
3
) that

\|(K_{n}-K)U_{n}\Lambda\|_{HS}\,\leq\,2\,\Phi(KU_{n})\,=\,2\,\|Y_{n}-KX_{n}\|_{HS}\,\leq\,2\sqrt{n}\,\delta\,.

(13)

Accordingly, since the singular values in
\Lambda

can be much smaller than the noise level when some images
x_{k}
are
almost linearly dependent, the approximation
K_{n}\approx K
on
{\cal X}_{n}

can be arbitrarily bad in the noisy case. For example, if
u_{k}
is one of the singular functions of
X_{n}
and

\sigma_{k}
the associated singular value, cf. (
5
), then (
13
) gives

\|(K_{n}-K)u_{k}\|\,\leq\,2\frac{\sqrt{n}\,\delta}{\sigma_{k}}\,.

(14)

For a remedy out of this inherent difficulty one can resort, e.g., to using
a truncated singular value decomposition of
X_{n}
in (
11
),
cf.
[
2
,
4
]
.

Assume next that noisy data

y\,=\ Kx^{\dagger}\,+\,\eta

(15)

be given for some
x^{\dagger}\in{\cal X}
and some noise term
\eta
.
Even if
K
were known exactly,
the problem of determining stable approximations of
x^{\dagger}

is known to be ill-posed, and we therefore want to employ some kind of
regularization by projection.
In
[
1
]
two classical approaches of this sort, investigated in detail
in
[
3
, Section 3.3]
, have been discussed for the above setting.

Method I, known as least-squares projection, defines

x_{n}^{I}\,=\,(KP_{{\cal X}_{n}})^{\dagger}y\,,

(16)

respectively its approximation

x_{n}^{I}\,=\,K_{n}^{\dagger}y

(which is exact according to (
12
) in the case of noise-free data, provided that
x^{\dagger}\in{\cal X}_{n}
)
in terms of
K_{n}
of (
10
), where the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
K_{n}^{\dagger}
of
K_{n}
is
typically computed via the singular value decomposition of
K_{n}
.

The shortcoming of Method I is the fact that it is known
(Seidman
[
8
]
) that even for exact data pairs,
i.e., for
\delta=0
and
\eta=0
,
the least-squares projections
x_{n}^{I}
may fail to converge to
x^{\dagger}
for

n\to\infty
. On the positive side, sufficient conditions for convergence
have been stated in
[
1
]
.

In general the recommended way of regularizing by projection is Method II,
namely the dual least-squares method proposed by Natterer
[
7
]
.
In contrast to Method I it starts with a sequence of nested subspaces

{\cal Y}_{n}\subset{\cal Y}
. These can be
{\cal Y}_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}

(as suggested in
[
1
]
),
but it may be preferable to think of some other subspaces with suitable
approximation properties. Whatever the choice, Method II determines the
solution
x_{n}^{I\!I}
of minimal norm of the problem

\text{minimize}\ \|P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}\!Kx-w_{n}\|_{\cal Y}\,,\qquad w_{n}=P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}y\,,

(17)

where
P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}
is the orthogonal projection of
{\cal Y}
onto
{\cal Y}_{n}
.
As pointed out in
[
1
]
, however, in our setting

P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}\!K
is unknown on
{\cal X}\setminus{\cal X}_{n}
, and so is its null space,
and therefore the computation of the
solution of (
17
)
of minimal norm
is not possible
from the given data.

3
A Bayesian framework

In practice the given sequences
(x_{k})_{k}
and
(y_{k})_{k}
will often correspond
to a given set of training data, where the
x_{k}
are “plausible images”
and
y_{k}
are the associated data. In a Bayesian
framework one would argue that
(x_{k})_{k}
consists of samples from
a given probability distribution on
{\cal X}
. In the simplest case this
distribution is Gaussian, i.e.,

x\,\sim\,{\cal N}(x^{\circ},\mathchar 256\relax)

(18)

with mean
x^{\circ}\in{\cal X}
and covariance operator
\mathchar 256\relax\in{\cal L}({\cal X})
,
the latter being a self-adjoint positive semidefinite compact operator of
trace class. In this framework it is recommended to replace the
space
{\cal X}
by the affine subspace
x^{\circ}+{\cal E}
, where
{\cal E}
is the Cameron-Martin space
{\cal E}={\cal R}(\mathchar 256\relax^{1/2})\subset{\cal X}
, equipped with the inner product

\langle\,u,u^{\prime}\,\rangle_{\cal E}\,=\,\langle\,u,\mathchar 256\relax^{-1}u^{\prime}\,\rangle_{\cal X}\,,\qquad u,u^{\prime}\in{\cal E}\,,

cf. Stuart
[
9
, Section 6.3]
, and to approximate
x^{\dagger}\in x^{\circ}+{\cal E}
,
by minimizing the Tikhonov functional

\text{minimize}\ \|Kx-y\|^{2}_{\cal Y}\,+\,\alpha\|x-x^{\circ}\|^{2}_{\cal E}\,,

(19)

over
x\in x^{\circ}+{\cal E}
,
where the regularization parameter
\alpha
depends on the noise level
in the data according to (
3
) and (
15
). Note that if
K
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator then
the minimizer of (
19
) is the

maximum a posteriori estimation
(MAP) of
x^{\dagger}
under a Gaussian
white noise model for
\eta
and the assumed distribution of the images;
see
[
9
]
or Kaipio and Somersalo
[
5
]

for further details.

Without any further information on the probability distribution in
{\cal X}

it is natual to use the sample mean and sample covariances

x^{\circ}_{n}\,:=\,\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathchar 256\relax_{n}\,:=\,\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n})(x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n})^{*}

(20)

as approximations of
x^{\circ}
and
\mathchar 256\relax
,
respectively, where for
x\in{\cal X}
we let
x^{*}
from the dual space of
{\cal X}

be given by
x^{*}v\,=\,\langle\,x,v\,\rangle_{\cal X}
for
v\in{\cal X}
.
In other words, one makes the assumption that

x\,\sim\,{\cal N}(x^{\circ}_{n},\mathchar 256\relax_{n})\,,

and defines the Cameron-Martin space
{\cal E}_{n}
accordingly.
Note that if
p=\dim{\cal X}_{n}
, then
\mathchar 256\relax_{n}
is a positive semidefinite operator
with

p-1\,\leq\,p^{\prime}\,:=\,\operatorname{rank}\mathchar 256\relax_{n}\,\leq\,\min\{p,n-1\}\,,

(21)

cf. the appendix, and
x^{\circ}_{n}+{\cal E}_{n}
is a
p^{\prime}
-dimensional affine
subspace of
{\cal X}_{n}
: either this is
{\cal X}_{n}
itself, or it is an affine subspace
of
{\cal X}_{n}
of codimension one – depending on whether
p^{\prime}=p
or
p-1
,
respectively; compare Figure
1
. In particular, intuitively speaking,
every
x\in{\cal X}\setminus(x^{\circ}+{\cal E}_{n})
has “infinite”
{\cal E}_{n}
-norm.

Figure 1:
A sketch of the sets
{\cal X}_{n}
and
{\cal E}_{n}
for
p=n=2
and
p^{\prime}=1
.

Accordingly, in this Bayesian framework the dual least-squares method takes
the following form: Let

y^{\circ}_{n}\,=\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}\,,

which is close to
Kx^{\circ}_{n}
by virtue of (
3
),
and determine the element
u\in{\cal E}_{n}
of minimal
{\cal E}_{n}
-norm, which solves

\text{minimize}\ \|P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}K_{n}u-w_{n}\|_{\cal Y}\,,\qquad w_{n}\,=\,P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}(y-y^{\circ}_{n})\,.

(22)

Then return
x^{\circ}_{n}+u
as approximate solution of the inverse problem.
Note that (
22
) is similar to (
17
); it only differs in the definition
of
K_{n}
and the use of centered data. This approximation still depends on the choice of
{\cal Y}_{n}
;
we refer to it as Method III, if

{\cal Y}_{n}\,=\,\operatorname{span}\{y_{1}-y^{\circ}_{n},\dots,y_{n}-y^{\circ}_{n}\}\,,

and denote the corresponding approximation as
x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}
.

The numerical computation of
x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}
is similar to
the computation of
x_{n}^{I}
in Section
2
, and for this
reason we adopt the corresponding notation,
although the matrices are different from those in Section
2
.

1.

Let the columns of
X^{\circ}_{n}=[x^{\circ}_{n},\dots,x^{\circ}_{n}]\in{\cal X}_{n}^{n}

consist of
n
identical copies of the sample mean,
and compute the singular value decomposition

X_{n}-X^{\circ}_{n}\,=\,U_{n}\Lambda\mathchar 260\relax^{*}\quad\text{with}\quad U_{n}\in{\cal E}_{n}^{p^{\prime}},\;\Lambda\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}\times p^{\prime}},\;\mathchar 260\relax^{*}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}\times n}

of
X_{n}-X^{\circ}_{n}
.
Then the columns of
U_{n}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{p^{\prime}}]
contain the
eigenvectors of the covariance operator
\mathchar 256\relax_{n}
defined in (
20
) associated with its positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
,
the square roots of which are sitting on the diagonal of

\Lambda\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}\times p^{\prime}}
multiplied by
\sqrt{n}
.
This implies that if
x^{\circ}_{n}=0
then
\sigma_{k}=\sqrt{n\lambda_{k}}
is the singular value
which appears in (
14
).

Note that the columns of
\mathchar 260\relax
form an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal
complement of the null space of
X_{n}-X^{\circ}_{n}
, and hence, they are all
orthogonal to the vector of all ones, because

\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{k}-x_{n}^{\circ})\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\,-\,nx_{n}^{\circ}\,=\,0\,.

With this singular value decomposition it follows that

\|u\|_{{\cal E}_{n}}^{2}\,=\,\langle\,u,\mathchar 256\relax_{n}^{-1}u\,\rangle_{\cal X}\,=\,\begin{cases}n\,\|\Lambda^{-1}U_{n}^{*}u\|_{2}^{2}\,,&u\in{\cal E}_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{u_{1},\dots,u_{p^{\prime}}\}\,,\\[4.30554pt]
+\infty\,,&\text{else}\,,\end{cases}

i.e.,

J\,:\,{\cal E}_{n}\,\to\,{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}}\,,\qquad u\,\mapsto\,{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\,=\,\sqrt{n}\,\Lambda^{-1}U_{n}^{*}u\,,

(23)

is an isometry.

2.

Similar to Section
2
– compare with (
9
) and (
10
) – we consider now centered data
y_{i}-y^{\circ}_{n}
as input, let

Z_{n}\,=\,[y_{1}-y^{\circ}_{n},\dots,y_{n}-y^{\circ}_{n}]\,\mathchar 260\relax\Lambda^{-1}\,,

and define

K_{n}\,=\,Z_{n}U_{n}^{*}\,\approx\,KP_{{\cal E}_{n}}\,.

Note that here
K_{n}
is approximating the restriction of
K
to the Cameron-Martin space, while in (
10
) it has been the restriction of
K
to
{\cal X}_{n}
.

One advantage of this framework is that here we can avoid computing
K_{n}
or
Z_{n}

and rather evaluate

L_{n}\,=\,[y_{1}-y^{\circ}_{n},\dots,y_{n}-y^{\circ}_{n}]\,\mathchar 260\relax

instead. Take note that by virtue of (
23
), and because
U_{n}
is orthogonal,

L_{n}\,=\,Z_{n}\Lambda\,=\,K_{n}U_{n}\Lambda\,=\,\sqrt{n}\,K_{n}J^{-1}\,.

(24)

3.

Accordingly, in the noisy case the least squares problem (
22
) becomes

\text{minimize}\ \|K_{n}u-w_{n}\|_{\cal Y}\,=\,\|K_{n}J^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}-w_{n}\|_{\cal Y}\,,\qquad w_{n}\,=\,P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}(y-y^{\circ}_{n})\,,

over
u\in{\cal E}_{n}
, respectively
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}}
, in such a way that
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=Ju

has minimal Euclidean norm.
The solution of this minimization problem is
given by
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}=L_{n}^{\dagger}(\sqrt{n}\,w_{n})
, and the corresponding approximation
of
x^{\dagger}
is

x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}\,=\,x^{\circ}_{n}\,+\,U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}(y-y^{\circ}_{n})\,.

(25)

The computation of
x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}
requires the singular value decomposition
of
L_{n}
.

If we want to compute the MAP estimate (
19
) of
x^{\dagger}

instead, then we have to replace
L_{n}^{\dagger}
by
(L_{n}^{*}L_{n}+\alpha I)^{-1}L_{n}^{*}

in (
25
). Again, the evaluation of this estimate simplifies when the
singular value decomposition of
L_{n}
is computed first.

It is a nice feature of Method III that it circumvents the inversion of the
singular values in
\Lambda
which was necessary in the evaluation
of
K_{n}
in (
10
) for computing
x_{n}^{I}
. Thus the computation of
x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}

is more stable in the presence of clustered images.

A basic error estimate

{\cal X}

K_{n}x=P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}y

Note that when
x^{\circ}_{n}=0
and
y^{\circ}_{n}=0
then

{\cal E}_{n}={\cal X}_{n}
, but their norms are different,
and Method III is the least-squares
projection method using the Cameron-Martin norm.
If
K_{n}|_{{\cal X}_{n}}
is injective, then Method I and
Method III coincide, cf. Lemma
4.1

below: they both determine the unique
solution of
Kx=P_{{\cal Y}_{n}}y
in
{\cal X}_{n}
.
In particular, this implies that the aforementioned Seidman example
is also relevant for Method III.
If
K_{n}|_{{\cal X}_{n}}
fails to be injective then the
methods are different, because they return the
respective solutions of minimal norm;
see Figure
2
.

Finally, the reason that the connection to the dual least squares method is somewhat
weaker, is due to the fact that
x^{\circ}_{n}+{\cal E}_{n}
varies
with
n
, and does not belong to the orthogonal complement of the null space of
K
,
in general.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
U_{n}
,
\Lambda
,
K_{n}
and
L_{n}
be defined as in
Section
3
, and let
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}
be injective.
Then
K_{n}^{\dagger}=U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}
.

Proof 1
.

We show that
A=U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}
satisfies all four defining equations
for the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse, listed, e.g.,
in
[
3
, Proposition 2.3]
.
We start by noting that it follows from
(
24
)
that

K_{n}A\,=\,K_{n}U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}\,=\,L_{n}L_{n}^{\dagger}\,=\,P_{{\cal R}(L_{n})}\,=\,P_{{\cal R}(K_{n})}\,,

(26)

because the range spaces
{\cal R}(L_{n})
and
{\cal R}(K_{n})
of
L_{n}
and
K_{n}

coincide. Accordingly, we have

AK_{n}A\,=\,U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}P_{{\cal R}(L_{n})}\,=\,U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}\,=\,A\,,

and

K_{n}AK_{n}\,=\,P_{{\cal R}(K_{n})}K_{n}\,=\,K_{n}\,.

Note that these three identities hold independent of whether
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}

is injective, or not. We only need this assumption to prove the remaining
identity. In fact, due to this assumption the range of
L_{n}
has dimension

p^{\prime}
, and hence,
L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n}
is the identity in
{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{p^{\prime}}
.
It therefore follows from
(
24
)
and
(
23
)
that

AK_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,U_{n}\Lambda L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n}J\,=\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,U_{n}\Lambda J\,=\,J^{-1}J

is the identity in
{\cal E}_{n}
, whereas
AK_{n}x\,=\,0
for every
x\in{\cal E}_{n}^{\bot}
.
This establishes that

AK_{n}\,=\,P_{{\cal E}_{n}}\,.

Accordingly,
A
is the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse of
K_{n}
.

The assumption that
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}
is injective is not a very restrictive one.
For example, this is likely to be the case when
K:{\cal X}\to{\cal Y}
is known to be
injective. Also, if the range of
[y_{1}-y^{\circ}_{n},\dots,y_{n}-y^{\circ}_{n}]
has
the largest possible dimension
n-1
, so that its null space is spanned by

[1,\dots,1]^{T}
, then the dimension of
{\cal R}(L_{n})={\cal R}(K_{n})
is
p^{\prime}
,
and again,
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}
is injective.
This second case occurs with high probability if the noise components in
the data
y_{k}
are uncorrelated random variables.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
y=Kx^{\dagger}
for some
x^{\dagger}\in{\cal X}
, and assume that
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}

is injective. Furthermore, let
\mu_{n}
be the smallest singular value of
K_{n}
.
Then

x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}-x^{\dagger}\,=\,(I-P_{{\cal E}_{n}})(x^{\circ}_{n}-x^{\dagger})\,+\,d_{n}\,,

(27)

where

\|d_{n}\|_{\cal X}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}\,\Bigl(\|(K-K_{n})(x^{\circ}_{n}-x^{\dagger})\|_{\cal Y}\,+\,\|y^{\circ}_{n}-Kx^{\circ}_{n})\|_{\cal Y}\Bigr)\,.

Proof 2
.

According to
(
25
)
and Lemma
4.1
there holds

x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}-x^{\circ}_{n}\,=\,K_{n}^{\dagger}K_{n}(x^{\dagger}-x^{\circ}_{n})\,+\,K_{n}^{\dagger}r_{n}\,,

where

r_{n}\,=\,y-y^{\circ}_{n}-K_{n}(x^{\dagger}-x^{\circ}_{n})\,=\,(K-K_{n})(x^{\dagger}-x^{\circ}_{n})\,+\,Kx^{\circ}_{n}-y^{\circ}_{n}\,.

(28)

Since
K_{n}^{\dagger}K_{n}=P_{{\cal E}_{n}}
because of the injectivity of
K_{n}|_{{\cal E}_{n}}
,
we thus obtain

x_{n}^{I\!I\!I}\,=\,P_{{\cal E}_{n}}x^{\dagger}\,+\,(I-P_{{\cal E}_{n}})x^{\circ}_{n}\,+\,d_{n}

with

d_{n}\,=\,K_{n}^{\dagger}r_{n}\,.

(29)

From this we readily conclude the error representation
(
27
)
,
where the estimate of
\|d_{n}\|_{\cal X}
follows from
(
28
)
and the fact, that

\|K_{n}^{\dagger}\|_{{\cal Y}\to{\cal X}}=1/\mu_{n}
.

The error representation (
27
) with
d_{n}
of (
29
) extends
the corresponding formula for the least-squares projection as stated, e.g.,
by Kaltenbacher and Offtermatt
[
6
, (2.9)]
.

Appendix A
On the rank of the sample covariance matrix

Here we discuss the sharpness of the inequalities in (
21
)
concerning the rank
p^{\prime}
of the sample covariance operator
\mathchar 256\relax_{n}

and the dimension
p
of
{\cal X}_{n}
.

Let
u\in{\cal X}
be any vector. Then it follows that

\langle\,u,\mathchar 256\relax_{n}u\,\rangle_{\cal X}\,=\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl|\langle\,x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n},u\,\rangle_{\cal X}\bigr|^{2}\,\geq\,0\,,

with equality if and only if
u\bot\operatorname{span}\{x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n}\}
. This means
that

p^{\prime}\,=\,\dim\bigl(\operatorname{span}\{x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n}\}\bigr)\,.

Since this span is a subset of
{\cal X}_{n}
we obviously have
p^{\prime}\leq p
.
Further, the set
\{x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n}\}
is always linearly dependent,
because these vectors add up to zero; hence
p^{\prime}\leq n-1
.
On the other hand, since

\operatorname{span}\bigl(\{x_{k}-x^{\circ}_{n}\}\,\cup\,\{x^{\circ}_{n}\}\bigr)

is the entire subspace
{\cal X}_{n}
, we necessarily have
p^{\prime}\geq p-1
.

In particular, if
p=n
, then
p^{\prime}=n-1
.

Example A.1
.

Let

x_{1}\,=\,\left[\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{*\c@MaxMatrixCols c}1\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right]\,,\quad x_{2}\,=\,\left[\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{*\c@MaxMatrixCols c}0\\
1\\
0\end{array}\right]\,,\quad\text{and}\quad x_{3}=5x_{2}-4x_{1}\,.

In this example we have
p=2
and
x^{\circ}_{3}=2x_{2}-x_{1}
.
It follows that
x_{1}-x^{\circ}_{3}\,=\,2(x_{1}-x_{2})
,

x_{2}-x^{\circ}_{3}\,=\,x_{1}-x_{2}
, and
x_{3}-x^{\circ}_{3}\,=\,3(x_{2}-x_{1})
are all
collinear, hence
p^{\prime}=1=p-1
.
This example is similar to the one which is displayed in Figure
1
, except that here all the three points
x_{k}
are lying in the same one-dimensional affine subspace

\diamond

Note that we always have
p^{\prime}=p
, when the sample mean vanishes. But this is
not the only possibility:

Example A.2
.

Let

x_{1}\,=\,\left[\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{*\c@MaxMatrixCols c}1\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right]\,,\quad x_{2}\,=\,\left[\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{*\c@MaxMatrixCols c}0\\
1\\
0\end{array}\right]\,,\quad\text{and}\quad x_{3}=2(x_{2}+x_{1})\,.

In this example we have
p=2
again. This time
x^{\circ}_{3}=x_{2}+x_{1}
, so that

x_{1}-x^{\circ}_{3}\,=\,-x_{2}
and
x_{2}-x^{\circ}_{3}=-x_{1}

are linearly independent. Accordingly, in this example we have
p^{\prime}=p=2
.

\diamond
