Application of chitosan-based nanogels for dermal and transdermal delivery systems
Authors: Ahmadreza Soroush Fard, Zahra Sabouri, Majid Darroudi, Roghaye Arezumand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We review the utilization of chitosan-based nanogels as advanced vehicles for dermal drug delivery. Dermal delivery allows local treatment with a simple application for the patient, while also providing an alternative route that avoids gastrointestinal degradation and first-pass metabolism to help reduce doses and systemic side effects. Nanogels are nanoscale hydrogel networks that preserve the drug-loading capacity and tunable release characteristics of hydrogels, while retaining the colloidal stability of nanoparticles. The addition of chitosan into nanogels provides bioadhesive and permeability-enhancing characteristics; for example, chitosan’s positive charge promotes adhesion to negatively charged skin and can transiently open tight junctions to improve uptake of drugs. Chitosan nanogels exhibit higher drug encapsulation efficiency and more controlled release than conventional topical or bulk gels, and improved penetration into biological membranes due to their small size and surface charge. Chitosan nanogels can be prepared by various methods, including via ionic gelation (electrostatic crosslinking), emulsification (with emulsion polymerization), self-assembly, and radical (covalent) polymerization. These systems have been investigated in dermatology, skin care, cosmetics, and dermal cancer therapy. Overall, chitosan nanogels represent a unique and versatile platform in which mucoadhesion, biocompatibility, and permeation enhancement can collectively improve the efficacy and safety of topical and transdermal therapies.

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ in the body and provides an excellent protective barrier, thereby limiting the absorption of drugs and pathogens, and foreign substances [1, 2]. The stratum corneum (SC) is the outermost layer of the epidermis that provides the primary barrier function. It is composed of dead keratin-filled cells, which are embedded in a lipid-rich matrix to protect against external substances [3]. Delivering therapeutics through the skin (i.e., dermal or transdermal administration) can be very beneficial. Topically delivered drugs avoid gastrointestinal degradation and first-pass metabolism, enabling them to act directly at the target site [1, 4, 5]. Dermal therapy can provide fast local drug action with large local bioavailability (for example, for wound healing or the treatment of skin infections). Many drugs exhibit low efficiency because they are lost by dissolution before reaching their desired target sites. Nanogels offer controlled and targeted drug release, enhance drug permeation, and minimize toxicity to healthy cells [1, 4, 6]. Traditional topical formulations (ointments, creams, gels, films…) are acceptable, but they are often limited in skin penetration and their ability to control release. For traditional designs, nanoscale carriers have partially alleviated the limitations of skin delivery. Nanoscale formulations-particularly nanogel systems-have superior dermal delivery based on their small size and modified performance parameters; they permit enhanced skin permeation and sustained release in comparison with traditional preparations [1, 7, 8]. Nanogels are either chemically or physically polymer networks. They have hydrogel properties (water content and biocompatibility) and nanoparticle properties (small size and customizable surface characteristics). Nanogels can absorb and retain large amounts of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic actives: large volumes of water, biological fluids, and a diverse range of therapeutics, including small molecules, proteins, and DNA/RNA, while maintaining structural integrity [9, 10]. Their nanoscale sizes provide relatively high surface area and substantial internal space, establishing drug stability and circulation times within the body. These systems effectively protect the payload until it reaches the specific target tissue or organ. Controlled or sustained drug release occurs in response to a specific reaction or bond cleavage within the nanogel [1, 9]. In dermal applications, the nanoscale and rheological behavior of nanogels all favor skin retention: they typically form a thin and spreadable film on skin and resist wash-off. In general, nanogel formulations often provide improved stability and longer drug release with lower risk of skin irritation compared to unstructured (bulk) gels [1, 11]. Another interesting feature is stimuli-responsive nanogels (e.g., pH- or thermo-sensitive), which can release drugs or provide on-demand controlled release [10, 12].

Chitosan nanogels: comparative advantages

Chitosan nanogels possess a three-dimensional network with high internal volume and surface area, allowing exceptional drug-loading capacity compared to traditional gels. Their superior swelling ability further enhances water and drug absorption [10, 13]. Drug loading can occur via physical entrapment, covalent attachment, or self-assembly through polyelectrolyte interactions, depending on polymer composition, molecular weight, and functional groups [14]. Due to their small size (<200 nm), chitosan nanogels can penetrate intercellular spaces and accumulate within hair follicles, enhancing drug diffusion into deeper skin layers [6, 15]. They can distribute through both transcellular and paracellular pathways, resulting in improved epidermal and dermal deposition. Chitosan (CS) is a biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic biopolymer widely used in biomedical and pharmaceutical applications [16–18]. N-acylated chitosan functions as an efficient carrier and sustained-release agent [19]. Chitosan’s innate antimicrobial activity, mucoadhesiveness, and solubility in acidic aqueous media make it particularly suitable for topical nanogels [1, 4, 20]. Its positive charge under neutral-to-acidic pH enables strong interactions with negatively charged skin components such as keratin and mucin, promoting tighter adhesion and enhanced drug permeation by loosening the stratum corneum structure [1, 21]. Chitosan’s degree of deacetylation and molecular weight significantly affect its permeation-enhancing ability, with higher values showing greater efficacy [4]. Its film-forming capacity creates a breathable protective barrier that retains moisture, prevents transepidermal water loss, and shields the skin from environmental irritants, thereby improving barrier function [22–25]. In topical formulations, chitosan-based nanogels demonstrate excellent safety and tolerability, producing minimal irritation compared with systems using harsh surfactants or solvents. Studies such as those on Mirtazapine-loaded chitosan hydrogels and levofloxacin chitosan systems confirmed their non-irritant nature and effective sustained delivery [1, 4, 26–29]. By adjusting polymer composition and cross-linking density, chitosan nanogels can be tailored to provide controlled and prolonged drug release, maintaining therapeutic levels for extended durations [1, 10].

Overall, chitosan’s multifunctional properties, biocompatibility, mucoadhesiveness, film formation, and controlled-release behavior make it an ideal polymer for designing advanced drug delivery systems [30–33]. The stratum corneum (SC), the outermost layer of the skin, acts as a key barrier to transdermal drug delivery. Transdermal nanogels are being explored for treating various conditions such as eczema, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, depression, cardiac disorders, and skin cancer [6]. Recently, increasing interest has been directed toward localized drug-delivery systems that sustain therapeutic levels at targeted sites, such as the periodontal region, while minimizing systemic effects. Sustained-release formulations enable lower dosages with prolonged and localized therapeutic action [34]. Chitosan nanoparticles exist as natural biodegradable substances that offer better safety than synthetic cationic dendrimers, which show potential cytotoxic effects. Chitosan nanoparticles create a stable solid structure, which differs from liposomes because their phospholipid bilayer structure causes instability and early drug release. Dendrimers provide perfect monodispersity and extensive functionalization potential, but their manufacturing process stands in direct opposition to the straightforward, cost-effective production of chitosan. Chitosan provides two main benefits through its natural ability to stick to mucous membranes and its capacity to temporarily open tight junctions. This improves mucosal permeability, a characteristic that SLNs and polymeric micelles do not possess. The physical stability of chitosan against polymorphic transitions exists as a strength that SLNs sometimes lack. The main distinction between chitosan and PLGA nanoparticles as drug carriers emerges from chitosan’s natural antimicrobial effects, which synthetic PLGA nanoparticles do not possess. The main drawback of chitosan emerges from its poor solubility at physiological pH, but liposomes and dendrimers maintain stable performance throughout various pH levels, which demonstrates the trade-off between natural and synthetic materials.

Synthesis methods

Ionic gelation

Ionic gelation employs polyelectrolytes and ions as a quick and easy process of NG formation. Ionic gelation is based on the attraction of oppositely charged molecules. In general, ionic gelation is performed by preparing ionic geometries from ionizable polymers such as chitosan, alginate, hyaluronic acid, or poly (glutamic acid). When ionic gelation occurs in aqueous environments, polymer chains are physically crosslinked with in situ changes in the properties of the polymer, such as pH, temperature, or ionic strength. In terms of pH, notable shifts can drive ionic bond formation between polymer chains [34]. Chitosan has unique properties that allow it to participate in ionic gelation. Under acidic conditions, typically when <pH 6, chitosan is a cation and readily engages in electrostatic interactions with negatively charged species. Chitosan has a pKa that ranges from 6.3 to 7.2, depending on acetylation, and consequently, can readily give rise to charge density and therefore reactivity. In the case of ionotropic gelation, chitosan acts as a polycation and crosslinks with multivalent anions such as tripolyphosphate (TPP), pyrophosphate (PPI), or natural polyanions, such as hyaluronate or alginate. Crosslinked networks can be formed, resulting from chitosan’s interactions with the anions that can also allow for crosslinked networks at the micro- or nanoscale, producing colloidal carriers or particles. The size, morphology, and uniformity of the crosslinked particles can appear to be controlled by mixing techniques and the indistinguishable stoichiometry between chitosan and the anionic cross-linkers to produce reproducible particles (38). Limited mechanical stability and the difficulty in generating homogenous macrogols are some limitations of chitosan, which have impeded its application and use. New methods have been developed to overcome these limitations. For example, efficient three-dimensional chitosan networks can be made with 6-phosphogluconic trisodium salt, which yield biocompatible, non-toxic hydrogels suitable for applications on the skin. Studies have also reported ionic gels with mannuronan oligomers and metal-based polyanions such as molybdates in an acidic environment. These methods indicate that there is a diversity of chitosan-based ionic gelation systems that can result in customized structures for biomedical applications, especially in topical delivery systems and wound healing [13].

Emulsion-based polymerization

The microemulsion method is a widely accepted method of CNPs synthesis, providing high control over particle size and uniformity [14]. Microemulsions restrict polymerization to inside droplets; there is a direct relationship between droplet size and the eventual particle size. In traditional emulsion polymerization, both direct emulsion polymerization (oil-in-water, O/W) and inverse emulsion polymerization (water-in-oil, W/O), the droplet is the smallest unit. In direct emulsion, the dispersed phase consists of organics containing reactive monomers in water, while in inverse emulsion, the dispersed phase consists of water droplets in an organic medium. Nanogel formulation uses monomers, initiators, catalysts, and crosslinking agents to form the polymers, then continues to undergo three steps: nucleation, growth of the nanoparticles, and polymerization. In one method, all of the reagents used to make a given nanogel are initially in the dispersed phase, while the photo-initiators stimulate the reaction to take place. The other method consists of having all the monomers between both phases, the initiator in the continuous phase, and the crosslinking agents/catalysts remaining in the dispersed phase. In both methods, in many cases, thermal initiators are employed when possible, or they use radiation as an initiation process (i.e., water radiolysis. Once the initiators (i.e., free radicals) are generated, the polymerization occurs by diffusion and reaction within the droplets. When the emulsion is finally formed, surfactants or the hydrophilic-lipophilic balance of the initiators aid in stabilization of the emulsion while promoting a nanonetwork [15].

Díaz-Zepeda et al. created Oil-in-water microemulsions (O/W MEs) and coated the microemulsions in chitosan to form advanced curcumin delivery systems. Ternary phase diagrams were used to optimize emulsions made up of Tween 20, Tween 80, ethanol, and monopropylene glycol (MPG). The curcumin release from chitosan-coated MEs was maximized at pH 5.8, with 58% of curcumin released. The release kinetics were found to adhere to the Korsmeyer–Peppas model and to have a Fickian diffusion mechanism. This reveals the pH-responsive ability for size-controlled curcumin delivery via chitosan-coated Mes [16]. Farzaneh et al. prepared a PBA-CS-based nanogel from an o/w emulsification method and cross-linked the nanogel with UV light, and they found effective drug loading and biocompatibility to deliver drugs via the ocular route. This example also demonstrates the versatility of emulsification-based methods to customize nanogels according to therapeutic requirements and to tailor nanogels [17]. Kafshgari et al. bioencapsulated bovine serum albumin (BSA)-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (CNPs)-based hydrogel through the same methods and, similar to the other study, reported greater amounts of protein release with a lower chitosan concentration. Overall, we suggest that the reverse micellar method is very versatile with finely tunable nanoparticle characteristics and wide applicability in drug and gene delivery systems [14]. Self-assembly

How chitosan self-assembles depends predominantly on the unique molecular structure, including cationic methylamino, hydrophobic, and hydrogen bonding functionalities at its molecular weight, hydrophobicity, and degree of acetylation (DA) level. DA is significant in self-assembly; in terms of hydrophobicity, a high DA slows down hydrophobicity, and the charge density to taller ratio causes hydrophobicity to increase, yet charge density to decrease decreases electrostatic interactions, leading to inadequate assembly/structure stabilization. Chemical modification also allows for controls in chitosan self-assembly, where new chemical composition and functionalities can be introduced with hydrophobic modifiers (e.g., lipids, alkyls, cholesterol) or hydrophilic modifiers (e.g., glycol), subsequently adjusting self-assembly in a new direction. In regard to the degree of substitution, as well as the molecular structure and nature of the modifiers (e.g., molecular weight), electrostatic modifications introduce systematic controls in terms of surface charge; specifically, ions can be added with carboxymethyl or trimethyl groups. Electrostatic assemblies are classified and stable measures of electrostatic complexation assembly when regard to pressures (charge density, degree of ionization, distribution of surface charge). Depending on the stimulus, chitosan self-assembles due to conformational flexibility of molecularly based nano-structure formation, and structural stiffening may regulate ordered assemblies. Effective structural adjustments may subsequently lead to high-performance, designed self-assembled structures for biomedical applications. Chitosan and its derivatives will self-assemble in one of two approaches, which fall under monocomponent or multicomponent systems. In monocomponent systems, chitosan alone will self-assemble, and for the most part, is driven by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. In multicomponent systems, self-assembly occurs when chitosan interacts with other molecules; this occurs primarily through electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonding. Although van der Waals forces are being employed, they are not of primary importance. Hydrophobic interactions are dependent on the arrangement of water molecules since non-polar groups aggregate to decrease water contact [18].

Nanogels (NGs) can be created by electrostatic interactions between charged polymers. A self-assembly method has the added benefit of limiting the use of organic solvents or surfactants. By being able to limit the use of organic solvents, you can reduce the amount of harmful residue in your end product, which ensures the safety of the nanogel. Chitosan (CS) is a naturally occurring polysaccharide that can electrostatically polymerize with negatively charged macromolecules such as acids or even albumin [17]. Alina et al. developed a self-assembled nanogel system utilizing maleic anhydride functionalized chitosan and polyaspartic acid (PAS) to encapsulate and control the release of Amoxicillin (Amox) in order to increase the bioavailability. In this process, Amox is dissolved in MAC5 (a chitosan derivative) and mixed with PAS at room temperature to allow for the NGs to form thermodynamically stable NGs. This simple and efficient method provides good demonstrated results and offers a short and simple pathway to controlled drug delivery methods [19]. In a different study, silver nanoclusters were synthesized at elevated temperatures by condensing thiolated Ag (I) complexes on an in situ-generated silver core using glutathione (GSH) as the reducing and stabilizing agent. The interaction of Ag and thiol binding groups led to the spontaneous formation of a GSH monolayer and bilayer shell around the Ag core. To directly incorporate Ag NCs into a chitosan matrix, the reaction pH was adjusted to pH 6.5, to both protonate chitosan’s amino groups while also stabilizing the GSH carboxylates. As such, at this pH, highly charged GSH carboxylates and cationic amines of chitosan interact through strong electrostatic adhesion to further yield nanoscale Ag NC-Chitosan hydrogels [20]. In another study, Xu et al. prepared an optimized nanogel (OVM-COS) with chitosan (COS) to encapsulate LUT to improve LUT bioavailability. The prepared OVM-COS-LUT nanogel appeared to be spherical with a smaller size and a narrow size distribution. Results indicated that LUT appeared well encapsulated in a nanogel, which retained an amorphous form due to the effects of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, or other interactions. Furthermore, the conditions increased LUT solubility and dispersion and improved antioxidant capacity, thus bioavailability [22]. Radical polymerization

The process of free radical polymerization (FRP) relies on the generation of reactive radicals, typically through the thermal decomposition of initiators, to sustain the chain-growth polymerization of polymers such as polystyrene or polyvinyl acetate. Unlike the use of catalysts in traditional reaction processes, FRP techniques utilize radical initiators to maintain the polymerization of the growing chain as long as the monomer is available in sufficient quantities. Atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) allows controlled polymerization through the reversible activation and deactivation of the radicals produced, resulting in well-defined polymer structures that can be more readily modified with various functionalization procedures. The fundamental mechanism of radical polymerization can be broken into four main processes, which include initiation, propagation, termination, and chain transfer. With ionic polymerization, either anionic or cationic, the active centers are charged, and the ionic polymerization is limited by the electronic nature of the monomer. Ionic polymerization precursors require very high levels of purity and are sensitive to the polarity of any solvent used. All of these processes provide a way to produce polymers with tailored physical and/or chemical properties [35]. pH- or temperature-responsive nanogels (NGs) can be formed using radical polymerization methods, and smart polymers – poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) is an example of a smart polymer. Since it undergoes a volume phase transition at 32.1 °C, it can be used to form nanoparticles that are both pH- and temperature-responsive. Polymers like PNIPAM become more water soluble below 32.1 °C due to hydrogen bonding that water forms with the polymer, and thus can be used to form copolymers with minerals like chitosan to create thermosensitive nanogels that can enhance the solubility of hydrophobic drugs such as efavirenz. Poly (N-vinyl caprolactam) (PNVCL) is also a thermo-responsive polymer that transitions around 32 °C and is able to be tunable when copolymerized. Chitosan modifies this transition as seen in the development of heat-responsive nanogels encapsulated with BSA using chitosan. In the case of some nanogels, PTVCL has been copolymerized with chitosan with incorporated Fe₃O₄ to produce magnetic carriers that are responsive to both pH- and temperature-conditions, conditions suitable for drug delivery of proteins around 37 °C [17]. A separate study, Ghaem et al. prepared acryoic acid-N-isopropylacrylamide (A-N) nanogel/chitosan-acetic acid (CTS-A) hydrogel using conventional free radical polymerization. The system was initiated by ammonium persulfate (APS) at 80 °C under reflux. APS produced sulfate and hydroxyl radicals to activate the monomers, acrylamide (AA), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM), and N, N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) in the presence of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for (A-N) nanogels. When hydroxyl radicals synthesized from APS reacted with chitosan and produced chitosan radicals, the AA was polymerized, and the first crosslinked copolymer was produced. The (A-N) nanogel powder was added, and the nanogel was entrapped in the hydrogel crosslinked network. Free radical polymerization was employed to prepare chitosan nanogels. Chitosan (Cs) was first dissolved in a 1% acetic acid solution. Ammonium persulfate was incorporated into the Cs solution to induce free radical polymerization. Methacrylic acid (MA) was then added to the Cs+APS solution, followed by N’, N’- methylene bis(acrylamide) (MBA). The three components were ultimately dissolved in a water/ethanol mixture at 50 °C. The mixture was stirred until a clear solution was produced, before sparging with nitrogen to deoxygenate the solution. The solution was cast into molds and polymerized in the oven at an elevated temperature of 70 °C for 7 h [30]. Application of chitosan-based nanogel

Wound healing

CS utilizes several well-studied mechanisms to assist in wound healing, which include: modulation of inflammation, promotion of hemostatic response, scavenging of free radicals, and providing antibacterial properties [31, 32]. CS’s unique physicochemical properties and polycationic nature allow for the promotion of hemostasis around the wound site through a variety of routes. For example, when exposed to the positively charged CS, negatively charged erythrocytes, platelets, and plasma proteins (such as fibrinogen) can all aggregate. All of these interactions have the potential to act in concert to support hemostasis and blood aggregation [26, 31]. CS employs its antibacterial mechanisms to avoid bacterial invasions by resting on interactions between the polycationic polysaccharide chain and bacterial cells’ negatively charged membrane components. To a lesser extent, its antibacterial properties are also partially mediated through its ability to chelate small amounts of metal ions on the membrane of the bacterial cells [36]. Numerous amino acids have a positive charge at acidic pH, which contributes to the excellent antimicrobial activity of chitosan coats, along with effective lysis of bacterial cells. Additionally, overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) around the wound site may cause indirect damage to the surrounding tissue by continuously stimulating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines or directly harmful effects by altering tissue, such as lipid peroxidation and modifications of DNA [23, 24, 33]. While CS has been described to have moderate free radical scavenging ability, the mechanism of free radical scavenging of CS works by reducing ROS molecules by donating a hydrogen atom. In this reaction, the amino groups on the polymer chain and hydroxyl groups are contributors to the free radical scavenging [25, 27]. Injurious side effects of traditional wound healing techniques, such as tissue grafting, antibiotics, and proteolytic enzymes, are associated with heightened disadvantages, for example, invasiveness and cost [28]. Thus, there is a need to find a good target for wound healing with anti-inflammatory properties. PDE4 (phosphodiesterase 4) inhibitors are the new nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs being studied for the treatment of skin disease. Almost all inflammatory cells express this isoform, which regulates inflammatory cytokine production and intracellular cAMP levels in these cells [29]. PDE4 inhibitors modulate a range of inflammatory responses by increasing cAMP levels [37]. Crisaborole (CRB), a PDE4 inhibitor, is employed for the treatment of mild to moderate atopic dermatitis [38]. In one study, the CRB-loaded nanoemulsion (CRB-NE1) encapsulated into a chitosan nanogel exhibited significant anti-inflammatory and wound healing properties with a high rate of tissue regeneration [39]. Recent studies indicate that reducing the number of bacteria in a wound facilitates the treatment of the infection. The best method of treating wound infections is the use of antibiotics directly onto the skin. There are so many wound types that there have been many variations in bandage design to make it possible to delicately apply healing materials on the spot that it is needed (12). Silver sulfadiazine (SSD), an antibiotic, has broad-spectrum activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and other microorganisms. SSD has been utilized frequently for treating burn wound infections. The traditional antibacterial cream has inaccurate dosages of medication. SSD cream has to be applied several times daily, and the non-biodegradable cream is removed before each application, which adds discomfort and trauma to the patient. Additionally, creams typically do not provide controlled topical release of SSD, therefore preventing the concentration in serum from exceeding toxic levels. Polymeric SSD nanogel could be a reasonable alternative to mitigate the limitations of the cream, delivering SSD at lower application frequency (64). In another study, various SSD-loaded nanogel were prepared. In the end, the optimized formulation has a better therapeutic efficacy in vivo in comparison to the marketed product [40].

Both Nigella sativa oil and atorvastatin exhibit a range of pharmacological effects that are helpful in wound healing, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and immunomodulatory activities. Topical application of a thymoquinone-rich fraction from N. sativa extract was shown to reduce nuclear factor kappa B (NF-jB) signaling and inhibit 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA) induced cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression. This fraction also influenced mouse skin to express glutathione S-transferase (GST) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). In a study, Bagheri F et al. formulated an NG based on chitosan–carboxymethyl cellulose (CS–CMC) loaded with N. sativa oil, to utilize its beneficial effects related to skin penetration, and was then made into an atorvastatin-loaded ONG; reported results confirmed fibroblast migration and showed to proliferate positively in wound healing processes [41]. Several studies illustrated the effect that IL-2 could have on wound healing. By promoting the release of lymphocytes as well as interferon γ, IL-2 may influence wound healing. Providing the target tissue with the correct amount of IL-2 release would allow a proper treatment; thus, it is important to develop an effective delivery system. Nanogel systems seem to be a good system for delivering drugs. In another study, they developed rh-IL-2-loaded chitosan-based nanogels. The study found it to be an appropriate system for promoting wound healing because it reduced the malondialdehyde (MDA) and increased the glutathione GSH levels in wound tissues, when compared to the control group [42]. Topical corticosteroids that act on numerous pathways of the inflammatory cascade are presently the primary treatment for skin diseases. That said, topical corticosteroids have limitations to their clinical application due to local dose-dependent side effects such as skin atrophy, redness (telangiectasia), acne, and secondary infection. Spantide II (SP), a neurokinin 1 (NK1) receptor antagonist, also contains anti-inflammatory properties along with traditional topical application. Both Spantide II + ketoprofen were able to accelerate skin repair by a topical formulation with a potent NSAID (ketoprofen). The study of Shah PP and Colleagues indicated the nanogel with surface-modified nanoparticles (NPSO) was able to significantly enhance the skin permeation of spantide II and ketoprofen by also increasing skin contact time, skin hydration, and allowing nanoparticles to more easily traverse into deeper layers of the skin with the aid of a thin continuous surface film to facilitate penetration [43]. Trinitroglycerin (TNG), with angiogenic, antimicrobial properties, and oxidation properties, is also utilized in wound healing. However, the systemic administration of trinitroglycerin (TNG) drug-saturated NO is limited due to drawbacks such as high rates of mitochondrial superoxide production, endothelial dysfunction, peroxynitrite production, and the short half-life of NO. More recently, to address these limitations, a recent study introduced a new topical TNG nanoformulations and showed that TNG nanoformulations accelerated wound healing via collagen deposition and angiogenesis and reducing inflammation. It was suggested that TNG-Ngs would robustly vascularize in the wound area for the region and they would be more efficacious on a topical application [44]. Methotrexate (MTX) is an effective psoriasis treatment, although patient compliance is low due to systemic toxicity with oral dosing. Topical MTX decreases toxic effects, but the ability to penetrate the skin is poor due to the hydrophilicity of the drug and the characteristics of psoriatic plaques. Antimicrobial acid (ALA) photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) is a non-invasive and FDA-approved treatment, although ALA produces a poor response due to its hydrophilicity. The use of MTX-ALA PDT as a combination therapy may allow for more potent and effective treatment of psoriasis, which could enhance drug delivery and treatment efficacy. In a study, MTX-ALA NGs effectively reduced the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-17A in psoriatic mice. Whereas topical application of MTX-ALA NGs to mice showed no hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity [45]. Cancer treatment

Chitosan-based nanogels (CS-NGs) have been investigated extensively in relation to clinical use due to their biodegradability, biocompatibility, and high drug loading efficiency. Surface modifications of nanogels can further enhance the penetration and transport of drugs, without toxicity. Penetration enhancers are used in conjunction with natural or synthetic compounds in order to maximize efficacy for the topical delivery of drugs, while maintaining skin homeostasis. They should ideally be biocompatible, non-toxic, and used in small amounts in use. The enhancers can also function as depots for the drug, providing for a more gradual release. Lipophilic fatty acids, including maleic, oleic, and linoleic acid, for example, have been shown as effective penetration enhancers in nanogel systems [46]. Nanogels can enhance paracellular and transcellular transport of the encapsulated drug. Nanogels can also localize the delivery of the anticancer agents to tumor cells, while minimizing toxicity to normal tissues. Further, due to the encapsulation, nanogels can protect unstable drugs from internal and external degradation factors/hostile environments and provide added benefit for improving pharmacological efficacy and therapeutic safety. In one study, doxorubicin encapsulated in chitosan–alginate nanoparticles retained its anticancer activity and increased accumulation at tumor sites when delivered to melanoma cells [47]. In a separate study aimed at improving the stability of doxorubicin, it was encapsulated in a chitosan-albumin “nanogel” for the treatment of skin cancer. The nanogel showed decreased photosensitivity of doxorubicin and increased cytotoxicity against skin carcinoma cells, and verified that it is a successful drug delivery agent [48]. It is clinically demonstrated that Eucalyptus essential oils can improve the penetration of bioactives. In one study, eucalyptus oil improved 5-FU absorption and penetration in tumor tissue. The enhanced delivery of a nanogel allows for more drug to reach the target site, resulting in improved antitumor effect [49]. Currently used first-line treatments for melanoma consist of chemotherapy, radiation, and immune checkpoint inhibitors, which are very expensive, limiting access. Nanogel-based drug delivery provides a low-cost and effective alternative, favorably for topical chemotherapy administration. With topical administration, the delivery system bypasses first-pass metabolism and is a less invasive option that adheres to patient compliance better. 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), an antimetabolite for various cancers, is limited to use for melanoma because the conventional gels do not penetrate well into tissue. 5-FU is known to be absorbed superficially, but is not effective in attacking melanoma cells that are potentially deeper in the tissue. The traditional 5-FU topical therapy requires to be placed for extended periods and can cause local skin reactions. One strategy to minimize the local skin reactions is to rejuvenate existing drugs by formulating them in nanosuites. One study reported a chitosan-based pH-responsive biodegradable nanogel (FCNGL) that was made and encapsulated with 5-FU. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of the tumor, comparing FCNGL/5-FU with the standard 5% 5-FU and control mice group, showed improvement of the subcutaneous layer alignment and even regeneration of the epithelial layer of skin [50]. Nanomedicine has made significant advancements in the use of stimuli-responsive materials for targeted cancer drug delivery approaches that aim to improve efficacy while limiting side effects. Smart carriers are designed to respond to specific internal or external stimuli, including internal signals like acidic pH, glutathione (GSH), and enzymes, or external inputs like glucose, or ATP. Doxorubicin is an example of a chemotherapeutic that intercalates with DNA, but this requires the delivery of doxorubicin to the intracellular environment with specificity to the nucleus. Delivery of doxorubicin relies on an accurate delivery to the cytoplasm, release from the endosome/lysosome, and controlled release from the carrier. There are different polymers such as basic amino acids, and carboxymethyl-chitosan that allow endosomal escape using the proton sponge effect. Redox-sensitive nanocarriers rely on the high levels of GSH present in tumor tissue to displace disulfide bonds in a two-part nature, which releases doxorubicin. Dual-sensitive systems (ie pH/redox) allow controlled and targeted release of doxorubicin. These different strategies and modes of circulation will ensure doxorubicin has improved intracellular availability and subsequently improved therapeutic prospects [51]. Delivery of therapeutic agents

Drug development appears to fail due to poor aqueous solubility, poor efficacy, and the toxicity of drugs. Drugs with poor solubility and absorption continue to be overlooked or abandoned. According to the Biopharmaceutics Classification System, 40% of FDA-approved drugs and almost 90% of new drug candidates have poor solubility and may not reach therapeutic levels in body fluids. Curcumin is a bioactive turmeric compound from Curcuma longa, which has anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antimicrobial, and tissue-protective activities. Curcumin has shown efficacy in a multitude of disease models and is generally accepted as safe, even at very high doses. However, poor water solubility, low absorption, instability, and rapid metabolism restrict the pharmaceutical beneficial uses of curcumin. These factors often severely reduce the bioavailability of curcumin when taken orally. It has been well documented in the literature that new and advanced delivery systems would be needed to overcome limitations associated with the bioavailability of curcumin. A thermo-responsive nanogel system based on chitosan has been developed for the delivery of curcumin to resolve poor solubility, stability, and absorption issues with curcumin. The CS-g-pN nanogel formulations were illustrated to have no cytotoxicity against NIH-3T3 and HeLa cell lines, and the curcumin-loaded variants of the nanogel systems displayed dose-dependent cytotoxicity against the cell lines. The NIH-3T3 cells can effectively internalize the curcumin-loaded variants of the nanogels. The NIH-3T3 cells can effectively internalize the curcumin-loaded variants of the nanogels [52]. Because ibuprofen has a low solubility, a short half-life, and is quickly cleared, it often requires a high frequency of dosing in high amounts, increasing the possibility of wasting doses and increasing the chance of side effects such as bleeding. Conversely, another study employed electrostatic interactions between the drug and polymers to create a new chitosan–ibuprofen–gellan based nanogel to develop an ibuprofen transdermal delivery system. Chitosan improved the skin penetration, skin permeability, and transdermal release rate of ibuprofen due to the strength of ibuprofen–chitosan ionic interactions and high content of chitosan. Although diffusion was the primary mechanism of ibuprofen release through pig skin, drug partitioning and matrix erosion also played a role to a lesser extent [2]. Glibenclamide (Gb) stimulates insulin secretion and improves insulin sensitivity but causes side effects, like, nausea and vomiting, with oral administration that decreases patient adherence. Creating a topical nano-formulation for Gb would improve the absorption of Gb, reduce side effects, and provide a more convenient way for medication to be used and absorbed. Quercetin (Qc) is a natural antioxidant that works with Gb to stimulate insulin secretion and protect beta cells from oxidative stress. In one study, a chitosan-based nanogel was developed and characterized for transdermal co-delivery of Gb and Qc to improve diabetes management. Chi served as a polymeric material for the encapsulation of Gb and Qc. The nanogel exhibited sustained release properties for the drugs. And, the in vitro assessments showed a remarkable skin permeation [53]. As a drug, terbutaline (TBN) is typically administered either orally or via inhalation. However, both dosage forms are limited for various reasons. Oral TBN has low bioavailability (~15%) because of first-pass metabolism, leading to fast clearance effects and a short half-life. Inhalation using a nebulizer will improve bioavailability, but drug aerosol therapies can potentially be associated with unwanted side effects. Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDS) have also been suggested as an alternative, because they can improve bioavailability, serve controlled, sustained release, with reversibility. Nevertheless, due TBN being hydrophilic and permeability restriction imposed by the barrier function of the stratum corneum, TBN permeated poorly and needed enhancers and proniosomal gels to improve transdermal absorption. However, to circumvent the limitations, the TBN-CTS-BLS gel was prepared. Formulation tolerability suggested the TBN gel formulation was well-tolerated and no inflammatory reactions were evident upon histopathological examination. The pharmacokinetic study suggested the optimized TBN-CTS-BLS formulation largely improved the bioavailability of TBN by about 2.33-fold and was elevated as compared to the oral solution [54]. Ketoprofen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that is commonly prescribed to treat inflammatory disorders, but its delivery through the oral route is hindered due to first-pass metabolism and poor gastrointestinal absorption. Transdermal delivery provides sustained release of drugs and eliminates the drawbacks of oral delivery. A recent study developed a chitosan-based ketoprofen nanogel utilizing nanotechnology in the form of a nanoemulsion to increase retention and permeation through skin. The nano-sized droplet resulted in an increased surface area and better drug transport and bioavailability. In vivo results showed there were significantly greater anti-inflammatory effects compared to conventional formulations, lending support to the potential for effective pain management [55]. Cosmetic

Physical appearance has a direct effect on self-esteem and overall well-being. The presence of healthy skin through appropriate skincare results in a good aesthetic appearance and quality of life [56]. Chitosan is not as commonly utilized in cosmetics as biopolymers such as collagen or hyaluronic acid, primarily from being poor water solubility. However, there is a developing interest in it based on its versatile functional qualities in a formulation. More chitosan derivatives are starting to appear in cosmetic formulations, and the potential for better benefits is increasing [57].

Skin and hair care

Hair consists principally of keratin, and hair is easily damaged by different types of physical and chemical stress, such as mechanical stress, UV light, heat, and treatments performed in hair salons. Chitosan and other biopolymers provide a thicker consistency to the formulation and improve adherence of active ingredients to the hair [58]. The conditioning effect of chitosan is due to its positive charge neutralizing the hair’s negative charge caused by damage. Chitosan provides a film on the hair surface that condenses hair structure, which leads to softer and thicker hair. In a study, a Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCH) was developed, the results showed 0.5% H-CMCH provided the best degrees of skin moisturizing (DM) after 30 min on pig skin by application of the solution [59]. Kojima et al. studied chitosan’s penetration into hair using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). They noted more absorption for dyed hair than for undyed hair, which suggests that hairdressing treatments for the hair caused an increase in the porosity and vulnerability of the hair. One of their findings was that chitosan was able to integrate into the structure of the damaged hair, demonstrating that there is potential for repair by chitosan [60].

Chitosan is also promising for cosmetic applications due to its ability to protect against UVR. As a biopolymer that absorbs light below 400 nm, it has substantial photoprotection potential [57, 58, 61]. Bikiaris et al. studied chitosan nanoparticles encapsulating pomegranate juice to incorporate into emulsions. The formulations containing chitosan demonstrated UV protection compared to the control, indicating promise in applications for sunscreens [61].

Anti-aging

The aim of cosmetics is not merely to have an anti-aging effect. Cosmetology can facilitate the management of skin conditions or may even serve to lessen their impact on the skin. Notably, the most systemic skin conditions requiring cosmetology involvement include: acne, hyperpigmentation, depigmentation, psoriasis, and post-surgical scarring [57]. In an investigation, Jiangxiu Niu et al. were interested in developing a curcumin (Cur), which is a polyphenol that has antioxidant, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory properties, but has also been described as having poor water solubility and limited skin bioavailability [62, 63]. Therefore, the researchers developed a curcumin-loaded microemulsion combined with a keratin–chitosan gel (CME-KCS gel). The resultant formulation displayed favorable viscoelasticity, bioadhesion, and controlled drug release. In vitro and in vivo assessments revealed that CME-KCS gel provided excellent skin penetration, retention, and efficacy compared to conventional Cur solutions, and no skin irritation was observed [64]. Another study assessed (CH-gamma-CD-Fu) nanogels made from chitosan, gamma-cyclodextrin, and fucoidan and determined that they had a better antioxidant effect and moisturizing effect compared to non-nano fucoidan. In terms of human studies, 2.5 mm skin moisture significantly increased after 2 and 4 weeks of using the nanogel formulation. After 4 weeks, there was a greater hydration improvement with CH-gamma-CD-Fu (Nanogel) as compared to the basic treatment.

Antimicrobial activity

Chitosan has antimicrobial properties based on its potent chelation ability, plus the ability to alter the permeability of microbial cell walls, thus limiting intracellular and extracellular signaling and nutrient exchange is limited which in turn decreases microbial growth (80). As a result, this is the property that allows it to be used in deodorants and antiperspirants because it reduces the bacteria in the sweat that produce metabolites that cause bad odor [57, 65, 66]. Because of its antimicrobial activity, chitosan has its usability in deodorants and antiperspirants [67].

Film forming and dressing

Chitosan can form films, which allows it to be used in the application of cosmetic masks, which would offer protection and decrease skin hydration loss in a similar way as wound dressings. Chitosan can be utilized in various cosmetic forms, which can be emulsions, gels, foams, sticks, sprays, or aerosols, for its effects on skin, hair, nails, and oral health care. Chitosan film is a hydrophilic film that holds moisture and helps keep skin moisturized by acting to minimize transepidermal water loss [68, 69]. Conclusion and future perspective

Dermal (topical/transdermal) drug delivery offers distinct advantages and is clinically valuable since it can bypass hepatic first-pass metabolism and maintain steady-state drug levels for extended durations compared to most other delivery methods. Nanogels, as nanoscale crosslinked hydrogel networks, enhance dermal drug delivery by combining hydrogel swelling with advanced nanoparticle engineering. Chitosan-based nanogels exploit the inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and positive surface charge of the polymer to improve skin adherence and permeation. These systems allow for high drug-loading capacity and controllable release profiles; for instance, a chitosan nanogel has successfully delivered a hydrophobic drug pair (glibenclamide and quercetin) to the skin. Compared to traditional carriers, chitosan nanogels offer a unique combination of properties—a safe, bioadhesive matrix capable of controlled release and effective penetration through skin barriers. Recent studies highlight their multifunctionality, including enhanced wound healing and anti-inflammatory activity (via moisture retention, antibacterial effects, and tissue regeneration), targeted oncological delivery enabled by tunable stimuli-responsiveness, and innovative cosmetic formulations benefiting from their moisture retention and film-forming ability. Furthermore, chitosan nanogels improve the solubility, uptake, and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs through optimized carrier design. Various fabrication methods—such as ionic gelation, emulsion polymerization, self-assembly, and radical polymerization—provide control over nanogel size, surface charge, and crosslinking density, thereby allowing precise tuning of drug release kinetics and stability. The growing body of evidence demonstrates the strong potential of chitosan nanogels for dermal drug delivery applications. Future research should focus on bridging the gap between laboratory findings and clinical implementation by developing standardized formulation protocols, assessing long-term biocompatibility, and performing in vivo and clinical studies to evaluate pharmacokinetics and therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, integration of chitosan nanogels with emerging technologies such as microneedles, 3D bioprinting, and smart wearable systems could accelerate their clinical translation and lead to personalized, patient-oriented therapies. Overall, the continuous innovation in chitosan nanogel design is expected to expand their application in biomedical, dermatological, and cosmetic fields, reinforcing their role as a new generation of “smart” dermal delivery systems.
