Peripheral trigeminal nerve branch lesions: CT and MRI findings in a pathologically confirmed case series
Authors: Aonan Wang, Suwei Liu, Haibo Wu, Huishu Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe peripheral branches of the trigeminal nerve (V1, V2, and V3) follow an intricate and tortuous course through various skull base foramina and soft tissue spaces. Due to the rarity of lesions specifically involving these distal segments, radiological diagnosis remains a significant challenge. This study aims to summarize the characteristic imaging patterns of diverse pathologically confirmed lesions affecting the peripheral trigeminal branches using CT and MRI.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 18 patients (2008–2022) with pathologically proven peripheral trigeminal lesions. The cohort included schwannoma (n = 10), neurofibroma (n = 3), adenoid cystic carcinoma (n = 1), intraneural perineurioma (n = 1), malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (n = 1), lymphoid hyperplasia (n = 1), and IgG4-related disease (n = 1). All patients underwent comprehensive multi-planar CT and contrast-enhanced MRI.ResultsV1, V2, and V3 divisions were involved in 5, 6, and 5 cases, respectively. We identified three descriptive imaging groupings: Benign Neurogenic Grouping: Characterized by fusiform or nodular thickening with “smooth expansile remodeling” of bony foramina (e.g., foramen rotundum and ovale). Malignant Infiltrative Grouping: Exemplified by adenoid cystic carcinoma, showing aggressive “creeping” spread along neural pathways, often involving the pterygopalatine fossa hub and traversing multiple anatomical compartments. Diffuse/Symmetric Grouping: Observed in intraneural perineurioma and lymphoproliferative disease/IgG4-related disease, manifesting as long-segment, uniform, and often bilateral nerve thickening without focal mass formation.ConclusionAccurate diagnosis of peripheral trigeminal lesions requires a segmental anatomical approach. While MRI is superior for evaluating neural architecture and signal changes, CT provides critical surrogate markers via bone remodeling patterns. Recognizing these descriptive imaging groupings as observed in our series—ranging from indolent remodeling to aggressive perineural spread—is helpful for differentiating rare neural pathologies and guiding clinical management.

Introduction

The trigeminal nerve, as the largest cranial nerve, provides critical sensory and motor innervation to the maxillofacial region. Its complex anatomical trajectory is conventionally divided into five segments: brainstem, cisternal, Meckel’s cave, cavernous sinus, and peripheral divisions [1]. Recent microanatomical studies have further elucidated the complex meningeal architecture surrounding Meckel’s cave. Wysiadecki et al. [2] demonstrated that Meckel’s cave is completely surrounded by a dural sleeve that separates it from the lateral sellar compartment, with arachnoid granulations consistently found near the trigeminal ganglion and its divisions. Zhang et al. [3] provided quantitative morphometric data on the trigeminal ganglion, revealing significant side-to-side differences that may influence the laterality of lesion spread. While intracranial pathologies are well-documented, lesions originating in or extending to the distal peripheral branches—the ophthalmic (V1), maxillary (V2), and mandibular (V3) nerves—present a distinct diagnostic challenge due to the intricate bony architecture and diverse deep facial spaces they traverse [4].

Precise localization and characterization of peripheral trigeminal neuropathy are of paramount clinical importance, as these branches serve as potential conduits for both benign neoplastic growth and aggressive perineural invasion (PNI) [5]. However, isolated involvement of these distal segments is relatively rare in clinical practice. The existing literature largely consists of isolated case reports or studies focused on the proximal trigeminal segments, leaving a significant gap in the systematic understanding of imaging patterns affecting the distal V1-V3 pathways [6, 7].

The diagnostic cornerstone for evaluating these “hidden” segments lies in the synergy of multi-modal imaging. MRI is the gold standard for visualizing intrinsic neural architecture and signal alterations, such as the “target sign” in neurofibromas or the enhancement patterns in inflammatory conditions [8]. Conversely, CT provides indispensable surrogate markers of disease chronicity; for instance, the “smooth expansile remodeling” of the foramen rotundum or ovale is highly suggestive of indolent neurogenic tumors, whereas permeative bone destruction often heralds malignancy [9, 10].

Recent advancements in head and neck imaging emphasize the role of anatomical “hubs,” such as the pterygopalatine fossa (PPF), in the multi-directional spread of disease [11]. Understanding how different pathologies exploit these pre-existing neural pathways—whether through the indolent remodeling seen in schwannomas or the aggressive “creeping” spread typical of adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC)—is essential for surgical planning and radiation oncology [12]. Furthermore, rare systemic conditions like IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) and intraneural perineurioma (INP) further complicate the diagnostic landscape, often manifesting as diffuse, symmetric nerve thickening that can mimic more common entities [13, 14].

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed 18 pathologically confirmed cases to provide a comprehensive, pattern-based approach to peripheral trigeminal lesions. By integrating CT and MRI findings, we aim to categorize these rare pathologies into three distinct imaging patterns—Benign Neurogenic, Malignant Infiltrative, and Diffuse/Symmetric—thereby potentially aiding in the differential diagnosis and informing clinical management in this anatomically complex region.

Methods

Subjects

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study was approved by Peking University Third Hospital Medical Science Research Ethics Committee and the requirement for informed consent was waived. We reviewed our institutional pathology and radiology databases from January 2008 to December 2022 to identify patients with pathologically confirmed lesions involving the trigeminal nerve. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathological diagnosis obtained via surgical resection or biopsy; (2) availability of preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI and CT examinations; and (3) imaging studies of sufficient quality for detailed evaluation of the trigeminal nerve and its branches.

Patient cohort

A total of 18 patients (12 females, 6 males; mean age 45.2 years; range 17–85 years) met these criteria and were enrolled in this study. The pathological spectrum included schwannoma (n = 10), neurofibroma (n = 3), adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC, n = 1), intraneural perineurioma (INP, n = 1), malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST, n = 1), lymphoid hyperplasia (n = 1), and IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD, n = 1). All patients underwent both contrast-enhanced MRI and CT with multiplanar reconstructions prior to treatment.

Imaging approach

CT protocol

CT imaging the trigeminal nerve include helical acquisition scans from the orbital roof to the mandibular symphysis reconstructed in the axial, coronal and sagittal planes, on both soft tissue and bony algorithms.Slice thickness, 2 mm, without interslice gap.

MRI protocol

MRI was performed using a 3.0T scanner (uMR 770, United Imaging, Shanghai, China). The protocol included: axial T2-weighted fast spin-echo (FSE) [TR/TE, 3480/82.24 ms; slice thickness, 3 mm; matrix, 320 × 256], and pre- and post-contrast axial and coronal T1-weighted FSE [TR/TE, 614/10.18 ms; slice thickness, 3 mm; matrix, 320 × 224] with fat suppression. Gadolinium-based contrast agent was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg.

General principles

CT and MRI serve as complementary modalities for evaluating patients with suspected trigeminal nerve pathology. A systematic, segmental approach to imaging the trigeminal nerve is essential for accurate localization and characterization of lesions. Based on the established framework in the literature [1, 4], we subdivided the nerve into five distinct segments along its course:

Imaging analysis

Two experienced head and neck radiologists, blinded to the specific pathological diagnosis, independently reviewed the images. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. A systematic segmental approach was employed to assess the trigeminal nerve from its brainstem nuclei to its distal peripheral branches (V1: ophthalmic, V2: maxillary, V3: mandibular).

Image interpretation should systematically assess each segment for abnormalities, including nerve thickening or atrophy, signal alteration, abnormal contrast enhancement, and mass effect. Particular attention should be paid to the skull base foramina (superior orbital fissure, foramen rotundum, foramen ovale) and their associated canals (infraorbital canal, inferior alveolar canal), as osseous changes often provide indirect evidence of underlying neural pathology. Multiplanar reformations, especially coronal and oblique sagittal planes parallel to the nerve course, are invaluable for visualizing the smaller peripheral branches and their relationship to adjacent structures.

Schwannomas (n = 10): These were the most frequent lesions in our series. They typically appeared as well-defined, ovoid, or lobulated masses. As demonstrated by the imaging of Case 5, MRI showed heterogeneous hyperintensity on T2-weighted images and robust contrast enhancement, with internal cystic necrosis often present in larger tumors. CT revealed smooth, expansile remodeling of the associated foramina, such as the superior orbital fissure or foramen ovale (Fig. 1). In Case 5, subtotal resection of the mass was performed. The tumor was found intraoperatively to originate from and occupy the right foramen ovale region, with partial tumorous infiltration of the trigeminal ganglion. During resection, careful attention was required to protect the intracranial segment of the right internal carotid artery as well as the adjacent oculomotor nerve (CN III), trochlear nerve (CN IV), and abducens nerve (CN VI).

IgG4-related Disease (IgG4-RD) (n = 1): Case 18 exhibited a radiographic picture indistinguishable from lymphoid hyperplasia, featuring smooth thickening of the trigeminal ganglion and its V1/V2/V3 branches. A key diagnostic feature was its significant responsiveness to corticosteroid therapy, with post-treatment images showing marked resolution (Fig. 6). Notably, in both lymphoid hyperplasia and IgG4-related disease, the affected nerves demonstrated smooth, uniform thickening rather than focal nodular enlargement, with homogeneous enhancement on post-contrast imaging. This pattern, distinct from the often-heterogeneous appearance of schwannomas, may serve as a helpful distinguishing feature.

Discussion

The peripheral branches of the trigeminal nerve (V1–V3) function as critical “anatomical highways” for a wide array of neoplastic and inflammatory pathologies. Based on our retrospective analysis of 18 pathologically confirmed cases, we observed that while these branches are encased in complex bony and soft-tissue structures, their imaging manifestations follow predictable biological patterns. Our study proposes a shift from simple lesion identification to a descriptive grouping-based approach, emphasizing the synergy between MRI-defined neural morphology and CT-detected osseous surrogate markers.

The most prevalent findings in our series were benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors (PNSTs), including schwannomas and neurofibromas, whose imaging appearances align with established literature.Mechanisms of Bone Remodeling: Unlike the cisternal segment where masses expand freely, the peripheral branches are confined within rigid skull base foramina. The “smooth expansile remodeling” of the superior orbital fissure (V1), foramen rotundum (V2), and foramen ovale (V3) observed on CT is highly characteristic of an indolent, chronic process [15]. This finding indicates that the lesion’s pressure has been applied slowly enough to permit periosteal bone adaptation, a feature that distinguishes benign PNSTs from aggressive malignancies.Intra-pattern Differentiation: Schwannomas frequently exhibited internal cystic necrosis—a radiographic reflection of Antoni B hypocellular areas. Conversely, neurofibromas tended toward more uniform, fusiform thickening.

The transition from Grouping 1 to Gourping 2 marks a fundamental shift from expansion to infiltration, characteristic of perineural invasion (PNI).The Pterygopalatine Fossa (PPF) as a strategic hub: Our findings in Case 1 (ACC) visulize the concept of the PPF as a strategic “hub” for perineural spread, as emphasized by Biau et al. [5]. The PPF functions as a critical anatomical crossroads connecting the infraorbital canal, foramen rotundum, and sphenopalatine foramen. Biological behavior: This “creeping” spread reflects the biological behavior of ACC, which follows paths of least resistance along nerve sheaths while initially preserving the underlying skeletal architecture [16, 17]. Recognition of this infiltrative pattern is important for accurate radiotherapy planning and surgical margin assessment. ACC involving the orbital nerves represents a highly challenging head and neck malignancy. Due to its high propensity for perineural invasion and its characteristic of late recurrence, treatment strategies must achieve a delicate balance between radical tumor eradication and preservation of neurological functions (such as eye movement and vision). “Surgery plus postoperative radiotherapy” is currently the main treatment paradigm. Surgery requires resection of the main tumor mass, along with tracking of the involved nerves (e.g., branches of the trigeminal nerve) to their skull base foramina of exit (e.g., foramen rotundum, foramen ovale), in order to ensure clearance of the perineural spread pathway. For patients with extensive involvement of the orbital apex, cavernous sinus, or critical neurovascular structures, functional subtotal resection may be performed to preserve function, with residual lesion subsequently controlled by radiotherapy.

Grouping 3 represents the most significant diagnostic challenge, as it typically lacks a focal mass and often presents bilaterally. Intraneural Perineurioma (INP): The bilateral, multi-branch involvement in Case 7 is exceptionally rare. While Mauermann et al. [14] describe INP as a focal hypertrophic neuropathy, our case expands this phenotypic spectrum to include long-segment, serpentine thickening. Wang et al. [18] also reported INP involving the intracranial segment, further illustrating the diverse presentation of this rare entity. Fibroinflammatory Mimicry: IgG4-RD and lymphoid hyperplasia (Cases 17, 18) present a “smooth, uniform thickening” rather than a focal nodular mass. As described by Wang et al., these conditions are characterized by intrinsic nerve enlargement without an extraneural soft-tissue component. The dramatic response to corticosteroid therapy in Case 18 confirms the inflammatory nature of the process and can serve as a diagnostic-therapeutic test.

The differential diagnosis for diffuse, symmetric cranial nerve thickening, as observed in our series (INP, lymphoid hyperplasia, and IgG4-RD), is not exhaustive. Other entities should be considered, most notably IgG4-related inflammatory pseudotumor of the trigeminal nerve. Katsura et al. [19] described a case where this pseudotumor presented as isolated trigeminal nerve thickening, occurring in the absence of systemic IgG4-RD. This highlights that even without multi-organ involvement, a localized IgG4-related process can produce the same imaging phenotype and must be included in the differential. Other inflammatory conditions, such as neurosarcoidosis, can also present with diffuse or asymmetric thickening of the cavernous sinus and trigeminal nerve branches. As reported by Gao et al. [20], neurosarcoidosis may manifest as meningeal thickening and enhancement along the cavernous sinus wall, extending to the superior orbital fissure, with involvement of the ophthalmic division (V1). Unlike the indolent bone remodeling seen in schwannomas (Grouping 1) or the aggressive ‘creeping’ spread of ACC (Grouping 2), neurosarcoidosis typically lacks a discrete mass and shows no characteristic bony changes. It should be considered in the differential diagnosis of Grouping 3 (diffuse/symmetric thickening), particularly when accompanied by systemic findings such as hilar lymphadenopathy or elevated serum/CSF angiotensin converting enzyme(ACE) levels. Furthermore, morphometric variations in skull base neurovascular canals may influence imaging interpretation. Dedeoglu et al. [21] demonstrated that pathological conditions can induce measurable changes in the diameters of key skull base foramina, including the foramen rotundum. This finding underscores the value of CT-based bone assessment in evaluating trigeminal nerve pathology, as quantitative canal measurements may serve as surrogate markers for chronic pathological processes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and small sample size (n = 18) limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, the pathological heterogeneity of the included lesions, while reflecting real-world clinical practice, makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about any single entity. Third, the proposed three imaging groupings are descriptive observations based on our single-center case series and have not undergone external validation. Consequently, they should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as a formally validated classification system. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts and standardized quantitative imaging parameters (e.g., nerve thickness ratios, standardized enhancement values) are needed to validate these preliminary observations. Furthermore, given the complexity of the trigeminal nerve system and the rarity of these lesions, continued integration of detailed microanatomical studies [2, 3], high-resolution imaging of peripheral neural pathways, and clinical validation cohorts will be essential to improve diagnostic precision and to better understand lesion spread patterns.Recent morphometric analyses of skull base canals [21] suggest that quantitative CT parameters may offer additional value in future research.

Conclusions

Accurate evaluation of the peripheral trigeminal branches requires a segmental approach that integrates morphology with biological behavior. By recognizing these three descriptive imaging groupings as observed in our series—indolent remodeling, infiltrative spread, and diffuse symmetry thickening—clinicians may better approach the wide differential diagnosis of lesions in this complex region. This framework not only aids in accurate diagnosis but also directs appropriate management, ranging from steroid therapy in inflammatory cases to aggressive surgical margins in perineural malignancies.
