High risk HPV among HIV positive women with normal and abnormal cervical cytology in Bahir Dar, north west Ethiopia
Authors: Alemayehu Abate, Melkamnesh Azage
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cervical cancer is a malignant tumor that arises from the cells of the uterine cervix, primarily caused by persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV). Women living with HIV are at a heightened risk of infection with high-risk HPV types. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and type-specific distribution of HPV among HIV-positive women with cervical cytologic abnormalities in Bahir Dar, northwestern Ethiopia. A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among HIV-positive women in this region between January and March 2025. A total of 344 women were enrolled. Of these, 171 (49.7%) had a high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) infection. The type-specific distribution among the positive cases was as follows: HPV16, 37 (21.6%); HPV18, 15 (8.8%); HPV16 with other types, 24 (14.0%); HPV18 with other types, 7 (4.1%); both HPV16 and HPV18, 6 (3.5%); and other high-risk types, 82 (47.9%). Cytological analysis revealed that 81 women (23.55%) had abnormalities, while 263 (76.45%) had normal results. Among the abnormal cases, the findings were: 26 (32.1%) ASC-US, 22 (27.2%) LSIL, 14 (17.3%) ASC-H, 9 (11.1%) LSIL-H, and 10 (12.3%) HSIL. The study also found that HIV-positive women aged 31 and older had significantly higher odds of HR-HPV infection compared to younger women, with the peak risk observed in the 41–50 age group. Additional risk factors included higher parity, an early sexual debut, and having multiple sexual partners. Given the high prevalence of HR-HPV and its strong association with abnormal cytology, targeted preventive measures including enhanced screening, vaccination, and patient education are crucial for HIV-positive women, particularly in low-resource settings like northwestern Ethiopia.

Introduction

A malignant growth called cervical cancer arises from cells in the uterine cervix1. Annually, it accounts for 600 000 new cases and 340 000 deaths, making it the fourth most frequent cancer in women, after lung, colorectal, and breast cancer2. Cervical cancer is a risk for 29 million Ethiopian women who are 15 years of age or older2.

The fact that 88% of cervical cancer deaths and roughly 83% of new cases occur in LMICs makes it noteworthy. In 36 countries, including sections of sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and India, cervical cancer does, in fact, cause the majority of cancer-related deaths3. Due to the immunosuppression caused by HIV infection and the longer life expectancy brought on by anti-retroviral therapy (ART), cervical cancer has become one of the most common cancers in women with HIV4.

The prevalence of HPV rises as cervical abnormalities get more severe; it ranges from 12% in normal cytology to 89% in invasive cervical cancer. The prevalence of HR-HPV is much higher in women with abnormal cervical cytology (92.4%) than in those without abnormal cytology (71%)5. Pre-cancer lesions are less likely to regress in women living with HIV (WLWH), and recurrence rates are higher as a result of the faster progression of HR HPV infection to pre-cancer lesions and CC6.

Genital HPV types are divided into high-risk (HR) and low-risk (LR) categories based on epidemiological studies that look at their capacity to cause invasive malignancy. Types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68, 73, and 82 of HPV are now categorized as HR, types 26, 53, and 66 as potentially HR, and types 6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 70, 72, and 81 as LR7.

The HR HPV DNA test is a Cervical Cancer (CC) screening test, which detects the presence or absence of HPV, the virus that causes CC. Priority is given to women living with HIV for HPV testing at an earlier age starting from the time HIV is detected and to re-screen every two years8.

Only a few epidemiological studies carried out in Ethiopia have demonstrated that oncogenic HPV infection is responsible for over 90% of invasive cervical malignancies8.

HIV is thought to raise a person’s risk and susceptibility to oncogenic HPV infections, which can accelerate the development of invasive cervical carcinoma (ICC). HIV-positive women with chronic HR-HPV infection had abnormal cytology in 86% of cases, and more frequently than HIV-negative women. HIV-positive women with persistent HR-HPV infection had a 38% risk of being diagnosed with HSIL or worse cytology9.

Cytological data suggests that women with HIV are more likely to experience high-risk HPV type infections, which can lead to invasive cervical cancer and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL)4. HIV infections are believed to be the cause of changes in the range of HPV types in both invasive cervical carcinomas and normal cervical epithelium, leading to a complex morbidity in the interaction between the two viruses10.

Optimal preventive vaccination and early disease detection are essential for the optimal management of cervical cancer, particularly in women with HIV. It is very impossible to estimate the prevalence rate of the disease in women with HIV because routine screening for cervical abnormalities is not commonly carried out. To the best of our knowledge, no epidemiological research has been done on the distribution of HPV types in women with HIV who have cytological abnormalities in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia. The aim of this study was to determine the HPV prevalence and type-specific distribution among HIV-positive women with cervical cytologic abnormalities in Bahir Dar, northwestern Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

A cross-sectional facility-based study was conducted among HIV-positive women in Bahir Dar, northwestern Ethiopia, between January and March 2025. A total of 344 women living with HIV were enrolled in the study. HIV-positive women were offered screening with partial genotyping of the HPV DNA test and cervical cytology examination with the PAP test. Participants with no other known chronic diseases and no history of cervical neoplasia or urectomy were eligible. All participants gave informed consent to participate in the study.

Data collection tools and procedure

A standardized questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic, reproductive, sexual behavior and clinical data. Medical records were reviewed to extract data on the use of ART treatment and management and other laboratory records. Trained gynecologists currently working on cervical cancer unit collect data using standardized questionnaire, provide gynecological, physical examinations and collect swab for HPV DNA test and cytological examinations,

Laboratory investigations and cervical cytology

All 344 study participants were screened for HPV DNA and cervical cytology. HR-HPV DNA tests detect high-risk HPV DNA in vaginal or cervical swabs. A HR-HPV testing were performed by the clinically validated COBAS 4800 HPV assay (Roche Molecular Systems, Pleasanton, CA, USA), based on real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) technology. The method detects 14 HPV types (the 12 designed as high-risk by the IARC, plus types 68 and 66) and provides specific results for HPV16 and HPV18, and altogether for the other 12 types (31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68b) referred to as non16/18HPV. The smears were stained with the Pap stain and were independently diagnosed by two experienced cytopathologists. When the diagnosis differed between the two cytopathologists, the sample was reviewed by a third cytopathologist and consensus obtained. Cytology results were diagnosed according to the 2001 Bethesda system and classified as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy, ASC-US, ASC-H, LSIL, HSIL, and squamous cervical carcinoma. A diagnosis of ASCUS or higher was considered cytological abnormality.

Data processing and analysis

Collected data were reviewed, coded, and entered into Epi info version 7.2.5 and exported to SPSS version 26.0 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequency and cross tabulation were performed to summarize the data. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to test the relationship between individual variables and the outcome variable. Those variables that had a significance level of < 0.5 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model for further analysis, and a stepwise backward analysis (likelihood ratio) was performed to account for confounding variables. Odds ratios (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to assess the degree of association between independent and dependent variables. Finally, variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Limitation of the study

There were certain limitations to our study that should be taken into account when interpreting our findings. First, the results of the cytology were not confirmed by histology. Second, using detection technology that is already accessible in our lab, the study partially identifies high risk HPV genotypes. Third, because it was a cross-sectional study, there was no follow-up system in place to detect recurrent or persistent HPV infections or the advancement or recurrence of cytological abnormalities.

Discussion

This study investigated partial genotyping of the HPV DNA test and cervical cytology using the PAP test in women living with HIV who visited the gynecology unit of Felege Hiwot Specialized Referral Hospital during the study period. The principal finding of this study showed that the overall prevalence of HR-HPV among these study participants was 171 (49.7%). Overall, there were 81(23.5%) abnormal cervical cytology, ASC-US, LSIL, ASC-H, LSIL-H and HSIL + accounted for 26 (32.1%), 22 (27.211.3%), 14 (17.3%), 9 (11.1%) and 10 (12.3%) respectively.

The current study indicated that the prevalence of HR-HPV, which was 171 (49.7%) of the 344 women infected with HR-HPV, was in close agreement with cross-sectional investigations carried out in public health facilities in the Kweneng East District of Botswana and Cameroon, which had corresponding magnitudes of 40.4% and 46.43%, respectively11,12. The current study’s findings were higher than those of a study conducted in another region of Ethiopia, which found that the prevalence of HR HPV among WLWH was 173 (35.2%), 95% CI (30.5% − 40.1%) (6). The discrepancy between the two studies may have been caused by differences in geography, socioeconomic status, study area, and study period.

This study revealed that, the overall prevalence of HR-HPV in abnormal cervical cytology among women with HIV was 96.3%. The highest prevalence of high-risk HPV genotypes was found in types other than 16 and 18, which accounted for 43 (55.1%) cases, while genotypes 16 and other HPV infections accounted for 17 (21.8%) cases, type 16 alone accounted for 14 (18%) cases, and type 18 alone accounted for 3 (3.8%) cases. This result is consistent with a study done in Turkey that reported 110 (25.3%) of the 435 h HPV-positive samples had HPV16, 17 (3.9%) had HPV18, and 310 (71.3%) had neither type 16 nor 1813. The study conducted in China reported that the five most frequent types were HPV 16 (4.4%), 58 (2.9%), 52 (2.7%), 33 (2.2%), and 11 (1.9%)14.

This study identified only three (3.7%) patients who tested negative for HR-HPV showed aberrant cervical cytology in their cytological grading. The prevalence of HR-HPV among women with normal cervical cytology was 93 (35.4%) of these, 23 (24.7%) were type 16, 12 (12.9%) were type 18, 7 (7.5%) were type 16 and others, 3 (3.2%) were type 18 and others, 4 (4.3%) were type 16 and 18, 44 (47.3%) were other HPV types, 263 (76.5%) were negative. This finding is greater than that of a study conducted in Turkey that discovered HR-HPV in 18.7% of NILM samples and in 79.7% of samples with aberrant cytology (7). The disparity in the results may be caused by regional and economic disparities.

This study identified that the rate of HR-HPV infection increased with increasing cytological findings: Normal, ASC-US, LSIL, ASC-H, LSIL-H, and HSIL were 35.4%, 92.3%, 95.4%, 100%, 100%, and 100% respectively. This finding is consistent to the study done in China which indicated that the rate of HPV detection increased with the severity of cytological grading, from 13.0% for ASC-US, 30.8% for ASC-H, 39.5% for 56.3%, and 100.0% for LSIL, HSIL, and SCC, respectively15.

In this study, HR-HPV infection among women living with HIV who had a history of STI was significantly associated with HR-HPV infections which was17 times more than those WLWH who did not have STI infections. This finding was in line with a study conducted in a different region of Ethiopia, which found women with HIV who also had STIs were three times more likely to get HPV than women without STIs (6).

The current study found that women living with HIV who had multiple sexual partners showed an increased risk of HR-HPV infection compared with women living with HIV who had only one sexual partner. This finding is in agreement with a study done in South Africa and another part of Ethiopia which revealed that the risk of HR-HPV infection was nearly doubled for WLWH with many lifetime sexual partners compared to those with only one lifetime sexual partner4,16.

In this study, we found that HIV positive women who had sexual intercourse at age 18–20, 20–25, and > 25 had a preventive effect for harboring HR-HPV than sexual intercourse below the age of 18. This finding was in line with a study done in China which found the link between early sexual debut and CIN2+, which might be because such sexual conduct elevated the probability of exposure to HPV5.

In the present study, Women living with HIV with 4–6, 7–10, and > 10 children had increased risk of infection for HR HPV than women living with HIV with parity less than 3. This result was similar to another study done in China which states the prevalence of HR-HPV increased significantly as gravidity and parity increased, possibly as a consequence of pregnancy’s hormonal changes17.

In the current study, HIV positive women with greater than 1000 copies/ml viral load had 4 times more risk for HR HPV infection than HIV positive women with less than 1000 copies/ml with OR 3.914 95%CI 2.406–6.367. This finding was consistent with different studies which states the most commonly reported risk factors linked to high-risk HPV infection were high viral load and CD4 count18–21., and22.

Conclusion

The prevalence of HR-HPV was very high among women living with HIV (49.7%), with 81 (23.5%) of the study participants had abnormal cervical cytology, HR-HPV was detected in 96.3% of patients with abnormal cytology. Women living with HIV need \more frequent cervical cytological diagnosis. The most frequent genotypes circulating among women living with HIV were types other than type 16 and 18. Most affected individuals are middle aged and of low economic status. HR-HPV infection was more influenced by Socio-demographic, sexual and reproductive factors. So that HPV related health intervention programs like, behavioral change, life style change and awareness creation are important. It would also be necessary to strengthen surveillance of HR-HPV through screening, treatment, and vaccination of HIV-infected women.
