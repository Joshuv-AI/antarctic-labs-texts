Comparison of cannulated screw fixation versus cannulated screws combined with lateral femoral neck support plate in treating Pauwels type Ⅲ femoral neck fracture
Authors: Qinyang Zhang, Guolin Chen, Sicheng Gao, Yang Li, Zeyu Jiang, Zhenxiang Zhang, Bin Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPauwels type Ⅲ femoral neck fractures are characterized by poor stability and high postoperative complication rates, and the optimal internal fixation regimen remains to be verified. The aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness between simple cannulated screws internal fixation and cannulated screws combined with lateral femoral neck support plate internal fixation in the treatment of Pauwels type Ⅲ femoral neck fractures.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 68 patients with Pauwels type Ⅲ femoral neck fractures. Control group was treated with 3 cannulated screws internal fixation, and observation group was treated with lateral femoral neck support plate on the basis of control group. The operation time, intraoperative blood loss, hospitalization time, visual analogue scale (VAS) score, Harris Hip score (HHS) and postoperative complications were compared between two groups.ResultsThe central finding of this study is that the observation group provided significantly superior patient-reported and functional outcomes. Regarding postoperative complications, including fracture nonunion, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, and wound infection, the incidence was lower in the observation group compared with the control group. Specifically, the observation group demonstrated markedly better hip function, with HHS significantly higher than the control group at both 3-month (68.35 ± 2.37 vs. 59.41 ± 2.55, P < 0.001) and 6-month (92.06 ± 2.46 vs. 80.03 ± 5.23, P < 0.001) follow-ups. Moreover, patients in the observation group experienced substantially less pain, as reflected by a significantly lower VAS score at the critical 6-month time point (0.47 ± 0.56 vs. 1.09 ± 0.67, P < 0.001).ConclusionCompared with simple cannulated screws fixation, the combination of cannulated screws and a lateral femoral neck support plate provides more stable fixation, reduces postoperative pain, and facilitates faster recovery of hip joint function, without increasing the risk of complications. This approach allows for satisfactory fracture reduction and favorable functional outcomes, making it a reliable surgical strategy for the treatment of Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures.Keywords: Pauwels-Ⅲ femoral neck fractures; Lateral femoral neck support plate, Cannulated screw, Internal fixation

Introduction

Femoral neck fractures are a common type of hip fracture, accounting for 57% of all hip fractures, and their incidence has been increasing year by year, showing obvious age-related differences in injury mechanisms [1–3]. Specifically, in elderly patients, femoral neck fractures are mostly caused by low-energy trauma, typically falls from a standing height, which is closely associated with age-related osteoporosis and reduced muscle strength; in young and middle-aged individuals, however, such fractures are mainly induced by high-energy trauma, such as traffic accidents, falls from heights, or high-impact sports injuries [4–6]. The mechanisms of injury and treatment strategies for femoral neck fractures vary significantly across different age groups, with higher surgical expectations for middle-aged and young patients [7, 8]. In young and middle-aged individuals, femoral neck fractures are typically the result of high-energy injuries [9]. As such, the primary goal in treating these fractures is to restore the normal anatomical alignment and biomechanics of the hip, while ensuring proper fracture healing.

The Pauwels classification is a classic system for evaluating femoral neck fractures, based on the angle between the fracture line and the horizontal plane (the Pauwels angle) as measured on an anteroposterior hip radiograph [10]. This angle directly reflects the shear force acting on the fracture site, thereby determining fracture stability and influencing the risk of postoperative complications. The classification criteria are as follows: ①Pauwels type I: Pauwels angle < 30°. The fracture line is relatively horizontal, resulting in minimal shear force and favorable intrinsic stability. Pauwels type II: Pauwels angle 30°–50°. The fracture line is moderately inclined, generating intermediate shear forces and corresponding stability. ②Pauwels type III: Pauwels angle > 50°. The fracture line is steeply inclined, producing substantial shear forces and poor intrinsic stability. This subtype carries the highest risk of postoperative complications. ③Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures represent the most severe subtype of this injury, characterized by a steeply inclined fracture line, marked displacement, and inherently poor stability [11, 12]. These fractures are associated with the highest risk of postoperative complications, with reported nonunion rates ranging from 7 to 33%, in addition to a substantial risk of avascular necrosis and fixation failure [13, 14]. They occur most frequently in young and middle-aged patients following high-energy trauma, posing a major challenge in clinical practice. This high-risk profile provides the rationale for the present study, which specifically investigates Pauwels type III fractures to assess the effectiveness of combined internal fixation strategies.

Several internal fixation options are available for treating femoral neck fractures, including hollow nails, dynamic hip screws, locking plate fixation, and biplanar fixation [15, 16].While biomechanical evaluations of these internal fixation devices have been conducted, the relative advantages of rach in the treatment of Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures remain unclear [17]. In recent years, the combination of cannulated screws with a lateral support plate has gained traction as a more advantageous treatment modality, as it has been shown to reduce the occurrence of postoperative complications, facilitate fracture healing and promote functional recovery [18]. The aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness between simple cannulated screws internal fixation (control group) and cannulated screws combined with lateral femoral neck support plate internal fixation (observation group) in the treatment of Pauwels type III femoral neck fracture, focusing on differences in surgical indicators, postoperative pain, hip function recovery and complication rates.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study adheres to the relevant principles of the Helsinki Declaration and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou People's Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University. As this study is a retrospective study, it only involves the collection and analysis of previous clinical data, without involving direct contact or intervention with patients. The ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent. All patient data have been anonymized before analysis to ensure confidentiality and privacy.

A total of 68 patients with Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures, who were admitted to Taizhou People's Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University between January 2020 and June 2023, were enrolled in this study. Patients were retrospectively divided into two groups based on the type of treatment received, with 34 patients in each group. The following were the inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 to 60 years old; (2) A diagnosis of Pauwels type III femoral neck fracture based solely on imaging measurement; (3) indications for internal fixation and signed informed consent from both the patients and their families. The exclusion criteria were: (1) fracture caused by pathological factors; (2) incomplete follow-up data or the follow-up duration of less than 6 months (the minimum requirement); (3) presence of serious medical conditions or unstable vital sign, which would preclude the patient from tolerating anesthesia and surgery.

Surgical methods

The choice of surgical technique was determined by the attending surgeon's assessment and was not randomized. All patients underwent surgical treatment following standardized preoperative evaluation (including assessment of vital signs, comorbidity control, and imaging confirmation of fracture type). The time from injury to surgery (time to surgery) was defined as the interval between the patient’s reported time of injury (or time of emergency department admission for patients with unclear injury time) and the start of the operation. According to the clinical guidelines for femoral neck fractures in our center, patients with stable vital signs and no contraindications to urgent surgery were scheduled for operation within 24–72 h after injury; patients with unstable comorbidities (e.g., poorly controlled hypertension, acute myocardial ischemia) underwent surgery after 72 h, following optimization of their general condition. The actual time to surgery for all enrolled patients ranged from 24 to 96 h, with no patient experiencing a delay exceeding 96 h due to medical contraindications. After anesthesia, the patient was positioned on the fluoroscopic traction bed, and the surgical area was routinely disinfected and draped.

Control group: the patients were positioned on the traction bed, and lower limb restoration was performed through traction, flexion, abduction, adduction, and internal rotation. Three guide pins were then inserted into the femoral neck of the affected side. The subcutaneous tissue was carefully dissected to the periosteum, and the depth was measured by drilling. Subsequently, three screws were inserted. C-arm X-ray confirmed that the femoral neck fracture was properly aligned and reduced.Once the alignment was deemed satisfactory, the fracture was closed with layer by layer suturing. All patients in both groups achieved satisfactory closed reduction under fluoroscopic guidance prior to fixation, and no cases required conversion to open reduction or were excluded on this basis. Observation group: After manual reduction after anesthesia, a 4–6 cm longitudinal incision was made along the lower edge of the greater trochanter of the affected femur. A femoral neck bone plate was positioned laterally on the femoral shaft, with locking hole on the upper femoral neck to restore the neck-shaft angle to 125°—130°. A guide needle was placed in the upper third of the femoral neck using the anteroposterior view, and the guide pin was placed at the anterior part of the femoral neck using the lateral view. The plate was fixed on the femoral shaft, and then two guide pins were inserted through the locking hole of the plate. The three guide pins were arranged in an inverted triangle configuration within the femoral neck. If a significant gap gap between the fracture ends was noted,, 1–2 compression cannulated screws were used to compress the fracture site through the plate. Finally, the femoral shaft is secured with a locking screw, while three femoral neck cannulated screws and the lateral femoral neck support plate provided additional stability Satisfactory reduction was confirmed with fluoroscopic imaging, and a drainage tube was placed before the wound was closed with layer-by-layer suturing. Postoperative management

To prevent postoperative infection, patients received anticoagulation therapy, analgesic treatment, and were instructed on lateral ankle pump exercise. On the first postoperative day, quadriceps isometric contraction exercise and hip and knee flexion–extension exercise were initiated. Following surgery, regular follow-up visits was conducted, including review of X-ray images both hips in the anterior position. The follow-up aimed to monitor for any complications such as incision infection, fracture nonunion, internal fixation denailing, and femoral head necrosis.

Data collection

A comparison was made between the two groups regarding operation time, intraoperative blood loss, hospitalization time and postoperative complications. The VAS was used to evaluate the pain levels in both groups preoperatively, as well as 3 and 6 months after postoperatively. Hip function was assessed using the HHS before surgery, and at 3 months and 6 months following operation [19]. All patients completed the 6-month follow-up, which was the minimum follow-up period for study inclusion.

When evaluating pain levels using the VAS, we implemented the following standardized procedures to minimize bias. 1) The VAS score was assessed using a standardized 10-cm horizontal line (0 cm = "no pain at all," 10 cm = "the most severe pain imaginable") along with printed written instructions. The instructions explicitly explained the scale’s meaning, how to mark the pain level. To minimize assessment bias, all patients received identical verbal instructions. 2) All assessments were conducted by the same 2 trained orthopedic nurses to reduce inter-assessor variability. The nurses received prior training on standardized VAS administration. 3) Patients were asked to complete the VAS assessment independently in a quiet consultation room whenever possible. For patients with limited vision or motor function, the nurse read the scale and instructions aloud verbatim, and marked the position indicated by the patient—ensuring the patient’s true perception was recorded without interference.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages (n, %). For baseline and operative continuous variables, the normality of distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. For data that met both assumptions, the independent samples t-test was used; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was employed. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. For the longitudinal comparison of VAS and HHS across different time points (preoperative, 3 months, 6 months), a one-way repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction was applied to control for the increased risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons.

Discussion

Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures are a common bone and joint injury, typically resulting from high-energy trauma [20]. These farctures are characterized by obvious displacement and severe disruption of femoral neck blood supply, which, if not treated timely, can lead to fracture nonunion and avascular necrosis of the femoral head [21]. The choice of treatment should be individualized, taking into account patient age, fracture type, and comorbidities. Although conservative management is sometimes considered, it often results in complication such as respiratory tract infection, lower extremity deep vein thrombosis, pressure ulcer and other complications, and increase the mortality of patients [22, 23].

Surgical strategies for Pauwels type III fractures vary across age groups [24]. For patients younger than 65 years, closed reduction and internal fixation are generally preferred with open reduction used when satisfactory alignment cannot be achieved [25]. In those aged 65–75 years, the choice between fixation and arthroplasty is based on patients preferences and overall health [26]. For patients over 75 years of age, femoral head replacement is generally recommended, provided there are no contraindications to surgery [27].

Cannulated screws fixation is well established for Pauwels type I and II fractures [28]. It creattes a stable three-dimensional structure, provides rotational stability, and facilitates healing [29]. However, in Pauwels type Ⅲ fracture, this technique is less effective,as these fractures are prone to complications such as nonunion, delayed union, malunion, screw pullout and emoral head necrosis [30]. Therefore, enhancing internal fixation to resist the high shear forces at the fracture site line is critical [31]. While the Dynamic Hip Screw (DHS) provides stability against vertical shear, it is associated with significant surgical trauma, substantial bone loss and limited rotational control [32]. Similarly,simple locking plates have a high failure rates due to insufficient interfragmentary compression. The surgical approach evaluated in this study combines cannulated screws with a lateral femoral neck support plate. This construct is designed to resist both vertical and rotational shear forces, thereby preventing femoral head rotation and redisplacement while maintaining vascular supply to the femoral head. By ensuring stable reduction and reliable fixation, this technique promotes fracture healing.Postoperatively, strict bed rest and avoidance of early weight-bearing further support the fracture healing.

Based on the findings of this study, the combined technique can be recommended as a viable and potentially superior option for specific patient populations with Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures. We particularly advocate for its use in young and active patients, where the primary goals are to achieve absolute stability, maximize the chance of anatomic union, and preserve the native hip joint. The enhanced construct stability provided by the lateral plate may be especially beneficial in cases with comminution at the posterior or inferior femoral neck cortex, where standard cancellous screw fixation alone is often insufficient to counteract the high shear forces. Furthermore, for fractures with extreme verticality (Pauwels angle approaching or exceeding 70°), or in scenarios where intraoperative instability is noted after provisional fixation with cannulated screws alone, the addition of a lateral support plate serves as a robust salvage and reinforcement strategy. While the technique requires a slightly longer operative time, we believe this is a worthwhile trade-off in these high-risk patients to mitigate the devastating consequences of fixation failure, nonunion, and avascular necrosis. For older, lower-demand patients, or those with less vertically oriented fractures, three cannulated screws may remain a sufficient and less invasive option.

DHS has been a mainstay for unstable femoral neck fractures due to its ability to provide dynamic compression and resist shear forces [33]. However, the DHS is associated with significant surgical trauma, substantial bone removal, and suboptimal anti-rotation performance due to its single screw design, potentially leading to higher failure rates in very vertical fractures [34]. In contrast, our technique aims to achieve similar stability against shear forces through the lateral plate while augmenting anti-rotation with multiple cannulated screws, and potentially preserving more bone stock. More recently, the Femoral Neck System (FNS) was introduced as an integrated device combining a bolt for compression and an anti-rotation pin with a lateral plate [35]. While our study did not directly compare against FNS, the biomechanical principle of combining a lateral plate with multiple points of fixation in the femoral head is similar. The FNS offers the advantage of being a pre-assembled system, potentially simplifying surgery [36]. However, our construct may offer greater flexibility in screw placement to accommodate specific fracture patterns and could be more accessible in settings where specialized implant systems like FNS are not available. Compared to simple locking plates, which have shown high failure rates due to lack of adequate fracture site compression, our technique incorporates lag screws through the plate to achieve interfragmentary compression, thereby enhancing stability. Therefore, this technique appears to occupy a valuable middle ground, offering the stability benefits of fixed-angle devices like FNS and DHS while leveraging the familiarity and flexibility of cannulated screws, though future direct comparative studies are warranted to confirm these potential advantages.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, as a single-center, retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, there is an inherent risk of selection bias and limited generalizability. Second, the variability in surgical technique, despite being performed by experienced surgeons, could have influenced the outcomes. Factors such as the precise placement of screws and plates, the quality of reduction achieved, and individual surgical nuance are difficult to fully standardize in a retrospective setting and may introduce confounding. Although the follow-up period was sufficient to assess fracture union and early functional outcomes, a minimum of six months is relatively short for the definitive evaluation of avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Future studies with a planned follow-up of 2–5 years are warranted to conclusively determine the long-term effect of lateral support plate fixation on the incidence of this serious complication. In subsequent research, we aim to increase the sample size and extend the follow-up duration to provide more robust and definitive evidence regarding the clinical efficacy of this treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, our comparative study demonstrates that for treating Pauwels type III femoral neck fractures, the combination of cannulated screws with a lateral femoral neck support plate is associated with superior outcomes compared to cannulated screws alone. Specifically, the combined fixation method provided better hip function recovery (as indicated by higher HHS) and reduced postoperative pain (as indicated by lower VAS) at the 6-month follow-up. Although the combined technique required a longer operation time, it did not lead to a significant increase in major postoperative complications. Therefore, the cannulated screws combined with lateral femoral neck support plate internal fixation system present a viable and effective surgical option for managing this challenging fracture type, particularly in scenarios where enhanced stability is desired.
