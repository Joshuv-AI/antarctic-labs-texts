Asymptotics of analytic torsion for congruence quotients of SL ⁡ ( n , ℝ ) / SO ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)
Tim Berland

Abstract




Abstract.

In this paper we prove a sharpened asymptotic for the growth of analytic torsion of congruence quotients of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)
in terms of the volume. The result is based on a new bound on the trace of the heat kernel in this setting, allowing control of the large time behaviour of certain orbital integrals, as well as a careful analysis of error terms. The result requires the existence of
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations, which we define and show exists in plenitude for any
\lambda
. The motivation is possible applications to torsion in the cohomology of arithmetic groups, although the connection in this setting is as of yet conjectural.

1.
Introduction

Analytic torsion is an invariant of Riemannian manifolds, defined by Ray and Singer in the
70
’s (
[
RS71
]
). More recently, it was used by Bergeron and Venkatesh to study the torsion in the homology of cocompact arithmetic groups (
[
BV13
]
). This made it desirable to extend the definition of analytic torsion to manifolds associated to non-cocompact arithmetic groups. This was first accompished by Raimbault (
[
Rai19
]
) and Müller–Pfaff (
[
MP13
]
) for finite volume hyperbolic manifolds. Later, it was extended further by Matz and Müller, first for congruence subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}(n)
in
[
MM17
]
, and later for more general arithmetic groups in
[
MM23
]
.

One of the reasons for studying torsion in the homology of arithmetic groups is the Ash conjecture (
[
Ash92
]
), partially proven by Scholze (
[
Sch15
]
). In rough terms, this conjecture links systems of Hecke eigenvalues occurring in the mod
p
cohomology of arithmetic groups to Galois representations with matching Frobenius eigenvalues. See (
[
BV13
]
, Section
6
) for a discussion on the connection between analytic torsion and the Ash conjecture. As explained in Scholze’s paper, by applying the work of Bergeron–Venkatesh and others, in certain situations the Ash conjecture predicts the existence of many Galois representations, more than is predicted by the global Langlands correspondence.

Further control of the behaviour of analytic torsion should translate to better understanding of torsion homology, and conjecturally to existence of Galois representations. With this motivation we give improved asymptotics of analytic torsion in terms of the volume of the associated manifolds. To state our theorem and the connection to homology, we recall the setups of
[
BV13
]
and
[
MM20
]
.

1.1.
Analytic torsion in the compact setting

Let
X
be a compact oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension
d
, and let
\rho
be a finite-dimensional representation of
\pi_{1}(X)
. To such a representation, one may associate a flat vector bundle
E_{\rho}\to X
over
X
. Fixing a Hermitian fiber metric on
E_{\rho}
, we let
\Delta_{p}(\rho)
be the Laplace operator on
E_{\rho}
-valued
p
-forms. For
t>0
, we define
e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\rho)}
to be the heat operator, and we let
k_{p}(\rho)=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\Delta_{p}(\rho)
. One can associate a zeta function
\zeta_{p}(s,\rho)
to
\Delta_{p}(\rho)
, a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
defined by

\displaystyle\zeta_{p}(s,\rho)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\operatorname{Tr}e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\rho)}-k_{p}(\rho))t^{s-1}dt

(1.1)

for
\Re(s)>\frac{d}{2}
. We then define analytic torsion
T_{X}(\rho)
of
M
by

\displaystyle\log T_{X}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{d}(-1)^{p}p\frac{d}{ds}\zeta_{p}(s;\rho)|_{s=0}.

(1.2)

There is a corresponding
L^{2}
-invariant, called
L^{2}
-torsion denoted
T^{(2)}_{X}(\rho)
, defined by Lott and Mathai (resp.
[
Lot92
]
,
[
Mat92
]
). For
\tilde{X}
the universal covering of
X
, it may be expressed as

\displaystyle\log T^{(2)}_{X}(\rho)=t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\rho)\operatorname{vol}(X),

(1.3)

where
t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau)
is an invariant associated to
\tilde{X}
and
\rho
.

To relate this to arithmetic groups, let
G
be a semisimple algebraic group with
G(\mathbb{R})
of non-compact type, let
K
be a maximal compact subgroup of
G(\mathbb{R})
, and suppose
\Gamma\subseteq G(\mathbb{Q})
is a torsion-free congruence lattice. Assume further that
\Gamma
is cocompact in
G(\mathbb{R})
. Then
\tilde{X}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{R})/K
is a Riemannian symmetric space, and
X\coloneqq\Gamma\backslash\tilde{X}
is a compact locally symmetric space, and as
\tilde{X}
is contractible, we have
\pi_{1}(X)=\Gamma
. Thus, given
\Gamma
and a finite-dimensional representation
\rho
of
\Gamma
, we associate to it the analytic torsion
T_{X}(\rho)
of the space
X
as defined above.

By a theorem of Cheeger and Müller (
[
Che79
]
,
[
Mül78
]
,
[
Mül93
]
), there is an equality of analytic torsion and
Reidemeister torsion
on
X
, the latter being a topological invariant of
X
computed in terms of homology (see
[
Rei35
]
,
[
Fra35
]
). Since
X
is a classifying space for
\Gamma
, the homology of
X
computes the homology of
\Gamma
, and thus one may study the homology of
\Gamma
through its associated analytic torsion.

Now, consider a descending chain of finite index subgroups
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}\supseteq\Gamma_{1}\supseteq\dots
in
G(\mathbb{Q})
satisfying
\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{i}=\{1\}
, and let
X_{i}\coloneqq\Gamma_{i}\backslash\tilde{X}
. Let
\tau
be an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
. By abuse of notation, we set
T_{X_{i}}(\tau)\coloneqq T_{X_{i}}(\tau|_{\Gamma_{i}})
. Given a non-degeneracy condition on
\tau
, namely requiring that it be
strongly acyclic
, Bergeron and Venkatesh prove (
[
BV13
]
, Theorem
4.5
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\log T_{X_{i}}(\tau)}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{i})}=t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau).

(1.4)

Bergeron and Venkatesh show that
t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau)
is non-zero if and only if the
deficiency
of
G
, defined
\delta(G)\coloneqq\operatorname{rank}G(\mathbb{R})-\operatorname{rank}K
, is
1
(
[
BV13
]
, Proposition
5.2
). The assumption that
\tau
is strongly acyclic also guarantees that the free homology is trivial, so Reidemeister torsion is expressed solely in torsion homology. Using the Cheeger-Müller theorem, this is interpreted as there being a lot of torsion in the homology when
\delta(G)=1
, and little torsion in all other cases. ”Little torsion” should here be understood in a weak sense, as we only know that there is not full exponential growth of analytic torsion in terms of the volume. A natural question is then, also presented in
[
AGMY20
]
: what growth rate should we expect when the deficiency is not
1
, and can we more precisely describe the behaviour of analytic torsion in terms of the volume? The results of this paper is a first step in answering these questions.

The result (
1.4
) can be thought of as an approximation theorem. In particular, noting that every
\Gamma_{i}
is a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
, we see that
\operatorname{vol}(X_{i})=\operatorname{vol}(X)[\Gamma:\Gamma_{i}]
, and thus the result is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\log T_{X_{i}}(\tau)}{[\Gamma:\Gamma_{i}]}=\log T^{(2)}_{X}(\tau).

(1.5)

In words, analytic torsion associated to a tower of subgroups can be used to approximate the
L^{2}
-torsion associated to the group. Keeping in mind the equality of analytic torsion and Reidemeister torsion, this statement should be thought of as a torsion version of the analogous result on Betti numbers
b_{p}(X)
and
L^{2}
-Betti numbers
b_{p}^{(2)}(X)
, proven by W. Lück (
[
Lüc94
]
), which we state here in our setting.

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{b_{p}(X_{i})}{[\Gamma:\Gamma_{i}]}=b_{p}^{(2)}(X).

(1.6)

To further the analogy, here there is a criterion based on the deficiency as well: We have that
b_{p}^{(2)}(X)=0
unless
\delta(G)=0
. For a comprehensive survey on approximation of
L^{2}
-invariants, see
[
Lüc16
]
.

For certain approximation theorems, their rate of convergence has been explored (see e.g.
[
BLLS12
]
, and
[
Lüc16
]
Chapter 5), but is not very developed for analytic and
L^{2}
-torsion. This is mostly due to the fact that this approximation result is still conjectural in general, and only proven in very particular cases, such as the setting of this paper. Viewed in this light, Theorem
1.1
presented below can be seen as a first example of a rate of convergence result in this setting.

Many of the most important arithmetic groups in number theory are not cocompact. In the general setting of non-cocompact arithmetic groups in semisimple Lie groups, we do not have a replacement for the Cheeger-Müller theorem, though certain special cases have recently been proven (see
[
MR20
]
,
[
MR24
]
). The non-cocompact setting do, however, have one advantage, as it allows certain terms to contribute (namely the non-identity unipotent part, see section
7.2
) which vanish in the cocompact setting, and these terms are very probable candidates for where second order terms should show up. For this reason, the present paper will focus on the non-cocompact setting.

1.2.
Analytic torsion in the non-compact setting

To state our main theorem, let us recall the setup of Matz and Müller (
[
MM17
]
,
[
MM20
]
). We switch to an adelic framework and focus our attention on
\operatorname{SL}(n)
,
n\geq 2
. Let
\mathbb{A}
be the ring of adeles of
\mathbb{Q}
, with
\mathbb{A}_{f}
the finite adeles. Let
X=\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)
. For
N\geq 3
, let
K(N)\subseteq\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{A}_{f})
be the open compact subgroup given by
K(N)=\prod_{p}K_{p}(p^{\nu_{p}(N)})
, with
K_{p}(p^{e})\coloneqq\operatorname{ker}(\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p^{e}\mathbb{Z}_{p}))
. Define

\displaystyle X(N)\coloneqq\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Q})\backslash(\tilde{X}\times\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{A}_{f}))/K(N)).

By strong approximation, we get that
X(N)=\Gamma(N)\backslash\tilde{X}
, with
\Gamma(N)\subseteq\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
the standard principal congruence subgroup of level
N
. Given a finite-dimensional representation
\tau
of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})
, one can now follow the construction of the Laplace operator
\Delta_{p}(\tau)
of the Laplace operator on
p
-forms with values in
E_{\tau}
, as above. As
X(N)
is not compact, however,
\Delta_{p}(\tau)
has continuous spectrum, and hence we need a refined definition of the analytic torsion. This is constructed by Matz and Müller in
[
MM17
]
. Most strikingly, they define the regularized trace of the heat kernel
\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})
as the geometric side of the
Arthur trace formula
for
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})
applied to the test function
h_{t}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{K(N)}
, where
h_{t}^{\tau,p}
is the trace of the heat kernel and
\chi_{K(N)}
the normalized characteristic function of
K(N)
. We give the full definition in
Section
5.4
. This reduces to the standard definition in the cocompact setting. In
[
MM20
]
, they prove the same limit behaviour as Bergeron-Venkatesh for this setup, namely the approximation formula

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log T_{X(N)}(\tau)}{\operatorname{vol}(X(N))}=t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau).

Formulated in terms of asymptotics of analytic torsion, the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle\log T_{X(N)}(\tau)=\log T^{(2)}_{X(N)}(\tau)+o(\operatorname{vol}(X(N)))\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

(1.7)

1.3.
Results

As a step towards better understanding the behavior of analytic torsion in terms of the volume, in this paper we prove a stronger version of the asymptotic (
1.7
). In particular, the new result provides an improved upper bound on analytic torsion when the deficiency is not
1
, as well as a bound on second order terms when the deficiency is
1
. We need a slightly stronger non-degeneracy assumption on
\tau
which we call
\lambda
-strongly acyclic. Our result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
\tau
is a
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representation of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})
, for a certain
\lambda
depending only on
n
. Then there exists some
a>0
such that

\log T_{X(N)}(\tau)=\log T_{X(N)}^{(2)}(\tau)+O(\operatorname{vol}(X(N))N^{-(n-1)}\log(N)^{a})

as
N
tends to infinity.

Computing the size of
\operatorname{vol}(X(N))
in terms of
N
(see the appendix), the theorem implies a more intrinsic version:

\displaystyle\log T_{X(N)}(\tau)=\log T_{X(N)}^{(2)}(\tau)+O(\operatorname{vol}(X(N))^{1-\frac{1}{n+1}}\log(\operatorname{vol}(X(N))^{a})

(1.8)

as
N\to\infty
.

To ensure that the technical constraint is justified, we prove the existence of infinitely many
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations for any connected semisimple algebraic group
G
with
\delta(G)\geq 1
. This result is an extension of the result (
[
BV13
]
, Section
8.1
), stating that strongly acyclic representations always exist for semisimple
G
with
\delta(G)=1
.

It seems likely that up to log-terms, the bound in the theorem is strict in the case of deficiency
1
, though this is not proven here. When the deficiency is not
1
, a better upper bound is expected. A lower bound would be extremely interesting as well, but this is not in the scope of this paper.

Because of technical reasons, we will work with
\operatorname{GL}(n)
instead of
\operatorname{SL}(n)
. For
Y(N)
the analogous adelic locally symmetric space of
\operatorname{GL}(n)
, one can define analytic torsion
T_{Y(N)}(\tau)
in a similar manner (see (
5.4
) for the general definition). Our proof is then that of Theorem
5.1
, which is the analogous statement of Theorem
1.1
in the setting of
Y(N)
, and we show the two theorems are equivalent.

The proof is based on the framework of
[
MM20
]
. The main contribution of this paper lies in the change of perspective to allow certain parameters, namely the compactification parameter (see Section
5.4
) and the truncation parameter
T
(see (
7.1
)) to vary with the level
N
, and in the work needed to allow for this variation. In particular, we show the existence of infinitely many
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations of any connected semisimple algebraic group (Proposition
3.2
), and we prove a new bound on the trace of the heat kernel in the large time aspect (Proposition
4.2
) which may be of independent interest. The bound is used to gain control over large time behaviour of the archimedean orbital integrals showing up in the analysis of the Arthur trace formula.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we present the setup for the heat kernel on symmetric spaces. In Section
3
, we prove the existence of infinitely many
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations for any semisimple algebraic group. Section
4
gives a proof of large
t
asymptotics of the trace of the heat kernel. We give a brief introduction to the Arthur trace formula and its geometric expansions in Section
5
and define analytic torsion, as well as express the trace formula applied to a compactification of our test function. In Section
6
, we handle the asymptotics of our local orbital integrals. Finally, we combine all the ingredients in Section
7
to prove our main theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Arithmetic manifolds

This section will deal with the general setup of
G
being a reductive algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with
\mathbb{Q}
-split zenter
Z_{G}
. See (
[
Art05
]
, Section I.
2
) for a partial reference. Let
K_{f}\subseteq G(\mathbb{A}_{f})
be any open compact subgroup. We write
G(\mathbb{A})=G(\mathbb{A})^{1}\times A_{G}
for
A_{G}
the identity component of
Z_{G}
, and we set
G(\mathbb{R})^{1}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{A})^{1}\cap G(\mathbb{R})
, which is then a semisimple real Lie group. We fix a Cartan involution
\theta
of
G(\mathbb{R})
and let
K
denote its fixpoints. We set
\tilde{X}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{R})^{1}/K
. The group
G(\mathbb{R})
has a natural action on the finite double coset space,

\displaystyle A_{G}(\mathbb{R})^{0}G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A})/G(\mathbb{R})K_{f}.

Taking a set of representatives
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}
for the double cosets in
G(\mathbb{A}_{f})
and defining

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}\coloneqq(G(\mathbb{R})\times z_{j}K_{f}z_{j}^{-1})\cap G(\mathbb{Q})

for all
1\leq j\leq m
, the action induces the decomposition

\displaystyle A_{G}(\mathbb{R})^{0}G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A})/K_{f}\cong\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}(\Gamma_{j}\backslash G(\mathbb{R})^{1}).

(2.1)

Now we define the arithmetic manifold associated to
K_{f}
. Set

\displaystyle X(K_{f})\coloneqq G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash(\tilde{X}\times G(\mathbb{A}_{f}))/K_{f}.

(2.2)

Using (
2.1
), we get

\displaystyle X(K_{f})\cong\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}(\Gamma_{j}\backslash\tilde{X}).

(2.3)

Here,
\Gamma_{j}\backslash\tilde{X}
is a locally symmetric space. We will assume that
K_{f}
is neat such that
X(K_{f})
is a locally symmetric manifold of finite volume.

2.2.
The heat kernel

In the setup above, given a finite-dimensional unitary representation of
K
, we let
\tilde{E}_{\nu}
be the associated homogeneous Hermitian vector bundle over
\tilde{X}
. Using homogeneity, one can push down this vector bundle to give locally homogeneous Hermitian vector bundles over each component
\Gamma_{j}\backslash\tilde{X}
. Taking the disjoint union of these vector bundles, we get a locally homogeneous vector bundle
E_{\nu}
over
X(K_{f})
.

As it is sufficient to work on each component in (
2.3
), we continue this section with the simplifying assumption that
G
is a connected semisimple algebraic group. Let
K\subseteq G(\mathbb{R})
be a maximal compact subgroup, and
\Gamma\subseteq G(\mathbb{R})
a torsion-free lattice. We let
\tilde{X}=G(\mathbb{R})/K
and
X=\Gamma\backslash\tilde{X}
. Take a finite-dimensional unitary representation
(\nu,V_{\nu})
of
K
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\nu}
, and define

\displaystyle\tilde{E_{\nu}}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{R})\times_{\nu}V_{\nu}

as the associated homogenous vector bundle over
\tilde{X}
. The action of
K
on
G(\mathbb{R})
is by right multiplication. The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\nu}
induces a
G(\mathbb{R})
-invariant metric
\tilde{h}_{\nu}
on this vector bundle.
Let
E_{\nu}\coloneqq\Gamma\backslash\tilde{E_{\nu}}
be the associated locally homogenous vector bundle over
X
, with metric
h_{\nu}
induced by
\tilde{h}_{\nu}
using
G(\mathbb{R})
-invariance.
Let
C^{\infty}(\tilde{X},\tilde{E}_{\nu})
denote the space of smooth sections of
\tilde{E}_{\nu}
. Now, set

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(G(\mathbb{R}),\nu)\coloneqq\{

\displaystyle f:G(\mathbb{R})\to V_{\nu}\mid f\in C^{\infty},

\displaystyle f(gk)=\nu(k)^{-1}f(g)\>\forall k\in K,g\in G(\mathbb{R})\}.

The following isomorphism (
[
Mia80
]
, p. 4) allows us to understand this geometric setup through representation theory.

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(\tilde{X},\tilde{E}_{\nu})\xrightarrow{\sim}C^{\infty}(G(\mathbb{R}),\nu).

(2.4)

This extends to an isometry of corresponding
L^{2}
-spaces. Let
\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{R}))
denote Harish-Chandra’s Schwartz space, and
\mathcal{C}^{q}(G(\mathbb{R}))
Harish-Chandra’s
L^{q}
-Schwartz space.

We now specify the above to our setting. Let
(\tau,V_{\tau})
be an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
,
E_{\tau}\coloneqq E_{\tau|_{K}}
, and
F_{\tau}
the flat vector bundle over
X
associated to the restriction of
\tau
to
\Gamma
. Then we have a canonical isomorphism (see
[
MM63
]
, Proposition
3.1
)

\displaystyle E_{\tau}\cong F_{\tau}.

As
K
is compact there exists an inner product on
V_{\tau}
with respect to which
\tau|_{K}
is unitary. Fix such an inner product. From this we induce a metric on
E_{\tau}
, and hence on
F_{\tau}
as well. Define
\Lambda^{p}(X,F_{\tau})\coloneqq\Lambda^{p}T^{*}(X)\otimes F_{\tau}
. Under the isomorphism above, this is isomorphic to the vector bundle associated to the representation
\Lambda^{p}\text{Ad}^{*}\otimes\tau
of
K
on
\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau}
. We will denote this representation by
\nu_{\tau,p}
.

Let
\Delta_{p}(\tau)
be the Laplace operator on
\Lambda^{p}(X,F_{\tau})
, and let
\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)
be its lift to the universal covering
\tilde{X}
. Let also
\tilde{F}_{\tau}
be the pullback of
F_{\tau}
to
\tilde{X}
. Then
\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)
is an operator on the space
\Lambda^{p}(\tilde{X},\tilde{F}_{\tau})
of
\tilde{F}_{\tau}
-valued
p
-forms on
\tilde{X}
. By (
2.4
), we get an isomorphism

\displaystyle\Lambda^{p}(\tilde{X},\tilde{F}_{\tau})\cong C^{\infty}(G(\mathbb{R}),\nu_{\tau,p}).

(2.5)

Let
R
be the right regular representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
on
C^{\infty}(G(\mathbb{R}),\nu_{\tau,p})
, and
\Omega
the Casimir element of
G(\mathbb{R})
. With respect to the above isomorphism, Kuga’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)=\tau(\Omega)-R(\Omega).

(2.6)

The operator
\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)
is formally self-adjoint and non-negative. Regarded as an operator with domain the space of compactly supported smooth
p
-forms, it has a unique self-adjoint extension to
L^{2}(\tilde{X},\tilde{F}_{\tau})
, the
L^{2}
-sections of
\tilde{F}_{\tau}
, which we by abuse of notation will also denote
\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)
. This extension inherits non-negativity. We denote by
e^{-t\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)}
, with
t>0
, the heat semigroup associated to
\tilde{\Delta}_{p}(\tau)
. Considered as a bounded operator on
L^{2}(G(\mathbb{R}),\nu_{\tau,p})
under the extension of the isomorphism (
2.5
), it is a convolution operator, and thus it has a kernel

\displaystyle H_{t}^{\tau,p}:G(\mathbb{R})\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})

called the heat kernel. It satisfies the following covariance property

\displaystyle H_{t}^{\tau,p}(k^{-1}gk^{\prime})=\nu_{\tau,p}(k)^{-1}\circ H_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)\circ\nu_{\tau,p}(k^{\prime}),\quad\forall k,k^{\prime}\in K,g\in G(\mathbb{R}).

(2.7)

By analogy of the proof of (
[
BM83
]
, Proposition
2.4
), we have that

\displaystyle H_{t}^{\tau,p}\in\mathcal{C}^{q}(G(\mathbb{R}))\otimes\operatorname{End}(\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})

(2.8)

for any
q>0
. We may define

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}H_{t}^{\tau,p}(g),\quad g\in G(\mathbb{R}),

(2.9)

for
\operatorname{tr}
being the trace over
\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau}
. By (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we have that
h_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)\in\mathcal{C}^{q}(G(\mathbb{R}))_{K\times K}
for any
q>0
, with
\mathcal{C}^{q}(G(\mathbb{R}))_{K\times K}
the subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{q}(G(\mathbb{R}))
consisting of left and right
K
-finite functions.

3.
Strongly acyclic representations

We continue with the setup from Section
2.2
. In particular,
G
is a connected semisimple algebraic group. In the setting that
\Gamma\backslash\tilde{X}
is compact, (
[
BV13
]
, §
4
) defines an irreducible finite-dimensional representation
(\tau,V_{\tau})
of
G(\mathbb{R})
to be
strongly acyclic
if there exists some positive constant
\eta>0
such that every eigenvalue of
\Delta_{p}(\tau)
is
\geq\eta
for every choice of
\Gamma
and every
p
. Furthermore, they show that for any such
\tau
not fixed by the Cartan involution, then
\tau
must be strongly acyclic. This latter condition is shown to give the necessary bounds in the noncompact setting in (
[
MM20
]
, Lemma
6.1
), and is used throughout that paper.

For our purposes, we need a slightly stronger condition that we define now. This will be the central object of this section.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(\tau,V_{\tau})
be a finite-dimensional representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
, and let
\lambda>0
. We say that
\tau
is
\lambda
-strongly acyclic
if

\displaystyle\tau(\Omega)-\pi(\Omega)\geq\lambda

for every irreducible unitary representation
\pi
satisfying
\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}\otimes V_{\tau}^{*},\pi)\neq 0
, for some
p
.

We see the analogy to the definition of strongly acyclic by comparing to (
2.6
). For
\tau
a
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representation, we will sometimes say that
\lambda
is the
spectral gap
of
\tau
. We now prove that there are plenty of such representations to choose from.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
\lambda>0
, there exists infinitely many
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations of
G(\mathbb{R})
if
\delta(G(\mathbb{R}))\geq 1
.

The proof takes up the remainder of the section. Let
F=V_{\tau}^{*}
be the dual of the representation space. Consider
\mathfrak{g}\coloneqq\text{Lie}(G(\mathbb{R}))
with
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
its complexification. Recall that
\theta
is a Cartan involution on
\mathfrak{g}
with Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
. This also gives the decomposition
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathfrak{k}_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\mathfrak{h}^{+}
be a Cartan subalgebra in
\mathfrak{k}
. Denote by
\mathfrak{h}
the centralizer of
\mathfrak{h}^{+}
in
\mathfrak{g}
, which is then a Cartan subalgebra in
\mathfrak{g}
.

The following two lemmas are elementary and certainly well known, but we were not able to find precise references. Hence, we will give the proofs.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists an abelian subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{-}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
such that
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{-}
.

Proof.

Take any basis
(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
of
\mathfrak{h}^{+}
, and extend it to a basis

(K_{1},\dots,K_{d},X_{1},\dots,X_{s})

of
\mathfrak{h}
. Using the Cartan decomposition, write
X_{i}=M_{i}+P_{i}
for
M_{i}\in\mathfrak{k}
and
P_{i}\in\mathfrak{p}
. Since
[K,K^{\prime}]\in\mathfrak{k}
and
[P,K]\in\mathfrak{p}
for any
\lambda,K^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{k}
and
P\in\mathfrak{p}
, and
\mathfrak{k}\cap\mathfrak{p}=0
, we see that
[M_{i},K_{j}]+[P_{i},K_{j}]=[X_{i},K_{j}]=0
for all
i,j
implies that
[M_{i},K_{j}]=[P_{i},K_{j}]=0
. In particular,
M_{i}\in\mathfrak{k}
commutes with all of
\mathfrak{h}^{+}
, but as this is a
maximal
abelian subalgebra of
\mathfrak{k}
, we get
M_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}^{+}
. Thus, it becomes clear that

\mathfrak{h}=\text{span}\{K_{1},\dots,K_{d},P_{1},\dots,P_{s}\}.

∎

Let
\Phi_{k}
be the roots of
\mathfrak{h}^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}
in
\mathfrak{k}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
\Phi
be the roots of
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
in
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Pick
\Phi^{+}_{k}
a set of positive roots of
\Phi_{k}
, and let
\Phi^{+}
be a
compatible
choice of positive roots of
\Phi
(see
[
BW00
]
, II, §
6.6
). A compatible root system
\Phi^{+}_{k}
is defined as a root system which is closed under the Cartan involution (acting by precomposition), and every element in
\Phi^{+}_{k}
is the restriction of some element in
\Phi^{+}
to
\mathfrak{h}^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\rho
and
\rho_{k}
be the respective half-sum of positive roots. Set
W
to be the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
W^{1}
be the subset of elements
w
such that
w\Phi^{+}
is again a compatible system of positive roots.

We will be concerned with the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
, which lives inside the real span of the roots,
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi
. To utilize our decomposition above, we need the following elementary lemma. We extend
(\mathfrak{h}^{+})^{*}
to a subspace of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
be setting elements to be identically zero on
\mathfrak{h}^{-}
, and extend
(\mathfrak{h}^{-})^{*}
by setting elements identically zero on
\mathfrak{h}^{+}
. We further identify these as (real) subspaces of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
using that
\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathfrak{h}^{*}\oplus i\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have a decomposition
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi=i(\mathfrak{h}^{+})^{*}\oplus(\mathfrak{h}^{-})^{*}
. The Killing form induces a
W
- and
\theta
-invariant inner product on this space such that the decomposition is orthogonal.

Proof.

Define
\mathfrak{h}_{0}^{*}\coloneqq\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi
. Given
H\in\mathfrak{h}
, note that the values
\varphi(H)
for
\varphi\in\Phi\sqcup\{0\}
are exactly the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{ad}_{H}
, by definition. Also, it is an elementary fact that for any element
X
in a compact subalgebra, all eigenvalues of
\operatorname{ad}_{X}
are imaginary – one could argue as follows: Its Lie group is compact, thus its action on the Lie algebra by conjugation has eigenvalues of absolute value
1
. Now take logarithms.

As
\mathfrak{k}
is a compact Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the above implies that for
K\in\mathfrak{h}^{+}
and
\varphi\in\Phi_{k}
we have
\varphi(K)\in i\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the roots must take values in
\mathbb{R}
on
i\mathfrak{h}^{+}
. Similarly, as
i\mathfrak{p}
is a subspace of the compact real form
\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus i\mathfrak{p}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the roots must take values in
i\mathbb{R}
on
i\mathfrak{p}
, and thus values in
\mathbb{R}
on
\mathfrak{p}
. This proves the inclusion
\mathfrak{h}_{0}^{*}\subseteq i(\mathfrak{h}^{+})^{*}\oplus(\mathfrak{h}^{-})^{*}
, and equality follows from comparing dimensions.

To prove the second statement, recall that the Killing form
B
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is negative definite on
\mathfrak{k}
, thus positive definite on
i\mathfrak{k}
, and positive definite on
\mathfrak{p}
. Hence, it is positive definite on
i\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, giving an inner product on the space, and in particular also on the subspace
i\mathfrak{h}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{-}
. Using this to identify
i\mathfrak{h}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{-}
with its dual, we get an inner product on
\mathfrak{h}_{0}^{*}
. It is well known that the Killing form is invariant under any
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
-automorphism, and in particular it is invariant under the Weyl group
W
and the Cartan involution
\theta
. Finally, the fact that
\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
is an orthogonal decomposition under the Killing form immediately implies the same for
i\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, which also translates to its dual.
∎

As this may be a nonstandard choice of positive root systems to the reader, let us consider an example.

Example 3.5
.

Consider
G(\mathbb{R})=\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})
with the usual Cartan involution on its Lie algebra
\theta(X)=-X^{t}
and Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
into skew-symmetric and symmetric traceless real
3\times 3
matrices. Let

\displaystyle K=\begin{pmatrix}&1&\\
-1&&\\
&&\end{pmatrix}\>,\quad P=\begin{pmatrix}1&&\\
&1&\\
&&-2\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathfrak{h}\coloneqq\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{K\}
is a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{k}
, and its centralizer is
\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{K,P\}
. Complexifying, we have
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{K,P\}
. One can check that the adjoint action of
K
on
\mathfrak{k}_{\mathbb{C}}
has eigenvalues
0,i,-i
with respective eigenspaces

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{+}_{\mathbb{C}},\quad\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\
&&i\\
-1&-i&\end{pmatrix},\quad\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\
&&-i\\
-1&i&\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, we may choose a positive root system
\Phi^{+}_{k}\coloneqq\{\varphi\}
for
\mathfrak{h}^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}
with
\varphi:\mathfrak{h}^{+}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
\varphi(K)=i
.

Consider now the dual space
\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
and the dual basis
\{K^{*},P^{*}\}
defined by
K^{*}(K)=1
,
K^{*}(P)=0
and analogously for
P^{*}
. One can then check that the six roots of
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
, in terms of this basis, are
\pm(2i,0),\pm(i,3)
and
\pm(i,-3)
. As the Cartan involution acts by precomposition, we see that the dual basis inherits the action of the Cartan involution from the original basis, i.e.
\theta(K^{*})=K^{*}
and
\theta(P^{*})=-P^{*}
.

In particular, the action of the Cartan involution on a vector written in the dual basis is
(a,b)\mapsto(a,-b)
. We then have exactly one choice of a compatible positive root system for
\mathfrak{h}
: As it must restrict to
\Phi_{k}^{+}
, we must pick
(i,3)
or
(i,-3)
, and as we have to be closed under the Cartan involution we have to pick both. Thus, by the axioms of positive root systems, we see that
\Phi^{+}=\{(i,3),(i,-3),(2i,0)\}
.

We return to the general setting. As the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{k}
preserves the Killing form on
\mathfrak{p}
, which defines
\mathfrak{so}(\mathfrak{p})
, we get a natural map
\mathfrak{k}\to\mathfrak{so}(\mathfrak{p})
, and thus, we get an induced representation of
\mathfrak{k}
on
S\coloneqq\text{Spin}(\mathfrak{p})
. In the proof of (
[
BV13
]
, Lemma
4.1
), it is noted that every highest weight of
F\otimes S
is of the form
\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\theta\mu)+w\rho-\rho_{k}
for some weight
\mu
of
F
and some
w\in W^{1}
. We use this to give a more precise statement.

Lemma 3.6
.

Any highest weight of
F\otimes S
is always of the form
\frac{1}{2}(\nu+\theta\nu)+w\rho-\rho_{k}
for
\nu
the highest weight of
F
, and some
w\in W^{1}
.

Proof.

Let
\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{0}+\theta\mu_{0})+w_{0}\rho-\rho_{k}
be a highest weight of
F\otimes S
, for some
\mu_{0}
and
w_{0}\in W^{1}
. We show that this
\mu_{0}
is
\nu
. Let
M(\mu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\theta\mu)+w_{0}\rho-\rho_{k}
. We claim that if
\mu\succ\mu^{\prime}
, then
M(\mu)\succ M(\mu^{\prime})
. Indeed, as the Cartan involution fixes the set of positive roots, it fixes the fundamental Weyl chamber, such that
\mu
is a positive combination of positive roots if and only if the same is true for
\theta(\mu)
. Now we see that

M(\mu)-M(\mu^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}((\mu-\mu^{\prime})+\theta(\mu-\mu^{\prime})),

and so, if we assume
(\mu-\mu^{\prime})
is a positive combination, so is
\theta(\mu-\mu^{\prime})
, and thus so is their half-sum, proving the claim.

As every irreducible representation has a unique highest weight, we have a unique highest weight
\nu
for
F
. By the above, we immediately get
M(\nu)\succeq M(\mu_{0})
, with equality iff
\nu=\mu_{0}
.
∎

It is shown in (
[
BV13
]
, Lemma
4.1
) that

\displaystyle\tau(\Omega)-\pi(\Omega)\geq\eta(\tau)

(3.1)

for each irreducible unitary representation
\pi
of
G(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}\otimes V_{\tau}^{*},\pi)\neq 0
, where
\eta(\tau)
is given by

\displaystyle\eta(\tau)=|\nu+\rho|^{2}-\left|\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\theta\mu)+w\rho\right|^{2}

(3.2)

for the choice of
\mu
and
w
given above (
[
BV13
]
, (
4.1.2
)), and the norm associated to the inner product induced by the Killing form. By Lemma
3.2
, this
\mu
must be
\nu
, so we have

\displaystyle\eta(\tau)=|\nu+\rho|^{2}-\left|\frac{1}{2}(\nu+\theta\nu)+w\rho\right|^{2}

Consider
(K_{1}^{*},\dots,K_{d}^{*},P_{1}^{*},\dots,P_{s}^{*})
, an orthonormal basis respecting the decomposition in Lemma
3.4
. With respect to this basis, we will write any weight
\mu
as
\mu=\mu^{+}+\mu^{-}
, where
\mu^{+}=(\mu^{+}_{1},\dots,\mu^{+}_{d},0,\dots,0)
and
\mu^{-}=(0,\dots,0,\mu^{-}_{1},\dots,\mu^{-}_{s})
. Recall the fact that
\mathfrak{k}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{C}}
are the eigenspaces of
\theta
with eigenvalues
1
and
-1
, respectively. As the dual basis inherits the action from the Cartan involution, we get that
\theta(\mu^{+}+\mu^{-})=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}
. In particular,
\frac{1}{2}(\mu+\theta\mu)=\mu^{+}
. Thus, for
\nu=(\nu_{1}^{+},\dots,\nu_{d}^{+},\nu_{1}^{-},\dots,\nu_{s}^{-})
the highest weight of
F
, expressed in the basis above, we may write

\displaystyle\eta(\tau)=|\nu+\rho|^{2}-\left|\frac{1}{2}(\nu+\theta\nu)+w\rho\right|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}|\nu_{i}^{-}|^{2}+\text{linear terms in }\nu.

(3.3)

Note that the second equality above follows from the orthogonality of the basis. By (
3.1
), we need only argue that there exists infinitely many
\tau
such that
\eta(\tau)\geq\lambda
. By the expression above, it is sufficient to be able to find infinitely many representations with highest weight having large
\mathfrak{h}^{-}
-part. This is possible by the theorem of the highest weight, stating that every dominant integral element is the unique highest weight of an irreducible representation. The dominant integral elements constitute a lattice in the fundamental Weyl chamber associated to
\Phi^{+}
, which is some cone in the weight space. By the assumption
\delta(G(\mathbb{R}))\geq 1
, we know that
s\geq 1
.

To be precise, we do the following: Fix any half-line in the fundamental Weyl chamber starting at
0
and passing through a lattice point, and not lying in the hyperplane given by
x_{d+1}=x_{d+2}=\dots=x_{d+s}=0
. This line is guaranteed to pass through infinitely many lattice points. For any
\lambda>0
and any linear polynomial
p
in
d+s
variables there exists some
r>0
such that at distance at least
r
from
0
, every point on this line will have
\sum^{s}_{i=1}|x_{d+i}|^{2}-|p(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+s})|
larger than
\lambda
. Considering the expression (
3.3
), we see that any lattice point lying on the line with distance at least
r
to
0
must have
\eta(\tau)\geq\lambda
. Thus, the line contains infinitely many lattice points associated to
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations. This finishes the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

As
\delta(\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R}))\geq 1
for
n\geq 3
, we have an immediate corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

For any
\lambda>0
and
n\geq 3
, There exists infinitely many
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representations of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})
.

4.
A bound on the heat kernel

We continue in with the same notation as the previous section, such that
G
is a connected semisimple algebraic group. From now on, we assume that
\tau
is an irreducible
\lambda
-strongly acyclic representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
, with
\lambda>0
to be chosen later. The content of this section is an upper bound on the trace of the heat kernel in the
t
-aspect for large
t
. This will be used to bound certain orbital integrals in Section
6
.

4.1.
Induced operators

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
be a unitary representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
. For any
f\in\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{R}))
, the operator

\displaystyle\pi(f)\coloneqq\int_{G(\mathbb{R})}f(g)\pi(g)dg

is a trace class operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
. If furthermore
f
is left- and right
K
-finite, the operator is of finite rank. We consider

\displaystyle\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})\coloneqq\int_{G(\mathbb{R})}\pi(g)\otimes H_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)dg

as a bounded operator acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau}
. Let
P
be the orthogonal projection to the
K
-invariant subspace
(\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K}
. We note that as
\pi
and
\nu_{\tau,p}
are unitary, so is their tensor product, and thus
P
has the form

\displaystyle P=\int_{K}\pi(k)\otimes v_{\tau,p}(k)dk.

Using the covariance property, one easily checks that

\displaystyle P\circ\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})=\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})\circ P=\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p}).

Following the argument of (
[
BM83
]
, Corollary
2.2
), we can then use (
2.6
) to get

\displaystyle\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})=e^{-t(\tau(\Omega)-\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}(\Omega))}P.

(4.1)

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\pi,\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
be an admissible unitary representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
, and let
A:\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\to\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
be a bounded operator. Then
(A\otimes 1)\circ\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})
is of trace class, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left((A\otimes 1)\circ\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(A\circ\pi(h_{t}^{\tau,p})\right),

Here, the
\operatorname{Tr}
on the left-hand side is the trace over
{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau}}
, while on the right-hand side it is the trace over
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
.

Proof.

By (
4.1
), we may restrict computing the trace of
(A\otimes 1)\circ\pi(H_{t}^{\tau,p})
to computing it over
(\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K}
, and as
\pi
was assumed admissible, this is finite-dimensional, thus the trace is well defined and finite. The equality now follows by arguing as in the proof of (
[
BM83
]
, Lemma
5.1
).
∎

4.2.
Application of the Plancherel formula

Let
P=MAN
be a real standard parabolic subgroup of
G(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\mathfrak{a}
be the Lie algebra of
A
. We denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the inner product on the real vector space
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
induced by the Killing form, and
||\cdot||
its associated norm. Fix a restricted positive root system of
\mathfrak{a}
and let
\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}
denote their half sum. Let
(\sigma,W_{\sigma})
be a discrete series representation of
M
, i.e. an irreducible unitary subrepresentation of the left regular representation of
M
on
L^{2}(M)
, and let
i\nu\in i\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. We denote by
(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu},\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu})
the induced
principal series representation
of
G
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu}=\{f:G\to W_{\sigma}\mid f(gman)=a^{-(\nu+\rho_{\mathfrak{a}})}\sigma(m)^{-1}f(g)

\displaystyle\>\forall g\in G(\mathbb{R}),man\in MAN,\>\>f|_{K}\in L^{2}(K,W_{\sigma})

\displaystyle\},

\displaystyle(\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(g)f)(x)=f(g^{-1}x).\hskip 168.00027pt

This is an irreducible unitary representation of
G(\mathbb{R})
, in particular admissible, and thus by Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(H_{t}^{\tau,p})\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(g))=\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(h_{t}^{\tau,p})\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(g)).

(4.2)

As in the lemma, the traces are over the appropriate spaces. Applying (
4.1
) to the above, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(h_{t}^{\tau,p})\pi_{\sigma,\nu}(g))=e^{-t(\tau(\Omega)-\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}(\Omega))}\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}^{K}(g)),

(4.3)

where we by
\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}^{K}(g)
denote the operator
P(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}(g)\otimes\text{Id})P
on
\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau}
, with
P
the projection onto the
K
-fixed vectors. We consider this as an operator on
(\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K}
, as this subspace contains its image and it is
0
elsewhere. This subspace is a finite-dimensional space, and by Frobenius reciprocity we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K}=\operatorname{dim}(W_{\sigma}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K_{M}},

with
K_{M}\coloneqq K\cap M
. By virtue of being unitary, we have the inequality

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}^{K}(g))|\leq\operatorname{dim}(W_{\sigma}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K_{M}}.

(4.4)

With this bound established, we turn to the scalar appearing in (
4.3
). Define a constant
c(\sigma)
by

\displaystyle c(\sigma)\coloneqq\sigma(\Omega_{M})-||\rho_{\mathfrak{a}}||.

Then we have that (see
[
Kna01
]
, Proposition
8.22
)

\displaystyle\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}(\Omega)=c(\sigma)-||\nu||^{2}.

Assume that
\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{H}^{\sigma,i\nu}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K}\neq 0
. By the assumption that
\tau
is
\lambda
-strongly acyclic, we immediately get that

\displaystyle\tau(\Omega)-c(\sigma)\geq\lambda-||\nu||^{2},

and as this is true for all
\nu\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, we get

\displaystyle\tau(\Omega)-c(\sigma)\geq\lambda.

(4.5)

Now we are ready to prove the large
t
asymptotic for
h_{t}^{\tau,p}
. Using the Harish-Chandra Plancherel formula (see
[
Olb02
]
, Theorem
2.2
) and (
4.2
) we get that

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P}\sum_{\sigma\in\hat{M}_{d}}\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}}\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}(h_{t}^{\tau,p})\pi(g^{-1}))p_{\sigma}(i\nu)d\nu

\displaystyle=e^{-t(\tau(\Omega)-c(\sigma))}\sum_{P}\sum_{\sigma\in\hat{M}_{d}}\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}}e^{-t||\nu||^{2}}\operatorname{Tr}(\pi_{\sigma,i\nu}^{K}(g))p_{\sigma}(i\nu)d\nu,

where
p_{\sigma}:i\mathfrak{a}^{*}\to[0,\infty)
is the Plancherel density, an analytic function of polynomial growth. The first sum runs over
P=MAN
real standard parabolic subgroups, and the second over discrete series representations of
M
the Levi subgroup of
P
. Since the trace inside the integral on the right-hand side is bounded absolutely by
\operatorname{dim}(W_{\sigma}\otimes\Lambda^{p}\mathfrak{p}^{*}\otimes V_{\tau})^{K_{M}}
, and this is non-zero only for finitely many pairs
(P,\sigma)
(see
[
Olb02
]
, Corollary
2.3
), we may take the double sum to be finite. Furthermore, which pairs contribute is governed solely by
\tau
and
p
.

The latter integral is convergent and vanishing for
t\to\infty
, and so for
t\geq 1
it may be bounded independently of
t
. Putting these observations together and applying (
4.5
), we get the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume
t\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
only depending on
\tau
and
p
such that for any
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\displaystyle|h_{t}^{\tau,p}(g)|\leq C\,e^{-\lambda t}.

5.
Analysis of the trace formula

5.1.
Review of the geometric side of the trace formula

Let
G
be a reductive algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
with
G(\mathbb{R})
noncompact. Fix a minimal parabolic subgroup
P_{0}
of
G
with a Levi decomposition
P_{0}=M_{0}N_{0}
. For
\operatorname{GL}(n)
, we pick as minimal parabolic subgroup the subgroup of upper triangular matrices, with
M_{0}
the diagonal matrices in
G
. We set
\mathcal{F}
to be the set of parabolic subgroups of
G
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
containing
M_{0}
. We let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of subgroups in
G
containing
M_{0}
that are also Levi components of some group in
\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, any

is a reductive group, and for
M\in\mathcal{L}
a Levi subgroup we shall denote by
\mathcal{L}^{L}(M)
the set of Levi subgroups in

containing
M
. Finally we will write
\mathcal{P}^{L}(M)
for the set of parabolic subgroups of
L
for which
M
is a Levi component. If
L=G
, we drop the superscript and write
\mathcal{L}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}(M)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}_{f}
be the finite adeles over
\mathbb{Q}
. Given
K_{f}\subseteq G(\mathbb{A}_{f})
an open compact subgroup, we can define the adelic Schwartz space
\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1},K_{f})
as the space of smooth right
K_{f}
-invariant functions on
G(\mathbb{A})^{1}
all of whose derivatives lies in
L^{1}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1})
. We denote by
\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1})
the union of
\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1},K_{f})
over all such
K_{f}
.

For
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1})
, let
J_{\operatorname{geo}}(f)
be the geometric side of the Arthur trace formula (see
[
Art78
]
). This is a distribution with test function
f
. We give a very brief sketch of its construction (see
[
Art05
]
for an excellent introduction). In essence, we wish to integrate the function

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in G(\mathbb{Q})}f(x^{-1}\gamma y),\qquad x\in G(\mathbb{A})^{1}

(5.1)

over
G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A})^{1}
. In our non-compact case however, this function is often not integrable, and lacks some of our desired properties. Correction terms must be added, and it turns out to be a good idea to add one for each parabolic subgroup. Let
P\in\mathcal{F}
with canonical Levi decomposition
P=M_{P}N_{P}
, i.e. such that
M_{P}\in\mathcal{L}(M_{0})
. We then define

\displaystyle K_{P}(x,y)\coloneqq\int_{N_{P}(\mathbb{A})}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{P}(\mathbb{Q})}f(x^{-1}\gamma ny)dn,\qquad x,y\in G(\mathbb{A})^{1}.

From this, one constructs a kernel function
k^{T}(x,f)
as a sum over standard parabolic subgroups and with a truncation parameter
T
which serves as a replacement for the function (
5.1
), see (
[
Art78
]
) for a precise definition. One then defines
J^{T}(f)
as the
G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A})^{1}
-integral of
k^{T}(f,x)
. Finally, one picks a particular truncation parameter
T=T_{0}
and defines
J(f)\coloneqq J^{T_{0}}(f)
. In the case of
G=\operatorname{GL}(n)
, the canonical choice is
T_{0}=0
.

Recall that any element of an algebraic group has a Jordan decomposition, i.e. for any
g\in G(k)
with
k
a perfect field, we have a decomposition
g=g_{s}g_{u}=g_{u}g_{s}
with
g_{s}
semisimple and
g_{u}
unipotent. The terms
semisimple
and
unipotent
simply mean that their image has this property under some (equivalently, any) closed embedding
G\to\operatorname{GL}(n)
. Now, define an equivalence relation on
G(\mathbb{Q})
: Say that two elements are equivalent if their semisimple parts are
G(\mathbb{Q})
-conjugate to each other. Let
\mathcal{O}
be the set of equivalence classes in
G(\mathbb{Q})
. Then for
\mathfrak{o}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
, one could also consider the function

\displaystyle K_{P,\mathfrak{o}}(x,y)\coloneqq\int_{N_{P}(\mathbb{A})}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{P}(\mathbb{Q})\cap\mathfrak{o}}f(x^{-1}\gamma ny)dn,\qquad x,y\in G(\mathbb{A})^{1}.

(5.2)

From this, one can again construct a modified kernel function
k_{\mathfrak{o}}(x,f)
as above, and define another distribution with test function
f
analogously that we will denote
J_{\mathfrak{o}}(f)
(
loc. sit.
). We see that we have an equality

\displaystyle K_{P}(x,y)=\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}K_{P,\mathfrak{o}}(x,y).

This equality directly extends to the identity on kernel functions
k^{T}(f,x)=\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}k^{T}_{\mathfrak{o}}(f,x)
once proper definitions are given. The content of the
coarse geometric expansion
is the fact that the analogous identity holds true for the associated distributions. Precisely, it is the equality

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{geo}}(f)=\sum_{\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{O}}J_{\mathfrak{o}}(f).

(5.3)

The coarse geometric expansion has been extended to the domain
\mathcal{C}(G(\mathbb{A})^{1},K_{f})
in (
[
FL16
]
), which will be important for our applications.

We note that the distributions
J_{\mathfrak{o}}(f)
have a formulation in terms of integrals of
f(x^{-1}\gamma x)
, for
\gamma\in\mathfrak{o}
and
x\in G(\mathbb{A})
(e.g.
[
Art86
]
, Theorem
8.1
). In particular,
J_{\mathfrak{o}}(f)
is non-zero only if the union of
G(\mathbb{A})
-conjugacy classes of
\mathfrak{o}
intersects the support of
f
. This will be used in Section
5.4
to show that in some cases, only one particular class contribute.

5.4.
Compactification of the test function

We continue with the same setup, and assume that
G=\operatorname{GL}(n)
and
K_{f}=K(N)
,
N\geq 3
. Let
r(g)=d(K,gK)
be the geodesic distance of
g\in G(\mathbb{R})^{1}
from the identity on
\tilde{X}=G(\mathbb{R})^{1}/K
. We will often write
d(I,g)
for the same expression. Let
\varphi_{R}:G(\mathbb{R})^{1}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function identically
1
on
B(R)=\{g\in G(\mathbb{R})^{1}\mid r(g)<R\}
and identically
0
outside
B(R+\varepsilon)
for some small
\varepsilon>0
. We now define

\displaystyle h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}(g)\coloneqq\varphi_{R}(g)h_{t}^{\tau,p}(g).

In a moment, we will need this small lemma on this distance function.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
||\cdot||
denote the Frobenius norm on
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
. Then

\displaystyle r(g)\geq\log||g||,\quad\forall g\in G(\mathbb{R})^{1}.

Proof.

By (
[
BH99
]
, II. Corollary 10.42(2)), we have that
d(I,e^{X})=||X||
for
X
a symmetric matrix. Further, by the basic inequality
e^{||X||}\geq||e^{X}||
, we see that

\displaystyle d(I,e^{X})\geq\log||e^{X}||.

Since both the metric and the Frobenius norm are invariant under multiplication by orthogonal matrices, this inequality holds when replacing
e^{X}
with any
g\in G(\mathbb{R})^{1}
, using the Cartan decomposition
G(\mathbb{R})^{1}=KAK
with
A
the set of diagonal matrices in
G(\mathbb{R})^{1}
.
∎

Let
J_{\operatorname{unip}}
denote the distribution defined in Section
5.1
associated to the equivalence class of elements with semisimple part being the identity, i.e. the unipotent elements. Replacing
h_{t}^{\tau,p}
with
h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}
allows us to reduce the geometric side of the Arthur trace formula to only the unipotent contribution, if we keep
R
small relative to the level
N
. This is the content of the following theorem.

Proposition 5.3
.

For
N
large enough and
R\leq C_{n}\log N
, the constant
C_{n}>0
only depending on
n
, we have that

\displaystyle J_{\operatorname{geo}}(h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{{X(N)}})=J_{\operatorname{unip}}(h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{{X(N)}}).

Proof.

In the coarse geometric expansion of the Arthur trace formula (
5.3
), we are summing distributions indexed over the equivalence classes in
G(\mathbb{Q})
. For our test function
f
, we wish to pick
R
such that
J_{\mathfrak{o}}(f)=0
for any
\mathfrak{o}
not the unipotent class. As explained at the end of Section
5.1
, for this it is sufficient to show that the
G(\mathbb{A})
-conjugacy classes of
\mathfrak{o}
does not intersect the support of
f
.

Our test function
f=h^{\tau,d}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)}
has its support inside
B_{R}K(N)\subseteq G(\mathbb{A})
. We will pick
R
such that for any
\gamma
with semisimiple part
\gamma_{ss}\neq I
in
G(\mathbb{Q})
, every conjugate lies outside the support. Take such a
\gamma
, and let
g=x^{-1}\gamma x
for some
x\in G(\mathbb{A})
. Write
g=g_{\infty}\prod_{p}g_{p}
and assume
\prod_{p}g_{p}\in K(N)
. We will then pick
R
such that
g_{\infty}\notin B_{R}
.

Let
q(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be the characteristic polynomial of
\gamma-I
. Note that by conjugation invariance, this is also the characteristic polynomial of
g_{\nu}-I
for all places
\nu
. As
\gamma
is not unipotent by assumption, this polynomial has a non-leading, non-zero coefficient, say for the degree
k
term,
0\leq k\leq n-1
. Recall that this polynomial is
q(x)=\det(xI-(g_{\nu}-I))
. Then for every prime
p
this coefficient, call it
a_{k}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(a_{k})\geq(n-k)\cdot\nu_{p}(N),

since it is a sum of products of
n-k
elements of
p^{\nu_{p}(N)}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, as
g_{p}
lies in
K_{p}(p^{\nu_{p}(N)})
. In particular, it is integral, and as it is non-zero we get that
|a_{k}|\geq N^{n-k}
. This implies that at least one of the entries of
g_{\infty}-I
has norm greater than
c_{n}N
, for some constant
c_{n}>0
only depending on
n
, and this in turn implies the same lower bound on the Frobenius norm of
g_{\infty}-I
. Applying Lemma
5.2
, we get that there exists some
C_{n}>0
such that

r(g_{\infty})=d(I,g_{\infty})\geq\log||g_{\infty}||\geq C_{n}\log N

for
N
large enough, only depending on
n
. The last inequality is just using the reverse triangle inequality on our lower bound on
||g_{\infty}-I||
. Thus, it is clear that picking
R\leq C_{n}\log N
, we have that
g_{\infty}\notin B_{R}
as desired.
∎

We must ensure that in replacing
h_{t}^{\tau,p}
with its compactification, we can control the change in the trace formula. This was shown in (
[
MM20
]
):

Proposition 5.4

(
[
MM20
]
, Proposition

)
.

There exists constants

such that

\displaystyle\big|J_{\operatorname{spec}}(h_{t}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{{X_{N}}})-J_{\operatorname{spec}}(h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{{X_{N}}}))\big|\leq C_{3}e^{-C_{1}R^{2}/t+C_{2}t}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

for all

,
R\geq 1
and

.

It is in controlling this error term that we need to vary
R
with

. The specifics will be discussed in Section

. Importantly, we may pick

independent of the representation
\tau
.

5.5.
The fine geometric expansion

By the coarse geometric expansion (
5.3
), Proposition
5.3
and Proposition
5.4
, when analyzing
\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})
we may restrict our attention to

. This distribution can be expressed as a finite sum of weighted orbital integrals with certain global coefficients, known as the fine geometric expansion (
[
Art85
]
, Corollary
8.3
). First we need a bit of notation.

Let
S
be a finite set of primes containing
\infty
, and set
\mathbb{Q}_{S}=\prod_{\nu\in S}\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{S}=\prod_{\nu\notin S}^{\prime}\mathbb{Q}_{\nu}
, with
\prod^{\prime}
the usual restricted product. We define
G(\mathbb{Q}_{S})^{1}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{Q}_{S})\cap G(\mathbb{A})^{1}
, and write

for the space of functions

.

Given

In the case of

, where

There exist

\displaystyle\left|J_{M}^{L}(\chi_{{K(N)},Q},\mathfrak{u}_{\text{fin}})\right|\leq cN^{-\operatorname{dim}^{G}_{M}\mathfrak{u}/2}(\log N)^{d}\operatorname{vol}(K(N))^{-1}.

Unless

6.
Asymptotic time behaviour

We continue in the setting of Section
5
with
G=\operatorname{GL}(n)
and
K_{f}=K(N)
. It is clear from the definition that to understand analytic torsion, we must analyze the terms

\displaystyle\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})t^{s-1}dt\right),

(6.1)

where we by the integral in fact means its meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
as a function of
s
. As
\Gamma(s)
has a simple pole at
s=0
with residue
1
, we see that
\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}
is holomorphic at
s=0
with value
1
. Thus, we are reduced to examining the meromorphic continuation of the Mellin transform of
\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})
.

From the standard theory of Mellin transforms (see e.g.
[
Zag06
]
), we can understand the meromorphic continuation of the Mellin transform of a function
f
by giving asymptotics of
f(t)
for
t\to 0
and
t\to\infty
. More precisely, it is sufficient to establish the following:

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=O(e^{-ct})\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty

(6.2)

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{B}\sum_{j=0}^{r_{i}}c_{ij}t^{\alpha_{i}}(\log t)^{j}+O(t^{\alpha_{B+1}})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

(6.3)

for
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
a sequence of real, possibly negative numbers with
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{i+1}
and tending to
+\infty
, and
c>0
. Then we know that the Mellin transform of
f
converges in some half plane and has a meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, its residues at its poles can be described in terms of the coefficients
c_{i,j}
. In the following, we present such asymptotics in the different settings we will need for our proof.

6.1.
Large
t
asymptotic of the spectral side

During the proof of the main theorem, we will need to control large
t
behaviour of the entire trace formula at once to control the error term incurred from truncating the the Mellin transform. This was done in (
[
MM20
]
, Corollary
6.7
), using the fine spectral expansion of the spectral side of the Arthur trace formula and results on logarithmic derivatives of intertwining operators. The result is

\displaystyle\left|J_{\text{spec}}(h_{t}^{\tau,p}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})\right|\leq Ce^{-ct}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(6.4)

for some
C,c>0
, for all
t\geq 1
,
p=0,\dots,n
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 6.1
.

7.
Proof of the main theorem

To prove Theorem
5.1
, we first turn to an analysis of the terms (
6.1
). We truncate the integral in this expression with respect to a parameter
T>0
, leaving a remainder term.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})t^{s-1}dt=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})t^{s-1}dt

(7.1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})t^{s-1}dt.

(7.2)

We focus first on the second integral on the right hand side, what we call the remainder. It is convergent for all
s
, in particular it is holomorphic at
s=0
. For any meromorphic function
f
holomorphic at
0
it holds that
\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}(f(s))=f(0)
, thus by linearity, we need only analyze

\displaystyle E_{0}(0,T)\coloneqq\int_{T}^{\infty}\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{reg}}(e^{-t\Delta_{p}(\tau)})t^{-1}dt.

By applying our large
t
asymptotic for the spectral side (
6.4
) and its following remark, and using the trace formula, i.e. the equality of the geometric side and spectral side, we get the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

There exists a
C>0
only depending on
G
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle|E_{0}(0,T)|\leq Ce^{-\lambda(1-\varepsilon)T}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N)).

7.1.
Restricting to unipotent contribution

We return to the first integral on the right-hand side of (
7.1
). We want to substitute the test function with its compactified version. By Proposition
5.4
, we may write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{geo}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt=\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{geo}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt+E_{1}(s,R,T),

with the error term
E_{1}(s,R,T)
given by an integral convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
and satisfying

\displaystyle|E_{1}(0,R,T)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-C_{1}R^{2}/t+C_{2}t}t^{-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(7.3)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}e^{-C_{4}R^{2}/T+C_{2}T}\int_{0}^{T/R^{2}}e^{-C_{4}/t}t^{-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N)).

(7.4)

Again,
C_{2},C_{4}>0
both only depend on the group
G
. We will return to this estimate in a moment. The reward for compactifying our test function is that by Proposition
5.3
, only the unipotent part of the coarse geometric expansion (
5.3
) contribute if we keep the radius of the support small enough. Assume
R\leq C_{n}\log N
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{geo}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt=\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{unip}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt

7.2.
Applying the fine geometric expansion

To deal with the truncated Mellin transform of
J_{\operatorname{unip}}
, we recall the fine geometric expansion (
5.7
). We isolate the term for
(M,\mathfrak{u})=(G,\{1\})
, which is exactly
h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}(1)\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))
, and denote the remaining sum by
J_{\operatorname{unip}-1}
. This allows us to write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{unip}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}(1)t^{s-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(7.5)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{unip}-1}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt.

(7.6)

The first integral on the right-hand side is the truncated Mellin transform of
h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}(1)
. As we are evaluating at
1\in G(\mathbb{R})
, the compactification has no effect, and we can ignore
R
. By (
[
MM17
]
, (
5.11
)) we have an asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle h_{t}^{\tau,p}(1)\sim\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}t^{-d/2+j},\quad t\to 0.

Furthermore, as a special case of Theorem
4.2
we have for
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|h_{t}^{\tau,p}(1)|\leq D_{\tau,p}e^{-\lambda t}.

Thus, the integral is convergent for
s
in some half-plane and has a meromorphic extension to all of
\mathbb{C}
. We may write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}h_{t,R}^{\tau,p}(1)t^{s-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{t}^{\tau,p}(1)t^{s-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))+E_{2}(s,T),

with the error term
E_{2}(s,T)
given by an integral convergent for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
and satisfying

\displaystyle|E_{2}(0,T)|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}t^{-1}dt\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(7.7)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\lambda T}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(7.8)

for
T\geq 1
. We return to the second integral on the right-hand side of (
7.5
). Using the splitting of the orbital integrals into local parts, i.e. equation (
5.9
), we may express this integral as

\displaystyle\sum_{(M,\mathfrak{u})\neq(G,\{1\})}a^{M}(S(N),\mathfrak{u})\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(M)}d_{M}(L_{1},L_{2})A_{M}^{L_{1}}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty},T)J_{M}^{L_{2}}(\chi_{K(N)},\mathfrak{u}_{\text{fin}}),

(7.9)

with the archimedean part defined as

\displaystyle A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty},T)=\int_{0}^{T}J^{L}_{M}((h^{\tau,p}_{t,R})_{Q},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})t^{s-1}dt.

We will treat this term like we did the integral of
h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}(1)
above. By the asymptotics given in Proposition
6.2
, this is convergent for
s
in some half plane. By Proposition
6.4
, we may write

\displaystyle A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty},T)=\int_{0}^{\infty}J^{L}_{M}((h^{\tau,p}_{t,R})_{Q},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})t^{s-1}dt+E_{3}(s,T),

(7.10)

with
E_{3}(s,T)
some error term, given by an integral convergent for all

, analogously to
E_{2}
satisfying

\displaystyle|E_{3}(0,T)|\leq D_{\tau,p,k}e^{-\lambda T}R^{a}(\log R)^{b}

(7.11)

for
T\geq 1
and some
a,b>0
. We will write
A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})
for the integral on the right-hand side of (
7.10
), which is convergent in some half plane with a meromorphic extension to all of
s\in\mathbb{C}
. This means that

\displaystyle\text{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})\right)

is well defined.

Lemma 7.2
.

There exists
C>0
only depending on
\tau,p
and
\lambda
and
a,b,c>0
only depending on the group such that for
T\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{unip}-1}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\>\>

\displaystyle CN^{-(n-1)}(1+\log N)^{c}R^{a}(\log R)^{b}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N)).

Proof.

Considering the expression (
7.9
) and the discussion above, it suffices to appropriately bound the three following terms:

\displaystyle a^{M}(S(N),\mathfrak{u}),

\displaystyle J_{M}^{L_{2}}(\chi_{K(N)},\mathfrak{u}_{\text{fin}})\>\>\text{for}\>\>(M,\mathfrak{u})\neq(G,\{1\}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}\left(A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})+E_{3}(s,T)\right)\right).

The global coefficients
a^{M}(S(N),\mathfrak{u})
were bounded in Lemma
5.5
, and the local orbital integrals
J_{M}^{L_{2}}(\chi_{K(N)},\mathfrak{u}_{\text{fin}})
were bounded in Lemma
5.6
and its remark. Note that
\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))=c\operatorname{vol}(K(N))^{-1}
for some constant

depending only on normalisations of measures (see the appendix).

By Proposition
6.2
and its remark, alongside Proposition
6.4
, the term

\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}A_{M}^{L}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R},\mathfrak{u}_{\infty})\right)

is uniformly bounded by a constant times

, following the theory of Mellin transforms. The bound can be chosen to be uniform over all

, as there are only finitely many such triples. Finally, the term

\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}E_{3}(s,T)\right)=E_{3}(0,T)

was bounded in a similar way in (

). Collecting all these bounds and applying to (

), we arrive at the desired bound.
∎

7.3.
Asymptotics of analytic torsion

Recall the definition of analytic torsion (
5.4
). By collecting our initial analysis in the previous subsections, we may write

\displaystyle\log T_{Y(N)}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{p=1}^{d}(-1)^{p}p\,h^{\tau,p}_{t}(1)t^{s-1}dt\right)\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))

(7.12)

\displaystyle+\operatorname{FP}_{s=0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{d}(-1)^{p}p\,\frac{1}{s\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{T}J_{\operatorname{unip}-1}(h^{\tau,p}_{t,R}\otimes\chi_{K(N)})t^{s-1}dt\right)

(7.13)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{d}(-1)^{p}p\,\left(E_{0}(0,T)+E_{1}(0,R,T)+E_{2}(0,T)\right)

(7.14)

The first term on the right-hand side is the
L^{2}
-torsion of
Y(N)
(see
[
Lot92
]
). We set

\displaystyle t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{p=1}^{d}(-1)^{p}p\,h^{\tau,p}_{t}(1)t^{s-1}dt\right)\Bigg|_{s=0}.

Note that although
\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}h^{\tau,p}_{t}(1)t^{s-1}dt
may not be holomorphic at
s=0
, swapping
h^{\tau,p}_{t}(1)
with this alternating sum over
p
seen above turns the meromorphic extension holomorphic at
s=0
, from which the above definition is well defined (see
[
BV13
]
, §
4.4
). Then the first term is exactly
\log T^{(2)}_{Y(N)}=t^{(2)}_{\tilde{X}}(\tau)\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))
. All that is left to prove Theorem
5.1
is to show that the second and third term above both have the form
O(\operatorname{vol}(Y(N)N^{-(n-1)}\log(N)^{a})
.

This was accomplished for the second term in Lemma
7.2
, as we assume
R\leq C_{n}\log N
. Note that the sum over
p
contributes at most a constant multiple, as the bound was independent of
p
. To arrive at our desired bound for the third and final term, we wish to pick our parameters
R
and
T
such that the error terms
E_{0},E_{1},E_{2}
are all as small as possible. This is done in the following section.

7.4.
A dance of error terms

We pick
T=\beta\log N
and
R=C_{n}\log N
, with
C_{n}
from Proposition
5.3
and
\beta>0
still to be determined. We see that the integral
\int_{0}^{T/R^{2}}e^{-C_{4}/t}t^{s-1}dt
is then vanishing in
N
, in particular bounded by a constant for
N\geq 3
. Thus, by Lemma
7.1
, (
7.3
), and (
7.7
) respectively we have

\displaystyle E_{0}(0,\beta\log N)

\displaystyle=O(N^{-\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\beta}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))),

\displaystyle E_{1}(0,\beta\log N)

\displaystyle=O(N^{-C_{4}C_{n}^{2}/\beta+\beta}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))),

\displaystyle E_{2}(0,\beta\log N)

\displaystyle=O(N^{-\lambda\beta}\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))).

The implied constants may depend on
\tau
. The choice of
R
also implies that our bound on (
7.13
) has the form

\displaystyle O(\operatorname{vol}(Y(N)N^{-(n-1)}(\log N)^{a})

(7.15)

for some
a>0
and
N
large enough. We now ensure that the error terms above are bounded by a similar expression. Here is where the independence of the constants
C_{2},C_{4}
on
\tau
becomes important.

Pick
\beta
such that
C_{4}C_{n}/\beta-C_{2}\beta>n-1
. This choice of
\beta
then only depends on
n
, i.e. on the group
G
. Then, with
\beta
fixed, let
\lambda
be chosen such that
\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\beta>n-1
. As
\varepsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
0
, it is in fact sufficient to satisfy
\lambda>\frac{n-1}{\beta}
. Note that this choice of
\lambda
also only depends on
C_{2}
and
C_{4}
, which only depend on
G
. This implies that for
\tau
being
\lambda
-strongly acyclic for the chosen

, all the error terms are of the form (
7.15
) as desired.

We have now proven Theorem
5.1
, from which Theorem
1.1
follows.

8.
Appendix

For the convenience of the reader, in this section we prove the well known formula of the volume of the group
K(N)
and show its inverse relation to the volume of its associated adelic locally symmetric space. This is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
K(N)
be the open compact subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{A}_{f})
given by
K(N)=\prod_{p}K_{p}(p^{v_{p}(N)})
, where
K_{p}(p^{k})=\operatorname{ker}(\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p}))\to\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}))
, and let
Y(N)
be the associated adelic locally symmetric space

\displaystyle Y(N)=\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Q})\backslash(\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{A})/\operatorname{SO}(n)K(N)).

Similarly, let
K^{\prime}(N)
be the analogous open compact subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{A}_{f})
, with adelic locally symmetric space
X(N)
. Then the following formulas hold:

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(K(N))

\displaystyle=\left(N^{n^{2}}\prod_{p\mid N}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{k}}\right)\right)^{-1}

(8.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(K(N))

\displaystyle=\varphi(N)^{-1}\operatorname{vol}(K^{\prime}(N)).

(8.2)

Here
\varphi
is Euler’s totient function. Furthermore, let

c(n)\coloneqq\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\backslash(\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)))^{-1}.

This constant depends only on normalizations of measures. We have the relations

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(K(N))

\displaystyle=c(n)\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))^{-1}

(8.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(K^{\prime}(N))

\displaystyle=c(n)\operatorname{vol}(X(N))^{-1}.

(8.4)

Proof.

As
K(N)=\prod_{p}K_{p}(p^{v_{p}(N)})
, and the measures respect this decomposition, it is sufficient to compute
\operatorname{vol}(K_{p}(p^{v_{p}(N)}))
. Fix
p
, and let
m=v_{p}(N)
.
K_{p}(p^{m})
is the kernel of the surjective map
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p})\to\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z}_{p})\cong\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})
. Thus,

\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p})=\bigsqcup_{a\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})}(a\cdot K_{p}(p^{m}))

where we by abuse of notation let
a\in\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})
also denote any lift to
\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. Since the cosets are disjoint, and they are homeomorphic through multiplication, they all have the same measure,
\operatorname{vol}(K_{p}(p^{m}))
. Since the left-hand side has measure
1
in our normalization, we get that

\operatorname{vol}(K_{p}(p^{m}))=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|}.

To determine
|\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|
, we use the following short exact sequence of finite groups

0\to\{I+pA\mid A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})\}\to\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})\to\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})\to 0.

The rightmost group has cardinality
\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(p^{n}-p^{k})
by counting number of bases, using that
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a field. The leftmost group is bijective to
\{pA\mid A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})\}
, which is the kernel of
M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})
, induced by the obvious quotient map. These have cardinality
p^{n^{2}m}
and
p^{n^{2}}
respectively, so their kernel has cardinality
p^{m^{2}(n-1)}
. Plugging everything in, we get that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|\>

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})|\cdot|\{I+pA\mid A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})\}|

(8.5)

\displaystyle=p^{n^{2}(m-1)}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(p^{n}-p^{k})=(p^{m})^{n^{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{n-k}}\right).

(8.6)

Reindexing the product, this establishes formula (
8.1
). For the next formula, running through the same argument for
K^{\prime}(N)
, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(K_{p}^{\prime}(p^{m}))=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|}.

(8.7)

As the number of units of
\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z}
is exactly
\varphi(p^{m})
, we get that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|=\varphi(p^{m})|\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|,

and this proves (
8.2
).

Now, let us relate
\operatorname{vol}(Y(N))
to
\operatorname{vol}(K(N))
. By combining (
5.5
) and (
8.2
), we see that the two latter statements of the proposition are equivalent, and so it is sufficient to prove (
8.4
). By strong approximation,
X(N)=\Gamma(N)\backslash\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)
, with
\Gamma(N)
the classical principal congruence subgroup of level
N
. The fundamental domain of
X(N)
can be viewed as
[\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma(N)]
copies of a fundamental domain of
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n)
, and hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(X(N))=\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(n))[\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma(N)].

Note that
[\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma(N)]=|\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})|
essentially by definition of
\Gamma(N)
. By the Chinese remainder theorem and (
8.7
), we have that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})|=\prod_{p\mid N}|\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z}/p^{v_{p}(N)}\mathbb{Z})|=\operatorname{vol}(K^{\prime}(N))^{-1}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The proposition immediately implies
\operatorname{vol}(X(N))=O(N^{n^{2}-1})
. In particular, we get the inequality

\displaystyle N^{-(n-1)}=O\left(\operatorname{vol}(X(N))^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}\right),

with which we can rewrite the main theorem into its invariant form (
1.8
).
