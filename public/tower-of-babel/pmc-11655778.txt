Addressing lung truncation in 99mTc-MAA SPECT/CT for 90Y microsphere radioembolization treatment planning
Authors: M Allan Thomas, Richard Laforest, John Karageorgiou, Dan Giardina, Tyler J Fraum, Chris D Malone, Justin K Mikell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior studies have established that macroaggregated albumin (MAA)-SPECT/CT offers more robust lung shunt fraction (LSF) and lung mean absorbed dose (LMD) estimates in 90Y radioembolization in comparison to planar imaging. However, incomplete SPECT/CT coverage of the lungs is common due to clinical workflows, complicating its potential use for LSF and LMD calculations. In this work, lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT was addressed via correction strategies to improve 90Y treatment planning.MethodsLung truncation was simulated in 56 cases with adequate (> 90%, mean: 98%) lung coverage in MAA-SPECT/CT by removing slices in ~ 5 mm increments from the lung apices to the diaphragm. A wide range of lung coverages from 100% to < 1% in ~ 2% increments were created. LSF and LMD were calculated with four methods. (1) 2D planar imaging standard (not truncated), truncated lung SPECT/CT data was: (2) used with no corrections (SPECTTrunc), (3) uniformly extrapolated to full lung coverage (SPECTUniform), (4) fit with an empirical model to predict lung counts at full lung coverage (SPECTFit). To determine counts for LSF, full lung volumes, those modified at the lung/liver boundary (Lungs 2-cm), and those isolated to the left lung (Left Lung) were used. The correction methods were then applied to 31 independent cases without full lung coverage (< 90%, mean: 74%). The variations in LSF and LMD estimates from each correction method were analyzed.ResultsAveraged across simulated lung coverages from 40 to 80%, percent errors relative to non-truncated data for SPECTTrunc were (mean ± σ) − 22% ± 15% for LSF and 34% ± 29% for LMD. SPECTUniform had similar errors with 29% ± 26% for both LSF and LMD. SPECTFit yielded the most accurate and precise estimates for LSF and LMD, with errors of 11% ± 20% for both. The Left Lung approach equalized LMD errors in all three correction methods, with percent errors of 3% ± 17% (SPECTTrunc), 2% ± 17% (SPECTUniform), and 4% ± 13% (SPECTFit). In the 31 cases without ground truth LSF or LMD, Left Lung produced highly comparable LMD estimates, with a mean (max) coefficient of variation across the three correction methods of 4% (20%).ConclusionLSF and LMD can be estimated for 90Y radioembolization using truncated lung coverage data in MAA-SPECT/CT. Empirical models to predict lung counts at full lung coverage produced LSF and LMD estimates with minimal bias and uncertainty. With lung/liver boundary adjustments, all SPECT/CT methods assessed in this work yielded LMD estimates comparable to ground truth, even down to 50% lung coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-024-00705-4.Keywords: Lung shunt fraction, Lung mean dose, 90Y microspheres, Radioembolization, MAA SPECT/CT

Background

A requirement in 90Y radioembolization is a planning procedure that utilizes 99mTc-tagged macroaggregated albumin (MAA) particles as a surrogate for 90Y microspheres. The imaging of the MAA particles enables lung shunt fraction (LSF) estimation, an assessment of the relative magnitude of 90Y microspheres that may shunt to the lungs. The lung mean absorbed dose (LMD) can also be estimated given a planned administered activity (AA) of 90Y microspheres. Both primary vendor devices for 90Y radioembolization (Theraspheres™ and SirSpheres®) list high LSF and/or LMD as contraindications for therapy. SirSpheres® [1] and Theraspheres™ limit LMD to 30 Gy for a single procedure, while Theraspheres™ also limits LMD to 50 Gy aggregate over multiple procedures [2].

Historical lung toxicity events and recommended safety thresholds were evaluated with 2D planar-based LSF and LMD [3, 4]. Large LMD may occur when traditional 2D planar calculations show high LSF or the targeted AA is high. Prior studies have shown that 2D planar methods as outlined in vendor instructions-for-use (IFU) can overestimate LSF, and in turn LMD [5–13]. These overestimates push more 90Y cases toward the upper thresholds of lung dosimetry. In these situations, the 90Y treatment plan may be adjusted toward a lower target radiation dose that could result in inadequate treatment. It is also possible that the dosimetry thresholds are exceeded, resulting in non-treatment.

Single photon emission tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) employs intrinsic scatter and attenuation corrections, and its 3D nature offers improvement in defining lung and liver volumes. As a result, SPECT/CT can likely provide more accurate LSF values, but most clinical sites do not use a 2-bed acquisition on all patients to ensure the full lungs are in the field of view (FOV). This results in many MAA-SPECT/CT datasets suffering from incomplete coverage of the lungs [8]. Strategies for utilizing MAA-SPECT/CT to provide LSF and LMD estimates for every 90Y case should be developed to advance the field and improve patient selection on the basis of LSF/LMD.

This work outlines both theoretical and clinical assessments of methods to estimate LSF and LMD from the available data in MAA-SPECT/CT when lung coverage is < 100%. The limitations of assumptions tied to extrapolating uniform count density to 100% lung coverage, as well as corrections for non-uniform count densities, were assessed. This work informs the biases and limits of agreement for various LSF and LMD estimation methods in the context of limited lung coverage and non-uniform densities. Our results demonstrate that LMD estimates are minimally impacted by lung truncation when corrections are applied to the lung/liver boundary for establishing lung and liver regions in LSF and LMD calculations. The methods are an important strategy for two key components of 90Y radioembolization treatment planning and a more systematic understanding of lung toxicity risk: (1) retrospective analysis of historical MAA-SPECT/CT data, even with lung truncation, and (2) future 90Y cases at institutions unable to acquire two bed positions for MAA-SPECT/CT.

Methods

Patient selection

The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board. The need for informed written consent was waived. The primary starting group of cases was collected from September 2022 to June 2024, with two qualifying characteristics establishing inclusion: (1) a high 2D planar LSF (≥ 8%), and (2) at least one CT with full lung coverage to establish a best estimate of full lung mass. All cases involved Theraspheres™ 90Y glass microspheres.

Lung mass estimate: MAA-CTs, 90Y-CTs, and diagnostic CTs

In order to establish a best estimate of full lung mass, MAA-CTs, 90Y-CTs, or diagnostic CTs covering the chest were searched and collected amongst the patients in the cases assembled. No restrictions were applied for collecting other CT datasets related to the use of iodinated contrast or time interval between longitudinal CT scans of each patient. CT scans from outside institutions were also allowed. For patients where multiple CTs with full lung coverage were available, the primary choice was the MAA-CT associated with the case at hand. The secondary choice was the CT closest in time to the MAA planning procedure. All CT datasets were used to define lung and liver volumes via auto-segmentation (Contour Protégé AI, MIM, Inc. v7.1.5). The lung and liver segmentations were reviewed and refined as necessary by a medical physicist (MAT) and then approved by at least one clinician. Cases were separated into groups on the basis of lung coverage in the MAA-SPECT/CT field of view. If the MAA-SPECT/CT maintained 100% coverage of the lungs, no other CT was needed. For cases with MAA-SPECT/CT lung truncation, lung coverage was estimated by comparing the MAA-CT lung mass to the estimated lung mass from a secondary CT. Due to the possibility of the SPECT and CT acquisitions maintaining different coverages of the lungs, the lung coverage was determined by the SPECT rather than the CT. As an example, a case may have shown 100% lung coverage in the CT but < 100% in the SPECT. CT contours of the lungs were adjusted to reflect the limits of coverage in the SPECT. Cases were categorized into two groups based on this strategy: (1) FOV ≥ 90%, where MAA-SPECT/CT lung coverage was complete or nearly complete (≥ 90%), and (2) FOV < 90% where lung coverage was < 90%.

Planar and MAA-SPECT/CT acquisition and reconstruction

Planar and MAA-SPECT/CT scans were acquired on one of four SPECT/CT scanners (Siemens Symbia T6, GE Optima 640, GE Optima 670, or GE 870). All scanners used low energy high resolution (LEHR) collimation except for the GE 870 which used LEHR Sensitivity. A photopeak window centered at 140 keV was used for all scanners, with 15% (Siemens) and 20% (GE) window widths, and 18% (Siemens) and 10% (GE) scatter windows. All planar acquisitions were two beds, one centered over the lungs and one centered over the liver (abdomen), at 5 min each. All SPECT/CT scans used a single bed position focused on encompassing the entire liver and relevant areas of the abdomen as opposed to ensuring full lung coverage. The scans were for 120 views over 360° at 15 s/view except for the GE 870 at 20 s/view, and all scans implemented auto-contouring. MAA-SPECT reconstruction was ordered-subset expectation maximization with a 128 × 128 matrix for all scanners, with 4 iterations and 10 subsets for GE and 8 iterations and 4 subsets for Siemens. Siemens reconstructions included a 8.4 mm Gaussian filter while GE used a 8.0 mm Gaussian filter as well as Resolution Recovery. The CT acquisitions in the MAA-CTs varied by scanner, but used 120 or 130 kV, a pitch from 0.8 to 1.4, 10–20 mm collimation, 0.8–1.0 s rotation time, 90–150 mA tube current, and a computed tomography dose index volume (CTDIvol) of 8–10 mGy. The GE Optima 640 used a much lower CTDIvol of ~ 3 mGy (30 mA). CT images were reconstructed using soft tissue kernels (GE: STD, Siemens: B31s) and no iterative reconstruction. SPECT voxel sizes were 4.4 × 4.4 × 4.4 mm (all GE) or 4.8 × 4.8 × 4.8 mm (Siemens), while CT voxels were 1 × 1 × 5 mm (GE 670 and 870), 1 × 1 × 2.5 mm (GE 640), or 1 × 1 × 4 mm (Siemens).

LSF and LMD calculations

LSF was calculated using Eq. (2) below, with different methods utilized to determine lung and liver counts.

For planar LSF, the geometric mean of anterior and posterior views from the 2D planar images was used for determining counts [8]. For the SPECT/CT methods, counts were computed from SPECT using the lung and liver contours defined on the CT from the relevant MAA-SPECT/CT. In some cases, a rigid registration was applied to the SPECT to correct for misregistration with the CT near the diaphragm. This was to avoid biasing the assignment of counts between the liver and lungs in cases with clear misregistration. Three different sets of liver and lung segmentations were used for establishing counts in the SPECT/CT methods. (1) Both Lungs: the liver and lung contours were used directly without any adjustments or corrections. (2) Lungs 2-cm: the liver contour was expanded by 2 cm in all directions, and this contour was used to determine liver counts. Any intersection of the expanded liver contour with the lungs was removed, then the adjusted lungs contour was used to determine a lung count density (counts/g). The lung count density was then applied to the full lungs mass to establish the corrected lung counts value [8, 11, 13]. (3) Left Lung: any intersection of the expanded liver contour with the left lung was removed, then only the left lung was used to determine a lung count density and corrected lung counts were calculated as with 2) above. The 2-cm expanded liver contour was again used for liver counts. For all three strategies to categorize counts to the liver and lungs, no counts were dually assigned to both regions. Lung count density variations and modeling

The equations outlined in the previous section apply only to assumptions tied to uniform count density throughout the entire lung volume. For cases with non-uniform count densities, LSF and LMD estimates based on a uniform extrapolation of truncated MAA-SPECT/CT data may lead to significant errors. Variations in lung count density were analyzed by computing lung counts/g at different simulated levels of lung truncation, and then calculating the changes in lung count density relative to full lung coverage. To collect this data, the lung segmentations from the FOV ≥ 90% cases were modified by removing axial slices starting at the most superior slice and continuing inferior toward the diaphragm in ~ 5 mm increments. A wide range of lung coverages from 100% to < 1% in ~ 2% increments were created for Both Lungs, Lungs 2-cm, and Left Lung. Lung counts, mean lung HU (lung mass), and lung volume were collected for all the adjusted contours.

Lung counts were modeled using lung mass as the input variable, masslung. Model fits were determined for simulated lung fractions from 20 to 90%. Lung counts at full lung mass were predicted from the empirical fits and then used in LSF and LMD estimates. Note that a generalized model was not developed using all cases. Rather, each case was independently fit using its unique lung mass and lung counts data. A main objective of the case-by-case empirical fits was to better understand the non-uniformities in count density for each case, and then use the results of the model fits to strategize corrections for LSF and LMD estimates. More details about the fitting process and information about how the fitting parameters were used to predict lung counts are included in the Supplemental Information.

Percent errors were also calculated between the various LSF and LMD estimates and ground truth. Detailed analysis of errors and the various correction methods was applied to simulated lung coverages of 40, 60, and 80% to best represent significant, average, and minimal lung truncation as observed in clinical cases.

Lung mass estimate errors: impact on LSF/LMD calculations

Several aspects of the proposed workflow and strategies for LSF and LMD corrections outlined in this work require a best estimate of full lung mass. Once a CT with full lung coverage is identified, there may still be concern that the lung mass estimate derived from that CT maintains bias or uncertainty. We therefore assessed the impact of lung mass uncertainty on the various calculation and correction methods proposed in this work. The details of this analysis are provided in the Supplemental Information. The relationships between lung mass error and LSF or LMD error were derived for both uniform and non-linear correction methods for lung counts. Assumed lung mass errors from − 50 to + 50% were applied to the derived LSF and LMD error equations to establish the impact of lung mass errors across the different correction methods analyzed.

Application of estimation methods to FOV < 90% cases

To showcase the utility of LSF and LMD corrections under truncated lung conditions, the methods described herein were applied to the FOV < 90% group of cases. LSF and LMD values using planar, SPECTTrunc, SPECTUniform, and SPECTFit were computed and compared. The estimated full lung mass (from a different CT) that determined the level of lung truncation was used in all LSF and LMD calculations. The full details of how the SPECTFit method was applied to the cases from the FOV < 90% group can be found in the Supplemental Information. To explore the impact of each estimation method on the corrections, the coefficient of variation (COV) between the LSF and LMD values as estimated by SPECTTrunc, SPECTUniform, and SPECTFit was computed. COV values were then correlated to the level of lung truncation in the clinical cases to better understand its effect on the variation in estimates from each method.

Statistical analysis

Datasets analyzed in this work were first tested with both Anderson–Darling and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests. Datasets were determined to be both normally and non-normally distributed. As a result, both parametric and non-parametric significance tests were used as appropriate. Mean ± σ was used for comparisons. For all matched comparisons among paired datasets, repeated measures one-way ANOVA analysis was used for normally distributed data while Friedman’s tests were used in other cases. If a statistically significant difference among all groups was established, multiple comparisons were assessed for more specific analysis among groups. A false discovery rate correction using the two-state step-up method of Benjamini et al. [15] was also implemented using a false discovery rate of 0.05, with all p values reported after adjustment for multiplicity. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) and statistical significance was considered true for p < 0.05. In order to better understand the different LSF and LMD calculation methods, mean differences and 95% prediction intervals (± 1.96σ, PI) of differences between calculation methods were computed as outlined in Bland–Altman analysis [16, 17]. Limits of agreement (LoA) were determined as mean ± 95% PI.

Results

Case characteristics

A total of 87 cases were identified from 78 unique patients. There were 19 cases that did not proceed to 90Y therapy, 6 of which were due explicitly to high LSF or LMD estimates calculated using IFU methods (2D planar imaging with assumed 1-kg lung mass). The number of planned treatments isolated to the right, left, and caudate lobes was 35, 34, and 4, respectively, with 14 plans involving two lobes. The CTs used to determine full lung mass estimates came from the following categories: 52 diagnostic CT from our institution, 26 MAA-CT, 5 90Y-CT, and 4 diagnostic CT from outside institutions. All but two of the CTs used for full lung mass estimates in the FOV < 90% group were diagnostic CTs. Table 1 outlines the breakdown of cases according to lung truncation, lung masses, planar LSF, and planar LMD distributions. There were 56 cases with ≥ 90% lung coverage in the MAA-SPECT/CT (22 with 100% coverage), and 31 cases with < 90% coverage. Lung mass estimates, on average, were comparable to the standard 1-kg mass used in IFU LMD recommendations. But specific patients showed a much wider variability in lung mass ranging from a − 40% to + 50% deviation from 1-kg. There was no significant difference in lung mass distributions between the FOV ≥ 90% and FOV < 90% groups (p = 0.86). Average planar LSF values were > 10% for both groups, with 3 cases each having LSF > 20% in the two respective groups. Average planar LMD values were approximately 16 Gy, with a total of 9 cases > 30 Gy due either to full lung mass < 1-kg or high planar LSF and the case not proceeding to 90Y therapy.

LSF and LMD errors from lung truncation: theory

Figure S1 in the Supplemental Information shows the results of comparing LSF and LMD estimates using truncated lung coverage data to those computed with a uniform extrapolation of count density to full lung coverage [Eqs. (4) and (5)]. Because lung counts are not fully represented at truncated lung coverage, yet liver counts are still 100% collected, LSFTrunc is always an underestimate. On the other hand, LMDTrunc is always overestimated despite LSFTrunc being underestimated. This dramatic shift is due to the assumption that the full AA is associated with a reduced lung volume (mass) at truncated lung coverage, increasing the estimated LMD. Interestingly, for low LSFTrunc the error in LMDTrunc is comparatively small even for significant lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT. Fig. S1(b) shows the effect of 1/Lungsfrac in Eq. (5) to reverse the underestimation of LSF into an overestimation of LMD. The fact that lung truncation partially offsets errors between LSF and LMD provides opportunity for clinical utility even when no lung truncation corrections can be made.

For Both Lungs, the majority of cases show a much higher count density closer to the diaphragm. This count density then decreases when moving toward the lung apices. Lung mass estimate error: impact on LSF and LMD

Errors in estimated lung mass affect both LSF and LMD when corrections to lung truncation must be made. As a result, the overall impact of these errors is generally non-linear and/or non-intuitive. Figure S4 in the Supplemental Information shows how LSF and LMD errors relate to errors in the full lung mass estimate. LSF error can become quite large, especially for large underestimates of full lung mass. But these LSF errors do not directly translate to the same effect on LMD. Lung mass error maintains opposite impacts on LSF and LMD that mostly balance out. For uniform corrections, the overall effect on LMD error is generally very small, but does increase in magnitude for larger LSF values. For non-linear correction methods (e.g. the power fit method), there is a greater effect of lung mass error because it changes predicted lung counts, and in turn LSF, in a more complicated way. The more straightforward offset in errors between LSF and LMD does not hold for power fit corrections—exponents > 1 lead to larger LSF errors that are not balanced when computing LMD, while exponents < 1 lead to smaller LSF errors that get overwhelmed by the lung mass error when computing LMD. Even still, only lung mass errors beyond ± 20% produce LMD errors greater than 10% in magnitude. These results are different from the more direct impact of lung mass error on LMD as used in the standard LMD equation (Eq. 3). As shown in Table 1, the assumption that all patients have a standard mass of 1-kg can lead to much more severe errors in lung mass, and in turn LMD.

SPECT/CT LSF and LMD correction methods relative to planar imaging

The biases and uncertainties associated with the LSF and LMD correction methods are worth comparing to observed differences in LSF and LMD between planar imaging and SPECT/CT. For the 56 cases in the FOV ≥ 90% group, the mean differences between planar and ground truth SPECT/CT LSF were + 5.7, + 8.7, and + 9.5 pp for Both Lungs, Lungs 2-cm, and Left Lung, respectively. The mean differences for LMD were + 7.6, + 11.0, and + 11.9 Gy. While the correction methods outlined herein do not perform ideally for all cases, and may contain some errors, the LSF and LMD estimates from 2D planar imaging consistently show much larger bias and uncertainty.

LSF and LMD estimates from FOV < 90% cases

Figure 5 shows correlation plots comparing planar LSF and LMD values with SPECTTrunc, SPECTUniform, and SPECTFit methods in the FOV < 90% cases. Only data for Left Lung is shown here, with similar results for Both Lungs and Lungs 2-cm included in the Supplemental Information (Fig. S6, Table S3). Each of the SPECT/CT methods maintained significant differences for both LSF and LMD from the planar calculations. LSF from SPECTTrunc produced lower values than the other SPECT/CT methods, as expected. But the distributions of LMD estimates were highly comparable among all three methods. In the correlation plots, there are minimal notable differences in LMD values for any case when comparing the three methods to each other. The COV values were higher for LSF due to the contribution from SPECTTrunc (biased to underestimate LSF). But for LMD, most cases showed low COV between the three estimates. For Left Lung, there were no statistically significant differences in LSF or LMD estimates between SPECTUniform and SPECTFit. These results suggest that each correction method could provide comparable information for 90Y treatment planning. Figure S7 in the Supplemental Information shows how the COV values for LSF and LMD estimates vary with the level of lung truncation. While LSF COVs scale very closely with the % of lungs in the FOV, LMD COVs do not correlate as strongly. This is likely due to the contribution of SPECTTrunc to the LSF COV, and the fact that for LMD SPECTTrunc errors can be offset in many cases. Fig. S7 also confirms that the lowest COV values for LMD are associated with Left Lung, indicating when it is used for LMD calculations the type of correction method applied for lung truncation is likely not critical.

Discussion

Lung truncation is an issue for clinics that are unable to perform MAA-SPECT/CT with two bed positions. In this work, strategies to address lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT were explored to improve 90Y radioembolization dosimetry. The flowchart in Fig. 6 outlines our suggested strategy for handling lung truncation in 90Y radioembolization treatment planning. In cases where the lungs are truncated in the MAA-SPECT/CT and a full lung mass estimate cannot be determined, the SPECTTrunc calculation method is the only option for LSF and LMD estimates. This method uses the available truncated data without any corrections and generally leads to underestimated LSF but overestimated LMD. Interestingly, the underestimation of LSF from lung truncation is offset by the truncated lung mass value in the equation for LMD, producing moderate LMD errors under many clinical conditions (limited truncation; estimated LSF < 10%). Nevertheless, if any CT data with full lung coverage is available so that a full lung mass can be estimated, our suggestion is to avoid the SPECTTrunc method.

Flowchart outlining a proposed workflow for using MAA-SPECT/CT data with truncated lung coverage for LSF and LMD estimates in 90Y radioembolization treatment planning. There are four possible estimation strategies: (1) SPECTStandard: full lung coverage in MAA-SPECT/CT, no corrections, (2) SPECTTrunc: lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT, no full lung mass estimate—no corrections applied, (3) SPECTUniform: lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT, full lung mass estimate available—uniform extrapolation to full lung mass, and (4) SPECTFit: lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT, full lung mass estimate available—empirical fit to predict data at full lung mass

SPECTUniform considers uniform count density throughout the lung volume and extrapolates the data at truncated lung coverage to the full lung mass [8]. This correction improves LSF and LMD estimates, especially if the magnitude of lung truncation is small and/or corrected lung volumes are used for the computation of lung counts (Lungs 2-cm, Left Lung) to increase the chance of uniform lung count density. Even still, the idea of uniformity in lung count density is not accurate for most 90Y cases according to the analysis in this work. This has been shown in prior studies as well [8, 13], though specific comparisons to this work are difficult due to different methods of computing count density variations. Assuming uniformity for the purpose of extrapolating truncated data to full lung coverage may lead to errors. Different lung regions and adjustments to segmentations impact these count density variations, with the most significant variability observed for the uncorrected Both Lungs. Different cases have different count density variations as well, with some showing mostly uniform count densities and others showing very strong non-uniformities. Isolating only the left lung for the computation of lung count density and/or lung counts as needed for LSF and LMD calculations offers the most reliable match with the assumption of uniformity. These results align with previous studies as well [7, 8]. In clinical cases, we encourage assessment of the case-specific count density variations in the lung prior to consideration of using the SPECTUniform strategy presented in this work. An example case that would likely be reasonable for SPECTUniform is Case D from Fig. 1a–c, whereas Case A may produce undesirable errors and uncertainty.

If necessary or desired, the non-uniformities in lung count density can be modeled on a case-by-case basis. Simple equations can reasonably predict lung count values that are needed for the inputs to various LSF and LMD calculation strategies. Having a full lung mass estimate from an alternative CT is required for this prediction process. Even under significant simulated lung truncation conditions (lungs ~ 50% truncated), SPECTFit produced LSF and LMD estimates with the least bias and uncertainty relative to ground truth. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that when SPECTTrunc, SPECTUniform, and SPECTFit were all applied to the 31 cases in the FOV < 90% group, only minimal differences were observed in their LMD estimates. This shows that under many clinically observed conditions, any correction method for estimating LMD to inform 90Y treatment planning can offer comparable information. Finally, the best strategy for predicting lung counts using truncated MAA-SPECT/CT data is not always associated with empirical model fitting—for some cases SPECTUniform remains best. As discussed in more detail in the Supplemental Information, the fitting parameters from the empirical fits for each case can be used to identify which method is most likely to yield the lowest prediction error.

Uniform correction methods for LSF and LMD based on lung count density have been used previously [8]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have explored case-specific, non-uniform correction methods to truncated lung SPECT/CT data for 90Y radioembolization treatment planning. The closest prior analysis relates to a limited, 4-views-only SPECT imaging method for LSF estimation [13]. This method aimed to address lung truncation in MAA-SPECT/CT with a very fast but quantitative imaging acquisition covering both the abdomen and chest (2-bed) rather than a full, 2-bed traditional SPECT/CT. In this prior work [13], LSF estimates were improved in comparison to traditional 2D planar methods, offering errors relative to SPECT/CT with a 95% PI of ± 2 pp and minimal bias (mean error: + 0.5 pp). In the current study, at 50% lung truncation SPECTFit achieved errors with mean ± 95% PI of 0.8 ± 2.8 pp, 0.0 ± 1.5, and 0.0 ± 0.8 pp for Both Lungs, Lungs 2-cm, and Left Lung, respectively. The closest data for a fair comparison in this study is Lungs 2-cm because a correction was applied to the lung/liver boundary in all calculations in [13]. Due to differences in clinical scanning conditions and other variables, caution is warranted in making direct comparisons to this work with that from the prior study [13]. But the results are roughly comparable, with each strategy offering improvements to LSF estimates.

A difficult detail to address is whether the small differences in LSF and LMD estimates between the three correction methods assessed in this work (SPECTTrunc, SPECTUniform, SPECTFit) are clinically relevant. For the current IFU LMD thresholds (> 30 Gy) they are likely of limited consequence. But it is possible that SPECT/CT based LSF and LMD calculations should use different thresholds for predicting the risk of lung toxicity, making the variations between methods potentially more important. Nevertheless, the differences between planar calculations and any version of the 3D SPECT/CT correction strategies assessed in this work were much greater than any variations between the SPECT/CT correction methods themselves. Therefore, there is likely no reason to avoid using truncated lung coverage data for MAA-SPECT/CT LSF and LMD estimates.

The strategies outlined in this work will provide much more consistent information for 90Y planning dosimetry than the standard IFU methods, and they do not require a 2-bed SPECT/CT for all patients. Several prior studies comparing 2D planar LSF with strategies based on 3D MAA-SPECT/CT ensured complete coverage of the lungs as part of their acquisition protocol [9, 11, 12]. While this is understandable for research purposes, it puts a significant limitation on the practical aspects of extending SPECT/CT methods for LSF and LMD to all clinical sites. This particular detail is relevant, because many clinics likely struggle to justify the difficulties associated with a 2-bed MAA-SPECT/CT workflow for all patients. Requiring a 2-bed MAA-SPECT/CT for LSF and LMD calculations also removes the possibility of looking at historical cases with 1-bed SPECT/CT retrospectively to better understand relationships between 3D LSF, LMD, and lung toxicity events in radioembolization.

The most important requirement for the SPECT/CT based LSF and LMD calculation strategies addressed herein is a CT with full lung coverage. This could be included in an MAA-SPECT/CT workflow without adding significant time, ensuring that the most updated estimate of lung mass is available for LSF and LMD calculations associated with patient selection and treatment planning. The overwhelming majority of 90Y patients will likely have had a diagnostic chest CT as recent part of their care, which could offer an alternative [8]. The methods outlined in this work should enable using nearly all MAA-SPECT/CT datasets, even those with clear lung truncation, to provide sensible estimates for LSF and LMD using SPECT/CT instead of 2D planar imaging. If more sites can collect data in this way, it would be feasible to re-assess lung toxicity thresholds in 90Y radioembolization and ensure that this treatment option could be administered optimally to as many eligible patients as possible.

Post-therapy dosimetry using 90Y-PET/CT could also likely benefit from the methods outlined in this work. Lung dosimetry from 90Y Bremsstrahlung SPECT/CT is not recommended due to the significant contribution of erroneous counts outside the liver, but 90Y-PET/CT has been used for post-therapy lung dosimetry previously [5, 11]. The same concerns regarding lung truncation will be associated with 90Y-PET/CT since most PET scanners will not have sufficient axial coverage to avoid lung truncation with a 1-bed acquisition. 90Y-PET/CT requires longer acquisition times than most traditional PET/CT, so 2-bed acquisitions are likely impractical for most clinics.

This study has limitations that should be addressed. Reliance on an estimated lung mass is key to multiple aspects of the methods outlined in this study. Establishing ground truth for lung mass is difficult, and using CT data for estimates is known to produce some level of variation and uncertainty. Different respiratory phases can impact lung mass, though only slightly (< 10%) as lung volume and lung density changes with respiration mostly offset [8, 14]. MAA- or 90Y-CTs in this study did not involve iodinated contrast, while some diagnostic chest CTs did. This distinction may also contribute to variations and uncertainty in estimating lung mass from different CT protocols [8]. Accurate HU values from CT data are also important [18], as well as CT acquisition parameters, reconstruction details, and voxel size [19]. Health issues outside of those related to the use of 90Y therapy may also cause changes in a patient’s lung mass over time. Nevertheless, we have shown in this study that when correcting for lung truncation, lung mass errors do not impact LSF and LMD as directly as what is expected from the standard LMD equation (Eq. 3). Our analysis provides support that an improved strategy is to use a patient-specific lung mass estimate, even with some uncertainty, along with correction methods for truncated SPECT/CT data to estimate LSF and LMD. This strategy provides estimates with much lower bias and uncertainty relative to the standard IFU methods of planar imaging and an assumed 1-kg lung mass.

MAA perfusion in the lungs, even through tumor shunts tied to cancer, may be impacted by each patient’s health conditions and history. Certain lung diseases such as emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary embolism, or pulmonary fibrosis may lead to abnormal MAA perfusion in the lungs and non-uniform count or lung densities [13]. For patients treated with 90Y therapy who also have such conditions, careful attention to lung count density non-uniformities and related potential for errors is warranted as they relate to the approaches outlined in this work. Additionally, the methods outlined in this work did not produce small errors in LSF or LMD under all conditions. Under severe lung truncation (lungs in FOV < 40%), or in cases with significant variability in lung count density (β > 1.5), making predictions at full lung mass was difficult. Such cases, though rare, should be treated with particular caution. The planar imaging may offer some insights in such cases, where information about the regions truncated in the SPECT/CT may still be usable and helpful [13]. There are potential issues associated with planar LSF calculations and workflows that may be influenced by specific personnel or procedures at a given clinical site. It is possible that the same input image data would yield different planar LSF and LMD estimates at a different institution. Finally, the empirical fitting methods utilized in this work involved quite standard, simple equations. More accurate and robust fits may be achievable through a more exhaustive search of potential fitting methods. Similarly, deep learning and/or artificial intelligence methods could very likely provide full image data predictions from truncated MAA-SPECT/CT as opposed to simple lung counts from empirical models. Future work should explore these more sophisticated methods to improve upon the analysis discussed herein.

Conclusion

Strategies were developed to provide LSF and LMD estimates for 90Y radioembolization using truncated lung coverage data in MAA-SPECT/CT. Empirical models to predict lung counts at full lung coverage from truncated lung data can be used to estimate LMD. Using corrections to address the complications at the lung/liver boundary, three different SPECT/CT methods with increasing levels of corrections for non-uniform lung count densities yielded LMD estimates with minimal bias relative to ground truth values at full lung coverage. The results support the strategy of using MAA-SPECT/CT to provide LSF and LMD estimates for every 90Y radioembolization case, even in the presence of significant lung truncation.
