Effects of progressive-versus constant-intensity high-intensity functional training on health-related physical fitness in untrained female university students: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hongxie Chen, Demin Liu, Jun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectiveHigh-intensity functional training (HIFT) may be feasible for university physical education, but the effects of different intensity-organization strategies with a fixed session duration remain unclear. This exploratory randomized controlled trial compared progressive-intensity HIFT, constant-intensity HIFT, and moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) in untrained female university students.MethodsOf 75 randomized participants, 60 completed the 9-week intervention and were included in the primary analysis: progressive-intensity HIFT (n = 20), constant-intensity HIFT (n = 20), and MICT (n = 20). All groups trained three times per week with matched session duration. Training dose was monitored using %HRmax, session-RPE, and internal load. The primary outcome was the Physical Fitness Index (PFI); secondary outcomes included BMI, vital capacity, and individual physical fitness indicators. Between-group effects were assessed using ANCOVA, with bootstrap sensitivity analyses for outcomes with unequal variances.ResultsPFI differed significantly among groups (F(2, 56) = 79.46, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.739), showing a graded pattern from progressive-intensity HIFT to constant-intensity HIFT and MICT. Significant group effects were also found for the 50-m sprint, standing long jump, sit-ups, and 800-m run (partial η2 = 0.341–0.475, all p < 0.001). Progressive-intensity HIFT also outperformed constant-intensity HIFT in sit-ups and the 800-m run.ConclusionUnder time-matched class conditions, both HIFT protocols were associated with greater improvements in health-related physical fitness than MICT, while Progressive HIFT showed additional improvements over Constant HIFT. These findings should be interpreted in light of the higher cumulative internal load observed with Progressive HIFT.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2600126609); retrospectively registered.

Introduction

Insufficient physical activity remains a major public health concern among young adults, particularly women. The latest global data show that, in 2022, approximately 31.3% of adults did not meet recommended physical activity levels, with women showing a higher prevalence of insufficient physical activity than men.1 In parallel, studies indicate that regular physical activity among university students remains insufficient, with time constraints, academic workload, and limited sustained motivation reported as major barriers to participation.2,3 Therefore, in university physical education settings with limited class time, there is a practical need to identify feasible training programs for female university students that can improve fitness efficiently.

High-intensity functional training (HIFT) has emerged as a time-efficient training modality. Characterized by multi-joint, whole-body functional movements, HIFT can provide simultaneous cardiorespiratory and neuromuscular stimuli within a short period and can be adapted to participants’ abilities.4 Current evidence suggests that HIFT can improve physical fitness in healthy individuals, including strength, endurance, and physical performance.5,6 Among university students, studies have also shown that high-intensity training has positive effects on cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and selected physical fitness indicators.7, 8, 9 Furthermore, studies in female university students have shown that high-intensity functional training can improve aerobic fitness, body composition, and certain cardiometabolic parameters, while also increasing physical activity levels.10, 11, 12 These findings support the potential of HIFT as an efficient and multidimensional training modality.13

University physical education is typically delivered within fixed class-time constraints, making it important to compare training strategies that are feasible under matched session duration. In this context, moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) represents a conventional moderate-intensity comparator, Constant HIFT provides a non-progressive high-intensity functional training comparator, and Progressive HIFT applies a phased intensity increase within the same HIFT format.14, 15, 16 However, it remains unclear whether HIFT provides greater benefits than conventional MICT under matched session duration, or whether progressive intensity organization provides additional benefits beyond a constant-intensity HIFT stimulus. Therefore, this exploratory randomized controlled trial compared the effects of Progressive HIFT, Constant HIFT, and MICT on health-related physical fitness in previously untrained female university students. We hypothesized that both HIFT protocols would be associated with greater improvements than MICT and that Progressive HIFT would show additional improvements over Constant HIFT.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a three-group parallel randomized controlled design to compare the effects of progressive-intensity HIFT (G1), constant-intensity HIFT (G2), and MICT (G3) on health-related physical fitness in untrained female university students during a 9-week intervention. An independent researcher assigned participants using computer-generated block randomization (1:1:1),17 with allocation concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes.18 Participants were not blinded to group assignment, but the outcome assessors and statisticians remained blinded until completion of the primary analysis.17 This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Normal University (ZSRT2026027). The trial was retrospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2600126609). All participants signed written informed consent before enrollment. The protocol included baseline testing (Week 1), familiarization (Week 2), intervention (Weeks 3–11), and post-intervention testing (Week 12).

Participants

Participants were recruited from female undergraduate students at Zhejiang Normal University who had no prior systematic training experience. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female students aged 18–22 years; (2) clearance for exercise participation based on screening with the PAR-Q & YOU questionnaire19,20; and (3) no regular structured exercise participation during the previous 6 months, defined as engaging in moderate-to-vigorous exercise less than twice per week.10,21,22 The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) known cardiovascular, respiratory, or metabolic diseases; (2) musculoskeletal injuries that could limit exercise capacity or be aggravated by high-intensity training; (3) pregnancy or lactation; and (4) students in the School of Physical Education or members of the university's elite athletic teams.

The a priori sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1, with α = 0.05, power = 0.80, three groups, and an assumed large effect size (f = 0.40). This effect-size assumption was based on Cohen's conventional benchmark for a large effect and previous HIIT/HIFT studies reporting moderate-to-large changes in field-based physical fitness outcomes.10,23, 24, 25 The estimated minimum sample size was 64 participants; therefore, 75 participants were randomized to allow for potential dropout. Because 60 participants completed the intervention and were included in the primary complete-case analysis, an intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis including all 75 randomized participants was also conducted. All training sessions were supervised by two researchers with backgrounds in physical training and physical education, who completed standardized training before the intervention to ensure consistency in training delivery.

Intervention

The intervention was prescribed according to the frequency, intensity, time, and type framework and American College of Sports Medicine intensity-classification guidance.26 All three groups trained three times per week for 9 consecutive weeks.7,10,11,25 The 9-week intervention was organized into three 3-week phases and delivered during regular university physical education classes. All sessions were conducted on the same outdoor synthetic track and supervised onsite by trained researchers. Each session lasted 37 min, including a 10-min warm-up, a 22-min main training period, and a 5-min cool-down (Fig. 1A). The two HIFT groups used the same exercise selection and session structure (Fig. 1B). The main HIFT session consisted of three circuits, each including six exercises: goblet squat, mountain climbers, alternating reverse lunge, burpee without a push-up, butterfly sit-up, and high knees.27, 28, 29 Each exercise was performed for 30 s, followed by 30 s of rest,30 with a 2-min passive recovery period between circuits. Exercises involving external resistance used light-to-moderate loads, with approximately 40%–60% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) used as a general reference range for resistance intensity.16 Participant-specific 1RM values were not formally measured or estimated. During familiarization, the actual external loads were individualized according to each participant's ability to maintain correct technique while reaching the prescribed heart-rate and perceived-exertion targets. The MICT group performed 22 min of continuous jogging as the main training component,31 with running speed adjusted according to real-time heart-rate feedback (Fig. 1C).

The Progressive HIFT group followed a three-phase intensity progression: Weeks 1–3 at 70%–80% of maximal heart rate (HRmax) with a rating of perceived exertion (RPE) of 12–14, Weeks 4–6 at 80%–90% HRmax with RPE 14–16, and Weeks 7–9 at >90% HRmax with RPE 16–18.26 The Constant HIFT group maintained 80%–90% HRmax with RPE 14–16 throughout the intervention.26 The MICT group maintained 65%–75% HRmax, targeting approximately 70% HRmax with RPE 12–13 (Fig. 1D).32

Heart rate was continuously recorded during each session using the Polar Team Pro system,33 and relative intensity was expressed as %HRmax using the age-predicted HRmax automatically calculated by the system from each participant's age (HRmax = 220 − age). Approximately 30 min after each session, session rating of perceived exertion (session-RPE) was recorded using the Borg 6–20 scale.34,35 Internal load was calculated using the session-RPE method as follows: internal load (AU) = session-RPE × session duration (min).35,36 Weekly internal load was calculated as the sum of the three weekly sessions, and cumulative internal load was calculated as the sum of all sessions across the 9-week intervention.34,35 Intervention fidelity was monitored using attendance, heart-rate responses, session-RPE, and adherence to the prescribed intensity range.34,35,37

In the HIFT groups, training was adjusted according to predefined rules38 based on real-time heart rate, RPE, and movement quality. When heart rate and RPE remained below the target range and technique was maintained, exercise cadence was increased first, followed by modest increases in external load or exercise density when appropriate. When perceived exertion was excessive, discomfort was reported, or movement quality declined, participants first performed a regressed exercise variation, followed by load reduction, reduced exercise density, or additional rest. Detailed adjustment rules and examples of exercise regression are provided in Supplementary Appendix A. Before each session, participants were asked to report menstrual-related or other physical discomfort; when moderate-to-severe discomfort was reported, exercise intensity was reduced, additional rest was provided, or the session was discontinued according to the participant's condition.

Study outcomes

Study outcomes were assessed using the Chinese National Student Physical Fitness Test issued by the Chinese Ministry of Education, which has been widely used to assess multiple domains of student physical fitness in Chinese educational settings.39, 40, 41 Before formal testing, participants received standardized instructions and demonstrations for each test item and were allowed to perform familiarization trials when applicable to reduce learning effects. All tests were administered by trained researchers who followed standardized procedures, including consistent testing order, warm-up, equipment, testing location, and verbal instructions. Assessments were conducted at baseline and after the intervention (Week 12).

Primary outcome

The Physical Fitness Index (PFI) was prespecified as the primary outcome in the study protocol before participant enrollment. It was used to summarize overall adaptation across multiple field-based fitness domains in this educational setting.42,43 The PFI was derived from five components: the 50-m sprint, standing long jump, 1-min sit-ups, sit-and-reach, and 800-m run. Each component was standardized using the baseline mean and standard deviation of the total sample. For standing long jump, 1-min sit-ups, and sit-and-reach, Z-scores were calculated so that higher values indicated better performance. For the 50-m sprint and 800-m run, Z-scores were reverse-coded because lower times indicated better performance. The final PFI was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the five standardized component scores:PFI = (Zstanding long jump + Zsit-ups + Zsit-and-reach + Z50-m sprint, reversed + Z800-m run, reversed) / 5

Thus, a higher PFI indicated better overall field-based physical fitness. Further calculation details are provided in Supplementary Appendix B.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes consisted of individual physical fitness test items, including body mass index (BMI), vital capacity, 50-m sprint, standing long jump, sit-and-reach, 1-min sit-ups, and 800-m run.39 These outcomes were used to characterize the specific responses of individual test items to the intervention and to complement interpretation of the primary outcome (PFI). All tests were administered in accordance with the relevant standards and operational guidelines.39,44 Details are provided in Supplementary Appendix C.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Baseline characteristics and baseline outcome values were compared among the three groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Training intensity and internal load were analyzed to characterize intervention fidelity and the delivered training dose. Phase-level %HRmax, session-RPE, and internal load per session were examined using repeated-measures ANOVA, with group as the between-subjects factor and phase as the within-subjects factor. Cumulative internal load across the 9-week intervention was compared among groups using Welch's ANOVA, followed by Games–Howell post hoc comparisons when appropriate.

Between-group intervention effects on the primary outcome (PFI) and secondary outcomes were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with post-intervention values as dependent variables, group as the fixed factor, and corresponding baseline values as covariates.45,46 The homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was examined using the group × baseline interaction term. When the overall group effect was significant, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed. For outcomes showing heterogeneity of variance, bootstrap-based ANCOVA was conducted as a sensitivity analysis using 5000 resamples and bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals.47,48 An intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis was also conducted including all 75 randomized participants, with missing post-intervention outcomes handled using multiple imputation by fully conditional specification.49 The imputation model included randomized group, baseline outcome values, and available baseline and post-intervention outcome variables; 20 imputed datasets were generated and analyzed using the same ANCOVA models. Overall group effects were reported as F values, p values, and partial eta squared (partial η2), and pairwise comparisons were reported as adjusted mean differences, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values.

Secondary outcomes

For secondary outcomes, significant between-group effects were observed for the 50-m sprint, standing long jump, 1-min sit-ups, and 800-m run (F = 14.51–25.31, all p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.341–0.475; Table 4). Both HIFT protocols showed better performance than MICT in the 50-m sprint and standing long jump, whereas no significant difference was observed between Progressive HIFT and Constant HIFT. For 1-min sit-ups and the 800-m run, Progressive HIFT showed better performance than Constant HIFT and MICT, with Constant HIFT also outperforming MICT. No significant between-group differences were observed for BMI, vital capacity, or sit-and-reach.

Sensitivity analyses

The homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was satisfied for all outcomes. For outcomes showing heterogeneity of variance, namely PFI and the 800-m run, bootstrap-based ANCOVA was conducted as a sensitivity analysis using 5000 resamples and BCa 95% confidence intervals. The bootstrap results were consistent with the primary ANCOVA (Fig. 4).

The intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis including all 75 randomized participants yielded results consistent with the primary complete-case analysis. For PFI, the pooled adjusted means were 0.87 (95% CI, 0.80 to 0.95) for Progressive HIFT, 0.66 (95% CI, 0.59 to 0.73) for Constant HIFT, and 0.24 (95% CI, 0.17 to 0.31) for MICT. The corresponding pooled adjusted mean differences were 0.21 for Progressive HIFT versus Constant HIFT, 0.63 for Progressive HIFT versus MICT, and 0.42 for Constant HIFT versus MICT, confirming the same pairwise pattern as the primary analysis. The direction and statistical conclusions for the secondary outcomes were unchanged (Supplementary Appendix D).

Discussion

This exploratory randomized controlled trial compared progressive-intensity HIFT, constant-intensity HIFT, and MICT in previously untrained female university students under time-matched class conditions. Both HIFT protocols were associated with greater improvements in overall field-based physical fitness than MICT, and Progressive HIFT showed additional improvements over Constant HIFT in PFI, 1-min sit-ups, and the 800-m run. However, the Progressive HIFT group also accumulated a higher internal load than the other groups. Thus, the additional improvements observed with Progressive HIFT should be interpreted in light of its higher cumulative internal load.

Compared with MICT, HIFT may have produced broader adaptations by combining high-intensity intervals with multi-joint, whole-body functional movements, thereby providing cardiorespiratory, neuromuscular, and motor-control stimuli within the same training duration.4,6,30,31,50 This interpretation is consistent with evidence that HIFT improves physical fitness in healthy individuals5,6 and that high-intensity or functional training benefits cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular fitness, and overall physical fitness in university students.7,8,10,11,25 In the present study, both HIFT protocols produced greater improvements than MICT in several performance-related outcomes, suggesting that HIFT may be a useful field-based option for university physical education when instructional time is fixed.

The comparison between the two HIFT protocols requires cautious interpretation. Although the Progressive HIFT and Constant HIFT groups used the same exercise selection and session structure, the protocols differed in intensity organization, and cumulative internal load was also higher in the Progressive HIFT group. Progressive HIFT was associated with greater improvements in PFI, 1-min sit-ups, and the 800-m run. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that intensity progression alone produced superior adaptations; the independent contributions of intensity organization and cumulative internal load cannot be separated in the present study design. In previously untrained participants, a phased increase in intensity may also have supported exercise tolerance and training quality, consistent with principles of progressive overload and periodized exercise prescription in inactive or novice populations.15,16,35

At the component level, PFI changes were not driven uniformly by all outcomes. For speed- and power-related outcomes, including the 50-m sprint and standing long jump, both HIFT protocols showed better performance than MICT, whereas differences between the two HIFT protocols were limited. This suggests that these outcomes were responsive to HIFT but less sensitive to differences between the two HIFT protocols over the 9-week period. In contrast, 1-min sit-ups and the 800-m run, which are more closely related to muscular endurance, cardiorespiratory endurance, and accumulated training stimulus,32,51 showed a clearer graded pattern across groups. Thus, the between-group differences in PFI appeared to be driven more by cumulative gains in endurance-related components than by uniform superiority across all individual measures. No significant between-group differences were observed for BMI, vital capacity, or sit-and-reach, suggesting that these outcomes may be less sensitive to short-term HIFT protocols or may require a longer intervention period or more targeted training.5,52,53 Therefore, PFI should be interpreted as a practical summary of overall field-based fitness performance, with component-level outcomes used to identify the specific domains underlying the composite change.54,55

From a practical perspective, the present findings suggest that HIFT may be implemented as a feasible strategy for improving field-based physical fitness in female university students under fixed class-time conditions.10,11,25 The progressive HIFT protocol may be particularly useful when participants are previously untrained, provided that intensity progression is carefully supervised and adjusted according to individual tolerance and movement quality. The combined use of heart-rate monitoring and session-RPE also provides a practical approach for regulating and documenting training dose in university physical education settings.34, 35, 36.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was recruited from a single university and included only previously untrained female students, limiting generalizability. Second, although training frequency and session duration were matched, cumulative internal load differed between the two HIFT protocols; therefore, the additional improvements observed with Progressive HIFT cannot be attributed to intensity organization alone. Third, reliance on field-based outcomes improved ecological validity but limited mechanistic interpretation because direct VO2max, isokinetic strength, and physiological biomarkers were not measured.56,57 Fourth, menstrual-related discomfort was monitored before each session, but menstrual-cycle phase was not objectively verified or used to standardize the timing of physical fitness testing; therefore, uncontrolled variability related to menstrual-cycle phase in perceived exertion, adherence, or performance cannot be excluded.58, 59, 60 Fifth, no follow-up assessment was conducted, so the durability of training effects and longer-term adherence remain unknown. Future studies should include larger and more diverse samples, tighter workload matching, laboratory-based outcomes, menstrual-cycle tracking, and follow-up assessments.

Conclusion

In this exploratory trial of previously untrained female university students, both HIFT protocols were associated with greater improvements in health-related physical fitness than MICT under time-matched class conditions. Progressive HIFT showed additional improvements over Constant HIFT in overall physical fitness, muscular endurance, and cardiorespiratory endurance. However, the effects of intensity organization and cumulative internal load cannot be distinguished in this study.
