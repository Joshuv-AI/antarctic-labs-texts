Pharmacotherapy sequencing strategies for patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction: a cost–utility analysis
Authors: Minh Tri Van, Peter Loewen, Mohsen Sadatsafavi, Wei Zhang, Nathaniel M. Hawkins, Ricky D. Turgeon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStandard pharmacotherapy for patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) comprises “quadruple therapy” with a renin–angiotensin system inhibitor, a β-blocker, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and a sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor. Randomized controlled trials evaluating different sequencing strategies for initiating and titrating these medications are lacking. We sought to compare costs for various HFrEF quadruple therapy sequencing strategies.MethodsWe used an individual-based state-transition microsimulation model to compare cost utility for 12 sequencing strategies with either weekly or biweekly medication adjustments for treatment-naive patients with HFrEF. We conducted a probabilistic analysis with a lifetime horizon from the public-payer perspective, along with a deterministic sensitivity analysis and 2 scenario analyses. We estimated costs, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), incremental net monetary benefit (NMB) at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50 000/QALY, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) of each strategy versus the conventional approach.ResultsOver a lifetime, the 12 strategies resulted in costs ranging from $76 440 to $79 338, QALYs ranging from 9.55 to 9.72, and incremental NMB ranging from $2233 to $6529. Compared with the conventional sequencing strategy of starting and titrating biweekly 1 medication at a time, other strategies had ICERs ranging from $11 175 to $22 492. The simultaneous initiation of all 4 medications with subsequent biweekly adjustment had the highest probability of being the most cost-effective strategy at the specified willingness-to-pay threshold.InterpretationSimultaneous initiation of the 4 standard HFrEF medications with biweekly adjustment provided the highest cost utility compared with other strategies. Systems of care are needed to enable rapid initiation and sustained use of HFrEF medications.

Heart failure causes substantial morbidity and mortality and consumes considerable health care resources.1–4 Heart failure is the third most common reason for hospital admission in Canada,2 with a median length of stay of 7 days.2,3 In 2013, 45 600 Canadians were admitted to hospital for heart failure as the primary reason, costing the health care system $482 million, and it is estimated that by 2033, this number will increase to 54 000 hospital admissions, with costs reaching $722 million.4 Standard pharmacotherapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) consists of “quadruple therapy,” which is a combination of (1) an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor, an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), or an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor; (2) a β-blocker; (3) a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist; and (4) a sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2I).5–8 Moreover, recent guidelines preferentially recommend angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors over ACE inhibitors or ARBs on the basis of trials showing fewer heart failure hospital admissions and deaths in patients with chronic HFrEF.9,10

Although each of these medications reduces morbidity and mortality when added to background standard-of-care, no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated different approaches to initiating and titrating these medications, termed “sequencing strategies.” The conventional sequencing strategy involves starting 1 medication and titrating it to the target (or maximum-tolerated) dose employed in RCTs before starting the next medication following the historical order in which each medication was evaluated.11–14 However, this conventional approach has been challenged because of its extended adjustment period (minimum 3–6 months with strict biweekly adjustment),13,15 leaving patients vulnerable to unnecessary morbidity and mortality.16–25 Conversely, alternative strategies supported mainly by expert opinion, involving simultaneous adjustment of 2 or more medications, may entail trade-offs, including higher upfront medication and implementation costs, as well as a potential increase in adverse events.

In the absence of evidence from RCTs that directly compare clinical value and feasibility of these sequencing strategies, we compare costs of various treatment strategies to inform decisionmakers regarding the allocation of health care resources to optimize patient outcomes and costs in the management of HFrEF.

Methods

Model overview

We followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards statement and Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) guidelines in the design and reporting of this cost–utility analysis.26,27 We selected a microsimulation model because of its ability to track individual patient histories, which allowed for the modelling of medications adjustment based on adverse events during each cycle. We used a weekly cycle length, corresponding to the minimum frequency between medication adjustments, and to the median length of hospital stay for heart failure in Canada.28 We modelled over a lifetime horizon with a half-cycle correction.

Figure 1 shows the model structure. All patients started in the stable heart failure state, and could remain in this state, be admitted to hospital for heart failure, or die. While patients were stable and undergoing medication adjustments, they could transiently experience 1 of the following adverse events: bradycardia, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and renal impairment. These adverse events could be dose-limiting or non–dose limiting, but did not lead to permanent discontinuation. Patients admitted to hospital could die or return to the stable state. The probabilities of a heart failure hospital admission, death, and experiencing any adverse events depended on each individual patient’s combination of medications and dose levels (i.e., sub-target or target dose) in the current cycle.

Sequencing strategies

We modelled 12 strategies based on 6 profiles, representing a range of approaches to HFrEF sequencing strategies (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240116/tab-related-content), with 2 versions of each profile based on biweekly (a) or weekly (b) medication adjustments. Profiles 1 (“conventional” strategy) and 2 started a single medication on the initial encounter (renin–angiotensin inhibitor and SGLT2I, respectively); these were determined to be the least- and most-effective single-medication starting strategies in a prior modelling study.16,23,29 Profiles 3 (“rapid” strategy) and 4 involved starting 2 medications on the first visit; profile 3 prioritized SGLT2I owing to its capacity to enhance the tolerability of other medications and lack of titration,16,17,24,30,31 whereas profile 4 prioritized angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor and β-blocker, as often done in practice.16–18,24,25,30,31 Profile 5 (“cluster” strategy) involved starting 3 medications with the least overlap in adverse events on the first visit as proposed by Canadian heart failure experts,19,21,32,33 while profile 6 (“simultaneous” strategy) involved starting all 4 medications at the initial visit as recommended by international heart failure experts and various narrative reviews on the topic.20,22,23,34

Figure 2 illustrates how the initiation and titration processes for each strategy were translated into a series of “steps” and “walks.” A “step” involves the initiation and/or titration of medications and is associated with a combination of medications and dose levels defined within a strategy (e.g., step 2 of profile 2a represents the initiation of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist at half the target dose while maintaining SGLT2I). A “walk” breaks down each step into weekly intervals (e.g., step 2 of profile 2a comprised walks 3 and 4, corresponding to the minimum 2-week duration for a patient on this step). Patients begin in the first walk and can transition through the sequencing strategies by moving to the next walk, staying in the same walk, or moving back to the walk corresponding to a previous step, following a medication adjustment algorithm (Appendix 2, Supplemental Figure 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240116/tab-related-content).

Each walk represents 1 week in practice and 1 cycle in the model, with each step corresponding to each initiation or titration. Study population and base scenario

We modelled a hypothetical cohort of treatment-naive patients with HFrEF without contraindication to quadruple therapy. This cohort shared the same characteristics as the placebo arm of the Studies of Left Ventricular Dysfunction trial, from which we derived the baseline event rates for both heart failure hospital admission and mortality.35,36 For the base scenario, we assumed that patients would undergo medication adjustments (and their associated transient adverse events and costs) only during the first year based on the observation in practice that most adjustments are made early after diagnosis followed by minimal changes.37,38 Subsequently, the combination of medications and dose received at the end of week 52 was continued for the remainder of each patient’s lifetime. At that point, we assumed patients would not experience new adverse events or incur medication adjustments, but they would continue to derive transition probabilities for death and hospital admission until death, as well as incur costs associated with the treatment and hospital admissions. Appendix 2 outlines additional model assumptions and their rationales.

The utilities associated with stable heart failure and heart failure hospital admission, and disutility owing to an adverse event were taken from previous economic evaluations and studies that derived these values from patients with the relevant adverse events.63–67,71 Utilities over different periods were converted to a weekly period. We applied a CADTH-recommended annual discount rate of 1.5% for both cost and utility.27

Analyses

We performed a probabilistic cost–utility analysis through simulation of 5000 patients over 5000 sets of sampled parameters to emulate the uncertainty in model parameters and generate a distribution of expected costs and QALYs. Log-normal distributions were used for ratios, β distributions for probabilities and utilities, and γ distributions for costs and rates. We reported the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and the incremental net monetary benefit (NMB) of each strategy compared with profile 1a, using a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50 000/QALY.72 The advantage of NMB, equal to (QALY × willingness-to-pay threshold) – cost, over ICER is that it allows cost-effectiveness comparisons across multiple strategies, with the strategy with the largest NMB having the greatest cost-effectiveness.73 The probabilistic results were presented as a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve. We conducted all analyses with TreeAge Pro 2023.

Variability and uncertainty

Parameter uncertainty was estimated to be within their 95% confidence intervals, or wide distributions (± 50% of the point estimate) when confidence intervals were unavailable. For deterministic sensitivity analyses, we assessed the baseline event rates and costs, and varied the discount rate from 0% to 3%.27 We performed 2 scenario analyses: alternative scenario 1 assumed that medications adjustment ended early after 3 months (13 cycles); alternative scenario 2 assumed that the benefit of medications accrued only after target doses were reached.

Ethics approval

No ethics approval was required as all data were obtained from literature or publicly available data sources.

Results

Base scenario cost-effectiveness probabilistic analysis

Table 2 displays the results of the base scenario probabilistic analysis. For all strategies, the lifetime average cost ranged from $76 440 to $79 338 and lifetime QALYs ranged from 9.55 to 9.72. Compared with the conventional sequencing strategy (profile 1a), all other strategies resulted in a positive ICER below the willingness-to- pay threshold ($11 175–$22 492). The resulting incremental NMB ranged from $2233 for the strategy of initiating 1 medication at a time starting with SGLT2I and biweekly adjustment (profile 2a) to $6529 for the strategy of simultaneous initiation of all 4 medications with biweekly adjustment (profile 6a). Based on the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve, profile 6a was the most likely optimal strategy for a willingness-to-pay threshold of $14 000 or greater and was the optimal choice in 75% of simulations at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50 000 (Figure 3).

Sensitivity and alternative scenario analyses

The NMB tornado diagram of 5000 deterministic simulations showed that the discount rate, incidence rate of death, and heart failure hospital admission had the greatest impact on NMB, that they outweighed the impact of adverse events, and that costs had minimal impact (Figure 4). However, none of the input parameters led to a negative NMB, indicating that analyses were robust at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50 000/QALY. In pairwise comparisons, the strategy of simultaneous initiation of all 4 medications with biweekly titration (profile 6a) continued to show greater NMB than every other strategy when each model parameter was varied across its range (Appendix 2, Supplemental Figure 4).

In scenario 1 (medication adjustments ended after 3 mo), the conventional sequencing strategy (profile 1a) yielded lower NMB compared with the primary analysis, whereas other strategies changed minimally (Appendix 2, Supplemental Table 3 and Supplemental Figure 2). In alternative scenario 2 (assumed individual medications were ineffective until target dose achieved), QALYs for all strategies decreased, though less in strategies with weekly adjustments (version b). As such, the strategy of simultaneous initiation of all 4 medications with biweekly adjustment (profile 6a) was optimal at a willingness-to-pay range of $15 000–$25 000, whereas the same strategy with weekly adjustment (profile 6b) was the most cost-effective strategy for a willingness-to-pay threshold of $25 000 or greater (Appendix 2, Supplemental Table 4 and Supplemental Figure 3).

Interpretation

In this microsimulation model–based cost–utility analysis comparing 12 sequencing strategies, a strategy involving simultaneous initiation of all 4 components of angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor–based HFrEF quadruple therapy at low doses followed by biweekly adjustments emerged as the most cost-effective sequencing strategy. This finding reaffirms the incremental but modest benefits associated with starting medications earlier, the modest influence of specific sequencing strategies and adjustment frequency on lifelong outcomes, and the relative greater importance of clinical outcomes over the impact of adverse effects and medication cost in dictating long-term cost-effectiveness of sequencing strategies.

In the present analysis, the incremental benefit from the most effective strategy (profile 6a) compared with the least effective strategy (profile 1a) was 0.17 QALY, or 2 quality-adjusted life months. Although this increase in QALY is modest, it reflects a change in the approach to the use of the same medications and accounts for burdens including adverse events associated with a higher number of medications. Additionally, in a population-based analysis, these differences tally up and can result in substantial gain in population QALYs. Finally, although not reflected in the QALY measurements, protracted sequencing strategies that emphasize dose increases before starting the next medication are more vulnerable to interruptions, leading to worsened outcomes, as shown in scenario 1.

Both established and proposed sequencing strategies for pharmacotherapy in HFrEF have largely been based on non–systematic reviews and expert opinion.16–25,29–34 This reflects the evolution of HFrEF management based on sequential, predominantly placebo-controlled RCTs, combined with the challenges of feasibly comparing treatment strategies as opposed to individual medications.29,74 A different modelling study, which compared the risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospital admission across different sequencing strategies — limited to approaches starting with 1 or 2 medications at a time — showed that initiating treatment with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist and SGLT2I, followed by other medications, provided the greatest efficacy.29 However, this study was restricted to modelling clinical outcomes at 1 year and did not incorporate costs, utility, adverse events and their disutility, or variable rate of HFrEF medication adjustment. In contrast, the present study modelled emergence of medication adverse events, as well as outcomes over a patient’s lifetime, capturing relevant benefits and harms. By using these factors and simulating medication adjustment algorithms across different sequencing strategies, this model better emulated clinical practice. Finally, by quantifying both benefits and harms into utility measures, in combination with the associated monetary costs, our model allowed for a more comprehensive comparison of strategies.

Our analysis emphasized the overall cost-effectiveness of biweekly versus weekly medication adjustments, partially owing to the added fixed costs associated with physician encounters to make medication adjustments. It is unknown whether more frequent visits might provide additional benefits. Future studies should focus on strategies to implement standardized, structured HFrEF medication optimization in practice. There is increasing recognition of prescribing by pharmacists and other nonphysician clinicians as a strategy to improve medication utilization and reduce health care costs in other cardiovascular conditions,75,76 and further research is needed to inform funding models to support multidisciplinary care in heart failure.77 In the meantime, clinicians and other decision-makers can use the results of this analysis, which shows the cost-effectiveness of sequencing strategies that emphasize rapid initiation of all components of quadruple therapy, to implement order sets and other strategies to enable rapid initiation and sustained use of quadruple therapy for HFrEF.

Limitations

The model had some limitations. We derived rates of death, heart failure hospital admissions, and adverse events from RCTs, some of which employed a run-in phase.41–43 Therefore, we may have underestimated adverse event rates, as trials with active run-in by design retain patients who tolerate medications early on. Additionally, use of trial-reported adverse events, which are typically reported as incidence over the entire trial duration, to derive weekly adverse event rates may have further led to underestimation if adverse events were front-loaded during medication initiation. However, these limitations applied similarly to all strategies in our comparison, and changes in adverse event rates generally had minimal impact in sensitivity analysis.

Adverse events in the model either resolved after 1 cycle without intervention or resulted in temporary decrease in treatment intensity, and we did not incorporate any cost for these transient adverse events beyond medication adjustments.

The model did not account for comorbidities, polypharmacy, social determinants of health, or patient preferences. These factors could influence deviations from standard approaches, yet the purpose of this study was to evaluate potential “default” approaches.

This model captured additive and independent adverse drug effects but could not account for more complex interactions that could theoretically occur with simultaneous initiation.

Finally, since the model primarily incorporates treatment effects from RCTs, additional caution should be exercised when applying the results to non-RCT populations.

Conclusion

Among possible HFrEF medication sequencing strategies, simultaneous initiation of all 4 standard HFrEF medications with biweekly titration had the highest cost utility, and the conventional sequencing strategy had the lowest cost utility, although differences were modest. Systems of care that improve efficiency of medication adjustments and access to available HFrEF medications are needed to enable rapid initiation and sustained use.
