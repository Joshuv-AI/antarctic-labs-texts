Experimental vibration time-series data for rotor crack detection and condition monitoring
Authors: Maria Jesus Gomez Garcia, Cristina Castejon, Higinio Rubio, Juan Carlos Garcia-Prada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCondition monitoring and fault diagnosis in rotating machinery require high-quality datasets that capture the progressive evolution of structural defects under controlled conditions. This paper presents a comprehensive dataset acquired using SpectraQuest MFS-Lite, designed to study the dynamic behavior of rotating shafts with transverse cracks. The dataset includes measurements from shafts with ten crack conditions focusing on both centrally and laterally located progressive shaft cracks: a healthy state and nine progressive crack levels. These fault levels correspond to relative crack depths ranging from 4.15% to 50.00% of the shaft diameter, which translates to a cross-sectional area reduction from 1.36% to 50.00%. Experiments were conducted under three different rotational speeds (20 Hz, 40 Hz, and 60 Hz) to capture the influence of operating conditions on the vibration response. Time-series data were recorded using a single uniaxial accelerometer at a sampling frequency of 6,000 Hz, with each individual signal containing 16,384 data points across a duration of 2.73 seconds. To guarantee statistical repeatability, a minimum of 1,000 independent repetitions were acquired for each crack level and speed configuration, and 2,000 repetitions for the healthy baseline, all archived in standard CSV format. For each crack level and rotational speed, vibration signals were recorded using a consistent experimental setup to ensure repeatability and comparability across measurements, comprising records from two distinct shafts to evaluate the physical influence of both middle and lateral crack positions. The progressive crack depths enable the analysis of fault severity and its effect on the dynamic response of the system, while the multiple rotational speeds allow the investigation of speed-dependent fault signatures. This controlled framework provides a valuable quantitative benchmark for developing, validating, and comparing signal processing, machine learning, and physics-based approaches for crack detection and severity estimation in rotating machinery.

Background & Summary

The operational reliability of rotating machinery is a cornerstone of modern industrial infrastructure. Among various mechanical anomalies, transverse shaft cracks are particularly hazardous due to their potential for catastrophic structural failure. Early detection and precise severity estimation are critical for shifting from reactive maintenance to robust prognostics and health management (PHM) strategies. However, developing reliable diagnostic algorithms requires high-fidelity datasets that capture the progressive evolution of defects under diverse operating conditions.

There is a lack of experimental data in this field, and most authors base their work on analytical models or numerical simulations. To bridge the gap between these theoretical models and industrial reality, specialized experimental platforms such as the SpectraQuest Machinery Fault Simulator (MFS-Lite) have become essential. Previous research has extensively utilized the MFS-Lite to explore various mechanical faults. For instance, Ansari et al. analyzed the influence of unbalance1, while Verellen et al. applied ultrasound analysis to detect bearing defects2. The cumulative impact of this simulator is well-documented in the review by Mustafa and Nayfeh, highlighting its role as a global benchmark for structural health monitoring3.

While the rotating machinery community benefits from well-established open benchmarks for bearing and gear diagnostics—such as the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) dataset4, the Intelligent Maintenance Systems (IMS) repository5, or the Paderborn University dataset6—open-access experimental time-series data for shaft cracks remain exceptionally scarce. To the best of our knowledge, there is a severe lack of comprehensive, publicly available, experimental time-series datasets dedicated specifically to progressive transverse shaft cracks in rotating machinery. Due to the destructive nature and technical complexity of physically inducing controlled, gradual cracking in rotating shafts, the majority of rotor crack detection studies in the literature are forced to rely purely on numerical simulations or analytical models (e.g., Jeffcott rotor variants).

In recent years, intelligent data-driven methodologies and deep learning architectures have significantly advanced condition monitoring (CM) and automated asset surveillance in rotating machinery, effectively bypassing the inherent uncertainties of complex analytical physical models7. Within this paradigm, substantial research has focused on benchmarking classical machine learning classifiers against deep learning approaches—such as using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) fed by raw signal representations—to isolate structural cracks from other common anomalies like unbalance or misalignment during steady-state operations8. However, standard recurrent neural network layers processing raw time-series often encounter intensive computational training burdens and vanishing gradient limitations; consequently, a prominent trend in contemporary CM involves preprocessing continuous, high-resolution vibration sequences into two-dimensional spatial representations-such as through the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) or Synchrosqueezing Transform (SST)—to train Convolutional Autoencoders (CAEs) more effectively9. Despite these methodological advances, a major bottleneck in deploying such frameworks across real-world applications remains the severe scarcity of comprehensive, open-access failure datasets, which inherently limits the benchmarking of advanced transfer learning strategies10. This challenge is further compounded by operational variability—particularly changing rotational speeds—which introduces critical domain shifts that significantly degrade conventional diagnostic accuracy11. While several open-source repositories successfully address localized component defects (such as bearing or milling tool degradations) under variable conditions7,11, experimental time-series captured across highly granular, progressive structural shaft cracks remain exceptionally scarce. By delivering a high-volume statistical repository of completely unaveraged and raw multi-class vibration records across discrete operational speeds, this dataset directly addresses these literature gaps, providing the ideal empirical foundation required to develop and benchmark both advanced time-frequency image-based diagnostic pipelines and speed-normalization algorithms.

In this work, we present a dataset acquired using the SpectraQuest Machinery Fault Simulator, focusing on the progressive development of a transverse crack12. The dataset includes measurements corresponding to ten crack conditions: a healthy shaft and nine increasing crack levels. Experiments were conducted at three rotational speeds—20 Hz, 40 Hz, and 60 Hz—to capture the influence of operating conditions on the system response. It should be noted that the operational profile in this repository is framed around three discrete, stable rotational speeds (20 Hz, 40 Hz, and 60 Hz). While this discrete configuration limits the study of continuous transient speed variations (such as run-up or coast-down states), evaluating these three distinct speeds provides essential data to study how velocity influences the crack-induced dynamic responses. The dataset presented here is the product of a long-standing research line by the MAQLAB Group, focusing on advanced signal processing and intelligent classification for crack diagnosis. This database has been foundational to several previous studies. For instance, experimental evidence, validated by an analytical Jeffcott rotor model, demonstrated that the 3x harmonic of the rotational speed serves as a robust indicator of crack presence13. Furthermore, the dataset was employed to evaluate the reliability of automated diagnostic systems using Probability of Detection (POD) curves14. This study confirmed high sensitivity even for incipient cracks, representing just 4% of the shaft diameter. Additionally, other research utilized these data to detail the effectiveness of the Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT)15. By analyzing energy distribution across various decomposition levels, the authors pinpointed specific frequency bands where energy increments directly correlate with crack progression. Besides, the data was used to select the optimal parameters of the Wavelet Packets Decomposition16.

This dataset provides a controlled experimental framework for analyzing the effect of crack severity and rotational speed on vibration signals. The progressive crack levels allow researchers to investigate fault evolution, while the multiple operating speeds enable the study of speed-dependent diagnostic features. The dataset is intended to support the development, validation, and comparison of methods for fault detection, fault severity estimation, and structural health monitoring in rotating machinery.

The manuscript is organized as follows: The ‘Methods’ section describes the experimental test rig setup, the specifications of the geometry and materials, the progressive crack levels, and the precise measurement chain. ‘Data Records’ provides the repository structure and file formatting. ‘Technical Validation’ evaluates the data quality, statistical reliability, and consistency against established physical models, while also addressing its boundary limitations. Finally, ‘Data availability’ presents brief guidelines for data loading, code availability, and potential applications for the research community.

Methods

The general objective of this work is to obtain data from a mechanical system to diagnose the condition of a specific element during operation. The first step is to design an experimental methodology that allows the acquisition of data representative of the different defect conditions to be differentiated. Next, based on both a theoretical study13 and the analysis of the signals obtained, the extraction and selection of possible valid patterns is carried out, that is, the selection of all the parameters that seem to change when the conditions to be differentiated are modified. Subsequently, for each possible valid pattern selected, a classification system can be designed.

There are other fundamental considerations, such as the selection of machine conditions during measurement acquisition (speed, load conditions, etc.). These must be representative of nominal operation, since it is in this state that the diagnosis is to be performed. If the machine can operate under variable conditions, it will be advisable to establish a range of these conditions so that it is possible to discern under which conditions the optimal diagnosis can be made. As for the defects to be studied, at least those that occur most frequently should be tested, as these are the ones that are of interest to detect. It will be necessary to define the geometry of the defects, as well as their location. Another fundamental decision is the range of defect sizes to be investigated. It is interesting to start with very small sizes in order to obtain information about the smallest size that can be reliably detected. The largest size inspected must be critical, as it is also important to know the largest defect that might not be detected. The number of intervals inspected is also crucial, as it will determine the resolution of the POD curves.

Selection of sensors and signal parameters

A single uniaxial accelerometer (Brüel & Kjær) is used to measure vibration in the radial direction of the shaft. The sensor was factory-calibrated by the manufacturer, and its official calibration certificate is available to ensure measurement reliability. Its characteristics are shown in Table 2. Because only one channel is required to capture the radial vibration of the shaft, channel synchronization is not applicable in this experimental setup. The sensor is placed in the vertical direction at the top of the bearing housing, where it is attached to the fastening screw with a magnet. Two additional accelerometers were evaluated on the test bench in order to select the most suitable one. The first was a uniaxial accelerometer that allowed for a higher sampling frequency, and the second was a triaxial accelerometer. After analyzing the signals obtained with the three sensors, both were discarded because a higher sampling frequency was not considered necessary in the case of the first, and no difference was found in the acceleration measurements with respect to the direction of measurement.

In this case, knowing that the permissible range for the sampling frequency for the chosen accelerometer is in the range [0.1–8.4 kHz], a sampling frequency of 6,000 Hz is selected. To handle signal noise and avoid aliasing, a Brüel & Kjær NEXUS signal conditioning system was utilized. The signal was filtered using a high-pass filter at 0.1 Hz to eliminate the DC component, and a low-pass anti-aliasing filter at 3000 Hz, in accordance with the Nyquist theorem for the selected 6000 Hz sampling rate. Additionally, to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio, the accelerometer signal was amplified by a factor of x10 using the signal conditioner, which was the highest possible gain without causing signal saturation.

To select the number of points that define each signal, it is necessary to determine the rotational speed (ω) at which the tests will be performed and the sampling frequency (Fs), in order to measure a significant number of machine cycles in each acquisition. Considering the slowest speed, 20 Hz or 1,200 rpm, 20 machine cycles are performed every second. In this case, the acquisition of fifty machine cycles in each signal has been considered, which would imply an approximate time of 2.5 seconds per signal. To optimize Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) computation and avoid zero-padding artifacts, the sample size was selected as a power of two. Otherwise, the phenomenon of zero padding occurs, i.e., the signal is artificially padded with zeros until its size reaches the next power of two, which would introduce errors.

For all these reasons, and taking into account that the sampling frequency is 6,000 Hz, the total sample size selected is 16,384 points (N = 214), i.e., the power of two closest to the requirements. This value implies that each signal is acquired in a total time of 2.73 s. Measurement chain

Following the accelerometer in the measurement chain is a Brüel and Kjaer NEXUS signal conditioner and a Keithley KUSB-3100 acquisition card. The measurement chain diagram can be seen in Fig. 3. The signal conditioner performs both amplification and filtering functions so that the signal has the appropriate characteristics for acquisition. From the signal conditioner, the measurements are sent to the acquisition board, which is connected via USB to a computer that archives the measurements using software specifically designed for this purpose called Btool20, developed in MATLAB. This software allows a defined number of measurements to be acquired automatically, waiting between acquisitions for a time also determined by the user. The parameters of the acquired signals are defined by the sampling frequency and the number of points each one contains.

Test conditions

The shaft rotates under the sole load of its weight, without adding any external load.The measurements have been acquired during steady state, which is considered to be a more representative state of the machine's operation, avoiding transient phases such as start-up or shutdown. The tests will be carried out at three different rotation speeds, representative of the nominal operation of the machine, which are: 20 Hz (1,200 rpm), 40 Hz (2,400 rpm) and 60 Hz (3,600 rpm).

The data obtained at each rotational speed is processed independently in order to ultimately analyze the influence of speed on the diagnostic results. The test speeds are well below the first critical speed of the shaft (ωc = 321.7Hz), which was obtained from Eq. 1.

Two shafts with different crack sizes and locations were tested to generate the dataset12: axle6 (crack in the middle of the shaft) and axle7 (crack in a lateral position), as shown in Fig. 4.

Scheme to illustrate the spatial positioning of cracks induced in the shaft (a) middle section crack (MSC) (b) side section crack (SSC).

The depth of the crack was the parameter chosen to define it for the purpose of generating them and is presented in Table 3. However, the most appropriate parameter for defining the dimensions of a defect is the percentage of the shaft section affected, as this makes the response independent of the geometry of the defect. The progressive faults were precisely manufactured as transverse cracks with a straight crack front using a mechanical cutting tool equipped with a 2 mm thick blade. To completely eliminate potential misalignment errors or mounting discrepancies across the 9 progressive levels, the entire cutting sequence was executed directly on the test rig sequentially without ever removing, dismantling, or reinstalling the shaft. The target depth for each progressive level was verified through direct mechanical validation using a high-precision digital depth gauge and calipers, ensuring strict dimensional control and a negligible tolerance margin. As the shaft has a cross-sectional area of 338.81mm2, the crack size data converted to percentage of the affected section is also presented in the Table 3.

It has been considered acceptable to obtain a minimum of 1,000 different measurements (Nm) for each axis and each study condition (levels), which would imply a random error of Er = 44% in each specimen, based on Eq. 319. For the particular case of the healthy shaft condition, it is decided to take at least 2,000 measurements (a higher number because it is the baseline for comparison). In this case the random error would result of 3.16%.

Vibration measurements for each condition are taken consecutively in groups of 100. These groups of 100 consecutive measurements offer the possibility to be averaged since, being taken consecutively, they are under the same environmental conditions. This averaging would serve to eliminate random or specific factors that may affect the measurements obtained, since the experimental system is not isolated and may be affected by both noise and external vibrations unrelated to the machine. Also, since the measurement trigger was not synchronized with the angular position of the shaft, it is necessary to take into account that it is not possible to perform a direct averaging of the commonly used time signals, known as time synchronous averaging (TSA)21. For this reason, averaging (non-synchronous) must be performed once the temporal information has been removed, i.e., after pattern extraction.

To ensure maximum dataset flexibility and prevent any potential bias in downstream machine learning tasks, it is important to emphasize that the shared repository contains completely raw vibration time-series data. Apart from the essential analog hardware-level filtering and amplification performed by the signal conditioner to guarantee signal quality, no digital preprocessing, software-based filtering, or signal segmentation has been applied to the archived CSV files. This allows future users full autonomy to implement and critically analyze their own custom windowing, segmentation, and preprocessing strategies.

The files have been named with a code that includes the date the measurement was taken, the condition of the measured shaft, and the rotational speed of the test.

The encoding of data files follows a format of the type YYYY.MM.DD_SS–n.csv, where YYYY.MM.DD corresponds with the date in year (YYYY) month (MM) and day (DD), SS is the rotational speed of the test in Hz (20, 40 or 60), CC is the condition of the tested shaft (from HS which means Healthy condition, L1 first level of crack to L9) and the last number n is the number of file measured during the test. Technical Validation

The technical reliability and high fidelity of this dataset are ensured through rigorous sensor selection, hardware-based signal conditioning, and a robust statistical averaging methodology designed to mitigate environmental noise. All vibration measurements were exclusively acquired during the steady-state operation of the motor, avoiding transient artifacts from start-up or shutdown phases to capture the representative nominal behavior of the machine. Furthermore, tests were constrained to rotational speeds (20, 40, and 60 Hz) safely below the experimentally determined first critical speed of the shaft (ωc = 321.7 Hz). Since the experimental test bench is not strictly isolated and can be affected by external vibrations and random environmental noise, an extensive data collection protocol was implemented.

Statistical reliability and repeatability

To comprehensively characterize the dataset and ensure its representativeness, a statistical analysis of the time-series data was performed. For each of the two shafts and the three rotational speeds, the amount of data points recorded is: a minimum of 2,000 time-domain signals corresponding to 2.73 seconds of acquisition for the healthy shaft and 1,000 signals for each of the nine faulty conditions. The high amount of data can be useful for future machine learning applications.

Furthermore, to assess the distribution and variability of the data, key statistical time-domain feature such as Root Mean Square (RMS) were computed across the different conditions. To demonstrate the physical validity and usefulness of this dataset, it is important to note that the experimental measurements have been previously validated against established analytical models. Specifically, in a prior study by the authors13, the dynamic behavior of this centrally cracked shaft dataset was compared with a classical Jeffcott rotor model incorporating a breathing crack mechanism. The experimental data successfully corroborated the analytical predictions, confirming the expected theoretical relationships between crack depth and vibration harmonics. This alignment with a standard physical model serves as a strong baseline validation, ensuring the reliability of the dataset and its readiness for the research community to test new diagnostic algorithms or dynamic models.

Dataset discussion

In this study, a comprehensive experimental vibration time-series dataset was presented and documented utilizing a SpectraQuest MFS-Lite test rig to support advanced rotor crack detection, severity estimation, and condition monitoring benchmarking. The work introduced a novel, highly scarce benchmark dedicated strictly to progressive transverse shaft cracks. The core novelty of the dataset relied on its exceptional granularity of fault severity, capturing a healthy baseline alongside nine progressive crack levels that ranged from an incipient 1.36% cross-sectional area reduction to a severe 50.00%. Furthermore, the study incorporated vital spatial diversity by evaluating both Middle Section Crack (MSC) and Side Section Crack (SSC) configurations across three discrete operational speeds. To ensure the technical quality of the dataset, the statistical reliability and repeatability of the measurements were evaluated. Under identical operating conditions (same rotational speed and crack depth), the time-series signals demonstrated high consistency across different acquisition windows, showing that the experimental setup provides stable and reproducible dynamic responses.

When using this dataset for subsequent tasks, such as training machine learning models for structural condition monitoring, it is essential to carefully consider the simplified and controlled nature of the laboratory test rig relative to the harsh complexities of real-world industrial environments. First, the structural and vibration dynamics recorded are strictly tied to the specific geometry, shaft diameter, and boundary conditions of the utilized bench setup; consequently, models trained directly on this data may suffer from degraded diagnostic accuracy when applied to different machinery profiles without incorporating domain adaptation or transfer learning techniques. Second, the dataset represents an idealized scenario by isolating the progressive shaft crack under steady-state conditions, omitting severe industrial background noise, variable operational speeds, transient load fluctuations, and the presence of concurrent faults (such as misalignment or bearing wear) typical of a factory floor. Finally, specific experimental constraints bound the dataset, as the cracks were precisely manufactured using straight mechanical cutting rather than natural fatigue propagation—which alters the true breathing mechanics of the crack—the trials were conducted under strict zero-external-load conditions (preventing torque-induced modulation), and data monitoring relied on a single uniaxial accelerometer, which restricts the spatial richness of the data compared to multi-axial configurations or orthogonal proximity probes.

This dataset isolates the shaft crack under controlled conditions, which is ideal for benchmarking and baseline model validation, but represents a simplified scenario compared to actual factory-floor deployments. Nevertheless, while variable loads or multi-fault interactions are not present in this specific repository, the dataset does incorporate multiple operating conditions via three different rotational speeds. To overcome the limitation of isolated faults, researchers can combine these records with our previously published open-access datasets for mechanical looseness, as well as upcoming repositories currently being prepared by the authors that will cover misalignment, unbalance, and multi-fault scenarios.
