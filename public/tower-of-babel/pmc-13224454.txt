Development and validation of a combined model based on MRI radiomics for predicting placenta accreta spectrum and adverse pregnancy outcomes: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Zhongjie Zhou, Huaizhi Ge, Pinfa Zou, Tianyi Ye, Ting Zou, Qin Fang, Yan Zeng, Yu Wang, Xiaofen Zhang, Zhangye Xu, Xianping Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate a radiomics-clinical model of MRI fusion model for the prenatal prediction of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) and pregnancy outcomes.MethodsThis multicentre retrospective study enrolled 610 singleton pregnancies (292 with PAS vs.318 controls). After stratified randomization, the participants were allocated to training (n = 408), validation (n = 102), or external testing (n = 100) cohorts. Radiomics features were extracted from T2-weighted sagittal images, and MRI radiomics models, prenatal clinical models, and combined prediction models were developed.ResultsPrenatal clinical characteristics were significantly correlated with the presence of PAS in the training cohort (p < 0.05, top = 0.001). The newly constructed integrated prediction model incorporating radiomics features and the three prenatal clinical features had a greater area under the curve (AUC) for predicting PAS than the MRI radiomics and prenatal clinical models in the training cohort (0.767, 0.764, and 0.644; p < 0.05), validation cohort (0.795, 0.759, and 0.708; p < 0.05), and external testing cohort (0.792, 0.777, and 0.758; p < 0.05). Furthermore, in terms of pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with PAS, the combined prediction model had a greater AUC than the MRI radiomics and prenatal clinical models in the training cohort (0.852, 0.834, and 0.673; p < 0.05), the validation cohort (0.879, 0.804, and 0.747; p < 0.05), and the external testing cohort (0.907, 0.839, and 0.700; p < 0.05).ConclusionThe MRI-based multimodal integrated framework significantly outperforms conventional models, achieving dual prediction of PAS diagnosis and pregnancy outcomes with clinical-grade accuracy.Critical relevance statementOur study introduces the models provide objective and efficient decision support for the management of pregnant women with high-risk placenta previa and are expected to become a core tool for the multidisciplinary management of PAS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02348-6.Keywords: Placenta accreta spectrum, MRI, Radiomics, Pregnancy risk prediction

Introduction

PAS refers to the abnormal invasion of trophoblastic cells into the myometrium, resulting in the failure of placental separation during delivery. Clinically, PAS is classified into three subtypes based on the depth of placental villi invasion into the uterine wall: placenta accreta, placenta increta, and placenta percreta [1]. The frequency of PAS is about 1 in every 300 to 500 pregnancies, and it is significantly connected to the increase in cesarean section rates [2]. It is one of the most severe obstetric complications, leading to intractable bleeding before and after delivery, shock, hysterectomy, extensive blood transfusions, bladder incisions, ureteral damage, intensive care unit stays, extended hospitalization, and potentially maternal mortality [3–5]. Similar to most obstetric complications, newborns can also be affected. Neonatal morbidity and mortality mainly result from hypoxia, primarily caused by preterm delivery and maternal hemorrhage [6]. The occurrence of severe postpartum hemorrhage (SPPH), a type of uncontrollable postpartum bleeding that fails to respond to conservative treatments such as uterotonics, continuous uterine massage, or compression, and necessitates surgical intervention or even hysterectomy, has risen significantly [7, 8].

PAS is a significant independent risk factor for severe postpartum hemorrhage. Therefore, early detection and appropriate management of PAS are crucial for improving pregnancy outcomes [9]. Hence, precisely forecasting the risk of adverse outcomes in patients with PAS prior to delivery is crucial for formulating standardized and individualized clinical management approaches. The key prenatal tools for screening PAS are ultrasound and MRI. Studies demonstrate that ultrasound achieves a sensitivity and specificity of over 80% in diagnosing PAS during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy [10, 11].An MRI serves as a valuable complement to ultrasound, offering multi-angle imaging that clearly depicts the placenta’s relationship with surrounding tissues and its depth of implantation. It is safe, well-tolerated, and offers sensitivity and specificity comparable to ultrasound in diagnosing PAS [12, 13].

The precision of PAS evaluation using ultrasound or MRI is contingent upon the subjective interpretation and expertise of physicians; senior radiologists exhibit greater sensitivity (90.9%) and specificity (75%) than junior radiologists (81.8% and 61.8%) [14]. With AI advancements, radiomics and deep learning offer new approaches for predicting maternal outcomes in PAS using MRI [15]. Radiomics and machine learning, in conjunction with a PAS prediction model, use textural features from placental imaging to detect the presence and types of PAS by identifying differences in intraplacental heterogeneity between normal and abnormal placentas [16]. Peng et al. constructed a prediction model using a radiomics approach to determine the presence of PAS via prenatal MRI with high accuracy, achieving an AUC of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve greater than 0.87 [17]. A previous study has analysed the clinical characteristics and risk factors associated with severe intraoperative postpartum haemorrhage and created a flow chart for the management of PAS based on the scores of the “Ultrasound Scoring System for Placental Implantation Disease” [18]. Zong et al. compared different deep learning models and determined that they have good application for assessing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [19].

The aim of the present study was to determine whether multiomics-based prenatal MRI examinations for assessing PAS and predictive models of pregnancy outcomes can provide information support for precise clinical diagnosis and treatment, as well as important reference indicators for preoperative preparation and the selection of surgical protocols, in order to better protect the lives of pregnant women and fetuses.

Materials and methods

Study patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Centre 1, approval number: 2023-K-155-01) and the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Centre 2, approval number: 2024LYYJ166), and written informed consent was waived. Pregnant women suspected of PAS based on clinical and ultrasound exams were selected from two centers: Centre 1 (544 cases, 2016–2024) and Centre 2 (150 cases, 2017–2025). The flow chart of the present study is shown in Fig. 1. All underwent cesarean section and were confirmed by post-surgery pathology. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (A) pregnant women with singleton pregnancies; (B) pregnant women who underwent MRI examination before caesarean section surgery; and (C) pregnant women with complete pathological and clinical information. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (A) pregnancies or vaginal deliveries delivered outside of these two centres (8 cases in Centre 1 and 18 cases in Centre 2); (B) patients with severe MRI artefacts affecting assessment (6 cases in Centre 1 and 14 cases in Centre 2); and (C) pregnant women with incomplete clinical data information (20 cases in Centre 1 and 18 cases in Centre 2). Finally, 510 pregnant women in Centre 1 (235 in the PAS group and 275 in the non - PAS group) and 100 pregnant women in Centre 2 (57 in the PAS group and 43 in the non - PAS group) were enrolled in the present study, as shown in Table 1. Pregnant women from Centre 1 were randomly divided into a training set (n = 408 cases) and a validation set (n = 102 cases), with a ratio of 8:2. Pregnant women in Centre 2 were assigned to an external test set queue (n = 100 cases). Among the 292 cases confirmed by surgery and pathology in the PAS group, 108 cases experienced adverse pregnancy outcomes during surgery (77 cases in Centre 1 and 31 cases in Centre 2).

Risk of adverse pregnancy during surgery

The intraoperative adverse pregnancy outcomes in this study referred to SPPH and haemostatic surgical treatment, including intraoperative bleeding ≥ 1500 ml or uterine ascending branch artery ligation, uterine cavity filling (including balloon or gauze filling), uterine B-Lynch binding, uterine repair, bladder repair, partial hysterectomy or total hysterectomy. Blood loss was estimated quantitatively by summing the volume in the suction canister and weighing surgical sponges (subtracting the dry weight, assuming 1 g ≈ 1 mL). Anesthesiologists and surgeons collaborated to record the estimated blood loss (EBL) immediately after surgery [7]. The pregnant women with PAS included in the study were confirmed by pathological examination during or after caesarean section.

The scanning sequences included T2WI transverse, T2WI sagittal, T2WI coronal, T1WI transverse, and diffusion-weighted imaging, and the specific sequence parameters are shown in Table 2.

Patient characteristics

We retrospectively gathered data from women with singleton placenta previa who received prenatal care and underwent cesarean delivery at either of the two participating hospitals (Center 1 and Center 2) between 2016 and 2025. The detail shown in Table 1 of patients’ characteristics include age, gestational week, blood loss, hysterectomy, blood loss ≥ 1500 mL, and adverse outcomes.

Placental annotation and image segmentation

The flow chart of this study is shown in Fig. 2. A three-dimensional evaluation of the MR placental images was conducted, and two radiologists with years of work experience (one attending physician and one associate chief physician) performed three-dimensional ROI delineation on the preoperative T2WI sagittal images using ITK-SNAP software (version 4.1.0, http://www.itksnap.org). The radiologists had no knowledge of clinical or pathological information. Owing to the independent segmentation of the ROI by two radiologists, a senior chief physician reviewed both results before they were used as the gold standard for segmentation. The high-performance nnUNet framework was adopted to establish a deep learning segmentation model that automatically segments the placental region. The original in-plane resolution ranged from [0.684 × 0.684 mm] to [1 × 1 mm], and slice thickness ranged from [3 mm] to [7.2 mm]. As nnU-Net requires a fixed tensor size, we implemented a preprocessing pipeline where all images were resampled to a uniform spacing (e.g., 1.0 × 1.0 × 3.0 mm³) using nearest neighbour interpolation before being fed into the network. Furthermore, for each image, the intensity values were normalized using Z-score normalization. This standardization ensures zero mean and unit variance, mitigating intensity variability and potential model bias prior to feature extraction. The segmentation model was trained and validated using data from Center 1 (our primary institution). Before applying the model to an external test set (Center 2), we randomly split the data in the Center 1 dataset according to an 8:2 strategy. During training, we adopted the 3D_fullres mode, with a batch_size set to 2, and a cropped patch_size of [256, 320, 20], to ensure the robustness of the model. Feature extraction

To reduce heterogeneity between MR images, bilinear interpolation was used to resample all images to isotropic voxel sizes (1 × 1 × 3 mm3), and z scores were used to normalize the images, reducing the dimensionality impact of different features to obtain the standard normal distribution of image features. The uAI research platform including PyRadiomics (https://pyradiomics.readtHedocs.io/en/v3.0), was utilized for extraction of radiomics features. A total of 2264 radiological features were extracted from the sequence, including first-order image intensity statistical features, shape features, texture features, grey level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), grey level dependency matrix (GLDM), grey level size region matrix (GLSZM), grey level run length (GLRLM), and other features.

Feature selection

In this study, the data from Center 1 were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set in a ratio of 8:2, while all data from external hospitals (Center 2) served as an independent test set. For clinical features, univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) analyses were conducted to examine the correlation (p < 0.05) of clinical radiological risk factors during preoperative pregnancy and PAS with intraoperative delivery risk and to screen the features with the strongest correlation as independent clinical risk factors. R software (version 4.1.1, https://www.r-project.org) was used to filter the radiomics features. First, we randomly selected a subset of images (50 patients), and then extracted radiological features from radiologists and AI segmentation files separately to calculate ICC. We use a bidirectional mixed effects model to obtain absolute consistency (ICC (3,1)). Features with ICC > 0.75 are considered to have good reproducibility and are retained for subsequent analysis, and the variance threshold method was used to remove features with variances below 0.8. Via the maximum relevance and minimum redundancy (mRMR) algorithm, the feature set (500) with the strongest correlation and minimum redundancy to the target was identified. Finally, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was used to determine the optimal λ. We used 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal lambda (λ), where the minimum mean cross-validated error was reached (In the two tasks, λ = 0.05175 and 0.03054, respectively). The importance ranks were determined based on the absolute value of the coefficients derived from the LASSO logistic regression model. Features with larger absolute coefficients were considered to have greater importance in the prediction model, which can help identify the risk of PAS and delivery outcomes.

Model construction

In the present study, the data from our hospital were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at an 8:2 ratio, and all the data from external hospitals were used as independent test sets. Three models were established and compared for the prediction of PAS and delivery risk, including clinical, radiomics, and clinical radiomics comprehensive models. The clinical model was developed by initially applying univariate logistic regression to clinical indicators to identify potential variables related to PAS (P ≤ 0.05). A logistic regression classifier was constructed based on the best omics features selected from the training set to develop the radiomics model (We have described the specific features and model coefficients in Supplementary Tables 1–2.). On the basis of the best radiomics model, the radiomics score was combined with independent clinical predictive factors to establish a final joint predictive model, which improved the predictive ability of the clinical radiomics model (Fig. 3, Nomogram = 0.19601843 × Rad_Score + 0.023032404 × Catastrophe placenta previa + 0.016249482 × Caesarean section + 0.012144637×Parity + 0.32765958).

Model evaluation

The AUC analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the constructed model (Fig. 4). Several experiments were conducted to compare the diagnostic efficiency of the established models (Fig. 5). Other model performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1 score, were calculated to quantify the predictive performance of each model in both the training and validation sets. In the training set, a combination model based on LR was used to construct a column chart. A calibration curve was drawn to evaluate the degree of deviation between the predicted results obtained via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and the actual results. To quantify the net benefits under different threshold probabilities, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical applicability of the models (Fig. 3). Finally, an independent test set was used to evaluate the performance of the optimal model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0, USA) to compare all the clinical data, and a bilateral P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Quantitative variables with clinical significance were compared via the t test, and qualitative variables were compared via the chi square test or Fisher’s exact test. The normally distributed data are represented as the means ± standard deviations (x ± s), whereas the nonnormally distributed data are represented as the medians and quartile ranges. The classification data are presented in the form of counts or percentages. We utilized Python software (version 3.10) and the “sklearn” package for the analysis of ROC, calibration curves, and DCA. All the statistical tests were two-sided tests, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed separately for the training and validation/test cohorts to ensure no data leakage.

Placenta accreta spectrum diagnostic performance

As shown in Table 5, the prediction task of PAS had relatively low performance in the internal training and validation sets. The AUC values of the training and validation sets were 0.644 (0.590–0.697) and 0.708 (0.605–0.811), respectively, and the sensitivity and specificity of the validation set were 0.723 and 0.527, respectively. The AUC of the radiomics model was greater than that of the clinical model, with AUC values of 0.764 (0.718–0.810) and 0.759 (0.667–0.851), respectively. Moreover, in an independent external test set, the AUC of the radiomics model reached 0.777 (0.687–0.866), with a sensitivity of 0.719. The AUC of the combined model reached 0.792 (0.702–0.881). The results showed that in the external test set, the AUC of the combined model was significantly higher than that of the clinical model and radiomics model statistically (p < 0.05, p = 0.034). with a sensitivity and specificity of 0.737 and 0.721, respectively, indicating relatively stable performance.

Clinical applications

A nomogram was developed on the basis of the clinical radiomics model (Fig. 3). The DCA results of the clinical, radiomics, and clinical radiomics models are shown in Fig. 3a-b. The results of Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) indicate that within the clinically relevant threshold probability range, using a combined model to guide clinical decision-making provides higher net benefit than both clinical model methods and pure radiomics models, especially in task 2. In Task 1, when the probability threshold is less than 0.4, the clinical model misjudges some patients who are actually negative as positive, resulting in unnecessary treatment and related harm, and the cost of these harms exceeds the benefits of correctly identifying true positive patients. The combination model has a huge improvement compared to the clinical model. When the threshold probability was 0.5–1.0, the net benefit of the clinical radiology models in distinguishing PAS and predicting pregnancy outcome risk was similar to or slightly greater than that of radiology models, both of which were much greater than that of clinical models.

Discussion

The exact pathophysiologic mechanism of PAS is unknown, but defects at the endometrium‒myometrium interface are thought to be the main aetiologic factor, with endometrial defects leading to placental and trophoblastic infiltration of the myometrial wall attachments [3, 20]. PAS occurs mainly in patients with placenta previa combined with a scarred uterus. The increasing incidence of PAS is directly related to the increase in the caesarean section rate [21–23]. In this study, univariate logistic regression analysis showed that the number of pregnancies, history of cesarean sections, and the presence of pernicious placenta previa were significantly associated with both placenta accreta and the risk of intrapartum complications (P < 0.001). These important factors were then chosen as key risk factors to build further clinical models. The main clinical challenges are high rates of postpartum haemorrhage and hysterectomy, and in severe cases, PAS may even lead to maternal mortality [24]. Early diagnosis and management of PAS is therefore critical for improving pregnancy outcomes. Although ultrasound and MRI are the main tools used to diagnose PAS [25], they are very dependent on the doctor’s experience and can be subjective [26, 27]. A deep learning evaluation model has been proposed by some scholars to solve this problem with good results [28, 29].

Yoshiko U retrospectively evaluated prenatal MR images of 70 women to assess the interobserver variability of the developed MRI-based PAS radiological scoring system and reported that the system has good diagnostic performance; however, the sample size of this previous study was small, suggesting that the findings may be biased [14]. Some studies have also reported good agreement between MRI in assessing the presence of PAS and the depth of implantation but lower inter-examiner agreement in assessing the area of PAS [30]. Guo C et al. noted that the fusion model presents superior diagnostic efficacy compared with radiology diagnosis, MR results, and joint radiomics–clinical models. In addition, when the joint radiomics–clinical model is used, the diagnostic accuracy of PAS is significantly greater than that when only radiology diagnoses or MR findings are used (AUC values of 0.867, 0.623, and 0.648 for the joint radiomics–clinical model, radiology diagnoses, and MR findings, respectively) [31]. Rongrong X also proposed a prenatal prediction and staging method for PAS using MRI deep learning and radiomics features; the proposed method has an average accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of 0.877, 0.857 and 0.954, respectively, and an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.904, which is superior to those of traditional radiomics-assisted diagnosis methods [32]. In this study, the clinical model had low accuracy in predicting PAS, with AUC of 0.644 (training) and 0.708 (validation). The radiomics model performed better, with AUC of 0.764 and 0.759. The best results came from the integrated model, with AUC of 0.767 and 0.795, showing a 12% improvement over the clinical model. In an external test, the radiomics model had an AUC of 0.777, while the integrated model had an AUC of 0.758, with balanced sensitivity and specificity, showing stable performance.which is a relatively stable performance and is in line with the abovementioned studies.

Controlling hemorrhage and improving patient outcomes remain significant challenges for clinicians, especially in the management of refractory postpartum hemorrhage [33, 34]. In PAS, there is evidence that multidisciplinary team (MDT) management significantly reduces adverse outcomes. MDT management depends on the preoperative prediction and assessment of PAS [35]. Routine diagnostic modalities of prenatal MRI are used mainly to determine the presence and approximate extent of PAS but do not predict the probability of various adverse events during labour or intraoperative adverse events. Charis B et al. reported that the identification of specific features by MRI may help to further predict pregnancy outcomes and neonatal prognoses in pregnant women at high risk of PAS. Radiomics machine learning is data driven and based on a comprehensive model for the diagnosis of PAS [36]. This study developed models to predict PAS and pregnancy outcomes in affected women, using data from 292 patients. The clinical model had moderate performance with AUC of 0.673 (training) and 0.747 (validation). The radiomics model performed better, with AUC of 0.834 and 0.804. The best results were from the combined model, achieving AUC of 0.852 and 0.907. In an external test, the clinical model had an AUC of 0.700, the radiomics model reached 0.839, and the integrated model achieved an AUC of 0.907 with high sensitivity and specificity (0.839 and 0.769), showing strong generalization and potential for clinical use. Zong M et al. developed a deep learning MRI model that showed good predictive value for major bleeding or hysterectomy in PAS patients, based on data from 215 PAS cases and 108 non-PAS cases. This aligns with the current study’s findings. Methodologically, we chose to construct the diagnostic model using nnU-Net automated segmentations rather than manual ground-truth masks. This decision was guided by the aim of clinical translation; a fully automated pipeline removes the bottleneck of manual annotation, ensuring the tool is practical for routine workflow. While manual segmentations represent the anatomical ideal, our results demonstrate that the robust segmentation accuracy of nnU-Net (Dice > 0.91) allowed the radiomics features extracted from automated masks to retain high diagnostic performance. This validates that the ‘segmentation-to-prediction’ workflow is resilient to minor segmentation variations encountered in automation. Clinicians should prepare blood products and discuss surgical plans with patients, and consider referrals if resources are limited. Future work will involve a prospective, multi-center study to evaluate the model’s value in managing PAS.

Typical case analysis and comparison

The first pregnant woman was 37 years old, G4P2, with a history of two previous caesarean deliveries. An ultrasound examination at 22 weeks of pregnancy suggested that the placenta completely covered the intrauterine orifice, raising a clinical suspicion of possible placenta accreta. Antenatal MRI revealed that the placenta was located on the left and posterior walls of the uterus, with the lower edge completely covering the cervical canal. The anterior lower wall was deviated to the left side of the myometrium, showing an inhomogeneous signal, and the posterior lower portion of the placenta was poorly demarcated from the myometrium. During the caesarean section, clinicians observed that the placenta was attached to the lower uterine segment, completely covering the cervical canal, and implanted into the anterior and posterior lateral walls of the lower uterine segment and approximately two-thirds of the cervix. The placenta was also attached to the anterior and lateral walls of the cervix, with partial penetration. Additionally, intraoperative hemorrhage amounting to 4,000 ml was recorded, and the patient was admitted to the ICU postoperatively for comprehensive evaluation and continuous monitoring. The diagnosis of PAS was confirmed by intraoperative pathology. The PAS model predicted a probability of 0.89 for positive discrimination, and the pregnancy risk outcome model identified a high-risk group (P = 0.95 (Fig. 7, column a). In this study, a comprehensive model was utilized, demonstrating effectiveness in managing adverse clinical outcomes in pregnant women diagnosed with PAS. Notably, for patients showing signs of placental attachment to the cervix and the posterior wall of the uterus, the model showed excellent predictive capabilities. It demonstrated superior generalization performance and higher sensitivity compared to ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging, suggesting its potential for clinical application.

This study had some limitations. First, the deep learning model requires more data to better capture different PAS imaging features and improve its generalization. Second, as a retrospective study with data from different times and hospitals, there may be biases in imaging quality and clinical practices. Future work should include prospective studies and multi-center collaborations to validate the model’s clinical use and improve its ability to handle complex anatomical variations. Additionally, lightweight models could be developed for real-time use during surgery, and the model could be expanded to predict postoperative complications like retained placenta and infection risk.

Conclusion

In summary, the increasing incidence of PAS poses a significant threat to maternal and child safety, necessitating more accurate prenatal assessment systems for better clinical guidance. AI models, which integrate multimodal images like MRI with clinical data, have notably improved the prenatal diagnosis, intraoperative risk prediction, and prognosis of PAS by using radiomics and multisource data fusion. These models offer objective, efficient decision support for managing high-risk pregnancies and are poised to become essential tools in multidisciplinary PAS management. These AI models still need to be combined with doctors’ experience, and improvements in data quality and real-time performance are needed to make AI more precise and standardized for PAS diagnosis and treatment.
