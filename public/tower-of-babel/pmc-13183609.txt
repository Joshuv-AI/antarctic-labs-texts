Zwitterionic Penicillin‐Derived Sulfone Inhibitor for Combating β‐Lactamase‐Mediated Antibiotic Resistance
Authors: Diana Rodríguez, Emilio Lence, Juan C. Vázquez‐Ucha, Alejandro Beceiro, Concepción González‐Bello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
β‐Lactam antibiotics, which account for nearly 70% of all prescriptions for bacterial infections, remain the cornerstone of antimicrobial chemotherapy. However, their clinical effectiveness is increasingly compromised by the widespread emergence of β‐lactamase‐mediated resistance. These enzymes hydrolyze the β‐lactam ring through an addition–elimination mechanism, abolishing antibacterial activity. Preserving the efficacy of β‐lactam antibiotics, particularly carbapenems, regarded as the last line of defense against multidrug‐resistant (MDR) pathogens, is therefore critical. Here, we report the design and synthesis of compound 3, a zwitterionic penicillin‐derived sulfone that integrates two key principles: enhanced permeability of zwitterionic molecules and improved inhibitory potency through (2‐pyridyl)methylene introduction within the sulbactam scaffold to achieve an adduct stable against hydrolysis. In vitro assays demonstrated that compound 3 significantly restored β‐lactam activity against MDR pathogens producing extended‐spectrum β‐lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases. It notably improved imipenem and ceftazidime efficacy against strains expressing OXA‐48 and PDC‐1, whose inhibitory capacity was determined by kinetic analysis with the isolated enzymes. Mechanistic studies using mass spectrometry and molecular dynamics simulations revealed ligand‐induced pocket formation, π‐stacking modulation, and hydrophobic engagement, supporting a covalent, water‐shielded inactivation pathway via indolizine adduct formation. The nonsterically hindered amine in 3 favored PDC‐1 binding and overall permeability while exerting a neutral effect on OXA‐48 binding. These results underscore the potential of compound 3 as a next‐generation β‐lactamase inhibitor for combating infections caused by ESBL‐ and carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Introduction

Despite more than seven decades of clinical use, β‐lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems) remain the cornerstone of antimicrobial chemotherapy, representing approximately 70% of all antibiotics prescribed worldwide for bacterial infections [1, 2]. Among these, carbapenems are often regarded as antibiotics of last resort and represent the only remaining treatment option for many multidrug‐resistant (MDR) infections [3]. Unfortunately, bacteria have evolved sophisticated strategies to evade these life‐saving drugs, with the production of drug‐inactivating enzymes—particularly β‐lactamases—being the most prevalent and clinically significant resistance mechanism [4]. These enzymes efficiently hydrolyze the β‐lactam ring through an addition–elimination mechanism, thereby inactivating the compound and abolishing its ability to target essential bacterial processes. β‐Lactamase‐mediated hydrolysis constitutes the primary cause of antibiotic resistance among Gram‐negative pathogens. Among the most clinically relevant MDR isolates associated with high morbidity and mortality are: (i) extended‐spectrum β‐lactamase (ESBL)‐producing Enterobacterales, which are resistant to most broad‐spectrum β‐lactams, including penicillins and cephalosporins [5]; and (ii) carbapenem‐resistant Acinetobacter baumannii and Enterobacterales, in which carbapenem resistance is primarily driven by the horizontal acquisition of genes encoding carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases, such as OXA‐23, OXA‐24/40, and OXA‐48.

To address this antibiotic‐resistant challenge, recent efforts have focused on strategies that minimize the emergence and impact of β‐lactamase enzymes using β‐lactamase inhibitors in combination with β‐lactam antibiotics, thereby restoring the efficacy of these drugs against resistant pathogens [6]. However, designing small‐molecule inhibitors to block β‐lactamase activity remains particularly challenging due to the intrinsic characteristics of these enzymes. Thus, β‐lactamases exhibit remarkable diversity in both amino acid sequence and active‐site architecture, with more than 12,700 variants reported to date [7]. They also display distinct hydrolytic profiles against different substrate classes and employ fundamentally different catalytic strategies: (i) covalent catalysis mediated by serine hydrolases (classes A, C, and D; serine‐β‐lactamases) or (ii) noncovalent catalysis driven by zinc‐dependent hydrolases (class B; metallo‐β‐lactamases). Furthermore, the increasing emergence and dissemination of bacterial pathogens capable of simultaneously producing multiple β‐lactamases further complicates treatment options and accelerates the spread of resistance. As a result, the development of broad‐spectrum inhibitors capable of effectively targeting this diverse array of β‐lactamases remains a huge challenge in antimicrobial drug design.

Notable examples of β‐lactamase inhibitors include (Figure 1) [6]: (i) 1,6‐diazabicyclo[3.2.1]octanes (DBOs), with avibactam being the first clinically FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approved DBO in 2014 for use in combination with ceftazidime, and later with aztreonam in 2024. Other approved DBOs include relebactam, combined with imipenem/cilastatin (2019), and durlobactam, combined with sulbactam (2023); (ii) cyclic boronate derivatives such as varborbactam, which is the first boron‐based β‐lactamase inhibitor approved by the FDA in 2017 for use with meropenem, and taniborbactam, currently in phase III clinical trials in combination with cefepime; and (iii) penicillin‐derived sulfones, such as sulbactam (used with ampicillin, cefoperazone or ceftriaxone) and tazobactam (used in combination with ceftolozane or piperacillin), which were the first β‐lactamase inhibitors developed. The sulfone moiety in these compounds, absent from β‐lactam antibiotics, is critical for inhibitory activity as it enables the formation of a sulfinate group that establishes strong interactions with the target.

Chemical structures of relevant FDA‐approved and under development β‐lactamase inhibitors.

Studies by Richter et al. [8, 9] on the structure–activity relationships of thousands of reported antibiotics demonstrated that drugs capable of entering Gram‐negative bacteria through porins typically possess a nonsterically hindered amine and some nonpolar functionality, while also being relatively rigid and exhibiting low globularity. Charge distribution on the antibacterial agent is also critical for permeability, as better charge complementarity with the narrowest region of bacterial porins enhances uptake [10]. This feature accounts for the generally higher permeability rates of zwitterionic compounds [11]. Enmetazobactam, a β‐lactamase inhibitor derived from methylation of the terminal nitrogen atom in tazobactam, resulting in an overall neutral compound, is a representative example, along with relebactam and taniborbactam (Figure 1) [12]. In 2024, enmetazobactam combined with cefepime, which is also zwitterionic, was approved by FDA for the treatment of complicated urinary tract infections [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. On the other hand, researchers at Pfizer reported that incorporating a (2‐pyridyl)methylene group at the C6 position of sulbactam (compound 1) significantly enhances its efficacy against β‐lactamases from S. aureus and E. coli (micromolar range), due to the formation of an indolizine adduct that is resistant to hydrolysis (Figure 2) [18]. This initial finding was further optimized by Pattanaik et al. [19] through the introduction of a catechol moiety into the scaffold (compound LN‐1‐255), which facilitates internalization across the outer membrane via bacterial iron uptake pathways in a Trojan horse strategy. LN‐1‐255 showed to be a highly efficient inhibitor of the clinically relevant carbapenemase OXA‐48, commonly found in MDR K. pneumoniae strains, as well as all carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases of A. baumannii (including OXA‐23, OXA‐24/40, OXA‐58, OXA143, OXA‐235, and chromosomal OXA‐51) and exhibits excellent in vivo activity in preclinical murine pneumonia models [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Further studies by our group led to the identification of compound 2, demonstrating that linkage through an aromatic ester markedly improves bacterial internalization [25].

Chemical structures of 6‐(2‐pyridyl)methylene penicillin‐derived sulfones, the resulting indolizine adduct, and targeted compound 3.

Given the attractive characteristics of zwitterionic compounds, together with the promising results obtained with (2‐pyridyl)methylene derivatives, we aimed to combine both features within a single scaffold. To this end, we report herein the synthesis of compound 3—a zwitterionic penicillin‐derived sulfone having a (2‐pyridyl)methylene group at position 6 modified with a nonsterically hindered amine (Figure 2). We present in vitro studies evaluating the ability of compound 3 to restore β‐lactam antibiotic efficacy against difficult‐to‐treat pathogens harboring ESBLs of classes A and C, as well as carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases, along with kinetic studies with the most relevant β‐lactamases identified. Additionally, the inhibitor's mechanism of action and molecular basis of its inhibitory potency were investigated through a combination of mass spectrometry and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on the most relevant targets.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Compound 3

The synthesis of compound 3 was accomplished from commercially available (+)‐6‐aminopenicillanic acid (10) using a Wittig reaction as the key step to introduce the (pyridin‐2‐yl)methylene moiety (Scheme 1). The required phosphonium salt 9 was first prepared in five steps from commercially available methyl 4‐bromopicolinate (4). First, a Sonogashira carbon–carbon cross‐coupling between methyl 4‐bromopicolinate (4) and N‐Boc‐propargylamine, catalyzed by dichlorobis(triphenylphosphine)palladium(II) in the presence of CuI and triethylamine, afforded alkyne 5 in 98% isolated yield. Catalytic hydrogenation of alkyne 5 provided methyl ester 6 in 93% isolated yield, which was subsequently reduced with lithium aluminum hydride to give benzyl alcohol 7 in 42% isolated yield. Conversion of alcohol 7 into phosphonium salt 9 was achieved in two steps: (i) formation of bromide 8 via an Appel reaction (64% yield) and (ii) nucleophilic substitution by heating bromide 8 with triphenylphosphine (65% yield). Next, the carboxylic acid of commercially available (+)‐6‐aminopenicillanic acid (10) was protected as a benzhydryl ester by treatment with 1,1‐diphenyldiazomethane (16), freshly prepared via oxidation of benzophenone hydrazone (15) with MnO2, affording compound 11 in 50% isolated yield. The synthesis of the ketone intermediate 12 required for the Wittig reaction was initially explored by the generation of the diazonium salt intermediate of amine 11 by treatment with isopropyl nitrite and catalytic trifluoroacetic acid, followed by oxidation of the crude product with propylene oxide in the presence of catalytic rhodium octanoate. However, these conditions provided, in most cases, variable results and low yields. More reproducible results were obtained through oxidative deamination of the primary amine 11 using triflic anhydride and triethylamine, followed by hydrolysis with dilute HCl. Following the latter conditions and without further purification, the Wittig reaction between the resulting ketone intermediate 12 and the phosphonium salt 9 led to compound 13 in an overall isolated yield of 64%. Finally, oxidation of sulfide 13 with m‐chloroperbenzoic acid afforded sulfone 14 in 61% yield. It was observed that oxidation did not proceed completely in a single step, appearing to halt partially at the sulfoxide stage. Adding extra oxidizing agent during the reaction did not improve conversion; however, the isolated sulfoxide was successfully oxidized to the sulfone using the same procedure. Finally, simultaneous deprotection of both amino and carboxylic acid groups with trifluoroacetic acid, followed by treatment with sodium bicarbonate, provided the desired compound 9 in 44% isolated yield.

In Vitro Activity and Enzymatic Studies

The ability of compound 3 to restore antibiotic activity against diverse β‐lactamase‐mediated resistant pathogenic bacterial strains was evaluated by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of each antibiotic (ampicillin, ceftazidime, and imipenem) and their combinations with compound 3. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of antibiotic or antibiotic/compound combination that inhibited visible growth after 20 h of incubation at 37°C. MIC values were determined using the broth microdilution method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [26]. Assays were performed at a compound concentration of 16 μg mL–1. The β‐lactamase inhibitor avibactam was included as a comparator. The bacterial strains tested included Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterobacterales (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Klebsiella oxytoca). The latter strain corresponds to a clinical isolate. These strains produce challenging β‐lactamase enzymes, specifically: (a) ESBLs of classes A (CTX‐M‐14, CTX‐M‐2, and GES‐6) and class C (CMY‐2, DHA‐1, FOX‐4, and PDC‐1), and (b) carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases (OXA‐23, OXA‐24/40, and OXA‐48) (Table 1). Full details of the bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are provided in Table S1.

In vitro studies revealed that compound 3 increased susceptibility to imipenem for carbapenemase‐producing strains as follows: (i) OXA‐48‐producing K. pneumoniae by 32‐fold compared with no inhibitor (2 µg mL–1 vs. 64 µg mL–1, respectively); (ii) OXA‐23‐producing A. baumannii by fourfold, which is twice the improvement observed with avibactam (2 µg mL–1 vs. 4 µg mL–1, respectively); and (iii) OXA‐24/40‐producing A. baumannii by fourfold, similar to avibactam (16 µg mL–1). For EBSL‐producing strains, ceftazidime activity in combination with compound 3 was: (i) fully restored in E. coli strains producing DHA‐1 and in P. aeruginosa strains harboring PDC‐1; (ii) almost restored in E. coli strains producing CMY‐2 and FOX‐4; and (iii) improved by 32‐fold in E. coli strains producing CTX‐M‐14. For K. oxytoca clinical isolate producing GES‐6, the activity of ceftazidime was enhanced 16‐fold, with the MIC decreasing from 128 µg mL–1 to 8 µg mL–1 upon combination with 3.

To confirm that β‐lactamase enzymes are the primary targets of compound 3, its inhibitory activity against carbapenemases OXA‐23 and OXA‐48 and the ESBL PDC‐1 was then evaluated by determining the kinetic parameters: IC50, inhibition constant (K I), apparent inhibition constant (K i app), inactivation rate (k inact), and inhibition efficiency (k inact/K I), using nitrocefin as a reported substrate and following previously described protocols (Table 2) [21, 22, 23]. The results showed that the parameters for compound 3 against PDC‐1 are remarkable, showing high affinity for this enzyme (K I = 73 nM, IC50 = 21 nM), a good inactivation rate of 25 ms–1, and an inhibition efficiency of 3.7 × 105 M−1 s−1. Moreover, its activity against OXA‐48 was superior to avibactam, with an eightfold higher inhibition efficacy (19 × 103 vs. 2.4 × 103 M−1 s−1, respectively), driven by both higher binding affinity and improved inactivation rate. For OXA‐23, the inhibition efficacy of compound 3 was comparable to that of avibactam (2.9 × 103 M−1 s−1). Although compound 3 exhibited greater affinity for OXA‐23 than avibactam, its inhibition rate was lower.

Taken together, the kinetic and in vitro results underscore the remarkable activity of compound 3 against two highly challenging β‐lactamase enzymes: PDC‐1 and OXA‐48. PDC‐1 (P seudomonas‐derived cephalosporinase) is particularly problematic in infections caused by P. aeruginosa because it is a chromosomally encoded class C β‐lactamase (ampC gene) that can be induced by exposure to certain β‐lactam antibiotics. This inducible mechanism renders P. aeruginosa intrinsically resistant to multiple β‐lactams, including aminopenicillins and cephalosporins, and represents a major contributor to resistance against many antipseudomonal β‐lactam therapies [27, 28]. On the other hand, Enterobacterales (primarily K. pneumoniae and E. coli) harboring carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamase OXA‐48 are endemic in several regions (Middle East, North Africa, Spain, France, and Belgium) and are severely compromising the global effectiveness of carbapenem therapy [29, 30].

Mechanism of Action of Compound 3

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the inhibitory potency of compound 3 against PDC‐1 and OXA‐48 enzymes, its mechanism of action and binding mode were studied. The formation of an indolizine intermediate following nucleophilic β‐lactam ring opening was first investigated by methanolysis of compound 3 after treatment with 1 equivalent of NaOMe (1 M in MeOH) at 25°C. The reaction progress was monitored by UV–vis spectroscopy by recording spectra over the 200–500 nm range for 30 min (Figure 3). An initial band centered at 297 nm, corresponding to the formation of the imine intermediate I, was observed. This band rapidly decreased as a new band at 394 nm emerged, indicating the formation of the indolizine derivative II. The identity of this product was confirmed by HPLC–MS analysis (Figure S1).

UV–vis monitoring of indolizine derivative formation from compound 3 after treatment with 1 equiv of NaOMe (1 M in MeOH) at 25°C over 30 min. The apparent discontinuity at 350 nm is an instrumental artifact caused by the automated filter changeover (Visible → UV) in the spectrometer, which is preset at this wavelength according to the manufacturer's default configuration.

The binding mode of compound 3 against PDC‐1 and OXA‐48 enzymes was then studied in silico. To this end, both the formation of enzyme–ligand (Michaelis) complexes, which trigger covalent enzyme modification, and the corresponding indolizine adduct were analyzed. Docking studies were first performed using the GOLD program version 2025.3.0 [31], followed by MD simulations on the most plausible complexes and adducts to provide a realistic picture of target conformational changes upon inhibitor binding and inactivation. These in silico studies also enable prediction of the binding mode of transient species, such as the Michaelis complex, which initiates formation of the acyl–enzyme adduct. The enzyme coordinates from the crystal structures of the wild‐type PDC‐1 (PDB ID 4GZB [32], 1.79 Å) and OXA‐48 inactivated by avibactam (PDB ID 4S2N [33], 2.0 Å, chain A) were employed. The resulting Michaelis complexes and acyl–enzyme adducts obtained by docking were solvated in a truncated octahedron of TIP3P water molecules and neutralized with sodium ions. Each system was subjected to 100–200 ns of MD simulation using AMBER 20 ff14 SB [34] force field following our previously reported protocol [21].

The predicted binding mode of compound 3 with the PDC‐1 enzyme revealed that the β‐lactam carbonyl group would be anchored in the oxyanion hole through two hydrogen bonds with the backbone NH groups of residues S64 (essential) and S319 (Figure 4A). The (2‐pyridyl)methylene moiety of compound 3 would be buried within a tunnel‐like pocket adjacent to the catalytic serine, formed by folding of helix H10 (residues 289–292) upon ligand binding. This pocket, which houses the Lys–Tyr–Lys triad (K67–Y150–K315) and is isolated from the environment by helix H10, is not fully evident in available crystal structures of PDC‐1 or other AmpC enzymes, suggesting a ligand‐induced conformational rearrangement. It has been proposed that the phenolate form of Y150, which exhibits an unusually low pKa (<9) at neutral pH, activates the catalytic serine during acyl–enzyme adduct formation for catalysis with the assistance of residues K67 and K315 [35, 36]. The pyridine ring of compound 3 appears to restrict the flexibility of the essential residue Y150 by enforcing a parallel arrangement through a strong π–stacking interaction, which may contribute to its inhibitory potency. In addition, the ammonium group of compound 3 would establish hydrogen bonds with the side chains of T290 and N346 (via a water molecule), along with the backbone carbonyl of N287. Collectively, these favorable contacts stabilize helix H10 in a more open conformation, facilitating accommodation of the ligand and reinforcing complex stability. The low RMSD (root–mean‐square deviation) values obtained for both the enzyme backbone and ligand 3, together with the analysis of interatomic distances for the polar and π–stacking interactions in this arrangement, underscore its stability throughout the simulation (Figures S2 and S3).

Simulations performed with the acyl–enzyme adduct indicated that the modified catalytic serine would be shielded from water solvent by occupying the tunnel‐like pocket created by helix H10, which would be stabilized by a network of favorable nonpolar interactions on both sides of the aromatic ring (Figure 4B). In addition, strong hydrogen bonds would be formed between its ammonium group and the side chains of N287, Y274, and N314, along with a water–mediated interaction involving the backbone carbonyl of N314. These interactions remained highly stable, as confirmed by monitoring of key interatomic distances throughout the simulation (Figures S4). Globally, these simulation studies suggest that the flexible chain modified with a terminal amino group also contributes to binding by promoting polar interactions within the ligand‐induced tunnel‐like pocket adjacent to the catalytic site.

Our simulation studies with the OXA‐48/3 Michaelis complex indicated that compound 3 appears to be correctly positioned within the active site for nucleophilic attack by the catalytic serine residue S70, facilitated by a network of hydrogen bonds involving: (i) the β‐lactam carbonyl group and the backbone NH groups of residues S70 and Y211 (oxyanion hole); and (ii) the carboxylate group and the side chains of residues R250, T209 and S118 (Figure 5A). The latter residues are part of two of the three conserved motifs of the OXA‐48 enzyme, specifically SVV (motif II, residues S118, V119, and V120) and KTG (motif III, residues K208, T209, and G210) [37]. These interactions remained strong and stable throughout the simulation, as evidenced by minimal variation in interatomic distances and consistently low RMSD values for both the enzyme backbone and the ligand (Figures S5 and S6). Notably, the pyridyl moiety of compound 3 is positioned within an apolar region adjacent to the reactive center, engaging in favorable hydrophobic interactions with residues I102, W105, V120, W157, and L158. This extensive hydrophobic pocket is a distinctive feature of OXA‐48 and plays a critical role in stabilizing carbapenem conformation for hydrolysis. Combined with the enzyme's rapid hydrolysis of the carbapenem adducts, this structural characteristic likely explains why the carbapenemase efficiency of OXA‐48 is comparable to that of OXA‐23 and OXA‐24/40, which possess a unique tunnel‐like entrance that similarly locks the optimal carbapenem arrangement for catalysis [37, 38]. In addition, the positively charged ammonium group of compound 3 would be oriented toward the solvent, a position stabilized by an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the sulfonamide group mediated by a water molecule.

Conversely, the simulation studies performed on the OXA‐48 inactivated by 3 (denoted as 3*; OXA‐48/3* acyl–enzyme adduct) indicated that the resulting indolizine moiety is positioned within the apolar region adjacent to the active site, thereby enhancing its hydrophobic character and hindering the approach of the water molecule required for hydrolysis and catalytic turnover (Figure 5B). Once the indolizine adduct is formed, the presence of a positively charged chain does not appear to impede accommodation of this moiety within the enzyme's apolar pocket, as the chain is oriented toward the bulk solvent. The acyl–serine adduct remained stabilized through multiple hydrogen–bonding interactions, specifically between: (i) its carboxylate group and the guanidinium group of R250 and the hydroxyl group of T209; and (ii) its ester moiety and the ϵ‐amino group of K208. All these contacts were consistently maintained throughout the entire simulation (Figure S7). Therefore, for the OXA‐48 enzyme, the primary amino group does not appear to contribute to ligand binding, as it remains exposed to the solvent; however, it does not hinder efficient binding either.

A comparison of the Michaelis complexes PDC‐1/3 and OXA‐48/3, which represent the initial states leading to acyl–enzyme formation, with the plausible binding mode of sulbactam (a clinically available penicillin‐derived sulfone) highlights the favorable contribution of the (2‐pyridyl)methylene group and its substituent in 3. Docking studies revealed that: (i) in PDC‐1, sulbactam would adopt a conformation that is not suitable to nucleophilic attack by the catalytic serine; and (ii) in OXA‐48, the characteristic hydrophobic region of the active site remains unoccupied, in clear contrast to compound 3 (Figure S8). These observations are consistent with the lack of inhibitory activity of sulbactam against both enzymes.

Cytotoxicity Assays

The cytotoxicity of compound 3 was assessed in the HepG2 cell line [human hepatocellular carcinoma; purchased from ATCC (RRID:CVCL_0027, male)] using the MTT assay [3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)−2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide], following a previously reported protocol [39]. Assays were performed in triplicate at a compound concentration of 16 µg mL−1 (39.9 µM), with cisplatin included as a positive control. Compound 3 exhibited no relevant cytotoxicity under these conditions, showing a cell growth inhibition of 3 ± 3%. In contrast, cisplatin produced 60 ± 2% inhibition and an IC50 of 15.8 ± 1.4 µM.

Conclusions

In summary, the zwitterionic penicillin‐derived sulfone 3 was synthesized in ten steps from commercially available (+)‐6‐aminopenicillanic acid (10) and methyl 4‐bromopicolinate (4), using a Wittig reaction as the key step. The design of compound 3 was guided by two principles: (i) the favorable permeability of zwitterionic molecules and (ii) the enhanced inhibitory potency conferred by (2‐pyridyl)methylene substitution in the sulbactam scaffold. By integrating both features into a penicillin‐derived sulfone framework with a nonsterically hindered amine, we aimed to exploit both factors in β‐lactamase inhibitor development. In vitro studies demonstrated that compound 3 significantly restored the activity of β‐lactam antibiotics against MDR pathogens producing ESBLs and carbapenem‐hydrolyzing class D β‐lactamases. Notably, compound 3 improved the efficacy of imipenem and ceftazidime, particularly against strains expressing OXA‐48 and PDC‐1, two clinically challenging enzymes. Kinetic analyses confirmed high affinity and inhibition efficiency, outperforming avibactam against OXA‐48 and showing remarkable activity against PDC‐1. Mechanistic investigations using mass spectrometry and MD simulations provided insights into the molecular basis of its potency. Simulations revealed ligand‐induced pocket formation and π‐stacking control in PDC‐1, as well as hydrophobic pocket engagement with robust catalytic motif interactions in OXA‐48. The data support a covalent, water‐shielded inactivation pathway at both targets via formation of an indolizine adduct, while indicating that the nonsterically hindered amine primarily benefits PDC‐1 binding and overall permeability, but has a neutral effect on OXA‐48 binding. These insights provide structure–mechanism guidance for optimizing next‐generation β‐lactamase inhibitors that combine zwitterionic permeability with target‐tailored pocket engagement. Compound 3 exhibited negligible cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells, supporting its potential as a promising β‐lactamase inhibitor for combating infections caused by β‐lactamase‐mediated resistance, including EBSL‐harboring and carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa.

General Methods

All starting materials and reagents were commercially available and were used without further purification. 1H (250, 300, and 500 MHz), 13C (63, 75, and 125 MHz), and 31P NMR spectra (202 MHz) were recorded in deuterated solvents. Coupling constants (J) are reported in hertz. NMR assignments were established using a combination of 1D, COSY, and DEPT‐135 experiments. NMR data analysis and figures were performed with MNova software. FTIR spectra were obtained on a PerkinElmer Two FTIR spectrometer with an attenuated total reflectance accessory. [α]D20 values are given in deg mL g−1 dm−1. Milli‐Q deionized water was used in all buffers. Melting points were determined using a Büchi M‐560 apparatus. UV–vis spectra were recorded on a Varian Cary 100 spectrophotometer with a 1 cm path‐length cell fitted with a Peltier temperature controller. The purity of compound 3 was analyzed by HPLC and by NMR. HPLC analyses were performed on a Thermo Dionex UltiMate 3000 system equipped with a Bruker amazon SL mass spectrometry detector, using a Phenomenex Kinetex XB‐C18 column (particle size = 1.7 µm; dimensions: 50 mm × 2.1 mm, pore size = 100 Å), and eluting at 0.35 mL min− 1 with a gradient of 5−75% B over 10 min [A = Milli‐Q water + 0.1% TFA; B = acetonitrile + 0.1% TFA]. The purity of compound 3 was ≥95%.
