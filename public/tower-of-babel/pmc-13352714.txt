Neuropathic pain in superficial radial neuropathy: neurophysiological correlations in a retrospective series of 20 patients
Authors: Halil Can Alaydın, Halit Fidancı, İrem Yakıcı, Cemre Cöddü, Seda Mencekoğlu Baştin, Ahmet Yusuf Ertürk, Semir Mazman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuperficial radial neuropathy (SRN) is an uncommon sensory mononeuropathy, and data on neuropathic pain in SRN are limited. This study aimed to determine the frequency of neuropathic pain in patients with SRN and to evaluate its associations with neurophysiological severity.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed adult patients diagnosed with SRN. Neuropathic pain was evaluated using the Douleur Neuropathique 4 Questions (DN4) and the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS). Neurophysiological severity was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe according to the superficial radial sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude. Associations between neuropathic pain and SRN severity, etiology, and coexisting upper-limb neuropathies were examined.ResultsTwenty patients were included (mean age 36.2 ± 11.9 years; 75% male). SRN was trauma-related in 18 patients and entrapment-related in 2. Ten patients (50%) met screening criteria for neuropathic pain according to DN4 and 9 (45%) according to LANSS. Screening positivity tended to be more frequent in severe SRN than in mild–moderate SRN (DN4: 6/7 vs. 4/13, p = 0.057) and was significantly higher according to LANSS (6/7 vs. 3/13, p = 0.012). Screening positivity was also observed in 3/4 patients with coexisting median or ulnar neuropathies.ConclusionApproximately half of patients with SRN met screening criteria for neuropathic pain. Higher screening positivity was observed in electrophysiologically severe SRN and in patients with coexisting median or ulnar neuropathies; these findings warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.Keywords: Neuropathic pain, Nerve conduction study, Peripheral nerve injury, Superficial radial nerve, Trauma

Introduction

The superficial radial nerve is a purely sensory nerve supplying the dorsolateral aspect of the hand, including the dorsal thumb and the proximal dorsal surfaces of the index and middle fingers [1, 2]. Superficial radial neuropathy (SRN) is an uncommon sensory mononeuropathy that may occur after forearm or wrist fractures and lacerations, or due to entrapment and external compression (e.g., tight wristbands or handcuffs) [3–7].

Clinical examination and nerve conduction studies are central to diagnosis [1–4]. SRN typically presents with sensory symptoms confined to the superficial radial nerve territory without motor weakness [3–8]. The lack of motor deficits may contribute to underrecognition or misdiagnosis, particularly in early or mild cases. Neuropathic pain can be a prominent feature, with substantial inter-individual variability in intensity and impact [3, 7, 8]. Although some patients improve with conservative management, others develop persistent or disabling pain requiring surgical decompression [3, 7, 8].

Given the limited data on neuropathic pain in SRN, this study aimed to determine its frequency and to examine clinical and neurophysiological correlates in patients with SRN.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective study included patients aged ≥ 18 years who were evaluated in the Clinical Neurophysiology Laboratory of Adana City Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences, between February 2020 and February 2025. The study was approved by the hospital’s local ethics committee (approval no. 12/438; 2025). Given the retrospective design and use of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the ethics committee. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were identified from our laboratory’s electromyography (EMG) database using diagnostic labels and keyword searches related to superficial radial neuropathy. Because referral diagnoses were not coded uniformly as “superficial radial neuropathy” in administrative records throughout the study period, case identification relied on the EMG database. Patients were included if they met both of the following criteria: (1) paresthesia in the sensory territory of the superficial radial nerve with corresponding hypoesthesia on neurological examination; and (2) a superficial radial nerve sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude below the lower reference limit (10 µV) or a > 50% reduction compared with the contralateral side [3, 4, 8]. Exclusion criteria were: (1) radial neuropathy proximal to the superficial radial segment, (2) neurodegenerative disease, (3) polyneuropathy or chronic conditions associated with polyneuropathy, and (4) current use of antidepressants or medications for neuropathic pain (to avoid potential effects on symptom reporting and questionnaire scores).

Neuropathic pain was evaluated using two previously published screening instruments: the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) questionnaire and the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) [9, 10]. In our cohort, the validated Turkish versions of DN4 and LANSS were administered [11, 12]. Consistent with established thresholds, neuropathic pain was defined as a DN4 score ≥ 4 and/or a LANSS score > 12 [13]. In our laboratory, DN4 and LANSS are routinely completed as part of clinical practice, typically after the EMG examination during the same visit, and documented in the medical record. The questionnaires were administered by members of the clinical neurophysiology laboratory team, including study authors who work regularly in the EMG unit. Accordingly, assessors were not blinded to the NCS findings.

Neurophysiological assessment

Neurophysiological evaluations were conducted using a Cadwell Sierra EMG device (Cadwell Laboratories, Kennewick, WA, USA). Nerve conduction studies (NCSs) were performed when the skin temperature of the extremity exceeded 32 °C; otherwise, the limb was warmed prior to testing. Standard techniques were used to evaluate bilateral median, ulnar, radial, and superficial radial sensory nerves [14, 15]. For superficial radial nerve studies, the active electrode was placed over the anatomical snuffbox and the reference electrode 4 cm distal over the thumb. The lower reference limits for superficial radial SNAP amplitude and sensory conduction velocity were 10 µV and 46 m/s, respectively [15–17]. Non-recordable superficial radial nerve responses were rechecked during the same session after optimizing technique, including adjustment of stimulus intensity and electrode repositioning as needed.

Electrophysiological severity of SRN was classified as: mild (SNAP amplitude > 10 µV with a > 50% reduction compared with the contralateral side), moderate (recordable SNAP amplitude < 10 µV), and severe (non-recordable SNAP). The 10 µV cut-off was selected because it represents the published lower reference limit (lower limit of normal) for superficial radial sensory response amplitude in adult nerve conduction studies; therefore, values below this threshold were classified as moderate involvement, while non-recordable responses were classified as severe [15–17].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0. Categorical variables are reported as n (%) and continuous variables as mean ± SD (range). Given the small sample size, categorical comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test. For selected 2 × 2 comparisons, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Correlations between DN4/LANSS scores and electrophysiological measures (e.g., superficial radial SNAP amplitude) were assessed with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean DN4 and LANSS scores were 4.9 ± 2.3 (range 2–8) and 11.7 ± 5.9 (range 3–24), respectively. Based on these scores, 10 patients (50%) met the screening criteria for neuropathic pain according to DN4, and 9 patients (45%) according to LANSS.

Figure 1 shows the distribution of patients who met versus did not meet screening criteria for neuropathic pain across neurophysiological severity categories (mild, moderate, and severe). For DN4, the proportions did not differ significantly across severity groups (pairwise comparisons shown in the figure). For LANSS, patients with severe SRN more frequently met screening criteria than those with moderate SRN (p = 0.015), whereas the other pairwise comparisons were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Figure 2 summarizes the primary comparison between severe and non-severe (mild–moderate) SRN. According to LANSS, patients with severe SRN more frequently met screening criteria for neuropathic pain than those with mild–moderate SRN (6/7 vs. 3/13; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.012; OR 20.0, 95% CI 1.68–238.64). DN4 showed a similar pattern without reaching statistical significance (6/7 vs. 4/13; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.057; OR 13.5, 95% CI 1.20–152.22).

There was no significant correlation between DN4 score and superficial radial SNAP amplitude (Spearman’s ρ = −0.384, p = 0.095) or between LANSS score and SNAP amplitude (ρ = −0.341, p = 0.141). Similarly, DN4 and LANSS scores were not significantly correlated with neurophysiological severity category (DN4: ρ = 0.342, p = 0.140; LANSS: ρ = 0.283, p = 0.226).

In four patients, SRN was accompanied by median and/or ulnar neuropathies. Two of these patients had mild and two had severe SRN. Among the patients with SRN accompanied by median and ulnar neuropathies, all but one demonstrated neuropathic pain according to both DN4 and LANSS assessments. Both patients who developed SRN secondary to entrapment had DN4 and LANSS scores of 2 and 8, respectively, with no clinical evidence of allodynia. These two patients with SRN due to entrapment were classified as having moderate SRN.

Discussion

This study examined neuropathic pain screening positivity in patients with superficial radial neuropathy (SRN), an uncommon sensory mononeuropathy most often related to trauma but also encountered in entrapment. Approximately half of patients met screening criteria for neuropathic pain on DN4 and/or LANSS, and screening positivity was observed more often in electrophysiologically severe SRN than in mild–moderate cases.

In clinical practice, pain in SRN can be persistent and functionally limiting in a subset of patients despite conservative management, and surgical decompression has been reported to reduce pain intensity in selected cases [3, 4, 7, 8].

Neuropathic pain after peripheral nerve injury is thought to involve ectopic activity arising from injured axons and/or dorsal root ganglion neurons, together with ion-channel dysregulation (including upregulation of voltage-gated sodium channels) that increases fiber excitability [18, 19]. Inflammatory mediators may further facilitate central sensitization within spinal and brainstem circuits, lowering activation thresholds and contributing to exaggerated responses to normally non-painful stimuli such as allodynia [17, 18]. In line with these mechanisms, allodynia was observed in 9 (47%) patients in our cohort.

In our cohort, four patients had concomitant median and/or ulnar neuropathies in addition to SRN, and three of these cases met screening criteria for neuropathic pain on both DN4 and LANSS. Given this overlap, we cannot exclude that neuropathic pain symptoms were partly attributable to the coexisting neuropathies rather than SRN alone. Therefore, neuropathic pain screening results in SRN should be interpreted in the context of concurrent focal nerve involvement on clinical and electrodiagnostic evaluation. A plausible mechanism is cumulative nociceptive input from multiple injured nerves, which may increase peripheral ectopic activity and promote central sensitization, potentially lowering pain thresholds in adjacent or overlapping sensory territories [18, 19].

Isolated SRN is rare, and trauma is a common underlying cause, with prior reports frequently implicating wrist/forearm fractures and lacerations [3, 5–8]. Entrapment or external compression (e.g., tight bracelets or wristwatches) is also recognized [3, 4, 8]. In our cohort, entrapment-related SRN occurred in two patients, including one case attributed to a bracelet and one associated with a malignant mesenchymal tumor. These findings underscore that, when SRN is suspected, particularly in atypical presentations or when a space-occupying lesion is possible, adjunct imaging of the wrist and forearm (ultrasonography and/or MRI) should be considered. SRN can also occur after local procedures or surgery in the wrist/forearm region and has been reported even after minor interventions such as venipuncture, highlighting the nerve’s vulnerability to superficial injury [5].

Neurophysiological testing is essential for confirming SRN and excluding alternative or proximal lesions, and it may also provide prognostic information [1–4, 7]. Prior studies suggest that axonal degeneration is associated with poorer outcomes than predominantly demyelinating lesions [20, 21]. In our cohort, patients with non-recordable superficial radial SNAPs more often met screening criteria for neuropathic pain, which is compatible with more severe axonal involvement [20, 21]. By contrast, neither of the two entrapment-related cases (both classified as moderate SRN) met screening criteria for neuropathic pain or reported allodynia; given the very small number of entrapment cases, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, limiting statistical power; therefore, subgroup findings should be viewed as exploratory, especially for the entrapment-related cases (n = 2). Second, neuropathic pain was assessed using DN4 and LANSS, which are screening tools rather than definitive diagnostic instruments. Standardized documentation of symptom phenotype and focal examination findings (e.g., Tinel’s sign and detailed mapping of mechanical allodynia) was not consistently available in the records, and pain metrics (intensity ratings, symptom duration, and follow-up) were also captured inconsistently, precluding item-level analyses. Third, although current use of antidepressants or neuropathic pain medications was an exclusion criterion, prior or intermittent use and other pain-modifying treatments could not be standardized and may have influenced symptom reporting. Finally, concomitant median or ulnar neuropathies may have contributed to the pain presentation, and we did not perform multivariable adjustment.

Conclusion

This study found that neuropathic pain was present in approximately half of patients with superficial radial neuropathy. Neuropathic pain was observed more often in electrophysiologically severe SRN, particularly when the superficial radial SNAP was not recordable, and in patients with coexisting median or ulnar neuropathies. Given the retrospective design and small sample size these observations warrant confirmation in larger, prospectively designed cohorts.
