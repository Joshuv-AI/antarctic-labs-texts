Assessment of the canalis sinuosus and its branches in Taiwanese individuals using cone beam computed tomography
Authors: Ting-Hsuan Wu, Yen-Wen Shen, Lih-Jyh Fuh, Heng-Li Huang, Jui-Ting Hsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe popularity of dental implant surgeries, particularly in the maxillary anterior region, is growing, and understanding variations in relevant anatomical structures such as the canalis sinuosus (CS) and its accessory canals (ACs) is critical to avoid complications, including nerve injury and tissue necrosis. This study determined the prevalence, diameter, location, and anatomical variations of the CS and AC in a Taiwanese population by using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT).MethodsCBCT data were collected from 240 participants (aged 20–80 years, with an equal sex distribution) at China Medical University Hospital. Medical imaging software was used to analyze the number, diameter, and spatial relationships of the CS and ACs relative to adjacent teeth. Two diameter thresholds (≥ 0.5 and ≥ 1 mm) were applied. The participants were divided into three age groups for comparison, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS.ResultsThe results revealed that the prevalence of ACs was 80.4% and 51.7% when the threshold was ≥ 0.5 and ≥ 1 mm, respectively. At both thresholds, men a had significantly higher prevalence of ACs and larger average diameters than did women. Although younger adults had a greater number of ACs, the largest average diameters were observed in the older group, suggesting an age-related increase in canal size. The most common AC location was between the lateral incisor and canine. No significant differences were observed between the left and right sides.ConclusionsThis study revealed distinct age- and sex-related differences in the CS and ACs in the Taiwanese population. CBCT is a valuable tool for accurately identifying these anatomical structures that can improve implant planning and reduce the risk of surgical complications.Keywords: Cone beam computed tomography, Canalis sinuosus, Accessory canals of the canalis sinuosus

Introduction

The canalis sinuosus (CS) is a bony canal in the anterior maxilla that contains the anterior superior alveolar nerve and vascular branches [1, 2]. The CS is primarily responsible for sensory innervation and blood supply to the maxillary anterior region. The CS originates at the infraorbital groove, passes through the infraorbital canal, and continues approximately 25 mm below the infraorbital foramen, ultimately extending to the anterior margin of the nasal cavity at the anterior end of the inferior nasal concha. The CS was first described by Jones in 1939 [3], and its reported prevalence ranges from 88% to 100%. The CS often has several smaller branches, collectively referred to as accessory canals (ACs), that have diameters ranging from 0.5 to 2 mm. These branches are most commonly located in the area between the central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines, and this area is considered a clinically high-risk zone. Another anatomical structure, the incisive canal, is commonly observed in the region of the central incisors. The incisive canal is located between the bilateral central incisors and is a universally present anatomical feature. Its diameter is typically much larger than those of CS branches, and the canal should not be mistaken for an extension of the CS.

In the early 2000s, dental implant placement in the maxillary anterior region became increasingly common. This procedure not only restores basic chewing function but also substantially improves appearance and psychological well-being [4, 5]. However, because of the anatomical complexity of this region, inadequate preoperative assessment can lead to complications such as nerve injury, sensory abnormalities, or bleeding [6]. Although cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides high-resolution, three-dimensional imaging that can aid in preoperative planning [7, 8], insufficient anatomical knowledge of the CS and its ACs can result in overestimation of the safe distance, which can increase the risk of iatrogenic injury [9]. Several clinical case reports have reported that the presence of the CS or ACs during maxillary anterior implant placement or root canal treatment can result in severe postoperative complications, including persistent pain, sensory abnormalities, bleeding, tissue necrosis, and even implant failure [10–14]. In most such cases, symptoms appear shortly after the procedure and are resistant to conventional drug therapy. In addition, in such cases, CBCT scans frequently reveal the CS or ACs to be near the affected site, with canal diameters ranging from 0.7 to 2.0 mm. These findings indicate that even small-diameter ACs have clinical relevance, highlighting the importance of high-resolution CBCT evaluation before surgery to thoroughly understand the anatomical course of the CS [15] and thereby reduce the risk of iatrogenic complications associated with dental implants [16, 17].

Most imaging studies on the CS and its ACs have been conducted in Western and Latin American populations, and data regarding Asian populations, including the Taiwanese population, are limited. To provide more region-specific data for dental practitioners in Taiwan, this study conducted an imaging analysis of the CS and its ACs in the maxillary anterior region in a Taiwanese population. The objective of this study was to obtain data that can assist clinicians in performing more comprehensive risk assessments during anterior implant surgeries and reduce the incidence of iatrogenic injury. Specifically, this study examined the CS and ACs in the maxillary anterior region and examined their associations with sex and age. The findings of this study can serve as a reference for preoperative risk assessment and can thereby enhance surgical safety and overall treatment quality.

Materials and methods

Dental CBCT examination

This retrospective study was conducted from 2019 to 2024 in the Department of Dentistry, China Medical University Hospital, Taiwan. Imaging data were obtained using a Promax 3D Max CBCT scanner (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) with a voxel size of 200 μm, a voltage of 96 kV, and a current of 12 mA. The study included 240 individuals, and the sex distribution was balanced (120 men and 120 women). All individuals were divided into three groups, namely, groups aged 20–35, 36–50, and over 50 years; 80 individuals were included in each group, and the sex ratio was equal for all groups (40 men and 40 women per group). Individuals were included who had (a) CBCT imaging data fully covering the relevant anatomical regions of the maxilla, (b) age ≥ 20 years, and (c) images with a resolution of 200 μm or higher. Individuals were excluded who had (a) significant pathology (e.g., tumors or cysts) or a history of trauma in the maxillary anterior region, (b) abnormal dental conditions in the maxillary region (e.g., supernumerary teeth, retained teeth, or malocclusion), (c) previous surgeries or medical interventions in the maxillary anterior region, or (d) CBCT images with artifacts from metal objects or severe motion blur that prevented clear identification of structures. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of China Medical University Hospital (approval number CMUH 111-REC3-205). Because of the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All procedures adhered to applicable ethical guidelines and regulations.

Analysis of the diameter, distance, and location of CS branches

After screening was completed, collected CBCT DICOM images were imported into MIMICS image processing software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). The ACs of the CS were identified in the transverse and sagittal views of each image (Fig. 1A). Once identified, AC measurements were obtained in the sagittal view, and the distribution of the ACs was evaluated in the transverse view. AC locations were annotated on two-dimensional images by using a drawing tool, and corresponding three-dimensional models were generated (Fig. 1B).

(A) Observed in the transverse and sagittal views, with a red circle indicating an AC of the CS. (B) 3D model created using imaging software, with an orange line representing an AC of the CS

After confirmation of an AC’s location, the diameter of the canal opening was measured in the sagittal view relative to the adjacent teeth (Fig. 2A). The method described by Manhães et al. [18] was employed, and the following distances were measured with the distal end of the AC opening serving as a reference point (Fig. 2B): (1) the distance from the AC of the CS to the bottom of the nasal cavity (NC; marked in red), (2) the distance from the AC of the CS to the buccal cortical bone (BC; marked in green), and (3) the distance from the AC of the CS to the alveolar ridge crest (RC; marked in blue).

(A) In the sagittal view, the diameter of the AC of the CS opening was measured; the red arrow points to the opening of the accessory canal. The diameter of the AC of the CS in the image is 1.70 mm. (B) The red line represents the distance to the nasal cavity (NC), which is 8.51 mm; the green line represents the distance to the buccal cortical bone (BC), which is 10.69 mm; and the blue line represents the distance to the alveolar ridge crest (RC), which is 18.19 mm

The transverse view was then analyzed to determine the AC distribution. Because the ACs were predominantly located in the maxillary anterior region, a dental template of this region was created, with the Fédération Dentaire Internationale dental notation system used to label each tooth (Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, the red arrow indicates the AC cross-sectional image positioned on the mesial side of the left canine (tooth 23).

(A) Template illustrating the distribution of the canalis sinuosus. (B) The position of the accessory canal is observed in the transverse view. The red arrow points to the position of the accessory canal, and the yellow circle indicates the incisive canal

This study analyzed the following four parameters: (1) the prevalence of ACs of the CS, (2) the diameters of ACs of the CS, (3) the distances from ACs of the CS to adjacent teeth, and (4) the distribution location of ACs of the CS. This study classified AC diameters into two groups: ≥0.5 mm and ≥ 1 mm (Fig. 4A and B, respectively). Historically, ≥ 1 mm has been considered the threshold for clinical significance. However, a case report indicated that even when the diameter is smaller than 1 mm, implant contact may lead to sensory abnormalities or severe tissue necrosis [19]. Therefore, in the present study, 0.5 mm and 1 mm were both used as threshold values for grouping and statistical analysis, with this enabling more comprehensive examination of clinical risks.

Statistical analysis

Before parameter measurements were obtained, interexaminer and intraexaminer reliability analyses were conducted to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the results, with an intraclass correlation coefficient used as an evaluation metric. Before parameter measurements were obtained, interexaminer and intraexaminer reliability analyses were conducted to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the results, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) used as an evaluation metric. Ten CBCT images were randomly selected for evaluating intraexaminer and interexaminer errors. To calculate interexaminer errors, the four length parameters of a certain CBCT slice were measured once each by two examiners; the ICC values ranged from 0.91 to 0.93. To calculate intraexaminer errors, the four length parameters of a certain CBCT slice were measured five times by a single examiner; the ICC values ranged from 0.94 to 0.96. These results demonstrate that the measurement method used in this study has satisfactory reliability and reproducibility, with interexaminer and intraexaminer errors within acceptable ranges.

Data were subjected to a normality test conducted using SPSS Statistics version 19 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) to determine whether parametric or nonparametric methods were appropriate. Because the sample size was greater than 50, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used, and the results revealed p values for all data that were greater than 0.05, which indicates that the data did not follow a normal distribution. Therefore, nonparametric statistical methods were used for subsequent analysis.

Because the sample size was relatively large but the characteristics of the data creating the possibility of a skewed distribution, the AC diameters were categorized into two groups: ≥1 mm and ≥ 0.5 mm. The following five variables were analyzed: (1) the associations between AC presence and sex and age, determined using a chi-square test, and (2) AC diameter, NC, BC, and RC, determined using nonparametric methods. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for comparisons among three or more groups. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference, Dunn’s test was used for pairwise comparisons to examine differences in the frequencies and diameters of ACs among different age groups and sexes. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses. In addition, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the statistical power of the study for detecting significant differences in the prevalence and diameter of accessory canals (ACs) between sex and age groups. The analysis was based on the observed effect sizes from the chi-square test for prevalence and the Mann–Whitney U test for diameter comparisons. With a sample size of 240 participants (120 males and 120 females), an alpha level of 0.05, and the observed effect sizes (Cohen’s w = 0.18 for sex differences in AC prevalence at ≥ 1 mm threshold), the achieved power was 0.89, indicating sufficient power (> 0.80) to detect significant differences. Similarly, for age group comparisons (three groups, n = 80 each), the power exceeded 0.85 for diameter and distance measures, confirming the adequacy of the sample size for the analyses performed.

Association of AC diameter with sex and age

The results revealed that men had larger average AC diameters under both the ≥ 0.5-mm and the ≥ 1-mm condition, which indicates the presence of an association between sex and AC diameter (Table 2). Further analysis by age group revealed that although the young adult group had a higher AC prevalence, the largest average AC diameter was observed in the older group. The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated that regardless of the diameter condition, AC diameters significantly differed among the age groups (p < 0.001). To identify specific differences between the age groups, this study conducted pairwise comparisons by using Dunn’s test. The results revealed that under both the ≥ 0.5-mm and the ≥ 1-mm condition, significant differences were present between the young and older groups (p < 0.05). This finding may be related to the young and older groups representing the lowest and highest ends of the age range, which may have led them to exhibit the most significant differences in AC diameter.

Association of AC distance with sex and age

Analysis of the distance of ACs to adjacent teeth and of sex (Table 3) revealed that under the > 0.5-mm diameter condition, men had ACs located further from the NC and positioned more toward the back of the teeth compared with those in women. Under the > 1-mm diameter condition, the ACs in men were positioned closer to the RC compared with those in women, although the only significant difference between men and women was a smaller RC distance in men. All other distance measurements were greater in men than in women. Statistical analysis revealed a significant sex difference (p < 0.05) that was primarily attributable to the larger NC distance observed in men. Further analysis of the relationship between distances from ACs to adjacent teeth and age groups revealed that regardless of diameter condition, the ACs in the older group were located further from the NC but closer to the BC and adjacent teeth compared with those in the younger group. Kruskal–Wallis testing indicated that with the exception of the RC under the > 1-mm diameter condition, which did not significantly differ, all distances significantly differed between the age groups (p < 0.05). Pairwise comparisons conducted using Dunn’s test further demonstrated that all distance measures significantly differed between the young and older groups. This result may be related to the young and older groups representing the lowest and highest ends of the age range, respectively.

Association of AC location with sex and age

Under the different diameter conditions, the distributions of the ACs exhibited distinct trends and associations with sex and age. For ACs with a diameter of > 0.5 mm, the most common overall location was between the lateral incisor and canine (teeth #22 and #23; Fig. 5), with both sexes exhibiting the highest concentration in this area; both men and women had the highest distribution between teeth #22 and #23 (Fig. 6). When the diameter condition was set to > 1 mm, the overall distribution still occurred predominantly between the lateral incisor and canine (teeth #22 and #23) (Fig. 7), but slight sex differences were observed: men continued to exhibit the highest distribution between teeth #22 and #23, whereas women had the highest distribution at the buccal side of tooth #22 (Fig. 8). In the age group analysis, for ACs with a diameter of ≥ 0.5 mm, the young group exhibited the most frequent distribution between teeth #12 and #13 and between teeth #22 and #23; the young adult group exhibited the highest frequency between teeth #22 and #23; and the older group exhibited the highest frequency around tooth #12 (Fig. 9). For ACs with a diameter of ≥ 1 mm, the young group exhibited the highest frequency between teeth #22 and #23, the young adult group exhibited the highest frequency between teeth #12 and #13, and the older group exhibited no significant change, maintaining the highest frequency around tooth #12 (Fig. 10). These results indicate that AC distribution is affected by diameter and is correlated with both sex and age.

Bilateral comparison of the ACs of the CS in the lateral incisor and canine region

ACs were most commonly distributed in the area between the lateral incisors and canines in the maxillary region. In investigating correlations between the left and right sides, this study categorized ACs by diameter into two groups: ≥0.5 mm and ≥ 1 mm. It further considered sex, age group, AC diameter, and distances from ACs to adjacent anatomical structures (i.e., the NC, BC, and RC; Table 4). Fisher’s exact test was used to investigate age and sex differences, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the diameters and anatomical distances on the two sides. All p values exceeded 0.05, indicating a lack of significant differences between the left and right sides for the analyzed variables. These findings suggest that the ACs on both sides likely develop independently, without mutual effects from shared etiological factors or developmental pathways.

Discussion

The growing popularity of dental implant surgery has increased the importance of gaining a thorough understanding of the anatomical structures in the jawbone region. This is particularly critical in the anterior region, where implants can restore chewing function and affect aesthetics and speech. Careful assessment of the anatomical risks in this region is therefore essential when implant surgery is being considered. The CS and its ACs are vital anatomical structures containing nerves and blood vessels. Inadvertently damaging these structures during implant placement can cause neurovascular injuries, leading to numbness, bleeding, or other complications. This is particularly challenging when an AC is located in the planned implant site because it can directly affect surgical planning and execution. In consideration of this, dentists must gain a comprehensive understanding of the distribution of the CS and its ACs to ensure surgical safety and improve outcomes.

This study examined the distribution characteristics of the maxillary canalis sinuosus (CS) and its accessory canals (ACs) in a Taiwanese population under two diameter conditions (≥ 0.5 mm and ≥ 1 mm). The rationale for analyzing both thresholds deserves clarification: although some authors have proposed ≥ 1 mm as the cutoff for clinically significant canals, previous case reports have documented that even accessory canals smaller than 1 mm may be associated with postoperative complications, such as neurosensory disturbances or tissue necrosis, if injured. Therefore, adopting both ≥ 0.5 mm and ≥ 1 mm thresholds allowed us to provide a more comprehensive assessment of potential risks. At the same time, we acknowledge that extremely wide canals (> 2 mm), although less common, are of particular clinical concern because of their higher probability of causing significant bleeding or neurovascular injury during implant placement. In addition, this study investigated correlations between AC characteristics and sex and age, addressing a lack of information regarding such correlations in Taiwan. On the basis of its findings, this study recommends that clinicians perform thorough radiological evaluations before dental implant surgery regardless of AC diameter to reduce the risk of iatrogenic injury and enhance surgical safety and treatment outcomes.

The current study results reveal that when the diameter condition was ≥ 0.5 mm, the prevalence of ACs was 80.4%, whereas when the diameter condition was ≥ 1 mm, the prevalence was 51.7%. That is, the prevalence is approximately halved when larger diameter conditions are employed. Sex and age analysis revealed that men and the young adult group had a higher prevalence of ACs, although the differences between the age groups were not significant. In addition, diameter analysis indicated that men and the older group had larger maximum average AC diameters, and the difference was significant for the young and older groups. This finding suggests that AC diameter tends to increase with age. Regarding distribution, the ACs of the CS were mainly concentrated in the area between the lateral incisor and canine, which confirms that they are primarily located in the upper anterior region of the jaw. In summary, these findings provide crucial reference data for dentists conducting preimplantation evaluations, helping to minimize iatrogenic risks.

When the diameter condition was ≥ 0.5 mm (Table 7), the prevalence of AC was 80.4%, which is comparable to findings from South Africa (99.6%) [20], Iran (78.35%) [21], and Australia (98.5%) [22]. Notably, the Australian study reported a higher prevalence, which may be explained by its lower minimum diameter threshold of 0.42 mm. By contrast, studies from Brazil and Turkey reported substantially lower prevalence rates of 52.1%, 34.66%, and 35.5% [23, 24], with the difference in rates possibly related to population differences or variations in analytical methods. Regarding average diameter, the results of the present study (0.99 ± 0.019 mm) are similar to those of the aforementioned Australian study (1.08 ± 0.39 mm) [22] but slightly lower than those reported in the Turkish study (1.3 ± 0.44 mm) [24], suggesting that AC size distribution may vary among different populations.

In the current study, when the diameter condition was ≥ 1 mm (Table 5), the prevalence of AC was 51.7%, which is markedly higher than the rates reported in studies conducted in Brazil (36.2%) [18], Switzerland (27.8%) [25], and Iran (15.26%) [21]. This difference may be related to differences in imaging parameters. The average diameter in this study (1.31 ± 0.027 mm) is nearly identical to that reported in a Swiss study (1.31 ± 0.26 mm) [25]. In terms of AC distribution, ACs were most commonly located between the maxillary lateral incisors and canines in this study. This finding differs slightly from previous findings from South Africa (between the central incisors and canines) [20], Australia (central incisors) [22], and Turkey (lateral incisors) [24]. These variations may be attributable to racial differences, sample age composition, or anatomical variation.

Regarding adjacent tooth distances, the results of the present study (Table 7) indicate that with the exception of the distance from ACs to the NC, which was smaller (8.32 ± 0.21 mm for both ≥ 0.5 mm and ≥ 1 mm) than that reported in other studies [18, 23, 24, 27], the distance values in this study, including for the BC and RC, were larger than those reported in the literature. For example, when the diameter condition was ≥ 0.5 mm, the distance from ACs to the BC was 11.38 ± 0.17 mm and that to the RC was 13.7 ± 0.26 mm. When the diameter condition was ≥ 1 mm, the distance to the BC was 10.82 ± 0.29 mm, and that to the RC was 12.52 ± 0.41 mm. These differences may be related to the more posterior distribution of ACs observed in this study, with the distribution primarily being between the lateral incisors and canines, or even further back. A 2023 Turkish study performed sex-based subgroup analyses and reported significant differences in distances between ACs and the NC (p = 0.039) and the RC (p = 0.012). Although the present study observed a significant difference in distances between ACs and the NC (p < 0.001), no significant difference was observed for the distances to the RC (p = 0.938).

No consistent or clear standard has been established for age grouping in research on the ACs of the CS. Most studies on this topic, including those from South Africa [20], Brazil [26], Turkey [23, 24], and Switzerland [25], have classified individuals aged 20 years and younger into a group for independent analysis. However, dental development is incomplete in individuals in this age group. Consequently, the present study excluded participants younger than 20 years to avoid variability that could have affected the results. This difference in grouping criteria may partly explain several differences between the findings of the present study and those of others. Notably, in the current study, in a comprehensive comparison of variables such as AC presence and diameter, only AC diameter was significantly correlated with age (p = 0.004). This finding may be related to the three-age-group classification used in this study, which enabled this study to account for observed differences in outcomes more effectively that other studies that applied more age intervals have been able to (Table 8). Additionally, discrepancies between the findings of this study and those of previous studies may have occurred because of factors such as imaging slice thickness (voxel size), measurement threshold settings, and participant demographic characteristics. Variations in definitions and diagnostic criteria for ACs across studies may also have contributed to differences in results.

This study systematically analyzed the maxillary CS and its ACs in a Taiwanese adult population and identified significant associations between several factors and sex and age. However, it has several limitations should be noted. First, although this study included 240 individuals, which is a larger sample size compared to previous studies [22, 25, 28–30], its sample size can still be considered limited. Future studies should include larger cohorts to enhance the precision and generalizability of the results. Second, to avoid potential interference from incomplete dental development, participants younger than 20 years were excluded from the present analysis, which prevented this study from assessing AC developmental changes during adolescence. Including adolescent samples in future research could address this gap. Finally, consistent with prior studies, this work did not account for several potentially influential factors, such as genetic background, nutritional status, history of dental treatment, systemic conditions, oral habits (e.g., thumb sucking, bruxism), occlusal status, and oral hygiene levels, all of which may affect AC development. Future research incorporating these clinical and lifestyle variables could provide a more comprehensive understanding of AC morphology and variation. Future investigations could incorporate these and other clinical or lifestyle variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of ACs.

Conclusion

This study used CBCT to examine branches of the maxillary CS and its ACs and analyzed their prevalence, diameters, and distances to adjacent teeth in different sex and age groups. The findings of this study can help dentists understand patients’ anatomical structures before and after dental implant surgery, which can enable more accurate assessment of implant positioning, reducing the risk of neurovascular injury and improving surgical safety and outcomes.

The following conclusions can be drawn on the basis of the current findings:

Prevalence of the ACs of CS: With a diameter condition of ≥ 0.5 mm, the prevalence of the ACs of the CS was 80.4%. When this diameter condition was increased to ≥ 1 mm, the prevalence decreased to 51.7%, indicating that the prevalence declines as the diameter condition increases.

Significance Between Age Groups: The most pronounced differences were those between the younger and older groups. Distribution of the ACs of CS in the Taiwanese Population: In the Taiwanese population, the ACs of the CS were most frequently located between the lateral incisor and canine, regardless of diameter condition. This result corroborates the findings of previous studies indicating that the ACs of the CS are predominantly located in the anterior maxilla and provides a more precise understanding of the distribution of ACs.

These findings provide valuable reference data for clinicians conducting preoperative and postoperative CBCT evaluations for dental implant treatments. The information of this study can enable more accurate localization of anatomical structures during surgical planning, help prevent avoidable anatomical injury, and ultimately improve treatment safety and prognosis.
