Role of collagen VI and IX in mechanotransduction in the cartilage microenvironments: A multiscale in silico examination
Authors: Malek Adouni, Afif Gouissem, Yasin Y Dhaher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectivesChondrocytes are mechanosensitive cells involved in the biosynthesis and maintenance of articular cartilage. Histologically, these cells are surrounded by a pericellular matrix (PCM) characterized by a relatively high concentration of collagen VI and a low concentration of collagen IX in the junction area with the extracellular matrix (ECM). However, the impact of PCM composition and its interaction with the ECM, such as alteration of collagen content (VI, IX), on chondrocyte mechanics is largely unknown. This study aimed to develop a multiscale model to investigate the role of collagens VI and IX in regulating cellular and tissue-level cartilage mechanics.MethodsA poro-hyperelastic multi-scale finite element cartilage model was developed to connect aggregate tissue-level load to cellular mechanics. The cell-enriched cartilage model included anatomical cell distributions and collagen types II, VI, and IX, representing the building block network of the ECM and PCM, respectively. An unconfined compression test was simulated under normal and perturbed collagen VI and IX nonlinear properties. Cellular- and tissue-level mechanics were simulated simultaneously using an explicit coupling approach.ResultsAt the aggregate level, cartilage reaction force varied substantially with alteration of collagen VI, while it was largely unaffected by changes in collagen IX properties within the range of variations considered. Furthermore, alterations in collagen VI and IX stiffness were associated with changes in cellular volume, supported load, and the mechanical microenvironment of the cells.ConclusionThese findings indicate that minor collagen networks can modulate the cellular mechanical environment while exerting a limited effect on the macroscopic cartilage response. Delineating this association will likely improve understanding of cellular function under conditions in which collagens VI and IX are compromised, such as focal injuries or enzymatic degradation.Translational potential statementThis study demonstrates that alterations in minor collagen networks (types VI and IX) can modulate chondrocyte mechanotransduction, positioning them as potential mechanobiological markers in understanding the onset and progression of degenerative joint diseases such as osteoarthritis. The realistic multiscale cartilage model offers a physiologically relevant framework that, when integrated with joint-level simulations, could predict cell-level loading during daily physical activities—informing diagnostics, therapeutic strategies, and biomaterial design.Keywords: Articular cartilage, Cell mechanics, Chondrocytes, Multiscale, Pericellular matrix, Poroelasticity

Introduction

Cartilage is a connective tissue that provides structural support in articular joints. It consists of specialized cells called chondrocytes embedded in an extracellular matrix (ECM) primarily composed of collagen fibers of varied types with the most abundance is collagen type II, VI and IX.

Histologically, these cells are surrounded by a pericellular matrix (PCM) characterized by a relatively high concentration of collagen VI and a low concentration of collagen IX in the junction area, known as territorial matrix (TM), with the extracellular matrix [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Collagen II fibers provide tensile strength and contribute to the overall load-bearing capacity of the cartilage. When a load is applied to the cartilage, it results in mechanical stress on the collagen fibers within the ECM. These mechanical forces are subsequently transmitted to the chondrocytes within the tissue through the TM and PCM. As chondrocytes experience cell loading, mechanosensitive receptors on their surface detect these mechanical cues and initiate a signaling cascade [7]. These signaling pathways trigger intracellular events that activate various transcription factors, including those involved in gene expression and the synthesis of extracellular matrix components. Excessive mechanical stress can lead to the activation of catabolic pathways in chondrocytes. In these cases, chondrocytes may activate cell signaling pathways, which contribute to releasing enzymes that degrade ECM components, including collagen [8,9]. Hence, along with the biochemical environment, the micromechanical state plays a cruciate role in modulating chondrocyte catabolic and anabolic function and hence cartilage health and degenerated states.

In addition to the classical mechanical and morphological factors, pathological initiation and progression of cartilage degeneration have been linked to aberrate changes in the chondrocyte's metabolism and its microenvironment, two mutually inclusive drivers [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Alteration of collagen behavior and specifically the nearest networks surrounding the chondrocytes, such as collagen VI and IX, are key structural components affecting the mechanotransduction process [13,15,[18], [19], [20]]. Changes in the intrinsic properties of these collagens will affect the chondrocyte's mechanical stimuli, leading to changes in the cell's metabolic function. Identifying the relative contribution of collagen VI and IX in the mechanotransduction process will help our understanding of the role of chondrocytes in the synthesis and maintenance of the ECM.

Simultaneous in vitro examination of cartilage mechanics at the micro and macro scales is technically difficult [14,21,22]. Therefore, computational biomechanics represents an alternative avenue to obtain additional insights into the load transfer mechanism between different spatial scales. In this context, most of the proposed approaches treating the coupling between the chondrocyte and its micro or even macro environment can be classified under homogenization or postprocessing modeling framework [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Perturbation studies seeking to identify the relative role alterations of collagen II content and permeability of the ECM on the micro-mechanics using these approaches are technically challenging. These challenges are due to a strong link between convergence issues at micro and macro scales in the case of homogenization [31] or due to the number of assumptions that must be respected to satisfy the consistency between scales in the case of the postprocessing technique [32]. Thus, examination of the impact of TM and PCM collagen composition and abundance (e.g., Collagen VI, IX) on chondrocyte mechanics is largely unknown. In this study, we seek to develop an explicit multiscale cartilage model, incorporating the cell distributions with their microenvironment, to investigate the effect of collagens type VI and IX properties alterations on the micromechanics of the cells and the aggregate response of articular cartilage simultaneously. We hypothesized that the integrity and relative abundance of the collagen types VI and IX network play a significant and differential effect on cellular loading and deformation expression in response to an aggregate tissue level loading. Hence knowing the functional mechanisms associated with the relative contribution of one of the tissue's key constituents may considerably improve articular cartilage treatment strategies and, hence, the joint's overall healthcare.

Methods

Explicit finite element mesh

A 3D micromodel included a chondrocyte surrounded by the PCM, TM, and ECM (cartilage cellular unit CCU). The cell and its surrounded matrices (PCM and TM) were represented as an ellipsoid encapsulated with successive thin layers (average thickness PCM; ∼4 μm and TM; ∼2 μm) to represent the PCM and TM. The above dimensions and the depth-dependent cell orientations (hence the ellipsoid) were considered based on histological literature on the cartilage (Table 1) [1,12,33] (Fig. 1). The extracellular, territorial, and pericellular matrices were modeled as an isotropic porous solid matrix (filled with water) reinforced by specific fibrillar networks without offering any compressive resistance. The fibrillar networks were simulated by truss elements (T3D2) and the porous matrix by elements with nonlinear pressure and displacement fields (C3D10MP) (Fig. 1) [[34], [35], [36]]. Collagen type II of the ECM was distributed in the superficial and transitional layers with horizontal and random orientations, respectively. In the deep layers, fibrils are oriented in the vertical direction [37]. The collagen types VI and IX were oriented randomly and modeled similarly to the collagen type II of the ECM.

Using the instance regeneration technique of Abaqus, the above micro models were then employed to create a cell distribution that accurately represents the characteristics of the microstructure [38]. This cell distribution was identified based on different cell densities across layers that decrease gradually over the cartilage depth according to a 3:2:1 ratio (Fig. 2) [38]. Due to the limitation linked to the merging technique, the created cell distribution was merged first at the ninth level of the cylindrical sector. Then, using the same merging technique of the cylindrical sector, a 3D model was constructed with dimensions that resemble those of the articular cartilage samples (plug) used in the reported unconfined testing paradigm [39,40]. The indenter and the subchondral bone were assumed to be rigid and impermeable due to their much greater stiffness than the cartilage (Fig. 2). Due to the high level of the computational cost, two cartilage models were created, the first one [39] for the validation and cross-verification of the macro mechanics of the articular cartilage and the second one, with a much smaller dimension [40], to study the effect of the alteration of the minor collagen networks on both macro and micromechanics of the articular cartilage (Fig. 2). All structures were built using an in-house user graphic Python subroutine.

Parameter provenance and robustness assessment: The constitutive framework employed in this study follows the established class of fibril-reinforced poro-(hyper) elastic cartilage models that combine (i) a nonfibrillar porous matrix with finite-strain response and swelling stresses, (ii) tension-only collagen fibril networks with nonlinear stiffening, and (iii) strain-dependent permeability. This modelling class has been extensively used and validated in the cartilage biomechanics literature over the last two decades across common specimen and anatomical configurations and under multiple loading modes (e.g., confined/unconfined compression, indentation, and physiological joint loading) [41,45,[48], [49], [50], [51], [47], [52], [53]]. In the present work, this established framework was selected deliberately because the explicit micro–macro coupling of a heterogeneous, cell-resolved construct poses substantial nonlinear convergence challenges; therefore, numerical stability and a proven ability to reproduce physiologically plausible aggregate cartilage mechanics are essential prerequisites for interpretable cellular-scale predictions [54].

With this rationale, ECM parameters controlling collagen II stiffening behaviour, fibril volume fraction, matrix shear response, swelling stresses, and permeability were adopted from validated prior modelling studies and maintained within physiologically accepted ranges (Table 2, Table 3). For the pericellular and territorial regions, PCM/TM parameters were derived using a structured scaling strategy relative to the ECM baseline, consistent with the compositional differences reported for PCM/TM in histological and modelling literature [2,[4], [5], [6],24,26,41,45]. Collagen VI parameters were assigned based on prior PCM-focused modelling and histological evidence (Table 3) [55]. Due to the limited availability of direct experimental mechanical characterisation for collagen IX networks in situ, collagen IX fibril constitutive parameters were assumed to follow the same functional form as collagen VI as a pragmatic placeholder, while differing in localisation and volume fraction (Table 3). In this formulation, the chondrocyte, PCM, TM, and ECM are treated as poro-hyperelastic continua and continuity of pore pressure/flux is enforced at their interfaces; no additional semi-permeable membrane resistance, osmotic pressure jump, or membrane tension is introduced at the cell–PCM boundary. These assumptions enable a computationally feasible, explicitly coupled cell-resolved construct; their implications are discussed in the Discussion, and the results are interpreted primarily in terms of comparative trends (e.g., relative VI versus IX sensitivity under unconfined compression) rather than as definitive identification of collagen IX or cell-intrinsic material parameters.

Global sensitivity analysis: To assess robustness of macro- and micro-scale outputs to material parameter uncertainty, we performed a variance-based global sensitivity analysis during model development using a reduced-size 3D construct selected for computational feasibility while preserving the same constitutive structure. A total of 41 material parameters (12 ECM, 12 PCM, 12 TM, 5 cell) were treated as uncertain. The parameter space was sampled using Latin Hypercube Sampling, centred at the baseline values reported in Table 2, Table 3 with bounded ranges of ±40%. We generated 250 parameter sets and performed corresponding FE simulations to evaluate two peak-loading outputs: (i) peak aggregate reaction force and (ii) mean cell forces in the superficial and transitional layers. To enable global sensitivity without an excessive number of FE runs, an RBF surrogate model was trained on 196 samples and validated using withheld samples; prediction accuracy was assessed using PRESS and RMSE metrics [56,57]. Sensitivity indices were then computed using variance decomposition, allowing quantitative ranking of parameter contributions to output uncertainty (For further details, please refer to the supplementary materials section).

Loading and boundary conditions

The loading protocols used in this study mimicked experimental articular cartilage unconfined compression testing by DiSilvestro and Suh [39]. In this experimental study, 5% nominal strain was applied to each sample with a strain rate of 0.001/s. The cartilage upper and bottom (at the subchondral bone) surfaces were impermeable, and the latter was axially fixed. To examine the role of collagens type VI and IX properties on the micromechanics of cells and the aggregate response of articular cartilage, the TM and PCM materials parameters were altered separately or combined by changing collagen VI and IX linear and nonlinear properties by 30% (Table 4). This symmetric perturbation was selected as a moderate change that is (i) consistent with tens-of-percent alterations in PCM micromechanics reported in injury/degeneration models (e.g., early post-injury PCM modulus reductions on the order of ∼10–60% depending on stage) [58,59] and (ii) sufficiently large to reveal sensitivity at both tissue and cell scales while maintaining numerical stability in the transient poro-hyperelastic simulations. The interpretation of outcomes is therefore framed primarily in terms of relative trends and sensitivities, rather than as quantitative predictions for a specific pathological severity. All simulations were conducted using Abaqus 2019 commercial software. A full newton algorithm for solving nonlinear equations under soil transient consolidation analysis without automatic stabilization was employed. All the presented materials behaviors were implemented via UMAT, and VOIDRI subroutines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted on the cell micromechanical outputs, forces and volume changes of 1336 cells (630 at the superficial layer/437 at the transitional layer/269 at the deep layer), to test whether there is a significant difference between the reference (without materials alteration) and the collagen altered models (COLL(VI)±30% and COLL(IX)±30%). Paired-sample t-test (dependent) was employed with a level of significance α = 0.05. The statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB.

Results

Cross-examination of the model aggregate simulations were consistent with the experimentally reported explants cartilage reaction forces under a similar compression test (Fig. 3a). Alterations in collagen VI stiffness significantly affected the reaction forces, particularly at the peak loading point. The reaction force decreased from 1.282 N to 1.156 N when the stiffness was reduced by 30%, and increased to 1.384 N when the stiffness was increased by 30%. However, this variation was substantially reduced during the relaxation process, reaching ∼2% at the equilibrium loading state (Fig. 3b). Less than 3% reaction force variation was computed when the collagen IX properties were altered by 30% at the peak loading point. This alteration almost vanished at equilibrium. The combined alteration of these collagens (VI, IX) properties has a similar cartilage macro reaction as the alteration of collagen VI only.

(a) Axial reaction forces measured from unconfined compression test [39] along with FE model predictions. The highlighted area indicates the standard deviation. (b) Predicted axial reaction forces for normal and altered collagen VI and IX stiffness (±30%) (Table 4) [40].

The global sensitivity analysis performed during model development quantified the relative influence of ECM-, PCM-, TM-, and cell-level parameters on both peak aggregate reaction force and representative mean cell forces (superficial and transitional layers) at peak loading. For peak reaction force, the dominant contributors were ECM-level determinants: collagen II stiffness/volume fraction accounted for ∼31% of output variance (including ∼18% associated with the combined action of collagen II stiffness coefficients and ∼13% associated with superficial/transitional collagen volume fractions), followed by ECM permeability (∼21%) and the combined effect of matrix stiffness and swelling stresses (∼15%). Microenvironment-related families contributed in a structured manner: collagen VI accounted for ∼8% of reaction-force variance, whereas collagen IX-related parameters contributed substantially less to the macro reaction variance (Fig. 4). In contrast, mean cell forces were driven primarily by collagen VI and PCM/cell properties: collagen VI stiffness/volume fraction contributed ∼18% of the variance, followed by PCM permeability (∼12%), cell stiffness (∼12%), and cell permeability (∼8%); collagen IX stiffness/structure contributed ∼7% of the variance, while ECM collagen stiffness/structure (∼9%), ECM permeability (∼7%), and ECM matrix stiffness/swelling (∼6%) remained secondary contributors. Most remaining parameters and higher-order interactions contributed ≤2% individually (Fig. 4). These rankings support the robustness of the main trends reported in this study and explain why collagen VI perturbations produce substantially larger microenvironmental effects than collagen IX within the explored range.

Variance distributions (Sensitivity indices) based on the surrogate model prediction for the cartilage reaction forces and cellular forces, in response to the unconfined compression test at the Peak loading instance.

To further interpret the sensitivity results, we quantified the response of macro and micro outputs to incremental increases in collagen VI and collagen IX stiffness from 5% to 40% (Fig. 5). Increasing collagen VI stiffness over this range increased peak reaction force by ∼14% and decreased superficial-layer mean cell forces by ∼29%, consistent with enhanced PCM reinforcement and stress-shielding at the cell boundary. In contrast, increasing collagen IX stiffness over the same range altered peak reaction force by ∼3% while increasing superficial-layer mean cell forces by ∼12%, indicating modest cellular-scale modulation with minimal macroscopic signature. We also report a dimensionless plausibility check using the normalized peak reaction force (peak force normalized by the equilibrium mean force). Across the sampled parameter space, the FE model produced a normalized peak reaction force with a mean of 7.8325 (max 13.1984, min 3.8984), comparable to the experimental measurements of DiSilvestro and Suh [39] (mean 9.4144, max 13.7519, min 6.5061). This normalization enables comparison despite geometry differences and supports the physiological plausibility of the baseline transient force response.

Computed response of both cartilage reaction forces and cellular forces as collagen VI and IX stiffness increases from 5% to 40%, at the Peak loading instance.

Individual cells experienced different total circumferential forces under normal and altered collagen (VI, IX) properties (Fig. 6). These forces were maximum in the superficial layers with an average value of 118 ± 32 nN and decreased significantly over the depth of the articular cartilage to reach 9 ± 3 nN at the bone-cartilage junction layer. Increasing the collagen VI material properties by 30% decreased significantly (P < 0.001) the average cell-supported forces' by an average of 39% in the superficial layers (Fig. 6a). Lower changes in the cell-supported forces were observed when the collagen IX material properties changed with the same proportion (∼13% in the superficial layers, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6b). In addition, model predictions indicated a lower change in the computed cell-supported forces along the lateral direction (perpendicular to the cartilage depth). The average cell-supported forces slightly decreased as the distance from the axis of symmetry increased.

To place the predicted chondrocyte volumetric deformations in context, we compared our baseline outputs with reported in situ measurements and recent multiscale simulations available in the literature. At peak loading under 5% unconfined compression, the model predicted chondrocyte volume changes ranging from a maximum of 29.1% in the superficial zone to a minimum of 5.12% in the deep zone, indicating a progressive reduction in volumetric deformation with increasing cartilage depth (Fig. 7). Reported in situ measurements under superficial-zone compressive loading (using indentation/contact protocols) commonly show mean volume decreases on the order of ∼5–15%, with site- and injury-dependent deviations [60], whereas some recent multiscale fibril-reinforced poroelastic simulations driven by indentation protocols have reported larger mean volumetric changes while capturing depth- and site-dependent trends [26]. Together, these comparisons indicate that the predicted magnitudes in the present construct fall within the experimentally observed order of magnitude for moderate compressive loading and remain below the largest compaction magnitudes reported in some multiscale simulations.

Discussion

In this study, we sought to theoretically examine the role of what we proposed to be drivers in the cellular mechanotransduction process in cartilage, namely, the collagens type VI and IX fibril networks. While several multiscale chondrocyte–cartilage models have previously been reported, many rely on homogenization, sub-modelling, or post-processing strategies to link tissue loading to cellular mechanics [23,[25], [26], [27], [28],61]. In the present work, we implement an explicitly coupled, 3D, cell-resolved construct that embeds anatomical cell distributions within a specimen-scale unconfined-compression model, allowing tissue-scale reaction forces and cell/PCM-scale mechanics to be solved simultaneously within a single FE framework and enabling systematic perturbation of collagen VI and IX networks in the local cellular microenvironment. In this explicit multiscale cartilage model, we investigated the effects of variation in collagen VI and IX fibril network material properties on the predicted cellular loading and deformation. Our results showed a meaningful correlation between collagen VI and IX behaviour and cellular mechanics (loading/deformation) and the associated change in their micro- and macro-mechanical environment. This correlation is likely to play an important role in cellular biological function in cases where collagens VI and IX have been compromised, for example by focal injuries or enzymatic degradation.

Simulation results of the composite articular cartilage model were consistent with the unconfined-compression response reported by DiSilvestro and Suh [39] (Fig. 3a). Although the predicted aggregate reaction force trends toward the upper bound of the experimental variability and does not coincide with the experimental mean, this behaviour is consistent with uncertainty in the dominant macro-scale determinants of reaction force. Using a geometry-independent metric (peak force normalized by equilibrium mean force) to enable comparison between the reduced-size construct and the experimental plug, the model yields normalized peak reaction forces comparable to DiSilvestro and Suh [39], further supporting the plausibility of the predicted transient force response. We acknowledge that the use of a normalized peak-to-equilibrium force ratio supports comparison of the transient response despite geometry differences, but it does not remove the residual offset in the predicted absolute aggregate reaction force relative to the experimental mean. Because cell-scale forces in the present explicitly coupled construct are driven by the tissue-level mechanical response, this macroscale offset may introduce a corresponding bias in the absolute magnitude of predicted cell-supported forces and may also indirectly influence absolute cell deformation or volume-change estimates. Accordingly, the absolute cell-scale magnitudes reported here should be interpreted with caution, whereas the comparative trends across simulated conditions, particularly the relative effects of collagen VI versus collagen IX perturbations and the depth-dependent response, provide the primary basis for the study conclusions.

Changes in the collagen networks (VI, IX) properties altered the aggregate stiffness of the cartilage. Specifically, remarkable changes in the cartilage aggregate mechanics have been associated with collagen VI alterations, where a 30% increase in collagen stiffness led to a ∼8% increase in cartilage reaction force at the full loading point. Collagen IX alteration showed a lesser effect on changing the macro mechanical response of cartilage, a 30% collagen IX yielded almost a 2 % increase in the cartilage estimated reaction force at the full loading point. The difference in the effect of collagen change on the estimated reaction forces during the dynamic phase may be attributed in part to the relative abundance of collagen VI and IX in cartilage, higher abundance of collagen VI [[62], [63], [64]]. It has been postulated that the primary mechanical role of collagen IX is load transfer [3], while the role of collagen VI is primarily to mechanically reinforce the PCM and hence provide stress shielding to the chondrocytes [2,3]. These differential roles may also contribute to the observed relative effect of both collagen types' of variation on the tissue's aggregate mechanics. It is intriguing to note that our computed results agree with the reported measurement in the in-vivo mice model, where degradation of the articular cartilage mechanical properties has been observed over time [13,65]. Nevertheless, differences in boundary conditions and the non-localisation of collagen properties alterations between the current model and the in-vivo investigation may contribute to the discrepancy in the findings [18].

Chondrocytes’ total circumferential forces were higher in the superficial and middle zones and much lower in the deep zone. Radially, cell forces decreased along the lateral direction, with a maximum force located near the symmetrical axis. The cells' supported forces alerted substantially (more than 30%) with changes in the collagen VI stiffness. A 30% increase in collagen VI stiffness was associated with a deferential reduction of cell forces across cartilage depth, with a maximum of 39% and a minimum of 16% in the superficial and transitional layers, respectively (P < 0.001). A similar inverse proportional effect, with less intensity, was computed, only in the superficial layers, with the alteration of the stiffness of collagen IX. It is interesting to note that despite the observed disappearance of the effect of micro collagen alteration on macro cartilage reaction between the full and equilibrium loading instances, the changes in the cells' forces were slightly altered. This supports the earlier observation that cartilage steady state loading may not help detect macro reactions associated with the micro alteration, especially in cell environments [28]. It is worth noting here that the computed cell forces may provide data on the level of forces one can exert on cells to understand the internal cellular mechanics under physiological loading conditions, presumably using atomic force microscope (AFM) nanoindentation experiments [66].

In normal cartilage, the predicted chondrocyte volume changes at peak loading ranged from ∼8 to 26% on average, decreasing gradually across cartilage depth, with negligible additional change at steady state. These magnitudes are consistent with the order of magnitude of experimentally reported chondrocyte volume changes under compressive loading, which span approximately ∼1 to 29.3% across reported in situ studies (mean ∼15.3%) [[67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72]]. In addition, more recent in situ superficial-zone measurements under indentation/contact protocols typically report mean volume decreases on the order of ∼11.2 ± 5.8% (with site- and injury-dependent deviations) [60], whereas some multiscale fibril-reinforced poroelastic simulations driven by similar indentation protocols have reported substantially larger mean cell-volume decreases, reaching up to 52%, while still reproducing key depth- and location-dependent deformation trends [26]. Despite this encouraging agreement in order of magnitude, we emphasise that validation in the present study was performed primarily at the aggregate tissue level (reaction force under unconfined compression) [39], and the current construct is not calibrated to match single-cell deformation metrics under a fully matched imaging/testing protocol. Accordingly, cell-scale results should be interpreted quantitatively with caution. The experimentally observed spread is wider than the model-predicted range, and this discrepancy may reflect factors not fully represented here, including: (i) realistic heterogeneity in cell morphology/composition across zones and radial locations (cells are assumed homogeneous in the present implementation), (ii) transport assumptions at the cell–PCM interface, and (iii) the absence of explicit membrane–osmotic coupling. While the difference with prior computational investigations [23,[25], [26], [27], [28],61] illustrates the sensitivity of absolute magnitudes to modelling strategy and loading regime. Consequently, the present model is best suited to capture robust relative trends—including depth/lateral dependence and the differential impact of collagen VI versus IX on cell/PCM mechanics—while providing cell-scale magnitudes that remain physiologically plausible when contextualised against available in situ measurements.

Importantly, the present construct treats the chondrocyte as a poro-hyperelastic continuum and assumes continuity of permeability/flux at the cell–PCM interface; no explicit semi-permeable membrane hydraulic resistance, osmotic pressure difference, or membrane tension is included, and active ion-channel–mediated volume regulation is not represented. Consequently, the predicted volume changes should be interpreted as effective poromechanical outcomes of the coupled cell–PCM–ECM system rather than as a fully resolved mechano-osmotic response. Osmotic effects and membrane-level transport regulation can influence the kinetics and absolute magnitude of cell volume changes [[73], [74], [75]]; therefore, quantitative interpretation of predicted volume change should be conservative and focused primarily on relative trends (depth dependence and between-condition differences). Within this simplified transport framework, the principal value of the model is to quantify how alterations in collagen VI and IX networks modulate relative changes in cell volume and cell-level loading under a fixed tissue-level loading condition.

The increase in the collagen VI properties by 30% enhanced significantly (P < 0.001) the volumetric change of the cells by almost 14% in the superficial and deep layers. The modest deformation of the cells is likely due to the shielding role the PCM has on the cell. On the other hand, we expected that the alteration of collagen IX would have a lower effect on the volumetric changes of the cells due to the lower volume fraction and the non-direct link of collagen IX to the chondrocytes. These results confirm the earlier observation of the important role of the PCM in modulating chondrocytes' mechanical response [4]. In addition, the altered collagen properties (IX, VI) did not change the trend of the location-dependent reaction of the cells across the different layers of the cartilage. The observed differences in cells deformation across the depth and lateral directions may be linked to the heterogeneity of the ECM where a clear depth-dependent fibrillar and nonfibrillar behavior was employed. Earlier investigations reported a high correlation between the chondrocytes and collagen II strains [23,28]. The decomposed stress picture presented in this study confirmed this earlier observation, where a similarity in the stress concentration area was predicted between collagen II and the cells and its microenvironments (Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Lower cell stresses in the deep zone of the cartilage might also be linked to a lower collagen II stress, which is normal since the unconfined test was well characterized by the silent reaction of the vertical fibrils of the cartilage [46,76].

Taken together, the aggregate, cellular, and constituent-level results provide a coherent multiscale picture of how minor collagen networks can strongly affect cell mechanics while only modestly altering the macroscopic response. Although collagen VI and IX perturbations produced only modest changes in the aggregate reaction force under unconfined compression (Fig. 3b), the same perturbations produced substantially larger changes in the mechanical environment experienced by chondrocytes. In particular, collagen VI stiffening reduced average circumferential cell-supported forces (Fig. 6a) and decreased associated cell volume changes (Fig. 7a), whereas collagen IX perturbations produced smaller but still measurable effects on superficial-zone cell forces and volume change (Fig. 6, Fig. 7b). Mechanistically, because collagens VI and IX are concentrated in the PCM and TM, which act as a mechanical interface or “filter” between the collagen II–reinforced ECM and the cell, altering their stiffness redistributes stress between the PCM/TM and chondrocyte (Fig. 8, Fig. 9) without substantially changing the overall tissue-scale load-bearing system dominated by the collagen II network and fluid flow [36,[76], [77], [78], [79], [80]]. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the loading mode and parameter space explored here, and conditional on the present constitutive representation in which collagen IX is modeled as an independent tension-only fibril network sharing collagen VI's functional form. Because collagen IX is biologically a FACIT collagen with an important fibril-associated/cross-linking role, its contribution to network connectivity, inter-fibril load transfer, and damage tolerance is not explicitly captured in the current implementation. Within these bounds, collagen VI exerts a more pronounced influence on the cell-scale mechanical environment due to its direct localisation within the PCM, while collagen IX is better interpreted as a context-dependent modulator of load transfer whose contribution may vary with loading mode, spatial distribution, and disease-mediated changes in the surrounding ECM/PCM [72,81,82]. Because poro-hyperelastic predictions are inherently rate-dependent- the partitioning between fibrillar elastic support and fluid-pressurisation support shifts with loading rate - the differential roles of collagen VI and IX reported here should be interpreted as specific to the relatively slow ramp-and-hold compression rate (0.001 s−1) employed in this study [39]. Under faster, more physiologically representative loading rates, transient fluid pressurisation and fibril network engagement are expected to play a larger role [35], which may amplify the influence of fibril-network parameters on both aggregate and cell-scale responses and could modify (without necessarily reversing) the relative ranking of collagen VI versus collagen IX contributions.

Our multiscale results provide a mechanical basis for formulating hypotheses about cartilage mechanotransduction, but they do not explicitly model ion-channel gating or biochemical signaling. Specifically, the present construct quantifies how tissue-level loading and collagen-network perturbations reshape the local mechanical environment of the chondrocyte, including cell-supported forces, chondrocyte deformation/volume change, and stress redistribution within the PCM/TM and surrounding ECM. Therefore, any links to mechanosensitive ion channels are presented here as hypothesis-generating interpretations grounded in established mechanotransduction literature, rather than as direct outputs of the simulations.

In this context, mechanosensitive Ca2+ channels such as TRPV4 and Piezo1/2 have been widely discussed as important chondrocyte mechanosensors whose activation depends on loading regime and local physical cues [83,84]. TRPV4 in chondrocytes is widely associated with responses to both mechanical cues, such as local deformation and membrane-stretch-related signals, and osmotic/hypotonic gradients, whereas Piezo channels are more strongly implicated in high-strain or injurious mechanical regimes [73,[83], [84], [85]]. We emphasise that, in the present framework, predicted chondrocyte volume changes arise from coupled poromechanical compression of the cell–PCM–ECM system; explicit osmotic regulation, semi-permeable membrane resistance, membrane tension, and active ion-channel-mediated volume control are not represented. Consequently, the model cannot be used to infer the osmotic component of TRPV4 activation. We therefore restrict our TRPV4-related interpretation to mechanically mediated signals resolved by the model, namely PCM-dependent redistribution of stress around the cell and the associated cell-boundary deformation as a proxy for membrane-stretch-related mechanical input. Recent mechanotransduction frameworks emphasise that the PCM and its constituents, including collagen VI, shape the mechanical input experienced by membrane-associated mechanosensors [83]. Within this restricted mechanically mediated interpretation, the pronounced collagen VI-dependent changes in chondrocyte deformation and PCM/TM stress redistribution predicted by our model are consistent with the hypothesis that alterations in PCM mechanics may modulate the deformation-related cues available to TRPV4- and/or Piezo-mediated pathways in a loading-regime-dependent manner [85]. The osmotic component of TRPV4 activation lies outside the scope of the current model and should be addressed in future mechano-osmotic extensions that explicitly incorporate semi-permeable membrane transport and intracellular ion/osmolyte balance.

The added global sensitivity analysis strengthens the interpretation of the present findings by explicitly quantifying how uncertainty in ECM-, PCM-, TM-, and cell-level parameters propagates to both aggregate mechanics and cell-scale force transmission. The results demonstrate that bulk reaction force variance is dominated by classic ECM determinants (collagen II stiffness/volume fraction and ECM permeability), whereas cell-force variance is governed primarily by collagen VI and PCM/cell properties, with collagen IX contributing a smaller but non-zero effect at the cellular scale. This quantitative ranking supports the robustness of our principal conclusion that collagen VI exerts a stronger influence than collagen IX on cell microenvironmental loading within the explored parameter bounds, while also motivating conservative interpretation of collagen IX as a context-dependent modulator rather than a uniformly secondary constituent [55]. We note that the variance-based GSA was performed on a reduced-size 3D construct selected for computational feasibility, while preserving the same constitutive structure, parameter families, and loading framework used in the full explicitly coupled model. This reduction may influence the absolute magnitudes of the reported sensitivity indices through geometry-dependent effects, including changes in boundary-to-volume ratio, lateral fluid exudation paths, fluid-pressurisation versus fibril-network load sharing [54], and the representation of spatial/cell-to-cell variability within the smaller cell ensemble. Therefore, the sensitivity indices should be interpreted primarily in terms of the relative hierarchy and order-of-magnitude separation of dominant parameter families rather than as geometry-independent absolute percentages. Within this interpretation, the observed hierarchy remains informative: ECM collagen II/permeability/matrix-swelling parameters dominate aggregate reaction-force variance, whereas collagen VI, PCM, and cell-related parameters contribute more prominently to cell-scale force transmission. A full-scale GSA using the larger specimen geometry would be valuable to refine the absolute sensitivity magnitudes, but it remains computationally prohibitive within the present explicitly coupled, cell-resolved framework and is therefore identified as a future-work target.

In spite of the significant complexity of the model, future improvements are warranted. For example, the inclusion of cellular subcomponents, such as the cytoskeletal fibrils and nucleus, may provide further insight into intracellular transduction mechanisms. In the present study, chondrocytes were modeled as homogeneous poro-hyperelastic continua; this assumption was adopted to maintain computational tractability in the explicitly coupled, cell-resolved whole-tissue construct and because sufficiently resolved experimental data needed to prescribe depth-, region-, and subcellular-scale cell properties are not yet standard. Accordingly, the predicted spread of cell deformation and volume change is expected to be narrower than in situ measurements where cell-to-cell heterogeneity and membrane-regulated transport may broaden responses. Nevertheless, because the same cell constitutive representation was applied consistently across all simulated cases, the comparative trends between conditions remain informative, while the absolute magnitudes should be interpreted conservatively. In addition, because absolute cell-scale forces inherit uncertainty from the predicted absolute aggregate reaction force, these values should be interpreted as order-of-magnitude estimates rather than exact quantitative predictions. The quantitative interpretation of the present study is therefore focused primarily on between-condition and depth-dependent contrasts obtained under the same baseline construct and loading protocol.

Furthermore, because direct measurements of the nonlinear mechanical properties of collagen VI and IX fibril networks in situ remain limited, the chosen ±30% perturbation of their linear and nonlinear fibril parameters should be viewed as a phenomenological representation of a moderate loss or reinforcement of minor collagen networks in the pericellular/territorial microenvironment. Quantitative micromechanical mapping studies in disease and injury models demonstrate that PCM mechanical properties can change by tens of percent and can evolve rapidly with pathology stage (e.g., early post-injury PCM modulus reductions of ∼14% within days and ∼58% within one week in a murine PTOA model) [58,59]. Importantly, the stiffness-sweep trend analysis (collagen VI/IX stiffness scaled across a broader moderate range (Fig. 5)) shows monotonic trends and persistence of the relative ranking across the explored range, supporting generality beyond ±30% within moderate perturbations. Nevertheless, a wider parametric sweep over an extended VI/IX property space would be valuable to formally identify potential nonlinearities or saturation effects at more extreme perturbations but would require substantially greater computational resources. In addition, due to limited collagen IX-specific mechanical characterisation at the network scale represented here, collagen IX fibril parameters were initially assigned using collagen VI as a baseline functional form (Table 3); importantly, the global sensitivity ranking indicates that collagen IX contributes substantially less to peak reaction-force variance and a smaller but non-zero portion of cell-force variance than collagen VI, suggesting that the main qualitative trends are not driven by this placeholder assumption within the explored bounds. We further emphasise that collagen IX is a FACIT [5] collagen whose mechanical function is not fully represented by an independent tension-only fibril network. The present formulation does not explicitly capture collagen IX-mediated cross-linking, inter-fibril connectivity, or associated shear-transfer mechanisms. Therefore, the relatively minor/modulatory role of collagen IX inferred here should be regarded as conditional on this modelling assumption. Incorporating an explicit collagen IX cross-linking description, such as discrete inter-fibril link elements or a homogenised cross-link contribution to PCM/TM shear and damage behaviour, represents an important direction for future work and may reveal a more substantive role for collagen IX under loading modes that mobilise inter-fibril shear or under degenerative conditions in which cross-link integrity is compromised. Finally, the full heterogeneous picture of the cartilage matrix (protein/proteoglycan networks) was not considered, and a relatively slow ramp-and-hold compression rate (0.001 s−1, matching the experimental benchmark of DiSilvestro and Suh [39]) was employed. Because poroelastic load partitioning between fibril networks and fluid pressurisation is intrinsically rate-dependent, the reported roles of collagen VI and IX should be interpreted in the context of this slow-rate regime; their relative contributions may differ under faster, dynamic, or impact-level loading representative of in-vivo joint activity, and a systematic rate-sweep study using the present multiscale framework is a natural next step.

The present model generates testable multiscale predictions that can be validated using feasible ex vivo and in vivo workflows. A direct ex vivo strategy is to combine unconfined compression of cartilage explants under boundary conditions comparable to those simulated here with in situ optical imaging (e.g., confocal microscopy) [72] to quantify depth-dependent chondrocyte deformation and volume changes during loading/relaxation, while simultaneously measuring aggregate reaction force for macro-scale benchmarking. Targeted perturbation of collagen VI and/or collagen IX—via genetically modified tissue (e.g., collagen VI–or collagen IX–deficient models) or controlled biochemical/enzymatic disruption [13,65]—could then be used to test the model's key comparative predictions: that collagen VI perturbation produces larger changes in cell-level forces/deformation and PCM/TM stress redistribution than collagen IX under the same tissue-level loading, whereas collagen IX perturbation may show a minimal macroscopic signature yet still modulate superficial-zone cell mechanics. Complementary validation can include AFM-based micromechanical mapping of PCM/TM stiffness together with collagen VI/IX localisation assessments to confirm the intended microstructural changes and relate them to measured cell-scale deformation [86]. If supported, these validation pathways would enable the model to inform (i) disease modeling by parameterizing PCM/TM alterations during degeneration or injury, (ii) therapeutic concepts aimed at preserving/restoring pericellular mechanical shielding, and (iii) biomaterial design strategies that tune pericellular-scale mechanics to achieve desired mechanosensitive cellular responses.

In summary, we investigated the effect of changes in key collagen VI and IX constituencies of the articular cartilage on chondrocytes and aggregate cartilage mechanics using an explicit poro-hyperelastic multi-scale finite element model of the cartilage. Within the present tension-only fibril representation of collagens VI and IX, our theoretical examination showed a meaningful association between collagen VI and IX behaviour and cellular mechanics, including cell loading/deformation and changes in the mechanics of the surrounding microenvironment, mainly the PCM. Because the cross-linking function of collagen IX as a FACIT collagen is not explicitly captured here, its modulatory rather than dominant role in the present results should be interpreted as conditional on this modelling assumption and warrants dedicated future investigation. The results demonstrate the ability of the current construct to capture the mechanical significance of the cartilage cells' environment, which could help understand the impact of the structural composition change on cartilage maintenance and integrity. This model is an initial step to examine the mechanical influence of tissue-level loading, local cell membrane mechanics, and factors affecting the activation of mechanosensitive ion receptors like the Piezo channels to help identify therapeutic targets for cartilage diseases.
