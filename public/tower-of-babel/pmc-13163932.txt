Real‐World Treatment and Outcomes in Resectable Early‐Stage Non‐Small Cell Cancer: THASSOS‐INTL Australia Cohort
Authors: Pei Ding, Kevin Jasas, Victoria Bray, Abhijit Pal, Rebecca Moor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesLimited data exist on real‐world treatment strategies and their impact on survival in Australian patients with NSCLC. THASSOS‐INL (NCT04808050) evaluated the treatment patterns and survival outcomes in patients with early‐stage NSCLC.MethodsAustralia cohort of THASSOS‐INTL, non‐interventional, multicenter, retrospective study enrolled patients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with stage IA‐IIIB resectable NSCLC between January 2013 and December 2017. The primary outcomes included treatment patterns and associated 3‐year survival rates. Secondary endpoints included clinico‐demographic characteristics and their association with overall survival (OS).ResultsOf 199 patients recruited (median [range] age: 67.0 [35.0–88.0] years), 53.8% were males, 87.4% were current smokers/ex‐smokers, and 52.2% had an ECOG score of ≤ 1. A total of 42.2% patients had stage I, 28.1% had stage II, and 29.6% had stage III NSCLC. Adenocarcinoma was the predominant (56.8%) histological subtype. Overall, 97.0% patients underwent surgery. Neoadjuvant therapy was reported in 9.5%; adjuvant therapy was reported in 39.2%. The overall median OS was 6.0 years (95% CI: 5.200 NE) with a median 3 year survival probability rate of 70.5% (95% CI: 64.4%–77.2%). Age, increasing stages of NSCLC, disease progression, and EGFR mutations were associated with significant worsening of OS.ConclusionsThe study underscores the diverse treatment approaches prior to approval of immuno‐targeted therapy, showing that fewer than half of the patients received AT with only a quarter of those receiving adjuvant ST. The study highlights the need for integrating molecular diagnostics, improving access to novel therapies, and a multidisciplinary team approach to improve survival in these patients.Keywords: chemotherapy, early‐stage lung cancer, non‐small cell lung cancer, survival outcomes, treatment patterns

Introduction

As the fifth most common cancer in Australia, lung cancer was reported in 9.0% of all new cancer cases and accounted for 17.0% of all cancer‐deaths in 2023 [1, 2]. Between 2015 and 2019, the 5‐year survival rate of patients with lung cancer was 24% [3] with the majority diagnosed with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [2]. Around 50% of patients with NSCLC are diagnosed with stage IA to stage IIIB disease [4] where the 5‐year survival ranges from 58%–92% in patients with stage I and 10%–22% with stage III disease [5, 6, 7]. Additionally, the 5 year recurrence rate of early‐stage NSCLC ranges from 30% to 70% following surgery [7, 8, 9].

Prior to recommendation of targeted and immune therapies for early stage NSCLC, international guidelines recommended surgery with complete resection and microscopically negative margins (R0) for stage I and 4 cycles of adjuvant therapy with platinum‐based doublet chemotherapy (CT) for patients with resectable stage IIA‐IIIA disease [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. The development of targeted therapies has allowed more personalized treatment approaches in selected patients with actionable driver mutations [12]. Administration of adjuvant osimertinib improved 2‐year disease‐free survival (DFS) in patients with surgically‐resected stage IB to IIIA epidermal growth factor receptor mutated (EGFRm) NSCLC in the ADAURA trial [13]. Since June 2024, adjuvant osimertinib has been listed in the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) of Australia for the treatment of eligible patients with EGFRm stage IB to IIIA NSCLC [14]. Similarly, the advent of immunotherapies has redefined the treatment strategies for early‐stage NSCLC. Neoadjuvant nivolumab plus CT demonstrated significantly improved event‐free survival and pathological complete response versus CT alone in patients with stage IB to IIIA NSCLC (CHECKMATE‐816 trial) [15]. Nivolumab is listed on Australian PBS from August 1st 2024 for the treatment of resectable NSCLC [16]. Perioperative treatment (i. e, neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery and adjuvant therapy) with nivolumab was also found to significantly improve event‐free survival versus CT alone in patients with resectable early‐stage NSCLC (CHECKMATE‐77 T) [17]. Perioperative durvalumab with neoadjuvant CT reported significantly prolonged 1‐year event‐free survival versus neoadjuvant CT alone in patients with resectable NSCLC (AEGEAN) [18]. Adjuvant pembrolizumab significantly improved DFS versus placebo in completely resected, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD‐Ll)‐unselected, stage IB‐IIIA NSCLC (PEARLS/KEYNOTE‐091) [19], whereas perioperative pembrolizumab with neoadjuvant CT significantly improved overall survival (OS), event‐free survival, major pathological response, and pathological complete response versus neoadjuvant CT alone in patients with resectable, early‐stage NSCLC (KEYNOTE‐671) [20]. Adjuvant atezolizumab has recently been listed in the PBS for the treatment of patients with PD‐L1 expression > 50% and stage II‐IIIA NSCLC based on the results of the IMpower‐010 trial [21].

With therapeutic advances, optimum sequencing of novel drugs is a challenging part of clinical decision‐making. There is a paucity of real‐world studies on the diagnosis, treatment patterns, and prevalence of EGFRm and PD‐L1 expression among patients with early‐stage NSCLC, which can have significant clinical implications. In Australia, studies have reported delays in initiating the appropriate treatments and underutilization of curative treatment [22, 23, 24]. The THASSOS‐INTL real‐world study was conducted to determine the treatment patterns and their associated survival outcomes in patients with resectable early‐stage NSCLC outside the United States (US) and Europe (EU). The current study reports the treatment practices and survival outcomes for the Australian cohort of the THASSOS‐INTL study.

Methodology

Study Design

The detailed study design is reported in the THASSOS‐ INTL manuscript. This was a non‐interventional, multicenter, retrospective subgroup analysis conducted across four centers in Australia. The study protocol (NCT04808050) was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards/Independent Ethics Committees (approval number 2021/ETH00576) from all the participating centers before the study initiation. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients or their legal representatives before any data collection. The study was conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki, International Council for Harmonization and Good Clinical Practices guidelines. The current manuscript is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [25].

Study Population

The study population included patients aged ≥ 18 years (or ‘adults’ as per local regulations) diagnosed with primary stage IA to IIIB resectable NSCLC as per 7th edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) between 01 January 2013 and 31 December 2017 and were followed up until 31 December 2020, as per the medical records. Patients with follow‐up data of at least 12 months from the index date (date of diagnosis of early‐stage resectable NSCLC) were included unless the patient died within 12 months of diagnosis. Patients who provided written or electronic informed consent at study entry were included (for deceased patients, next of kin/legal representative provided informed consent, unless a waiver was granted). Patients with concomitant cancer (within 5 years of NSCLC diagnosis), except for nonmetastatic nonmelanoma skin cancers, or in situ or benign neoplasms, stage IV NSCLC, tumor with small cell lung cancer, neuroendocrine in origin, or a mixed histologic type were excluded.

Study Outcomes and Data Collection

The primary outcome was treatment patterns and their associated 3 year survival outcomes per clinical and pathologic staging. Treatment patterns were described as patients who underwent surgery only, those receiving neoadjuvant therapy only (systemic therapy [ST] +/− radiotherapy [RT]), adjuvant therapy only (ST +/− RT), and both neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies. Secondary endpoints included the sociodemographic and clinicopathological characteristics, the prevalence of EGFR mutations and PD‐L1 expression (> 1%), biomarker testing strategies, and the association between age, clinical stage, EGFR mutations, PD‐L1 status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scores, and OS. Definitions of the study outcomes are provided in Table S1.

Data were collected retrospectively from patient's medical records and transcribed into an electronic data capture system. Data collection included socio‐demographics (age, gender, ethnicity, alcohol consumption, body mass index, smoking status), clinicopathological characteristics (ECOG performance status, tumor‐node‐metastasis [TNM] staging at index diagnosis, clinical and pathologic stage as per 7th edition of AJCC, histology, genetic mutations, comorbidities), prevalence of EGFR mutations and PD‐L1 expression and treatment patterns. The data were extracted from the date of index diagnosis to the end of the follow‐up, defined as death, the last medical record entry, or the date of data extraction, whichever was the earliest.

Statistical Analyses

Considering the primary endpoint, based on the assumption of exponential survival, the precision shown was the 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the Kaplan Meier estimate of 3‐year OS. Detailed sample size estimation along with precision analysis are reported in THASSOS‐INTL manuscript. All analyses were performed using the Full Analysis Set comprising all the patients who fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in the study. Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS system (Version 9.2 or higher). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio‐demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment modalities. Categorical variables were presented using frequencies, percentages, and corresponding 95% Clopper Pearson CI. Continuous variables were presented as the number of observations, arithmetic mean (standard deviation [SD]), or median (range). Median survival rates for time‐to‐event data were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method and reported with a two‐sided 95% CI. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to assess the association between the OS and clinicodemographic variables. A p‐value of less than 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The Australia cohort recruited a total of 199 patients. The median (range) age of patients at the index date was 67.0 (35.000–88.000) years; 53.8% were males, 87.4% were current smokers/ex‐smokers with a median of 50.0 (0.200–132.500) pack‐years, and 52.2% had an ECOG score of ≤ 1. Per clinical classification, 42.2% (n = 84) had stage I NSCLC (stage I A: 24.1%; stage I B: 18.0%), 28.1% (n = 56) in stage II (stage II A: 13.5%; stage II B: 14.5%), and 29.6% (n = 59) in stage III (stage III A: 27.6%; stage III B: 2.0%). Adenocarcinoma was the predominant (56.8%, n = 113) histological subtype with 55.7% (n = 111) tumors reported in the right lung. According to the TNM classification, 34.6% (n = 69) of patients had tumors diagnosed in the T1a‐T1b stage and 41.7% (n = 83) in the T2a‐T2b stage with N0 status in 61.8% (n = 123) (Table 1). At the time of data collection, 47.2% (n = 94) of patients were deceased.

Treatment Modalities

Overall, 97.0% (n = 193) patients underwent surgery across all stages (stage I A: 95.8% [n = 46]; stage I B: 100.0% [n = 36]; stage II A: 96.3% [n = 26]; stage II B: 100.0% [n = 29]; stage III A: 96.4% [n = 53]; stage III B: 75.0% [n = 3]) with complete surgical resection (R0) reported in 85.0% [n = 164]). Lobectomy (83.9% [162/193] was the preferred surgery type with video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) approach in 81.3% (157/193) of patients. (Table S2).

In total, 106/193 (54.9%) patients underwent surgery only of which the majority of these had stage I disease (65.1% [n = 69]). Neoadjuvant therapy and surgery were reported in 9.5% (n = 19) patients with 52.6% (10/19) patients receiving ST, 10.5% (2/19) receiving RT, and 36.8% (7/19) receiving both RT and ST. Most patients received platinum‐based systemic chemotherapy (n = 17) (carboplatin‐containing combination, n = 7, and cisplatin‐containing combination, n = 8) in neoadjuvant setting.

Surgery with adjuvant therapy was reported in 39.2% (n = 78) (stage IA: 10.4% [n = 5]; stage IB: 19.4% [n = 7]; stage IIA: 44.4% [n = 12], stage IIB: 51.7% [n = 15], stage IIIA: 69.1% [n = 38], stage IIIB: 25.0% [n = 1]). In the adjuvant setting, ST was administered in 64.1% (n = 50/78), RT in 7.6% (6/78), and both ST and RT in 28.2% (22/78) of patients. Majority of the patients in stage IIb 34.4% (n = 10), and stage IIIA: 34.5% (n = 19) received ST (Table 2). In the adjuvant setting, platinum‐based systemic chemotherapy was administered in (n = 64) patients (carboplatin‐containing combination, n = 18, and cisplatin‐containing combination in n = 45) (Table S3). Neoadjuvant therapy, surgery, and adjuvant therapies were administered in 3.0% (6/199) patients, all with stage IIIB NSCLC. Immunotherapies were administered in four patients (durvalumab: n = 3; pembrolizumab: n = 1). Post‐progression/recurrence, 12 (6.0%) patients underwent surgery, 55 (27.6%) received ST, and 55 (27.6%) received RT (Table S4).

The median (range) duration of neoadjuvant ST was 63 (24.000–91.000) days and of adjuvant ST was 70 (0.000–1036.000) days. Adjuvant ST had a higher treatment completion rate with 73.1% (57/78) of patients completing planned treatment versus 68.4% (13/19) patients receiving neoadjuvant ST. Toxicity‐related discontinuations were reported in 15.4% (12/78) and 10.5% (2/19) of patients receiving adjuvant and neoadjuvant therapies, respectively.

Survival Outcomes

Disease progression was reported in 89/199 (44.7%) patients (local recurrence: 16.8% [15/89], extra‐thoracic metastasis: 28.0% [25/89], intra‐thoracic metastasis: 15.7% [14/89], CNS metastasis: 10.1% [9/89]).

The overall median OS was 6.0 (95% CI 5.200‐ Not estimable [NE]) years with a 3 year median survival probability rate of 70.5% (95% CI 64.4%–77.2%). Patients with stage IA had a median OS of 6.0 (95% CI 5.200–NE) years while the median OS was NE for stage IB; a 3‐year survival probability rate of 81.1% (95% CI 70.6%–93.0%) for stage IA and 88.9% (95% CI 79.2%–99.8%) for stage IB. The median OS was 6.0 (95% CI 4.700‐NE) and 3.4 (95% CI 2.600‐NE) years for stage IIA and IIB and the 3‐year survival probability rates were 77.8% (95% CI 63.600–95.200) and 55.2% (95% CI 39.7%–76.6%), respectively. Patients with stage III A and III B had a median OS of 4.8 (95% CI 2.600‐NE) and 1.8 (95% CI 0.500‐NE) years with a 3‐year survival probability rate of 56.6% (95% CI 44.8%–71.7%) and 25.0% (95% CI 4.58%–100.0%), respectively (Figure 1,b Table 3).

EGFR Mutations, Other Driver Mutations, and PD‐L1 Expression

More than one‐third of patients (37.7% [75/199]) underwent testing for EGFR mutations, of which 12.0% (9/75) tested positive. Among patients tested for PD‐L1 expression (9.5%, 19/199), 57.8% (11/19) had PD‐L1 expression ≥ 1% (≥ 1%‐ < 25%: 3/11 [27.3%]; ≥ 25%‐ < 50%: 1/11 [9.1%]; > 50%: 7/11 [63.6%]) (Table S5). The commonly used antibody for PD‐L1 testing was DAKO22C3 (73.6%, [14/19]). Other genetic alterations (ALK, BRAF, KRAS, MET, and TP53) were found in 10.0% of patients (20/199), with KRAS mutations being the most common (7.0% [14/199]) (Table S6).

Association Between Clinical Parameters and OS

No significant association was reported between age and OS in the univariate analysis, while the multivariate analysis demonstrated that age was significantly associated with worsening of OS (p = 0.030). A significant association was reported between increasing stage of NSCLC and OS in both univariate (hazard ratio [HR] range from 1.24 to 4.19; p < 0.050 for stage II B and III B) and multivariate analyses (HR range from 1.32 to 2.41; p < 0.050 for stage II B and III A) compared to stage IA NSCLC. A similar trend was observed in patients who had disease progression (p < 0.001) and had a significantly higher likelihood of mortality compared to patients with no disease progression in both univariate (HR: 3.500 [95% CI 2.260–5.440]) and multivariate analysis (4.019 [95% CI 2.580–6.800]) (Table 4).

Discussion

This real‐world study evaluated the treatment landscape and survival outcomes among patients with resectable stage IA to IIIB NSCLC between 2013 and 2020 before the approval of adjuvant immunotherapies and targeted therapies for early‐stage NSCLC in Australia. The baseline socio‐demographic characteristics were consistent with other real‐world studies in Australia [26, 27]. The median age of patients enrolled in the Australian cohort (67 years) was higher than the global cohort (62 years), which is in line with the other Australian studies [26, 27]. Nearly half of the patients (52.8%) in our cohort were smokers, despite heavily implemented tobacco control legislation in Australia as opposed to only about one‐third of patients in the global cohort (32.2%) [28].

Curative surgery was the primary treatment modality in concurrence with most patients being recruited at the early stages. About a third of the patients (n = 78, 36.2%) received adjuvant therapy, while very few (n = 19, 4.5%) received neoadjuvant therapy, mostly platinum‐based CT. Most of the patients (approximately 84%) who received neoadjuvant (n = 16) or adjuvant therapies had stage IIA to IIIB NSCLC (n = 66), which is in line with the recommendations during the study period. Very few patients received RT only (neoadjuvant: 10.5%, [02/19]; adjuvant: 7.6%, [06/78]) and RT + ST (neoadjuvant: 36.8% [07/19]; adjuvant: 28.2% [22/78]) in the current study. Similar results were reported by a population‐level cohort study in Australian patients with early‐stage NSCLC undergoing curative RT based on disease stage (5%–11%) [26]. Two Australian studies have shown variations in the proportion of patients receiving curative RT for early‐stage NSCLC as 14% and 57% due to different study populations and the availability of various RT technologies [27, 28, 29, 30].

Although immunotherapies offer a new standard of care for patients with NSCLC, they were approved for the treatment of patients with early‐stage NSCLC after 2017 in Australia. Monotherapy with adjuvant atezolizumab was approved in patients with PD‐L1 expression > 50% and stage II–IIIA NSCLC patients in 2020 [21]. As our data collection period was before the approval and reimbursement of immunotherapy for early‐stage NSCLC in Australia, its use was limited (in 4 patients with stage III disease).

Compared with the overall THASSOS‐INTL data, the median OS in Australian patients was lower (7.6 versus 6 years). Patients with stage IIB‐IIIB NSCLC had comparatively low median OS (1.8 to 4.8 years) compared with patients with stage I and IIA NSCLC (median OS: 6 years). A study conducted among Australian patients with stage III NSCLC has reported a median OS of 1.9 years [31]; another registry study on patients with stage III NSCLC of whom 30.1% [420/1396] underwent surgery reported an OS of 2.2 years. A retrospective study on 88 patients with stage III NSCLC of whom 48% [42/88] underwent surgery showed a median OS of 3.1 years [32, 33]. An Australian study reported an overall 2 year survival rate of 84% in stage I and 64% in stage II‐III [26].

We also conducted a logistic analysis to understand the factors associated with survival. As expected, an increase in the stage of NSCLC was associated with an increased likelihood of mortality with a significant association reported in multivariate analysis for stage II B and III A (p < 0.050). Understandably, disease progression was significantly associated with the worsening of OS (HR 4.19 [95% CI, 2.580–6.8000], p < 0.001). Patients with EGFRm had higher mortality compared with patients with no EGFRm. However, a conclusive interpretation is difficult due to the low testing rate. In a retrospective study on Australian patients with stage III NSCLC, higher age, higher ECOG score, and higher stage of NSCLC were significantly associated with worsening of OS [32]. A similar association was observed in European patients with NSCLC [34].

Determining the EGFR mutation status and PD‐L1 expressions is crucial in designing treatment strategies to enhance survival rates. In the current study, the rate of EGFR mutations among patients who were tested was 12.0% which is lower than that reported in other Australian studies reporting prevalence in the range of 15% to 23.8%, [35, 36, 37] which reinforces the need to identify patients who might benefit from EGFR‐targeted therapies. We found PD‐L1 expression > 1% in 57.8% of patients similar to a real‐world Australian study on 6690 PD‐L1 positive patients, which identified the prevalence of tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥ 1% in 62%, TPS < 1% in 38% of patients [38]. With the PBS listing of adjuvant osimertinib in EGFR‐mutated resected stage I B‐III A NSCLC [13], adjuvant atezolizumab in PD‐L1 > 50% stage II‐III NSCLC [39], and neoadjuvant nivolumab for resectable NSCLC, Australian patients with NSCLC can have increased access to guideline‐recommended novel therapies. Moreover, the Medicare Benefits Schedule provides reimbursement for next generation sequencing (NGS) and PD‐L1 testing to patients with NSCLC, which enables patients to leverage targeted therapies [39]. These adjuvant therapies have shown significant survival benefits and herald a new era for patients with early‐stage NSCLC. With the advent of immunotherapy and targeted therapies and their inclusion in the PBS listings (osimertinib and atezolizumab), increased biomarker testing would be beneficial to all patients to inform optimal treatment selection. Additionally, in July 2025, a national lung cancer screening program was launched in Australia [40]. Studies have established an association of screening programs with an increase in proportion of patients being diagnosed at an earlier stage [41, 42, 43]. With an expected stage shift in Australian population, our study provides a strong baseline status on the real‐world presentation and management of NSCLC.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective nature, small sample size, and registry design, which can result in missing data as a few patients might have been lost to routine clinical follow‐up. Second, changes in treatment guidelines over the study period could affect the prognosis for patients who underwent treatment during that duration. Furthermore, our study did not consider the socioeconomic position of patients, migration status, or locality, and did not classify the population into distinct groups (indigenous Australians). The study was also not designed to capture how resectability was determined, limiting the information on the decision of handling such patients for surgery in case of early progression. Despite these limitations, our study provides novel, real‐world insights specific to an Australian cohort of patients with early‐stage NSCLC. Our study underscores the treatment patterns and survival outcomes for early‐stage NSCLC and emphasizes the need for international guideline‐concordant treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes. Our research serves as an important historical reference point for comparing with the current immunotherapy‐based treatment paradigm. Additionally, the study reports the low testing rates for EGFR and PD‐L1 mutations calling for streamlined action for screening and biomarker testing for NSCLC.

Conclusion

The THASSOS‐Australia results provide crucial insights into the treatment strategies, clinical outcomes, and biomarker testing patterns in patients with early‐stage NSCLC. The study highlights the heterogeneities in the treatment patterns with less than half of the patients receiving adjuvant therapy, of which a quarter of them received adjuvant ST. Additionally, as the study was conducted in the pre‐immunotherapy era, biomarker testing and the uptake of novel treatments remain low. This study can act as a benchmark to integrate optimum diagnostic molecular work‐up, improved access to novel immunotherapies and targeted therapies, along with multi‐disciplinary team care to potentially enhance survival outcomes.
