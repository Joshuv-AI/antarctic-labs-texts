Duodenal atresia associated with apple-peel configuration of distal small bowel in a late preterm neonate: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Minale Menberu Kebede, Jejaw Endale Mihretie, Mesfin Tesera Wassie, Asratu Getnet Amare, Temesgen Agegnehu Abebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The association of duodenal atresia with apple-peel (Christmas tree) configuration of the distal small bowel is exceptionally rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature. This unusual combination is of particular interest because it may provide evidence supporting a vascular etiology for selected cases of duodenal atresia. We report a neonate with type III duodenal atresia associated with apple-peel bowel and absent proximal superior mesenteric artery anatomy.Case presentation:A late-preterm (35 + 5 weeks) female neonate with antenatal suspicion of jejunal atresia presented on the second day of life with bilious vomiting and mild abdominal distension. Plain abdominal radiography demonstrated a double-bubble sign suggestive of duodenal obstruction. Exploratory laparotomy revealed type III duodenal atresia immediately distal to the ampulla of Vater. The third and fourth portions of the duodenum, the ligament of Treitz, and the proximal superior mesenteric artery were absent. The distal small bowel exhibited an apple-peel configuration, with a long segment of jejunum and ileum spiraling around a single vascular pedicle in the absence of normal mesentery.Diagnosis, therapeutic intervention, and outcomes:A diagnosis of duodenal atresia associated with apple-peel small bowel was established intraoperatively. Duodenojejunostomy was performed with preservation of maximal bowel length. The postoperative course was uneventful, with successful establishment of enteral feeding and discharge in stable condition.Conclusion:This rare association supports an intrauterine vascular etiology for both duodenal atresia and apple-peel bowel. Awareness of this variant is essential for careful surgical planning and bowel preservation, while trans-anastomotic feeding offers a crucial alternative for early enteral nutrition in total parenteral nutrition–limited settings

Introduction

Duodenal atresia (DA) occurs in approximately 1 in 5000–10 000 live births and represents one of the most common causes of neonatal intestinal obstruction[1,2]. It is classically attributed to failure of duodenal recanalization during embryogenesis. The coexistence of DA and jejunoileal apple-peel configuration is exceedingly rare, with only sporadic cases reported in the literature[4–7]. We present a case of a late preterm neonate with antenatal suspicion of jejunal atresia; subsequently, during laparotomy, DAand an apple-peel configuration of the distal bowel were diagnosed.

This case report has been reported in line with the Surgical Case Report (SCARE) criteria.

Case presentation

A 2-day-old female neonate was born to a 28-year-old para 2 mother at 35 weeks and 5 days’ gestation via cesarean section for prolonged PROM and failed induction. RVI status and viral markers were negative. Antenatal ultrasonography suggested jejunal atresia with polyhydramnios. The baby cried immediately after birth and weighed 2.4 kg, with Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at the 1st and 5th minutes of life, requiring no resuscitation.

On the second day of life, she developed frequent bilious vomiting with mild abdominal distension. Otherwise, she has no history of failure to suck, yellowish discoloration of the body, cough, or fast breathing.

Objectively, she was well-appearing and alert. Vital signs were PR 150 bpm, RR = 54/min, T 37°C, and SpO2 = 92% on room air. She had pink conjunctivae and non-icteric sclerae. Chest and cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable. There was mild upper abdominal distention, but no palpable mass or tenderness. Per-rectal examination showed a normal anal opening with some meconium-stained fluid. Other systems were normal.

On workup, abdominal ultrasound was unremarkable, describing no free fluid, no mass, and a normal hepatobiliary system. Echocardiography was also normal, with normal cardiac anatomy and motion. On a plain abdominal radiograph, there was a “double bubble” sign, suggestive of a duodenal obstruction (see Fig. 1). The baby was stabilized with intravenous fluids, IV ampicillin 120 mg BID, IV gentamicin 9 mg daily, and nasogastric tube decompression. Written informed consent was obtained, and the patient was transferred to the OR for surgery.

Intraoperatively, a type III DA was identified immediately distal to the ampulla of Vater. Instead of the normal distal duodenum, the third and fourth portions of the duodenum were absent, along with the ligament of Treitz. Thus, the proximal duodenum terminated at the atretic segment, and no normal distal duodenal continuity was present. The distal small bowel exhibited a classic apple-peel (Christmas tree) configuration, consisting of a long segment of jejunum and ileum spiraling around a single vascular pedicle with an absent mesentery. The bowel appeared to originate directly beyond the atretic duodenal segment. Furthermore, the proximal portion of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) was absent; only a solitary vessel supplying the apple-peel bowel was identified (see Figs 2 and 3). There was minimal peritoneal ascitic fluid. The other viscera appeared normal. A diamond-shaped duodenojejunostomy was performed while preserving maximal small bowel length. Two tubes were passed through a single nostril: one for gastric decompression and the other as a trans-anastomotic feeding tube. The trans-anastomotic feeding tube was intentionally used to facilitate early enteral nutrition because total parenteral nutrition (TPN), which is routinely used after complex neonatal intestinal surgery in many centers, was not available in our setting. This strategy allowed early nutritional support while minimizing the risks associated with prolonged fasting and dependence on intravenous fluids. The apple-peel bowel was viable and was therefore preserved without further resection.

Postoperatively, the neonate was managed with nil per os and maintenance intravenous fluids for the first 36 hours. Preoperative intravenous antibiotics were continued. Trophic feeding was initiated after 36 hours through the trans-anastomotic feeding tube. Two doses of an amino acid infusion were administered as supplemental nutritional support. On the third postoperative day, the gastric decompression tube was removed, and oral feeds were gradually introduced with good tolerance. The trans-anastomotic feeding tube was removed on postoperative day 5. Recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in good clinical condition.

At a follow-up visit 3 weeks after surgery, the surgical wound had healed well. The infant was alert, feeding adequately, and showing improvement in nutritional status. Her weight increased from 2.4 kg at discharge to 2.8 kg at the three-week postoperative follow-up, demonstrating satisfactory weight gain. There were no signs of feeding intolerance, recurrent vomiting, abdominal distension, or bowel obstruction. The infant continues with regular follow-up at the pediatric surgical referral clinic, with ongoing monitoring of growth and nutritional status.

Discussion

DA is usually an isolated anomaly but can coexist with other congenital malformations, including trisomy 21, congenital heart disease, and annular pancreas[1,2]. Classically, DA is thought to result from failure of recanalization during the early embryonic period, while jejunoileal apple-peel atresia arises from a vascular insult to the superior mesenteric artery during late fetal life[3]. The coexistence of the two lesions has challenged traditional embryological theories, suggesting that a vascular insult may also underlie some cases of DA[4,7].

Clinically, both conditions present with bilious vomiting and abdominal distension. The “double-bubble” sign is typical for duodenal obstruction, though the apple-peel configuration is rarely diagnosed before surgery[2,6].

Surgical correction requires individualized planning. In our patient, duodenojejunostomy relieved the proximal obstruction, while preservation of the apple-peel distal bowel was crucial to avoid short bowel syndrome. Postoperative outcomes depend on residual bowel length, the presence of associated anomalies, and neonatal stability[5–7].

Our case adds to the limited literature supporting a vascular etiology for at least some cases of DA, as well as its coexistence with a Christmas tree (apple-peel) abnormality of the distal small bowel. Furthermore, our case report highlights the importance of both meticulous intraoperative assessment to preserve bowel length and the use of a trans-anastomotic feeding tube in settings where TPN is unavailable.

Strengths

This case provides rare clinical evidence supporting a vascular etiology for DA by documenting its coexistence with a “Christmas tree” deformity. Furthermore, it offers highly practical utility for resource-limited settings by demonstrating that a trans-anastomotic feeding tube is a safe alternative when TPN is unavailable.

Limitations

As this is a single case report (n = 1), these findings cannot be generalized. Additionally, the vascular origin was inferred macroscopically without genetic or histopathological confirmation, and long-term nutritional and developmental follow-up data were limited.

Conclusion

The coexistence of DA associated with an apple-peel configuration of the distal small intestine is extremely rare. This unusual association supports a possible vascular etiology for DA. Early recognition, prompt surgical management, and bowel-preserving strategies are essential for favorable outcomes.
