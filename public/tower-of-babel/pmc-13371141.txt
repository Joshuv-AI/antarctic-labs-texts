Effect of balance training exercises with stroboscopic glasses on postural control, gait, and physical performance in the elderly population: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Neshat Javid Jahromi, Masumeh Hessam, Maryam Saadat, Mohammad Mehravar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging is the process that declines all the sensory elements that are involved in postural control—“particularly vestibular and proprioceptive”—and these changes are generally compensated with reliance on visual inputs for postural control in this population. Stroboscopic glasses can intermittently disrupt the visual feedback to possibly stimulate sensory reweighting toward the vestibular and proprioceptive inputs. In this study, the impact of balance training with stroboscopic glasses against balance training with non-stroboscopic sunglasses on postural control, gait, and physical function among the elderly is evaluated.MethodsA total of fifty participants aged 60 years and above will be assigned randomly to either intervention (with stroboscopic glasses that intermittently block visual information) or control (the same exercises with regular sunglasses) groups. Primary outcomes postural control (force plate assessments under eyes-open and eyes-closed condition in feet-together and tandem stance), gait (10-Meter Walk Test), and physical function (Timed Up and Go, Five Times Sit-to-Stand, Functional Reach, and Push and Release Tests) will be measured at baseline, post-intervention, and 4-week follow-up. Exercises based on the Otago Exercise Program will take three sessions per week for 4 weeks, with the last session used as an attendance record session.DiscussionPositive outcomes from this study will support the practice of older adult balance training under stroboscopic glasses. We hypothesized greater improvements in all outcome measures for the stroboscopic balance exercise group compared to the control group.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20201112049361N2), registered 2025-02-21, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/user/trial/80977/view

Background

Aging, per se, contributes to proprioceptive, visual, and vestibular dysfunctions [1], resulting in a decline in joint proprioception and in nerve conduction, which compromises postural control: the greater magnitude of sway as well as the displacements of the center of pressure (COP) [2–6]. That is to say, such dysfunctions—or the less-than-ideal integration of these sensory systems—produce incorrect adjustments of the center of pressure beneath the feet, which is a relative measure of the therapeutic efficacy in regulating the center of mass [7], thus affecting postural adjustments and interference to fall and injury, all of which lead to reduced quality of life [2, 3, 7]. As people age, they lose their physical capacity due to the degeneration of cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and nervous systems. This implies that they will have impaired walking and balance [8–11]. Gait speed, the so-called sixth vital sign, predicts the likelihood of falls, disability, and mortality in older adult years [12–14–15]. Stroboscopic vision therapy (SVT) uses the alteration of visual input so as to help lower visual dependence and, in turn, help in enhancing the proprioceptive and auditory sensitivity, which in turn, will enhance sensory integration and balance control [16, 17]. In a randomized controlled trial, it was shown that a 6-week balance program with SVT reduced visual dependence in athletes with chronic ankle instability (CAI) [18]. Tsai et al. suggested that in older adults, SVT acts on unstable surfaces to stimulate neuroplastic adaptations and alter sensory reliance toward non-visual systems [19]. SVT was also shown to facilitate open-loop postural control by modifying cortical-postural interaction in a study of young individuals using stabilogram diffusion analysis [19]. Moreover, Lee Hyunwook et al. conducted a study whereby 4 weeks of balance training with stroboscopic eyewear saved improvements in stability and coordination in CAI patients [20]. One can hypothesize that balance exercises with SVT may improve the body awareness, sensory integration, and postural control of older adults. While the main sensory systems of elderly people deteriorate most when compared to younger individuals, they rely more on visual inputs for balance [19, 21–23]. It is hypothesized that these interventions would also positively impact gait, postural stability, and physical performance in this population.

Methods

Study purpose

This study intends to investigate the changes in balance control of the elderly from pre-training to post-training, gait, and physical performance after 4 weeks of balance training with wearing stroboscopic glasses.

Study design, randomization, and allocation

Conforming to the principles of single-blind randomized control trial, participants to be used in the research will be randomized to either of two groups—an intervention group consisting of balance exercise along with SVT or a control group consisting of balance exercise only—by employing stratified permuted block randomization according to sex in a 1:1 allocation ratio. The randomization entailing digital generation of a random sequence with variable block sizes of 4 and 6 will be achieved by an independent researcher who has no contact with the study subjects. A separate person who is also blind to the outcome assessments will break the sealed envelopes with group allocations and reveal them.

Sample size calculation

A total of 50 participants (25 in each group) are going to be recruited. Sample size estimation was done using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2) at 95% of statistical power, an alpha level of 0.05, effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.25, and 10% dropout rate.

Data monitoring

A formal data monitoring committee is not required due to the low-risk, non-invasive nature of the interventions. Study investigators will oversee participant safety and data quality, with any adverse events reported to the institutional review board as per ethical guidelines. The study investigators will monitor trial conduct internally on a monthly basis, including participant adherence to the exercise protocol, completeness of data collection forms, and occurrence of any adverse events. Any deviations from the protocol or safety concerns will be addressed promptly by the investigators.

Ethics and trial registration

Before the inception of the stated experiment, each participant will receive a full lecture on the aims of the study and will then be asked to go through and sign the informed consent documents. Access to identifiable data will be provided only to the principal investigator and authorized study personnel. Data will be analyzed and presented in anonymized form, with average results. Adverse events from the low-risk intervention will be mild, and no serious adverse effects are anticipated. Any unforeseen event will receive typical medical care, and participants will be informed of their rights to compensation per institutional policies. Participant safety will be continuously monitored by the study investigators, and any serious adverse events will be reported immediately to the institutional review board. The investigators, in consultations with the ethics committee, will have the authority to pause or terminate the trial if unexpected safety concerns arise. Setting and recruitment

In this study, people aged sixty and above will be recruited through advertisements on social media, hospitals, and retirement centers for participation. Data collection will be continued at the Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Science Rehabilitation Research Center (AJUMS) supervised by the physical therapy clinic and rehabilitative hospitals of its rehabilitation department and directly supervised by a research assistant of AJUMS. Participants in this trial did not contribute to the design or conduct of the study. However, following completion of the study and analysis of results, dissemination activities are planned to involve key stakeholders in the field of aging and rehabilitation. Specifically, results will be shared with clinicians and policymakers responsible for older adult care, through meetings and presentation, to facilitate the translation of findings into practice. This approach aims to ensure that the outcomes of the study can inform the design of more effective balance training interventions for older adults, enhancing their safety and functional abilities.

Inclusion criteria

Eligibility thresholds: (1) age between 60 and 80; (2) a score of less than 45 on the Berg balance scale test, which indicates a risk of falling; (3) standing and walking without the use of assistive devices; (4) adequate proficiency in Persian.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) epilepsy, (2) sensitivity to light, (3) migraine.

Potential subjects will be excluded from the study for any of the following reasons: (1) significant neurological or psychosocial disorders (speculative effect on study response); (2) score below 30 on the MOCA cognitive test; (3) hearing communication impairment; (4) dizziness; (5) spectacle use (6).

Procedure

The assessment of pre-intervention will occur before random allocation to either intervention or control groups in a random manner via a team member not involved in data collection. Primary outcome measurements will be taken at three different time points: pre- and post-intervention as well as 4 weeks post-intervention. All outcome assessments will be performed by a physiotherapist blinded to the group allocation.

The aforementioned training program is devised for participants to do their training for 12 different sessions each week over four successive weeks, i.e., three different sessions are to be done every week for the 4 weeks, each session entailing near about 45–60 min. Both the groups will perform the same balance exercises, which will be derived from the Otago Exercise Program consisting of 12 progressive exercises having four levels of difficulty [25]. Progressing from supported exercise to unsupported exercise (Table 2) will be practiced for 30 min each session. Group differences will only be in those assigned to the intervention using stroboscopic glasses for training against the control group, who will use ordinary sunglasses in their training (see SPIRIT Fig. 2 for study schedule). This design allows isolation of the effect of the stroboscopic stimulus beyond standard training. There will be Senaptec Strobe Glasses (USD 299, Senaptec LCC, Beaverton, OR, USA), specifically designed to alternate their lenses from transparent to opaque with liquid crystal technology, for blocking visual input at intervals. In this study, the glasses are set at a frequency of 3 Hz with a combination of 1/6-s opaque and 1/6-s clear phases (Table 3). For the protocol adherence to be enhanced, there are going to be reminders for the participants. The reminders will be in form of phone calls and text messages before each session. To promote retention to participation, follow-up reminder phone calls and text messages will be sent to each participant before each assessment appointment. Flexible appointments and transportation arrangements will be provided when possible. The outcome data to be collected for participants who discontinue from the intervention will include all primary and secondary outcomes as assessed at each time point. Reasons for discontinuation will be recorded. All paper-based forms will be stored in locked cabinets within the chief investigator’s office. The internal team for study management will be conducting monitoring of trial conduct monthly. Thus, the team will check recruitment logs, check data entry accuracy, and check adherence to given intervention (see Fig. 2). Postural control

Force-posterior evaluating with an instrument (Kistler 9286BA, 100 Hz sampling rate) under four conditions will include feet together: open eyes and closed eyes and tandem stance: open eyes and closed eyes. Participants are standing quietly on the force plate for 30 s under each condition. Each test will be performed three times, and the average of the three trials will be used for the statistical analysis. Balance performance will be evaluated by means of movement and by average velocity of the center of pressure (COP) steadiness in anterior–posterior and medial–lateral trajectories [22].

Gait

Subjects will then be instructed to start walking at their desired speed while the measurement of the distance will be recorded with the help of a stopwatch, from which the walking speed will be calculated and recorded. A 14-m walkway marked at 0, 2, 12, and 14 m will create a central 10-m testing segment, with 2 m at each end for acceleration and deceleration [26, 27].

Physical performance

Tests in various functional areas will be used to measure physical performance.

Reactive balance

Reactive balance will be determined using the push-and-release test. In this test, participants stand with their feet a comfortable distance apart and arms at their sides. They are instructed to lean back gently against the examiner’s hands. Then, with unexpected force, a backward push resembling a disturbance of equilibrium will be given to the shoulder or hip region of the participant. The time taken by the participant to respond dynamically and regain balance will be measured according to the push-and-release ordinal scale in which 0–4 represents independent recovery with one step to fall/inability to stand without assistance, respectively [28].

Pro-active balance

The proactive balance will be rated by the Functional Reach Test. Trial participants are set up with their feet shoulder-width apart and arms beside them, while a measuring stick is attached at shoulder height to a wall. The participant will make a fist with the dominant hand, raise the arm to shoulder level, and lean forward as far as possible without stepping or losing balance. The distance attained from the starting point to the third metacarpal will be recorded. The test will be run three times, and the mean of the final two trials will be considered. A reach distance below 15.24 cm will be identified as a higher fall risk [29].

Timed Up and Go (TUG)

A fall risk assessment will be conducted according to the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, in which participants will be assessed in their usual footwear and with any walking aids if needed. Following the seated position, an examiner will give the cue to the participant to start: standing, walking 3 m, turning, walking back, and sitting. The only timing information required will be the time taken to complete the entire task, stopping at the time the participant again is seated. Any participant requiring 12 s or longer to complete the TUG will be considered as having an increased risk for falls [29–31]. An elaborated word count is required.

Five Times Sit to Stand

Balance and risk of falling will be measured by Five Times Sit to Stand. Participants are instructed to sit on a standard chair with back support by the chair’s back and having their arms folded across their chest. After crossing their arms across their chest, participants are required to complete five consecutive sit-to-stand motions as quickly as possible in response to the examiner’s “go” command, such that their back and legs do not touch the chair in between attempts. Timing starts from the examiner saying the word “start” and ends when the subject’s bottom touches the chair after the fifth attempt. Assistance or use of upper extremity support for any of the five repetitions means that the subject has failed the test. Less time taken to complete the exercise suggests better performance on this measure [32].

Statistical analysis

A main effects analysis of group, time (with levels of baseline, post-intervention, and 1-month follow-up), and group-by-time interactions is conducted on all dependent measures using a two-way (2 × 3) repeated measures analysis of variance. For all analyses, a threshold of p < 0.05 will be set to determine significance. Effect sizes will be calculated by dividing the mean change score by the standard deviation of the pretest scores. The calculation of Cohen’s d effect sizes will be determined, where values equal to and above 0.0 to less than 0.2 will be considered small, 0.21 to less than 0.5 will be considered a medium effect, and 0.51 to less than 0.8 will be defined as a significant effect. Any data that might be missing or incomplete will be dealt with according to ITT [33]. The second stage in data validation will involve conducting the double entry of data with regular consistency check backs. No interim analyses are planned for this study. The study will proceed until it reaches the intended sample size and all follow-up phenomena are complete unless unexpected safety issues arise. Concerns regarding early termination of the study on safety grounds would be decided on by the principal investigator after consulting the ethics committee. Data entry will be double-checked (double data entry) to minimize errors. For each variable, range checks and validation rules will be applied, and descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, and distribution patterns will be examined to ensure normality of continuous variables. Data will be stored securely on password-protected computers and encrypted servers, with access limited to the study team. Regular backups will be performed to prevent data loss. No formal interim analysis is planned for this study due to the low-risk, non-invasive nature of the intervention. There are no predefined statistical stopping rules.

Discussion

There will also be an inevitable progressive deterioration in the sensory system functions with aging, especially in the non-visual modalities. The consequence of this then would be less integration of non-visual information for postural control, thus increasing the chances of falls among the older adults [23, 34–37]. Stroboscopic visual training (SVT) refers to the rapid, intermittent disruption of visual input, which creates a need for maximization of the use of limited visual cues together with the increasing reliance on other sensory information such as proprioceptive and auditory signals [38]. Currently, this research involves SVT through stroboscopic eyeglasses to enhance sensory integration, possibly through increased dependence on proprioceptive inputs and vestibular inputs from performing standard balance training exercises (OTAGO), intended to improve sensory reweighting [16, 17]. This adaptive reorganization of senses clarifies temporal integration, such multisensory processing efficacies [39]. However, from a physiological viewpoint, this increased demand on the visual system during SVT usually promotes neuroplastic changes that later result in the improvement of perceptual acuity and temporal resolution. Thus, continuous vision, if restored, usually results in subjects perceiving tasks as less challenging, usually more efficient visual processing capabilities [40]. The Otago Exercise Program, which is known to improve strength, balance, and mobility among older adults [25], may provide the most benefit if the program is combined with SVT. We hypothesize that this combined intervention will lead to greater improvements in postural control, gait, and functional performance. Synergistic effects on both static and dynamic balance from the combination of OTAGO exercises with SVT may lead to better mobility and hopefully a fall reduction. The glasses’ portability and ease of application in clinical, home, and community-based settings should improve adherence and reward participants with long-term benefits. The main limitation is the inability to fully blind the subjects due to the conspicuous nature of the eyeglass intervention.
