Tissue-specific CLOCK isoforms modulate circadian feedback loops to govern reproductive fitness in Drosophila
Authors: Yishi Wang, Pengfei Lv, Yahong Li, Xingzhuo Yang, Juan Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms, conserved across life, are governed by molecular feedback loops in Drosophila clock neurons, though how these loops adapt in peripheral tissue clocks remains unclear. In this study, we discovered that in reproductive tissues, the expression profile of Clock is notably different. It is expressed at very low levels in the ovary, while a shorter isoform of Clock is particularly abundant in the testis. Downregulation of these isoforms impaired fertility in both male and female flies. The short isoform of CLOCK inhibited the binding of the longer isoform to CYCLE, leading to a reduction in CLOCK / CYCLE co-binding in the testis. Using ChIP-seq to identify CLOCK and CYCLE binding sites in the head, ovary and testis, we found that the binding profile of CLOCK / CYCLE was distinct between these tissues, in which CLOCK / CYCLE targets to genes involve in spermatogenesis specifically in testes. Immunostaining revealed knock down of CLOCK short form results in defects in spermatogenesis. This study reveals the expression profiles and functional mechanisms of specific Clock isoforms in reproductive tissues, which highlighted the modification of clock regulatory loop and essential role of Clock in reproductive organs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05927-y.Keywords: Testis, Ovary, Alternative splicing, Clock, cycle, Drosophila

Introduction

Circadian rhythm is a universal phenomenon in living organisms. In Drosophila, it is regulated by a molecular feedback loop within a circuit formed by clock neurons in the brain. However, circadian rhythms are not limited to the brain; many other tissues also exhibit rhythmic functions. Peripheral clocks have been identified in various organs, including chemosensory hairs, excretory organs, digestive organs, reproductive tissues, and fat bodies, among others, in Drosophila [1].

Peripheral circadian rhythms are regulated in different modes. In Drosophila, the clock in Malpighian tubules [2, 3], antenna [4, 5] and fat body [6] operate independently of the central clock. In contrast, the clock in oenocytes [7, 8] is governed by the central clock. For prothoracic gland [9, 10], both the central clock and the local clock cooperate in regulation. Circadian rhythms are present in the germline of both male and female Drosophila. Egg-laying behavior in Drosophila is under circadian control [11]. Oscillations of reporter genes have been detected in isolated testes. Mutations in clock genes lead to significant changes in male fertility in Drosophila [12]. However, in mouse testes, clock genes show no discernible oscillation [13]. It remains an open question whether and how the molecular feedback loop is modified in these peripheral tissues.

Evidence suggests that CLOCK (CLK) plays a critical role in initiating the circadian clock during development. CLK is a core component of the circadian circuit, forming a heterodimeric complex with CYCLE (CYC) to activate the rhythmic transcription of clock-controlled genes, including PERIOD (PER) and TIMELESS (TIM). These proteins initially accumulate in the cytoplasm and later enter the nucleus to repress CLK/CYC activity [14]. CLK is the earliest expressed clock gene, detectable several hours before PER in late embryos and early first instar (L1) larvae [15, 16]. In the Drosophila mushroom body, ectopic expression of CLK, which stabilizes CYC, along with CRY, is sufficient to initiate circadian clock function [17]. Cryptochrome (CRY) is a photoreceptor protein expressed in a specific subset of circadian neurons and ocular tissues in Drosophila. It plays a key role in mediating light-induced resetting of the circadian clock [18]. This evidence suggests that CLK is one of the key factors required to establish the circadian rhythm. Given its central role in circadian regulation, CLK is likely an important target for circadian control. However, Clk is expressed in many tissues beyond the brain, raising the intriguing question of how its expression is regulated in various tissues and how it coordinates its function within the feedback loop alongside other physiological processes.

In this study, we found that the alternative splicing of Clk contributes to the tissue-specific regulation of Clk function. We identified a shorter isoform of Clk that is particularly abundant in the testis. Functional studies revealed that the short isoform of CLK inhibits the binding of the longer isoform to CYC. Loss of function of different Clk isoforms resulted in reduced reproductive success. Through ChIP-seq analysis of CLK and CYC binding sites in the head and testis, we discovered distinct binding profiles of CLK/CYC in these tissues, highlighting tissue-specific differences in their regulatory roles.

Materials and methods

Fly strains

All fly strains were maintained on conventional fly food enriched with yeast in an incubator following a 12-h light–dark cycle at 25 °C and 60% humidity. The following fly lines were used in this study. Clk-GFP; Clkout and CYC-GFP; cyc01 were gifts from Paul E Harding’s lab (Texas A&M University, USA), while nos-GAL4 was a gift from Junzheng Zhang’s lab (China Agricultural University, China). w1118 (NO.5905), UAS-ClkDN (NO.36318), Clkout (NO.56754), and UAS-dicer (NO.24646) were from Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center. UAS-Clk-all-RNAi (NO.107576) and UAS-Clk-L-RNAi (NO.104507) were from Vienna Drosophila Resource Center. Tj-GAL4 (NO.104055) was from Kyoto Stock Center.

UAS-Clk-L and UAS-Clk-S transgenes were generated by cloning the CDS of Clk-L into the vector pATTB-UAS-4V5 and the CDS of Clk-S into the vector pATTB-UAS-3FG. These were inserted into the attP40 site on chromosome 2 via PhiC31-mediated transgenesis. Additionally, UAS-Clk-S-RNAi1, UAS-Clk-S-RNAi2, and UAS-Clk-S-RNAi3 transgenes were generated in this study following methods previously described [19]. We cloned 100 bp gene fragments specifically expressed by Clk-S as inverted repeats into the piggyBac vector and constructed transgenic flies through random insertion.

Productivity analysis of male flies and female flies

Three-day-old virgin female w1118 flies and three-day-old virgin males of the appropriate genotype (or three-day-old virgin male w1118 flies and three-day-old virgin females of the appropriate genotype) were mated in standard medium for 24 h. Then, each mated female was transferred to grape medium for another 24 h. The number of larvae was counted three days later.

Luciferase assay

Per-E-LUC were obtained from Luoying Zhang’s Lab (Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China). Clk-L-4V5, Clk-S-3FG were cloned into pAC5.1. In all experiments, 30 ng of pCopia Renilla Luciferase and 300 ng of per-E-pGL3 Luciferase were used. Each of the remaining plasmids added was 300 ng, and the blank plasmid pAc was used to control the variable. Appropriate plasmids were co-transferred into S2 cell by X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (06366546001, Roche). It works by forming a complex with DNA, facilitating its transport into animal or insect cells. The ratio of the added X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (μl) to plasmid DNA (μg) was 3:1, which was the optimal dosage.

Luciferase activity was measured 48 h after transfection using the Dual-Lumi™ Luciferase Assay Kit (RG088S, Beyotime). The activities of firefly and Renilla luciferases were measured at end-point sequentially from a single sample by GloMax® 20/20 Luminometer (E5311, Promega). We aspirated and discarded the culture medium, gently washed the transfected cells twice with 1 × PBS, and lysed the cells with 100 μl luciferase reporter gene cell lysate (RG088S, Beyotime) for 10 min with shaking. We then added 20 μl of cells lysate to 50 μl substrate and measured the luciferase signal. Next, we added 50 μl of stop buffer and took another reading. The ratio results were calculated using Prism 8. All primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 1 (Table S1).

Immunoprecipitation

Clk-L-4V5, Clk-S-3FG, Clk-L-3FG, cyc-HA were cloned into pAC5.1. The appropriate plasmids were co-transferred into S2 cell using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (06366546001, Roche). After 48 h, we harvested the cells by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. The cells were lysed with 200 μl of RIPA strong lysis (CW2333, CWBIO) buffer containing protease inhibitors (CW2200, CWBIO). The lysates were chilled on ice for 20 min and then centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. We collected 30 μl cell lysates, added 2 × loading buffer and boiled the samples for 10 min to prepare the input samples.

The remaining cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 μl Mouse anti V5-Tag (AE017, ABclonal) or Mouse anti HA-Tag (AE008, ABclonal) at 1:100 dilution ratio respectively. We then added 40 μl of RIPA-balanced rProtein A/G beads (BE6868, EASYBIO) and incubated the mixture at 4 °C for 3 h. The beads were washed three times with ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Finally, we added 100 μl 2 × loading buffer, incubated the sample at room temperature for 10 min, boiled them for 10 min, and performed Western Blotting to analysis the results.

Western blot

The antibodies used were Mouse anti V5-Tag (AE017, 1:2000, ABclonal) and Mouse anti HA-Tag (AE008, 1:2000, ABclonal) and Rabbit anti DDDDK-Tag (2368, Binds to same epitope as Sigma's Anti-FLAG® M2, 1:2000, CST).

Quantitative real-time PCR and gene expression analysis

Head, testes, and ovary were dissected in PBS at ZT7-8 and total RNA were extracted using RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (vazyme, R401-01). One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix (vazyme, R333). Real-time PCR analysis was performed in triplicate (three technical replicates per sample) using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (High ROX Premixed) (vazyme, Q341) in an ABI StepOnePlus instrument. Data were normalized to rp49 using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The PCR program used was 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Primers used are listed in Table S1. The tissue specific expression pattern of Clk and cyc was analyzed based on previous single cell RNA-seq data [20].

ChIP-seq and data analysis

The ChIP protocol was modified based on Le Thomas et al. (2014). For each biological replicate, 200 heads, 200 pairs of testes, or 200 pairs of ovaries were collected at ZT10, fixed in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, quenched with 25 mM glycine for 5 min, and washed three times with PBS. The fixed tissues were then homogenized in RIPA buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, protease inhibitor, 10 mM NaF, 0.2 mM Na3VO4). Chromatin was sheared by sonication to fragments of 100–500 base pairs. The sample was then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was pre-cleared with Dynabeads Protein G beads (Invitrogen) for 2 h at 4 °C. 5% of the pre-cleared sample was reserved as the Input, while the remaining sample was incubated with 5 μl anti-GFP antibody (A-11122, Invitrogen) overnight at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitated (IP) sample was then incubated with Dynabeads Protein G for 5 h at 4 °C to allow bead binding. After that, beads were washed 5 times in LiCl Wash Buffer (10 mM Tris pH7.4, 500 mM LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate), while the Input sample was incubated with 1 μl 10 mg/mL RNase A at 37 °C for 1 h. Rinsed in TE buffer (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8), and finally resuspended in Proteinase K buffer with 100 μg of Proteinase K. Finally, both samples were incubated with 100 μg of Proteinase K in PK Buffer (200 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 25 mM EDTA, 300 mM NaCl, 2% SDS) first at 55 °C for 3 h, followed by overnight incubation at 65 °C.

Illumina library preparation and sequencing were performed at the Novogene sequencing platform (https://cn.novogene.com/). The sequencing data for each sample is 6 GB. Raw sequencing data were processed and quality controlled using fastp, followed by alignment to the Drosophila melanogaster (dm6) reference genome (https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/dm6/bigZips/) using Bowtie 2 [21, 22]. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard Tools (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The deepTools multiBamSummary and plotCorrelation functions were used to assess the correlation between ChIP-seq samples [23]. The SAMtools merge function was used to merge deduplicated BAM files [24]. NarrowPeak was called using MACS2 with the default settings, but to obtain more peaks, we adjusted the p-value to 0.05 [25]. Peak annotation and analysis were conducted using the R package ChIPseeker [26], with the Transcript Database (TxDb) set to "fly" and the annotation database specified as "org.Dm.eg.db". The deepTools bamCompare function and multiBigWigSummary function were used to generate ChIP-seq signal heatmaps [23]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler and the org.Dm.eg.db annotation database [27], with a significance threshold of p-value < 0.05. The average binding peak signal intensity of CLK and CYC across the three tissues was generated using the deepTools multiBigWigSummary function to process ChIP-seq signals and visualized using R [23]. Two biological replicates were included in the ChIP-seq experiment.

Immunostaining

Testes of adult males were dissected in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The samples were then washed three times with wash buffer (1 × PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100) for 15 min each. Following this, the samples were blocked using 3% BSA for 1 h. Next, all samples were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the samples were washed three times with wash buffer. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies in the dark for 2 h at room temperature. The samples were then washed three times with wash buffer and mounted using mounting medium. The primary antibodies used in this experiment were anti-Vasa rat (1:50, DSHB), anti-Fasciclin III mouse (Fas III) (7G10, 1:100, DSHB), anti-eya mouse (10H6, 1:100, DSHB). The secondary antibodies employed were Alexa Fluor™ 568 (A11004, 1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor™ 647 (A21247, 1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a 20 × objective or 63 × oil objective. The number of testes GSCs and eya + cells was counted on the confocal images using ImageJ software.

Results

Two types of Clock transcripts are differentially expressed in Drosophila tissues

Clock (Clk) has 2 types of transcripts in Drosophila. One type, including Clk-RA, Clk-RD and Clk-RF (Flybase: http://flybase.org), contains 9 exons (Fig. 1A). The other types, including Clk-RG and Clk-RH (Flybase: http://flybase.org) contains 6 exons (Fig. 1A). We refer to these as Clk-long (Clk-L) and Clk-short (Clk-S), respectively (Fig. 1A). And Clk is used to generally refer to all Clk gene products. Protein domain analysis revealed that the Clk-RA and Clk-RD variants of Clk-L isoforms contain both a bHLH domain and three PAS domains at their N-terminus (Fig. 1A). Clk-RF variants of Clk-L contains only three PAS domains at its N terminus (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the Clk-S isoforms, including Clk-RG and Clk-RH, lack the bHLH domain and one PAS domain (Fig. 1A).

To test whether alternative splicing of Clk contributes to the remodeling of molecular clocks in peripheral tissues, we analyzed the expression levels of Clk transcript variants across different tissues. Interestingly, Clk-L and Clk-S are differentially expressed in various tissues of adult Drosophila. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis revealed that the adult testis is particularly enriched in Clk-S compared to head tissue (Fig. 1C). While the Clk-L form is relatively more abundant in the gut and head (Fig. 1B). Both Clk-S and Clk-L are expressed at lower levels in the ovary compared to the head (Fig. 1B, C). The relative ratio of Clk-L to Clk-S was significantly lower in the testis and higher in the ovary compared to the head (Fig. 1D). Quantitative isoform analysis revealed that Clk-L is the predominant transcript in head tissues, accounting for 79% of total Clk transcripts. In contrast, Clk-S represents 97% of Clk transcripts in the testis (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). Consistently, Western blot results indicated that CLK-L is the predominant protein isoform in head tissues. In contrast, both CLK-L and CLK-S proteins were detected in body tissues excluding the head (Supplementary Fig S1C). These results suggest a distinct distribution of Clk isoforms in the testis and ovary, implying that the different forms of Clk may play distinct roles in these tissues.

Detection of Clk-S and Clk-L expression at different time points revealed their oscillatory patterns. RAIN method [28] was used to perform circadian statistics. Consistent with previous data, Clk-L in the adult Drosophila head displayed a clear oscillation over the course of a day (Fig. 1E). In the testis, Clk-L also showed oscillation, but with a much lower amplitude (Fig. 1E). The relative expression of Clk-S in the testis was particularly high compared to the head (Fig. 1F), although no clear oscillation was observed for Clk-S in either the head or the testis (Fig. 1F). Similarly, the total level of Clk in the testis was significantly higher, but did not exhibit noticeable oscillation compared to the head tissues (Fig. 1G). In contrast, the relative expression levels of Clk-S, Clk-L, and the total Clk were much lower in the ovary and showed no oscillation compared to the head (Fig. 1H-J).

In conclusion, these results reveal distinct spatial and temporal expression patterns of Clk-S and Clk-L, particularly in the testis and ovary. This raises the question of whether these two forms of Clk play different roles in the reproductive biology of males and females.

Clk-short is required for male reproductive function

To investigate the role of Clk in the ovary and testis, we examined the expression patterns of various clock genes in these tissues in single-cell sequencing datasets (Supplemental Fig. S2A, B) [20, 29]. The results revealed that clock genes are predominantly expressed in Terminal epithelial cells of testis, Seminal vesicle and Male gonad associated epithelium, with significant expression also observed in both germline and somatic cells in the testis (Supplemental Fig. S2C-H). Overlap analysis showed that Clk expression co-localized with nos and Traffic Jam (Tj), which are expressed in the germline and somatic cells, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S2I-L). Additionally, clock genes are predominantly expressed in follicle cells in the ovary (Supplemental Fig. S2M-R) and Clk expression co-localized with Tj, which is also expressed in follicle cells (Supplemental Fig. S2S-V). To explore the functions of different Clk forms in reproduction, we examined the phenotype of Clk loss-of-function by overexpressing the dominant-negative form, ClkDN, using nos-Gal4 and Tj-Gal4 to target the germline and somatic cells, respectively. The CLKDN construct was generated by deleting the basic DNA-binding domain of CLK [5]. It acts as a dominant-negative because it retains the ability to dimerize with its partner CYC but forms transcriptionally inactive CLKDN:CYC complexes due to the deletion. This sequesters CYC and competitively inhibits functional CLK:CYC heterodimer formation, thereby blocking CLK-dependent transcription. The results indicated that overexpression of ClkDN using Tj-Gal4 led to a significant reduction in the number of offspring in both males and females, compared to nos-Gal4 (Fig. 2A, B). This suggests that Clk function is particularly critical in Tj-Gal4-marked somatic cells for reproductive success.

We generated transgenes expressing the coding region of the Clk-L and Clk-S independently. Overexpressing of Clk-L and Clk-S using Tj-Gal4 resulted in a loss of fertility in females (Fig. 2C). In males, overexpressing of Clk-S led to significant reduction in the number of offspring, while overexpressing of Clk-L had no significant effect (Fig. 2D). In females, both Clk-L and Clk-S overexpression resulted in a significant decrease in offspring number (Fig. 2C). To determine whether these phenotypes were dependent on endogenous Clk, we conducted the same experiment in a Clkout background. Clkout is a null allele. It was generated by FLP/FRT-mediated recombination, which created a 5398 bp deletion encompassing the Clk transcription start site and essential upstream regulatory sequences. This deletion completely abolishes functional Clk expression [30]. The phenotypes were similar to those observed in the w1118 genetic background (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting that the phenotypes were not dependent on the endogenous Clk. These results indicate that both excessive Clk-L and Clk-S are detrimental to female fertility, while excess Clk-S, but not Clk-L, negatively affects male fertility.

To further investigate the roles of Clk-L and Clk-S in female and male fertility, we examined RNAi lines specifically targeting Clk-L, Clk-S, and all Clk isoforms. Phenotypic analysis showed that knockdown of Clk-L resulted in female fertility defects, indicating that Clk-L is essential for female fertility (Fig. 2G). In males, RNAi targeting Clk-L, Clk-S, and all Clk isoforms resulted in fertility defects, suggesting that both forms are critical for male fertility (Fig. 2H). This finding indicated that both Clk-L and Clk-S are needed in the testis. We next sought to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

CLK-S affected the binding of CYC with CLK-L

To investigate the mechanism of Clk-S function, we assessed its effect on the Per-luciferase reporter (Fig. 3A). We found that, unlike Clk-L, which activated reporter gene expression, Clk-S did not exhibit transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 3A, columns 3 and 4). Interestingly, the addition of Clk-S in the presence of Clk-L significantly reduced the Clk-L effect (Fig. 3A, column 5). In the circadian rhythm regulatory loop, PER interacts with CLK to inhibit its function. To explore this interaction further, we compared the effects of Per and Clk-S. We found that adding Per in the presence of Clk-L also alleviated the Clk-L effect (Fig. 3A, column 6). Similarly, Clk-S exerted a comparable effect on Per-luciferase expression, regardless of the presence of Per (Fig. 3A, column 7). When both Per and Clk-L were present, adding more Clk-S did not further downregulate reporter gene expression (Fig. 3A, column 8), suggesting the functions of these two genes do not exhibit an additive effect.

To investigate the mechanism of CLK-S function, we conducted an immunoprecipitation experiment. First, we compared the binding affinity of CLK-L for itself and for CLK-S. After tagging CLK-L with different tags, we observed a significantly higher affinity between CLK-S and CLK-L compared to the affinity between CLK-L and CLK-L (Fig. 3B). As CLK functions in CLK/CYC complex, we examined the interaction between CLK and CYC in the presence of CLK-S. We found that the presence of CLK-S abolished the interaction between CLK and CYC (Fig. 3C), suggesting that CLK-S competes with CYC for binding to CLK-L.

Consistent with these findings, binding affinity predictions made using AlphaFold confirmed our results. We calculated the binding energies between CLK-L, CLK-S, and CYC (Table 1). The strongest binding occurred between CLK-L and CYC. In contrast, CLK-S showed the weakest binding to CYC, indicating little to no direct interaction (Consistent with Fig. 3C). Further evidence is needed to determine whether CLK-S promotes CYC dimer formation. Based on these results, we predict that in the testis, the interaction between CLK and CYC is diminished, which would lead to a reduction in the common targets of CLK and CYC in this tissue.

The binding profile of CLK differs between the testis, ovary and brain

To test this hypothesis, we performed ChIP experiments to examine the CLK and CYC binding profiles across the genome in testis, ovary, and head tissues. We used GFP tagged transgenes CLK and CYC Drosophila lines in this experiment [31]. To confirm the tagged CLK represents the endogenous isoforms of CLK, we did PCR to detect the isoforms in this line. The results indicated that the predominant CLK isoforms were labeled and the ratio of the different isoforms was comparable to endogenous ones (Supplemental Fig. S1D).

The ChIP results revealed that the co-binding regions of CLK and CYC varied between these three tissues. The correlation heatmap of ChIP-seq read counts confirmed the consistency of the samples of the same genotype (Supplemental Fig. S3A, B). To demonstrate the quality of the ChIP-seq data, we included genome browser views of genomic tracks showing CLK/CYC co-occupancy at the circadian gene per across the three tissues (Supplemental Fig. S3C). As expected, a strong overlap of CLK and CYC signals was observed in the brain, whereas no such overlap was detected in the ovary and testis (Supplemental Fig. S3C). This is likely due to the very low expression levels of CLK-L in these tissues. In both the testis and ovary, the signal intensity of CLK binding peaks in CYC-bound gene regions was significantly lower compared to head tissues (Fig. 4A-C, Fig. S3D). Conversely, in CLK-bound gene regions, CYC binding peak signals were significantly weaker in the testis (Fig. 4A-C, Fig. S3D). The Venn diagram shows the proportion of overlapping CLK and CYC binding peaks across different tissues (Fig. 4D). In the head, overlapping peaks represent 11.5% of CLK binding peaks and 29.6% of CYC binding peaks; in the ovary, they account for 31.3% of CLK binding peaks and 5.5% of CYC binding peaks; and in the testis, they make up 8.9% of CLK binding peaks and 14.1% of CYC binding peaks. This finding supports our hypothesis that the presence of Clk-S leads to reduced CLK/CYC interaction.

Further analysis of the data revealed distinct binding profiles across the three tissues. Enrichment analysis showed that different gene families were enriched in the CLK and CYC peaks specific to each tissue (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Table 2). Notably, clock genes were enriched in the head and ovary tissues, but not in the testis (Fig. 4E, H). Comparison of the binding profiles between tissues highlighted distinct CLK and CYC binding peaks in each of the three tissues (Fig. 4F, G).

In conclusion, CLK/CYC exhibits distinct binding profiles in the head and reproductive tissues. More importantly, these results suggest that the gene regulatory networks involving these transcription factors are predominantly reorganized in reproductive tissues. Analysis of the CLK/CYC ChIP-seq data supports the idea that the tissue specific Clk isoform contribute to these differential CLK/CYC binding patterns. This variation may also arise from tissue-specific gene expression networks. Next, we explored how CLK/CYC contributes to the regulation of fertility.

CLK/CYC binds to gene loci important for fertility in reproductive tissues

To identify the targets of CLK/CYC binding in reproductive tissues, we examined the overlapping gene loci co-bound by CLK/CYC and compared them with known genes involved in spermatogenesis and oogenesis (https://flybase.org/, Supplementary Data 2). The results revealed that 13 genes in spermatogenesis and 2 genes in oogenesis were targeted by CLK/CYC (Fig. 5A-C, Supplementary Data 3–4). These genes are known to play critical roles in fertility. Notably, they are rarely targeted by CLK/CYC in the brain. Previous results indicated that eya expresses in cyst cell nuclei as spermatocytes differentiate from amplifying spermatogonia and eya mutant testes exhibit degenerating young spermatocytes [32]. Consistently, immunostaining of vasa and eya revealed that the germ line and somatic cells were significantly reduced upon knocking down of CLK-S (Fig. 6A-D). Fasciclin 3 (Fas3) encodes a cell adhesion molecule and is used to label hub-cell [33]. These findings explain the fertility phenotypes observed in male and female flies with altered Clk expression.

In conclusion, we summarized the function of CLK in head and reproductive tissues (Fig. 6E). CLK expresses at very low levels in the ovary, while a shorter isoform of Clk is particularly abundant in the testis. Downregulation of these isoforms impaired fertility in male flies. ChIP assay revealed that together with the regulation of tissue specific gene network, CLK/CYC binding profiles were diversified in these three tissues. The short isoform of CLK competes with the longer form for binding to CYC, resulting in reduced CLK/CYC co-binding in the testis and thereby modulating binding profile of downstream genes.

Discussion

This study provides an example of how the core circadian regulatory machinery is adapted to tissues beyond the brain. Starting with the verification of Clk isoform expression in different tissues, we found that distinct Clk isoforms regulate fertility differentially in Drosophila testis and ovary. ChIP-seq analysis of Clk and cyc in the head, ovary, and testis revealed distinct CLK/CYC regulatory landscapes in the head and reproductive tissues of Drosophila. This study highlights the different isoform profiles and their functional mechanisms in regulating the circadian clock in reproductive tissues. The ChIP-seq results also uncovered changes in the global regulatory networks across tissues, which can partly be attributed to differential isoform expression.

The abundance of the short form of Clk correlates with dampened oscillations of clock genes. Our results show that the presence of the short form of Clk leads to a weaker per-LUC signal. Published data also indicate that the amplitude of per oscillations is significantly reduced in the testis (Fig. 5 in Beaver LM et al. 2002) [12] compared to the brain (Fig. 3C in Allada R et al. 1998) [34]. This may be due to the abundance of the short form of CLK, which inhibits the binding of CLK-L to the per locus. We also examined the cry expression pattern in both the testis and ovary (Figure S2). The results indicate that cry and Clk do exhibit overlapping expression, though the number of cells co-expressing both genes is relatively low. Previous studies by Kilman and Liu demonstrated that cry is essential for a functional clock, in addition to Clk expression [17, 35]. Thus, this evidence aligns with the evidence that a functional circadian clock exists in the testis, albeit with dampened strength. We speculate that the presence of CLK-S, along with the downregulated cry expression in the testis, may facilitate the production of fertility-related genes while attenuating circadian rhythm-associated gene expression. This finding suggests that the short form of CLK serves as a mechanism to dampen the circadian clock’s activity, despite the presence of all core components of the clock. Furthermore, the detection of CLK binding in testis tissue revealed no significant binding to the endogenous per locus. This could imply either that the binding is too weak to detect, or that other factors are inhibiting the binding in the testis.

The abundance of the short isoform of Clk provides one mechanism for reducing the circadian rhythm of the clock transcription factor while maintaining its activity at gene loci critical for spermatogenesis in the testis. Ectopic expression of Clk in nervous system results in oscillation of the downstream genes [36], indicating that it’s a master regulator of circadian rhythm. CLK was found to have functions other than circadian rhythm in peripheral tissues in this study and others [37]. Thus, Clk function need to be controlled under these conditions. The compromised circadian rhythm during spermatogenesis may confer reproductive plasticity. Maintaining sperm readily available could be advantageous for males in the wild. Our results suggest that the CLK protein may be maintained in the testis under selective pressure to target key genes involved in spermatogenesis. Additionally, tissue-specific cofactors may play a role in facilitating CLK/CYC binding to gene loci essential for spermatogenesis.

How dose CLK-S regulate reproductive fitness? We found in this study that the presence of CLK-S complete with CYC for the CLK-L binding. As a result, it may change the abundance of CLK-L/CYC, which may change the downstream binding on chromosome according to the affinity of the specific gene locus to CLK-L/CYC complex. Although CLK-S likely does not bind DNA directly, it may regulate target genes by competing with CLK-L. Several lines of evidence support this model. First, CLK-S lacks the bHLH DNA-binding domain (Fig. 1A), making direct DNA binding improbable. This is further supported by ChIP-seq data showing that while CLK-S expression is higher in the testis than CLK-L expression (Supplemental Fig. S1A-B), CLK binding peak intensity in the testis is the lowest (Supplemental Fig. S3D), suggesting CLK-S does not participate in DNA binding. Second, the higher binding energy between CLK-S and CYC compared to CLK-L/CYC or CYC/CYC dimers (Table 1) makes it unlikely that CLK-S and CYC form a functional regulatory complex. Finally, our Per-luciferase and IP experiments demonstrate that CLK-S modulates target genes through competitive binding with CLK-L (Fig. 3). Taken together, these findings indicate that in the testis—where CLK-S is highly expressed—male fertility-related genes like eya are regulated by CLK-L and CYC rather than by CLK-S alone or a CLK-S/CYC complex.

What we found in this study explains why the Ectopic Clk (e.g. tim-Gal4 > UAS-Clk) leads to lethality [36]. As shown in Figure S2, clock genes are extensively expressed in the male gonad-associated epithelium (including te: Terminal epithelial cells of testis; sv: Seminal vesicle; ep: Male gonad associated epithelium.), which serves as an important supporting tissue for the male gonad. Proper regulation of clock genes and their downstream targets may be essential for the fate of these cells. Our ChIP-seq data reveal that Clk controls many genes critical for spermatogenesis. Overexpressing Clk could lead to the misexpression of key transcriptional regulators, such as eya, among others. Excessive expression of spermatogenesis-related genes (e.g., eya) in the male gonad-associated epithelium may result in developmental defects.

Interestingly, while the Clkout null mutation impaired female fertility, it did not affect fertility in males (Supplementary Fig. 1E, reanalysis of data in Fig. 2C, D, E, F). In contrast, males with germline-specific knockdown of clk RNAi exhibited a significant fertility defect (Fig. 2H). This apparent discrepancy may be explained by robust compensatory biological mechanisms. It is plausible that the circadian network undergoes adaptive rewiring, wherein the loss of CLK protein prompts other transcription factors [38–42] or through network reorganization [43]. Alternatively, spermatogenesis may exhibit intrinsic biological robustness, reliant on redundant pathways that buffer it against the loss of a single circadian regulator. While our standard mating assays revealed no gross defects, more subtle impairments in sperm quality or competitive fitness under stress cannot be ruled out. The acute and partial nature of RNAi-mediated knockdown may fail to induce such adaptation, leading to a more pronounced phenotypic effect. While directly proving the occurrence and exact mechanism of compensation is beyond the scope of this study, it remains a plausible explanation for the differential phenotypes observed between the genetic mutant and the RNAi knockdown. The presence of such a mechanism in male flies highlights the robustness of the circadian system in safeguarding fertility and presents a fascinating area for future investigation.

In vertebrates, such as mice, a short isoform of Clk similar to that found in Drosophila exists (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/XP_036020664.1). This suggests that the mechanism identified in this study may be conserved across vertebrates. However, the specific details of this mechanism require further investigation. Recently circadian rhythm regulation of zebrafish spermatogonial differentiation was reported [44]. Diversified mechanisms may exist in different species.

The case reported in this study aligns with the alternative transcriptional start sites (ATSS) described previously. One outcome of ATSS is the inclusion or exclusion of the 5' upstream open reading frame (uORF) [45]. Specifically, transcripts from the clock locus were found to contain 5' uORFs, which were included in the long form of Clk in this study, while the short form lacks the 5' uORFs. According to a previous study on the role of uORFs in regulating Clk translation [46], the abundance of the short form in the testis suggests the loss of this layer of translational regulation. This loss may contribute to the adaptation of Clk function in the testis.

The upstream regulation of the tissue specific expression of the different isoforms needs further study. We speculate that some splicing factors may be responsible regulators. Due to its sextual dimorphism expression pattern, it’s highly possible that one of the upstream signaling may be the sex determination pathways including sxl etc.

The short form of Clk appears to have distinct functions in the male testis compared to the female ovary. This phenomenon is reminiscent of sexual conflict observed in many evolutionarily new genes in Drosophila [47]. Sex conflict is the evolutionary mechanism than function whenever different values of a shared trait are favored in the two sexes. It is well exampled by the case of Apollo and Artemis. Notably, the short form of Clk is abundant in the testis but scarce in the ovary. More importantly, changes in the levels of different Clk isoforms seems exert opposite effects in the testis and ovary. Whether and how the short form of Clk relates to sex conflict needs further study. It remains an open question whether sexual conflict extends to different transcripts of a single gene, in addition to its occurrence between genes.
