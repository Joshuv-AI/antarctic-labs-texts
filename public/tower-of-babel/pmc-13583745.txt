Low-carbohydrate diet score subtypes and all-cause mortality in general and chronic disease populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies
Authors: Tran Quang Duc, Khanh Nguyen Di
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine associations of overall, healthy and unhealthy low-carbohydrate diet (LCD) scores with all-cause mortality in general and chronic disease populations. Healthy and unhealthy subtypes were compared with assess whether the observed associations depend on macronutrient quality rather than carbohydrate restriction alone.DesignSystematic review and pairwise category meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, MEDLINE, ProQuest Medical Database and Web of Science Core Collection were searched from inception to 12 May 2026.Eligibility criteriaProspective cohort studies of adults assessing LCD adherence using a validated three-macronutrient composite score and reporting HRs for all-cause mortality were eligible.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently extracted data and assessed study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Random-effects meta-analyses compared each higher reported LCD category with the lowest category, stratified by LCD score subtype and population type. Certainty of evidence was assessed using NutriGrade.Results18 prospective cohort studies included 779 158 participants and 219 457 deaths; all scored 7–9/9 on the NOS. In chronic disease populations, the highest healthy LCD category was associated with lower mortality than the lowest category (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.76; I²=0%; high certainty), as was the highest overall LCD category (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.96; I²=68%; high certainty). In the general population, the highest healthy LCD category was not associated with lower mortality than the lowest category (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.01; I²=69%; moderate certainty), and neither was the highest overall LCD category (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.03; I²=87%; low certainty). Unhealthy LCD scores were not associated with mortality in either population.ConclusionsHealthy LCD adherence was associated with lower all-cause mortality, particularly among individuals with chronic diseases. Unhealthy LCD scores were not associated with mortality in either population, suggesting that macronutrient quality and source may matter more than carbohydrate reduction alone.

Introduction

Low-carbohydrate diets (LCDs), characterised by reduced carbohydrate intake with a compensatory increase in protein and fat, have become increasingly popular for weight management and metabolic health improvement. Previous studies have suggested that LCDs may improve short-term cardiometabolic outcomes, including body weight, glycaemic control and insulin resistance.1 However, despite these potential metabolic benefits and their widespread adoption, the long-term association between LCD adherence and all-cause mortality remains uncertain.

Prospective cohort studies have reported inconsistent findings on the association between LCDs and all-cause mortality. Some large-scale cohorts found no association between higher overall LCD scores and mortality,2 3 whereas others reported increased mortality risk,4 5 U-shaped associations or inverse associations.6 7 These discrepancies may partly reflect differences in how LCD adherence was defined and whether macronutrient quality was considered. Some studies constructed LCD scores using only carbohydrate and protein intake,4 5 whereas others incorporated all three major macronutrients: carbohydrate, protein and fat.6 7 Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the health effects of LCDs may depend not only on the degree of carbohydrate restriction but also on the sources and quality of the macronutrients replacing carbohydrates. A ‘healthy’ LCD, characterised by higher intake of plant-based protein, unsaturated fat and high-quality carbohydrates, has been associated with lower mortality.3 8–10 In contrast, an ‘unhealthy’ LCD, characterised by higher intake of animal-based protein, saturated fat and low-quality carbohydrates, has often been associated with higher mortality risk.3 6 10 Thus, evaluating overall LCD adherence without accounting for macronutrient quality may obscure important differences in mortality risk.

Several meta-analyses have examined LCDs in relation to all-cause mortality, but their conclusions remain inconsistent, with reports of increased risk,11 no association12 or the lowest mortality risk at moderate carbohydrate intake levels.13 These inconsistencies may partly reflect methodological differences across studies, particularly in LCD definitions, score construction, comparison categories and study populations. First, because there is no universally accepted definition of LCDs, previous meta-analyses pooled two-macronutrient (carbohydrate and protein) and three-macronutrient (carbohydrate, protein and fat) LCD scores within the same analysis.11–13 Second, most previous meta-analyses focused primarily on overall LCD scores and did not sufficiently distinguish healthy from unhealthy LCD patterns according to macronutrient quality and food sources.11 12 Third, most previous meta-analyses focused mainly on highest-versus-lowest comparisons, leaving mortality risk across intermediate ranges of LCD adherence unclear.11–13 Finally, prior meta-analyses often combined general and chronic disease populations, providing limited evidence on whether the association differs by population type.11–13

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and pairwise category meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies examining the associations of overall, healthy and unhealthy LCD scores with all-cause mortality in general and chronic disease populations. This approach assessed risk patterns across several levels of LCD adherence, beyond the conventional highest-versus-lowest comparison. We also examined energy intake and nutrient patterns across LCD categories. This analysis assessed whether healthy and unhealthy LCD scores captured distinct dietary profiles consistent with their predefined definitions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) reporting recommendations. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261391326), and the review was completed without major deviations from the registered protocol.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed (National Library of Medicine), MEDLINE (Ovid), ProQuest Medical Database (ProQuest) and Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate) from database inception through 12 May 2026, with no restrictions on language or publication date. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian and used free-text keywords related to two core concepts: (1) LCD and (2) mortality outcomes. The full search strings for each database are provided in online supplemental file S1. Reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews identified during screening were additionally hand-searched to capture any studies not retrieved electronically.11–13 All records were imported into Mendeley and deduplicated prior to screening. Two reviewers independently conducted the literature search, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included based on the PICOS framework (online supplemental file S2). Eligible studies were required to meet all of the following criteria: (1) prospective cohort design; (2) inclusion of adults aged ≥18 years from either the general population or a confirmed chronic disease subgroup; (3) assessment of LCD adherence using a validated composite score that incorporated all three macronutrients as percentages of total energy intake, with carbohydrate inversely scored and protein and fat positively scored and (4) reporting of all-cause mortality as the outcome, expressed as a HR with 95% CI from the maximally adjusted model, using the lowest LCD category as the reference group.

Studies were excluded if: (1) participants were children, adolescents, pregnant or lactating women, or non-human; (2) data were insufficient or the article was not original research; (3) the study was non-English or (4) LCD adherence was reported only as a continuous per-unit measure, without a categorical (eg, quantile-based) comparison using a defined lowest-category reference group. Where multiple publications from the same cohort were identified, only the study with the longest follow-up or larger sample size was retained.

Study selection

After duplicates were removed, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. At each stage, inclusion and exclusion decisions were made independently, and disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a predefined data extraction sheet. Extracted items included first author, publication year, country, cohort name, study population, sample size, participant age, follow-up duration, number of deaths, dietary assessment method, energy and macronutrient intakes in the lowest and highest reported categories, LCD score definition, and HRs with 95% CIs for all-cause mortality across all reported LCD score categories (eg, tertiles, quartiles, quintiles or deciles) from the maximally adjusted model. When studies reported multiple LCD score subtypes, data were extracted separately for each subtype, according to the definitions used in the included studies. In the present analysis, plant- or vegetable-based scores were grouped as healthy LCD scores, whereas animal-based or meat-based scores were grouped as unhealthy LCD scores, consistent with their food-source profiles.14 Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by discussion.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies,15 and the certainty of the meta-evidence was evaluated using the NutriGrade scoring system.16 Two reviewers independently performed the assessment, with disagreements resolved by discussion.

Data analysis

All included studies reported HRs with 95% CIs. Multivariable-adjusted estimates were pooled using DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models with inverse-variance weighting. Reported categories were assigned to up to five ordered analysis positions, labelled C1–C5, with C1 consistently representing the lowest category. The single tertile study contributed to C1–C3, quartile studies to C1–C4, and quintile studies to C1–C5. Each decile study contributed two effect-estimate series to the positional analyses, both using D1 as the reference: D2/D4/D6/D8 and D3/D5/D7/D9 were mapped to C2–C5, respectively. D10 versus D1 was analysed separately as the highest-versus-lowest comparison. This approach retained all reported category-specific HRs and their variances without interpolation or recalculation while preserving the ordinal structure of the exposure categories.

Separate pairwise meta-analyses compared each available higher position with C1. The highest versus lowest reported category was analysed separately. Because the studies used different category structures, C2–C5 do not represent equivalent original categories or common percentile ranges across studies. Original category formats are reported in online supplemental file S4. This approach was selected instead of pooling a continuous LCD score because score units and distributions were not sufficiently comparable across cohorts.

The symbol k denotes the number of effect estimates contributing to a pooled result. Heterogeneity was tested with Cochran’s Q (p<0.10) and quantified using I². Values of approximately 25%, 50% and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Leave-one-out analyses assessed the influence of individual estimates on pooled effects and I². Analyses were stratified a priori by LCD score type (overall, healthy and unhealthy) and population type (general vs chronic disease).

Subgroup analyses were performed for the highest-versus-lowest LCD score comparison. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, stratified by: (1) geographic region (USA vs Asian countries); (2) disease subgroup (disease subgroup (type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), pre–type 2 diabetes mellitus (pre-T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, cancer)); (3) follow-up duration (<10 years vs ≥10 years); (4) sample size (<30 000 vs ≥ 30 000); (5) mean age at baseline (≤60 years vs >60 years) and (6) dietary assessment method (Fod Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) vs other methods). Publication bias was assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test; the trim-and-fill method was applied where significant asymmetry was detected.

To characterise dietary profiles by LCD score type, energy and macronutrient intakes at the lowest and highest LCD categories were extracted and summarised across studies. Eleven nutrients were assessed: total energy (kcal/day); total carbohydrate (including high-quality and low-quality); total protein (animal and plant); total fat, saturated fat, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), expressed as % energy (%E). Summary values are presented as mean±SD, and differences between the lowest and highest categories were evaluated using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

All analyses were conducted using R V.4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio version 2026.04.0+526 (Posit Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA), using the meta, metafor and ggplot2 packages. A two-tailed p<0.05 was considered significant, unless otherwise specified.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in developing the research question or in the design, conduct, interpretation or reporting of this review.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The electronic database search identified 1592 records across four databases. After removal of 778 duplicates, 814 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 41 were assessed in full text for eligibility. Following full-text review, 18 prospective cohort studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final meta-analysis (online supplemental file S3).

Study characteristics

Detailed characteristics are presented in online supplemental file S4. The 18 included prospective cohort studies, published between 2014 and 2025, comprised 779 158 participants and 219 457 deaths. 13 studies were conducted in the USA,2 6 8–10 17–24 3 in Japan,7 25 26 and 2 in China,3 27 with follow-up durations ranging from 2 to 34 years. Nine studies enrolled general population samples,2 3 6 7 18 21 26 27 while nine included participants with chronic diseases at baseline, including T2DM,8 9 prediabetes,8 10 hypertension,17 CVD,20 COPD,22 myocardial infarction19 and cancer.23 24 Baseline age ranged from 49.72 to 67.3 years20 across studies reporting mean or median age. Among general population studies, the reported proportion of participants with diabetes or pre-diabetes ranged from 3.6%7 26 to 17.5%.2 Dietary intake was assessed using FFQs in 11 studies,3 6–8 18 19 21 23 24 26 27 24-hour dietary recalls in 6 studies2 9 10 17 20 22 and a 3-day weighed food record in one study.25 LCD adherence was categorised into quartiles in eight studies, quintiles in six, deciles in three and tertiles in one.

LCD score construction and subtype definitions across included studies

The full assessment of LCD definitions is provided in online supplemental file S5. All LCD scores were derived from a three-macronutrient composite framework (carbohydrate inversely scored; protein and fat positively scored; range, 0–30). Beyond the overall score, most studies reported at least one subtype reflecting macronutrient quality or source, while one reported the overall score only.18 Some studies defined healthy and unhealthy scores using both carbohydrate quality and protein/fat source; others reported plant-based or vegetable-based and animal-based or meat-based subtypes based primarily on macronutrient source.7 18 19 21 23–26 Definitions varied modestly but generally classified whole grains, fruits, legumes and non-starchy vegetables as high-quality carbohydrates, and refined grains, added sugars and starchy vegetables as low-quality carbohydrates.

Dietary composition underlying LCD scores

Figure 1 and online supplemental file S6 compare energy intake and macronutrient composition between the lowest and highest LCD categories across overall, healthy and unhealthy LCD scores, using data extracted from all included studies. Across all LCD scores, the highest LCD category had markedly lower total carbohydrate intake and higher total protein and total fat intake than the lowest LCD category, confirming that higher LCD scores reflected LCD patterns. However, the quality of macronutrient replacement differed by LCD subtype. For the healthy LCD score, the highest category was characterised by lower low-quality carbohydrate intake, higher plant protein intake and a more favourable fat composition, including higher PUFA and MUFA intake. In contrast, the highest unhealthy LCD category showed higher animal protein, saturated fat and total fat intake, with lower high-quality carbohydrate intake. Overall, these results support that healthy and unhealthy LCD scores captured distinct dietary profiles, while both represented reduced carbohydrate intake.

Sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessment

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted for all three LCD score types across all reported-category comparisons (online supplemental file S7). Pooled estimates remained materially unchanged after sequential exclusion of one effect estimate. This supports the stability of the results but does not remove the limitations of observational evidence or between-study heterogeneity.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot visual inspection and Egger’s test across all reported-category comparisons for each LCD score type and population subgroup (online supplemental file S8). For overall and healthy LCD scores, no significant asymmetry was detected in chronic disease populations across most comparisons. Exceptions were C5 versus C1 for the healthy LCD score (p=0.04) and the highest-versus-lowest, C3-versus-C1 and C5-versus-C1 comparisons for the overall LCD score in the general population (all p≤0.004). For the unhealthy LCD score, significant asymmetry was observed in chronic disease populations across several comparisons, from the highest-versus-lowest through C4-versus-C1 analyses (all p≤0.01). In contrast, no significant asymmetry was observed in the general population (all p≥0.50). Where asymmetry was identified, trim-and-fill analyses did not materially alter pooled estimates. Publication bias nevertheless remains possible because several analyses included few effect estimates.

Subgroup analyses

For the overall LCD score (online supplemental file S9), heterogeneity was reduced in T2DM and pre-T2DM strata and was lower among younger participants and studies using dietary recalls, but remained high in CVD, cancer, older-participant and FFQ strata. In the general population, heterogeneity was lower in Asian cohorts and in studies with shorter follow-up, smaller samples, younger participants or recall-based dietary assessment, but remained high in US cohorts and in longer-follow-up, larger-sample, older-participant and FFQ strata.

For the healthy LCD score (online supplemental file S10), subgroup heterogeneity was generally low in chronic disease populations, supporting the consistency of the association. In the general population, heterogeneity was lower among younger participants but remained moderate or high in most other strata, including US cohorts, older participants and larger samples.

For the unhealthy LCD score (online supplemental file S11), heterogeneity remained moderate to high in most subgroups and was reduced to 0% only in selected strata, including chronic disease studies with at least 10 years of follow-up and general-population studies with samples below 30 000. The latter subgroup also differed in direction from larger studies (HR 0.92 vs 1.14).

Study quality and NutriGrade certainty of evidence

As shown in online supplemental file S12, nine studies were rated 9/9, seven were rated 8/9 and two were rated 7/9. Downgrades most commonly reflected limited cohort representativeness and unclear reporting of follow-up completeness.

The certainty of evidence varied across LCD score types, populations and reported-category comparisons (online supplemental file S13). For the highest-versus-lowest comparison, certainty was high for healthy and overall LCD scores in chronic disease populations. It was moderate for the healthy LCD score in the general population. It was low for the overall LCD score in the general population and for unhealthy LCD scores in both populations. Across positional comparisons, certainty ranged from high to moderate for healthy LCD scores and from moderate to low for unhealthy LCD scores. Lower ratings reflected imprecision, heterogeneity, possible publication bias and the absence of a consistent gradient.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest and most comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to date on LCDs and all-cause mortality in adults. It provides three key findings. First, the healthy and unhealthy LCD scores captured distinct dietary profiles. The healthy LCD score reflected lower intake of low-quality carbohydrates and higher intake of plant-based protein and unsaturated fat. The unhealthy LCD score reflected lower intake of high-quality carbohydrates and higher intake of animal protein and saturated fat. Second, healthy LCD estimates were generally inverse across the ordered reported-category comparisons in both populations. The association was stronger in chronic disease populations. Third, for the overall LCD score, inverse associations were observed for C2–C4 versus C1 in both populations. However, the highest-versus-lowest comparison was significant only in chronic disease populations. In contrast, unhealthy LCD adherence was not significantly associated with lower mortality in either population.

Although the healthy and unhealthy LCD subtypes achieved comparable total carbohydrate reduction in the highest reported category (online supplemental file S6), their divergent associations with mortality suggest that macronutrient replacement quality, rather than carbohydrate quantity, may be an important contributor to differences in LCD-associated mortality. Several biological mechanisms may underlie the protective association of the healthy LCD score. First, reducing low-quality carbohydrate intake may attenuate postprandial hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress and inflammation. In healthy humans, acute elevation of blood glucose rapidly increased circulating IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-18 within 2 hours through an oxidative stress-dependent mechanism.28 In parallel, coadministration of the antioxidant glutathione abolished these cytokine increases, establishing a direct causal role of postprandial hyperglycaemia in driving systemic vascular inflammation.28 Elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-18 have each been independently associated with higher all-cause mortality in prospective cohort studies.29–31 Second, preservation of high-quality carbohydrate sources maintains dietary fibre intake, which is fermented by colonic microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids, mainly propionate. In an RCT of 60 overweight adults, propionate supplementation significantly reduced hepatic lipid accumulation and abdominal adiposity after 24 weeks,32 both of which are established risk factors for mortality.33 34 Consistently, higher dietary fibre intake was associated with a 23% lower risk of all-cause mortality in a recent updated meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies including 3.5 million participants.35 Third, substitution towards plant protein and unsaturated fat reduces low-density lipoprotei (LDL) cholesterol, triglycerides and systemic inflammatory biomarkers, each independently associated with mortality. In a meta-analysis of 112 RCTs in 5774 adults, substituting plant protein for animal protein significantly reduced LDL cholesterol by 4%,36 and elevated LDL cholesterol is a well-established causal risk factor for mortality.37 In a large prospective cohort of 3.8 million adults, elevated triglycerides were independently associated with higher all-cause mortality across the full range of triglyceride concentrations.38 Similarly, in a nationwide prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, elevated hs-CRP was independently associated with higher all-cause mortality, with a causal relationship further supported by Mendelian randomisation.39 Consistent with this evidence, plant protein intake was associated with 8% lower all-cause mortality in a pooled meta-analysis of 32 prospective cohort studies,40 and isocaloric replacement of 5% of energy from saturated fat with plant polyunsaturated fat was associated with 15% lower all-cause mortality among 521 120 participants followed for 16 years.41

Conversely, the unhealthy LCD score may remove these protective dietary components while promoting adverse biological pathways through two principal mechanisms. First, higher animal protein intake, particularly from red and processed meat, may increase gut microbiota-mediated conversion of dietary L-carnitine to trimethylamine, which is subsequently oxidised in the liver to trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). In murine experiments, chronic L-carnitine supplementation increased TMAO synthesis, impaired reverse cholesterol transport, and promoted atherosclerotic lesion formation through a microbiota-dependent mechanism.42 Consistently, among 2595 adults undergoing cardiac evaluation, omnivores produced significantly more TMAO than vegans after L-carnitine ingestion, and plasma TMAO independently predicted incident major adverse cardiovascular events.42 In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort, higher plasma TMAO levels were prospectively associated with a 1.12-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality over 16.9 years of follow-up.43 Second, higher saturated fat intake may suppress hepatic LDL receptor expression, thereby reducing LDL clearance and increasing circulating LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B. In a controlled feeding experiment, palmitic acid reduced hepatic LDL receptor protein by 40% and mRNA by 60% compared with linoleic acid.44 In the DELTA randomised crossover trial of 25 healthy adults, reducing dietary saturated fat was associated with an 11.8% reduction in LDL cholesterol, partly through significant upregulation of LDL receptor abundance.45 Consistent with this, higher saturated fat intake was independently associated with 8% higher all-cause mortality among 1 164 029 participants in prospective cohort evidence.46

Our quality assessment supported a reasonably robust evidence base, although limited representativeness, incomplete follow-up reporting, and the observational design temper causal interpretation (online supplemental file S12). Confidence was greatest for the healthy LCD association in chronic disease populations because the primary analysis showed a consistent inverse association with no observed heterogeneity, and heterogeneity remained generally low across subgroups (figure 3 and online supplemental file S10). This comparison was also rated as high-certainty evidence using NutriGrade (online supplemental file S13). By contrast, heterogeneity for overall and unhealthy LCD scores, particularly in general populations, likely reflects genuine differences in what a higher LCD score represented across cohorts. For example, the large US National Institutes of Health-AARP (NIH–AARP) cohort associated higher overall and unhealthy LCD scores with increased mortality but found a modest inverse association for the healthy LCD score,6 whereas the Guangzhou cohort of older Asian adults reported higher mortality with the vegetable-based score and lower mortality with the meat-based score.3 These contrasting findings may partly reflect differences in background macronutrient intake, replacement food sources and the interpretation of cohort-relative score rankings between Western and Asian dietary settings. Population composition may also contribute, as MESA found no association in the overall cohort but observed lower mortality at moderate LCD levels specifically among Hispanic participants.21 Exposure assessment also ranged from one or two 24-hour recalls10 to repeated postdiagnostic FFQs capable of capturing dietary changes over time,8 resulting in different degrees of measurement error and exposure classification. Thus, although subgroup analyses reduced heterogeneity in selected strata, they did not identify a single consistent explanation.

To our knowledge, no prior study has directly compared LCD score-associated mortality responses between chronic disease and general population groups within a single analytical framework. The stronger inverse association observed for healthy and overall LCD scores in chronic disease populations may be explained by several factors. First, individuals with established chronic diseases, such as T2D and CVD, have a higher baseline metabolic burden, including insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and dyslipidaemia. In these high-risk conditions, the macronutrient substitutions captured by the healthy LCD score may more directly target key pathophysiological mechanisms, potentially leading to greater mortality reductions than in metabolically healthier individuals with preserved metabolic reserve. Second, regular medical follow-up and structured disease management may promote greater and more sustained dietary adherence in chronic disease populations, thereby strengthening the observed inverse association. In a prospective study of adults with established CVD risk factors, structured dietary programmes that included regular follow-up and motivational support were associated with a 1.7% absolute reduction in all-cause mortality.47 However, given the limited evidence, further studies are needed to clarify whether this stronger association is driven by greater metabolic responsiveness, improved adherence, disease-specific clinical management or their combined effects.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this meta-analysis include the exclusive use of a three-macronutrient composite LCD scoring framework across all included studies, which improved methodological consistency and comparability across cohorts. A priori stratification by LCD subtype and population type allowed more precise evaluation of differential mortality associations. Reported-category analyses assessed risk patterns beyond the highest-versus-lowest comparison. NutriGrade also provided a structured assessment of evidence certainty.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because all included studies were observational, causal inference cannot be drawn from the pooled estimates, and residual confounding cannot be eliminated through meta-analytic pooling. Second, dietary intake was assessed using FFQs or 24-hour dietary recalls, generally at a single time point. Both methods are subject to measurement error, which may introduce regression dilution bias and attenuate diet–mortality associations toward the null, potentially underestimating the true magnitude of effect. Third, category boundaries differed across studies. The C2–C5 comparisons therefore represent ordered reported categories rather than common exposure intervals. Fourth, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed for several overall and unhealthy LCD analyses. Subgroup analyses reduced I² in some strata, but no factor consistently explained it. This limits the precision of these pooled estimates and warrants cautious interpretation. Fifth, 13 of the 18 studies were conducted in the USA. Only five were conducted in Asia, and no studies represented other regions. This may limit global generalisability. Sixth, few studies contributed to some reported-category comparisons and disease subgroups. Evidence was particularly limited for COPD and cancer. These estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Seventh, the food groups classified as high-quality or low-quality carbohydrates varied across studies. This may introduce residual heterogeneity despite the generally consistent scoring framework. Finally, the composite LCD scoring framework assigns equal weight to broad dietary components. It does not distinguish their specific food sources or nutritional properties. For example, protein from legumes and nuts, or MUFA from olive oil and animal sources, may have different health effects. This approach may mask source-specific associations with mortality risk.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that greater adherence to a healthy LCD pattern was associated with lower all-cause mortality, with a stronger association observed among individuals with chronic diseases. In contrast, unhealthy LCD scores were not significantly associated with mortality in either the general population or chronic disease populations. These observational findings suggest that the health implications of LCDs may depend on carbohydrate quality and the sources of replacement protein and fat, rather than on carbohydrate restriction alone. Further prospective studies should use repeated dietary assessments and standardised score definitions.
