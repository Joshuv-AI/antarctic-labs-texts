Artificial intelligence in cytopathological applications for cancer: a review of accuracy and analytic validity
Authors: Priya Hays
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCytopathological examination serves as a tool for diagnosing solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted methods have been widely discussed in the literature for increasing sensitivity, specificity and accuracy in the diagnosis of cytopathological clinical samples. Many of these tools are also used in clinical practice. There is a growing body of literature describing the role of AI in clinical settings, particularly in improving diagnostic accuracy and providing predictive and prognostic insights.MethodsA comprehensive search for this systematic review was conducted using databases Google, PUBMED (n = 450) and Google Scholar (n = 1067) with the keywords “Artificial Intelligence” AND “cytopathological” and “fine needle aspiration” AND “Deep Learning” AND “Machine Learning” AND “Hematologic Disorders” AND “Lung Cancer” AND “Pap Smear” and “cervical cancer screening” AND “Thyroid Cancer” AND “Breast Cancer” and “Sensitivity” and “Specificity”. The search focused on literature reviews and systematic reviews published in English language between 2020 and 2024. PRISMA guidelines were adhered to with studies included and excluded as depicted in a flowchart. 417 results were screened with 34 studies were chosen for this review.ResultsIn the screening of patients with cervical cancer, bone marrow and peripheral blood smears and benign and malignant lesions in the lung, AI-assisted methods, particularly machine learning and deep learning (a subset of machine learning) methods, were applied to cytopathological data. These methods yielded greater diagnostic accuracy, specificity and sensitivity and decreased interobserver variability. Data sets were collected for both training and validation. Human machine combined performance was also found to be comparable to standalone performance in comparison with medical performance as well.ConclusionsThe use of AI in the analysis of cytopathological samples in research and clinical settings is increasing, and the involvement of pathologists in AI workflows is becoming increasingly important.

Introduction

Cytology, the study of the structure and function of cells and its components is considered an integral component of diagnostic pathology, and is part of the workflow in the diagnosis of many cancers. Cytopathological examination is an important component of cancer pathology and diagnosis. Cytopathology has markedly advanced in gynecological applications, such as detecting cervical cancer through Papanicolaou (Pap) smears, and nongynecological applications, such as detecting lung cancer, through bronchial aspirations and exfoliative methods, and thyroid malignancy applications, such as fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC).

Artificial intelligence is a new innovation that analyzes huge reservoirs of data to come with information on patterns inherent in that data reservoir and is increasingly being used in healthcare settings. Big data, which forms one of the foundation of artificial intelligence, is now being applied to cytopathology analysis to increase accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of whole slide imaging.

Histopathology, another area of pathological diagnostics, is the study of tissue samples from various organs, such as the liver, spleen, skin, and lymph nodes, to examine and identify any abnormalities or diseases present. Much like investigations into cytopathology, histopathology has also been advanced by digital methods. In one review on histopathology, bioinformatics was used to analyze unknown histopathological samples, finding the original source of the cancer [1]. A systematic review was also performed on the literature surrounding on artificial intelligence of ovarian cancer histopathological samples, and a number of AI models was revealed for real-world implementation [2].

Digital pathology has undergone major development recently, particularly with regards to the analysis of whole slide imaging. Practical guides to whole slide imaging have been reported that present immediate applications of whole slide imaging in pathology practice, discussing in depth how whole slide imaging encompasses the digitization of histology slides [3]. Studies have been conducted on validation of breast and gynecological pathology in which improvements in intra-observer variability have been shown. Potential benefits and pitfalls have been elaborated in the literature including higher intra-observer concordance rates between whole slide imaging and routine light microscopy, overall reporting a mean sensitivity of 96.3% (CI 94.1–97.7) and mean specificity of 93.3% (CI 90.5–95.4). Whole slide imaging cytopathology reflected these advances. One example of digital examination of cytopathology has been developed by Caputo et al. in an international, multi-institutional study called the Sydney system which improved the validation of digital pathology applications to routine cytopathology by addressing reproducibility and standardization of lymph node cytopathology [4].

Z-stack and scanning are also important for obtaining diagnostic accuracy of WSIs. Z‑stack enables “pathologists to use a software focus adjustment, comparable to the fine‑focus knob of a conventional light microscope” [5], through viewing software enables the user to navigate, zoom up and down the different planes to detect the three-dimensional regions in focus, an example being OptraScan. Another digital technology applicable to cytology is scanning, an example being the application Panoptiq, a microscope-based scanning software through which the operator can combine low-power panoramic digital images with z-stacks at regions of interest with a much smaller image size than that obtained by whole-slide scanning, and was compared to conventional light microscopy in one study [6].

Artificial intelligence (AI) has increased the analytical validity of traditional cytopathological techniques, increasing diagnostic accuracy and overall sensitivity, leading to more robust clinical efficacy. AI methods are mainly separated into machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods. Deep learning methods include convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and artificial neural networks (ANNs).

Yang et al. provided an outstanding introduction to AI-based cytopathology, including the cytology fundamentals and AI foundations in medical applications. Cytopathology involves the microscopic examination of cells collected individually from solid organs such as the lungs, thyroid and cervix and pleural and serous effusions. This is usually achieved through FNAC or exfoliative brushing and scraping methods. The collected samples are then stained to determine their cellular features, making structures such as the nucleus and other cellular components more visible [7, 8].

Cytopathology plays a major role in cancer diagnosis and prognosis and recurrence prediction. However, the visual examination of cellular morphology involved in these analyses makes the process relatively subjective and prone to variability and diagnostic errors in cytopathological interpretations. Detecting cellular abnormalities and dysplasia requires technological expertise and clinical experience, which may not always be available. In addition, tumor samples may have low cell volumes, limiting diagnostic accuracy and leading to less than optimal sensitivity and specificity.

AI methods, through algorithmic and statistical models, identifies patterns in cytological specimens and make predictions that aid in decision-making. Cytological images can be analyzed, and abnormalities among cellular features can be detected. By leveraging AI methods, more efficient and accurate decision-making can be realized, leading to accurate and personalized diagnoses [7–10].

Huang et al. [11] summarized the characteristics of and relationships between ML and DL methods. They highlighted that ML is a subset of AI, and DL is a subset of ML that employs artificial networks such as CNNs and ANNs to “enable more complex learning” processes. These methods are becoming relevant in cytopathology, particularly in cancer diagnosis, because through ML approaches, large amounts of data can be used for training, and specific tasks can be optimized for algorithmic learning. Through DL methods, independent learning is achieved, and several features can be extracted, selected and classified. AI methods can add to the inherent advantages of cytopathological examination, such as cost-efficiency and speed, while, addressing challenges such as labor intensity, time consumption and low diagnostic accuracy in interobserver variability [12].

For example, AI methods can increase the sensitivity and specificity of lung cancer diagnosis, which traditionally exhibits low diagnostic performance in cytopathology. The sensitivity ranges from 0.49 to 0.71 for exfoliative sputum cytology and from 0.43 to 0.59 for abrasive cytology obtained from bronchoscopy [13–15].

Results

A recent systematic review was conducted to analyze 26 studies focused on AI applications for cytopathological samples from nongynecological cases. Thakur et al. reported several malignances for which cytopathological samples, including thyroid, bladder, lung, breast, pancreas, ovary and prostate samples, were analyzed by AI approaches. For papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTCA), benign thyroid nodules (BTNs), follicular adenoma (FA), and follicular carcinoma (FC), DL models achieved accuracies of close to 100% in an analysis of 908 whole-slide images (WSIs). For breast cytopathology, an ANN model was used to analyze 112 image patches, distinguishing between fibroadenoma or invasive ductal carcinoma on the basis of nuclear morphology. This model achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity [16].

Wu et al. trained an AlexNet model using 85 WSIs from FNAC and classified histological subtypes, such as serous carcinoma, mucinous carcinoma, endometrioid carcinoma, and clear cell carcinoma, with an accuracy of 78.20% [17]. For pancreas and prostate FNAC classifications, slightly lower accuracy and sensitivity values, each close to 80%, were reported. These methods address the limitations of cytological sampling, which, despite being cost-effective and rapid, can suffer from reduced diagnostic accuracy. According to Thakur et al., cytology offers advantages over histology in terms of cell clustering and nuclear segmentation; however, these features pose challenges for AI modeling due to the difficulty in annotation.

In another study, 81,000 cervical cytopathological smear samples were collected from major medical centers for the annotation of more than 1.7 million images. An Xception classification model was employed for feature extraction, followed by a logical decision tree to fit the extracted features. An AI-assistive diagnostic solution for the Bethesda system named AIATBS was introduced to detect and classify digital cervical liquid-based cytopathology (LBC) smear that allows rapid analysis of < 180 s/smear and has high specificity (82.14%) and good sensitivity (> 83.00%). According to the authors, this system is adaptable to different staining, scanning and sample preparation methods [18].

Cervical cancer cytopathology and AI

The population screening for cervical cancer relies primarily on cytology. A typical diagnosis is made by a cytopathologist through a traditional cytology smear. However, this manual process is prone to variability, laborious and susceptible errors, often leading to high false-positive and false-negative rates, which leads to relatively low diagnostic accuracy [19, 20]. In addition to these obstacles, there is a shortage of cytopathologists needed for effective large-scale screening.

Tang et al. developed an AI microscope (Sunnyoptic) based on a conventional microscope, integrated with a computer-aided system for diagnosing cervical cancer specimens. This system includes an augmented reality (AR) add-on in the eyepiece and a camera that captures high-resolution images in the field of view. The DL object-detection network is RetinaNet, which is composed of a CNN to identify regions of interest [20, 21]. RetinaNet was trained on annotated data sets in a supervised manner via a backpropagation algorithm. When cytopathologists analyzed samples at 10 × magnification, the AI algorithm was able to classify cervical abnormalities through a binary classification system. The effectiveness of the algorithm was verified and validated through interrater agreement during rounds of annotation. The sensitivity and precision were 0.996 and 0.254, respectively, for abnormal cervical cell detection [20].

Other AI-assisted diagnostic systems for population-based cervical cancer screening have been introduced. Bao et al. conducted a prospective cohort study involving 700,000 women using a validated system that resulted in a 5.8% increase in sensitivity compared with that of traditional reading methods [22]. Wang. et al conducted a similar study with an established DL-based cervical diagnosis system for high squamous intraepithelial lesions. This system achieved an accuracy of 0.93 [23]. Zhu et al. developed an AIATBS that showed greater sensitivity in detecting cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) than manual reading by senior cytologists did, with a value of 94.74% through prospective clinical validation [24, 25].

Bone marrow cytopathology and AI

Bone marrow cytopathology is commonly employed for diagnosing hematologic malignances, assessing disease progression and guiding optimal therapeutic options. Digital health is starting to play a role in the automated processing of WSIs when analyzing peripheral blood and bone marrow samples to determine cell counts and morphological features. In one study, AI approaches were applied to diagnosis acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), a type of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and a single-cell DL algorithm was used to detect the morphological characteristics of APL. Eckhardt et al. used a CNN-based model to characterize AML in bone marrow smears and predict NPM1 mutational status. Their model accurately predicted NPM1 mutation with an accuracy of 0.86. This prediction was associated with condensed chromatin and perinuclear lightening dysplasia patterns [26].

Bone marrow smears from AML patients were also analyzed using AI-based models to evaluate dysplasia. These models have predictive value through the detection of fewer granules. Two cytopathologists used these AI-based measures to assess the degree of dysplasia on a four-point scale. A dysplasia grade (DG) prediction score was calculated through regression analysis, yielding a positive predictive value (PPV), specificity, sensitivity, accuracy and negative predictive value (NPV) of 76.3%, 97.7%, 91.0%, 97.2% and 99.3%, respectively. After excluding the lowest dysplasia grade (DG1), the PPV, specificity, sensitivity, accuracy and NPV improved to 80.6%, 98.9%, 85.2%, 98.2% and 99.2%, respectively.

Gedefaw et al. [26] reviewed AI-assisted methods for the diagnostic efficacy of peripheral blood smears (PBS) and bone marrow cytology. AI has the “potential to revolutionize the field of hematology” through better clinical outcomes. ML and DL models analyze large sets of data from hematology cytopathological images of peripheral blood smears, bone marrow aspirates and lymph node biopsies to aid in diagnosis.

Current AI applications in hematologic cytopathology include CellaVision and Morphogo to examine PBS and bone marrow aspirate (BMA) slides, respectively. CellaVision, or DM96, uses an ANN to identify blasts, with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 94%. Similar results are achieved for the identification of white blood cells and nucleated red blood cells, with sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 97%, respectively. This system also allows for remote consultation, review and quality control on a cell-by-cell basis [26, 27]. However, CellaVision cannot differentiate between myeloblasts, lymphoblasts, and monoblasts [26].

Morphogo, a 27-layer CNN AI system for BMA smear analysis captures multiple high-resolution images and differentiates between diverse sets of nucleated cells. By using image techniques such as sharpening, blurring and detecting edges, the convolution filter of the CNN increases the overall quality of the cell cluster image by manipulating specific cell characteristics [26–28]. Fu et al. applied Morphogo and reported a classification accuracy of 85.7–91% in analyzing 230 cases of hematologic lineage cells, with average sensitivity and specificity of 69.4% and 97.2%, respectively. [29, 30]

Similarly, Lin et al. reported that Morphogo exhibited accuracy and specificity of 82% and 91%, respectively, compared with those of cytopathologists in characterizing metastatic tumor cells. [27] Morphogo was also able to differentiate “challenging metastatic nonhematopoietic tumor cells in BMSs”, such as metastatic cancer cells, with 82.2% accuracy and 91.3% specificity.

The study revealed that this AI system was reliable (reliability coefficient of 0.827; area under the curve (AUC) of 0.865) [31]. Additionally, another investigator used bone marrow digital images with CNNs that were able to identify megakaryocytes with sensitivity and specificity of 96.6% and 89.7%, respectively [32]. The authors concluded that “[c]onsequently, it is thought that Morphogo has potential as an AI tool for BMS analysis in the future, possibly negating the need for additional analyses such as flow cytometry and molecular analysis” [33].

Scopio, an advanced computational photography imaging system, uses a browser-based application that received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for producing high-quality hematology digital morphology images through its analysis of full-field peripheral blood smears, “with an accuracy of 96.3% and a sensitivity and specificity of 87.9% and 97.6%, respectively, compared to manual blood smear analysis.” Gedefaw et al. identified a study conducted by Li et al., who used multiple CNNs to classify pathologic images of human diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) from non-DLBCL, resulting in an accuracy of 100% [34].

Lung cancer cytopathology and AI

Pei et al. discussed the effects of an artificial intelligence cytopathological diagnosis system in lung cancer diagnosis through further optimization of rapid and straightforward cytopathology, providing a strong basis for early diagnosis and treatment [35]. They cited a study that “developed an automatic pathological classification model of lung cancer cells in microscopy images using a deep convolutional neural network (DCNN).” The authors referred to this network as antagonistic and adversarial, or a generative adversarial network (GAN) that automatically generates cytological images. In combination with real-world generation of diagnostic lung cytopathology, the classification accuracy increased by 4.3% to 85.3%. The authors concluded “[t]he above stud[y] indicates that the AI cytopathological diagnosis system can classify lung cancer cells and effectively improve the efficiency of the cytopathological diagnosis of lung cancer.” [36].

In lung cancer diagnosis, bronchoscopy is frequently used to evaluate nodules. Rapid onsite cytological evaluation (ROSE) can provide adequate and accurate sample confirmation, but it is limited by time and personnel resources. Ai et al. constructed an AI-based ROSE model using DL-CNN for assisting in the diagnosis of lung cytopathological images and classifying them as benign or malignant. In their study, 627 patients with ROSE slides were enrolled, with 374 randomly assigned to the training group and 91 patients randomly assigned to the validation groups. The AI-ROSE results were compared with the results of junior and senior cytopathologists who conducted a real-world comprehensive diagnosis, which is considered the gold standard. “The accuracies achieved by one senior cytopathologist, two junior cytopathologists and the AI-based ROSE model were 96.90%, 83.30%, and 84.57%, respectively.” [37].

The investigators presented a case study to confirm the application of AI in rapid onsite cytopathology.

“A 73-year-old male whose PET image suggested the possibility of a malignant tumor in the upper lobe of the right lung. Transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) with ROSE was conducted, and histopathology combined with immunohistochemistry yielded a clear diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma. The onsite junior cytopathologist judged the ROSE slide as benign; the senior cytopathologists judged it as malignant in the final report. The AI model recognized 61 malignant patches from the entire WSI with 1312 patches and classified the WSI as malignant.”

However, the authors concluded that the potential limitations are that only a small number of metastatic lung cancer cases were included, resulting in a relatively small data set, and that future studies that require more data sets for performance and application optimization are needed. Another limitation is that only a small number of metastatic lung cancer and sarcoidosis cases were included in the data set, and a data set with more subtypes should be used for training to optimize the performance and application in clinical settings [38].

Kim et al. conducted a retrospective, multicenter study utilizing a DL model in the cytopathological analysis of lung samples from quality control centers in 200 institutions, resulting in greater lung diagnostic accuracy. A total of 557 benign lesion and 715 lung cancer cytopathological images were used for training and validation, respectively. The WSIs were divided into lung patches for training, validation and testing. The DenseNet121 model was used with six CNNs for analysis. The AI model outperformed experienced senior pathologists, with sensitivity, specificity and accuracy values of 95.9%, 98.2% and 96.9%, respectively. Interobserver variability was also mitigated, with greater diagnostic performance. The student pathologist achieved sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy values of 71.5%, 86.5%, and 77.4%, respectively, without AI assistance; however, these values increased to 83.1%, 82.7% and 82.9%, respectively, with AI assistance. Similarly, there was an increase as a result of AI assistance with experienced pathologists (83.1% vs. 95.2%; 82.7% vs. 96.8%; and 82.9% vs. 95.9%).

Different sensitivities and specificities were also observed for three experienced pathologists. These variations could result in false positives and false negatives. However, the use of AI tools could greatly improve these variations. Specificity and sensitivity improvements could lead to “enhancing the weaker part of each pathologist.” The authors concluded that “[o]ne of the important lessons from the results of this study is that AI will be a potential augmentation tool for human pathologists by enhancing and adjusting the imbalanced performance of each observer.” [13].

Standalone performance compared to human–machine combined performance

AI-driven applications in research and healthcare have led to rapid advances in enhanced diagnosis of cancer and research, and may lead to breakthroughs in cost reduction, however in the previous studies, medical performance was not compared to machine standalone performance. However, in some studies it has been reported that the standalone performance models have an accuracy comparable to trained medical professionals such as a Google platform that developed a model for detecting diabetic retinopathy. Excellent performance has also been detected in breast cancer with deep learning techniques of mammography and digital breast samples when assessed on extensive data sets.

Wang et al. proposed an artificial intelligence assistance cervical cancer screening model (AICCS) as result of the growing need for precision medicine, whereby deep learning was preferred over automated intelligence medical diagnosis. To advance early cervical cancer screening, particularly in detecting abnormal or malignant cells in a cervical thin liquid-based test, an Artificial Intelligence Cervical Cancer Screening (AICCS) system as developed. In this study, medical performance, standalone performance and human machine combined performance were quantitatively assessed and compared.

As referred to earlier, manual inspection of cytopathological samples is time-consuming and draining for cytopathologists, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis and decreasing medical performance standards. A human-machine combined model with AI technology was also developed that leveraged the expertise of experienced cytopathologists. developed an human–machine combined model with AI technology leveraged with the expertise of experienced cytopathologists. A deep-learning neural network was trained with well-annotated whole slide images in conventional medical practice, and subsequent management protocols that resulted vary based on the classification of cervical cytology grades. For cases diagnosed as negative for no intraepithelial lesions, no additional investigative procedures were necessitated.

“For cases classified as negative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy (NILM), no additional investigative procedures were warranted. However, for cytological results categorized as low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) or higher, colposcopy is recommended. In instances where atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US) are detected, human papillomavirus (HPV) testing is advised. A negative HPV test suggests a follow-up cervical cytology screening after 1 year. While a positive result necessitates colposcopy. Based on these stratified protocols, subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the efficacy and outcomes of the AICCS system.”

The impact of diagnostic performance in cytopathologists with the assistance of AICC was prospectively validated in a data set. Discussion

AI approaches are actively being implemented in clinical workflows, increasing both efficiency and replicability in diagnostic analysis through their ability to analyze a large amount of data. This is particularly evident in cancer-related histopathology and cytopathology, which are the primary focus of this paper. AI-assisted models have demonstrated their use in cervical cancer screening, lung cancer diagnosis, and hematologic slides of bone marrow and peripheral blood. Machine learning and deep learning methods are able to assess meaningful characteristics from these images and have prognostic and predictive value [2, 38–40].

While the malignancies discussed here include cervical cancer, lung cancer and hematologic malignancies, AI methods have also shown applicability in the fine needle aspiration of thyroid nodules and breast cancer samples. Alabrak et al. described the role of AI in the subcytological classification of thyroid nodules, and Fritz et al. conducted a single-center study of AI approaches in the diagnosis of fine needle aspirates of breast diseases.

McAlpine and Michelow [39] proposed that cytopathologists embrace AI workflows in diagnostic settings, emphasizing the need for ethical assessment and quality assurance. The increasing use of AI methods would necessitate an understanding of their role in the cytopathology of cancers. While AI-assisted workflows for personalized medicine currently exist, sample stratification would enable a precision medicine approach for further clinical and translational applicability of AI methods in cytopathology. As diagnostic accuracy improves, this would likely encourage further exploration of umbrella and basket clinical trials.

Conclusions and future directions

Much attention—and concern—has been given to artificial intelligence approaches in biomedicine, both in research and clinical settings. As outlined here, many reviews have highlighted the positive aspects of AI models. While there are limitations in terms of the data requirements, future studies may be directed at further improving the diagnostic, prognostic and predictive value of AI and deep learning and machine learning.

Giernani et al. assert that AI will revolutionize diagnostic pathology, decreasing the cytopathologists’ challenges for highly time-consuming work and tediousness. Technology involving AI has led to remarkable progress in medicine, including remarkable progress in medicine as well as including image interpretation and computer-assisted diagnosis. This is relatively early stage in the field but the introduction of AI in medical routines will lead to further development of algorithms and validation. Deep learning, as a branch of machine learning and as the major tool in the current AI wave, has greatly accelerated the development of computational cytopathology-exploiting algorithms and specific architecture designs” [41].

Further innovation can take place in the field of precision medicine as a result of AI. Due to the potential for predictive analysis of AI applications for diagnostic cytopathology, personalized treatment options may become a reality for patients since the integration of genomics with generative AI could identify biomarkers or mutations in cytopathology samples, leading to the potential for the development of new targeted therapies or cancer immunotherapies. Future research and innovation could focus on this area for improved pathology workflows and patient care [42].
