Investigation of cellular senescence in the mouse liver caused by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy
Authors: Xin Lan, Lina Cai, Lingyu Zhang, Yashi Cai, Linqian Zhou, Weiyi Ke, Guanyou Chen, Yanting Chen, Xiaoman Zhou, Weixu Huang, Jianming Zou, Huifeng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe expanding use of medical diagnostics and radiotherapy has increased public exposure to low dose ionizing radiation (LDIR). A more clinically pressing concern, however, is the unintended irradiation of normal tissues adjacent to the radiotherapy target. Notably, the biological effects of lower cumulative doses on the liver remain poorly characterized.MethodsThirty-two 6 week-old C57BL6/J mice, half male and half female, were selected and randomly divided into control, 0.1 Gy, 0.2 Gy, and 0.5 Gy groups according to body weight and radiated every 48 hours for a total of 16 times. The body weights of mice were recorded 24 h after the end of each radiation exposure. The liver histopathology and liver index of mice were examined. The number of peripheral blood cells in mice was counted and serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), ceruloplasmin (CP) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were determined. Mouse liver tissues were screened for differentially expressed genes using high-throughput sequencing and subjected to gene ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analyses. The senescence - associated SA-β-Gal staining as well as mRNA levels of cellular senescence genes, anti - apoptotic factors, and senescence associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs) were detected. The levels of oxidative stress and oxidative damage related indexes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), and 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) were detected and correlated with the levels of inflammation, oxidative damage level, and cellular senescence indicators level in mouse liver.ResultsCompared with the control group, the difference in body weight of mice in different dose groups did not show significant changes (p > 0.05). Compared with the control group, hepatocytes in the 0.1 Gy × 16 group showed mild edema; hepatocytes in the 0.2 Gy × 16, and 0.5 Gy × 16 groups all showed mild to moderate edema, with structural disorganization of the hepatic lobules, hepatic cords, central veins, and mild steatosis. The results of liver indices showed no significant changes in the 0.1 Gy × 16 and 0.2 Gy × 16 groups (p > 0.05) and a significant decrease in the 0.5 Gy × 16 group (p < 0.05) compared to the control group. As the radiation dose increased, leukocyte, neutrophil, lymphocyte counts, C-reactive protein and CP levels all decreased significantly (p < 0.05), while serum AST levels increased significantly (p < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis of mouse liver showed that there were 46 genes differentially expressed across all dose groups. GO analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes in each dose group were mainly enriched in biological processes such as reactive oxygen metabolism process, NF-κB signaling pathway, and exogenous stimulus response. KEGG analysis showed that the pathways differentially expressed in the liver of mice in each dose group were mostly significantly enriched in the p53 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway. GSEA-GO analysis showed that the 0.1 Gy × 16 group was enriched in biological processes such as acute inflammatory response, and the 0.2 Gy × 16 and 0.5 Gy × 16 groups were enriched in cell cycle regulation. The degree of SA-β-Gal staining of liver was increased in all dose groups compared to the control group. Compared with the control group, p53, p21, p16 mRNA and protein levels were significantly higher in the 0.2 Gy × 16 and 0.5 Gy × 16 groups (p < 0.05); IL-8, GM-CSF, TGF-β1, CXCL-1, CXCL-12, MMP-14, and MMP-15 mRNA levels were significantly elevated (p < 0.05). Compared with the control group, the level of the anti-apoptotic factor BCL2L1 mRNA was significantly increased in all dose groups (p < 0.05), the levels of MDA, SOD and GSH in the liver and the levels of 8-OHdG in both serum and liver of mice increased with increasing radiation dose. The levels of systemic inflammation and oxidative damage in mice were significantly correlated with the levels of indicators related to cellular senescence in liver (p < 0.05).ConclusionsLow dose fractionated X-ray therapy induces cellular senescence in mouse liver tissues, and the levels of systemic inflammation and oxidative damage in mice are correlated with indicators related to cellular senescence in liver tissues.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Low dose ionizing radiation, Oxidative stress, Cellular senescence, Liver injury, Inflammatory factors

Background

With the development of industrial production and the increased frequency of medical diagnosis and radiotherapy, the public is facing increasing opportunities for exposure to ionizing radiation from artificial radiation sources [1]. However, a more clinically pressing issue has emerged with the widespread application of radiotherapy: the unintended irradiation of adjacent normal tissues outside the target volume [2]. The definition of low dose ionizing radiation (LDIR) varies across different fields. In radiation protection, radiobiology, and many other disciplines, doses of 100 mGy or below, which have not been definitively linked to carcinogenicity, are defined as LDIR. In contrast, in the field of clinical radiation oncology, due to species-specific variations in the concept of “low dose”, a single fraction of 1000 mGy used in radiotherapy is often classified as a low or subtherapeutic dose [3]. Highly conformal radiotherapy for abdominal tumors concentrates high doses on the target volume but inevitably exposes some normal tissues to sub-therapeutic doses from scatter or peripheral radiation [4]. In experimental research, such subtherapeutic doses of fractionated irradiation have been widely used to investigate the long-term effects of low dose or low dose rate radiation on cellular senescence and DNA damage response. For example, numerous animal and cellular experiments have adopted dose fractions of ≤ 0.5 Gy per session, administered consecutively over multiple days or through repeated exposures, to simulate scenarios of chronic, long-term radiation exposure [5]. In clinical treatments such as three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) or stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), classic radiation-induced liver disease (RILD) is generally observed when the mean liver dose reaches approximately 28–32 Gy or higher [6]. In radiation oncology and experimental radiobiology, a fractionated irradiation regimen employing “low-to-moderate doses per fraction delivered in multiple cumulative sessions” is frequently utilized to investigate normal tissue injury, late effects, or tissue regeneration responses [5]. However, the biological effects of lower cumulative doses on adjacent normal liver tissue during radiotherapy remain poorly characterized.

It has been shown that ionizing radiation induces a variety of cellular biological effects such as adaptive responses, excitatory effects and off-target effects involved in the regulation of cell fate outcome [7]. In addition, ionizing radiation may also lead to bystander effects, altering the long-term genetic stability of recipient cells [8–10] or leading to a cellular stress response with the release of various danger signals, inflammatory mediators, and immune activation [11–13]. Ionizing radiation can induce oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and producing malondialdehyde (MDA), leading to oxidative damage to biological molecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids [14–17]. Oxidative DNA damage or impaired function of the body’s damage repair system causes an increase in DNA oxidative damage markers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and the appearance of phosphorylated histone H2AX (γ-H2AX) foci [18], leading to the sustained activation of the DNA damage response which in turn causes cellular senescence [19–21]. Meanwhile, the body’s antioxidant system such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione (GSH) acts as an important defense system to antagonize the effects of oxidative stress by removing excess ROS either directly or indirectly [22], and plays a role in maintaining the dynamic balance of oxidative and antioxidant systems in the body.

Cellular senescence is the process of gradual decline in cell proliferation and differentiation capacity and physiological functions over time in the course of performing cellular life activities [18]. Cells responding to various stressors can induce senescence, involving cell cycle arrest, chromatin rearrangement, morphological changes, metabolic shifts toward glycolysis, and release of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors [23, 24]. Cellular senescence is important in the development of liver disease. In the early stages of liver disease, hepatocellular senescence reduces the risk of cellular carcinogenesis by preventing the proliferation of damaged hepatocytes and maintaining the stability of organ function. However, in the later stages of liver disease, the levels of SASP factors secreted by senescent cells, such as pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines (CXCL-1, CXCL-12), matrix metalloproteinase (MMP), granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF), are elevated. In addition, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) also promotes the transformation of normal hepatocytes into senescent cells in a paracrine manner, mediates the increase in the body’s inflammatory level, and thus accelerates the disease progression [25].

With the increase of medical radiation exposure [26], the study of biological effects and mechanisms of long term or low dose fractionated X-ray therapy exposure is of great importance in the field of radiation protection [27]. Ionizing radiation may cause the loss of normal structure and function of liver cells through oxidative stress and inflammatory cascade reaction, and eventually lead to liver cell necrosis [28]. In this process, ceruloplasmin (CP), synthesized by the liver, may play a significant role. Apart from playing a role in copper and iron metabolism, CP is an acute-phase reactant that may work as an antioxidant but can also generate free radicals that may lead to several diseases [29]. A radiation epidemiology study found that chronic exposure to low dose fractionated X-ray therapy resulted in increased detection of liver function abnormalities in radiation workers, particularly in aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase levels (ALT) alterations [30]. Existing research has primarily focused on the clinical risk of RILD at total dose ≥ 30 Gy, with limited attention given to chronic low-to-moderate dose radiation-induced damage to normal liver tissue, biological responses, and particularly cellular senescence, and the mechanism of hepatocyte senescence in this context is unclear.

This study aims to simulate the damaging effects caused by low-to-moderate cumulative doses on adjacent normal tissues during radiotherapy. We constructed a low dose fractionated X-ray therapy induced liver tissue cell senescence model in mice to investigate the changes in the levels of oxidative stress, oxidative damage, and systemic inflammation in the process of liver tissue cell senescence, and to provide new insights into the mechanism underlying key events or targets of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy induced liver cell senescence in the context of radiation-induced liver injury.

Materials and methods

Reagents and instruments

Paraformaldehyde fixative (Biosharp, USA); Hematoxylin (Sigma, USA); Eosin Y (Tianjin Guangfu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China); Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Concentration Determination Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology C o., Ltd. Shanghai, China); Mouse 8-OHdG Detection Kit (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China); MDA kit, SOD kit, GSH kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China); Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR kit (Takara, Japan); CIX3 X-ray bioreactor (Xs-trahl, UK); Tissue slicer (Leica, Germany); Microscope (Olympus, Japan); Ultra-micro UV Spectrophotometer, Full-wavelength Enzyme Labeler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); Fully Automatic Sample Grinder (Jingxin industrial development Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China); SL16R high-speed freezing centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); GYQ0080 automatic blood cell counter (Nihon Kohden Corp, Japan); Lightcycler96 real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction instrument (Roche, Switzerland).

Animals and radiation

Thirty-two 6-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6J mice (half male and half female, purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, weighing 15–18 g) were used for the experiment. Mice of different sexes were randomly divided into control, 0.1 Gy, 0.2 Gy, and 0.5 Gy groups according to body weights, with 8 mice (half male and half female) in each group. A CIX3 X-ray bioirradiator was used for whole-body radiation with a dose rate of 0.7577 Gy/min, a voltage of 300 kV, a current of 10 mA, and single radiation times of 8 s, 16 s, and 40 s, respectively. Based on the literature, this study employed single dose of 0.1 Gy, 0.2 Gy and 0.5 Gy administered every 48 hours for a total of 16 times, with group settings of the 0.1 Gy × 16, the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16, respectively [5, 31–34]. The dose levels employed in this study were substantially higher than environmental or occupational exposure, and were clinically relevant to the cumulative doses received by adjacent normal tissues in fractionated radiotherapy. The body weight of mice was observed and recorded 24 h after each radiation. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangdong Province Hospital for Occupational Disease Prevention and Treatment (Permit Number: GDHOD AEC2022016).

Animal tissue sampling

Twenty-four hours after the mice received 16 rounds of radiation, they were weighed and then given isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. Blood was collected by enucleating the eyeballs for a routine blood test. After collecting the blood samples, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and then dissected. Mouse liver tissue was taken and weighed, and the liver coefficient was calculated according to “organ coefficient = organ mass/body mass”. The condition of the liver tissue macroscopic specimens of mice in each group were observed and recorded, including the color, texture, presence or absence of congestion and hemorrhage, surface smoothness, elasticity, and whether it is enlarged or not. Excised portions of liver tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin sections and stored at room temperature. The remaining liver tissues were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in a −80 °C refrigerator.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining (HE staining)

The 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed mouse liver tissue was subjected to gradient dehydration, then dipped in wax, embedded, and made into paraffin sections. Sections were dewaxed to water, stained with hematoxylin for 10–15 min, rinsed under running water for 20 s, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid in alcohol for 10 s, rinsed slowly with tap water for 15 min, stained with eosin for 30 min, stained with 90% ethanol for 30 s, stained with 95% ethanol for 30 s × 2 times, stained with anhydrous ethanol for 30 s × 3 times, and cleared with xylene for 30 s × 4 times, and fixed with a neutral resin before being examined under a light microscope.

Mouse peripheral blood cell count and serum AST, ALT, ceruloplasmin (CP) and C-reactive protein (CRP) assays

The white blood cell (WBC), lymphocyte (LY), and neutrophil (NEU) counts in peripheral blood of mice were detected using a fully automated blood cell analyzer. Serum levels of AST, ALT, CP and CRP in mice were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer.

Transcriptomic analysis of mouse liver tissue

Total RNA of mouse liver tissue was extracted and submitted to Aksomics for sequencing. After the sample concentration was determined by NanoDrop ND-1000, 1 ~ 2 μg of total RNA was selected to construct an RNA sequencing library, and then the library was sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer after library quality identification and quantification. Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) calculations at the gene level were performed by the software StringTie with the R software Ballgown, and the genes with a mean FPKM value of more than 0.5 were statistically analyzed in each group. Differentially expressed genes were screened according to the threshold of |log2 Fold Change| >1. Cluster analysis of differentially expressed genes, Gene Ontology (GO)-biological process (BP) analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were carried out using the company’s custom program (Python/R/Shell), and visual mapping was performed.

SA-β-Gal staining of mouse liver tissue

Mouse liver tissue was embedded using OCT embedding reagent and frozen sections of liver tissue were prepared. The tissues were washed in PBS three times for not less than 5 min each time. Add appropriate volume of β-galactosidase staining fixative, cover the tissue well, and fix for 15 min at room temperature. The tissues were washed in PBS three times for not less than 5 min each time. After aspirating the PBS, 10 µL of β-galactosidase staining solution A, 10 µL of β-galactosidase staining solution B, 930 µL of β-galactosidase staining solution C, and 50 µL of X-gal solution were added, incubated at 37 °C for 2 days, and the slices were sealed with neutral resin, observed under a light microscope and photographed.

Detection of protein levels of aging-related genes in mouse liver tissue

20 mg of mouse liver sample was added to pre-cooled PBS solution at a ratio of 1:9 by mass (g): volume (mL). The sample was ground and homogenized using a cryomiller, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 5 min. Experiments were performed according to the instructions of p53, p21 and p16 protein detection kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) to determine the levels of p53, p21 and p16 proteins in the mouse liver.

Detection of mRNA levels of aging-related genes, anti-apoptotic factors and SASP factors in mouse liver tissue

10–15 mg of mouse liver was ground, RNA was extracted by Trizol method, and then was reverse transcribed to cDNA and subjected to real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction. The relative expression of target genes was calculated by using mouse-derived β-actin as an internal reference for normalization. The PCR primer sequences of each gene (Table 1) were designed and commissioned to be synthesized by Sangon Biotech.

Detection of oxidative stress indicators in mouse liver tissue

After adding the mouse liver samples to pre-cooled PBS solution, the samples were ground and homogenized using a cryomill, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4 °C, 12,000 rpm for 5 min. Protein concentration was determined by BCA protein concentration assay kit. The experiments were performed according to the instructions of the MDA, SOD, GSH and 8-OHdG kit (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China), and readings were taken at 532 nm, 532 nm, 532 nm and 450 nm using an enzyme labeling instrument to detect the MDA (nmol/mgprot), SOD (U/mgprot), GSH (μmol/gprot) and 8-OHdG (ng/ml) levels.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was applied for statistical analysis, and the measurements were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison between multiple groups, and the LSD method was used for two-by-two comparisons between groups, and the difference was considered to be statistically significant at p < 0.05. Spearman’s correlation was used to analyze the correlation between the level of systemic inflammation in mice and the level of indicators related to cellular senescence and damage in liver tissues, with a test level of α = 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Effects of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy on body weight, gross liver morphology, liver histopathology and liver index in mice

For the long-term radiation of mice, single dose of 0.1 Gy, 0.2 Gy, and 0.5 Gy was administered every 48 hours for a total of 16 times (Fig. 1A). Compared with the control group, there was no significant change in the body weight of mice in each radiation dose group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Gross observation of the livers showed that the livers in the control group were bright red with a smooth, soft and elastic surface; the livers of the mice in each radiation dose group were brownish yellow with a rough surface. Compared with the control group, the liver volume of the 0.5 Gy × 16 group decreased significantly (Fig. 1C). The histopathological results of the liver showed that the control group had a clear structure of hepatic lobules, hepatic cords and central vein with radial shape, and hepatocytes were of uniform size with no obvious lesions. Hepatocytes in the 0.1 Gy × 16 group were mildly edematous. The structure of hepatic lobules, hepatic cords, and central vein was clear, with small foci of lymphocytic infiltration seen. Hepatocytes in both the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups showed mild to moderate edema, structural disorganization of hepatic lobules, hepatic cords and central veins, and cytoplasmic vacuolization, in which the 0.5 Gy × 16 group showed siltation of the confluent area (Fig. 1D). The results of the liver index showed that, compared with the control group, there was no significant change in the liver index of the 0.1 Gy × 16 group and the 0.2 Gy × 16 group (p > 0.05), while the liver index of the 0.5 Gy × 16 group decreased significantly (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1E).

Effects of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy on peripheral blood cell counts and C-reactive protein levels in mice

The results showed that, among the peripheral blood indicators reflecting systemic radiation effects, compared with the control group, the numbers of peripheral blood white blood cells and lymphocytes in mice of each radiation dose group were significantly decreased (p < 0.05), showing a dose-response relationship (Fig. 2A–B). Compared with the control group, the 0.1 Gy × 16 group and the 0.2 Gy × 16 group respectively, the numbers of neutrophils and the levels of C-reactive protein in the 0.5 Gy × 16 group were significantly decreased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C–D).

Effects of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy on serum AST, ALT and CP levels in mice

The results showed that, compared with the control group, the AST level in each radiation dose group were significantly increased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Compared with the control group, the 0.1 Gy × 16 group and the 0.2 Gy × 16 group respectively, the ALT level in the 0.5 Gy × 16 group was significantly decreased (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Compared with the control group, the CP levels in each dose group were significantly decreased, and a dose-response relationship was presented (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes, GO analysis, KEGG analysis and GSEA in liver tissues of mice after low dose fractionated X-ray therapy

The results of the Venn diagram and clustered heatmap analyses showed that there were 46 common differentially expressed genes across radiation dose groups (Fig. 4A).

The results of GO function annotation showed that, compared with the control group, the 0.1 Gy × 16 group was mainly involved in biological processes such as the response to stimuli, NF-κB signaling-related pathways, inflammatory responses, and the regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolic processes; the 0.2 Gy × 16 group was mainly involved in biological processes such as the cell cycle, stimulus response regulation, cell proliferation, positive regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolism and cellular response to DNA damage; the 0.5 Gy × 16 group was mainly involved in biological processes such as stimulation response, NF-κB signaling pathway, and reactive oxygen species metabolic process (Fig. 4B).

The results of KEGG analysis showed that, compared with the control group, the 0.1 Gy × 16 group was mainly enriched in the TNF, IL-17, and p53 signaling pathways; the 0.2 Gy × 16 group was mainly enriched in PPAR and p53 signaling pathways; and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group was mainly enriched in TNF, IL-17, p53 and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 4C).

The results of GSEA showed that the 0.1 Gy × 16 group was enriched in biological processes such as acute inflammatory response; the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups were mainly enriched in biological processes such as cell cycle regulation (Fig. 4D).

Effects of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy on SA-β-Gal staining, senescence related genes, anti-apoptotic factors and SASP factor levels in mouse liver tissue

The results showed that, compared with the control group, the staining intensity of SA-β-Gal in both the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group was significantly increased(Fig. 5A). Compared with the control and the 0.1 Gy × 16 groups, respectively, the p53 protein level was significantly higher in the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups (p < 0.001); compared with the control group, the p21 and p16 protein levels were significantly higher in all radiation dose groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Compared with the control group, p53 and p16 mRNA levels were significantly higher in all radiation dose groups (p < 0.05). Compared with the control group and the 0.1 Gy × 16 group respectively, the levels of p21 mRNA in the liver tissues of mice in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group were significantly increased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). Compared with the control group, the levels of BCL2L1 mRNA in each radiation dose group were significantly increased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). Compared with the control group, IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β1 and CXCL-12 mRNA levels were significantly higher in all radiation dose groups (p < 0.05), and GM-CSF, CXCL-1, MMP-14, and MMP-15 mRNA levels were elevated in the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

The liver is an important organ in maintaining metabolism and redox homeostasis in the body [28]. Ionizing radiation induces not only changes in the morphology of the liver, pathology and mechanism of action of liver cells, but also a series of effects on the activity and metabolism of biological enzymes [35]. In the present study, we found that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy of mice resulted in deepening of liver color and reduction in volume; the liver index was significantly reduced in the 0.5 Gy × 16 group; the hepatocytes showed different degrees of edema, especially in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group, which showed disorganization of the hepatic lobules, hepatic cords, central venous structure, and siltation in the confluent area. It is suggested that the degree of pathological damage to the liver tissue gradually increases with the increasing of radiation dose. In the early stage, the damage of ionizing radiation to the body is mainly manifested as a decrease in peripheral blood cells. The mechanism mainly involves ionizing radiation inducing apoptosis and premature senescence of hematopoietic stem cells, disrupting the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells, which leads to a decrease in various types of blood cells, disorders of the hematopoietic microcirculation, and damage to the hematopoietic function [36]. This is similar to the result observed by Zhang [37] et al. who found that the WBC counts of mice in all groups significantly decreased when observed at 1, 3 and 7 days after a single 2 Gy X-ray radiation, however, it is contrary to their result that the serum CRP levels of mice significantly increased at 3 and 7 days after radiation. This may be due to the fact that inflammatory factors and chemokines, in high or persistent concentrations, inhibit the function of immune cells, so that the immune function of leukocytes is suppressed, and the body, in order to maintain immune homeostasis, may reduce the production and release of leukocytes, leading to a decrease in WBC counts [38]. It is suggested that a single moderate dose or low dose fractionated X-ray therapy can induce changes in systemic inflammation levels in mice, and that the inflammatory response induced by a fractionated low dose therapy may be different from that induced by a single moderate dose. When ionizing radiation causes hepatocellular injury, the permeability of the cell membrane increases and ALT and AST, which are responsible for catalyzing the amino transfer between amino acids and keto acids in hepatocytes, are released into the bloodstream, resulting in elevated serum ALT and AST levels [39], which are important and sensitive biochemical markers for assessing liver function. Serum ceruloplasmin (CP) is an acute-phase protein synthesized by hepatocytes that serves as a biomarker for systemic inflammation and oxidative stress status [40]. CP is a copper-rich plasma protein that possesses three key functions: copper transport, ferroxidase activity, and antioxidant capacity. As an acute-phase reactant protein, it typically exhibits mild elevation and can show mild to moderate increases in response to tissue damage, infection, and inflammatory conditions [40–42]. The results of this study showed that compared with the control group, the serum AST level was significantly increased and the CP level was significantly decreased in all radiation dose groups of mice, the ALT level was significantly decreased in the 0.5 Gy × 16 group, and the AST/ALT ratio was significantly increased in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups. Tanaka [43] et al. found an increase in the incidence of hepatic steatosis and the severity of liver injury with age in mice after continuous radiation for 400 days using a low dose rate of γ-radiation (20 mGy/day), which was similar to the results of the present study. It is suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy can induce liver tissue damage in mice. Existing studies have revealed that the response of AST/ALT to radiation-induced liver injury exhibits significant complexity in both temporal and dose-volume dimensions. Higher radiation doses do not consistently correlate with higher enzyme levels, indicating that changes in AST/ALT during hepatic radiation injury are highly contingent upon factors such as the time point of assessment, irradiated volume, baseline liver function, and the extent of hepatic regeneration/repair. Consequently, the highest dose group does not necessarily demonstrate the greatest elevation in enzyme levels, reflecting a non-monotonic trend [44]. The levels of AST and ALT in serum mainly reflected hepatogenic function and injury signals in systemic circulation, acting as molecules detectable throughout the body but primarily synthesized or released by the liver. In this study, serum AST and ALT levels were measured 24 hours after the completion of low dose fractionated X-ray therapy in mice. The observed phenomenon of higher AST levels in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group compared to the 0.5 Gy × 16 group may be attributed to the intermediate-dose group being at the peak stage of acute radiation injury, while the highest-dose group had entered the repair phase following radiation damage. These findings suggested that abnormal liver function detected after whole-body irradiation in mice may serve as supportive evidence of hepatocyte injury to a certain extent.

GO enrichment analysis of transcriptomics sequencing of liver tissues from mice after low dose fractionated X-ray therapy revealed that each dose group was mainly involved in biological processes such as reactive oxygen species metabolic process, NF-κB signaling pathway and exogenous stimulus response. The results of this study are similar to those of Pustovalova [45] et al. who induced elevated levels of SASP-related factors and activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway after irradiating lung cancer cells by fractionated ionizing radiation, suggesting that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy can elicit cellular stimulatory and inflammatory responses. KEGG analysis revealed that each radiation dose group was mainly enriched in p53 and NF-κB signaling pathways, and this finding is consistent with the findings of Ishwarya [46] et al. who study on the effects of ionizing radiation on transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation in human fibroblasts. It is suggested that the induction of cellular senescence by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy may be related to the p53 and NF-κB signaling pathways. The results of GSEA-GO BP analysis showed that each radiation dose group was mainly enriched in biological processes such as acute inflammatory response and cell cycle regulation. It suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy can affect the changes in liver aging and inflammatory levels in mice. A distinctive feature of senescent cells is the increased expression of cell cycle inhibitory proteins (collectively known as cell cycle protein dependent kinase inhibitors), p21 and p16 are two cell cycle protein-dependent kinase inhibitors that are components of the tumor suppressor pathway controlled by p53 and RB, with p16 playing the most important role in the accumulation of senescent cells during senescence [47]. Cellular senescence activates the p53 and p16 pathways through the DNA damage response, generating DNA damage sites that initiate and maintain cell cycle arrest. In addition, activation of p53 can further activate p21, leading to cell cycle arrest [48]. Studies have confirmed that in models of radiation-induced liver injury, the presence of typical cellular senescence phenotypes in hepatocytes is closely correlated with the degree of liver dysfunction and hepatic fibrosis [49]. The results of the present study showed that after low dose fractionated X-ray therapy, the degree of SA-β-Gal staining of mice liver tissues in the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups increased with increasing radiation doses, and the levels of liver tissue senescence-related genes, p53, p21, and p16 mRNA and proteins, were all significantly increased. Azimzadeh [50] et al. also found elevated levels of both p21 and p16 proteins in microvascular endothelial cells after local radiation of mouse hearts with 8 Gy X-ray. In the present study, we further analyzed the alterations in mRNA levels of SASP factor in mice liver tissues after low dose fractionated X-ray therapy. The IL-6, IL-8, TGF-β1 and CXCL-12 mRNA levels were significantly increased in all radiation dose groups, and GM-CSF, CXCL-1, MMP-14 and MMP-15 mRNA levels were increased in the 0.2 Gy × 16 and the 0.5 Gy × 16 groups. This is similar to the results of Baselet [51] et al. who showed an increased secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 in human coronary artery endothelial cells after X-ray radiation at a dose rate of 1.5 Gy/min (single dose of 2 Gy). Increased anti-apoptotic capacity of senescent cells has been demonstrated to promote cell survival by resisting programmed cell death in senescent cells [52, 53]. The present study also found that the transcript level of the anti-apoptotic factor BCL2L1 was significantly increased in all dose groups. It is suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy can cause elevated levels of senescence-related factors and induce cellular senescence in mouse liver tissues.

Ionizing radiation can ionize water molecules in the body, forming free radicals, hydroxyl groups, and peroxides, etc. A large amount of reactive oxygen species accumulate in cells, leading to the disruption of the balance between the intracellular oxidation-antioxidation reactions and causing oxidative stress [54]. This study found that the MDA levels in the livers of mice in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group were significantly increased. It is suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy induces an increase in reactive oxygen species production in mouse liver tissue, leading to an increase in the level of lipid peroxides. The levels of GSH and SOD, as important intracellular antioxidants, reflect the intracellular redox status. The SOD levels in all dose groups were significantly increased, and the GSH levels in the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group were significantly increased. Avti [55] et al. found a significant increase in oxidation levels (MDA) and antioxidant levels (SOD, GSH) in liver tissues after administering of 25 cGy 60Co γ radiation to mice for 12 h. This is similar to the results of this study. It is suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy may affect the oxidative-antioxidant balance by increasing the levels of oxidative stress in liver cells.

8-OHdG is internationally recognized as the gold standard for oxidative DNA damage [56]. In this study, the levels of 8-OHdG in both the serum and the liver of the 0.2 Gy × 16 group and the 0.5 Gy × 16 group were significantly higher than those of the control group. Bellés [57] et al. found a significant increase in renal 8-OHdG levels on day 10 post-injection in mice given a low dose of 4000 Bq/kg of cesium source intraperitoneally. This is consistent with the results of the present study. It is suggested that low dose fractionated X-ray therapy may cause oxidative DNA damage in tissue cells by elevating the level of oxidative stress.

Studies have shown that the accumulation of macromolecules such as lipid peroxidation products (e.g., MDA) and DNA oxidative damage products (e.g., 8-OHdG) is the cause of inflammation and that cellular senescence is central to the inflammatory process [58]. The results of correlation analysis showed that the WBC counts of mice in each radiation dose group were significantly negatively correlated with the levels of SOD, p53, TGF-β1, IL-8, BCL2L1, CXCL-1 and CXCL-12, and significantly positively correlated with the levels of CRP. This may be due to the fact that some organs are characterized by a pro-inflammatory microenvironment and impaired resolution during aging, others display elevated levels of pro-resolving or an overall decrease in inflammatory signaling [59]. The serum 8-OHdG level of mice in each radiation dose group was positively correlated with GSH, SOD, p21, IL8, MMP14, CXCL1, and the liver 8-OHdG level. The liver 8-OHdG level of mice in each radiation dose group was positively correlated with GSH, SOD, p21, IL8, MMP14, CXCL12, and the serum 8-OHdG level. This suggests that as oxidative stress within cells increases, DNA oxidative damage accumulates and promotes the aging process. Moreover, the degree of aging induced by low dose radiation might be influenced by the levels of oxidative stress and inflammation in tissues [60]. This study also has certain limitations: firstly, it did not account for the potential sex-based heterogeneity in cellular senescence induced by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy in mouse liver tissues. Secondly, the senescence staining results of liver tissues in this study were unable to distinguish the specific types of senescent hepatocytes induced by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy. Thirdly, as this study employed whole-body irradiation rather than localized liver irradiation, the radiation-induced liver-specific effects and their relationship with systemic effects require further elucidation. Fourthly, this study was not powered to detect sex-specific differences. Future studies employing more animals per sex (n ≥ 5) are warranted to ensure adequate statistical robustness. Subsequent investigations should implement fractionated localized liver irradiation in mice of both sexes to detect expression changes of senescence- and inflammation-related markers (such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) in liver tissues, systematically analyze the dynamic evolution of liver-specific inflammation levels, and facilitate an in-depth exploration of sexual heterogeneity in hepatic senescence and inflammatory responses. This will elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the induction of liver aging and elevated inflammation levels in mice by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy.

Conclusions

Low dose fractionated X-ray therapy induces cellular senescence in mouse liver tissues, and the levels of systemic inflammation and oxidative damage in mice are correlated with indicators related to cellular senescence in liver tissues. This study can provide an experimental basis for the selection of clinical radiation therapy dose and the optimization of fractionated radiation therapy time, and offer a new idea for the study of the mechanism of cellular senescence of liver tissue induced by low dose fractionated X-ray therapy.
