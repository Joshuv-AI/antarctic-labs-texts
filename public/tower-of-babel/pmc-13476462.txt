Ionizing Radiation and Ultraviolet Light Irradiation‐Associated DNA Damage Increasing Genomic Instability Risk
Authors: Ken‐ichi Yoshioka, Yusuke Matsuno, Rika Kusumoto‐Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer frequently develops in association with genomic instability, which includes massive induction of chromosomal structural variants (SVs) and single nucleotide variants (SNVs). SVs are typically caused by erroneous repair of DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) and are tightly linked to cancer risk. Indeed, cancers often arise under DNA repair‐deficient backgrounds such as BRCA1/2 mutations. However, many cancers exhibiting genomic instability occur without detectable defects in canonical repair pathways. The major exogenous risk factors include ionizing radiation (IR) and ultraviolet (UV) light, both of which induce multiple types of DNA damage. Long‐standing questions are which specific types of DNA lesions induced by the irradiation contribute to genomic instability and how these lesions promote mutagenesis in cancer‐driver genes. In this review, we summarize current knowledge regarding how IR and UV irradiation lead to genomic instability associated with mutation induction in cancer‐driver genes.

Introduction

Cancer cells are characterized by extensive genomic instability, including large numbers of SNVs and SVs [1, 2, 3]. SNVs and SVs are primarily caused by distinct pathways: while SNVs arise from DNA polymerase errors that escape proofreading by replicative polymerases δ and ε or mismatch repair (MMR) [4, 5, 6], SVs are generated through erroneous end‐joining of unpaired DSB ends [7, 8]. These DSBs often originate from replication stress, which is usually repaired by homologous recombination (HR) [9, 10]. Accordingly, HR‐deficient cancers—such as those harboring BRCA1/2, BARD1, PALB2, or MRE11 mutations—show marked genomic instability [11, 12, 13]. However, many cancers with genomic instability lack mutations in repair genes [14, 15, 16, 17, 18], raising the critical question of how instability arises in cells with otherwise intact repair systems.

One important source of such instability is exogenous damage, including that caused by IR and UV exposure. Indeed, cancers associated with IR or UV exposure frequently display high levels of genomic instability [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Recent studies have clarified which specific damage types induced by IR and UV irradiation contribute to instability and how they promote SV and SNV induction. Here, we focus on summarizing the mechanisms through which IR and UV irradiation‐associated DNA damages drive genomic instability, induce mutations in cancer‐driver genes, and promote clonal evolution.

Genomic Instability Arising From Replication Stress and Its Role in Clonal Evolution

Genomic Instability and Cancer‐Driver Mutations

Accumulating evidence indicates that the significance of genomic instability lies in its ability to promote the clonal evolution of cells harboring mutations in cancer‐driver genes. This concept was clearly demonstrated in an in vitro model using mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) [24, 25]. In this model, replication stress‐associated DSBs were shown to increase the likelihood of clonal expansion of cells with mutations in key tumor‐suppressor pathways, such as the ARF/p53 axis (Figure 1). Replication stress‐induced DSBs are primarily repaired by HR, an error‐free repair mechanism. Supporting this, defects in HR‐related genes—including BRCA1, BRCA2, BARD1, PALB2, and MRE11—are strongly associated with genomic instability and cancer predisposition [26, 27, 28, 29], in which genomic instability is induced through error‐prone repair pathways, such as non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) and micro‐homology mediated end joining (MMEJ), also called polymerase theta‐mediated end joining (TMEJ) [30].

Genomic instability induced by replication stress. DSBs generated under replication stress persist and continuously accumulate. These DSBs lead to extensive erroneous repair, resulting in genomic instability characterized by the formation of micronuclei, chromosomal translocations and deletions, LOH, and the induction of SVs and SNVs. Under these conditions, clonal evolution is promoted in cells harboring mutations in cancer‐driver genes.

A central question in cancer biology has been how mutations that drive tumorigenesis are generated. The conventional view attributes most mutations to stochastic DNA polymerase errors during replication [17, 31]. However, this explanation is insufficient to account for the frequent occurrence of biallelic mutations in tumor suppressor genes, which are statistically unlikely to arise through random polymerase errors alone. Instead, genomic instability—through mechanisms such as loss of heterozygosity (LOH) or chromosomal deletion—can facilitate the inactivation of both alleles [32, 33], a phenomenon frequently observed in cancer cells [34]. Together, these findings suggest that replication stress‐associated DSBs play a major role in generating mutations that drive cancer development.

Association Between SV and SNV Induction

The induction mechanisms of SVs and SNVs differ: SVs arise from erroneous repair of DSBs [7, 8], whereas SNVs result from polymerase misincorporation [4, 5, 6] However, SNVs are frequently observed near SV breakpoints. Indeed, SNV density within 1 Mb of SV loci is significantly higher than in other genomic regions [25, 35]. Moreover, specific single base substitution (SBS) signatures such as SBS3 (HR deficiency), SBS36 (base excision repair deficiency), and SBS2/13 (APOBEC activity) are enriched near SV sites, highlighting a mechanistic link between these mutation types [35]. Similar correlations between SV and SNV induction have also been observed in MEFs undergoing genomic instability and subsequent clonal evolution [25, 35]. Given that SVs are primarily generated through replication stress‐associated DSBs and their erroneous repair, it is likely that the accompanying SNVs also arise during replication stress and/or DSB formation.

Multiple pathways could underlie SV‐associated SNV generation, including: (1) cytosine deamination at 5‐methylcytosine residues (5mC > T), and (2) base misincorporation by low‐fidelity DNA polymerases. The former typically targets exposed single‐stranded DNA (ssDNA), which is frequently generated during DSB repair. The latter may occur when replication forks employing high‐fidelity polymerases δ or ε stall under replication stress, allowing low‐fidelity translesion synthesis (TLS) polymerases to take over [24, 36, 37].

Genomic Instability and Clonal Evolution

As demonstrated in MEF models, clonal evolution of cells harboring mutations in CDKN2A (which encodes ARF) or TP53 can be triggered by genomic instability arising from replication stress‐associated DSBs [24]. This suggests that a sufficient number of mutations—including potential cancer‐driver mutations—can accumulate as a consequence of widespread induction of SVs and SNVs caused by genomic instability. The frequency of such clonal evolution events in vitro is approximately one in 3 × 105 cells in wild‐type (WT) MEFs and increases to about one in 1 × 104 cells in MMR‐deficient Msh2‐knockout MEFs [24]. In MEFs, cells exhibiting replication stress often display senescence‐associated phenotypes and can be driven toward genomic instability by exogenous insults such as IR or UV light irradiation [25, 38]. As is well established, replication stress can also arise for multiple reasons, including the presence of difficult‐to‐replicate genomic regions, such as telomeres, centromeres, and common fragile sites (CFSs), many of which overlap long genes, as well as regions where DNA replication–transcription conflicts frequently occur [39, 40]. Furthermore, in cancer cells, hyperproliferative growth signals enhance both DNA replication and transcription, thereby increasing replication–transcription conflicts [41, 42]. As a result, cancer cells experience elevated replication stress compared with normal cells.

IR Damage and Genomic Instability

IR exposure is well known to increase the risk of cancer development associated with genomic instability [43, 44, 45]. Traditionally, this cancer risk has been attributed to DNA damage induced by IR; however, two long‐standing questions remain unresolved: (1) which specific types of DNA damage among the multiple lesions generated by IR are most closely linked to cancer risk [46, 47, 48, 49, 50], and (2) how mutations leading to clonal evolution are induced following irradiation. Here, we review current knowledge addressing these issues.

Acute Damage Responses to IR Irradiation

As well established, cells exposed to IR primarily activate acute DNA damage checkpoint responses, resulting in cell cycle arrest, senescence, and apoptosis [51, 52]. These effects are triggered not only by DSBs but also by cytosolic DNA derived from micronuclei, which form when unrepaired DSBs persist [53, 54]. Such acute responses typically occur following high radiation doses (≥ 5 Gy) and are characteristic of cells undergoing radiation therapy. However, epidemiological and experimental data indicate that increased cancer risk can also arise from much lower IR doses and dose rates [44, 55]—ranges that seldom elicit acute damage responses. This observation implies that the mechanisms underlying radiation‐induced genomic instability and cancer risk are at least partly independent of the acute DNA damage response.

Replication Stress‐Associated DSB Formation After Repair of IR‐Induced DSBs

IR generates multiple types of DNA lesions, including DSBs, single‐strand breaks (SSBs), and reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐mediated nucleotide adducts such as 8‐oxo‐guanine [46, 47, 48, 49, 50]. A critical question is which of these lesions contributes most strongly to cancer risk—specifically, to the induction of genomic instability, mutations in cancer‐driver genes, and clonal evolution. A previous study revealed that, following IR exposure, the risk of genomic instability increases due to the accumulation of replication stress‐associated DSBs during the subsequent S phase [25, 56]. These secondary DSBs arise after the direct IR‐induced DSBs have already been repaired (Figure 2). Importantly, in MEF models, while the primary IR‐induced DSBs are efficiently repaired, secondary DSBs generated under replication stress persist and promote genomic instability and clonal evolution, particularly in cells harboring mutations in the ARF/p53 pathway [25]. Thus, replication stress‐associated DSBs that emerge following IR exposure likely represent a key driver of genomic instability and subsequent clonal evolution.

IR‐induced replication stress and genomic instability. Although ionizing radiation generates multiple types of DNA damage, irradiated cells widely accumulate replication stress–associated DSBs. This accumulation leads to the induction of genomic instability and promotes accelerated clonal evolution.

Unlike directly induced DSBs, replication stress‐associated DSBs can occur over a broad range of radiation doses and dose rates—for example, 0.25–2 Gy and 1.39–909 mGy/min in MEF models [25]. Consequently, genomic instability and clonal evolution can also be induced across similarly wide irradiation conditions. This observation is consistent with epidemiological data showing that IR‐associated cancers develop over a broad spectrum of exposure levels [43, 57]. However, the mechanisms by which replication stress‐associated DSBs are induced across these diverse dose ranges remain to be elucidated.

SV and SNV Induction Following IR Irradiation

Specific SBS signatures are often induced by exogenous DNA‐damaging agents. In MEF models, IR irradiation increases the prevalence of SBS17—a signature associated with ROS generation [58]. This observation aligns with established knowledge that ROS levels rise upon IR exposure [59, 60, 61, 62]. Importantly, although genomic instability is indeed induced by irradiation, the genomic distribution of SV breakpoints in irradiated cells closely mirrors that observed in non‐irradiated controls [35, 58]. This finding suggests that the overall risk of SV induction is not directly attributable to the immediate DNA lesions caused by irradiation. Rather, it reflects a secondary effect in which IR exposure induces a cellular state that is intrinsically more prone to genomic instability. Such a state may also arise through other mechanisms that trigger replication stress‐associated DSB formation.

UV Damage and Genomic Instability

The primary DNA lesions induced by UV light irradiation are cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and pyrimidine–pyrimidone (6‐4) photoproducts (6–4PPs) [63, 64, 65]. UV exposure also increases the risk of melanoma, a cancer type that develops in association with genomic instability [63, 64, 65]. Long‐standing questions in the field include: (1) which UV photoproducts contribute to genomic instability, including the induction of SVs and SNVs; (2) how the UV‐associated mutational signature SBS7 is generated; and (3) how UV‐induced DSBs associated with SV formation are produced. Here, we summarize current knowledge addressing these questions, together with relevant insights into repair mechanisms.

Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) and TLS

It is well established that UV‐induced photoproducts are typically repaired through two major NER pathways: global genome NER (GG‐NER) and transcription‐coupled NER (TC‐NER) (Figure 3A) [66, 67, 68]. GG‐NER recognizes helix distortions caused by UV photoproducts, whereas TC‐NER is initiated when RNA polymerase II stalls at a lesion. Defects in NER components XPA–XPG and XPJ underlie xeroderma pigmentosum (XP), a disorder characterized by extreme UV sensitivity and a markedly increased risk of skin cancer [67, 69]. In addition, mutations in specific NER factors cause UV‐sensitive syndrome (UVSS) and Cockayne syndrome (CS), where UVSSA deficiency results in UVSS, and CSA or CSB mutations are responsible for CS [67].

NER and the risks of SV/SNV induction following UV irradiation. (A) While 6–4PPs are efficiently repaired by GG‐NER, CPDs are removed less efficiently and can give rise to DSBs during TC‐NER. (B) Unrepaired CPDs are bypassed by TLS polymerases, and erroneous nucleotide incorporation by TLS polymerases at these sites can lead to SNVs.

DNA synthesis over UV photoproducts cannot be performed by the high‐fidelity replicative polymerases δ and ε. Instead, these lesions are bypassed by TLS polymerases such as pol η, ι, κ, ζ, and REV1 (Figure 3B) [70]. Because TLS polymerases generally possess low fidelity, DNA synthesis at these sites represents mutational hotspots [70]. UV‐induced SNVs, characterized by the SBS7 signature commonly found in melanoma, correspond closely to the mutations generated by TLS polymerases during replication over CPD sites [18, 71]. Patients with the XP‐variant form of xeroderma pigmentosum carry mutations in the POLH gene encoding pol η [72, 73].

Genomic Instability Inductions by CPDs but Not by 6–4PPs

The primary DNA lesions caused by UV exposure are CPDs and 6–4PPs, raising the question of which of these is responsible for genomic instability. A recent study demonstrated that both SVs and SNVs are induced by CPDs but not by 6–4PPs (Figure 4) [38]. This difference correlates with γH2AX expression patterns. γH2AX foci can form in response to 6–4PPs, and these lesions trigger cell cycle arrest, allowing time for repair prior to DNA replication [38]. In contrast, because CPDs are inefficiently repaired and do not induce cell cycle arrest, DNA replication proceeds in their presence [38]. Consequently, genomic instability arises during DNA replication when CPDs remain unrepaired. The mechanistic distinction between 6–4PPs and CPDs likely reflects the degree of helical distortion they introduce. Specifically, 6–4PPs cause more pronounced distortion than CPDs [74, 75]. Consistent with this, 6–4PPs are repaired broadly across the genome, as expected for lesions preferentially recognized by GG‐NER, which primarily targets DNA helical distortions.

SV and SNV formation in the presence of CPDs. (A) UV irradiation induces both 6–4PPs and CPDs. 6–4PPs, which induce γH2AX foci, are associated with cell cycle arrest and hence are repaired before the onset of DNA replication. Resulting cells undergo the S phase in the presence of CPDs. (B and C) Replication stress‐associated DSBs that elevate SV‐formation risk are induced in the presence of CPDs within actively transcribed genes, thereby promoting clonal evolution in cells harboring mutations in cancer‐driver genes (B). In contrast, SV‐formation risk decreases once CPDs in inactive genes are repaired through TC‐NER, although CPDs are still continuously accumulated in heterochromatin regions (C). Those CPDs are likely associated with SNV induction, especially associated with the SBS7 signature.

CPD‐Induced DSB Formation During TC‐NER and DNA Replication

Following UV irradiation, DSBs that elevate the risk of genomic instability are generated during the subsequent S phase. However, not all CPDs contribute to DSB formation. DSBs are primarily induced at CPD sites located within actively transcribed genes, owing to the involvement of TC‐NER [38]. Consequently, DSB formation depends on both transcriptional activity and DNA replication. The level of DSBs decreases once CPDs within active genes are repaired, even though CPDs remain abundant in heterochromatic regions, which are transcriptionally inactive [38].

UV‐Related SBS7 Signature Induction in the Presence of CPDs

UV‐associated SNVs are characterized by the SBS7 mutational signature [16, 18]. Human melanoma genomes can be broadly categorized into two clusters based on SBS profiles: (1) Cluster 1, exhibiting a massive accumulation of SBS7‐associated SNVs that occur independently of SVs, with significantly higher SNV density in non‐transcribed regions than in transcribed regions; and (2) Cluster 2, showing fewer SBS7‐associated SNVs but higher SV levels, suggesting an SV‐dependent mutation pattern [38]. SBS7‐associated SNVs located near SV breakpoints have also been observed in the in vitro single UV‐exposure model, supporting the relevance to those in human melanoma [38]. Although the overall mutation burden of melanoma—particularly in Cluster 1—is among the highest across all cancer types, the majority of these SNVs are biasedly accumulated in non‐transcribed regions where CPDs persist due to the absence of TC‐NER activity. Thus, these SNVs are likely generated by low‐fidelity TLS polymerases during DNA replication across unrepaired CPD sites.

The genomic landscapes of cancers associated with CS and XP have not yet been well characterized. Based on the current evidence, it is conceivable that cancers associated with CS and XP may preferentially correspond to clusters 1 and 2, respectively, given that TLS polymerases primarily mediate SBS7 signature induction whereas TC‐NER intermediates are implicated in SV formation.

Perspectives

Accumulating evidence suggests that the cancer risk associated with IR and UV exposure is largely attributable to an increased risk of genomic instability. Although IR and UV irradiation generate multiple types of DNA lesions, replication stress‐associated DSBs appear to be the critical form of damage responsible for promoting genomic instability in both contexts. These DSBs arise during the subsequent S phase following irradiation. This mechanism is analogous to that observed in BRCA1/2‐mutated cells, which are defective in repairing replication stress‐associated DSBs, leading to erroneous repair and the consequent induction of genomic instability. Thus, replication stress‐associated DSBs may represent a generalizable form of DNA damage underlying genomic instability across various contexts. Furthermore, these findings indicate that cells exposed to IR or UV accumulate DSBs at HR intermediates. However, it remains unclear how such DSBs persist at HR intermediates without being fully repaired, despite the presence of genetically intact DNA repair machinery.
