Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis reveal metabolic responses of Eriocheir sinensis to Aeromonas hydrophila infection
Authors: Tongtong Kong, Hanyu Liu, Chun Liu, Yaoying Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAeromonas hydrophila, a widespread aquatic pathogen, can infect a range of aquatic organisms, such as fish and crustaceans (including Eriocheir sinensis). Understanding the host resistance mechanism against A. hydrophila infection is of significant importance. ResultsIn this study, the metabolic and transcriptomic profiles of crabs (E. sinensis) at different stages of A. hydrophila infection (early-infection stage: Ah_6h, mid-infection stage: Ah_24h, and late-infection stage: Ah_72h) were investigated. Metabolomic analysis showed that differentially expressed metabolites were predominantly enriched in purine metabolism pathways. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the infection might activate the crab’s immune response through key signaling pathways, including NF-κB and RIG-I-like receptor pathways, at the early-infection stage, while potentially maintaining immune function throughout infection via apoptosis, phagocytosis, and lysosomal pathways. Notably, the mid-infection stage was the pivotal period in the regulation of the crab’s immune response, with the highest levels of differential metabolites and genes. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis further highlighted the potential key roles of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and purine and pyrimidine metabolisms in the immune response of E. sinensis against A. hydrophila.ConclusionsThese findings provide a better understanding of the immunity of E. sinensis in response to bacterial infection.Keywords: Eriocheir sinensis, Aeromonas hydrophila, Metabolome, Transcriptome

Background

The Chinese mitten crab (Eriocheir sinensis), an important aquaculture species, is highly valued by consumers due to its delicious meat and high nutritional value. However, during the crab farming process, bacterial and viral diseases have severely constrained the development of the crab aquaculture industry [1]. Among these, bacterial diseases, particularly those caused by Aeromonas hydrophila, have emerged as one of the primary diseases in aquaculture [2]. A. hydrophila is a widely distributed aquatic bacterium capable of infecting fish and crustaceans [2, 3]. Antibiotics are commonly used in aquaculture to control diseases. However, research has reported that A. hydrophila is increasingly resistant to antibiotics, with residual antibiotics posing significant risks to human health [4]. Moreover, the increasing antibiotic resistance of A. hydrophila impedes effective disease control in aquatic animals (including Chinese mitten crabs) and poses a major challenge to the development of the aquaculture industry. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the host immune response is essential for effective prevention and control of A. hydrophila infections.

E. sinensis is widely thought to combat pathogens through two innate immune pathways: cellular immunity and humoral immunity [5]. Studies on cellular immunity have mostly focused on hemocyte populations, phagocytic capacity, and apoptosis, whereas humoral immunity has mainly addressed antimicrobial peptides, lectins, and the phenoloxidase system [6, 7]. Current studies have demonstrated that A. hydrophila infection in crab results in a reduction in hemocyte population and an increase in phenoloxidase activity, expression of antimicrobial peptides, and hemocyte phagocytic activity [8, 9]. Furthermore, studies of immune-related genes in E. sinensis is increasing. For instance, the autophagy-related proteins, ATG5 and ATG7, have been reported to be important in modulating the immune response of E. sinensis against Spiroplasma eriocheiris infection [10]. The transmembrane cytoplasmic tail of Dscam in the E. sinensis regulates antibacterial responses by regulating genes involved in cell proliferation in hemocytes [11]. Class B scavenger receptor and nuclear factor Akiri have been reported to promote the clearance of A. hydrophila by upregulating antimicrobial peptide expression [11, 12]. Studies on the immune response of E. sinensis to A. hydrophila have made significant progress, but have primarily focused on the role of individual genes.

Omics technology has greatly enhanced our understanding of host-pathogen interactions. Integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis enables comprehensive evaluation of differential gene expression and metabolite profiles, thereby providing further insight into biological processes [13, 14]. This approach has been successfully applied to elucidate immune response in plant–pathogen interactions [15]. In intestinal porcine epithelial cells, key metabolites and regulatory factors associated with transmissible gastroenteritis virus infection have been revealed through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis [16]. While this approach has been performed to investigate the responses of Scylla paramamosain to white spot syndrome virus (WSSV) or Vibrio parahaemolyticus [17, 18], it has not yet been applied to study E. sinensis infection by A. hydrophila. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying E. sinensis infection by A. hydrophila, we performed transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of E. sinensis hemolymph at different time points following A. hydrophila infection. By integrating pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes and metabolites, we uncovered distinct immune responses of E. sinensis across early, middle, and late stages of infection.

Materials and methods

Animals and samples preparation

Healthy E. sinensis (mean weight approximately 50 g) were purchased from a commercial aquaculture farm located in Nanyang Lake, Shandong, China. Prior to the experiments, the crabs were acclimated in oxygenated freshwater at about 23 °C for a week. A. hydrophila was cultured in Tryptic-Soy-Broth at 28 °C until 0.6–0.8 of OD600. Then, the bacterial cells were collected and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as previously described [18]. Because the A. hydrophila suspension was prepared in PBS, an equivalent volume of sterile PBS was injected into the control group to eliminate any effects caused by the injection vehicle itself. Subsequently, each crab was injected with 100 µL of A. hydrophila suspension (2 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) or 100 µL of PBS (control group) through the base of the fourth pereiopod. Hemolymph samples were collected at 6-, 24-, and 72-hours post-injection using a randomized sampling method. At each time point, hemolymph from five crabs was pooled into a single sample, with three pooled samples generated per time point, encompassing a total of 15 crabs per time point. To prepare metabolomic samples, hemolymph was mixed with an equal volume of EDTA anticoagulant (1.5 g/L), centrifuged at 3,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomic sequencing [17]. For transcriptomic analysis, hemolymph was mixed with an equal volume of anticoagulant solution (27 mmol/L sodium citrate, 336 mmol/L NaCl, 115 mmol/L glucose, 9 mmol/L EDTA, pH 7.0), then centrifuged at 800 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The collected hemocytes were collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent transcriptomic sequencing.

Extraction and quantification of metabolites

For metabolite extraction, the hemolymph samples (100 µL) were resuspended in a pre-chilled solution of 80% methanol and 0.1% formic acid, incubated on ice for 5 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was diluted to a final concentration of 53% methanol by LC-MS grade water, and then centrifuged (at 15,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C). Finally, the supernatant was used for lipid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis by Novogene Company (Beijing, China).

Metabolites were separated using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Hypersil GOLD column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) at 0.2 mL/min with a 17-minute linear gradient using 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0) and methanol as mobile phases. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive™ HF-X system with electrospray ionization (ESI) with standard instrument parameters. Raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 for peak alignment, feature detection, and quantification. Molecular formula prediction was based on adduct, molecular ion, and fragment ion information. Features were matched against mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList for compound identification and relative quantification. Statistical analyses were performed using R (R version R-3.4.3), Python (Python 2.7.6 version), and CentOS (CentOS release 6.6), with area normalization applied when data were not normally distributed.

Metabolomic data analysis

Metabolite annotation was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/metabolites). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using metaX. Univariate analysis (t-test) was applied to assess statistical significance. Metabolites with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1, P < 0.05, and a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 were defined as differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs). DAMs were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis.

RNA extraction, library construction, and RNA-Seq

Total RNA was isolated from hemocytes of E. sinensis using TRIzol® Reagent (Ambion, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated utilizing the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit in conjunction with the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The sequencing library was prepared using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA) and assessed for quality using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The subsequent RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) process was carried out on the Illumina Novaseq platform by Novogene Company (Beijing, China).

Transcriptome profiling

Clean reads were obtained from the raw data by removing reads containing adapters, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. Transcriptome assembly was subsequently accomplished using Trinity. Meanwhile, Q20, Q30 and GC content of the clean data were calculated. Assembled unigenes were annotated using public databases, including Nr (NCBI non-redundant protein sequences), Nt (NCBI non-redundant nucleotide sequences), Pfam (protein families), KOG/COG (clusters of orthologous groups of proteins), Swiss-Prot (a manually curated protein sequence database), KEGG Orthology (KO, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes Orthology), and GO (Gene Ontology). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0). Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs, including GO and KEGG pathway enrichment, was conducted using the cluster Profiler R package, designating significant enrichment at a corrected P-value < 0.05.

Validation of the transcriptomic data by RT-qPCR

To validate the transcriptomic expression profiles, five representative genes, including Heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90), Prophenoloxidase (ProPo), whey acidic protein (WAP), interferon gamma inducible lysosomal thiol reductase (GILT), and serine protease inhibitor (Serpin), were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. In addition, to determine whether PBS injection had any significant effect on gene expression, we additionally examined the expression profiles in uninjected (WT) crabs. Total RNA was extracted from hemocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the FastKing RT Kit with gDNase (Tiangen, China), with simultaneous removal of genomic DNA. RT-qPCR was carried out using the PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China), with β-actin as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct algorithm. Experiments were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess differences among groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis

To gain deeper insight into the dysregulated metabolic pathways, an integrated analysis of metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling was performed. DEGs and DAMs were simultaneously mapped onto the KEGG pathway database to identify overlapping pathways. This integrative approach facilitates the identification of key pathways that are co-regulated at both the transcriptional and metabolic levels, thereby providing mechanistic insights into the observed phenotypic alterations.

Results

Overview of metabolomic profiles

The distinct metabolic profiles between the A. hydrophila-infected groups and the control group (Control) were analyzed using PCA. Notably, metabolic profiles of the Ah_24h group exhibited the greatest differences compared to the other groups, whereas the differences between the Ah_6h and Ah_72h groups were negligible (Fig. 1A). A total of 63, 79, and 76 DAMs were significantly increased in the Ah_6h, Ah_24h, and Ah_72h groups, respectively, compared to the Control (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, comparative analysis across the three infection time points revealed that 18 metabolites were consistently altered at 6-, 24-, and 72-hours post-infection (Fig. 1C), suggesting their role in the response of crabs to A. hydrophila infection.

Changes in DAMs. (A) PCA of A. hydrophila-infected and control groups. (B) Statistical analysis of DAMs in A. hydrophila-infected group; metabolites with VIP > 1, P-value < 0.05, and FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 were considered DAMs. (C) Venn diagram showing the distribution of DAMs among different groups

KEGG pathway analysis of dams

To elucidate the metabolic pathways involved in the crab’s response to A. hydrophila infection, a systematic enrichment analysis of DAMs was conducted using the KEGG database. The results revealed that DAMs from infected crabs were primarily enriched in purine metabolism and other fundamental metabolic pathways (Fig. 2A-C). These findings indicated a potential involvement of purine metabolism in the antibacterial immune response of E. sinensis during A. hydrophila infection, although further studies are needed to elucidate the specific regulatory mechanisms involved.

Pathway functional enrichment of DAMs. KEGG analysis of DAMs between (A) Ah_6h and Control groups, (B) Ah_24h and Control groups, and (C) Ah_72h and Control groups

Transcriptome overview

The transcriptomic data, deposited in the Sequence Read Archive of the NCBI with the accession number of PRJNA1292138, yielded an average of 22,279,975, 22,542,629, 22,634,968, and 21,081,318 raw reads from the Control, Ah_6h, Ah_24h, and Ah_72h groups, respectively. Following the removal of low-quality reads, approximately 21,392,797, 21,618,667, 21,758,386, and 20,097,553 high-quality reads were retained from the Control, Ah_6h, Ah_24h, and Ah_72h, respectively. The average Q-scores of these high-quality reads at Q20 and Q30 ranged from 96.73% to 96.94% and 92.06% to 92.49%, respectively. The total GC content exhibited an average of 49.55%, with a standard deviation of 1.10% (Table 2). DEG analysis identified 2,561 upregulated and 1,002 downregulated genes in the Ah_6h group, 2,342 upregulated and 2,884 downregulated genes in the Ah_24h group, and 2,091 upregulated and 2,196 downregulated genes in the Ah_72h group (Fig. 3A). The Venn diagram revealed that 565 genes were differentially expressed across all three time points, suggesting their potential importance in the response of crabs to infection (Fig. 3B).

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO functional enrichment analysis provides insights into the functional distribution and biological mechanisms of gene regulatory networks under specific conditions. The results revealed that, in the Ah_6h group, the DEGs were predominantly enriched in GO terms related to biological processes and molecular functions, such as proteolysis, transposition, peptidase activity, transposase activity, and endopeptidase activity (Fig. 4A). In Ah_24h and Ah_72h groups, the DEGs were primarily enriched in the molecular functions, with the Ah_24h group showing enrichment in hydrolase activity and catalytic activity, and the Ah_72h group associated with hydrolase activity (Fig. 4B and C).

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs. GO enrichment analysis of DEGs in (A) Ah_6h, (B) Ah_24h, and (C) Ah_72h groups (BP: Biological process; MF: Molecular function)

KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs

To further reveal the role of DEGs in A. hydrophila infection, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. In the Ah_6h group, significant changes were observed in pathways involved in pathogen recognition and immune activation, including the NF-κB, RIG-I-like receptor, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways, and pathways associated with pathogen clearance, such as apoptosis, phagosome, lysosome, and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 5A). These results showed that immune-related pathways were significantly enriched during early stage of infection, indicating a rapid activation of the host defense system. In Ah_24h and Ah_72h groups, processes such as apoptosis, phagosome, lysosome, and antigen processing and presentation remained active, whereas gap junction, ribosome biogenesis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum were dysregulated (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, the Ah_72h group showed significant reduction in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, bile secretion, and purine metabolism (Fig. 5C).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs. KEGG analysis of DEGs between (A) Ah_6h and Control groups, (B) Ah_24h and Control groups, and (C) Ah_72h and Control groups

RT-qPCR validation of DEGs

To validate the transcriptome sequencing data, five DEGs (Hsp90, ProPo, WAP, GILT, and Serpin) were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. The results revealed that the mRNA expression patterns of these genes were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results (Fig. 6A), with upregulation of Hsp90, WAP and Serpin and downregulation of ProPo and GILT (Fig. 6B), thereby validating the reliability of the transcriptomic data. In addition, no significant differences in gene expression were detected between the uninjected (WT) and PBS-injected control groups, indicating that PBS injection itself did not trigger a measurable immune or transcriptional response.

Combined metabolome and transcriptome analysis

Combined metabolome and transcriptome analysis was conducted to explore the correlation between the two regulatory networks. In the Ah_6h group, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed alterations in purine metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and bile secretion pathways (Fig. 7A). In the Ah_24h group, significant changes were observed in bile secretion, purine metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle pathways (Fig. 7B). In the Ah_72h group, the prominently affected pathways included purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation pathways (Fig. 7C).

Integrated KEGG pathway analysis of DAMs and DEGs. Integrated KEGG pathway analysis of DAMs and DEGs between (A) Ah_6h and Control groups, (B) Ah_24h and Control groups, and (C) Ah_72h and Control groups

Among these enriched pathways, we conducted a detailed analysis of the dysregulated DAMs and DEGs to gain a comprehensive understanding of the underlying metabolic alterations. In the Ah_6h group, metabolites in the purine metabolism pathway (such as guanosine and inosine) and the genes 5’-NU, PNP, XDH, and URAD, were significantly upregulated, while xanthosine and urate, along with GUCY1A gene, were downregulated (Fig. 8A). In the Ah_24h group, the key intermediate in the TCA cycle (citrate), along with genes ACLY, ACO, LSC1, and LSC2, exhibited significant upregulation, while pckA was downregulated (Fig. 8B). In the Ah_72h group, components of the pyrimidine metabolism pathway exhibited differential regulation, with upregulation of methylmalonate, along with polA, rpoA, and nrdJ genes, and downregulation of 5′-NU and ALDH (Fig. 8C).

Discussion

A. hydrophila is one of the major pathogens of E. sinensis, which is associated with substantial economic losses in aquaculture [2]. Given the important role of the innate immune response in the host defense against bacterial infections, this study used an integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis to elucidate the temporal responses of E. sinensis at early, mid, and late stages of A. hydrophila infection. Results demonstrated that A. hydrophila infection at three time points led to significant alterations in metabolite accumulation and gene expression compared with the control group, with these DAMs and DEGs participating in several essential metabolic pathways.

PCA of the metabolome showed that the Ah_24h group showed the most distinct metabolic profile compared with the control group. Consistent with findings, this group also had the highest number of DAMs and DEGs, indicating that the mid-stage was a critical phase in the crab’s response to bacterial infection. Furthermore, a total of 18 metabolites and 565 genes were significantly altered during infection. The identified DAMs were mainly enriched in purine metabolism and other fundamental metabolic pathways. Notably, among these DEGs, the antibacterial protein (WAP) was significantly upregulated in E. sinensis after A. hydrophila infection, consistent with its previously reported response to LPS [20, 22]. Serpin, which plays a crucial role in innate immunity during bacterial infection [23], was found to be significantly upregulated in Manduca sexta larvae after Micrococcus lysodeikticus injection [24] and in Lampetra japonica after Vibrio anguillarum or Staphylococcus aureus infection [25]. This study found that Serpin was significantly upregulated in E. sinensis following A. hydrophila infection, further supporting its conserved immune regulatory function in host defense against bacterial infections.

GO enrichment analysis revealed a broader spectrum of enriched GO terms at the early-infection stage, whereas the number of enriched categories decreased at the middle and late stages of infection. This suggested that crabs may rapidly initiate diverse innate immune and stress responses at the early stage of infection, whereas these responses might become more focused as infection progresses. These findings are consistent with previous observations in Drosophila, where bacterial infection induced dynamic and time-dependent changes in both miRNA and mRNA expression profiles [26]. Interestingly, we found that DEGs associated with hydrolase activity were significantly enriched at all stages of A. hydrophila infection in E. sinensis. Hydrolases participate in physiological and metabolic pathways and play an important role in the host’s innate immune response [27]. Transcriptome sequencing in black rockfish (Sebastes schlegelii) has revealed significant changes in hydrolases activity following Edwardsiella tarda infection [28]. Moreover, increased hydrolase activity has been shown to enhance potato resistance against Rhizoctonia solani infection [29]. Taken together, these findings suggest a potentially important role of hydrolases in the host immune defense against bacterial infection.

Numerous studies have shown that apoptosis, phagocytosis, and the lysosomal pathway are important components of innate immunity, playing crucial roles in the host’s response to pathogen invasion [30–32]. For instance, apoptosis contributes to the resistance of E. sinensis against V. parahaemolyticus infection, whereas phagocytosis plays a key role in its defense against S. eriocheiris infection [33, 34]. The ubiquitination–autophagy–lysosome pathway constitutes a major branch of the lysosomal degradation system. Insights the Pacific oyster Crassostrea gigas further demonstrate that CgLRSAM1 acts as an E3 ligase mediating anti-Vibrio-associated ubiquitination and regulates bacterial degradation through the ubiquitination–autophagy–lysosome pathway [35]. Consistent with these findings, the present study found significant enrichment of apoptosis, phagosome, and lysosome at all stages of infection, reflecting their critical roles in the antibacterial response of crabs. Similarly, the NF-κB, RIG-I-like receptor, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways are well-documented in pathogen recognition and immune activation [36–38]. Many studies support the view that the canonical NF-κB–dependent signaling pathway is activated in response to bacterial or fungal infections and also contributes to antiviral defense [39, 40]. In Bombyx mori, for example, the viral small peptide vSP27 activates the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, which in turn induces the production of antimicrobial peptides that inhibit B. mori cypovirus infection [41]. In addition, the RIG-I–like receptor and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways have been reported to participate in the antibacterial response of Macrobrachium rosenbergii [42]. Here, in the Ah_6h group, numerous DEGs were enriched in these pathways, further supporting their involvement in the crab’s immune defense against bacterial infection. Additionally, the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway has been demonstrated to contribute to the antibacterial immune response [43]. Our results found that, in the Ah_72h group, DAMs were significantly enriched in this pathway. This is similar to the previous reports of dysregulated aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway in the hepatopancreas of Litopenaeus vannamei following Vibrio alginolyticus infection [44].

Purine metabolism plays an important regulatory role in physiological and pathological processes, including anti-tumor immunity, host antibacterial defense, and the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease [45–47]. Previous studies have shown significant changes in the purine metabolism pathway in S. paramamosain infected with WSSV and in L. vannamei infected with V. alginolyticus [18, 38]. In this study, integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis revealed notable changes in the purine metabolism pathway in the Ah_6h group, suggesting the importance of the purine metabolism pathway in the crab’s response to bacterial infection. Furthermore, our findings revealed the dysregulated metabolites in the TCA cycle (in the Ah_24h group) and the pyrimidine metabolism pathway (in the Ah_72h group). The TCA cycle, a central metabolic pathway in aerobic organisms, serves as the final common pathway for carbohydrates, fat, and protein metabolism [48]. Meanwhile, the pyrimidine metabolism pathway plays an important role in RNA and DNA synthesis, protein biosynthesis, and antimicrobial immunity [49, 50]. Prior research has shown that both the TCA cycle and pyrimidine metabolism pathway are critically involved in the immune response of L. vannamei to V. parahaemolyticus infection [51], which aligns with our current results.

Conclusions

In summary, this study explored the immune response of E. sinensis to A. hydrophila infection by analyzing enriched pathways of DAMs and DEGs. Our findings revealed that during the early stage of infection, crabs might activate immune responses through classic signaling pathways, including NF-κB and RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathways, while sustaining immunity via apoptosis, phagocytosis, and the lysosomal pathway. Notably, the mid-infection stage showed the highest number of DAMs and DEGs, suggesting a crucial phase for immune regulation. In addition, the TCA cycle and purine and pyrimidine metabolism pathways appeared to be potentially important in the immune response of E. sinensis to A. hydrophila infection. However, experimental studies are needed to confirm and elucidate their specific regulatory mechanisms.
