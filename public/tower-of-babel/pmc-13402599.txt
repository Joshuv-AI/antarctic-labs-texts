Intermetallic charge redistribution restructures the oxygen-bound intermediate network for efficient ethylene electrosynthesis
Authors: Limin Liu, Rongxin Xia, Chen Deng, Huiyan Zhang, Shangqian Zhu, Qi Hao, Xunhua Zhao, Daolun Liang, Jason Chun-Ho Lam, Dekui Shen, Wenlei Zhu, Richen Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe precise regulation of Cu surface electronic structure governs C-C coupling pathways and intermediate adsorption to enhance ethylene selectivity. However, how heteroatom dopants modulate the flux of oxygen-bound intermediates remains unclear. Herein, we establish a predictive framework based on six dopant elements’ electron orbital characteristics, demonstrating that p-orbital metal doping enables favorable orbital-center proximity for hybridization with Cu active centers. Al-incorporated Cu balances adsorption affinities for *CO, *H, and *O, thereby reducing the *OCCO formation barrier. Controlled Al doping in CuAl single-atom alloy (CuAlSA) induces lattice expansion and d-band center downshifting (ΔεCu = −2.94 eV), achieving favorable d-p orbital proximity (δd, p = −1.00 eV) and a low C-C coupling energy barrier (ΔE = 0.30 eV). In situ Raman spectroscopy confirms that the optimized d-p proximity promotes C-C bond formation and *OCCO hydrogenation to *CH2CHO, redirecting intermediate flux from methane toward ethylene. CuAlSA consequently exhibits 78.8% ethylene Faraday efficiency under pure CO2 and retains 70.2% under 15% CO2. This work establishes a strategy for directing oxygen-bound intermediates in CO2-to-C2H4 electrosynthesis.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Catalytic mechanisms, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

Copper-based catalysts have demonstrated remarkable efficiency in electrochemically converting CO2 to multi-carbon products, with ethylene (C2H4) being a primary target. However, their broad product distribution-gas (methane, olefins) and liquid-phase products (ethanol, acetic acid, n-propanol)- combined with limited production rates, presents critical challenges for industrial implementation1. Achieving C2H4 selectivity necessitates precise control over intermediate flux direction and stabilities for avoiding reaction pathway bifurcation2. Central to this challenge is the fate of surface-adsorbed *CO, the pivotal intermediate in ethylene electrosynthesis3,4. Its transformation is governed by tripartite adsorption equilibria: weak *CO binding induces premature desorption as CO5, while excessive *CO/*H affinities drive parasitic hydrocarbon formation via over-hydrogenation6. Expected selectivity emerges only under balanced *CO, *H, and *O adsorption strengths that promote *CO dimerization to form oxygen-bound intermediates (e.g., *OCCO, *CH2CHO, and *CHO), which are determined as selectivity-determining intermediates (SDIs) for C2H4 production7. Stabilizing these SDIs requires fine-tuned modulation of *CO, *H, and *O binding energies. Established strategies for binding energy regulation-heteroatom doping8, grain-boundary engineering, and interfacial functionalization9-demonstrate varying efficacy10. Metal-atom doping proves particularly effective by altering active-site electronic states through charge redistribution and orbital hybridization. Dopants with distinct valence states induce localized charge density variations11, generating hybridization profiles that dictate adsorption energetics. However, prevailing approaches predominantly focus on *CO adsorption optimization, neglecting the coupled energetics of *H and *O species12,13. This oversight stems from the absence of a unified framework linking dopant-induced electronic perturbations to triadic adsorption equilibria (*CO-*H-*O). Critically, existing *CO-centric design paradigms fail to address the need for simultaneous control over all three intermediates, a prerequisite for steering the reaction network toward specific C2 products. The lack of mechanistic understanding regarding dopant-mediated synergies between charge redistribution, lattice strain, and orbital hybridization further impedes the rational design of Cu-based catalysts.

Fundamentally, modulating active-site affinities for SDIs hinges on precise electronic structure engineering of catalysts, where the d-band center position serves as the primary theoretical descriptor. Current strategies for tuning Cu d-orbitals predominantly employ single-parameter approaches-either dopant concentration variation or elemental substitution14. These methods remain intrinsically limited by host material constraints, providing only partial control over the interdependent *CO, *H, and *O intermediate binding energetics. The incorporation of heteroatoms-spanning isolated single atoms to clustered configurations-introduces facet-specific lattice strain that simultaneously modulates electronic structure and enhances d-d/p orbital-center proximity15. Dopant loading-dependent strain fields drive orbital hybridization-mediated adjustments in active-site spatial distribution, inducing localized charge polarization that reorganizes d-orbital occupancy16, modifies crystal-field splitting parameters, and alters electronic bandwidth. Nevertheless, prevailing investigations disproportionately focus on aligning the Fermi level with electronic structure centers17, while neglecting critical couplings between Cu and dopant electronic states. This oversight masks the authentic electron-transfer pathways and orbital hybridization mechanisms, consequently obscuring dopant-induced cascading effects on proximal active sites and critical intermediates. Notably, the cooperative interplay between dopant-Cu orbital proximity and its dual role in governing triadic adsorption affinities (*CO, *H, *O) and modulating activation barriers for *OCCO → *CH2CHO transformations remains unexplored in practical CO2 electroreduction systems18. Existing frameworks fragmentarily address individual adsorbate binding energetics, failing to integrate electronic-structure modulation with the concerted conversion kinetics of coupled intermediates. This disconnect underscores the necessity for atomic-scale strategies that synergize intrinsic dopant electronic properties with lattice-strain effects to engineer *CO-*H-*O species adsorption networks, thereby steering the reaction intermediates’ network toward selective C2H4 production.

Herein, we incorporate s/p/d-block metals (Mg, Ca; Al, Bi; Co, Zn) with distinct electronic configurations into copper crystals. The dopants’ characteristic orbital energy distributions induce localized charge polarization, creating d/p-band center proximity, defined as the energy offset between Cu and dopant d/p-band centers, alongside surface charge redistribution. Aluminum emerges as the suitable dopant: its p-band electronic structure enables near-perfect alignment with Cu’s d-band during hybridization (δd, p = −1.00), maximizing orbital overlap. This Al-induced electronic restructuring synergistically tunes intermediate binding strengths through d-band center downshifting (Δε, Cu = −2.94 eV) and lattice strain (Δr = 1.44%), achieving balanced adsorption energetics for *CO (−0.24 eV), *H (−0.09 eV), and *O (−0.55 eV). Controlled Al doping concentrations enhance Cu 3d-Al 2p orbital hybridization, boosting C2H4 production while suppressing parasitic CH4 formation via *CHO pathway inhibition. The dopant’s oxygen affinity critically regulates C-O bond scission in *CH2CHO intermediates. Al’s strong *O binding (vs Zn’s weaker interaction) preferentially stabilizes C2 pathways. This electronic steering shifts product distribution from mixed CH4/C2H4 to C2H4 dominance. The resulting catalyst delivers 78.8% ethylene Faraday efficiency at −1.4 V vs RHE with current density reaching 530 mA cm−2 in pure CO2, retaining 70.2% efficiency under 15% CO2, demonstrating potential conversion of industrially relevant dilute CO2 streams.

Results

D-band theory-guided principles for bi-metallic alloy electrocatalyst design

The CO intermediate emerges through sequential proton-coupled electron transfer steps (*CO2 → *COOH → *CO) and serves as the critical determinant for C2 product formation. The multi-electron transfer complexity inherent (Fig. S1) to electrochemical CO2 reduction (ECO2R) drives three distinct branching pathways from adsorbed *CO, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. P1: Weak *CO binding induces premature desorption, favoring CO evolution while suppressing C-C coupling. P2: Moderate *CO binding stabilizes adsorbed species, enabling C-C dimerization toward liquid/gaseous C2 products. P3: Strong *CO/*H co-adsorption drives over-hydrogenation to methane, with excessive *CO binding risking catalyst poisoning19. Existing studies on the reaction pathways of adsorbed *CO have identified *OCCO and *CHO as the key SDIs that govern the formation of ethylene and methane, respectively (*OCCO-mediated C-C coupling pathway constitutes the dominant pathway for ethylene formation, Fig. S2). These intermediates represent critical branching points in the reaction network, where stabilization of *OCCO directs the pathway toward C-C coupling and ethylene production, while *CHO favors competitive hydrogenation to methane.

DFT calculations were employed to evaluate the favorability of the *CO-to-SDI pathway. The binding strength of *CO, *H, and *O at the active sites was systematically analyzed, serving as key indicators to provide theoretical insights into the factors that govern selectivity toward desired target products20. Based on previous studies on the influence of H2O dissociation ability on C2H4 selectivity21, we hypothesize that a moderate *CO binding energy, coupled with relatively weak *H binding energy, is favorable for promoting the *CO-to-*OCCO reaction. This hypothesis guided our computational screening of six single-site dopants (Mg, Ca from s-block; Al, Bi from p-block; Co, Zn from d-block) in Cu matrices, denoted as CuX, to modulate the d-band electronic structure of Cu. Selected based on their distinct electronic configurations and distribution of energy levels, these dopants enable precise modulation of Cu’s d-band structure to tailor *CO-*H-*O adsorption networks, thereby optimizing SDI formation toward selective C2H4 production.

The electronic perturbation induced by metal doping in copper matrices exhibits strong element-dependence, governed by the intrinsic density of states (DOS) profiles and orbital characteristics of dopants. These factors collectively dictate charge redistribution dynamics and orbital hybridization patterns at active sites. Representative transition metals such as Co and Zn possess d-orbital energy levels comparable to that of the Cu 3 d orbital, resulting in significant d-d orbital hybridization effects (Fig. S3, S4). Co’s interaction induces notable changes in the electronic structure of the Cu surface, prominently shifting the Cu d-band center closer to the Fermi level (Fig. 1b). Nevertheless, the disparate electronegativities of Cu (10.2), Co (11.9), and Zn (15.9) correspond to variations in valence-electron distribution and electron-transfer propensity, resulting in distinct degrees of d-d orbital hybridization22. For instance, doping with Zn enhances electronic delocalization23, shifting the d-band center to lower energy levels (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, because the d-orbital electron distribution in transition metals is concentrated near the Fermi level within a narrow energy range, the modulation of the electronic structure via d-d orbital hybridization is relatively localized, exerting only a limited impact on the core electronic states.

To achieve precise electronic structure engineering in Cu lattices, we selected s/p-block dopants (Mg, Ca, Al, Bi) based on their anisotropic orbital distributions and distinct hybridization potentials (Fig. S3, S4). The directional character of spherical s-orbitals and dumbbell-shaped p-orbitals induces broadened energy-level dispersion (Fig. S4), enabling effective modulation of deeper valence-band electrons in Cu24. The strong s/p-d orbital hybridization of main-group elements, exemplified by dopants such as Mg, Ca, Al, and Bi (electronegativity: Mg, 7.7, Ca, 6.1, Al, 9.1, and Bi, 10.7)22, significantly modulates the electronic structure of adjacent Cu atoms, effectively tuning the energy levels of active sites away from the Fermi level (Fig. 1b)25,26. Additionally, p-d orbital hybridization enhances the structural stability of the Cu lattice19. For example, Al doping significantly stabilizes the lattice due to Al’s high oxophilicity and low electronegativity, facilitating electron donation from the p-block metal to adjacent Cu active sites. This electron transfer effectively reduces the oxidation tendency of Cu under open-circuit potential conditions. The interplay between s/p-d hybridization emerges as a key factor in tuning the electronic properties and stability of Cu-based catalysts. Energy offset between the d/p-band centers (d/p-band center proximity) can serve as a quantitative descriptor of orbital hybridization between s/p/d-block metal and Cu. A minimal energy disparity facilitates orbital overlap and hybridization27, with Al and Bi exhibiting the smallest d/p-band center proximity and thus suitable hybridization and electron delocalization (Fig. S5). This augmented orbital interaction fortifies the Al-Cu chemical bond, thereby modulating the material’s stability and electronic properties.

While d/p-band center proximity provides initial insights into catalyst electronic structure, it fails to fully rationalize the divergent selectivity between Bi- and Al-doped systems or adequately capture oxygen-binding effects. We therefore employ combined *CO/*H/*O adsorption energies as composite descriptors to predict selectivity in the post-CO hydrogenation-deoxygenation cascade. Dopant-induced electronic perturbations create distinct local bonding environments that directly modulate intermediate adsorption behavior (Fig. S6a, b) and SDI formation barriers (Fig. S6c, d). D-block transition metals, such as Zn (−0.53 eV for *CO, −0.59 eV for *H) and Co (−0.17 eV for *CO, −0.55 eV for *H), induce an upward shift in the d-band center, thereby strengthening *CO and *H binding due to the strong electronic localization of their d-orbitals, which facilitates stable intermediate adsorption28. Comparative energy barrier analysis for competing reaction pathways, *CHO formation vs. *OCCO dimerization (Fig. S6d), indicates that Zn doping preferentially favors the *CO-to-*CHO pathway leading to CH4 formation. Conversely, doping with s- and p-block metals of low electronegativity (e.g., Mg, Ca, Al, and Bi) induces a downward shift in the d-band center, reflecting enhanced electronic delocalization and moderate electron-donating capabilities of the dopant atoms (Fig. 1b). This shift weakens *H binding and reduces the energy barriers for the rate-limiting *CO-to-*OCCO step relative to *CHO formation (Figs. 1c, S6d), thereby promoting C-C coupling reactions.

As the Cu d-band center shifts, the *OCCO formation barrier (free energy of transition state, TS) exhibits noticeable variations, initially decreasing and subsequently increasing as the d-band center descends (Fig. S7). This observation aligns with literature findings, which show that elements with higher valence electron activity, such as Co and Zn, possess high-energy valence electrons that are more easily excited or transferred, resulting in enhanced reactivity29. Using *H binding energy as an indicator of proton affinity30, the ranking of doped catalysts is as follows: CuZn > CuCo > Cu > CuBi > CuAl > CuMg > CuCa (Fig. 1d). This ordering reflects the catalysts’ varying abilities to facilitate *CO protonation, a critical step in pathway selection. Notably, CuMg (−0.31 eV for *CO, −0.05 eV for *H) and CuAl (−0.24 eV for *CO, −0.09 eV for *H) exhibit moderate *H and *CO binding energies, paired with reduced activation barriers for C-C coupling compared with initial Cu (−0.51 eV for *CO, −0.46 eV for *H)31,32. These attributes promote the critical *CO-to-*OCCO reaction step while effectively suppressing the competing pathway leading to CH4 formation via *CO → *CHO → *CH2O → *CH3O → CH4 (Fig. S1). High ethylene-selectivity catalysts typically feature low *H binding energies (<−0.1 eV) and moderate *CO binding energies (from −0.25 to −0.35 eV), suggesting that CuAl and CuMg may be particularly effective for enhancing C-C coupling activity, as illustrated by the volcano-type relationship in Fig. 1d. The impact of proton affinity and the hydrogenation capability of catalysts on product distribution was examined under acidic conditions (Fig. S8). The pH of the KHCO3 electrolyte was adjusted to 1 using 1 M H3PO4, creating a proton-rich electrochemical environment. Under these conditions, CuZn, Cu, and CuCo catalysts, which exhibit strong proton-binding affinities, show pronounced interactions with hydrogen species, resulting in a Faraday efficiency for the hydrogen evolution reaction (FEHER) of up to 52% and FECH4 up to 38.8%. In contrast, the CuAl catalyst still delivers a sustained ethylene selectivity under the same acidic conditions, reaching 48.5%, suggesting it suppressed HER activity and favorable C-C coupling capability even in a highly protonated environment.

According to previous studies20, the binding strength of the active center to *O plays a crucial role in determining the selectivity and distribution of C2+ liquid products. Strong *O binding facilitates C-O bond cleavage in *CO, resulting in *OH formation (further hydrogenation towards H2O) and subsequent H2O desorption20. In contrast, weaker *O binding supports the hydrogenation of *OCCO intermediates (e.g., *OCCO → *CHOCO → *CCO → *CHCO → *CHCHO → *CH2CHO) while preserving the C-O bond within the intermediates, ultimately driving the formation of C2H5OH. Computational analysis of *O binding strengths across various doped combinations reveals the sequence: Ca > Mg > Al > Co > Cu > Zn > Bi (Fig. 1d). Elements with weaker oxophilicity, such as Co, Zn, and Bi, exhibit lower *O binding strengths, resulting in greater stabilization of oxygen-containing intermediates compared to metals with strong oxophilicity, thereby favoring reaction pathways that retain the C-O bond in intermediates2. In contrast, Ca, Al, and Mg, with stronger *O binding capabilities, are more conducive to the formation of highly reduced products like C2H4 (Fig. S9, S10).

The affinities for *CO, *H, and *O are essential descriptors for predicting C-C coupling efficiency and optimizing the formation of C2 hydrocarbon products. Among the evaluated doping strategies, Mg and Al demonstrated well-matched alignment with these performance criteria (Fig. 1e). However, the high reactivity of Mg and Ca in aqueous environments (a typical characteristic of alkali earth metals) and their susceptibility to oxidation under ambient conditions shifted the focus of this study toward the development and investigation of CuAl alloy catalysts as a more stable and practical alternative.

Electrochemical evaluation of pulse-electrodeposited catalysts (CuAl, CuBi, CuCo, CuZn, pristine Cu) revealed distinct product distributions governed by adsorption energetics. CuAl catalysts, exhibiting moderate *CO and *H binding alongside strong *O adsorption (−0.24 eV for *CO, −0.09 eV for *H, −0.55 eV for *O), achieved a high FEethylene (65%, Figs. 1f, S11). In contrast, Cu and CuZn catalysts with robust *CO (−0.51 eV for Cu, −0.53 for CuZn) and *H binding (−0.46 eV for Cu, −0.59 for CuZn) preferentially promoted the deep hydrogenation of *CO towards CH4 generation, as reflected by the selectivity of CH4 (36% for Cu, 35% for CuZn) observed in Fig. S11. Weak *CO adsorption in CuBi and CuCo systems promoted CO desorption, increasing gaseous CO selectivity (8.1% for CuBi, 15% for CuCo, Fig. S12). Catalysts with weak *O binding (e.g., Co, Zn, and Bi) exhibit enhanced formation of liquid-phase products (including formate, ethanol, acetate, and n-propanol), whereas those with strong *O binding (e.g., Al and Cu) preferentially produce hydrocarbon compounds (Fig. S13), yielding a lower hydrocarbon-to-oxygenate ratio (Fig. 1g). Consequently, subsequent studies will focus on optimizing the Al doping level in CuAl catalysts.

Structural investigation of electrocatalysts

Guided by first-principles calculations, we engineered a series of Al-doped Cu catalysts via pulsed electrodeposition to systematically tune electronic distributions at active sites and regulate electrochemical kinetics (Fig. S14). By varying Cu: Al precursor molar ratios, we synthesized Cu100-xAlx alloys (x = 0, 2, 5, 8, 10, 14, 20; Table S1) with controlled dopant incorporation. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) quantified Al contents as 0.85 wt%, 2.19 wt%, 3.56 wt%, 4.51 wt%, and 6.47 wt% (Table S2, Fig. S15), confirming compositionally graded doping. Based on atomic ratios, samples were classified into three distinct groups: undoped Cu nanoparticles (Cu NP, x = 0), single-atom Al-doped Cu (CuAlSA, 0 <x ≤ 8), and Al nanoparticle-incorporated Cu (CuAlNP, x > 8).

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analysis confirms that the crystal structure belongs to the cubic morphology (Fig. 2a). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping reveals homogeneous spatial distribution of Cu and Al throughout the material architecture (Fig. 2b). In both Cu NP and CuAlSA samples, periodic bright-field contrast variations and well-defined lattice fringes (Fig. S16) confirm substitutional incorporation of isolated Al atoms into the Cu matrix. At low Al doping levels (CuAlSA), TEM images show discrete low-intensity regions corresponding to individual dopant sites. Increased Al loading induces progressive aggregation of these regions into contiguous domains, accompanied by Al-Al bond formation and cluster nucleation (Table S3). Lattice expansion induced by Al substitution is evidenced by increased interplanar spacing measurements, consistent with DFT-optimized crystal models (Table S4). The grazing incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD) results reveal that they exhibit characteristic Cu (111), (200), and (220) peaks (PDF#04-0836) without detectable Al crystalline phases, confirming atomic-level dispersion of Al dopants in Cu lattice. The comparison of XRD patterns across all samples indicates a similar crystalline structure (Fig. S17). Progressive peak broadening and intensity reduction from Cu NP to Cu86Al14 reflect decreasing crystallinity and enhanced lattice distortion, correlating with low-coordination site generation. A systematic low-angle shift in the (111) peak position arises from lattice expansion (Fig. 2c) due to substitutional replacement of Cu atoms (r = 116 pm) by larger Al (r = 118 pm)33. Surface morphological analysis reveals densely packed cubic crystallites in all samples (Figs. S18, S19), demonstrating preserved long-range ordering despite localized lattice strain.

High-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis reveals the presence of coordinate states, including Cu2+, Cu+, and Cu0, on the surface of both Cu NP and CuAlSA samples (Fig. 2d). The characteristic peak at 931.8 eV corresponds to metallic Cu0/Cu+ states34, while Al 2p spectra reveal Al3+ signatures (Fig. S20), confirming surface oxidation of aluminum dopants with intensity proportional to doping concentration35. Ar+ sputtering depth-profile XPS reveals that, below the surface passivation layer, bulk Al increasingly exists in a Cu-Al coordinated environment with increasing sputtering depth (Fig. S21). Compared to pristine Cu NP, CuAlSA exhibits a 0.1 eV negative shift in Cu 2p3/2 binding energy, consistent with enhanced electron density at Cu sites through Al to Cu charge transfer driven by their electronegativity difference (Cu: 1.90 vs Al: 1.50). The Cu 2p3/2 peak broadens significantly from 1.07 eV (Cu NP) to 1.22 eV (CuAlSA), reflecting increased chemical state heterogeneity that includes metallic Cu0 domains, Cu-Al interfacial sites, and strain-induced Cuδ+ species. These results imply surface incorporation of Al atoms in the alloy catalyst compared to the pristine Cu NP, leading to the formation of abundant surface defects and undercoordinated Cu species. Al incorporation, by forming a solid solution alloy, induces defects and lattice distortions within the Cu crystalline matrix. These local defects break the periodicity of the Cu lattice, thereby placing a subset of Cu atoms in a lower coordination environment. XPS and ICP measurements further reveal a higher Al content on the catalyst surface, indicating that Al doping primarily occurs near the surface of the Cu lattice and induces lattice distortion that breaks Cu coordination symmetry20. (Table S2, Fig. S15). With the increase in Al doping, the XPS intensity of surface Al was significantly enhanced (Fig. S20)36. Low-energy electron-induced Auger electron spectroscopy (LMM) analysis confirms a limited Cu+ content (Fig. 2e) on the Cu NP surface, predominantly arising from ambient oxidation during sample transfer from the electrochemical cell and exposure to air. Al doping competitively passivates surface oxygen, stabilizing metallic Cu0 and suppressing oxidative degradation, highlighting the role of Al doping in preserving the metallic state of Cu37. XPS and ICP measurements further reveal surface-enriched Al distribution (Table S2, Fig. S15), with dopant accumulation inducing lattice distortion that breaks Cu coordination symmetry. This defect engineering generates low-coordination Cu centers while maintaining bulk crystallinity, achieving a favorable balance between catalytic activity and structural stability17.

Synchrotron-based X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis, including XANES and EXAFS, was employed to probe the electronic and coordination environments of Cu species in Cu NP and CuAlSA catalysts (Fig. S22, Table S5). XANES reveals that the Cu species in both samples exist between the Cu1+ and Cu0 states (Fig. 2f), with the Cu valence state in the CuAlSA sample shifting closer to that of Cu foil as Al doping. This shift confirms electron transfer from Al to Cu through covalent Cu-Al bonding, consistent with LMM analysis demonstrating enhanced surface stability. In the CuAlSA sample, the Cu absorption edge shifts by 0.5eV to lower energy compared to Cu foil, suggesting the formation of Cu-Al chemical bonds and electron injection into the Cu sites. Conversely, the Cu NP sample shows a positive shift in the absorption edge to higher energy, indicating electron loss from Cu atoms, which can be attributed to partial oxidation as Cu donates electrons to oxygen. Fourier transform (FT) analysis of the k2-weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) further confirms that the chemical bonds in the CuAlSA sample are predominantly Cu-Cu (R = 2.13 Å), with negligible Cu-O contributions (Fig. 2g)34,38. The reduced Cu-Cu coordination number (10.5 for CuAlSA vs. 10.8 for Cu NP) highlights defect generation and alloying effects that create undercoordinated Cu sites. Substitutional defects induce local strain and lattice distortions (Δr = 1.44%), disrupting the periodicity of the crystal lattice and placing some Cu atoms in a lower coordination environment. This reduction in local coordination typically results in the elongation of chemical bonds due to the diminished average bonding strength. Furthermore, when combined with the larger ionic radius of the dopant, these effects lead to an expansion of the lattice constant, consistent with the peak shifts observed in X-ray diffraction measurements. To specify the coordination environment and valence of Al in CuAlSA, Al K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy was carried out. At the Al K edge, the results show that the edge energy of CuAlSA shifted towards high energy relative to Al foil and far below Al2O3 (Fig. S23a), indicating a slight depletion of electron density at Alδ+ rather than Al3+ and electron donation from Al to Cu. Fourier-transformed EXAFS further supports the atomic dispersion of Al: CuAlSA displays a single prominent coordination shell at 2.2 Å attributable to Cu-Al scattering, while the Al-Al contributions expected at 2.8 Å are absent (Fig. S23b–d). These features rule out Al clustering and confirm isolated Al substituents embedded within the Cu lattice (Table S5).

Electrocatalytic performance of Al-doped Cu catalysts for CO2-to-C2H4 conversion

The electrocatalytic CO2 reduction performance of Cu NP, CuAlSA, and CuAlNP was systematically evaluated in a flow-cell configuration under CO2-saturated 1 M KOH electrolyte (Fig. S24, S25, based on the calibration curves for products in Fig. S26). All catalysts achieved >80% selectivity toward carbonaceous products at −1.4 V vs RHE (iR-corrected), while suppressing the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) to <20% (Fig. S24). The incorporation of Al into Cu catalysts enhances the selectivity for C2H4 production in CO2 reduction. The FE distributions of C2H4 and CH4 under different doping conditions are shown in Fig. 3a, b. The selectivity for ethylene exhibits a distinct dependence on both applied potential and Al doping levels. At −1.4 V vs. RHE, Al doping in the range of 2 - 10 at. % leads to an FE of C2H4 exceeding 70%, suggesting that a low Al content promotes C-C coupling on the Cu catalyst surface. In contrast, the FE of CH4 remains lower than 30%, with its formation significantly suppressed at doping levels of 5-14 at. % and applied potentials between −1.3 and 1.6 V. These results indicate a shift in product selectivity from CH4 toward C2H4. The C2-to-C1 products ratio was influenced by both the aluminum doping level and the choice of dopant metal (Fig. 3c, d). Compared to pristine copper (Cu NP), increased aluminum doping enhanced the FEC2H4 and reduced the FECH4, reaching a FEC2H4 of 78.8% at CuAlSA under a cathodic potential of -1.4 V versus RHE (Fig. 3b). CuAlSA (78.8%) exhibits significantly higher ethylene selectivity than Cu NP (48.1%). This led to an increased FEC2H4/ FECH4 ratio (Fig. 3c), highlighting the role of atomic doping in modulating the binding strength of active sites for *CO and *H intermediates. Such modulation shifts the reaction pathway toward the formation of *OCCO, as corroborated by DFT calculations, thereby favoring the production of C2H4 over CH4. However, further increases in Al doping led to excessive surface Al content, causing Al crystallization and aggregation, as confirmed by the existence of Al-Al chemical coordination in the TEM patterns (Fig. S16). The over-doping effect (CuAlNP/Cu86Al14, Cu20Al80, and Al) is associated with a significant decline in the FE for carbonaceous products and a suboptimal FEHER of 20%, and a decreased charge transfer capacity (Fig. S25).

The electrochemical performance and product selectivity of the catalysts exhibit consistent potential-dependent trends (Fig. 3d). With the cathodic potential downshift, the FECH4 shows an increasing trend. Notably, the CuAlSA serves as a representative catalyst, demonstrating performance over a broad potential window (from −1.2 to −1.8 V vs RHE over 65%, Fig. 3d). Specifically, CuAlSA achieves the FEC2H4 by reducing the formation of CH4 and C2H5OH, thereby attaining a high FEC2H4 to FECH4 ratio within the potential range of −1.2 to −1.8 V versus RHE. Benefiting from the high activity and selectivity of C2H4, the CuAlSA catalyst presents long-term stability in a flow cell at −1.5 V vs RHE. The CuAlSA catalyst reaches a current density of 530 mA cm−2 and FEC2H4 of 78.8%. The stability test is conducted over 10 h, exhibiting FEC2H4 over 69% (Fig. 3e). In comparison, pristine copper yields a FEC2H4 of 41.7% and a current density of 407mA cm−2 under the same overpotential (Figs. S27, S28). The 80% enhancement in ethylene selectivity, coupled with a 30% current density increase, underscores Al doping’s dual role in enhancing both activity and pathway specificity.

The electrochemically active surface areas (ECSA) of several representative catalysts were measured by determining the double-layer capacitance (Fig. S29). Compared to pristine copper NP, the Al-doped sample CuAlSA exhibited enhanced electrochemical activity (Fig. 3f), reduced interfacial impedance, and accelerated reaction kinetics. The LSV curve shifts positively, indicating a lower overpotential and enhanced reaction kinetics in CuAlSA39. Figure 3g schematically depicts the potential-dependent adsorption energies of *CO, *H, and *O on the catalyst surface alongside their selectivity towards C1 (CO and CH4), C2+, and liquid (HCOOH, acetate, ethanol, n-propanol). As the applied potential is decreased, the *CO adsorption energy remains essentially unchanged, whereas *H adsorption is progressively strengthened (range within -0.2 ~ -0.35 eV), concomitant with a rise in ethylene selectivity. In addition, CO stripping voltammetry was carried out to experimentally probe *CO binding on Cu NP and CuAlSA (Fig. S30). Compared with CuAlSA, Cu NP exhibits a CO-stripping peak at more positive potentials with a larger integrated charge, indicating overall stronger *CO binding on Cu NP in line with the DFT predictions. The broader stripping feature observed on CuAlSA further suggests a wider distribution of *CO adsorption energies, consistent with dopant-induced heterogeneity in the local Cu coordination environment. Remarkably, the *O adsorption energy on CuBi remains lower than on Cu NP and CuAlSA, affording a consistently high liquid-phase product selectivity (>15 %) over a broad potential window. After post-stability testing, the concentrations of Cu and Al on the catalyst surfaces and in the solution were subsequently quantified. ICP-MS detected that Al concentration in catholyte remains below 0.1 wt.%, indicating minimal dissolution of surface aluminum active sites (Table S6).

Limited atomic-scale reconstruction under reaction conditions is characterized by in situ XAS (Fig. S31). Post-electrolysis characterization revealed that both Cu and CuAlSA catalysts underwent surface mesoscopic coarsening under cathodic bias (Fig. S32), resulting in reduced active site coverage. The ICP-MS results indicate that Al and Cu exhibit negligible dissolution (Table S7). Elevated cell voltages may coincide with undesired particle coarsening and catalyst aggregation40, which lead to a rapid shift in product selectivity toward methane41. However, the specific mechanistic pathway linking electrochemically driven surface reconstruction to this selectivity drift remains under active debate and is a frontier topic in Cu-based CO2 electroreduction42,43.

Orbital hybridization mechanism in CuAl alloy catalysts

Based on theory-guided screening, a diverse set of metal dopants highlights Al incorporation potential, its pronounced p-d orbital hybridization with Cu endowing surface sites with balanced affinities toward *CO, *H, and *O species. Calculations reveal that adsorbed *CO undergoes an energetically favorable coupling-hydrogenation-deoxygenation sequence to yield C2H4. Tuning the Al dopant concentration modulates the Cu d-band width and reduces antibonding orbital occupancy (Fig. 4a), thereby enhancing p-d overlap, lowering the C-C coupling activation barrier, accelerating reaction kinetics, and increasing the ethylene partial current density. The intrinsic electronic structure of Al endows doped Cu sites with comparable adsorption energies for *H and *O, thereby imparting negligible impact on overall reaction-pathway selectivity. In contrast, the strength of *CO binding and the magnitude of C-C coupling barriers critically govern ethylene formation, with a lowered *CO-to-*OCCO conversion barrier profoundly accelerating ethylene production. Accordingly, systematic investigations into the influence of Al loading on the electronic-structure centers and catalytic performance of atomically dispersed Cu sites are essential to advance the rational design of high-selectivity CO2-to-C2H4 electrocatalysts. The tunability of ECO2R pathways, especially during subsequent *CO transformations such as dimerization, hydro-protonation, and desorption, is strongly influenced by the chemical composition of Cu100-xAlx surface alloys (Fig. S33), as demonstrated by in-situ spectral analyses. By systematically varying the surface compositions19, it is anticipated that a favorable *CO binding energy can be achieved to direct the reaction towards the desired SDI formation pathway, ultimately guiding the production of target products. To further elucidate the principles underlying this selectivity, the binding strength of *CO intermediates on Cu NP and CuAlSA surfaces was analyzed through DFT calculations, serving as a key descriptor44. Notably, a weak *CO binding energy facilitates the desorption of *CO intermediates, favoring the selective formation of CO. In contrast, strong *CO binding energies stabilize *CO species, promoting either protonation to *CHO or dimerization to *OCCO, thereby enabling pathways toward C2 product formation45.

In the projected density of states (PDOS) analysis, the electron density distribution of Al is significantly lower compared to the d-electrons of Cu (Fig. 4b), with the majority of Al’s electrons located in deeper energy levels below the Fermi level (Fig. S34, experimental data calculated from XPS are concluded in Table S8). Figure 4c illustrates the precise modulation and corresponding variation of the d/p-band center proximity induced by changing the Al doping level and lattice strain (ε, %). Lattice strain, as quantified from TEM-derived lattice constants, emerges as the principal driver of d-p orbital-center proximity variations (Fig. S16, Table S3). The incorporation of Al into Cu shifts the electronic band structure of Cu, lowering the energy of antibonding orbitals and band energy (Fig. 4a)14,46. According to the d-band center theory, a downshift in the d-band center weakens adsorbate-catalyst binding strength by lowering antibonding state energy and increasing electron filling (Fig. S34)47. P-d orbital hybridization shifts the Cu d-band center downward, concomitantly stabilizing the molecular antibonding orbital, occupation of this antibonding state by transferred electrons thereby weakens adsorption. This suggests that Al does not directly participate in adsorption as an active site but instead acts as a structural modifier or promoter of Cu’s electronic properties, facilitating a shift in the reaction pathway from the CH4 pathway towards the dimerization of *CO (Fig. 4d)48. The adjustment of the d-band center endows Cu active sites with moderate *CO binding energy, as the electronic activity shifts away from the Fermi level, weakening the interaction and binding strength with *CO (Fig. 4e, f)14. Consequently, the adsorbed *CO exhibits an elongated bond length, making it more likely to migrate to adjacent active sites, where it can interact with neighboring *CO to facilitate C-C coupling49. UPS spectrum confirm that variations in electron density after Al incorporation in CuAlSA markedly alter the surface Cu energy-level alignment, as evidenced by corresponding shifts in the work function (Fig. S35). These work function changes validate the tunability of the surface electronic structure. Furthermore, the degree of orbital hybridization is elucidated via differential charge density isosurfaces (Fig. 4g, h), where an isovalue corresponding to a -0.15 |e| electron transfer is observed. Notably, compared to CuAlSA, the CO adsorption on Cu NP exhibits a more pronounced charge transfer, while in the CuAlSA sample, charge redistribution is predominantly localized on the Al-doped regions. This indicates that Al incorporation induces significant p-d orbital hybridization, leading to a localized electronic reconfiguration on the Cu sites. The PDOS and differential charge density isosurfaces analysis reveals strong p-d orbital hybridization and charge transfer between Cu and Al, consistent with the bonding interactions and atomic doping effects indicated by peak shifts in XRD and XPS characterization analyses50,51.

As the applied potential becomes more negative, the increased electron availability at the electrode-catalyst-electrolyte interface enhances the binding strength of *CO and *H on the active sites, driving the reaction pathway toward CH4 production (Fig. S36)14. The CH4 formation pathway relies on a high *CO binding strength, as it involves sequential protonation and electron transfer steps to produce intermediates such as *CHxO (x = 1-3) and *CH352. Strong *CO binding ensures these intermediates remain stable on the catalyst surface, allowing sufficient time to achieve the reaction while preventing premature desorption or protonation side reactions (Fig. S36). For the subsequent reaction steps of *OCCO leading to ethylene formation, a modest *CO binding strength is required, with ΔG*H < ΔG*CO < ΔG*O on the catalyst surface. Consistent with the theoretical predictions in the first section, the downward shift of the d-band center weakens the binding energy with *H, thereby suppressing the HER8. This difference in binding strength ensures that *CO remains efficiently stabilized on the surface without being prematurely converted into C1 products or oxidized (Table S6). The selectivity for C2H4 is optimized by achieving a kinetic balance between C-O bond cleavage and the hydrogenation of *O adsorbates, governed by the O binding strength. Furthermore, a clear correlation between the adsorption energy of *H, *CO, and *O with the selectivity towards C2H4 production has been established, as demonstrated by DFT predictions. The computationally derived volcano-shaped relationship for key intermediates binding strength is consistent with experimental results, confirming its reliability in guiding the design of efficient ECO2R catalysts with specific controllable selectivity toward value-added chemicals.

To elucidate the reaction mechanism of ECO2R, in situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor the vibrational signals of the key intermediate during the reaction. This technique enables the sensitive detection of surface-adsorbed intermediates, including *CO, *CHO, and *CH2CHO, providing dynamic insights into their behavior on various catalyst surfaces as a function of cathodic potential and reaction time. To investigate the key factors influencing tunable selectivity toward target products (e.g., CH4, C2H4, C2H5OH, and CO) based on surface chemical composition, Cu NP and CuAlSA were selected as representative systems. The in situ Raman measurements were conducted under cathodic potential ranging from 0 to −1.4 V (vs. RHE) in an electrochemical flow cell (Fig. 5). For both Cu NP and CuAlSA samples, distinct Raman bands at 280 cm−1 and 360 cm−1 were observed as the applied cathodic potential increased (Fig. 5a). These bands are attributed to the stretching vibration (vs) of the C≡O bond in the adsorbed *CO intermediates and the Cu–CO bond on the adsorbed Cu surface, respectively. Additionally, characteristic peaks corresponding to surface-adsorbed *CO were observed in the 2000–2100 cm−1, which could be attributed to bridge-, low-frequency band (LFB), and high-frequency band (HFB)-*CO, with the peak positions exhibiting a red shift as the potential decreased (Fig. 5a)37. A decrease in the peak intensity of the HFB at 2100 cm−1 and the Cu-CO signal at 280 cm−1 was observed in CuAlSA catalysts (Fig. 5a), indicating enhanced C-C coupling dynamics on low-coordination Cu sites53. The higher proportion of HFB *CO adsorption peaks observed in CuAlSA (Fig. 5a) is attributed to the electron-donating effect of Al doping. This low-electronegativity element alters the electronic structure of Cu active centers. This doping increases the availability of unsaturated coordination sites, thereby enhancing the C-C coupling process. These results align with DFT predictions and are supported by prior studies. Additionally, the *CO signal increased firstly and then decreased with decreased potential in CuAlSA, and the *CO signal intensity of Cu NP was higher than that of CuAlSA at −1.4 V (Fig. 5a)46. In time-dependent evolution of *CO adsorption on Cu NP and CuAlSA catalyst at −1.4 V vs. RHE, the Cu-CO rotational and C≡O stretching mode are observed, with variations in intensity over time (Fig. 5b, c). Compared with Cu NP, the CuAlSA exhibits an initial increase followed by a decrease in *CO vibrational peak intensity, indicating a more dynamic *CO dimerization and coupling process. This suggests that more *CO was utilized for C-C coupling at high potentials on CuAlSA, highlighting the favorable C-C dimerization kinetics of doped CuAlSA.

The high coverage resulting from the strong binding energy of *CO on Cu NP and CuAlSA surface reduces the availability of active sites for H2 formation, effectively suppressing the competing HER side reaction. Furthermore, a vibrational peak at 530 cm−1, associated with the CH2CHO intermediate (Fig. S37)53, was identified in the CuAlSA, while a peak at 2712 cm−1, corresponding to the *CHO intermediate, was more prominent in the Cu NP sample (Fig. 5a)14. These findings validate the DFT predictions and experimental results, demonstrating that Cu NP exhibits significantly lower ethylene selectivity and slower reaction kinetics, while favoring CH4 production compared to CuAlSA. In situ characterization reveals that Al atom doping shifts the product distribution by enhancing C-C coupling with faster kinetics. These intermediates play a pivotal role in clarifying product distribution during CO2 reduction, emphasizing their importance in reaction pathways leading to CH4 and C2H4. The formation of reduction products, including CH4 and C2H4, was observed for both Cu NP and CuAlSA (Fig. 3a, b), strengthening the critical role of the coordination environment and electronic structure of active sites in determining reaction pathways.

Electrocatalytic performance under diluted CO2 conditions

To circumvent costly feedstock purification, we performed direct electrocatalytic CO2 reduction using industrially relevant dilute CO2 streams (50% and 15%), mimicking concentrations found in biogas upgrading and flue gas emissions. To enhance catalyst stability, suppress flooding, and mitigate surface reconstruction, the CuAlSA catalyst was modified with the polymeric ionic liquid (IL) 1-vinyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([VMIM][TfS]), forming CuAlSA@IL. This interfacial engineering strategy aims to enrich CO2 molecules near active sites. The presence and physical nature of the ionic-liquid (IL) overlayer on CuAlSA@IL were established by spectroscopic and microscopic analyses. FT-IR spectra display the characteristic imidazolium54,55 and triflate56 vibrational signatures, consistent with successful IL incorporation (Fig. S38a). The XRD pattern shows invariant Cu diffraction features relative to the parent catalyst, excluding any IL-induced phase transformation (Fig. S38b). XPS (N 1 s and Cu LMM) reveals no Cu-N coordination or measurable chemical shift, indicating a physiosorbed film rather than chemisorption (Fig. S37c–e). Consistently, TEM directly resolves a conformal IL sheath with an average thickness of ~5 nm enveloping the catalyst surface (Fig. S39). Under 15% CO2, a C2H4 FE of 70.2% was achieved, whereas 50% CO2 resulted in a higher C2H4 FE of 78.2%. This performance difference highlights kinetic limitations in CO2 activation at lower concentrations. Nevertheless, the high C2H4 selectivity maintained under diluted CO2 conditions underscores the effectiveness of interfacial regulation by the IL (Fig. 6a, b). The influence of CO2 flow rate on product selectivity was further investigated (Fig. 6c, d). Under 15% CO2, higher flow rates are required to sustain sufficient interfacial CO2 concentration and maintain C-C coupling selectivity. By contrast, product distribution under 50% CO2 exhibits minimal sensitivity to flow rate, resembling the behavior observed under pure CO2. These findings suggest that CuAlSA@IL catalysts can sustain performance under moderately diluted CO2 conditions when interfacial CO2 availability is preserved.

To evaluate practical applicability, long-term electrolysis was carried out in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) system, achieving continuous operation for 102.7 hours under 15% CO2 (Fig. 6e). Comparisons with state-of-the-art catalysts can be found in Supplementary Information. Current reports can be broadly grouped into three categories: (i) mechanistic studies with well-elucidated pathways but modest current densities; (ii) systems reaching industrial‑level current densities (>200 mA cm−2) but with compromised selectivity; and (iii) emerging approaches targeting dilute CO2 feeds. The CuAlSA@IL catalyst bridges these gaps by combining high C2H4 selectivity with industrially relevant current densities under low CO2 concentrations. To clarify how this overlayer reshapes the reaction microenvironment, the multiphysics finite-element simulations are performed, resolving CO2 transport and electrolyte-ion distributions across the catalyst-electrolyte boundary. 1D gas-solid-liquid model and resulting concentration profiles are concluded in Fig. S40: gas feed at x = 0, porous catalyst layer 0-10 µm, catalyst-catholyte interface at x = 10 µm, liquid boundary layer 10–60 µm. The IL overlayer increases the effective CO2 diffusivity (DCuAlSA@IL = 2169.5 mol m−3 s−1 > DCuAlSA = 2071.5 mol m−3 s−1), leading to a higher CO2 activity at the interface (x = 10 µm). At 100% CO2, the interface approaches the dissolution limit, resulting in a modest gain. At 15% CO2, the CO2-enrichment effect is particularly pronounced57, raising interfacial CO2, sustaining *CO coverage, and promoting C-C coupling55,56. Consistent with this behavior, CuAlSA@IL retains high ethylene selectivity at 15% CO2. The IL increases the effective CO2 diffusivity/partitioning and thus the interfacial CO2 activity55, with a particularly pronounced enrichment under a 15% CO2 feed (Fig. S41). Its fixed imidazolium charge imposes Donnan exclusion, repelling K+ and enriching OH- near the reaction zone (Fig. S41), while the partially hydrophobic interphase mitigates electrolyte flooding58. Together, these effects reconstruct the catalyst-electrolyte microenvironment: achieving CO2 enrichment, K+ exclusion, higher local pH, and anti-flooding.

In this work, the high CO2-to-ethylene selectivity may arise from IL-mediated CO2 enrichment, coupled with a Cu d-p band alignment that subtly tailors the restructured oxygen-bound intermediate network under dilute CO2 conditions. Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated that Al incorporation into the Cu lattice drives an intermetallic charge redistribution that tunes the p- and d-band centers’ proximity for oxygen-bound intermediates reoriented. This Al-induced charge redistribution balances the adsorption strengths of *CO, *H, and *O, thereby shifting the CO2 reduction pathway away from *CO → *CHO (for CH4) toward *CO → *OCCO coupling (for C2+ products). Electronic exchange between Al and Cu induces a downshift of the Cu d band center, lowering the energy of the adsorbate-metal (*CO-Cu) antibonding states with electrons filled, thereby weakening the adsorbate-metal binding strength. In situ Raman spectroscopy confirmed the accelerated *CO coupling. As a result, the SDI barrier for C2H4 formation is reduced from 0.53 to 0.30 eV, enabling a CuAlSA catalyst to achieve 78.8% ethylene Faraday efficiency at 530 mA cm−2 in a flow cell using pure CO2. Under 15% diluted CO2, after coating with ionomer, a Faraday efficiency of 70.2% for ethylene is achieved, with a current density reaching 800 mA cm−2 in the MEA system. Multiphysics finite-element simulation results confirm CO2 enrichment by the polymer layer and show Donnan exclusion from fixed imidazolium charges that modulate interfacial K+ and OH-. The combination of electronic-structure control and interfacial microenvironment design enables selective, high-rate value-added products formation from dilute CO2 and provides a generalizable route for catalyst and interphase engineering.

Methods

Theoretical calculation

Density Functional Theory (DFT) Calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)59–61. The Cu (111) surface was selected as the model catalyst for CO2 reduction, employing a slab model consisting of four atomic layers with lateral dimensions of 4 × 4-unit cells, encompassing a total of 64 Cu atoms. To simulate Cu-Al alloys, 2, 5, 8, 10, and 15 Cu atoms were randomly substituted with Al atoms, corresponding to Cu98Al2, Cu95Al5, Cu92Al8, Cu90Al10, and Cu85Al15 compositions, respectively. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was applied to eliminate periodic interference between unit cells. The geometry of the Cu100-xAlx structures, including atomic positions, cell shape, and cell volume, was optimized using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)62. The theoretical lattice spacings for the (111) planes of the Cu100-xAlx samples were calculated as 2.08, 2.18, 2.30, 2.35, and 2.37 Å, respectively (Table S1). A plane-wave cutoff energy of 500 eV was selected after verifying the accuracy against higher cutoff values. The Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid was set to 4 × 4 × 1 for all models. Convergence criteria for the electronic structure calculations were set to 1.0 × 10−5 eV per atom for energy and 0.01 eV Å−1 for the maximum force. To enhance the accuracy of adsorption energy calculations, dispersion corrections were included using the DFT-D3 scheme63. The optimized atomic structures are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

This model treats the system as charge-neutral, incorporating N additional electrons to account for the charge partitioning between the catalyst surface and adsorbates64. Catalysts preparation

Pre-treated and thoroughly washed carbon paper was used as the cathode65, with a Pt serving as the anode and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. Electrodeposition was carried out in a conventional three-electrode H-cell, with 0.5 M KOH solution as the anolyte. Based on calculations using the Nernst equation, five molar concentration ratios of ions were selected: Cu2+∶Al3+ = 1∶0.1, 1∶0.2, 1∶0.3, 1∶0.4, and 1∶0.5 to obtain Cu100-xAlx (x = 0, 2, 5, 8, 10, and 14; Table S2). When x = 0, 8, and 14, the samples were renamed Cu NP, CuAlSA, and CuAlNP, respectively. CuCl2 ⋅2H2O and AlCl3⋅6H2O were dissolved in a 1-vinyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([VMIM][TfS])-based ionic liquid to serve as the cathodic electrolyte for electrodeposition. For the co-deposition of Co, Bi, and Zn, the ion ratio of Cu2+∶Mn+ was set at 1∶0.1. A square-wave pulsed current with a frequency of 50 Hz, a peak current of -8 mA cm-2, and a minimum current of -3 mA cm-2 was applied to regulate the deposition rate of Cu2+, ensuring its uniform incorporation into the lattice. This study investigated the effects of metal species and ion concentrations on the composition and structure of CuX alloys. To stabilize the cathodic electrolyte, 10 mM CTAB was added as a complexing agent. Experimental results confirmed the stability of the system at the selected molar ratios. The composition of the cathodic electrolyte under various experimental conditions is detailed in Table S2. The CTAB concentration was maintained at 10 mM, and the electrolyte pH was adjusted to approximately 5 using diluted sulfuric acid or diluted ammonium hydroxide.

The polymeric ionic liquid 1-vinyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([VMIM][TfS]) was uniformly coated onto the surface of CuAlSA using in situ grafting polymerization to obtain CuAlSA@IL. Cu electrodes (1 × 1 cm2; Cu loading 1 mg cm−2) were immersed in methanol (40 mL) containing 1-vinyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate (0.10 g) and AIBN (0.01 g). The mixture was ultrasonicated for 15 min and purged with N2 for 30 min. Under N2, the suspension was stirred at 80 °C until complete solvent evaporation, allowing AIBN-initiated vinyl polymerization and in situ grafting of a PIL film on Cu. The electrodes were washed six times with methanol and deionized water and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h to afford Cu@IL.

Diluted CO2 conditions were simulated by mixing pure CO2 with N2 to mimic typical flue gas compositions, corresponding to 15% and 50% CO2 concentrations. The long-term electrolysis under 15% CO2 was performed in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) reactor to evaluate catalyst stability. Diluted CO2 electrolysis in MEA was carried out in a zero-gap membrane-electrode assembly electrolyzer with experimented flow rate. The CuAlSA electrode was used as the cathode with a geometric area of 1 × 1 cm2. The cathodic and anodic compartments were separated by an anion exchange membrane (AEM, FAA-3-PK-130). A Pt electrode was used as the anode with 1 M KOH as anolyte. Before assembly, the AEM was pretreated by immersion in 1 M KOH for 24 h and rinsed with deionized water.

Product quantification

The gas-phase products from ECO2R were analyzed using an in-line gas chromatography system (Agilent 8860) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and two flame ionization detectors (FIDs). The TCD was used to detect H2 and CO, while the FIDs were employed for CH4, C2H4, and C2H6. Liquid-phase products, including HCOOH, CH3OH, CH3COOH, C2H5OH, and C3H7OH were collected and quantified using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The liquid products generated during electrolysis were collected and quantified using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy on a Bruker NEO 400 MHz spectrometer. For each sample, 500 μl of electrolyte was mixed with 100 μl of an internal standard solution containing 5 mM dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in D2O. The standard deviation (s.d.) values were calculated based on measurements conducted using three independent electrodes.

Finite element analysis

We performed multiphysics finite-element simulations methodology by using a one-dimensional, steady-state transport-reaction model to resolve how ionomer grafting within the cathode catalyst layer modulates CO2-reduction performance.
