TRANSSET: A drone-captured dataset for enhancing vehicle detection and traffic monitoring algorithms
Authors: Tewodros Syum Gebre, Quincy Blackston, Leila Hashemi Beni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents TRANSSET, a comprehensive dataset of drone-captured imagery designed to advance traffic detection and vehicle classification research. The dataset consists of over 4704 high-resolution images extracted from 4 K video footage collected at two highway locations in North Carolina, USA (Interstate 40 and Interstate 440). Data acquisition was performed using a DJI T600 multi-rotor drone hovering at altitudes between 140 and 200 feet, providing unique oblique perspectives of traffic flow under varying weather conditions. Each image is meticulously annotated with bounding boxes and class labels utilizing a comprehensive eight-class vehicle taxonomy (sedan, SUV, pickup truck, van, car, truck, trailer, hatchback) in three widely used formats: PASCAL VOC XML, COCO JSON, and TXT. This dataset addresses the scarcity of high-quality aerial traffic data by offering diverse viewing angles, exposures, and backgrounds. It serves as a vital resource for training, validating, and testing machine learning models, particularly object detection algorithms, thereby supporting the development of robust intelligent transportation systems and automated traffic monitoring solutions.Keywords: Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), Vehicle detection, UAV Imagery, Traffic monitoring, Aerial object detection, Deep learning dataset

Background

Aerial imagery plays a pivotal role in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), enabling efficient traffic flow monitoring and vehicle detection. However, the majority of existing aerial datasets, such as DOTA and UAVData, predominantly feature nadir (top-down) perspectives. While useful for mapping, these datasets often fail to capture the multi-faceted details of vehicles, such as side profiles and rear views, that are critical for robust classification and re-identification tasks in realistic surveillance scenarios. This oblique perspective is technically advantageous for vehicle classification as it improves the visibility of body shape, height profiles, and vehicle-type cues that are difficult to distinguish from standard nadir imagery. To address this gap, the TRANSSET dataset introduces a collection of high-resolution drone-captured imagery with unique oblique viewing angles. By providing perspectives that reveal the top, sides, front, and rear of vehicles, TRANSSET enhances the diversity of training data available for developing more generalized and accurate computer vision models for traffic monitoring.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Acquisition protocol

Video footage was captured using a DJI T600 multi-rotor drone equipped with a high-resolution camera recording at 4 K resolution (4096 × 2160 pixels) at 30 frames per second. The drone was operated in a hovering position to ensure stable video data. The drone remained in a stationary hover position at each site for the full acquisition interval to ensure stable video data. Data collection occurred at two specific highway locations in North Carolina:

Interstate 40 (Exit-270): Captured on Sept 23, 2021 (4:00 PM - 6:00 PM) under clear/sunny conditions. The drone altitude varied between 140 and 200 feet (Fig. 2).

Sample image and Google Map snapshot, with camera orientation shown of Interstate 40 (Exit-270), captured on September 23, 2021, between 4:00 PM and 6:00 PM under clear/sunny conditions. Map not to scale [5].

Interstate 440 (Exit 13A): Captured on Oct 7, 2021 (3:00 PM - 5:00 PM) under cloudy conditions. The drone maintained a consistent altitude of approximately 200 feet (Fig. 3). The drone captured an oblique view of the traffic, revealing the top, sides, front, and rear of vehicles. The approximate camera tilt angle was maintained between 45 and 75 degrees from nadir, as estimated from the resulting imagery.

Preprocessing

From approximately 45 min of raw video footage, frames were extracted using a custom Python script utilizing the OpenCV library at a fixed interval of 2 frames per second (fps) to ensure sufficient spatial displacement of vehicles and reduce temporal correlation. Following extraction, an automated script applied a systematic center-crop to the original 4 K frames (4096 × 2160), extracting a 1024 × 1024 pixel region using fixed, site-based crop coordinates for all frames within each respective video to focus exclusively on the active traffic lanes. No scaling, resizing, or padding was applied to ensure the original aspect ratio and vehicle geometries were perfectly preserved. This resulted in an initial extraction of approximately 5400 frames before a manual visual inspection was performed to remove off-target or poorly framed instances, yielding the final dataset of 4704 images.

Annotation method

The cropped images were manually annotated using the free web-based tool MakeSense.ai. Trained annotators drew bounding boxes around vehicles and assigned class labels (Fig. 4). To clearly illustrate the visual characteristics, varying scales, and annotation precision for each category, representative zoomed-in crops for all eight vehicle classes are provided in Fig. 5. Bounding boxes were drawn to encompass the full visible extent of the vehicle. For partially occluded vehicles, bounding boxes were estimated to cover the visible extent of the vehicle, excluding occluded parts, if at least 50% of the vehicle was visible. Highly ambiguous or severely occluded vehicles (<50% visible) were excluded from annotation to prevent training noise. Fig. 6 demonstrates examples of these challenging real-world detection scenarios successfully retained in the dataset, highlighting partial occlusions, shadow overlaps, and small object instances.

A multi-stage quality control process, including secondary review and automated checks, was employed to ensure annotation accuracy. The primary annotations were performed by two trained annotators. A secondary review was conducted on a randomly selected 10% subset of the dataset (470 images), sampled using stratification across both highway locations and all vehicle classes, by a third independent reviewer. This validation phase yielded an Inter-Annotator Agreement (Cohen’s Kappa) of 0.92 for class label assignments and an average bounding box Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.88, demonstrating high consistency. Vehicle classes were defined following standard traffic classification principles, considering vehicle size and their impact on traffic flow and pavement, while also accounting for class stability and balance in machine learning modeling. Limitations

While TRANSSET offers high-quality oblique aerial imagery, it is subject to specific limitations regarding environmental and geographic scope. The data acquisition was conducted exclusively during daylight hours (late afternoon) under clear and cloudy weather conditions (approximately 60% of the images were captured under clear/sunny conditions at I-40 and 40% under cloudy conditions at I-440); consequently, the dataset does not account for low-light environments (nighttime) or adverse weather scenarios such as rain, snow, or heavy fog. Additionally, the data collection was restricted to two specific highway interchanges in North Carolina, USA. As a result, the dataset may not fully represent traffic patterns, road infrastructure, or vehicle types found in other geographic regions or dense urban settings. Finally, the natural class imbalance (e.g., higher frequency of cars and SUVs relative to hatchbacks) reflects true North American highway traffic distributions, which inherently challenges and tests the robustness of object detection models, requiring algorithmic mitigation during model training, such as utilizing focal loss functions, class-weighted sampling, or synthetic oversampling of minority classes (e.g., hatchbacks and trucks).

Ethics Statement

The work did not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or data collected from social media. The drone video footage was acquired in strict compliance with Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) requirements and local regulations, with permission and operations conducted by the North Carolina Department of Transportation (NC DOT). To ensure privacy, the drone operated at altitudes between 140 and 200 feet; consequently, no personally identifiable information (PII), such as human faces or vehicle license plates, is discernible in the dataset.
