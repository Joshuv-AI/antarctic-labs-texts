Parabolic Anderson Model in the Hyperbolic Space. Part II: Quenched Asymptotics
Xi Geng, Sheng Wang, Weijun Xu

Abstract
We establish the exact quenched asymptotic growth of the solution to the parabolic Anderson model (PAM) in the hyperbolic space with a regular, stationary, time-independent Gaussian potential. More precisely, we show that with probability one, the solution u u to PAM with constant initial data has pointwise growth asymptotics u ⁡ ( t , x ) ∼ e L ∗ ​ t 5 / 3 + o ⁡ ( t 5 / 3 ) u(t,x)\sim e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}+o(t^{5/3})} as t → + ∞ t\rightarrow+\infty . Both the power t 5 / 3 t^{5/3} on the exponential and the exact value of L ∗ L^{*} are different from their counterparts in the Euclidean situation. They are determined through an explicit optimisation procedure. Our proof relies on certain fine localisation techniques, which also reveals a stronger non-Euclidean localisation mechanism.

The main theorem

This article is the second part of our investigation on the long time asymptotics of the parabolic Anderson model (PAM) on the hyperbolic space with a regular, stationary, time-independent Gaussian potential. We consider the solution
u
to

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u+\xi\cdot u,\qquad u(0,\cdot)\equiv 1

(1.1)

on the standard hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
with constant curvature
\kappa\equiv-1
. Here
\xi
is a Gaussian field satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

\xi
is a real-valued, centered, time-independent, stationary Gaussian field on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Furthermore, its covariance function
1

Stationarity means for every
n
, every
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
and every
g\in SO^{+}(d,1)
(the group of orientation preserving isometries over
\mathbb{H}^{d}
), one has

\big(\xi(g\cdot x_{1}),\dots,\xi(g\cdot x_{n})\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{law}}}{{=}}\big(\xi(x_{1}),\dots,\xi(x_{n})\big)\;.

By stationarity, the covariance function of
\xi
must be a function of the distance only (see
[
GX25
, Lemma 2.1]
).

C(x,y)=C\big(d(x,y)\big)=\mathbb{E}\big(\xi(x)\xi(y)\big)

has two continuous derivatives on

, and there exists
R_{0}>0
such that
C(\rho)=0
whenever
\rho>R_{0}
. Here

Except for the finiteness of correlation length,

[

]
. A simple way to construct such a

In the first part
[

, we established the second order annealed asymptotics and demonstrated the same moment intermittency property as in the Euclidean case. One interesting phenomena there is that the fluctuation exponent is determined by the geometry

is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For almost every realisation of
\xi
, one has

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)=\frac{3\cdot 2^{4/3}}{5^{5/3}}(\sigma^{2}(d-1))^{2/3},

(1.2)

where

is a given fixed base point and
\sigma^{2}\triangleq{\rm Var}[\xi(o)]

Remark 1.1
.

The solution

is the same as the total mass (whole-space integral) of the PAM with

Unlike the moment asymptotics, the almost sure behaviour revealed

1.1
Introduction

Background and motivation.
Motivated from problems in physics, the study of the asymptotic behaviour of directed polymers in random environments have attracted much interest over the past decades in both discrete and continuous settings (cf.

[
Com17
,
Kön16
]
and the references therein). Mathematically, a random polymer is described by a random walk or a Brownian motion propagating through space where random rewards and penalties are distributed. One important question is to understand how the global behaviour of the polymer (i.e. the
random walk or Brownian motion) is affected by the impurities of the random environment. A typical phenomenon is that the polymer would exhibit certain
localisation property (e.g. favouring particular locations or trajectories) under the polymer measure (Gibbs measure). Such behaviour is intimately related to the (quenched) long time asymptotics of the so-called partition function, which is
the solution to the parabolic Anderson model with potential function describing the random environment.

The long time behaviour of PAM in Euclidean settings (over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with various potentials) have been extensively
studied in the literature. The monograph
[
Kön16
]
contains an excellent exposition on various asymptotic results in the discrete setting. The annealed and quenched asymptotics for the continuous PAM with a regular Gaussian potential were studied in the seminal works
[
CM95
,
GK00
,
GKM00
]
. The quenched asymptotics was extended to singular Gaussian potentials in
[
Che14
]
and the white noise potential in
[
KPZ22
]
.

A basic motivation of the current work is to investigate
a similar model in a different geometric setting and look for new non-Euclidean asymptotic behaviour and localisation mechanism. As an initial attempt, we choose to work on the standard hyperbolic space where
the global geometry and large scale behaviour of Brownian motion differ from the Euclidean situation in several fundamental ways. The underlying
random environment is simply described by a time-independent, regular Gaussian
potential whose distribution is invariant under isometries. Mathematically,
the PAM under consideration is described by the parabolic equation
(
1.1
). In the first part of our investigation
[
GX25
]
,
we established the annealed asymptotics for the PAM (
1.1
). It turns out that the moment asymptotics is identical to the Euclidean result established in
[
GK00
]
and the PAM exhibits the same moment intermittency property as in the Euclidean case. A surprising point over there
is that the fluctuation exponent is described by a variational problem
induced by the
Euclidean
(rather than hyperbolic) Laplacian.

Main result and novelty
. The goal of the present article
is to investigate the quenched asymptotics of the PAM (
1.1
).
Our main finding is that the almost sure growth of the solution, as revealed by (
1.2
), is much faster
than the Euclidean situation where the growth rate was found to be (cf.
[
CM95
,
GKM00
]
)

u(t,o)=\exp\big(\sqrt{2d\sigma^{2}}t\sqrt{\log t}+o(t\sqrt{\log t})\big)\ \ \ \text{as }t\rightarrow\infty.

(1.3)

The
t^{5/3}
-scale and the exact growth constant in (
1.2
) are determined through an explicit optimisation
procedure that is related to the competition between the reward from
peak values of the Gaussian field and the cost of travelling to those
regions. There is a new and interesting localisation mechanism for the Brownian motion in this situation: the Brownian motion over
[0,t]
intends to reach the peak region of the Gaussian field
over
a definite ball
(precisely, with radius
K^{*}t^{4/3}
where
K^{*}
is an explicit number)
at a definite proportion of time
(precisely,
at
s=t/5
) and stay there for the rest of time. This localisation mechanism is different from the Euclidean situation (cf. Section
1.2.3

below for a more detailed comparison). We will discuss our methodology
for proving this result as well as the main difficulties in Section
1.2
below.

Related works.
There are several related works on the behaviour of PAM in non-Euclidean spaces. As a closely related setting (discrete analogue of hyperbolic space),
[
HKS21
]
established the quenched asymptotics on a Galton-Watson tree and
[
HW23
]
established the annealed asymptotics on a regular tree. In both works, the random potential is assumed to have a double-exponential tail and as a result, the asymptotic theorems obtained in these works are very different in nature from the ones presented in
[
GX25
]
and here.

In the continuous setting, the PAM with white noise potential and the analysis of the singular Anderson operator on Riemann surfaces (heat kernel estimates, spectral properties etc.) were studied in depth in a series of works
[
DDD18
,
Mou22
,
BDM25
]
(see also the references therein). The general framework for singular SPDEs in manifolds was established recently by
[
HS23
]
.

On the other hand,
[
BCO24
]
considered the PAM with a time-dependent (white in time and coloured in space) Gaussian potential on a Cartan-Hadamard manfold, where the authors obtained well-posedness results as well as moment estimates for the solution. The role of non-positive curvature (and global geometry) also plays an essential role in the recent work
[
CO25
]
where similar questions as in
[
BCO24
]
were investigated. Other works related to the asymptotic behaviour of PAM with a time-dependent Gaussian potential in a geometric setting include e.g.
[
TV02
,
BOT23
,
BCH24
,
COV24
]
. We should point out that the case of time-dependent potential is different from the time-independent case in several fundamental ways (the asymptotic results, methodology and underlying mechanism have very different nature in these two settings). The current work focus on the time-independent case and we will not comment much on the other situation.

1.2
Heuristics: methodology and localisation mechanism

In this section, we discuss how Theorem
1.1
is proved
at a heuristic but rather non-rigorous level. We will also describe
the localisation mechanism for achieving the optimal growth rate
e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}

(
L^{*}
is the exact constant given in (
1.2
)) and
compare such mechanism to the Euclidean situation. The proof of Theorem

1.1
contains two parts:

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\underline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)\geqslant L^{*}\ \text{a.s.}\ \ \ (\text{lower asymptotics})

(1.4)

and

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\overline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)\leqslant L^{*}\ \text{a.s.}\ \ \ (\text{upper asymptotics}).

(1.5)

The starting points are the following two basic facts.

(i) The solution admits the Feynman-Kac representation:

u(t,o)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds}],

(1.6)

where
W
is a Brownian motion starting from
o
and the expectation
is taken with respect to
W
.

(ii) Sample functions of the Gaussian field
\xi
admit the following almost-sure growth property:

\max_{B(o,R)}\xi(x)\approx\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}\ \ \ \text{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

(1.7)

According to (
1.6
) and (
1.7
), it
is natural to expect that the growth of
u(t,o)
is intimately related
to the quantitative competition between peak values of
\xi
(reward)
and probabilities of reaching and staying at those peak regions by
the Brownian motion
W
(cost). This viewpoint is robust for studying this
kind of models in various general settings (Euclidean / discrete /
other potentials).

1.2.1
Formal derivation of optimal Brownian scenario
and growth exponent

In order to prove the lower asymptotics (
1.4
), one
needs to identify a particular Brownian scenario under which the growth
rate
e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}
is essentially achieved.

Let
K(t)
and
\varepsilon(t)
be parameters to be determined later
on (
K(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
and
\varepsilon(t)\in[0,t]
).
On the ball
B(o,K(t))
, the Gaussian field
\xi
achieves and maintains
its maximal value
\approx\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K(t)}
on some (random)
island
B^{t}
near
\partial B(o,K(t))
. Inspired by the Euclidean
situation, we consider the following ansatz (localisation) for the
Brownian motion which potentially yields the main contribution of

\mathbb{E}[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds}]
.

Ansatz
. The Brownian motion
W
enters
the island
B^{t}
at time
\varepsilon(t)
and stays inside it
over
[\varepsilon(t),t]
to pick up the peak values of
\xi.
In
addition, the integral
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon(t)}\xi(W_{s})ds

and the probability of staying inside
B^{t}
during
[\varepsilon(t),t]
do not contribute to the growth of
u(t,o)
.

By localising at the above scenario, one can formally
write down a lower bound for
u(t,o)
:

\displaystyle u(t,o)\gtrsim

\displaystyle e^{(t-\varepsilon(t))\cdot\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K(t)}}\times\mathbb{P}(W_{\varepsilon(t)}\in B^{t}),

where the exponential term comes from (
1.7
).
Since
B^{t}
is near the boundary
\partial B(o,K(t))
, the hyperbolic
heat kernel estimate shows that

\mathbb{P}(W_{\varepsilon(t)}\in B^{t})\approx e^{-\frac{K(t)^{2}}{4\varepsilon(t)}}.

As a consequence, one has

u(t,o)\gtrsim\exp\big((t-\varepsilon(t))\cdot\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K(t)}-\frac{K(t)^{2}}{4\varepsilon(t)}\big).

(1.8)

It is elementary to see that the right hand side of (
1.8
)
can only be large
when
\varepsilon(t)
is proportional to

t
and
K(t)
is of scale
t^{4/3}
. Therefore, one is led to
taking

\varepsilon(t)=\varepsilon t,\ K(t)=Kt^{4/3}

where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
K>0
are given parameters. With
these choices, the right hand side of (
1.8
) becomes

e^{f(\varepsilon,K)t^{5/3}}
where

f(\varepsilon,K)\triangleq(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K}-\frac{K^{2}}{4\varepsilon}.

(1.9)

Since
\varepsilon,K
can be arbitrary, one obtains that

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\underline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)\geqslant\max\big\{f(\varepsilon,K):\varepsilon\in(0,1),K>0\big\}.

A standard optimisation procedure yields that

\max f(\varepsilon,K)=\frac{3\cdot 2^{4/3}}{5^{5/3}}(\sigma^{2}(d-1))^{2/3},

(1.10)

which then gives the optimal growth constant
L^{*}
. The maximum
of
f
is attained at

\varepsilon^{*}=\frac{1}{5},\ K^{*}=\frac{2^{5/3}}{5^{4/3}}(\sigma^{2}(d-1))^{1/3}.

(1.11)

To summarise, we have formally obtained a potentially optimal Brownian scenario
which is given by the following event:

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{t}^{*}=\

“
W
enters the peak region of
\xi\
on
B(o,K^{*}t^{4/3})
at time
\varepsilon^{*}t

and stays there over
[\varepsilon^{*}t,t]."

(1.12)

Under this scenario, one expects the following lower bound to hold
true:

u(t,o)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};{\cal O}_{t}^{*}\big]\approx e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}

for all sufficiently large
t
.

We should point out that the negligibility of the integral
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds

(which is part of the ansatz) is non-trivial. Intuitively, by requiring
the Brownian motion
W
to reach the peak region
B^{t}
of
\xi

at time
\varepsilon^{*}t
which is near
\partial B(o,K^{*}t^{4/3})
,
the Brownian motion is forced to travel at a speed much faster than
its normal speed. As a result, the Brownian motion intends to travel
like a geodesic with high probability. This makes it reasonable to
replace
W|_{[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]}
by the corresponding geodesic

\gamma^{t}
connecting
o
and the center of
B^{t}
. Once this
is possible, it is then not hard to see that the integral
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds

is negligible since the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
rarely passes through
very negative values of
\xi.

1.2.2
The upper asymptotics

The proof of the matching upper asymptotics (
1.5
) is more challenging. Essentially, one needs to analyse every Brownian
scenario (i.e. all possible ways of propagating through
\xi
-peak
values) and show that none of these scenarios (nor their total contribution)
produces a growth rate that is larger than
e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}.
Here
we give an intuitive explanation about why this is expected to be
true and also point out a major difficulty in the argument.

Optimality of the Brownian scenario (
1.12
)
. Let
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in(0,1)
be
such that
\varepsilon_{1}+\eta_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\eta_{2}=1
and
let
0<K_{1}<K_{2}
. For
i=1,2
, there is a peak region (say,
B_{i}^{t}
)
of
\xi
near the boundary of
B(o,K_{i}t^{4/3})
with
\xi
-values

\approx\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{i}t^{4/3}}
(
\mu_{0}\triangleq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)}
).
Consider the following Brownian scenario:
the Brownian motion

W
enters
B_{1}^{t}
at time
\varepsilon_{1}t
, stays there
for a period of
\eta_{1}t
, then jumps to
B_{2}^{t}
using
\varepsilon_{2}t

amount of time and stays there for the rest of time
. It turns out
that the contribution produced by this scenario is given by

\displaystyle\exp\big[\big(\sqrt{\mu_{0}}(\eta_{1}\sqrt{K_{1}}+\eta_{2}\sqrt{K_{2}})t^{5/3}\big]\times\exp\big[\big(-\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{4\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}-\frac{(K_{2}-K_{1})^{2}}{4\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}\big)t^{5/3}\big].

(1.13)

The first exponential comes from the periods where
W
stays inside

B_{i}^{t}
(
i=1,2
) to pick up the maximal values of
\xi.
The
second exponential comes from the jumping probabilities. Here we have
implicitly presumed that neither the
\xi
-integral outside the staying periods
nor the staying probabilities would contribute to the leading growth (which is of course
not obvious).

The crucial observation is the following elementary inequality:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mu_{0}}(\eta_{1}\sqrt{K_{1}}+\eta_{2}\sqrt{K_{2}})-\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{4\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}-\frac{(K_{2}-K_{1})^{2}}{4\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \leqslant\sqrt{\mu_{0}}(1-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})\sqrt{K}_{2}-\frac{K_{2}^{2}}{4(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})}.

(1.14)

This inequality suggests that the current scenario is not as good
as just directly jumping to
B_{2}^{t}
in
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})t

amount of time and then staying there for the rest of time. An
extension of this argument shows that the same conclusion holds even
when there are
n
different
\xi
-peak regions and
n
steps of
hoppings / stayings among these regions in any order. Therefore,
hopping and staying among intermediate peak regions of
\xi
(in any arbitrary
way) is not as good as directly travelling to the furthest peak region
and staying there for the rest of time. The latter is defined by the
following event:

\displaystyle{\cal O}_{t}(\varepsilon,K)=

“entering a
\xi
-peak region at time
\varepsilon t
of distance
Kt^{4/3}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and staying there over $[\varepsilon t,t]$"},

whose corresponding contribution (at the logarithmic
t^{5/3}
-scale)
is just
f(\varepsilon,K)
defined by (
1.9
). The optimisation (
1.10
) then leads to the determination
of the parameters
(\varepsilon^{*},K^{*})
given by (
1.11
).

A major difficulty
. The above argument is only formal and
there is a major challenge to make it work. The elementary inequality
(
1.14
), or its extension to
n
hoppings, is good enough
if one pretends that the
\xi
-peak regions had no size (i.e. they
are just degenerate points). This is unfortunately not true in our
situation. It turns out that a natural size scale of
\xi
-peak regions
one shall consider is of order
\eta t^{4/3}
where
\eta
is an
arbitrarily small number (for otherwise, one would lose control on
the length of hoppings and also cannot make the jumping probabilities
in the expression (
1.13
) accurate). In other words, the

\xi
-peak regions (more precisely, clusters of these regions) now
have size of order
\eta t^{4/3}.
If one considers a scenario with

N
hoppings among the clusters (
N
needs to be of order
\eta^{-1}

to make scenarios with
>N
hoppings negligible), the use of the
inequality (
1.13
) (more precisely, its
N
-step extension)
would introduce an error of order
N\eta t^{4/3}.
This is due to
the fact that the entrance and exit locations in these clusters are
generically different; one has to correct the triangle inequality
with the cluster size multiplied by the length of steps.
The issue is that this error term
cannot
be made negligible
(i.e.
\ll e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}
) because
N\eta\gtrsim 1.
The resolution
of this difficulty relies crucially on finer analysis of the geometry
of the Gaussian field
\xi
as well as the consideration of the Brownian
discrete route through clusters at a finer scale.

1.2.3
Comparison with the Euclidean mechanism

Apart from the
t^{5/3}
-growth rate, the localisation mechanism
in the hyperbolic space is also quite different from the Euclidean
situation. In the Euclidean case, the almost sure growth of
the Gaussian field is given by

\max_{x\in B(o,R)}\xi(x)\approx\sqrt{2d\sigma^{2}\log R}\ \ \ \text{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

If one applies the same ansatz (localisation) leading to (
1.8
),
one is led to optimising the following inequality instead:

u(t,o)\gtrsim\exp\big((t-\varepsilon(t))\cdot\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}d\log K(t)}-\frac{K(t)^{2}}{4\varepsilon(t)}\big).

The maximum of the right hand side (under suitable choices of
\varepsilon(t)

and
K(t)
) is given by

\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}d}t\sqrt{\log t}+o(t\sqrt{\log t}),

(1.15)

which explains the growth rate (
1.3
). The interpretation
of the quantity

\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}d}t\sqrt{\log t}\approx t\times\max_{B(o,O(t))}\xi

is very simple: the Brownian motion picks up the maximal values of

\xi
on a ball with
O(t)
-radius for essentially all time during

[0,t]
. However, there are a few key differences between the hyperbolic
and Euclidean situations.

(i)
The maximum (
1.15
)
is attained e.g. by taking

\varepsilon(t)=\frac{t}{(\log t)^{\alpha}},\ K(t)=\frac{Kt}{(\log t)^{\beta}}

(1.16)

for any
\alpha,\beta>0
with
\alpha-2\beta<1/2
and any
K>0
. In other words, the Euclidean asymptotics (
1.3
) is insensitive to a unique localisation scale (there is not a uniquely favorable scale
K^{*}t

on which the Brownian motion is localised). In the hyperbolic case,
there is such a uniquely defined localisation scale
K^{*}t^{4/3}
which is determined by the optimisation problem (
1.10
).

(ii) In the Euclidean case, the Brownian motion takes essentially
no time (
\varepsilon(t)/t\rightarrow 0
) to enter the
\xi
-island; neither the location
of the island nor the cost (probability) of reaching there in time

\varepsilon(t)
would contribute to the growth of
u(t,o)
. In
other words, the growth constant
\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}d}
in the asymptotics
(
1.3
) only reflects the maximum of
\xi
. In the
hyperbolic case, the Brownian motion needs to spend a non-trivial
proportion of time (i.e.
\varepsilon^{*}t=t/5
) to enter the
\xi
-island
which is located near the boundary of
B(o,K^{*}t)
. In addition,
there is a competition between the
\xi
-maximum (reward) and the
probability of travelling to the island (cost); the exact growth constant

L^{*}
is determined from the compromise between the two
quantities.

(iii) As a consequence of (ii), in our modest
opinion the upper bound techniques developed in
[
CM95
]
,
[
GKM00
]

may not apply in the hyperbolic case. In fact, both arguments over there (which
are very different indeed) are quite global and do not reflect the
Brownian localisation at
\xi
-islands since its cost is negligigle
anyway. In the hyperbolic case, such a localisation has to enter the
upper bound argument in an essential way. In other words, one needs
to compare every generic Brownian scenario to the optimal one (defined
by (
1.12
)) and show that none of them produces a larger
growth.

We conclude this section with a few remarks on our results.

Remark 1.2
.

One can consider the hyperbolic space with curvature
-\kappa
(instead of just having
\kappa=1
). The curvature constant
\kappa
will be reflected in the Gaussian field growth as well as the heat kernel estimate which is used to compute probability costs. The aforementioned constants
L^{*},K^{*}
will both depend on
\kappa
. An interesting point is that the constant
\varepsilon^{*}
is always
1/5
regardless of what
\kappa
is. It is also interesting to note that
L^{*}
is non-linear with respect to the strength of the Gaussian field (i.e. its standard variation
\sigma
) in contrast to the Euclidean case.

Remark 1.3
.

To some extent, our proof of Theorem
1.1
implicitly
reveals a localisation phenomenon for the Brownian motion under the
Gibbs measure: with high (Gibbs) probability,
W

reaches a particular location near
\partial B(o,K^{*}t^{4/3})
at time
\varepsilon^{*}t
and
stays there for the rest of time.
On the other hand, it is not clear whether the PAM is
completely localised
, i.e. does there exist a
\xi
-measurable site
Z(t)
such that
v(t,Z_{t})/u(t,o)\rightarrow 1
in probability? Here
v(t,x)
is the solution to the PAM with
\delta_{o}
-initial condition and recall from Remark
1.1

that
u(t,o)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}v(t,x)dx
.

Remark 1.4
.

Although we work on the standard hyperbolic space, we do not use fine
geometric properties in a substantial way (only the exponential volume
growth plays an essential role). Therefore, we expect that the methods
developed in the current work can be utilised and extended to establish
(sharp) asymptotic estimates for similar kind of models in more general
geometric settings.

Further questions

The current article (along with the first part
[

]
) provides
a simple initial attempt towards a deeper understanding on the fine
asymptotic behaviour of PAM in non-Euclidean settings. We mention a few natural questions for future investigation.

1.

Can one derive the quenched fluctuation asymptotics? Since the
precise localisation regime is identified in our methodology, the
fluctuation behaviour might be described in a suitably rescaled picture
with respect to the current localisation. It could be the case that
the fluctuation exponent is again determined by the Euclidean Laplacian
in a similar fashion as in the annealed asymptotics.

2.

How does the asymptotic behaviour of the PAM change when one considers
the Gaussian potential with a more singular covariance structure or
more intrinsically, the spatial white noise (on the 2D/3D hyperbolic space
in this case)? For the white noise case, one needs to apply techniques
from singular SPDEs and the picture is not clear at this stage.

3.

What would the picture look like if one considers a time-dependent
Gaussian potential (e.g. white in time and coloured in space)? In general, questions related to annealed and quenced asymptotics, fluctuation asymptotics, description of localisation vs delocalisation
mechanism (in low vs high temperature) and the large scale behaviour
of Brownian motion under Gibbs measure are widely open in continuous geometric settings.

2
Some preliminary tools

We list a few preliminary tools that will be used frequently in the sequel. The reader is referred to
[
GX25
, Section 2]
for a more general review. Throughout the rest of this article, we fix a base point

o\in\mathbb{H}^{d}.
We will use
Q_{R}
to denote the geodesic ball of radius
R
centered at
o
.

Volume form and heat kernel estimates

The exponential volume growth of
Q_{R}
as
R\rightarrow\infty
plays an essential role in our study. The volume form on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
under geodesic polar coordinates is given by

\mathrm{vol}(d\rho,d\sigma)=\sinh^{d-1}\rho d\rho d\sigma.

The following two-sided uniform estimate for the hyperbolic heat kernel
will also be important to us (cf.
[
DM98
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p(t,x,y)
be the heat kernel on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.
Then one has

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)\asymp

\displaystyle\ t^{-d/2}\exp\big[-\frac{(d-1)^{2}t}{4}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4t}-\frac{(d-1)\rho}{2}\big]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times(1+\rho+t)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}(1+\rho),

(2.1)

where
\rho\triangleq d(x,y)
. Here the notation
f(\cdot)\asymp g(\cdot)

means that there are positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that

C_{1}g(\cdot)\leqslant f(\cdot)\leqslant C_{2}g(\cdot)

uniformly for all variables in the arguments of
f,g.

Hyperbolic Brownian motion

The Brownian motion on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is the Markov family
\{W_{t}^{x}:t\geqslant 0\}

(
x\in M
denotes its starting point) generated by
\Delta
. Its
behaviour differs from the Euclidean Brownian motion in several fundamental
ways. For instance, it travels a distance of order
t
when
t

is large (more precisely,
\frac{d(W_{t}^{x},x)}{(d-1)t}\rightarrow 1

a.s. as
t\rightarrow\infty
) and its angular component with respect
to the starting point
x
converges to a definite limiting angle
(cf.
[
Sul83
]
). In other words, the Brownian motion converges a.s. to a (random) limiting point on the boundary at infinity (which is homeomorphic to the sphere
S^{d-1}
).

The following lemma gives an SDE description for the radial process

R_{t}\triangleq d(W_{t}^{o},o)
. It is a direct consequence of the
explicit expression of
\Delta
under geodesic polar coordinates
with respect to
o
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The process
R_{t}
satisfies an SDE

dR_{t}=\sqrt{2}d\beta_{t}+(d-1)\coth R_{t}dt,

(2.2)

where
\beta_{t}
is a one-dimensional Euclidean Brownian motion.

We also recall an exit time estimate which was proved in
[
GX25
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\tau_{R}
be the exit time of Brownian motion
(starting at
o
) for the ball
Q_{R}.
There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0

such that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{R}\leqslant t)\leqslant C_{1}e^{-C_{2}R^{2}/t}

for all
R\gg t>1.

Ball packing

The following elementary observation will be used for several times
in the article. Let
E
be a subset of a metric space
(X,d)
. An

r
-ball packing of
E
is a maximal way of putting balls of radius

r
inside
E
such that they are all disjoint to each other. Suppose
that
{\cal P}=\{B(x_{i},r):i\in{\cal I}\}
is an
r
-ball packing
of
Q_{R}
(
r<R
). Then
{\cal P}^{\prime}=\{B(x_{i},2r):i\in{\cal I}\}

is a covering of
B(x,R-r).
Similarly, if
{\cal P}
is an
r
-ball
packing of the annulus
A_{a,b}\triangleq\{x:d(x,o)\in[a,b]\}
, then

{\cal P}^{\prime}
is a covering of
A_{a+r,b-r}
.

Feynman-Kac representation

It is standard that (cf.
[
KS88
]
) the solution
u(t,o)
to the
PAM (
1.1
) admits the following stochastic representation:

u(t,o)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds}\big],\ \ \ t\geqslant 0.

(2.3)

Here
W=\{W_{s}:s\geqslant 0\}
is a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{H}^{d}

starting from
o
and the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is taken with
respect to
W
. Our entire analysis will be based on this representation as a starting point.

3
The lower asymptotics

In this section, we establish the precise lower asymptotics of
u(t,o)
.
Recall that

L^{*}\triangleq\frac{3\cdot 2^{4/3}}{5^{5/3}}(\sigma^{2}(d-1))^{2/3}

is the growth constant which comes from optimising the function
f(\varepsilon,K)

(cf. (
1.10
)).

Theorem 3.1
.

With probability one,

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\underline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)\geqslant L^{*}.

(3.1)

The proof of (
3.1
) is conceptually easier than the corresponding
(matching) upper asymptotics, which will be the main task in Section

4
below. Essentially, one only needs to identify a
particular (optimal) Brownian scenario under which the growth
e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}

is achieved. In the following subsections, we develop the major steps
towards proving Theorem
3.1
in a precise mathematical
way.

3.1
Sample function growth of Gaussian field

We begin by establishing a simple but important lemma about the growth
of the Gaussian field
\xi
. This plays a prominent role in the proof
of the main theorem.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Q_{R}
denote the geodesic ball of radius

R
centered at the origin
o
. Then with probability one,

\underset{R\rightarrow\infty}{\overline{\lim}}\frac{\underset{x\in Q_{R}}{\max}|\xi(x)|}{\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}}\leqslant 1.

In particular, for each fixed
\mu>\mu_{0}\triangleq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)}
,
with probability one

\max_{x\in Q_{R}}|\xi(x)|\leqslant\mu\sqrt{R}

(3.2)

for all sufficiently large
R.

Lemma
3.1
is a simple consequence of the following
well-known result (cf.
[
Adl90
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Borell’s Inequality
. Let
\{X_{t}:t\in T\}

be a centered Gaussian process with a.s. bounded sample paths. Then
one has

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\in T}|X_{t}|>\lambda\big)\leqslant 4e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}])^{2}/\sigma_{T}^{2}}

(3.3)

for all
\lambda>\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]
, where
\sigma_{T}^{2}\triangleq\sup_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}].

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
r>0
be a fixed number. For each
R\gg r,
let
{\cal P}_{r}^{R}

be an
r
-ball packing of
Q_{R+r}
so that
\{B_{i}\triangleq B(z_{i},2r):i\in{\cal P}_{r}^{R}\}

is a covering of
Q_{R}.
Then one has

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{R}}|\xi(x)|>\lambda\big)\leqslant\sum_{i\in{\cal P}_{r}^{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in B_{i}}|\xi(x)|>\lambda\big)=|{\cal P}_{r}^{R}|\times\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2r}}|\xi(x)|>\lambda\big),

where
|{\cal P}_{r}^{R}|
denotes the cardinality of
{\cal P}_{r}^{R}

and the last equality follows from the stationarity of
\xi.
Since

\{B(z_{i},r):i\in{\cal P}_{r}^{R}\}
is a disjoint collection of
balls inside
Q_{R+r},
one knows that

|{\cal P}_{r}^{R}|\leqslant\frac{{\rm vol}(Q_{R+r})}{{\rm vol}(Q_{r})}\leqslant C_{d,r}e^{(d-1)R}.

In addition, according to Borell’s inequality (
3.3
) one
has

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2r}}|\xi(x)|>\lambda\big)\leqslant 4e^{-\frac{(\lambda-K_{r})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\ \ \ \forall\lambda>K_{r}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{x\in Q_{2r}}\xi(x)\big].

Therefore, one obtains that

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{R}}|\xi(x)|>\lambda\big)\leqslant 4C_{d,r}e^{(d-1)R-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\lambda-K_{r})^{2}}

(3.4)

for all such
\lambda.

Given fixed
\varepsilon>0,
we choose
\lambda\triangleq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}\cdot\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}.

The exponent on the right hand side of (
3.4
) becomes

(d-1)R-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\lambda-K_{r})^{2}=-\varepsilon(d-1)R+O(\sqrt{R}).

As a result, (
3.4
) is summable over
R
. By the
first Borel-Cantelli lemma, the following estimate

\max_{x\in Q_{R}}|\xi(x)|\leqslant\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}\cdot\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\ \ \ \text{$\forall$\ large $R$}

holds with probability one. The lemma follows since
\varepsilon

is arbitrary.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

Technically, one needs to discretise
R
in order to apply the Borel-Cantelli
lemma. This causes no issue due to the monotonicity of
R\mapsto\max_{x\in Q_{R}}|\xi(x)|
.

The following lemma is also a direct consequence of Borell’s inequality
(
3.3
). This will be useful to us later on.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
R>0,
there exists a positive constant

C_{R}
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{x\in Q_{R}}\xi(x)>\lambda\big)\leqslant e^{-C_{R}\lambda^{2}}

for all
\lambda>0.

3.2
Peak of Gaussian field near boundary

Next, we show that Lemma
3.1
is indeed sharp. More
precisely, there is a (random) location
z_{*}
near the boundary
of
Q_{R}
where
\xi
maintains its peak value
\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}+o(\sqrt{R})

near
z_{*}
.

We need to introduce some notation in order to state the main technical
lemma here. Throughout the rest, we will set
K(t)\triangleq K^{*}t^{4/3}

where
K^{*}
is the optimal scale factor defined by (
1.11
)
(cf. Section
1.2.1
for the reason behind this particular
choice). We will also fix another parameter
\delta
(
0<\delta\ll K^{*}
)
as well as a scale function
\delta(t)
. The parameter
\delta

will be sent to zero eventually and
\delta(t)
will be specified
in (
3.10
) later on (for now, one only needs to keep in mind
that
\delta(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
).

Consider the annulus (with width
\delta t^{4/3}
) defined by

A_{\delta}^{t}\triangleq\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}:(K^{*}-\delta)t^{4/3}<d(x,o)<K^{*}t^{4/3}\}.

(3.5)

Recall that
R_{0}
is the correlation length of
\xi.
Let
{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}

be a given fixed
R_{0}
-ball packing of
A_{\delta}^{t}
. Elements
of
{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}
are disjoint balls
B(z_{i},R_{0})
(
i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}

by abuse of notation). Let

h_{R}\triangleq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)R}\ \ \ (R>0).

(3.6)

The main result of this subsection is stated as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following statement holds true with probability
one: for all sufficiently large
t
there exists some
i^{*}\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}
,
such that

\xi(x)\geqslant h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}}

(3.7)

for all
x\in B(z_{i^{*}},2\delta(t))
.

We need a basic fluctuation estimate in order to prove Lemma
3.3
.
Here we introduce one more scale function
\zeta(h)\triangleq h^{-\beta}

where
\beta\in(1/4,1/2)
is given fixed (e.g. just choose
\beta=1/3
).
The choice of
\delta(t)
will be closely related to the function

\zeta(h)
. We also set

H(t)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}t^{2},\ L(h)\triangleq\frac{h^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},\ \rho(h)\triangleq\frac{h}{\sigma^{2}}.

Basically,
H(t)
is the cumulant generating function of
\xi(o)
,
the function

L(h)=\sup_{\rho>0}\{\rho h-H(\rho)\}

(3.8)

is its Legendre transform and
\rho(h)
is the maximiser in (
3.8
)
(explicitly,
L(h)=\rho(h)h-H(\rho(h))
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For any given
\eta\in(0,1)
, one has

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}}\big|\xi(x)-h\big|<\eta\sqrt{h}\big)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}e^{-L(h)-\eta\rho(h)\sqrt{h}}

for all sufficiently large
h
.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
GKM00
, Lemma 3.3]
. Given fixed
h>0,

consider the change of measure

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{h}}{d\mathbb{P}}\triangleq e^{\rho(h)\xi(o)-H(\rho(h))}.

Let

G_{\eta}\triangleq\big\{\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}}\big|\xi(x)-h\big|<\eta\sqrt{h}\big\}.

Then one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G_{\eta})

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf 1}_{G_{\eta}}e^{H(\rho)-\rho(h)\xi(o)}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf 1}_{G_{\eta}}e^{-\rho(h)(\xi(o)-h)-L(h)}\rangle_{h}\geqslant e^{-L(h)-\delta\rho(h)\sqrt{h}}\mathbb{P}_{h}\big(G_{\eta}\big).

(3.9)

Now a basic observation is that under
\mathbb{P}_{h},
the field

\xi
is a Gaussian with mean function
x\mapsto\rho(h)C(d(x,o))

and the same covariance function
(x,y)\mapsto C(d(x,y)).
This can
be seen e.g. by explicit calculations based on the characteristic
function.

Since
C^{\prime}(0)=0
, there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\big|\frac{C(d(x,o))}{\sigma^{2}}-1\big|\leqslant Ld(x,o)^{2}

for all
x\in Q_{1}.
It follows that

\big|\rho(h)C(d(x,o))-h\big|\leqslant 4Lh\zeta(h)^{2}=4Lh^{1-2\beta}\ \ \ \forall x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}.

Since
\beta>1/4
, one has

\big|\rho(h)C(d(x,o))-h\big|<\frac{\eta}{2}\sqrt{h}\ \ \ \forall x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}

provided that
h
is large. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{h}(G_{\eta})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}_{h}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}}\big|\xi(x)-\rho(h)C(d(x,o))\big|<\frac{\eta\sqrt{h}}{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(h)}}|\xi(x)|<\frac{\eta\sqrt{h}}{2}\big).

Note that the last probability converges to
\mathbb{P}(|\xi(o)|<\infty)=1

as
h\rightarrow\infty
. As a consequence, the right hand side of
(
3.9
) is bounded below by
e^{-L(h)-\eta\rho(h)\sqrt{h}}/2

for all large
h
. The lemma thus follows.
∎

We now proceed to prove Lemma
3.3
. We define

\delta(t)\triangleq\zeta(\tilde{h}(t))=\tilde{h}(t)^{-\beta},

(3.10)

where
\tilde{h}(t)\triangleq h_{K(t)}-\sqrt{h_{K(t)}}.
Note that

\delta(t)=O(t^{-2\beta/3})
as
t\rightarrow\infty.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be a given fixed number. Recall that
{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}

is an
R_{0}
-ball packing of the annulus
A_{\delta}^{t}
defined
by (
3.5
) and we also set
\hat{B}_{i}^{t}\triangleq B(z_{i},2\delta(t))

(
i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}
). For each
t>0
, consider the event

E(t)\triangleq\bigcup_{i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}}\big\{\xi(x)>\tilde{h}(t)-\eta\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}\ \forall x\in\hat{B}_{i}^{t}\big\}.

By definition, one has

\displaystyle E(t)^{c}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}}\big\{\max_{x\in\hat{B}_{i}^{t}}|\xi(x)-\tilde{h}(t)|\geqslant\eta\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}\big\}.

Since the correlation length of
\xi
is
R_{0}
and
\delta(t)\rightarrow 0

as
t\rightarrow\infty
, the Gaussian families
{\cal G}_{i}^{t}\triangleq\{\xi(x):x\in\hat{B}_{i}^{t}\}

(
i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}
) are independent from each other when

t
is large. By the stationarity of
\xi,
one has

\mathbb{P}(E(t)^{c})\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(t)}}|\xi(x)-\tilde{h}(t)|\geqslant\eta\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}\big)^{|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|},

(3.11)

where
|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|
denotes the cardinality of
{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}
.
Let us set

p\triangleq\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{2\zeta(t)}}|\xi(x)-\tilde{h}(t)|<\eta\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}\big).

The right hand side of (
3.11
) is further estimated
as

(1-p)^{|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|}\leqslant e^{-p|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|}.

On the one hand, by using Lemma
3.4
as well as the explicit
expressions of
L(h),\rho(h)
, one finds that

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}e^{-L(\tilde{h}(t))-\eta\rho(\tilde{h}(t))\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp\big[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(h_{K(t)}^{2}-2h_{K(t)}^{3/2}+h_{K(t)})-\frac{\eta}{\sigma^{2}}(h_{K(t)}-\sqrt{h_{K(t)}})^{3/2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp\big[-(d-1)K(t)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}^{3/2}-\frac{\eta}{\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}^{3/2}(1-h_{K(t)}^{-1/2})^{3/2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}\big].

On the other hand, note that
\{B(z_{i},2R_{0}):i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}\}

is a covering of the
R_{0}
-reduced annulus

\hat{A}_{\delta}^{t}\triangleq\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{d}:(K^{*}-\delta)t^{4/3}+R_{0}<d(x,o)<K^{*}t^{4/3}-R_{0}\}.

In particular, one has

|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|\geqslant\frac{{\rm vol}\hat{A}_{\delta}^{t}}{{\rm vol}Q_{2R_{0}}}=\frac{\int_{K(t)-\delta t^{4/3}+R_{0}}^{K(t)-R_{0}}\sinh^{d-1}\rho d\rho}{\int_{0}^{2R_{0}}\sinh^{d-1}\rho d\rho}.

(3.12)

It is easily seen that when
t
is sufficiently large,

|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|\geqslant C_{d,R_{0}}\big(e^{(d-1)(K(t)-R_{0})}-e^{(d-1)(K(t)-\delta t^{4/3}+R_{0})}\big)\geqslant C^{\prime}_{d,R_{0}}e^{(d-1)K(t)}.

Therefore, with a suitable constant
C=C^{\prime\prime}_{d,R}
one concludes that

p|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|\geqslant C\exp\big[\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}^{3/2}-\frac{\eta}{\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}^{3/2}(1-h_{K(t)}^{-1/2})^{3/2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}\big].

By fixing
\eta\in(0,1/2)
, it follows that

e^{-p|{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}|}\leqslant\exp\big[-C\exp\big(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}h_{K(t)}^{3/2}+o(h_{K(t)}^{3/2})\big)\big]

(3.13)

for all sufficiently large
t
.

It is now clear that (
3.13
) is summable in
t
. According
to the first Borel-Cantelli lemma,

\mathbb{P}(E(t)^{c}\ \text{for infinitely many }t)=0.

In other words, with probability one
E(t)
occurs for all sufficiently
large
t
. By further reducing
\eta
if necessary, one can ensure
that

\tilde{h}(t)-\eta\sqrt{\tilde{h}(t)}>h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}}

for all large
t
. In particular,

E(t)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in{\cal P}_{R_{0}}^{t}}\big\{\xi(x)>h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}}\ \forall x\in\hat{B}_{i}^{t}\big\}.

The result of the lemma thus follows.

∎

3.3
The main localisation

We are now able to define an optimal Brownian scenario under which
the lower asymptotics for
u(t,o)
can be obtained precisely. Let

\varepsilon^{*},K^{*}
be the optimal parameters given by (
1.11
).
We choose
K(t)\triangleq K^{*}t^{4/3}
and the parameters
\delta
,

\delta(t)
as in Section
3.2
. In what follows,
we fix a realisation of the Gaussian field
\xi
(outside a suitable
null set where the relevant almost sure properties of
\xi
hold
true). Recall that
B(z^{t},\delta(t))
is the (
\xi
-dependent)
ball contained in the annulus
A_{\delta}^{t}
such that the estimate
(
3.7
) holds on
B(z^{t},2\delta(t))
. We let
s\mapsto\gamma_{s}^{t}

(parametrised on
[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]
) be the geodesic connecting

o
and
z^{t}
.

Our optimal Brownian scenario is defined by

{\cal M}(t)\triangleq C(t)\cap E(t)\cap S(t),

where the three events

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq\big\{W_{s}\in B(\gamma_{s}^{t},\delta)\cap Q_{K(t)}\ \forall s\in[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]\big\},

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq\{W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))\},

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq\{W([\varepsilon^{*}t,t])\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\}

correspond to “concentrating near the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
”,
“entering
B(z^{t},\delta(t))
” and “staying inside
B(z^{t},2\delta(t))
”
respectively. The role of
C(t)
is to allow one to replace the Brownian
trajectory by the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
, along which the integral
of
\xi
over
[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]
is easier to estimate. The
role of
S(t)
, as we explained in Section
1.2.1
,
is to let the Brownian motion stay at the peak region of
\xi
to
pick up its maximal values.

By localising on
{\cal M}(t)
and conditioning on
{\cal F}_{\varepsilon^{*}t}
,
one has

\displaystyle u(t,o)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{o}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};C(t)\cap E(t)\cap S(t)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{o}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds}{\bf 1}_{C(t)\cap E(t)}\mathbb{E}[e^{\int_{\varepsilon^{*}t}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds}{\bf 1}_{S(t)}|{\cal F}_{\varepsilon^{*}t}]\big].

According to Lemma
3.3
,

\int_{\varepsilon^{*}t}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds\geqslant(1-\varepsilon^{*})t\big(h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}}\big)\ \ \ \text{on }S(t).

It follows from the Markov property that

\displaystyle u(t,o)\geqslant

\displaystyle e^{(1-\varepsilon^{*})t(h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}})}\mathbb{E}_{o}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds}{\bf 1}_{C(t)\cap E(t)}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\mathbb{P}_{W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}}\big(\tilde{W}([0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\big)\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle e^{(1-\varepsilon^{*})t(h_{K(t)}-2\sqrt{h_{K(t)}})}\times\inf_{x\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))}\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(\tilde{W}([0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t])\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\int_{B(z^{t},\delta(t))}\mathbb{E}_{o}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds}{\bf 1}_{C(t)}|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q\big]p(\varepsilon^{*}t,o,q){\rm vol}(dq).

(3.14)

Here
\tilde{W}
denotes a new hyperbolic Brownian motion and
p(s,x,y)

is the heat kernel on
\mathbb{H}^{d}.
Our main task is now reduced
to estimating the quantities appearing on the right hand side of (
3.14
).
For this purpose, we divide the discussion into several individual
steps.

3.3.1
Step 1: Staying probability after time
\varepsilon^{*}t

We first estimate the “staying probability”:

\inf_{x\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))}\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(\tilde{W}([0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t])\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\big).

This is achieved by the lemma below.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exist universal constants
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}>0
,
such that

\inf_{x\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))}\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(\tilde{W}([0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t])\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\big)\geqslant\Lambda_{1}e^{-\Lambda_{2}\frac{(1-\varepsilon^{*})t}{\delta(t)^{2}}}

(3.15)

for all sufficiently large
t
.

Proof.

First of all, for any
x\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(\tilde{W}([0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t])\subseteq B(z^{t},2\delta(t))\big)\geqslant

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}\big(d(\tilde{W}_{s},x)<\delta(t)\ \forall s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]\big).

To estimate the latter probability, let
\tilde{R}_{s}
be the process

d\tilde{R}_{s}=\sqrt{2}d\beta_{s}+(d-1)F(\tilde{R}_{s})ds,

where
F(r)=\coth r
for
r\leqslant\delta(t)/4
and
F(r)=2/\delta(t)
for
r\geqslant\delta(t)/4
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]}R_{s}<\delta(t)\big)\geq\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]}\tilde{R}_{s}<\delta(t)\big)\;.

The latter event contains the event that
\tilde{R}
reaches
\delta(t)/2
at some
\tau\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]
and then remains in the interval
(\delta(t)/4,3\delta(t)/4)
. Hence, by strong Markov property, one has

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]}\tilde{R}_{s}<\delta(t)\big)\geq\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]}\big|\beta_{s}+\frac{Cs}{\delta(t)}\big|<\frac{\delta(t)}{8}\big)

for some
C>0
independent of
t
. By scaling, one has

\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{s\in[0,(1-\varepsilon^{*})t]}\big|\beta_{s}+\frac{Cs}{\delta(t)}\big|<\frac{\delta(t)}{8}\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{s\in[0,64(1-\varepsilon^{*})t/\delta^{2}(t)}]|\beta_{s}-C^{\prime}s|<1\Big)

for some possibly different
C^{\prime}
. The claim then follows from standard large deviation estimates for Brownian motion.

∎

3.3.2
Step 2: Analysis of the
\xi
-integral before time
\varepsilon^{*}t

Next, we show that the integral
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds

is essentially negligible on the event
C(t)
(with respect to the
desired growth
e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}
). The main estimate is summarised
as follows.

Lemma 3.6
.

There exist deterministic constants
\Lambda_{3},\Lambda_{4,\delta}>0

depending on
d
and the distribution of
\xi
(
\Lambda_{4,\delta}

depends additionally on
\delta
), such that with (
\xi
-)probability
one, the following estimate

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds\geqslant-\Lambda_{3}\delta t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{4,\delta}t^{1/3}

(3.16)

holds true on the (
W
-)event
C(t)
for all sufficiently large

t
.

Our strategy for proving Lemma
3.6
is to first analyse
the integral where
W
is replaced by the geodesic
\gamma^{t}

and then to estimate the error. We discuss these two steps separately,
each being achieved by a key lemma.

I. Estimation of the integral
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds

Lemma 3.7
.

There exists a deterministic constant
C_{d,\xi,\delta}>0
,
such that with probability one, the following estimate

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds\geqslant-\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{(K^{*}-\delta)}\big[C_{d,\xi,\delta}t^{1/3}+\delta K^{*}t^{5/3}\big]

(3.17)

holds true for all large
t
.

To prove Lemma
3.7
, we will borrow another lemma from
Section
4.2
below which will be used in the upper bound
argument in an essential way.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}\triangleq\{x\in Q_{K(t)}:\xi(x)<-\delta t^{2/3}\}.

Then there exist deterministic constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending
only on
d
and the distribution of
\xi
, such that by setting

L_{\delta}\triangleq\frac{C_{1}}{\delta^{2}},\ \eta_{\delta}\triangleq C_{2}\delta^{4}

the following statement holds true:

“With probability one, for all large
t
no
balls of radius
\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}t^{4/3}
inside
Q_{K(t)}

can contain
\geqslant L_{\delta}
points in
\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}

that are at least
9R_{0}
-apart from each other”.

Proof.

This is just Lemma
4.2
(i) below with
K_{0}

replaced by
K^{*}
and
\xi
replaced by
-\xi
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

First of all, a simple change of variables shows that

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds=\frac{\varepsilon^{*}t}{d(o,z^{t})}\int_{0}^{d(o,z^{t})}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds,

(3.18)

where
\hat{\gamma}^{t}
denotes the unit speed reparametrisation
of
\gamma^{t}
. In order to apply Lemma
3.8
, let us
partition the geodesic arc
\hat{\gamma}^{t}
into equal sub-arcs

\{\Gamma_{i}\}
of length
2\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}t^{4/3}
; the total
number of sub-arcs is estimated as

N(t)=\frac{d(o,z^{t})}{2\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}t^{4/3}}\leqslant\frac{K^{*}}{2\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}}.

(3.19)

Each sub-arc
\Gamma_{i}
is further divided into equal pieces
\{\Gamma_{i,j}:1\leqslant j\leqslant 2\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}t^{4/3}/9R_{0}\}

of length
9R_{0}
. Note that
\Gamma_{i}
is contained in the ball

B_{i}
of radius
\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}t^{4/3}
centered at the
midpoint of
\Gamma_{i}
. According to Lemma
3.8
, at
most
2L_{\delta}
number of
\Gamma_{i,j}
’s (with
i
fixed)
can have non-empty intersection with
\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}
(for
all large
t
). Indeed, suppose that
\Gamma_{i,j_{1}},\cdots,\Gamma_{i,j_{2L_{\delta}}}

all contain elements in
\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}
. One would obtain

L_{\delta}
points

x_{1}\in\Gamma_{i,j_{2}}\cap\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t},\ x_{2}\in\Gamma_{i,j_{4}}\cap\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t},\cdots,\ x_{L_{\delta}}\in\Gamma_{i,j_{2L_{\delta}}}\cap\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}

inside
B_{i}
which are all
9R_{0}
-apart from each other. This
leads to a contradiction to Lemma
3.8
.

Let
\zeta_{i}
denote the union of the sub-arcs
\Gamma_{i,j}

inside
\Gamma_{i}
which have non-empty intersection with
\bar{I}_{\delta}^{t}
.
One can write

\int_{0}^{d(o,z^{t})}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds=\sum_{i}\big(\int_{\zeta_{i}}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds+\int_{\Gamma_{i}\backslash\zeta_{i}}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds\big).

For the integral over the “bad” region
\zeta_{i}
, since
\hat{\gamma}^{t}\subseteq Q_{K(t)}

one simply applies the crude lower bound (cf. Lemma
3.1
)

\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})\geqslant-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\ \ \ \forall s\in\zeta_{i},

where
\mu>\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)}
is a given fixed number. It follows
from (
3.19
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{\zeta_{i}}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds

\displaystyle\geqslant-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\times 2L_{\delta}\cdot 9R_{0}\times\frac{K^{*}}{2\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}}=-\frac{9\mu R_{0}L_{\delta}K^{*3/2}}{\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}}t^{2/3}.

(3.20)

For the “good” region
\Gamma_{i}\backslash\zeta_{i}
, one has

\sum_{i}\int_{\Gamma_{i}\backslash\zeta_{i}}\xi(\hat{\gamma}_{s}^{t})ds\geqslant-\delta t^{2/3}\times d(o,z^{t})\geqslant-\delta K^{*}t^{6/3}.

(3.21)

By substituting (
3.20
,
3.21
) into (
3.18
),
one obtains that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds

\displaystyle\geqslant-\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{(K^{*}-\delta)}\big[\frac{9\mu R_{0}L_{\delta}K^{*3/2}}{\sqrt{\eta_{\delta}}}t^{1/3}+\delta K^{*}t^{5/3}\big]

for all sufficiently large
t
. The estimate (
3.17
) thus
follows.

∎

II. Estimation of the difference
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds-\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds

Lemma 3.9
.

There exists a deterministic constant
D_{d,\xi}>0,

such that with (
\xi
-)probability one, the following estimate

\big|\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds-\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds\big|\leqslant D_{d,\xi}\varepsilon^{*}\sqrt{K^{*}}\delta t^{5/3}

(3.22)

holds true on the (
W
-)event
C(t)
for all sufficiently large

t
.

Proof.

On the event
C(t),
the Brownian motion
W
is within the
\delta
-tubular
neighbourhood of the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
. In particular, one knows
that

\big|\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(W_{s})ds-\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{*}t}\xi(\gamma_{s}^{t})ds\big|\leqslant\|\nabla\xi\|_{\infty;Q_{K(t)}}\delta\varepsilon^{*}t.

(3.23)

It remains to estimate the growth of the field
\nabla\xi
.

We claim that there exists a deterministic constant
D_{d,\xi}>0,

such that with probability one,

\sup_{x\in Q_{R}}|\nabla\xi(x)|\leqslant D_{d,\xi}\sqrt{R}\ \ \ \forall\ \text{sufficiently large }R.

(3.24)

The proof of this claim is essentially the same as the one for Lemma

3.1
. Indeed, on the geodesic ball
Q_{r}
(under
some coordinate system), one has

|\nabla\xi|^{2}\leqslant C_{r}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\sup_{x\in Q_{r}}|\partial_{i}\xi(x)|^{2}.

(3.25)

Since
\{\partial_{i}\xi(x):x\in Q_{r}\}
is also a Gaussian field,
one concludes from Borell’s inequality (
3.3
) and (
3.25
)
that

\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in Q_{r}}|\nabla\xi|>\lambda\big)\leqslant e^{-C_{r,\xi}\lambda^{2}}

for all large
\lambda
. The same estimate holds for any ball of
radius
r
due to the stationarity of the field
|\nabla\xi|.
The
rest of the argument is identical to the proof of Lemma
3.1
.

By substituting (
3.24
) into (
3.23
), the
desired estimate (
3.22
) thus follows.
∎

It is now clear that Lemma
3.6
follows directly
from Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.9
.

3.3.3
Step 3: Localisation around geodesic before time
\varepsilon^{*}t

We need one more estimate to complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
:
the conditional probability

\mathbb{P}_{o}(C(t)|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q)=\mathbb{P}_{o}\big(W_{s}\in B(\gamma_{s}^{t},\delta)\cap Q_{K(t)}\ \forall s\in[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q\big)

(3.26)

where
q\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))
. This step is technically more involved
than the previous ingredients; some geometric consideration (nonpositive
curvature) is needed along with large deviation estimates for Brownian
bridge. The main estimate for this part is contained in the lemma
below.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\delta,\eta
be fixed parameters such that

\delta<K^{*},\ \eta<\min\big\{\frac{\delta}{24},\frac{\delta^{2}}{2560K^{*}}\big\}.

(3.27)

There exist deterministic constants
\Lambda_{5},\Lambda_{6,\eta}>0

depending on
d
and the distribution of
\xi
(
\Lambda_{6,\eta}

depends additionally on
\eta
), such that

\mathbb{P}_{o}(C(t)|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q)\geqslant\exp\big(-\Lambda_{5}\eta t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{6,\eta}t^{4/3}\log t\big)

(3.28)

for all sufficiently large
t
and
q\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))
.

The probability
\mathbb{P}_{o}(C(t)|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q)
is
essentially concerned with a Brownian bridge. To summarise our main
strategy, we partition the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
into normal scale
so that one can apply large deviation estimates for Brownian bridges
on each piece. This allows one to easily get rid of the supremum in
the event
C(t)
, leaving a suitable event that is only concerned
with the bridge at the partition points. The probability of the latter
event is expressed in terms of the heat kernel, which is then easily
estimated by using heat kernel bounds.

In what follows , we develop the main steps towards proving Lemma

3.10
. Given
u<v
and
x,y\in M
, we will use
\mathbb{P}^{u,x;v,y}(\cdot)

to denote the law of the Brownian bridge from
x
(at time
u
)
to
y
(at time
v
). This is the Brownian motion starting from

x
at time
u
conditioned on reaching
y
at time
v
.

Remark 3.2
.

It is not surprising to expect a reasonable lower bound for the probability

\mathbb{P}_{o}(C(t)|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q)
. Indeed, the condition
“
W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q
” forces the Brownian motion to travel
a distance of order
t^{4/3}
in time scale
t
, which is much faster
than its normal speed. As a result, it should travel like a geodesic
(in order to reach
q
fast). We do not expect the estimate (
3.28
)
to be sharp but it is enough for our purpose.

I. Geodesic partition

We partition the geodesic
\gamma^{t}
into
N\triangleq d(o,q)/K^{*}

equal pieces. This means partitioning
[0,\varepsilon^{*}t]
into

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N-1}<t_{N}=\varepsilon^{*}t

so that
d(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})=K^{*}
and

t_{i}-t_{i-1}=\frac{\varepsilon^{*}t}{N}\in\big[\frac{\varepsilon^{*}t^{-1/3}}{K^{*}},\frac{2\varepsilon^{*}t^{-1/3}}{K^{*}}\big].

One also notes that the intersection with
Q_{K(t)}
in (
3.26
)
is not needed; since
B(z^{t},R_{0})\subseteq Q_{K(t)}
one has

\displaystyle d(W_{s},o)

\displaystyle\leqslant d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})+d(\gamma_{s}^{t},o)\leqslant d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})+d(z^{t},o)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta+K(t)-R_{0}<K(t)

provided that
W_{s}\in B(\gamma_{s}^{t},\delta)
and
\delta<R_{0}.

Under above partition, one can further write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{o}(C(t)|W_{\varepsilon^{*}t}=q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t_{N}}d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})<\delta\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\big\{\sup_{t_{i-1}\leqslant s\leqslant t_{i}}d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})<\delta,\ d(W_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})<\eta\big\}\big).

Here
\eta\ll\delta
is another independent parameter which is to
be specified later on. We will denote the right hand side by
\phi_{\delta,\eta}(t)
.

Remark 3.3
.

Technically, one needs to take integer part of
d(o,q)/K^{*}
to
define the number of partition points. This will not affect the main
discussion and we decide not to bother with this small technicality.

II. A uniform LDP estimate for Brownian bridge

We will make use of a large deviation estimate to analyse the probability

\phi_{\delta,\eta}(t)
. The key technical lemma is summarised as
follows.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\delta,\eta
be given fixed which satisfy the
relations in (
3.27
). Then there exist positive constants

C,\kappa,s_{*}
depending on
d,K^{*},\delta,\eta
, such that the
following estimate

\mathbb{P}^{0,x;s,y}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant u\leqslant s}d(W_{u},\gamma_{u}^{x,y;s})>\frac{\delta}{2}\big)\leqslant Ce^{-\kappa/s}

(3.29)

holds uniformly for all
s\in(0,s_{*})
,
x\in B(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\eta)
,

y\in B(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},\eta)
and all
i
. Here
\gamma^{x,y;s}

denotes the geodesic parametrised on
[0,s]
joining
x
to
y
.

Proof.

The detailed proof of this lemma is deferred to Appendix
A
.
The exponential decay is a consequence of Hsu’s LDP for Riemannian
Brownian bridge (cf.
[
Hsu90
]
); the extra technical effort here
is to obtain uniform estimates with respect to the endpoints
x,y
,
which is not immediate from Hsu’s original work. The positivity of

\kappa
follows from the positivity of energy excess for paths deviating
from the geodesic (cf. Lemma
A.2
). This part relies
on the geometry of nonpositive curvature and is not true in general.
∎

III. Small ball estimate at partition points

The LDP estimate (
3.29
) is used for getting rid of the supremum
inside
\phi_{\delta,\eta}(t)
, leaving the small ball events at
the partition points. We now summarise another lemma which is used
for estimating such events.

Lemma 3.12
.

There are positive constants
C_{\eta},D
depending
on
d,\varepsilon^{*},K^{*}
(
C_{\eta}
depends additionally on

\eta
) and a universal power function
P(t)
of
t
, such that

\mathbb{P}^{(t_{i-1},x;t_{N},q)}\big(d(W_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})<\eta\big)\geqslant C_{\eta}P(t)\exp\big(-D\eta t^{1/3}\big)

(3.30)

for all
i\leqslant N-1
,
x\in B(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\eta)
,
q\in B(z^{t},\delta(t))
,

\eta\in(0,1)
and
t>1
.

Proof.

The distribution of the Brownian bridge is given by

\mathbb{P}^{(t_{i-1},x;t_{N},q)}(W_{t_{i}}\in dy)=\frac{p(t_{i}-t_{i-1},x,y)p(t_{N}-t_{i},y,q)}{p(t_{N}-t_{i-1},x,q)}{\rm vol}(dy).

Using this formula and the two-sided heat kernel estimate (
2.1
),
one finds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t_{i-1},x;t_{N},q)}\big(d(W_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})<\eta\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{B(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},\eta)}\frac{p(t_{i}-t_{i-1},x,y)p(t_{N}-t_{i},y,q)}{p(t_{N}-t_{i-1},x,q)}{\rm vol}(dy)

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{d,\varepsilon^{*},K^{*}}P(t)\int_{B(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},\eta)}\exp\big[-\frac{1}{4}\big(\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}+\frac{d(y,q)^{2}}{t_{N}-t_{i}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ -\frac{d(x,q)^{2}}{t_{N}-t_{i-1}}\big)-\frac{d-1}{2}\big(d(x,y)+d(y,q)-d(x,q)\big)\big]{\rm vol}(dy),

(3.31)

where
P(t)
denotes some power function of
t
(of the form
t^{-r}

for some
r>0
). By the assumptions on
x,y,q
and noting that
\delta(t)<\eta

for
t
large, one has

\displaystyle d(x,y)

\displaystyle\leqslant d(x,\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t})+d(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})+d(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},y)<2\eta+\frac{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}{t_{N}}d(o,z^{t}),

\displaystyle d(y,q)

\displaystyle\leqslant d(y,\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})+d(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},z^{t})+d(z^{t},q)<2\eta+\frac{t_{N}-t_{i}}{t_{N}}d(o,z^{t}),

\displaystyle d(x,q)

\displaystyle\geqslant d(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},z^{t})-d(x,\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t})-d(z^{t},q)>\frac{t_{N}-t_{i-1}}{t_{N}}d(o,z^{t})-2\eta.

After substituting the above three estimates into (
3.31
)
and simplification, one arrives at the following estimate:

\mathbb{P}^{(t_{i-1},x;t_{N},q)}\big(d(W_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})<\eta\big)\geqslant C_{d,\varepsilon^{*},K^{*},\eta}P(t)\exp\big[-\big(\frac{3\eta K^{*}}{\varepsilon^{*}}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\big)t^{1/3}-3(d-1)\eta\big].

The desired inequality (
3.30
) follows by absorbing and
renaming constants.
∎

IV. Proof of Lemma
3.10

We are now in a position to prove Lemma
3.10
. Let
\delta,\eta

be given fixed parameters as in Lemma
3.11
. To ease notation,
we write

\psi(t)\triangleq\mathbb{P}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}(S_{i}\cap E_{i})\big)

where

S_{i}\triangleq\big\{\sup_{t_{i-1}\leqslant s\leqslant t_{i}}d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})<\delta\big\},\ E_{i}\triangleq\big\{d(W_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})<\eta\big\}.

By conditioning on
{\cal F}_{t_{N-1}}
and noting that
E_{N}

is trivially satisfied (since
\delta(t)<\eta
for large
t
), one
has

\psi(t)=\mathbb{E}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big[\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-1},W_{t_{N-1}};t_{N},q}\big(\sup_{t_{N-1}\leqslant s\leqslant t_{N}}d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})<\delta\big);\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-1}(S_{i}\cap E_{i})\big].

Let
\alpha
be the geodesic parametrised on
[t_{N-1},t_{N}]
joining

W_{t_{N-1}}
to
q
. Since
\eta\vee\delta(t)<\delta/2
(for
t

large), it is easily seen from the geodesic convexity property in
hyperbolic space (cf. (
A.6
) below) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-1},W_{t_{N-1}};t_{N},q}\big(\sup_{t_{N-1}\leqslant s\leqslant t_{N}}d(W_{s},\gamma_{s}^{t})<\delta\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-1},W_{t_{N-1}};t_{N},q}\big(\sup_{t_{N-1}\leqslant s\leqslant t_{N}}d(W_{s},\alpha_{s})<\frac{\delta}{2}\big).

(3.32)

In addition, note that

t_{N}-t_{N-1}=\frac{\varepsilon^{*}t}{N}\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{*}t^{-1/3}.

According to Lemma
3.11
, the right hand side of (
3.32
)
is bounded below by
1-Ce^{-\frac{\kappa}{2\varepsilon^{*}}t^{1/3}}

provided that
t>(2\varepsilon^{*}/s_{*})^{3}.
Here
C,\kappa,s_{*}

are constants depending on
d,K^{*},\delta,\eta
. We further assume
that
t>T\triangleq(2\varepsilon^{*}\kappa^{-1}\log 2C)^{3}
so that

1-Ce^{-\frac{\kappa}{2\varepsilon^{*}}t^{1/3}}>1/2
. As a consequence,
one obtains that

\psi(t)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-1}(S_{i}\cap E_{i})\big)

(3.33)

for all
t>T
.

The next conditioning involves the use of Lemma
3.12
.
One has from (
3.33
) that

\displaystyle\psi(t)\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big[\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-2},W_{t_{N-2}};t_{N},q}(S_{N-1}\cap E_{N-1});\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-2}(S_{i}\cap E_{i})\big].

The inner probability is estimated as (
x\triangleq W_{t_{N-2}}
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-2},x;t_{N},q}(S_{N-1}\cap E_{N-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{t_{N-2},x;t_{N},q}\big({\bf 1}_{B(\gamma_{t_{N-1}}^{t},\eta)}(W_{t_{N-1}})\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-2},x;t_{N-1},W_{t_{N-1}}}(S_{N-1})\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}^{t_{N-2},x;t_{N},q}\big(d(W_{t_{N-1}},\gamma_{t_{N-1}}^{t})<\eta\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}C_{\eta}P(t)\exp\big(-D\eta t^{1/3}\big).

The first inequality above follows from the same argument leading
to (
3.33
) based on the use of Lemma
3.11
. The
second inequality is a consequence of Lemma
3.12
.
Therefore, one obtains that

\psi(t)\geqslant 2^{-2}C_{\eta}P(t)\exp\big(-D\eta t^{1/3}\big)\times\mathbb{P}^{0,o;t_{N},q}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{N-2}(S_{i}\cap E_{i})\big).

One can now proceed by conditioning backward recursively to obtain
that (recall
N\leqslant t^{4/3}
)

\psi(t)\geqslant(2^{-1}C_{\eta})^{t^{4/3}}e^{t^{4/3}\log P(t)}e^{-D\eta t^{5/3}}.

Since
P(t)
is a power function, the estimate (
3.16
)
thus follows by renaming and merging constants.

3.3.4
Step 4: Completing the proof of Theorem
3.1

We are now in a position to complete the proof of the main lower asymptotics
(
3.1
).

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We shall apply Lemma
3.5
, Lemma
3.6
,
Lemma
3.10
as well as the heat kernel estimate (
2.1
)
to the initial lower bound (
3.14
). First of all, one
has from (
2.1
) that

p(\varepsilon^{*}t,o,q)\geqslant C_{d}P(t)\exp\big(-\frac{K^{*2}}{4\varepsilon^{*}}t^{5/3}-\frac{(d-1)K^{*}t^{4/3}}{2}-\frac{(d-1)^{2}\varepsilon^{*}t}{4}\big)

where
C_{d}
is a positive constant depending only on
d
and
P(t)

is some universal power function of
t
. After all these substitutions,
one arrives at the following lower estimate:

\displaystyle u(t,o)\geqslant

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}C_{d}\delta(t)^{d}P(t)\exp\big[(1-\varepsilon^{*})t(h_{K(t)}-\sqrt{h_{K(t)}})-\frac{(1-\varepsilon^{*})\Lambda_{2}t}{\delta(t)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\Lambda_{3}\delta t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{4,\delta}t^{1/3}-\Lambda_{5}\eta t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{6,\eta}t^{4/3}\log t

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\frac{K^{*2}}{4\varepsilon^{*}}t^{5/3}-\frac{(d-1)K^{*}t^{4/3}}{2}-\frac{(d-1)^{2}\varepsilon^{*}t}{4}\big]

(3.34)

for all
\delta,\eta
satisfying (
3.27
) and all large

t
.

Recall from Section
1.2.1
that the optimal parameters

(\varepsilon^{*},K^{*})
are defined so that the optimal growth
exponent

L^{*}=(1-\varepsilon^{*})\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K^{*}}-\frac{K^{*2}}{4\varepsilon^{*}}.

(3.35)

Also recall that
h_{K(t)}=\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}(d-1)K^{*}}t^{2/3}
and

\delta(t)=O(t^{-2\beta/3})
with
\beta\in(1/4,1/2)
. By substituting
(
3.35
) into (
3.34
), one finally concludes
that

u(t,o)\geqslant\exp\big(L^{*}t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{3}\delta t^{5/3}-\Lambda_{5}\eta t^{5/3}-o(t^{5/3})\big)

where the
o(t^{5/3})
-term can depend on
\delta,\eta
. The desired
lower estimate (
3.1
) thus follows by first taking
t\rightarrow\infty

and then sending
\eta,\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is now complete.

∎

4
The matching upper asymptotics

In this section, we prove the matching upper bound for (
3.1
).
The main theorem is stated as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
L^{*}
be the number defined by (
1.10
).
Then with probability one,

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\overline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log u(t,o)\leqslant L^{*}.

(4.1)

In the lower bound proof, we essentially identified a particular scenario
of Brownian motion under which the solution
u(t,o)
picks up the
growth of
e^{L^{*}t^{4/3}}.
To prove the matching upper bound (
4.1
),
one has to take into account all possible Brownian trajectories and
show that
no Brownian scenarios (nor their total contribution)
could produce a growth exponent that is larger than

L^{*}.
This
is much harder than the lower bound. In the following subsections,
we develop the major steps for the proof of Theorem
4.1

in a precise mathematical way.

4.1
An initial localisation

As an initial step, we first perform a simple localisation of Brownian
motion on a large fixed ball (in the scale of
t^{4/3}
).

Lemma 4.1
.

There exist deterministic numbers
K_{0},C_{K_{0}}>0
,
such that for almost every realisation of
\xi,
one has

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(W_{s},o)>K_{0}t^{4/3}\big]\leqslant C_{K_{0}}

for all large
t
.

Proof.

Given
t,K>0,
let us denote

{\cal N}_{K}^{t}\triangleq\big\{\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(W_{s},o)>Kt^{4/3}\big\}.

One further writes

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};{\cal N}_{K}^{t}\big]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};{\cal N}_{n}\big]

where

{\cal N}_{n}\triangleq\big\{\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(W_{s},o)\in[K_{n}t^{4/3},K_{n+1}t^{4/3}]\big\}\ \ \ (K_{n}\triangleq nK).

According to Lemma
3.1
, on each event
{\cal N}_{n}

one has

\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}\xi(W_{s})\leqslant 2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{n+1}t^{4/3}}

for all sufficiently large
t
(for almost every realisation of
\xi
).
It follows from Lemma
2.3
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};{\cal N}_{n}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{2\mu_{0}t\sqrt{K_{n+1}t^{4/3}}}\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}d(W_{s},o)\geqslant K_{n}t^{4/3}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{n+1}}t^{5/3}}e^{-CK_{n}^{2}t^{5/3}}=e^{\big(2\mu_{0}\sqrt{(n+1)K}-CK^{2}n^{2}\big)t^{5/3}}.

(4.2)

By choosing a sufficiently large
K=K_{0}
, one can ensure that

2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}(n+1)}-CK_{0}^{2}n^{2}<0\ \ \ \forall n\geqslant 1.

For this choice of
K_{0},
with probability one

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};{\cal N}_{K_{0}}^{t}\big]\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{2\mu_{0}\sqrt{(n+1)K}-CK^{2}n^{2}}=:C_{K_{0}}.

(4.3)

The result of the lemma thus follows.
∎

From now on, we fix the parameter
K_{0}
as in Lemma
4.1
.
Our task thus reduces to proving the upper bound (
4.1
)
with the unconditional expectation replaced by

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\big],

(4.4)

where
M^{t}\triangleq\{d(W_{s},o)\leqslant K_{0}t^{4/3}\ \forall s\in[0,t]\}.

4.2
Islands and clusters of the Gaussian field

Next, we discuss some aspects about the geometry of the background
Gaussian environment through which the Brownian motion propagates.
In particular, we will introduce the notion of islands and clusters
for the Gaussian field
\xi
and prove a few properties that will
be important to us later on.

In what follows, we fix two parameters
\delta,\eta>0
(
\eta\ll\delta
).
Let
t>0
be given.

Definition 4.1
.

(i) A
\delta
-island
of
\xi
(inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
)
is a connected component of the set

I_{\delta}^{t}\triangleq\{x\in Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}:\xi(x)>\delta t^{2/3}\}.

(4.5)

The collection of
\delta
-islands is denoted as
{\cal I}_{\delta}^{t}
.

(ii) Two
\delta
-islands
E,E^{\prime}\in{\cal I}_{\delta}^{t}

are said to be
\eta
-connected
if there exists a finite
sequence of islands

E_{0}=E,E_{1},\cdots,E_{n-1},E_{n}\triangleq E^{\prime}

such that the distance between
E_{i-1}
and
E_{i}
is not greater
than
\eta t^{4/3}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
) An
\eta
-cluster

(of
\delta
-islands) is an equivalence class of
\delta
-islands
with respect to
\eta
-connectedness. The collection of
\eta
-clusters
of
\delta
-islands is denoted as
{\cal J}_{\eta,\delta}^{t}
.

By definition, different
\eta
-clusters are at least
\eta t^{4/3}
-apart
from each other. Note that the islands and clusters are random sets
since they are defined for each realisation of
\xi
. The following
lemma summarises the essential properties of
\eta
-clusters that
are needed for our purpose. Recall that
R_{0}
is the correlation
length of the Gaussian field
\xi
and
C_{R_{0}}
is the constant
appearing in Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\delta>0
be given fixed. Define

L_{\delta}\triangleq 1.01\times\frac{2(d-1)K_{0}}{C_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}}.

(4.6)

Let
\eta
be another parameter such that

0<\eta<\eta_{\delta}\triangleq 0.99\times\min\big\{\frac{1}{4L_{\delta}^{2}},\frac{C_{R_{0}}^{2}\delta^{4}}{36(d-1)^{2}}\big\}.

(4.7)

Then with probability one, for all large
t
the following properties
hold true:

(i) No balls of radius
\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}

inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
can contain
\geqslant L_{\delta}
points
in
I_{\delta}^{t}
(cf. (
4.5
) for its definition)
that are at least
9R_{0}
-apart from each other;

(ii) No
\eta
-clusters inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}

can have diameter
>\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
;

(iii) No
\eta
-clusters inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}

can contain
\geqslant L_{\delta}
points in
I_{\delta}^{t}
that
are at least
9R_{0}
-apart from each other.

Proof.

We just write
L=L_{\delta}
for simplicity. It is obvious that (iii)
is an immediate consequence of (i) and (ii), since any
\eta
-cluster
has diameter
\leqslant\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
and is thus contained
in a ball of radius
\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
. Next, we show that (ii)
is a consequence of (i). Suppose that (i) is true and assume on the
contrary that
\mathfrak{c}
is some
\eta
-cluster with diameter

>\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
. Let
x,y\in\mathfrak{c}\subseteq I_{\delta}^{t}

be two points achieving this diameter so that
d(x,y)>\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
.
According to the definition of
\eta
-cluster, for each
k=1,\cdots,\sqrt{\eta}^{-1}

there is at least one point (say
x_{k}
) in
\mathfrak{c}
that
is contained in the annulus

A_{k}\triangleq\{z:(k-1)\eta t^{4/3}\leqslant d(z,x)\leqslant k\eta t^{4/3}\}.

In particular, the points
x_{2},x_{4},\cdots,x_{1/\sqrt{\eta}}

are contained in the ball
B(x,\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3})
but are all at
least
\eta t^{4/3}
-apart from each other. Since
\eta
satisfies
(
4.7
), the total number of such points is

N=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\eta}}>L.

This contradicts Property (i) when
t
is large enough so that
\eta t^{4/3}>9R_{0}
.
Therefore, all
\eta
-clusters must have diameter
\leqslant\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}.

This proves Property (ii).

It remains to prove Property (i). We will use
C_{i}
to denote constants
depending only on
d
and the distribution of
\xi
. First of all,
let
B
be a given fixed ball of radius
3\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
inside

Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
. Let
{\cal U}_{R_{0}}
be an
R_{0}
-ball packing
of the
R_{0}
-enlargement of
B
, so that
{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}

(defined by enlarging the radius of each member in
{\cal U}_{R_{0}}

to
2R_{0}
) is a covering of
B
. Let
E
denote the event that

B\cap I_{\delta}^{t}
contains
\geqslant L
points that are at
least
9R_{0}
-apart from each other. Respectively, let
F
denote
the event that there exist
L
members in
{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}
such
that each of them has non-empty intersection with
I_{\delta}^{t}

and they are all at least
R_{0}
-apart from each other. We claim
that
E\subseteq F
. Indeed, let
\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{L}\}\subseteq B\cap I_{\delta}^{t}

be such that

d(y_{i},y_{j})>9R_{0}\ \ \ \forall i\neq j.

Each
y_{i}
is contained in some ball
B(x_{i},2R_{0})\in{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}.

As a result, one has

d(x_{i},x_{j})\geqslant d(y_{i},y_{j})-d(x_{i},y_{i})-d(x_{j},y_{j})>9R_{0}-4R_{0}=5R_{0}.

In particular, the balls
B(x_{i},2R_{0})
(
i=1,\cdots,L
) are
at least
R_{0}
-apart from each other and
B(x_{i},2R_{0})\cap I_{\delta}^{t}\neq\emptyset

(because it contains
y_{i}
). Therefore, one has
E\subseteq F
.

To estimate the probability of
F
, for each
B_{i}\in{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}

we define

\Xi_{i}\triangleq\sup_{B_{i}}\xi.

Suppose that
B_{i_{1}},\cdots,B_{i_{L}}\in{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}
are
at least
R_{0}
-apart from each other. Since the correlation length
of
\xi
is
R_{0},
one knows that
\{\Xi_{i_{1}},\cdots,\Xi_{i_{L}}\}

are independent from each other. It follows from Lemma
3.2

that

\mathbb{P}\big(\Xi_{i_{l}}>\delta t^{2/3}\ \forall l=1,\cdots,L\big)\leqslant e^{-LC_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}t^{4/3}}.

On the other hand, the cardinality of
{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}
is estimated
as

\big|{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}\big|\leqslant\frac{{\rm Vol}(Q_{3\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}+R_{0}})}{{\rm Vol}(Q_{R_{0}})}\leqslant C_{2}e^{3(d-1)\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}.

(4.8)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(F)

\displaystyle\leqslant{|{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}|\choose L}e^{-LC_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}t^{4/3}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|{\cal U}_{2R_{0}}|^{L}}{L!}e^{-LC_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}t^{4/3}}\leqslant\frac{C_{2}^{L}}{L!}e^{-L(C_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}-3(d-1)\sqrt{\eta})t^{4/3}}.

Now suppose that
{\cal U}_{\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}
is a
\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}
-ball
packing of
Q_{(K_{0}+\sqrt{\eta})t^{4/3}}
(so
{\cal U}_{2\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}

covers
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
). Similar to (
4.8
), the
cardinality of
{\cal U}_{\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}
is estimated as

\big|{\cal U}_{\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}\big|\leqslant\frac{{\rm Vol}(Q_{(K_{0}+\sqrt{\eta})t^{4/3}})}{{\rm Vol}(Q_{\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}})}\leqslant C_{3}e^{(d-1)K_{0}t^{4/3}}.

Let
G^{t}
denote the event that there exists a member
B\in{\cal U}_{3\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}

which contains
\geqslant L
points in
I_{\delta}^{t}
that are
at least
9R_{0}
-apart. Then one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G^{t})\leqslant\big|{\cal U}_{\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}\big|\times\mathbb{P}(E)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C_{3}C_{2}^{L}}{L!}e^{(d-1)K_{0}t^{4/3}}e^{-L(C_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}-3(d-1)\sqrt{\eta})t^{4/3}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C_{3}C_{2}^{L}}{L!}e^{((d-1)K_{0}-LC_{R_{0}}\delta^{2}/2)t^{4/3}},

(4.9)

where the last inequality follows from the condition (
4.7
)
of
\eta.
The exponent on the right hand side of (
4.9
)
is negative due to the definition (
4.6
) of
L
. According
to the first Borel-Cantelli lemma, one concludes that with probability
one, for all large
t
no members in
{\cal U}_{3\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}

can contain
\geqslant L
points in
I_{\delta}^{t}
that are at
least
9R_{0}
-apart. To demonstrate the desired Property (i), one
only needs to observe that any ball of radius
\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}

inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
is contained in some member of
{\cal U}_{3\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}

because
{\cal U}_{2\sqrt{\eta}t^{4/3}}
is a covering of
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}.

∎

Remark 4.1
.

When one applies the Borel-Cantelli lemma, technically one should
discretise
t
(e.g. by looking at
\lfloor t\rfloor
and
I_{\delta}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}

instead). This will not cause any essential difference to the above
argument (e.g. by a monotonicity consideration).

The following corollary of Lemma
4.2
provides
an estimate on the size of a generic
\eta
-cluster.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
L_{\delta},\eta_{\delta}
be defined by
(
4.6
,
4.7
) respectively. Let
\eta\in(0,\eta_{\delta})

be given fixed. With probability one, for all large
t
no
\eta
-clusters
can have diameter
\geqslant 2L_{\delta}\eta t^{4/3}.

Proof.

Suppose that such a cluster (say
\mathfrak{c}
) exists and let
x,y

be two points on
\mathfrak{c}
that are at least
2L_{\delta}\eta t^{4/3}
-apart.
Consider the annulus

A_{k}\triangleq\{z\in Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}:(k-1)\eta t^{4/3}\leqslant d(z,x)\leqslant k\eta t^{4/3}\},\ \ \ k=1,\cdots,2L_{\delta}.

By the definition of
\eta
-cluster, it follows that each
A_{k}

contains at least one point (say
x_{k}
) from
\mathfrak{c}
. In
particular, the
\eta
-cluster
\mathfrak{c}
contains
L
points

\{x_{2k}\}_{k=1}^{L_{\delta}}
that are all at least
\eta t^{4/3}
-apart.
This contradicts Lemma
4.2
(iii) since
\eta t^{4/3}>9R_{0}

when
t
is large.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In a similar way, it can be shown that with probability one, for all
large
t
no islands in
{\cal I}_{\delta}^{t}
can have diameter

>18R_{0}L_{\delta}
.

4.3
Discrete routes for Brownian motion and a further localisation

In order to upper bound the expectation (
4.4
) for each
fixed realisation of
\xi
, one needs to carefully analyse the contributions
from
all possible ways
of propagating through islands and
clusters by the Brownian motion (discrete routes).

4.3.1
Discrete and reduced routes

From now on, we assume that
\lambda<\eta<\delta
are given fixed
parameters where
\eta<\eta_{\delta}
(
\eta_{\delta}
is defined
by (
4.7
)). Recall that
{\cal J}_{\eta,\delta}^{t}

is the collection of
\eta
-clusters (of
\delta
-islands) inside

Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}.
We shall also consider the collection
{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}

of
\lambda
-clusters. Since
\lambda<\eta,
it is clear that every

\lambda
-cluster is part of an
\eta
-cluster and every
\eta
-cluster
is a disjoint union of certain
\lambda
-clusters. As the following
lemma shows, the number of
\lambda
-clusters constituting any
\eta
-cluster
is at most
L_{\delta}
(
L_{\delta}
is defined by (
4.6
)).

Lemma 4.3
.

With probability one, for all large
t

every
\eta
-cluster inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
contains at most
an
L_{\delta}
number of
\lambda
-clusters.

Proof.

Note that different
\lambda
-clusters are at least
\lambda t^{4/3}
-apart.
When
t
is large, one has
\lambda t^{4/3}>9R_{0}
. The claim thus
follows immediately from Lemma
4.2
(iii).
∎

Now we introduce the notions of discrete routes that will be used
in our proof. Let
W
be a Brownian motion starting from
o
.

Definition 4.2
.

The
\eta
-
(discrete) route
of
W
over
[0,t]
, denoted
as
D_{\eta}^{t}
, is the (finite) sequence of
\eta
-clusters in

{\cal J}_{\eta,\delta}^{t}
visited by
W|_{[0,t]}
in order.

It is possible that
D_{\eta}^{t}
has repeated letters (revisiting
the same cluster after visiting other clusters) but they cannot be
adjacent to each other. In our proof, we need to analyse the discrete
route through the finer
\lambda
-clusters and we need a slightly
different definition. By abuse of notation, a cluster can either refer
to a label or the actual point set of the cluster depending on its
use in the specific context. Given
\mathfrak{c}\in{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t},

we denote
\hat{\mathfrak{c}}
as the
\lambda t^{4/3}/2
-neighbourhood
of
\mathfrak{c}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
W
be the Brownian motion starting from
o
.
We first set
\tau_{0}\triangleq 0
and

\sigma_{1}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[0,t]:W_{s}\in{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}\},\ \tau_{1}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\sigma_{1},t]:W_{s}\in\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}^{c}\}

where
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
denotes the
\lambda
-cluster to which

W_{\sigma_{1}}
belongs. Then we set

\sigma_{2}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\tau_{1},t]:W_{s}\in{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}\},\ \tau_{2}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\sigma_{2},t]:W_{s}\in\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}^{c}\}

and inductively

\sigma_{i}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\tau_{i-1},t]:W_{s}\in{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}\},\ \tau_{i}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\sigma_{i},t]:W_{s}\in\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}^{c}\}

where
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
denotes the
\lambda
-cluster to which

W_{\sigma_{i}}
belongs. Let
m
be the largest integer such that

\sigma_{m}<t
. The
extended

\lambda
-
(discrete)
route
of
W
on
[0,t]
is the word over
{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}

defined by
\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}\triangleq\mathfrak{c}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{m}
.

The extended
\lambda
-route allows consecutive letters to be identical.
It is easily seen from the definition that one can read out the
\lambda
-
and
\eta
-routes from the extended
\lambda
-route, provided that
the configurations of
\lambda
- and
\eta
-clusters are known.
For our purpose, we need to introduce one more simple operation: word
reduction.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
{\bf w}=\mathfrak{c}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{m}

be a given word. The
reduced word
of
{\bf w}
, denoted
as
\bar{{\bf w}}
, is defined in the following way. Let
i_{1}

be the last location in
{\bf w}
where
\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}}=\mathfrak{c}_{1}
.
Let
i_{2}
be the last location where
\mathfrak{c}_{i_{2}}=\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}+1}

and so forth. This reduction procedure must terminate at finitely
many steps, say
\bar{m}
. The reduced word
\bar{{\bf w}}
is then
defined by
\bar{{\bf w}}\triangleq\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}}
.

Example 4.1
.

Suppose that
{\bf w}=abcabbacbccb.
Then
\bar{{\bf w}}=acb.

Remark 4.3
.

The reason for considering the extended route (rather than the usual
one) is just technical; it allows one to apply Markovian argument
to analysis probabilities related to Brownian excursions outside the

\lambda
-clusters which themselves have a non-Markovian nature.
The consideration of reduced
\lambda
-route will play a critical
role in the proof; it allows one to effectively control the error
arising from the ambiguity of entrance locations for the
\lambda
-clusters
(cf. Lemma
4.7
below).

4.3.2
Reduction to bounded discrete routes

We now return to the main course of proving Theorem
4.1
.
Before studying a generic Brownian scenario we shall first perform
an addition step of localisation, i.e. on the event that discrete
routes are bounded. As before,
\lambda<\eta<\delta
(
\eta<\eta_{\delta}
)
are given fixed.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
be given fixed. Then with
probability one, for all large
t
one has

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big]\leqslant F(t;\eta,N)\cdot\exp\big[-\big(\frac{(\eta N)^{2}}{32}-2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}}\big)t^{5/3}\big].

Here
F(t;\eta,N)
is an explicit (deterministic) function which
tends to zero as
t\rightarrow\infty
for every fixed
\eta,N.

Proof.

Since we are working on the event
M^{t},
we will just trivially
bound the exponential by

e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds}\leqslant e^{2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}}t^{5/3}}

(4.10)

for a.e. realisation of
\xi
and all large
t
(cf. (
3.2
)
with
\mu=2\mu_{0}
). Our task is thus reduced to estimating the
probability
\mathbb{P}(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}).

To this end, let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots
denote the successive
visit times of the
\eta
-clusters by the Brownian motion (cf. Definition

4.3
with
\lambda
replaced by
\eta
). By conditioning
on
{\cal F}_{\sigma_{N-1}},
one has

\mathbb{P}\big(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{N}<t)=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-1}<t\}}\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N}<t\}}|{\cal F}_{\sigma_{N-1}}]\big].

(4.11)

According to the Markov property, one can write

\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N}<t\}}|{\cal F}_{\sigma_{N-1}}\big]=h^{t}(W_{\sigma_{N-1}},t-\sigma_{N-1}),

(4.12)

where

h^{t}(x,s)\triangleq\mathbb{P}\big(W^{x}\text{ will visit a new $\eta$-cluster before time $s$}\big).

(4.13)

Here
W^{x}
denotes a Brownian motion starting at
x
. We assume
that
x
is a point on some
\eta
-cluster and
s\in[0,t]
.

To estimate the function
h^{t}(x,s)
, one first observes by the
definition of
\eta
-cluster that

\{W^{x}\text{ will visit a new $\eta$-cluster before time $s$}\}\subseteq\text{$\big\{$$\sup_{0\leqslant u\leqslant s}R_{u}$>$\eta t^{4/3}$\big\lx@text@rbrace},

where
R_{s}\triangleq d(W_{s}^{x},x).
Next, recall from Lemma
2.2

that
R_{s}
satisfies the SDE (
2.2
) with some Euclidean
Brownian motion
\beta_{s}
. Let
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a non-decreasing, smooth function such that

f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\ \text{on }[0,\frac{1}{2}];\ \ f(x)=x\ \text{on [1,$\infty)$};\ \ f^{\prime}(x)\leqslant 1\ \forall x.

By applying Itô’s formula to
f(R_{s}),
one finds that

\displaystyle f(R_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\int_{0}^{s}f^{\prime}(R_{u})\sqrt{2}d\beta_{u}+\int_{0}^{s}\big((d-1)f^{\prime}(R_{u})\coth R_{u}+f^{\prime\prime}(R_{u})\big)du

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{2}+M_{s}+Q_{s}.

On a possibly enlarged probability space, one can write
M_{s}=\sqrt{2}\gamma_{\langle M\rangle_{s}/2}

with
\gamma
being a one-dimensional Euclidean Brownian motion.
Let
\bar{\sigma}^{t}
denote the hitting time of the level
\eta t^{4/3}

by the process
R
. By the construction of
f
, this is also the
hitting time by the process
f(R)
if
t
is large enough (so that

\eta t^{4/3}>1
).

Let
\hat{\sigma}^{t}
denote the hitting time of the
level
\eta t^{4/3}/2\sqrt{2}
by the Euclidean Brownian motion
\gamma
.
Note that

\langle M\rangle_{s}=2\int_{0}^{s}f^{\prime}(R_{u})^{2}du\leqslant 2s;\ Q_{s}\leqslant C_{Q}s

where

C_{Q}\triangleq\sup_{x\geqslant 0}\big((d-1)f^{\prime}(x)\coth x+f^{\prime\prime}(x)\big)<\infty.

(4.14)

Since
s\leqslant t,
it is clear that
\hat{\sigma}^{t}\leqslant\bar{\sigma}^{t}

when
t
is large. As a result, one finds that

h^{t}(x,s)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\bar{\sigma}^{t}<s)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\hat{\sigma}^{t}<s).

(4.15)

On the other hand, it is well-known that (cf.
[
RW94
, Section I.9]
)
the hitting time
\hat{\sigma}^{t}
has an explicit density function

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\sigma}^{t}\in ds)=\frac{\eta t^{4/3}/2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}s^{3/2}}e^{-\eta^{2}t^{4/3}/16s}ds.

(4.16)

By substituting this into the estimate (
4.15
), it
follows that

h^{t}(x,s)\leqslant\frac{\eta t^{4/3}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{s}u^{-3/2}e^{-\eta^{2}t^{4/3}/16u}du=:\phi_{N-1}(s).

Note that
\phi_{N-1}
is an increasing function. In view of (
4.11
)
and (
4.12
), one thus obtains that

\mathbb{P}\big(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-1}<t\}}\phi_{N-1}(t-\sigma_{N-1})\big]

(4.17)

for all large
t.

To estimate the right hand side of (
4.17
) we
further condition on
{\cal F}_{\sigma_{N-2}}
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-1}<t\}}\phi_{N-1}(t-\sigma_{N-1})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-2}<t\}}\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-1}<t\}}\phi_{N-1}(t-\sigma_{N-1})|{\cal F}_{\sigma_{N-2}}]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-2}<t\}}\tilde{h}^{t}(W_{\sigma_{N-2}},t-\sigma_{N-2})\big],

(4.18)

where

\tilde{h}^{t}(x,s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma^{t}<s\}}\phi_{N-1}(s-\sigma^{t})\big]

and
\sigma^{t}
is the hitting time of a new
\eta
-cluster by

W^{x}
. Let us define the hitting times
\bar{\sigma}^{t},\hat{\sigma}^{t}

in exactly the same way as before (on a possibly enlarged probability
space). Note that
\hat{\sigma}^{t}\leqslant\bar{\sigma}^{t}\leqslant\sigma^{t}

(for all large
t
). By the monotonicity of
\phi_{N-1}
and the
density formula (
4.16
), in a similar fashion one finds
that

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{t}(x,s)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\hat{\sigma}^{t}<s\}}\phi_{N-1}(s-\hat{\sigma}^{t})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta t^{4/3}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{s}u^{-3/2}e^{-\eta^{2}t^{4/3}/16u}\phi_{N-1}(s-u)du=:\phi_{N-2}(s).

We again remark that
\phi_{N-2}
is an increasing function. By substituting
this estimate into (
4.18
), it follows from (
4.17
)
that

\mathbb{P}\big(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-2}<t\}}\phi_{N-2}(t-\sigma_{N-2})\big]

for all large
t.

One can now argue recursively (by further conditioning backward) to
conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{N-3}<t\}}\phi_{N-3}(t-\sigma_{N-3})\big]

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{1}<t\}}\phi_{1}(t-\sigma_{1})\big]\leqslant\phi_{0}(t)

(4.19)

for all large
t
. Here the increasing functions
\phi_{j}:[0,t]\rightarrow[0,\infty)

are defined recursively by

\phi_{j-1}(s)\triangleq\frac{\eta t^{4/3}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{s}u^{-3/2}e^{-\eta^{2}t^{4/3}/16u}\phi_{j}(s-u)du.

Explicitly, one has

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0<u_{1}+\cdots+u_{N}<t}\big(\frac{\eta t^{4/3}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\big)^{N}(u_{1}\cdots u_{N})^{-3/2}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{16}\eta^{2}t^{8/3}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{u_{i}}\big)d{\bf u}

\displaystyle=\int_{0<v_{1}+\cdots+v_{N}<t^{-5/3}}\big(\frac{\eta}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\big)^{N}(v_{1}\cdots v_{N})^{-3/2}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{16}\eta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{v_{i}}\big)d{\bf v}.

(4.20)

To finish the proof, the key observation is that

\frac{N}{1/v_{1}+\cdots+1/v_{N}}\leqslant\frac{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{N}}{N}\leqslant\frac{t^{-5/3}}{N},

(4.21)

which is a consequence of the harmonic-arithmetic mean inequality.
By applying this elementary estimate to (
4.20
), it follows
from (
4.19
) that

\mathbb{P}\big(M^{t}\cap\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N\}\big)\leqslant F(t;\eta,N)\times e^{-(\eta N)^{2}t^{5/3}/32},

(4.22)

where we define

F(t;\eta,N)\triangleq\int_{0<v_{1}+\cdots+v_{N}<t^{-5/3}}\big(\frac{\eta}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\big)^{N}(v_{1}\cdots v_{N})^{-3/2}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{32}\eta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{v_{i}}\big)d{\bf v}.

It is apparent that
F(t;\eta,N)
vanishes as
t\rightarrow\infty

for each fixed
\eta,N
. The result of the lemma thus follows by
combining (
4.10
) and (
4.22
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

In the above proof, we should point out that (technically) the visit
of the first
\eta
-cluster (say
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
) is a bit different
from the rest (since one does not know if
d(o,\mathfrak{c}_{1})>\eta t^{4/3}
).
However, since
\xi(x)>\delta t^{2/3}
for any
x\in\mathfrak{c}_{1},

one knows from (
3.2
) (with
\mu=2\mu_{0}
) that
d(o,\mathfrak{c}_{1})>(\delta/2\mu_{0})^{2}t^{4/3}.

To make the argument for the first visit consistent with the rest,
one could further impose that
\eta<(\delta/2\mu_{0})^{2}.

In exactly the same way, one can also show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\{|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}|>N\}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant F(t;\frac{\lambda}{2},2N-1)\cdot\exp\big[-\big(\frac{((\lambda/2)(2N-1))^{2}}{32}-2\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}}\big)t^{5/3}\big].

(4.23)

In fact, by the definition of the extended
\lambda
-route, if
|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}|>N

the Brownian motion needs to jump a
\lambda t^{4/3}/2
-distance
for at least
2N-1
times. One just replace
\eta
be
\lambda/2

and
N
by
2N-1
in the above proof to conclude (
4.23
)
directly.

Let us define

N_{\eta}\triangleq\eta^{-1}\sqrt{64\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}}},\ \hat{N}_{\lambda}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\big(1+(\lambda/2)^{-1}\sqrt{64\mu_{0}\sqrt{K_{0}}}\big).

(4.24)

It follows from Lemma
4.4
and (
4.23
)
that

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\big(\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|>N_{\eta}\}\cup\{|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}|>\hat{N}_{\lambda}\}\big)\big]\leqslant F(t;\eta,N_{\eta})+F(t;\lambda/2,2\hat{N}_{\lambda}-1).

This is negligible as
t\rightarrow\infty
. As a result, in order
to prove Theorem
4.1
one can further localise the expectation
(
4.4
) on the event
\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|\leqslant N_{\eta},\ |\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}|\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}\}.

A benefit of this is that the total number of such words is of order

e^{O(t^{4/3})}
, which is also negligible with respect to the leading

e^{L^{*}t^{5/3}}
-growth. As we will see, the intermediate localisation

\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|\leqslant N_{\eta}\}
will also play a crucial role
in an error estimate.

4.4
The main estimate on generic routes

In this subsection, we estimate the contribution from a generic discrete
route. The main result is stated as follows. Let
{\cal A}_{\lambda,\eta,\delta}^{t}

denote the set of all words
{\bf w}
over
{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}

such that
|D_{\eta}^{t}({\bf w})|\leqslant N_{\eta}
and
|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}({\bf w})|\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
0<\lambda<\eta<\eta_{\delta}
be given where

\eta_{\delta}
is defined by (
4.7
). Suppose further
that
\lambda,\eta
satisfy the following constraint:

\frac{1-\alpha}{16}\big(\frac{\delta}{\mu}\big)^{4}>\frac{\lambda K_{0}}{2}\times\big[N_{\eta}L_{\delta}(6L_{\delta}+1/2)+2L_{\delta}\big],

(4.25)

where
K_{0}
is the constant in Lemma
4.1
,
L_{\delta}

is defined by (
4.6
) and
N_{\eta}
is defined by (
4.24
).
Then with probability one, the following estimate

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big]\leqslant G(t;m,\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)e^{(\delta+L^{*}(\alpha,\mu))t^{5/3}}

(4.26)

holds for all large
t
uniformly
{\bf w}\in{\cal A}_{\lambda,\eta,\delta}^{t}
.
Here
L^{*}(\alpha,\mu)
is defined by

L^{*}(\alpha,\mu)\triangleq\max_{K>0,v\in(0,1)}\big\{\mu\sqrt{K}(1-v)-\frac{\alpha K^{2}}{4v}\big\}.

The function
G(t;m,\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)

is an explicit
(deterministic) function which vanishes as
t\rightarrow\infty
uniformly
with respect to
m\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}
when all other parameters
are fixed.

Remark 4.5
.

It is elementary to figure out the exact value of
L^{*}(\alpha,\mu)

(which will not be done here). The point is that
L^{*}(\alpha,\mu)\rightarrow L^{*}

(the optimal exponent defined by (
1.10
)) when
\alpha\uparrow 1

and
\mu\downarrow\mu_{0}
, as a simple consequence of continuity.

Remark 4.6
.

Since the right hand side of (
4.26
) precisely corresponds
to the scenario defined by (
1.12
), the estimate (
4.26
)
thus shows that no other Brownian scenarios can produce a larger growth
than the optimal scenario (
1.12
) does.

In what follows, we develop the main steps towards proving Proposition

4.1
.

4.4.1
Step 1: Decomposition of
\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds
into “excursion”
and “staying” parts

Let
{\bf w}=\mathfrak{c}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{m}
be given fixed
where
\mathfrak{c}_{i}\in{\cal J}_{\lambda,\delta}^{t}.
We define

\tau_{0}\triangleq 0
and for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m,

\sigma_{i}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\tau_{i-1},t]:W_{s}\in\mathfrak{c}_{i}\},\ \tau_{i}\triangleq\inf\{s\in[\sigma_{i},t]:W_{s}\in\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}^{c}\},

where we recall that
\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}
is the
\lambda t^{4/3}/2
-neighbourhood
of
\mathfrak{c}_{i}.
Note that the stopping times
\{\tau_{0},\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{m-1},\tau_{m-1},\sigma_{m}\}

are different from the ones in Definition
4.3
; here
they are defined with respect to the given fixed
\lambda
-clusters

\mathfrak{c}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{c}_{m}
. However, on the event

\{\hat{D}_{t}^{\lambda}={\bf w}\}
the two definitions clearly coincide.
In what follows, our analysis will be performed on this event.

In order to estimate the integral
\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds
, we
decompose time into two parts:

{\cal S}^{t}\triangleq\big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m-1}[\sigma_{i},\tau_{i}]\big)\bigcup[\sigma_{m},t],\ {\cal E}^{t}\triangleq\bigcup_{i=0}^{m-1}[\tau_{i},\sigma_{i+1}].

Correspondingly, one can write

\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds=\int_{{\cal E}^{t}}\xi(W_{s})ds+\int_{{\cal S}^{t}}\xi(W_{s})ds.

During the excursion period
{\cal E}^{t}
(which means
\xi
is
small), one just trivially bounds
\xi
from above by
\delta t^{2/3}

because the Brownian motion is outside the
\delta
-islands by definition.
We refer to
{\cal S}^{t}
as the “staying period”. We pretend
that
W
stays inside
\lambda
-clusters during this period (although
this is not true!), in the sense that over this period one bounds

\xi
from above by its possible maximal value over the
\lambda
-clusters

\mathfrak{c}_{i}^{j}
.

More precisely, let us set

K^{*}\triangleq t^{-4/3}\sup\{d(x,o):x\in\cup_{i=1}^{m}\mathfrak{c}_{i}\}.

(4.27)

Note that
K^{*}
also depends on
t
but it satisfies

\big(\frac{\delta}{\mu}\big)^{2}\leqslant K^{*}\leqslant K_{0}

(4.28)

for all large
t
(cf. (
3.2
)). On the event
\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}
,
at a generic time
s
there are precisely two possibilities: either
the Brownian motion
W_{s}
is outside
I_{\delta}^{t}
in which
case
\xi(W_{s})\leqslant\delta t^{2/3}
or
W_{s}
belongs to one
of the
\lambda
-clusters
\mathfrak{c}_{i}^{j}
in which case
\xi(W_{s})\leqslant\mu\sqrt{K^{*}t^{4/3}}

(for all large
t
). As a result, one obtains that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta t^{2/3}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})+\mu\sqrt{K^{*}t^{4/3}}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta t^{5/3}+\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\delta t^{5/3}}\times\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big].

(4.29)

Here we set

\Xi(t)\triangleq\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big).

which corresponds to the “staying” part.

4.4.2
Step 2: Estimation of the “staying” part

Now our aim is to estimate the expectation
\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big].

The strategy is quite similar to the proof of Lemma
4.4

(i.e. applying Markov property recursively and reducing to explicit
calculations to Euclidean hitting times). The main difference is that
the use of the harmonic-arithmetic mean inequality (
4.21
)
will be replaced by the elementary inequality (
4.34
)
in combination with a simple optimisation procedure.

To start with, one first observes that

\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}\subseteq{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{1}<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\sigma_{m-1}<\tau_{m-1}<\sigma_{m}<t\}}(={\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m}<t\}}).

By conditioning on
{\cal F}_{\tau_{m-1}},
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)}{\bf 1}_{\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}}|{\cal F}_{\tau_{m-1}}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big)\big){\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m-1}<t\}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}(\sigma_{m}-\tau_{m-1})\big){\bf 1}_{\{\tau_{m-1}<\sigma_{m}<t\}}|{\cal F}_{\tau_{m-1}}\big]

\displaystyle=\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big)\big){\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m-1}<t\}}\times g^{t}\big(W_{\tau_{m-1}},t-\tau_{m-1}\big).

(4.30)

Here
g^{t}:\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{m-1}\times[0,t]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is the function defined by

g^{t}(x,s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\sigma^{t}}{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma^{t}<s\}}\big],

where
\sigma^{t}
is the hitting time of
\mathfrak{c}_{m}
by

W^{x}
(Brownian motion starting at
x\in\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{m-1}
).
Let us set

D_{m-1}\triangleq{\rm dist}(\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{m-1},\mathfrak{c}_{m}).

Note that
D_{m-1}\geqslant\lambda t^{4/3}/2.
In the same way as
the proof of Lemma
4.4
, one sees that

g^{t}(x,s)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\hat{\sigma}^{t}}{\bf 1}_{\{\hat{\sigma}^{t}<s\}}\big].

Here
\hat{\sigma}^{t}
denotes the hitting time of the level
\hat{D}_{m-1}/\sqrt{2}

by a one-dimensional Euclidean Brownian motion
\gamma
, where

\hat{D}_{m-1}\triangleq D_{m-1}-\big(\frac{1}{2}+C_{Q}t\big)

and
C_{Q}
is the constant defined by (
4.14
). By using the
explicit density formula (
4.16
) for
\hat{\sigma}^{t}
,
one finds that

g^{t}(x,s)\leqslant\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}u}\frac{\hat{D}_{m-1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u^{3/2}}e^{-\hat{D}_{m-1}^{2}/4u}du=:\psi_{m-1}(s).

By substituting this back into (
4.30
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m-1}<t\}}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)}{\bf 1}_{\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}}|{\cal F}_{\tau_{m-1}}\big]\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\psi_{m-1}(t-\sigma_{m-1}){\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m-1}<t\}}\big],

(4.31)

where we also used the fact that

\psi_{m-1}(t-\tau_{m-1})\leqslant\psi_{m-1}(t-\sigma_{m-1}),

which holds since
\sigma_{m-1}<\tau_{m-1}
and
\psi_{m-1}
is
increasing.

To estimate the right hand side of (
4.31
), we further
condition on
{\cal F}_{\tau_{m-1}}
. By the same argument, one finds
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}\big(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-3}(\sigma_{i+1}-\tau_{i})\big)\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\psi_{m-2}(t-\sigma_{m-2}){\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{m-2}<t\}}\big].

Here

\displaystyle\psi_{m-2}(s)

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}u}\psi_{m-1}(s-u)\frac{\hat{D}_{m-2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u^{3/2}}e^{-\hat{D}_{m-2}^{2}/4u}du,

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{m-2}

\displaystyle\triangleq D_{m-2}-\big(\frac{1}{2}+C_{Q}t\big),\ D_{m-2}\triangleq{\rm dist}(\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{m-2},\mathfrak{c}_{m-1}).

By construction, one has
D_{m-2}\geqslant\lambda t^{4/3}/2.

Now one can continue the above argument recursively to obtain the
following estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}(t-\sigma_{1})}\psi_{1}(t-\sigma_{1}){\bf 1}_{\{\sigma_{1}<t\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}(t-u)}\psi_{1}(t-s)\frac{\hat{D}_{0}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u^{3/2}}e^{-\hat{D}_{0}^{2}/4u}du,

where one defines (with
j=2,\cdots,m
)

\psi_{j-1}(s)\triangleq\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}u}\psi_{j}(s-u)\frac{\hat{D}_{j-1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u^{3/2}}e^{-\hat{D}_{j-1}^{2}/4u}du

and (with
j=0,\cdots,m-1
)

\hat{D}_{j}\triangleq D_{j}-\big(\frac{1}{2}+C_{Q}t\big),\ D_{j}\triangleq{\rm dist}(\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{j},\mathfrak{c}_{j+1})>\frac{\lambda t^{4/3}}{2}.

By fully expanding the function
\psi_{1}
, one arrives at the following
inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\Xi(t)};\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0<u_{0}+\cdots+u_{m-1}<t}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u_{i}^{3/2}}\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}(t-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}u_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\exp\big(-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{4u_{i}}\big)d{\bf u}.

(4.32)

We denote the integral on the right hand side as
I(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)
.

4.4.3
Step 3: An elementary inequality and reduced
\lambda
-routes

Recall that
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a given fixed parameter. In order
to analyse the integral
I(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)
, one can write

\exp\big(-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{4u_{i}}\big)=\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}}\big)\times\exp\big(-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}}\big).

(4.33)

To analyse the former exponential, we first present a rather elementary
inequality which plays a critical role in deducing the optimality
of the scenario (
1.12
).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
D_{1},\cdots,D_{r}
and
t_{1},\cdots,t_{r}

be positive numbers. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{D_{i}^{2}}{t_{i}}\geqslant\frac{(D_{1}+\cdots+D_{r})^{2}}{t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r}}.

(4.34)

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
r=2
, i.e.

\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{t_{1}}+\frac{D_{2}^{2}}{t_{2}}\geqslant\frac{(D_{1}+D_{2})^{2}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}.

(4.35)

Setting
\lambda\triangleq\frac{D_{1}}{D_{1}+D_{2}}
and
\rho\triangleq\frac{t_{1}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}
,
the inequality (
4.35
) is equivalent to that

\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}}{(1-\rho)}\geqslant 1\iff(\lambda-\rho)^{2}\geqslant 0.

The result thus follows.
∎

The next crucial point is to consider the reduced
\lambda
-route
from
\mathbf{w}
. Recall that
K^{*}
is the maximal distance from
the
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
’s to
o
(cf. (
4.27
)). Let
m^{\prime}

be such that
\mathfrak{c}_{m^{\prime}}
contains the furthest location.
Define
\bar{{\bf w}}=\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}}

to be the reduced word obtained from
\mathfrak{c}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{m^{\prime}}

in the sense of Definition
4.4
. It is not hard to see
from the definition that
\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l}+1}=\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l+1}}

and thus

{\rm dist}(\partial\hat{\mathfrak{c}}_{i_{l}},\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l+1}})=D_{i_{l}}

for all
l=0,\cdots,\bar{m}-1
(
i_{0}\triangleq 0
). It follows
from the above discussion and Lemma
4.5
that

\displaystyle\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i_{l}}^{2}}{u_{i_{l}}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha}{4}\frac{(\hat{D}_{i_{0}}+\cdots+\hat{D}_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})^{2}}{u_{i_{0}}+\cdots+u_{i_{\bar{m}-1}}}\big).

(4.36)

To further upper bound the right hand side, we need two important
observations.

Lemma 4.6
.

One has
\bar{m}\leqslant N_{\eta}L_{\delta}.

Proof.

Note that every the
\lambda
-clusters
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
is contained
in some
\eta
-cluster along the
\eta
-discrete route
D_{\eta}^{t}({\bf w})
.
In addition, the letters
\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l}}
are all distinct
by the definition of reduced word. Since
D_{\eta}^{t}({\bf w})\leqslant N_{\eta}

by assumption and every
\eta
-cluster can contain at most
L_{\delta}

\lambda
-clusters (cf. Lemma
4.3
), one immediately
knows that
\bar{m}\leqslant N_{\eta}L_{\delta}.

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

One has

K^{*}t^{4/3}\leqslant\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}D_{i_{l}}+\big(N_{\eta}L_{\delta}(6L_{\delta}+1/2)+2L_{\delta}\big)\lambda t^{4/3}.

Proof.

Recall from Corollary
4.1
that when
t
is large,
the diameter of any
\lambda
-cluster inside
Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}

is
\leqslant 2L_{\delta}\lambda t^{4/3}
. Let
x^{*}\in\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}}

be such that
d(x^{*},o)=K^{*}t^{4/3}.
Then one has

K^{*}t^{4/3}\leqslant{\rm dist}(o,\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}})+2L_{\delta}\lambda t^{4/3}.

(4.37)

In addition,

\displaystyle{\rm dist}(o,\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}})

\displaystyle\leqslant{\rm dist}(o,\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}})+{\rm dist}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{1}},\mathfrak{c}_{i_{2}})+\cdots+{\rm dist}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}-1}},\mathfrak{c}_{i_{\bar{m}}})+\sum_{l=1}^{\bar{m}}{\rm diam}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=1}^{\bar{m}}D_{i_{l}}+\sum_{l=1}^{\bar{m}-1}\big(\frac{\lambda}{2}t^{4/3}+{\rm diam}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l}})+{\rm diam}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l+1}})\big)+\sum_{l=1}^{\bar{m}}{\rm diam}(\mathfrak{c}_{i_{l}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}D_{i_{l}}+\bar{m}\times\big(6L_{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\big)\lambda t^{4/3}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}D_{i_{l}}+N_{\eta}L_{\delta}\big(6L_{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\big)\lambda t^{4/3},

(4.38)

where the last inequality comes from Lemma
4.6
.
The result follows by substituting (
4.38
) into (
4.37
).
∎

It follows from Lemma
4.7
that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}\hat{D}_{i_{l}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}D_{i_{l}}-N_{\eta}L_{\delta}\big(\frac{1}{2}+C_{Q}t\big)\geqslant(K^{*}-{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta))t^{4/3}.

Here the error term
{\cal R}^{t}
is defined by

{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)\triangleq\lambda\big(N_{\eta}L_{\delta}(6L_{\delta}+1/2)+2L_{\delta}\big)+N_{\eta}L_{\delta}\big(\frac{1}{2}+C_{Q}t\big)t^{-4/3}.

(4.39)

In particular, one has

(\hat{D}_{i_{0}}+\cdots+\hat{D}_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})^{2}\geqslant(K^{*}t^{4/3})^{2}-2K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)t^{8/3}.

By substituting this back into (
4.36
), one finds that

\displaystyle\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i_{l}}^{2}}{u_{i_{l}}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha K^{*2}t^{8/3}}{4(u_{i_{0}}+\cdots+u_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})}\big)\times\exp\big(\frac{\alpha K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)t^{8/3}}{2(u_{i_{0}}+\cdots+u_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha K^{*2}t^{8/3}}{4(u_{i_{0}}+\cdots+u_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})}\big)\times\exp\big(\frac{K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)t^{8/3}}{2u_{0}}\big).

In view of the decomposition (
4.33
), the integral
I(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)

can thus be estimated as

\displaystyle I(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0<u_{0}+\cdots+u_{m-1}<t}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u_{i}^{3/2}}\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K^{*}}t^{2/3}(t-\sum_{l=0}^{\bar{m}-1}u_{i_{l}})\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\exp\big(-\frac{\alpha K^{*2}t^{8/3}}{4(u_{i_{0}}+\cdots+u_{i_{\bar{m}-1}})}\big)\times\exp\big(\frac{K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)t^{8/3}}{2u_{0}}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \times\exp\big(-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}}\big)d{\bf u}

\displaystyle\leqslant J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)\times L(t;\alpha,\mu).

Here the main term
L(t;\alpha,\mu)
is defined by

\displaystyle L(t;\alpha,\mu)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{K>0,\ \rho\in(0,t)}\exp\big(\mu\sqrt{K}t^{2/3}(t-\rho)-\frac{\alpha K^{2}t^{8/3}}{4\rho}\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(t^{5/3}\times\sup_{K>0,v\in(0,1)}(\mu\sqrt{K}(1-v)-\frac{\alpha K^{2}}{4v})\big)

\displaystyle=:\exp(L^{*}(\alpha,\mu)t^{5/3}).

The error term
J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)
is defined by

\displaystyle J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)\triangleq

\displaystyle\int_{0<u_{0}+\cdots+u_{m-1}<t}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}u_{i}^{3/2}}\exp\big(-\frac{(1-\alpha)}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\exp\big(\frac{K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)t^{8/3}}{2u_{0}}\big)d{\bf u}.

The final step of the proof is to estimate this function.

4.4.4
Step 4: Estimation of the error term
J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)

First of all, by applying the change of variables
u_{i}=v_{i}t^{8/3}

one can rewrite

\displaystyle J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)=

\displaystyle\int_{0<v_{0}+\cdots+v_{m-1}<t^{-5/3}}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}t^{-4/3}}{2\sqrt{\pi}v_{i}^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\exp\big(-\frac{1-\alpha}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\hat{D}_{i}^{2}t^{-8/3}}{v_{i}}+\frac{K^{*}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)}{2v_{0}}\big)d{\bf v}.

(4.40)

Next, one observes that

K^{*}\leqslant K_{0},\ \hat{D}_{i}\leqslant D_{i}\leqslant 2K_{0}t^{4/3},

\hat{D}_{i}\geqslant\frac{\lambda t^{4/3}}{4}\ (\text{for large }t),\ \hat{D}_{0}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{\delta}{\mu}\big)^{2}t^{4/3}.

By applying these relations to (
4.40
), one finds that
(for large
t
)

\displaystyle J(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta)\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0<v_{0}+\cdots+v_{m-1}<t^{-5/3}}\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{2K_{0}}{2\sqrt{\pi}v_{i}^{3/2}}\times\exp\big(-\frac{1-\alpha}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16v_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\exp\big(-\big(\frac{1-\alpha}{4}\frac{(\delta/\mu)^{4}}{4}-\frac{K_{0}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)}{2}\big)v_{0}^{-1}\big)d{\bf v}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle F(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)

To complete the proof, we now fix a choice of
\lambda
and
\eta

(
\lambda<\eta
) such that the constraint (
4.25
)
holds. According to the definition (
4.39
) of
{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta),

this will imply that the exponent

\frac{1-\alpha}{4}\frac{(\delta/\mu)^{4}}{4}-\frac{K_{0}{\cal R}^{t}(\lambda,\eta,\delta)}{2}

is uniformly positive for all large
t
. In particular, the integral

F(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)
is always finite. It is easily
seen from its expression that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}F(t;\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)=0.

Such a convergence is clearly uniform with respect to all
m\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}

(with
\lambda
fixed). This completes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

4.5
Completing the proof of Theorem
4.1

Now we are in a position to complete the proof of the main upper bound.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Given fixed
(\delta,\alpha,\mu)
, let
(\lambda,\eta)
be chosen
(and fixed) to satisfy the constraint (
4.25
). Because
of Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.4
, one
only needs to consider localised expectation

\displaystyle Q_{t}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\big(\{|D_{\eta}^{t}|\leqslant N_{\eta}\}\cap\{|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}|\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}\}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf w}\in{\cal A}_{\lambda,\eta,\delta}^{t}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(W_{s})ds};M^{t}\cap\{\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}={\bf w}\}\big],

(4.41)

where we recall that
{\cal A}_{\lambda,\eta,\delta}^{t}
is the
set of all extended
\lambda
-routes such that
|D_{\eta}^{t}({\bf w})|\leqslant N_{\eta}

and
|\hat{D}_{\lambda}^{t}({\bf w})|\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}
.
For a.e. fixed realisation of
\xi
and any large
t
, one has Note
that the total number
P_{\lambda}
of
\lambda
-clusters inside

Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}}
is trivially estimated as

P_{\lambda}\leqslant\frac{{\rm Vol}(Q_{K_{0}t^{4/3}})}{{\rm Vol}(Q_{\lambda t^{4/3}/2})}\leqslant C_{d}e^{(d-1)K_{0}t^{4/3}}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\sharp\{{\bf w}\in{\cal A}_{\lambda,\eta,\delta}^{t}\}

\displaystyle\leqslant P_{\lambda}+P_{\lambda}^{2}+\cdots+P_{\lambda}^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}}\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}C_{d}^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}}e^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}(d-1)K_{0}t^{4/3}}.

(4.42)

It follows from Proposition
4.1
that

Q_{t}\leqslant\hat{N}_{\lambda}C_{d}^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}}e^{\hat{N}_{\lambda}(d-1)K_{0}t^{4/3}}\cdot G(t;m,\lambda,\eta,\delta,\alpha,\mu)\cdot e^{(\delta+L^{*}(\alpha,\mu))t^{5/3}}.

As a consequence, one has

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\overline{\lim}}\frac{1}{t^{5/3}}\log Q_{t}\leqslant\delta+L^{*}(\alpha,\mu).

The desired estimate (
4.1
) thus follows by taking
\delta\downarrow 0,\alpha\uparrow 1

and

∎

Appendix A
Uniform LDP estimate for Brownian bridge

In this appendix, we develop the proof of Lemma
3.11
. Since
the argument is technically quite involved, we divide it into several
major steps.

Step 1: Decomposition

First of all, we need to fix an additional parameter
\zeta\ll\eta
.
The purpose for this is to consider the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{0,x;s,y}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant u\leqslant s}d(W_{u},\gamma_{u}^{x,y;s})>\frac{\delta}{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1-\zeta}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y})>\frac{\delta}{2},\ d(\omega_{1-\zeta},\hat{\gamma}_{1-\zeta}^{x,y})\leqslant\eta\big)+

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}\big(\sup_{1-\zeta\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y})>\eta\big)=:A_{s}^{x,y}+B_{s}^{x,y}.

(A.1)

Here
\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}
denotes the law of the Brownian bridge
reparametrised on
[0,1]
; more precisely, if
\{W_{u}^{0,x;s,y}:0\leqslant u\leqslant s\}

is the underlying Brownian bridge, then
\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}
is the
law of
[0,1]\ni v\mapsto W_{sv}^{0,x;s,y}.
Note that
\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}

is a probability measure on the path space

\Omega_{x,y}=\{\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow M\ |\ \omega\ \text{continuous,}\ \omega_{0}=x,\ \omega_{1}=y\}.

We always use
(\omega_{v})_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}
to denote a
generic element in
\Omega_{x,y}
. The path
[0,1]\ni v\mapsto\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y}

denotes the reparametrisation of the geodesic
\gamma^{x,y;s}
on

[0,1]
. The decomposition (
A.1
) holds since
\eta<\delta/2
.
In general, we will use
\hat{\gamma}^{p,q}
to denote the geodesic
parametrised on
[0,1]
joining
p
to
q
.

Step 2: Reduction to a Brownian LDP event

This and the next steps are devoted to estimating
A_{s}^{x,y}
(the
main estimate for
A_{s}^{x,y}
is provided by (
A.9
)
below). We begin by recalling a simple relation between Brownian bridge
and Brownian motion (cf.
[
Hsu90
, Equation (2.1)]
). Let
\mathbb{Q}^{s,x}

denote the law of
[0,1]\ni v\mapsto W_{sv}^{x}
where
W^{x}
is
the Brownian motion starting at
x
. Then

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}}{d\mathbb{Q}^{s,x}}\big|_{{\cal F}_{v}}=\frac{p(s(1-v),\omega_{v},y)}{p(s,x,y)}\ \ \ (0\leqslant v<1)

(A.2)

where
\{{\cal F}_{v}\}
is the natural filtration on path space.
By using the relation (
A.2
) with
v=1-\zeta
, one has

A_{s}^{x,y}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{s,x}}\big[\frac{p(s\zeta,\omega_{1-\zeta},y)}{p(s,x,y)};\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1-\zeta}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y})>\frac{\delta}{2},\ d(\omega_{1-\zeta},\hat{\gamma}_{1-\zeta}^{x,y})\leqslant\eta\big].

It follows from the two-sided heat kernel estimate (
2.1
)
and a further reparametrisation that

\displaystyle A_{s}^{x,y}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(1)}e^{\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{4s}}\mathbb{Q}^{s(1-\zeta),x}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{\zeta}})>\frac{\delta}{2},\ d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{\zeta}})\leqslant\eta\big),

(A.3)

where
y_{\zeta}\triangleq\hat{\gamma}_{1-\zeta}^{x,y}
.

Our task is thus reduced to estimating

Q_{\delta,\eta,\zeta}^{s,x,y}\triangleq\mathbb{Q}^{s(1-\zeta),x}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{\zeta}})>\frac{\delta}{2},\ d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{\zeta}})\leqslant\eta\big).

(A.4)

To this end, let us put
Q_{\delta,\eta,\zeta}^{s,x,y}
into a more
standard form of an LDP event for the Brownian motion. Recall from
the assumption of the lemma that
x\in B(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\eta)
,

y\in B(\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t},\eta)
. Therefore,

d(\gamma_{t_{i-1}}^{t},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{t})=K^{*}\implies d(x,y)\in[K^{*}-2\eta,K^{*}+2\eta]\ (\text{and }d(x,y)\leqslant 2K^{*}).

Let
y_{K^{*}}
denote the point on the geodesic
\hat{\gamma}^{x,y}

that is of distance
K^{*}
to
x.
Then for any
\omega
in the
event of (
A.4
), one has

\displaystyle d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})

\displaystyle\leqslant d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{\zeta}})+d(\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{\zeta}},y)+d(y,\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})\leqslant 3\eta+2K^{*}\zeta.

In addition, suppose that
d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{\zeta}})>\delta/2

at some
v
. Then one also has

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{\zeta}})-d(\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{K^{*}}},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{\zeta}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\delta}{2}-(3\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)>\frac{\delta}{4},

where the last inequality follows since
\eta<\delta/12
by assumption
and we further assume that
\zeta
is small enough to make the inequality
hold.

As a consequence, one obtains the following relation:

Q_{\delta,\eta,\zeta}^{s,x,y}\leqslant\mathbb{Q}^{s(1-\zeta),x}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})\geqslant\frac{\delta}{4},\ d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})\leqslant 3\eta+2K^{*}\zeta\big).

Due to the rotational symmetry of Brownian motion, the probability
on the right hand side depends only on the distance
d(x,y_{K^{*}})=K^{*}

and the parameters
s,\delta,\eta,\zeta
. In particular, it is independent
of
x,y
. We denote this probability as
\phi(s;K^{*},\delta,\eta,\zeta).

Step 3: Estimation of
\phi(s;K^{*},\delta,\eta,\zeta)

The estimation of
\phi(s;K^{*},\delta,\eta,\zeta)
relies on the
classical LDP for Brownian motion which we now recall (cf.
[
FW98
]
).

Lemma A.1
.

Define the energy functional

E(\omega)\triangleq\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\omega}_{v}|^{2}dv,&\text{if }|\dot{\omega}|\in L^{2}([0,1]);\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
F
be a closed subset of the path space

\Omega_{x}\triangleq\{\omega:[0,1]\rightarrow M\ |\ \omega\text{ continuous,}\ \omega_{0}=x\}

with respect to the uniform topology. Then one has

\underset{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\overline{\lim}}s\log\mathbb{Q}^{s,x}(F)\leqslant-\frac{1}{4}\inf_{\omega\in F}E(\omega).

In our case, we choose
F
to be the closed subset defined by

F\triangleq\big\{\omega\in\Omega_{x}:\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})\geqslant\frac{\delta}{4},\ d(\omega_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{1}^{x,y_{K^{*}}})\leqslant 3\eta+2K^{*}\zeta\big\}.

One needs to demonstrate an effective lower bound for the energy functional

E(\omega)
on
F
. This is given by the lemma below whose proof
relies on the assumption of nonpositive curvature.

Lemma A.2
.

One has

E(\omega)\geqslant d(x,y)^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)

(A.5)

for all
\omega\in F
.

Proof.

We first recall the following convexity property of geodesics in the
hyperbolic space (cf.
[
Jos05
, Theorem 4.8.2]
). Let
\alpha,\beta:[u,v]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}

be geodesics. Then one has

d(\alpha_{s},\beta_{s})\leqslant\max\big\{d(\alpha_{u},\beta_{u}),d(\alpha_{v},\beta_{v})\big\}

(A.6)

for all
s\in[u,v].
Now fix
\omega\in F
. Let
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}

be the geodesic from
x
to
\omega_{1}
. By applying (
A.6
)
to the geodesics
\alpha,\hat{\gamma}^{x,y_{K^{*}}}
, one easily
finds that

\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\alpha_{v})\geqslant\frac{\delta}{4}-(3\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)\geqslant\frac{\delta}{8}

where the second inequality follows again from the assumption (
3.27
)
on
\eta
and that
\zeta
is small enough to make the inequality
hold.

Let
v\in(0,1)
be such that
d(\omega_{v},\alpha_{v})\geqslant\delta/8
.
Let
\bar{\omega}
be the path defined by

\bar{\omega}_{r}\triangleq\begin{cases}\text{geodesic from $o$ to $\omega_{v}$,}&0\leqslant r\leqslant v;\\
\text{geodesic from }\omega_{v}\ \text{to }\omega_{1},&v\leqslant r\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

Since (minimising) geodesics minimise energy, one has

E(\omega)=\int_{0}^{v}|\dot{\omega}_{r}|^{2}dr+\int_{v}^{1}|\dot{\omega}_{r}|^{2}dr\geqslant\int_{0}^{v}|\dot{\bar{\omega}}_{r}|^{2}dr+\int_{v}^{1}|\dot{\bar{\omega}}_{r}|^{2}dr=E(\bar{\omega}).

It remains to lower bound
E(\bar{\omega})
. We will use the second
variation of energy formula to achieve this.

Let
F(r,u):[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}
be the variation
of
\bar{\omega}
defined in the following way. We first denote
[0,1]\ni u\mapsto\varphi_{v}(u)

as the geodesic joining
\alpha_{v}
to
\omega_{v}=\bar{\omega}_{v}
.
For each fixed
u\in[0,1]
, we then define

F(r,u)\triangleq\begin{cases}\text{geodesic from $x$ to $\varphi_{v}(u)$,}&0\leqslant r\leqslant v;\\
\text{geodesic from }\varphi_{v}(u)\ \text{to }\omega_{1}=\bar{\omega}_{1},&v\leqslant r\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

We set
\dot{F}\triangleq\partial_{r}F
and
F^{\prime}\triangleq\partial_{u}F

to be the tangential and variational fields respectively. Let
E(u)\triangleq\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{F}(r,u)|^{2}dr

denote the energy of
F(\cdot,u)
. According to the second variation
of energy formula (cf.
[
Jos05
, Theorem 4.1.1]
), one has

E^{\prime\prime}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla_{\partial_{r}}F^{\prime}|^{2}dr-\int_{0}^{1}\langle R(\dot{F},F^{\prime})F^{\prime},\dot{F}\rangle dr

where
R(\cdot,\cdot)\cdot
is the Riemannian curvature tensor. Since

\mathbb{H}^{d}
has negative curvature, one obtains that

E^{\prime\prime}(u)\geqslant\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla_{\partial_{r}}F^{\prime}|^{2}dr.

(A.7)

To lower bound the integral on the right hand side, let
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{d}\}

be an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{d}
and parallel translate
it along the geodesics
[0,v]\ni r\mapsto F(r,u)
to obtain an orthonormal
frame field
\{E_{1},\cdots,E_{d}\}
. We write

F^{\prime}(r,u)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}W^{i}(r,u)E_{i},\ \ \ r\in[0,v].

Since
W^{i}(0,u)\equiv 0
, one has

W^{i}(r,u)^{2}=\big(\int_{0}^{r}\partial_{\rho}W^{i}(\rho,u)d\rho\big)^{2}\leqslant v\int_{0}^{v}(\partial_{\rho}W^{i}(\rho,u))^{2}d\rho

and thus

|F^{\prime}(r,u)|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}W^{i}(r,u)^{2}\leqslant v\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{v}(\partial_{\rho}W^{i}(\rho,u))^{2}d\rho\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla_{\partial_{r}}F^{\prime}|^{2}dr

(A.8)

for all
r\in[0,v]
. The same inequality also holds for
r\in[v,1]

(by the same argument). As a result, one concludes from (
A.7
,

A.8
) that

E^{\prime\prime}(u)\geqslant\sup_{r,u\in[0,1]}|F^{\prime}(r,u)|^{2}\geqslant\frac{\delta^{2}}{64},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
|F^{\prime}(v,0)|=d(\omega_{v},\alpha_{v})\geqslant\delta/8.

Since
E^{\prime}(0)=0,
it follows that

E(\bar{\omega})=E(1)=E(0)+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}E^{\prime\prime}(u)du\geqslant d(x,\omega_{1})^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}.

By a further application of the triangle inequality

d(x,\omega_{1})^{2}\geqslant(d(x,y)-d(y,\omega_{1}))^{2}\geqslant d(x,y)^{2}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta),

one obtains the desired inequality (
A.5
).
∎

Remark A.1
.

Lemma
A.2
may fail in positively curved manifolds. For
instance, all great semicircles connecting the north and south poles
on the sphere are geodesics and they have the same energy. The lemma
holds on general Riemannian manifolds when the geodesic does not contain
conjugate points and the deviation is small.

One can now apply Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
A.2

to conclude that

\phi(s;K^{*},\delta,\eta,\zeta)\leqslant\exp\big[-\frac{1}{4s}\big(d(x,y)^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)\big)\big]

for all
s<s_{1}
, where
s_{1}
is a constant depending only on

K^{*},\delta,\eta,\zeta.
By substituting this estimate into (
A.3
),
one thus concludes that

A_{s}^{x,y}\leqslant C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(1)}\exp\big[-\frac{1}{4s}\big(\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)\big)\big].

(A.9)

This completes the estimate of
A_{s}^{x,y}.

Step 4: Estimation of
B_{s}^{x,y}

Recall that

B_{s}^{x,y}=\mathbb{Q}^{s,x,y}\big(\sup_{1-\zeta\leqslant v\leqslant 1}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{x,y})>\eta\big).

The strategy of estimating this term is quite similar to the previous
step. First of all, by the time reversal symmetry of Brownian bridge
(as a consequence of the symmetry of the heat kernel) one has

\displaystyle B_{s}^{x,y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}^{s,y,x}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant\zeta}d(\omega_{v},\hat{\gamma}_{v}^{y,x})>\eta\big)\leqslant\mathbb{Q}^{s,y,x}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant\zeta}d(\omega_{v},y)\geqslant\eta-2K^{*}\zeta\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{s,y}}\big[\frac{p(s(1-\zeta),\omega_{\zeta},x)}{p(s,y,x)};\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant\zeta}d(\omega_{v},y)\geqslant\eta-2K^{*}\zeta\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(2)}e^{\frac{K^{*2}}{s}}\mathbb{Q}^{s,y}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant\zeta}d(\omega_{v},y)\geqslant\eta-2K^{*}\zeta\big),

where the last inequality follows from the heat kernel estimate (
2.1
).
We assume that
\zeta
is small enough so that
\eta-2K^{*}\zeta>0.

Let
\omega
be a path such that
d(\omega_{v},y)\geqslant\eta-2K^{*}\zeta

for some
v\in[0,\zeta]
. Let
\beta:[0,v]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{d}

be the geodesic joining
y
to
\omega_{v}
. Then one has

E(\omega)\geqslant\int_{0}^{v}|\dot{\beta}_{r}|^{2}dr=\frac{d(\omega_{v},y)^{2}}{v}\geqslant\frac{(\eta-2K^{*}\zeta)^{2}}{\zeta}.

It follows again from Lemma
A.1
that

\mathbb{Q}^{s,y}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant v\leqslant\zeta}d(\omega_{v},y)\geqslant\eta-2K^{*}\zeta\big)\leqslant e^{-\frac{(\eta-2K^{*}\zeta)^{2}}{4\zeta s}}

for all
s\in(0,s_{2})
where
s_{2}
depends on
K^{*},\eta,\zeta
.
As a consequence,

B_{s}^{x,y}\leqslant C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(2)}e^{-\frac{1}{4s}\big(\frac{(\eta-2K^{*}\zeta)^{2}}{\zeta}-4K^{*2}\big)}

(A.10)

for all
s\in(0,s_{2}).

Step 5: Conclusion

Now one can further reduce
\zeta
to make sure that

\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta)>0,\ \frac{(\eta-2K^{*}\zeta)^{2}}{\zeta}-4K^{*2}>0

With such fixed
\zeta
(depending on
K^{*},\delta,\eta
), one
then set

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\triangleq C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(1)}+C_{d,K^{*},\zeta}^{(2)},\ s_{*}\triangleq s_{1}\wedge s_{2}

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{4}\min\big\{\frac{\delta^{2}}{128}-4K^{*}(5\eta+2K^{*}\zeta),\frac{(\eta-2K^{*}\zeta)^{2}}{\zeta}-4K^{*2}\big\}.

The desired estimate (
3.29
) with the above constants thus
follows by substituting (
A.9
,
A.10
) into (
3.29
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
3.11
.
