Nutrient stress-induced FTH1 safeguards redox balance and fuels pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma growth while serving as a therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker
Authors: Ziwei Zhang, Xuyang Chen, Jiaqi Wu, Saijun Xiao, Xiujuan Wei, Xunjun Yang, Yingjie Dai, Leyi Xu, Liqin Jin, Qiongya Zhao, Zhengquan Yang, Jianxin Lyu, Xiaojun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nutrient-poor tumor microenvironments drive metabolic reprogramming in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells; however, how iron homeostasis is remodeled under nutrient stress and how this influences tumor fate remains unclear. Here, we show that low-glucose conditions markedly increase free, bioavailable iron levels in PDAC cells, likely due to impaired iron-sulfur cluster assembly and enhanced heme degradation by upregulated heme oxygenase-1 (HMOX1). Concomitantly, nutrient stress activates the AMPK–NRF2–FTH1 axis; induced FTH1 sequesters excess iron, preserves redox balance and fuels proliferative capacity. FTH1 is markedly elevated in human PDAC tissues, and high expression is associated with poor survival. In a collected PDAC serum cohort, ferritin levels were found to be elevated in at least 71% of cases. In vivo, a high-iron diet combined with FTH1 inhibition arrests tumor growth, establishing FTH1 as a tractable therapeutic target. Functionally, the proliferative impact of FTH1 loss depends on tumor cell nutritional status; exogenous metabolic substrates such as pyruvate and fructose fully rescue proliferation defects caused by FTH1 depletion. Furthermore, across metabolic interventions, the tumor proliferation rate inversely correlates with FTH1 abundance. An analysis of paired serum samples from pancreatic cancer patients revealed that gemcitabine-based first-line therapy elicits a marked increase in circulating FTH1 protein, underscoring its potential as a real-time indicator of therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, FTH1 orchestrates iron rewiring and redox homeostasis under nutrient stress within the tumor microenvironment, driving PDAC progression and offering dual clinical value as both a target and a therapeutic-response indicator.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01237-y.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, often referred to as the “king of cancers”, is notorious for its dismal five-year survival rate and poor prognosis. This malignancy is characterized by its insidious onset, rapid progression, and high metastatic potential [1]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), accounts for the majority of pancreatic tumors, is notoriously challenging to early diagnose and treat effectively. In its early stages, PDAC typically presents with non-specific symptoms, resulting in late-stage diagnoses when curative treatment options are no longer viable [2]. Currently, clinical management and treatment development for PDAC remain in urgent need of breakthroughs, only a small subset of patients are candidates for curative surgery, while conventional systemic therapy such as chemotherapy is still the standard treatment for PDAC [3, 4]. The lack of therapeutic targets or drug resistance remains the most significant obstacle in the PDAC treatment. Although immunotherapy has recently emerged as a powerful anti-tumor strategy, yet PDAC is considered a “cold” tumor that effectively evades immune detection and restricts its responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors [5]. With the in-depth understanding of molecular landscape of PDAC, new therapeutic strategies are continuously progressing. Growing evidence links tumor metabolic reprogramming to drug resistance, so metabolism-targeted strategies are emerging as promising adjuncts for PDAC therapy [6–8].

Approximately 90% PDAC cases harbor activating KRAS that drive profound metabolic rewiring, markedly enhancing glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and the channeling of glucose into hexosamine and ribose-5-phosphate biosynthesis [9]. However, within the nutrient-poor and highly stromal tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer cells must withstand and overcome both energy shortage and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress to maintain their survival [10, 11]. Glucose deficiency is a distinguishing feature of PDAC tumors, which have developed various mechanisms to survive under conditions of low-glucose stress [12, 13]. Mammalian AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is known to be activated by glucose deficiency, signaled by rising AMP/ATP and ADP/ATP ratios, and maintains energy homeostasis by stimulating energy-producing pathways while suppressing energy-consuming processes [14, 15]. Previous studies have shown that activated AMPK promotes iron utilization through upregulating iron-sulfur cluster scaffold protein IscU2 under glucose deprivation [16]. Although AMPK is recognized as a key regulator of ferroptosis in tumors [17–19], whether its activation by nutritional stress remodels iron metabolism in cancer cells remains incompletely understood.

Cancer cells display dysregulated iron homeostasis and demand more iron for metabolism and growth [20]. Consequently, their labile iron pool expands, and loosely bound iron catalyzes Fenton reactions that generate reactive oxygen species, making the prevention of iron overload a critical challenge for tumor cell survival. Ferritin, a particle consisting of 24 subunits of ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and ferritin light chain (FTL), plays a key role in preventing this iron toxicity by sequestering excess iron in a non-reactive form, thereby limiting Fenton chemistry and maintaining cellular iron homeostasis [21]. Targeting ferritin and iron metabolism in cancer cells is an emerging, formidable field of therapeutics [22, 23]. Predictably, driven by the energy and oxidative stress of the TME, ferritin involved reprogramming of iron metabolism is critical for maintaining cancer cell proliferation and survival. However, how iron homeostasis is remodeled in nutrient-deprived settings remains poorly understood.

In this study, we sought to investigate how cancer cells remodel iron balance under glucose-deficient conditions. We found that iron-sulfur cluster assembly was inhibited in PDAC cells cultured in low-glucose medium, accompanied by increased free Fe2 + levels, indicating dysregulation of iron metabolism induced by glucose deficiency. Notably, low-glucose conditions markedly upregulated ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) expression in PDAC cells independently of elevated free Fe2 +, and this induction was reversed upon supplementation with other nutrients. Furthermore, FTH1 functions as a downstream effector of tumor cell nutritional status, with its expression escalating as nutrient availability declines, indicative of a nutrient-specific regulatory mechanism. We found that the FTH1 transcription is regulated by AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). In addition, we observed significantly higher FTH1 expression in pancreatic cancer tissues versus adjacent normal tissues, and more than 72% of pancreatic cancer patients had elevated serum ferritin concentration. Elevated FTH1 in glucose-starvation cells can inhibit oxidative stress-induced cell death, including ferroptosis and apoptosis, in an iron store-dependent manner. Our findings deepen the understanding of iron remodeling in response to nutrient stress and reveal a novel glucose-downstream effector role for FTH1 that promotes cancer cell survival under low-glucose conditions, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target or efficacy biomarker.

Results

Nutrient limitation robustly induces FTH1 expression in PDAC cells

The PDAC tumor microenvironment is nutrient-poor; consequently, PDAC cells are exquisitely sensitive to oxidative stress due to the scarcity of anti-oxidative metabolites such as glucose and glutamine [24]. Given the critical role of iron homeostasis in the regulation of oxidative stress, we hypothesized that nutrient deprivation may trigger an adaptive rewiring of iron metabolism in tumor cells in response to the consequent oxidative stress. To investigate whether iron homeostasis could be disrupted by nutrient limitation, we assessed FTH1 expression, a key indicator of altered iron status, in PDAC cells cultured at graded glucose concentrations. The results showed that lowering glucose dose-dependently increased FTH1 mRNA (Fig. 1A, B) and protein (Fig. 1C, D) levels in PaTu-8988t and Mia-PaCa2 cells. Moreover, glucose starvation induced FTH1 expression in a time-dependent manner at both the mRNA and protein level (Fig. 1E–H). To clarify whether glucose regulates FTH1 via energy alterations, we treated PaTu-8988t and Mia-PaCa2 cells with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) to block glucose metabolism. Similar to glucose deprivation, 2-DG markedly increased FTH1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1I–J), indicating that FTH1 expression is regulated by glucose-related energy metabolism. Furthermore, we deprived cells of glutamine, the other major energy substrate; elevated FTH1 expression was similarly observed (Fig. 1K–L), confirming that FTH1 acts as a nutrient-stress responsive effector whose expression reflects cellular energy status.

Energy depletion drives FTH1 upregulation through the AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of NRF2 in PDAC cells

Since glucose limitation elevated FTH1 mRNA levels (Fig. 1A, B), we next asked whether this effect occurs at the transcriptional level or via increased mRNA stability. Actinomycin D chase assays revealed that glucose limitation did not diminish FTH1 mRNA stability in PaTu-8988t cells (Fig. 2A), indicating that glucose regulates FTH1 expression primarily at the transcriptional level. Under low-glucose conditions, we observed a rise in cellular free Fe2 + (Fig. 2B), which was reversed upon glucose supplementation (supFig. 1A). This phenomenon may be attributable to a corresponding inhibition of iron utilization, as reflected by reduced iron–sulfur cluster levels (Fig. 2C) in PDAC cells. To validate this hypothesis, we depleted NFS1 to generate an Fe-S cluster-deficient model. Intracellular free Fe2 + measurements confirmed that iron–sulfur cluster disruption increases free Fe2 + levels, establishing impaired Fe-S assembly as a major driver of free Fe2+ accumulation in glucose-deprived PDAC cells (supFig. 1B, C). Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed that the expression levels of genes involved in cellular iron uptake under glucose limitation were not increased (Fig. 2D), indicating that intracellular free Fe2+ increase under low glucose conditions might be caused by an impaired iron utilization but not impaired uptake. Notably, glucose limitation markedly upregulated the expression of heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), the inducible enzyme that oxidizes cellular heme to release biliverdin, carbon monoxide and free iron, while concurrently suppressing the protein level of the iron-regulatory protein IREB2 (Fig. 2D). HMOX1 depletion could markedly reduce intracellular free Fe2 + levels in PaTu-8988t cells (supFig. 1D, E). Given that FTH1 expression is regulated by intracellular iron homeostasis at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [25, 26], we sought to determine whether the up-regulation of FTH1 under low-glucose conditions is caused by elevated free Fe2 +. Thus, we treated glucose-starved cells with the iron chelator DFO and measured FTH1 mRNA levels. The results showed that DFO could not reverse but even increased FTH1 transcription, suggesting that low-glucose-induced FTH1 upregulation is independent of intracellular iron levels (Fig. 2E, F). Furthermore, we treated PDAC cells with varying concentrations of FeCl3 under glucose-replete or -deficient conditions and found that although iron supplementation increased FTH1 mRNA levels, low-glucose stress exerted a significantly more pronounced effect on FTH1 transcription (SupFig. 1F). These results indicate that, compared with iron-dependent induction, nutrient stress serves as the predominant driver of FTH1 transcription.

As an established cellular energy sensor, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is central to energy metabolism and stress responses [27]. We therefore asked whether low-glucose-induced AMPK activation drives FTH1 upregulation. Using siRNAs targeting AMPKα (a subunit of AMPK), we silenced the AMPK pathway in PaTu-8988t cells (SupFig. 1G). qPCR analysis revealed that AMPKα depletion had no effect on FTH1 transcription under high glucose (25 mM) conditions, but markedly reduced FTH1 mRNA levels under low glucose (1 mM) conditions (Fig. 2G). Consistently, the pharmacological activation of AMPK by using AICAR significantly increased FTH1 mRNA levels under high-glucose conditions (Fig. 2H), confirming that AMPK activation is necessary for glucose-limitation-induced FTH1 transcription. Additionally, we found that the proliferation of glucose-starved PDAC cells was promoted by the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and that glucose-limitation-induced AMPK phosphorylation could be reversed by NAC treatment (SupFig. 1H–J). This finding indicates that glucose limitation causes both energy depletion and oxidative stress, which jointly promote AMPK activation. Meanwhile, glucose-limitation-induced FTH1 upregulation was also reversed by NAC in PDAC cells (SupFig. 1K, L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that FTH1 transcription is elevated by glucose-limitation-induced energy depletion and oxidative stress through the AMPK pathway.

FTH1 is a well-established transcriptional target of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [28], and AMPK-mediated phosphorylation is known to regulate Nrf2 transcriptional activation [29, 30]. Our experimental data show that Nrf2 knockdown abolishes both glucose-stress-induced (Fig. 2I, SupFig. 2M) and AMPK activation-mediated (Fig. 2J) upregulation of FTH1 expression in PDAC cells. Moreover, AMPK inhibition with compound C under low-glucose conditions reduces Nrf2 phosphorylation and FTH1 protein levels in PaTu-8988t cells (Fig. 2K). Collectively, FTH1 transcriptional expression in glucose-limited PDAC cells is controlled by the AMPK–Nrf2 axis.

The ferritin complex, comprising FTH1 and FTL subunits, serves a critical role in iron storage. Cellular free iron can enhance the stability of the ferritin protein complex via inhibiting NCOA4 mediated ferritinophagy [31, 32]. Consistent with this, we found that DFO treatment significantly reduced ferritin protein levels under glucose deprivation, indicating that ferritin stability is also regulated by low-glucose stress (supFig. 1N). Furthermore, treatment of PaTu-8988t cells with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) revealed that ferritin protein stability was increased under low-glucose conditions compared to glucose-replete conditions, consistent with iron-dependent stabilization (supFig. 1O).

Together, our data reveal that glucose limitation activates the AMPK-Nrf2 signaling axis to drive FTH1 transcription, while concomitantly elevated free Fe2 + independently enhances FTH1 protein stability.

Elevated FTH1 expression in glucose-starved PDAC cells shields them from oxidative damage

Excess labile iron is toxic and triggers ferroptosis, a process that FTH1 effectively suppresses [34, 35]. Under low-glucose conditions, FTH1 knockdown similarly induces this iron-dependent cell death, evidenced by elevated lipid peroxidation in PDAC cells (Fig. 3I) and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) rescued the impaired cell proliferation resulting from FTH1 depletion (supFig. 2C). Moreover, FTH1 depletion increases ROS levels (Fig. 3J, K) and apoptosis (Fig. 3L, M) in PaTu-8988t and Mia-PaCa2 cells, highlighting the protective role of FTH1 against oxidative stress during glucose deprivation.

Thus, glucose deprivation drives free-iron accumulation, triggering oxidative-stress-mediated ferroptosis and apoptosis. However, an adaptive cellular mechanism was activated; the AMPK–Nrf2 -induced upregulation of FTH1 expression sequesters excess iron, markedly attenuating oxidative damage and thereby promoting cell survival under metabolic stress.

FTH1 fuels PDAC tumor growth and can potentially serve as a target for cancer treatment

TCGA analysis revealed that FTH1 expression is markedly elevated in pancreatic cancer versus normal pancreatic tissue (Fig. 4A); furthermore, its expression increases progressively with tumor grade (Fig. 4B). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed higher FTH1 staining in cancerous versus adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 4C). We collected serum samples from clinical cases of digestive system tumors and measured the concentration of ferritin. We found that ferritin levels were significantly elevated in the serum of at least 71% of pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 4D). Notably, higher tumoral expression of FTH1 was associated with worse overall survival rates, among patients with pancreatic cancer (Fig. 4E). These correlative findings preliminarily indicate that FTH1 may hold exploratory potential as a therapeutic target for pancreatic cancer. FTH1 knockdown markedly inhibited PDAC cell proliferation under nutrient-deficient conditions (Fig. 3A, B), but had no effect under nutrient-rich conditions (Fig. 4F, G). This effect results from the significantly higher free Fe2+ levels under nutrient-deficient conditions (Fig. 2B); thus making FTH1 is essential for PDAC cell survival under these conditions. However, when excess iron is supplied to nutrient-replete cells, FTH1 depletion markedly inhibits proliferation in an iron concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4G), confirming its cytoprotective role under iron overload. Therefore, controlling iron levels in the tumor microenvironment while inhibiting FTH1 represents a theoretically feasible and highly effective therapeutic strategy. To test this hypothesis, we performed subcutaneous tumor assays, revealing that with a normal diet, FTH1 depletion does not affect PDAC tumor growth, whereas a high-iron diet combined with FTH1 knockdown dramatically suppresses tumor growth (Fig. 4H–K). Under high-iron diet, oxidative stress maker 4-HNE was increased in FTH1-depleted tumors (supFig. 3A). The proliferation defect induced by FTH depletion is reversible and nutrient-dependent in PDAC cells

In the nutrient-poor tumor microenvironment, tumor cells extensively utilize nutritional substrates other than glucose, including pyruvate, fructose, and amino acids [36–39]. Because pyruvate supplementation reduces Fe2+ levels in glucose-starved cells, we next to examined whether pyruvate can compensate for the cellular damage caused by FTH1 knockdown in PDAC cells under glucose-limited conditions. As expected, the inhibition of cell proliferation induced by FTH1 knockdown under low-glucose conditions was completely reversed by exogenous pyruvate (Fig. 5E, F). The decreased colony formation capabilities of glucose-starved PDAC cells with FTH1 depletion were also restored by pyruvate supplementation (Fig. 5G). Similar to pyruvate, fructose, another metabolic substrate, also promoted cell proliferation and decreased FTH1expression under low-glucose conditions (supFig. 4A, B), and compensated for the growth defect caused by FTH1 knockdown under these conditions (supFig. 4C). In addition, exogenous pyruvate blunted the ROS surge triggered by FTH1 knockdown (Fig. 5H, I) and markedly reduced the resulting apoptosis in PDAC cells under low-glucose conditions (Fig. 5J, K). These results strongly suggest that iron homeostasis is tightly linked to energy stress. Moreover, within the tumor microenvironment, improved nutritional conditions can compensate for the oxidative damage caused by FTH1 deficiency.

FTH1 can serve as a potential biomarker for metabolic intervention and chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer

Our data demonstrate that FTH1 expression is exquisitely sensitive to the nutritional microenvironment of PDAC cells. Under glucose or glutamine deprivation, FTH1 transcript and protein levels markedly increased (Fig. 1A–D, K, L), whereas supplementation with alternative energetic substrates such as pyruvate or fructose suppressed FTH1 expression (Fig. 5B, C; sup-Fig. 4B). The integrative analysis revealed a reciprocal relationship between nutrient availability and FTH1 abundance: well-nutrient cells displayed accelerated proliferation coincident with low FTH1, whereas nutrient restriction suppressed growth and elevated FTH1 expression. Across a panel of PDAC lines, slowly proliferating cells express high FTH1, whereas rapidly expanding lines express minimal levels (Fig. 6A), implying that metabolically quiescent cells are more reliant on FTH1 to sustain growth. Notably, CFPAC cells, which constitutively express relatively high levels of FTH1 (Fig. 6A), exhibited sensitivity to FTH1 depletion under both glucose-replete and glucose-deficient conditions (supFig. 5A, B), in contrast to PaTu-8988t cells, which were sensitive to FTH1 loss only under low-glucose conditions (Fig. 3A; Fig. 4G). To establish causality, we silenced the metabolic reprogramming driver KRAS in PaTu-8988t and Mia PaCa-2 cells (Fig. 6B–D) and introduced activating KRAS mutations (G12D or G12V) into non-KRAS activated BXPC3 cells (Fig. 6E). qPCR profiling demonstrated an inverse correlation between KRAS activation and FTH1 transcript abundance (Fig. 6B–F). Consistently, rescue of glucose-starved PDAC cells with NMN or AKB—both of which replenish NAD+ and reinvigorate metabolism—accelerated proliferation (Fig. 6G, H) and concurrently repressed FTH1 expression (Fig. 6I, J). These results identified FTH1 as a sensitive metabolic effector whose expression inversely correlates with proliferative rate and oncogenic KRAS signaling, validating its utility as a biomarker for the metabolic and proliferative state of PDAC cells.

Treatment of PDAC cells with the first-line nucleoside analogue gemcitabine simultaneously suppressed proliferation and markedly induced FTH1 expression (Fig. 6K, L). This observation was corroborated clinically, as serum FTH1 rose significantly in pancreatic cancer patients receiving gemcitabine-based therapy (Fig. 6M). Furthermore, FTH1 depletion sensitized PaTu-8988t cells to gemcitabine treatment compared to control cells (Fig. 6N). High FTH1-expressing CFPAC cells exhibited greater gemcitabine resistance than PaTu-8988t and Mia-PaCa2 cells with low FTH1 levels (Fig. 6O). These data indicate that FTH1 holds potential as real-time biomarker for evaluating chemotherapeutic response in pancreatic cancer.

Collectively, under nutrient-poor stress or metabolic intervention, FTH1 expression is enhanced via AMPK-NRF2-regulated pathway, thereby promoting tumor growth. FTH1 has significant clinical value as a potential therapeutic target and a predictive biomarker for gemcitabine sensitivity. To asse whether the AMPK–NRF2–FTH1 axis is tumor-type specificity, we silenced AMPKα or NRF2 in HPNE (human pancreatic normal epithelial) and DLD-1 (a human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell) under low glucose conditions and detected FTH1 mRNA levels. Results showed that the expression of FTH1 was also decreased duo to AMPK or NRF2 depletion in those cells (supFig. 5C–H), indicating that this regulatory axis is likely a general characteristic of cancer or non-cancer cells in response to metabolic stress.

Discussion

The metabolic hallmarks of PDAC fuel tumor aggressiveness and chemoresistance while simultaneously presenting new opportunities for therapy [40]. Iron metabolism, indispensable yet inherently toxic, is stringently governed regulated at both the systemic and cellular levels by complex mechanisms. Ferritin—the first identified iron regulator—safely sequesters surplus iron within its protein cage, and its heavy-chain subunit FTH1 has the ferroxidase center where catalyzes the conversion of Fe2 + to Fe3 +, thereby averting redox damage. In this study, we report that FTH1 is sensitive to metabolic alteration and protect PDAC cells from oxidative stress-induced cell death under nutrient-poor microenvironment. Glucose limitation induced FTH1 up-regulation is relied on AMPK mediated NRF2 phosphorylation. Glucose starvation raises cytosolic free Fe2 + and simultaneously amplifies apoptosis and ferroptosis in PDAC cells, this situation underscoring the greater protective role of FTH1 against iron-driven cytotoxicity under low-glucose conditions compared with cells supplied with ample glucose (Fig. 7). In rapidly proliferating PDAC cells, FTH1 is dispensable for growth; however, when iron overload or nutrient limitation elevates free Fe2 +, FTH1 becomes indispensable. In vivo, tumor-bearing mice fed a high-iron diet show markedly impaired tumor growth, underscoring the clinical potential of targeting FTH1. Nevertheless, several critical issues warrant consideration. The systemic consequences of high-iron intervention remain unclear, as chronic iron overload may perturb systemic iron homeostasis and damage normal organs. Moreover, ferritin is essential for physiological iron storage and redox balance in normal cells; thus, the adverse effects of FTH1-targeted therapies on normal tissues cannot be fully assessed at present. These limitations underscore the need to optimize therapeutic strategies for enhanced tumor specificity and diminished systemic toxicity in future translational research. Overall, our findings unveil the regulatory crosstalk between iron homeostasis and nutrient stress, and define the specific protective role of FTH1 within the nutrient-deprived tumor microenvironment, thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for clinical intervention.Figure 7FTH1 upregulation during glucose starvation protects against oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis and apoptosis in an iron store-dependent manner. Why Fe2+ is increased in glucose-starved cells? Mitochondria are the central hub of iron utilization, orchestrating iron-sulfur cluster assembly and heme synthesis [41]. Our study has shown that that low-glucose conditions reduce iron-sulfur cluster levels and up-regulate the heme-catabolizing enzyme HMOX1, indicating suppressed iron utilization and enhanced iron release. The decrease in iron-sulfur cluster synthesis is likely attributable to diminished NADPH production, which impairs the FDXR–FDX1/2 electron-transfer step essential for ISC biogenesis [42, 43]. Zhiyu Qian et al. reported that glucose deprivation causes iron overload in renal epithelial cells by disabling the V-ATPase–mTOR axis, thereby promoting ferritin degradation and iron release [44]. In stark contrast, we observed that glucose starvation markedly increased assembled ferritin heavy- and light-chain protein levels in PDAC cells. This divergence underscores a context-specific, tumor-type-dependent rewiring of iron homeostasis under nutrient stress.

FTH1 expression is significantly higher in pancreatic cancer tissues than in normal tissue, and high expression predicts worse prognosis, indicating that elevated FTH1 promotes tumor progression. When cell proliferation is suppressed by drug treatment or metabolic intervention, FTH1 levels in patient serum and tumor cells increase further, suggesting that this rise in FTH1 may be one mechanism underlying resistance. Our findings highlight the clinical potential of FTH1 as a prognostic indicator and therapeutic target. Despite the promising findings of FTH1 in our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Serum ferritin is influenced by multiple systemic factors and lacks tumor specificity.

In summary, our study establishes a tight link between energy metabolism and iron-homeostasis rewiring. PDAC cells cultured in low glucose endure iron-overload-driven oxidative stress; AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of NRF2 counteracts this threat by up-regulating FTH1, thereby fostering tumor-cell proliferation and survival. Notably, we identified a distinctive regulatory pattern of ferritin in response to glucose deprivation. At the transcriptional level, ferritin expression appears to be moderately linked to iron availability, whereas the stress induced by glucose deprivation seems to exert a more predominant influence on its transcription. This observation supports a stress-dominant regulatory model of ferritin that functions largely independently of iron availability, which differs from the canonical iron-dependent regulatory pathway.

Materials and methods

Cells and culture conditions

Human PDAC cells of PaTu-8988t, Mia PaCa-2 and HEK 293T cells were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China). Cell authentication was carried out using short tandem repeat loci analyses (Genetic Testing Biotechnology Corporation, Jiangsu, China). Both PDAC cells, Mia PaCa-2 cells and HEK 293T cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), supplemented with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Gentamicin Solution (absin, Shanghai, China) and 12% calf serum (Sigma-Aldrich). A low concentration of mycoplasma removal agent (0.5 µg/mL, Beyotime) was added in those culture medium, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, to prevent mycoplasma contamination. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 5% CO2. For cells cultured under nutrient-stress conditions, DMEM and calf serum were substituted with Dulbecco′s Modified Eagle′s Medium without glucose (Servicebio) and dialyzed serum without glucose, additionally, glucose was added to the culture medium to increase the glucose concentration to 1 mM. That is, low glucose conditions containing 1 mM glucose.

Preparation of dialyzed serum

Serum without glucose was prepared by dialysis using dialysis membranes with a 1kDa molecular-weight cutoff (Spectrum Labs, California, USA) at 4 °C for 12 h. The dialyzing buffer (151.425 g Tris-base, 37.275 g potassium chloride, and 400.314 g, added to 5 L of deionized water, pH = 7.35, all from Sigma-Aldrich) was replaced every 1 h for a total of 10 times, to remove the low-molecular-weight metabolites, as described previously.

Transfection and infection

siRNA transfection and transfection of plasmids were respectively conducted by employing Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Lentivirus particles for infection were generated through cotransfecting HEK293T cells with one of the expression plasmids and two packaging plasmids (pMD2G and pSPAX2) at a ratio of 1:1:2. The medium containing infectious lentivirus particles was gathered 72 h after transfection, centrifuged and filtered via a 0.45 μm filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). HEK293T or PDAC cells infected with lentiviral particles were screened using 2 μg/mL puromycin (Sangon Biotech) for 10 days before the subsequent experiments. The siRNAs used in this study were synthesized by genepharma Biological Technology (Nanjing, China). The related siRNA or shRNA sequences are listed in Supporting Table.

RT and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and then reverse transcription 1 μg of total RNA into cDNA was performed with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Novazan, Nanjing, China). The qPCR experiments were conducted with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Novazan) on a Quantagene q225 system (KUBO-Tech AG, Switzerland). The mRNA levels of the target genes were normalized against the expression of β-actin. Sequences of qPCR primers were as follows: FTH1-F: GCCAGAACTACCACCAGGAC; FTH1-R: GCGGTCAAAGTAGTAAGACATGG; Nrf2-F: AGGTTGCCCACATTCCCAAA; Nrf2-R: AGTGACTGAAACGTAGCCGA.

Cell proliferation

For the cell proliferation assay under nutrient-stress conditions, 1 × 104 optimal condition cells were digested into a single-cell and plated in triplicate in 12-well plates. Cell adherence after 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with customized culture medium supplemented with 1 mM glucose culture medium were conducted as described previously. After 72 h, the cells were then collected and counted using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies). In addition, pyruvate, FeCl3, DFO as well as gene interference and various culture conditions were also added to assess their effects on cell proliferation.

Colony formation assay

Cells were plated in 6-well plates with a density of 500 cells/well. The cells were cultivated for 15 days, and the culture medium was changed every 3–4 days. the medium was removed, and 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to the plates for 20–30 min. Subsequently, colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime) for 20–30 min, and colonies with diameters exceeding 0.5 mm were counted under a light microscope.

Analysis of apoptosis

The cell apoptosis rate was measured using Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme). In the early apoptotic stage, phosphatidylserine (PS) is everted to the outer plasma membrane, which can be specifically recognized by Annexin V-FITC. In contrast, propidium iodide (PI) is membrane-impermeable and only penetrates damaged membranes of late apoptotic and necrotic cells, thereby distinguishing early apoptotic cells from late apoptotic/necrotic cells. according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Shortly, 2 × 105 cells were cultured in six-well plates. After the cells adhere to the surface, the culture medium is changed to normal cultured conditions, low glucose conditions or other conditions and continue cultured for 24 h before measurement. Then, all cells, including those floating in the medium, were harvested and rinsed with pre-warmed PBS. Cells were incubated with 5 μM PI and 5 μM Annexin V-FITC for 25 min in the dark. Following co-staining with Annexin V-FITC and PI, fluorescence intensities were detected using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies) via the Annexin V-FITC channel (Ex/Em = 488/530 nm) and PE channel (Ex/Em = 488/572 nm). The percentage of Annexin V-FITC/PE double-positive cells was quantified to assess the proportion of late apoptotic cells.

Immunoblotting and antibodies

Total proteins were extracted from whole cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cell Signaling Technology). Cells were lysed on ice for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of Proteins (20 μg) were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto 0.22-μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, then primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C at the following dilutions: The anti-β-actin (sc-47778; 1:4000) was purchased from Santa Cruz. The anti-FTH1 (3998S; 1:1000), anti-AMPK (5832T; 1:1000) and anti Phospho-AMPK alpha (Thr172) (2535T; 1:1000) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. The anti-Nrf2 (ab62352; 1:1000), anti-Nrf2 (phospho S40) (ab76026; 1:1000) antibodies were purchased from Abcam. After three washes with TBST (5 min each), followed by related secondary rabbit or mouse anti-IgG antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. 7076 and cat. 7074). Following washes, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using Gel-Pro Analyzer 4.0 software (Media Cybernetics).

Analysis of ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a reactive oxygen species assay kit with DCFH-DA as the fluorescent probe (Beyotime). according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. Following a 24-hour incubation, the medium was replaced with the respective conditioned medium for an additional 24 hours. After collection of the cell pellet, DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 in PBS. Add 100 µL of the diluted probe to each sample and incubate at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 20 minutes. To ensure adequate interaction between the probe and the cells, gently mix the samples by inversion every 3–5 minutes. The excitation and emission wavelengths for DCF detection were 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively. Notably, the fluorescence spectrum of DCF closely resembles that of FITC (Ex/Em = 488/530 nm). Accordingly, the FITC channel configuration was adopted to detect intracellular ROS levels.

Lipid peroxidation assay

The levels of lipid peroxidation were measured using Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit with BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe (Beyotime). The procedure for cell seeding, culture, and medium change was the same as that described for the ROS detection assay. Cells were collected and washed with PBS, BODIPY 581/591 C11 was diluted 1:1000 in PBS and at 37 °C for incubator 30 min. Reduced BODIPY 581/591 C11 correspond to the PI channel (Ex/Em = 488/585 nm). The PE channel (Ex/Em = 488/572 nm) of our Agilent Technologies flow cytometer exhibits high spectral overlap with the intrinsic PI channel. Therefore, the PE channel was used to detect the reduced form of the probe. Upon oxidation by intracellular lipid hydroperoxides, the maximum excitation and emission wavelengths of the probe shifted to 488/510 nm, which was detected using the FITC channel. The degree of lipid peroxidation was determined by calculating the fluorescence ratio of the reduced state to the oxidized state.

Fe-S cluster fluorescent assay

Fe-S cluster fluorescent sensor plasmids were generated by cloning genes encoding N173 (Venus’s residues 1–173) or C155 (Venus’s residues 155–243) fused to GRX2 through a 15 amino acid (GGGGS)3 linker into pCDNA3.4. N173- and C155-terminal Venus-GRX2 fusion constructs were mixed in 1:1 v/v ratio and co-transfected into PaTu-8988t cells. PaTu-8988t cells co-transfected with N173–GRX2 and C155–GRX2 were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, the cells were treated with culture media containing different glucose concentrations (0, 1, 5, and 25 mM) for another 24 hours. Fe-S cluster fluorescent assay was then analyzed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies).

Intracellular iron measurement

Intracellular iron levels were measured using the Fe2 + -sensitive fluorescent probe, FerroOrange (DOJINDO), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well in 100 µL of cell suspension and cultured overnight in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. After cell attachment, the cells were rinsed with PBS, followed by the addition of 100 µL of fresh conditioned medium per well. The cells were then cultured for an additional 24 hours. After rinsing with PBS, the cells were incubated with 100 µL of working solution containing 0.5 µM FerroOrange per well. The plates were protected from light and incubated for 30 minutes. Following incubation, the fluorescence was measured directly without washing using a multi-mode microplate reader. Following fluorescence measurement, the fluorescence values were normalized against the cell count determined by flow cytometry.

Animals

Nude mice (BALB/C nude, male, 4 weeks old) were obtained from Hangzhou Medical College and housed in the Animal Experiment Center of Wenzhou Medical University under specific-pathogen-free conditions. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (wydw2023-0106). PaTu-8988t cells (5 × 106) were injected into mice (6 weeks old) under the armpit or shoulder after resuspension in a PBS and Matrigel mixture (Corning) at a ratio of 1:1. Provided with normal diet and high-iron diet (contain iron 4.15 g/kg) for four weeks. Once the tumors were established, their sizes were measured using a Vernier caliper once or twice weekly, with volume calculated using the following formula: volume = (length × width × height) × 0.5236. After approximately 1 month, all mice were euthanized and carefully dissected to obtain tumors, which were photographed and weighed.

IHC analysis and scoring

The human PDAC tissue microarray samples used in this study were obtained from Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital, affiliated with the Hangzhou Medical College. This procedure was approved by the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Ethics Committee under ethical approval number 2022QT010. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first baked at 60 °C for 1 h to ensure adhesion, then removed using xylene and the sections were rehydrated in ethanol solutions of different concentrations(100%, 95%, 85%, 75%) for 5 min each. The activity of the antigen was restored by incubating sections in 10 mM citric acid (Beyotime) at 95 to 100 °C for 5 min; repeat this process four times, for a total of 20 minutes. Hydrogen peroxide (Beyotime) was added to block the activity of endogenous peroxide enzymes. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-FTH1 and visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB; Beyotime). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated with different concentrations of ethanol solution, cleared with xylene and mounted with neutral resin. Protein expression was observed under a light microscope, and the integrated optical density (IOD) was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics). The mean optimal density (MOD), representing FTH1 intensity, was calculated as IOD/area.

Patients’ serum samples analysis

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to analyze serum ferritin levels in patients diagnosed with various digestive tract tumors between January 2019 and May 2025. Only patients with a confirmed histopathological diagnosis and available baseline serum ferritin measurement prior to initiation were included. Statistical comparisons of ferritin levels across different tumor groups were performed.

Patients’ plasma samples collection and proteomic analysis

In this study, patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) were recruited between November 2021 and March 2024. All participants provided written informed consent. Venous blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning after an 8 to 12-hour overnight fast. Approximately 5 mL of whole blood was drawn per participant. The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 1600 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C to separate cellular components from plasma. Transcriptomic analysis

Samples for transcriptomic analysis were collected according to the protocol provided by LC-Bio (Hangzhou, China). Cells were first treated under two conditions: 25 mM glucose or 1 mM glucose for 8 hours. Subsequently, cells (1 × 106 cells per sample) were harvested, washed twice with PBS, added TRIzol reagent and stored at −80 °C until further RNA sequencing analysis.

Proteomic analysis of cells

For proteomic analysis, sample collection was performed according to the protocol provided by Majorbio (Shanghai, China). PDAC cells were treated with either 25 mM or 1 mM glucose for 8 hours. Subsequently, 5 × 106 cells per sample were harvested on ice, washed twice with PBS, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen cell pellets were then shipped to Majorbio on dry ice for subsequent proteomic profiling.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative experiments were conducted with a minimum of three biological replicates. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent two-tailed Student’s t-test. All statistical analyses were carried out with SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM) and plotted using GraphPad Software (Prism 10). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
