A modular version of the Brunn-Minkowski inequality and its applications
Yuchen Ding, Huixi Li and Zihan Zhang

Abstract
. Let α > 1 \alpha>1 be an irrational number and k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 a positive integer. Let f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) be a polynomial with positive integer coefficients. Solving a 2001 problem of Sárközy on special sequences, Hegyvári proved in 2003 that there exists an infinite sequence A A with density 1 k − 1 k ​ α \frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha} such that { f ( a 1 ) + … + f ( a k ) : a i ∈ A , 1 ≤ i ≤ k } ∩ { ⌊ n α ⌋ : n ∈ ℕ } = ∅ . \big\{f(a_{1})+\ldots+f(a_{k}):a_{i}\in A,1\leq i\leq k\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}=\emptyset. 1.
Introduction

In section 6 of his 2001 problem list, Sárközy
[
6
, Conjecture 47]
posed the following attractive conjecture involving special sequences.

Conjecture 1.1
.

If
A
and
B
are infinite sequences of positive integers, the lower asymptotic densities of
A
and
B
are positive, and
\alpha
is an irrational number, then

1)

ab=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor,\quad a\in A,~b\in B,

2)

a^{2}+b^{2}=\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor,\quad a\in A,~b\in B

have infinitely many solutions, respectively.

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers. We need only to consider the case
\alpha>1
in Conjecture
1.1
, since
\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}=\mathbb{N}
for
0<\alpha<1
in which case Conjecture
1.1
is correct trivially.

Hegyvári
[
2
]
answered Conjecture
1.1
negatively. Surprisingly, as illustrated by Hegyvári even a weaker form of Conjecture
1.1
fails.
Let
\alpha>1
be an irrational number and
k\geq 2
a positive integer. Let
f(x)
be a polynomial with positive integer coefficients. Then Hegyvári proved that there exists an infinite sequence
A
, for which the density of
A
exists,
d(A)=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}>0
and

\displaystyle\big\{f(a_{1})+\ldots+f(a_{k}):a_{i}\in A,1\leq i\leq k\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}=\emptyset,

(1.1)

where
d(A)
is the density of
A
. Hegyvári
[
2
]
also showed that the density
\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
he provided is optimal in the case
k=2
. Specifically, let
A
be an infinite sequence of positive integers with
\overline{d}(A)>\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}
, where
\overline{d}(A)
is the upper asymptotic density of the set
A
. Then Hegyvári proved that

\displaystyle\Big|\big\{f(a_{1})+f(a_{2}):a_{i}\in A,i=1,2\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\Big|=\infty.

(1.2)

It is of interest to determine whether the density
\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
of
A
satisfying (
1.1
) is optimal for all
k\geq 2
. We will show that this is in fact the case.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha>1
be an irrational number and
k\geq 2
a positive integer. Let
f(x)
be a polynomial with positive integer coefficients. Let
A
be an infinite sequence of positive integers with
\overline{d}(A)>\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
. Then

\displaystyle\Big|\big\{f(a_{1})+\ldots+f(a_{k}):a_{i}\in A,1\leq i\leq k\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\Big|=\infty.

Obviously, Theorem
1.1
is an extension of (
1.2
).

Corollary 1.1
.

For any
k\geq 2
, the density
\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
of
A
satisfying
(\ref{eq-1-1})
is optimal.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we need a certain version of the Brunn-Minkowski inequality. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality in
R^{m}
was first investigated by Brunn
[
1
]
and Minkowski
[
3
]
. For a historical account of the Brunn-Minkowski inequality, one can refer to the article of Ruzsa
[
5
]
.
Let
\mathbb{R}
be the set of real numbers. For any measurable set
S\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mu(S)
be its Lebesgue measure. Let
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
two compact sets of
\mathbb{R}
. Then the Brunn-Minkowski inequality (see e.g.,
[
7
, Page 35]
) states that

\displaystyle\mu(S_{1}+S_{2})\geq\mu(S_{1})+\mu(S_{2}),

(1.3)

where

S_{1}+S_{2}=\{s_{1}+s_{2}:s_{1}\in S_{1},s_{2}\in S_{2}\}

is the sumset as usual. More generally, let
m
be a fixed integer.
Let

\displaystyle kS=\big\{s_{1}+\cdots+s_{k}:s_{i}\in S,1\leq i\leq k\big\}.

From (
1.3
) for compact set
S
of
\mathbb{R}
we clearly have

\displaystyle\mu(kS)\geq k\mu(S).

(1.4)

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need a variant of (
1.4
) above. Let
\mu^{*}
be the Lebesgue outer measure.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a given integer and
0<\beta<1
a given number. Let
A\subset(0,1)
be a set with
\mu^{*}(A)>\beta/k
and
B\subset(0,1)
be an open set with
\mu(B)\geq 1-\beta
. Then there exist
k
elements
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
of
A
(not necessarily different) such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in B.

Remark 1.1
.

If
A
is a open set with
\mu(A)>\beta/k
, then by Theorem
1.2
we have

\displaystyle\mu\big(kA\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\big)>\beta,

which can be viewed as a modular version of (
1.4
). It is also worth pointing out that the condition
\mu^{*}(A)>\beta/k
in Theorem
1.2
is sharp. Actually, if we take
A=(0,\beta/k)
and
B=(\beta,1)
, then

\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\in(0,\beta)

for any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in A
, and the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
fails.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We will make use of the Cauchy-Davenport theorem (see e.g.,
[
4
, Theorem 2.2]
).

Lemma 2.1

(Cauchy-Davenport)
.

Let
p
be a prime number, and let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Then

|A+B|\geq\min\big\{p,|A|+|B|-1\big\}.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Since
\mu^{*}(A)>\beta/k
and
\mu(B)\geq 1-\beta
, we can assume that

\displaystyle k\mu^{*}(A)+\mu(B)=1+\delta

for some
\delta>0
. Noting that
B
is open, it is the union of countablly many open intervals, and we will write it as

B=\cup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(a_{i},b_{i})

with
a_{i}<b_{i}
, say.
Then there are finitely many ones of the open intervals
(a_{i},b_{i})
satisfying that

\displaystyle B_{1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i},b_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(B\setminus B_{1})<\delta/4.

(2.1)

Let

t=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\{b_{i}-a_{i}\}>0

and

\displaystyle B_{2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big(a_{i}+r,b_{i}-r\big),

(2.2)

where
r=\min\big\{\frac{t}{4},\frac{\delta}{4n}\big\}>0
. Hence, by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\mu(B_{2})=\mu(B)-\mu(B\setminus B_{2})\geq\mu(B)-\frac{\delta}{4}-2nr\geq\mu(B)-\frac{3\delta}{4}.

(2.3)

Next we introduce two more sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. For
A
, there is an open set
A_{1}
such that

\displaystyle A\subset A_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{*}(A_{1}\setminus A)<\min\left\{\frac{r}{2k},\frac{\delta}{12nk}\right\}.

(2.4)

Similarly,
A_{1}
can be presented as the union of countablly many open intervals
(c_{j},d_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
A\cap(c_{j},d_{j})\neq\emptyset
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
.
Also, there exist finitely many open intervals
(c_{j},d_{j})
satisfying that

\displaystyle A_{2}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}(c_{j},d_{j})\subset A_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(A_{1}\setminus A_{2})<\frac{\delta}{12nk}.

(2.5)

Hence, by (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) we have

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(A_{2}\triangle A)\leq\mu^{*}(A_{1}\setminus A)+\mu(A_{1}\setminus A_{2})<\frac{\delta}{6nk}

(2.6)

in view of
A,A_{2}\subset A_{1}
, where
A_{2}\triangle A
is the symmetric difference of
A
and
A_{2}
. We now conclude from (
2.6
) that

\displaystyle\mu(A_{2})\geq\mu^{*}(A)-\mu^{*}(A_{2}\triangle A)>\mu^{*}(A)-\frac{\delta}{6nk}.

(2.7)

We will further introduce another two sets
A_{3}
and
B_{3}
. Let
p
be a sufficiently large prime such that

\displaystyle p>\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq n\\
1\leq j\leq m\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{2}{b_{i}-a_{i}-2r},\frac{2}{d_{j}-c_{j}}\right\}+\frac{24(kmn+n)}{\delta}.

(2.8)

We divide
[0,1]
into
p
closed intervals
I_{s}=\left[\frac{s-1}{p},\frac{s}{p}\right]
, where
s=1,2,\cdots\,,p
.
Let

A_{3}=\bigcup_{I_{s}\subset A_{2}}I_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{3}=\bigcup_{I_{s^{\prime}}\subset B_{2}}I_{s^{\prime}}.

We claim that we there exist
\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\alpha_{k}^{\prime}\in A_{3}
(not necessarily different), such that

\alpha_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}^{\prime}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in B_{3}.

For the proof of our claim, we first note that

\mu(A_{3})\geq\mu(A_{2})-2m/p

and

\mu(B_{3})\geq\mu(B_{2})-2n/p

by the constructions of
A_{3}
and
B_{3}
.
By (
2.8
) we have

\displaystyle 2mk/p+2n/p<\delta/12,

from which it follows that

\displaystyle k\mu(A_{3})+\mu(B_{3})

\displaystyle\geq k\left(\mu(A_{2})-\frac{2m}{p}\right)+\left(\mu(B_{2})-\frac{2n}{p}\right)

\displaystyle>k\mu(A_{2})+\mu(B_{2})-\frac{\delta}{12}.

(2.9)

Inserting (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) into (
2
) we get

\displaystyle k\mu(A_{3})+\mu(B_{3})

\displaystyle\geq k\mu^{*}(A)+\mu(B)-\frac{\delta}{6n}-\frac{3\delta}{4}-\frac{\delta}{12}

\displaystyle\geq k\mu^{*}(A)+\mu(B)-\delta

\displaystyle=1.

(2.10)

Let

\tilde{A_{3}}=\left\{q\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p):\frac{q}{p}\in A_{3}\right\}

and

\tilde{B_{3}}=\left\{q\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p):\frac{q}{p}\in B_{3}\right\}.

Then, by the selection of
p
in (
2.8
), we know that
\big|\tilde{A_{3}}\big|\geq 2m
and
\big|\tilde{B_{3}}\big|\geq 2n
.
To prove our claim, it suffices to find
q_{1},\cdots,q_{k}\in\tilde{A_{3}}
such that

\displaystyle q_{1}+\cdots+q_{k}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p)\in\tilde{B_{3}}.

(2.11)

We note that for any positive integer
s
if there are
s
continuous closed intervals of length
\frac{1}{p}
in
A_{3}
or
B_{3}
, then there correspond with
s+1
continuous residues modulo
p
in
\tilde{A_{3}}
or
\tilde{B_{3}}
, respectively.
By the analysis above and constructions of
A_{3},\tilde{A_{3}},B_{3},\tilde{B_{3}}
and (
2
), we have

\frac{k\big(|\tilde{A_{3}}|-m\big)}{p}+\frac{|\tilde{B_{3}}|-n}{p}\geq 1,

which clearly means that

\frac{k\big(|\tilde{A_{3}}|-1\big)}{p}+\frac{|\tilde{B_{3}}|-1}{p}\geq 1,

or equivalently

\displaystyle k|\tilde{A_{3}}|-k\geq p-|\tilde{B_{3}}|+1.

(2.12)

We are now in a position to use the Cauchy-Davenport theorem which would be the key ingredient of the proof. Using Lemma
2.1
repeatedly, we obtain that

\displaystyle\underbrace{\big|\tilde{A_{3}}+\tilde{A_{3}}+\cdots+\tilde{A_{3}}\big|}_{k\text{ times}}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ p)\geq\min\big\{p,k\big|\tilde{A_{3}}\big|-k+1\big\}\geq p-\big|\tilde{B_{3}}\big|+2,

where the last inequality follows from (
2.12
). Since there are at most
p-\big|\tilde{B_{3}}\big|
integers (mod
p
) not in
\tilde{B_{3}}
, (
2.11
) is proved now and hence our claim follows.

Finally, let
\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\alpha_{k}^{\prime}\in A_{3}
be the numbers satisfying the claim, i.e.,

\alpha_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}^{\prime}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in B_{3}.

For
1\leq\ell\leq k
, clearly we have
\alpha_{\ell}^{\prime}\in A_{3}\subset A_{2}\subset A_{1}
. Suppose that
\alpha_{\ell}^{\prime}\in(c_{j_{\ell}},d_{j_{\ell}})
for some
j_{\ell}
satisfying
1\leq j_{\ell}\leq m
.
By the assumption of
A_{1}
, we know that
A\cap(c_{j_{\ell}},d_{j_{\ell}})\neq\emptyset
.
If every
\alpha\in A\cap(c_{j_{\ell}},d_{j_{\ell}})
satisfies

\big|\alpha-\alpha_{\ell}^{\prime}\big|>\frac{r}{2k}.

Then it follows that

\mu^{*}(A_{1}\setminus A)\geq\inf_{\alpha}\big|\alpha-\alpha_{\ell}^{\prime}\big|\geq\frac{r}{2k},

which is a contradiction to (
2.4
).
Therefore, for any
\ell\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
there exists some
\alpha_{\ell}\in A
such that

\big|\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{\ell}^{\prime}\big|\leq\frac{r}{2k}.

Thus, by the triangle inequality we have

\big|\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{1}^{\prime}-\cdots-\alpha_{k}^{\prime}\big|\leq\frac{r}{2}<r.

By the constructions of
B_{2}
and
B_{3}
we have

\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\in B,

which completes the proof of our theorem.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers. We need Weyl’s equidistribution theorem
[
8
,
9
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Weyl)
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
and
\rho
be an irrational number. Let
J\subseteq(0,1)
be a finite union of intervals. Then the asymptotic density of the set

H=\big\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\{\rho f(n)\}\in J\big\}

exists and
d(H)=\mu(J)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will prove this conclusion by contradiction. Suppose that there is an infinite
A
with
\overline{d}(A)>\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle\Big|\big\{f(a_{1})+\ldots+f(a_{k}):a_{i}\in A,1\leq i\leq k\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}\Big|<\infty.

Deleting finitely many elements of
A
, we can assume without loss of generality that

\displaystyle\big\{f(a_{1})+\ldots+f(a_{k}):a_{i}\in A,1\leq i\leq k\big\}\cap\big\{\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}=\emptyset.

(3.1)

Let

A_{\alpha}:=\left\{\frac{f(a)}{\alpha}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1):a\in A\right\}.

We claim that

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(A_{\alpha})\leq\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}.

(3.2)

Suppose the contrary, i.e.,
\mu^{*}(A_{\alpha})>\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}
. Then by Theorem
1.2
with
\beta=1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
there are
k
elements
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
of
A_{\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},1\right).

(3.3)

By the definition of
A_{\alpha}
, there are
k
elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
of
A
such that for any
1\leq i\leq k
we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(a_{i})}{\alpha}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)=\alpha_{i}.

(3.4)

By (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(a_{1})}{\alpha}+\cdots+\frac{f(a_{k})}{\alpha}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},1\right).

It follows that there is a positive integer
n
such that

\displaystyle n-\frac{1}{\alpha}<\frac{f(a_{1})}{\alpha}+\cdots+\frac{f(a_{k})}{\alpha}<n.

Hence, we get

f(a_{1})+\cdots+f(a_{k})=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor,

which is a contradiction to (
3.1
). So, the claim (
3.2
) is proved.

Now, by (
3.2
) and the definition of outer Lebesgue measure, for any
\epsilon>0
there exist finitely many open intervals
I_{j}
, where
1\leq j\in J
such that

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}\subset\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq J}I_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu\Big(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq J}I_{j}\Big)<\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}+\varepsilon.

(3.5)

We choose

\varepsilon=\frac{\overline{d}(A)-(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha})}{2},

and now consider

A^{*}=\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{f(n)}{\alpha}\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 1)\in\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq J}I_{j}\right\}.

Then
A\subset A^{*}
since
A_{\alpha}\subset\cup_{1\leq j\leq J}I_{j}
. By Lemma
3.2
we know that the density of
A^{*}
exists and

d(A^{*})=\mu\Big(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq J}I_{j}\Big)<\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k\alpha}+\varepsilon<\overline{d}(A),

which contradicts
A\subset A^{*}
.
∎
