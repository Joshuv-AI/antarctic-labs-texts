Red Man Syndrome Diagnosed in an Orthopedic Patient
Authors: Kwabena Poku‐Yeboah, Patience Adjepong, Akosua Adoma Antwi‐Kwarteng, Urijah Ntiamoah Osei Mensah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRed man syndrome (RMS) is an allergic reaction to vancomycin. Following an intravenous administration of vncomycin, it manifests as a rash on the face, neck, and upper trunk. No such incident has ever been documented in Ghana. Here, we talk about a rare incident of RMS in an orthopedic patient. She received 500 mg of intravenous Vancomycin, which was given over 5 min. She showed signs and symptoms of an allergic reaction 90 s after the administration of the drug and was further resuscitated for about 30 min. She underwent surgery successfully, recovered fully, and was discharged home in a stable condition. Common signs and symptoms of RMS include nausea, malaise, hypotension, and an erythematous rash on the face, arms, and upper trunk. Early resuscitative measures are paramount in preventing severe complications, including cardiac arrest.Keywords: adverse drug reaction, emergency resuscitation, red man syndrome, vancomycin

Summary

Introduction

Vancomycin is a bactericidal antibiotic that inhibits the bacteria's cell wall synthesis, making it suitable against most Gram‐positive cocci like Staphylococcus aureus (S aureus) [1]. S aureus is a common cause of skin, soft tissue, and bone infections such as septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and pyomyositis [2]. Treatment of infections by S. aureus has seen resistance to many antimicrobial drugs, including penicillin, sulphonamides and tetracyclines, but vancomycin has been found potent to treat severe methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [3].

Vancomycin use has been associated with red man syndrome (RMS), also known as vancomycin infusion reaction (VIR), which is the most frequent adverse reaction to intravenous vancomycin [4]. It is an anaphylactoid reaction of a histamine‐mediated hypersensitivity reaction involving mast cells and basophils [5]. The anaphylactoid reaction is not mediated through antibodies, but rather from nonspecific mast cell degranulation; hence, previous exposure cannot necessitate sensitization and may invariably recur on repeated exposure to vancomycin [6]. Opioids and muscle relaxants can trigger histamine release, and hence, RMS is worse in patients who have received such medications. It occurs in about 4% to 50% of patients treated with intravenous vancomycin [7, 8]. This is a clinical diagnosis, and many signs and symptoms have been reported. Some of these include a pruritic and erythematous rash on the face, neck and upper torso; hypotension; angioedema; muscle spasms; and rarely cardiac arrest, if not intervened with early [7, 9, 10, 11, 12]. These are often seen after 4 to 10 min from the start of the first dose of intravenous vancomycin [8, 13].

With the use of vancomycin, no such incidence has been noted in Ghana. This case report shows how the first reported diagnosis of red man syndrome was made in Ghana.

Case History

History

We present a 29‐year‐old woman who has been experiencing pain in her left thigh for over 6 weeks, which has now increased in intensity. Pain was dull, and she graded the pain as moderate. However, she was still ambulant. She has never been diagnosed with any medical conditions. She has no allergies to any known medication or food.

There was a bulge, measuring 16 cm by 10 cm, on the anterolateral side of her left thigh. The midthigh circumference on the left was 73 cm, and on the right, it was 61 cm. The bulge was firm with an overlying healed sinus tract. Her white cell count was increased with differential neutrophilia. X‐ray of the thigh showed increased soft tissue intensity with no bony involvement, and an ultrasound of the left thigh confirmed hypoechoic areas with irregular borders within the affected muscles. She was seen by the Orthopedic Surgery team on duty. After further assessment, she was diagnosed with left thigh pyomyositis and was scheduled to have an incision and drainage as this was rapidly progressing.

Treatment

The patient underwent surgery to drain the collected abscess. The patient had fasted from both solids and fluids for 4 h. Prehydration was done with 0.5 L of 0.9% Sodium chloride (normal saline). When the patient was brought to the operating theater, monitoring was established. Initial vital signs read were pulse 84 beats per minute (bpm), respiratory rate (RR) 18 cycles per minute (cpm), blood pressure (BP) 121/74 mmHg, saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2) 100% in room air, temperature 37.0 degrees Celsius and a normal sinus rhythm was shown on the monitor. Also, 10 mg (mg) of intravenous (IV) metoclopramide and 1 mg of IV granisetron were given as antiemetic prophylaxis, as the patient's fasting time was inadequate. Ten milliliters (ml) of NS was added to a vial of 500 mg powdered vancomycin as an empiric antibiotic for the infection and given via a peripheral vein intermittently over 5 min and flushed with NS (at a rate of 100 mg/min). Before this administration, no opioids had been given. At about 90 s after completing the injection, the patient's vitals now read pulse 120 bpm, BP 85/49 mmHg, RR 32 cpm. The patient complained of nausea and general malaise, and she also developed flushing on the cheeks, a swollen face and started developing wheals on her trunk. However, the patient was still communicating but felt exhausted. No clinical sign of bronchospasm was noted. One liter of NS was run immediately, and 200 mg IV hydrocortisone was given. An extra 1 L of NS was given. The patient's vital signs were back to the normal range after 30 min of resuscitation, with the rash disappearing. No antihistamines were given.

Subarachnoid block was given with a 25‐gauge spinal needle using subcutaneous lidocaine for local infiltration, intrathecal (IT) bupivacaine and IT fentanyl into the L3/L4 space. The sensory level was up to the T8 dermatome. There was a healed sinus tract in the midsubtrochanteric region and an active sinus tract communicating with the posterior greater trochanter, which had been eroded. No fluid or pus was seen. Via a lateral approach, the sinus tract was excised to the femur. Curettage was done, and the wound was irrigated with hydrogen peroxide solution and sodium chloride. The wound was sutured with Vicryl 2 to the subcutaneous tissue and nylon 2‐0 to the skin. The patient's postoperative recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged home in a stable condition on the second day following surgery.

Discussion

Vancomycin infusion reaction (VIR) is a non‐IgE‐mediated infusion reaction characterized by histamine release from mast cells and basophils, leading to erythema, flushing, pruritus, and hypotension [14, 15]. It is typically associated with rapid infusion of vancomycin and does not require prior sensitization. This contrasts with IgE‐mediated hypersensitivity reactions from vancomycin, which involve prior exposure, mast cell degranulation via IgE crosslinking, and often include bronchospasm, urticaria, and angioedema [13, 15].

The differential diagnosis in our patient included anesthetic‐induced hypotension, anaphylaxis to intraoperative drugs, or even sepsis‐related shock. However, the temporal association with vancomycin administration, absence of bronchospasm or airway involvement, and rapid resolution with corticosteroids and fluids without epinephrine or antihistamines favored a diagnosis of VIR [4]. Antihistamines were unavailable in the operating theater at the time of the reaction, underscoring a resource limitation that can impact emergency drug administration in low‐resource settings. Unlike most documented cases where VIR follows infusion rates of 10 mg/min over 60 min, our patient received a rapid bolus infusion of 500 mg vancomycin over 5 min at 100 mg/min, which is about 10 times the recommended rate, a practice not commonly reported in the literature and possibly more prevalent in low‐resource settings. This reflects not only a clinical oversight but also exposes a critical knowledge–practice gap in antibiotic administration in such settings. The lack of standardized protocols, inadequate staff training, and limited access to essential emergency medications highlight a significant knowledge–practice gap in antibiotic administration in low‐resource settings.

Southorn et al. reported 5 cases of VIR that occurred preoperatively after intravenous vancomycin infusion as a prophylactic antibiotic. Unlike our patient, all five patients underwent cardiac surgeries and were allergic to penicillin [12]. Similarly, the onset of signs and symptoms began within 4 to 10 min after administration. The common symptoms they identified were nausea, hypotension and a rash on the face, arms and upper trunk, as we also identified in our patient. However, as part of their resuscitative measures, they received steroids and antihistamines [16]. Total recovery from the signs and symptoms occurred within 20 min to 4 h. Despite the occurrence of VIR, the planned surgeries for the patients were carried out [12].

It has been found that VIR most frequently occurs with intravenous vancomycin, at a rapid infusion rate of 1 g in less than an hour, and rarely from oral or intraperitoneal [17, 18, 19]. However, VIR has been diagnosed in a 79‐year‐old woman who took 250 mg four times daily orally. It is recommended to be administered at a rate no faster than 1 g per hour or 10 mg per minute [8, 13, 20]. It has been reported that rapid, continuous IV administration in small doses of vancomycin with progressively increasing dosage is an alternative option in patients who do not respond to slowing of the infusion rate. Longer infusion time may be required in patients requiring higher doses of vancomycin [8, 21]. The recommended loading dose is 15 to 20 mg/kg in a volume of 250 mL NS with an infusion time of 2 h [22].

Similar to managing most allergies, these protocols may be considered in VIR. The patient should be nursed in the Trendelenburg position, with supplementary oxygen. The surgery may be postponed if symptoms persist even with adequate resuscitation [23]. Pretreatment with antihistamines such as diphenhydramine may be appropriate. In adults, a 500 to 1000 mg dose of vancomycin should be diluted in 100 to 200 mL of isotonic saline or 5% dextrose and infused for at least 60 min [9]. The initial dose of vancomycin should be administered with qualified supervision, and the patient should be observed for adverse reactions before anesthesia is induced [5]. This was something that was observed in our patient. The subarachnoid block was delayed until the administration of the antibiotic. Repeated intraoperative administration of vancomycin should not be less than 6 h. It is imperative that when VIR develops, the ongoing IV antibiotic infusion should be stopped immediately and supportive care provided [12, 24, 25, 26].

Usually, most episodes resolve within 20 min, and vancomycin infusion may be restarted slowly [8]. Although our patient received the full dose of vancomycin, her symptoms resolved within 20 min of resuscitation. Further doses may be given at a decreased infusion rate. The best preventive measure to avoid VIR is maintaining infusion rates below 10 mg/min. If alternative antibiotics are available, they should be resorted to [14, 25]. With our patient, vancomycin was switched to IV Clindamycin 600 mg 6 hourly, for 5 days, and then discharged home. Since VIR is non‐IgE‐mediated, it is therefore not considered a true medical allergy and should not be mistaken as such, as it can lead to unnecessary avoidance of an effective antibiotic, potentially worsening outcomes in settings with limited antibiotic options [14, 23]. It can be reused safely [24].

Finally, the absence of routine pharmacovigilance or adverse drug reaction reporting mechanisms in many Ghanaian health facilities may contribute to the underreporting of such events. Given the increasing use of vancomycin in tertiary hospitals due to rising antimicrobial resistance, this case underlines the urgent need to develop local guidelines and training on the recognition and management of vancomycin‐related hypersensitivity reactions.

Conclusion

VIR, though not a medical allergy, can occur repeatedly. It occurs either through rapid infusion, as in our patient or slow release. Physicians need to realize that vancomycin can cause hypotension, especially in the perioperative period. Adequate resuscitation, including administration of an antihistamine and steroids, may help manage VIR.
